## Chapter 113: Little Brother

Clarisse knew she was getting sick when the practice spear felt heavy.

That annoyed her more than the headache. The spear was ash wood with a blunted tip, lighter than the weapon she usually carried. She had balanced it in her hand often enough that she could tell when somebody had sanded the shaft unevenly. This morning it felt as though someone had put a stone inside it.

She changed her grip and blamed the weather.

The arena was cold beneath a gray sky, and a wet wind had come up from the Sound. Two younger campers were setting shields against the practice posts. Another was trying to pull a cart loaded with training armor over the uneven ground. Clarisse watched the cart stop at the first patch of mud and shouted for somebody to put a plank down before they broke the axle.

"Morning," Percy said behind her.

"You're late."

"I'm six minutes early."

"Then I suppose the rest of you are late by association."

He grinned and came to stand beside her. He was carrying a bundle of rope under one arm and a pair of practice gloves in the other. He looked more rested than he had a few days ago, which was irritating when she felt as if she had slept under a pile of stones.

"What's the plan?" he asked.

"Four stations. Shield control, footwork, rescue carry and an obstacle course. Each team rotates when I blow the whistle."

"Who's scoring?"

"I am."

"By yourself?"

"Chiron will check the numbers afterward."

Percy watched the younger campers struggle with the cart.

"Can I help with the stations?"

"Move that first shield line six feet farther from the archery wall. I don't want anybody ricocheting a practice spear into somebody who's drawing a bow."

"Got it."

He went to work without arguing. Clarisse was glad of that. They had spent most of yesterday fixing equipment, and he seemed to have accepted that being the youngest didn't disqualify him from useful jobs. She liked giving him things to do, especially when he didn't insist on turning every instruction into a debate.

She went to the center of the arena and blew the whistle.

The sound stabbed behind her eyes.

One of the younger campers winced. Clarisse snapped at him to get his helmet on. She could hear that her voice was too sharp, but she couldn't make herself soften it. She had a schedule, and the first group had already lost time to the muddy cart.

At the shield station, she demonstrated how to absorb an impact without locking the elbow. She lifted the practice spear and drove it against the padded shield. The camper holding it stumbled backward, more surprised by her force than hurt.

"Feet apart," she ordered. "Don't put your weight on your heels."

She demonstrated again. This time the impact jarred up her arm, and the arena seemed to tilt by a fraction. She adjusted her stance before anyone noticed.

Except Percy did.

He was standing by the rope markers, watching her with a look she recognized from the previous week. It was the expression he made when he was trying to solve a problem without embarrassing the person who had it.

Clarisse turned away.

The next station went well enough. A girl from Hermes surprised her by recovering from a slip without losing her shield, and Clarisse told her so. Another camper tried to carry a weighted dummy alone and nearly fell. Clarisse called a halt, explained why the exercise required two people, and watched him try again with a partner.

Then the sun came through a break in the clouds. The light struck her eyes with an intensity that made her stomach turn. She gripped the whistle and waited for the spinning to stop.

"Clarisse?"

Percy was close enough that she could hear the concern beneath his ordinary voice.

"What?"

"Your face looks strange."

"Thank you."

"I mean you're pale."

"I'm standing in the cold."

"It's not that cold."

"Go check the rope markers."

"I already did."

"Then check them again."

He looked at her for another second, then walked away. Clarisse blew the whistle for the next rotation. The sound made her wince, and this time she couldn't hide it.

At the far end of the arena, Silena had arrived with a crate of fresh padding from the supply shed. She set it down and watched Clarisse with narrowed eyes.

Clarisse pretended not to notice.

By the time the second rotation ended, her knees were shaking. She told herself it was because she'd been on her feet since dawn. She had trained through bruises, sprains and worse. A headache wasn't going to stop her supervising a group of children.

She started toward the obstacle course to correct a badly tied knot.

The ground dipped beneath her foot. She caught herself on a post, but not before the world gave a sickening lurch. One of the younger campers stopped in the middle of the course.

"Are you okay?" he asked.

Clarisse opened her mouth to say yes.

Silena reached her first and put a hand against her forehead.

"Oh, you're burning up."

"Don't."

"You're feverish."

"I'm working."

"Not anymore."

Clarisse stepped away. The air felt too bright, and Silena's hand had been cold enough to make her want it back.

"Finish the last station," Clarisse told Percy, who had come up beside them. "I'll sit down for a minute."

"No," Silena said.

Clarisse turned on her. "You don't give orders in my arena."

"I do when you can't stand straight."

"I'm standing."

"You're holding on to the post."

Clarisse realized her hand was still closed around the wood. She let go. The motion made her sway, which was considerably worse than the post.

Percy caught her elbow without making a show of it.

"I'm getting Will," he said.

"Don't you dare."

He looked at her. For a moment she thought he'd obey. He had been the little brother who fetched tools and carried ropes and listened when she told him to move a marker. She could still make him angry by reminding him he was the youngest.

Then Percy said, "I'm getting Will."

He didn't shout. He didn't argue. He turned and ran toward the infirmary.

Clarisse would have gone after him if she hadn't been so busy trying not to be sick on her own boots.

---

Will Solace arrived with a medical bag and the expression of someone who had already been told the patient would be difficult.

He didn't start by ordering Clarisse to bed. He asked how long she'd felt ill, whether she'd eaten, whether the dizziness was new and whether she had any trouble breathing. Clarisse answered the first two questions badly and the last two with more honesty than she wanted.

"Sit down," he said.

"I'm sitting."

"On the bench, not on a spear crate."

"What's wrong with the crate?"

"It has sharp edges and you're dizzy."

Clarisse sat on the bench. Will checked her temperature and pulse, then asked whether she was willing to drink some water.

"I know how to drink water."

"Good. That'll save us time."

Percy brought the cup. Clarisse took it from him with a glare and drank. The water settled badly in her stomach, but it stayed down.

Will listened to her breathing and checked her throat. He didn't pretend he could diagnose everything from one look. He said she had a fever and needed to stop training, rest, keep drinking fluids and be watched for symptoms that required more attention. He would check on her again later.

"Can I finish the judging from a chair?" she asked.

"Not today."

"It's a training assessment."

"Then someone else can assess the training."

"Nobody knows the plan."

"I do," Percy said.

She turned toward him. "You know the stations."

"I helped set them up. I've got your scoring sheet. You told me how the rotations worked."

"You don't know what I'm looking for."

"Then tell me."

Clarisse stared at him. He was trying to keep his voice steady, but he looked worried. He'd been worried about Atlantis recently and had still come to training. He had obeyed most of her orders. Now he was refusing the one order that would let her pretend she was fine.

Silena spoke before Clarisse could say anything cruel.

"Chiron can supervise. Percy can read the scores. I'll help the younger campers with the padding. Nobody needs you to prove you're strong by passing out in front of them."

Clarisse hated that argument because she could already hear how it would sound if a different camper were the one making it.

Will rose. "Can you walk to the infirmary?"

"Yes."

"With someone beside you?"

"I don't need an escort."

"That wasn't the question."

She glared at him. He waited.

"Fine."

Percy stepped forward, but Clarisse shook her head.

"You've got the assessment. Finish it."

He glanced at Will, who nodded.

"All right," Percy said. "I will."

Clarisse hadn't expected the answer to sting. She wanted him to stay and wanted him to do exactly what she'd told him, both at the same time. It was an unreasonable pair of wants. She was too ill to sort them out.

Silena took her arm. Together they crossed the arena while Chiron came down the path to take over the session.

Clarisse heard Percy calling the next rotation as they left. His voice was younger and less intimidating than hers. The younger campers still obeyed.

She tried not to be annoyed by that.

---

The infirmary smelled of clean linen, bitter herbs and a faint trace of the soap Will insisted everybody use after changing bandages.

Clarisse disliked it on principle. It was a place where other people decided when she could get up. The bed was too narrow, the pillow was too high, and the window looked toward the practice field but didn't open far enough for her to hear the commands.

Will checked her again and said she should stay until the fever settled and her dizziness improved. It might be a short illness, but he wasn't going to guess a timetable just to make her feel better.

Silena helped her sit up long enough to drink a little tea. Clarisse pushed it away after two sips.

"I don't want that."

"It's not a punishment," Silena said.

"It tastes like bark."

"It is partly bark."

"That explains the problem."

Silena set the cup where Clarisse could reach it. She didn't demand she finish. Then she took out a notebook and began making a list.

Clarisse narrowed her eyes. "What are you doing?"

"Writing down the things that still have to be done today."

"I can tell you."

"I know. That's why I'm asking."

Clarisse closed her eyes for a moment and went through the list. The assessment scores needed to go to Chiron. The practice shields should be inspected before they were put away. The torn padding needed replacing. Someone had to tell the younger campers when their results would be posted.

Silena wrote each item down, then marked the ones Percy and Chiron were already handling.

"And tomorrow?" she asked.

"Tomorrow I'll do it."

Silena looked at her. "If you're well enough."

"I will be."

"That's not something you can decide."

Clarisse pulled the blanket higher. She was cold again, and she hated the shaking that made it obvious.

"Fine. If I'm well enough."

Silena wrote that down too.

"Now," Clarisse said, "what were you planning to do today before you came to rescue me?"

Silena blinked. "What?"

"You had paint on your cuff. You were working."

"I was supposed to deliver a sign to the garden room. Katie wants to hang it before the new beds open."

"Then go."

"Clarisse, you're sick."

"I noticed."

"I can stay."

"You can also finish the thing you spent three days painting."

Silena looked toward the door. Clarisse saw the conflict on her face: the desire to stay with a sick sister and the frustration of putting her own work aside again because somebody needed something.

"Will's here," Clarisse said. "I'm not going to fall out of the bed and drown in the mattress."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't intended to be."

Silena set her notebook down. "I'll go and come back after."

"Good."

"And Percy will come by when the assessment is finished."

"Good."

Silena hesitated. Then she leaned over and adjusted the blanket at Clarisse's feet, tucking it in so it wouldn't drag across the floor.

"Drink some water while I'm gone," she said.

"Yes, Mother."

Silena stopped.

Clarisse immediately regretted the words. Amphitrite had been kind to Percy in a way neither Clarisse nor Silena experienced in exactly the same way. They had talked about that recently, about the danger of treating every difference in their household as an insult. Silena wasn't trying to be anybody's mother. She was being a sister, and Clarisse had made it sound like an accusation.

"Silena," she said.

Silena looked back.

"I meant you're bossy."

"I know what you meant."

"Not exactly."

Silena held her gaze. Clarisse rarely apologized well, and she wasn't improving while her head felt full of hot stones.

"Go deliver the sign," she said finally. "I want to see it hung up when I can walk there."

Silena's expression softened. "You will."

She left. Clarisse settled against the pillow and stared at the ceiling. She felt worse without anybody beside the bed. That didn't mean she'd been wrong to send Silena away.

It did mean she wished being sick came with fewer stupid feelings.

---

Percy discovered two things during the third rotation.

The first was that Clarisse's scoring sheet was much harder to use than it looked. She had made small marks beside each station, and some of them were obvious enough: a checked square for a completed rescue carry, a line for the number of successful shield blocks. Other marks made sense only to her. One looked like a fishbone. Another was a circle with a slash through it and the letters L.F.

The second thing he discovered was that telling younger campers to do something safely was not the same as making them listen.

"Stop," he called as a boy from Hermes tried to drag a weighted dummy across the arena by one wrist.

The boy continued moving.

"Stop the exercise."

"I've almost got it!"

"You're supposed to lift with a partner."

"I can do it."

"That's not the point."

The dummy's shoulder caught on a stone. The boy tugged harder, lost his footing and sat down heavily in the mud. Nobody was hurt. Percy had still let the situation go too long.

Chiron came over from the shield station.

"What did your sister tell them?" he asked.

"Two-person carry. One person supports the shoulders, the other the knees. The team gets marked down if they drag."

"And what did you do?"

"I told him to stop."

"Once?"

"Twice."

"Then the exercise should have stopped before the second attempt. Call the station to a halt, reset the rules and have them demonstrate the safe method before they begin."

Percy looked at the boy, who was getting up with an expression of wounded pride. He didn't like admitting that he'd hesitated. He had spent years in memories of fights where a hesitation could cost much more than an embarrassing fall. Here, in a younger body, he had to remember that being kind sometimes meant speaking firmly before somebody learned a lesson the hard way.

He blew the whistle.

The sound cut across the arena. Every camper stopped, including two who were doing their station correctly.

"Reset," Percy called. "Everyone back to the starting marks. We're going over the carry again."

A few campers groaned. He let them. Chiron stayed beside him while he demonstrated the hold with a volunteer.

"This is what Clarisse wanted you to learn," Percy said. "Not how fast you can get the dummy to the end. How to move someone who can't move themselves without making the injury worse."

A girl from Apollo raised her hand. "Are we losing points?"

"You lose points if you ignore the safety instruction after I repeat it. You don't lose points because I stopped to explain it better."

Chiron nodded once. Percy felt a little steadier.

The next run went better. The boy from Hermes completed the carry with a partner and didn't look at Percy when they finished. Percy marked the result anyway.

He was halfway through entering the scores when he realized the fishbone symbol meant a stance correction Clarisse had given earlier. He went back through the sheet and adjusted the marks where he'd misunderstood it. That took time, and the last group had to wait.

When the rotation ended, he told the campers the results would be posted later. He wasn't about to give somebody the wrong score just because he'd been embarrassed to ask.

Chiron took the sheets and checked them beside the arena gate.

"Your sister runs a disciplined session," he said.

"She does."

"You kept it safe. You also needed help."

Percy shrugged. "I noticed."

"Both facts can be true."

Percy looked toward the path to the infirmary. He wanted to go at once, but the last practice shields still had to be inspected. Clarisse would have expected him to finish the job. She would also have told him not to pretend that finishing it made him indispensable.

He asked one of the older campers to help check the straps. They found a cracked buckle on the third shield and marked it out of service. Then Percy carried the reports to Chiron, returned the whistle to the supply room and headed toward the infirmary.

His sister had trusted him to finish. He had made mistakes, fixed them and finished.

That was what he could tell her.

---

Silena arrived at the garden room with a painted sign under one arm and an uneven hammer in her hand.

Katie Gardner was kneeling beside a row of new seedlings, pressing soil around their stems. She sat back on her heels when Silena came through the gate.

"You finished it!"

"Almost. The paint's dry, but I want to check where you're hanging it before I put the last screw in."

Katie wiped her hands on her apron and came closer. The sign showed the garden path under morning light, with the names of the new beds written along the lower edge. Silena had kept the lettering small enough that the vines were still the main part of the design.

"It's beautiful," Katie said.

"It's useful, I hope."

"Both. We keep getting campers who walk through the planting rows because they can't tell where the path starts."

Silena followed her to the gate and studied the two posts. One had been repaired recently. The other leaned slightly outward.

"That one won't hold a heavy sign," she said.

"I was afraid of that."

"We can use the fence rail, but the screws need to go through the wider wood."

Charlie arrived a few minutes later with the right drill bit and a short board he'd cut to fit the space behind the rail. He had promised to help earlier in the week, before Clarisse became ill. Silena was relieved he hadn't forgotten, and a little embarrassed by how relieved she felt.

"I thought you'd be at the forge all afternoon," she said.

"I moved the hinge repair to tomorrow." He set down the tools. "This won't take long if the holes line up."

Silena held the sign while he checked the measurements. Katie went to fetch a smaller wrench. For a moment Silena and Charlie were alone by the gate.

"Everything all right with Clarisse?" he asked.

"Will says she needs rest. She hates the infirmary."

"Most people do."

"She's going to pretend she could have finished the training assessment herself."

"She probably could have. Right up until she fell over."

Silena smiled despite herself. "Don't let her hear you say that."

"I won't."

He checked the last measurement and stepped back. "You made the color of the leaves darker along this edge."

"To make them look raised."

"It works."

Silena looked down at the sign, then at him. "You noticed."

"I spend a lot of time looking at things people have made. I try to notice when somebody knows what they're doing."

He said it plainly, without teasing. That made it harder for her to decide what to say next.

"Thank you," she managed.

Charlie tapped the rail. "Hold it there. I'll drill."

They hung the sign without incident. Katie stood back and smiled at it from the garden path. The letters were readable, the vine looked alive in the afternoon light, and the board didn't move when she tested it with two fingers.

Silena felt a small, fierce pleasure. The work was finished. It had a purpose outside the cabin, outside her brother's worries, outside the endless sequence of things she could do for people she loved.

Then she thought about Clarisse in the infirmary and felt guilty.

Katie noticed her expression. "You need to go?"

"My sister's sick."

"Then go. The sign's done. Thank you."

Charlie gathered the tools. "I'll bring the small brush you left at the forge when I come back."

"You don't have to."

"I know." He looked at the sign once more. "I'd like to."

Silena was smiling when she headed down the path. She couldn't remember the last time she'd finished something without mentally apologizing to somebody else for the time she'd spent on it. She hoped Clarisse would want to come see the sign when she was better.

For now, the fact that she could leave a finished piece of her own work standing in the garden was enough.

---

Percy found the infirmary quiet except for the movement of someone washing bowls in the far room.

Will was writing notes at a desk. He looked up when Percy came in, then pointed toward the second bed.

"She's awake. Don't give her anything without checking first."

"Wasn't planning to."

"Good. And don't stay long enough to exhaust her."

Percy nodded and went to the bed.

Clarisse was lying with her arms folded over the blanket and an expression that suggested she was waiting for somebody to challenge her. Her hair had escaped its braid. A few strands were stuck to her forehead. Percy had seen her bruised and bleeding after training before, and the sight had never been easy. Fever was worse in a different way. There was nothing obvious to fight, no opponent to force back, and Clarisse couldn't insult it into leaving.

"How'd it go?" she asked.

"We finished."

"Scores?"

"With Chiron. They'll be posted when he's checked them."

"Any injuries?"

"No. One kid fell in the mud. I should have stopped his station sooner."

Clarisse looked at him sharply. "What happened?"

He told her. He described the boy pulling the dummy, the fall and the reset, including the part where Chiron had intervened because Percy hadn't ended the exercise quickly enough.

Clarisse listened without interruption.

"You made the right call on the reset," she said when he finished. "But you can't let someone keep going after you've said stop. If they don't hear you, blow the whistle."

"I know now."

"Don't make me repeat it."

"I won't."

"Did you understand the scoring marks?"

"Most of them."

"Most?"

"What does L.F. mean?"

"Left foot. He keeps turning it in and losing his balance."

"Oh."

"And the fishbone?"

"Stance correction. I worked that out."

"Eventually."

He laughed. "Eventually."

Clarisse shifted against the pillow and winced. Percy saw it, and she saw him seeing it.

"Don't start fussing," she said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You have the expression."

"Maybe I'm trying to figure out whether I can borrow your whistle."

"If you touch my whistle, I'll throw this pillow at you."

"It's an excellent threat, but Will says you need to rest."

Clarisse glared. Percy smiled at her. For a minute they were simply brother and sister again, talking about an arena job they both understood. Then the smile slipped from her face.

"Did Silena go?" she asked.

"To the garden room. Yeah. She'll come back."

"Good."

"She said you told her to finish the sign."

"I did."

Percy reached for the cup beside the bed and checked whether it still had water. It was nearly empty. He refilled it from the pitcher.

"Have some," he said.

Clarisse accepted it, annoyed at herself for being thirsty.

"Where's Annabeth?" she asked.

"Working on the beach map. She said she'd come by later if you're awake."

"She doesn't have to."

"I know. She's your friend. She gets to decide whether she wants to."

Clarisse took a drink. That sounded suspiciously like the sort of thing she had been telling him lately. She didn't appreciate having her own advice handed back to her.

"You're getting clever," she said.

"I've had good teachers."

"Don't flatter me."

"I wasn't."

She nearly smiled.

Percy sat for a few minutes while she finished the water. He didn't insist on talking when she closed her eyes, and he didn't pretend she was asleep the moment her breathing slowed. He'd learned that Clarisse could be quiet without being angry.

When Will came to check her again, Percy got up.

"You don't have to stay," Clarisse said.

"I know."

"You've got other things."

"I do. Silena asked whether I could bring the soup from the kitchen later, if Will says you can have it."

"Soup."

"Try to contain your enthusiasm."

She rolled her eyes. "Fine. Bring the soup."

"Any requests?"

"Something that doesn't taste like the infirmary smells."

"That's a very broad specification."

"Work with it."

Percy went out grinning. Clarisse watched him leave and was startled by how much she wished he'd been able to stay five minutes longer.

She pulled the blanket up, closed her eyes and tried to sleep before she changed her mind and called him back.

---

Percy had made soup before. He had even made edible soup before, though Clarisse would have disputed the definition if anybody asked her.

The camp kitchen was nearly empty when he arrived. Two satyrs were washing pots, and a girl from Demeter was sorting herbs into bundles. Silena stood at the counter with a notebook and a small pot set over the lowest flame.

"Did Will say she can have soup?" she asked.

"Yes. Small portions, nothing heavy."

"Then we can do this."

"Why do you sound nervous?"

"Because you said you wanted to make it yourself."

"I have an excellent record."

"The last time you said that, you put three spoonfuls of salt into a stew because you thought you were using sugar."

"In my defense, they were in identical bowls."

"One had SALT written on it."

"On the side facing away from me."

Silena shook her head and pushed the knife toward him. "Chop these. Small pieces, not the enormous ones you like."

He cut the vegetables while she measured broth into the pot. When he tried to add everything at once, she stopped his hand and explained why some pieces needed longer to soften. He listened. This wasn't a contest, and he wasn't trying to impress anybody by pretending to know things he didn't.

Silena tasted the broth and pointed to the herbs. "One pinch."

"Mine or yours?"

"Mine."

"That's discrimination."

"It's accuracy."

He added the herbs. The soup smelled better as it warmed. Percy stirred slowly, watching the pieces soften.

"Did Katie like the sign?" he asked.

"She did. It's hanging in the garden now."

"Charlie helped?"

"He put in the mounting board."

Percy glanced up. "Did he?"

"Do not."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to."

He smiled into the pot. She caught his expression and shook her head.

After a while, he asked the question he had been saving. "Are you upset with Clarisse?"

Silena kept her eyes on the broth. "Why?"

"She sounded annoyed when she asked if you'd gone. I don't know exactly what happened."

"She called me Mother. Not in a particularly kind way."

"Oh."

"She's sick and angry about it. I know."

"That doesn't mean it was fair."

"No," Silena said. "I do things for both of you because I want to. Sometimes people forget I have things to do for myself as well."

Percy nodded. He had needed her help with the soup. She had given it, even after spending most of the afternoon on a painting project. He didn't want to confuse her generosity with an obligation.

"You can go if you've got other things," he said.

"I'm here because I want to finish this. Don't start assigning me free time, either."

He laughed. "Right."

She handed the spoon back. "Taste it."

He did. It was salty in the good way and a little thin. He considered adding something.

Silena pointed at him. "Don't."

He closed his mouth.

She ladled a portion into a covered bowl and wrapped a cloth around the base so it was safe to carry. Percy took it with both hands.

"Thanks for helping," he said.

"You're welcome."

"You did most of the work."

"I told you what to do. You did it. That's how teaching works."

He looked at her. She said it as a simple fact. Maybe being the youngest didn't mean waiting for someone older to fix everything; maybe it meant being shown how to start and then being expected to try.

Silena tucked a spoon beside the bowl. "Go on, before you eat it yourself."

"I'd never."

"Percy."

"I'll resist."

He left with the soup warming his hands.

---

Clarisse smelled the soup before she saw him.

She had spent the previous hour trying to read the training report Chiron had sent with a note to leave it alone until she was well. She had read the first page twice and kept losing her place. At last she'd folded it under her pillow so Will wouldn't see.

Percy came in carrying the covered bowl as though he were transporting a fragile artifact.

"Delivery," he announced.

"Did you steal it?"

"Only from the kitchen."

"Then it's stolen."

"Will approved the operation."

Will, changing a water pitcher at the next bed, looked over. "I approved a small portion, if she wants it."

Clarisse wanted it. She hadn't realized how hungry she was until the smell reached her.

Percy lifted the lid. Vegetables floated in a clear broth, cut into pieces just small enough for her to eat without making a mess.

"Looks suspicious," she said.

"I didn't poison it."

"That's what someone who poisoned soup would say."

He held out the spoon. She took it to prove she could. Her hand shook a little as she lifted it. Percy steadied the tray so the bowl wouldn't slide. He didn't reach for the spoon or try to feed her.

The first mouthful was warm and surprisingly good, like the kitchen at the end of a long training day when someone had saved the hot food because she'd been late. There was salt, onion and a little sharpness from the herbs. Not perfect, but good.

She took another spoonful.

"You made this?"

"With Silena."

"How much did she make?"

"She supervised."

"So most of it."

"She says this counts as teaching."

Clarisse studied him over the bowl. He looked hopeful and tired. There was a pale smudge along one cheek, and his hair refused to lie flat. She wanted to tease him. She also didn't want to ruin the look on his face.

"It's good," she said.

Percy blinked. "Really?"

"Don't make me repeat it."

He grinned. She went on eating.

When the bowl was half empty, he asked how she felt. She said fine, which was a lie. He looked at her but didn't argue. Instead he told her about the assessment, including the mistakes he'd made and how the younger campers had behaved afterward. He didn't claim he'd run it as well as she would have.

"They listened to you," she said.

"Eventually."

"They're new. They listen to anybody holding a whistle."

"That was painfully clear."

She laughed, then coughed and had to stop. Percy reached toward her water. She took the cup herself.

"Thanks," she said when the cough settled.

There was something humiliating about being seen this way: too weak to lift a bowl without it rattling against the tray. Clarisse had built so much of her life around being the person who could stand when other people fell that she didn't know what to do with lying down.

"Don't you have somewhere to be?" she asked.

"Annabeth's checking the beach map. I'll see her later."

"You should go."

"Do you want me to?"

The easy answer was yes. She could send him away and recover her pride, then spend the afternoon resenting the empty chair. He had given her a chance to be honest without demanding honesty.

"Not yet," she said.

"Okay."

He sat back.

"Tell me about the fishbone mark," Percy said.

"It's a reminder about footwork."

"Why a fishbone?"

"Because that's what the stance looks like when somebody gets it wrong."

"That is extremely unkind."

"It's accurate."

He laughed. She explained the rest of her shorthand while he listened. When Will came to check her temperature, Percy got up.

"You don't have to stay," Clarisse said.

"I know."

"You've got other things."

"I do. I'll come back if you're up for company."

She nodded.

The soup was almost gone when he left. Clarisse had eaten it herself. That mattered more than she expected.

---

Annabeth visited with a book, not a medicine bottle.

She'd checked with Will before coming in, and he'd told her Clarisse was awake but tired. Annabeth took a chair beside the bed and opened a battered history of the Big House she'd borrowed from the library.

Clarisse watched her for two full minutes.

"Are you going to read to me?" she asked.

"Only if you ask."

"Then why are you here?"

"To sit with you. I can read while you rest."

Clarisse looked at the book. "Architecture?"

"History. Mostly building history."

"You and Percy deserve each other. He thinks old repair reports are entertaining."

"Sometimes they are."

"That's horrifying."

Annabeth smiled and turned the page. Clarisse wanted to keep talking, but the room was warm, and it was easier than she expected to listen to someone else breathe.

After a while she said, "You love my brother."

Annabeth looked up. She didn't make a show of considering it.

"Yes."

"And he loves you."

"Yes."

Clarisse nodded. She had watched the two of them through arguments, awkward group meals and quiet walks. She knew their relationship wasn't based on both of them being cheerful all the time. They liked each other when nobody was watching and could disagree without the other person leaving.

"Good," she said. "He's a good kid."

"I know."

"He deserves somebody who tells him when he's being an idiot."

"I do that already."

"Keep doing it."

Annabeth closed the book over one finger.

"Clarisse, you know he doesn't stop being your brother because he loves me, right?"

Clarisse's face heated for a reason that had nothing to do with the fever.

"I know."

"Good."

Annabeth reopened the book.

Clarisse stared at her. "That's it?"

"That's it."

"You're not going to tell me I deserve people who care about me too?"

"Would you like me to?"

"No."

"Then I won't."

Clarisse tried to hold on to the glare. It didn't work. She laughed, then winced at the pain in her throat.

Annabeth turned another page. After several minutes she asked if Clarisse wanted to hear about an older version of the Big House roof that had been built at the wrong angle and leaked for years.

Clarisse considered. It sounded ridiculous. It also sounded a little like their own cabin.

"Fine," she said. "But if you start listing measurements, I'm going to fall asleep."

Annabeth started reading.

Clarisse did fall asleep a little later. It wasn't pretend.

---

By morning, the fever had not vanished. Will checked Clarisse again and said she was improving, but still needed rest and fluids. No arena. No long walks. No dramatic return to duty because she'd managed to keep down two bowls of soup.

Clarisse objected to every part and obeyed it anyway.

Silena came after breakfast with news from the garden room. The sign was up. Katie had sent fresh mint and a thank-you note. Clarisse made Silena describe where they'd hung it and whether the painted vine looked crooked from the path.

"It looks good," Silena said.

"I'll judge when I see it."

"You can judge when Will says you can walk that far."

"Everybody keeps saying that."

"Everybody keeps being right."

Clarisse turned the mint sprig between her fingers. It smelled sharp and clean. Silena had brought something from outside the infirmary, something she'd made while Clarisse was stuck in bed. Clarisse was glad to hear about it, and ashamed of the small part of her that had wanted Silena to stay miserable beside her until she was better.

"I'm glad you went," she said.

Silena looked surprised.

"I mean the sign. I want to see it."

Silena sat beside her. "You will."

They didn't talk about the remark Clarisse had made the day before. Not yet. But Silena stayed a little longer, and Clarisse didn't ask her to prove anything by canceling the rest of her afternoon.

---

The second day in bed was worse than the first, not because Clarisse was sicker but because she was awake enough to be bored.

Will had moved her out of the infirmary and back to Cabin Three once her symptoms were steady and she no longer needed continuous observation. He had made Silena repeat the instructions: rest, keep drinking, return if the fever climbed or breathing became difficult, and do not treat one decent afternoon as permission to restart training.

Clarisse had said she understood.

By noon, she'd reorganized the tools in the cupboard from her bed, corrected three pages of the assessment records and threatened to throw a rolled bandage at Percy because he kept putting the wrong lid on the water pitcher.

"That lid belongs to the kettle," she said.

"They both fit."

"That doesn't make them the same."

Percy checked the cupboard and changed the lid. "Satisfied?"

"Temporarily."

He set her fresh water beside the bed. She waited for him to tell her to drink it. He didn't. He went to the other worktable and started fixing a strap on one of the practice shields.

Clarisse watched him thread the leather through the buckle.

"You're using the wrong hole."

"I know."

"Then why are you using it?"

"To see how much slack is left before I punch a new one."

"There's a reason we punch those holes at fixed distances."

Percy looked up. "Would you like to tell me the reason?"

She opened her mouth, then stopped. He was asking her to teach, not challenging her right to be in charge.

"Yes," she said. "Bring it here."

He brought the shield and sat on the floor beside her bunk. Clarisse showed him how to align the strap so it wouldn't twist when the weight shifted. She didn't take the work out of his hands. She explained it once, then watched him do it.

"Again," she said when the first hole was too close to the edge.

He grumbled, found another scrap of leather and tried again.

A little later, Chiron arrived with the final assessment scores.

"I wanted you to look at these before I post them," he said.

Clarisse sat up so quickly the room gave a faint lurch. She waited until it stopped, then took the sheets.

There were three entries she would have scored differently. One camper had been marked down for stopping during the rescue carry, although Clarisse had told the group to check the dummy's position before lifting. Another had been given credit for a shield block despite poor foot placement. The third had a mark Percy couldn't explain because he'd copied Clarisse's shorthand without asking what it meant.

She was halfway through listing the corrections when Percy interrupted.

"That's the one I got wrong," he said. "I thought the mark meant they had to finish in a certain time."

Chiron nodded. "Then we need to ask what skill that station was intended to measure."

"Control," Clarisse said. "The point isn't who gets through fastest. It's whether they can carry someone without dropping them when they get tired."

"Then the time mark shouldn't count against them," Chiron said.

Clarisse looked down at the sheet. She had made the shorthand because it saved her a little time while she watched four stations at once. Now it was making the job harder for anyone who needed to help.

"I'll rewrite the symbols," she said.

"You can explain them," Chiron answered. "Someone else can do the copying."

She bristled. "I can hold a pencil."

"I'm sure you can. But you can also tell Percy how the form should work, and then we have a system more than one person understands."

Clarisse disliked the implication that her work had become a problem because she hadn't explained it. She also knew it was true.

Percy took out a clean sheet. "Which marks do we actually need?"

She started listing them. He asked questions that were sometimes obvious and sometimes useful. Chiron settled into a chair to check the final version.

By the time they finished, the scoring key could be read without asking Clarisse what every symbol meant. She was still responsible for the design. Someone else could use it if she wasn't there.

"That's better," Chiron said.

Percy looked at the form. "It's definitely less fish-shaped."

"Don't get attached to the fishbone," Clarisse warned. "That one stays."

She caught Percy smiling and felt a reluctant pride. He had made mistakes in the arena, and instead of hiding them he'd turned them into questions. She'd been ill, and instead of pretending that meant her judgment was worthless, she'd helped make the next assessment safer.

Neither was the kind of victory she usually counted.

It counted anyway.

---

Silena waited until Chiron left before bringing up the word Clarisse had used in the infirmary.

She wasn't holding a cup or arranging the blankets this time. She was sitting at her own worktable, sharpening a pencil for the next painting. The fact that she had chosen to talk while doing something else made it harder for Clarisse to evade.

"You called me Mother," Silena said.

Clarisse looked up from the scoring key. "I know."

"I didn't like it."

"I know."

"I don't mean I didn't like being teased. You tease me all the time. You made it sound as if I was trying to replace somebody."

Clarisse put down the page.

"That wasn't what I meant."

"What did you mean?"

Clarisse searched for an answer she could stand behind. It was easy to say she had been sick and cranky. It was true. It wasn't enough.

"I meant I don't like being told to stay in bed," she said.

Silena waited.

"And I don't like needing you to bring me things."

"You brought me things when I was ill."

"That's different."

"Why?"

"Because you don't complain about it."

Silena laughed once, without much humor. "I complain. I just don't always do it in front of you."

Clarisse stared at her.

"Sometimes I want to do something else," Silena went on. "Sometimes I would rather paint than wash somebody else's dishes or check whether Percy's found his clean clothes. That doesn't mean I don't love either of you."

"I never said it did."

"Not in those words."

Clarisse looked toward the window. The garden path was beyond it, too far for her to see the new sign. Silena had made something beautiful for another person and had still come back to help her. Clarisse had tried to make her feel guilty for doing both.

"All right," she said. "I was being unfair."

Silena studied her face. "That's not quite an apology."

Clarisse clenched her teeth, then let the breath out slowly.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I shouldn't have called you that. I was angry because I felt useless, and I wanted somebody else to be wrong about it."

Silena softened, but she didn't rush to make it disappear.

"Thank you," she said.

"I still don't like being told what to do."

"I know."

"And I'm going to complain the next time somebody gives me tea that tastes like bark."

"That's your right."

Clarisse hesitated. "You don't have to stay every time I get hurt. Or sick."

"I know."

"Good."

Silena picked up her pencil. "And you don't have to chase me away to prove you aren't depending on me."

Clarisse looked down at the score sheet. That was harder to answer. She had sent Silena away to deliver the sign partly because she wanted Silena to have her own life, and partly because she couldn't bear the feeling of needing her.

"I'll try not to," she said at last.

Silena nodded and went back to sharpening the pencil. The apology hadn't changed their personalities. Clarisse would still snap when she was frightened, and Silena would still notice when something needed doing before anyone else did. But the next time Clarisse wanted company, she might manage to ask for it without turning the request into an order.

For now, she asked Silena about the garden sign.

Silena described how the light fell across the painted vine near sunset. She was halfway through explaining the color of the leaves when Percy came in with the repaired shield and asked where it belonged.

"On the rack," Clarisse said.

"Which side?"

"The side with the other shields, genius."

Silena laughed. Percy made a face. Their cabin sounded almost normal again.

---

Will allowed Clarisse a short walk two mornings later, provided she went slowly and stopped if she felt dizzy.

She resented every condition.

Silena insisted on coming with her, but she didn't insist on holding her arm. Percy walked ahead carrying the note Katie had sent about the garden sign. Annabeth had offered to join them and then decided to finish a drawing for Chiron instead. Clarisse liked that her friends were allowed to have their own afternoons, even if she still wanted to know where everybody was.

The first part of the path was easy. Clarisse got down the cabin steps without help, crossed the grass and climbed the small rise beside the dining pavilion. The air smelled of damp wood and strawberries. The sky was clear, and the sun on her shoulders felt good after so much time indoors.

Halfway to the garden she began to sweat. Not from exertion in the ordinary way. Her legs felt weak, and the bright day grew uncomfortable.

Silena noticed immediately. "Want to stop?"

"No."

They walked another twenty steps. Clarisse found a low stone wall and sat on it without being asked.

"Fine," she said. "A minute."

Percy stopped ahead and came back. He didn't ask whether she was all right. He took out the water flask he'd brought and set it beside her.

"Want some?"

"Yes."

She drank. The water was cool. She looked across the garden toward the painted sign, visible now between the posts where Charlie had helped mount it. The vine curved along the board, and the path beyond it led between fresh rows of seedlings.

"It's good," she told Silena.

Silena blinked. "You can see it from here?"

"Of course I can see it."

"I thought you'd want to get closer."

"I will, another day."

Silena sat beside her. Percy stayed standing, looking toward the sign as though trying to imagine where the afternoon light would fall across it. He had his own opinions about paintings, mostly ridiculous, but he knew better than to comment when Silena was waiting for Clarisse to say something real.

"The green's right," Clarisse said. "It's the color the leaves get before rain."

Silena smiled. "That's what I was trying for."

Clarisse looked down at her hands. She was tired. She hated that a walk which should have taken five minutes had become a medical event. She hated needing to sit while her brother could run up the hill without thinking.

But she had seen the sign. She had asked to come. She had not pretended her body was ready for more than it was.

"Let's go back," she said.

Percy didn't argue. Silena stood and offered her an arm for the downhill stretch. Clarisse took it.

They returned to Cabin Three at a slow pace, with enough silence for her to hear the ordinary sounds of camp again.

---

Three days later, when her fever had stayed down and Will was satisfied with her recovery, Clarisse was cleared for light training duties.

Light meant watching. It meant demonstrating a stance without taking hard impacts and stepping away before her legs started trembling. It did not mean resuming the full assessment schedule she'd written before she got sick.

Clarisse disliked the terms, but she had been given a choice: accept them or remain off duty longer.

She accepted them.

The arena looked different from the low bench beside the equipment rack. From there she could see how the younger campers grouped themselves before an exercise, which ones watched the instructors and which ones watched their friends. She could also see Percy moving between stations with the revised scoring sheet tucked under his arm.

He wasn't pretending to be her. He spoke more quietly, asked questions when he didn't know a rule and stopped a task when somebody was careless. He still made jokes at bad moments. One camper laughed so hard at something he said that she dropped her practice shield onto her foot.

Clarisse rolled her eyes and made a note.

Chiron came to stand beside her.

"You're supposed to be observing," he said.

"I am."

"Then perhaps you could observe without writing another assessment plan."

"This is one page."

He looked at the stack beneath her hand.

"Fine. Three pages."

Chiron said nothing.

Clarisse sighed. "I know. I'm not leading the whole session."

"What do you see from here that you couldn't see while giving commands?"

She looked toward the rescue-carry station. The younger camper who had fallen earlier was waiting for his turn again. This time he watched two older campers demonstrate the lift. He didn't look happy, but he was paying attention.

"He's doing it differently," she said.

"Who?"

"That one from Hermes. He was rushing before."

Chiron nodded. "And why do you think he changed?"

"Because Percy made him start over."

"Or because he was given another attempt without being treated as a failure."

Clarisse looked at Chiron.

"Both," she said.

"Quite possibly."

She hated when he was reasonable. It made her feel as though she had been arguing with him for years about things he had already considered.

At the next rotation, Percy came to the bench and showed her the scores.

"Any problems?" he asked.

"Two. That one's bracing too high, and the left foot mark needs a clearer explanation."

Percy examined the sheet. "I thought you'd say that."

"Then why'd you mark it this way?"

"Because I wanted you to confirm it before I changed the score. We're supposed to use the same standard."

Clarisse studied him. He looked tired, but not uncertain. He wasn't handing her the whole job because he was afraid to do it. He was checking a detail that mattered.

"All right," she said. "Bring the group here for one demonstration."

He called them over. Clarisse stayed seated and showed them with a wooden practice stick how the foot should turn before the body followed. When one camper copied the motion too quickly, she stopped him and explained why the weight needed to stay over the center of the stance.

She didn't raise her voice. They could hear her anyway.

"Again," she said.

They repeated the movement. The boy from Hermes did it properly this time, then looked at her as though expecting a correction.

"Good," Clarisse said. "Now do it slowly enough that you know why it works."

His shoulders loosened. He tried again.

When the rotation resumed, Percy took the sheet back and marked the change. He didn't thank her in front of everyone. He knew she would have hated that. Instead he set her water flask within reach and asked whether she needed a break.

"Not yet," she said. "But I'll tell you."

He nodded and went back to work.

The answer had surprised her. She'd said it without calculating how to sound stronger than she felt. She could tell him when she was tired. He could believe her.

She reached her limit before the last rotation. She got up, told Chiron she was finished for the day and walked back to the cabin without being ordered. It stung less because she'd made the decision herself.

---

Silena had arranged an early supper on the Cabin Three porch when Clarisse returned.

There was a loaf of bread, a pot of soup from the kitchens and a small bowl of sliced fruit. Annabeth sat on the steps with her drawing board balanced on her knees, comparing two sketches of the amphitheater stage. Percy was at the table, trying to mend the handle of a basket with string.

"You brought the whole workshop home," Clarisse said.

Percy looked at the basket. "It was broken."

"Everything you touch is broken."

"That's not fair. Sometimes I break things on purpose."

Annabeth looked up. "Please don't."

Silena slid a bowl toward Clarisse. "How was light duty?"

"Light."

"Did you stay the whole session?"

"No."

"Good."

Clarisse sat down and tasted the soup. This one had more vegetables and a thicker broth than the batch Percy had made.

"Not bad," she said.

Silena lifted an eyebrow. "I'll tell the kitchen staff you approve."

"Don't. They'll make me review the menu."

Annabeth set down her sketches and came to the table. She asked whether the younger campers had improved on the stance exercise, and Clarisse spent several minutes explaining it. Annabeth listened closely enough to compare the foot placement to the way a building carried weight.

"That's an interesting comparison," Clarisse said.

Percy turned toward her. "Was that a compliment?"

"No."

"Sounded like one."

"You're hearing things."

He grinned, and she threw a piece of bread at him. He caught it and ate it.

They talked through supper. Silena described Katie's plans for the garden room. Annabeth told them Chiron had approved the amphitheater repair and that she wasn't going to redesign the entire structure. Percy announced that this was a historic victory for restraint. Annabeth informed him he was not allowed to put that phrase in any report Chiron might read.

Clarisse watched them. Before she'd become ill, she'd feared that missing the assessment would prove how little she was needed. The session had continued without her. Scores had been recorded, shields repaired, campers trained. Nothing terrible had happened because she'd spent several days in bed.

The result should have made her feel irrelevant. Instead she could see what her teaching had done. Percy knew how to run the stations because she'd taught him. Silena knew when to step away because the two of them had spent years negotiating what care meant. Annabeth could sit at their table without becoming a substitute sister, bringing her own work and asking questions that didn't sound like anyone else's.

There was space for all of them, even when one person had nothing useful to do.

When supper was finished, Percy collected the bowls. Clarisse offered to carry the pot inside. Silena passed it to her without giving a speech about whether she should be lifting things. It was light, and Clarisse could carry it.

That felt good.

---

A few days after that, Will checked Clarisse one last time and gave her permission to resume ordinary practice gradually. He reminded her that stamina could lag behind the end of a fever. Clarisse promised to start slowly and meant it, at least for the first few hours.

Percy found her in Cabin Three sorting the practice weapons.

"Want to test something?" he asked.

Clarisse looked up. "What?"

He held out a wooden spear. "Footwork. No hard contact. A slow-motion duel."

"You're challenging me in my own cabin?"

"I wouldn't dare challenge you in public until I had a chance of winning."

"Smart."

Silena was at the worktable, putting away brushes. Annabeth had come by to return a book and was standing near the door. Both looked over.

"Are you supposed to be sparring?" Silena asked.

"Light practice," Clarisse said. "He asked for slow. I can do slow."

"That's what he says until he gets impatient," Annabeth replied.

Percy looked offended. "I can be patient."

Clarisse pointed the wooden spear at him. "Prove it."

They cleared a space between the bunks. Nobody swung hard enough to strike the walls, and the rule was simple: a point for touching the opponent's shoulder with the padded end, no body checks and no running. Clarisse moved carefully, concentrating on balance instead of speed. Percy tried to draw her into a faster exchange, then caught himself and slowed down.

She took the first point with a clean turn.

"You're still the baby," she told him.

"That wasn't fair."

"Your feet were in the wrong place."

"Again."

They tried another round. He nearly caught her with a feint she'd taught him. She blocked, shifted her weight and tapped his shoulder before he could recover.

"Two," she said.

"You're enjoying this."

"Very much."

He lowered the spear, laughing. She lowered hers too. Her legs were tired, but it was ordinary tiredness now, not the sickening weakness she'd felt in the arena.

"Next time," he said.

"You can try."

Silena came over with a cup of water. Clarisse took it and drank while Percy put away the practice weapons. Annabeth slipped her hand into his when he returned. He turned his palm up and held hers.

Clarisse saw it. She didn't tease them.

Not because she'd become solemn about love, or because she thought Percy belonged to one person more than another. She'd spent days learning that people could care about her without stopping everything else in their lives. Percy could be her little brother, Silena's partner in ridiculous kitchen disasters and Annabeth's favorite person. None of those relationships needed to defeat the others.

She set down the cup and looked at Percy.

"By the way," she said, "you still owe me a proper rematch."

He grinned. "When?"

"When I say I'm ready. And when you stop leaving your left foot open."

Annabeth laughed. Percy groaned and began arguing about how the last point should have counted.

Silena went back to her brushes. Clarisse sat on the edge of her bunk and listened to them bicker. She had a training sheet to finish tomorrow, a sister's painting to admire and a little brother who was getting harder to beat.

Those were good problems to have.
