## Chapter 111: The Date Committee

Clarisse declared herself chair of the date committee while Percy was trying to rescue the last piece of toast from a plate of scrambled eggs.

She didn't ask whether there was a committee. She put her elbows on the Cabin Three table, pushed his cup away from the spreading egg yolk, and said, "You're taking Annabeth somewhere."

Percy looked at the cup. "Good morning to you too."

"Somewhere that isn't the lake."

"I like the lake."

"Everybody knows you like the lake. That's why it doesn't count."

Silena arrived carrying two bowls of strawberries and the expression she wore when Clarisse had begun a fight before she had finished breakfast. She sat across from them, set one bowl in the middle, and tasted a berry before speaking.

"Where is he taking her?"

"That's the first item," Clarisse said. "He needs options."

Percy gave Silena a look of betrayal. "You're both on this committee?"

"I'm observing," Silena said. "Clarisse appointed me secretary."

"I appointed you the person who stops him from choosing the lake."

"I'm not sure you know how secretaries work."

Clarisse reached across Percy for a strawberry. He moved the bowl just out of reach.

"I've been dating Annabeth for a long time," he said.

"We're aware," Clarisse answered. "We live with you."

"And we've seen you take her places," Silena said. "We just haven't seen you ask her what she'd actually like to do lately."

That landed more cleanly than Percy wanted it to. He had walked with Annabeth after the campfire. They'd worked on the shrine drainage channel together and stolen time at the library when other people wanted one or both of them. Those hours mattered. He knew they did.

He could also remember the last time he had planned something only because he thought it would make her happy, without checking. He had ended up holding a basket of bread outside a meeting she was already running. She'd come out amused and a little annoyed and told him, quite reasonably, that she loved the bread but wanted to finish what she was doing.

Clarisse watched him think and mistook the silence for surrender.

"Good. The orchard at sunset."

"No," Percy said.

"Fine. The climbing wall."

"You want me to take her to a place where the rocks are trying to kill us?"

"You're both good at it."

"That's not the problem."

Silena pushed the strawberry bowl back to the middle. "Stop giving him assignments. He's supposed to ask her."

"Then why do we need a committee?"

"We don't," Percy said.

"That is the least cooperative attitude I've heard all morning."

Percy raised his eyebrows. "You spent breakfast yelling at Connor Stoll about a spoon."

"He stole my spoon."

"It was the communal spoon."

"It was in my hand."

He laughed despite himself. Clarisse stole three strawberries while his guard was down.

Across the pavilion, Annabeth was sitting with Thalia and a pair of younger campers. A roll of drawing paper stood against her chair. She was listening to the campers describe a leak near the Zeus cabin roof, and she had already begun drawing something in the margin of her breakfast napkin. When she concentrated, she held her pencil closer to the point than anybody Percy knew. He could tell she was frustrated by the problem from the little crease between her eyebrows. He could also tell she wanted to solve it.

In the life they remembered, he had loved that about her. He still did. It made him reluctant to interrupt.

"Now," Clarisse said.

"What?"

"Go ask."

"She's busy."

"You are using her being busy as a reason not to ask whether she's free," Silena pointed out.

"That's different."

"Is it?"

He looked at Annabeth again. She glanced up, caught him watching, and made a small, questioning gesture with the pencil. Percy lifted his own spoon in answer. She rolled her eyes, smiling, and went back to the drawing.

"I can talk to her later," he said.

Silena took another strawberry. "Then tell her later. It doesn't need a speech."

Clarisse pushed her chair back. "All right. New committee agenda. We make sure he doesn't bring the same wilted bunch of flowers he once pulled out of the strawberry field."

"Those were wildflowers."

"They had roots," Clarisse said.

"They were alive when I found them."

"Exactly. You had no idea what to do with them."

That was unfair, mostly because it was true. Percy had stood at the Zeus cabin porch with a fistful of dirt and flowers, and Annabeth had calmly supplied a jar. They were still together. He considered that evidence in his favor.

Silena, who remembered the incident with painful accuracy, patted his wrist. "Ask her first. Then decide whether it needs anything fancy."

Percy stood with his plate. "This committee is dissolved."

Clarisse saluted with the communal spoon, which she'd somehow recovered. "Motion denied."

He took his dishes away before she could demand a vote.

---

By midmorning, Annabeth had heard about the committee from three different people, none of whom were actually on it.

Katie Gardner wanted to know if she needed flowers from the gardens. Connor Stoll offered to arrange music for a small fee. Grover asked whether the whole thing was a surprise and looked relieved when Annabeth said she hoped it wasn't.

She carried her roll of drawings up the Zeus cabin steps, set it on the porch, and found Thalia kneeling beneath the gutter with a screwdriver between her teeth.

"Your boyfriend's planning something," Thalia said around the tool.

"Apparently."

"Clarisse has a list."

"That's more concerning."

Thalia removed the screwdriver. "She says you're overdue for a date."

Annabeth planted her hands on her hips. "Overdue according to whom?"

"According to Clarisse. That should tell you everything."

The gutter sagged between two brackets. Water had stained the paint where it spilled over the edge. Someone had tried fixing it with a bent nail, and the nail had given up. Annabeth crouched by the lower step to check the angle.

"We need another bracket," she said.

"We have another bracket."

"Then why is that one holding on with string?"

"Because somebody borrowed the ladder."

"Who?"

"Your boyfriend's sister."

"Which one?"

"Both, depending on who you ask."

Annabeth took the screwdriver from Thalia, reached under the gutter, and found that the timber holding the first bracket was damp enough to crumble beneath her nail. The problem was worse than the visible sag. Refastening the bracket would only make it fail again.

"Don't climb on that section," she said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were looking at the roof."

"I look at things. It's a hobby."

"Thalia."

"Annabeth."

They stared at each other until Thalia leaned back on her heels.

"All right. No roof," she said. "But we promised the little ones their bunks wouldn't get wet again."

"They won't. We can use a temporary downspout until we replace the board."

Thalia looked dubious. "You can make one?"

"With a bucket, a length of pipe and somebody who can cut the end at the right angle."

"Sounds like a forge problem."

"It is a forge problem. Which means you should go get Beckendorf before you improvise another nail."

Thalia's mouth opened.

Annabeth held up a hand. "I'm serious."

"I know. You've been serious since you were about four."

There was affection in it and an old confidence that Thalia knew what Annabeth had been like when she was four. Annabeth didn't share that memory. She had learned to stop correcting the stories merely because they made her feel absent from her own childhood, but sometimes the absence still caught.

"Then you ought to know I don't like seeing jobs done twice," she said.

Thalia studied her face, then rose and dusted off her knees.

"Fine. I'll get Charlie. You going to spend the entire afternoon drawing pipe fittings?"

"I might."

"You have other plans, apparently."

Annabeth tightened the cord around the rolled drawings. "I haven't agreed to any plans."

"You know he's going to ask."

"I know there's a rumor about asking."

Thalia's expression changed just enough to be irritating. "You're excited."

"I haven't said that."

"You don't have to."

"That's not an answer."

"Neither is that."

Annabeth picked up the drawings. "I can decide whether I'm free without everybody running a meeting about it."

Thalia's teasing faded. "I didn't say you couldn't."

"You said I have other plans."

"I said apparently."

"Then leave it there."

Thalia glanced at the exposed rotten wood. It was easier to argue about a gutter than about Annabeth deciding how to spend an afternoon, and they both knew it. She went to the door and collected her spear from the hook.

"I'll find Charlie," she said. "Do you want me to carry the plans to the Big House?"

"I can carry them."

"I was offering."

Annabeth looked at her. "I know. Thank you."

It wasn't a perfect exchange. Thalia still looked as though she wanted to ask three more questions. Annabeth still felt watched. But when her sister headed down the steps, she didn't demand a schedule, and Annabeth didn't follow to reassure her.

She went inside, spread her drawings across the table, and made a note in the margin: replace rotten board before the next rain.

Then, because she had been thinking about it before the committee rumor started, she turned to a clean page and drew the amphitheater.

From the top row, the stage looked almost flat. From the lower seats, the columns seemed taller than they were. Somebody had chosen those proportions carefully. She wanted to know how.

And she knew who wouldn't mind sitting beside her while she found out.
