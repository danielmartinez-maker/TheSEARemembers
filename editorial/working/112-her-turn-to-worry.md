## Chapter 112: Her Turn to Worry

Annabeth first noticed the change because Percy stopped complaining about the swimming markers.

The small buoys at the camp beach had been damaged during a storm, and Chiron wanted them reset before the younger campers began another round of water exercises. Percy volunteered. So did two campers from Hermes, partly because they wanted to skip spear drills and partly because they thought a son of Poseidon could do all the difficult work while they stood on shore with the rope.

Normally Percy would have objected to both ideas, loudly.

That afternoon he worked without a word. He swam the marker lines out, checked their lengths, and came back to the shallows to explain where the anchors should go. When Connor Stoll tied a knot that slipped under tension, Percy took the rope, demonstrated the proper loop and handed it back. He didn't make a joke about land people or ask whether Connor intended to tie the whole beach into a bow.

Annabeth stood above the sand with her drawing board, recording the positions of the markers. She needed the measurements for an updated beach diagram. Every year someone moved a sign or shortened a rope, and the safety map Chiron kept in the Big House slowly drifted away from what was actually in the water. Annabeth hated that kind of problem. It could hurt somebody because a line on paper made adults think they knew something they hadn't checked.

"Another foot out," she called.

Percy nodded, slipped beneath the surface, and moved the anchor. The buoy settled a little farther from the shore. It bobbed twice, then held.

"Better?"

"Yes. Keep it there."

He rose through the water, wiped his eyes and looked beyond the marked swimming area. The Sound was blue-gray beneath the low clouds. A long way past the camp boundary, a pair of fishing boats were moving toward the harbor. Percy watched them until Connor shouted that the rope was tangling around his ankle.

Annabeth marked the new location and looked up again. Percy was already helping Connor out of the mess. Nothing had happened. Nothing was visibly wrong.

But she had watched him look at the sea three times in half an hour, and each time the look lasted a little longer.

She knew that expression. She had seen versions of it after difficult dreams, after letters from family, after ordinary things that reminded them of lives no one else could remember. Percy's face didn't always show fear the way other people's did. Sometimes it grew quieter. Sometimes he made more jokes, trying to keep himself moving before anybody asked him to stop.

Today there were no jokes.

By the time they finished, the Hermes boys were arguing about who had done more work. Percy handed them the last coil of rope and went to rinse the sand from his hands. Annabeth checked the notes against her measurements.

"You put this one exactly where I wanted it," she said.

"Good."

"Connor was trying to convince me the old line was safer."

"Connor doesn't like swimming farther than he can stand."

"He said the old line was traditional."

"That's Connor for 'I don't want to carry another anchor.'"

There he was. A small version of the Percy she knew.

She smiled and rolled up the map. "Do you want to walk back with me?"

"Sure."

They climbed the beach path slowly. Normally he would have carried the heavier board without being asked. Today he offered and then seemed to forget he'd offered when a gull passed overhead and cried toward the shore. Annabeth shifted the board in her arms and didn't remind him.

She could ask what was wrong. She could also wait until they had somewhere to talk without people stepping around them with wet ropes. Both choices felt possible.

At the top of the path, Clarisse was waiting with a list of the afternoon's equipment returns. She saw Percy coming and held out a pencil.

"You owe me an account of four missing practice spears," she said.

"I was in the water."

"I noticed."

"So I don't have your spears."

"I wasn't accusing you. I'm asking whether you saw Connor borrow the boat rack key."

Percy paused. "Maybe."

"Percy."

"He had a key. I didn't ask who gave it to him."

Clarisse frowned, then looked at Annabeth's drawing board. "Did you finish the markers?"

"Yes," Annabeth said. "The last buoy is farther out. Chiron should update the posted map before the next swim group."

"Fine." Clarisse made a note. "I'll find Connor."

She started down the path, but stopped to glance at her brother.

"You coming to dinner?"

"Of course."

"Then don't forget this time."

Percy looked surprised. "I haven't forgotten dinner."

"You forgot lunch yesterday."

"I had a late lunch."

"You ate two apples standing outside the forge."

"Apples are food."

"They're not lunch."

Clarisse went on before he could argue. Annabeth watched her leave. She was never subtle when she thought Percy wasn't taking care of himself. She was also usually right about whether he had eaten.

"Are you hungry?" Annabeth asked.

"Not really."

"That wasn't a trick question."

"I know."

They walked in silence for another few steps. At the intersection where the paths split toward Cabin One and the Big House, Percy finally looked at her.

"I'll see you at dinner."

"You can tell me if you want to go somewhere else."

"I know."

He gave her a quick smile and headed toward Cabin Three. She stood with her board under her arm until he turned the corner.

She didn't follow him.

It took more effort than she wanted to admit.

---

Annabeth spent the next morning inside the Big House trying to make a table sit level on a floor that had never been built level.

The old room was full of maps, and Chiron wanted the updated beach diagram fitted beneath the glass on one of the long worktables. There was no point redrawing every swimming marker if the resulting map warped and couldn't be read. Annabeth set a narrow scrap of wood beneath the shortest leg and checked the surface with a little bubble level she'd borrowed from the forge.

The bubble moved almost to the center, then rolled away again.

"You're fighting a losing battle," said Grover from the doorway.

"I'm fighting poor carpentry."

"That table was old when I got here."

"That doesn't make it better."

He came closer and helped her lift the corner while she slid a second shim into place. The bubble settled. Annabeth checked it from the other side, then nodded.

"Thank you."

"You're welcome. I was supposed to help with the beach inventory, but I got sent here because Chiron thought you'd need another pair of hands."

"Chiron was right."

Grover grinned and leaned over the map. He studied the shapes of the marked swimming lanes, then pointed to a small symbol near the far buoy.

"What's that?"

"Current warning. If the wind comes from the east, the water shifts there differently."

"Percy told you?"

"He showed me the position while we were setting the markers."

Grover looked at the note. "He knows a lot about the water."

"He does."

Grover glanced toward the window. "He's been pretty quiet."

Annabeth straightened. "You've noticed?"

"Hard not to. Usually I can tell where he is from the noise."

"He was fine during the work. Just distracted."

"Maybe he misses home."

She knew what Grover meant. She wasn't sure Percy would define home the same way.

"He lives here," she said.

Grover's ears moved beneath his cap. "I know. I meant Atlantis."

Annabeth looked at the sea line on the map. She had gone there with Percy on visits and seen enough to understand its scale. It wasn't only a palace or a collection of underwater rooms. It was where Amphitrite knew when he preferred breakfast and Triton knew which ridiculous story would make him laugh. It was a place full of people who remembered a childhood Percy had never experienced from the inside.

"I think he does," she said.

Grover waited. That was one of the things she liked about him. He didn't fill silence merely because somebody else looked uncomfortable.

"Did he say something to you?" she asked.

"Not really. We talked about the canoe race and he kept asking whether the water outside the border was calm."

"Was it?"

"Yes. I thought he wanted to swim."

Annabeth pressed her palm against the smooth glass. "Maybe he did."

Grover began sorting the inventory sheets into separate stacks. The room smelled of paper dust and the wood polish Chiron insisted on using every week. It was a practical, familiar smell. Annabeth put away her pencil, then took it out again because she had forgotten to mark the new location of the last warning sign.

"Do you want me to ask him?" Grover said.

"No."

He looked up.

"I mean, not on my behalf. You can talk to him because you're his friend. Just don't make it sound like we're holding a meeting about him."

Grover smiled faintly. "We'd need more chairs."

"We already have enough committees."

"Clarisse still mad you dissolved the last one?"

"She claims she was never a member."

Grover laughed, and Annabeth felt better for a moment. They finished arranging the map. When Chiron came to inspect it, the glass sat flat, the symbols were readable and the safety notes matched the actual markers. The work had been worth the time.

Then Chiron asked whether she wanted to help with an arena drainage diagram next, and Annabeth almost said yes before remembering she'd promised Thalia she'd check the cabin's repaired gutter after lunch.

"I can't today," she said.

Chiron looked pleased. "Then the arena will wait."

Annabeth gathered her notes and left. She'd been trying so hard to understand what Percy needed that she had nearly volunteered away the only free part of her afternoon.

That was not, she thought, how to be a good girlfriend.

---

They met at the lake after the evening meal.

Percy was sitting on the lowest dock plank with his shoes beside him. His toes were just above the water. He had rolled his sleeves to his elbows, and he was carefully untangling a piece of fishing line he'd found caught around one of the cleats.

Annabeth sat down at the other end of the board.

"Do you want help?"

"No. I've nearly got it."

She watched him work. He loosened the line, freed the hook without scraping his finger, and wound the coil into a small circle. He put it beside his shoes so somebody could dispose of it properly. Then he looked at her.

"You're very patient tonight."

"Am I?"

"You've been sitting there for three minutes and you haven't asked me anything."

"Would you like me to start?"

"Not especially."

Annabeth considered taking that literally. She was capable of giving him all the space he wanted if she knew what that space meant. The difficult part was that his silence was not automatically a request. She couldn't turn a few quiet days into an accusation, but she also couldn't pretend she hadn't noticed.

"All right," she said. "I'll ask one thing. You've seemed worried. Is it Atlantis?"

Percy stopped swinging his feet.

"A little," he admitted.

"Is someone hurt?"

"No. Nobody's hurt." He rubbed his palms against the plank. "Amphitrite sent an Iris-message last week. Everyone's fine. Triton showed me a picture of a hippocamp somebody had trimmed so badly it looked like a startled goat."

Annabeth laughed despite the tension. "Why?"

"He said the animal needed to see better. The hippocamp disagreed."

"That sounds like Triton."

"It does."

Percy looked across the lake. Freshwater reeds grew along the far bank. The water lay almost still, dark beneath the trees, nothing like the deep blue open sea outside camp. He had helped repair this dock. He'd trained near it, played games beside it, brought Annabeth here when neither of them could sleep. It was a place that belonged to his present life.

"Sometimes I think about going back for more than a visit," he said.

Annabeth held still.

"Not instead of being here forever," he added quickly. "I just think about how easy it would be to stay for a while. Amphitrite would find me something useful to do. Triton would make fun of me until I learned all the things I should know already. Nobody would ask when I'm coming back to camp, because they would assume I was home."

"Do you want to stay there?"

"I don't know."

She let the answer stand.

"I love them," Percy went on. "I love Clarisse and Silena. I love this place. I love being with you. It's not a choice between people I like and people I don't. If it were, this would be easier."

"I know."

"And I don't want Amphitrite to think I come home only when there's nothing interesting happening at camp. I don't want my sisters to think they're temporary either."

Annabeth reached for his hand, then stopped halfway. He noticed. He turned his palm upward and held it out to her.

She took it.

"You can miss Atlantis while you're here," she said. "You can miss camp while you're there."

"That doesn't give me two bodies."

"No. It doesn't."

He laughed softly. "You'd probably find a way to build a second body out of plywood if I asked."

"I'd at least start with a better material."

"Good point."

She watched a small ring spread across the lake where a fish had risen for an insect. Her instinct was to make a plan. She could suggest a trip, ask Chiron about leave, arrange dates, draft letters, compare travel times and decide which nights should belong to which family. Planning was what she did when an unsolved problem frightened her.

But Percy hadn't asked for an itinerary. He had told her he was lonely for a place he loved.

"You don't have to decide tonight," she said.

"I know."

"Do you want me to help you think through the options?"

"Maybe later."

"Then what would help now?"

Percy looked surprised that she had asked.

"Can I tell you about the last time I went? Not the whole thing. Just some of the parts I keep thinking about."

"Yes."

He began with the gardens, where Amphitrite grew pale sea flowers in enclosed pools beneath glass so thin Annabeth could barely see it. Then he described a corridor where the ceiling glowed blue when a school of silver fish passed over it, and the way Triton had challenged him to a ridiculous contest involving a shell and three currents. Those were memories Percy had actually made on recent visits. He knew their rooms and the sound of their voices from being there, not from the changed childhood everyone assumed he remembered.

Annabeth listened. She asked where the flowers got their light, whether the glass could withstand pressure and how badly Triton had beaten him at the contest.

"He didn't beat me," Percy said indignantly. "He cheated."

"How?"

"He didn't explain the rules until after I lost."

"That does sound like cheating."

"He says it's an important lesson in royal etiquette."

"Then perhaps you should write the rules down before you play again."

Percy smiled. It was the first time that evening his smile had reached his eyes.

They stayed on the dock until the lamps around camp began to come on. Annabeth didn't tell him where he belonged. She knew what she wanted, and it mattered, but she wasn't going to turn his affection for Atlantis into evidence that he loved her less.

When they stood to leave, he picked up the fishing line and carried it to the disposal bin. It was an ordinary piece of care for a place he also called home.

---

Annabeth came back to Cabin One with damp shoes and a mind full of things she had promised herself she wouldn't organize.

Thalia was at the table, sorting clean bandages into a wooden drawer. The drawer had three compartments. Thalia had filled them according to her own system: strips she could grab quickly, strips she thought she might need and strips she called useless until someone else asked for them.

"You're late," Thalia said.

"Percy and I went for a walk."

"I assumed."

Annabeth put her shoes beside the door rather than beneath the bed. She wanted them to dry, and she didn't feel like having another argument about muddy footprints.

"Do you know how camp leave works?" she asked.

Thalia's hand stopped over the bandages.

"For what?"

"If someone wanted to visit family for a while. Not forever. Just longer than a weekend."

"Depends on the family."

"I mean the permission."

"Chiron has to know where you're going, how you'll travel and whether somebody will be expecting you."

"Do you need a signed letter?"

"If the person asking is still under camp supervision, probably. Why?"

Annabeth realized she'd opened the drawer of questions she'd told herself to leave closed.

"No reason," she said.

Thalia gave her a look.

"Fine. There is a reason. Percy's been thinking about Atlantis."

"Is he going?"

"He hasn't decided."

"And you're finding out how he'd get permission?"

"I thought I could save time."

"Did he ask?"

"No."

Thalia slid the remaining bandages into their compartments. "You know what you're about to do, right?"

Annabeth sat on her bed, irritated by how quickly her sister had found the point. "I'm asking one question."

"You started with one question."

"That's generally where people start."

Thalia closed the drawer. "Then what happens? You find the travel rules, you work out a schedule, you go to Chiron, and Percy discovers everybody's discussed a trip he hasn't decided he wants."

"I wasn't going to do all that."

Thalia waited.

Annabeth looked away. She had already worked out which camp duties Percy would need covered if he left for more than a few days. She hadn't written them down, but she had been thinking about them. That didn't make her a bad person. It made her someone who knew how easily plans failed when nobody did the work in advance.

"I hate not being able to fix things," she said.

"So do I."

"You were going to say that's not what he needs."

"I was going to ask whether you've eaten anything since dinner."

Annabeth frowned. "I was at dinner."

"And you spent most of it drawing in the margin of a napkin. I saw how much you actually ate."

"Oh, don't start."

"I haven't started. There's bread in the basket. Eat some."

Annabeth took the bread, mostly to keep Thalia from making another point. It had gone a little stale, but there was honey on the table. She sat at the workbench and spread some on a piece.

Thalia pulled out her own chair. "You want to know what I think?"

"Will it be about bread?"

"No."

"Then go ahead."

"You're allowed to ask Percy what he wants. You're allowed to say you'd miss him if he went away for a while. You don't have to pretend you have no preferences."

"I know."

"But if he hasn't made a decision, don't make one for him because the waiting is uncomfortable."

Annabeth bit into the bread. She didn't like the advice because it was harder than a travel plan. At least a travel plan had blanks she could fill in.

"What would you do about Jason?" she asked.

Thalia's face changed. She didn't reach for the bandages again. "What do you mean?"

"If he told you he didn't know whether he wanted to stay at Camp Jupiter or visit more often. Would you wait for him to figure it out?"

"That depends what he told me and what was possible."

"You'd make a list."

"Probably," Thalia said. "I might even annoy him by asking too many questions."

Annabeth smiled faintly. "There you go."

"But I wouldn't book a trip he hadn't asked for."

"You'd want to."

"Very much."

Thalia picked up the empty honey jar and turned it to see whether there was any left in the bottom.

"The worst part of loving somebody who has a life somewhere else," she said, "is that sometimes you want to pull all the pieces close enough that you can see them. It doesn't make them fit."

Annabeth looked at her. Thalia was staring at the jar, not at her. It was rare for her sister to speak plainly about Jason being far away. Normally she made a joke about Roman discipline or sent him a letter full of practical advice. Annabeth had asked an unfair question, and Thalia had answered it anyway.

"You miss him," Annabeth said.

"Yes."

"Do you want to write to him?"

"I already started." Thalia pointed at a sheet of paper near the bandage drawer. Half a page was filled with cramped handwriting. "I keep putting it aside because I can't think of how to finish it without sounding like his mother."

"You're his sister."

"I know. I helped raise him for a while, and sometimes I forget I don't get to tell him where to be."

Annabeth pulled the paper closer, then stopped. "Can I read it?"

"Not yet."

"Okay."

They sat for a little while without talking. Annabeth finished the bread and closed the honey jar. Then she spread out the roof diagram they'd been working on together.

"The outlet still points into the walkway," she said. "If we rotate the last piece here, the water will miss the step."

Thalia leaned in to look. "Would it hit the rosebush?"

"Only if we let the pipe end too low."

"Then we can raise it."

They worked until the late lamps were lit. Annabeth liked that her sister could be practical and stubborn and funny about a piece of gutter, and could also tell her something true about missing Jason without making the whole evening about him.

Before bed, she put the leave question aside. She didn't write down a schedule. She would have liked to.

That was probably why she needed not to.

---

The next morning Percy went to the canoe lake with Clarisse, and the argument started before they had turned over the boat.

Clarisse had volunteered to fix a loose thwart in one of the smaller canoes. Percy had volunteered after she did, which she suspected was the problem. He'd been following her around the cabin for two days, offering to fetch tools she could reach and carry loads she could lift. Ordinarily she'd have enjoyed making him do the annoying parts of her jobs. Today she wanted the work to be quiet.

"Hold the bow," she told him.

Percy took the bow.

"Not like that. If you pull toward yourself, the whole thing tips."

"Got it."

He adjusted his grip. They rolled the canoe onto a pair of padded supports, and Clarisse checked the wood around the loose seat brace. The screws had been pulling out for months. She could have fixed the damage with a new block and two bolts, but she wanted to see how far the rot had spread first.

Percy leaned over her shoulder.

"Can I help?"

"You're helping. You're holding the boat."

"I meant with the repair."

"Then get me the chisel."

He handed it over. She worked beneath the seat, careful not to gouge the sound timber. The canoe rocked against the supports when Percy shifted.

"Hold still," she snapped.

"I am."

"You're moving."

"Because my foot is falling asleep."

"Then change feet without moving the boat."

He looked at her for a moment, then adjusted his stance.

A minute later he said, "Triton wouldn't use wood for this."

Clarisse stopped cutting. "Wouldn't he?"

"He said they use a different frame for the smaller boats. The joints can move with the current without coming loose."

"That's interesting."

"I could ask him about it."

"We're fixing a canoe. Not building an Atlantean fleet."

Percy looked hurt, and Clarisse was immediately annoyed with herself for noticing.

"I wasn't saying we should change it," he said. "I was just telling you."

"Then tell me after I've got the seat off."

He went quiet.

Clarisse kept working, but the silence settled around them like bad weather. She had heard his sea stories before. He was proud of Triton and Amphitrite, proud of belonging somewhere that had rooms he knew and people who could find him without asking. She liked that he had them. She also remembered what it felt like to be the daughter who hadn't grown up with the same kind of place waiting for her.

Percy was the youngest. That didn't mean he knew less. Sometimes it meant he had been given things the older two had only learned existed.

She pulled the damaged block loose. The wood came away in pieces.

"Well," she said. "That's worse than I thought."

Percy leaned forward, then stopped himself. "Can I see?"

"Yes."

He studied the damaged joint. "We could replace the whole brace."

"That's what I was thinking."

"Charlie might have the right wood."

"He does. I checked."

"You already asked him?"

"Yesterday."

Percy whistled under his breath. "You are annoyingly prepared."

"One of my more attractive qualities."

He laughed, briefly. Then he reached for the old block and turned it in his hand.

"Sorry I kept interrupting," he said.

Clarisse took the chisel back. "Sorry I snapped."

"Was the Triton part annoying?"

She didn't want to lie. "A little."

"Why?"

"Because we're working here. And sometimes it feels like you're comparing what we have to what they have."

Percy looked from her to the damaged canoe. "I wasn't trying to."

"I know."

"Do you want me to stop talking about Atlantis?"

"No. Then you'd go around acting as if we were made of glass, and I'd have to hit you with something."

He grinned. "That seems fair."

"Percy, listen. You get to miss people. You get to talk about them. I can be glad you've got them and still not want every problem in our cabin solved by something somebody built under the sea."

He was quiet for a while. Then he set the old block aside.

"Can I ask you something?"

"Depends."

"Do you like visiting Atlantis?"

Clarisse looked toward the lake. She had never been able to feel its currents the way Percy could, but she knew how the surface changed when wind came through the trees. Small ripples were moving toward the beach. She had wanted to finish the canoe before the afternoon training group arrived.

"Some parts," she said.

"Which ones?"

"The training courts. A kitchen where nobody expects me to be polite. A corridor where the windows look out over the dark water and you can see the lights from other buildings."

"Those sound like good parts."

"They are. Other parts make me feel as if I'm borrowing somebody else's clothes."

Percy frowned. "What do you mean?"

"I mean they know you. Not just your name. They know where you used to sit and what you liked to eat and what you said when you were little. They look at me and try to figure out what kind of daughter I am."

Percy pressed a thumb into the rotten block. He understood less of this than Clarisse wanted him to, and perhaps more than she realized. She could see him thinking.

"They expect things from me too," he said.

"Sure."

"Sometimes I don't know what they're talking about."

She glanced at him. "You mean they tell old stories you don't remember?"

"Yeah."

That was true enough, and it wasn't everything. Clarisse could tell there was something he couldn't explain, but she had learned not to pry every time he went quiet. Everybody had pieces of childhood they didn't enjoy talking about.

"Then ask," she said. "Let them tell you the story again."

"They think I should know it."

"People can think all sorts of stupid things."

He smiled. "You've built a philosophy around that."

"It's served me well."

Clarisse fitted the new block against the cleaned joint. Percy held the brace level while she marked the bolt holes. They worked together in a much better silence. When the bolts were tight, they lifted the canoe onto the ground and tested the seat. It didn't move.

"There," Clarisse said. "One thing that works."

Percy sat on the edge and bounced experimentally. The canoe creaked but held.

"One thing," he agreed.

She handed him the sponge and told him to clean the sawdust out of the bottom. He obeyed without argument, which was enough to convince her the conversation had helped.

When they finished, Clarisse signed the repair sheet and gave it to him to carry to the boat rack. She didn't say anything else about Atlantis. She wasn't sure she was finished being jealous of pieces of his childhood, and she wasn't going to pretend otherwise.

But when Percy asked whether she'd be willing to visit together someday, she considered before answering.

"Maybe," she said. "If it's a visit and not a campaign to make everybody happy."

"Okay."

"Ask Silena too. Don't assume she wants the same thing."

Percy nodded. "I will."

Clarisse pushed the canoe toward the water. "Now help me launch this thing. I want to know whether we fixed it before the younger campers get here."

He grabbed the opposite side. Together they carried it down to the lake.

---

Annabeth reached the canoe lake just as Clarisse and Percy were bringing the repaired boat back to shore.

She had a note from Chiron for the equipment shed, and she'd been about to turn toward the cabins when she heard Clarisse call for more weight on the stern. Percy adjusted his position, and the canoe came in level. They lifted it together, dripping water onto the grass.

For a moment she was about to hurry down to help.

Then she saw Clarisse laughing at something Percy had said. He laughed too, and they set the boat on the supports without dropping it. Whatever had been bothering them, they had done their own work through it.

Annabeth kept walking.

She delivered the note, then went to find Thalia. For the first time that morning, she was able to think about the gutter without imagining what Percy might be doing at the same moment.

It wasn't indifference. It was the simple fact that other people had relationships with him that didn't require her management.

---

Silena had turned the corner of the forge into a place where paint could dry without anybody mistaking it for grease.

She had borrowed a narrow shelf, covered it with clean cloth and put her unfinished work on the upper boards. A sheet of paper at eye level said PLEASE DO NOT TOUCH. Beneath that, in Clarisse's handwriting, someone had added ESPECIALLY YOU, PERCY.

Annabeth found her there after lunch, bent over a painted wooden sign for the garden path. Silena held a small brush in one hand and a jar of water in the other. She was trying to add a thin line along a vine without disturbing the color she'd already laid down.

"Can you hold this steady?" she asked.

Annabeth put her drawings on a stool and held the sign at the angle Silena indicated. The board was light, but the paint was wet enough to run if they tilted it too far.

"Thank you," Silena said.

"You could set it on a stand."

"I had one. The forge borrowed it for a weapon display."

"Charlie?"

"No, somebody who didn't ask Charlie and then told me he'd approved it." Silena smiled as she worked. "He helped me find another one, but the legs are crooked."

"Everything here has crooked legs."

"That's what you get for living in buildings designed by demigods who never finish the second half of their measurements."

Annabeth looked at the vine. The line was delicate and purposeful. Silena had used a darker green along the underside of the leaves so they seemed to lift from the wood.

"I like that," Annabeth said.

"The vine?"

"The shadow. It makes the edge look raised."

"I spent all morning trying to get it right."

"Did you?"

"Yes. People keep offering me things to paint for them. I like doing it, but if I say yes to everything I won't have time to finish my own work." She added the last stroke and leaned back. "There. You can put it down."

Annabeth laid the sign on the cloth, careful not to smear the fresh green.

"Do you ever feel guilty saying no?" she asked.

Silena rinsed the brush. "Often."

"Even when you know you should?"

"Especially then. It's easy to say no to something I don't want to do. It's harder when somebody I love has a real problem and my first thought is that I could probably help."

Annabeth watched her set the brush upright in its jar. They'd never talked much about the work of refusing people, because Silena was so good at helping that it was easy to assume she liked every request.

"How do you decide?"

"I ask what happens if I don't. Sometimes something terrible. Often somebody else learns how to use a paintbrush."

Annabeth laughed.

Silena turned the sign toward her. "You didn't come all the way here to admire the garden markers."

"I was looking for a ruler."

"There are six on the shelf."

"Thank you."

She chose one, then hesitated. Silena was busy. Annabeth could leave without putting her latest worry onto another person's table.

"Percy's been quiet," she said at last.

"I've noticed."

"Has he talked to you?"

"About what?"

"Atlantis."

Silena set down her brush. She didn't look surprised.

"A little. He asked me whether I liked the last time we visited. I told him I liked some parts of it."

"What parts?"

"The library, the kitchens and the way the palace gardens are lit. I didn't like everybody treating Percy as if he were the only child in the room with a reason to feel at home."

Annabeth understood the answer, but there was more underneath it.

"Did you tell him that?"

"Not exactly. I said I'd rather visit when we have somewhere to go and something to do, not just when a royal household wants a family portrait."

"Would you go if he asked you now?"

"I don't know. That's what I told him."

Annabeth looked at the sign. The vine seemed to curl across the board toward the empty edge, stopping before it reached the border. Silena had chosen to leave room for another line.

"I want to make it easier for him," Annabeth admitted.

"Of course you do."

"Thalia says I'm starting to make plans he hasn't agreed to."

"That sounds familiar."

"Don't say it like that."

Silena smiled. "I didn't say she was right."

"Was she?"

"I wasn't in the room. But if you're asking whether Percy likes having his life arranged for him, I'd say he likes it about as much as Clarisse does."

"That's not encouraging."

"Then don't arrange it. Ask him how you can help."

"I did."

"What did he say?"

"He wanted to talk."

"And did you let him?"

"Yes."

"Then perhaps that was enough for that evening."

Annabeth picked up her ruler. "I keep thinking I ought to be doing something."

"You are doing something. You're listening. And you're doing your own work. Both count."

Silena took the ruler back from her for a moment and ran a fingernail along the edge. There was a small chip near the end.

"Use the other one," she said. "That could throw off a measurement."

Annabeth exchanged it. Silena had not given her an elegant speech about love. She'd given her a ruler that was straight. Annabeth appreciated the distinction.

"Thanks," she said.

"Anytime. Now go before I ask you to redesign my shelf."

"I already have ideas."

"I know. That's why I'm sending you away."

They laughed, and Annabeth left with the ruler. Behind her, Silena returned to the painted sign. The garden path was going to get its marker whether Percy went to Atlantis or not.

---

At dinner, Clarisse watched Annabeth turn a piece of bread over in her hands without eating it.

"Your turn to worry about him?" she asked.

Annabeth looked up. "Is it obvious?"

"To people who know him, yes."

Percy was farther along the table helping a younger camper find the right piece of a broken practice shield. He held the strap in his lap while the camper explained how it had come loose. He looked more animated than he had at the beach. Annabeth was glad, though she wished she didn't immediately want to interpret every movement of his face as a sign.

"Did he tell you about the canoe?" she asked.

"Yes. We fixed it."

"I saw you."

"Then you know he didn't fall apart when I asked him to hold the boat."

"That's not what I'm worried about."

"I know." Clarisse took a bite of bread. "You're worried about what he'll choose next."

Annabeth hesitated. "Partly."

"Do you think he'll leave camp?"

"He says he doesn't know what he wants."

"Then he doesn't know."

"That's what everybody keeps telling me."

"Maybe because you keep asking everybody except him."

"I have asked him."

"Then believe the answer."

Annabeth looked at her. Clarisse had a way of cutting to the part of a problem people wished would stay vague. Sometimes she could be mean. This time the words were blunt enough to sting, but not meant to hurt.

"I know he loves Atlantis," Annabeth said. "I don't need him to stop."

"Good. Because he won't."

"I just don't know how to stand beside him while he decides."

Clarisse reached for the water pitcher. "Stand beside him. Let him see you're there. Don't climb into his head and start moving furniture."

Annabeth laughed despite herself. "That's a terrible image."

"Then remember it."

Clarisse filled her cup and put the pitcher back within reach. Annabeth finally tore off a piece of bread and ate it.

"Does it bother you that he thinks of Atlantis as home?" she asked.

"Sometimes."

Clarisse didn't elaborate immediately. She looked over at Silena, who was showing Charlie a small card covered in paint swatches. Charlie turned the card so he could see it in the pavilion light, then pointed at one of the colors. Silena shook her head and showed him another.

"I don't have the same history there," Clarisse said. "Neither does Silena. When Percy talks about a room where everybody knows his name, I can hear it like he's describing a place I ought to know. But that doesn't mean I want to take it away from him."

"Have you told him?"

"Some of it. Enough that he knows it's not simple."

Annabeth thought of Thalia's unfinished letter to Jason. Families could be separated by distance, by bad decisions, by the years two people had lived without each other. There was no diagram that could make every relationship symmetrical.

"Thanks," she said.

Clarisse shrugged. "Don't thank me yet. I'm going to make him clean the canoe tomorrow. He can miss the ocean with a brush in his hand."

Annabeth smiled. "He'll complain."

"He can complain all he wants. That's how I'll know he's feeling better."

A little farther down the table, Percy laughed at something the younger camper said. The sound carried across the pavilion. Annabeth looked toward it, then down at her own plate.

She wanted him with her. She wanted him happy. Neither want was shameful. The problem would be pretending she could always satisfy both by choosing for him.

She took another bite of bread and asked Clarisse about the next day's drills. Her friend's answer involved a complicated grievance about shields that had been left outside in the rain, and for several minutes the conversation had nothing to do with Percy.

Annabeth was grateful.

---

Percy spent the evening in Cabin Three with an open piece of paper and no idea how to begin.

He had promised himself he would write to Amphitrite. That was not difficult in theory. He knew how letters worked. He also knew what he wanted to say: he missed her, he missed Triton, and he wanted them to know that camp mattered to him without mistaking it for a rejection of his family.

The difficulty was that every version sounded like an announcement.

Dear Amphitrite, I may visit soon.

He crossed it out. Too definite. She might start making arrangements, and then he would have to explain why he'd sounded certain when he wasn't.

Dear Amphitrite, I miss home.

That made it sound as if Camp Half-Blood wasn't home at all.

Dear Amphitrite, I wish I knew where I belonged.

He stared at that one for a long time before crossing it out too.

It wasn't a sentence he could send. He couldn't tell her the full reason he felt displaced. She knew the boy she had raised in the altered history, and he remembered returning to that life carrying another childhood, one that included Sally alive for years Amphitrite had never known. Amphitrite had been kind to him despite the little distances between what she expected him to remember and what he could actually tell her. He didn't want to reward that kindness by making her feel she'd failed him.

Silena passed behind his chair carrying the finished garden sign. She stopped when she saw the paper.

"Bad letter?"

"Several bad letters."

"Want me to look?"

"Not yet."

"Okay."

She went to the door, then paused. "If it's to Amphitrite, you don't have to explain everything all at once."

Percy looked at her sharply.

Silena lifted an eyebrow. "You were looking at the shell seal on the table. I'm not reading your mind."

He glanced down. The little blue-waxed seal from an earlier letter lay beside his elbow. He had taken it out for no better reason than wanting something familiar near the page.

"Oh."

"You can tell her what you're doing this week," Silena said. "You don't have to decide the rest of your life in the first paragraph."

"That sounds suspiciously like advice."

"Then I'll deny giving it."

She went out with the sign. Percy listened to her footsteps fade toward the path. The simple suggestion had helped more than all the sentences he'd crossed out.

He began again.

He told Amphitrite about the beach markers and the canoe seat Clarisse had repaired. He told her about Silena's painted sign and Annabeth's amphitheater drawings. He asked after the gardens and whether Triton had stopped threatening the hippocamp with another haircut. At the bottom, he wrote that he had been thinking about another visit, but didn't know when it would make sense, and that he'd like to talk about it before anybody made plans.

This time the page sounded like him.

Clarisse came in with damp hair and dropped her training gloves onto a chair.

"Why is there a shell on the table?" she asked.

"Letter."

"To Atlantis?"

"Yeah."

She nodded, then went to change. A minute later she came back and examined the paper from a respectful distance.

"Are you asking us to go?"

"I'm asking whether we can talk about it. I don't want to make plans without you."

Clarisse seemed to consider how much she wanted to know.

"Good," she said. "And if you go without us, that's allowed too. Just tell us."

Percy felt a little of the pressure in his chest ease. "Thanks."

"Don't make that into a big thing."

"I won't."

She reached for the gloves and started to put them away. Then she turned back.

"Have you eaten?"

"Yes."

"A proper meal?"

"Yes, Clarisse."

"All right."

He folded the letter and left it under the small shell until he could ask Chiron about sending it. He hadn't made a travel decision. He had made a piece of communication he could stand behind.

It was enough for tonight.

---

Annabeth didn't see Percy after dinner. She learned later that he was writing to his family, though she didn't ask to read what he'd written.

Instead she sat at the Zeus cabin table with Thalia's unfinished letter to Jason nearby, a stack of drawings between them and the replacement gutter pipe waiting in the corner. When Thalia asked her to check one measurement, she did. When Thalia declined to show her the letter's ending, she didn't push.

Annabeth knew how to work beside somebody without possessing every part of their thoughts. The skill wasn't new.

Using it when she was afraid was the harder part.

---

The letter stayed under the shell through breakfast.

Percy checked it before he left for the arena, then put it back. He told himself he'd deliver it after drills. When drills were over, he remembered that Chiron would be busy with the younger campers until lunch. He spent the next hour helping replace a broken practice target and almost convinced himself the letter could wait until the end of the week.

Clarisse found him in Cabin Three after noon, putting the folded page into an envelope and taking it out again.

"Is it a letter or a training exercise?" she asked.

"A letter."

"Then why does it look like you're trying to defeat it?"

Percy looked at the envelope. "I don't know if I should send it."

"Does it say anything you don't mean?"

"No."

"Does it promise something you can't do?"

"No."

"Does it say something mean about us?"

"Clarisse."

"Then what are you afraid of?"

He sat down on the edge of his bunk. She was standing in front of the open door, the sun bright behind her, looking more like the person who would argue with a monster over an unfair rule than somebody offering advice about family letters. He was glad it was her anyway.

"If I tell Amphitrite I miss her, she'll be glad," he said. "And then she'll want to see me. That's reasonable. I'd want to see her too. But if I can't go for a while, I don't want her to think I was saying it just to make her happy."

Clarisse leaned against the doorframe. "You're assuming she's going to make an entire set of plans because you wrote that you missed her."

"She might."

"Then the two of you can discuss those plans."

"That's what the letter says."

"Then you've already done the part you can do."

He stared at her.

"What?" she demanded.

"That's very reasonable."

"Don't get used to it."

He picked up the envelope. "I feel like I'm disappointing somebody no matter what I choose."

"That might happen," Clarisse said.

He looked up again. Most people tried to reassure him that it wouldn't. Annabeth was more honest than that, but he could tell when she wanted to make every possible future less painful for him. Clarisse didn't bother.

"You can make a good choice and somebody can still be unhappy," she went on. "It doesn't always mean you made the wrong choice. It means other people wanted something different."

"Do you want me to stay here?"

"Yes."

The answer was so quick he laughed in surprise. Clarisse didn't laugh back.

"I like having you around," she said. "You do the annoying jobs when I ask, you steal all the clean towels, and you make Silena laugh when she's mad at me. The cabin would be quieter without you. That doesn't mean I'll chain you to the bunk."

"Thanks, I think."

"You're welcome."

He turned the envelope in his hands. "What about Silena?"

"Ask her."

"I did."

"What did she say?"

"She said she'd want to know how long and whether she could come if she wanted."

"Then you've got your answer."

He stood, slid the letter into his bag and checked that the seal would hold.

"You coming to the Big House?" he asked.

"No. I've got a practice target to inspect because somebody was hitting it wrong."

"Somebody?"

"You."

"That target was falling apart before I touched it."

"Then my inspection will be very fair."

Clarisse left him laughing. Percy followed after a moment, but he went in the other direction, toward Chiron's office.

The Big House porch was crowded with campers waiting to ask questions. One wanted to change archery groups. Another had lost a camp necklace bead. A third was trying to explain why a damaged shield did not count as his fault. Percy waited behind them without interrupting.

When his turn came, he held out the envelope.

"Could this go to Atlantis?"

Chiron looked at the address. "Amphitrite?"

"Yes."

"Certainly. It can go with the next dispatch. Is there a time requirement?"

"No. There's no emergency."

"Good." Chiron put the envelope into a folder and made a note. "Would you like me to tell you when it leaves?"

Percy considered. "Yes, please."

"Then I'll do that."

The exchange was so ordinary it startled him. Nobody asked why he was writing. Nobody demanded a return date. The envelope went into a folder beside other messages, and Percy walked back down the porch steps into sunlight.

He couldn't see what Amphitrite would think when she read it. He couldn't prepare every answer she might want. But the words he'd chosen were his, and he'd sent them.

He went to the arena to help Clarisse with the target.

---

Annabeth learned about the letter from Percy, not from anybody else.

He found her late that afternoon at the beach diagram table in the Big House. She was setting the updated map into a frame so Chiron could hang it where the younger campers would see the warning marks before heading to the water. Grover stood beside her, holding a corner level while she fixed the last pin.

"How's it going?" Percy asked.

"One more corner. Grover, lift yours a little."

Grover did. Annabeth checked the level and pressed the pin home.

"Done."

She stepped back. The map lay flat. The markers on paper matched the buoys Percy had helped set, and the warning near the far side was large enough to read without squinting.

"That looks good," Percy said.

"It should. You spent half a day putting those markers where I told you."

"I remember."

Grover stepped away. "I'm going to get Chiron to come look."

"Thanks," Annabeth said.

When they were alone, Percy leaned against the table, careful not to touch the framed map.

"I sent a letter to Amphitrite."

Annabeth set down the hammer. She was glad he'd told her. She was surprised by how much she wanted to know what was in it.

"What did you say?"

"Some things about camp. I asked about the gardens and Triton. I said I'd like to visit again, but I don't know when."

"Did you ask for a date?"

"No."

"Good."

Percy tilted his head. "Good?"

Annabeth heard the word the way he must have. It had sounded like approval, as though she'd been waiting to review his decision.

"I mean, I'm glad you didn't make a plan because you felt pressured."

"I didn't."

"That's what I meant."

He didn't move away, but his expression shifted. "You asked me three days ago what I wanted. I told you I wasn't sure. Are you relieved I still haven't decided?"

Annabeth felt her face warm.

"Not exactly."

"Then what?"

She could have said she only wanted him to be happy. It would have been true, but too easy. She wanted him happy in a way that also left her a place. She wanted him to stay at camp often enough that she could see him without scheduling a visit. She wanted him to love Amphitrite without leaving Clarisse or Silena behind, and to miss Sally without taking that grief out on himself. She couldn't have all of those wants arranged into a single neat solution.

"I'd miss you," she said.

Percy looked at her.

"If you stayed in Atlantis for a long time, I'd miss you. I know you could come back, and we could talk, and it wouldn't mean anything had changed between us. But I'd still miss you."

"Annabeth, I know."

"I don't think I said it clearly before."

"No. You mostly told me what I was allowed to do."

She took that in. He wasn't being cruel. She had given him permission he hadn't asked for because it was easier than admitting how frightened she felt.

"I asked Thalia about leave rules," she said.

"When?"

"The night after we talked on the dock."

"You didn't tell me."

"No."

He looked down at the diagram. "Were you going to arrange it?"

"I thought about it."

"Why?"

"Because I didn't like not knowing what would happen."

Percy looked tired suddenly, not from training but from having the same conversation about his life in another form. Annabeth felt the impulse to defend herself. She had only asked one question. She hadn't filled out a form or gone to Chiron or talked to Amphitrite without his knowledge.

But she'd been standing in the doorway of doing exactly those things.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I shouldn't have started planning something you hadn't chosen."

Percy was quiet for a few seconds.

"I don't want to keep explaining myself every time I think about home," he said.

"You shouldn't have to."

"And I don't want you to act like you don't care if I leave."

"I do care."

"I know. That's why I'd rather you tell me."

Annabeth crossed her arms, then uncrossed them. She had been angry at herself for being afraid and had nearly made that anger Percy's responsibility. The map behind him was finished. She could see the clean black labels and the carefully measured swimming lines. Some problems really could be solved by rules. This one couldn't.

"I need time to get used to not knowing," she said.

Percy sighed, then smiled faintly. "I can probably help with that. I spend a lot of time not knowing things."

"You're not supposed to enjoy that."

"Believe me, I don't."

She laughed, a little reluctantly.

He reached for her hand. "I don't want to leave you out. I just don't want the decision to belong to everybody except me."

"It doesn't."

"And when I do know what I want, I'll tell you."

She squeezed his fingers. "I want that. And when I want something, I'll tell you too, even when it makes the conversation harder."

"That's fair."

The apology hadn't made everything comfortable. Percy was still uncertain about Atlantis, and Annabeth was still afraid of having him away for long stretches. She appreciated that he didn't promise to stay simply to make her expression change.

Grover came back with Chiron before either of them could try to turn the conversation into another grand agreement.

"The map looks excellent," Chiron said.

Annabeth let go of Percy's hand and went to show him the warning symbols. Percy stayed to answer the questions about the buoys. They completed the inspection together, not as a couple performing a lesson for anyone, but as two campers who had helped with a safety job and knew where the work was done.

When they left the Big House, Percy didn't ask whether she was still worried. She didn't ask whether his letter would be answered quickly.

They walked toward the cabins together.

---

Over the following days, Annabeth paid attention to what Percy actually said instead of treating every glance at the Sound like the beginning of a departure.

Some mornings he was cheerful. On others he was quiet, and sometimes the quiet meant he'd slept badly or was thinking about training, not Atlantis. He argued with Clarisse over weapon storage. He helped Silena deliver the garden sign and returned with paint on his sleeve. He spent an afternoon with Grover trying to keep a young satyr from eating a map of the local trails.

Annabeth worked too. She drew the final amphitheater repair notes, revised the cabin gutter plan and helped teach a younger camper how to use a ruler without moving the measurement halfway through. None of those things required Percy to be there.

One evening, as the dining pavilion emptied, Clarisse stopped beside Annabeth's table with the water pitcher.

"Better?" she asked.

Annabeth thought about pretending not to know what she meant. "Somewhat."

"He's still thinking about Atlantis."

"I know."

"You're still thinking about him thinking about it."

"Probably."

Clarisse filled Annabeth's cup. "Then you're both being consistent."

Annabeth laughed. "That's one way to describe it."

"He's my little brother," Clarisse said. "He'll be annoying wherever he goes. You don't have to stop caring to let him choose."

"Did Silena tell you that?"

"No." Clarisse paused. "She told me I couldn't go around calling him an idiot every time he did something I didn't like."

"That's different."

"It's similar enough."

Clarisse went on to the next table, complaining that the younger campers were leaving cups everywhere. Annabeth watched her work. She'd learned to recognize Clarisse's kindness when it came disguised as an order. She'd also learned that Silena sometimes did the harder thing by refusing to step in.

Neither of them had solved Percy's problem. Both of them had made room for it.

---

On the last evening before the next set of drills, Annabeth asked Percy if he wanted to walk around the cabins.

They went the long way, past the strawberry fields and the sleeping archery targets, then around the edge of the pavilion. The night air was cool enough that Percy had borrowed a light jacket. Annabeth kept her hands in her pockets until they passed the campfire circle.

"Have you heard anything about the letter?" she asked.

"Chiron said it went out."

"Good."

He glanced at her. "You can ask questions, you know. I didn't mean you had to stop asking about Atlantis forever."

"I was trying to figure out which questions were mine to ask."

"What did you decide?"

"That I want to hear about the place you love. And that I can tell you when I'm afraid of losing time with you. I don't need to arrange the answers."

Percy thought about that while they walked.

"I like that you want to know," he said. "Sometimes I wish you could come into every room and see what I see. It would make explaining easier."

"I don't need to be in every room."

"I know."

"But I'd like to visit the gardens again."

"You would?"

"Yes. There are things Amphitrite was growing that I still haven't figured out. I wanted to ask her about the glass overhead and how she keeps the light from scattering."

Percy smiled. "I think she would like that question."

"I'd also like to hear the terrible jokes you keep telling me Triton makes."

"They're much worse in person."

"Then I'll prepare myself."

They rounded the last cabin and came to the path that led toward the beach. From there, they could hear the Sound, though it was out of sight behind the dunes. Percy stopped for a moment. Annabeth stopped beside him.

"Are you thinking about going?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Do you know when?"

"No."

She put her hand out. He took it.

"All right," she said.

They stood together while the distant water moved. Percy had two homes in his heart, and Annabeth knew something about that kind of division. She remembered years that her family here didn't remember, and she loved a sister who could tell stories about a childhood she had not lived as herself. Their displacements weren't identical. It mattered that they could describe the differences without turning them into a competition.

Percy tugged gently on her hand. "You know what I like about camp?"

"What?"

"That when I come back to Cabin Three, Clarisse asks whether I've eaten, and Silena asks whether I need help, and then they both annoy me about something ridiculous before I can answer."

"That does sound like home."

"It is."

He was smiling when he said it. Annabeth didn't ask him to repeat the word or decide what it meant for Atlantis.

At the fork toward the Zeus cabin, she stopped.

"I want to know your whole life," she said. "Not only the parts that include me."

Percy's smile turned smaller and more serious.

"I want you to know it," he answered. "I just need to figure out some parts of it myself."

"I know."

He drew her closer, and she kissed him, briefly, before stepping back. He was warm and familiar, his hand still around hers for the last second before they parted.

"Tomorrow?" he asked.

"Tomorrow. Unless Clarisse drafts you into another canoe repair."

"She will."

"Then after that."

He laughed. "After that."

Annabeth went up the Zeus cabin steps and found Thalia at the table, finishing her own letter to Jason. There was no reason to ask what she'd written. Her sister would tell her if she wanted to.

Annabeth collected her drawings from the corner and sat down across from her. The room was quiet except for paper moving and the faint drip of water into the bucket beneath the repaired gutter.

Thalia looked up after a while. "Everything okay?"

"Not everything," Annabeth said.

Thalia studied her, then nodded. "Want to talk?"

"Maybe later."

"Okay."

They worked for another hour, two sisters with different plans and things they couldn't fix for the people they loved. Annabeth's pencil moved across the page. Thalia folded her letter and set it by the door.

Outside, the sea remained where it was, far beyond the camp boundary. The next morning would come, with errands and drills and people needing the things they had agreed to do. Annabeth couldn't arrange the future to keep Percy at her side in every moment, but she could make room for him to tell her what he chose.

For the first time that week, she left the travel question alone long enough to finish her own drawing.
