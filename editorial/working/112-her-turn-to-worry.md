## Chapter 112: Her Turn to Worry

Annabeth first noticed the change because Percy stopped complaining about the swimming markers.

The small buoys at the camp beach had been damaged during a storm, and Chiron wanted them reset before the younger campers began another round of water exercises. Percy volunteered. So did two campers from Hermes, partly because they wanted to skip spear drills and partly because they thought a son of Poseidon could do all the difficult work while they stood on shore with the rope.

Normally Percy would have objected to both ideas, loudly.

That afternoon he worked without a word. He swam the marker lines out, checked their lengths, and came back to the shallows to explain where the anchors should go. When Connor Stoll tied a knot that slipped under tension, Percy took the rope, demonstrated the proper loop and handed it back. He didn't make a joke about land people or ask whether Connor intended to tie the whole beach into a bow.

Annabeth stood above the sand with her drawing board, recording the positions of the markers. She needed the measurements for an updated beach diagram. Every year someone moved a sign or shortened a rope, and the safety map Chiron kept in the Big House slowly drifted away from what was actually in the water. Annabeth hated that kind of problem. It could hurt somebody because a line on paper made adults think they knew something they hadn't checked.

"Another foot out," she called.

Percy nodded, slipped beneath the surface, and moved the anchor. The buoy settled a little farther from the shore. It bobbed twice, then held.

"Better?"

"Yes. Keep it there."

He rose through the water, wiped his eyes and looked beyond the marked swimming area. The Sound was blue-gray beneath the low clouds. A long way past the camp boundary, a pair of fishing boats were moving toward the harbor. Percy watched them until Connor shouted that the rope was tangling around his ankle.

Annabeth marked the new location and looked up again. Percy was already helping Connor out of the mess. Nothing had happened. Nothing was visibly wrong.

But she had watched him look at the sea three times in half an hour, and each time the look lasted a little longer.

She knew that expression. She had seen versions of it after difficult dreams, after letters from family, after ordinary things that reminded them of lives no one else could remember. Percy's face didn't always show fear the way other people's did. Sometimes it grew quieter. Sometimes he made more jokes, trying to keep himself moving before anybody asked him to stop.

Today there were no jokes.

By the time they finished, the Hermes boys were arguing about who had done more work. Percy handed them the last coil of rope and went to rinse the sand from his hands. Annabeth checked the notes against her measurements.

"You put this one exactly where I wanted it," she said.

"Good."

"Connor was trying to convince me the old line was safer."

"Connor doesn't like swimming farther than he can stand."

"He said the old line was traditional."

"That's Connor for 'I don't want to carry another anchor.'"

There he was. A small version of the Percy she knew.

She smiled and rolled up the map. "Do you want to walk back with me?"

"Sure."

They climbed the beach path slowly. Normally he would have carried the heavier board without being asked. Today he offered and then seemed to forget he'd offered when a gull passed overhead and cried toward the shore. Annabeth shifted the board in her arms and didn't remind him.

She could ask what was wrong. She could also wait until they had somewhere to talk without people stepping around them with wet ropes. Both choices felt possible.

At the top of the path, Clarisse was waiting with a list of the afternoon's equipment returns. She saw Percy coming and held out a pencil.

"You owe me an account of four missing practice spears," she said.

"I was in the water."

"I noticed."

"So I don't have your spears."

"I wasn't accusing you. I'm asking whether you saw Connor borrow the boat rack key."

Percy paused. "Maybe."

"Percy."

"He had a key. I didn't ask who gave it to him."

Clarisse frowned, then looked at Annabeth's drawing board. "Did you finish the markers?"

"Yes," Annabeth said. "The last buoy is farther out. Chiron should update the posted map before the next swim group."

"Fine." Clarisse made a note. "I'll find Connor."

She started down the path, but stopped to glance at her brother.

"You coming to dinner?"

"Of course."

"Then don't forget this time."

Percy looked surprised. "I haven't forgotten dinner."

"You forgot lunch yesterday."

"I had a late lunch."

"You ate two apples standing outside the forge."

"Apples are food."

"They're not lunch."

Clarisse went on before he could argue. Annabeth watched her leave. She was never subtle when she thought Percy wasn't taking care of himself. She was also usually right about whether he had eaten.

"Are you hungry?" Annabeth asked.

"Not really."

"That wasn't a trick question."

"I know."

They walked in silence for another few steps. At the intersection where the paths split toward Cabin One and the Big House, Percy finally looked at her.

"I'll see you at dinner."

"You can tell me if you want to go somewhere else."

"I know."

He gave her a quick smile and headed toward Cabin Three. She stood with her board under her arm until he turned the corner.

She didn't follow him.

It took more effort than she wanted to admit.

---

Annabeth spent the next morning inside the Big House trying to make a table sit level on a floor that had never been built level.

The old room was full of maps, and Chiron wanted the updated beach diagram fitted beneath the glass on one of the long worktables. There was no point redrawing every swimming marker if the resulting map warped and couldn't be read. Annabeth set a narrow scrap of wood beneath the shortest leg and checked the surface with a little bubble level she'd borrowed from the forge.

The bubble moved almost to the center, then rolled away again.

"You're fighting a losing battle," said Grover from the doorway.

"I'm fighting poor carpentry."

"That table was old when I got here."

"That doesn't make it better."

He came closer and helped her lift the corner while she slid a second shim into place. The bubble settled. Annabeth checked it from the other side, then nodded.

"Thank you."

"You're welcome. I was supposed to help with the beach inventory, but I got sent here because Chiron thought you'd need another pair of hands."

"Chiron was right."

Grover grinned and leaned over the map. He studied the shapes of the marked swimming lanes, then pointed to a small symbol near the far buoy.

"What's that?"

"Current warning. If the wind comes from the east, the water shifts there differently."

"Percy told you?"

"He showed me the position while we were setting the markers."

Grover looked at the note. "He knows a lot about the water."

"He does."

Grover glanced toward the window. "He's been pretty quiet."

Annabeth straightened. "You've noticed?"

"Hard not to. Usually I can tell where he is from the noise."

"He was fine during the work. Just distracted."

"Maybe he misses home."

She knew what Grover meant. She wasn't sure Percy would define home the same way.

"He lives here," she said.

Grover's ears moved beneath his cap. "I know. I meant Atlantis."

Annabeth looked at the sea line on the map. She had gone there with Percy on visits and seen enough to understand its scale. It wasn't only a palace or a collection of underwater rooms. It was where Amphitrite knew when he preferred breakfast and Triton knew which ridiculous story would make him laugh. It was a place full of people who remembered a childhood Percy had never experienced from the inside.

"I think he does," she said.

Grover waited. That was one of the things she liked about him. He didn't fill silence merely because somebody else looked uncomfortable.

"Did he say something to you?" she asked.

"Not really. We talked about the canoe race and he kept asking whether the water outside the border was calm."

"Was it?"

"Yes. I thought he wanted to swim."

Annabeth pressed her palm against the smooth glass. "Maybe he did."

Grover began sorting the inventory sheets into separate stacks. The room smelled of paper dust and the wood polish Chiron insisted on using every week. It was a practical, familiar smell. Annabeth put away her pencil, then took it out again because she had forgotten to mark the new location of the last warning sign.

"Do you want me to ask him?" Grover said.

"No."

He looked up.

"I mean, not on my behalf. You can talk to him because you're his friend. Just don't make it sound like we're holding a meeting about him."

Grover smiled faintly. "We'd need more chairs."

"We already have enough committees."

"Clarisse still mad you dissolved the last one?"

"She claims she was never a member."

Grover laughed, and Annabeth felt better for a moment. They finished arranging the map. When Chiron came to inspect it, the glass sat flat, the symbols were readable and the safety notes matched the actual markers. The work had been worth the time.

Then Chiron asked whether she wanted to help with an arena drainage diagram next, and Annabeth almost said yes before remembering she'd promised Thalia she'd check the cabin's repaired gutter after lunch.

"I can't today," she said.

Chiron looked pleased. "Then the arena will wait."

Annabeth gathered her notes and left. She'd been trying so hard to understand what Percy needed that she had nearly volunteered away the only free part of her afternoon.

That was not, she thought, how to be a good girlfriend.

---

They met at the lake after the evening meal.

Percy was sitting on the lowest dock plank with his shoes beside him. His toes were just above the water. He had rolled his sleeves to his elbows, and he was carefully untangling a piece of fishing line he'd found caught around one of the cleats.

Annabeth sat down at the other end of the board.

"Do you want help?"

"No. I've nearly got it."

She watched him work. He loosened the line, freed the hook without scraping his finger, and wound the coil into a small circle. He put it beside his shoes so somebody could dispose of it properly. Then he looked at her.

"You're very patient tonight."

"Am I?"

"You've been sitting there for three minutes and you haven't asked me anything."

"Would you like me to start?"

"Not especially."

Annabeth considered taking that literally. She was capable of giving him all the space he wanted if she knew what that space meant. The difficult part was that his silence was not automatically a request. She couldn't turn a few quiet days into an accusation, but she also couldn't pretend she hadn't noticed.

"All right," she said. "I'll ask one thing. You've seemed worried. Is it Atlantis?"

Percy stopped swinging his feet.

"A little," he admitted.

"Is someone hurt?"

"No. Nobody's hurt." He rubbed his palms against the plank. "Amphitrite sent an Iris-message last week. Everyone's fine. Triton showed me a picture of a hippocamp somebody had trimmed so badly it looked like a startled goat."

Annabeth laughed despite the tension. "Why?"

"He said the animal needed to see better. The hippocamp disagreed."

"That sounds like Triton."

"It does."

Percy looked across the lake. Freshwater reeds grew along the far bank. The water lay almost still, dark beneath the trees, nothing like the deep blue open sea outside camp. He had helped repair this dock. He'd trained near it, played games beside it, brought Annabeth here when neither of them could sleep. It was a place that belonged to his present life.

"Sometimes I think about going back for more than a visit," he said.

Annabeth held still.

"Not instead of being here forever," he added quickly. "I just think about how easy it would be to stay for a while. Amphitrite would find me something useful to do. Triton would make fun of me until I learned all the things I should know already. Nobody would ask when I'm coming back to camp, because they would assume I was home."

"Do you want to stay there?"

"I don't know."

She let the answer stand.

"I love them," Percy went on. "I love Clarisse and Silena. I love this place. I love being with you. It's not a choice between people I like and people I don't. If it were, this would be easier."

"I know."

"And I don't want Amphitrite to think I come home only when there's nothing interesting happening at camp. I don't want my sisters to think they're temporary either."

Annabeth reached for his hand, then stopped halfway. He noticed. He turned his palm upward and held it out to her.

She took it.

"You can miss Atlantis while you're here," she said. "You can miss camp while you're there."

"That doesn't give me two bodies."

"No. It doesn't."

He laughed softly. "You'd probably find a way to build a second body out of plywood if I asked."

"I'd at least start with a better material."

"Good point."

She watched a small ring spread across the lake where a fish had risen for an insect. Her instinct was to make a plan. She could suggest a trip, ask Chiron about leave, arrange dates, draft letters, compare travel times and decide which nights should belong to which family. Planning was what she did when an unsolved problem frightened her.

But Percy hadn't asked for an itinerary. He had told her he was lonely for a place he loved.

"You don't have to decide tonight," she said.

"I know."

"Do you want me to help you think through the options?"

"Maybe later."

"Then what would help now?"

Percy looked surprised that she had asked.

"Can I tell you about the last time I went? Not the whole thing. Just some of the parts I keep thinking about."

"Yes."

He began with the gardens, where Amphitrite grew pale sea flowers in enclosed pools beneath glass so thin Annabeth could barely see it. Then he described a corridor where the ceiling glowed blue when a school of silver fish passed over it, and the way Triton had challenged him to a ridiculous contest involving a shell and three currents. Those were memories Percy had actually made on recent visits. He knew their rooms and the sound of their voices from being there, not from the changed childhood everyone assumed he remembered.

Annabeth listened. She asked where the flowers got their light, whether the glass could withstand pressure and how badly Triton had beaten him at the contest.

"He didn't beat me," Percy said indignantly. "He cheated."

"How?"

"He didn't explain the rules until after I lost."

"That does sound like cheating."

"He says it's an important lesson in royal etiquette."

"Then perhaps you should write the rules down before you play again."

Percy smiled. It was the first time that evening his smile had reached his eyes.

They stayed on the dock until the lamps around camp began to come on. Annabeth didn't tell him where he belonged. She knew what she wanted, and it mattered, but she wasn't going to turn his affection for Atlantis into evidence that he loved her less.

When they stood to leave, he picked up the fishing line and carried it to the disposal bin. It was an ordinary piece of care for a place he also called home.

---

Annabeth came back to Cabin One with damp shoes and a mind full of things she had promised herself she wouldn't organize.

Thalia was at the table, sorting clean bandages into a wooden drawer. The drawer had three compartments. Thalia had filled them according to her own system: strips she could grab quickly, strips she thought she might need and strips she called useless until someone else asked for them.

"You're late," Thalia said.

"Percy and I went for a walk."

"I assumed."

Annabeth put her shoes beside the door rather than beneath the bed. She wanted them to dry, and she didn't feel like having another argument about muddy footprints.

"Do you know how camp leave works?" she asked.

Thalia's hand stopped over the bandages.

"For what?"

"If someone wanted to visit family for a while. Not forever. Just longer than a weekend."

"Depends on the family."

"I mean the permission."

"Chiron has to know where you're going, how you'll travel and whether somebody will be expecting you."

"Do you need a signed letter?"

"If the person asking is still under camp supervision, probably. Why?"

Annabeth realized she'd opened the drawer of questions she'd told herself to leave closed.

"No reason," she said.

Thalia gave her a look.

"Fine. There is a reason. Percy's been thinking about Atlantis."

"Is he going?"

"He hasn't decided."

"And you're finding out how he'd get permission?"

"I thought I could save time."

"Did he ask?"

"No."

Thalia slid the remaining bandages into their compartments. "You know what you're about to do, right?"

Annabeth sat on her bed, irritated by how quickly her sister had found the point. "I'm asking one question."

"You started with one question."

"That's generally where people start."

Thalia closed the drawer. "Then what happens? You find the travel rules, you work out a schedule, you go to Chiron, and Percy discovers everybody's discussed a trip he hasn't decided he wants."

"I wasn't going to do all that."

Thalia waited.

Annabeth looked away. She had already worked out which camp duties Percy would need covered if he left for more than a few days. She hadn't written them down, but she had been thinking about them. That didn't make her a bad person. It made her someone who knew how easily plans failed when nobody did the work in advance.

"I hate not being able to fix things," she said.

"So do I."

"You were going to say that's not what he needs."

"I was going to ask whether you've eaten anything since dinner."

Annabeth frowned. "I was at dinner."

"And you spent most of it drawing in the margin of a napkin. I saw how much you actually ate."

"Oh, don't start."

"I haven't started. There's bread in the basket. Eat some."

Annabeth took the bread, mostly to keep Thalia from making another point. It had gone a little stale, but there was honey on the table. She sat at the workbench and spread some on a piece.

Thalia pulled out her own chair. "You want to know what I think?"

"Will it be about bread?"

"No."

"Then go ahead."

"You're allowed to ask Percy what he wants. You're allowed to say you'd miss him if he went away for a while. You don't have to pretend you have no preferences."

"I know."

"But if he hasn't made a decision, don't make one for him because the waiting is uncomfortable."

Annabeth bit into the bread. She didn't like the advice because it was harder than a travel plan. At least a travel plan had blanks she could fill in.

"What would you do about Jason?" she asked.

Thalia's face changed. She didn't reach for the bandages again. "What do you mean?"

"If he told you he didn't know whether he wanted to stay at Camp Jupiter or visit more often. Would you wait for him to figure it out?"

"That depends what he told me and what was possible."

"You'd make a list."

"Probably," Thalia said. "I might even annoy him by asking too many questions."

Annabeth smiled faintly. "There you go."

"But I wouldn't book a trip he hadn't asked for."

"You'd want to."

"Very much."

Thalia picked up the empty honey jar and turned it to see whether there was any left in the bottom.

"The worst part of loving somebody who has a life somewhere else," she said, "is that sometimes you want to pull all the pieces close enough that you can see them. It doesn't make them fit."

Annabeth looked at her. Thalia was staring at the jar, not at her. It was rare for her sister to speak plainly about Jason being far away. Normally she made a joke about Roman discipline or sent him a letter full of practical advice. Annabeth had asked an unfair question, and Thalia had answered it anyway.

"You miss him," Annabeth said.

"Yes."

"Do you want to write to him?"

"I already started." Thalia pointed at a sheet of paper near the bandage drawer. Half a page was filled with cramped handwriting. "I keep putting it aside because I can't think of how to finish it without sounding like his mother."

"You're his sister."

"I know. I helped raise him for a while, and sometimes I forget I don't get to tell him where to be."

Annabeth pulled the paper closer, then stopped. "Can I read it?"

"Not yet."

"Okay."

They sat for a little while without talking. Annabeth finished the bread and closed the honey jar. Then she spread out the roof diagram they'd been working on together.

"The outlet still points into the walkway," she said. "If we rotate the last piece here, the water will miss the step."

Thalia leaned in to look. "Would it hit the rosebush?"

"Only if we let the pipe end too low."

"Then we can raise it."

They worked until the late lamps were lit. Annabeth liked that her sister could be practical and stubborn and funny about a piece of gutter, and could also tell her something true about missing Jason without making the whole evening about him.

Before bed, she put the leave question aside. She didn't write down a schedule. She would have liked to.

That was probably why she needed not to.

---

The next morning Percy went to the canoe lake with Clarisse, and the argument started before they had turned over the boat.

Clarisse had volunteered to fix a loose thwart in one of the smaller canoes. Percy had volunteered after she did, which she suspected was the problem. He'd been following her around the cabin for two days, offering to fetch tools she could reach and carry loads she could lift. Ordinarily she'd have enjoyed making him do the annoying parts of her jobs. Today she wanted the work to be quiet.

"Hold the bow," she told him.

Percy took the bow.

"Not like that. If you pull toward yourself, the whole thing tips."

"Got it."

He adjusted his grip. They rolled the canoe onto a pair of padded supports, and Clarisse checked the wood around the loose seat brace. The screws had been pulling out for months. She could have fixed the damage with a new block and two bolts, but she wanted to see how far the rot had spread first.

Percy leaned over her shoulder.

"Can I help?"

"You're helping. You're holding the boat."

"I meant with the repair."

"Then get me the chisel."

He handed it over. She worked beneath the seat, careful not to gouge the sound timber. The canoe rocked against the supports when Percy shifted.

"Hold still," she snapped.

"I am."

"You're moving."

"Because my foot is falling asleep."

"Then change feet without moving the boat."

He looked at her for a moment, then adjusted his stance.

A minute later he said, "Triton wouldn't use wood for this."

Clarisse stopped cutting. "Wouldn't he?"

"He said they use a different frame for the smaller boats. The joints can move with the current without coming loose."

"That's interesting."

"I could ask him about it."

"We're fixing a canoe. Not building an Atlantean fleet."

Percy looked hurt, and Clarisse was immediately annoyed with herself for noticing.

"I wasn't saying we should change it," he said. "I was just telling you."

"Then tell me after I've got the seat off."

He went quiet.

Clarisse kept working, but the silence settled around them like bad weather. She had heard his sea stories before. He was proud of Triton and Amphitrite, proud of belonging somewhere that had rooms he knew and people who could find him without asking. She liked that he had them. She also remembered what it felt like to be the daughter who hadn't grown up with the same kind of place waiting for her.

Percy was the youngest. That didn't mean he knew less. Sometimes it meant he had been given things the older two had only learned existed.

She pulled the damaged block loose. The wood came away in pieces.

"Well," she said. "That's worse than I thought."

Percy leaned forward, then stopped himself. "Can I see?"

"Yes."

He studied the damaged joint. "We could replace the whole brace."

"That's what I was thinking."

"Charlie might have the right wood."

"He does. I checked."

"You already asked him?"

"Yesterday."

Percy whistled under his breath. "You are annoyingly prepared."

"One of my more attractive qualities."

He laughed, briefly. Then he reached for the old block and turned it in his hand.

"Sorry I kept interrupting," he said.

Clarisse took the chisel back. "Sorry I snapped."

"Was the Triton part annoying?"

She didn't want to lie. "A little."

"Why?"

"Because we're working here. And sometimes it feels like you're comparing what we have to what they have."

Percy looked from her to the damaged canoe. "I wasn't trying to."

"I know."

"Do you want me to stop talking about Atlantis?"

"No. Then you'd go around acting as if we were made of glass, and I'd have to hit you with something."

He grinned. "That seems fair."

"Percy, listen. You get to miss people. You get to talk about them. I can be glad you've got them and still not want every problem in our cabin solved by something somebody built under the sea."

He was quiet for a while. Then he set the old block aside.

"Can I ask you something?"

"Depends."

"Do you like visiting Atlantis?"

Clarisse looked toward the lake. She had never been able to feel its currents the way Percy could, but she knew how the surface changed when wind came through the trees. Small ripples were moving toward the beach. She had wanted to finish the canoe before the afternoon training group arrived.

"Some parts," she said.

"Which ones?"

"The training courts. A kitchen where nobody expects me to be polite. A corridor where the windows look out over the dark water and you can see the lights from other buildings."

"Those sound like good parts."

"They are. Other parts make me feel as if I'm borrowing somebody else's clothes."

Percy frowned. "What do you mean?"

"I mean they know you. Not just your name. They know where you used to sit and what you liked to eat and what you said when you were little. They look at me and try to figure out what kind of daughter I am."

Percy pressed a thumb into the rotten block. He understood less of this than Clarisse wanted him to, and perhaps more than she realized. She could see him thinking.

"They expect things from me too," he said.

"Sure."

"Sometimes I don't know what they're talking about."

She glanced at him. "You mean they tell old stories you don't remember?"

"Yeah."

That was true enough, and it wasn't everything. Clarisse could tell there was something he couldn't explain, but she had learned not to pry every time he went quiet. Everybody had pieces of childhood they didn't enjoy talking about.

"Then ask," she said. "Let them tell you the story again."

"They think I should know it."

"People can think all sorts of stupid things."

He smiled. "You've built a philosophy around that."

"It's served me well."

Clarisse fitted the new block against the cleaned joint. Percy held the brace level while she marked the bolt holes. They worked together in a much better silence. When the bolts were tight, they lifted the canoe onto the ground and tested the seat. It didn't move.

"There," Clarisse said. "One thing that works."

Percy sat on the edge and bounced experimentally. The canoe creaked but held.

"One thing," he agreed.

She handed him the sponge and told him to clean the sawdust out of the bottom. He obeyed without argument, which was enough to convince her the conversation had helped.

When they finished, Clarisse signed the repair sheet and gave it to him to carry to the boat rack. She didn't say anything else about Atlantis. She wasn't sure she was finished being jealous of pieces of his childhood, and she wasn't going to pretend otherwise.

But when Percy asked whether she'd be willing to visit together someday, she considered before answering.

"Maybe," she said. "If it's a visit and not a campaign to make everybody happy."

"Okay."

"Ask Silena too. Don't assume she wants the same thing."

Percy nodded. "I will."

Clarisse pushed the canoe toward the water. "Now help me launch this thing. I want to know whether we fixed it before the younger campers get here."

He grabbed the opposite side. Together they carried it down to the lake.

---

Annabeth reached the canoe lake just as Clarisse and Percy were bringing the repaired boat back to shore.

She had a note from Chiron for the equipment shed, and she'd been about to turn toward the cabins when she heard Clarisse call for more weight on the stern. Percy adjusted his position, and the canoe came in level. They lifted it together, dripping water onto the grass.

For a moment she was about to hurry down to help.

Then she saw Clarisse laughing at something Percy had said. He laughed too, and they set the boat on the supports without dropping it. Whatever had been bothering them, they had done their own work through it.

Annabeth kept walking.

She delivered the note, then went to find Thalia. For the first time that morning, she was able to think about the gutter without imagining what Percy might be doing at the same moment.

It wasn't indifference. It was the simple fact that other people had relationships with him that didn't require her management.
