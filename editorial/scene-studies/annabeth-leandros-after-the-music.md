# Scene study: After the Music
**Placement:** Historical alternate study for provisional Chapter 223. **Superseded on setting**: author's later instruction establishes the first kiss by the lake at dusk, not the pavilion. This remains noncanonical and should **not** be used verbatim. Exact timing and circumstances depend on Chapters 217–222 being drafted.  
**POV:** Annabeth-limited. **Ages:** Annabeth ~14, Leandros 16. **Content:** age-appropriate, nonsexual romantic intensity; no timeline secret disclosed.

---

Annabeth had gone outside because Leandros was looking at her. That was the stupid, humiliating truth of it, and she was furious that she could not improve upon it by finding a better one. Inside the pavilion, the Apollo campers had been playing something fast enough to make people stomp along, and Percy had spent the better part of an hour demonstrating that he could dance with enthusiasm, which was very different from dancing well. He had made her laugh so hard she'd nearly spilled her cider. She still remembered the warmth of his hand around hers when he'd spun her, badly, into the end of a bench. She should have been thinking about that. Instead she'd looked across the room and caught Leandros watching the two of them with an expression she couldn't read, and the rest of the music had become unbearable.

It was cold beyond the lamps. She pulled Percy's scarf tighter around her neck, annoyed all over again at the tenderness of the gesture: he had handed it to her without asking when she complained about the draft, then immediately complained himself about being cold. That was Percy. A boy who could look straight at something terrible and find a joke crooked enough to get them through it, a boy who had known her when they were older and the world had taken things from them neither of them could name at camp. She loved him. She was not uncertain about that, and the certainty only made what she was doing worse. She had promised she'd be back after getting some air. The promise had taken no effort at all.

A door scraped against the pavilion's frame. Annabeth didn't turn, because if it was Percy she'd know his footsteps, and if it was Leandros she was going to be angry enough to say something she'd regret. It was Leandros. Of course it was. She could hear him hesitate before the steps, boots stopping on the boards rather than coming closer. For a moment she hoped he'd turn around and spare both of them whatever foolish conversation was about to happen. Then she hated herself for hoping, because half the reason she'd come out here was that she wanted him to follow.

“Are you hiding from the music,” he asked, “or from the fact that your boyfriend dances like a man trying to shake bees out of his trousers?”

“That was almost a decent insult. Have you been practicing?” Annabeth kept her gaze on the lawn. The frost hadn't settled yet, but the grass held a dull silver shine under the lamps and she could see her breath when she spoke. “And Percy doesn't care if he's bad at it. That's one of the things I like about him.”

“I know. I watched you laughing.” He came as far as the post on the other side of the porch, leaving the length of the railing between them. “You were happy.”

“I *am* happy.”

Leandros rubbed a hand over his face. He had the same frustrated expression he'd worn the first time she corrected his route calculations, when he'd mistaken her question for an attempt to make him look incompetent. She had expected him to be smug now, or cruel, or to turn the conversation toward some imagined competition with Percy. Instead he looked tired and embarrassed, and that made her angrier than smugness would have. She could argue with a boy who believed he was owed everyone's attention. She had no idea what to do with one who seemed to be ashamed of wanting hers.

“Then I shouldn't have come out,” he said. “I wasn't planning to. I was going to get another cup, tell Clarisse the music was awful, and go to bed. Then you walked out and I thought—” He stopped and laughed once, without any pleasure in it. “Never mind. It sounded better in my head.”

“Finish it.”

“No, Annabeth.”

“Don't start something and then treat me like I'm too delicate to hear the rest.” She turned toward him, more sharply than she meant to. “I've listened to you talk for two weeks about how much you hate people making decisions for you. But you do it to me whenever the conversation gets difficult. You decide I'll be offended, or that I'll laugh at you, and then you walk away before I have a chance to be either.”

“I thought you'd want me to follow you.” The answer came out rough and immediate. He looked as startled by it as she was. “There. Is that plain enough?”

It wasn't fair how quickly her anger changed shape. Annabeth stared at him across the porch, aware of a hundred details she had no business noticing: the way one sleeve of his coat had been repaired with mismatched thread, the white mark on his knuckle from the cold, the fact that he hadn't come close enough to touch her. He wasn't her kind of boy. He was proud in ways that made her want to throw things. He could be dismissive, particularly when a girl corrected him in front of somebody else. She had spent weeks telling him exactly why that was unacceptable, and he had spent weeks making it difficult to believe any of her words were getting through. Yet he'd remembered the first time she'd admitted she was frightened of losing control of her powers. He'd never mentioned it where others could hear.

“You need to go back inside,” she said, because she could not think of anything else that might rescue them.

“I know.”

She almost laughed at how much she hated those two words. “You don't get to say them like they solve everything.”

“Fine. I don't know.” He stepped away from the post, not toward her but toward the stairs, and for one painful instant she thought he really would leave. “I don't know what to do with you, Annabeth. You're the only person here who can make me feel like an idiot before breakfast and then make the rest of the day seem worth getting through. That's not a compliment. It's ruining my life a little.”

“That makes two of us,” she said.

The words escaped before she could take them back. He stopped. Inside, the musicians changed songs, and a girl shrieked with laughter as somebody knocked over a chair. The sound was ordinary and bright and belonged to a world in which Annabeth knew what she was doing. Out here she was fourteen again in all the worst ways, her body full of feelings that outpaced the explanations she could give them. She remembered being seventeen, remembered choosing Percy with the certainty of somebody who'd already lost him once and refused to do it again. She could not tell Leandros any of that. He saw the girl standing in front of him, with no other life peering over her shoulder, and there was something terrible in how much she wanted to be seen that way.

“Don't look at me like that,” she said.

“Like what?”

“As if you've just won an argument.”

He gave a short, incredulous laugh. “You think I'm pleased? I can't sit across from my own brother without thinking about you. Do you know how disgusting that makes me feel?”

Annabeth flinched. It was the first time either of them had said Percy's place in this aloud, not as an abstract fact but as someone who ate breakfast beside Leandros and trusted him with the small kindnesses of family. She wanted to tell Leandros he was being dramatic, that nothing had happened and there was nothing to feel guilty about. The lie rose almost effortlessly. She could make it sound precise and convincing, and in the morning she would hate herself for it. Instead she pulled the scarf away from her throat because it had suddenly become too warm.

“Don't use Percy to make me feel worse,” she said.

“I wasn't trying to.”

“Well, you managed.” She looked at his face and heard the ugliness in her own voice. “I'm sorry. That wasn't fair.”

“It was fair enough.” He leaned back against the post and closed his eyes for a second. “I keep telling myself this is about him. That I'm angry he had Atlantis and a father who bothered to remember him, and now I'm angry he's got you. It's the kind of thing I would have said when I first arrived.”

“Isn't it still true?”

“Some of it.” He looked at her again. “I hate that he gets things I've wanted for years. But when I'm talking to you about why the shoreline bends where it does, I forget about him. Then I remember, and I feel like the worst brother in the world. Both things are true. If I had a better answer, I'd give it to you.”

Annabeth had wanted him to admit that his interest was a competition, because then she could have despised him and gone back inside without risking anything. Instead he'd given her the truth, including a part that made him look small. She thought about the afternoon they'd spent arguing over a current mark, when she'd finally understood why he was furious that the map didn't bear his name. He had wanted proof that he existed in somebody else's story. She knew that hunger. She'd built whole cities in her imagination partly because she wanted to leave something in the world that couldn't be overlooked. It frightened her how easily she could mistake that resemblance for permission.

“You could leave,” she said quietly.

“So could you.”

“I was here first.”

He smiled then, reluctantly. “That is the most Annabeth answer you could possibly have given.”

She smiled back before she could stop herself. The tenderness of it undid her more thoroughly than his confession. He was still the same stubborn, infuriating boy who'd spent twenty minutes arguing about the ownership of a route sheet, and she suddenly wanted to tell him everything that had frightened her since she woke in this changed life. She wanted to talk until the lanterns went out and the camp woke again. She wanted him to look at her without knowing what she'd promised to anybody else, and the selfishness of that desire made her chest ache.

Leandros took a step down from the porch. “I'm going.”

Annabeth moved before she'd decided to. She caught his sleeve, the rough wool scraping her fingers, and he stopped at once. He didn't turn back immediately. She could feel how still he'd gone, and for a long, absurd moment the most important thing in the world seemed to be whether she loosened her grip before he looked at her. She could. Nothing had happened that couldn't still be explained as an argument. Percy was inside, probably telling someone an elaborate lie about his dancing. Her hand opened slightly.

“Annabeth,” Leandros said, facing her at last, “tell me to go.”

She hated him for saying it. She hated that he was leaving the choice with her when all she wanted was a world in which she wouldn't have to make it. Percy had been kind to her all evening. Thalia had been laughing for once. Nobody had asked Annabeth to carry a burden or solve an impossible problem, and she'd still found a way to turn an ordinary night into something she might never be able to repair. She looked at Leandros, at his uncertainty and his pride and the way he had finally stopped pretending either of them was simply annoyed by the other.

“I don't want you to,” she said.

Neither moved for a heartbeat. Then Annabeth stepped toward him and kissed him, brief and fiercely certain in the instant before certainty deserted her. He kissed her back, and when they drew apart he was still holding his hands at his sides as though he didn't trust them. She could hear her own heartbeat, ridiculous and loud, while the music continued through the wall. Leandros looked almost angry, and she understood because she was angry too: at him, at herself, at the fact that wanting something could be so simple while living with it would be anything but.

“That was a mistake,” he said.

“Then why did you kiss me back?”

“Because I wanted to.” He swallowed. “Because I've wanted to for days, and I was stupid enough to think I could make it go away by being rude to you.”

She wanted to laugh and cry and slap his arm all at once. Instead she stepped back, pressing her cold hands against the railing. “I'm not going to pretend Percy doesn't exist. I love him.”

“I know.” Leandros's face tightened. “I see it every day.”

“Then what are we doing?”

“I haven't got a clue.” He laughed bitterly. “You keep asking me questions like you think if I find the right answer we'll both get to be decent people again.”

Annabeth looked toward the pavilion door. She could go inside, find Percy, tell him something honest and terrible before the next song ended. She could save them from the worse damage that would come with secrecy. Instead she stood there, unable to bear the picture of his face when he heard, unable to bear Leandros leaving without another word. The failure was hers; she felt it happening in real time and made no move to stop it.

“Don't tell anyone,” she whispered.

Leandros stared at her. “You think that's going to make it better?”

“No. I think it'll give me until morning to figure out what to do.”

“And tomorrow?”

She looked away. “I don't know.”

The pavilion door opened before he could answer. Someone called her name from inside, and for a second Annabeth thought it was Percy. It was only Silena, asking whether she'd seen the extra cider jug. Relief hit hard enough that she nearly staggered. Leandros stepped back into the darkness beyond the steps, out of Silena's line of sight, and Annabeth hated the swift, practiced movement more than anything he'd said tonight. They had already learned how to hide.

“It's beside the bench,” Annabeth called, and was appalled by how ordinary she sounded.

“Thanks!” Silena let the door fall shut again. Music rose through the gap and faded as the latch caught. Annabeth's breath came out shakily, a small white cloud in the cold.

Leandros didn't move closer. “Go back to him,” he said.

She wanted to ask whether he'd be here tomorrow. The question was selfish and unfair and she asked it anyway. “Will you be at the shore in the morning?”

“I have work.”

“That's not what I asked.”

“I know.” His smile was crooked and miserable. “I'll be there.”

Annabeth went inside before she could say anything else. Percy was near the hearth, laughing with Grover, and when he saw her he lifted a hand to bring her back into the warmth. She loved the familiar gesture so much it hurt. She crossed the room and took his hand, knowing precisely what she had done outside, and when he asked if she was cold she nodded because the alternative was to tell him why she couldn't stop trembling.
