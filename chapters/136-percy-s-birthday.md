## Chapter 136: Percy's Birthday

Salt burned Percy's nose before his eyes opened.

Morning light cut through the porthole of Cabin Three in a green-gold bar, laying itself across his blanket. Somewhere beyond the walls, the surf at Long Island Sound kept its slow metronome. Fourteen. The number sat in his chest like a smooth stone found on the beach. He turned it over again. Fourteen.

A weight hit his stomach.

"Oof — Tyson!"

Tyson, visiting from the undersea forges for the birthday, beamed down at him, one large brown eye bright with mischief, his arms full of something lumpy wrapped in sailcloth. "YOU ARE AWAKE! Fourteen years! I counted!"

"Did you count by sitting on me?" Percy's ribs ached. Laughter bubbled up anyway.

From the doorway came a snort. "Get up, barnacle. Birthdays don't excuse laziness." Clarisse leaned against the frame with her arms crossed, spear already strapped to her back though it was barely past dawn. A smudge of sawdust clung to her cheek. Behind her, Silena slipped inside with a basket balanced on her hip, hair braided back with blue ribbon, smiling like sunrise over calm water.

"Don't listen to her," Silena said. "We brought breakfast."

Percy sat up, rubbing his face. The air smelled of warm honey and sea air and sawdust. "You guys didn't have to—"

"Eat," Clarisse ordered, tossing a still-warm muffin at his head. He caught it one-handed. "Before I change my mind and dunk you."

Tyson settled cross-legged on the floor, unwrapping his sailcloth bundle with enormous care to reveal three misshapen blueberry muffins and a shell polished to a shine. "I picked the berries. Clarisse did the oven. Silena stopped the fire."

"There was a small fire," Silena confirmed, perching on the edge of Percy's bunk. Her shoulder pressed against his. "Small. Mostly contained."

"To Percy," Tyson said solemnly, lifting his muffin like a goblet.

"To fourteen," Clarisse added, rolling her eyes but lifting hers too.

"To my baby brother," Silena said softly, and bumped her muffin against Percy's.

Percy took a bite. Blueberry juice burst tart and sweet across his tongue, the crumb still steaming. Outside, a gull cried once, sharp and distant. For a moment the four of them sat in the green-gold light chewing in companionable quiet, knees knocking under the blanket, salt on everything.

"So," Percy said around a mouthful. "Is Dad doing the whole—"

"Palace day. Yes." Clarisse wiped crumbs from her chin with the back of her hand. "Pack your sandals. We're leaving after inspection. And Percy? Try not to embarrass us."

"Me? Embarrass you?"

Silena laughed into her hand. "She means don't track sand on Amphitrite's floors."

---

The morning did not move as quickly as Clarisse had promised. By the time the palace escort was ready, Percy had been asked to answer three questions he couldn't answer without thinking too hard.

The first came from a younger camper who wanted to know whether a prince of Atlantis could choose the flavor of his own birthday cake. The second came from Grover, who asked whether Percy wanted anyone at camp to save him a slice of the blue cake being prepared for the next evening's celebration. The third came from Silena, who found him standing beside the cabin window with a muffin crumb caught on his sleeve.

"Is there anything you don't want us to bring up down there?" she asked.

Percy turned. "Like what?"

"Like the way everyone says you remember every corner of the palace. They ask you to tell stories, and sometimes you look as if you'd rather be somewhere else."

His first instinct was to say that he was fine. Amphitrite didn't know about his memories of another life; neither did his sisters. They knew the altered history, in which he'd been brought to Atlantis after Sally died when he was two. In the life Percy remembered, his mother had baked him blue food in a small Manhattan kitchen and lived long enough to see him become a teenager. The memory of that birthday cake came without effort. Recalling the nursery Triton talked about felt like reading the back of a photograph someone had labeled for him.

"I like hearing the stories," he said carefully. "I just don't always know what to add."

"Then don't add anything." Silena took the crumb from his sleeve and flicked it into the bin. "People can tell a story without making you perform in it."

"Easy for you to say. You wrote a song."

She gave him a look. "You weren't supposed to know that."

"I didn't. Until now."

Silena put a hand over her mouth, then laughed. "Forget I said anything."

"Can't promise."

She sat on the edge of the bed. The little bed in Cabin Three was nothing like the rooms the palace apparently had kept for Percy. Here his blankets smelled of camp detergent and the window had a small chip in its wooden frame. He knew where he belonged in this room because he'd made choices in it, not because someone told him he had slept there as a child.

"Can I ask you something instead?" he said.

"You already have."

"Do you mind going down? I mean really. Not because it's my birthday."

Silena's expression changed. She smoothed the ribbon of her bag between two fingers.

"I want to go," she said. "I don't always want to be introduced as if the palace made me. I grew up somewhere else. Sometimes I feel as if people want me to become a perfect daughter of the sea by the time dessert arrives."

"I don't want that."

"I know. Amphitrite's usually careful. But some of the attendants don't know what to do with us."

"Do you want me to tell Triton?"

"I can tell him." She paused. "I wouldn't mind if you stayed while I did."

"Then I will."

Clarisse appeared in the doorway with the travel bags. "Are we going or are we conducting interviews?"

"Both," Silena said.

"Terrible scheduling."

Clarisse set the bags down anyway and sat on the floor long enough to check the straps. Percy noticed the small cuts across her fingers, the result of finishing a wooden model ship under a deadline she'd invented herself. She hid them when she noticed him looking.

"You could have asked for help," he said.

"No," she replied.

"Why not?"

"Because then you would know what the present was."

"You still didn't have to hurry."

Clarisse's answer was unexpectedly quiet. "I wanted it ready."

The three of them sat for another minute, surrounded by packed clothes and presents Percy wasn't supposed to know about. Silena leaned her head against the bedpost. Clarisse flexed one hand and pretended the stiff fingers didn't matter. Percy let the smell of blueberry muffins and sea salt settle around them.

His birthday was not one family trying to replace another. He had a dead mother he remembered alive, an undersea household that loved a childhood he couldn't recall firsthand, and two older sisters who were still figuring out whether their father's palace had room for them. None of it could be solved before the escort arrived.

Then Tyson called from the path, loud enough for the entire Poseidon cabin to hear. "WE MUST GO IF WE WANT THE CAKE!"

Percy laughed. Clarisse hauled the bags upright. Silena picked up the basket of gifts and checked that its ribbon hadn't torn. They met Chiron at the shore rather than cutting across the freshwater canoe lake; Atlantis lay beyond Long Island Sound, where the saltwater currents could carry them safely under escort.

Percy looked back once at the camp buildings. Tomorrow there would be another celebration, another cake, a day in which the fourteenth year could mean nothing more demanding than growing into a new shirt. Today belonged partly to the sea. He was willing to go, and he was glad the others had chosen to come with him.

Beneath the Sound, light fell differently.

Percy had made the descent dozens of times, but the pressure change still popped his ears on the way down, and the cold still kissed the back of his neck before the palace warmth took hold. They rode the current together — Clarisse streamlined as a javelin, Silena trailing ribbons of bubbles as she spun once for fun, Tyson cannonballing with his arms tucked and shouting all the way down.

Atlantis rose from the blue gloom in tiers of pearl and coral, domes catching filtered sunlight and throwing it back in shards. Banners of woven kelp snapped in the current for the birthday, deep green marked with Poseidon's trident. Minnows scattered as the four of them touched down on the shell-paved courtyard.

A Nereid waited, her hair piled high, a conch tucked under her arm. "Lord Percy," she said, bowing. "Queen Amphitrite sends word. Luncheon in the tide garden. Lord Triton has arrived. Your father awaits."

Tyson clapped his hands. "CAKE?"

The Nereid's mouth twitched. "Her Majesty said something about cake, young Cyclops."

Clarisse elbowed Percy in the ribs as they followed. "See? Manners. You bow to Amphitrite first, you thank her for hosting, you do not — repeat, do not — call Triton 'fish-dude' to his face again."

"That was one time," Percy protested. "And he laughed."

"He bared his teeth," Silena corrected, linking her arm through Percy's to steer him. "There's a difference, sweetie."

Laughter echoed off the coral arches overhead. Servants darted past with platters of sea-grapes and urchin roe on ice. Somewhere a lyre tuned, strings plucked bright as rain. The palace smelled of brine and citrus and cold stone, and under it all, sugar.

Poseidon waited in the tide garden beneath a canopy of living anemones, their tentacles swaying violet and rose. He wore no crown, only a simple chiton the color of deep water, his beard braided back. When he saw them, his face split into a grin that crinkled the corners of his eyes.

"There's the birthday boy." His voice rolled like breakers. He pulled Percy into a hug that smelled of thunderstorms and lifted him half off his feet. "Fourteen. Hmm. Taller. When did that happen?"

"Last Tuesday, I think," Percy gasped.

Poseidon laughed and set him down, then clasped Clarisse's shoulder and kissed Silena's forehead and ruffled Tyson's hair with open affection. "My children. All together. Good."

On a stone bench beside him sat Amphitrite, regal in sea-silk the pale green of shallow water over sand. Pearls circled her throat. Her expression held its usual cool assessment, but her eyes softened a fraction when they landed on Percy.

"Perseus." She inclined her head. "Many returns."

"Thank you for having us, Queen Amphitrite," Percy said, remembering Clarisse's elbow. "And — uh — for the banners. They're nice."

"They are traditional for a son of the house coming of age." She gestured to a servant, who stepped forward carrying a dome-covered platter. "I had the kitchens prepare something."

The servant lifted the dome with a flourish.

Gasps rose around the garden.

The cake stood three tiers high, frosted in ombré blues from midnight at the base to foam-white at the peak. Sugar waves curled around its sides, frozen mid-crash. Tiny marzipan hippocamps leapt between them. At the top, fourteen candles burned though submerged, their flames teardrop-shaped and impossibly steady, casting gold flecks across the frosting. The scent of vanilla and sea-salt caramel drifted outward, rich enough to make Percy's mouth water instantly.

Tyson moaned. "Ohhhh."

"How do the candles burn underwater?" Percy asked.

Amphitrite allowed herself a small smile. "A queen keeps her secrets. Blow them out before luncheon, so we may eat."

"All at once!" Tyson urged. "Make a wish!"

Percy leaned in. Heat kissed his cheeks. The flames danced, reflected in the sugar glaze. He thought of Camp and the sea and Annabeth's laugh and Thalia's fierce grin and his mom's blue cookies back home. He filled his lungs with vanilla-scented water and blew.

Fourteen flames bent, guttered, and died in a swirl of silver smoke that spelled, for half a second, a trident.

Applause rippled through the garden. Poseidon clapped Percy on the back hard enough to stagger him. "Well done! Now — before your brother eats the table —"

Too late. Tyson had already sidled toward a bowl of candied kelp.

A shadow fell across the shell pavement, tall and green-gold.

"Father." The voice carried the snap of a sail in high wind.

Triton approached in full armor, scales polished, twin tails of his hippocamp team still churning the water behind the garden gate. His face held Poseidon's bone structure sharpened to severity, his eyes the same green but colder, like water over slate. He stopped before Percy and looked him up and down.

"So. Fourteen." Triton crossed his arms. "You look less scrawny than last year."

"Thanks?" Percy said. "I think?"

Clarisse coughed into her fist. Silena studied a flower with sudden fascination.

Triton studied Percy a moment longer, then his stern mouth eased at one corner. "Father tells me you held the line at the strait last month. And towed a sinking trireme with your bare hands."

Percy shrugged, heat climbing his neck. "The crew was still aboard. Couldn't let it go down."

"Hmm." Triton nodded once, as if confirming something to himself. "Adequate. For a surface-raised half-blood." He jerked his chin toward the gate. "Come. I brought your present. It waits outside. It bites if startled."

Excitement fizzed in Percy's stomach. He glanced at Poseidon, who winked.

Beyond the gate, in the open blue, two hippocamps pawed the water, their forelegs ending in foam-white hooves, their hindquarters tapering into powerful scaled tails banded turquoise and gold. Their manes floated like silk. One, smaller and dappled, tossed her head and whinnied — a sound like dolphin-song through a conch.

Triton laid a hand on the dappled mare's neck. "Her name is Breaker. Three years old. Quick, but not reckless. Or so her trainer claims." His tone turned dry. "She is yours for the afternoon. A full ride, the outer reefs and back, with me as escort. If you can keep your seat."

Percy's jaw dropped. "Seriously?"

"Do not make me repeat myself, little brother."

Tyson bounced on his toes. "HORSES!"

"Half horses," Silena said, laughing.

Triton swung up onto the larger stallion with practiced ease, armor flashing. He extended a hand to Percy. "Mount up. Legs tight. Hands light. And Perseus — hold on."

Percy gripped Triton's forearm and hauled himself up behind the saddle ridge onto Breaker's warm back. The mare's muscles bunched beneath him, scales smooth as polished glass under his thighs. The scent of her mane filled his nose, clean brine and sun-warmed kelp.

Breaker surged.

Water tore past Percy's face, cold needles giving way to exhilaration. The palace shrank behind them to a pearl gleam. Ahead, the reef spread in riotous color — staghorn coral orange as embers, sea fans swaying purple, schools of silver fish parting like curtains. Triton rode alongside, shouting instructions over the rush.

"Lean with her! Not against! There — good!"

Percy leaned, and Breaker responded instantly, banking left around a coral head. Joy shot through him, bright and fizzy as soda. He whooped, and the sound ripped away behind him. Breaker flicked an ear back, as if amused, and poured on speed.

They raced along the drop-off where the seafloor fell away into indigo. A sea turtle glided below, unhurried. Sunlight speared down in pillars, full of drifting motes. For a stretch there was only motion and breath and the drum of hooves against water.

Triton pulled even, his stallion snorting foam. "You ride well!" he called, grudging admiration in his voice. "For someone taught on ponies!"

"Thanks!" Percy shouted back. "You steer well! For someone old!"

Triton's laugh cracked out, startled and genuine. He shook his head. "Cheeky brat."

They slowed near a grove of kelp to let the animals breathe. Breaker nuzzled Percy's knee, whiskers tickling. Percy stroked her muzzle, feeling the velvet-soft skin and the strong jaw working.

"She likes you," Triton observed, quieter now. His armor had dulled with exertion, his hair coming loose. He looked younger, less like a statue. "Good horses know."

"She's amazing. Thank you." Percy met his brother's eyes. "I know I'm — the other family. The surface one. I appreciate you coming."

Triton was silent for a beat, watching the light move through the kelp. "You are my brother," he said at last, simply. "Surface or sea, you carry his blood. And today you are fourteen. That deserves acknowledgment." He cleared his throat, suddenly brisk. "When we return, do not tell Clarisse I said anything sentimental. She will never let me hear the end of it."

Percy grinned. "Deal."

"And Perseus." Triton turned his stallion toward home. "Happy birthday."

Wind — current — rushed past again as they wheeled and ran for Atlantis, neck and neck, laughing.

---

The ride finished at the stable gate, but Triton didn't take Percy straight to the garden. One of the handlers was waiting beside the water trough with a frightened young hippocampus, its mane floating in a dark cloud around its neck. It had caught a hoof against the coral step at the edge of the practice channel and refused to move.

"Not injured badly," the handler reported. "We've checked the hoof. She just won't enter the channel again."

Triton swung down from his mount. The older prince was wearing ceremonial armor for the birthday, polished enough that Percy could see the blue water reflected in the scales. He didn't seem to care that the stable floor was covered with sand and dung. He crouched beside the frightened animal and let it smell his hand.

"She slipped," he said to Percy. "And now she expects the same thing to happen again."

"Can we move the step?"

"We can check whether the step is the problem. Don't start tearing up stable fittings because an animal is afraid."

Percy wanted to argue that if a hoof had caught there once, it could catch again. The handler showed him the edge: smooth, level, not new or damaged. The young hippocampus had made a bad turn at speed. She had been startled more than hurt.

"Would you like to take her through slowly?" Triton asked.

Percy looked at the animal. Her eyes rolled toward him. "I don't want to scare her more."

"Then don't. Hold a lead. Let her choose."

He took the rope and stood beside the open channel. Triton moved to the other side with a bucket of water plants the young mare liked. The handler walked away from the entrance so the animal wouldn't feel surrounded.

Percy waited. He didn't summon a wave or order the water to lift her over the step. Those things might have worked in a fight. They would have taught the mare that she could cross only when a prince carried her.

She took one step, stopped and snorted. Percy backed up half a pace, giving the line slack. Triton didn't tell him to hurry. A minute later the mare stepped forward again. Her first crossing was ugly and awkward, with one hoof lifted absurdly high and her tail curled tight. She crossed anyway.

"There," Percy said softly.

"Again?" the handler asked.

"Not immediately," Triton replied. "Let her settle."

Percy scratched the mare's neck and watched her breathe. There was something useful about a creature so strong needing patience from other people instead of a display of their strength. He'd ridden Breaker at racing speed only minutes earlier; now he was grateful to be standing still.

The handler thanked him. Percy looked at Triton.

"Was she afraid of the step or of getting hurt?"

"Probably both. Would you be able to tell the difference if you fell?"

"Maybe not."

"Then stop expecting animals to be simpler than people."

Triton checked that the handler had the rest of the afternoon covered. Only then did he turn toward the tide garden.

"You do this on your own?" Percy asked.

"I'm responsible for the stables."

"I thought you mostly raced."

"I race because I know the animals, little brother. Not the other way around."

Percy absorbed that. Triton had plenty of titles and could have spent the afternoon boasting about them. Instead he'd crouched in mud beside a frightened young horse until she crossed a shallow channel under her own power. It made their competition earlier feel warmer, less like a prince indulging another prince for a birthday.

They walked toward the palace together. The corridor smelled of warm kelp bread and fresh flowers. At the garden entrance Triton hesitated, looking suddenly older than he had on the racetrack.

"One thing," he said.

"What's that?"

"You may hear stories at lunch about when you were small. The household remembers them fondly. You don't have to answer every story with one of your own."

Percy looked up sharply. "Did Silena say something?"

"She asked me not to put you on display. She was right."

"You listened?"

"Don't look so surprised. I listen more often than you think."

Percy laughed. Triton had no idea how much the permission mattered. If he asked why Percy sometimes looked lost during those childhood stories, the answer would pull at a secret Percy was not ready to give the palace, or Amphitrite, or his siblings. But Triton hadn't asked. He'd noticed enough to make the evening kinder without demanding an explanation.

"Thank you," Percy said.

"Don't thank me yet. I fully intend to tell everyone you nearly lost the race."

"I won."

"You attempted a creative definition of the finish line."

"That's called strategy."

"That's called being fourteen."

They entered the tide garden still arguing. Percy smelled caramel and fresh bread before he saw the tables. Whatever strange stories the household told during the meal, he would have people beside him who knew how to laugh and when not to ask.

By the time they returned, salt-streaked and starving, the tide garden had transformed.

Long tables of driftwood bore platters: grilled sea-bass with lemon kelp, black-bean seaweed salad, bread rolls warm from volcanic vents, bowls of bright fruit Percy didn't know the names of. Amphitrite presided at the head, Poseidon at her side already deep in conversation with an old merman about tides. Clarisse had saved Percy a seat between her and Tyson, with a look that said skip it and die.

"You're glowing," Silena whispered as Percy slid in, dripping. She pressed a warm towel into his hands. "Good ride?"

"Best ever," Percy whispered back. "Triton's actually — nice? When no one's watching."

Silena's eyes sparkled. "Our secret."

Tyson leaned over, his plate already mountainous. "Percy! I saved you the crunchy bits!" He dumped half his bread rolls onto Percy's plate.

"Thanks, big guy." Percy ruffled his hair. Flour dust puffed off Tyson's knuckles.

Luncheon unfolded in a happy clatter of shells and laughter. Poseidon told an embarrassing story about Triton at age fifty getting tangled in fishing nets and needing rescue by dolphins. Triton retaliated with a story about Poseidon trying to lecture a hurricane and getting sneezed on. Amphitrite listened with regal patience, but Percy caught her hiding a smile behind her cup.

Between courses, servants cleared space, and Tyson stood, suddenly shy, twisting his huge hands together.

"I made a thing," he mumbled. "For Percy. It took a long time. It is not sharp."

Clarisse groaned fondly. "Go on, Tyson. Show him."

Tyson ducked under the table and emerged with a long shape wrapped in kelp cloth. He laid it before Percy with reverence.

Percy unwrapped it.

A sword lay in the cloth, forged from rose-pink coral, its hilt wrapped in dark leather, a pearl set in the pommel. Light glowed through the blade's edges, translucent as sunrise. Along the fuller, Tyson had carved careful wavy lines and, near the guard, in crooked Greek letters: PROTECTOR OF FRIENDS.

Percy lifted it. The balance sat light and eager in his palm. He tested the edge with his thumb. Dull as a spoon.

"It's blunt," Tyson said quickly, anxiously. "So you cannot get hurt in practice. And so your mom — Amphitrite — will not worry. I made it blunt on purpose. I can sharpen later when you are fifteen. Or sixteen. Or never, if Silena says."

Percy's throat tightened. The coral warmed in his grip, humming faintly with the love poured into its shaping.

"Tyson." He stood and hugged his brother hard around the middle, coral sword still in one hand. Tyson's laugh rumbled through both of them. "It's perfect. I love it."

"You mean it?" Tyson's eye shone wet.

"I will. I'll practice with it every day. And when monsters come, I'll bonk them."

"Bonk," Tyson repeated happily, tasting the word. "BONK!"

Around the table, laughter erupted. Even Triton chuckled. Amphitrite raised an eyebrow but said nothing, which from her amounted to approval.

Clarisse stood next, carrying a wooden box the length of her forearm. Sawdust still clung to her sleeves. Her usual scowl had softened into something almost bashful.

"Okay, so. Don't make a big deal." She set the box before Percy and flipped the latch. "I made it in the workshop. Took three weeks. Stabbed myself twice."

Inside, nestled in straw, lay a model trireme, carved from dark oak and pine, every oar tiny and even, sails of stitched canvas, a trident painted blue on the hull. The nameplate at the stern read PERSEUS in neat capitals. The craftsmanship showed hours — sanded smooth, joints tight, rigging knotted with sailor's precision.

Percy lifted it as if it might shatter. The wood smelled of oil and pine resin. Oars clicked softly when he tilted it.

"Clarisse..." He turned it over, marveling at the tiny rudder. "This is incredible. Did you — all the oars move?"

"One hundred and seventy," she muttered, ears pink. "Counted. Don't break it."

"I won't. I swear." He looked up at his sister, fierce and awkward and brilliant with a chisel. "Thank you. This must've taken forever."

She shrugged, but her eyes stayed on his face, hungry for his reaction. "You're my brother. You're fourteen once. Besides—" her voice dropped, gruff with feeling she tried to hide — "ships bring you home. Figured you could use one that fits on your shelf. So you remember."

Percy stood and hugged her, careful of the ship in his hands. She stiffened a second, then hugged back hard, one-armed, her armor cold against his cheek.

"Best sister-warrior ever," he murmured.

"Yeah, yeah," she said, but she didn't let go right away.

Silena rose before Percy had fully sat down, her hands empty.

"I don't have a box," she said. "Mine won't fit in one."

She stepped into the open circle of sand at the center of the garden and nodded to the lyre-player, who stilled. Silence rippled outward. Water hushed through anemones.

Silena sang.

Her voice rose clear and sweet, no accompaniment at first, a song of her own making, woven for Percy. The melody curled like smoke, telling of a boy found in the surf, of sisters with spears and laughter, of a brother huge and gentle, of storm and shelter. The words were simple, concrete: rope and salt, bread and blankets, hands held in dark water.

*Little tide, little light,*
*carried home on foam,*
*sister keeps your left side,*
*brother walks you home...*

Percy sat motionless, the model ship cradled in his lap, Tyson's coral sword across his knees. Silena's eyes never left his, bright with tears she didn't shed. When she reached the last verse, her voice lifted, strong and sure, promising to sing him home from any sea.

The final note hung in the water, pure as a bell, then dissolved.

Quiet held for a heartbeat. Then Poseidon clapped, Amphitrite inclined her head deeply, and the garden burst into applause. Tyson wept openly.

Percy crossed to Silena and wrapped his arms around her. She smelled of jasmine and varnish.

"Did you write that?" he whispered, voice thick.

"Last month. In secret. Clarisse threatened anyone who listened." Silena laughed wetly against his shoulder. "Happy birthday, baby brother."

"Nobody's ever — thank you." He squeezed her tighter. "I'll remember every word."

"You'd better," she said, kissing his temple. "I'll quiz you."

Cake followed, thick slices of vanilla sponge with salted caramel and blueberry tide-swirls, eaten with mother-of-pearl spoons. Percy ate two pieces, Tyson ate four, Clarisse stole the marzipan hippocamp off Percy's plate when she thought he wasn't looking. He was looking. He let her.

Poseidon leaned back, arm draped along Amphitrite's chair, watching his children with deep contentment. "Fourteen," he mused. "A good age. Old enough to ride hippocamps and wield — blunt — swords. Young enough to still eat cake with both hands."

"I used a spoon," Percy protested, licking caramel from his thumb.

"Mostly," Silena said.

---

Percy found Poseidon standing beside the terrace rail after the meal, holding a small wooden box.

His father had been at the table for part of the afternoon, laughing when Tyson declared the bread a superior engineering achievement and listening while Triton described the new stable supports. Then a messenger had summoned him away. He'd returned without an explanation, and Percy had tried not to count the minutes.

"Come here a moment," Poseidon said.

Percy joined him. The box was no bigger than a book, its surface marked with waves and a little trident.

"Another present?"

"Something I meant to give you before the guests arrived."

Percy opened it. Inside lay a small bronze instrument with two arms and a ring in the center, the sort of tool Annabeth might use to measure a slope.

"What does it do?"

"It measures the pull of a current against an anchored point. Triton helped design it."

Percy turned the instrument over. Its markings were fine, and one arm had a flexible joint that would move in running water.

"That's actually useful," he said.

"I hoped it might be."

His father sounded pleased, but Percy heard the uncertainty under the answer. Poseidon could command tides and storms; he was less sure how to give a fourteen-year-old something that didn't feel like an attempt to purchase the right kind of gratitude.

"Thank you," Percy said.

Poseidon nodded. "There is something else. I have to leave before the evening ends."

Percy's hands stopped moving.

"Why?"

"An issue with the eastern boundary. Triton can explain."

"Is it dangerous?"

"Not here."

"Then why can't you stay?"

Poseidon looked toward the open sea. The silence lasted long enough that Percy began to regret asking.

"I have responsibilities beyond this house," he said at last.

"I know that."

"And I cannot always arrange them around a celebration."

"I know that too."

Percy closed the instrument case. He did understand that the sea belonged to more people than him. Understanding did not make the disappointment smaller.

"Did you know this morning that you might have to leave?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Then you could have told us. Silena spent half the meal wondering whether you'd come back."

Poseidon looked toward the dining room. "She did?"

"Yes. Clarisse pretended she wasn't counting, but she was. They've had their whole lives learning not to expect you. I don't want my birthday to turn into another day where they feel foolish for hoping."

His father was quiet. Percy felt his heart hammer in his throat. He wasn't sure whether he had the right to speak for his sisters, but he knew what he'd watched at the table.

"I should have said something sooner," Poseidon answered.

"Could you tell them yourself before you go?"

"Of course."

Percy looked at him. "And don't say you'll be back tonight unless you know."

Poseidon's mouth tightened. For a moment he looked less like a father and more like a king unaccustomed to having his promises examined. Then his shoulders shifted.

"I won't promise it," he said.

They went inside. Poseidon explained the departure to Clarisse and Silena without waiting for Percy to interpret it. Silena listened quietly. Clarisse asked when the eastern boundary would be checked again and whether a report would come back. Poseidon answered what he knew and admitted what he didn't.

"Will you be here in the morning?" Clarisse asked.

"I can't say."

"Then don't tell the kitchen to set a place."

He accepted that. Percy knew a single truthful conversation would not make up for years of uneven attention. But it prevented the evening from becoming a new promise his sisters were expected to defend on his behalf.

After Poseidon left, Silena found Percy near the dessert table.

"That wasn't easy," she said.

"What wasn't?"

"Standing there when he told us he had to go. I know you wanted him to stay."

"Didn't you?"

"Yes. I still do."

She put her hand on the wooden case. "You can like his present and be disappointed. Those are different things."

Percy looked at the current gauge. It was a good gift, and he intended to use it. He would also remember the hour his father had left, not because he wanted to nurse a grievance but because he was tired of pretending absence didn't have a shape.

Clarisse joined them with three small plates of cake.

"I saved you a slice before Tyson ate everything," she said.

"Heroic."

"Don't make me regret it."

They ate together beside the open terrace. Silena described the way the kelp fibers had been woven into the napkins. Clarisse challenged Percy to test his new instrument when they returned to the coast. Neither asked him to stop wanting his father. Neither tried to make the day perfect for him. The party continued because they were all still there, and their presence did not have to compete with the place Poseidon had left empty.


---

Sunset filtered down through fathoms in shafts of copper when the Grace sisters arrived through the passage Chiron and Triton had arranged.

Percy heard them before he saw them — sandals slapping on shell pavement, Thalia's laugh, low and bright, and Annabeth's voice, slightly breathless, asking directions. His stomach flipped. He set down his third cup of nectar and stood so fast his chair tipped.

Annabeth rounded the coral arch first, hair coming loose from its ponytail, cheeks flushed, eyes scanning until they found him. She wore a blue dress for the occasion, simple, with a silver owl pin at the shoulder, and carried a long scroll case. Behind her strode Thalia, tall in black and silver, spiked bracelet glinting, a pinecone-topped staff in hand, grinning like she'd already pranked someone.

"There's the old man!" Thalia shouted, opening her arms. "Fourteen! Practically ancient!"

"Thalia!" Percy laughed as she crushed him in a hug that smelled of pine and ozone. Static crackled off her jacket. "You made it!"

"Wouldn't miss it, kelp-head." She ruffled his hair mercilessly. "Zeus threatened storms. I told him to stuff it."

Annabeth waited, smiling, suddenly shy. Percy turned to her, his face heating. Fourteen felt young and ancient at once.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi." He wanted to hug her and didn't know how with everyone watching. She solved it by hugging him first, quick and fierce, her cheek cool against his. The owl pin pressed his collarbone. She smelled of lemon and old paper.

"Happy birthday, Percy," she murmured.

"Thanks for coming. All the way down here."

"Thalia carried me half the way. Don't tell anyone." She pulled back, eyes bright. "Can we — walk? I have something for you. Not cake. I ate your slice already."

"You what?"

"Tyson gave it to me. He said you wouldn't mind."

Percy glanced over. Tyson waved, mouth full. Percy laughed. "Come on. I know a spot."

They slipped away as Thalia commandeered Percy's chair and demanded cake from a startled servant. Clarisse immediately engaged her in an arm-wrestling challenge. The garden roared.

Percy led Annabeth along a path of crushed shell to a quiet overlook where the reef fell away and the open water stretched blue to black. A stone bench sat tucked under sea-lilies. They sat shoulder to shoulder, knees touching, scroll case across both their laps.

Salt tingled on Percy's lips. Far below, a whale sang, so low he felt it in his sternum more than heard it.

"Okay," Annabeth said, twisting her ring — the coral ring he'd given her, he noticed with a jolt. She still wore it. "Mine's not — it's not carved or sung or ridden. It's kind of nerdy."

"I like nerdy," Percy said. "I'm dating nerdy."

Her ears turned pink. "Shut up and open it."

He grinned and unrolled the scroll.

Stars bloomed across dark blue papyrus.

Hundreds of them, inked in silver and gold, precise as mathematics, constellations linked with fine lines. At the center, larger than the rest, two figures: Perseus holding the head of Medusa in one hand, sword in the other — and beside him, Andromeda, chained to rock but unbowed, one hand outstretched toward him. Between them, a date inscribed in Annabeth's neat architect's hand: the night they'd met. Beneath, in smaller script, coordinates and a line from an old hymn: *when the sea met the sky and neither looked away.*

Percy's breath caught. The silver ink caught the fading light and seemed to move.

"Annabeth..." He traced Andromeda's outstretched hand with one fingertip. "Is this — that night? The sky?"

She nodded, tucking hair behind her ear. Her voice went soft, earnest. "I calculated it. Backwards. From the date, the latitude of Camp, the time you said the bus dropped you — I charted what was overhead when we first talked in the life we remember. See?" She pointed to a cluster near Perseus's shoulder. "That was rising right then. And that — that's the star I used to call Athena's, back then. Watching." She swallowed. "I wanted you to have the sky you walked under to find me. So you always know the way back."

Percy stared, throat tight, eyes stinging with salt that wasn't sea. The chart blurred slightly. He blinked hard.

"This is — Annabeth, this is the best —" Words tangled. He looked at her instead, at the gray eyes steady on his, at the smudge of ink on her finger from drawing stars for him. "Thank you. I'll keep it by my bunk. Forever."

She smiled, relieved and glowing. "Good. Because Thalia helped carry the ink and she'll kill me if you don't like it."

"I love it." He carefully rolled the chart and held it like something sacred. Then, impulsively, he bumped his shoulder against hers. "You're the best girlfriend in either life. Official ruling. As a fourteen-year-old man of wisdom."

"As a fourteen-year-old man of wisdom, you're a dork," she said fondly, bumping back. Her hand found his on the bench, fingers lacing brief and warm and chaste, a promise more than a press. "Happy birthday, Seaweed Brain."

They sat a while, watching silver fish flicker through lily stems, saying nothing, which said everything.

---

By the time they returned from the shore, Percy had remembered a birthday promise he'd made to Tyson and forgotten to keep. He had said they would visit the equipment shed to see the new hinges Tyson had designed for the visitors' bunk, and the afternoon was almost gone.

"We can do it tomorrow," Percy offered.

Tyson looked down at the wrapped parcel in his hands. "I leave early."

Percy felt his stomach sink. In the rush of cake and visitors and presents he'd managed to make his brother's small request the easiest thing to postpone.

"Then let's go now," he said. "Before everyone starts the next game."

They walked to the shed together. Tyson unfolded the drawing on the workbench and explained how the hinge would let the bed frame fold against the wall when no guest was staying. Percy had thought it was a complicated solution to a simple problem. Then Tyson showed him how much room it would leave for Clarisse's training equipment.

"You're making the cabin work when you're not there," Percy said.

"Yes. I am not there most days. That is still our cabin when I visit."

Percy examined the measurements and pointed out that one hook would hit the window latch. Tyson checked, grinned and shifted the hinge line half an inch.

They didn't build it that evening. Percy promised to bring the amended plan to Chiron for approval, and Tyson agreed to wait until the cabin's occupants had seen it.

"Thank you for looking," Tyson said.

"I'm sorry I nearly forgot."

Tyson hugged him hard enough to crease the drawing. "Birthdays are very busy."

"Not too busy for you."

They returned to find the arm-wrestling competition noisier than before. Percy slipped the folded design into his pocket so it wouldn't vanish under the table with the ribbons and cake plates.

When they returned, arm-wrestling had escalated.

Thalia had her sleeve rolled up, boot braced against the table leg, face red with effort. Clarisse matched her, teeth bared, biceps trembling. A crowd ringed them, shouting. Tyson refereed with a breadstick.

"FOUL!" Tyson cried. "Clarisse lifted elbow!"

"I did not!"

"Rematch!" Thalia gasped. "Best of — oof — three!"

Poseidon caught Percy's eye over the fray and beckoned him to a quieter alcove where Amphitrite had withdrawn with her ladies, leaving the birthday chaos to the young. Annabeth squeezed Percy's hand once and went to rescue cake for him, mouthing *good luck*.

Thalia disentangled herself, breathing hard, and followed Percy, clapping chalk dust from her hands. "Truce, La Rue. Your brother needs his old-lady blessing."

"I'm fourteen!" Percy protested.

"Exactly. Ancient." Thalia slung an arm around his shoulders and steered him to the alcove's edge where the current ran cool and quiet. Her expression shifted, teasing falling away to something older, fiercer, tender. "Walk with me a sec, barnacle boy? Big-sister stuff."

They stepped beyond the lantern light, where anemones glowed faintly. The party noise softened to a happy hum.

Thalia stopped and faced him, hands on his shoulders. Up close, her eyes — Annabeth's eyes, storm-gray — held no mischief now.

"Okay. Listen." She took a breath. Pine and ozone clung to her. "When Annabeth first told me about you — this scrawny kid with sea-green eyes who wouldn't leave her behind — I thought, great, another hero who's gonna get her killed." Her grip tightened. "Then I met you. And you kept showing up. In the Sea of Monsters, at Westover, at the mountain. You show up, Percy. Even when it's stupid. Especially when it's stupid."

Percy snorted. "Thanks?"

"I'm not done." She shook him lightly. "You're fourteen now. That's — the gods know what's coming for us is only gonna get bigger. I can't — I can't always be there. Annabeth can't always be there. You're gonna have nights where the water's dark and nobody's singing you home." Her voice roughened. "When that happens, I need you to remember you're not alone in it. You've got sisters who'd spear the sky for you. You've got a brother who'd carry the world. You've got a girl who charts stars to find you. And you've got me."

Percy's eyes burned. He stared at the shell pavement to keep from embarrassing himself.

Thalia tipped his chin up with one knuckle, forcing him to meet her gaze. "So here's my blessing, as the eldest Grace sister and self-appointed pain in your butt. May your heart stay soft when the world tries to harden it. May your courage outlast your fear. May you live — you hear me? Live — past sixteen, past twenty, past a hundred, annoying and brave and kind." She smiled, tears bright in her own eyes. "And may you always know which Grace sister to call when you need lightning and which to call when you need plans."

"Lightning is you," Percy managed, voice cracking. "Plans is Annabeth."

"Damn right." She pulled him into a hug, fierce and brief, her cheek pressed to his temple. Static hummed through him, warm, not shocking. "Happy birthday, little brother. Not by blood. By choice. Which is stronger."

Percy hugged her back hard, memorizing pine and ozone and strength. "Thank you, Thalia. For choosing me."

"Always," she said simply.

She released him and punched his shoulder, grin returning. "Now come on. Your girlfriend saved you cake and your sisters are about to start singing without you and Triton looks like he's going to cry if anyone mentions feelings again."

Percy laughed, wiping his eyes quickly. "He already got sentimental on the ride."

"He WHAT? Oh, this is blackmail gold."

They walked back arm in arm into light and laughter.

The evening wound down in a golden-blue haze. Amphitrite presented Percy with a small pearl that glowed from within — "for courtesy," she said, which Clarisse later translated as high praise indeed. Poseidon gave him a hug that cracked his back and a whispered promise of sailing lessons in the mortal world with his sisters. Tyson fell asleep with his head on the table, coral sword tucked under his arm for safekeeping. Silena taught Annabeth the chorus of her song, and the two girls sang it softly together while Thalia and Clarisse pretended not to listen, elbows linked, cheeks flushed with nectar.

Percy sat in the middle of it all, model ship in one hand, star-chart safe in its case across his knees, cake crumbs on his shirt, sisters and brothers and father ringing him close. Annabeth's shoulder rested against his. Thalia's boot knocked his under the table in solidarity. The whale sang again, far off, low and sweet.

Fourteen. The number no longer sat like a stone. It floated, light as foam, bright as silver ink.

He thought of his wish on the candles — *let this last, let them stay* — and looked around at salt-sticky faces laughing in lantern light.

For tonight, at least, the sea remembered to be kind, and the sky forgot to be distant, and Percy Jackson, son of Poseidon, brother to warriors and singers and giants, beloved of a star-charting girl, was home.
