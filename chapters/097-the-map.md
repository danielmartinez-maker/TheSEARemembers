## Chapter 97: The Map

The morning Annabeth finished the map, it rained for exactly eleven minutes and then stopped as if someone had turned off a faucet.

Annabeth Grace knew because she counted. She sat cross-legged on the floor of Cabin One with the big sheet spread out around her like a frozen lake, a ruler in one hand and a magnifying glass in the other, listening to the rain tick against the high windows and watching the light change across the paper. Zeus's cabin smelled like ozone and clean wool and pencil shavings. Her gray blanket was folded on her bunk. Her work lamp burned warm yellow. The rain made a soft hush on the roof, and then, just as she inked the last tiny olive leaf on the rise by the shrine, the hush cut out. Silence. Then birds.

Thalia, sprawled on the top bunk with one arm hanging down, lifted her head.

"Was that it?" Thalia asked. "Done?"

Annabeth did not answer at first. She set down the pen very carefully, the way Beckendorf had taught her to set down a hot weld, and sat back on her heels and stared.

The map covered almost the entire floor. Five feet by six feet, heavy watercolor paper, edges taped flat with masking tape to keep it from curling. It had taken her months. It had started, in truth, back in the spring with the shrine project, when she had drawn that first little site map with the three olive trees and the yarn circle and the compass rose pointing north. Chiron had kept that seven-page proposal in his office and had shown it to her again in June with the corners soft from handling.

"You draw places like you love them," he had said. "Have you ever thought of drawing all of it?"

She had thought of it ever since.

Now it lay finished under the morning light. Camp Half-Blood in ink and watercolor and colored pencil, north at the top, the Sound glittering blue-gray at the right edge, Half-Blood Hill with the pine and the Golden Fleece at the left, the highway crawling along the bottom. Every cabin in its place around the green, each one drawn not as a rectangle with a number but as itself, porches and columns and paint colors and gardens. The dining pavilion with its twelve tables and its brazier. The Big House with its blue porch railing. The arena, the forges, the stables, the strawberry fields in neat rows, the lake, the canoe dock, the climbing wall with its lava seams, the amphitheater, the woods with paths winding in dotted lines, the shrine on its low rise between lake and woods with three olive trees spreading silver-green.

And everywhere, small marks in gold ink. Stars and dots and tiny handwritten notes. *Best blackberries. Do not pick without asking the dryad Mallow. She likes jokes.* *Quietest step on the dock is the third board from the end.* *Echo spot. Stand here and whisper.*

There were hundreds of them.

Annabeth's hands were stained with ink to the second knuckle. Her neck ached. Her eyes burned. She felt like she might cry and also like she might laugh until she fell over.

"Annabeth?" Thalia said, swinging down. She landed barefoot and padded over in her pajamas, careful not to step on the paper. She crouched at the edge. "Hey. Talk to me, baby girl."

"It's done," Annabeth whispered. "It's actually done."

Thalia studied the map, eyes wide and bright. She understood buildings the way soldiers understand ground, and she understood Annabeth the way only an older sister could. She did not touch the wettest ink. She just leaned close, her black hair falling forward, her breathing going quiet.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, Annie. It's beautiful."

Annabeth swallowed hard. "You really think so? Not too crowded? Chiron said not to make it too crowded, and I tried to layer it, but there was so much everyone wanted in—"

"Stop architect-braining for one second," Thalia said. She bumped Annabeth's shoulder with her own. "Look at it with your heart, not your ruler. It's beautiful. It's home."

The door creaked open and Percy Jackson stuck his head in, damp hair plastered to his forehead from the rain, a paper bag in one hand and two apples in his pockets.

"Permission to enter the sacred drafting temple?" he asked. "I bring offerings."

"You're tracking water," Thalia said.

"I'm a son of Poseidon. I am water," Percy said, stepping in anyway and toeing off his sneakers by the door out of long habit. In his remembered original life, Sally Jackson had raised him to remove shoes when paper was on the floor. He crossed in socks and stopped dead at the edge of the map. "Whoa."

He went to his knees. The paper bag crinkled. He forgot it completely.

"Annie," he said. "Is this— is this the map? The big one?"

Annabeth nodded, suddenly shy. It was one thing to show Thalia, who had watched every draft curl at the edges. It was another to show Percy, who found his way by feeling and story more than by north arrows.

Percy set the bag aside and leaned forward, hands hovering over the surface without touching. His sea-green eyes moved fast, the way they did when he read the water before a swim.

"There's Cabin Three," he breathed. "You drew the fountain. And the shell trim. And— is that seaweed on the roof?"

"The gutters grow a little seaweed in summer," Annabeth said. "Grover said it was healthy. I checked. It is."

Percy laughed, bright and startled. "And the lake. You put the lake sparkles. And the canoe—"

He stopped. In the lower corner of the lake, near the reeds, Annabeth had drawn a tiny gray rock half in the water with a small figure sitting on it, knees pulled up, head tilted back. Beside it in her neat architect's printing: *Percy's thinking rock. Best at sunrise. Ask Percy before bringing friends. He will almost always say yes but likes to be asked.*

Percy stared at that label for a long time. His throat worked.

"You put my rock," he said quietly.

"Of course I put your rock," Annabeth said. "It's important."

"It's just a rock."

"It's never just a rock with you," Thalia said gently. "That's why she put it."

Percy looked up at Annabeth, eyes shining. "Can I— can I look at the rest? Properly?"

Annabeth's chest loosened. "That's what it's for. But careful, the shrine corner is still wet. And don't step on the woods. The ink in the woods smudges if you breathe on it wrong."

Percy grinned and lay down flat on his stomach, chin in his hands, like he had done since they were eight and Annabeth did puzzles on the living room floor. "Tell me everything."

---

By noon the whole camp knew the map was finished, because there was no keeping a secret in Cabin One when Thalia Grace was excited.

Thalia had run to breakfast with Annabeth's rolled practice sketch — not the real map, which was still taped to the floor drying under weighted string — and unrolled it on the Zeus table and shouted, "My baby sister drew the entire camp and you're all going to cry," loud enough that Mr. D looked up from his Diet Coke and said, "No crying at the tables before noon," and then came over to look anyway.

Chiron arrived with his wheelchair wheels squeaking on the pavilion stones. He examined the practice copy through his glasses, his expression moving from polite interest to something deeper and wetter around the eyes.

"Is today the unveiling?" he asked Annabeth, who had followed Thalia in with ink still on her fingers and her bandana crooked.

"I— I guess," Annabeth said. "The final needs until this afternoon to dry. The gold ink takes forever. But if people want—"

"We want," said Clarisse, appearing over her shoulder with a tray. Clarisse La Rue, daughter of Poseidon, big and scarred and surprisingly gentle with paper, peered at the sketch. "Is that the arena? You drew the new dummy rack?"

"I drew everything," Annabeth said. "Or tried."

"Good," Clarisse said gruffly. "About time someone drew us right."

Silena, radiant in a yellow sundress with her charm bracelet jingling, looped her arm through Clarisse's and leaned in. "Is there a legend? Please tell me there's a beautiful legend with little symbols. I love legends."

"There's a legend," Annabeth promised. "Three kinds of marks. Gold stars for gathering places. Gold dots for quiet places. Blue waves for water places. And every secret spot has the name of who it belongs to and whether you need permission."

Grover trotted up with his reed pipes in his pocket and clover in his curls, balancing his breakfast plate. He took one look and his ears — well, his horns hidden under his rasta cap — seemed to perk up. "Did you put the wild corner? By the shrine?"

"I put the wild corner," Annabeth said. "And the rosemary. And the thyme path. You'll check it, right? Before we hang it? Make sure I got the plants right?"

"Of course," Grover said, already emotional. "Of course I will."

Lee Fletcher from Apollo whistled. "You got the chimes?"

"Got the chimes."

Beckendorf rumbled, "You got the forge vents?"

"Got the vents, with your warning about hot air in summer."

Travis and Connor Stoll peered upside down from the Hermes table. "You got the hiding spots?"

"I got only the hiding spots people said I could put," Annabeth said firmly. "No prank routes without consent. House rules."

"Damn," Travis said admiringly. "Ethical cartography. Hot."

Even Nico di Angelo, sitting at the end of the Hades table with his Mythomagic cards in a neat stack, looked up with interest. "Did you put the shadow niche?" he asked quietly. "With the stars?"

Annabeth crossed the pavilion to him, crouched so their eyes were level, the way she had learned to do in the dust behind his cabin during their recent shrine work. "I put the shadow niche with moonstone stars exactly where you placed them. And Bianca's name where you carved it. I sealed it with fixative so it won't smudge. Do you want to see before everyone else? To make sure it's right?"

Nico's pale cheeks pinked. He nodded hard. "Please."

Chiron clapped his hands once, and the pavilion quieted.

"Then it is settled," he said, eyes twinkling. "This afternoon, at four o'clock, in front of the Big House, we will unveil Annabeth's map of Camp Half-Blood. All campers who wish to attend are welcome. Which is to say, all campers are expected to attend unless on sickbed or patrol. Annabeth, you will need a frame and an easel and a cloth. I believe the arts-and-crafts cabin has an unveiling cloth from the solstice pageant. Grover, will you gather wildflowers for the border? Silena, will you oversee refreshments?"

A cheer went up, loud enough to startle the pigeons off the pavilion roof. Percy stood on the bench — he could never not stand on benches when excited — and shouted, "MAP! MAP! MAP!" until Annabeth pulled him down by his shirt and hissed, "You are going to wrinkle it just by thinking about it," and everyone laughed.

Annabeth sat back down, heart hammering. She had presented buildings before. She had defended measurements to Chiron and argued mortar ratios with Beckendorf. But this was different. This was not a wall or a shrine. This was everything.

Percy slid onto the bench beside her, shoulder to shoulder, and passed her the paper bag, now slightly damp.

"Forgot," he said. "Brought you blue cookies. Mom sent them. She said architects need sugar."

Annabeth opened the bag. Inside, wrapped in wax paper, were six perfect blue chocolate-chip cookies, still soft. Amphitrite's blue cookies, made from the recipe Percy had described, the color of summer sky and lake water, the taste of home.

"She remembered," Annabeth whispered.

"She asked me to tell her when the map was ready," Percy said simply. "She said to tell you she's proud of you and she wants a photo of the final. And she said—" He fished out a folded note. "Here."

Annabeth unfolded it. Amphitrite's flowing handwriting: *For the brilliant girl who draws her way home. With love, Amphitrite. P.S. Save Percy a cookie or he will perish dramatically.*

Annabeth laughed, tears pricking, and broke a cookie in half and handed Percy the bigger half.

"Amphitrite knows me," Percy said with his mouth full. "Tell her I perished anyway, for effect."

Thalia stole a cookie and ruffled Annabeth's curls. "Eat, Wise Girl. You've got a long afternoon. The people demand cartography."

Annabeth ate, and the sugar steadied her hands, and the pavilion buzzed around her with map talk, and for the first time all morning the tight knot between her shoulder blades began to ease.

It was real. It was happening. At four o'clock she would show them home.

---

At three-thirty the Big House lawn looked like a festival.

Silena had outdone herself. Long tables borrowed from the dining pavilion held pitchers of lemonade and iced tea and strawberry juice, plates of cheese and crackers and grapes, a bowl of ambrosia squares cut small for sharing, and, in the place of honor, a tray of Amphitrite's blue cookies with a handwritten sign: *ONE PER PERSON UNTIL ANNABETH HAS HAD THREE. — P.J.*

Grover and the Demeter kids had woven garlands of clover and late summer flowers and draped them between the porch posts. Juniper the dryad had contributed vines that curled politely around the easel legs without touching the paper. The satyrs had tuned their pipes. Even the visiting hellhounds wore flower collars and lay panting happily in the shade while Peleus guarded the border, thumping their tails when anyone passed.

Beckendorf and the Hephaestus kids had built the easel — a massive, sturdy oak frame with braces and wheels, capable of holding the five-by-six sheet in its pine frame without wobbling in wind. They had sanded it smooth and oiled it and burned a small owl into the top crossbar for Athena, and a trident and a lightning bolt beside it, intertwined.

"For all of you," Beckendorf said gruffly when Annabeth traced the carvings with her fingertip, eyes stinging. "You three. Together."

The map itself waited under a deep blue cloth sewn with silver stars — the old solstice pageant curtain, washed and pressed. It stood on the easel on the porch steps so everyone on the lawn could see. Chiron sat beside it in his wheelchair, Mr. D lounging in a lawn chair with sunglasses on, pretending not to care and adjusting the cloth when the wind lifted a corner.

Campers gathered in clumps on the grass. Ares kids with arms crossed, trying to look cool and failing. Aphrodite kids with cameras. Apollo kids with a song half-written already. Demeter kids with dirt still under their nails. Hermes kids taking bets on who would cry first. Hypnos kids half-asleep in the sun. Hecate kids whispering about preservation spells. Iris kids debating frame lighting. Even the Hunters, visiting for the week, stood in a neat silver line at the back with Thalia bouncing between them and her sister like a charged particle.

Percy saved a spot on the grass in front with his jacket. "For the architect's boyfriend," he told anyone who tried to take it. "Official business. Move."

Annabeth stood behind the porch railing with Thalia, peeking out, stomach churning.

"There are so many people," she whispered. "What if I misspelled something? What if the scale is off? What if the Hecate cabin really is twelve feet west of where I put it and now everyone will get lost forever because of me?"

Thalia put both hands on her shoulders and turned her around, the way she had done before spelling tests and sword tests and the first day Annabeth ever led a capture-the-flag team.

"Hey," Thalia said firmly. "Look at me. Breathe. You walked every path with that measuring wheel until your arms gave out. You knocked on every cabin door and asked permission. You sat with Ella for three days while she cross-checked names. You erased the stables four times because you wanted the horses to look happy. If a shed is twelve feet off, we will move the shed. Got it?"

Annabeth laughed shakily. "Got it."

"Good. Now, do you remember what you told Nico when he was scared people would laugh at his shadow corner?"

Annabeth frowned, trying to recall. "I said— I said if they laughed, they'd have to laugh at all of us, because we built it together."

"Exactly," Thalia said. "Same applies. This map is all of us. If there's a mistake, it's our mistake. But there isn't, because you're Annabeth Grace and you don't do mistakes on maps."

"That's not true. I do mistakes constantly—"

"Shh," Thalia said, hugging her hard. "Big sister privilege. I'm right."

Percy appeared at the railing, slightly out of breath from running. He held out his hand.

"Come on, Annie," he said. "It's four. Chiron's looking at his watch. You don't want Chiron to come get you. He'll do the whole centaur trot and it'll be embarrassing for everyone."

Annabeth took his hand. His palm was warm and slightly sticky with lemonade. Familiar.

"Will you stand with me?" she asked quietly. "Up there? I don't want to stand alone."

Percy's expression softened completely. "Always. Duh. Boyfriends stand. It's the job."

He led her around to the porch steps, Thalia falling in on her other side. The crowd quieted as the three of them climbed into view — tall Thalia crackling faintly with good-day static, sturdy Percy with lake water still drying in his hair, and Annabeth between them, small and blonde and ink-stained, clutching her note cards so hard they bent.

Chiron smiled at her. "Annabeth," he said, pitching his voice to carry. "Whenever you are ready."

Annabeth stepped forward. The blue cloth rippled in the breeze. The gold easel gleamed. The lawn smelled like cut grass and strawberries and hot pine. Hundreds of eyes looked up at her, kind and curious.

She had prepared a speech. Three cards, bullet points, scale ratios, methodology. She looked at the cards, then at Percy, who nodded encouragingly, then at Thalia, who mouthed, *Heart, not ruler.*

She put the cards in her pocket.

"I started this map because of the shrine," she said, and her voice shook only a little. "This spring, when we built the shrine for— for Big Three kids, I had to draw a site map. Just a little one. Trees and a circle. And I realized I loved knowing exactly where we were. Not just the hill, but the roots. The path. The way the light fell."

She gestured toward the covered frame.

"So I asked Chiron if I could draw all of it. Every cabin. Every field. Every path. And he said yes, if I did it right. If I asked permission. If I didn't guess."

She took a breath.

"So I knocked on doors. For three months. I asked who lived where and what mattered and what I could put down and what I should leave off. Some people told me right away. Some people needed time. Some people walked with me and showed me. Grover showed me which trees were awake. Ella checked every name. Nico checked the shadows. Clarisse checked the arena measurements with a spear shaft because she didn't trust my tape."

Laughter rippled. Clarisse grinned and flexed.

"This map isn't perfect," Annabeth said. "The woods move a little when no one's looking, and the lake is deeper than anyone will tell me, and the Stolls have at least four tunnels I know about that they wouldn't let me put even with consent."

"Five!" Travis shouted. "There's five!"

"See?" Annabeth said, grinning. "But it's honest. Everything on here is here because someone who loves camp said, yes, put that. That's mine. That's ours. You can share it."

She looked at Percy. He smiled at her, steady as a lighthouse.

"Percy says I find my way by drawing," she said softly. "He finds his way by swimming and by story. But we both— we both needed a way home when we were little. And camp was it. So this is— this is for camp. For everyone who ever needed a rock or a corner or a quiet step."

Her throat closed. Thalia squeezed her elbow. Percy nudged her foot with his.

"Okay," Annabeth whispered. "Okay. Unveil?"

Chiron nodded. Mr. D stood, surprising everyone, and took one corner of the cloth. Thalia took another. Percy took the third. Annabeth took the last.

"One," Percy said. "Two—"

"Three!" everyone on the lawn shouted.

They pulled.

The blue cloth lifted, silver stars flashing, and the map stood revealed in full afternoon sun.

For a moment no one spoke. The wind moved the garlands. A bee investigated the lemonade. One of the visiting hellhounds sneezed.

Then the crowd surged forward.

---

From the porch, Annabeth watched them find themselves, and forgot to breathe.

It began with running — not the capture-the-flag kind, but the soft, eager kind, sandals slapping on grass, campers crowding around the easel in a loose half-circle, pointing, laughing, exclaiming.

The map glowed. She had painted the green in layered washes, light to dark, so the fields looked alive. The lake shone with white gouache highlights. The cabins stood in warm browns and grays and whites, each with its tiny garden, each with its tiny sign. The woods rose in textured greens, paths dotted in brown. The shrine sat on its rise in pale stone color, three olive trees spreading, the rain bowl a bright blue dot in the center.

And the gold ink caught the sun everywhere — stars and dots shimmering.

Clarisse got there first, because Clarisse always got there first when she wanted something. She stood with her hands on her hips, Silena clinging to her arm, both of them scanning.

"There," Silena shrieked, pointing. "There! Ares training square! Oh, look, she drew their spear rack and the— Clarisse, look!"

Annabeth had drawn Cabin Five in careful detail — red walls, boar-head door knocker, weapons polished on the porch rail, the small herb bed Silena had insisted on planting for burns and bruises. And beside it, in gold: *Clarisse's drill square. Mornings before breakfast. She will train anyone who asks, but you have to show up on time. No excuses.*

Clarisse stared at that label. Her mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

"She put my square," Clarisse said hoarsely. "Silena, she put my—"

"And mine!" Silena said, bouncing. She pointed to the Aphrodite cabin porch, where Annabeth had drawn a white swing with two figures, and beside it: *Silena's visiting hours at the porch swing. Best for heart-to-hearts. She always has tea and will always listen. Knock twice.*

Silena's eyes filled instantly. "She put my swing," she whispered. "Clarisse, I'm on the map."

Clarisse cleared her throat loudly and blinked very fast. "Yeah. Me too." She looked up at Annabeth on the porch, and her expression was fierce and tender all at once. She thumped her chest with her fist, the Ares salute. "Good work, Grace!"

Annabeth's knees went weak with relief. Percy caught her elbow.

"Told you," he murmured.

Thalia pushed through next, Hunters crowding behind her, and stopped in front of the Zeus cabin. Annabeth had drawn Cabin One tall and gray with high windows, storm clouds carved over the door, the lightning rod on the side. On the roof, a small figure with spiky black hair sat with legs dangling. Label: *Thalia's roof spot. Best for thinking during storms. She will share if you bring hot chocolate. Do not touch the weathervane without asking. It bites.*

Thalia laughed so loud heads turned. "It does bite! It shocked Jason last week!" She traced the figure with her fingertip, gentle. Then her eyes moved to the pine at Half-Blood Hill, to the Hunters' camp drawn as silver tents in the woods edge, to the shrine with its wind chimes marked in tiny musical notes. Her smile softened.

"You put the chimes," she said up to Annabeth. "Sky music."

"I put the chimes," Annabeth confirmed. "Tuned to A minor, like Lee said."

Thalia climbed the porch steps two at a time and hugged Annabeth hard enough to lift her off her feet. Static snapped pleasantly. "My brilliant baby sister," Thalia said into her curls. "You put us all in."

Grover arrived with his hands clasped, afraid to touch. He studied the woods section, where Annabeth had drawn individual old trees with names — *Mallow. Juniper's cousin Birch. Old Chestnut who tells the best stories if you bring water.* — and the wild corner by the shrine marked with tiny flowers and the note: *Grover's wild corner. Leave it wild. Do not weed. Do not tidy. The wild knows what it's doing. Ask Grover for a tour. He will cry happy tears.*

"I'm crying happy tears right now," Grover announced, tears indeed running down his face into his beard. "Annabeth, you spelled Mallow right. You put the rosemary. You put the thyme path."

"I walked it with you four times," Annabeth said, coming down to stand beside him. "You corrected my thyme leaf shape twice."

"It was important," Grover said solemnly, then hugged her, smelling like clover and rain. "Thank you for leaving the wild in."

Ella came last, as Ella always did, sidling through the crowd with her wings tucked tight and her notebooks clutched to her chest. The harpy had been Annabeth's secret weapon — she remembered everything she ever read, and she had memorized every cabin roster and every path name and every historic plaque.

Annabeth knelt to Ella's eye level. "Ella? Want to check?"

Ella peered at the map through her feathered bangs, muttering. "Ella remembers. Cabin One, Zeus, three bunks, lightning rod southeast ten feet clear. Cabin Two, Hera, empty but clean, peacock feathers on windowsill. Cabin Three, Poseidon, fountain trickles, guest bunk by window. Cabin Four, Demeter, strawberry starts—"

She went through all twenty cabins, tapping each one, reciting details. The crowd fell silent, listening. Annabeth held her breath. This was the true test. If Ella found an error, it was real.

Ella stopped at the Big House library window, where Annabeth had drawn a tiny harpy silhouette curled in a sunbeam among books. Label: *Ella's sunbeam. Afternoons. Quiet please. She is memorizing. If you bring a new book, she will love you forever.*

Ella stared at that spot for a long moment. Her talons tightened on her notebooks.

"Ella is on the map," she whispered. "Ella has a sunbeam. Ella— Ella is part of camp."

"You are part of camp," Annabeth said firmly. "You checked every name. The map is right because of you. See? Here." She pointed to the bottom corner, where the legend box held tiny credits in her neatest hand: *Checked by Ella. Planted by Grover. Measured by Clarisse's spear. Tuned by Lee. Built by all.*

Ella made a soft, croaking sound and dropped her notebooks to hug Annabeth fiercely with thin, feathered arms. "Ella will remember this day. Ella will remember Annabeth finished the map and put Ella in the sunbeam and the sun was warm."

The crowd applauded. Silena cried openly. Even Mr. D blew his nose loudly and claimed it was pollen.

Nico slipped in quietly while everyone watched Ella. He went straight to the shrine close-up — Annabeth had added a small inset circle in the corner showing the shrine larger, with each wall labeled. He found the north wall, the shadow niche drawn in cool grays with tiny silver dots for moonstone stars, and the minuscule name carved in the corner: *Bianca.*

He touched it with one fingertip, very lightly, as if it might be warm.

"She's there," he said to Annabeth when she came to stand behind him. "You kept her there."

"I promised," Annabeth said simply. "Precision specialists get what they were promised."

Nico nodded, eyes bright, and leaned his shoulder against her arm the way he had years ago in the dust. "It's perfect. The stars are right. That's the constellation she liked. The— the little bear."

"Ursa Minor," Annabeth said. "Ella confirmed it."

"Thank you," Nico whispered. "For keeping her safe."

Annabeth put her arm around his thin shoulders and held on until he was ready to let go.

---

Percy waited until the first rush eased to do his own slow tour, and Annabeth walked with him, hand in hand the way they had walked since they were small and the camp paths seemed huge.

He found the thinking rock first, then the canoe dock with its third-board note, then the strawberry field row marked *Percy's row. He weeds crooked but with enthusiasm. Best berries at the far end. Ask Demeter kids first.*

"Hey!" Percy protested. "My weeding is not crooked."

"Your weeding is extremely crooked," Annabeth said. "Katie showed me photos."

Percy laughed and moved on, finding the Cabin Three fountain ledge — *Percy's ledge. Best for talking late at night. He will listen.* — and the lake-bottom spot marked in blue: *Deep spot. Only for Poseidon kids with buddies. Percy knows the way. Do not follow without permission.*

He stopped at the shrine inset. There, beside the rain bowl, Annabeth had drawn two small figures sitting on the sea wall — one with black hair, one with blonde curls — sharing a cookie. Label: *Founders' bench. Percy and Annabeth planned it here. If the bowl has water, dip your fingers in and remember you belong.*

Percy's hand tightened around hers.

"You remembered the cookie," he said thickly.

"You always bring cookies to important places," Annabeth said. "It's your thing. Salt water and sugar."

He looked at her then, really looked, the way he did when the world got too loud and he needed to find true north. "Annie, this is— I don't even know what to say. It's like you took the inside of my head where camp lives and put it on paper where I can see it."

Annabeth's eyes stung. "That's the nicest thing anyone's ever said about my drafting."

"I'm serious," Percy said. "When I first arrived, before I knew the paths, I got lost every day. I'd end up in the woods crying because everything looked the same. And you'd find me and you'd draw me little maps on napkins. Remember? With X marks and arrows? 'You are here, Seaweed Brain. Dining pavilion is this way.'"

Annabeth remembered. Napkin maps with ketchup stains. "You kept them in your pocket until they fell apart."

"I still get lost sometimes," Percy admitted quietly. "Not on paths. Inside. When quests go bad or I dream about— you know. The pit. The water helps. You help. But sometimes I need something to look at to remember where home is."

He tapped the map's center, where the green spread out like open arms.

"This is that," he said. "This is home I can fold up and carry."

Annabeth had been saving the dedication for last, for when the crowd thinned and it was just family. Now, with Percy looking at her like that, she knew it was time.

She led him around the easel to the back — no, to the front bottom corner, where the title cartouche curled in elaborate lettering she had practiced for weeks: *CAMP HALF-BLOOD. Surveyed and drawn by Annabeth Grace, Cabin One, with help from all. Summer.*

Below it, in smaller script, in blue ink the exact color of the blue cookies and the summer sky reflected in the lake:

*For Percy, who finds his way home.*

Percy read it twice. His lips moved the second time. Then he went very still.

"For me?" he whispered.

Annabeth nodded, suddenly terrified. "Because— because you taught me that maps aren't just measurements. They're— they're promises. That there's a way back. You always find your way back, even from— even from Tartarus, even from the sea, even when you're lost. And you always come find me, too. So I wanted— I wanted to make you something that finds its way back to you."

Percy stared at the dedication, then at Annabeth, then back at the words. His eyes filled, spilled over, unashamed. Sons of Poseidon cried like rain when they needed to.

"Annie," he said, voice breaking. He pulled her into a hug that lifted her sneakers off the grass, spinning her once the way he had on their remembered quests when he was showing off new strength. The crowd cheered. Thalia whooped. Clarisse shouted, "Careful with the architect!"

Percy set her down but did not let go, hands on her shoulders, forehead pressed to hers.

"You found your way home, too," he murmured, so only she could hear. "Every time. Even when the sky was too loud and the fever was too high. You drew yourself home. I'm so proud of you I could burst."

Annabeth clung to his shirt, crying now too, laughing through it. "You're going to get tears on the map and Ella will have to re-memorize the smudge."

"Let her," Percy said, kissing the top of her head the way he had kissed her in their old life. Loving, fierce, absolute. "It's waterproof. Like me."

Thalia joined the hug from the side, wrapping them both in long arms, chin on Annabeth's curls. "Group hug tax," she announced. "Pay up, Wise Girl."

Silena and Clarisse piled on, Grover trotting to squeeze in, Nico allowing himself to be pulled in, Ella fluttering above to drape her wings over the top. The crowd laughed and clapped and Lee Fletcher started strumming, and someone pressed lemonade into Annabeth's hand, and a visiting hellhound nosed her pocket for cookies.

Chiron watched from the porch, hands folded, eyes wet. Mr. D, sunglasses pushed up, took a photo with an old-fashioned camera.

"For Amphitrite," he said gruffly when Percy looked surprised. "She'll want the moment the dedication was read. Don't look so shocked, Jackson. Even gods recognize home when they see it."

The party lasted until sunset.

Campers brought blankets and sprawled on the lawn, taking turns standing guard by the map to point out details to newcomers. Younger kids played find-the-star, racing to locate gold marks. The Stolls tried to add a fake tunnel with pencil and Ella caught them in under four seconds. "Ella remembers. No tunnel there. Tunnel is fifteen feet east. Ella will not tell you where." The Hermes table groaned.

Katie from Demeter brought a flat of strawberries and showed Percy his row on the map versus real life, confirming the far-end berries were indeed best. Beckendorf showed a group of Hephaestus kids how Annabeth had drawn the forge vents with heat shimmer lines. Will Solace pointed out the infirmary window Annabeth had marked *Best breeze for fever. Ask Will before opening.* Juniper kissed Grover's cheek when she found her birch cousin labeled correctly.

As the light turned gold, Annabeth sat on the porch steps with a plate of food she barely touched, map towering behind her, watching her family find themselves over and over.

Clarisse sat beside her, plate balanced on knees, uncharacteristically quiet.

"You put Bianca's name," Clarisse said finally. "Small. Not showy."

Annabeth nodded. "Nico asked. It was his condition for helping."

"It was right," Clarisse said gruffly. "My patrol partner—" She stopped, swallowed. In this camp, everyone knew Clarisse had lost a friend to a dracaenae ambush on patrol, a boy who loved shell collecting as much as she did. "I put a shell for him in the sea wall at the shrine. Silena knows where. You— did you put anything for him on here?"

Annabeth's heart thudded. She turned to the map, to the shrine inset, to the sea wall mosaic drawn in blues and whites. There, almost hidden in the wave curl, she had drawn a tiny spiral shell in darker blue.

"There," she whispered, pointing. "Silena told me which one was his favorite. The little whelk. I put it in the wave. I didn't label it because Silena said you didn't want a label. Just— just him in the water."

Clarisse stared at the tiny shell. Her big shoulders shook once. She put her arm around Annabeth, heavy and warm.

"Thank you, little architect," she said hoarsely. "You draw people like you love them. Chiron was right."

She got up quickly and walked toward the lake before anyone could see her cry, Silena slipping after her to hold her hand. Annabeth watched them go, chest aching in the good way that meant something true had happened.

Grover flopped beside her next, flower crown crooked. "The satyrs voted," he announced. "Your wild corner is now an official pilgrimage site. We will bring young satyrs there to teach them not to tidy."

Annabeth laughed through tears. "Tell them not to eat the thyme all at once."

"Already in the bylaws," Grover said solemnly, then hugged her sideways. "You did good, Annabeth. Pan would have liked this map. It leaves room for the wild."

Ella settled on her other side, notebooks in lap, pencil in hand. "Ella found one mistake," she whispered anxiously. "Ella is sorry. Very small mistake. Cabin Six, Athena, window count is seven not six. Ella counted this morning. New window for ventilation. Ella is sorry."

Annabeth grabbed her pencil. "Show me. We'll fix it right now. Before the fixative. Ella, you're amazing. Thank you for catching it."

Ella glowed as Annabeth added the seventh window with three careful strokes, initialed the correction, and let Ella initial beside it. "E.E. and A.G.," Ella recited, delighted. "Correction logged. Map is now perfect. Ella remembers perfect."

Thalia dropped down behind them, legs sprawled, head resting on Annabeth's shoulder. "You alive, baby girl? You've been hugged by like forty people."

"Barely," Annabeth said, leaning back into her. "Is it okay? Really?"

Thalia turned her face to kiss her temple. "It's more than okay. It's the best thing anyone's made since the shrine. And that was you, too."

Percy arrived last, with two lemonades and a blue cookie with a bite taken out of it.

"Delivery," he said, handing her a lemonade and the unbitten half of the cookie. "Sorry, tax. I was weak."

Annabeth took the cookie and leaned her head against his shoulder. Thalia leaned from the other side. The three of them sat on the steps as the sun sank toward the woods, painting the map gold and rose, gold stars gleaming.

Below, campers lingered, talking quietly, pointing. Someone had started a soft song on the pipes. The lake glittered through the trees. The shrine hill stood green and quiet in the distance, olive leaves silver in evening light, wind chimes too far to hear but present on the map in tiny notes.

"Read me the secret spots one more time?" Percy asked drowsily. "My favorites?"

Annabeth smiled, too tired to tease, and recited from memory, the way she remembered Sally reciting stories in their old life.

"Percy's thinking rock. Sunrise. Ask first. Percy's row, far-end berries. Percy's ledge, late-night talks. Deep spot, buddies only. Founders' bench, dip your fingers and remember you belong."

"And the dedication?" Percy murmured.

Annabeth whispered it against his sleeve. "For Percy, who finds his way home."

Thalia's arm tightened around them both. "For all of us," she corrected gently. "Who find our way home. Together."

The sun dipped. Chiron rang the dinner bell softly, not to end the party but to move it, and campers began folding blankets and stacking plates, carrying garlands to compost, wheeling the map carefully toward the Big House rec room where it would hang in honor until a permanent shelter could be built — with a roof, Annabeth had already designed, with UV glass and a bench opposite for viewing.

Percy stood and pulled Annabeth to her feet. Thalia stood and stretched, static popping.

"Come on, Wise Girl," Percy said, holding out both hands. "Dinner. You need protein after cartography. I heard chicken and rice. And Mom's cookies for dessert if Silena didn't eat them all."

"She ate three," Silena called, overhearing. "I counted! One per person until Annabeth has three!"

Laughter rippled across the lawn. Annabeth took Percy's hands and let him pull her up, swaying with exhaustion. Thalia steadied her from behind.

They walked toward the dining pavilion together, three silhouettes against the sunset — tall dark sister, steady boyfriend, small blonde architect between them, ink on her fingers and flower petals in her curls.

Behind them, on its grand easel, the map glowed in evening light, every cabin and path and secret spot held safe in line and color, every name checked, every permission honored, every love made visible.

*For Percy, who finds his way home.*

And for Annabeth, who drew the way.

And for all of them, who belonged.

The wind lifted the corner of the unveiling cloth where it lay folded on the porch rail, silver stars flashing once, like a wink, like a promise.

Home.
