## Chapter 97: The Map

The morning Annabeth finished the map, it rained for exactly eleven minutes and then stopped as if someone had turned off a faucet.

Annabeth Grace knew because she counted. She sat cross-legged on the floor of Cabin One with the big sheet spread out around her like a frozen lake, a ruler in one hand and a magnifying glass in the other, listening to the rain tick against the high windows and watching the light change across the paper. Zeus's cabin smelled like ozone and clean wool and pencil shavings. Her gray blanket was folded on her bunk. Her work lamp burned warm yellow. The rain made a soft hush on the roof, and then, just as she inked the last tiny olive leaf on the rise by the shrine, the hush cut out. Silence. Then birds.

Thalia, sprawled on the top bunk with one arm hanging down, lifted her head.

"Was that it?" Thalia asked. "Done?"

Annabeth did not answer at first. She set down the pen very carefully, the way Beckendorf had taught her to set down a hot weld, and sat back on her heels and stared.

The map covered almost the entire floor. Five feet by six feet, heavy watercolor paper, edges taped flat with masking tape to keep it from curling. It had taken her months. It had started, in truth, back in the spring with the shrine project, when she had drawn that first little site map with the three olive trees and the yarn circle and the compass rose pointing north. Chiron had kept that seven-page proposal in his office and had shown it to her again in June with the corners soft from handling.

"You draw places like you love them," he had said. "Have you ever thought of drawing all of it?"

She had thought of it ever since.

Now it lay finished under the morning light. Camp Half-Blood in ink and watercolor and colored pencil, north at the top, the Sound glittering blue-gray at the right edge, Half-Blood Hill with the pine and the Golden Fleece at the left, the highway crawling along the bottom. Every cabin in its place around the green, each one drawn not as a rectangle with a number but as itself, porches and columns and paint colors and gardens. The dining pavilion with its twelve tables and its brazier. The Big House with its blue porch railing. The arena, the forges, the stables, the strawberry fields in neat rows, the lake, the canoe dock, the climbing wall with its lava seams, the amphitheater, the woods with paths winding in dotted lines, the shrine on its low rise between lake and woods with three olive trees spreading silver-green.

And around the edges, small gold marks for shared places: a gathering star, a blue wave by the water, a gray dot at a bench anyone could use. Her notes named hazards, public paths, and plants that could be gathered only with permission. The private routines and hiding places stayed out of the map.

There were hundreds of them.

Annabeth's hands were stained with ink to the second knuckle. Her neck ached. Her eyes burned. She felt like she might cry and also like she might laugh until she fell over.

"Annabeth?" Thalia said, swinging down. She landed barefoot and padded over in her pajamas, careful not to step on the paper. She crouched at the edge. "Hey. Talk to me, baby girl."

"It's done," Annabeth whispered. "It's actually done."

Thalia studied the map, eyes wide and bright. She understood buildings the way soldiers understand ground, and she understood Annabeth the way only an older sister could. She did not touch the wettest ink. She just leaned close, her black hair falling forward, her breathing going quiet.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, Annie. It's beautiful."

Annabeth swallowed hard. "You really think so? Not too crowded? Chiron said not to make it too crowded, and I tried to layer it, but there was so much everyone wanted in—"

"Stop architect-braining for one second," Thalia said. She bumped Annabeth's shoulder with her own. "Look at it with your heart, not your ruler. It's beautiful. It's home."

The door creaked open and Percy Jackson stuck his head in, damp hair plastered to his forehead from the rain, a paper bag in one hand and two apples in his pockets.

"Permission to enter the sacred drafting temple?" he asked. "I bring offerings."

"You're tracking water," Thalia said.

"I'm a son of Poseidon. I am water," Percy said, stepping in anyway and toeing off his sneakers by the door, a habit carried from the life they remembered with Sally. He crossed in socks and stopped at the edge of the map. "Whoa."

He went to his knees. The paper bag crinkled. He forgot it completely.

"Annie," he said. "Is this— is this the map? The big one?"

Annabeth nodded, suddenly shy. It was one thing to show Thalia, who had watched every draft curl at the edges. It was another to show Percy, who found his way by feeling and story more than by north arrows.

Percy set the bag aside and leaned forward, hands hovering over the surface without touching. His sea-green eyes moved fast, the way they did when he read the water before a swim.

"There's Cabin Three," he breathed. "You drew the fountain. And the shell trim. And— is that seaweed on the roof?"

"The gutters grow a little seaweed in summer," Annabeth said. "Grover said it was healthy. I checked. It is."

Percy laughed, bright and startled. "And the lake. You put the lake sparkles. And the canoe—"

He stopped. In the lower corner of the lake, near the reeds, Annabeth had drawn a small gray rock above the waterline. It had no figure and no label.

Percy stared at the mark for a long time. His throat worked.

"You drew the sunrise rock," he said quietly.

"I can take it out if you'd rather," Annabeth said. "I didn't label it."

Percy studied the shore mark. "Leave the stone. It belongs on the public shoreline. Just don't put my name or what I do there."

"I won't," she said.

Percy looked up at Annabeth, eyes shining. "Can I— can I look at the rest? Properly?"

Annabeth's chest loosened. "That's what it's for. But careful, the shrine corner is still wet. And don't step on the woods. The ink in the woods smudges if you breathe on it wrong."

Percy grinned and lay down on his stomach, chin in his hands, the same way he used to when she spread maps and puzzles between them in the life they remembered. "Tell me everything."

Thalia rose from the floor, careful to keep clear of the wet corner. "I promised Chiron the practice sketch before breakfast. I'll bring that one down, and you two can carry the finished sheet when it's dry." She took the smaller copy from the desk and left. The door clicked softly behind her.

Percy stayed beside the map. His finger followed the shoreline without touching it, then stopped at a pale gap where the woods met the northern rise.

"That's the part you erased," he said.

Annabeth looked at the blank. An early draft had carried a path through that stretch, drawn from memory before she had walked the ground during her survey. She had checked the terrain and never found a route that matched. The line belonged to a map she remembered from the life they had lost; it did not belong on this survey just because she could still picture it.

"I kept trying to fit one life over the other," she said. "The paper wouldn't tell me which was right."

"The paper only has to tell us where we can go today."

"I know." The words came out sharper than she meant them to. She set down the ruler and rubbed the dried ink from the side of her thumb. "It still felt like losing the line when I erased it."

Percy lowered his chin onto his hands. "You didn't erase what we remember. You just stopped the map from claiming it was here."

That was the distinction she had been trying to make by herself for weeks. A remembered path could be real in their memories and still be wrong as a present direction. She opened her small field notebook and wrote beside the old pencil sketch: *remembered line; not located in current survey*. No name, no route instruction, nothing another camper could mistake for a way through the woods.

Percy read the note upside down. "That sounds like you."

"It sounds like a footnote."

"A very Annabeth footnote."

She closed the notebook and tucked it beneath her ruler. The map on the floor showed what they had checked in this life. The smaller one held what she wanted to remember. She could carry both without asking one to prove the other false.

---

By noon, everyone in the pavilion knew the map was nearly ready, because Thalia had carried Annabeth's practice copy to breakfast.

Thalia unrolled Annabeth's practice sketch on the Zeus table. The real map stayed taped to the floor to dry. "She drew the whole camp," Thalia told the first curious camper, then stepped back so Annabeth could answer questions herself. Mr. D looked up from his Diet Coke. "No crying at the tables before noon." He came over to inspect the copy anyway.

Chiron arrived with his wheelchair wheels squeaking on the pavilion stones. He examined the practice copy through his glasses. The map from yesterday's beach exercise had already been rolled back into his office; this was Annabeth's separate, larger survey of camp.

"Is today the unveiling?" he asked Annabeth, who had followed Thalia in with ink still on her fingers and her bandana crooked.

"I— I guess," Annabeth said. "The final needs until this afternoon to dry. The gold ink takes forever. But if people want—"

"We want," said Clarisse, appearing over her shoulder with a tray. Clarisse La Rue, daughter of Poseidon, big and scarred and surprisingly gentle with paper, peered at the sketch. "Is that the arena? You drew the new dummy rack?"

"I drew everything," Annabeth said. "Or tried."

"Good," Clarisse said gruffly. "About time someone drew us right."

Silena, radiant in a yellow sundress with her charm bracelet jingling, looped her arm through Clarisse's and leaned in. "Is there a legend? Please tell me there's a beautiful legend with little symbols. I love legends."

"There's a legend," Annabeth said. "Gold stars mark shared gathering places. Blue waves mark water. The gray dots are places anyone can sit, not someone else's private spot. Names and personal routines go on the map only if the person named approves them."

Grover trotted up with his reed pipes in his pocket and clover in his curls, balancing his breakfast plate. He took one look and his ears — well, his horns hidden under his rasta cap — seemed to perk up. "Did you put the wild corner? By the shrine?"

"I marked where the protected ground begins," Annabeth said. "I left out the exact plant locations. Could you check that boundary before the map goes up?"

Grover studied the line and nodded. "Yes. That's better. People can ask me for a walk if the ground can take visitors. No one should follow a map into a patch that's trying to recover."

Lee Fletcher from Apollo whistled. "You got the chimes?"

"Got the chimes."

Beckendorf rumbled, "You got the forge vents?"

"I drew them, but I still need your wording for the warning."

Travis and Connor Stoll peered upside down from the Hermes table. "You got the hiding spots?"

"I left out routes the owners asked me to keep private," Annabeth said. "If you change your minds, tell me later. This copy has a date; it can be revised."

"Damn," Travis said admiringly. "Ethical cartography. Hot."

Even Nico di Angelo, sitting at the end of the Hades table with his Mythomagic cards in a neat stack, looked up with interest. "Did you put the shadow niche?" he asked quietly. "With the stars?"

Annabeth crossed the pavilion to him and crouched so their eyes were level. "I drew the shrine inset, but I haven't copied it onto the public sheet. I wanted to ask you first. Do you want Bianca's name shown there? I can leave out the path to the niche either way."

Nico set his Mythomagic cards down in a square and followed the shrine wall with one finger, careful not to touch the ink. "The stars can stay too. They were hers."

"And the path?" Annabeth asked. "People could read the drawing as an invitation, even if I don't write one."

"Leave it out. I can show someone if I choose to." He looked toward the small space where she had left room for the name. "Her name can stay. Small, like the carving. Don't make it a stop on a tour."

"I won't," Annabeth said. "I'll show you the final mark before it goes up, and I can remove it in a later copy if you ask."

Chiron clapped his hands once, and the pavilion quieted.

"Then we can hold an open viewing at four," he said. "Anyone who is free may come. Those on patrol or in the middle of their duties can see it another time. Annabeth, the map needs a stable frame and somewhere dry to hang. Would you like help arranging those?"

A cheer went up, loud enough to startle the pigeons off the pavilion roof. Percy stood on the bench — he could never not stand on benches when excited — and shouted, "MAP! MAP! MAP!" until Annabeth pulled him down by his shirt and hissed, "You are going to wrinkle it just by thinking about it," and everyone laughed.

Annabeth sat back down, heart hammering. She had presented buildings before. She had defended measurements to Chiron and argued mortar ratios with Beckendorf. But this was different. This was not a wall or a shrine. This was everything.

Percy slid onto the bench beside her and passed her a paper bag.

"I told Mom last week that the map was nearly done," he said. "She sent these and asked whether you'd want a picture sent when it's finished."

Inside were six blue chocolate-chip cookies under wax paper and a folded note from Amphitrite: *For the girl who makes room for everyone. With love, Amphitrite. P.S. Save Percy one or he will perish dramatically.* Percy explained that his mother had used the camp version of the recipe he had shared with her, adjusted for the Atlantis kitchen; it was not Sally's exact recipe.

Annabeth smiled at the note. "A picture of the whole map is fine. No close-ups of the notes."

"I'll tell her." Percy broke one cookie and offered Annabeth half, then ate the other half before she could ask for it.

Thalia took another cookie. "Eat, Wise Girl. You've got a long afternoon."

Annabeth ate while the campers traded questions about the scale and the symbols. The map would be ready to show; some details would remain private, and that was part of the work.

---

After breakfast, Chiron rolled the practice copy onto the Big House table. Annabeth brought her measuring wheel, a pencil, and the small stack of permission cards she had kept beside the map for months. The original sheet was still drying in Cabin One. It would not go outdoors until the ink had set.

"Let's start with the paths people use every day," Chiron said. He had positioned his wheelchair beside the table, where he could see the whole drawing without leaning over it. "A map can be beautiful and still send someone somewhere they cannot go."

Annabeth knew the trails from walking them. She had not tested all of them from his chair. She drew a thin blue line from the Big House to the pavilion, then looked at him. "Would you try this route with me?"

"I'd be glad to."

The line on the paper took them between the cabins, around a root that had lifted the path, and toward the lawn. Halfway there, Chiron's front wheel stopped against a shallow rut. Annabeth crouched and measured the gap. She could have added a small bridge or marked the rut as temporary. Instead, she asked whether he could pass safely with help.

"Not without someone lifting the chair," he said. "That means the route isn't accessible."

She crossed out the blue line. It left a pale scar through the watercolor. "I'll mark the longer path by the Big House ramp."

"Mark that it is the route we tested today," Chiron said. "A storm can change it. One person's chair isn't every person's chair."

Annabeth added a date beside the symbol and wrote *checked with Chiron; ask if conditions change*. The note made the map less clean. It also made the line true.

When they returned, she checked the woodland edge. The north trail from her previous sketch still had no inspection report. She marked it with a gray bar and wrote *closed pending brace inspection*. The dotted footpath stopped before the closed section.

"I don't know whether the brace crew has gone out," she said.

"Neither do I," Chiron answered. "Keep the closure. Don't turn an unknown into a route because the empty space bothers you."

Annabeth pressed the pencil harder than she meant to. Then she loosened her grip and wrote *status unknown beyond closure*. It was a note that admitted what she couldn't verify. She disliked the gap; she left it anyway.

The review table filled as breakfast ended. Beckendorf arrived from the forge with soot along one sleeve and a folded scrap of paper. He set it beside the map and tapped the vent symbol.

"You have the vent and the summer warning," he said. "The warning needs to say when to keep clear. The forge doesn't run by calendar."

Annabeth looked at her note. *Hot air in summer* was true, but it could sound as if the vent were harmless the rest of the year. "What should someone who hasn't been here know?"

"Keep clear while the forge is operating. They don't need to know what's happening inside. They need a way to find the public entrance and a reason not to stand beside the vent."

He walked the outside approach with her and the practice copy. The path turned away from the vent before it reached the door. Annabeth had drawn the entrance correctly, but a gold gathering star sat too close to the hot-air mark. Beckendorf pointed to the space between them. "That symbol says people can wait here. It would put them in the wrong place when the forge is running."

She moved the star to the open gravel beyond the path and crossed out the seasonal wording. The replacement read: *Forge entrance; keep clear of vent while forge is operating.* Beckendorf read it twice, then nodded. She dated the correction so the next copy would show when it had been checked. The building and the warning belonged on the public sheet; the forge's changing interior did not.

When they returned to the table, Clarisse checked the arena square against the spear-rack measurements she had taken. She allowed the map to show a weekly open practice, with a note to ask her before joining. She crossed out *no excuses* from Annabeth's draft. "People have other jobs. And if I'm not there, they shouldn't wait around with a sword." Annabeth corrected it.

Will came from the infirmary and stopped at the small note Annabeth had written beside its public entrance: *cool breeze by the east window*.

"What does this mark mean?" he asked.

"The window is a comfortable place to wait. I thought it might help someone who needs the infirmary." Annabeth had meant it as a kindness, a small human detail beside the route.

Will studied the spot on the page. "The entrance is useful. The window could make someone think they should wait outside it or look in. People need privacy when they're being treated. If someone needs help, the door is where we want them to go."

Annabeth crossed out the breeze note. "The entrance and the route stay?"

"Yes. The door is public. What happens inside can change, and it isn't for the map." He checked the symbol and signed the correction card. Annabeth copied only the entrance and the verified path onto the clean proof, leaving the window unmarked.

Silena looked over the porch swing label and asked her to remove *she will always listen*. "I like talking with people. I don't want a map to promise them my time before I know what day I'm having." She suggested *shared swing; ask first*. Annabeth wrote the shorter line, and Silena initialed the card.

Thalia came down from Cabin One and put a finger on the outline of its roof. "The roof stays in the building shape. Take my spot off. People already try the ladder when it rains." Annabeth erased the tiny figure and marked the ladder access as restricted. Thalia watched her finish. "You can put my name in the credits. Not a pin where I sit."

Ella checked the cabin names, one at a time, against the list she had brought. She caught one spelling mistake and asked to be credited for the roster check, but not for the patch of afternoon light by the library. "The sunbeam is quiet," she said. "It doesn't need an address." Annabeth thanked her and kept the sunbeam off the public drawing.

Grover and a Demeter counselor compared the meadow boundary to the plants they had observed. They moved the protected line farther from the rosemary and thyme. The exact patch stayed unmarked; visitors could ask Grover about a guided walk when the ground could support one.

Nico read the shrine inset with Annabeth beside him. He approved Bianca's name in small script, but not the trail or a star that would make the niche look like a destination. He checked the mark twice before signing his card. Annabeth slipped the card into the review stack rather than pinning it to the map.

By the time the group finished, the practice copy carried a few clean corrections and the table had a pile of signed approvals. Annabeth had expected the marks to make the map feel diminished. Instead, each person had set a boundary and chosen what to share. The blank spaces had become part of the record too.

Chiron asked where she wanted to hang the final sheet. The Big House rec room was dry, had an even wall, and could be reached by the ramp they had just tested. The outer door would need to stay open during the viewing so the frame could pass through without turning sideways.

"Temporary display for a week," Annabeth said. "Then I want to check the light and the paper before we decide whether it stays there."

"That is a sensible first edition," Chiron said. "We can make a schedule for later revisions."

Annabeth added a box at the lower edge of the practice copy: *Map dated this summer. Shared paths and checked routes shown; private places omitted by request. Corrections to the Big House desk. Ask before entering any closed or restricted area.* She left enough room beneath it for a future date.

"It's not complete," she said.

"The camp isn't complete," Chiron replied. "You can finish this version without pretending it will be the last one."

She studied the crossed-out route and the blank roof. For the first time, she thought she might be able to let the map go to the wall without solving every empty patch first.

---

Grover asked her to walk the protected boundary once more before the afternoon viewing. Annabeth took her measuring wheel but left the ink pens in the case. The soil near the shrine still held the morning rain. A small track curved from the public path toward the wild corner, narrow enough that she had drawn it as a footpath on the first draft.

"I thought this was one of the satyr paths," she said.

Grover crouched beside it. A few crushed leaves lay across the mud, and tiny green shoots had pushed up near the roots. "It isn't. Deer have been using it. If we mark it as a route, people will follow."

Annabeth looked from the track to the public path. The boundary she had drawn was only a pencil line on the practice copy. It ran too close to the young plants.

"How far back should it go?"

"Far enough that someone can stop before the soft ground." Grover did not choose a number for her. He walked a few steps along the public path and pointed to a stretch of firm earth. "Here. If they stand on this side, they can see the three trees without stepping through the new growth."

Annabeth measured the distance twice. The first boundary cut across the path; the second kept the public route intact and gave the wild corner more room. She drew the revision in pencil.

"Should the map name the trees?" she asked. "People will ask what they're looking at."

Grover shook his head. "A name can turn into a reason to come closer. If someone asks, I can explain why the line is there. The map doesn't have to answer everything."

She erased the three little tree labels from the proof. The paper roughened where the pencil had been. She pressed a clean scrap over the patch and traced the path again, leaving the interior blank.

"That's harder to read," she said.

"It's easier to leave alone," Grover replied.

Annabeth tested the new line from the public path. It marked a clear stopping point without pretending the ground beyond it was empty. She wrote *protected area; ask Grover before entering* beside the boundary, then asked him to check the wording.

He read it and tapped the word *ask*. "That gives people a choice without promising that I'll always be available. Sometimes the answer will be no."

"Then the no belongs in the map too," Annabeth said.

"The boundary does. The answer can change with the season."

She added the review date beside the line. As they walked back, she kept the measuring wheel folded. The blank space had not made the map less useful; it had stopped the drawing from sending someone where the ground could not bear them.

---

Before she returned the practice copy to Cabin One, Chiron asked if she wanted to test it with someone who had not helped draw it. The younger camper who had watched the beacon from the viewing line was finishing lunch by the pavilion. Chiron invited them over, then left the choice to them.

"Do you want to see if this makes sense?" Annabeth asked. "You can stop whenever you want."

The camper nodded and came to the table. Percy stayed at the far end, where he could watch without pointing. Annabeth turned the practice sheet so north lined up with its arrow, then stepped back.

"I need to get from here to the dining pavilion," the camper said. "Which line is the path?"

Annabeth let the child study it. They traced a dotted brown line that curved toward the north trail. The map had been drawn north-up, but the table faced east. Their finger paused over a little gray bar, then continued along the dotted marks.

"Is this the way?"

Chiron moved his chair beside the table. "That part is closed until the brace inspection is complete. The map should make that clear."

The camper pulled their hand back. "I thought the dots meant a path. The gray line looked like a fence."

Annabeth looked at the legend. It named the dotted trails but did not explain the bar. On the paper the closure note sat between trees and nearly disappeared. She had known what it meant because she had drawn it; a stranger did not.

"Thank you," she said. "I wouldn't have noticed that from my side of the table."

She crossed the gray bar out and drew a wide red X across the closed route. In the legend she added *closed path: do not enter*. She left no dots beyond the mark. When she turned the map back, the child traced the route from the Big House along the wider path and around the cabins to the pavilion.

"This one?"

"That's the path we tested with Chiron's chair," Annabeth said. "It takes longer, but it's open and level today. If the path changes, the date on the map will tell you to ask again."

The camper followed the blue line to the dining pavilion. "Then this blue mark is water?"

"Yes. The route is brown. The water is blue. The gray dots are public benches."

"And if I want somewhere nobody else is sitting?"

Annabeth glanced at the gray dots and thought of the marks she had left in her notebook. "The map can't promise a place will be empty. You can ask a counselor for somewhere quiet, or look for a bench yourself. I won't put another person's private place on here."

The camper studied the sheet again, then pointed to the public route. "I can use this one."

"You can," Chiron said. "If you want, I can walk the first part with you."

The camper shook their head. "I can find it. I just wanted to know which line was real."

Annabeth handed them the practice copy and let them lead. They rolled past the Big House, followed the wider path around the cabins, and stopped at the edge of the lawn. The way appeared longer outside than it had on paper. The child checked north, compared the pavilion roof with the small drawing, then chose the next turn without help.

At the last fork, they looked back. "The map got me here. The rest is easy."

"Then the map did its job," Annabeth said. She watched the child reach the pavilion before looking down at the paper. The red X was not attractive. She liked it better than the hidden gray bar.

She added the symbol to the clean copy in red ink and wrote the date beside it. The closed route stayed crossed off until someone checked it. The correction did not make the map less exact; it made the limit visible to someone who did not already know the answer.

Percy returned the practice sheet. "Good test."

"I thought the symbols were obvious," she said.

"They were obvious to the person who made them."

Annabeth tucked the page into the map case. She had another reason to keep the correction sheet with the final: a line could be true to the survey and still lead a person wrong if she forgot who would read it.

Percy glanced at the note beside the dock. "Did you check that board again?"

"I did. It still flexes. The draft says to step over it until it's repaired."

"Step over it how?" he asked. "If somebody is already standing on the dock, that's different from somebody coming in from the shore."

Annabeth looked at the wording. In her mind, the instruction was simple: keep your foot off the loose plank and cross by the solid section. The map did not show which way a reader would approach, though, or whether they would understand that the warning meant the board itself was unsafe.

"Let's check the mark from the path," she said.

They carried the practice copy to the shoreline. Percy stayed on the bank while Annabeth compared the symbol to the third board. Neither stepped onto it. From the public path, the note looked like a direction to cross the dock, not a warning to avoid the plank. Annabeth drew a short bar across the board symbol and added *third board flexes; do not step on it until repaired*. The route to the rest of the dock stayed in place, but no line crossed the damaged board.

Percy watched her add the correction. "That tells me what the problem is. I can decide where to go from there."

"And nobody has to guess which board I mean." She initialed the note and dated the check. They returned the practice copy to Chiron before the cloth was raised. The repair itself remained pending; the map would not suggest that a safe crossing existed until someone verified one.

Back in Cabin One, Annabeth compared the practice copy with the full-size sheet. She transferred the corrected red X, the dock warning, the forge wording, the protected-ground boundary and the infirmary's public entrance without the window note, then checked the signed cards against each change. The final map now showed what had been tested and what remained closed. The repairs themselves and every unverified path stayed on the correction list. She waited for each fresh mark to dry before covering the sheet.

---

At three-thirty the Big House lawn looked like a festival.

Silena had outdone herself. Long tables borrowed from the dining pavilion held pitchers of lemonade and iced tea and strawberry juice, plates of cheese and crackers and grapes, a bowl of ambrosia squares cut small for sharing, and, in the place of honor, a tray of Amphitrite's blue cookies with a handwritten sign: *ONE PER PERSON UNTIL ANNABETH HAS HAD THREE. — P.J.*

Grover and the Demeter kids had woven garlands of clover and late summer flowers and draped them between the porch posts. Juniper the dryad had contributed vines that curled politely around the easel legs without touching the paper. The satyrs had tuned their pipes. Even the visiting hellhounds wore flower collars and lay panting happily in the shade while Peleus guarded the border, thumping their tails when anyone passed.

Beckendorf and the Hephaestus kids had built a sturdy oak easel with braces and wheels, capable of holding the five-by-six sheet without wobbling. They had sanded it smooth and oiled it, then burned a compass rose into the top crossbar beside a trident and a lightning bolt.

"For the mapmaker and the camp," Beckendorf said when Annabeth traced the carvings. "It can be repaired if the weather gets to it."

The map waited under a deep blue cloth sewn with silver stars — the old solstice pageant curtain, washed and pressed. The easel stood on the level lawn, with clear space around it for Chiron's chair and for people to approach without crowding the frame. Mr. D lounged in a lawn chair with sunglasses on, pretending not to care and adjusting the cloth when the wind lifted a corner.

Beckendorf had added wheel locks, but the grass dipped toward the porch. Before anyone arrived, he asked Annabeth to try the frame once without the map on it. The left wheel held; the right one rolled half a turn. The easel moved enough to bring its corner near the clear lane.

"If a crowd leans on it, it could move," Beckendorf said. "I can pin the wheel, but then we can't roll it inside."

Annabeth crouched beside the frame. "Can we use a removable wedge? The map needs to be movable after the viewing."

He tested a short wooden stop under the wheel. It held when Chiron came through in his chair and turned toward the map. He backed out, then circled the frame once to make sure the lane did not narrow at the far side.

"I can get around it," he said. "And people can see the lower labels from here."

Annabeth shifted the sign table away from the ramp. The garland on one porch post had drooped across the path, so Thalia lifted it with her left hand while Percy moved the table. The arrangement looked less symmetrical. The route was clear.

"It works," Annabeth said. Beckendorf marked the wheel stop on the build note, and she added *remove before rolling* to her list. The frame could be steady for the unveiling and still travel afterward. She left the note on top, where the people carrying it could see.

Campers gathered in clumps on the grass. Ares kids with arms crossed, trying to look cool and failing. Aphrodite kids with cameras. Apollo kids with a song half-written already. Demeter kids with dirt still under their nails. Hermes kids taking bets on who would cry first. Hypnos kids half-asleep in the sun. Hecate kids whispering about preservation spells. Iris kids debating frame lighting. Even the Hunters, visiting for the week, stood in a neat silver line at the back with Thalia bouncing between them and her sister like a charged particle.

Percy asked Annabeth where she wanted him. When she pointed to a place beside the easel, he laid his jacket there and told anyone who asked, "Official boyfriend business. She said I can stand here."

Annabeth waited beside the Big House door with Thalia, looking toward the level space around the easel. Her stomach tightened as more people arrived.

"There are more people than I expected," she whispered. "What if the scale is off? What if someone follows the wrong line?"

Thalia touched her shoulders lightly and turned her around, careful with the tender palm Will had asked her to protect.

"Hey," Thalia said. "You checked the routes you could check. You asked people before putting their names on the page. If a line is wrong, we can correct it. You don't have to swear the camp will never change."

Annabeth laughed shakily. "Got it."

"I still hate the gaps," Annabeth admitted.

"I know," Thalia said. "You can hate them and leave them blank anyway."

Annabeth let out a short laugh. Thalia hugged her, using her left arm and keeping the right palm clear.

Percy reached them, slightly out of breath from running, and held out his hand.

"Come on, Annie," he said. "It's four. Chiron's looking at his watch. If he comes to get you, he'll do the centaur trot and then you'll have to give the speech while laughing."

Annabeth took his hand. His palm was warm and slightly sticky with lemonade. Familiar.

"Will you stand with me?" she asked quietly. "Up there? I don't want to stand alone."

Percy's expression softened completely. "Always. Duh. Boyfriends stand. It's the job."

He walked with her to the lawn beside the easel, Thalia on Annabeth's other side. They waited until the lane around the frame was clear. The crowd quieted; Percy stayed close enough for her to find his hand without moving her from the speaking place.

Chiron smiled at her. "Annabeth," he said, pitching his voice to carry. "Whenever you are ready."

Annabeth stepped forward. The blue cloth rippled in the breeze. The gold easel gleamed. The lawn smelled like cut grass and strawberries. People looked up at her, curious, but no one crowded the map.

She had prepared a speech. Three cards, bullet points, scale ratios, methodology. She looked at the cards, then at Percy, who nodded encouragingly, then at Thalia, who mouthed, *Heart, not ruler.*

She put the cards in her pocket.

"I started this map because of the shrine," she said, and her voice shook only a little. "This spring, when we built the shrine for— for Big Three kids, I had to draw a site map. Just a little one. Trees and a circle. And I realized I loved knowing exactly where we were. Not just the hill, but the roots. The path. The way the light fell."

She gestured toward the covered frame.

"So I asked Chiron if I could draw all of it. Every cabin. Every field. Every path. And he said yes, if I did it right. If I asked permission. If I didn't guess."

She took a breath.

"So I knocked on doors. For three months. I asked what I could draw and what should stay off the page. Some people told me right away. Some wanted to check the line first. Some asked me to leave a space blank. Grover helped me mark protected ground without showing a route through it. Ella checked the cabin names. Nico checked the shrine inset. Clarisse checked the arena measurements with a spear shaft because she didn't trust my tape."

Laughter rippled. Clarisse grinned and flexed.

"This map is a first edition," Annabeth said. "The woods change, the water level moves, and some routes are closed. We tested the marked level path with Chiron today, but conditions can change. If you find a line that's wrong, or want something removed, bring the correction to the Big House."

Travis opened his mouth, then shut it. Connor leaned over and whispered something to him. They both nodded, then pulled their hands away from the frame.

"Some places belong on a public map," Annabeth said. "Some belong to the people who use them. This sheet shows the shared routes and the boundaries we checked. A mark doesn't give anyone permission to enter a closed place or use someone else's corner."

She looked at Percy. He smiled at her, steady as a lighthouse.

"Percy says I find my way by drawing," she said. "He notices water and stories. Camp is big when you first arrive, and a map can help you find the pavilion, the infirmary, or a place to sit. That's what I hope this does."

Thalia stood beside her. Percy gave her a small nod.

"Okay," Annabeth whispered. "Okay. Unveil?"

Chiron nodded. Mr. D stood and took one corner of the cloth. Thalia took another with her left hand. Percy and Annabeth took the remaining corners.

"One," Percy said. "Two—"

"Three!" everyone on the lawn shouted.

They pulled.

The blue cloth lifted, silver stars flashing, and the map stood revealed in full afternoon sun.

For a moment no one spoke. The wind moved the garlands. A bee investigated the lemonade. One of the visiting hellhounds sneezed.

Then the crowd surged forward.

---

From the porch, Annabeth watched them find themselves, and forgot to breathe.

It began with running — not the capture-the-flag kind, but the soft, eager kind, sandals slapping on grass, campers crowding around the easel in a loose half-circle, pointing, laughing, exclaiming.

The map glowed. She had painted the green in layered washes, light to dark, so the fields looked alive. The lake shone with white gouache highlights. The cabins stood in warm browns and grays and whites, each with its tiny garden, each with its tiny sign. The woods rose in textured greens, paths dotted in brown. The shrine sat on its rise in pale stone color, three olive trees spreading, the rain bowl a bright blue dot in the center.

And the gold ink caught the sun everywhere — stars and dots shimmering.

Clarisse got there first, because Clarisse always got there first when she wanted something. She stood with her hands on her hips, Silena clinging to her arm, both of them scanning.

"There," Silena said, pointing to the arena. "The rack, the marked square, the storage shed. Clarisse, it all lines up."

Annabeth had drawn the arena in careful detail — practice squares, the dummy rack, and the equipment shed. Beside it, in gold, the approved note read: *Weekly open practice. Ask Clarisse or the counselor before joining.*

Clarisse stared at that label. Her mouth opened. Closed. Opened again.

Clarisse leaned closer. "That's the arena. You didn't put me in the wrong cabin. Good."

"And the swing," Silena said, pointing to the shared porch seat outside the cabins. The note beside it read: *Shared swing. Ask before borrowing the blanket.*

Silena smiled. "That's what I wanted. A place people can use without assuming I'm always available."

Clarisse nodded toward the arena. "Good work, Grace." She gave Annabeth a thumbs-up, then went back to the practice lane to answer a younger camper's question.

Annabeth's knees went weak with relief. Percy caught her elbow.

"Told you," he murmured.

Thalia came over with two Hunters and stopped in front of Cabin One. Annabeth had drawn it tall and gray, with high windows and the lightning rod clear of the path. The ladder access was marked restricted; Thalia's roof spot was not shown.

Thalia glanced at the roof and gave a satisfied nod. Her eyes moved to the pine at Half-Blood Hill, the Hunters' temporary tents at the woods edge, and the shrine's wind chimes marked in tiny musical notes.

"You put the chimes," she said up to Annabeth. "Sky music."

"I put the chimes," Annabeth confirmed. "Tuned to A minor, like Lee said."

Thalia hugged Annabeth with her left arm. "You left my roof alone," she said into her curls. "Thank you."

Grover arrived with his hands clasped and studied the woods section. Annabeth had drawn the public path and a protected-ground boundary; she had not named the trees or shown a route into the wild corner.

"That's the edge we agreed on," Grover said. "People can see where to stop. They can ask me about a walk when the ground can handle it."

"I walked the public path with you four times," Annabeth said. "You checked where the boundary should go."

Grover nodded. "That's the part I needed. The plants can keep their names to themselves."

Ella came over with her wings tucked tight and her notebooks clutched to her chest. She had checked the public cabin names against the roster and marked which details the map should leave out.

Annabeth knelt to Ella's eye level. "Ella? Want to check?"

Ella walked the outer edge of the drawing, matching the cabin names and signs rather than anyone's rooms or routine. She stopped at Cabin Six and pointed to a window Annabeth had missed. "One more here. New ventilation window. The exterior count is seven."

Annabeth checked the structure against the site sketch, then took out the correction sheet. "You're right. I'll add it to the next clean copy and note the change. I don't want to paint over the finished sheet while everyone is standing here."

Ella inspected the approval box at the bottom. The credits read: *Cabin names checked by Ella. Protected boundary checked with Grover. Arena measured with Clarisse. Route tested with Chiron. Easel built by the Hephaestus cabin.*

"Ella's name is there," she said.

"Only for the work you approved," Annabeth replied. "The quiet place stays yours."

Ella looked at the drawing once more, then signed the correction sheet beside Annabeth's initials. "That is accurate. Ella approves."

Nico slipped in quietly while everyone watched Ella. He went straight to the shrine close-up — Annabeth had added a small inset circle in the corner showing the shrine larger, with each wall labeled. He found the north wall, the shadow niche drawn in cool grays with tiny silver dots for moonstone stars, and the minuscule name carved in the corner: *Bianca.*

He touched it with one fingertip, very lightly, as if it might be warm.

"She's there," he said to Annabeth when she came to stand behind him. "You kept her there."

"I promised," Annabeth said simply. "Precision specialists get what they were promised."

Nico nodded, eyes bright, and leaned his shoulder against her arm the way he had years ago in the dust. "It's perfect. The stars are right. That's the constellation she liked. The— the little bear."

"Ursa Minor," Annabeth said. "Ella confirmed it."

"Thank you," Nico whispered. "For keeping her safe."

Annabeth put her arm around his thin shoulders and held on until he was ready to let go.

---

Percy waited until the first rush eased to walk the marked route with Annabeth, their hands linked. They followed the line Chiron had tested, staying clear of the frame and letting the next group come forward.

The small unlabelled rock by the reeds was the first place he recognized. Percy looked at the mark, then at Annabeth. She nodded; no one else knew it was his. At the dock, the third board carried the new bar and the note: *third board flexes; do not step on it until repaired*. They stayed on the shoreline path; the map showed no crossing there until the board was repaired. The strawberry rows had a shared path and a note to ask the Demeter cabin before picking.

"You didn't put a name on the rock," he said.

"You asked me not to."

He nodded once. "Good."

They passed Cabin Three's public fountain and the lake's blue depth boundary. The note said to stay inside the buoy line and use the water with a buddy; it did not point to a private entrance or name anyone as a guide.

At the shrine inset, he found the rain bowl and a public bench. There were no figures or instructions to touch the bowl; the note asked visitors to leave offerings and the water undisturbed.

Percy's hand tightened around hers.

Percy leaned toward her, keeping his voice low while the crowd moved to the other side of the easel. "In the life we remember, you made me those napkin maps when I kept getting turned around. I kept them until they fell apart."

Annabeth remembered the ketchup stains and the X marks. "I thought you threw them out."

"I did. Eventually." He looked at the map again. "This feels like a way to find the camp when I need it."

He looked from the map to her. "I know where the pavilion is now, even if I still get turned around by the cabins."

Annabeth's eyes stung. "That's the nicest thing anyone's ever said about my drafting."

"The map's good," Percy said. "It tells you where to go. It doesn't tell you what you're supposed to feel when you get there."

Annabeth looked back at the green and the paths. "That part changes too."

The dedication was the only sentence on the sheet that did not help someone find a building, read a hazard or understand a boundary. She had written it after the labels were done, then moved it between the title cartouche and the back of the page while the ink dried. A public map could carry a personal line, but she could not decide that for Percy. She had left the final placement open until he saw the words himself.

She had also wondered whether the line gave away more than she meant to tell. It did not name the other life or explain why home mattered to both of them. It was a gift he could accept, move or refuse, and she wanted that choice to be his.

Annabeth had been saving the dedication for last, for when the crowd thinned and it was just family. Now, with Percy looking at her like that, she knew it was time.

At the bottom of the front sheet, the title cartouche read: *CAMP HALF-BLOOD. Surveyed and drawn by Annabeth Grace, Cabin One, with help from all. Summer.*

Below it, in smaller script, in blue ink the exact color of the blue cookies and the summer sky reflected in the lake:

*For Percy, who finds his way home.*

Percy read it twice. Then he looked at Annabeth.

"For me?" he asked.

"If you're comfortable with it being on the public sheet," Annabeth said. "I can move it to the back."

Percy read the line again. "Leave it. I like that you kept it simple."

"You taught me that maps aren't just measurements," she said quietly. "They can help you find a way back when you need one."

He hugged her without lifting or turning her near the frame. The crowd applauded, and Thalia called, "Careful with the architect," which made Annabeth laugh into his shoulder.

When they stepped apart, Percy kept one hand in hers. "I'm proud of you," he said. "And I like the map."

"That's the best review I've had all day."

Thalia offered Annabeth her left arm. Annabeth leaned against her for a moment, then asked whether the others wanted to join them. Silena and Clarisse came over first; Grover, Nico and Ella followed at their own pace. They gathered beside the easel without touching it.

Chiron watched from the porch. Mr. D raised his old camera, then lowered it. "Wide shot only," he said. "The map and the people. No close-up of anyone's notes."

Annabeth glanced at Percy. He nodded. "That one can go to Mom."

The viewing lasted until sunset. Campers came and went between chores, stopping at the dated legend and following the public routes with a finger. The Stolls began to sketch a tunnel on a loose sheet. Ella pointed to the correction box. "Use that paper. The map stays clean until Annabeth reviews it." They grumbled but moved the pencil.

Katie from Demeter brought a flat of strawberries and checked the route to the rows against the ground. She suggested a second path around the wet furrow, and Annabeth wrote it on the correction sheet for a later check. Beckendorf pointed out the forge vents and approved the revised warning. Will checked the public entrance on the final map; the east-window note had been removed before the cloth was raised. Juniper checked the path beside the birch grove and agreed that the protected boundary should remain where Grover had placed it.

As the light turned gold, Annabeth sat on the lawn with a plate she was actually eating, the map on its wheeled easel a few yards away. People were reading it in small groups, then asking one another where they wanted to go. She did not have to stand beside it and explain every mark.

Clarisse came over from the arena, carrying the correction sheet. "The east practice square is still open on the next version," she said. "But I want the time listed as flexible. The schedule changes when patrol does."

"I'll leave the hour off," Annabeth said. "People can ask you or the counselor."

Clarisse checked the line, then handed the sheet back. "Good. I don't want them showing up at dawn because a map told them to."

Silena joined them with two cups of water. She had helped with the table but left the serving to the younger campers once they had a system. "I like the swing note now," she said. "It lets people use the place without putting me on call."

Annabeth folded the correction sheet once and tucked it into the map case. "The next copy should be better because people keep using this one."

"And because they can tell you what needs to change," Clarisse said.

Grover sat on the grass near them, keeping the flower crown out of the protected section. "The satyrs can meet at the public path and talk about why the ground needs to rest. We won't lead a group into the wild corner."

"Put that on the later copy if you want it there," Annabeth said. "Your words, your choice."

"I'll think about it," Grover said. He watched a younger camper stop at the gray boundary and turn back toward the lawn. "That part worked."

Ella brought the correction sheet back, now signed beside the missed window count. She had not asked Annabeth to repaint the map in front of everyone. "Seven windows in the next copy," she said. "Ella checked the count. The first edition has one missing."

Annabeth wrote the correction in the log and signed underneath. "Thank you. We'll print it when the sheet is dry and we've checked the other notes."

"The map can be wrong and still useful," Ella said, testing the words as she said them.

"It can be corrected," Annabeth replied. "That's part of being useful."

Thalia dropped down beside them and rested her head against Annabeth's shoulder. "You alive, baby girl? You've been hugged by like forty people."

"Barely," Annabeth said, leaning back into her. "Is it okay? Really?"

Thalia turned her face to kiss her temple. "It's more than okay. It's the best thing anyone's made since the shrine. And that was you, too."

Percy arrived last, with two lemonades and a blue cookie with a bite taken out of it.

"Delivery," he said, handing her a lemonade and the unbitten half of the cookie. "Sorry, tax. I was weak."

Annabeth took the cookie and leaned her head against his shoulder. Thalia leaned from the other side. The three of them sat on the lawn as the sun sank toward the woods, painting the map gold and rose.

Below, campers lingered, talking quietly, pointing. Someone had started a soft song on the pipes. The lake glittered through the trees. The shrine hill stood green and quiet in the distance, olive leaves silver in evening light, wind chimes too far to hear but present on the map in tiny notes.

Percy leaned toward her. "You left the personal notes in your notebook?"

"Yes. The public sheet has only the shared routes."

"Good. Keep them there."

She took his hand. "And the dedication stays on the map because you said so."

"It stays," he said.

The sun dipped. Chiron rang the dinner bell softly, not to end the viewing but to move it. Campers folded blankets, stacked plates, and carried the garlands to compost. Beckendorf unlocked the wheel stops, then Percy and Clarisse walked beside the easel as it rolled through the Big House's wide doors. The map stayed under its cloth until they reached the rec room.

Chiron had cleared the wall opposite the windows. The room's lamps made the gold notes shine, but a bright stripe from the west window crossed the top corner. Annabeth asked Beckendorf to move the frame a foot to the left. The glare disappeared; the frame then narrowed the chair's turn into the room.

"We can move the bench," Percy said.

"The bench isn't the only approach," Chiron said. "Leave enough room for someone to read from the side."

They tested the space with his chair. Annabeth shifted the frame again, then rolled the case of correction sheets to a small table where a visitor could reach it without leaning over the map. She set out the legend and wrote the date and review week on a separate card. The card explained that this was the first public edition and that corrections could be brought to the Big House desk. It stayed beside the page, not attached to it.

Chiron took the chair to the room's center and looked back. The low notes were legible, though the upper edge needed a second person to read aloud. Annabeth brought the legend card down to the table and rewrote the symbols in larger letters. Chiron checked it from his chair; the gold stars and blue waves were clear, and the red closure mark had its own line. The small notes at the top of the map still needed someone to read them aloud.

Annabeth took a clean correction sheet and copied the upper labels in larger, plain writing. She kept the list separate from the map so a changed route could be corrected without altering the inked page. Chiron read the first few lines from his chair, then asked her to move the sheet a little closer. When he could read the whole card without leaning, she set it beside the legend rather than attaching it to the frame.

She wrote that limit on the review card instead of calling the display accessible for everyone. The stool could help someone else reach the high labels, but it would not solve the problem for every reader. At the end of the week, she and Chiron would ask the people who used the room what helped and what still got in their way. The first edition could stay up while they learned how to make the next one easier to read.

"A week," she said. "Then we check the light and the paper before deciding whether to leave it here."

"Agreed," Chiron said. "The shelter sketch can wait for that review."

Beckendorf locked the wheels. Percy helped carry the folded cloth to the side table. Annabeth looked once more at the map: routes tested today, closure marked in red, private places omitted, corrections kept in their own folder. It was not finished forever. It was ready for this wall and this week.

Percy stood and pulled Annabeth to her feet. Thalia stood and stretched, static popping.

"Come on, Wise Girl," Percy said, holding out both hands. "Dinner. You need protein after cartography. I heard chicken and rice. And Mom's cookies for dessert if Silena didn't eat them all."

"Annabeth hasn't had her three yet," Silena called, overhearing. "One per person until she does!"

Laughter rippled across the lawn. Annabeth took Percy's hands and let him pull her up, swaying with exhaustion. Thalia steadied her from behind.

They walked toward the dining pavilion together, Thalia on one side of Annabeth and Percy on the other, still discussing which notes belonged on the next copy.

Behind them, the first edition stood on its wheeled easel, dated and open to review. It showed the routes they had checked, the boundaries they had agreed to, and the blank spaces they had chosen to keep.
