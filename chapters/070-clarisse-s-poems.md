## Chapter 70: Clarisse's Poems

The April afternoon at Camp Half-Blood smelled like cut grass and hot bronze and salt coming off the Sound, and the training arena rang with the usual clatter of swords being dropped by newer campers who still flinched every time Clarisse La Rue looked at them.

Percy Jackson sat on the bottom bleacher with his chin in his hand, watching his girlfriend argue with a practice dummy. Annabeth Grace stood in the middle of the ring with her Yankees cap pushed back on her head, her wooden sword tucked under her arm while she sketched something in the dirt with the toe of her sneaker — probably a new formation for capture the flag, probably something that would get Percy killed in a tactically interesting way.

“It’s not a democracy, Jackson,” Clarisse shouted across the arena at a terrified son of Demeter. “Your shield goes up or your teeth come out. Pick one.”

The son of Demeter picked shield.

Thalia Grace, sprawled upside down on the bleacher above Percy with her boots in the air and her black hair hanging down, snorted. “She’s in a good mood today.”

“She smiled once,” Percy said. “I saw it. Last July 14th. I marked it down.”

Annabeth glanced up and caught his eye and smiled at him in that private, quick way she had, just for him even in a crowd, and Percy felt his whole chest go warm despite the heat.

That was when the screaming started from the library side — not monster screaming, which had a particular pitch at Camp, but Ella screaming, which was harpy-excited screaming.

“Ella has found words! Ella has found words that sing! Red feathers and iron words! Oh, oh, the sea is a spear and also a mother!”

Everyone in the arena turned.

Ella the harpy came bursting around the corner of the tool shed at full run-waddle, her wings half-open, feathers puffed huge with delight, clutching something small and black and battered to her chest like it was a baby chick. Tyson lumbered behind her, trying to keep up, his one big eye wide with fond panic.

“Ella! Wait for Tyson! Is it shiny? Is it a shiny book?”

“It is a secret! A secret with a spine! ‘Property of C.L.R. STEAL THIS AND DIE’ — Ella loves a title with stakes!”

Percy went very still.

Across the arena, Clarisse La Rue went even stiller. Then white. Then red.

“Give me that,” Clarisse said, very quietly, which was somehow worse than shouting.

Ella skidded to a stop in the dust, blinking rapidly, her talons tightening on the little black notebook. “But Ella hasn’t finished the sea poem. ‘I polish my spear and the gulls—’”

“ELLA.” Clarisse vaulted the arena fence like it wasn’t there, spear still in her hand, face burning. “That’s mine. That’s private. Give it here right now or I swear on the Styx—”

Ella shrieked and ducked behind Tyson, who held up both huge hands.

“Whoa! No fighting friends! Ella didn’t know it was secret-secret! She thought it was library-share!” Tyson said. “Right, Ella? We say sorry?”

Ella’s crest flattened. Her voice went small. “Sorry is a feather that falls down. Ella is sorry. Ella didn’t mean to steal. It was shining under the bench. Ella loves shining words.”

She held the notebook out with both wings, trembling.

Clarisse snatched it so fast the pages fluttered. A loose slip of paper escaped and spiraled in the sea breeze toward the bleachers.

Annabeth caught it without thinking.

Clarisse saw. “Grace. Don’t you dare read that.”

Annabeth felt the paper turn against her fingers in the wind. She folded it without looking at the writing and held it out, face down, to Clarisse. The apology came a beat late, but it came.

“Clarisse,” she said carefully. “I caught it. I haven't read it.”

“Then give it back,” Clarisse said, the words rougher than they needed to be. “Please.”

Percy stood up. He could feel his little-brother instincts firing before his brain caught up. Thalia elbowed him anyway because she knew that look on his face. That was his *someone’s getting ganged up on* look, and this time it was his own sister in the middle.

“Hey,” Percy said, walking down into the ring. “Leave her alone. So what if she wrote it?”

“Nobody’s ganging up,” said Sherman Yang from the Ares bleachers, but he looked curious and a little protective in the way Ares kids always got around Clarisse. “Clarisse, you want us to—?”

“I want everyone to mind their own business!” Clarisse snapped, shoving the notebook into her armor strap. Her ears were scarlet. “It’s nothing. It’s stupid. Training’s over. Get out.”

“I wasn't going to read it,” Annabeth said quietly. “Ella said enough to make everyone curious, but you get to decide what happens to the rest. It's yours.”

“I said shut up, Grace!”

The arena had gone dead silent. Younger campers stared. Even the dryads at the edge of the woods had paused.

Percy reached Annabeth and stood beside her while Clarisse took back the folded slip. His sister looked like she wanted to sink straight through the earth into Tartarus and stay there. He could defend her without looking at the words she'd told them not to read.

He raised his voice just enough to carry. “For the record, if anyone makes fun of someone for writing poems at this camp, they’re going to have to go through me. And I once fought a god with a ballpoint pen, so think about it.”

Thalia sat up and swung her legs around, her electric-blue eyes sharp. “And me. I like poems. Jason sends me terrible ones from Camp Jupiter. Rhymes ‘eagle’ with ‘beagle.’”

A nervous laugh rippled.

Clarisse stared at Percy like he’d just spoken in Ancient Minoan. “Jackson? You’re defending me?”

“Yeah,” Percy said, shrugging. “You defended me when I first got here and everybody thought I stole the bolt. You put a spear to my throat, which where you come from is basically a hug. So yeah.”

Sherman snorted. “That’s true. That’s Ares for ‘welcome.’”

Clarisse’s jaw worked. She wouldn’t look at anyone.

Ella peeked out from behind Tyson, her feathers still flat with guilt. “Ella will pluck her own feathers if the fierce one is sad. Ella didn’t know poems were secret-soft. At the library there were no secret-soft books. Everything was for sharing.”

Tyson patted her head gently with a hand the size of a dinner plate. “Ella is good finder. Maybe Clarisse can share one? Just one? Then it is not stolen, it is given.”

Silena Beauregard stepped out of the shade by the arena gate, where she had been watching her sister train. Lacy, the soft-spoken Aphrodite girl who braided everyone’s hair and never said a mean thing even when Drew Tanaka ruled the cabin, came with her. Silena had heard Ella’s cry and recognized the look on Clarisse’s face. She stayed close, leaving her sister room to breathe.

Lacy glanced at Silena, then at the notebook tucked against Clarisse’s armor. The curiosity in her expression softened. This was a secret somebody had let out before its owner was ready, and she knew how much that could hurt.

Silena squeezed Lacy’s hand once, encouraging her, then folded her arms beside Percy. No one was going to turn her sister’s words into a joke while she was standing there.

Lacy said, “Clarisse? I — I heard Ella reading. It was beautiful. Would you — would you let someone read one? Properly? Not stolen. Like — out loud because you want us to hear?”

Clarisse looked horrified. “Absolutely not.”

---

Percy knew that look. It was the look of someone who had built their entire personality out of iron and was terrified someone had just found the soft hinge.

He remembered the first time Annabeth had shown him her notebook of architecture sketches — how her hands had shaken, how she’d said *don’t laugh* like it was a threat, how precious and furious she’d been. He remembered showing Annabeth his own clumsy drawings of the ocean he’d done in Montauk as a kid and feeling his face burn.

He stepped a little closer to Clarisse, lowering his voice so only she and Annabeth and Thalia could hear.

“Hey,” he said. “For what it’s worth? In the life I told you about, Sally kept every dumb thing I wrote. I wrote a poem about blue waffles in third grade. It rhymed waffles with awfuls. She framed it.”

Clarisse blinked. “Your point, Jackson?”

“My point is everybody writes dumb stuff they don’t want read until someone they trust says it’s not dumb. Annabeth thinks yours is good. Annabeth doesn’t lie about writing. She once told me my essay on the Sirens sounded like it was written by a hippogriff with a concussion.”

“I did,” Annabeth said solemnly. “It did.”

Thalia grinned. “Percy's essays are legendary. I once saw him revise an introduction five times and end up with the same sentence.”

“My sister is mean,” said a new voice, and Jason Grace himself jogged into the arena, fresh off a Chiron-approved visit with the Camp Jupiter delegation. His Roman escort had sent him ahead while they talked at the Big House. His golden hair was damp with sweat, a practice sword over his shoulder. He nudged Thalia’s back and grinned up at her. “Hey, sis. What’d I miss? Why is Clarisse holding a spear like she wants to stab poetry?”

“Jason!” Thalia lit up in the way she only did for her little brother, punching his shoulder. “Perfect timing. Tell these people your poetry is worse than Percy’s so Clarisse feels better.”

Jason put a hand over his heart. “Hey! My haikus have gotten much better. *Eagle over hills / Beagle under table waits / Rome will stand forever.* See? Beagle is a metaphor.”

“For what?” Annabeth asked.

“For a beagle,” Jason said.

Everyone laughed, even Clarisse, just a short, startled huff through her nose — then she looked mortified to have laughed.

Ella crept forward a step, still half-hidden behind Tyson. “Ella is still sorry. Ella will do a sorry-song? Ella will trade? Ella knows many prophecies and many pizza coupons.”

Clarisse looked down at the small red-headed harpy. Ella’s eyes were huge and wet. Tyson’s too.

Clarisse sighed, a long, ragged sound, like air leaving a tire. She crouched down so she was eye-level with Ella.

“Look. Bird-brain. You didn’t know. It’s okay. Just — don’t go yelling my stuff around, all right?”

Ella nodded furiously. “Ella promises! On feathers and on Tyson! Ella seals beaks!” She mimed zipping her beak. Then, hopefully: “But… Ella loved the sea-mother poem. The spear that wants to be an oar. Is the spear Clarisse? Ella thinks the spear is tired.”

Clarisse froze.

Percy saw Annabeth’s breath catch. That line — *the spear that wants to be an oar* — hit something deep in every demigod who had ever held a weapon and wished they could put it down and just row home.

“That’s a good line,” Annabeth said softly. “Clarisse, honestly. That’s a *good* line.”

Clarisse stood up abruptly and turned away, her hand white-knuckled on her notebook. “It’s just scribbles. When I can’t sleep after patrol. Our dad — Poseidon doesn’t exactly — whatever. Forget it.”

Sherman stood. “Clarisse. Come on. If it’s about not sleeping, you should’ve said—”

“You don’t get it!” Clarisse whirled. “In this training circle you get mocked for anything soft! You know that! Mark my words, the second I—” She stopped, breathing hard.

Silence.

It was Lacy who broke it. The Aphrodite girl stepped into the ring, her hands twisted in her camp shirt. “My friend — I mean, Silena — she writes things too. Love notes she never sends. Little descriptions of everyone’s eyes. She told me that Aphrodite kids aren’t the only ones allowed to make beautiful things. Warriors make beautiful things every time they protect someone. She said your shield wall in the west woods was the most beautiful thing she ever saw.”

Clarisse’s eyes filled. She blinked furiously up at the sky.

“Don’t,” she muttered. “Don’t do that.”

“I’m sorry,” Lacy whispered. “I just — Ella’s right. That poem felt tired in a way I feel tired. I think a lot of us would like to hear it. If you want.”

Jason looked at Thalia. Thalia looked at Percy. Percy looked at Annabeth.

Annabeth sat on the lowest arena step, leaving the open training ring to Clarisse.

“You said no,” she told her. “I heard you. I'm sorry I made you say it twice.”

It was a harder apology than Percy had expected, because Annabeth didn't qualify it with good intentions. Clarisse kept her eyes on the folded page in her fist.

“The memorial idea is an invitation,” Annabeth continued. “Not an assignment. We can talk about it some other day, or never.”

Thalia stepped away from the spectators and pointed her spear toward the equipment racks. “Training's finished. Everyone who isn't on cleanup, go get dinner or something. Jason, can you help Tyson take the empty water jugs back?”

“The burdens of having older sisters,” Jason said, but he did as she asked. Tyson followed, and Ella went with him, still looking miserable.

Sherman signaled the Ares campers toward the gate. A few protested, and he silenced them with a look. Lacy started to follow. Silena waited without reaching for her sister's arm.

Clarisse watched her audience disappear. “You don't have to evacuate the whole arena.”

“You told us to mind our business,” Percy said. “We can be surprisingly good at that when ordered.”

“Since when?”

“Today. New skill.”

For the first time, Clarisse seemed to have enough room to breathe. She tucked the loose page inside the black cover and pressed the notebook's split spine flat. “I didn't say I would never read anything,” she muttered. “I said I wasn't going to be made to.”

“There's a difference,” Silena said.

Clarisse glanced at her. Silena had made that same distinction around a campfire very recently, with the whole camp watching. She didn't refer to it aloud. She let her sister find her own words.

“I might want Lacy to read one,” Clarisse said after a long silence. “Maybe. I choose the page, I choose who listens, and nobody gets to copy it.”

Lacy stopped by the gate. “I can do that.”

“That's not a yes yet,” Clarisse warned.

“I know.”

Percy sat beside Annabeth on the step, with the arena mostly empty around them. Neither asked Clarisse to hurry. After a minute, Jason and Tyson returned with lemonade and cups, and even that ordinary interruption made it easier to sit together. Clarisse remained standing, notebook in hand, deciding.

“I hate all of you,” she said eventually, without much force.

“We know,” Percy replied. “And we'll still be here if you decide not to share.”

---

The lemonade cooler sat on the bleachers — watery, too sweet, full of ice that melted instantly in the heat. Tyson lifted paper cups out of it like holy offerings. Ella stayed near the gate with her wings tucked, and Jason passed the drinks around with the solemnity of a Roman priest.

Clarisse still hadn't opened the notebook. A few campers watched from beyond the gate, but nobody called her back into the center of the ring.

She turned it over and over in her hands. Percy could see the cover now: plain black composition book, the edges soft with handling, a piece of electrical tape holding the spine together. In silver marker, in angry block letters: PROPERTY OF C.L.R. STEAL THIS AND DIE. Underneath, in much smaller letters, almost rubbed away: *and if found please return to Cabin Three, Clarisse’s bunk, no questions.*

Thalia leaned forward, her elbows on her knees. “Hey. Clarisse. Can I tell you something as someone’s big sister?”

Clarisse eyed her warily. “What?”

“When Jason first came to Camp Jupiter, he used to sleepwalk and cry for me, and he told me later he was embarrassed because Romans aren’t supposed to miss their sisters. I told him missing someone doesn’t make your armor thinner. It makes you know what you’re protecting.” She shrugged, her spiked bracelet jingling. “Your poems sound like you know what you’re protecting.”

Jason, sitting behind her, went pink and shoved her. “Thalia! You promised you’d never tell that!”

“Sibling privilege,” Thalia said, not looking back. “I can tell it whenever I want, little brother. It’s in the contract.”

“What contract?”

“The one I just made up.”

Laughter again. Clarisse’s shoulders dropped a fraction.

Annabeth squeezed Percy’s hand. He knew what the gesture meant, because he was thinking it too: Thalia and Jason, Percy and Tyson — siblings who had been separated by gods and distance and found each other again. Annabeth had Jason now, a little brother she was learning to know. But she had told Percy she still missed Bobby and Matthew, the mortal boys in her original San Francisco childhood, who had thought she was just their weird older sister who went to boarding school and liked old buildings. Loving the brother here did not stop that other ache.

Percy leaned over and murmured, just for her, “You’re a good big sister too. I still remember Bobby telling me about that Lego Acropolis you built him. Our last Christmas before everything changed.”

Annabeth’s ears went pink. “He told you that?”

“On the phone at your dad’s place. You were in the kitchen. He wanted me to know his big sister could build anything.”

She smiled down at their joined hands.

Clarisse took a deep breath. “Fine. One. *One* poem,” Clarisse said. “The sea one. You can stay if you want, but nobody asks for another, nobody photographs the pages, and nobody takes the notebook out of Lacy's hands except me.”

Lacy nodded. Sherman waved the Ares campers who had been waiting at the gate back to the steps; the younger ones returned only after Clarisse gestured permission. Percy felt the distinction in his chest. An audience she'd chosen was not the same thing as a crowd refusing to leave.

“And if anybody laughs to make me feel small,” Clarisse added, “they leave.”

“Fair,” Percy said. He stayed beside Annabeth without promising to control everyone else's reaction.

Clarisse flipped the notebook open with fingers that weren’t quite steady. She stared at the page. Then she shoved the notebook at Lacy.

“You read it,” she muttered. “I can’t — your voice isn’t stupid.”

Lacy’s eyes went wide. “Me? Are you sure?”

“Just read it. The one with the dog-ear. The — the sea one Ella yelled about.”

Lacy took the notebook reverently, like it was Oracle material, and smoothed the page. She cleared her throat.

“‘Oar Wish,’ by Clarisse La Rue,” she read.

*I polish my spear till I see my face,*
*And my face is tired and wants the sea.*
*The gulls follow the trireme home*
*Whether we win or lose, they don’t care for glory,*
*Only that we come back to shore.*
*My spear wants to be an oar.*
*Let it pull, not pierce.*
*Let it bring my siblings home.*

Silence. Complete, ringing silence, except for the wind in the pines and the distant clang of the forge.

Percy felt Annabeth’s hand tighten on his. He felt his own throat close. He thought of the sea, always the sea, his father’s realm, Poseidon’s restless, cradling power — and he thought of Clarisse, who prayed to Poseidon with blood on her knuckles, wishing her weapon could be something that *brought people home.*

Tyson sniffed loudly. “That… that is good. That is very good. Tyson likes oars. Oars bring ships home.”

Ella, perched on the cooler, whispered reverently, “Ella will remember that. ‘Only that we come back to shore.’ Ella will keep it in her feather-heart forever.”

Sherman Yang cleared his throat roughly. “Yeah. Well. That’s… that’s our Clarisse.” His voice cracked. He pretended to cough. A couple of the younger Ares kids stared at Clarisse like they’d never seen her before — not just their fearsome training leader, but someone who lay awake and worried about bringing them home.

Clarisse stared at the ground, her face flaming. “It’s just — after the west woods, you know? After the hellhounds and everybody running. I kept thinking — what if my spear could’ve been something else. Something that saved instead of — whatever. It’s stupid.”

“It’s not stupid,” Thalia said fiercely. “Gods, Clarisse. That’s the least stupid thing I’ve heard all spring. You should’ve seen Jason’s face when I told him how you held the line. You *did* bring people home.”

Jason nodded. “Thalia told me. Your training group held. Romans respect that more than anything. *Fortuna* favors the ones who bring their people back.”

Clarisse swallowed hard and didn’t answer.

Lacy turned the page gently. “Oh — Clarisse, there are so many. ‘Harbor Lights’ — ‘Anchor Song’ — ‘For Chris, Who Learned to Swim’ —” She stopped, her hand flying to her mouth. “Oh! I’m sorry! Is that — is that Chris Rodriguez?”

Now Clarisse looked like she might actually pass out. “Give me that! That one’s private-private! That one’s not for—”

But she was too late. Every Aphrodite instinct in the arena had perked up.

Annabeth leaned forward, diplomat mode on. “Clarisse. You don’t have to share that one. But… for the record, Chris would probably love to know you wrote him a poem about learning to swim.”

Chris Rodriguez was working in the stables. Percy knew he and Clarisse were close; he didn't know how much of the black notebook Chris had already been trusted with. Sherman slid his phone behind his shield, typing much too quickly for Percy's liking.

“Sherman,” Clarisse said flatly, watching him shove his phone behind his back. “Whatever you're doing, stop.”

He swallowed. Percy wasn't sure whether the message had already been sent.

Percy laughed. He couldn’t help it. Annabeth laughed too, leaning her head on his shoulder for a second, and the tension broke like a wave.

“Read another sea one!” Ella begged. “Ella loves sea poems! Ella was born by the sea! The sea is loud like Ella!”

“Why do you write about the sea so much, anyway?” Thalia asked, genuinely curious. “No offense — you run drills like a siege. I’d expect poems about, like, explosions and punching.”

Clarisse hesitated. Then she muttered, staring at her boots, “Because it’s quiet. The sea. When I was little, before Camp, my mom used to take me to the Jersey Shore in winter when nobody was there. Just to walk. The waves — they don’t want anything from you. They just… go. And Jackson —” She glanced at Percy, almost angry. “Our dad’s whole thing. It’s — I dunno. It feels like the opposite of war. I like thinking about it.”

Percy felt something huge and tender swell in his chest. His fierce sister wrote about their father’s domain as peace.

“Our dad would like that,” he said quietly. “He would like that a lot. The sea doesn’t pick sides. It just holds everybody.”

Clarisse nodded jerkily.

Annabeth was watching Clarisse with her architect’s eyes — seeing structure under armor. “Clarisse,” she said slowly, “would you — this might be too much to ask today. But Thalia and I have been talking about redoing the memorial shrine. By the forest. For the campers who never made it home, the ones whose names Chiron keeps in the records. We want it to feel less like a tombstone and more like they’re still part of camp. We were going to carve names and leave offerings. But words would be better. Would you — would you let us use one? Or write one for them?”

Thalia nodded, suddenly serious, her punk edge softening. Her fingers went to the silver bracelet on her wrist. “I’ve read those letters. Sisters waiting for someone who never came back. Brothers keeping a bunk empty.” Her voice wobbled. Jason’s hand landed on her shoulder instantly, grounding. “I’d like something that sounds like sisters. Not heroes. Just sisters who miss each other.”

The arena went quiet again, but a different quiet. Holy.

Clarisse looked up, startled out of her embarrassment into something deeper. “You want one of *mine*? For the shrine?”

“For their families especially,” Lacy whispered. “Something they can keep with them. Silena thinks your words would help.”

Clarisse’s hands trembled on the notebook. She flipped past page after page — Percy could see cramped handwriting, crossed-out lines, little sketches of spears and waves in the margins. She stopped near the back.

“There’s this one,” she said roughly. “I wrote it after I visited the memorial last winter. I couldn’t sleep in our cabin, so I went down to the beach. It’s not finished. It’s rough. It’s about sisters. The ones in Chiron’s letters, the ones war took before we ever got here. I kept imagining somebody waiting for Silena or me.”

“Can we hear it?” Annabeth asked gently.

Clarisse looked at Percy. Percy nodded encouragingly. *You’ve got this.*

Clarisse took the notebook back from Lacy. She held it like a shield. And in a low, rough voice that shook only a little, she read:

*“For the Girls Who Don’t Come Back to Shore” —*

*We braided each other’s hair before battle,*
*Hands shaking, laughing too loud.*
*You stole my lip balm, I stole your courage,*
*Fair trade, sister.*
*The sea took you and didn’t give you back,*
*So I will be the sea for the ones left:*
*I will hold them up, I will bring them home,*
*I will remember your laugh in the mess hall,*
*Your hand in mine when the sky fell.*
*Sleep soft, sister.*
*Your watch is done. Mine isn’t.*

When she finished, no one spoke.

Ella was crying openly, little harpy sobs. “Sleep soft, sister! Ella loves that! Ella will lay feathers there!”

Tyson was crying too, big Cyclops tears splashing on the bleachers. “Tyson likes Beckendorf! He is forge friend! He braided Tyson’s hair once! It hurt! Tyson wants all friends home!”

Jason had his arm around Thalia, who was staring hard at the ground, jaw clenched, tears tracking silently down her face. Percy could not know which of the letters she was thinking of. His own memories supplied Bianca in the snow, Zoe dissolving into stars, Silena and Beckendorf lost in the war he and Annabeth remembered. Here, Silena stood beside him, alive, squeezing his shoulder. Annabeth met his eyes. They both knew how close a living friend could stand to an old grief.

Annabeth stood up and walked to Clarisse and did something no one at Camp had ever seen: she hugged Clarisse La Rue, hard, and Clarisse La Rue let her.

“Thank you,” Annabeth whispered, fierce and wet. “That’s perfect. That’s exactly — sisters. Can we carve that? By the shrine? Please?”

Clarisse stood stiff for a second, then awkwardly patted Annabeth’s back. “Yeah. Okay. Yeah, if — if you want. Just — fix the line about lip balm if it’s dumb. Silena has — she has opinions about lip balm.”

Lacy laughed through tears. “She does. She says it needs gloss.”

Everyone laughed wetly.

Percy stood too, and went to his brother. Tyson enveloped him in a hug that lifted him off his feet, as always, and Percy laughed and hugged back hard, thinking *this is my brother, my big soft brother who cries at poems and carries lemonade like treasure.* Over Tyson’s shoulder he caught Thalia’s eye, and she gave him a nod — sibling to sibling, the ones who understood what it meant to have a brother you’d drown gods for.

Jason pulled Thalia into a proper hug, stretching up while she bent to him and protested. “You Roman brick! I’m the older sister! I do the hugging!”

“You’re crying on me, sis,” Jason said gently, not letting her go. “It’s okay. Big sisters can cry.”

“I’m not crying. It’s pine pollen.”

“It’s definitely crying.”

“Shut up and get me more lemonade, little brother.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

That might have been the end of it, if Lacy hadn't stopped halfway through copying the memorial lines.

“Wait,” she said, pencil poised. “I should ask what you want people to do with this.”

Clarisse looked at her as if she'd asked whether a shield should be used as a hat.

“Read it, I guess.”

“Here, yes. But the memorial isn't here.” Lacy tapped the clean page. “If we put this up, people who weren't here today will see it. Visitors too. They might take pictures. They might ask who the sister was. They might decide it belongs to their own story.”

The question changed the expression on Clarisse's face. Percy could see her thinking about the notebook's battered cover, about Ella carrying it in the open, about all the little private things that had become public in less than an hour.

“I don't want them making up a dead sister,” she said. “Silena's alive. I'm not writing about her like she's gone.”

Silena moved closer. “You were writing about being scared I might not come back.”

“That isn't the same.”

“I know,” Silena said. “That's what I'm telling you.”

Thalia drew a line through the dust with the butt of her spear. “Then we make the difference obvious. A dedication for everybody who's lost someone, no names unless the family agrees. Nobody tells visitors the poem is about a specific person.”

Annabeth was already looking at the shrine plans differently. “And an inscription isn't permission for us to copy the whole notebook. We should ask Chiron which memorial names are meant to be public.”

“Names?” Clarisse said sharply.

“Not for the poem,” Annabeth replied. “For the shrine. I was going to carve the names from the camp records. I should have checked first. Some families may not want their loss posted where anybody can walk by.”

Percy watched her rub out a name she'd scratched into the dirt while explaining her first plan. Annabeth didn't look pleased about being wrong. She looked relieved to have discovered it before someone got hurt.

“We can ask,” she said. “And until somebody says yes, we don't include their name.”

A younger boy from Demeter, who had been sitting with his knees pulled to his chest, raised his hand.

Clarisse gave him a suspicious look. “This isn't school.”

“I know. Sorry.” He lowered his hand. “My cousin died last year. Chiron told us. I don't think my aunt would want her name on a wall.”

The group went still.

The boy kept staring at the lines in the dust. “I think she might like the words about coming home. But not if somebody said it was about her when it wasn't.”

Annabeth crouched at a careful distance from him. “Would you rather we didn't put anything there?”

“No.” He thought about it. “Maybe a place where you can leave a stone and not write who it's for.”

Percy could imagine that. A shelf, low enough for the youngest campers, with no demand to explain why someone had come.

“Would your cousin's name be on the stone?” he asked.

The boy shook his head. “I'd know.”

Clarisse looked toward the forest as if she could see the memorial from the arena. “Okay,” she said at last. “We need somewhere for that.”

The boy stood up and went to find his friends. Nobody made a show of thanking him for telling them something painful. Percy was grateful for that.

Chiron had arrived quietly enough that Percy didn't notice him until he spoke.

“You have discovered one of the more difficult parts of remembering people,” he said from the arena gate. “Their stories are not ours to arrange for our own comfort.”

“Were you listening?” Annabeth asked.

“Only to the part about asking me before publishing my records. I appreciated that part very much.”

Clarisse braced herself, the way she did when adults caught her doing something she hadn't decided whether to be proud of. “It's just a poem.”

“I believe I heard two poems,” Chiron said. “And some thought about their consequences.”

She looked down at her notebook. “Can we put a piece on the shrine?”

“You may propose it. The inscription itself needs your approval. The location and construction need mine. Names require separate consultation, and I will handle that conversation with families who wish to have it.”

“That's more permissions than building a warship,” Percy muttered.

“A warship usually has fewer people trying to protect someone's grief,” Chiron said.

Clarisse almost smiled.

Annabeth fetched her folder from beside the equipment racks. It contained a drawing of the existing memorial space, with dimensions in her small, neat writing. She had already marked a path, a bench, a raised planter. Everything looked possible on the page until Chiron put one finger over the line marked *new wall*.

“That wall crosses the route used by the infirmary cart,” he said.

“It isn't supposed to.”

“Nevertheless, it does. The path must remain clear even when a group gathers.”

Annabeth took the pencil from behind her ear and crossed out her proposed wall. “Then we use the existing fence. Or a separate board.”

“And make sure nothing catches a cloak or a wheel,” Clarisse added. She was looking at the drawing now instead of at the faces watching her. “It's stupid to build a memorial for people who didn't get home and turn it into a place where somebody gets hurt.”

“That's the most sensible thing anybody's said all afternoon,” Chiron replied.

“Put that on a plaque,” Percy said.

“Don't,” Clarisse told him.

They went to look at the actual space.

It was a short walk through the pines, with Chiron leading and Jason carrying the folder, because he wanted a job and Annabeth had made him promise not to crease the drawings. The memorial stood just beyond the nearest training trail: a modest arrangement of smooth stones, faded offerings, and a narrow wooden shelf. It was not as grand as Percy had imagined when Annabeth talked about it. That seemed right. Grief didn't always want a monument visible from the dining pavilion.

The ground beside the shelf dropped away slightly. One corner of the existing railing was loose. Someone had left a small blue bead in a crack between stones, and someone else had tucked a folded piece of paper beneath it.

Percy didn't touch either.

Clarisse did not touch them either. She stood with her arms folded, looking at the railing. He recognized the calculation in her eyes from practice: where to plant your feet, what would give way under weight, what kind of damage a careless move might cause.

“Not on the shelf,” she said.

“Why?” Jason asked.

“Because there's already stuff there that somebody chose to leave. I'm not moving it to make my poem look important.”

Silena glanced at her with an expression Percy couldn't read. She had been quiet since the arena. Now she stepped beside her sister.

“Could the words go above it?” she asked. “Not on the things people leave. Just where you can see them.”

“Small,” Clarisse said. “No huge title.”

“Small,” Annabeth agreed. She measured the clear section of the fence with a length of string from her pocket. It wasn't enough for the entire poem. She said so plainly instead of pretending the drawing could stretch.

Clarisse considered the pages in her hands. “The last lines,” she decided. “Those can stand alone. I don't want the other lines on public display.”

Lacy, who had followed them with the clean copy, read the selected lines back to her. This time she did so only after Clarisse nodded.

*Sleep soft, sister. Your watch is done. Mine isn't. I will be the sea for the ones left.*

A gust moved through the needles. Nobody applauded.

“That one,” Clarisse said.

Chiron looked at the railing, then the sky. “A removable olive-wood board would allow repair without disturbing what is already here. Beckendorf can check the fasteners. If the path stays clear and the existing offerings remain untouched, I approve a small board with those lines.”

Annabeth made a note: *No drilling into memorial stones. Check rail before hanging.*

“And the boy's stone?” Percy asked.

Clarisse pointed to a level patch beside the shelf. “A shallow tray. Not a pit where everything gets washed away.”

“The ground there floods during the spring storms,” Annabeth said, then frowned. “At least, it did on the route survey.”

“Then ask the repair crew,” Chiron told her.

“Beckendorf,” Percy supplied.

“Beckendorf,” Annabeth agreed.

They could have built an entire design around the idea before dinner. Instead, Chiron sent them back with four conditions to verify, and Clarisse kept the original poem folded inside the notebook against her chest.

On the return path Percy walked beside her, letting the others go ahead.

“You okay?” he asked.

“No.”

He waited.

“I'm not ashamed of writing it,” Clarisse said. “I think I'm ashamed of how I acted when Ella found it. She didn't know what it meant to me. I scared her.”

“She scared you first.”

“That doesn't make it even.”

Percy looked back at the empty path. “Then tell her. You already said she didn't know. You can say the other part too.”

“She'll quote me forever.”

“Probably,” he admitted. “I would.”

Clarisse punched his shoulder, not very hard. “You're a terrible little brother.”

“New information. Should we write it on the board?”

She snorted. After a few steps she said, “I want to tell her without everyone watching.”

“Then we can make that happen.”

It occurred to Percy that he'd spent the afternoon trying to protect Clarisse from an audience without asking whether an audience was the thing she wanted protection from. He'd made that mistake with other people before. Annabeth had just made a similar one with the shrine. Both of them could say the right words about consent and still get carried away by the feeling that they knew how a story ought to end.

He didn't tell Clarisse any of that. He walked beside her until the arena came back into view, where the last training shields were stacked neatly and the coolers were almost empty.

---

By sunset the arena had turned into something like a picnic. Someone ran to the mess hall and brought back trays of ambrosia squares and chips and cans of soda. The Ares kids built a small fire in the brazier even though it was eighty degrees, because Ares kids love fire. Ella perched on Tyson’s shoulder, reciting her favorite lines back to herself in a happy mutter: “‘My spear wants to be an oar! Let it pull, not pierce!’ Ella loves oar-spears!”

Chris Rodriguez arrived at a run, dusty from the stables, with his phone in his hand. Sherman had sent *CLARISSE EMERGENCY. POETRY.* Chris hadn't been able to tell whether the first two words meant an injury, and he was plainly angry about it.

“Clarisse? Are you okay? Sherman said poetry emergency—”

Clarisse went scarlet to the roots of her hair. “Sherman, you don't summon my boyfriend with a fake emergency. What if we'd actually needed help?”

Sherman looked down. “I shouldn't have. I'm sorry.”

“Then tell Chris that,” Clarisse said. “And next time you want to send something about me, ask me first.”

Chris checked her for injuries before putting the phone away. He looked relieved, but he didn't laugh with the crowd.

Chris looked around at the circle of campers, at the notebook in Clarisse’s hands, at Lacy’s tear-stained face. Understanding dawned slowly. “Wait. You — you shared them? The poems? The sea ones?”

“You knew?” Annabeth asked, surprised.

Chris sat down beside Clarisse in the dust like it was the most natural thing in the world, and bumped her knee with his. “Of course I knew. She reads them to me when I have nightmares. About the Labyrinth. Her voice —” He shrugged, suddenly shy. “It helps more than anything. She won’t let me tell anyone, though. Says it’ll ruin her reputation.”

“Your reputation is already ruined,” Sherman called cheerfully. “You cried during *The Little Mermaid*!”

“I CRIED BECAUSE TRITON IS A GOOD FATHER AND I HAVE FEELINGS,” Clarisse roared, and everyone howled.

Chris grinned at her, soft and proud. “See? Feelings. My fierce girl with feelings.” He kissed her temple, and Clarisse let him, grumbling, leaning into him just slightly.

Percy watched them and felt Annabeth’s hand slip into his again. He laced their fingers tight.

“Hey,” he murmured. “Wise Girl?”

“Mm?” She leaned her head against his shoulder, watching the fire. The sunset turned her blonde hair copper.

“You think — you think I could write one? A poem? For you?”

Annabeth turned to look at him, eyebrows raised. “Percy Jackson. Are you offering to write me poetry?”

“Bad poetry,” he clarified quickly. “Really bad. Like, Jason’s beagle level. But — I dunno. After today. I want to try.”

Thalia, eavesdropping from two feet away as big sisters do, cackled. “Oh, this I have to hear. Percy Jackson, poet. Annabeth, make him do it right now. Sibling override — as your actual sister, I demand entertainment.”

“I’m your actual brother’s best friend, does that give me immunity?” Percy asked.

“No,” Thalia and Jason said together.

Annabeth sat up, cross-legged, eyes bright with mischief and something tender underneath. “Okay, Seaweed Brain. Right here. Right now. One poem. I promise I’ll keep it forever no matter how bad it is.”

“No pressure,” Jason said.

“Shut up, little brother,” Thalia said. “Let the man work.”

Percy felt his face burn. He let go of Annabeth’s hand to pat his pockets for paper. Tyson immediately produced a crayon and a napkin from somewhere — Tyson always had art supplies. Ella donated a feather for inspiration.

Percy stared at the napkin. The fire crackled. Everyone watched.

He thought about Annabeth — really thought about her. Not just pretty (though gods, she was pretty in the firelight, gray eyes reflecting flames), but *her*: the girl who sat in the dirt until a frightened poet felt safe. The girl who had built Lego temples for her little brothers and designed Olympus in the life they remembered, who could grieve that world and still care for this one. The girl who held his hand like it was a promise.

He bent over the napkin, tongue sticking out in concentration, and wrote in messy block letters, crossing out twice.

Then he cleared his throat, stood up like an idiot, and read aloud, very fast:

*“Annabeth is smart, smarter than me,*
*She likes old buildings and the gray sea — wait, gray like eyes, I mean your eyes —*
*She hits me when I’m dumb but in a nice way,*
*And I’d sail through monsters every day*
*Just to sit next to her at dinner and steal her fries*
*Because she’s my Wise Girl and I love her wise eyes — uh, eyes again — THE END.”*

Dead silence for half a second.

Then the arena *erupted*.

Thalia fell backward off the bleacher laughing. “WISE EYES! EYES AGAIN! Oh gods, that’s the best worst thing I’ve ever heard!”

Jason applauded solemnly. “Beagle has met his match.”

Tyson cheered. “Yay! Percy did poetry! It rhymed me with — wait, it rhymed fries with eyes! That is good rhyming!”

Ella flapped in circles. “Steal her fries! Steal her fries! Love is french-fried! Ella will remember!”

Sherman wiped tears. “Framed. That needs to be framed. ‘She hits me when I’m dumb but in a nice way’ — that’s the truest love poem in history.”

Percy stood there, napkin trembling in his hand, ears burning red. “Okay, okay, I said it would be bad! You promised you’d keep it forever!” He looked anxiously at Annabeth.

Annabeth was laughing — laughing so hard she had to hold her sides — but her eyes were shining, and when she stood up she took the napkin from him with extreme care, like it was Oracle parchment, and folded it once and tucked it into the pocket of her jeans, right over her heart.

“Forever,” she said. “I swear on the Styx. This is the best terrible poem anyone has ever written me.”

“It’s the first poem he’s performed for this whole training group,” Thalia pointed out.

“It’s still the best.” Annabeth rose on her toes and kissed Percy, quick and warm and tasting like lemonade, her hands framing his burning face. “I love it, Seaweed Brain. I love that you tried for me. I’m keeping it in my notebook with my temple plans.”

Percy grinned so wide his face hurt. “Really?”

“Really. And for the record — you can steal my fries anytime.”

“Awwww,” the whole arena chorused, even Clarisse, who immediately looked disgusted with herself.

“Gods, get a room, Jackson,” Clarisse muttered, but there was no heat in it. She leaned against Chris, her notebook open in her lap now, no longer clutched like armor. Younger Ares kids crept closer to peer at the pages, and she didn’t snap at them. She even — Percy saw it — she even pointed out a line to one wide-eyed ten-year-old and said, gruffly, “That one’s about keeping your shield up. Poetry counts as training. Don’t tell anyone I said that.”

Lacy leaned against Clarisse’s other side, already copying “Sleep soft, sister” onto a clean sheet in her careful Aphrodite handwriting for the shrine. “We’ll carve it on olive wood,” she murmured. “With morning glories around it. Silena says she likes morning glories.”

“And stars,” Thalia added quietly. “Something the families can look up at from wherever they are. We’ll figure it out. Together.”

Jason squeezed Thalia’s hand. “I’ll help carve. Romans are good at carving. It’s like, ninety percent of what we do.”

Annabeth sat back down and pulled Percy down with her, tucking herself under his arm. He wrapped it around her automatically, and she settled against his chest with a sigh. Tyson leaned against Percy’s other side, huge and warm, Ella nestled in Tyson’s curls like a strange red bird-nest, humming poem-lines to herself.

The fire popped. The sky over the Sound went pink and gold and then violet, and the first stars pricked through — familiar stars, Percy thought, watching Thalia tilt her head back to look at them, Jason’s arm still around her shoulders.

Clarisse cleared her throat. She was looking at Percy.

“Jackson,” she said, awkwardly. “Thanks. For — earlier. For not letting them — you know.”

Percy nodded. “Anytime. Your poems are good, Clarisse. I meant it. The sea part — our dad would be honored a warrior like you thinks of his place as peace.”

Clarisse looked away quickly, but not before he saw her smile — a real one, small and unguarded.

“Don’t get sappy, fish-boy,” she said. “I’ll still kick your butt in sparring tomorrow.”

“Looking forward to it, spear-girl.”

“Wise Girl,” Annabeth murmured against his chest, her fingers tracing lazy patterns on his arm, “you kept my napkin?”

“I kept — wait, you kept *my* napkin?”

She patted her pocket. “Forever. Even when we’re old and embarrassing and Thalia’s still making fun of your rhymes.”

“I’ll never stop,” Thalia promised from across the fire. “‘Wise eyes’ will be in my wedding toast.”

“You’re never getting married, you’re married to patrol duty,” Jason said.

“I can still give toasts, little brother. I’ll toast your beagle.”

The camp laughed as darkness settled soft around them, the brazier glowing, the sea breathing somewhere beyond the pines. Ella had fallen asleep mid-mutter, her head tucked under her wing: “…come back to shore… come back to shore…” Tyson snored gently. Chris had his arm around Clarisse, and Clarisse had her head on his shoulder, notebook open, pen in hand — already scribbling something new in the margins by firelight.

Annabeth tilted her face up to Percy. “Hey, Seaweed Brain?”

“Yeah?”

“Write me another one sometime. Just as bad. I’ll keep that one too.”

Percy kissed her forehead, then her nose, then her mouth, slow and sweet, tasting smoke and lemonade and spring.

“Deal, Wise Girl. I’ll be your terrible poet forever.”

Above them, the stars burned steady — hunters and heroes and girls who didn’t come back to shore, all watching over a circle of siblings by blood and by choice, holding each other up, bringing each other home.

And in the morning, on olive wood by the forest shrine, in Clarisse’s fierce block letters traced over by Lacy’s careful hand, with morning glories and tiny painted stars and a beagle carved in the corner by Jason as a joke that made Thalia cry-laugh, the words would read:

*Sleep soft, sister.*
*Your watch is done. Mine isn’t.*
*I will be the sea for the ones left.*

But that night, there was only firelight, and lemonade, and bad poetry kept forever in a pocket over a wise girl’s heart.
