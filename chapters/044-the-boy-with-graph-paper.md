## Chapter 44: The Boy With Graph Paper

Percy knew the new kid was trouble the second he saw him with graph paper.

It was breakfast, which meant the dining pavilion smelled like sausage grease and syrup and wet wood from the morning rain. The sky over the Sound was still patched with gray, but light was coming through in clean shafts. Steam rose off plates. Harpies screeched over the rafters. Two hundred kids in orange shirts talked too loud, and Chiron was trotting between tables with a mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST CENTAUR, which Silena had given him for Winter Solstice as a joke and which he drank from every day after.

Percy sat at the Poseidon table with his sisters. Silena, fourteen, was braiding a piece of fishing line into her hair and telling Clarisse about a clasp that would not close right. Clarisse, thirteen, was eating bacon with the focus of someone defusing a bomb. Percy, twelve and the youngest, was trying to steal a piece of toast back when he saw the boy from the Hermes table get up with his tray and walk straight toward the Zeus table.

The boy was new. That was the first thing. Percy knew every face in camp the way he knew currents, and this one he only half-knew. He had arrived on the camp bus four days ago with a duffel bag bigger than he was and a black eye from a empousa on Route 9. Hermes kid. Twelve, like Percy. Name Theo. Theo something with an awkward laugh and hair that stuck up in back no matter how many times the Stolls tried to flatten it.

Theo stopped beside the Zeus table where Thalia and Annabeth Grace sat together, daughters of Zeus both, sisters by godly blood and by choice. Thalia had her boots up on the bench and was arguing with a piece of pancake. Annabeth had a book propped against the juice pitcher and was reading while she ate, hair pulled back in a loose braid, a pencil tucked behind her ear.

Theo said something Percy could not hear. Annabeth looked up. She smiled, that quick bright planner-smile she got when someone mentioned buildings, and nodded. Theo lit up like someone had handed him a torch. He held up a roll of graph paper like it was treasure.

Percy felt something small and hot turn over behind his ribs.

It was stupid. He knew it was stupid even while it happened. He was twelve. He had excellent reasons to know better. In his head, tucked behind the twelve-year-old mornings and the cabin jokes, he carried the weight of another life, another timeline where he had been seventeen. Seventeen, with salt in his jacket and a war behind him and Annabeth's hand in his on the beach at Montauk, laughing about something that had almost killed them. He remembered being seventeen and kissing her forehead on a flying horse and knowing exactly where he stood.

He remembered all that, and still his stomach tightened watching a Hermes kid show Annabeth graph paper.

Clarisse kicked him under the table. Hard.

"Ow," Percy said.

"You are staring," Clarisse said without looking up from her bacon. "It's creepy. Stop."

"I'm not staring."

"You are staring so hard your eggs are getting cold," Silena said. She reached over and fixed his collar, the way she always did, eyes flicking toward the Zeus table and back. "Oh. Oh, I see."

"I don't know what you're talking about," Percy said, and shoved eggs in his mouth to prove he was a normal person eating normal breakfast.

Clarisse finally looked up, followed his gaze, and her grin spread slow and terrible. "Oh no," she said. "Oh, this is perfect."

---

Theo had been at camp for four days and was still trying to understand which rules were written down and which ones everybody had apparently learned before he arrived.

The rule about not going near the border without a counselor was written on three different notices. The rule about avoiding Clarisse when she was carrying a practice spear was not written anywhere. The Stoll brothers told him that the daughter of Zeus who read at breakfast could answer any question about buildings, but neglected to mention that her older sister would inspect anyone who asked for help as though deciding whether to allow them through a gate.

Theo didn't mind the inspection. Thalia had asked what his project was, looked at the drawings and said, "At least you've made a plan that doesn't begin with stealing something." He took that as encouragement.

After breakfast he went back to the Hermes cabin for his references. He had packed the folded sheets in a waterproof envelope because the graph paper was nearly the last thing his mother had given him before he left Queens. She worked in an architecture office, mostly answering calls and keeping project files organized. She loved buildings in a way Theo hadn't understood when he was younger. She could stand in front of an ordinary apartment block and explain why the stairs were placed there, how the windows caught afternoon sun and what the builder had sacrificed to keep the roof from leaking.

Theo had watched her come home with her shoes wet from a construction visit and spread the drawings across their kitchen table. She couldn't design the whole building herself. She still knew more about its problems than half the people who spoke loudest in the meetings, and she'd shown him how a design could be improved by asking a simple question at the right time.

He wanted to make a little temple for Hermes because he was trying to belong somewhere. He also wanted to show his mother that something she had taught him could survive in this strange place where gods sometimes appeared at the dinner table and swords were stored beside the laundry.

The model he had drawn was too ambitious. He knew that. It had a portico, a working door and a sundial whose shadow was supposed to move across an inscription at noon. He hadn't worked out whether the architrave would hold or how to mount the door without splitting the wood. The Stolls had suggested covering the whole thing in glue and hoping for the best.

He had preferred to ask Annabeth.

When she saw his diagrams at breakfast, she didn't laugh. She took the roll and examined the first sheet, then asked whether he wanted advice about the proportions or the construction method. Theo had not realized those were different questions. He said both and watched her smile.

Now he stood outside the library with his papers, trying to decide whether he'd been too eager. He didn't want her to feel she was responsible for doing his project. He wanted someone who knew the subject to tell him where to start.

A smaller Hermes camper named Lila was sitting on the library steps, sorting a box of donated pencils. She looked up as he passed.

"You're the new kid with the temple," she said.

"That's me."

"Are you making it for a god or for a school assignment?"

"For an offering. Hermes is my father."

"Everybody in our cabin already knows that."

"I thought maybe he should know I can build something."

Lila considered. "He probably knows. But a model would be nice."

Theo smiled. She held out a pencil whose eraser had come loose, then changed her mind and gave him one in better condition.

"You'll want this," she said. "If you're asking Annabeth, she's going to make you redraw the whole thing."

"Probably."

"Good luck."

He thanked her and went inside. The library tables had been cleared for the morning study period. He chose one near the window, unrolled his diagrams and put the best pencil beside the ruler. He wanted the work to look as though he'd taken it seriously, but not as though he'd spent the morning pretending it was already finished.

Annabeth arrived with three books and asked to see the measurements. She pointed out that he'd drawn the front steps so steeply that nobody shorter than an adult would be able to use them comfortably. Theo had measured the proportions from a photograph and forgotten the scale.

"Then I need to change it," he said.

"Yes. And the door should open outward if this is going to stand on the hill. Otherwise the hinges will catch when the frame expands in damp weather."

He drew a new arrow beside the doorway. Her corrections were exact and practical. She didn't say the design was good merely because he'd brought it to her, which meant he could trust the praise she did give.

After twenty minutes he had a revised plan and a list of materials. Annabeth suggested asking Beckendorf about the hinge, because the forge had spare pieces too small for weapons but useful for models.

Theo looked toward the workbench. "Do you think he'd help?"

"Ask him. Tell him what you've tried and where you need advice."

He was relieved. The project still belonged to him. Annabeth had not taken the pencil and redrawn every line herself.

That was when Percy's cart struck the shelf hard enough to make the scroll cases rattle. Theo looked up and saw the son of Poseidon standing at the end of the table with a very determined expression that didn't match the friendly greeting that followed.

Theo knew Percy by reputation. The camp talked about his time in Atlantis, his family and the difficult water training exercises he was beginning to master. Theo had not yet been brave enough to ask him about any of it. He hoped they could work together. He didn't understand why Percy kept looking at the ruler as though it had personally insulted him.

When Annabeth invited Percy to join them, Theo was pleased. He would have another person to help carry the model when it was built, and perhaps somebody who could test whether the little water feature he'd considered adding was practical. He decided not to ask about it until he'd fixed the doorway. One problem at a time was already proving quite enough.



---

The library at ten smelled like lemon oil and old paper and rain drying out of the stone.

Percy was supposed to be shelving scrolls for Chiron as punishment for flooding the arena bathrooms last week, which had not been his fault except in the sense that water followed him when he got excited. He had a cart full of returns and a list in Ancient Greek that he could actually read now, which still felt like a magic trick. Sun came down through the high windows in tall bars. Dust floated in them. Outside, the gravel paths were steaming where the sun hit.

Annabeth was at the big table. Of course she was. That was her table the way the lake was Percy's lake. She had four books open, a ruler, a compass, a stack of blank papyrus, and her notebook with the blue cloth bookmark. Her pencil was behind her ear. Her braid had come a little loose and there were bits of hair curling around her face.

And Theo was sitting next to her. Close enough to see her notes.

Percy pushed his cart a little too hard into the shelf. It banged. Both of them looked up.

"Hey, Percy," Annabeth said, bright. "You remember Theo?"

"Hey," Theo said. He waved with a pencil. He had ink on his fingers and a smudge on his cheek. "Annabeth's helping me with my project."

"What project?" Percy asked. He meant it to sound casual. It sounded like a guard dog.

Theo did not notice. He unrolled the graph paper, and Percy saw it was covered in neat, eager, slightly wrong sketches of a temple. Columns too thin. Steps too steep. Measurements crossed out and rewritten three times.

"My mom — my mortal mom, she's an architect's assistant in Queens," Theo said fast, the way new kids talked when they were nervous and trying not to show it. "She always wanted to design something Greek. Like, really Greek. So I thought for my first offering, I'd build a model. A little temple to Hermes on the hill. With working doors and a sundial. Annabeth knows more about temples than anybody, Travis said, so I asked —"

"He asked nicely," Annabeth said. "And he brought references."

She sounded pleased. That was the worst part. Annabeth loved when people cared about buildings. It was like offering Percy a lake. Of course she said yes. Of course she pulled out her Parthenon book and her notebook full of column ratios.

Percy's chest did the hot tight thing again.

He told himself, firmly, in the voice of his seventeen-year-old self: you are being an idiot. You have held this girl's hand while the world ended. You have no reason to feel weird about graph paper.

It did not help.

"Cool," Percy said. "That's cool."

"Want to help?" Annabeth asked. She scooted over to make room, patting the bench. "You can be our structural integrity consultant. You know, you poke it and see if it falls over."

Theo laughed. Percy sat down because Annabeth asked and because his feet moved before his brain caught up. Up close the drawings smelled like fresh ink. Theo's ruler was chipped. Annabeth's compass left perfect circles.

For twenty minutes Percy tried to be helpful. He held the ruler. He listened while Annabeth explained entasis, how Greek columns bulged just slightly in the middle so they looked straight to human eyes. Theo took notes so fast his handwriting slanted off the lines. He asked good questions. He laughed when Annabeth made a dry joke about Doric being the plain oatmeal of orders. He kept saying "That's amazing" in a way that was completely sincere and not at all annoying except that it was.

Percy found himself counting how many times Theo said Annabeth's name.

Three times in twenty minutes. Which was normal. Which was fine. Which made Percy want to go swim to the bottom of the lake and sit there.

"You okay?" Annabeth asked him quietly, while Theo was erasing a column. "You're quiet."

"I'm good," Percy said. "Just thinking about offering stuff."

She studied him. Her gray eyes missed nothing. She had known him less than a year in this life, but she looked at him the way she had at seventeen, like she was measuring a wall to see if it was load-bearing.

"Okay," she said slowly, and smiled, and turned back to Theo's temple.

Percy stared at the Parthenon cutaway until the lines blurred.

---

Clarisse found him at the arena after lunch beating up a dummy with a sword.

It was raining again, light and sideways, drumming on the open roof beams. The sand was damp and packed. The air smelled like wet leather and oil. Percy had his sleeves pushed up and his hair stuck to his forehead. He was working through the form Thalia had taught him — cut, turn, block — but he was going too fast, sloppy, letting his shoulders do the work.

Clarisse stood with her arms crossed, spear over her shoulder, watching. Daughter of Poseidon, thirteen, his sister in every way that counted in this camp. She had known him since he arrived small and furious and seasick. She could read him like tide charts.

"You're doing it wrong," she said.

"I'm mad at it," Percy said, and hit the dummy again. Straw puffed out of the seam.

"You're mad at graph paper."

Percy stopped. "I am not."

"You are so." Clarisse walked into the ring, knocked his sword aside with her spear shaft, and shoved him lightly in the chest. "Spar me. No powers. You look like you need to lose at something."

"I don't want to spar."

"Too bad. Baby brother wants to brood, baby brother still gets trained." She grinned, sharp and fond. "Come on, Seaweed Brain. Show me your jealous face."

"I'm not jealous."

"Sure. And I'm Athena."

She came at him. Percy blocked out of instinct. They circled. The rain hissed faintly on the warm sand. Clarisse fought like the sea in a storm — sudden rushes, no wasted motion. Percy fought like water too, but younger water, still learning its banks.

She knocked his feet out in under a minute. He hit the sand with a thump that knocked the air out.

"Again," she said.

He got up. She knocked him down again. And again. By the fourth time he was laughing despite himself, breathless and sandy.

"Stop," he gasped. "Mercy."

"No mercy for lovesick idiots." Clarisse offered a hand up and hauled him to his feet. Her palm was calloused and warm. "Gods, Percy. Your face at breakfast. You looked like someone peed in your fish tank."

Percy wiped sand off his cheek. "I didn't — it's not — she's just helping him with a temple."

"Uh-huh."

"And I remember being seventeen," Percy said quietly, so the Ares kids drilling in the corner would not hear. It was strange to say out loud. Only a few people knew — the way he sometimes knew things before they happened, the way he looked at Annabeth like he had known her for years. Clarisse knew. Silena knew. Thalia had guessed. "I remember being older. I remember — all of it. So I should not feel like this over some kid with a ruler. It's embarrassing."

Clarisse was quiet for a moment, which was rare. She stuck her spear butt in the sand and leaned on it. Rain beaded on her shoulders.

"You think remembering makes you immune?" she asked. "That's dumb. Even for you."

"What?"

"You are twelve," she said bluntly. "Your brain is twelve. Your heart is twelve and also seventeen and also about five thousand years old because Dad is Dad. You can know it's stupid and still feel it. That's called being a person. Congratulations. Terrible prize."

Percy stared at her. "That almost sounded wise."

"Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation." She punched his shoulder, not too hard. "Look. You like her. She likes you. Everybody with eyes knows it. Some new kid asks her about columns and your little squid brain goes mine mine mine. It's normal. It's also hilarious."

"It's not funny."

"It is so funny," Clarisse said. "You — Percy Jackson, who fought — you know, whatever you fought — scared of Theo. Theo, who cried during orientation because the harpies scared him. Theo, who still calls Chiron Mr. Chiron sir."

"He didn't cry," Percy muttered.

"He totally cried. I was there." Clarisse's grin widened. "Do you want advice from your big sister, or do you want me to keep making fun of you?"

"Both sounds terrible."

"Advice first. Talk to her. Use words. The small ones you know. 'Hey Annabeth, I feel weird, please laugh at me gently.' She will. Then she'll explain entasis to you until you feel better."

Percy kicked at the sand. "And the making fun?"

"Oh, that's ongoing. Forever." Clarisse slung her arm around his neck and knuckled his head hard enough to hurt. "My baby brother's jealous. I'm telling Silena. She's going to die. We're going to call you Lord Jealousy of the Puddle."

"Clarisse—"

"LORD JEALOUSY!" she bellowed across the arena. Two Ares kids looked over and laughed. Percy turned red to his ears and tried to wrestle out of her headlock while she laughed so hard she almost dropped her spear.

---

By free hour the rain had stopped and the whole camp smelled like wet pine and cut grass.

Percy tried to avoid the library. He told himself he was giving them space to work. He told himself he was being mature, seventeen-year-old mature, totally secure. He went to the forge to see Silena, but Silena took one look at his face, set down her hammer, and said, "Oh no, Clarisse already told me," and started laughing so hard Beckendorf had to take the hot metal away from her for safety.

He went to the stables to see Grover, but Grover was in a gopher negotiation with the Demeter cabin and only had time to say, "Jealousy is natural among herd animals! Just don't pee on anything to mark territory!" which did not help.

He ended up at the canoe lake by himself, skipping stones. The lake was gray-green under the clearing sky, riffled by wind. Dragonflies stitched the air. At the far dock the naiads were braiding each other's hair and singing a song about a drowned prince that was probably about his dad. The air tasted like mud and water lilies.

He skipped seven times on one throw, his record, and nobody saw it. That felt fitting.

Footsteps on the dock behind him. Light, quick. He knew them.

"Hey," Annabeth said.

Percy turned. Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus, twelve, his — his Annabeth, even here, even now — stood with her hands in her jacket pockets, pencil still behind her ear, braid a little neater than this morning. Theo must have left. Or she had finished. Her cheeks were pink from the walk. Her gray eyes were bright with amusement she was trying very hard to hide.

"Hey," Percy said. "How's the temple?"

"Good. Theo's got the stylobate sorted. His columns are still too skinny, but he'll get there." She sat down on the dock and dangled her feet over the water. After a moment Percy sat beside her, leaving a careful six inches between them. The dock bobbed gently. Water lapped at the posts. "He says thanks. I told him temples are a group project."

"That's nice of you."

Annabeth bumped his shoulder with hers. "You're being weird."

"I'm not being weird."

"You are being so weird." She was smiling now, openly. "You've been weird since breakfast. Clarisse says you're jealous."

Percy closed his eyes. "I'm going to kill her."

"She said you'd say that. She said to tell you that death threats are the language of love in Cabin Three." Annabeth laughed, and the sound did something to Percy's chest that was worse than jealousy and better. "Are you? Jealous?"

"No," Percy said automatically. Then, because Clarisse's voice was in his head saying use words, idiot, he sighed. "Maybe. A little. Which is dumb."

"Why is it dumb?"

"Because —" Percy picked up a flat stone and turned it over in his hands. "Because I know better. Because I remember being seventeen and — and knowing you. Really knowing you. And you're twelve and he's twelve and he's just asking about architecture and you're just being nice. There's nothing to be jealous of. I know that. I still felt it. So it's dumb."

Annabeth was quiet. The wind lifted the loose hairs around her face. Out on the lake a fish jumped, silver flash, gone.

"It's not dumb," she said finally. "It's kind of sweet."

Percy looked at her. "Sweet?"

"Sweet in a stupid way." She grinned. "Percy, you have to understand how this looks from my side. You — you who once told me you'd fight a god for me, in another life — sitting in the library holding my ruler and trying not to glare at a kid who doesn't know the difference between Doric and Ionic. It's funny."

"Glad my pain amuses you."

"It does," she said cheerfully. "A lot. Thalia's going to think it's hilarious too."

Percy groaned and dropped his head into his hands. "Please don't tell Thalia."

"Too late. Clarisse already told her at lunch. She did an impression of your breakfast face. It involved a lot of eyebrow."

The six inches between them felt very large and very small at once. Percy could smell lemon oil from the library on her jacket and rain in her hair. He wanted to be seventeen again, confident, able to say exactly what he meant. Instead he was twelve with sandy shoes and a hot face.

"Sorry," he mumbled through his fingers. "I trust you. I do. I just — I like when it's us. When you explain stuff to me. I felt — I don't know. Left out."

Annabeth's amusement softened into something warmer. She reached over and pulled his hands away from his face so she could see him. Her fingers were ink-stained and cool.

"Percy Jackson," she said, very seriously, "you are my favorite person to explain things to. Theo asks about load-bearing walls because he has to for a grade. You ask about temples because you want to build a sinking boat with me. There's no competition."

Percy felt his ears go red. "Okay."

"Okay?" She tilted her head. "That's it? No tragic speech?"

"I'm working on it." He managed a small smile. "For the record, I don't want you to stop helping him. His mom — that's a good reason to build something."

"I know." Annabeth bumped him again, gentler this time. "And for the record, I like that you told me. Even if Clarisse had to translate first."

They sat there while the lake settled. The clouds thinned. Sun came through in patches, turning the water to hammered silver. Percy skipped another stone, five jumps, and Annabeth actually saw it this time and said "Nice" in a way that made it count twice.

---

Thalia found him before dinner on the path to the cabins, which was exactly as bad as Annabeth had promised.

Thalia Grace, daughter of Zeus, sixteen and tall and electric even standing still, with her black hair streaked and her silver bracelet clinking, fell into step beside him without asking. She had a spear over one shoulder and mud on her boots. She smelled like rain and ozone.

"So," Thalia said.

"So," Percy said warily.

They walked past the strawberry fields. Demeter kids waved. The sun was low, gold on the wet leaves. Somewhere up the hill a conch horn blew for cabin inspection.

"Annabeth told me you had feelings," Thalia said bluntly.

Percy tripped over a root. "She — that's private — Clarisse —"

"Relax. She said it was cute. I'm not here to tease you. Much." Thalia glanced sideways at him, and Percy was struck, not for the first time, by how much she and Annabeth looked alike around the eyes when they were trying not to smile. Sisters. In this life full sisters, both Zeus, both stubborn, both his problem and his luck. "I'm here for the big-sister talk."

"Oh no."

"Oh yes." Thalia stopped on the path and turned to face him. She suddenly looked awkward, which was strange on her. Thalia was never awkward sparring or leading or shouting at gods. She was awkward now, scuffing her boot in the gravel. "Look. This is weird for me. I'm sixteen. You're twelve. She's twelve. I feel like I should be confiscating your shoelaces or something. But — Annabeth's my little sister. My only sister. All her life. So I have to say it."

Percy braced himself.

Thalia pointed her spear at him, not threatening, just emphatic. "Hurt her and I kill you," she said. "That's rule one. I don't care if you're Poseidon's kid and I'm outnumbered by fish people. I will end you. Slowly. With lightning. Understood?"

"Understood," Percy said quickly.

"Good." Thalia lowered the spear. She was quiet for a beat, picking at her bracelet. The awkwardness came back tenfold. "Rule two — thank you."

Percy blinked. "What?"

"Thank you," Thalia repeated, louder, like it cost her. "For — you know. Liking her. The way you do. Respectful. Dumb, but respectful." She looked away, toward the Sound. "She talks about you all the time. 'Percy said this. Percy asked that. Percy wants to build a boat.' It's annoying. It's also — she hasn't had that before. Someone who listens when she talks about buildings like they're people. Someone who asks her to read out loud just to hear her voice."

Percy felt his throat get tight. "I do like hearing her voice."

"I know, pukey." Thalia punched his arm lightly. "Just — keep being like that. Keep telling her when you feel weird instead of going all broody hero. She likes you, idiot. She liked you when you were drooling on a library table over temple diagrams. Theo with his graph paper has nothing on you."

Percy laughed, surprised. "Clarisse said I looked like someone peed in my fish tank."

"Accurate." Thalia grinned, then sobered. "Seriously, Percy. You've told me things are different from what you remember, but I know what I see. You're good for her. You're good for all of us, actually. Cabin Three's weirdly wholesome for fish kids. Don't let that go to your head."

"I won't."

They started walking again. The cabins came into view, smoke starting from chimneys, lights flickering on. Thalia slung her arm around his shoulders for three steps, big-sister style, then shoved him away.

"And if you ever make her cry, I take it back and I kill you twice."

"Noted."

"Good." Thalia nodded sharply. "Talk over. Never speak of this again. If you tell anyone I said thank you, I'll deny it and electrocute your pillow."

---

Dinner was loud and normal and exactly what Percy needed.

He sat with Silena and Clarisse, shoulder to shoulder, Trojan War style against bacon theft. Tyson visited from the forges with a tray of mashed potatoes shaped like a volcano. Grover stopped by to report that the gophers had accepted the clover compromise and wanted to bless the negotiation with a song, which he then sang, off-key, while Katie Gardner clapped. Chris Rodriguez stole Clarisse's roll and Clarisse stole it back and then gave him half. The Stolls set a napkin on fire and Chiron pretended not to see.

At the Zeus table, Annabeth caught Percy's eye and smiled. Not the planner-smile. The other one, small and private. Theo was at the Hermes table showing Travis his temple sketch, and Travis was nodding like a good older brother, and everything was fine. Percy felt the last hot tight knot in his chest loosen and dissolve like salt in water.

After dinner, offerings. Percy scraped a piece of cornbread into the fire and whispered, *Thank you for today. Even the weird feelings.*

The fire flared warm gold.

Clarisse, beside him, whispered her own offering too quiet to hear, then elbowed him. "You good, Lord Jealousy?"

"I'm good," Percy said, and meant it. "Thanks. For the advice. And the beatdown."

"Anytime, baby brother." She ruffled his hair hard. "You know I'm going to tease you about this until we're old and gray."

"I know."

"Good. Love you."

"Love you too."

Silena, on his other side, leaned in and kissed the top of his head like he was still eight. "My sweet jealous seaweed. Come on. Walk me to the campfire."

---

The campfire was songs and smoke and sparks rising into a clearing sky. Apollo kids led with guitars. Percy sang off-key next to Tyson, who sang on-key and twice as loud. Annabeth sat three rows up with Thalia, and twice she turned and found Percy in the crowd and smiled, and both times Percy smiled back like an idiot and Clarisse dug her elbow into his ribs and whispered "Down, boy."

When Chiron dismissed them — *Go in peace. Sleep well* — cabins filed out by torchlight, laughing and shushing. The night was cool after rain, smelling like pine and wet earth and woodsmoke. Stars were coming out hard and bright.

Percy waited until Cabin Three was quiet.

Clarisse was out cold before her head hit the pillow, snoring softly. Silena mumbled about shell settings in her sleep, one arm thrown over her eyes. The guest hammock stayed folded in the corner. Percy lay in his bunk listening to his sisters breathe, feeling like a lighthouse keeper, then slipped out the window with a blanket over his arm, boots in his hand so the door would not creak.

Annabeth was already on the hill.

Half-Blood Hill, above the cabins, where the Golden Fleece glowed faint in the pine branches and the stars came out in full. Annabeth sat with her back against the rock, knees up, blanket around her shoulders, looking up. She had saved him a corner, like always.

"Hey," Percy said, out of breath from the climb.

"Hey." She lifted the blanket. He sat and tucked it around them both. Their shoulders touched. The grass was damp and cold under them. The blanket was warm.

Below, camp slept — dark cabins, the dying campfire's last ember, the lake a black mirror with the moon laid across it like a path. Crickets sang. An owl called once from the woods. The breeze was clean after rain.

They did not talk for a while. They had talked all day in pieces — at breakfast over bacon, in the library over columns, on the dock over skipping stones. Now there was nothing that needed saying right away.

Annabeth pointed. "Cassiopeia. See? The W."

"I see it."

"Theo asked me what my favorite building was," Annabeth said after a bit. "I told him the Parthenon. But that's not true anymore."

Percy turned to look at her. Starlight caught in her hair. "What's true?"

"I think it's whatever we build next." She glanced at him, shy and bold at once. "Even if it sinks."

Percy felt his face go warm. He remembered being seventeen on a beach, holding this same hand, larger and salt-crusted, and feeling like the world was ending and beginning. Now he was twelve with cold feet and a blanket that smelled like cedar, and it felt just as large.

"About today," he said. "I was jealous. Really. I know Theo's nice. I know you were just helping. I still didn't like sharing you."

Annabeth nodded, listening the way she did, full attention like a temple door opening.

"I think —" Percy struggled. Clarisse had said use small words. Small words were hard. "I think because I remember being older, I thought I wouldn't feel like this. Like I'd be above it. But I'm not. I'm twelve too. And I love you. That hasn't changed. I just didn't expect to get this flustered again."

Annabeth was quiet. Then she laughed softly, not mean, delighted. "Percy Jackson, did you just give me a disclaimer?"

"Thalia helped."

"Of course she did." Annabeth leaned her head against his shoulder. It was a small movement, but it rearranged the whole night. "I love you too. Even when you get flustered."

Percy let out a breath he had been holding since breakfast. The stars wheeled slow overhead. The Fleece glowed gold-green behind them. The wind moved through the pine.

"I don't want you to stop helping people," he said. "I like that about you. That you say yes when someone brings graph paper. I just want — can I still be your favorite person to explain things to?"

"You are," Annabeth said simply. "You ask the best questions. Theo asks how tall. You ask why it matters that it's tall. That's different."

Percy thought about that. He thought about entasis, columns bulging to look straight. Maybe feelings were like that too — a little curved so they looked true.

"Okay," he said. "Okay, good."

Annabeth laced her fingers through his. Hand to hand, palm warm, fingers ink-stained and sandy. They sat there while the moon climbed, while the lake shone, while camp breathed below them.

After a long quiet, Annabeth said, very softly, "For the record, you're cute when you're jealous. Don't be embarrassed."

"I am embarrassed."

"Good. Stay humble. Thalia says someone has to keep you humble."

Percy laughed. Annabeth squeezed his hand. They named constellations until they ran out — Cassiopeia, Zeus's eagle, the Terrible Boat That Sank Immediately — and then made up the rest. The Orange Peel. Theo's Temple. Lord Jealousy of the Puddle, which Annabeth invented and Percy protested and then allowed because she was laughing so hard she snorted.

The night deepened. The blanket slipped. Percy fixed it around both of them. Their shoulders stayed touching. Their hands stayed linked.

Before they went back to their cabins, Annabeth told Percy that Theo wanted help carrying the model into the library after breakfast. The frame was too broad for one person, and she had already promised to check the measurements. Percy was free to help or not.

"I'll come," he said. "I can carry the heavy end. Theo can explain what it's supposed to do."

"You don't have to prove anything."

"I know. I'm volunteering because I want to see whether his design actually works. And because I've spent all day acting strange to somebody who hasn't done anything to me."

Annabeth squeezed his hand. "He'll probably appreciate the help."

They stood, folded the blanket and checked they hadn't left anything on the grass. Percy still felt embarrassed when he thought about the questions he'd asked Theo in the library. That would probably happen again when they met in the morning. He couldn't control his first reaction to jealousy, but he could decide not to turn the next conversation into a test for someone else.

At the turn toward Cabin One, Annabeth leaned up and kissed his cheek. He watched her walk home, then took the folded blanket to Cabin Three. Tomorrow there would be a bridge model to move, a younger camper to treat fairly, and a few measurements he genuinely wanted to argue about.
