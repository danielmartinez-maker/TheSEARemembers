## Chapter 158: What Brothers Bring

Percy Jackson woke before the light had finished deciding what it wanted to be, and knew immediately that it was the day.

It wasn't the pool that told him. The pool at the end of Tempest Hall was still doing its small morning breathing, water moving faint over the mosaic. It was his hands that told him, already awake against the blanket the way they got before a sparring final. His whole body had the hum in it, low and bright.

Tyson was coming.

He sat up and put his feet on the cold tile and grinned at nothing. The cabin smelled of wet stone and lemon oil. Through the high window the sky was going from ink to ash to washed blue: mid-September, cool mornings that burned off by ten, afternoons still warm enough to swim.

He dressed fast. The faded orange camp shirt with the trident washed almost white across the shoulder, sleeves cut short back in June and fraying more every wash. The grey shorts with the pocket torn at the corner where he kept the smooth white pebble from the canoe lake and the hair tie Silena had pressed on him weeks ago. The canvas sneakers, soft as cloth now, laces knotted twice because the left one always came undone on docks. He ran a hand through his hair and gave up on it, the way he always did on days that mattered.

The two visitor bunks against the back wall were made up tight, towels folded on the hooks. Percy had done Tyson's bunk himself last night, hospital corners the way Beckendorf had shown him once in the armory, and stood there a minute with his hand on the footrail feeling foolish and glad. The round white rock still sat on the shelf by the door, the one Tyson had left on the pillow back in July, perfectly egg-shaped. Nobody had moved it. Clarisse dusted around it.

Tempest Hall was empty, which was wrong, because Clarisse did laps at dawn the way other people breathed. He heard them before he saw them: voices past the cabin, the clank of the satchel buckle, Silena laughing.

He pushed out the side door into air that smelled of pine and cold grass and lake somewhere downhill, and there they were.

Clarisse stood with her hands on her hips in the thin light, braid already damp over one shoulder, cutoff camp shirt gone ragged, old jeans white at the knees, cuffs turned up over twice-resoled boots. The canvas satchel hung across her chest, stuffed full, rope coil on top. She looked like a girl packed for a siege.

Silena sat on the cabin steps with a sewing kit open across her knees, stitching something small and blue, hair pinned up with the pencil that lived there, camp shirt tied at the waist over a thermal gone thin at the elbows, sandals kicked off in the dew. A paper sack from the pavilion sat by her foot, smelling of honey and apples.

"You slept," Clarisse said.

"I slept four hours," Percy said. "That's a full night by quest standards."

"You drooled. Fix your shirt, it's backwards."

"It is not backwards." Percy looked down. "You're lying for morale."

"I'm maintaining standards." But the corner of her mouth was negotiating. "You packed?"

"I packed last night. Blanket, towels, dry socks, the wax-paper cookie I was saving." He patted his pockets in order. "Pebble. Hair tie. Cookie wrapper that still smells like honey, for luck."

"That's not packing, that's hoarding." Clarisse shouldered the satchel straighter. "Tyson's hippocamp left the harbor at first light, per the Iris-message. Rainbow courier confirmed. He lands at the cove dock by nine, Friday morning. Chiron signed the visit slate. Bounded stay, four nights, sleeps here." She recited it like a roster, then added, quieter: "Towels are on the hooks. Bunk's made. We're ready."

Silena tied off her stitching and bit the thread and held up what she'd been working on. It was a small blue flag, hand-hemmed, a white trident stitched crookedly but with obvious care across the middle, the edges still fraying where she'd cut.

"For the canoe," she said. "The repair's done, but she looked bare. I thought — welcome-back colors. Tyson always notices flags."

Percy took it gently. "He'll love it. He notices everything nobody else does."

"He notices the important things," Silena corrected, folding the sewing kit. "There's a difference. Hand me my sandals? My feet are freezing and I'm pretending otherwise for pride."

Percy handed them over. Up the hill a conch sounded for breakfast. The light had gone gold at the edges, laying long shadows off the eaves.

"Nine o'clock," Percy said, counting on his fingers. "That's three hours."

"Two and a half," Clarisse said.

"Two hours and twenty minutes and I will not be rushed," Silena said, standing. "Come on. I negotiated extra bacon and I intend to collect before the Ares table stages a coup."

They walked up together through wet grass that soaked Percy's sneakers in four steps, Clarisse setting the pace, Silena swinging the paper sack, Percy with the little blue flag folded careful in his palm. Above them the sky kept paling, and downhill the freshwater lake lay flat and silver, waiting to be told.

---

Clarisse La Rue had a system for waiting, and the system was to refuse to do it.

Waiting was standing still. Standing still was thinking. So she did not wait. She prepared. She checked the satchel twice on the path to the pavilion and once more at the table. Rope, knife, tin cup, spare shirt, first-aid kit, two apples stolen fair and square from Silena's sack while Silena watched and allowed it. She ate breakfast standing up because sitting down felt like surrender, bacon in one hand and toast in the other, while Percy tried to eat and grin at the same time and failed at both.

"You've got honey on your chin," she told him.

"You've got a crease between your eyebrows that means you're happy," he said.

"I do not have a crease."

"You have a whole topographical feature. Annabeth could map it. She's official cartographer now, she'd do it to scale."

"Eat your eggs before I map your face to the table."

It was good, though. It was good in a way she would not have said out loud if you'd put a spear to her throat. Silena trading tea for extra butter with the harpy on duty, Percy with honey on his chin and the little blue flag tucked in his shirt pocket, Thalia sliding onto the bench across from them with her boots still muddy from the boundary survey and stealing Percy's toast without asking.

Thalia looked road-worn in the way she got after three days walking the far line, hair pulled back rough, black camp shirt with the sleeves pushed up and a borrowed army jacket with the cuffs turned back twice. Her boots left pine needles on the bench. She grinned at Clarisse with the particular insolence of an eighteen-year-old who had slept four hours in a lean-to and considered it a vacation.

"Morning, La Rue," Thalia said. "You look like you're about to invade something small."

"I'm escorting," Clarisse said. "There's a difference. One has paperwork."

"Tyson." Thalia's grin changed, went real around the edges. "Chiron told me. Four nights. That's good." She reached over and took Percy's toast, and he let her, which told Clarisse everything. "You okay, kelp head? You're vibrating."

"I'm fine. I've been great since five." Percy pressed his hands into his eyes. "Is it stupid that I'm nervous? He's my brother. It's Tyson."

"It's not stupid," Thalia said, dry but softer underneath. "Big brothers make you nervous when you love them. It's in the manual."

Silena slid back onto the bench with the tea and set a cup in front of Clarisse without being asked. "Thalia's right. And he'll be nervous too. You know how he gets in new places. Big hands, nowhere to put them."

"He'll have somewhere to put them," Clarisse said, before she could stop herself. "He's got us."

Nobody looked at her. Thalia kept eating Percy's toast, and Percy stared at his plate with his ears red, and Silena poured tea like a girl doing nothing at all.

After breakfast they walked down to the cove dock together, all five of them, because Annabeth met them at the top of the switchback with her map tube on her back and her hair in a braid gone fuzzy from the morning damp, wearing her orange camp shirt and a canvas overshirt with the pockets full, jeans with the hems damp and boots laced tight. She fell into step beside Percy without a word and took his hand, and he stopped vibrating quite so visibly, and Clarisse looked away because some things were private even from sisters.

The path went down through the pines, lake flashing between the trunks. By the boathouse clearing the air smelled warm, resin and varnish and rope. Outside the shed the rack stood with its row of overturned canoes, and at the end, upright on sawhorses, sat the repaired canoe.

Clarisse stopped. She always stopped. Two weeks of work: caulk, canvas, new ribs. Seams sealed dark and clean. The hidden panel with its thirty-nine tally marks was out — lifted from the sunken skiff, wrapped in sailcloth, in Chiron's office under lock. The architect still unnamed. The canoe itself was whole again, painted deep lake green — Tyson green, Annabeth had written in her book the day they'd picked the color — smelling of cured paint and oil.

Percy touched the gunwale with two fingers as they passed, and Clarisse pretended not to see because she did the same thing when nobody was looking.

The cove dock ran out into water gone glassy with the morning, freshwater green and cold and clean, nothing like the salt channels under Tempest Hall. The boundary rock sat mid-lake with its white X just visible. A wind moved the willows at the cove's edge where the half-sunk skiff still rested in the shade, and on its bow, grey and motionless, stood the heron, one leg folded, watching the dock like a posted witness.

"She's still here," Annabeth murmured. "Third week. I've got her on the survey now, fixed point."

"She's waiting for Tyson," Percy said. "She's got good taste."

"Everybody's waiting for Tyson," Thalia said, sitting on the dock's edge and letting her boots hang. "Even the bird."

They arranged themselves without discussion. Silena spread the blanket on the planks. Annabeth unrolled a corner of vellum and weighted it with two smooth stones, sketching because her hands were never idle. Percy stood at the very end of the dock with his hands in his pockets and then out of his pockets and then in again. Clarisse stood beside him with her arms crossed, boot tapping once, twice, against the boards.

"Stop," Thalia said, without looking up.

"I'm not doing anything."

"You're tapping. It's making me nervous and I'm not even the one with the brother on a seahorse."

"It's a hippocamp courier, not a seahorse, and I'm regulating."

"Regulate quieter."

Then Percy went still. Then Silena's head came up. Then Annabeth's pencil stopped mid-line.

Out past the boundary rock the water had changed. A long V-wake running fast toward the cove, and at its head a shape rising and falling, bronze-green and huge, and on its back, holding the harness with both hands, a boy with one large eye and a smile visible from a hundred yards.

Clarisse's breath left her all at once. She hadn't known she'd been holding it.

"Tyson," Percy said, very quietly, like a prayer, like a fact about gravity. And then louder, breaking open: "TYSON!"

The dock erupted. Percy waved both arms over his head. Silena was on her feet shouting. Annabeth was laughing, map forgotten. Thalia whistled fit to split the pines. And Clarisse, fifteen years old and owner of a reputation she had earned every tooth of, stood with her arms crossed and her boot still and her throat full, because somebody had to hold the dock steady.

The hippocamp came in the way hippocamps came in when the rider loved them, which was fast and then all at once gentle. A big bronze-green mare with a white star on her forehead, she slowed fifty yards out and glided the last of it with her head high, water sheeting off her flanks. Tyson sat her huge and careful, a canvas duffel strapped across his back and a long bundle wrapped in sailcloth balanced across his knees.

Percy was already at the end of the dock, half off it. "Easy! Come in right — come in —"

"I know how to land," Tyson called back, delighted. "I landed many times! Hello, brother!"

The hippocamp nosed the dock fenders with enormous delicacy. Tyson swung a leg over and stepped onto the planks, and the whole dock dipped two inches and came back, and then Percy hit him at full speed around the middle, and Tyson caught him one-armed and lifted him clear off the boards.

"Percy!" Tyson said into his shoulder. "You grew. You grew one whole finger." He set Percy down and held him at arm's length, studying him with his single large brown eye. "You cut your hair. It looks brave."

Percy laughed with his face wet. "The pool ate part of it. Tyson — you made it —"

"I took the early tide," Tyson said. "The hippocamp's name is Brine. She likes apples. I brought her two but I ate one because I was nervous." He looked past Percy, and his whole face changed. "Clarisse! Silena!"

Silena reached him first, arms wide, and Tyson folded her into a hug so careful it looked like he was holding something made of blown glass. "Little sister," he said. "You smell like lemons. I remembered correctly."

"You remembered perfectly," Silena said against his jacket, laughing and crying. "Look at you — your jacket's new —"

"My old one got small." Tyson plucked at the heavy canvas work coat, seams let out, pockets bulging with tools. "I made the pockets bigger. For surprises."

Clarisse was still standing where she'd been standing, arms crossed, boot planted. Tyson looked at her over Silena's head, and his smile went soft and certain.

"Big sister," he said.

Clarisse unfolded her arms. "You're blocking the dock, Cyclops. Some of us have schedules."

Tyson crossed the boards in three strides and picked her up bodily, satchel and all, and held her a foot off the planks while she swore into his shoulder. He set her down with care and kept one hand on top of her head for a second.

"You are strong," he said. "Still the strongest. I checked from the water. Your braid is longer."

"It's the same length." Clarisse swiped at her eyes and missed. "You're heavy and your coat smells like tar."

"Pine tar. For waterproofing." Tyson beamed. "I brought some. For the canoe."

Annabeth stepped forward then, map tube bumping her leg, eyes bright, and Tyson's face lit all over again. "Annabeth!" he said. "You are the planner! Percy writes about your maps in his letters. He says they are beautiful and also correct, which is harder."

Annabeth laughed, startled into pink. "Hi, Tyson. Welcome back. I marked the cove for your landing. I hope Brine found it all right."

"Brine says your dock is excellent." Tyson shook her hand with two of his fingers, gravely, like a treaty. "The water here is sweet. Not salty. Sweet water is strange. I like strange."

Thalia came up last, hands in her jacket pockets, grinning crooked. "Hey, big guy. Lightning girl. I saw you throw a boulder at a bull once. Best story I own."

Tyson nodded with dignity. "Thalia. Sister of Annabeth. You look tired. Did you walk a long way?"

"Three days on the boundary line." Thalia clapped him on the forearm. "Worth it to be here for this. Welcome to Camp Half-Blood, Tyson. Again. Temporarily. You know the drill."

"I know the drill," Tyson agreed happily.

Percy made a sound like he'd been hit gently in the chest. Silena reached over and squeezed his wrist without looking.

Brine the hippocamp bumped the dock with her nose, impatient, and Tyson turned at once. "Oh! Manners!" He fished in his coat pocket and produced the second apple, a little bruised from travel, and held it out flat-palmed while the great mare lipped it up. He stroked the white star on her forehead and said something low in the old tongue that made her ears prick, and she turned in a great rolling curve and was gone in a scatter of silver.

The dock was quiet for a beat. The heron in the willows stretched one wing and settled.

Tyson turned back, duffel over one shoulder, sailcloth bundle cradled like something asleep. "I brought things. One for everybody. And one for the canoe. Can I show the canoe first? Percy wrote that she was fixed. I want to see if the ribs sit true."

"I wrote that three days ago —" Percy said.

"I read it twice," Tyson said. "And then I packed the pine tar."

---

Tyson had learned early that new places were easier if your hands had something to do.

This was a thing he knew about himself the way he knew the weight of bronze or the smell of a forge going right. Big rooms and strange docks got smaller if there was wood to check or a seam to sight along. So when his brother and his sisters led him up the path from the cove in a noisy cluster, all talking at once, he let their voices wash over him like harbor water and fixed his eye on the canoe waiting on the sawhorses by the boathouse, green in the sun, and felt his shoulders come down an inch.

The canoe was good. He could see it from ten paces. The ribs sat fair. The canvas was tight. The seams were sealed dark where somebody careful had worked them. The smell — paint and oil and fresh caulk — was the smell of work done with love.

He set his duffel down in the pine needles and laid the sailcloth bundle beside it with great care and went to the canoe and ran his palm along the sheer line, sighting down it with one eye closed.

"Good," he said. "Very good. Who did the ribs?"

"Beckendorf's crew, mostly," Percy said. "And Clarisse did the stern three. The panel's out, by the way. With Chiron now. Logged."

"I know," Tyson said gravely. "Thirty-nine marks. You counted correctly. Thirty-nine is a careful number." He knelt and peered up into the hull, tapping each sister rib with a knuckle, listening. "The wood sings true. She will run straight." He looked up at Clarisse. "Your ribs are the straightest. I can tell. They have your temper."

Clarisse folded her arms, ears pink. "They're just ribs."

"They are good ribs." Tyson stood, dusting his knees, and untied the sailcloth bundle with thick, deft fingers. "I made something. For her. Because she was hurt and now she is healed, and healed things need a marker. Like a scar that is also a jewel."

Inside the sailcloth lay a carved prow piece, a hippocampus head for the bow stem, shaped from dark olive wood gone silky with handling, mane curling back, eye inlaid with polished shell. The cuts were bold and simple, but it had the particular life Tyson's carvings always had, as though the animal were about to move. Along the base ran a band of small notches, neat and even.

Percy went very quiet. "Tyson. Did you — is that —"

"It is for the bow. To watch the way forward. The notches are for luck. Twelve. One for each month you have been my brother. I counted." He offered it to Clarisse with both hands, because she was the eldest and the ribs were hers. "May I put it on? Ten minutes. Fifteen if we talk."

Clarisse turned it in her hands, thumb finding the shell eye. Her face did the thing it did when outflanked by kindness.

"Fifteen," she said hoarsely. "We talk. Silena, hold the stern. Percy, tool roll. Annabeth, sight the line. Thalia, useful or absent."

"Absent," Thalia said from an overturned canoe. "I'm excellent at supervising."

They set to. Tyson worked with the absorbed, humming focus he always had around wood, explaining each step out loud. Pine tar warmed in the sun. Annabeth sighted along the stem with her compass, calling adjustments. Silena steadied the hull and told Tyson about the flag. Percy handed tools before he was asked, the way little brothers did when they wanted to be worthy of the work.

When it was done the hippocampus head rode the bow like it had grown there, shell eye catching the light. Tyson stepped back and wiped his hands on his coat and nodded once, satisfied.

"Now she remembers being hurt, and she also remembers being mended. Both things. That is better than forgetting."

Percy put his hand on the carving. "Thank you. She looks brave."

"She learned it from you. All three of you." He tied the blue flag to the stern thwart himself. "Now she has colors. Now she is a ship."

"You always know the right thing, Tyson."

"No. I know the wood thing. The people thing I practice. Percy helps me in letters." He shouldered his duffel again and looked at them all, one by one. "Thank you for inviting me. Four nights. Visitor's bunk. Towels on the hooks. I do not live here. I visit."

"You couldn't get in the way with a battering ram," Thalia said, standing and stretching. "Come on, big guy. Let's get you to the cabin. And I want to see what else is in that duffel. It clanks."

"It clanks because there are gifts," Tyson said, delighted. "I told you. One for everybody."

---

Silena Beauregard had a theory about gifts, and the theory was this: the gift was never the object. The gift was the proof that somebody had been thinking about you when you weren't there to see it.

She thought about it all the way up the switchback, walking beside Tyson with her hand tucked in the crook of his enormous arm, listening to Percy chatter three steps ahead and double back every twenty feet to make sure Tyson was still there.

The light had gone full midmorning by the time they reached Cabin Three. Tyson stopped at the door and took a great breath of the cabin smell — wet stone, lemon oil, salt underneath — and his whole face softened.

"It smells the same," he said. "I remembered it right. In the forge I tried to remember and I got the lemon wrong." He ducked through the doorway the way he'd learned back in July, and stopped at the sight of the visitor bunks, towels squared on the hooks. "Oh. You made my bed."

"Percy made it," Clarisse said gruffly. "He did the corners himself. Took him three tries. I supervised. Supervision means I watched and criticized."

"It is a very good bed," Tyson said, sitting on it to test the springs, which creaked gamely. He bounced once, experimentally, and grinned. "Strong. Like Clarisse." He looked at the little shelf by the door where the round white rock sat, egg-smooth, and his eye went wide and soft. "You kept it."

"Of course we kept it," Silena said. "We dust around it. Clarisse does. With a cloth. She won't admit it."

"I maintain the shelf," Clarisse said. "The rock is incidental."

Tyson picked up the white rock and held it a moment in his palm, thumb over its curve, and set it in the middle of his pillow. "Good," he said. "Eggs are for waiting. This one can wait with me."

He unbuckled the duffel then, and they all gathered round on the tile the way kids did, Percy cross-legged, Annabeth on the bunk rail, Thalia in the doorway, Clarisse against the pool-room arch, Silena beside Tyson.

"One for everybody," Tyson repeated, reaching in with great ceremony. "I thought very hard. Percy helped in letters, but the thinking was mine."

First out: a short, blunt sword in a plain leather sheath, the blade of polished pink coral, the guard wrapped in cord, the grip sized for a girl's hand. He offered it to Clarisse with both hands, hilt first.

"For big sister," he said. "It is coral from the reef edge. Not sharp like bronze. Blunt, for practice. But strong. It will not break. I tested it on a rock. The rock broke." He looked at her earnestly. "You fight hard. I wanted you to have something that fights hard back. And the color —" He glanced at Silena for support. "The color is like the inside of a shell. Silena said you like things that are beautiful but do not admit it. So this is beautiful and also a weapon, so you can admit the weapon part."

Clarisse took it slowly, drew three inches of pink blade, tested the edge with her thumb. Her jaw worked. "Thank you," she said roughly. "It's balanced. I'll drill with it this afternoon. Don't let Percy touch it."

"I will not touch it," Percy said, hands up. "I'm scared of it already and I haven't even held it."

"Good. Fear is wisdom." Clarisse resheathed it with a click and laid it across her knees.

Second out: a small olive-wood box, hinged with brass, smelling of beeswax. Inside, nestled in cloth: carved toggles and needles, each toggle a different sea creature, turtle and dolphin and tiny hippocamp.

"For little sister," Tyson said. "Because you mend everything. The cabin. The clothes. The people." He said it with total matter-of-factness, enormous sincerity, no idea he'd said anything large at all. "The turtle is for coats. The dolphin is for shirts. The hippocamp is for flags. I made the eyes with a hot pin. Percy said in a letter that you cry when people leave. I thought — if the toggles are animals, they can stay. Even when people go."

Silena held the tiny dolphin toggle in her palm and looked at it until it blurred. "Oh, Tyson," she whispered. "Did you carve these at the forge? Between shifts?"

"On breaks. The foreman said I could, because my brackets were ahead." Tyson watched her face anxiously. "Do you like them?"

"The eyes are perfect." Silena leaned over and kissed his cheek, and he went pink to the ears and beamed. "They're staying on everything I own. Even things that don't need toggles. I'll find a way."

Third: a brass-bound waterproof notebook, pages of pressed sea-paper, with a pencil clipped to the spine and a small tide-table folded inside the cover. For Annabeth.

"For the planner," Tyson said. "Percy says you map everything. Even the heron. I thought — the sea has tides. The lake does not, but the pool does, a little, because of the channels. I wrote down the harbor tides for the next month in the back." He opened it to show pages of large, careful block printing, numbers in neat columns. "My writing is big because my hands are big. Sorry. But the numbers are correct. I checked twice. Now you can map the water coming and going."

Annabeth took it with both hands, turning pages, eyes widening. "Tyson, these are harbor soundings? With dates? This is real data. I can correlate this with the channel levels here." She stopped, overwhelmed into precision's opposite, which for her was quiet. "Thank you. I'll put it on the survey tonight."

"You are welcome." Tyson nodded, pleased. "Mapping is important. If you do not write it down, the water forgets. Percy told me you do not let things be forgotten."

Fourth: a coil of braided wire, dark and supple, with a lightning-bolt charm hammered flat at one end — a bracelet, sized big.

"For sister-of-Annabeth," Tyson said. "It is forge wire. It will not break if you catch it on a branch. The charm is for your father." He hesitated, suddenly shy. "Percy said you walk the boundary alone. I thought — if you wear it, it is like somebody walking with you. A little. Even if I am not there."

Thalia took it, turned it over, slipped it on. It fit. She looked at it a long moment, jaw tight, then reached up and ruffled Tyson's hair hard enough to move his whole head. "Thank you, big guy. I'll wear it on patrol. If anything jumps me I'll hit it with the charm end."

"You should run first," Tyson advised seriously. "Then hit. Percy says running is also brave."

"Percy's letters are full of wisdom," Thalia said, glancing at Percy with dry affection. "Who knew."

Last: a small cloth bag, soft with weight. Tyson held it a moment, suddenly solemn.

"For brother," he said. "Open it later. When it is quiet. It is not a sword or a tool. It is —" He searched for the word. "It is a remembering thing. I will tell you about it at the lake."

Percy took the bag, hefted it, felt shapes through the cloth — smooth, round, heavy. His throat moved. "Tyson—"

"It is nothing big," Tyson said quickly, worried he'd gotten it wrong. "Just stones. Good stones. You will see."

"It's perfect," Percy said fiercely. "Whatever it is. Thank you."

Tyson's whole face relaxed into radiance. "Good. All gifts given." He lay back on the bunk with a happy sigh. "I have been here one hour and already my bed is made and the canoe has an eye."

Clarisse stood abruptly, coral sword in hand, and went to the door. "I'm going to — drill forms. With this. Before lunch. Somebody come time me." It was a retreat and everybody knew it and nobody named it.

"I'll time you," Thalia said, pushing off the doorframe. "Annabeth, walk with us? I want to see this tide-table."

Annabeth glanced at Percy, who nodded. "Go," he said. "We'll catch up. Cabin stuff."

Annabeth squeezed his shoulder as she passed and followed them out into the gold light.

Silena let the door fall shut behind them and turned back to find Tyson watching Percy, and Percy watching the cloth bag in his hands, and the cabin gone suddenly, tenderly quiet, pool breathing at the end of the hall.

Tyson looked around the cabin slowly, taking inventory with his whole face. "The towels are still on the hooks," he said with satisfaction. "Nobody took them down. That means the waiting worked."

"It worked," Silena said, bumping his shoulder with hers. "Blanket folded twice. Pillow flat, not fluffed. Percy remembered."

"I wrote it down so I would not forget," Percy said, ears pink. "Fluffed pillows feel like sleeping on a cloud that's about to rain. Flat is better."

"Flat is honest," Tyson agreed. He looked at the pool-room arch where the light moved on tile. "Can I see Tempest Hall? I dreamed about it at the forge."

They took him in, the way you'd show someone a chapel. Tyson went to the edge and knelt and put his palm flat on the water without breaking the surface, feeling it, and closed his eye.

"The channels are strong today," he murmured. "The sea is thinking about us." He looked up at Percy. "You swim here every morning?"

"Most mornings. Clarisse does laps like the water owes her money. There's a Thursday thing. There is absolutely a Thursday thing."

"There is absolutely a Thursday thing," Silena confirmed. "On your hands and knees with a brush."

"It is discipline," Tyson said, nodding. "Work is love you can see." He stood, wiping his palm on his coat. "But work needs lunch." He patted his bulging coat pocket. "I brought sea biscuits. They are very hard. Clarisse likes hard things."

Percy laughed, sudden and real. "She'll pretend they're terrible and eat four."

"She can have six," Tyson said generously. "I made extra." He nodded at the cloth bag in Percy's hands. "Keep those safe until the lake. Stones like water."

"Stones like water," Percy said softly. "Okay. Lake it is."

---

Percy Jackson loved the lake at two in the afternoon in mid-September the way you loved a sibling in a new mood.

Morning water was glass, silver-green and held-breath still. Afternoon water had opinions. A breeze chopped the surface into small bright scales, and the shallows went clear over white sand, and the sun-warmed rock sat hot enough to sting. The air smelled of pine resin and that green cold freshwater smell that was nothing like salt and everything like home.

Percy led the way down the switchback with Tyson behind him, both loaded like mules — towels, blanket, peaches and bread and honey, dry socks, the little cloth bag of stones knotted safe in Percy's pocket — and the rest strung out behind in a noisy line, arguing about who had to carry the folding camp chair.

"I carried it last time," Percy called over his shoulder.

"You lost the race last time," Clarisse said. "Losers carry chairs. That's maritime law."

"This is a lake. Maritime law doesn't apply."

"Maritime law applies everywhere. It's in the name. Mari. Time." Clarisse shoved the chair at Thalia. "Zeus cabin carries the chair. Diplomacy."

"Diplomacy," Thalia agreed, taking it with bad grace and setting it on her head like a crown. "I look great. Somebody carve this."

They came out onto the north beach in a spill of noise and heat, and Tyson stopped dead at the tree line.

The beach ran down to the water in a half-moon nobody had built anything on, glassy shallows running out to a sandbar where the pale three-pronged scar still showed faint in the white sand. Past that the lake went green-deep, boundary rock mid-distance, willow cove east where the skiff rested and the heron stood grey on its bow.

Tyson stared at all of it with his mouth slightly open. "Oh," he said. "Oh, Percy. It is bigger than your letters. You said big and I thought forge-big. This is sky-big."

"It swims small," Percy promised, dropping his load on the rock. "Come on. Feet first. That's the rule. You have to let it introduce itself."

Tyson toed off his work boots, lining them up on the stone, and rolled his trousers to the knee with thick, precise cuffs. He waded in to his ankles and stopped, face transforming. "Cold! Sweet and cold!"

"That's the honest part," Silena called, spreading the blanket. "Give it a minute. It likes you."

Tyson waded deeper, to his shins, to his knees, holding his coat clear with both hands, laughing every time a wavelet slapped his legs. He crouched and plunged his whole forearm in to the elbow and came up dripping. "It is so clear! I can see my feet! In the sea I cannot see my feet unless the water is being polite!"

"Lake's got no manners and no mercy," Thalia said, kicking off her boots. "No salt, either. You'll float lower."

"I will expect the lake to catch me," Tyson said firmly. "Different water, different manners. I will learn." He looked back at the beach — his brother and sisters on the rock, Annabeth unrolling vellum, Thalia testing the water with one toe and swearing — and his face did the thing it did when happiness arrived too big to hold politely. "All of you are here. Nobody is on a different mountain or under a different sea. That is a whole hug made of a place."

Clarisse went still with her hands in the bread paper. Percy felt his throat close.

"Yeah," Percy said quietly. "That's what it is."

They launched the canoe together, all hands, because a repaired canoe's first launch with its crew was a ceremony whether anybody named it or not. Green hull light and stiff, blue flag snapping, hippocampus prow riding the bow. Tyson walked alongside with one hand under the gunwale, murmuring to the hull in the old tongue, and they set her down in the shallows and held her while the lake took her weight.

"Straight," Annabeth called from the shore, compass flat in her palm. "She sits higher at the bow than before. The new ribs changed the trim. She'll ride chop better. Negligible change forward."

"Negligible," Tyson repeated happily. "Small but important."

"Maiden voyage," Silena announced, clapping. "Crew of — how many can she take? Three safely, four if we're friendly?"

"Four if two of them are Poseidon kids and one is a Cyclops who counts as ballast," Clarisse said. "Get in. Percy, bow. Tyson, amidships, low. Silena, stern. I'll push off and — no, I'll push off and get in after, don't look at me like that, I know where the gunwale is."

"Somebody's got to stay ashore with the food," Thalia said, settling on the blanket with the paper sack. "Annabeth, stay with me. We'll chart. We'll judge. We'll eat peaches and offer no useful help."

"Agreed," Annabeth said, sitting cross-legged with vellum across her knees, pencil already moving. "I'll take times and lines. Shout when you round the point."

Percy shipped the paddle and looked back at the beach as Clarisse shoved them off with a grunt and swung herself in over the stern with a shower of drops, rocking them once, hard. Annabeth raised a hand, vellum flapping. Thalia lifted a peach in salute. The sun was hot on his shoulders. The hull moved under him like something alive and glad.

Tyson sat amidships with his knees up and his huge hands trailing in the water on both sides, face alight. "She runs straight," he breathed. "Feel it? No pull. The ribs sing. Ships are happiest when they are full of family."

They ran her out past the sandbar, Percy calling the stroke from the bow, Silena steering from the stern, Clarisse amidships using her paddle mostly to splash Percy when his timing slipped. The hull cut the chop clean, flag snapping, prow eye bright.

"Point and back," Clarisse ordered. "Same as always. Percy, don't cheat by going under. I can feel the current when you do."

"I don't cheat," Percy said. "I use my natural advantages."

"That's the definition of cheating."

"That's the definition of being Poseidon."

Tyson laughed so hard the canoe rocked, and steadied the gunwales with both hands, still laughing. "You fight like the forge hammers! Bang, bang, bang! It is music!"

"We're not fighting," Percy and Clarisse said together, and then looked at each other, and then Percy grinned and Clarisse's mouth negotiated, and Tyson laughed harder.

"Family teasing," Tyson said with satisfaction. "Percy wrote about it. Clarisse teases sharpest because she loves sharpest. Silena teases softest because she mends while she teases. Am I teased?"

The canoe went quiet except for water on the hull. Percy turned in the bow, paddle across his knees.

"You want to be teased?" Percy said.

"I want to be family the same way," Tyson said. "Just the love part. Not the mean part."

Clarisse shipped her paddle with a click. "Listen, Cyclops. You get the love part. You always got the love part." She flicked water off her blade into his face. "You're the only one of us who can carry the whole canoe. That's teasing. It means we're jealous and we love you."

Tyson wiped water from his eye and beamed. "I can carry it! It is light! Cedar is light!"

"You're supposed to deny it," Percy said. "That's the game. Somebody says you're strong and you say no, I'm just big-boned—"

"I am big-boned," Tyson agreed gravely. "My bones are very big. The foreman measured." He flexed one arm, solemn as a statue, and Silena and Percy squeezed the muscle and pronounced it adequate, and Clarisse pronounced it showing off, and Tyson glowed like a furnace door opened.

They rounded the point rock where every race ever contested by Cabin Three had turned, barnacle-rough stone, and Silena called the turn and they swung wide and ran for home with the wind behind them. From the beach Thalia's whistle split the air and Annabeth's arm went up with the time.

They beached her in the shallows, laughing, and hauled her up above the waterline, and Tyson walked around her once, checking the seams, nodding. "Dry," he reported. "Not one leak. The tar holds." He patted the prow carving. "Good girl."

Then, without warning, he straightened, folded his work coat on the rock, rolled his trousers higher, and waded straight out to chest depth, arms out, face to the sun.

"Oh," he said. "It holds me. Differently than the sea, but it holds me." He lay back, enormous body going buoyant with a splash that soaked Percy to the knees, floating with his ears under, work shirt billowing. "It is quieter! The salt sings loud. This hums low. Like the cabin when everybody is asleep and happy."

Percy waded in after him, to his waist, to his chest, the freshwater closing cold and clean over his shirt, and lay back alongside his brother while the chop slapped gently. Silena came in next with a whoop, and then Clarisse, cursing the cold with feeling, and the four of them made a ragged line in the shallows, holding wrists instead of ankles because Tyson was too big for ankles.

"This is the Group Float," Tyson said suddenly, ears still under, voice gone echoey. "Percy wrote about it. One floats, one holds, one says a true thing. Can we do it? Here? In the lake?"

Percy felt Clarisse go still on his other side. The tradition Silena had made at eleven: one true thing with ears under, nobody asks.

"We can do it," Silena said softly. "Same rules. One at a time. Ears under, one true thing. Nobody asks after."

Tyson went first. He took a huge breath and sank till the water closed over his face, and stayed down, and came up streaming, blinking water from his eye.

"The forge is loud," he said. "I am the biggest there and also the youngest. Sometimes the older ones laugh when I talk to the metal. Here nobody laughs. Here you listen. That is my true thing. Here I am not too big."

Nobody asked. That was the rule. Silena squeezed his wrist hard. Clarisse splashed water once over his head in benediction. Percy felt his chest ache with loving somebody who told the truth for free.

Silena went next, under and up, hair streaming. "I mended Tyson's coat pocket in July and put a lemon drop in it for him to find at the forge, and I never told anybody because it was a secret between me and the coat." She smiled, wet and unashamed. "That's my true thing."

Clarisse went third, quick duck under, straight up, shaking her hair like a dog. "One," she said, in the voice that was a little too steady, and did not explain it, and Tyson nodded as if that were a complete sentence, which in Cabin Three it was.

Percy went last. He filled his lungs and went down into the green cold and said it into water that would not tell anybody, one true thing, the ticket price. He came up and did not explain it, and Tyson held his wrist a little tighter for a second, as if he'd heard it anyway.

They floated till they got cold, till Thalia yelled from the beach that lunch was getting sandy. They thrashed ashore like siblings and sprawled on the hot rock in a tangle of towels while Silena dealt peaches. Tyson produced a jar of peanut butter from the satchel like a magician. "I promised in the letter. I bring peanut butter. Tomorrow Percy steers."

Tyson sat dripping and content, and held out his hand to Percy. "Now," he said. "The stones. It is quiet. Everybody is eating. That is the right time for remembering things."

Percy pulled the little cloth bag from his pocket, cloth damp and heavy. Inside lay a dozen smooth stones, sea-rounded, each a different color — grey and green and rose and black — and one larger flat one, pale, with something scratched into it.

Tyson took the flat one and turned it so Percy could see. Scratched in blunt, careful lines: three small figures holding hands, and beside them a fourth, bigger, with one big round eye. Underneath, in Tyson's large block printing: US.

"At the forge there is a Bin of Good Stones," Tyson explained, lining them up on the rock. "When a stone is exactly right — smooth, no cracks — the smiths put it there instead of throwing it away. I asked if I could have some for my brother." He placed a green one in Percy's palm and closed Percy's fingers over it. "This one is for holding when you are nervous. It is cold first and then warm. Like the lake."

Percy looked at the stones in his hand, at the scratched family on the flat one, at his huge gentle brother. "Tyson. This is —"

"It is nothing big," Tyson said quickly. "Just stones. But they are good stones. And the picture is us. All four. Even when I am at the forge and you are here. Even when —" He hesitated, searching. "Even when the panel has thirty-nine marks and nobody knows who made them. We are still us. Stones do not forget. That is why I brought them."

Percy closed his fist around the green stone, cold first and then warm, and felt his eyes burn. Behind Tyson's shoulder Clarisse was very busy with a peach she wasn't eating, and neither of them said a word.

"Thank you," Percy whispered. "I'll keep them in my pocket. With the pebble and the hair tie. They'll be — the pocket things. The staying things."

"Staying things," Tyson repeated, pleased. "Good. Pockets are for staying things."

Annabeth Chase had mapped the north beach eleven times, in every season and three different scales, and she had never once gotten the light right.

She sat on the warm flat rock in the long slant of midafternoon with her vellum weighted against the breeze, brass compass glinting, pencil moving in short sure strokes, and tried again. The problem was not the shape — she knew the shape. The problem was the use. Maps showed where things were. They did not show what they were for. And this beach was not a shoreline. It was a room. Cabin Three's room, with the lake for a floor.

She still wore her canvas overshirt with the sleeves pushed up, braid gone fuzzy, jeans rolled to the knee, boots lined up behind her. Tyson's waterproof notebook lay open beside her vellum, harbor tides in large careful columns, and she kept glancing between the two, correlating without permission. Data was a love language. Nobody else at camp brought her numbers.

Down the beach the afternoon had gone golden and drowsy. Thalia lay on the blanket with the camp chair's shadow across her legs, forge-wire bracelet catching the sun, heckling the swimmers with the drawl she used when she was happy and intended to bill it as boredom.

"That's not a float, that's a shipwreck," Thalia called. "Percy, your feet are sticking up. This is leisure. I read about it in a scroll."

"Come in here and say that," Clarisse called back, floating.

"I walked the boundary for three days. I have earned dryness." Thalia stole a peach. "Tyson! Report! Is the lake adequate?"

Tyson, floating with his ears half under, raised one huge thumb without opening his eye. "Excellent! Ten out of ten! Would float again!"

"Official rating," Thalia said to Annabeth. "Put it on the survey."

"Already noted," Annabeth said, writing it in the margin, and Thalia laughed, surprised into something soft.

"You're good for him, you know," Thalia said quietly, nodding toward the water where Percy floated beside his brother. "All of them, actually. The whole salt-shaker cabin. You map them like they matter."

"They do matter." Annabeth shaded a depth line. "It's my job. I'm camp cartographer."

"That's not what I meant and you know it." Thalia sat up, dusting sand off her shirt, with the protective edge she got around her little sister and the boy her little sister loved. "You drew their pool on the Athena wall. Got the channels wrong, but you drew it. Clarisse told me. She said it like it was an insult and meant it like it was a prayer."

Annabeth's pencil stopped. "She told you that?"

"She tells me things when she's tired. Don't tell her I told you." Thalia stretched her legs out, boots off, feet sandy. "Did I miss home out there? Every hour. That's the job. You walk the line so everybody inside it can float." She bumped Annabeth's shoulder with her own. "Go on. Go get your boy before he gets cold. I've got the vellum. I'll guard it with my life."

Annabeth hesitated — the map was half-finished, the light was going, the depth lines wanted doing — and then set the pencil down with great deliberateness and stood, brushing sand off her knees. "Don't let the wind take it. Weights on all four corners. If the heron moves, mark the time."

"If the heron moves I'll have a heart attack and you'll map that too," Thalia said. "Go."

Percy met her at the waterline, dripping, hair flat, shirt abandoned on the rock. He looked the way he looked after good water, loose and lit, guard down, smile easy.

"Hi," he said.

"Hi." Annabeth took his hand and led him a little way down the shore where the pines came close and the rock made a windbreak. They sat with their feet in the shallows, shoulders touching. "You okay? You've been vibrating since five a.m."

"I'm better now." Percy picked up a flat pebble and turned it over, thumb over its curve. "He brought stones. Good stones. And a picture. Us. All four." His voice went thick. "He said pockets are for staying things."

Annabeth leaned her head against his shoulder, braid spilling over his arm, and said nothing for a minute, because some things wanted silence first. The lake moved around their ankles, cold and bright. The pines hissed. Somewhere behind them Silena laughed at something Tyson said, deep joy answering bright.

"I put the lake rating on the survey," she said at last. "Ten out of ten, per Cyclops. Fixed point."

Percy laughed, wet and real. "He'll check. Next visit he'll ask to see it."

"Then it'll be there." Annabeth straightened, practical even in tenderness, and pulled something from her overshirt pocket — a folded square of vellum, creased soft. "Here. I made this this morning before breakfast. Don't laugh. It's not to scale."

He unfolded it. A sketch: the cove dock from above, five figures waiting, and out past the boundary rock a V-wake with a hippocamp and a boy with one big eye waving. Underneath: Sept. — the morning Tyson came. Water sweet. Wind fair.

Percy looked at it a long time. "Annabeth. This is —"

"It's a love note," she said, matter-of-fact, ears pink. "You keep yours in your pocket. I keep mine on paper. Pockets and paper. Same thing." She took his hand again. "I know the panel's still with Chiron. I know nobody knows who cut those marks. I know you're tired and pretending otherwise. I just wanted you to have the morning written down. So it doesn't wash out."

He folded the vellum with care and slid it into his pocket beside the stones and the pebble and the hair tie, patting it flat. "Pocket things," he said softly. "Staying things."

"Staying things," she agreed. She kissed him then, brief and warm, sun on their faces, lake cold around their feet: two fourteen-year-olds, no hurry in it. When she drew back her eyes were bright. "Come on. Your sisters are getting cold and Thalia's eating all the peaches."

They walked back hand in hand through the shallows, and Thalia wolf-whistled and Clarisse threw a peach pit at Thalia and Silena applauded, and Percy went red to the ears and Annabeth held his hand tighter, and the heron stretched its neck and settled, witness to all of it.

Percy Jackson had learned, in one summer at camp, that evenings were the truest part of the day.

Mornings were for plans. Afternoons were for water. Evenings were for the accounting: who was still there when the light went. He stood on the dock at sundown with a towel around his shoulders and his pocket full of staying things and watched his family do the evening math.

The canoe lay beached above the waterline, flag furled for the night. The beach was tidied — blanket folded, tin cup rinsed. Thalia had carried the chair up to the boathouse without being asked twice. Annabeth's vellum was tubed, heron still marked fixed point.

Tyson stood at the end of the dock in his damp work shirt and rolled trousers, bare feet planted on the boards, looking out at the lake going rose at the edges and then dark gold, pavilion lanterns ticking on up the hill. A loon called once from the cove and did not call again.

"The ferry comes Tuesday," Tyson said, not turning. "Chiron arranged it. That is the arrangement. Four nights. Tonight is the first."

Percy came up beside him, towel dripping. "Yeah. Four nights. That's a good visit."

"It is a good visit," Tyson agreed. He looked down at Percy, solemn and bright. "Are you sad that I go?"

The question landed clean. Percy felt it go straight through every practiced answer.

"Yeah," he said honestly. "A little. Even now, on the first night. Is that stupid?"

"No." Tyson shook his head. "Sad-before is part of love. The foreman's wife cries when he packs his lunch, and he is only gone till supper." He put one great hand on Percy's shoulder. "I am sad-before too. I was sad-before on the hippocamp the whole way here. Brine asked why I was smiling and crying. I said because visiting is happy and leaving is sad and they are the same trip."

Percy laughed, sudden and wet. "That's exactly it. Same trip."

"Same trip," Tyson confirmed, pleased. "You understand fastest."

He took his hand off Percy's shoulder and bent toward the dock boards. A brass washer had worked halfway out of the fastening beside his foot. Tyson pressed it with one thick finger, then shook his head.

"Tomorrow morning I must ask Chiron for a wrench," he said. "This one turns when feet cross it. The dock is good, but good things still need checking."

Percy crouched beside him. The washer moved under his thumb too. Yesterday he might have offered to fetch a wrench immediately, to prove he could make the visit safer and better and somehow last longer. The light was nearly gone. He could barely see the edge of the bolt.

"We'll tell Chiron before breakfast," he said. "Nobody should try to tighten that in the dark."

Tyson nodded, satisfied. "And Olive must not use this part of the dock until it is fixed. She runs and jumps. That is what ten-year-olds do when happy."

"She'll get a warning."

"With pictures," Tyson said. "She likes pictures."

Percy took the pencil stub from his pocket and made a small X on the dry board well away from the fixing. It would be easy to point out in daylight without persuading anyone that his little mark was a repair. Tyson examined the X, then added a second one beneath it, careful and round.

"Two marks," he said. "So we both remember."

Percy smiled. It was good to have something to do together tomorrow that was neither a celebration nor a goodbye.

Down the dock Clarisse was coiling rope, coral sword strapped across her back — she hadn't taken it off since the gift. Silena sat on the planks with her feet in the water, stitching the hippocamp toggle onto the blanket. Annabeth and Thalia stood at the dock's root, Thalia's arm around Annabeth's shoulders, laughing at something low and private.

Family, Percy thought. Not the people who decided to stay. The people who kept deciding it. He kept that in his pocket with the other staying things.

Clarisse finished the rope and came down the dock, boots in one hand. She stopped beside Tyson, looked out at the darkening water, and said, gruffly: "Your bunk's made. Towels on the hooks. Same as this morning. Percy did the corners."

"I saw," Tyson said. "Strong corners. Like you."

Clarisse's jaw worked. She looked at the lake, at the boundary rock going dark. When she spoke her voice had gone into the flat register it went into for true things.

"You'll go," she said. To Tyson. As if practicing on the safer grief. "Tuesday. The ferry. And the bunk'll be made up and the towels on the hooks and the rock on the pillow, and we'll be here."

Tyson nodded gravely. "Yes."

"And you'll come back?" It came out blunt, grudging, almost angry. Clarisse La Rue asking for reassurance the way she asked for everything, like a demand and a dare at once.

"Yes," Tyson said simply. "I visit. That is what I do. I do not live here. I have a sea, and you have a lake, and Triton says so kindly over tea he does not like." He smiled. "But visiting is good because then every hug is a special hug. I will come next time Chiron allows. I will bring more biscuits. You can have six."

Clarisse stared at him. Then she reached up and straightened his work-coat collar, two flat pats. "Fine," she said thickly. "Six. But you have to let me time you on the ribs next time. Your screws were a quarter-turn shy on the second one. Annabeth sighted it. Don't argue."

"I will not argue," Tyson said meekly, glowing. "You are the strongest and also the straightest. Like the ribs."

"Damn right," Clarisse muttered, and turned away fast, walking back up the dock with her shoulders set. But halfway she stopped and looked back, her face unguarded in the last light. "Hey, Cyclops. Thanks for coming. For — the sword. The prow. All of it."

"You are welcome, big sister," Tyson said. "Thank you for keeping my rock. Thank you for the towels."

She nodded once, sharp, and kept walking, and Silena looked up from her stitching and met Percy's eyes with a look that said, very clearly: let it breathe. Don't fix it. Just hold the ankle.

So Percy held the ankle. He stood in the going dark with his brother's hand on his shoulder and said nothing about palaces or quests or roads. He just stood there: all of them still there when the light went. Tyson staying latest, for four whole nights.

They walked up in the blue dusk, canoe secured, shoes sandy, Thalia carrying the chair, Annabeth's hand in Percy's, Silena singing with Tyson humming the harmony, Clarisse ahead setting the pace, counting heads at every switchback.

At the cabin door Tyson ducked the lintel without being reminded, and stopped at his bunk, and laid his big hand on the folded blanket, and looked at the three siblings gathered in the doorway — Percy, Silena with thread on her sleeve, Clarisse with salt drying white on her cuffs — and smiled the smile visible from a hundred yards.

"Good night, Cabin Three," he said. "Thank you for inviting me. Today was hugs and work and lake. Tomorrow can be whatever the water decides."

"Good night, Tyson," they said, ragged and together, and it sounded, Percy thought, like a Float's one true thing said aloud with ears above water for once.

He lay awake a while after lights-out, listening to the pool breathe and Tyson snoring in the visitor bunk, hand in his pocket where the green stone had gone warm, Annabeth's folded morning beside it.

Visited, he thought, was not the same as kept. Kept was a bunk and a towel and a rock on a pillow, waiting. Visited was somebody crossing a whole sea on Brine because your letters said the ribs sat true — somebody who loved you without reservation and without staying, and left the staying to you.

He turned over, and the cabin held him, salt and lemon and lake, and the lake held the canoe with its new eye, and the heron held the cove. Same trip, he thought, and slept."
