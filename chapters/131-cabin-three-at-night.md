## Chapter 131: Cabin Three at Night

Percy woke because the cabin had gone quiet.

Not truly silent. Water threaded the fountain's stone channels with a soft, continuous murmur, old floorboards ticked as they cooled, contracting with faint clicks, and beyond windows the lake tapped the shore in slow, even strokes. Familiar sounds had simply receded, leaving a hollowed-out quiet. The absence of everything else brought him awake, skin prickling in the cool air where the blanket had slipped.

Moonlight fell across the wall above his bed, silvering the plaster and pooling along the frame's edge. The Atlantis photograph hung in its crooked brass frame, glass glinting faintly. Faces blurred, but he knew where everyone stood: parents beneath the garden arch, Triton at the edge, Clarisse half-turned from the camera, Silena leaning into her, Annabeth's hand in his, Thalia behind her sister.

Since the return, the picture greeted him each time he entered. At night it caught differently. The palace seemed near enough to touch and far enough its currents stayed unheard. He missed it, though the ache wasn't wanting to leave camp. Both places had found ways to stay with him — salt air clinging to his pillowcase, pine sap on his sandals, the fountain's low talk underneath his dreams.

The air smelled of cold stone and lake water, blankets holding the day's warmth, wool faintly animal and comforting against his cheek. Somewhere an owl called once and went quiet again, leaving the fountain louder by comparison.

Clarisse sat upright in the other bed, knees drawn up, eyes on the fountain.

"How long awake?" Percy whispered, voice rough.

"Wasn't asleep."

"Not an answer."

"Then stop asking questions you know the answer to."

Moonlight edged the coral token on her wrist, turning its ridges silver-pink. Her spear rested beside the bed, within reach without leaning, shaft gleaming dully where the lamp had once oiled it. Percy registered it without comment, understanding the habit for what it was: not expectation of attack, simply the ease of having something trusted close.

"Bad dream?"

"No."

"Too warm?"

"Percy."

"Right."

He lay back, but sleep didn't return; the sheets felt cool then warm, cotton catching faintly on his calluses. Clarisse shifted, blanket rustling, its wool edge scraping softly. From the other bed Silena murmured and rolled over, hair spilling across the pillow with a whisper, smelling faintly of honey shampoo even in sleep.

"You don't have to keep me company," Clarisse said.

"Wasn't."

"Awake."

"So are you."

"Doesn't mean I need an audience."

"Good. Terrible at being an audience."

Clarisse leaned forward, elbows on knees, blanket pooling scratchy-warm around her hips. "Can't settle." Her voice came out rough, sleep-starved, and she rubbed once at her eyes with the heel of her hand.

Percy sat up, rubbing his face. "Want me to get Silena?"

"Asleep."

"Not," Silena said from the other bed. She pushed upright, sweeping hair from her face, eyes half-open. "Cabin's full of people pretending not to be awake. Lot of pretending."

"Wasn't pretending," Clarisse said, fingers worrying the blanket's edge, picking at a loose thread.

"Staring at the fountain as if it insulted you."

"It knows what it did."

Silena swung feet to the floor, toes curling against cool wood. "Making cocoa."

"At this hour?" Percy asked.

"Especially at this hour."

No demand in her voice that Clarisse explain herself before the kettle boiled. Silena moved as if cocoa were an ordinary answer to the hour: something warm, something to hold, a little time without a question attached, the spoon's soft clink steadying the dark. Percy had seen her do the same at camp when somebody drifted into the pavilion long after dinner, shoulders hunched against the cold. Room first; what was wrong only if they wanted to tell. Her hands moved surely among tins and mugs, sleeves pushed up, hair tied back with a practiced twist, the small domestic competence that steadied rooms without announcement, cuffs dusted with brown powder.

---

Silena crossed to the little stove, Percy trailing to help, floor cold underfoot, each board leeching warmth through their socks, lamp flame swaying and throwing long shadows. She measured cocoa and sugar while the kettle warmed, spoon clinking against tin, chocolate dust rising sweet and bitter to coat the tongue, sugar catching the light in brief white sparks. Clarisse stayed by the table, watching the blue flame shiver, chin propped on her palm, eyes reflecting fire in twin orange points.

"Want to tell me why?" Silena asked gently.

"Not yet."

"All right." Silena poured and passed a mug to Clarisse, the ceramic warm through to her palms, steam curling with a deep roasted sweetness. Percy took another, heat stinging his fingers pleasantly, steam fogging his face and dampening his eyelashes.

"You both have to stop waiting like I'm about to say something," Clarisse said.

"Drinking cocoa," Percy replied.

"Looking at me."

Silena lowered eyes to her mug. "We can look elsewhere."

"Didn't say you had to. I can be tired without needing a plan."

"You can," Silena said.

They sat in quiet, kettle ticking as it cooled. Then Silena nodded toward the storage chest. "We could make a circle on the floor."

"Why?" Clarisse asked.

"Chairs are in a line."

"Ridiculous reason."

"Good one," Percy said.

They brought wool blankets smelling of cedar and an old quilt stitched with white waves, edges soft with washing, one patch mismatched, its thread slightly darker. Percy settled by the fountain's murmur, cool mist touching his cheek in faint beads; Silena tucked legs under her, socks mismatched, one gray and one blue, toes flexing against the cold floor; Clarisse moved her chair close, legs scraping sharp against wood, then eventually joined them on the floor with a sigh, shoulder brushing the bedframe with a soft thud.

"This is a sleepover," Silena declared.

"Sitting up drinking cocoa," Clarisse said.

"How sleepovers start."

"Since when?"

"Since tonight."

Percy leaned against the bedframe, wood pressing his spine. "Counts if nobody wants back in bed."

"No one asked you to judge," Clarisse said, but her mouth's corner lifted.

The cabin felt less like a room they'd drifted into, more like one they'd chosen. Cocoa hot, blankets soft, nobody forced to decide what the night was for.

Clarisse rested her mug against one knee. Percy let the fountain set the pace of quiet. In the palace, quiet had meant current and stone and small noises of a place that knew him from childhood. Here it meant familiar walls and people who'd come back with him. He could miss one without making the other less home.

"Tell us about an Atlantis place not on the tour," Silena said, cradling her mug. "Something from small. A story you like."

Percy thought of the service corridor Triton had shown him behind the west stair, floor cool and faintly gritty, light filtering green through a high vent. Triton said that as a child he'd believed it ran through the whole palace, humming with hidden currents. It ended at a pantry smelling of flour and brine. His brother's story supplied the boy among the sacks.

"You got lost in the pantry," Clarisse said.

"Exploring."

"Told Triton the hallway moved."

"Looked different from the other side."

Silena laughed into her mug, shoulders shaking. Percy went on with Triton's version of the story.

That was how Triton had told it: Percy had been small enough to disappear between the flour sacks and too stubborn to admit he no longer knew which way led back to the hall. The burlap scratched his arms. The pantry smelled of dry grain, salt, and the sharp green peel of stored citrus. Percy knew those details because Triton had supplied them while they stood at the palace doorway, laughing at how a person could mistake one corridor for another after turning around twice. He had no picture of himself there. No remembered fear, no remembered hunger, no recollection of the pear. What he had was his brother's account, repeated over the years, and the tenderness in the way Triton always got to the part where he sat outside the door.

"He says I tried to pretend I meant to be there," Percy added. "Apparently I told him I'd found a secret room. Then got mad when he asked what the secret was."

"That does sound like you," Clarisse said.

"You have known me for less than two years."

"I have met you."

"That's not a strong defense."

Silena raised her mug. "I vote that it counts."

"Anyway," Percy said. "He says I was trying to reach the west terrace before he found me for a currents lesson. He'd shown me the turn before, but I took the service passage instead."

"And he didn't haul you out?" Clarisse asked.

"No. He sat by the door with a book and waited."

"Could've been waiting to see how long you'd keep pretending," she said.

"Probably. Triton can be patient and annoying at the same time."

"Those aren't opposites," Silena said.

"He says I didn't come out until he asked if I'd rather miss the lesson or have a pear first." Percy turned his mug between his hands. "I apparently said that was a trick question."

"Was it?" Clarisse asked.

"I don't know. He says he gave me the pear either way."

Silena rested her chin on her knuckles. "That seems fair."

"He also says I asked if he was angry." Percy glanced at the photograph. "He told me he wasn't angry I'd gotten lost. He was annoyed that I took the service passage after he'd shown me the way. Those were separate things."

"A lesson with snacks," Clarisse said.

"A lesson and a snack," Percy corrected. "He was very clear they weren't related."

"So he says," Silena teased.

Percy laughed. It was easier than saying what it meant that Triton had preserved the story. The recollection was not his, but the act of being told it belonged to the two of them now. He could ask Triton to tell it again and hear which parts changed. He could dislike the way the lesson came immediately after the pear without pretending he remembered the pantry himself.

In Triton's telling, he'd sat on the floor with his back to the pantry door, one leg stretched into the hall, book balanced on his knee. The currents had lifted the page corners whenever the door opened. He said Percy stayed quiet long enough that he'd wondered if the boy inside had fallen asleep. When Triton asked whether the sacks were comfortable, Percy had answered that they were terrible. When Triton asked if he still wanted to come out, he'd said yes, but not yet. Triton had waited a little longer, then asked if he was frightened or avoiding the lesson. Percy had apparently answered both.

"Asked why he waited instead of opening the door," Percy said. "Told me the door was mine to open. Then said I still had the lesson."

"Very Triton," Silena said, smiling.

"It was. Thought he was unfair." Percy smiled at his brother’s account, cocoa warming his throat. "Got lesson and pear. Said those weren't related."

Clarisse looked at the quilt's stitching. "That's what you had. Someone who waited."

"Some of the time," Percy said.

The answer came out before he had time to measure it. Clarisse's fingers stopped on the loose thread. Silena's hand remained open on the quilt, not quite an offer and not quite withdrawn. Percy could hear the fountain and the small tick of the cooling stove. In the quiet, the words sounded less like a defense than he had meant them to.

"I don't mean Triton didn't wait," he said. "He did. The story's his, though. The way he told it, he made it sound like waiting was easy for him. Probably wasn't."

Clarisse gave him a look. "You don't have to correct yourself every time you say something true."

"I wasn't correcting the truth. I was trying to make it less like I was saying he always got it right."

"He probably didn't," Silena said. "Nobody does."

Clarisse's thumb rubbed the seam of the quilt. "Mom didn't always know what I needed, either. Sometimes she packed snacks when I wanted her to ask what I was thinking. Sometimes she asked too many questions when I wanted to sit there and be mad."

"Did you tell her that?" Percy asked.

"Eventually. Usually after she'd already packed the snacks." Clarisse pulled one corner of the quilt across her knee. "She'd listen. Then she'd keep packing snacks."

Silena smiled. "That sounds like Mom."

"It was useful," Clarisse said, defensive for half a second, then not. "Not what I was asking for. Still useful."

Percy knew the shape of a story growing into a family verdict. One kind act became proof that someone was always kind, one missed cue proof that they never listened. The real memory resisted being made that tidy. He pictured no scene of his own; he had only Clarisse's words, the outline of her mother's towel and snacks, the car ride and the water she kept watching.

"I think I would've hated being told the snacks were enough," he said.

"I would've hated it too." Clarisse met his eyes. "Mom didn't say it. That's why I can tell the story without making her the villain."

Silena shifted closer, shoulder warm against Clarisse's. "And without making her perfect."

"Right." Clarisse's voice had gone quieter. "That's the part I want to remember when I get mad at her now. Not because I owe her a nicer version. Because the true version is more complicated."

Percy let that settle. He had been making the same attempt with Sally, trying to keep the memories clear without polishing every rough edge into a monument. He could miss the woman he remembered and still leave room for whatever she had been tired of, what she might have gotten wrong, how she might have laughed at him for making a speech about it.

"The sea didn't change when you called," he said. "But she stayed with you until you were ready to go."

Clarisse looked down at their hands. "Yeah."

"Those can both be true."

"I know." She said it as if she had spent a long time learning it and did not need a lesson from him. "That's why I keep telling the story."

The moment passed without anybody turning it into a promise. Silena rubbed warmth into her own hands around the mug. Clarisse let her fingers rest beneath Silena's. Percy picked up the cup again, though the cocoa had cooled enough that he could drink without waiting.

Silena's hand stayed open on the quilt. Clarisse moved hers closer, and Silena covered it, thumb still.

"I had people who waited," Percy said. "I know that."

Clarisse nodded. The fountain kept moving, steady as breath. Nobody told her the past was over.

Silena glanced at Percy, eyes reflecting lamplight. "Ever get out of the pantry without Triton?"

"No."

"Then right to wait."

"Pleased with himself."

"Usually is," Clarisse said.

"He brought me to the lesson too."

"Less waiting, more getting caught."

"Both true."

Percy looked toward the photograph above his bed, its glass catching moon. The palace lay only hours away, but the story felt close: cool stone, current's turn, Triton's book open beside the pantry door. He couldn't remember that afternoon. He could recognize the patience his brother still offered him.

"Do you ever ask him what happened after?" Silena asked. "Not the lesson. The rest of the day."

"No."

"Maybe you should."

"I don't know if I want the whole story."

Clarisse's brow drew together. "Why not?"

"Because I don't want to start acting like I remember it." He set his mug down. "If I ask enough questions, I can picture the pantry. The light, the sacks, the way I probably sounded. Then it's easy to forget those pictures came from Triton telling me."

Silena nodded, considering. "You could ask him to tell it again without asking for every detail."

"What would I ask?"

"What he remembers. What he thought when he found you. What he was reading."

"That last one's probably easier. He remembers books." Clarisse's voice had warmed. "You could ask if he was actually reading or just using it as an excuse to sit there."

"I think I know the answer."

"Ask anyway."

He could imagine asking in a room with Triton, where the answer could be corrected if Percy began turning it into his own. The conversation didn't have to be a recovered memory to matter. "Maybe I will."

"Only if you want to," Silena said. "You don't have to collect every story just because it's available."

That distinction helped. There were things he wanted to know because they connected him to the people who had raised him. There were other things he could leave as stories, held by whoever had lived them first. "I'll let him tell it when he wants to."

Clarisse gave a small nod. It seemed to satisfy her more than a promise would have.

Clarisse sipped cocoa. Not rested, but shoulders had eased, jaw less tight.

---

Clarisse leaned against the bedframe, blanket pooling. "I was going to say something about the first time I felt abandoned."

Silena lowered her mug. Percy stayed still, hands wrapped around warmth.

"It wasn't big. No shouting, no goodbye. I stood at the tide line with Mom. Other kids on the beach. One girl had a father who kept whistling her back from deeper water. Each call, she turned."

Clarisse looked at cocoa in her hands, surface trembling slightly. "Mom beside me with towels and snacks. Took care of everything. But I kept watching water because I knew who my father was. Thought if I went far enough in, Poseidon might know I was there."

"Did your mother say he'd come?" Percy asked quietly.

"No. She didn't promise what she couldn't keep. I asked if he could hear me. She said the sea carried many voices. Decided that meant yes. Shouted his name once and waited for water to change."

It didn't. The other girl's father kept calling, voice thin over surf, whistle piercing then fraying in the wind. Clarisse's mother asked if she wanted home, her hand warm and gritty with sand on Clarisse's shoulder. Clarisse said no, because leaving might mean missing him, throat aching with held-back tears. When dark came, her mother wrapped her in a jacket smelling of laundry and car seats, zipper cold against her chin, and drove them home. Clarisse watched water through the window, still hoping it might follow and bring him after all, streetlights smearing across the glass.

"Wasn't alone," Clarisse said. "Mom there. Knew she loved me and did everything she could. Still felt like someone else had left me where he could have found me."

Percy wanted to say he would have come if he'd known. He let the thought go; it would plant him at the memory's center where he didn't belong.

"Go back?" he asked.

"Lot. Got older. Stopped calling." Clarisse turned her palm up on the quilt. Silena covered her fingers, warm. "When I went to Atlantis and saw your room, that feeling returned. Glad you'd been loved. Also saw all the years someone showed up for you. Doesn't mean I wanted you to have less. Little piece of me still on that beach, asking what nobody can answer."

"I hear you," Percy said.

"No need to do anything with it."

"I won't."

Clarisse stared into her mug. "All I can tell tonight."

No door shut. Simply the edge of what she could reach in the dark. Silena's thumb passed once over her knuckles, then rested. Percy saw Clarisse register the touch and choose not to withdraw.

"Used to think anger meant blaming Mom," Clarisse added, quieter. "She never told me to stop. Only stayed beside me. Took a while to learn I could be angry at the empty part and still know she'd done everything she could."

"No need to make those feelings take turns," Silena said softly.

"I know." Clarisse looked toward the window's pale square. "Sometimes I still do."

Percy nodded. He knew how easily an old feeling posed as a decision. He wanted to say the years between them were neither her fault nor his. Instead he stayed with her truth: sea quiet, mother present, Clarisse wanting someone else to come.

"Thanks for telling us," he said.

"Said that's all for tonight."

"Heard you."

Enough, apparently. Clarisse turned the mug in her hands and let quiet return without defending it.

Percy set his cup down, ceramic clinking. "Can I say something about Sally without telling a story?"

Silena turned toward him. Clarisse's fingers stayed beneath hers.

"People tell me she loved me. They say she died when I was two. I believe them, and I still miss her. Sometimes I feel like I'm supposed to offer a story of my own before the sadness makes sense to anybody." He traced a loose thread on the quilt. "I don't know what to say except that I wish she were here."

"On my palace visits, Amphitrite sometimes sits with me when I wake. She doesn't try to fix the feeling or tell me what I ought to feel. She adjusts the coral night-light so it doesn't glare and waits until I'm ready to get up."

The remembered Manhattan hall stayed behind his teeth, amber light pooling on the floorboards. The sea-salt sheets and a patient hand on the blanket belonged to the nights he could share. He kept the older memory to himself.

He had almost explained the lamp to Clarisse and Silena once, then stopped before giving the memory shape in their hands. It mattered because it was his, not because they needed to carry it for him.

"Sometimes I think I should be able to tell you something about her," Percy said. "Then I worry I'm trying to make you understand before you can believe I miss her."

"You don't need to prove it," Silena said.

"I know."

Clarisse looked down at her mug. "You don't have to make her vivid for us."

Percy nodded. "I wanted you to know that I miss her."

The missing details did not have to be filled in for his grief to count. Amphitrite had never asked him to make the feeling smaller; she let him be quiet beside her until he was ready to get up.

"Sometimes I think I should tell you more about her," Percy said. "Then I can't tell if I'm doing it because I want to or because I think it would make you understand why I'm sad."

"You don't need to make us understand every time," Silena said. "We can know you miss her even when all you tell us is that you miss her."

"I believe that."

"You don't have to decide tonight whether you want to tell more stories," Clarisse said. "You can tell one when it comes up. Or not."

He nodded. There was no task hiding in the conversation, no assignment to make Sally vivid enough for her absence to count. "I wanted to say that much."

"Then I'm glad you did," Silena said.

"Sometimes I feel as if I should know exactly what to say about her. At the palace, Amphitrite doesn't ask me to explain the sadness or make it tidy. She listens when I can't find a sentence, then lets the quiet stay quiet."

"Still learning I can miss her without turning the feeling into a story," Percy said. "Sometimes I worry the sadness is too small to count unless I can explain it."

"What happened to Sally didn't empty the world of people who loved me. It left a place for her. Sometimes I worry if I miss her out loud, the people who are here will think they aren't enough."

"They weren't supposed to replace her," Clarisse said, voice low.

"No. Never asked to." Percy looked at his sisters. "I could love who was there and miss who wasn't. Still can."

Silena set down her mug. "No need to hide it to prove you love us."

"I know."

"Knowing doesn't make it easy," Clarisse said.

"No." Percy held the cooling mug between palms. "Helps when somebody else knows."

"We know," Silena said. Her shoulder brushed his, then rested. He leaned in without trying to make it more.

He looked down at where their sleeves touched, Silena's cuff soft against his wrist. He hadn't known what it would feel like to have sisters. The answer wasn't one thing: Clarisse's bluntness could make him want to argue, Silena's attentiveness could make him feel seen and occasionally caught, and both of them had histories with each other that had begun long before he arrived at camp. He didn't have to pretend he had been there for them. He could be their brother now, in the choices they made after he was old enough to know them.

"You don't have to lean on me if you don't want to," Silena said.

"I know." He stayed where he was. "I want to."

She smiled and let the moment stand without adding a promise to it.

"Can I say something?" Silena asked, twisting her empty sleeve.

Clarisse lifted an eyebrow. "Usually do."

"I worry if I'm not useful, people won't notice I'm there." Silena turned her mug between hands. "I remember what everyone likes. I listen. I make things, or make a room less awkward. Like being kind, but sometimes I'm afraid I've made it the only way people know me."

"We know you," Clarisse said firmly.

"I know. Not saying nobody cares." Silena looked from sister to Percy, lamplight soft on her face. "Afraid I'm who people remember only when a space needs filling. The gentle one who won't mind if plans change. Sometimes I'm working at being okay, and I'd like someone to notice before I must ask."

She rolled the mug between palms. "In Atlantis, I kept offering to take the picture or write what everyone wanted next. Liked doing it. But once I realized I hadn't said what I wanted to see. Everyone would have made room if I'd asked. I know that. Still waited to see if anyone would ask first."

Percy remembered the camera in her hands, how she'd moved around the garden ensuring each person had a place. She'd held the frame steady while others faced it. He'd mistaken her smile's ease for the whole of what she felt.

Clarisse started to answer, then stopped herself. Percy was glad she let Silena finish.

"Don't want to stop being kind," Silena said. "Only don't want to disappear inside it."

"I'll remember," Percy said. "Can't promise I'll guess every time, but I'll try to notice."

"You can try," Clarisse said. "And when you get it wrong, she can tell you."

Silena smiled, eyes bright. "All I'm asking."

Clarisse nudged her knee with her own. "You're not the easy one. Stubborn. Remember everything I say, even when I'd rather you didn't. Make the best tea, and know when people pretend to like the food."

"Very insulting compliment."

"Trying to be accurate."

"You are."

"Good." Clarisse raised her mug like a toast. "Not a space-filler."

Silena's eyes shone, wet and happy. "Thanks."

Percy resisted listing every way Silena mattered. She'd asked to be remembered, not turned into a speech.

---

A knock sounded. Percy opened to find Tyson outside, rolled blanket under one arm, clay jar in hand, moon silvering his shoulders. Chiron had offered him the Big House guest room for this visit from the undersea forges.

"Heard talking," Tyson said, ducking slightly. "Thought maybe not sleeping. Brought cocoa."

"From the Big House?"

"Fountain carries sound." He stepped through, floor creaking. "Water tells stories."

Clarisse eyed the jar. "What kind?"

"Honey. Warm still."

"Good."

Tyson settled on the floor beside the quilt, large and careful, boards creaking under his shifting bulk, his shadow huge and soft in lamplight. "Can I tell a story? Cyclops story my uncle told me." His voice rumbled low, gentle at the edges.

He told of a Cyclops who wanted a bell so valley people could hear from far, its bronze throat green with age. Enormous bell — when rung, birds burst from trees in a black flurry and sheep fled to the mountain's other side, bells on their own collars jangling. People disliked the noise, hands over ears. The Cyclops thought himself bad at bells, shoulders hunched to his ears.

"His sister came," Tyson continued, hands shaping the bell. "Didn't say the bell was good. Didn't tell him to make a new one. Listened while he told how it sounded. Then asked if he wanted help melting it. He said no. So she stayed."

Next morning the Cyclops asked her help making a smaller bell. She held the mold while he poured. It rang only across the valley, soft enough sheep didn't run. Shepherds liked it.

"So he fixed the bell," Clarisse said.

"Made a different one," Tyson said. "First still too loud. They didn't say it wasn't."

Percy watched Clarisse turn that over. Tyson had told it plainly, without insisting what it meant.

"Keep the big bell?" Silena asked, hugging knees.

"Kept in workshop. Useful for storm or monster. Didn't ring it for ordinary mornings."

Clarisse looked at Tyson, expression open in lamplight. "Like that one."

"Me too." He poured honey cocoa into spare mugs, sweet steam rising. He remembered Clarisse disliked hers too sweet, leaving hers shorter. When she noticed, she held his gaze a moment, then said, "Thanks."

Tyson said his uncle had told the story after a bell he made shook dust from every forge beam. Tyson had been there, small enough to hide behind the stool when it rang, hands over ears. His uncle had laughed, but not at Tyson for startling. He'd pressed a hand to the floor till stones stopped vibrating, then showed how sound had traveled the room.

The story had clarified for Tyson afterward. A sound could overwhelm without being bad. It could shake the room and still belong to its maker.

"Real bell still hangs in forge," Tyson said. "Sometimes he rings it when I visit."

"Why?" Percy asked.

"Because it sounds like him."

"Good reason."

They drank while Tyson described the forge, black stone walls open to sky where swallows nested, mud cups tucked under eaves, coals breathing orange with each bellows sigh, heat rippling the air and smelling of iron and ash, and a three-legged stool his uncle built because ordinary ones tipped beneath him, wood scarred and re-nailed, its seat polished by use. Silena asked questions, voice drowsy, chin in hand, eyelids fluttering. Clarisse listened cheek against the bedframe, wood grain pressing into skin, eyes half-closed, breathing slowing. Coals dimmed to gray threads, and the room settled into the rhythm of people who'd stopped waiting for night to end, cocoa skinning over in forgotten mugs, a thin wrinkled film catching lamplight.

Tyson noticed Silena eyeing the jar. "It is not too sweet?"

"It's honey. Honey is sweet."

"But is it too sweet?"

She took another sip. "No. You remembered Clarisse doesn't like much in hers. Did you remember mine?"

"You like honey."

"I do. But I don't always want it in everything."

Tyson considered this with the seriousness he gave a bent rivet. "Would you like a little less next time?"

"A little less would be nice."

"I can remember that." He set the jar beside his knee, where his hand wouldn't knock it into anyone's mug. "You can also say if I forget."

"I can," Silena said, smiling. "I don't always want to have to be the person who catches everything."

"Then I will try to catch some things." Tyson glanced at Clarisse. "I am not good at all of them."

"Nobody asked you to be," Clarisse said.

Tyson leaned back, careful not to put his weight against the shelf. "My uncle used to forget that I did not like the thick gloves. He thought they were warm. They made my fingers feel like they belonged to somebody else."

"Did you tell him?" Percy asked.

"After I dropped a hot piece of metal. He made me new gloves with thinner palms and long cuffs." Tyson pressed his fingers together as though testing the old fit. "He said he wished I had told him before. I said I did not know I could."

Silena rolled the mug between her palms. "That's the thing. People can want you to tell them and still need to make it possible to say."

"Yes," Tyson said. "He asked later if the new ones were good. I said they were better. He said better was not the same as good. So I told him they pinched."

Clarisse laughed. "Sounds like you've been a pain in the forge for a long time."

"I was small. It was harder to reach the tools."

"That doesn't answer whether you were a pain."

"I was a useful pain," he said, earnest enough that Percy laughed into his cup.

The exchange let the room loosen again. Silena asked whether Tyson still had the gloves. He did, though the palms were worn through now. Clarisse wanted to know if he could mend them. Tyson said yes, but the leather was not good enough for hot work. He would repair them as a reminder, not use them. Nobody asked him to turn that into a lesson about family. The practical answer was enough.

---

The door opened a little later, hinges whispering, cool corridor air slipping in smelling of night grass. Annabeth stood in the threshold, two blankets over one arm, lantern in the other hand, its flame cupped small and gold. Hair fell loose around her shoulders, still carrying the day's sun and faint woodsmoke; green glass charm caught dim light, glinting green-gold at her throat.

"Thought you might be awake," she said softly.

"Fountain gave us away?" Percy asked, smiling.

"Running faster for an hour."

"Could be wind."

"Could." She set the blankets beside him, wool smelling of the Zeus cabin. "Brought these because Zeus cabin has too many."

"Didn't have to."

"I know."

Thalia appeared behind her, hand on the doorframe, hair mussed with sleep. "Came to ensure my sister wasn't wandering camp alone."

"Standing at the door," Annabeth said.

"That's how I know you're not alone."

Silena patted the quilt. "Room."

Thalia crossed and settled near Annabeth, close enough to join without crowding, long legs folding. Annabeth sat beside Percy, shoulders touching, familiar warmth. He turned his hand palm-up; she slid fingers into his and pressed thumb against his knuckles.

Thalia's gaze moved over the cups and the blanket circle. "How many of you are actually drinking cocoa?"

"Most of us," Clarisse said.

"That isn't a number."

"It's a useful answer."

Thalia picked up the nearest mug and sniffed. "This one is mostly honey."

"Tyson made it," Silena said. "He adjusted it for Clarisse."

"You can say you don't like it," Clarisse told her sister.

"I didn't say I didn't like it." Thalia took a careful sip. "I said it was mostly honey."

"You could put that in your report to the cocoa committee." Clarisse reached for the jar, then stopped when Tyson moved it out of the way of Thalia's elbow. "Thanks."

"You're welcome." He turned toward Thalia. "I can make yours less sweet next time."

"I'm fine with this one. I just didn't know what I was getting."

"That's fair," Silena said. "I told him I like a little less next time."

Thalia glanced at her. "You did?"

"Yeah."

"Good." Thalia settled the mug between both hands. "I didn't know if you wanted us to help, or if you were trying to prove you were fine without it."

Silena's eyebrows lifted. "Those aren't the only choices."

"I know." Thalia's eyes dipped to the mug. "I'm trying to remember that."

Clarisse shifted to make a little more room by her side, a small scrape of blanket. "You can sit here if you want."

"I am sitting here."

"You can sit closer if you want." Clarisse looked away as she said it, as if the offer were a piece of furniture she had noticed might be useful.

Thalia's expression changed, amusement flattening into something more careful. "Okay." She moved until her shoulder touched Clarisse's. "Tell me if I'm in the way."

"I will."

There was no embrace, no explanation of how a sister ought to act. Clarisse kept her eyes on the cocoa, and Thalia's shoulder stayed against hers. Percy watched the space between them narrow by inches, then turned his attention back to Annabeth. She was looking at them too, mouth soft, not quite smiling. He brushed his thumb over her knuckle. She leaned into him, a quiet answer to a question he'd not asked aloud.

"You came out because you were worried about Thalia wandering alone," Percy said to Annabeth.

"I came because I heard the fountain making enough noise to wake the cabin."

"You mean you were worried about us?"

"I mean I was curious." She tilted her head against his shoulder. "And I brought blankets."

"That's practically a declaration of concern."

"Don't make me take them back."

"I wouldn't dare."

Her fingers stayed threaded with his. She looked around at Clarisse, Silena, and Tyson, all with different degrees of wakefulness and different reasons for being on the floor. "What have you been talking about?"

"The people who wait," Silena said. "And how someone can want you to ask without expecting you to read their mind."

Annabeth nodded. "That's a hard balance."

"It is," Thalia said. "I've spent a long time guessing what Annabeth needs before she asks. Sometimes I get it right. Sometimes I make a decision and call it help."

Annabeth lifted her head from Percy's shoulder. "You did that this afternoon."

Thalia's face tightened. "The training slot?"

"You told the instructor I wasn't ready for the upper platform. I had asked him about it. You answered for me before I got to."

"I thought you were going to ask him to let you use it alone."

"I was going to ask whether I could try it with someone spotting me."

Thalia looked at her sister. "You hadn't said that."

"I was trying to say it."

The blanket shifted under Thalia's hand. "I should've let you finish."

"Yeah."

"I won't do it again." Thalia paused. "Not tomorrow, I mean. I'll probably do it again eventually."

Annabeth's mouth quirked. "Then I'll tell you again."

"You will."

"And you can tell me when you're scared. You don't need to turn it into an instructor's decision."

Thalia held her gaze. "I can try that."

"Good. I can try to tell you what I want before we're already in the middle of it."

"That would help." Thalia adjusted the blanket at her sister's shoulder, then checked herself. "Do you want me to—"

"Yes," Annabeth said. "That part's fine."

Thalia finished tucking the edge around her. The motion was familiar. The question before it was new enough that Percy noticed.

Clarisse glanced over. "Do you two always negotiate blankets?"

"Only the important ones," Annabeth said.

"The rest are a hostile takeover," Thalia added.

"You're the one who steals mine."

"Borrowing isn't stealing."

"You keep the ones you borrow."

"Then they must have been better blankets." Thalia settled back, and Annabeth's shoulder met hers for a moment before Annabeth leaned against Percy again. Nothing had been resolved about how much fear belonged in Thalia's decisions. Tonight she had let her sister finish a sentence, and Annabeth had named what she wanted from her without turning it into a test.

Annabeth unfolded one blanket over her knees, wool scratchy then warming, and passed the other to Thalia, its folds smelling of cedar chests. Thalia started to refuse, then accepted when Annabeth nudged fabric toward her, fingers insistent. She tucked the edge around her sister's shoulder first, smoothing it flat, then settled under the rest herself. Practiced, a small ritual carried between cabins for years, done without looking.

"You okay?" Annabeth asked, eyes on him.

"Miss Sally tonight."

"I'm here."

"I know."

Annabeth rested her head against his shoulder, hair soft against his jaw, blanket heavy and warm over their legs. He kissed her hair and let his cheek rest against her temple — small, familiar comfort, smelling faintly of sun and soap, not an answer to missing.

Her hand stayed in his. Her fingers warm; his gone cool around the mug, ceramic leeching heat from his skin. She rubbed her thumb across his knuckles until tightness left, her touch dry and steady. They'd learned not to turn every sadness into a problem to solve together. Sometimes beside her was what he could manage, her shoulder solid against his, blanket rough over their knees, and sometimes enough.

Thalia glanced around the circle, taking in mugs and blankets and Tyson. "What did I miss?"

"Percy got lost in a pantry," Clarisse said, reviving.

"Exploring," Percy replied, on cue.

"Hiding from a lesson," Silena added.

"More likely," Thalia said, grinning.

Annabeth squeezed his hand. "You told them about Sally?"

He nodded. She didn't ask him to repeat it, only held tighter.

"And I told about the beach," Clarisse said. "First time I remember waiting for Poseidon."

Thalia's expression softened, but she reached for no ready answer. "Want me closer?"

Clarisse glanced at space between them. "You can sit there."

Thalia shifted till her knee touched Clarisse's. Clarisse stayed.

Silena looked around. "We've had cocoa, stories, a Cyclops bell, and a floor circle. Something's missing."

She turned toward the photograph above Percy's bunk. In the lamplight, its surface reflected the flame and made the people in it look as if they stood behind a thin sheet of water. The print had been taken in Atlantis, before they came back to camp. Percy had thought of it as proof that he could belong in a picture with people who remembered a childhood he didn't. Triton had used the camera's short delay to reach the edge of the frame; Silena, who had packed it, was last to step into place. Now the image was here with them, neither a record of every year nor an answer to what any of them ought to feel about the palace.

"We haven't looked at it together," she said.

Clarisse eyed the frame. "The people in the frame were in the garden when we took it."

"We were all looking at the camera. That's different."

"Do we have to take it down?"

"No. I was thinking we could each pick one thing we remember from that day. It doesn't have to be in the picture."

Annabeth shifted her blanket, considering the idea. "Wouldn't that make it a memory game?"

"Maybe. Or we can pass. I just thought it might be nice to see what we each noticed."

"I can see why," Annabeth said. "But I might remember a different day than you do."

"That's okay." Silena looked at Percy. "You can tell a story you were told, or something you remember directly. Whatever you want."

He appreciated that she had remembered. Still, there was a difference between choosing a story and having it sorted for him. "I'll say if I'm using Triton's version."

"Okay." She looked to Clarisse. "Do you want to start?"

"No."

Silena gave her a small smile. "That's allowed."

Thalia glanced at the frame. "I remember the photographer asking everyone to stand closer. Clarisse said if she moved any closer, she was going to step on your foot."

"I was warning him." Clarisse pointed her mug at Percy. "He was standing on my shoe."

"I wasn't."

"You were."

"The photograph doesn't prove it."

"You can see my face. That's proof." Clarisse turned to the picture and studied her expression. "I remember thinking I didn't want to smile on command. Then Silena stepped into the frame and said the camera already had enough people pretending they weren't hot."

Silena made a face. "I don't remember saying that."

"I do," Clarisse said. "It was funny."

"I was probably trying to make you laugh."

"It worked."

Silena looked at Thalia. "What about you?"

"I remember Annabeth correcting the angle of the frame."

"It was crooked," Annabeth said.

"She was right," Silena said.

"I remember the shadow from the arch cutting across half the frame," Annabeth said. "We had to move everyone toward the light. It made the picture better, but I liked the first arrangement too. The shadows were interesting."

"You didn't say that at the time," Thalia replied.

"I was busy fixing it."

"You can like both," Percy said.

Annabeth leaned back against him. "I know. I still wonder what the first one would have looked like."

That made sense to him. The first arrangement belonged to the moment before they corrected it. A thing could be imperfect and still be worth keeping, even if the final photograph told a clearer story.

Silena raised her hand, the way she did when she was asking a question in the pavilion. "I remember arranging everyone and then realizing I wanted to be in the picture too. I was usually the one behind the camera."

"You got in this one," Percy said.

"I did. I also remember that I wanted to. It wasn't only that I didn't want to be left out."

Clarisse glanced at her. "Good."

"Good?"

"You said what you wanted."

Silena looked down at her mug. "I know. It was nice when people moved over."

Percy looked up at the print. In it, Silena stood between Clarisse and Thalia, not hidden behind a camera or half a step outside the group. The framing showed her choice. It did not show the moment she decided to make it.

Tyson looked over the frame. "I wasn't there for the garden. I was at the forges."

"Right," Silena said. "This was the visit we took without you."

"I like it anyway. Everyone fits."

"We'll bring the camera next time," Clarisse said.

Tyson glanced at her. "That would be good."

"We had melon in the garden after the picture," Silena added. "Before dinner."

"Was it cold?" Tyson asked.

"Very," Clarisse said.

Tyson nodded. "I like cold melon."

"I like hearing about it, even if I wasn't there," Tyson said.

Silena smiled. "I'm glad. I like remembering it out loud."

"Then I'll listen," Tyson said. "Was the melon the best part?"

"One of them," Clarisse said.

Percy listened as the small facts settled into the room: a shadow, a joke, cold melon. Tyson leaned to see the faces more clearly.

"He got into the picture and took it?" Tyson asked.

"That was the camera's delay," Silena said.

"Smart." Tyson nodded, pleased by the trick.

The stories didn't line up into one agreed account. They made small overlaps instead: the arch, the shadow, the fruit, Silena's place in the frame. Percy had not lived every part of the day with the same understanding. He didn't have to borrow someone else's memory and call it his to be glad he had been there in the picture. He could hear them tell it and choose what the photograph meant to him now.

"I remember looking at you all and thinking I should be able to recognize myself," he said. "I don't mean the picture. I mean the family. I wanted the fact that I couldn't remember the palace to stop feeling like I was the one missing from it."

Nobody jumped to reassure him. Annabeth's hand found his. Clarisse leaned her mug against her knee. Silena listened. Tyson's broad shoulders rose once with a breath.

"I can be in the picture and still be learning what the day was like," Percy said. "Those don't cancel each other out."

"No," Annabeth said. "They don't."

Clarisse's gaze rested on the photograph. "You don't have to know every story to be in the family."

"I know." He turned his palm beneath Annabeth's fingers. "I need to keep learning what belongs to me and what belongs to the people who remember it."

"That's fair," Silena said. "And if we tell the story wrong, you can ask us."

"If you remember it wrong," Clarisse corrected, "I'll tell you."

"Also fair." Silena smiled. "I wasn't going to tell anyone they were wrong about their own memory."

"You might if they said I smiled."

"You did smile."

"I was baring my teeth."

"The camera doesn't show that."

"It absolutely does."

Thalia reached for her mug. "I remember you threatening to break the camera if the photographer asked for one more pose."

"I was kidding."

"The photographer believed you."

"He should have."

Silena leaned into her sister. "I'm glad Triton took it."

"Me too," Clarisse said, less loudly.

The flame bent in a faint draft. Silena picked up her mug again and passed the turn before anyone had to offer another story. Percy was grateful. He could sit with the photograph without making it answer questions that belonged to the people inside it.

"Terrible game," Percy said.

"Then we need a terrible game," Silena said, perking up.

Tyson suggested naming blue things. Clarisse objected too easy, so Annabeth narrowed rules to cabin things blue but not water. The argument outlasted the game's worth. Percy named his shirt; Tyson called the door blue-green; Thalia insisted mostly green. Clarisse claimed the brass frame counted because the photograph inside showed sea. Silena ruled that wasn't what the question meant.

Rules changed whenever someone neared losing. Percy and Annabeth disputed a palace fish's color. Clarisse tried counting a bruise, which Silena ruled purple. Nobody won, everyone accused.

After a while Silena's head dipped against Clarisse's shoulder, hair tickling warm against her neck, smelling of honey and cocoa steam. Clarisse offered the bed pillow, its case cool and smooth under her hand. Silena said she was fine, voice slurred with drowsiness, but took it when Clarisse set it in her lap, fingers lingering a moment on her sister's wrist. Clarisse sat close again, arm steadying her, solid along her side.

Beyond windows the sky began to pale, gray to pearl. No one fetched a clock.
---

When Tyson had settled with his blanket, nobody quite knew what to do with the fact that he was there.

His assigned bed for the visit was still in the Big House. Chiron had arranged it because Tyson had been invited to camp for a limited period and there wasn't yet a bunk in Cabin Three that suited his height. Percy knew Tyson could stay awake with them for as long as he liked, but that didn't tell them whether he should try to sleep on the floor.

"You should go to the guest room," Clarisse said.

Tyson looked disappointed. "I am not tired."

"I didn't say you're tired. I said the floor isn't made for a giant."

"I can fold small."

Percy considered the sheer impossibility of Tyson folding small and was about to suggest a blanket nest when Silena put down her cocoa mug.

"Can we ask what he actually wants?" she said.

Tyson looked at her. "I wanted to be here. It is the first night we were all awake together since I came."

"Then you're here," Silena said. "You can stay until you want to sleep. When you do, we walk you to the guest room."

"Together?"

"Together if you want."

Tyson smiled. "I do."

Percy noticed Clarisse relaxing. She hadn't wanted to send him away because she disliked him. She had worried about how to make room for someone whose body and habits were different from theirs, and she'd hidden it behind a practical objection. The concern was real. They would have to solve it if Tyson spent more time at camp.

"What would make a bed comfortable for you here?" he asked.

"Long," Tyson said.

"Very technical."

"And strong. A table leg is not a bed leg."

"You've slept on a table?"

"Only once. It was not very good."

Clarisse laughed despite herself. "We're not putting you on a table."

Tyson put both palms flat on the edge of Percy's bunk, testing the wood with the care of a craftsman. "If I had a bed here, I would want the bottom to have extra supports. And the corner near the wall is too close to the fountain. I turn in my sleep. I might hit it."

Silena took out the little sewing notebook she kept beneath her pillow and found a blank page.

"Can I write this down?"

"Yes."

She drew the rough shape of the room, marked the fountain and the position of the three occupied bunks. Percy showed her where he thought a fourth might fit. Clarisse pointed out that his idea would block the door during an emergency.

"Then not there," he said.

The next option was better: near the back wall, where an older storage chest had been sitting unused for months. But moving the chest meant finding somewhere else for the spare linens and repair supplies.

"That's doable," Silena said. "Not tonight."

"I do not need you to build me a bed tonight," Tyson said.

"Good," Clarisse replied. "Because I'm not lifting a chest in the middle of the night."

Percy looked at the drawing. "We're not promising you a permanent room either."

Tyson nodded. "I live at the forges."

"I know. You can still have a place to sleep when you visit, if Chiron agrees."

"Yes," Tyson said. "A guest place. Mine when I am guest."

Clarisse studied him. "You want to build it?"

"If I may. With Beckendorf and the other builders. Then it is strong, and no one is angry that I am tall."

"Nobody's angry you're tall," Percy said.

"Sometimes people are angry because tall people don't fit their furniture."

Percy thought of a few chairs Tyson had unintentionally broken. "Fair."

Silena added *ask Chiron about guest bunk* to her list. She didn't write a date. The request had become a real project, but it wasn't a promise to finish it before anybody had checked the dimensions.

They drank their cocoa while Tyson told them about a repair at the forges that had required three different kinds of rivet. Clarisse argued with his choice of metal; Tyson explained it; she listened. Percy had never been so grateful for a conversation that wasn't about what the family meant. The room was full of people doing what they normally did, which sometimes involved disagreeing about screws.

At last Tyson yawned. He tried to hide it behind his hand, but the sound filled the cabin.

"That was a large yawn," Percy said.

"I am a large brother."

"Guest room?" Silena asked.

"Yes. I want the guest room now."

Clarisse took a lantern. Percy put on shoes. They walked him along the path toward the Big House and stayed until he had found the bed Chiron prepared for him. It fit. Tyson sat on the edge and tested the mattress with one firm bounce.

"Strong," he pronounced.

Tyson had not needed help finding the door, but he waited beneath the Big House porch light while they said good night. He stood with his blanket gathered in one hand, the jar tucked under the other arm.

"You can bring it tomorrow," Silena said, pointing to the empty cocoa jar.

"I can wash it first."

"You don't have to."

"I would like to. It is mine."

"All right. Bring it clean, then." She smiled. "And don't forget the lid."

Tyson checked the lid, which was already screwed on. "I will remember."

"If you don't, I'll remind you," Clarisse said.

"That is also good." He ducked through the doorway, then turned back. "I liked being in your room tonight."

Clarisse shifted her weight. "We liked having you there."

"Even when I was too big for the floor?"

"Especially then. You made the boards complain more than Percy does."

"I am heavier than Percy."

"That's not why his footsteps are annoying." She started down the path before he could ask why.

Tyson laughed, soft enough not to wake Chiron, and disappeared inside. Percy watched the light from the hall catch on the jar's rounded shoulder as it went with him. Tomorrow it would come back clean. It was a small thing, no more a promise of permanence than the sketch for a guest bunk. It was a reason to see one another again.

They said good night and returned to Cabin Three. The short walk had cooled Percy's face and made him sleepy in a way the cocoa hadn't.

"Now we have a plan," Silena said as she put the lantern down.

"A drawing," Clarisse corrected.

"A drawing and a person to ask."

"Better than no plan."

Percy looked at the new note in Silena's book. A future guest bunk would not make Tyson a permanent resident or solve the sadness of a visit ending. But it would mean that when his brother next arrived, they wouldn't be improvising a place for him out of spare furniture and love alone.

---

A knock sounded at the cabin door before the first light had fully reached the bunks. Percy sat up, already braced for a summons. It was only one of the younger campers from the canoe shed, carrying a note for Clarisse about a borrowed practice shield.

Clarisse read it in the doorway. The boy had expected her to take the shield back immediately, and his face fell when she told him it could wait until after breakfast.

"But you said to return it first thing."

"I said before today's drills. The drills aren't until nine."

"I thought first thing meant now."

"Then I wasn't clear."

She took the note, wrote the new time on the back and handed it to him. "Bring it to the arena by eight-thirty. Don't wake up anyone else over a shield."

When she closed the door, Percy looked at her. "You just admitted you gave a confusing instruction."

"Keep talking and I'll give you another."

Silena, still beneath her blanket, laughed. "It's a historic morning."

Clarisse set the note on the worktable. "The kid was trying to do what I asked. I don't need him scared of being late when there isn't a deadline."

She went to the small kettle without explaining the decision again. Percy liked that she hadn't turned it into a speech. A shared cabin meant interruptions and misunderstandings; the family could fix one without deciding it threatened everything.

Windows turned gray, then pearl, light seeping like water. Percy watched light find the cabin piece by piece: Silena's mug lip glinting, worn quilt stitching casting tiny shadows, coral token at Clarisse's wrist warming to rose, and last the photograph above his bed, faces emerging from silver dark.

He thought of the beach Clarisse described, the hallway lamp he had chosen not to describe, Tyson's bell too loud for ordinary mornings, Silena waiting to see if room existed without asking. The night had not made their memories interchangeable. It had made room for each story to stay with the person who lived it.

Annabeth leaned against his shoulder, warm beneath the blanket, breathing slow. Thalia sat within reach of her sister. Silena had curled against Clarisse, one hand on her sleeve. Tyson had gone to the Big House guest room after the conversation about a proper visitor bunk. Silena's sketch remained on the worktable, waiting for Chiron and the builders to review it.

"Still tired," Clarisse said, voice hoarse.

"I know," Silena murmured, half-asleep.

"Not ready to sleep."

"Then don't."

Clarisse let breath out and rested her head against the wall, plaster cool against her temple, eyes closing, lashes dark against tired skin. Nobody asked her to feel better, or brave, or grateful for the night. Percy kept hold of Annabeth's hand, her thumb steady over his knuckles. They sat while first camp sounds rose outside — a door thudding, a laugh, gulls — with nothing to repair before breakfast and no reason to hurry toward it.

The fountain moved through its stone channel. For a little longer, night stayed with them.
