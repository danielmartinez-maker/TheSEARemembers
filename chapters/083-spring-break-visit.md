## Chapter 83: Spring-Break Visit

The late-May rain at Camp Half-Blood came sideways.

Percy Jackson, back from the May study block in Atlantis, stood on the porch of Cabin Three with his hood up and his hands jammed in his pockets, watching it needle across the cabins and hiss into the lake. The strawberry fields glistened at the edges. The climbing wall was closed. The pegasi had extra blankets and bad attitudes. The Hephaestus cabin had put little warming braziers along the paths, and the Hermes cabin had already stolen two of them and then, caught by Clarisse, had been forced to return them with an apology note and hot chocolate as tribute.

It was three days before the Roman spring-break feast, and Percy had never been invited to a Roman festival in this altered life.

"You're staring," Annabeth said behind him.

She came out of Cabin One with her gray travel cloak over her Camp hoodie, her blonde hair in a braid tucked down the back, her cheeks pink. She carried two mugs. She handed him one. Hot chocolate, blue, of course. Amphitrite had sent up a tin of blue cocoa mix for the spring year-rounders with a note in looping handwriting: *for Percy and his spring friends — share nicely.*

"I'm thinking," Percy said.

"Dangerous," Annabeth said, and leaned her shoulder against his arm. They stood like that easily now. Years of remembered Camp, and the new days they had made here. Steady and sure. Sweethearts and best friends who held hands crossing creeks and fell asleep shoulder to shoulder in the library and did not need to make a big deal of it because everyone already knew.

"What are you thinking about, Seaweed Brain?"

"That Romans have rules for everything," Percy said. "And I am bad at rules for everything."

Annabeth laughed, breath fogging. "You're good at important rules. You're bad at formation marching and Latin declensions."

"Those sound like important Roman rules."

"They are *all* important Roman rules," she said cheerfully. "Come on. Chiron wanted us in the Big House before lunch. Thalia's coming."

---

The Big House attic smelled like cedar and old quests and a bowl of oranges.

Chiron was in his wheelchair form by the planning table, a thick wool blanket over his legs, his beard braided back for the cold. Mr. D was in a Hawaiian shirt under a Roman-style cloak, which should not have worked and did not, sipping something steaming that was probably not cocoa. Grover sat cross-legged on the rug with a stack of travel snacks. Clarisse leaned against the wall with her arms crossed, Silena beside her with a clipboard.

And Thalia Grace stood in the middle of it all like a small thunderstorm that had decided to come indoors, her black hair with its silver streak pulled back, her patrol parka over her Camp shirt, her silver bracelet glinting. Her eyes went straight to Annabeth and Percy when they came up the stairs.

"There they are," Thalia said. "My favorite rule-breakers."

"We haven't broken anything yet," Percy said.

"Give it time," Thalia said, and crossed the room in three strides and hugged Annabeth hard enough to lift her half an inch off the floor, and then punched Percy's shoulder and pulled him into a one-armed hug. "Missed you, Seaweed Brain."

"Missed you too, Thunderhead," Percy said. His voice cracked a little on the laugh. In his remembered life Thalia had been a tree when he first came to Camp. Here she had been Annabeth's fierce older sister, alive and watchful. Now she was his friend as well — the one who taught him how to brace against thunder without flinching, who wrote him blunt two-line letters from her May scouting trip: *Be good. Don't die. — T.* And then a PS only for Annabeth: *Tell Wise Girl I saved her a wolf tooth.*

Annabeth was Thalia's sister too, and that had been harder won and more fiercely kept. Daughters of Zeus were not supposed to need anyone. They had spent a whole first year circling each other. Then patrols. Then late maps. Then the night the barrier flickered in a storm and they held it back to back. Now Thalia ruffled Annabeth's braid the way older sisters were legally required to do, and Annabeth leaned into it and rolled her eyes and did not move away.

Chiron cleared his throat, kind but formal.

"Thank you all for coming," he said. "As you know, for the first time in many years, Camp Jupiter has extended a formal invitation for a small Greek delegation to join a late-May spring-break exchange. This is an approved trip. Supervised. Blessed by both camps. Lord Dionysus has — graciously — agreed to the paperwork."

"Against my better judgment," Mr. D said. "Romans. Discipline. Early mornings. No fun."

"The spring feast is *entirely* fun," Grover said, leafing through his pamphlet. "They borrowed games from Saturnalia, the December festival. The rules flip for the visit? Officers serve recruits? Gifts? Candles? A Lord of Misrule? Pan would have loved this."

"This exchange is orderly fun," Chiron corrected gently. "Which is why we are sending exactly three campers. Thalia Grace, as camp scout and as sister to the young legionary Jason Grace, to represent our camp and family ties. Annabeth Grace, as architect and strategist, to observe and learn. And Percy Jackson, as — "

"Fish out of water?" Percy offered.

"As bridge," Chiron said firmly. "Son of Poseidon, honored by both seas. You will be a guest. You will follow Roman custom as best you can. Reyna Avila Ramirez Arellano and Frank Zhang and Hazel Levesque have written personally to assure your welcome."

He handed them three sealed letters on thick cream paper with SPQR stamped in red.

Annabeth opened hers carefully, eyes widening as she read.

*Annabeth Grace, Daughter of Zeus —* she read aloud, *"Hazel Levesque requests the honor of showing you the city, the temples, and the library cisterns. Bring your plans. We have arches to argue about. — H."*

Thalia's was shorter. Percy peeked.

*Come for spring break, sister. — J.*

Thalia stared at it a long moment, her thumb rubbing the edge until the paper softened. Jason. Her little brother. Lost to Juno as a toddler, raised Roman, found again through letters, Iris-messages, and the recent exchange visits. Seeing his Roman home would be different. She still pictured his toddler hands offering her half a mud pie. She cleared her throat hard.

"He still writes like that?" Clarisse asked quietly. "Short?"

"He's Roman," Thalia said gruffly. "They charge by the letter."

Silena handed Annabeth a small wrapped parcel. "Sigillaria gifts," she explained. "Little clay dolls like the winter gifts, for you to give. I made them. Hazel gets one with a quartz in her pocket for good luck. Reyna gets one with a spear so tiny Clarisse says it's disrespectful — "

"It's adorable," Clarisse said. "It's so small it can't hurt anyone. That's the point."

Silena hugged Percy fiercely. "Be polite. Eat what's offered. Don't duel anyone before breakfast."

"No promises on the dueling if it's after breakfast?" Percy said.

"Percy Jackson," Silena said, cupping his face like the big sister she was to half the camp, "you are sweet and brave and completely incapable of standing at attention. Try."

Clarisse punched his arm lightly. "If a centurion yells at you, yell back internally. If Hazel offers you cake, take it. If Frank looks stressed, help him carry something. That's Roman for I love you."

"Got it," Percy said. "Cake yes. Yelling no. Carry heavy things."

Grover hugged them all and pressed a bag of apples into Annabeth's pack. "For the road. For homesickness. For bribing harpies if Chiron's travel plan fails."

"The travel plan will not fail," Chiron said. "Argus will drive you to the airport. From there — " he glanced at Mr. D.

Mr. D sighed. "From there the gods will look the other way while you fly west on a mortal plane with misted tickets and do not tell Zeus I said so. Spring-exchange truce. Don't make me regret it."

Annabeth squeezed Percy's hand quickly under the table, excited and nervous. Thalia caught it and smirked, but her eyes were soft.

"Sisters trip," Thalia said. "Plus Seaweed Brain as luggage."

"Hey," Percy said.

"Beloved luggage," Annabeth corrected, and laced her fingers through his for just a second before letting go for the meeting.

---

The trouble started at the airport counter.

Percy had believed the exchange paperwork was finished, mostly because he was tired of looking at it. Chiron had checked their approval letters, the Roman hosts had confirmed arrival times, and Annabeth had made two copies of the route map. The three of them had packed light enough that Thalia insisted they would look like ordinary teenagers, if ordinary teenagers carried emergency bronze weapons inside approved protective cases.

Then the clerk looked at the tickets and frowned.

"There are three passengers, but only two confirmed contact numbers."

Percy reached for the folder. "We have three permission letters."

"That's not what I asked for."

Thalia leaned across the counter. "We're traveling together."

"Yes, but the airline needs the approved arrival contact for each minor. This one is incomplete."

She pointed to Percy's name. He looked down at the line. Under the destination contact, a code had been entered that belonged to the Atlantean academy. That was where he had started the journey, not where the Roman host would collect him.

Percy felt his stomach sink. He had written the code himself when he sent the preliminary request in May, the one marked *pending confirmation*. Somebody had copied it into the final ticket record without checking the updated letter from Chiron.

"That's my mistake," he admitted.

Annabeth took the folder. She didn't take it away from him; she held it so both could read it. "The Roman host is listed on the confirmation letter. Can we amend the passenger record?"

The clerk explained the procedure. The supervisor would need to verify the host number by calling the receiving organization, not simply accepting the name Percy provided. It would take a while. Their flight was not boarding yet, but the counter line was growing.

Percy thought of using the Mist to make the code disappear. That would be easy in the short term. It would also leave him on a flight with a record that couldn't tell anyone where he was supposed to be collected.

"No magic," Annabeth said quietly, reading his expression.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking it loudly."

Thalia grinned without much humor. "He's learning that bureaucracy is a kind of monster you can't stab."

Percy stepped aside and let the supervisor make the call. Annabeth kept the other two passports and tickets together. Thalia checked that their bags were still beside the counter and not blocking anyone's path.

The Roman host answered. The supervisor verified the receiving contact and corrected Percy's record. It took eleven minutes. Percy counted all of them.

"You're cleared," the clerk said.

"Thank you," he replied, meaning it.

On the way to security, Thalia nudged him. "You didn't argue."

"I did it wrong."

"You admitted it."

"That doesn't make me like airports."

"No one likes airports."

Annabeth looked at the corrected form. "We should tell Chiron which code got copied. Otherwise the next exchange group could have the same problem."

Percy nodded. He wrote a quick note in the margins of the copied approval and sent it by the authorized messaging route before turning off his phone. He also apologized to the other two for nearly making them miss their flight.

"We weren't going to miss it," Annabeth said.

"You don't know that."

"True. But we dealt with it before it became an emergency."

Thalia looked toward the gate. "That's how adults keep telling us to handle things. It is deeply irritating when they're right."

At the waiting area they found a small boy crying because he couldn't find his seat assignment. His mother was reading a reissued boarding pass that had been printed with the wrong row number. Percy almost offered to sort it himself. Instead he pointed them toward the gate agent, who could actually confirm the manifest.

The boy stopped crying once the agent showed him the correct seat. Percy watched him walk away holding his mother's hand. Small errors could become enormous when the people carrying the paperwork didn't know how to correct them.

Annabeth sat beside Percy and opened her architecture book. The page showed an old Roman aqueduct, all careful arches and stone joints.

"Why are you looking at that?" he asked.

"Because the Romans had to keep water moving even when things went wrong."

"Don't make this another engineering lesson."

"Then stop giving me perfect examples."

Percy laughed and leaned back in the chair. The flight was finally going to happen, not because everybody had been certain it would, but because the people involved had checked the facts and fixed the error.

Flying in late May was different than quest-flying.

No monsters. No leap from exploding monuments. Just three kids in coach with misted boarding passes, Thalia white-knuckled on the armrests, Annabeth with a Roman architecture book open on her knees and a notebook filling fast, Percy with the window seat and his forehead pressed to the cold plastic, watching the country unroll.

"You're humming," Thalia accused halfway over Colorado.

"I'm nervous humming," Percy said. "It's a coping strategy."

"Hum quieter."

Annabeth reached across Thalia without looking up from her book and found Percy's hand and squeezed. Thalia pretended not to notice and then, after a minute, put her hand over both of theirs and squeezed once, hard, sisterly.

"You'll be fine," she said gruffly. "Jason's good. If anyone gives you trouble, I'll zap them."

"You can't zap Romans on a diplomatic trip," Annabeth said.

"I can zap them a little," Thalia said. "Festive zapping."

They landed at dusk. The air smelled like eucalyptus and rain. A black SUV with SPQR on the plate waited at the curb, engine running, and a tall girl with long dark hair in a single braid and a purple cloak stepped out.

Reyna.

Even Percy, who remembered Reyna from another life and had heard stories about her here, stood straighter. She had that effect. Praetor Reyna, daughter of Bellona, gold and silver dogs at her heels that turned out to be living metal automatons named Aurum and Argentum. She surveyed them with dark eyes, and then her stern mouth broke into a real smile.

"Thalia Grace," she said. "You look like him when you scowl."

Thalia laughed, startled wet. "You must be Reyna."

Reyna clasped her forearm, Roman-style, and then, breaking protocol entirely, hugged her. "Welcome to California. Jason has not stopped pacing the Pomerian Line since noon."

Another car door slammed. A stocky Asian boy with a baby face and a centurion's crest tumbled out, followed by a girl with cinnamon skin and curly brown hair and a gold stripe in her cavalry jacket, her eyes warm and curious.

"Frank Zhang!" the boy said, shaking Percy's hand with both hands. "Son of Mars — well, Ares to you — praetor — well, acting — welcome! I brought snacks for the drive because Hazel said Greeks get carsick on the hill road — "

"I said *you* get carsick on the hill road, Frank," Hazel Levesque said, laughing, and hugged Annabeth like they had been writing for seasons, which they had. "Annabeth! Your letters about the Labyrinth drainage — we used your siphon sketch for the bathhouse. Come see!"

She turned to Percy and smiled. "Percy Jackson. Jason talks about you like you're a riptide with legs. Thank you for bringing our Jason his sister."

Percy felt his ears go hot. "Thanks for having us. I'll try not to break anything Roman."

"Oh, you'll break something," Reyna said dryly. "Everyone does. We have forms for it."

The dogs sniffed Percy's shoes and approved. Frank loaded their bags like he was proving something, lifting all three at once. Thalia raised an eyebrow.

"Strong," she said approvingly.

Frank turned red to his ears. Hazel beamed with pride.

The drive through the hills was dark and winding. Annabeth sat up front with a map on her knees, asking Reyna rapid-fire questions about the Little Tiber, the wards, the city layout, and Reyna answered with amused precision. In the back, Thalia stared out the window, her knee bouncing. Percy nudged her.

"Hey," he said quietly, so only she heard. "He's going to cry when he sees you."

"I'll cry harder and deny it," Thalia muttered.

"Good plan," Percy said. "I'll back you up. As luggage."

She bumped his shoulder with hers, hard, affectionate. Sisters and Seaweed Brain. Steady.

They rounded the last bend, and Camp Jupiter spilled below them in spring feast lights — the city on its hill, the temple on its ridge, the Field of Mars dark, the barracks in neat squares, hundreds of little welcome candles flickering in windows like stars fallen down to be counted.

Thalia went very still.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, gods."

---

The welcome at the gate was Roman, which meant it was loud, organized, and three minutes early.

Two full cohorts in rain-dark armor stood in formation on either side of the road, breath fogging, standards planted. Torches lined the Pomerian Line. Someone had hung evergreen and red ribbons on the gate. A banner read in Latin and, underneath in slightly crooked Greek, WELCOME GREEKS — PLEASE DO NOT FEED THE FAUN.

"The faun added that last part," Frank whispered to Percy as they got out. "We have a faun problem."

Percy grinned. "We have a pigeon problem. And a harpy problem. I feel at home."

Reyna snapped something in Latin. The cohorts snapped to attention. Percy tried to stand at attention and immediately locked his knees and swayed. Annabeth elbowed him subtly.

"Relax your knees, Seaweed Brain," she murmured. "Breathe."

"Romans make breathing a drill," Percy muttered back, but he unclenched.

Then the ranks parted, and Jason Grace walked through.

He was taller than Percy remembered from Iris-messages. Blonde, scarred lip, blue eyes, a legion cloak over armor, a thin gold crown of feast tinsel crooked on his head that someone had clearly put there as a joke and he had forgotten to remove. He saw Thalia and stopped dead.

For a second nobody moved. The torches crackled. Snow that wasn't snow — thin California rain glittering like ash — fell through the light.

"Thals?" Jason said, very quietly, like he was two again.

Thalia made a sound that was half laugh, half sob and crossed the space in four strides and caught her little brother in a hug that lifted him off his feet despite the armor. His arms locked around her. His face buried in her shoulder. The tinsel crown fell off and rolled.

"Hey, baby brother," Thalia whispered, fierce and shaking. "Gods, look at you. Look at you. You're huge. You're — you're Roman and huge."

"You came," Jason said into her parka. "You actually came. I kept thinking Juno would — something would — "

"Nothing keeps me from my brother on spring break," Thalia said. "Not gods. Not patrols. Not airlines."

Around them, the cohorts pretended very hard to be at attention and not crying. Reyna blinked rapidly and stared at the sky. Frank sniffled audibly. Hazel was openly crying and giving Annabeth a thumbs-up through it. Percy felt his own throat close. Annabeth slipped her hand into his and held tight, Thalia's sister and Percy's sweetheart.

Jason pulled back just enough to look at Thalia's face, his hands on her shoulders like he needed to prove she was solid. "You look like Mom," he said. "When you scowl."

"I'll take that," Thalia said, wiping her eyes angrily. "You look like Dad. Unfortunately."

"Unfortunately handsome?" Jason said, and Thalia laughed wetly and shoved him.

Then Jason turned, and his face changed — still shining, but softer. "Annabeth."

Annabeth stepped forward, suddenly shy. She was his sister by blood, but she lacked the childhood memories that made the word easy for him. Months of letters, of battle plans traded across the country, of *tell Thalia I —* and *tell Jason that bridge thing worked —* had given them new ways to know each other. A strategist sister. A Wise Girl who still felt shy beside the brother this history had given her.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi," Jason said, and hugged her carefully, Roman forearm-clasp turning into a real hug when she hugged back hard. "Thank you for keeping her alive to get here."

"She mostly keeps me alive," Annabeth said, muffled in his cloak.

"That tracks," Jason said.

Then he turned to Percy. "Percy Jackson."

"Jason Grace," Percy said. "Sir. Legionary. Uh — "

Jason grinned and pulled him into a hug that smelled like rain and metal polish. "None of that. You're family by Thalia-law, which outranks Roman law during the exchange. Welcome, brother."

Percy, who had been braced for inspection, melted. "Thanks. Nice tinsel."

Jason looked down at the fallen crown. Hazel retrieved it and plopped it back on his head. "Lord of Misrule made me wear it," he explained solemnly to Percy. "It's feast week. Everything's backwards. Tomorrow I serve the Fifth Cohort breakfast in an apron."

"I would pay to see that," Thalia said.

"You will see that," Reyna said. "For free. Attendance mandatory."

The cohorts cheered. Someone banged a shield. Aurum barked. The banner flapped. Annabeth squeezed Percy's hand again before letting go to stand properly for the welcome, but her fingers lingered.

Percy thought: *Okay. Maybe Romans aren't so scary.*

Then Reyna shouted, "Cohorts! Dismiss to feast!" and two hundred Roman demigods moved as one, and Percy jumped a foot in the air, and Annabeth and Thalia both laughed at him, and he thought: *Okay. A little scary.*

---

The spring welcome feast was in the mess hall, which was not like the Greek pavilion at all.

No separate tables for gods. Long Roman tables by cohort. Long benches. Everyone reclining a little, as tradition demanded, which Percy failed at immediately and slid half off his bench until Frank caught him by the hood.

"Romans recline at like a fifteen-degree angle," Frank advised, demonstrating. "Greeks sprawl. It's a whole thing."

"I can do fifteen degrees," Percy said, concentrating. He slid again. Annabeth, reclining perfectly with a scroll in one hand, reached over without looking and anchored his sleeve.

"Stay," she said.

"Woof," Thalia said, and Hazel choked on her juice.

The food was Roman comfort: roast pork, honeyed carrots, fresh bread, olives, cheese, dates stuffed with nuts. Sweets shaped like little gods. Hazel kept pushing dishes toward Annabeth. "Try this — this is Frank's mom's recipe, the legion adopted it — "

Frank, in an apron over his armor as promised for the welcome-night role-reversal supper, was serving the Fifth with exaggerated formality while the Fifth heckled him lovingly. "More bread, slave Frank!" "Yes, masters!" Frank said, bowing. "May I also refill your water, masters?" He winked at Percy. "Tomorrow Jason does this. Legionary-servant. It's tradition."

Reyna, released from praetor dignity for the night by a paper crown that declared her QUEEN OF MISRULE, sat cross-legged on the table — on the table — braiding Hazel's hair and arguing with Thalia about the best way to kill a dracaena.

"Greek way: spear through the throat, twist," Thalia said.

"Roman way: shield wall, pila, advance," Reyna said.

"Athenian way: don't get close enough to need either," Annabeth said. "Trap, drop net, architecture."

Reyna pointed at her. "I like her."

"I like her first," Thalia and Hazel said together, and everyone laughed.

Percy watched it all with his chin in his hand, warm down to his toes. This was what Camp felt like at its best — loud tables, sisters teasing, brothers showing off — just in Latin.

After dinner came the candles. Every Roman lit a small beeswax candle for return and welcome, passing flame down the tables until the hall glowed. Hazel lit Annabeth's, then Percy's, her hands steady.

"For return," she said softly. The old prayer. "For light in dark."

"For return," they repeated.

Then came the sigillaria — little clay dolls, joke gifts. Frank gave Percy a tiny clay trireme that sank in his water cup on purpose. Percy laughed so hard he snorted. Annabeth gave Hazel a tiny clay library with opening doors, and Hazel gasped and opened and closed them six times. Thalia gave Jason a tiny clay pine tree with a silver lightning bolt stuck in it, and Jason held it like it was gold.

"By my bunk," he said thickly. "So I remember."

Thalia ruffled his hair, sisterly, fierce. "You better."

A Fifth Cohort kid stood and declared Percy an honorary terrible Roman. "He stood at attention like a Greek! He reclined like a falling column!"

"Hail Percy, Falling Column!" the hall shouted, banging cups.

Percy stood and bowed, nearly falling off his bench again. "I accept this honor with honor!"

Annabeth hid her face laughing. Under the table, where no centurion could issue a demerit, she found his hand and squeezed. He squeezed back. Warm, hidden, steady.

The night ended with the upside-down toast. Service volunteers were thanked first, then guests, then hosts. Nobody was made to play a servant in a festival about hospitality. Jason, still in tinsel, raised his cup last of all.

"To guests!" he said. "To sisters — blood and chosen! To Greeks who stand funny and fight bravely! To spring break — when everything flips and we remember we're family underneath!"

"Family!" the hall roared.

Thalia's eyes shone. Annabeth leaned her head briefly against Percy's shoulder. Percy's chest felt too full for armor.

---

The next morning was Roman discipline, and Percy died a little.

Reveille before dawn. Cold air. Fog off the Field of Mars. The Fifth Cohort jogging in perfect step, chanting in Latin, while Percy, guesting with them as an honor, jogged half a step behind and chanted half a beat late.

"Left! Left! Left-right-left!" Frank called, jogging beside him encouragingly.

"I am lefting!" Percy panted. "Why is left so hard at dawn?"

"You fight fine," Frank said. "I saw you yesterday with a practice gladius. Your form is — very Greek."

"Is that Roman for bad?"

"It's Roman for enthusiastic," Frank said kindly.

After the run came drill. Shield wall. Percy, used to fighting loose and fast with Riptide and water and instinct, kept breaking formation to help the kid next to him who stumbled. The centurion, a stern girl named Leila, stopped the line.

"Jackson! Why did you break rank?"

"He was going to fall," Percy said.

"And now the wall has a hole and we all die," Leila said.

Percy blinked. "Oh."

Annabeth, observing from the sidelines with a wax tablet, mouthed *stay in the wall.*

Next time the kid wobbled, Percy stayed in the wall and stuck his shield out six inches to prop him without stepping. Leila nodded curtly. Frank gave him a thumbs-up. Percy felt absurdly proud.

After drill came baths — which were amazing — and then war games briefing, which was less amazing because it was entirely in military Latin abbreviations.

Annabeth thrived. She sat with Hazel and the engineers over a sand table, sleeves rolled, hair pinned, moving little markers and arguing happily about culverts and choke points. "If you angle the ditch *here,* you force them to the bridge — " "But the Little Tiber is still high after winter rain — " "So we use it! Percy can — oh. No water magic in war games. Right. Sorry."

Percy, sitting beside her as her designated muscle, said, "I can still carry heavy things. That's allowed, right?"

"Carrying heavy things is always allowed," Hazel said solemnly. "It's the most Roman power."

The war game was Greeks vs Romans, of course. Three Greeks against a cohort, to be fair — fair to the Romans, Leila said, which made Thalia grin wolfishly.

"You want Greek chaos?" Thalia said, cracking her knuckles. Electricity snapped. "I'll give you Greek chaos."

The game was Capture the Eagle: a gold eagle standard hidden in the Field of Mars ruins, first team to bring it to their base wins. Romans had numbers, shields, order. Greeks had Thalia being a thunderstorm, Annabeth being three steps ahead, and Percy being Percy.

What followed entered Camp Jupiter legend as the Spring-Break Skirmish.

Thalia went high, using the aqueduct as a lightning rod, drawing the Roman skirmishers into a chase that left their flank open. Annabeth went low, with Hazel — who had defected to the Greeks for the day under the exchange’s role-reversal rule — navigating the old tunnels Hazel knew from Pluto dreams, popping up behind the Roman line to move markers and leave confusing notes in Latin: *THE EAGLE IS ELSEWHERE. — A.*

Percy? Percy was the distraction.

He ran straight at the shield wall with a practice spear and a wild grin, shouting in terrible Latin, "VENI! VIDI! — UH — CURRI!" *I came! I saw! I ran!*

The Romans broke formation laughing. Frank, fighting for the Romans but clearly delighted, shouted, "That's not even — Percy, *currere* doesn't — "

Percy vaulted the front rank with a move Clarisse would have killed to see, grabbed the decoy eagle Hazel had planted — a painted chicken — and held it aloft. "I HAVE THE EAGLE! BEHOLD! CLUCK!"

Half the cohort chased him. While they chased him through the mud, slipping and yelling, Annabeth and Hazel walked calmly into the real eagle nest from the dry cistern side and carried it out under a cloak.

When Percy, muddy to the eyebrows, was finally tackled by six Romans including Frank, he heard the horn blow for the Greeks.

The field went silent, then erupted.

"GREEKS WIN! GREEKS WIN!"

Thalia slid down the aqueduct and landed in a crouch, arms raised. Annabeth emerged with the eagle, Hazel beside her, both flushed triumphant. Percy sat up in the mud, spat mud, and cheered.

Jason, refereeing in his apron — legionary-servant for the day, serving hot cider on the sidelines — ran onto the field and hugged his sister and then Annabeth and then Percy, mud and all, not caring about his apron.

"That chicken was inspired," he gasped. "Where did you get a chicken?"

"Spring break provides," Hazel said piously.

Reyna surveyed the muddy, laughing cohorts and shook her head, but she was smiling. "Note for the record," she told her scribe. "Greeks: undisciplined. Unorthodox. Undefeated today."

Leila helped Percy up and clasped his forearm. "Falling Column," she said respectfully. "You fight like a river. Stay in the wall next time."

"I'll try," Percy said earnestly. "Happy spring break."

She stared, then laughed despite herself. "Happy spring break."

That night there were more games — dice, riddles, a chariot race with very slow donkeys where Thalia and Reyna tied for first by cheating in opposite directions and then accused each other gleefully. Frank beat Percy at dice by being impossibly lucky and then felt so guilty he gave Percy his winnings. Hazel and Annabeth spent an hour in the temple of Minerva with their heads together, sketching a joint Greek-Roman library annex on a napkin, and would not show anyone until it was perfect.

Percy watched Annabeth across the fire, her face lit gold, her laugh bright when Hazel whispered something. She caught him looking and smiled, soft and steady, and mouthed *hi.*

He mouthed *hi* back like an idiot, and Thalia, sitting between them, groaned. "You two are disgusting. Adorably disgusting. Stop."

"We're not doing anything," Percy protested.

"You're holding hands with your eyes," Thalia said. "It's worse."

Hazel sighed happily. "It's cute."

---

The morning before the city's holiday, Hazel took Annabeth to inspect the old library annex.

Percy went with them because the Roman engineers had agreed to let visitors see the foundations, and because he had made the mistake of saying he enjoyed Annabeth's descriptions of drainage systems. Frank was also there, carrying a measuring tape and a little bag of tools borrowed from the Fifth Cohort.

The building looked solid from the outside. Inside, one corner of the floor had settled. The shelves were upright, but a thin crack ran from the nearest window to the base of a column.

"That's what I wanted you to see," Hazel told Annabeth. "The librarians say it's been like that for years. The engineers haven't cleared any extension while the corner is moving."

Annabeth crouched beside the crack. She didn't touch it.

"Do we have a record of the movement?"

"Some," Frank said. "There are marks on the wall. The previous survey was last winter."

Percy looked toward the far end of the room. The shelves held rolled maps and old histories, some with leather covers that had begun to curl in the damp air. The idea of a shared Greek-Roman archive had excited Annabeth since they reached camp. Now she was staring at a damaged column instead of a grand doorway.

"Can you just strengthen it?" Percy asked.

"Maybe," Annabeth replied. "But not before we know what caused it."

Hazel showed them the drainage channel beyond the wall. It was narrow and partially filled with silt. Recent rains had left puddles beside the building. Annabeth followed the channel to an old stone culvert, then asked Frank where it discharged.

He checked the plan. "Toward the lower gardens."

"Does it still?"

"We don't know."

They asked the supervising engineer to accompany them to the garden wall. She was not pleased to find three teenagers proposing changes to a historical building. Annabeth explained what they had observed and asked whether there was an approved way to inspect the culvert without digging up the garden.

The engineer listened and showed them an older survey. It confirmed that the discharge line had been narrowed when a new footpath was built ten years earlier. Nobody had measured how that affected the foundation during heavy rain.

"It may be relevant," the engineer said. "It may not. We can schedule a proper inspection."

Annabeth looked disappointed. "So we can't start planning the annex?"

"You can draw a concept," the engineer replied. "You cannot pretend it has an approved foundation. If you want a library people can use safely, start by finding out whether the old one will remain standing."

Percy smiled. It sounded exactly like a lecture Annabeth would give someone else.

Hazel opened her notebook. She had been sketching the building for months, mostly from memory. Now she added a note beside the affected wall: *Foundation movement, unverified cause. Engineer's survey required.*

Annabeth wrote the same line.

"That's the first thing we agree on," Hazel said.

"The first thing we can actually prove," Annabeth corrected.

They went back inside to consider how a library might serve both camps without requiring anyone to surrender custody of precious records. Hazel explained that some Roman histories were held by families and could not simply be shipped away. Annabeth said the Greek archives had similar private letters and donated stories that were not available for general display.

"Then maybe our shared annex doesn't need every original," Hazel suggested. "We could have copies people approve for study."

"Yes. And a list of what has been copied, with the permission attached."

Percy thought of Elena's opt-in campfire sheet, which she hadn't submitted until she knew the speakers had agreed. He told them about it without repeating the private statements, because it was the procedure, not the personal stories, that seemed relevant.

Hazel nodded. "That would help. We have veterans who don't want their family histories copied for children to read."

"Then we ask," Annabeth said.

They sat at a long table with the supervising engineer and drew a preliminary plan. It had two entrances and clear pathways. The lower shelves would be reachable without climbing. A reading room would contain approved copies, while the protected storage rooms would remain under their respective custodians' control. The plan left a blank space where the foundation investigation needed to happen.

Percy watched Annabeth hesitate over the blank rectangle. It would have been easy for her to fill it with a beautiful courtyard.

Instead she wrote *pending survey*.

Hazel leaned over. "I like that."

"It's not very impressive."

"It's honest."

Frank looked at the drawings and offered to carry them to the engineer's office for registration. Annabeth wanted to bring them back to camp, but Hazel asked whether the Roman council should keep a copy. They agreed to make two marked versions: one for the Roman project file, one for Annabeth's personal sketchbook. Neither was an approved building permit.

"Maybe we'll build it someday," Hazel said.

"Maybe," Annabeth replied. "But not if we rush it."

Percy smiled as they left. A few months earlier, he would have expected the big moment of the visit to be a battle or a prophecy. Instead it was a blank place on a drawing that both girls had decided not to fill with a guess.

The middle day of the exchange belonged to the city.

No drills. No war games. By arrangement for the exchange, the courts and schools closed for the day, and the legion stood down except for a token guard who wore paper crowns over their helmets and looked relieved about it. Masters served breakfast to servants. Praetors swept the principia steps while probatios supervised with clipboards and entirely too much enjoyment.

Percy woke to find Jason Grace, a young legionary of the Twelfth Legion Fulminata, son of Jupiter, outside the guest barracks in a flour-dusted apron holding a tray.

"Morning, Greeks," Jason said solemnly. "I am your slave for the hour. I bring toast."

Thalia stared. "You burned it."

"It's festive toast," Jason said. "The black parts are Saturn's shadow."

Percy took a piece and ate it to be polite and then to be kind ate another. "Excellent shadow," he said thickly. "Very crunchy."

Annabeth, already dressed with her notebook out, photographed the apron for Clarisse. "Proof," she said. "For blackmail and history."

"Ella will want a copy," Percy said. "Junior Archivist demands completeness."

Jason sat on the steps with them while they ate, his long legs stretched out, his slave-apron crooked. Without armor he looked younger, younger than both his sisters, which Thalia sometimes forgot beneath the armor. She kept staring at his hands — big, scarred, Roman hands — and remembering toddler hands offering mud-pies.

"You good?" she asked gruffly, bumping his knee.

"Better now," he said simply, and she nodded and did not cry and he pretended not to notice her eyes.

After breakfast Reyna led them down into New Rome proper for the spring feast market, and Percy understood at once why Annabeth had been vibrating with excitement for weeks.

It was not like Camp Half-Blood's craft stalls. It was a real city street, lined with shops and temples and houses with red tile roofs, all hung with evergreen and gold ribbons. Stalls sold candles and honey cakes and little clay sigillaria and silver jewelry and hot spiced cider. Kids ran underfoot wearing paper crowns. A faun tried to sell Percy a "genuine Roman shield, only slightly haunted" and Reyna chased him off with a look that could have drilled concrete.

Annabeth moved through it like she was memorizing it to rebuild from memory, which she was. Hazel walked beside her, pointing out the aqueduct arches, the underfloor heating vents, the inscriptions over the bathhouse door. They spoke the same language — not Greek, not Latin, but *builder* — and kept stopping to crouch over drains and argue lovingly about slope gradients while Frank carried their purchases and looked proud.

"See that temple?" Hazel said, pointing up the hill where white columns caught the spring sun. "That's Neptune. Romans don't — well. You know. He's not the most popular god here."

Percy looked up at his father's temple, small and neat and a little lonely on the ridge. "Yeah," he said quietly. "I know that feeling."

Hazel slipped her hand into his elbow, sisterly and sure. "Jason made sure they cleaned it for the exchange. He left an offering for you yesterday. Salt and sea glass. He said — " she hesitated. "He said Poseidon’s son deserved to be welcomed by Neptune too."

Percy’s throat went tight. "Tell him thanks," he managed.

"I will," Hazel said softly. "Romans get family wrong sometimes. But Jason gets it right."

At the bookseller’s stall Thalia bought Jason a terrible joke scroll titled *One Hundred Uses for Lightning (Mostly Illegal)* and bought Annabeth a silver hairpin shaped like an owl. Annabeth tried to pay her back and Thalia flattened her with a look.

"Older sister privilege," Thalia said. "Shut up and sparkle."

Annabeth wore it immediately.

Percy bought nothing until he found a stall selling sea-glass bracelets, blue and green, made by a daughter of Venus who smiled at his indecision. He picked two — one with a tiny trident charm for Hazel, who had welcomed him, and one plain blue cord for Annabeth, because blue was theirs. He hid the cord in his pocket for later, his ears hot.

Reyna watched him with amusement. "Romans give sigillaria openly," she said. "No need to hide, Falling Column."

"It's not gift time yet — I mean, it's private," Percy mumbled. "Greek custom. Secret gift panic."

Reyna’s mouth twitched. "We have that custom too. We call it being thirteen."

In the afternoon they visited the baths, and Percy died a second, steamier death.

The Roman baths were enormous, marble and mosaic, hot pools and cold plunges and a strict system of scrapers and benches and *do not splash* that Percy violated within ninety seconds by cannonballing into the tepidarium when he thought no one was looking. Everyone was looking.

"Jackson!" Leila shouted from the caldarium, paper crown askew. "No Greek diving!"

"Sorry!" Percy shouted, surfacing, hair plastered. "Instinct! Water!"

Frank hauled him out by the back of his towel and lectured him fondly on bathhouse etiquette while turning the same alarming shade of red as the hot room. Jason, wrapped in a towel and still wearing his slave-apron over it for feast week, laughed so hard he had to sit down.

Annabeth and Hazel and Thalia and Reyna took the women's side and emerged an hour later pink-cheeked and loose-haired and conspiring. Thalia had Roman braids. Annabeth had a new freckle of ink on her nose from sketching in steam. Hazel had convinced the attendant to let them stamp a little SPQR into a wax tablet as a souvenir.

"Did you behave?" Annabeth asked Percy, eyeing his damp hair.

"He cannonballed," Frank reported.

"He apologized in Latin," Jason said. "Mostly correct Latin. We’re counting it as growth."

Thalia threw a towel at Percy’s head. "Luggage," she said fondly. "My beautiful idiot luggage."

That evening the Lord of Misrule — a tiny probatio with a huge voice — declared that Greeks must teach Romans a game and Romans must teach Greeks a game. The result was chaos in two languages.

Romans taught *latrunculi,* a brutal board game of capture and blockade. Annabeth learned it in four minutes and then beat two centurions in a row while explaining her strategy aloud, which made it worse. "She narrates her victories," Reyna said admiringly. "Zeus's daughter, with an architect's eye."

Greeks taught capture-the-flag with bedsheets for flags and no armor. Thalia’s team won by tying Jason to a tree — gently, with feast ribbon — and leaving him as a decoy while Percy swam the Little Tiber with the flag in his teeth. He emerged freezing and triumphant and was wrapped in six towels by Hazel and Frank while Annabeth checked his hands for numbness with her brows knit.

"You’re blue," she scolded, rubbing his fingers between hers. "You’re literally Blue Percy."

"Worth it," Percy chattered. "Did you see Leila’s face?"

"I saw," Annabeth said, and tucked his cold hands under her cloak to warm them, practical and tender all at once, while Thalia pretended to gag and Hazel pretended not to see and Reyna made a note that read *Greek warming tactics: effective, unsanctioned.*

Later, by the barracks fire, Jason and Thalia sat a little apart with the red truck between them, talking low in the way only siblings separated by gods can talk — half memories, half updates, Beryl and Zeus and Artemis and Juno and all the sharp edges carefully set down for one week. Annabeth leaned against Percy’s shoulder, half-asleep, her owl hairpin glinting, and Percy kept very still so as not to wake her.

Frank spread a blanket over both of them without a word, Roman caretaking at its finest, and Reyna banked the fire, and the night settled soft as snow that never quite fell.

---

On the final night came the fireworks.

The spring exchange ended with lights for return — Roman fireworkers on the Field of Mars, sanctioned by the legion engineers, with Greek fire contributions from the Hephaestus cabin via Iris-message that Chiron pretended not to know about. The whole city gathered on blankets, cohorts mixed, ranks relaxed. Masters and servants sat together. Praetors and probatios shared popcorn.

Jason saved a blanket on the hill for his family. Just his. Thalia on his left, Annabeth on his right, Percy next to Annabeth. Reyna and Hazel and Frank sprawled in front with the dogs, passing a thermos.

"No drill tomorrow," Jason said contentedly, his head leaning briefly against Thalia's shoulder like he was a toddler again and she was his safe big sister and the world was safe. "Sleep in. Real spring-break miracle."

"Don’t get used to it, toast chief," Reyna said. "You still owe the Fifth breakfast."

"I make excellent toast," Jason said with dignity.

"You burned water yesterday," Hazel said.

"It was festive water," Jason said.

Thalia laughed and pulled her cloak tighter around both of them. She had been stern all week, scout-steady, lightning-ready. Now, with her brother warm against her side and the sky about to light up, her face was young and open. Percy had never seen her look so unguarded, except with Annabeth.

"You okay?" Annabeth asked her quietly, sister to sister.

Thalia nodded, eyes bright. "Yeah. I — I thought I'd lost him forever, you know? When Juno took him. I searched. Artemis helped me search. Nothing. And then he's — he's *here.* He's good. He's got people." Her voice wobbled. "He's got you two now too."

"He's always had you," Annabeth said firmly. "Even when you couldn't find him. You kept his toothbrush or whatever it was — "

"His little red truck," Thalia whispered. "I kept it in my pack for years. Through every patrol. I — I gave it to him yesterday. He cried. I didn't."

"You cried," Jason said muffled into her shoulder.

"I sweated through my eyes," Thalia corrected. "Scouts don't cry."

Annabeth leaned her head against Thalia's other shoulder for a second. Sister-stack. Percy smiled at them, his chest aching in the good way.

Then the first firework went up — a gold chrysanthemum that bloomed and hung — and everyone gasped, and the sky opened.

Red and green and blue, silver rain, golden willows. A trident that made Percy whoop. An owl that made Annabeth grab Hazel's arm. A lightning bolt that made Thalia throw her head back laughing. A wolf and a horse racing across the sky for the legion.

Percy felt Annabeth's hand find his on the blanket between them. Not eye-holding. Real holding. Her fingers cold, his warm. Simple. Steady.

He laced his fingers through hers and held on, his thumb tracing once across her knuckles. She leaned just enough that their shoulders touched. Neither looked away from the sky, but both smiled.

Thalia glanced over, saw, and said nothing. She just reached across Jason — who was pretending to be asleep to give them privacy and failing — and squeezed Annabeth's other hand once, sisterly blessing.

Hazel turned and saw and beamed. Frank gave Percy a thumbs-up so enthusiastic he spilled popcorn. Reyna nodded once, solemn praetor approval of hand-holding during sanctioned fireworks.

The finale was white light, so bright it washed the hillside, so loud Percy felt it in his ribs like surf. Gold sparks drifted down like snow. The cohorts cheered. The dogs barked. Jason threw an arm around both his sisters — daughters of Zeus and Beryl — and shouted with joy.

When the smoke cleared, the stars were out, hard and bright above the temple hill.

"For return," Hazel whispered, holding up her candle against the dark.

"For return," they all whispered back.

---

Departure was at dawn, Roman-early, which Percy protested and then slept through until Annabeth shook him awake with cocoa.

The gate was quieter without cohorts in formation, just friends in cloaks with red eyes. Frank had packed them so many snacks Grover would weep. Hazel pressed the napkin-library sketch into Annabeth's hands, now inked proper.

"Joint design," Hazel said. "Greek light, Roman arches. We'll build it someday. Both camps."

"We will," Annabeth promised fiercely, hugging her hard. "Write me about the cisterns."

Reyna gave Thalia a small iron wolf. "For your patrols. So you remember the West."

Thalia gave Reyna a silver arrowhead. "For the legion. So you remember the wild."

They clasped forearms, warrior to warrior, sister to sister.

Jason held Thalia longest. No words for a minute, just forehead to forehead like they did as kids, Thalia's hands tight on his shoulders.

"Write," she ordered. "Every month. Or I'll storm Olympus and then California."

"Every week," Jason promised. "Even if it's just *still alive, ate toast.*"

"Better be good toast," Thalia said, voice thick.

He turned to Annabeth and hugged her. "Sister," he said simply.

"Brother," she said back, smiling through tears.

Then Percy. Jason hugged him hard enough to crack his spine. "Bridge," he said. "Falling Column. Take care of them."

"I will," Percy said. "Take care of yours. And — thanks for having a Greek who can't march."

"You're a terrible Roman," Jason said fondly. "You're an excellent guest. There's a difference. Come back at summer. We'll teach you to recline."

" Fifteen degrees!" Percy said triumphantly.

"Ten," Frank corrected, hugging him. "You're improving."

Hazel hugged Percy last, standing on tiptoes. "Thank you for the chicken," she whispered. "Best war game ever."

The SUV pulled away. Thalia stared out the back window until Camp Jupiter vanished in the fog, her hand pressed to the glass. Annabeth leaned against her on one side, Percy on the other. No one spoke.

Finally Thalia exhaled, shaky, and pulled something from her pocket — the tiny clay pine tree Jason had given back to her that morning, with a note tucked underneath: *For my big sister. For return. — J.*

She held it tight, and Annabeth put her arm around her, and Percy put his arm around both of them as best he could from the middle seat, beloved luggage holding the Grace sisters together.

"Hey," he said softly. "Happy spring break."

Thalia laughed wetly and ruffled his hair. Annabeth took his hand again, open now, no need to hide, and held it all the way down the hill.

Above California, the spring sun broke through, thin and gold as candleflame. For return. For light in dark. For family — blood, chosen, Greek, Roman — holding on across every line on every map Annabeth could ever draw.

And for three kids heading home together, steady and sure, that was enough. That was everything.
