## Chapter 82: Letters (May)

May arrived in Atlantis sideways.

Percy Jackson noticed it first in the light. The shafts through the dome leaned longer in the mornings. Fresh green fronds unfurled in the kelp forests outside the training terraces. The current was still cool when he swam to school.

He was thirteen. For this short spring study block, he had his own desk in a dormitory of the Atlantis Academy, a low basalt table with a shell lamp and a drawer that stuck. He had a schedule etched on a wax tablet. He had a roommate named Nerit who collected current-charts and snored in whistles.

He missed Camp so hard it felt like a pulled muscle.

On the third morning of May, he sat at that basalt desk before first bell with blank kelp paper and cuttlefish ink. Nerit was still asleep. Through the membrane window Percy could hear horns for the tide change and younger kids racing for the refectory.

He dipped his pen.

The first thing he wrote was *Dear Mom*, then he stopped.

He had meant to write to camp. To Annabeth and his sisters and Grover, perhaps all together, because that seemed easier than choosing which person he missed most. His hand had gone to his mother before he could decide where the letter belonged.

Amphitrite wasn't the mother he remembered making blue pancakes in a Manhattan kitchen. In the changed history, she was the person who raised him in Atlantis after Sally died, and she had behaved like a mother every time Percy had asked her for help since he came to camp. He had begun to call her Mom without having access to the years that made the word ordinary to everyone else.

Now he was back in the palace's world, staying in a student dormitory because the May study block required it. She was only a few corridors away and he was writing as though the sea separated them.

Nerit rolled over in the next bunk. "Are you arguing with your paper?"

"Not yet."

"You have been looking at it for a long time."

"I'm thinking."

"That is what people say when they are arguing with paper."

Percy turned the page around. "I was going to write to Annabeth."

"Then why does it say Dear Mom?"

"That's the problem."

Nerit considered this and sat up. "You can write two letters."

Percy stared at him.

"That's a remarkably annoying solution."

"It's an excellent solution. You can use two sheets, and each person gets their own words."

Percy laughed despite himself. He set aside the sheet with the unfinished greeting and started again. This time he wrote *Dear Camp*, then immediately crossed it out because it sounded like a letter to a building.

Nerit watched with obvious fascination. "Do all surface people write like this?"

"Only the very advanced ones."

At breakfast Percy took the two sheets to the dining hall. Tyson was already there with a plate of kelp toast and an engineering diagram balanced against his cup. His undersea forge shift would start in an hour, and he was using the last part of the morning to work on the troublesome latch of the little box he'd left in Cabin Three.

"Percy," he said. "The spare hinge at the workshop is the wrong size."

"That's tragic."

"It is." Tyson showed him two drawings. "This one works for a larger case. Your case is small."

"Then you can fix it when you visit."

"Yes. You will bring it?"

"If Chiron approves the trip and you ask me in advance."

Tyson grinned. He had become used to Percy insisting that plans be checked, even when the person making them was one of his favorite people.

Percy put the unfinished mother letter beside his breakfast. Tyson read only the greeting because Percy held up the page and asked him what he thought.

"Should I give it to her?" Percy asked.

"Why wouldn't you?"

"Because she's right here. We could talk."

"Then talk. Letters are for things you want people to keep."

Percy looked at the blank space below the two words. He hadn't thought about that. Sometimes a conversation was easier because the other person could ask questions and he didn't have to guess how his words would sound. Sometimes writing meant he could say what he actually meant without his face giving him away.

"You've written to her?" Percy asked.

"Yes."

"What do you say?"

"She asks about the forge. I tell her about the forge. Sometimes I ask whether she is lonely."

Percy looked up. "Is she?"

"Sometimes," Tyson said. "She says sometimes she misses all of us in the same room. She doesn't make us leave work and come home just because she misses us."

Percy thought of the beach gifts she'd sent in April, each one chosen for somebody she knew. The letter he'd been meaning to write all spring was probably not going to tell her anything she didn't already suspect. He still wanted her to have it.

"I'll tell her about camp," he said.

"Good."

"And that I like the palace, even when I don't understand everything."

"Very good."

Tyson returned to his drawing. Percy finished breakfast and went to the small courtyard beside the dormitory, where the current pressed softly against a glass wall. He sat down and wrote a letter to Amphitrite about arriving safely, the friends he had missed at camp, and how strange it was to sleep in a school bed when his family was just across the palace grounds.

He did not disclose the erased timeline or claim to remember afternoons in the nursery wing that belonged to the childhood other people remembered for him. He wrote about last week's visit to the forge, when she had brought food after a late shift and asked whether he wanted company.

At the bottom he added: *Thank you for asking rather than assuming.*

He read the line twice, folded the sheet and wrote her name on the outside.

Only then did he return to the second page, the one for his friends.

He did not start with anything important. He started with the lamp.

---

Dear Camp,

It is spring here. The light leans farther into the garden. Does that make sense? It leans. The kelp outside Terrace Two is growing green tips like the trees at Half-Blood Hill do. I thought Atlantis would not do spring. It does its own version.

School is — okay. History of the Sea with Lady Amphitrite is my best class because she lets us argue. Today a son of Nereus said mortals invented sailing and I said sails invented mortals getting lost faster and the whole class laughed and she gave me an extra mussel cake. Current Math is my worst. The numbers move. Literally. You have to hold them still with your mind while you add them. Tyson is better at it. He says you sing to them. I tried singing. The numbers sank.

Tyson sits with me at lunch when his forge shift allows it. He is growing again. He ate four kelp loaves yesterday and told the cook they were practice loaves. She gave him two more.

Tell Chiron the young hippocampi in the east stable still shy from the left. I approach from the right like he taught me and they settle.

Tell Grover the trench lilies bloomed late here. I thought of him. I thought of Juniper too. I hope his searcher assignment is going okay. Tell him I saved him a smooth stone from the school garden. It fits in a palm exactly.

Tell Ella I am keeping a list for her archive. I have twelve new currents, three new songs the dorm sings while cleaning, and one teacher who sneezes like a conch.

Clarisse — you left your wrist wrap in my duffel. It smells like pine and iron. I am keeping it hostage until you write back. Training without you is weird. The Atlantean sparring master says I drop my left shoulder. You always said that too, only louder.

Silena — the refectory tried honey cakes and failed. They used sea honey and it was too salt. I told them yours were better and now three cooks want your recipe. Please send help before I get challenged to a bake duel.

Thalia — the sky here is water. I keep looking up expecting storms and getting fish instead. Send a storm. In a postcard, I mean. Do not actually send one. The dome would leak.

Annabeth — I saved the best paper for you. It has little shell flecks in it. I keep trying to describe the school so you can draw it, but I am bad at straight lines. The dorm is a half-circle. The classroom is a bigger half-circle. Everything is circles. You would hate it and then immediately fix it.

I miss breakfasts. I miss the pavilion and Mrs. O'Leary stealing bacon. I miss capture the flag planning where you move olives around like armies. I miss you all, but I miss you most on purpose.

Write back. All of you. Even you, Clarisse, with your terrible handwriting.

Yours,
Percy

P.S. Annabeth — check the envelope lining.

He folded a second small square of paper into the envelope before he sealed it with wax and a thumbprint. Inside the lining, pressed flat between two strips of oiled kelp, lay a pale seaflower no bigger than his thumbnail, white with a blush-pink center, picked from the deep-water garden behind the dorm where the gardeners let him weed in exchange for extra bread. He had pressed it in his history book for nine days under a coral weight. It smelled faintly of cold water and honey.

He stared at it before he sealed the edge. A flower that meant I thought of you while you were not here.

He shouldered his satchel and ran the letter to the dispatch shells before the bell.

At Camp Half-Blood, the correspondence had a problem before Annabeth even received Percy's first letter.

The dispatch box outside the Big House had filled with letters to people who weren't currently there. Some were addressed to campers away at school, some to satyrs traveling on assignments, and two to relatives who couldn't receive magical post at all. Ella had started sorting them by destination, then discovered several envelopes had no return address or forwarding permission.

Annabeth found her at the library table with the boxes open and a worried expression on her feathered face.

"Too many," Ella said. "Ella knows where most people went. But knowing is not the same as being allowed to send a letter there."

Annabeth sat opposite her. "Who owns the address list?"

"Chiron. Some are private."

"Then we don't copy them into a public forwarding ledger."

Ella's head snapped up. "Yes. That is what Ella said."

Annabeth smiled. "Did someone disagree?"

"Travis offered to make a map with every camper's location. Said he could make the mail faster."

"Of course he did."

"Grover would be on it. Tyson would be on it. Children who don't want their families to know where they live would be on it."

Annabeth felt the significance immediately. A map was useful only if the right person was reading it. She had nearly made the same mistake with the fort behind Cabin Thirteen, trying to build a structure for Nico without checking who would maintain it or what belongings he wanted to keep private. She had learned a similar lesson from Elena's account at the April campfire. Details had owners, even when those details seemed harmless to the person collecting them.

"Can we make a system that doesn't expose where everybody is?" she asked.

Ella looked at the envelopes. "Ella can give each person a private code. The public box has the code. Chiron keeps the address."

"And campers can ask Chiron not to forward something if it isn't safe."

"Yes."

"And no one can infer where a person is from the public box."

Ella considered. "Not unless they recognize the code."

"Then the codes shouldn't be names or cabin numbers."

Ella wrote that down.

They took the proposal to Chiron after breakfast. He read it without smiling, which was how Annabeth knew he was taking it seriously. Then he asked who would check whether an address was current, who would keep returned letters and what would happen if somebody wanted their location removed.

Ella had answers for the first two questions. The third required work.

"If someone asks to remove a forwarding address," Chiron said, "we should stop sending mail there until they give us new instructions. This isn't merely a filing problem."

Annabeth added the rule to the draft. She didn't like that it left some mail waiting. She liked even less the idea of a letter being delivered to the wrong person because somebody cared more about an efficient system than a camper's wishes.

That afternoon an envelope arrived for Milo. The boy was at the stables when Chiron received it; the address on the outside had been copied from an older document. Chiron checked the sender details privately and asked Milo whether he wanted the letter delivered to his cabin or held until he could collect it.

Milo chose to collect it.

Annabeth knew no more than that. She didn't ask what the envelope contained or whether it came from his mortal brother. The boy had been trying to reach his family since he arrived; knowing that made the moment important, but it didn't make the letter hers to read.

Ella labeled the sealed holding box with a neutral number.

"See?" she said. "The system works without knowing what is inside."

"Usually."

"Always if everyone follows the rules."

Annabeth looked at her. "People won't always."

Ella considered this, then added a small space to the ledger for exceptions and mistakes. They were going to need it.

The next day the dispatch box worked better. Chiron remained responsible for protected addresses. Ella tracked dates and delivery attempts, not private family information. Annabeth helped make clear labels that wouldn't tear when the damp weather reached the mail station.

They were still learning when the first Atlantean tube arrived.

At Camp Half-Blood, May arrived straight on.

Annabeth Grace woke to frost on the Zeus cabin shutters and Apollo scales in the cold air. She was thirteen, hair braided back for archery. On her desk lay three books, a half-drawn map, and a pottery shard she used as a paperweight.

Her sister's bunk across the cabin was empty and neatly made. Thalia had left two weeks earlier on a scouting trip Chiron arranged with a visiting group of Hunters. She went as a camp scout, with a return date and no vow to Artemis. She had left one silver arrowhead on Annabeth's pillow and a note: WISE GIRL. KEEP THE SKY UP. — T.

Annabeth kept the arrowhead in her pocket. She touched it every morning like a ritual.

The Hermes cabin ran the mail. Travis Stoll blew the conch for parcels at noon, with a sack nearly as big as he was, Connor sorting beside him.

"Zeus!" Travis called. "Grace! You got a fat one!"

Annabeth jogged up the steps, boots crunching on old leaves left under the new buds. The envelope was thick, made of pale kelp paper, sealed with blue wax. Her name was written on the front in Percy's slanted hand, the letters leaning as if hurried. Underneath, in smaller writing: OPEN CAREFULLY. LINING.

Connor leaned over. "Ooh. Lining. Secret."

"It is not secret if he labeled it," Annabeth said, but her ears went warm.

She carried it to her bench by the lake, the one with initials carved small underneath. Mrs. O'Leary found her halfway there, tail sweeping leaves, nose shoved into the envelope.

"Wait," Annabeth laughed, holding it up. "You will drool on it."

Mrs. O'Leary sat, enormous and obedient and quivering, and watched with total focus as Annabeth slit the seal with her thumbnail.

The seaflower fell into her palm, flat and perfect, white with pink at the heart. Nine days pressed and three days in transit and still holding its shape. Annabeth stared at it and felt her throat go tight in the way she hated and loved.

"Oh," she breathed.

Mrs. O'Leary whined softly and rested her massive head on Annabeth's knee.

Annabeth held the flower up to the light. She thought of the dock last spring, white buds on blue water while Percy skipped stones. She thought of him in a city of circles, pressing a flower for nine days because she would keep it.

She took out her sketchbook. She did not read the letter yet. She answered first the way she always answered important things — by drawing.

On a fresh page, she drew the bench, the lake, the envelope, Mrs. O'Leary's nose intruding from the corner. She drew the seaflower four times actual size, then enlarged, petal by petal, with notes in the margins: white outer, pink center, six petals? count again. Smells of cold. She drew a small box for it on the same page, with measurements, so she could build a case.

Then she pressed the flower between two clean pages and closed the book gently.

Only then did she unfold Percy's letter and read it straight through twice. By the second reading she was smiling so hard her cheeks hurt.

"Come on," she told Mrs. O'Leary, standing and tucking the sketchbook under her arm. "We have replies to make."

Percy had expected a letter back. He had not expected a portfolio.

It arrived eleven days later in a waterproof tube covered in stamps, with a pine needle stuck to the wax. Nerit whistled low when Percy carried it into the dorm.

"That is not a letter," Nerit said. "That is architecture."

Percy sat cross-legged on his bunk and eased the cap off. Inside, rolled tight, were six sheets of mortal paper, slightly damp at the edges, smelling of pine and woodsmoke.

The first sheet was a sketch of Annabeth's bench by the lake in spring. The lake was hatched with careful waves. The bench had old leaves caught between its slats and new buds overhead. Mrs. O'Leary's tail filled the lower corner with a note: tail moved, approximation. On the bench sat an envelope, drawn open, with a flower beside it.

The second sheet was the seaflower studies. Four views. Measurements. A note in Annabeth's neat hand: Case built. Cedar, glass front, fits in sketchbook pocket. See sheet 6.

The third sheet was a plan view of Atlantis Academy as she imagined it from his description. Half-circles, labeled in her writing: DORM? CLASSROOM? GARDEN WHERE HE PICKED THIS? She had drawn him in the garden, small and laughing, with an arrow pointing: scale probably wrong. Head too big. Keeping anyway.

Percy laughed out loud, ears hot.

The fourth sheet was a redraft: the same academy, but with straight lines imposed. A grid. A note: If they let you, suggest this for the new study hall. Load paths make sense. Ask Tyson to check my spans.

The fifth sheet was a map of Camp in spring, with little marks: new fence here, winter straw lifted from strawberries, archery range moved 10 paces west due to mud. Thalia's empty bunk drawn as a rectangle with a storm cloud over it: missing.

The sixth sheet was a letter, folded around a small flat packet.

He opened the packet first. Inside was a pressed oak leaf, gold-red, saved from Half-Blood Hill last autumn, with a hole chewed by some insect near the stem. Underneath, in Annabeth's hand on a strip: Hill, north side, where we watched the buses. Keeping even.

Percy held the oak leaf beside the memory of the seaflower and felt something steady settle behind his ribs.

Then he read.

---

Dear Seaweed Brain,

Your letter took three days. I counted. Ella counted too. She has a chart now. Transit times, by season, by carrier. She says hippocampus post is faster than Iris but less reliable in storms. She says to tell you your list of twelve currents and three songs has been entered as Appendix P-3.

Your description of light leaning was good. You are better at straight lines than you think. See enclosed. I fixed your circles. You are welcome.

Camp is quiet without summer. Twelve year-rounders plus the Hunters when they pass through. The strawberry fields are losing their winter straw. The lake is cold. Grover left for the Northwest three weeks ago. Juniper cried and then gave him seventeen pinecones and told him not to come back without a story. He sends his thanks for the stone. He says a stone that fits in a palm exactly is a listening stone. He will take it when he passes through later this spring, if you send it.

Ella is Junior Archivist officially. She has three boxes and better pens, as ordered. She catalogued your letter before I read it. She said, "Percy, May, first, homesick, brave." I told her brave was right.

Mrs. O'Leary misses bacon. I miss breakfasts too. I sit at the Zeus table now in the mornings because Thalia's seat is empty and someone should keep it warm. Clarisse sits with me half the time. She says the Poseidon table is too damp in May. She steals my toast. It is our routine.

Thank you for the flower. I built it a case. See sketch. I carry it in my sketchbook pocket. When my Current Math equivalent (sieve defense theory) gets stupid, I look at it and remember circles can hold.

Write again. Tell Lady Amphitrite I said her mussel cakes are bribes and to keep feeding you. Tell Tyson his forge foreman owes me a letter about spans. Tell Nerit his current-charts are wrong about Long Island Sound in May. I redrew them on the back of sheet 4. He can fight me.

I miss you most on purpose too.

Yours,
Annabeth

P.S. I pressed the oak leaf on the hill where we sat before the buses. It is not as pretty as yours. Keep it anyway. P.P.S. Thalia says if you look up expecting storms you will get fish, and fish is better. She sent you a postcard. It is enclosed in Ella's tube. Do not let it get wet. It is already wet. Sorry.

Percy read the postscript three times. Then he took the oak leaf and pressed it inside his history book, on the page where the seaflower had been, under the same coral weight.

Nerit peered over. "Is that a leaf?"

"It is a leaf," Percy said.

"It is a very flat leaf."

"It is a very important flat leaf."

Nerit nodded sagely. "Atlantis does not understand flat leaves. But I will protect it with my life."

"Good," Percy said, throat tight, smiling. "You are promoted to leaf guard."

Thalia Grace did not write letters. Thalia sent postcards, fast and slanted, with stamps stuck crooked and messages that ran off the edge.

Ella had tucked three of them into the tube behind Annabeth's sheets, each in a separate waxed sleeve, each smelling faintly of rain and woodsmoke.

The first showed a mountain ridge in lingering snow, blue-white under a huge sky. On the back, in thick pencil:

PERCY —
Saw this and thought of Annabeth explaining contour lines for an hour. Then thought of you falling off this mountain. Do not fall off mountains. East ridge, somewhere with too many pines. Hunters are good. Phoebe shot a storm spirit mid-gust. I held the sky up. Ha. Miss you, Kelp Head. Keep your left shoulder up. — T.

The second showed a gas station at dusk, neon buzzing, with a silver truck parked crooked. On the back:

PERCY —
Camp scout life: ambrosia at a gas station, Artemis driving (do not ask), Annabeth would kill me for the parking. Met a wind nymph who knew your dad when he was younger and meaner. Told her you are nicer. She did not believe me. Prove me right. Tell my sister she left her boots AGAIN and I am wearing them until she begs. — T.

The third was not a picture. It was a rubbing, taken on thin paper over a trail marker — a pine tree with a lightning bolt cut into the bark. Underneath, in ink gone shaky with cold:

Percy —
Night watch. Thinking about the kids who kept watch on the hill. You, me. Her down at camp keeping the sky up while I chase it. You taught me that trees hold. I am holding. You hold too, okay? Spring is weird out here. Too quiet. Send more of your dumb jokes so I have something to read aloud to annoy the Hunters. Tell Annabeth her sister says to sleep. Actually sleep. I can tell from three states away when she does not. — Thalia.

Percy sat on his bunk with the three cards spread out and felt the distance shrink. He could picture her — black hair choppy, silver headband pushed back, writing on her knee while the Hunters argued about watch order.

He took out a fresh sheet of kelp paper and wrote back immediately.

---

Thalia —

1. I am not falling off mountains. Atlantis has no mountains. Only very tall coral that looks like mountains but is softer to hit.
2. Left shoulder is UP. Clarisse will be proud. You will be prouder.
3. Joke for the Hunters: Why did the hippocampus bring a towel? Because the tide was out and he did not want to be embarrassed. Tell Phoebe I am sorry.
4. Annabeth is not sleeping. I can tell from under the sea. Go tell her yourself. You are scarier.
5. Kids from the hill hold. I am holding. You hold.

— P.

P.S. Fish IS better than storms. But storms are you. So storms win.

He addressed it to the Hunter drop at Camp, knowing Ella and Chiron would forward it. He added a sketch of his own at the bottom — a very bad pine tree with a lightning bolt, leaning hard to the left. Underneath: artistic. Do not judge.

Nerit looked over his shoulder and winced. "That tree is falling."

"It is holding," Percy corrected.

They arrived on water-resistant slate sheets, bound with a leather cord, smelling of sweat and pine sap and the forge. Her handwriting was blocky and furious, as if the letters had been ordered to stand at attention.

Percy got the first packet two days after Annabeth's tube, delivered by a very damp harpy who demanded a tip and got a mussel cake and left satisfied.

He unrolled the slates on the dorm floor while Tyson sat nearby and tried not to break the chair.

Tyson finished reading over Percy's shoulder and nodded solemnly. "Sister Clarisse is a good captain."

"She lost to Sherman?" Percy said, grinning. "No way. She let him win to make him confident before she crushes him."

"She is kind," Tyson agreed. "In a Clarisse way."

Percy rolled onto his stomach on the cold floor and wrote back on the back of a current-chart Nerit donated, in block letters as close to Clarisse's as he could manage.

He tied the cord tight and sent it with the next dispatch. He did fifty shield presses first, so he could write truthfully that he had.

That night his shoulders ached in the good way that meant sister.

Silena Beauregard wrote the way she braided hair — carefully, warmly, with extra tucked in for later.

Her letters came on cream mortal paper, edged with pressed petals, smelling of vanilla and roses even after nine days under the sea. She dotted her i's with tiny hearts. Clarisse teased her mercilessly for it. Silena did it anyway.

Percy's first Silena packet arrived with a recipe card, a sachet, and strict instructions.

Dearest Percy,

I am writing this at the Poseidon table with sea salt in my ink because Clarisse flicked water at me. Sisters are beasts. Love them anyway.

Thank you for defending my honey cakes to the Atlantean cooks. I have enclosed the true recipe, annotated, with a fallback for sea honey (add lemon, less salt, pray). I have also enclosed a sachet of mortal vanilla sugar. Do not eat it straight. Tyson will try. Stop him. Give the cooks this note: honey cakes need patience more than heat. Low oven, honey last, do not rush the rise. If they challenge you to a bake duel, accept, then send for me. I will arrive with apron and vengeance.

Camp news in order of sweetness:

Sweetest: Maya floated! Clarisse pretended not to cry. I cried enough for both of us. She is brave and misses her mom. I gave her your old blanket (the blue one you left). I hope that is okay. It smelled like you and salt and she sleeps better with it. You are a good friend and little brother, lending warmth without knowing.

Middle sweet: Annabeth is doing well, but tired. I braided her hair last night while she worked and she fell asleep mid-sentence. I left a rose on Thalia's pillow for her.

Least sweet but still sweet: The Ares cabin stole our practice dummy. Clarisse stole it back. Chiron pretended not to laugh. He laughed.

Now, recipe business.

Silena

P.S. Pressed flower on the edge — sea lavender from the lake path. For your book. For balance. Oak leaf from her, lavender from me. You are well-booked.

Percy read the letter aloud to Tyson and Nerit in the dorm, doing voices. Tyson laughed so hard he slid off the chair and apologized to it.

They made the honey cakes that weekend in the dorm kitchen, with permission begged from the cooks and vanilla sugar measured like gold dust. The first batch sank. The second burned on the bottom. The third rose golden and smelled like Camp in the middle of Atlantis.

Percy drizzled honey while warm, as ordered. He cut the corner piece and set it aside. Tyson reached for it. Percy slapped his hand.

"Hey!" Tyson protested.

"Silena says that one is mine," Percy said. "Because I always give it away."

Tyson blinked his big brown eye, then beamed and pushed the plate closer. "Then eat, little brother. Then give away the next one."

Percy ate the corner piece. It tasted of honey and salt and home. He closed his eyes and for a moment he was at the Poseidon table — no, the crowded spring table where everyone sat together because numbers were small — with Silena humming and Clarisse stealing toast and Annabeth drawing in oatmeal.

Nerit ate three and declared fusion a triumph. "Tell your sister the cooks weep," he said solemnly, mouth full. "They weep sweet tears."

Percy wrote back with crumbs on the paper.

---

Dearest Silena,

Batch three succeeded. See enclosed crumb as proof. (Sorry. Tyson ate the rest before I could press it. He says tell you it was transcendent. He used that word. He learned it for you.)

Comfort food here when it rains (which is always, because we are underwater, but storm-rain is different, heavier): hot kelp broth with bread for dunking, and sugar kelp chews. I will send chews. They are salty-sweet. You will invent something. Clarisse will say it is a trap and then eat four.

Thank you for giving Maya my blanket. Of course. Tell her the blanket has protected three people from nightmares (me, Tyson when he visits, and once a first-year who dreamed of drowning). Tell her it is strong. Tell her she is stronger.

Tell Annabeth to sleep. Everyone tells her. No one succeeds. Maybe try braiding sleep into her hair. You can do anything with braids.

The sea lavender is pressed next to the oak leaf. My book is now a garden. When I open it, it smells like both places.

The cooks say: honey last, low heat, patience. They bowed. I bowed. It was a whole thing. You would have loved it. No bake duel yet, but I am ready. I have apron. I have vengeance (borrowed from you).

All my love back,
Percy

P.S. Do not dot your i's with hearts in the recipe you send the cooks. They will think it is an ingredient and add berries. Actually — add berries. It would be good.

He enclosed a strip of sugar kelp chew and a drawing Nerit made of the honey cakes, labeled in Atlantean: SUCCESS. DO NOT LET TALL ONE EAT ALL.

Tyson framed the label. Or tried. He was very proud.

The first lost letter was not lost at all.

Percy discovered this after spending two days wondering why Silena hadn't replied to the recipe correction he sent her. Ella's ledger said the envelope had left Atlantis. The dormitory mail shell said it had been accepted. The surface receipt confirmed the transfer to Camp Half-Blood. Somewhere between one record and the next, Percy's letter had disappeared.

"Maybe she got it and didn't answer," Nerit suggested.

"She answers."

"Sometimes people are busy."

Percy made a face. He knew Nerit was right in principle. He also knew Silena liked to reply to food experiments. She'd asked Percy to tell her whether the berry powder made the honey cakes too sweet. Not answering that question was unlike her.

At breakfast Tyson listened to the problem, then asked to see the receipt.

"Why?"

"Because one of the forge shipment tickets was marked wrong last month," Tyson explained. "The parcel arrived. The name on the receiving sheet was different."

They went to the mail station together. The attendant showed them the outgoing list, where the envelope had been entered under Percy Jackson. The destination code belonged to Camp Half-Blood's general correspondence, not Silena's private forwarding channel.

"So it went to the camp," Percy said.

"Yes."

"Then where is it?"

The attendant looked at the line. "We can ask the receiver to check. We cannot open someone's mail to verify it ourselves."

Percy stared at him, frustrated. The easiest answer would be to use sea magic to follow the water still clinging to the envelope. He could probably do that over a short distance. He couldn't follow the paper through the entire world without knowing exactly which water had touched it, and trying to access a private mail room with his powers wasn't a reasonable solution to an administrative problem.

"Can you send a correction?" he asked.

"We can send an inquiry."

They did.

Two days later a message came back from Chiron. The letter had been placed in a sorting box with two other unsigned recipe notes. Silena had received it only that morning and was already testing a new batch. Nothing had been destroyed. Nobody had read the contents except the intended recipient.

Percy felt absurdly relieved.

Nerit looked over his shoulder as he read the short message.

"You thought the sea had eaten your mail."

"Maybe a little."

"Did you check whether the sea was hungry?"

"Not funny."

"It's a little funny."

Percy laughed. Then he wrote the attendant a thank-you note and asked whether they could mark private mail codes more clearly on the dispatch slip. The attendant suggested adding the recipient code beside the sender's name rather than writing it only on the outer shell, where the wax could smear.

They tried the change on the next collection.

Percy learned something about the weeks he would spend away from camp. Not every gap in an answer meant someone was frightened, hurt or angry. Sometimes paper went into the wrong tray. Sometimes a friend was busy. The only way to know which was true was to check with the people who actually handled the information.

The correction reminded him of what Grover had said by the lake before leaving. They had agreed not to treat a delayed letter as proof a person was missing. Percy had nodded solemnly and promptly forgotten the advice the first time a letter to his sister came back late.

He wrote an apology to Grover in his next message.

*Turns out I'm bad at my own rules*, he admitted. *I'm still practicing.*

Grover's reply came several days later, forwarded through Chiron.

*Me too. Yesterday I panicked because Juniper didn't answer for a whole afternoon. It turned out she was a tree and had things to do. Don't tell her I put that in a letter.*

Percy read the note twice, smiling, and returned it to its envelope without passing it around the dorm.

By the third week of May, letter season had its own rhythm.

Percy would wake before the bell, check the dispatch shells, and run back with whatever had arrived tucked inside his jacket to keep dry. Nerit would wake to the sound of paper and roll over with a whistle.

"Who today?"

"Annabeth," Percy would say, or "Clarisse," or "Silena," and Nerit would nod and pretend to go back to sleep while openly listening.

Annabeth's second tube arrived with more sketches — the Big House porch with frost, the strawberry fields freed from straw, Ella's archive boxes labeled SUMMER, SONGS, STORMS. There was a study of Clarisse's hands mending a net, with a note: hands tell. And a study of Silena braiding Maya's hair, with a note: gentle hands.

Underneath was a short letter.

---

Dear Percy,

Ella says transit is slowing with spring storms. Three days is now four. Plan accordingly. Do not worry, she says, worry slows mail. I told her worry is not mail. She catalogued that as ANNABETH, WIT, DRY.

I am sleeping better. Silena braided sleep into my hair. It worked twice.

Thalia returned to camp for one night with her Hunter escort. She wore my boots and pretended they were hers. I let her for an hour, then demanded tribute (one arrowhead, one story). She gave both. She sleeps like a storm — loud, then still. I slept best that night, with her breathing across the cabin. Sisters who keep choosing each other count double. Silena is right.

Clarisse is teaching Maya to spar. Maya is teaching Clarisse to be patient. Both are winning. You would be proud. I am proud for you until you come home.

I redrew your dorm as straight lines. Tyson says spans are good. He wrote to me separately about forge shifts and included a recipe for kelp broth. He is a good brother. You are a good brother. Poseidon cabin is lucky, even scattered.

Keep pressing flowers. Keep sending circles. I will keep making them hold.

Yours,
Annabeth

Percy carried that letter in his satchel for three days. He took it out between classes and reread the line about sleeping best with her sister breathing across the cabin.

He wrote back that night about the dome singing in high winds, Lady Amphitrite asking after Annabeth's maps, and holding a Current Math number still for a full minute by thinking of her grid.

He ended with:

I keep your oak leaf under coral. I keep Silena's lavender beside it. I keep Thalia's postcards on the wall where I can see mountains. I keep Clarisse's logs under my pillow so her yelling seeps into dreams. I keep you in the pocket over my heart, like your flower case. Cheesy? Probably. Keeping anyway.

The last week of classes gave Percy something he hadn't expected from Atlantis: a problem he couldn't solve by asking Annabeth.

In coastal engineering, his instructor asked each student to make a model of a current barrier for an emergency shelter. The model had to withstand three changes in pressure without trapping the creatures that might swim nearby. Percy's first design was beautiful. It bent water into an arch and kept the inside perfectly still.

It also stopped every little fish from leaving.

"You've protected the shelter," the instructor told him. "From the sea, from motion, and from anything that might want to go home."

Percy frowned at the little model, where three bright fish circled the inside of his barrier.

"I can let them out."

"Then show me how."

He loosened the arch. Two fish swam away. The third stayed near the wall, confused by the altered current. Percy tried again. This time the pressure change washed the whole shelter sideways.

Nerit watched from the next table, where he was building a much uglier barrier out of shell mesh and stone.

"Your model looks like a palace," he observed.

"Thank you."

"Mine looks like a basket."

"Also accurate."

Nerit lowered his model into the test tank. The water ran through it, slowed by the mesh. None of the creatures became trapped. When the instructor increased the pressure, the structure bent and then returned to its original shape.

Percy stared.

"You built a basket that works better than my palace."

"I built a basket because it is supposed to let water through. You're treating water like something you can order not to move."

Percy looked at his hands. He had always had the ability to bend water toward him, lift it, shield someone with it. He'd begun to think of control as the same thing as care. In the life he remembered, that instinct had saved people. In this one it could also become a way to keep them from making choices he couldn't supervise.

He thought of Tyson leaving on the Atlantean boat, with his own forge job and a correctly completed transfer record. He thought of Clarisse and Silena writing from camp, refusing to let him turn each message into a crisis. He thought of Annabeth choosing her own yellow ribbon because she liked it.

"Okay," Percy said. "I'm starting again."

The second design was less elegant. It had wider channels and a flexible membrane that released pressure through openings at the sides. The fish moved where they wanted. The shelter stayed in place for the first two pressure changes.

At the third, a seam split.

"Better," the instructor said.

"Better? It broke."

"It failed at one seam. You know where it failed. You can repair it. Your first design hid the problem until the whole system failed at once."

Percy wanted to argue, but he couldn't. Nerit lent him a small tool for tightening the seam. They repaired it together. Percy tested it again, this time making sure the fish could escape before increasing the pressure.

The model passed.

His instructor signed the assignment and asked Percy to write a reflection explaining which parts he'd changed and why. Percy had expected to be graded on power. He ended up being graded on whether he could explain his mistakes.

That evening he wrote Annabeth a separate page about the experiment.

*I built a perfect palace that trapped all the fish*, he told her. *Nerit built an ugly basket that saved them. Don't get smug. I already know which one you'd have picked.*

He described the seam failure and drew a rough cross-section of the repaired channel. He included the instructor's actual comments, not just his own version of what happened.

Annabeth replied with a full page of notes on drainage and a little drawing of a fish wearing a crown. Underneath she wrote: *You didn't have to stop being good at water. You had to let it do something you hadn't chosen.*

He laughed when he read it.

A few days later Amphitrite called him to a quiet terrace near the academy. She had received the letter he'd written on his first morning of the study block. Percy hadn't expected to hear about it in person, which suddenly made him nervous.

She brought two mugs of warm sea-herb tea. They sat overlooking the palace gardens while a gardener trimmed growth from a stair rail below.

"I liked your letter," she said.

Percy looked down at the mug. "I'm glad."

"You wrote about asking you before making plans."

"Yeah."

"I didn't always do that when you were small."

Percy felt a flash of panic. She was speaking about years he didn't remember. The easiest thing would be to nod as though he could recall the argument she was about to describe.

Instead he waited.

"You were young," Amphitrite continued. "You didn't always have enough words to tell me what you wanted. I thought I knew. Sometimes I did. Sometimes I made your frustration worse because I kept trying to solve it before you could show me."

Percy knew she was telling him her own remembered experience. He could listen without pretending it was his. There was no need to expose the secret of the rewrite to say something true.

"You ask me now," he said.

"More often. Not always."

"Nobody's perfect."

She smiled. "Not even queens."

He told her about the engineering experiment, the fish and the failed seam. She listened carefully, then asked to see the drawing. Percy unfolded it and showed her the little escape channels.

"Did you like the revised version better?" she asked.

"It was less pretty. But it worked."

"Then perhaps it was prettier in the way that mattered."

Percy laughed. "Annabeth said something like that."

"I'm sure she did."

Amphitrite handed back the drawing. She didn't ask for the private letters he'd received from camp. She didn't demand to know exactly why he'd thanked her for asking. She simply offered another mug of tea and wanted to know how his studies were going.

Percy left the terrace carrying a story he had witnessed himself: the afternoon his mother listened to him talk about a failed project and didn't try to make it easier than it had been.

He wrote that one down before the next dispatch, because it was his story now.

Talk of a spring thank-you feast started near the end of the study block, soft as new leaves.

Would Percy come home? Could he travel between school and the late-May Roman visit? Letters flew faster, as if speed could solve it.

Chiron wrote once, in his neat hand, to both Percy and Annabeth separately. To Percy: Camp keeps a bed. Atlantis keeps a prince. Hearts keep both. Come if the tides allow.

Thalia sent a postcard of a frozen lake, white and vast. On the back: SPRING BREAK? I HAVE LEAVE. IF YOU GO, I GO. WE RACE. I WIN. — T. Percy laughed and wrote: YOU WIN RUNNING. I WIN SWIMMING. WE TIE.

Clarisse sent a log that was mostly a list.

LOG 3 — SPRING BREAK PREP.

Air out cabins. Check pipes after winter. Oil armor. Mend cloaks.
Maya's mom sending cookies. Share with Poseidon cabin first.
Tyson's hinge still holding.
Annabeth's boots returned to Thalia? No. Thalia stole again. War pending.
Guest beds for the feast: counted. One for Percy if tides allow. One for Thalia if patrol allows. One for Grover if Wild allows. All if. All hoped.
NOTE FOR PERCY: Come home. That is an order. Disobey if you must. But come.

Silena sent cocoa mix and a recipe for spring feast buns with orange and clove, with a note: For the dorm. For sharing. For missing. Come home if you can. If you cannot, we send home to you. Every letter is a room.

Annabeth sent a sketch of the spring feast table, long and laden, with empty chairs drawn carefully and labeled: P., T., G. Underneath: saving seats is architecture too.

Percy sat on his bunk surrounded by paper — sketches on the wall, postcards pinned to the coral, logs stacked under his pillow, recipes stained with honey — and felt the ache and the warmth at once. Missing was not empty. Missing was full of people.

He took out his best kelp paper, the kind with shell flecks, and wrote the study block's last letter slowly, pressing hard enough to emboss.

---

Dear Camp, Dear Sisters, Dear Brothers, Dear Wise Girl,

This study block is ending here. The kelp is bright with new growth. The light reaches farther into the garden every morning. School lets out for spring break at the end of the week. I do not know yet if I can come for the thank-you feast before the Roman visit. Dad is talking to the council. Amphitrite is talking to Dad (palace corridor, very loud, very Mom). Tyson says the currents will decide. Ella would catalogue that as TIDES, WILL, WAITING.

If I come, I will bring sugar kelp chews and Nerit's charts (corrected by Annabeth) and my terrible pine tree drawing for Thalia and Clarisse's wrist wrap (hostage release) and Silena's honey cakes (attempted, probably burned in transit). I will hug Maya and ask about floating. I will sit at the table where everyone sits because numbers are small. I will sit shoulder to shoulder with Annabeth and not need to talk.

If I cannot come, know this: your letters made a cabin out of a dorm. I sleep surrounded by mountains and toast theft and honey and flat leaves. Nerit says my bunk is a shrine. I told him it is a camp.

Thank you. For sketches that fix circles. For postcards that hold sky. For logs that yell kindly. For recipes that taste like home. For cataloguing me as brave.

Annabeth — the seaflower made it? Mine did. Yours did. Press more. I will press more. We will have a book of kept things. That is architecture too.

Thalia — kids from the hill hold through spring storms. Hold for me until tides decide.

Clarisse — left shoulder up. Core not lazy. Eating more. See Log Response 3 enclosed. Do not cry if I come home. You will pretend it is cold. I will pretend to believe you.

Silena — vanilla sugar almost gone. Tyson did try to eat it straight. I stopped him twice. Third time I shared. It was good straight. Do not tell the cooks.

Grover — listening stone enclosed. Palm-sized exactly. Listen hard. Come home if the Wild lets you.

Ella — Appendix P-4 enclosed: fourteen currents, five songs, one teacher sneezes like conch, one roommate whistles in sleep, one flower pressed, one leaf kept. Record complete? Never complete. Keep going.

Chiron — hippocampi still shy from left. Approach from right. As taught.

Mrs. O'Leary — bacon enclosed? Cannot enclose bacon. Imagine bacon. Good girl.

I miss you most on purpose. All of you. On purpose.

Yours, from under the sea with love,
Percy

He sealed it. He tied the stone in a separate cloth. He rolled Annabeth's corrected charts with care. He pressed one last seaflower, white with pink heart, between oiled kelp for the lining — sister to the first, for keeping even.

Outside, the tide horns blew. Inside, Nerit whistled awake and sat up, hair wild.

"Spring-break letter?" he asked.

"The study block’s last," Percy said.

"Will you go?"

Percy looked at the wall of sketches, the postcards, the recipes, the logs. He looked at the coral weight holding down an oak leaf and sea lavender side by side.

"I hope," he said honestly. "Either way, I am held."

He ran the tube down to the dispatch shells through new green kelp and leaning light, jacket over it to keep dry, heart full and aching in the good way that meant family.

Far above, beyond dome and sea and miles, May lay bright green over Half-Blood Hill. On her bench by the cold lake, a girl with storm-gray eyes opened her sketchbook to a cased seaflower and began to draw a boy swimming home through circles made straight by love.

She labeled it, neat and sure: COMING. KEEP SEAT WARM.

Mrs. O'Leary sneezed on the ink. Annabeth laughed and kept drawing around the splotch, because some marks were love too.

But before the final dispatch left, Percy made one more stop at the academy office.

He had been carrying a note from Chiron for three days. The centaur wanted Percy to confirm the date of the late-May visit to Camp Jupiter, the contact who would meet them at the Roman gate, and the authorization for Annabeth and Thalia to travel with him. Percy had assumed somebody else would handle the details. Every morning he'd meant to ask, and every morning there had been another class or letter.

The office clerk looked at the note. "You need three separate approvals."

"Why three?"

"You're coming from an undersea school. Your companions are coming from camp. The Romans require confirmation of the actual visitors."

Percy looked at the empty boxes. He could see why he'd put off the question. A promise from a friend felt more solid than a form. But if they arrived at a gate without the right arrangements, it wouldn't be only Percy who stood waiting.

He asked what each approval required. The clerk explained, and he wrote the requirements on separate slips so he could send them to the right people. He didn't enter Annabeth's name on the final passenger list without asking her whether she wanted to go; he sent her an invitation and a clear description of the plan.

She replied two days later.

*Yes, I want to go. I have questions for the Roman architects. I also need to check my school assignments and ask Beryl what she thinks. Don't book the journey until we know.*

Thalia's reply was shorter.

*Ask first, book second. I may need a patrol swap. I'll confirm.*

Percy stared at the notes and realized that not having a final answer was not the same thing as losing the plan. His friends were allowed to have schedules that didn't arrange themselves around him.

He sent the office a preliminary request marked *pending traveler confirmation*. Then he wrote Chiron with the current status, listing the approvals he had and the ones he still needed. Chiron answered that the visit would be confirmed only when every required person had said yes and the Roman hosts agreed to the date.

Percy put the reply beside his engineering assignment. Both had taught him the same thing: an elegant plan that ignored the needs of the people inside it wasn't finished.

When Nerit saw the stack of letters on his desk, he whistled.

"Are all those people coming?"

"Maybe."

"That's not a very satisfying answer."

"It's the honest one."

Percy sealed the latest envelope and carried it to the post station. He had written to the people he loved without pretending they were all in the same room or that any of them could answer immediately. The letters didn't close the distance. They made the distance something he could work with.

And the mail moved on, brave through storms, carrying rooms to everyone who waited.
