## Chapter 65: Triton's Lesson

The morning Triton came back to Camp Half-Blood, the sea stood up to greet him.

Percy felt it before he saw it. He was down at the canoe lake at seven in the morning, half-asleep and trying to do his Atlantis currents homework before the portal opened, when the water in his canteen lifted straight up out of the bottle and hung in the air like a jellyfish.

He stared at it. "Okay. That's not me."

Behind him, boots crunched on the dock. Annabeth sat cross-legged with her correspondence algebra spread around her and her pencil behind her ear, and she looked up and squinted toward Long Island Sound.

"Percy," she said slowly. "Tell me the Sound is supposed to do that."

It wasn't. Out past the barrier, past the gray morning chop, a long green swell was rolling in against the wind. It didn't break. It just kept building, smooth as glass, three feet, five feet, eight feet high, and riding the crest of it, arms crossed and chin high, was a tall merman in polished bronze armor with a double conch-shell trumpet at his hip and a trident slung across his back.

Tyson, who had come up through the tide portal early to make sure Percy did his homework, clapped his hands and nearly dropped Percy’s backpack in the lake.

"TRITON!" he bellowed, delighted. "My brother is here! He is early! He is showing off!"

The wave carried Triton all the way to the beach and set him down gently on the sand, where his fish tail shimmered and split and became two long legs in greaves, like it had always been legs. Sea spray hung around him for a second too long, as if reluctant to let go. Gulls screamed and scattered.

Triton shook his dark hair back. He was an ageless adult sea god and looked it, tall and broad and too handsome in the way sea gods were, with Percy's same sea-green eyes and none of Percy's mess. He spotted them on the dock and his mouth twitched.

"The little tide," he said loudly. "Doing homework. Has the world ended?"

Percy was already on his feet, grinning before he could stop himself. "The big blowfish. Wearing armor to impress the Nereids. Did Dad make you come?"

"Father suggested," Triton said, with great dignity, "that your surface brawling has become embarrassing to the family. I volunteered to correct it."

Annabeth elbowed Percy hard in the ribs. "Be nice. He's your brother."

"We are nice," Percy said. "This is our nice."

Triton mounted the dock in three strides. The planks didn't even creak under the bronze. He looked Annabeth over, nodded at her notebook, and then did something that surprised Percy — he bowed, just slightly, the way Atlanteans bowed to Zeus's daughters.

"Annabeth Grace," Triton said. "Thalia speaks of you in Atlantis. She says you ask better questions than the palace scholars."

Annabeth flushed pink to the ears. "Thalia exaggerates."

"Thalia never exaggerates," Triton said. "She only shouts."

Tyson threw himself at Triton in a hug that would have cracked a mortal spine. Triton took it, patted Tyson's back twice, awkward and fond, and met Percy's eyes over Tyson's shoulder.

Percy felt that strange double tug he always felt around Triton now. Annoyance and pride braided together. This was his older brother. His older brother who had once called him a barnacle and left him to drown in court politics. His older brother who had, last month in Atlantis, stayed three hours late after sword drill to show him how to turn a rip current into a shield, and then pretended he hadn't.

"So you're really gonna train us?" Percy asked.

Triton straightened, Tyson still attached to his side like a large happy limpet. "Father and Chiron have agreed. Three mornings. Atlantean combat for those with water affinity or who fight beside those who have it. You. Clarisse. Silena. The Grace sisters will join for sky-sea cooperation."

"My sister?" Annabeth said.

"Thalia," Triton said. "Daughter of Zeus and Beryl. Your older sister, is she not?"

Annabeth glanced at Percy. She lived in Cabin One now, with its white marble and its crackling air, and she was still learning the shape of a childhood she could not remember. Thalia's bus ticket stubs were on her corkboard. Thalia's spare striped scarf was around her sketchbook. Sister was a fact; how to be this sister was something she was learning.

"Yeah," she said quietly. "My sister."

A conch blew from the Big House. Chiron's voice, magically amplified, rolled down the hill: "All trainees to the beach! Atlantean combat demonstration begins in twenty minutes!"

Triton smiled, sharp and eager. "Then let us see what you little minnows have learned."

---

The beach at eight on an early-April morning was cold enough to make Percy's toes go numb, but by eight-fifteen it was crowded.

Chiron had dragged a rack of practice weapons down to the sand. Mr. D had come to watch because, as he put it, "someone has to witness the liability." Grover had come back from satyr business the night before and sat on a dune with a thermos. The year-rounders clustered in hoodies: Connor and Travis Stoll already taking bets, Austin and Kayla from Apollo, Pollux with a blanket around his shoulders.

And in the front row, arms crossed, were Clarisse La Rue and Silena Beauregard.

Clarisse had her spear slung across her back and her hair in a tight knot that meant business. She was fourteen, broad-shouldered and scowling, and she looked Triton up and down like she was deciding where to stab him first.

Silena stood beside her in a blue windbreaker, her dark hair braided back, a practice short sword in her hand that she held like it might bite her. Silena was daughter of Poseidon and everyone still underestimated her, which Percy thought was insane because he had seen her talk a Cyclops into putting down its club last summer just by being kind and firm at the same time.

Triton stood barefoot in the wet sand in a plain sea-green tunic now, his armor stacked neatly by the dunes, his trident planted upright beside him. Without the bronze he looked younger. More like Percy's brother and less like a statue.

"Atlantean combat," Triton announced, "is not surface combat that happens to get wet. It is a different element. On land you fight gravity. In the sea you negotiate with it."

Clarisse snorted. "Great. Philosophy. Can we hit stuff?"

Triton's eyes narrowed. "Clarisse. You hit very well. I watched you at the last war games. You hit like a hammer. Underwater, hammers rust."

Clarisse's ears went red. "I don't fight underwater. I fight on boats. I fight on beaches."

"You fight where the sea allows you to fight," Triton corrected. "The sea allows you nothing unless you ask properly. Today you will learn to ask."

He lifted his hand. Water peeled off the Sound in a thin sheet and rose, hovering, twenty feet long and trembling.

Annabeth, sitting on a driftwood log with Thalia, sucked in a breath. Thalia had walked down from Cabin One after the dawn patrol, her black hair wild, her silver hairband glinting, her bow across her knees. She punched Annabeth lightly in the shoulder.

"Watch his feet," Thalia murmured. "He's not standing. He's floating a half-inch off the sand. Show-off."

"He's grounding through the water," Annabeth whispered back, already pulling out her sketchbook. She always carried two notebooks now — one for math and blueprints, one for drawing. "Look. The sand under him is wet in a circle. He's using it as a conductor — no, as an anchor."

Triton let the sheet of water fall into a ring around his feet. "First lesson. Breath. In Atlantis you do not hold your breath against the water. You share breath with it. Percy. Show them the bubble."

Percy groaned. "Why do I have to go first?"

"Because you are my brother and you are the only one here who can do it without drowning," Triton said bluntly. "Bubble."

Percy stepped forward, trying not to feel everyone's eyes. He closed his eyes, reached for the water in the air, in the sand, in his own blood, and asked.

It came easily now, after mornings in Atlantis. A sphere of air shimmered around his head, salt-sweet, and then expanded — wobbling — to include Clarisse and Silena where they stood.

Clarisse jumped. "Whoa — I can —" She inhaled. Air, cool and clean, under water-that-wasn't-water. "Huh."

Silena laughed in delight, her breath fogging the inside of the bubble. "Oh, Percy, it's lovely! It's like being inside a pearl!"

"Hold it," Triton ordered. "Walk the surf line. Keep the bubble stable while you move. If it pops, you start over. If you get dizzy, sit down. Go."

They walked. Or tried to. The bubble wobbled with every step. Percy sweated. He had never tried to hold one for three people while moving.

Clarisse elbowed him. "Quit shaking, Jackson. You're making it flicker."

"I'm not shaking, you're stomping," Percy muttered.

"I stomp with purpose."

Silena, between them, put a hand on each of their arms. "Breathe with me? In — two — three — out — two — three. Like rowing."

They tried it Silena's way. In, out. In, out. The bubble steadied, smoothing from lumpy to glassy. Percy's shoulders dropped an inch.

From the log, Annabeth sketched fast: three figures inside a circle, hands linked, water beading on the outside. She caught the way Silena's braid lifted in the charged air, the way Clarisse's scowl softened when she wasn't trying to be tough.

Thalia leaned over. "You draw them nicer than they are."

"I draw them how they are when they're trying," Annabeth said, not looking up.

Triton circled them, arms behind his back. "Better. Clarisse, you fight your own lungs. Stop. Silena — excellent rhythm. You could lead a trireme with that voice. Percy —"

"I know, terrible participles," Percy said.

"Terrible posture," Triton said, and kicked the back of Percy's knee lightly so he dropped into a lower stance. "Atlantean stance is low. Knees soft. You are kelp, not oak. Kelp bends and strangles. Oak snaps."

Percy stuck his tongue out at him when Triton turned. Clarisse saw and snorted so hard the bubble popped.

They all sat down laughing in the wet sand, gasping salt air.

"Again," Triton said, but there was a smile tugging at his mouth.

---

By ten o'clock the sun had burned off the mist and the second lesson had begun, and Percy's arms felt like cooked spaghetti.

Triton had moved them into the shallows to their ankles and given each of them a water whip — a three-foot length of seawater held together by will, heavy and alive in the hand.

"This is not a sword," Triton said, flicking his own whip so it cracked against a rock and left a white scar. "Swords cut. Water remembers. Strike and recall. Percy, Clarisse, spar. Slow. Silena, watch their currents and call the openings."

Percy and Clarisse faced off in the surf. Water tugged at their calves.

Clarisse grinned, feral. "Ready to lose, barnacle?"

"Barnacle is Triton's word," Percy said. "Get your own insult."

"Fine. Ready to lose, kelp?"

They circled. Percy tried to remember kelp-not-oak. He let his knees go loose, let the water in his whip hum.

Clarisse came in fast, the way she always did, whip slashing for his shoulder. Percy bent — really bent, water bending with him — and her whip passed through where his head had been, spraying mist.

"Oh, nice," Silena called from the side, hands cupped around her mouth. "Percy, your left is open when you dip! Clarisse, you're dropping your elbow on the backswing!"

"Whose side are you on?" Clarisse demanded, but she fixed her elbow.

They went again. And again. Percy landed a flick on Clarisse's wrist that stung. Clarisse swept his feet with a low curl of water and put him on his butt in the surf to cheers from the Stolls.

"Point to Clarisse!" Travis shouted.

"That was cheating! That was a wave, not a whip!" Percy sputtered, spitting salt.

"Water is water," Triton said. "Point stands. Again. Silena, trade with Clarisse."

Silena stepped in, tucking her braid back nervously. "I'm not as good at sparring —"

"You are better at seeing," Triton said. "Stop apologizing. Show me what you saw."

Silena versus Percy was completely different. She didn't charge. She waited, her whip low, her breathing audible — in-two-three, out-two-three — and when Percy struck, she sidestepped half an inch and let his whip slide past her like she was dancing.

Percy missed three times in a row. The Apollo kids ooh-ed.

"She's reading your shoulders," Annabeth called suddenly from the log, pencil paused. "Percy! You lift your right shoulder before every forehand! Sorry — sorry, keep going!"

Percy glanced toward her, distracted and pleased and annoyed all at once, and Silena tapped him neatly on the sternum with the butt of her whip.

"Point to Silena!" Connor yelled. "Pay up, Travis!"

Silena beamed and helped Percy up. "Sorry!"

"Don't be sorry, that was awesome," Percy panted. "How did you —"

"My mom says fighting is just another kind of conversation," Silena said. "I listen first."

Clarisse rolled her eyes, but she bumped Silena's fist. "Okay, that was pretty cool, Beauregard."

Triton watched all three of them with his arms crossed, and Percy caught something soft in his brother's face before it shuttered.

"Good," Triton said gruffly. "You listen. You adapt. You will need it. Rest. Drink. Next we bring the sky into it."

Percy waded out of the surf, dripping and shivering, and made straight for the log where Annabeth sat with her sketchbook full of flying water and stick-figure footwork diagrams in the margins.

She looked tired. She had been up late finishing her correspondence geometry, and her nose was pink with cold. Her pencil was moving slower now.

Without a word Percy picked up her canteen from the sand, walked back to the water's edge, and held his hand over it. Clean water — not salt, not sand — coaxed itself out of the air and spiraled down into the bottle, cool and sweet. He'd learned the trick in Atlantis from a Nereid who said surface girls always forgot to drink when they were thinking.

He brought it back and held it out. "Here, Wise Girl. You're doing that pale thing."

Annabeth took it, surprised. "I have water —"

"You had empty. I checked." He sat down hard beside her on the log, shoulder to shoulder, dripping all over her blanket. "Draw me handsome."

"You wish," she said, but she was smiling, and she drank, and her color came back. She flipped to a fresh page. "Hold still. You're all wet and glowy. It's hard to — no, don't pose, Seaweed Brain, you look constipated."

Thalia, on Annabeth's other side, laughed so hard she nearly fell off the log. "She's got you there, little brother."

Percy was Annabeth's boyfriend, in both the remembered life and this younger present — sea and sky, both year-rounders, bound by promises made on winter nights. When Annabeth looked at him over the top of her sketchbook with seawater beading on her eyelashes from his splash, grinning, he knew exactly why the warmth spread under his ribs.

"Just draw," he muttered, and she did, her knee pressed against his thigh to steady the book in the wind.

Thalia watched them with a small, knowing smirk and said nothing, which for Thalia was practically a speech.

---

After lunch — peanut butter and jelly eaten huddled in blankets while Triton lectured them on rip currents using french fries as visual aids — Triton called Thalia and Annabeth down to the sand.

"Sky," he said. "Your turn. Atlantean combat fails when storms come if sky and sea do not speak. Lightning in water kills everything, friend and foe. Lightning on water, guided — that wins battles."

Thalia cracked her knuckles, electricity sparking blue-white between them. "Finally. Something fun."

Annabeth hung back, hugging her sketchbook. "I'm — I'm not as strong as Thalia yet. My lightning still goes everywhere when I'm nervous. Chiron says —"

"Chiron says you are thirteen and learning control," Triton said, not unkindly. "I say you are Zeus's daughter and you will learn faster over water. Come. Stand in the surf. Both of you."

Percy, Clarisse, and Silena sat in a row on the log now, wrapped in towels, watching. Percy tried not to look nervous. He knew what Annabeth's lightning could do when it slipped. He'd seen her blow out every light in Cabin One last month when she had a nightmare about the manticore.

Thalia took Annabeth's hand and pulled her into the shallows. The water foamed white around their ankles.

"Okay, baby sis," Thalia said. "Remember what I taught you? Don't throw it. Invite it."

"I remember," Annabeth said. She sounded small.

"First exercise," Triton said. He raised both hands and pulled a curtain of seawater up, ten feet high, thin as glass, shimmering between the sisters and the beach. "Lightning through water. If you strike wild, the curtain grounds you and no one is hurt. Thalia, demonstrate."

Thalia rolled her shoulders. The air smelled suddenly of ozone and rain. She lifted her palm, and a bolt — thin, precise, forked once — snapped from her hand into the curtain.

The water lit up from within, veins of blue-white fire racing through it, beautiful and terrifying. The curtain held. The charge dissipated down into the wet sand with a hiss.

The beach erupted. Even Mr. D lowered his newspaper.

"Show-off!" Connor yelled admiringly.

Thalia bowed. "Years of practice."

Triton nodded. "Control. Path. Ground. Again, but hold the charge in the water for three seconds before grounding. Percy — lend her water. Keep the curtain fed."

Percy stood and walked down, shaking out his hands. He stood behind the curtain and fed it, pulling more water up from the Sound, feeling Thalia's electricity buzz against his own water-sense like bees behind glass.

Thalia struck again. This time the light stayed, trembling, held in the sheet. One. Two. Three. Then Thalia flicked her wrist down and the light sank into the sand.

"Yes!" Annabeth bounced on her toes. "The resistance — it's like the water holds it if you keep the salinity even! Thalia, did you feel —"

"I felt my hair standing up," Thalia said, grinning. Her ponytail was indeed frizzing. "Your turn, Annie."

Annabeth stepped up. She set her sketchbook carefully on a dry rock — Percy noticed she put it face-down so no one would see the page she'd been working on — and faced the curtain.

She breathed. Percy recognized the rhythm. In-two-three, out-two-three. Silena's rhythm.

She raised her hand. A spark sputtered, died. Another spark fizzled sideways and stung her own wrist. She winced.

"It's okay!" Percy called before he could stop himself. "Take your time!"

Clarisse elbowed him. "Let her focus, Jackson."

Annabeth closed her eyes. The wind picked up, lifting her hair. Percy felt the pressure change, the way it did before a storm — his ears popped.

When Annabeth opened her eyes, they were bright, and she thrust her palm forward.

Lightning — thinner than Thalia's, wobblier, but true — lanced into the water curtain. The curtain flared gold-white. Annabeth gasped, stumbled, and the charge wobbled toward the edge —

"Ground! Down!" Triton and Thalia shouted together.

Percy was already moving. He yanked extra water up under the weak spot, thickening the curtain, giving the lightning more path, and Thalia caught the edge with her own hand and dragged it down into the sand. The whole curtain exploded into steam with a crack like a firework.

Silence. Then the smell of hot salt.

Annabeth stood panting in the steam, her hand shaking. "Did I — did I get three seconds?"

"One and a half," Triton said. "And you nearly took your boyfriend's eyebrows off."

Percy touched his eyebrows to check. "Still there. Good job, Wise Girl!"

Annabeth laughed, shaky and exhilarated, and Thalia grabbed her in a headlock and rubbed her knuckles on her head while blue sparks snapped harmlessly off both of them.

"You did it!" Thalia shouted. "One and a half seconds of sky-sea! Dad would — Zeus would be —" She stopped, throat working. "He'd be proud. I am proud."

Annabeth hugged her back hard, face buried in Thalia's jacket. Over Thalia's shoulder, her eyes found Percy's. He gave her two thumbs up, dripping wet, grinning like an idiot.

Triton let the steam clear. "Good. Very good. Rest. In an hour, you face me."

Clarisse cracked her neck. "All of us? Against you?"

"All of you," Triton said. "Together. Atlanteans do not duel when they can drown you as a choir. Show me you have learned to sing."

---

The hour passed too fast and too slow.

Percy sat with Annabeth on the dune while she sketched Triton from memory — Triton with his trident planted, water sheeting off his shoulders, glaring at Clarisse. She'd caught him perfectly: proud, annoyed, fond, all at once.

"He's a good brother," Annabeth said quietly, shading the bronze greave. "Even when he's mean."

"He's mean because he's worried," Percy said. He picked at beach grass. "In Atlantis — the court — they say I'm the favorite now because Dad brags about my camp exploits. Triton's been the heir for three thousand years. Suddenly there's me. The little tide. He trains me anyway."

Annabeth bumped his shoulder. "You're allowed to feel weird about it. And he's allowed to be jealous and still love you. Like —" She hesitated. "Like I felt in my original life when Thalia came back from being a tree. I was so happy and so mad that she'd been gone and so scared she'd think I was a replacement kid in her cabin. It was all of it."

Percy stared at her. "You never said that."

"You never asked with your eyebrows like that," she said, and sketched his eyebrows furrowed. "See? That's the look."

Down the beach, Triton was stretching with Tyson, who was trying to mimic his lunges and falling over. Clarisse and Silena were whispering strategy by the rocks. Thalia was braiding her hair back, lightning flickering at her fingertips.

Annabeth turned to a fresh page. Her pencil slowed. Her eyelids drooped.

"Hey," Percy said softly. "Lean."

"What?"

"Lean. You're tired. Sketch later."

She didn't argue, which meant she really was tired. She leaned her head against his shoulder, sketchbook balanced on her knees, pencil still moving in small strokes — the curve of Triton's conch, the line of the wave.

Percy sat very still, afraid to breathe wrong and disturb her. The wind moved her hair across his cheek. His heart thumped loud enough he was sure Tyson could hear it in Atlantis.

"Percy?" she murmured.

"Yeah?"

"When we spar him — stay close to me? Your water steadies my lightning. I felt it earlier."

"Always," he said. "I've got you."

She hummed, half-asleep, and her pencil trailed off the page. Percy gently took the sketchbook from her lap before it could slide into the sand and set it on the rock. He draped his towel over her shoulders.

Thalia walked up, saw them, and stopped. For once she didn't tease. She just nodded at Percy, something serious and approving in her electric-blue eyes, and tossed him a square of ambrosia.

"For luck, little brother," she said quietly. "Take care of her in there."

"I will," Percy said.

Thalia squeezed Annabeth's ankle once and went to join Clarisse and Silena.

Percy let Annabeth doze for ten minutes, his shoulder going numb, the sea hissing in and out. Then Triton blew the conch, long and low, and Annabeth jerked awake.

"Time?" she asked, wiping her cheek.

"Time," Percy said, handing her back her sketchbook and pulling her to her feet. He handed her the refilled canteen again. "Drink first. Then we drown my brother."

She drank, grinning, and tucked the canteen into her jacket. "Together?"

"Together."

---

The final spar took place in the cove at low tide, where the rocks made a natural arena and the water was knee-deep and clear.

Triton stood in the center with his trident, barefoot, water coiling around his calves like loyal dogs. He looked every inch the prince now — calm, centered, dangerous.

Arrayed against him in a loose crescent: Percy with Riptide uncapped, its bronze blade gleaming; Clarisse with her spear and a water whip coiled around her left forearm; Silena with her short sword and a flat disc of water spinning over her other wrist like a shield; Thalia with her bow slung and a spear of crackling lightning in her hand; Annabeth beside Percy with a dagger in one hand and sparks crawling up the other, her stance low and kelp-like.

"Team Sparrow," Travis Stoll announced from the rocks, where the whole camp had gathered. "Versus His Barnacleness! Winner gets the last brownie at dinner!"

"The kitchen isn't making brownies today, you idiot," Connor said.

"Winner gets honor!" Grover bleated.

Triton raised his trident. "Rules. First to force me to one knee, or to disarm me, wins. I will not go easy. If you fall, roll clear. Tyson will pull you out. Begin when the wave breaks."

They waited. The Sound breathed in. Out. A wave lifted, curled, broke white on the rocks.

"Now!" Clarisse screamed, and they moved.

It was chaos, and then it wasn't.

Clarisse charged straight in, spear low, water whip snapping for Triton's legs. Triton sidestepped without looking, caught her spear shaft on his trident and turned her — straight into Percy's rising wave. Percy caught her, lifted her, threw her — not at Triton, but over him. Clarisse twisted mid-air, screaming with delight, and came down behind Triton, sweeping for his back.

Triton spun, grinning. "Good!"

Silena moved like water itself, circling wide, her disc-shield catching the spray Triton threw up and turning it aside. She called openings nonstop — "Percy, left! Clarisse, he's weighting right! Thalia, now!"

Thalia and Annabeth hung back at the edge, hands linked for a second to sync. Percy felt the air tighten.

"Percy! Curtain!" Thalia shouted.

Percy ripped a sheet of water up between them and Triton — ten feet high, trembling. Triton raised an eyebrow, recognizing his own lesson, and braced.

"Annie, on three!" Thalia said. "One — two —"

Annabeth's lightning hit the curtain at the same instant as Thalia's, gold and blue-white braiding together. The water lit up like sunrise. Percy screamed with the effort of holding it, knees buckling, kelp-not-oak, kelp-not-oak —

He threw the electrified curtain forward.

Triton met it with his trident raised and his own wall of water. Light and sea collided with a boom that knocked gulls off the rocks and sent a plume of steam fifty feet high. The shockwave flattened everyone’s hair and filled their mouths with the taste of pennies and salt.

When the steam cleared, Triton was still standing — but he had slid back six feet, his heels dug trenches in the sand, and his trident was smoking.

"Again!" Silena cried, darting in while he was braced. Her sword tapped his vambrace. Clarisse's whip cracked around his ankle. Percy sent a low undertow to pull his feet out.

Triton laughed out loud, delighted, and stomped. The undertow shattered. He spun his trident in a full circle, and a ring of water exploded outward, picking up all three of them and tossing them like driftwood.

Percy landed hard, rolled, came up sputtering. Clarisse landed on her feet, swearing. Silena landed in Tyson's waiting arms, giggling.

"Is that all, minnows?" Triton taunted. There was sweat on his brow now. His breathing was faster.

Annabeth looked at Percy across the churned water. Her hair was standing straight up. Her eyes were fierce. She mouthed: Together?

Percy nodded. He waded to her side and put his hand on the small of her back, steadying, feeding her clean water around her feet to ground her. She leaned into him, just slightly.

Thalia saw and grinned ferally. "Oh, I like this. Sky-sea triangle! Silena, call it! Clarisse, be the hammer!"

Silena understood instantly. "Percy — wall left! Clarisse — wait — wait — now!"

Clarisse charged. Triton turned to meet her, trident sweeping — and Percy dropped the water under Triton's left foot into a sudden pit while Thalia struck from the right with a bolt that Triton had to catch on his trident.

The trident, for one second, was busy conducting lightning into the sea.

That second was all Annabeth needed.

She ran forward, light on her feet, Percy feeding her a slick of water under her shoes so she slid faster, and she slammed both hands — sparks and all — into the flat of Triton's breastplate where seawater still clung.

The charge grounded through the wet bronze with a sharp crack. Triton's whole body jerked. His knee buckled —

He caught himself on his trident, one knee hovering an inch off the sand, eyes wide.

Percy saw his brother's face. Saw the choice there. Triton could recover. He could surge up, knock Annabeth flat, win clean. He was stronger than all of them put together.

Instead Triton looked at Annabeth — thirteen years old, shaking, triumphant, Percy's hand on her back holding her up — and at Percy behind her, and at Clarisse with her spear at his back and Silena with her sword at his throat and Thalia with lightning crackling at his temple, all of them breathing hard, all of them together —

And Triton let his knee touch the sand.

The beach exploded.

"SPARROWS WIN! SPARROWS WIN!" The Stolls tumbled down the rocks. Tyson waded in bellowing with joy and lifted Silena and Annabeth both off their feet. Grover played a wild riff on his pipes. Even Mr. D clapped twice, slowly.

Clarisse stood stunned, then threw her spear down and whooped. "We got him! We actually got him!"

Silena hugged Thalia, then Clarisse, then Percy, crying a little. "We sang! We sang!"

Thalia pulled Annabeth out of Tyson's grip and spun her around. "One and a half seconds to a full discharge! Did you see his face?"

Annabeth was laughing and crying at once, her hands still tingling. She turned and found Percy, and without thinking he caught her in a hug that lifted her clean off her feet and spun her once before he remembered they were in front of everyone and set her down fast, ears burning.

"You did it, Wise Girl," he said hoarsely.

"We did it," she corrected, breathless. "You held me up. Literally."

Triton rose slowly, shaking out his stinging arm. He planted his trident in the sand and looked at the five of them — muddy, soaked, grinning — and something in his proud, guarded face cracked wide open.

Percy held his breath. Was his brother angry? Had they embarrassed him?

Triton walked forward and, to everyone's shock, knelt again — deliberately this time — in front of Percy, and pressed his forehead to Percy's, Atlantean-style, water beading between them.

"Well fought, little brother," Triton said quietly, for Percy alone. "You lead like tide. You pull others with you, not under you. I —" He swallowed. "I have not always been a good brother to you. Today you were a good brother to me. You let me lose with honor."

Percy's throat closed. Around them the cheering faded to a hum. "You let us win," he whispered.

"I let you finish," Triton corrected, with a ghost of his old smirk. "There is a difference. Do not tell the court."

He stood and turned to the others, clasping Clarisse's forearm warrior-to-warrior until she stood straighter than Percy had ever seen her. He bowed to Silena and kissed her hand, making her blush furiously while Clarisse gagged. He bumped fists with Thalia, lightning snapping between their knuckles.

Then he stopped before Annabeth. "Little sky," he said. "May I see?"

Annabeth blinked. "See what?"

"The book you have been hiding all day. The one you draw in when you think no one watches."

Annabeth went scarlet. She clutched her sketchbook to her chest. Percy thought she might refuse. Then, slowly, she opened it and turned it around.

There was Triton on the page — not the prince, not the statue, but the brother: lunging with Tyson hanging off his waist, laughing; correcting Percy's stance with his foot, exasperated and fond; holding Thalia's lightning in a curtain of water, awed.

Triton stared for a long moment. His hand hovered over the page, not touching.

"You see clearly," he said at last, voice rough. "Keep this sight. It is rarer than power."

Annabeth tore the page out — carefully, along the perforation — and held it out. "For you. For Atlantis. So they know you taught us."

Triton took it like it was made of glass. He rolled it and slid it into the waterproof case at his belt. "I will hang it in my chambers," he said. "And lie that I am handsomer in person."

Everyone laughed, the tension breaking like a wave.

---

At sunset Triton left the way he came, riding a green swell back out past the barrier, his trident raised in farewell. Tyson swam with him to the edge of the wards, waving the whole way back.

The Sparrows — as the Stolls were already calling them, and selling T-shirts — sat in a row on the drying sand, too tired to move, sharing a blanket and a bag of pretzels Grover had produced from somewhere.

Clarisse's head was on Silena's shoulder. Silena's head was on Thalia's shoulder. Thalia's boots were in Percy's lap. Annabeth's head was on Percy's shoulder again, her sketchbook open to a new page: five figures in a crescent around a tall one kneeling, light and water braided between them. Underneath she had written, in careful Greek: *We sang.*

Percy sipped from the canteen — still full of sweet water — and passed it to her without being asked. She drank and passed it back, their fingers brushing.

"Same time tomorrow?" Clarisse mumbled, half-asleep. "He said three mornings. I'm gonna kill him tomorrow."

"Bring it, Clarisse," Percy said.

Silena yawned. "My arms hurt. My hair smells like lightning. Today was perfect."

Thalia threw a pretzel at Percy. "Hey. Tide. Thanks for grounding my sister. You kept her safe in there."

Percy looked down at Annabeth, dozing against him, pencil slack in her hand, and felt that warm tug under his ribs again, stronger than any current.

"Always," he said. "She's — she's my crew. My —" He fumbled. He knew the word girlfriend. In front of her sister, it still caught in his throat.

Thalia's smile was soft and unbothered, sister to him by choice. "I know, little brother. I know."

The sun sank into the Sound, turning the water Triton's green-gold. The tide came in, foaming around their feet, and none of them moved.

They had drawn with a god. They had sung.

Tomorrow they would train again.
