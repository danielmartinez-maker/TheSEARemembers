## Chapter 22: Storm Training


Annabeth Grace woke up before the conch horn.

That almost never happened. At Camp Half-Blood, mornings belonged to the Apollo kids with their too-loud singing and the Ares kids with their too-loud everything else, and Annabeth usually buried her head under her pillow until the last possible second and then sprinted for breakfast with one sandal on the wrong foot. Athena kids were supposed to be dignified. Zeus kids, apparently, were supposed to be disasters before nine a.m.

She sat up in Cabin One and stared at the ceiling for a moment. The cabin was too big for three people plus a guest cot. Thalia had claimed the bunk by the window and turned the sill into a graveyard of busted electronics — three watches, a transistor radio, a flashlight that only worked when she was in a good mood. Jason, ten and earnest, had arrived the previous afternoon for his first in-person visit since their Iris-message. Camp Jupiter had approved a supervised week, and he had a cot made up beside the door, his blanket tucked so tight around himself he looked like a burrito. And Annabeth had the bunk closest to the door, because she liked to be the first one out if anything happened.

She pressed her palm to her chest. Her heart was going fast for no reason. Not nightmare-fast. Storm-fast. That buzzy, restless feeling under her skin that she'd had since arriving in this twelve-year-old body, old memories confronting powers everybody else thought she should know.

In her first life — no. In the other life. The one where she was Annabeth Chase, daughter of Athena, architect, the girl who held up the sky. In that life her hands had been steady. She solved things. She built things. She read blueprints like other kids read comics. Her mind was her weapon and she knew exactly how to use it.

In this life she was Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus, and her weapon crackled when she got nervous and made all the metal doors at camp hum.

She slipped out of bed, pulled on her camp shirt and shorts, laced her sneakers, and shoved her hair back with a headband. Thalia's bunk was empty, blanket thrown back. A note on the pillow in Thalia's slashing handwriting: *Hill. Sunrise. Bring jacket. — T*

Annabeth smiled despite the nerves twisting in her stomach. Thalia Grace doing planning. Thalia Grace writing a note instead of just kicking the bunk and yelling "up." That meant today mattered to her.

She grabbed her denim jacket and went out into the blue-gray morning.

The grass was wet. Mist hung low over the strawberry fields. The bronze dragon coiled around the Big House weathervane glinted faintly pink. Annabeth jogged up Half-Blood Hill, past the boundary pine — which still made her throat tighten every time, because in her remembered life a tree on this hill had been Thalia, saving her friends — and found her sister sitting cross-legged at the top, facing the sunrise over Long Island Sound.

Thalia looked different at sunrise. At sixteen she was all sharp edges and eyeliner and don't-touch-me energy. But in the early light with her black hair loose down her back and no boots on, her socks damp from the grass, she looked softer. Younger. Almost approachable.

"You're late," Thalia said without turning around.

"The sun's not even up," Annabeth said.

"You're still late." Thalia patted the grass beside her. "Sit. Jacket on. It's cold and you're going to be sitting still, which you hate."

Annabeth sat. The grass soaked through her shorts immediately. "Charming. Is this how you teach? Insults first?"

"This is how I love," Thalia said dryly. "You ready to talk about today?"

Annabeth pulled her knees up. Below them, camp was waking. Tiny figures moving between cabins. Smoke starting from the forge. Someone laughing too loud by the showers. Normal sounds. Safe sounds.

"I'm nervous," she admitted quietly. It was easier to say to Thalia than to anyone else. Thalia listened. Really listened. She couldn't remember the other life Annabeth had described, but she knew what Annabeth had told her — Athena's daughter, the one with plans inside plans — and she knew the sister beside her now. "What if I can't do it? What if I'm just... bad at this? I've been trying for weeks and all I do is make the lights flicker and shock Percy when he holds my hand."

"Percy likes getting shocked by you," Thalia said. "He told me. He said it was like holding a firefly."

Annabeth stared at her. "He said that to *you*?"

"He tells me stuff. He has two big sisters, but apparently he needs his girlfriend's sister teasing him too. Poor kid." Thalia bumped her shoulder against Annabeth's. Static snapped between their jackets, a bright blue-white pop. Both of them laughed. "See? You're already doing it. You just don't know how to steer yet."

"In the other — before. I always knew how. With... you know. Thinking. Planning. I would look at a problem and see the structure. Now I look at the sky and feel something huge and I don't have words for it."

"Good," Thalia said. "Words will get in your way. Today no words. Today breathing."

Annabeth groaned. "Breathing? I thought we were doing lightning."

"Breathing first. Lightning later. Maybe. If you're good." Thalia shifted to face her, tucking her feet under her. "Look. When I first got my powers, I thought it was all about being angry. Because that's when it came out. I got mad, sky blew up. Easy. But that's not control. That's just... exploding. Jason's starting the same way — he gets excited and the forks on the table rattle. Real control is quiet. You have to listen before you can talk."

That sounded suspiciously wise for Thalia Grace, who once headbutted a Laistrygonian giant because he cut in line at the climbing wall.

"Who taught you that?" Annabeth asked.

Thalia's expression flickered. "Someone who had to learn it hard. Doesn't matter. Breathe with me. In through your nose for four. Hold for four. Out for six. Longer out than in. That's what calms the charge."

They breathed.

The sun crested the water and spilled gold across the Sound. Gulls cried. The mist burned off the fields. Annabeth tried to count and breathe and not think about whether she was doing it wrong, which was itself thinking about doing it wrong, which made her shoulders creep up toward her ears.

"Drop your shoulders," Thalia murmured. "Unclench your jaw. You're doing that Athena thing where you try to solve breathing."

"I am not—"

"You are. I can hear you thinking. Stop thinking. Feel."

Annabeth huffed, then tried again. In for four. The air smelled like wet grass and salt and pine resin. Hold for four. Her heartbeat thudded in her ears. Out for six. Slow. Her fingertips tingled.

"Again," Thalia said softly. Her voice had gone low and steady, nothing like her usual snap. Patient in a way she wasn't about anything else — not sparring, not capture the flag, not waiting in line for the showers. Only this. Only for Annabeth and Jason, when it came to the sky. "In. Two. Three. Four. Hold. Feel that buzz in your teeth? That's yours. Don't push it away. Just notice it. Out. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six."

They did it five times. Ten times. Annabeth lost count and stopped caring. The buzz settled from frantic bees to a low hum, like a refrigerator in another room. The hairs on her arms lifted slightly. When she opened her eyes, tiny blue sparks were crawling between her knuckles, delicate as spider silk.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, look."

"I see it," Thalia said, grinning wide. "Don't grab at it. Just let it hang out. That's you saying hi."

Annabeth held her hands up. The sparks danced, branching and rejoining. They didn't hurt. They felt like cold soda fizz. She laughed, bright and surprised, and the sparks flared and popped out.

"Hey! I lost it."

"You laughed it away. That's fine. Laughing moves air fast. Storms don't like fast happy air until you teach them it's safe." Thalia flopped back on the grass and stared at the sky. "Again. We'll do it until breakfast if we have to. I've got nothing but time and Jason snores."

Annabeth lay back beside her. Side by side, watching clouds gather and dissolve. Two daughters of Zeus on a hill that had once held a dragon and a fleece and so many battles. For a little while, just sisters breathing.

"Thalia?" she said.

"Yeah?"

"Thanks for not making it weird. That I'm... different now. That I'm not who I was."

Thalia was quiet for a beat. Then she reached over without looking and found Annabeth's hand and squeezed it. Another static pop, softer this time. "You're still the girl who figures things out. You're just finding another way now. And frizzier. Honestly the hair is the weirdest part."

Annabeth snorted and shoved her. They dissolved into giggles as the sun climbed higher, and somewhere below the conch horn finally blew, thin and distant, calling them down to pancakes.

Thalia Grace was a terrible teacher about almost everything.

She knew this about herself. She'd tried to teach Jason to swordfight and nearly cried when he kept dropping his sword because his hands were small. She'd tried to teach cabinmates to play guitar and snapped the high E string on day two. She had no patience for slow. She had no patience for careful. She wanted to run at things headfirst and hope her skull was thicker than the problem.

But this — sitting on a hill at dawn teaching her little sister how not to be scared of the storm inside her — this she could do. This she would sit still for.

Because nobody had sat still for her.

When Thalia had first started zapping things at seven, before Camp, before Luke, her mom had screamed and thrown a plastic cup at her. Three-year-old Annabeth had hidden behind a chair. Later, when they'd run with Luke and her little sister — the seven-year-old Zeus kid with tangled hair and enormous eyes who held Thalia's hand through thunderstorms — she'd been terrified to touch anyone. She'd slept with her hands tucked under her armpits so she wouldn't shock little Annabeth in her sleep. She had been afraid that every uncontrolled spark made her dangerous, and it took years of patient practice before she could let anyone take her hand without thinking about the next shock.

It took years of camp practice, burned-out lanterns, embarrassed apologies, and finding people who didn't flinch from her hands to unlearn that. Chiron kept asking her to try smaller, quieter. Annabeth kept reaching for her anyway.

So now, with her Annabeth — twelve, bright, furious, carrying unfamiliar memories while learning how to use the sky again — Thalia was determined to be the teacher she never got.

After breakfast, they took over the far corner of the arena. Not the sparring ring — the quiet practice yard behind the shed where the straw dummies went to retire. Thalia had cleared it with Chiron. No metal weapons. No audience. Just a circle of chalk on packed dirt, a wooden stool, and a storm lantern Chiron had lent from the Big House porch.

Annabeth arrived with her hair in a French braid (Silena had done it, because Silena could braid while running and talking about someone's love life at the same time) and her jacket tied around her waist and a look of grim determination that Thalia recognized from years of arguments, monster scares, and impossible projects.

"You look like you're about to storm the principal's office," Thalia said.

"I feel like that," Annabeth admitted. "Breakfast was awful. I tried to pour orange juice and the pitcher sparked. Connor Stoll says I'm going to be the first kid to get banned from the mess hall for being a fire hazard."

"Connor Stoll got banned from the mess hall for putting hot sauce in the milk," Thalia said. "You're fine. Come here. Chalk circle. Stand in the middle."

Annabeth stepped in. The chalk was white against brown dirt. The lantern sat on the stool just outside the circle.

"What's the lantern for?" Annabeth asked.

"That's the goal. End of the day, maybe end of the week, you're going to light that lantern without touching it, without a storm overhead, without yelling. Just... ask."

"Ask the lantern?"

"Ask the charge. There's always charge. Even on a blue-sky day like today, the air has it. You felt it this morning. Those little sparks? That's you hearing it. Now we're going to practice answering back on purpose."

Thalia stepped into the circle with her. Close. Shoulder to shoulder. She could feel the faint lift of Annabeth's hair, the crackle at the edge of her own vision like heat shimmer.

"Rule one," Thalia said, holding up a finger. "Feet on the ground. Always. Sky kids want to float when they get excited — literally, I did it once and nearly got stuck on top of the cabin doorframe — but grounding helps. Feel your soles. Toes, arches, heels. You're plugged in."

Annabeth wiggled her sneakers in the dirt. "Okay. Plugged in."

"Rule two. Breathe like this morning. Four in, four hold, six out. Do it now."

They breathed together. Thalia watched Annabeth's face smooth out by the third cycle. Good. The kid learned fast. Of course she did. She was Annabeth.

"Rule three. Small. Everyone wants lightning bolts on day one. No bolts. We do static. Hold out your hand."

Annabeth held out her right hand, palm up. Thalia held her own hand a few inches above it, palm down.

"Don't reach for me," Thalia murmured. "Just breathe and notice the space between. There's a river there. You don't have to build the river. It's already there."

A minute passed. Sweat beaded on Annabeth's forehead. Her hand trembled slightly from holding it up.

"Stop trying so hard," Thalia said gently. "Soft eyes. You're staring at my hand like it's a math test."

"I can't help it," Annabeth whispered. "I want to get it right."

"I know. But sky doesn't do right. Sky does... willing. Like coaxing a cat. You don't grab the cat."

"Did you just compare my powers to a cat?"

"I compare everything to cats and food. It's called being well-adjusted." Thalia grinned. "Keep breathing."

And then — *snap*. A tiny blue arc jumped between their palms, no thicker than thread, with a sound like a knuckle cracking. Annabeth gasped and yanked her hand back.

"I did it! Did you see—"

"I saw. I felt. Nice. Little zap." Thalia shook out her hand, smiling so wide her cheeks hurt. Pride swelled hot behind her ribs. "Again. And this time don't flinch. Flinching tells the charge you're scared of it. You're not scared. You're just... surprised. There's a difference."

They did it again. And again. Snap. Snap-snap. A rhythm. Annabeth's face lit brighter each time. Her braid started to lift at the edges, strands escaping and floating.

"Okay, hotshot," Thalia said after the fifth clean spark. "Now you make one by yourself. Rub your sneakers in the dirt a little — yeah, like that, build some friction — now hold your hands apart and think about bringing them close without touching. Slow."

Annabeth shuffled her feet, held her hands six inches apart, breathed. In. Hold. Out. Her brow furrowed. Her lips moved, counting.

*Snap-crackle.* A jagged little fork danced between her palms and vanished. The smell of ozone puffed in the still air.

"YES!" Annabeth shouted, jumping. Her hair lifted fully now, haloing around her head. "Did you— Thalia, did you see that?"

"I saw, Sparky." Thalia laughed and ruffled the floating hair, getting a shock for her trouble that made her yelp. "Okay, okay, hair check. You're entering mad-scientist territory."

Annabeth patted her hair down, giggling. "How bad?"

"Like you stuck your finger in a socket. A cute socket." Thalia pulled a rubber band off her wrist. "Here. Double braids won't float as much. Turn around."

She re-braided her sister's hair right there in the chalk circle, fingers quick from years of doing her own and Annabeth's, and now Jason's on his visit. The intimacy of it caught her off guard — the trust of turning your back, the smallness of Annabeth's neck, the way she hummed tunelessly while she waited. Thalia had to swallow hard.

*Don't get sappy, Grace. You'll short-circuit.*

"There," she said, tying it off. "Now. Break time before you fry your brain. Percy said he'd bring—"

As if summoned by his name — and Thalia was starting to suspect the sea kid had some kind of Annabeth-radar — footsteps crunched around the shed.

"Snack delivery!" came Percy's voice. "Permission to enter the high-voltage zone?"

Percy Jackson had learned many important things in the years he remembered and the weeks since he arrived in this twelve-year-old body: how to breathe underwater, how to swordfight with a pen, how to cook blue pancakes with smaller hands in the camp kitchen, and how to tell from fifty yards away when his girlfriend was getting frustrated with herself.

Annabeth was getting frustrated with herself. He could tell by the way she was standing in that chalk circle with her arms crossed and one sneaker digging a trench in the dirt, even though Thalia was talking calm and her hair was in two ridiculous sticking-out braids that made her look like a baby dragon.

He came around the shed carrying a paper bag from the mess hall and tried not to grin too hard, because grinning too hard would derail the lesson. The whole camp knew Percy Jackson and Annabeth Grace were together. Friends. Training partners. Fellow twelve-year-olds who happened to stare at each other during campfire songs. What most of camp didn't know was how long they'd already been choosing each other, or why watching her learn something new made him so absurdly proud.

"Snack delivery!" he called. "Permission to enter the high-voltage zone?"

Thalia waved him in. "Granted. But wipe your feet. This is a sacred space."

"Sacred chalk circle," Percy said solemnly, stepping in and scuffing his sneakers exaggeratedly. "Very sacred. I brought sacred offerings."

He upended the bag onto the grass outside the circle: three blue muffins (Tyson's recipe, smuggled via Iris-message from Atlantis — long story), two apples, a hunk of cheese wrapped in wax paper, and three juice boxes.

Annabeth's face transformed. "Are those *blue*?"

"Blueberry. Mostly blue. Tyson added extra food coloring and a note that says 'FOR ANNABETH'S LIGHTNING BRAIN.'" Percy handed her a muffin. Their fingers brushed. *Zzt.* A sharp little shock bit his fingertip. "Ow! Yep. Still spicy."

"Sorry!" Annabeth said, mortified. "I can't turn it off. Everything I touch today—"

"It's okay." Percy sucked his finger, eyes dancing. "I've been shocked by worse. Triton showed me an electric eel on my last lesson. I kept my hands to myself. That one could have made my whole arm go numb for an hour. You're way cuter than the eel."

Thalia gagged. "Flirting with faint electrical burns. Romance is dead."

"We're not flirting," Percy and Annabeth said at the same time, too fast, too innocent.

Thalia gave them a flat look that said *Sure, and I'm the Queen of England.* But she let them squirm, because their flirting was funnier when they pretended it wasn't flirting. She knew what they'd told her about being seventeen, wars survived side by side, a future chosen together. She couldn't remember any of it. She could see the way they looked at each other, and she knew which parts of their story they wanted to keep private. Camp gossip was loud enough without handing it their grief.

So they did the snack-support thing. Percy sat cross-legged just outside the circle and narrated like a sports commentator while Annabeth ate her muffin in three bites and Thalia stole the cheese.

"So what'd I miss?" Percy asked. "Did she shoot lightning from her eyes yet?"

"I made static," Annabeth said proudly, muffin crumbs on her chin. Percy resisted the extremely strong urge to wipe them off. Distracting her could wait. Boyfriends who remembered holding her hand in Tartarus could survive a few muffin crumbs. He stuffed his own muffin into his mouth instead. "Real sparks! Between my hands!"

"That's awesome, Wise Girl." The old nickname came out warm and familiar. Thalia's grin made him cough. "I mean — smart. That's smart. Sparks are smart."

Thalia rolled her eyes so hard Percy worried she'd sprain something. "He calls you Wise Girl?"

"It's— it's from training," Annabeth said quickly, ears pink. "Because I'm... wise. About training. I'm wise about training."

"You're wise about everything," Percy said, then realized how that sounded. "I mean—"

"You two are terrible liars," Thalia said cheerfully. "It's my favorite thing about you. Eat your snacks. Then back to work. Percy, you can stay if you promise not to distract her by being... you."

"Being me is distracting?" Percy asked.

"To her? Extremely." Thalia stood, brushing crumbs off her jeans. "Okay. Muffin break over. Round two."

Round two was harder.

Thalia had Annabeth try to sustain a spark — hold it, not just pop it. "Think of it like holding a note when you sing," Thalia said. "Not louder. Longer."

Annabeth tried. Her face scrunched. Her braids lifted again. Tiny crackles sputtered between her fingers and died.

"Argh!" Annabeth shook her hands. "Why can't I hold it? This morning I could feel it for like a whole minute."

"Because this morning you weren't trying to hold it. You were just noticing it." Thalia glanced at Percy. "Distraction might actually help. Percy, talk to her. Tell her something boring."

"Boring?" Percy said. "I'm never boring."

"Tell her about Atlantis plumbing. That put me to sleep last time."

Percy launched into a detailed explanation of how the palace in Atlantis filtered whale song out of the water pipes so the showers didn't sing. Annabeth giggled — and as she relaxed, a steady blue filament stretched between her palms, humming faintly, holding for three full seconds before fading.

"I did it!" she breathed. "Three seconds!"

"Three seconds!" Percy cheered, clapping. "That's longer than I can hold my breath when Clarisse sneaks up on me!"

Thalia nodded, satisfied. "See? Boring Percy is useful Percy."

"Hey!"

They kept going. Percy kept talking — about the blue-cookie recipe he and Annabeth had been trying to get right, about the old story of a hellhound who'd once tried to eat a canoe, about his half-sister Clarisse teaching him to throw a javelin backwards by accident. Annabeth kept sparking, longer each time. Five seconds. Seven. The air smelled like rain even though the sky was cloudless.

Then Grover showed up.

Grover Underwood arrived at a trot, his crutches long abandoned now that his legs were stronger (though he still carried them strapped to his pack "for emotional support"), his horns just starting to bud under his curly hair, a canvas tote banging against his hip.

"Am I late? I'm late," he panted. "Chiron kept me — goat politics — juniper nymphs filed a complaint about the strawberry fertilizer — anyway I'm here! Moral support! I brought — oh no, I dropped the tambourine — hold on —"

He dug in his tote and produced a slightly squashed orange, a pinecone, and a hand-lettered sign that said GO ANNABETH!!! with three exclamation points and a lightning bolt drawn in yellow highlighter.

Percy burst out laughing. "Grover, that's amazing."

"I made it during archery. Chiron said my form was 'enthusiastic,' which I think means bad, so I quit early to make the sign." Grover held it up proudly, then looked at Annabeth in the chalk circle with sparks drifting off her braids. "Whoa. You look awesome. Like a dandelion. A scary electric dandelion."

Annabeth touched her hair self-consciously. "It's floating again, isn't it?"

"A little," Thalia said. "It's cute. Means you're charged. Grover, sit with Percy. Wave the sign. Be encouraging but quiet."

"I can be quietly encouraging," Grover whispered loudly. "GO ANNABETH." He waved the sign. The pinecone fell out of his tote.

Annabeth laughed, and lightning laughed with her — a bright crackle that made all three of them jump.

"Okay!" Thalia clapped. "New drill. Moving target. Percy, stand up."

Percy stood, brushing grass off his shorts. "Am I the target? Do I get a shield?"

"You get your sea-kid reflexes. Annabeth, I want you to try to send a spark *toward* something. Not at Percy — at the stool. See the lantern? Aim for the air just above it. Don't touch it yet. Just... point and breathe and let a little bit go that way."

Annabeth turned to face the stool. She pointed her right hand, fingers together. She breathed. Percy could see her counting on her lips. Four in. Hold. Six out.

Nothing happened.

She tried again. Her hand shook. A faint pop near her wrist, nothing more.

"It's okay," Thalia said quickly. "Aiming is the hardest part. It took me months to—"

Annabeth's jaw tightened. Percy recognized that look. That was the other-life Annabeth look, the Athena look, the *I will solve this or die* look. She planted her feet harder, sucked in a breath, and *shoved* her hand forward like she was pushing a door.

*CRACK.*

A white-blue arc ripped from her palm, forked midair, and slammed into the nearest large conductive object.

Which was Percy.

Because Percy, son of Poseidon, twelve-year-old boy who was mostly salt water in a camp shirt, was basically a walking lightning rod.

"GAH!" Percy yelped as every hair on his head stood straight up. Blue sparks skittered across his shoulders and popped in his ears. He smelled burnt sugar. His teeth buzzed.

"PERCY!" Annabeth shrieked.

Grover dropped his sign. "Oh no oh no oh no — are you okay — do you need water — wait, water's bad for electricity — do you need *dirt*? Lie down! Roll in dirt!"

Percy stood very still, blinking. Then he looked at his reflection in the side of the lantern glass.

His black hair, usually a windswept mess, was now a spherical black dandelion puff. Straight out in all directions. Frizzy to the point of comedy. A few lingering sparks crawled through it and died with tiny *tinks*.

A beat of silence.

Then Thalia made a sound like a teakettle trying not to whistle.

"Don't," Percy warned, lips twitching. "Don't you dare—"

Thalia lost it. She howled with laughter, doubling over, slapping her knee. Grover followed a second later, wheezing and holding his sides. Annabeth stood with both hands over her mouth, eyes huge and wet, torn between horror and hilarity.

"Percy, I'm so sorry—" she gasped. "Your hair— oh gods, your hair—"

Percy patted his hair. It crunched with static and stayed straight out. He looked at Annabeth's stricken face, at Thalia crying laughing, at Grover waving his sign upside down, and made an executive decision.

He struck a pose. Chin up. Hands on hips. Hair blazing in all directions like a sea-urchin god.

"Behold," he announced solemnly. "The mighty Storm Lord Perseus. Tremble before my frizz."

That broke Annabeth. She laughed until she cried, until new sparks showered off her shoulders and fizzled in the grass. She stumbled out of the circle and threw her arms around Percy — and got shocked again, and he got shocked again, and they both yelped and laughed harder.

"You're smoking," she wheezed, patting at his shoulder where a faint wisp rose.

"It's fine. I'm moisturized. Sea kids don't burn." He grinned down at her, forgetting for a second that they were in the middle of a lesson. His forehead nearly touched hers. Her gray eyes were bright with laughter and leftover lightning. "You got aim, at least. You hit exactly what you didn't mean to. That's... precision-adjacent."

"Precision-adjacent," she repeated, giggling. "You're such a dork."

"Your dork," he said softly, so only she could hear, and her smile went soft and secret and perfect.

Thalia cleared her throat loudly. "Okay, lovebirds. Training partners with extremely distracting eye contact. Break it up. Percy, go dunk your head in the creek. Annabeth, back in the circle. We're going to talk about what happened."

Percy ruffled his electrified hair — it crackled — and saluted. "Yes, ma'am, Storm Captain, ma'am." He jogged toward the creek, hair bouncing like a dark halo, Grover trotting after him offering the orange as a peace offering.

Annabeth watched him go, still smiling. Then she turned to Thalia, smile fading. "I hurt him. I could have really hurt him."

"You startled him," Thalia corrected firmly. "There's a difference. And he walked it off. He's a Poseidon kid, Annabeth. It takes more than a static sneeze to hurt him. But you're right to take it seriously. That's why we learn control. Come here."

They sat in the grass inside the circle. Thalia took Annabeth's hands — both of them felt buzzy and hot — and held them loosely.

"Tell me what happened right before," Thalia said. "Not what you did. What you felt."

Annabeth stared at their joined hands. "I was... mad. Not at anyone. At myself. I wanted it so bad. I pushed."

"Yeah. Pushing is a sea thing, maybe. Or an Athena thing — force the solution. Sky doesn't like pushing. Sky likes... inviting." Thalia thought for a moment. "When I get angry now, I don't throw it. I breathe it out slow, like fogging a mirror. Try that next time. If you feel the mad coming, make your exhale longer. Give it somewhere to go that isn't Percy."

Annabeth nodded slowly. "Longer out than in."

"Exactly. And hey." Thalia nudged her. "You aimed. It went somewhere. That's huge for day one. Most kids just make their fillings hurt for a month. You threw a bolt six feet. On purpose-ish."

"On purpose-ish," Annabeth echoed with a watery laugh. "Can we keep going? I want to try again. Softer this time."

"That's my girl." Thalia stood and pulled her up. "Percy! Get back here! We need our lightning rod!"

"Coming!" Percy shouted from the creek, hair now wet and flat and back to normal, Grover attempting to dry it with the GO ANNABETH sign like a fan. "Do I need a helmet?"

"Yes," Thalia and Annabeth said together.

At noon, Chiron arrived to assess the training yard. Thalia had promised him a supervised session with a clear stop procedure. He found Annabeth sitting on a low bench with her hands in her lap, staring at the lantern as though she'd been asked to move the entire sky into it.

"How is the practice going?" he asked.

"Slowly," Thalia said.

"That's a description, not necessarily a problem."

Annabeth looked up. "I made a spark. Then I tried again and couldn't do it."

"What changed?"

"I was thinking about making it happen instead of letting it happen. Thalia said I was trying too hard."

Chiron examined the chalk circle and the wooden stool. The ground was dry, the area had been cleared of loose metal and a bucket of sand stood within reach of the instructor. He approved the preparations, then asked whether the first experiment had caused any pain.

"No," Annabeth said. "It surprised me."

"Then let's inspect what you actually did rather than what you wanted to do."

She described the position of her hands, Thalia's breathing instructions and the sensation of pressure in the space between them. Chiron made a note. He did not ask her to produce another spark while he watched.

"Why aren't you testing me?" Annabeth asked.

"Because you've already given me useful information. You noticed what happened before the spark and after it. The next step is learning whether you can repeat that experience without forcing it."

She frowned. "And if I can't?"

"Then we may need a different approach. Powers are not measured only by whether a demonstration succeeds on command."

Thalia leaned against the fence and watched the exchange. She was relieved that Chiron had not turned the session into a spectacle. She had promised Annabeth this would be safe, and some part of her feared that a formal assessment would make her sister feel she'd failed if nothing happened.

Chiron looked toward her. "What have you learned as the instructor?"

Thalia hadn't expected the question. "She needs breaks. Her hand gets tired when she's trying to hold it still. And when she gets frustrated, she goes very quiet before the charge starts collecting."

"How do you respond when that happens?"

"I tell her to breathe."

"Always?"

"Mostly."

"Then perhaps you should ask first whether she wants to continue."

Annabeth looked at her sister. Thalia looked back. It was a correction small enough that Thalia might once have ignored it, but she remembered the eclipse and the way she had nearly made her sister's safety into a question of whether Thalia could endure more.

"Do you want to continue?" she asked.

"Not for another ten minutes," Annabeth said.

"All right."

Thalia went to fetch water. Annabeth took the break without apologizing. Chiron used the pause to demonstrate how the lantern's protective glass could be inspected without touching any active spellwork. He explained which parts were ordinary material and which parts the ward team would consider if they ever tried a more advanced exercise.

When he left, the training yard seemed quieter. Thalia returned with two cups, one for each of them.

"I got told off," she said.

"You got feedback."

"I prefer told off. Sounds more dramatic."

Annabeth took the cup. "You were being a little pushy."

"I know."

"You also helped."

"I know that too."

They drank in silence. Thalia was tempted to fill it with advice. Instead she watched the younger campers leaving the arena and allowed Annabeth to think.

"Can we change the exercise?" Annabeth asked eventually.

"How?"

"I want to practice noticing the charge without producing a spark at all. If I can tell when it starts gathering, I might be able to stop before it hurts somebody."

Thalia considered it. The idea was almost the opposite of what they'd planned. She had wanted the lantern to light, something visible and easy to celebrate. Annabeth wanted to know whether she could stop a power before anybody else noticed it.

"That makes sense," Thalia said. "We can keep the lantern unlit."

They returned to the chalk circle. Thalia stood a few paces away, arms lowered. Annabeth closed her eyes and described the changes she felt in the air. At first there was nothing. Then the hairs on her forearm began to rise. She recognized the sensation early enough to step back and loosen her hands without sending a spark toward the stool.

"That felt different," she said.

"What did?"

"I knew it was coming before it happened. I didn't have to make it go anywhere."

Thalia marked the result in the notebook. It was not the impressive ending she had imagined for the day. It was, she suspected, the more important lesson.

They repeated the exercise twice. On the last attempt, Annabeth let the charge settle without moving her feet. Thalia watched carefully but didn't say anything until her sister opened her eyes.

"Good," she said.

Annabeth smiled, tired and pleased. Then she sat down on the bench before her hand began to tremble again. The lantern remained dark. She had learned something she could use the next time the sky frightened her.



---

By mid-afternoon, the practice yard smelled like thunderstorms and blue muffins.

Percy had returned with damp hair and a reed helmet Grover wove from creek grass ("It's mostly decorative," Grover admitted, "but it looks protective, which is half of protection"). He sat well outside the chalk circle this time, with Grover beside him holding the sign and offering a running commentary in a whisper that carried across the whole yard.

"She's breathing," Grover whispered. "Very good breathing. Excellent posture. Ten out of ten. I give her — oh! Spark! Small spark! Good spark!"

Annabeth tried not to laugh. She stood in the circle, feet bare now because Thalia said shoes insulated too much and she needed to feel the earth. The dirt was warm under her soles. Her braids had come half-undone again and frizzed at the ends. Sweat stuck her shirt to her back.

But something had shifted since the Percy incident. She was gentler with herself. When the charge built behind her sternum — that rising elevator feeling — she didn't shove. She breathed. Longer out than in. Fogging a mirror.

*Snap.* A thread of blue stretched between her palms and held. Four seconds. She let it fade on the exhale instead of chasing it.

"Good," Thalia murmured from the edge of the circle. "Really good. You're listening."

Annabeth tried aiming again. This time she didn't point like a weapon. She turned her palm toward the stool, open, like offering something. She breathed. She thought about Percy laughing with his hair on end, about Grover's crooked sign, about Thalia braiding her hair that morning with such careful hands. Warm things. Safe things.

A spark hopped from her palm and *tickled* the lantern glass. *Tink.*

"I touched it!" Annabeth gasped. "Thalia, I touched the lantern!"

"You did!" Thalia punched the air. Grover and Percy cheered so loud the straw dummies wobbled. "Okay, okay, keep going, you're in the groove, don't overthink—"

Too late. Annabeth overthought. The next three tries fizzled at her wrist. The one after that popped loudly and made her yelp.

"Break," Thalia ordered, seeing the frustration climbing Annabeth's neck in red patches. "Water. Sit. Percy, boring story. Now."

Percy launched into a saga about barnacle removal in Atlantis ("You have to sing to them or they cling harder, it's a whole thing") while Annabeth sat in the dirt and drank from a canteen and Grover fanned her with the sign.

"You know," Grover said conversationally, "when I first started playing the pipes, I was awful. Like, goats-fleeing-awful. My reed pipes squeaked and Juniper said a dying crow sounded better. But my dad said music isn't about being good at first. It's about staying long enough to get good. Powers are probably like that too."

Annabeth smiled at him. Grover's encouragement was always nervous — he stammered, he second-guessed, he offered snacks mid-sentence — but it was steady. He showed up. He made signs. He stayed.

"Thanks, Grover," she said.

"Anytime!" He beamed. "Also, if you ever want to quit and join the nature-spirit choir as a backup clapper, you'd be welcome. No lightning required. Just clapping."

"I'll keep that as plan B."

Silena stopped by on her way from the forge, took one look at the scene — Thalia cross-legged, Annabeth crackling faintly, Percy in a grass helmet, Grover waving a sign — and said, "Oh, this is adorable. Training montage? I love training montages. Can I watch for like five minutes? Clarisse says if I'm late to sparring she'll use my hair ribbon as a belt."

She sat, produced a pack of strawberry gum, shared it around. Her presence loosened something; Silena had that effect. Daughter of Poseidon in this life, fourteen and luminous, she treated everyone's powers like accessories to be celebrated. "Show me a spark, baby Zeus," she coaxed. "Come on."

Annabeth, laughing, held out her hand and produced a bright crackle on command. Silena and Grover applauded. Percy pretended to faint from pride.

Clarisse arrived ten minutes later looking for Silena, took in Percy's grass helmet, and said flatly, "I don't want to know."

"You want to know," Silena said sweetly. "Annabeth's learning lightning. She fried Percy's hair an hour ago. It was glorious. I have secondhand regret at missing it."

Clarisse eyed Annabeth's frizzy braids and Percy's damp head. A grin cracked her tough face. "Little brother got zapped by his girlfriend. By Annabeth. In a totally normal, impressively fluffy training accident."

"Exactly," Percy said quickly. "Normal boyfriend-zapping."

Clarisse snorted, stole Silena's gum, stole Percy's cheese, ruffled Annabeth's static hair and yelped when it shocked her, and left dragging Silena to sparring. "Keep practicing, Grace. Next time aim for Luke. Kidding. Mostly."

After they left, the yard felt quieter. Golden light slanted through the trees. The day was sliding toward evening.

Thalia checked the sun. "One more hour, then dinner. You good?"

Annabeth nodded, though her arms felt heavy and her head buzzy. Good-buzzy, like after swimming. She stood, rolled her shoulders, planted her bare feet.

"Last drill," Thalia said. "No aiming. No holding. Just... charge and release. Build it slow, let it go slow, into the ground. Like sighing. Watch me first."

Thalia stepped into the circle, toed off her boots, breathed. Annabeth watched her sister's face go calm and open. After a moment, faint blue light traced down Thalia's legs and sank into the dirt with a soft *hush*, like water hitting hot stone. The grass around her feet rippled.

"Whoa," Percy whispered. "Did the ground just... sigh?"

"Grounding out," Thalia said, stepping back. "Safest release. If you ever feel too full, too buzzy, too mad — send it down. Earth can take it. Try."

Annabeth breathed. In. Hold. Out. She imagined the buzz as light pooled in her chest, imagined tipping it gently downward, like pouring water from a cup. Her feet tingled. Warmth spread under her soles. A soft blue glow sank from her ankles into the dirt and vanished with a faint smell of rain.

She opened her eyes. Thalia was beaming.

"Did I—"

"You grounded out. First try. Annabeth, that's advanced. That's like— that's really good." Thalia's voice wobbled slightly. She covered it by coughing. "Okay. Okay, one more, then we eat. You're going to be sore tomorrow. Sky-tired is real. Feels like you ran a marathon in your sleep."

They practiced grounding until the light went amber and the conch blew for dinner. Annabeth's releases got smoother, quieter. By the last one, there was barely a sound — just warmth and a breath of ozone.

As they packed up, Percy produced two more muffins from his pockets ("Emergency muffins. Always carry emergency muffins") and Grover presented Annabeth with the pinecone ("For luck. I found it during your training, so it's charged with good vibes. Probably. Don't eat it.").

Annabeth hugged the pinecone to her chest and laughed until her braids sparked.

---


When the afternoon training ended, Thalia had expected Annabeth to run straight to Cabin One and write down everything that had happened. Instead her sister stopped beside the practice-yard noticeboard and looked at the list of scheduled sessions for the following week.

"What are you doing?" Thalia asked.

"Seeing whether we can reserve the quiet corner again."

"You want another whole day?"

"No. A shorter session, maybe half an hour. I want to try the stopping exercise before I'm tired."

Thalia considered the request. She had spent months worrying that Annabeth would take an uncontrolled burst of lightning as proof she was dangerous, and now her sister was asking to practice the least dramatic part of the power. It seemed worth supporting.

"Put us down for Thursday," she said.

"Only if Chiron approves."

"Fine. Write that too."

Annabeth wrote their names in pencil and added a question mark beside the time. She had learned not to treat a provisional booking as an official one, especially when other campers were trying to use the same safe area. That was one of the things Thalia had been insisting on since the eclipse, and it was both irritating and satisfying to see it applied to their own plans.

Percy arrived with a rolled-up practice banner and a pair of damp gloves.

"Are you finished?" he asked.

"For today," Thalia said.

"Good. Clarisse wants somebody to help move the spare markers before the next group starts. She says if Annabeth's session is over, then the whole corner needs to be cleared."

Annabeth looked at the chalk circle. It had taken her hours to arrange the space exactly as she wanted it. She didn't like the idea of erasing everything just as she was beginning to understand how it worked.

"Can we leave the measurements somewhere?" she asked.

"We can copy them," Percy said. "The next group needs the floor."

She nodded reluctantly. The training area belonged to camp, not to her. Thalia helped her measure the stool's position one last time, and Percy carried it to the equipment shed.

The younger campers waiting for the next session were a mixed group from Hermes and Demeter, mostly learning basic defensive spacing. One of them had been watching the storm training from the fence and asked whether Annabeth could show the little spark she'd made earlier.

"Not right now," Annabeth replied.

"Why not?"

"Because I'm tired, and I haven't learned to produce it reliably. Today I practiced noticing when the power was gathering and making it settle before it went anywhere."

The boy looked disappointed. His instructor stepped in to remind him that a demonstration wasn't part of the scheduled lesson. Annabeth was relieved. The question had made her feel as though all the work she'd done with Thalia mattered only if someone else could see lightning jump from her fingers.

They cleared the floor. Clarisse came to check the markers and noticed the small question mark beside Thursday's booking.

"You're using the yard again?"

"If the instructors approve," Annabeth said.

"Good. You should practice the same thing on more than one day. But don't turn it into an excuse to miss normal sword work."

"I won't."

Clarisse glanced at Thalia. "And you need to let somebody else supervise occasionally. You can't be the only person your sister feels safe training with."

Thalia opened her mouth. She had a dozen reasons why she was the best person to help Annabeth learn the power they'd inherited from Zeus. She knew the physical sensations, the impatience, the fear. She also knew Clarisse was talking about something larger than technical skill.

"I can ask Chiron to observe," she said.

"He should. And Zoe can inspect the equipment when you start doing more than small sparks."

Annabeth looked up. "You'd really want Zoe there?"

"If there's a magical ward involved, yes. If it's just you practicing concentration, probably not. Let people do their actual jobs."

Thalia almost laughed. Clarisse had spent the week arguing with Percy about the same thing. She was becoming alarmingly good at saying it to other people.

They delivered the markers to the practice shed. Percy carried the heavy bundle, leaving the lighter tags for Annabeth because her hand still ached sometimes after long sessions. She noticed, but didn't object. He hadn't snatched anything from her. He'd asked which load she wanted before they'd begun.

When they reached the shed, Silena was checking the timetable for the next training group. She looked at Annabeth's notes.

"You've recorded what happened each time?"

"Yes. The successful spark, the failed attempts, and the times I stopped before it discharged."

Silena turned the page. "Could you make a copy for the instructor?"

"Of course."

"And keep the private parts private. You don't owe the whole camp a report every time your powers do something unusual."

Annabeth looked surprised. "I wasn't going to post it."

"I know. I'm reminding you that the list belongs to you and the people responsible for the session. Everybody else can ask questions without expecting your notebook."

That was a boundary Annabeth hadn't thought about. She was so accustomed to treating her work as evidence that she sometimes forgot she could keep a personal account without immediately turning it into a public explanation.

She thanked Silena and went to make the official copy. Percy helped her set the pages in order, then let her write in her own hand. They didn't need to tell anyone about the memories that made her feel older than the body she was training.

By the time the conch sounded for supper, the practice yard had been used by three separate groups, the chalk circle was gone and the marked stop cord had been put back in its proper box. Nothing on the ground distinguished Annabeth's training from any other exercise. She had a notebook full of observations and a sore wrist, which was enough.

Thalia met her outside Cabin One with two clean jackets.

"Are you happy with today?" she asked.

Annabeth thought about the question. She had made a spark in the morning, learned how to stop one by afternoon and asked for another session without promising herself she would be better next time.

"Yes," she said. "I was frustrated for most of it. But yes."

"That's allowed."

"I know."

Thalia handed her a jacket. "Then eat before you start planning Thursday. I don't want to spend the evening convincing you that you need dinner."

Annabeth put it on, then noticed a tear in the sleeve where Thalia had caught it on a nail.

"You should let Silena mend that."

"She'll make fun of me."

"She'll fix it."

Thalia looked down at the tear. "Both things can happen."

They went to the pavilion. The day's success felt more manageable when Annabeth didn't have to perform it for anyone else. Thalia was there, still proud and still infuriating, and Percy had a plate of food waiting at his family's table. There would be time to celebrate the lantern later. First she wanted dinner.



---

Dinner was chaos, as usual.

Annabeth sat with Cabin One, fielding questions from Milo about sparks ("Can you teach me? Can you? Please?") and fending off Connor Stoll's request to charge his Game Boy ("Absolutely not, you'll melt it and then you'll cry"). Percy sat three tables over with his half-siblings, catching her eye over his meatloaf and mouthing *storm lord* while pointing at his hair, making her snort milk through her nose.

Thalia watched it all with a full plate she barely touched. Pride and something tender ached behind her ribs.

After dinner, instead of campfire, Thalia stole Annabeth away. "Walk with me," she said. "Bring your jacket. And the lantern."

"The lantern?" Annabeth asked, but she grabbed Chiron's storm lantern from the arena shed where they'd left it. "Why?"

"You'll see. Percy, Grover — you can come if you're quiet."

They were quiet. Unnaturally quiet. Percy fell into step beside Annabeth, close enough that their knuckles brushed every few steps on the path up the hill. Neither moved away. In the dark, with only the moon and the distant campfire glow, Thalia pretended not to notice when their hands linked for three seconds between the torches.

Half-Blood Hill at night was different. Quieter. The pine whispered. Annabeth remembered a Fleece hanging on the tree in her other life; here the branches held only needles and moonlight, and Thalia sat alive beneath them. The hill glowed silver under a rising moon. Stars pricked through in their thousands. No clouds. A perfect clear night.

Thalia led them to the top and sat. The others followed. The lantern sat unlit in the middle of their small circle.

"Okay," Thalia said. "Last lesson today. No chalk. No drills. Annabeth, do you remember this morning? What I said about listening?"

Annabeth nodded, hugging her knees. "Listen before you talk."

"Right. All day you've been talking — sparks, zaps, grounding. Loud stuff. Now try quiet. The lantern's got a candle inside." Thalia tapped the glass. "No storm. No clouds. Just you and a wick and a little bit of air. See if you can warm it enough to light. Not a bolt. A... breath. A warm breath."

Annabeth stared at the lantern. "Thalia, that's— that's tiny. That's so precise. I can't even aim at a stool yet."

"I know. That's why it's perfect. No pressure. If it doesn't happen today, it happens next week. Or next month. This isn't a test." Thalia leaned back on her hands. "Just breathe. Feel the night air. Find the charge — it's thinner now, sleepy — and invite a little of it to sit in the lantern with the wick."

Percy shifted closer, his knee touching Annabeth's. "You got this," he murmured. "No rush."

Grover whispered, "I'm sending good goat vibes. Very quiet vibes. *Maa*. I mean— sorry. Quiet."

Annabeth laughed softly, then settled. She pulled the lantern toward her, rested her hands on either side of the glass without touching it. She closed her eyes.

Thalia watched her sister breathe. Four in. Hold. Six out. Again. Again. The night went still around them. Crickets ticked. The wind eased.

Annabeth's hair lifted, just barely, a few strands. Faint blue light flickered at her fingertips — not wild, not crackling. Steady. Like candlelight deciding to exist.

She exhaled, long and slow, toward the lantern.

For a moment, nothing.

Then — a tiny golden spark caught on the wick. Flickered. Caught. Glowed.

The lantern lit.

A warm, ordinary, beautiful flame burned behind the glass, reflecting in Annabeth's wide gray eyes and Percy's stunned smile and Grover's open mouth.

"Oh," Annabeth whispered. "Oh — I did it —"

She looked up at Thalia, face glowing in lantern light, tears bright on her lashes. "Thalia, look, I—"

Thalia couldn't speak for a second. Her throat had closed. She reached out and tucked a frizzy strand behind Annabeth's ear — got a tiny shock and didn't flinch — and smiled so hard it hurt.

"I see it, baby," she said softly. "I see you."

Percy whooped and immediately clapped both hands over his mouth. Grover waved his sign in slow motion, tears in his eyes. "You lit it without a storm," he whispered. "That's — that's like playing a perfect note with no instrument. That's—"

Annabeth laughed through tears and launched herself at Thalia, nearly knocking the lantern over. Percy caught it just in time, then got pulled into the hug, then Grover piled on, and the four of them sat in a tangle on top of Half-Blood Hill with a single lantern burning between them like a small sun.

No monsters. No prophecies. No battles. Just warmth.

---


Percy carried the lantern down the hill.

He insisted. Annabeth's hands were shaking — good shaking, tired shaking, happy shaking — and Grover's tote kept hitting his knees, and Thalia kept pretending she wasn't wiping her eyes ("Allergies. Grass. Shut up."). So Percy took the lantern by its handle and walked in the middle with its light swinging gold across the path.

Annabeth walked beside him, close. Her shoulder bumped his every few steps. Her braids were half-out, her cheeks flushed, her eyes still shining. She looked exhausted and triumphant and twelve and seventeen all at once.

"You were amazing today," Percy said quietly, so Thalia and Grover — arguing three paces ahead about whether grass had pollen at night — wouldn't hear. "Seriously. The grounding thing? The lantern? That was... that was like watching you solve a puzzle, back when— you know. Before. You got that same look. Like the world clicked."

Annabeth glanced up at him. Lantern light caught the gray of her eyes and turned them gold. "I miss that feeling," she admitted. "The clicking. With Athena stuff, I always knew when I was right. With this... I'm guessing. All day I'm guessing."

"You're guessing really well." He nudged her shoulder. "And hey. You only electrocuted me once. That's growth. Last week you shocked me twice before breakfast."

She giggled and shoved him. The lantern swung, throwing light across the grass. "Your hair was the best part. I'm sorry, but it was. You looked like a sea urchin."

"I *am* a sea urchin. A handsome, mysterious sea urchin." He struck the pose again. She laughed so hard she had to stop walking.

Ahead, Thalia looked back. "You two coming? Curfew!"

"Coming!" they chorused.

They caught up. Grover was telling Thalia about his pinecone gift and why pinecones were superior to tambourines. Thalia was listening with that patient sky-teacher focus she only gave to people she loved. Percy caught her eye and mouthed *thank you.* Thalia nodded once, gruff, and looked away quickly. The Grace sisters by blood, Percy and Grover drawn close by choice.

At the cabin crossroads, they split. Grover hugged Annabeth ("You did great, you were so brave, come to the cloven council meeting next week, we're voting on moss"), Thalia squeezed Percy's shoulder ("Thanks for the muffins, Kelp Head. And for being... you know. Good for her."), and then it was just Percy and Annabeth for ten seconds in the blue dark between Cabin One and Cabin Three.

"Hey," Percy whispered.

"Hey," she whispered back.

He handed her the lantern. Their fingers touched. No shock this time — just warmth. Just skin.

"Sleep well, Wise Girl," he said.

"Sleep well, Seaweed Brain," she said, and the old names hung between them like the lantern light, secret and safe.

She turned to go. Then turned back, stood on tiptoes, and kissed his cheek — quick, daring, gone before the porch torches could catch it. His whole face went hot.

"For luck," she said primly, and ran for Cabin One with the lantern swinging, leaving Percy standing in the dark with his hand on his cheek and a grin so wide it hurt.

Thalia's voice floated from the Cabin One porch: "I saw nothing! I was blinded by pollen! Goodnight, Jackson!"

"Goodnight, Grace!" Percy called back, laughing.

He walked to Cabin Three with salt air in his lungs and lightning still tingling faintly on his cheek where she'd kissed him. Clarisse was already snoring. Silena had left a note on his pillow: *Heard you got fried. Proud of you for taking one for the team. — S.* He stuck the note in his trunk, changed into pajamas, and lay awake listening to the waves and thinking about lantern light in gray eyes.

Across the green, in Cabin One, Annabeth set the lantern on her nightstand and watched its flame — her flame, coaxed from clear air — burn down slow. Thalia, in the next bunk, pretended to be asleep but whispered, "Proud of you, Sparky," into the dark. Jason mumbled something about lightning in his sleep. Annabeth smiled and blew out the lantern and fell asleep to the smell of rain that wasn't there.

Annabeth woke once in the night and saw the lantern sitting where she'd left it, unlit and ordinary. She could light it again tomorrow if Chiron approved.

From the next bunk Thalia muttered, "Four in. Hold. Six out."

"I wasn't doing anything," Annabeth whispered.

"Good. Then go back to sleep."

Annabeth pulled the blanket up and followed her own breathing until it slowed.
