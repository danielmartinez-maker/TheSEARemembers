## Chapter 178: The Blank Under the Ridge

They were at the gate at six, which was the hour Annabeth had written on the wall, and the fact that everybody else was also at the gate at six was the first surprise of the day and she decided not to examine it.

The morning was hard and clear. There had been a frost overnight — a real one, not the thin first one off the reeds, but a proper October frost that had put a white crust on the grass all the way down to the shore — and the whole camp at six in the morning looked like a thing that had been left out on a shelf: every plank and leaf and rail edged in white, the lake under its own steam, the path up to the gate a black line drawn through the frost by somebody who had gone first.

Somebody had gone first. Annabeth stopped at the gate and looked at the prints in the frost, and they were boots, one pair, up the path and back, and she knew whose because she had spent a whole summer reading them off the mud in the boathouse yard.

"You walked it."

"I walked it at five," Percy said. "Twice. Out to the terrace and back to see if anything had blown across the path, and then up to the fence to look at the gully. It's fine. It's hard as rock, which is good news, and there's nothing down across the terrace, which is better news, and I've got the cake."

"It's six in the morning."

"It's a very good morning, and Silena's paper twist says EAT THE CAKE FIRST, SIGNS ADD NOTHING TO MORALE, and I've decided to follow instructions."

"Percy."

"I've already eaten the cake."

Thalia came up the path from Cabin One with the rope over her shoulder and her coat done to the neck and her survey book in her hand and the flat competence of a woman who had walked this ground four days in September and had a note for every stone of it. Marcus came from the Hephaestus side with the rod across his back like a rifle and both hands in his pockets and no socks in his bag that anybody could see. Olive arrived last, eleven minutes early, in boots, with a roll of brown paper in a tube and a pencil behind each ear and the expression of a person who had cleared her whole Saturday with a centaur and intended to spend it.

"Nobody's late," Thalia said, in the voice of a woman counting.

"Nobody's late." Annabeth did the thing she had been waiting three days to do, which was to take the folded route sheet out of her pocket and read the top line out loud to the gate, the crew, and the frost. "North spring survey. Gate, east fence, the long terrace, spring gully, ridge line, slab. Five hours up, two hours on, three hours down, back by dark. Turn at the terrace if the wind's over forty."

"Wind's eight," Thalia said.

"Then we're going to the top." Annabeth folded the sheet and put it in the bag and shouldered it and looked down the line at the four of them: her sister, a boy with a rope, a silent fifteen-year-old with a leveling rod, and a ten-year-old with a drawing tube. "Crew of five. Let's go and find out where the water comes from."

The first hour was the camp's own ground and they went up it in the dark, and did not talk much.

The east fence line ran behind the archery field and then up through the pines, and it was the oldest of the camp's boundaries, and Annabeth had drawn it in June with a tape and a stick and had never once been up it in a frost. The frost changed everything about it. Every fence post had a white cap. The wire sagged with it. The pines stood in the half dark with their needles whitened at the edges, and the ground underfoot was so hard that their boots made a sound like a hammer on a floor, and the cold came off the rocks in the way that rock holds the night and gives it back slowly to anyone walking through.

They came out above the treeline at half past seven with the sun just up over the ridge and the whole eastern half of the sky doing the thing October does, which is to be enormous and rose-colored and completely indifferent to whether anybody is looking.

"Stop," Annabeth said.

They stopped. Nobody argued. The five of them stood in a line on the cold grass above the pines with the camp laid out small and white and smoking below them, the lake silver at the bottom of it, the forge not yet lit, the paths going nowhere, and the sun coming up on all of it at once.

Annabeth looked at it for about forty seconds and then got out the field book, and in the top right corner of the new sheet, where the weather went, she wrote, in her clean small hand: 07:35. FIRST FROST ON THE FENCE LINE. WIND W 8. VIS. UNLIMITED. And under it, smaller, because nobody was going to read it except her, in three years, looking for a memory: *Every one of them came.*

The long terrace took them three hours.

It was not a climb. That was the thing about the north side of the ridge: nobody had ever built a trail up it because there was nothing up there to build a trail to, and so the ground went in long shelves, one above the next, each one flat for eighty feet and then a step of granite, and the shelves were littered with the rubble of a hundred winters and the low scrub that grew in cracks and nothing else. There was no water on the terrace. That was the point of the day, and Annabeth wrote it down as she walked: DRY ABOVE THE FENCE FOR 300 FEET. NO SEEP. NO STAIN. GRANITE SOUNDS HOLLOW AT SHELF 2.

They went up in a line with Thalia in front because Thalia had the ground and knew where the slab was by feel, and Percy behind her with the pack, and Annabeth third with the book, and Marcus with the rod, and Olive last with her tube and an iron determination not to be the one who asked for a rest, which she managed for two hours and nine minutes before she asked, and everybody pretended that they had been about to stop anyway.

At the top of the third shelf they stopped for real, and Percy produced the paper twist, and they ate the cake at half past nine in the morning in a frost-bitten hollow two hundred feet above the camp, and it was the best thing Annabeth had ever eaten, and she said so, out loud, without being asked, and Percy did not say anything about it, which was the correct response and which she would have paid him for if there had been any money at this camp.

The slab was on the fourth shelf.

Annabeth saw it from a long way off, which she had not expected, because a slab the size of a cabin door should have been hidden among a thousand other slabs, and instead it was there at the top of the shelf like a thing that had been put down: grey, wet-looking even in the frost, tilted a hand's width at the near corner, with the far edge running out into the ground at an angle you could read from forty feet.

And under it, coming out at the low corner, there was water.

She heard it before she got there, which was a thing Thalia had told her in the parlor on Thursday and which she had not believed until she was standing on it. It was not a trickle. It was a sound with a body to it, a low flat churn, water going somewhere in a hurry.

"It's running," Thalia said. She was ten feet ahead with her hands on her hips and her head cocked, in exactly the attitude of a woman on September the fourteenth with her hand in a seam, and she had gone completely still, and when she turned around her face was doing the crooked thing her face did when a thing she had found turned out to be more than she had said.

Annabeth walked the last forty feet. Then she put her bag down on dry stone, took out the field book, uncapped the pencil, and looked at the north spring of Camp Half-Blood for the first time in her life.

The seep came out from under the near edge of the slab as a clear line of water the width of two hands, and it ran thirty feet down the shelf in a trough it had cut for itself in the rubble, and then it collected in a shallow bowl of stone the size of a bathtub, and then it went over the lip of the shelf and down into the ground, and the ground, as far as Annabeth could see, drank it.

Which meant the water did not reach the camp as a stream. Which meant it went down, under the terrace, through whatever cracks a hundred winters had made in the granite, and came out a hundred feet lower somewhere nobody had ever looked, and fed the French drain and Olive's silt and the wet corner of the north path — which Percy had stood in on a Monday in September and called water from uphill, and had been right about, and had not once made a thing of being right about, which she was going to think about later when she was not working.

"Rod," Annabeth said.

Marcus had it off his back before she finished the word.

The next hour and a half was the best work Annabeth had done since she had started drawing this camp, and she did not know it while it was happening, because while it was happening it was just numbers.

Marcus held the rod on the bank above the seep, plumb and steady, and read the graduations off out loud in his flat voice while Annabeth wrote them down, and they were only nine feet apart and the rod never once moved, and after four stations she stopped checking it with her eye and simply took the numbers, which is the highest compliment in the trade.

Thalia ran the tape down the trough and put pins in the ground at every three feet and called the fall. Percy stood in the trough in his boots with the tape at the far end, which required being in running water up to the shin on a granite shelf in October, and he did it for an hour and forty minutes without one word of complaint, and his only comment on the whole procedure was "It's warmer than the lake," which Annabeth wrote in the margin under the depth figures because it was true and because it was his.

Olive drew. That was the whole of her assignment and she had been waiting for it since August: she sat on the dry stone at the head of the trough with her brown paper on her knees and drew the slab and the seep and the trough and the bowl and the place where the water went over the lip, at a scale of one inch to two feet, which is a hard scale for an adult and had been her own idea, and she did not talk for forty minutes, and when she was done she had drawn the north spring of Camp Half-Blood better than Annabeth could have drawn it in twice the time.

At twenty past twelve, with the wind at nineteen and the sun directly overhead and the whole ridge ticking as the frost melted off it, Annabeth took the last rod reading, wrote it down, and stopped writing.

Then she stood up and looked at what she had: two and a half fair pages of numbers, a ruled section of the trough every three feet, the fall over thirty-one feet, the width, the depth at six stations, the temperature (cold, then colder, then, three feet into the bowl, warmer than the air, which was the beginning of an explanation and would need a spring to finish), the position of the slab, the direction of the flow, the door-sized plate of granite that had moved, by Thalia's boots in September and by their tape today, about a foot since June.

That, all of it, was a water supply. That was where the wet corner of the path came from and where Olive's clay got its silt and what the French drain had been quietly fighting since somebody dug it.

Which meant it was going on the wall.

"That's it," she said. "That's the seep."

"That's the seep," Thalia said, and came and stood beside her sister at the head of the trough, and the two of them looked at the water going over the lip of the shelf and disappearing into the ground, and Thalia said, without any weight at all, "Hand me the rag."

Thalia Grace had tied a scrap of somebody's old shirt to a cairn on a granite shelf in September, and she had thought about it approximately four hundred times since, and she had never once said the whole of why.

The cairn was six stones, stacked at the dry corner of the slab, where the grey plate ran out into the ground and the water came up out of it. She had built it on the fourteenth in flat rain with her fingers numb and her coat over the seep and no one within four miles, and she had taken the blue rag out of her own survey pocket — a scrap of one of Silena's old shirts that had come apart in the wash in August and had been cut up for the mending basket, and that Thalia had taken out of that basket with a centaur's permission, which is to say she had asked Chiron whether she could have a piece of rag for a survey mark and Chiron had said yes in one word and had not asked why.

She had tied it to the top stone. And then she had stood in the rain and watched it move, which was the only thing on that whole ridge that had moved, and had come home and reported a seep to a mapmaker and got a piece of paper with her name on the wall.

Now Annabeth was holding out a new one: a strip of blue cloth, cut straight, hemmed on both edges by somebody who did not do anything by halves, in the same blue.

"You hemmed the rag," Thalia said.

"The old one was fraying."

"It's a *mark*. It's supposed to fray."

"It's supposed to be legible," Annabeth said. "A survey mark that fails gives a false negative to whoever comes next, and whoever comes next is me, in the spring, at a distance, in worse light. You can have a fraying mark or you can have a mark. You can't have both. Nobody ever hung a hemmed flag on a mountain and regretted it."

Thalia took it. She looked at the hem, which was four millimeters and even, and at the two little metal eyelets Annabeth had punched into the corners with the awl from the repair kit, and then she climbed up onto the slab and untied the old rag, which had gone grey-blue and stiff with weather and had frayed at both ends exactly as much as a mark on a ridge should have, and put it in her pocket, and tied the new one on in its place.

The wind took it immediately. Southwest, steady, twenty degrees off the ridge line, and the blue went out downwind and stood out straight, northeast, exactly the direction Annabeth had written on the wall on Sunday night at the map: *the cairn with the blue rag streaming northeast in a southwest wind.*

"Northeast," Thalia said.

"Northeast," Annabeth said, from the ground, with her field book already open, writing it down, because of course she was.

Thalia stood on the slab a moment with her hand still on the knot.

It is a strange thing to have a place put your name on it. She had spent four days on this ridge in September because a girl with a pencil had asked her to and because she had needed to be off the hill, and she had come back with a seam and a slab and a spring and had reported it in a room with a centaur in it and had then been stupid about it for a week, and now there was a hemmed blue rag on a cairn six stones high, and a heading on a map wall two miles away with her name inside brackets, and a boy with a rod putting in stations on her water. All of it out of four days of walking in the rain, which she had thought at the time was just something to do with her hands.

"It's yours," Annabeth said, from below, without looking up from the book. "You found it. I'm only drawing it."

"That's the nicest thing you've ever said to me and you said it at a seep."

"It's a good seep," Annabeth said, and went back to work, and Thalia got down off the slab and went to run the last tape line of the morning.

They worked the shelf until two.

It was the best two hours Thalia had spent on that ground. In September she had walked it alone, four days, in weather, taking notes for somebody else's map and not knowing whether any of it would matter; today there were five of them on one shelf of granite, and the shelf had a shape she could hear coming out of it — the trough, the fall, the bowl, the lip, and under it, going away down through the rock, the line the water took toward the camp. Marcus held the rod like a man holding a fence post and never moved it a quarter of an inch. Olive sat on the dry stone with her paper and her two pencils and drew the spring better than it deserved. Percy stood in the water for one hundred minutes and carried everything anybody handed him and told nobody that his feet were cold, which Thalia noted in the ledger she kept on people and did not mention.

And her sister did the arithmetic.

That was the thing Thalia kept coming back to, all afternoon, in the wind, while the tape ran out and the numbers came in: Annabeth Grace, fourteen years old, in a wool cap and a coat two sizes too big, in the cold, with a pencil, doing the thing she had wanted to do since August, and doing it exactly the way Thalia had watched her do everything since she was six years old, which was *properly*, with three columns, and a date, and the weather in the corner, and a note in the margin for whoever came next.

At half past one, while the boys were resetting the rod, Thalia watched Annabeth take a pair of dry socks out of the bag, walk down the trough to where Percy was standing in the water, hand them to him without a word, and walk back to the book. Percy looked at the socks for a second, and then at the back of her head, and then put them in his coat pocket and went on holding the tape, because there was a job on. And Annabeth sat down and wrote the next reading with the same hand she wrote everything with, and Thalia, twenty feet away on the dry stone, looked at the two of them and did the thing she was never going to say out loud, which was to be glad in a way that had a hollow in it and to be fine with the hollow.

"That's thirty-one feet of fall," Annabeth said, out loud, to the shelf. "Thirty-one feet in three hundred yards. That's a steep little river."

"It's a seep," Thalia said.

"It's a river that's pretending to be a seep so nobody asks it any questions," Annabeth said, and then pushed the pencil behind her ear and stood up and looked at the sun, and said the thing that everybody had been waiting for since seven o'clock that morning. "Two o'clock. Wind's nineteen. We've got the door, the slab, the trough, the fall, and the bowl. Pack it up. We turn here."

"Turn at the terrace," Thalia said, testing her.

"That was if the wind was over forty. It's nineteen. I'm turning because I've got what I came for and because the days are three hours shorter than they were in September and because Olive's fingers are blue and she won't say so, and because I want to come down in daylight with the sheet in my hand and not in my teeth." Annabeth closed the field book and put it in the oilcloth and looked at her sister with her chin up. "And because Chiron made me promise that turning was a decision I got to make, and I've made it, and I want it in the record."

"Wind nineteen," Thalia said. "Time fourteen hundred. Turned at the slab with the data complete. That's the record."

"That's the record," Annabeth said, and then did something Thalia did not expect, in front of the crew, on the top shelf of the north ridge, in the cold: she stepped in and put her forehead against her sister's shoulder for two seconds, hard, all collarbone and wool, the way they had done since they were small, and then stepped back and picked up the bag.

"Right," Thalia said, when she had her voice back. "Down. Olive in the middle, Marcus behind her. Percy, you're on the back of the line with the pack. Genius, you walk in front of me and you read me the numbers on the way down and I'll check you."

"You'll check me."

"I found it," Thalia said. "I get to check you to the gate."

They left the shelf at a quarter past two with the blue rag going northeast behind them, and it was the last thing Thalia looked at, and it was still there when she looked, and it was going to be there in the spring, which was, when you thought about it, the entire point of a mark.

Silena Beauregard spent Saturday doing the thing she did best, which was to run a camp that had five people missing from it.

The crew had gone at six with the cake and the rope and two of the three pairs of socks. By seven the whole hill knew they were up the fence line; by ten it knew they were on the terrace, because Marcus's brother had asked Chiron and Chiron had said that they were on the terrace; by noon half the camp was standing around the Big House porch looking at the north ridge for no reason whatsoever, which was one of the few things that never changed about Camp Half-Blood in any version of it, and which Silena, coming up the hill with a basket, found completely reasonable and did not laugh at once.

She had eleven kids to feed instead of five. She had a stove and two hours of light. She had a quilt on the long table with three rows done and the border pinned, and one problem with it, which had been bothering her since Friday night.

The problem was the corners.

A quilt that was going to live at a fire ring could not live on a beach without being staked to something. The north beach ring had iron poles at the two ends — Clarisse had hauled them down in August for a rain-fly that had never happened — and a quilt that went onto those poles needed rings, and rings meant brass eyelets, and brass eyelets meant the forge, and Silena had spent a week telling herself that the eyelets were a supply problem and not a reason.

On Friday night, before the crew left and while everybody was busy being Friday, she had walked to the forge with a note.

It had said, in her small round hand: *Sixteen eyelets, brass, quarter-inch, for the storm quilt, if the shop can spare them. — S.* And then, because a note of that length was a demand and not a request, and because she had stood in the doorway of the shop with the note in her hand for a full minute in the cold, she had added one line underneath: *No hurry. It's only a quilt.*

At ten past two on Saturday, Charlie Beckendorf came up the hill to Cabin Three with a tin of sixteen brass eyelets, a setting tool, a small anvil block he carried in one hand as though it were a sandwich, and no note.

"You're not on the ridge," Silena said, from the door, brilliantly.

"I've been on that ridge. It's got a seep on it and forty kids are watching it from the porch." He set the anvil down on the step with a thump that the boards felt. "You asked for sixteen."

"I said no hurry."

"It's Saturday," Beckendorf said, in the flat voice of a man stating an engineering fact. "Nothing's happening in the shop until Tuesday. Where's the quilt?"

They worked the quilt on the long table for three hours, and Silena thought afterward that if anybody had walked in at four o'clock and seen the two of them, they would have looked like the most ordinary thing in the world: two people on either end of a table with a quilt between them, a tin of brass, a hammer, a setting tool, and a small anvil, doing one job sixteen times.

It was not ordinary and she knew it the entire time.

He cut the border markers first, and then set the first eyelet to show her, and the setting tool made a soft clack and the brass opened out and gripped the cloth like a little flower, and he said, "You see the four points," and she said, "I see them," and he said, "You do the next one," and then he sat back and let her, and corrected her twice, exactly the way he corrected everything — flat, immediately, without any interest in whether she minded — and she got the second one right and the third one crooked and the fourth one right, and after that they did them pass-and-pass, one each, in a rhythm, and the rhythm turned out to be the conversation.

"Who's the good hammer brother," Silena said, at eyelet six.

"What?"

"Tyson writes about him. In his letters. *The brother with the good hammer saves me the good hammer.* Everybody in this cabin knows about the good hammer. Nobody knows his name."

Beckendorf set the next eyelet and turned the quilt, and did not look up. "His name's Theo. He's twenty. He had two summers at the camp forge and now he's out on the mortal side, machine shop, railings and gates and window casings — he makes things people look at from the street. That's where your brother knows him from. He got me my first hammer, and I've still got it, and it's not a good hammer, it's a terrible hammer, and he made me use it for a year so I'd know what I was holding." He turned the quilt again. "He writes to me when he's got money and he doesn't when he hasn't, so I know by the postmarks how the year's going."

"You keep his letters?"

"They're on the wall over the bench. There are four."

Silena did not say anything for a minute, and did two eyelets in a row, and got both of them right, and then she said, "I want to run a bakery."

"I know."

"You know?"

"You told me. In July, in the shop, while you were waiting for the pan. You said you'd run one, and you said not one single cupcake, and when I asked you what kind of floor you said scuffed." He set an eyelet and turned the quilt. "You didn't say where the table went. That's the part I filled in on Friday. You don't have to keep it."

Silena set the last one of the row and turned the quilt, and found that her hands were completely steady, which was not information she had been expecting to receive about herself.

"I'm keeping the table," she said. "All of it. The bench and the window and the forty inches. I've got it written down."

"Where."

"On the back of the recipe card for the honey bread. In pencil." She set the next eyelet and did not look up, and heard herself say, in the middle of a Saturday, on a table, to a boy with a hammer, "I've had that drawing since I was thirteen and I've never once shown it to anybody, and I've told you the whole thing twice in a week, and I don't know what to do about that, so I'm just going to keep setting eyelets."

Beckendorf was quiet for the length of two eyelets. The tin had four left. The stove ticked. Out past the window the whole camp was still standing around the Big House porch looking north at nothing, and somewhere above them on the shoulder of the ridge, five people were doing arithmetic in the wind.

"You could show me the card," he said.

Silena's hands stopped.

"It's on my shelf," she said. "In the recipe box. It's not a card anybody would look at twice. It's just the recipe and then a corner of a drawing and a note about a sink."

"I look at drawings all day," Beckendorf said. "It's not a big thing. I asked."

And that, in the end, was the whole of Saturday: a boy who fixed things saying *I look at drawings all day* in the flat voice he used for the strength of materials, and a girl who fed people taking the tin out of his hand and going into her own kitchen and coming back with a pencil-scrawled recipe card that she had never shown a living soul, and putting it on the table between them, and standing back with her arms folded and her chin up and her whole face completely unguarded for the first time in a week.

He looked at it for about a minute. He read it properly: the recipe, the shelf heights, the note about the sink and the light, the little plan in the corner with the table drawn under the window and the bench on the south side, done in pencil by a thirteen-year-old in a kitchen nobody measured.

"Your window's on the wrong wall," he said, at last.

"I know."

"That's the only thing wrong with it." He put the card down and squared it to the edge of the table, which was a thing he did with every drawing, and picked the hammer back up. "That and the sink. You've got the sink in the corner and the light in the middle, and people will stand in the light to wash up, and then they'll be standing in the way of the table. Move the sink under the window and put the bench where the sink was, and you'll have a room where somebody can wash and somebody can talk and nobody's in anybody's road."

"Move the sink."

"Move the sink," Beckendorf said, and set the fifteenth eyelet, and looked at her over the top of a quilt border with the whole flat attention of a person who had just finished the work and had nothing left to do with his hands. "That's a good drawing. I'd build it."

The three words went through the kitchen and out again and neither of them said anything about them.

They finished the sixteenth eyelet at a quarter to five, and he swept the brass curls off the table into his palm and put them in the tin and put the lid on and handed the tin back to her, which was for the eyelets she was going to want in November, which was a thing he assumed and did not say.

"It goes out tonight," Silena said, at the door.

"What does."

"The quilt. First night at the fire ring. Clarisse is taking it down at six, and there's soup, and it's not a party, it's just — " and here she had to reach for the true sentence and found it, and was aware of saying it in a doorway in the cold with her hands still smelling of brass, " — twelve weeks of evenings, and somebody has to be first under it."

Beckendorf stood on the step with the anvil block under one arm and looked at her for a moment, and then looked past her into the commons at the long table with the quilt on it, with its crooked row and its fish and its five stitches in a cross, and its sixteen brass rings catching the last of the light.

"Six," he said. "I'll bring the lantern oil. Your porch lamp's been on since Thursday and it's going to want filling before the frost."

"That's a work job."

"It's a lamp," he said, which was the closest he had come all week to saying anything that was not about materials, and went down the hill, and Silena closed the door and stood with her back against it and her hands flat on the wood and did absolutely nothing for the space of about ten seconds, and then went and put her coat on, because it was ten past five and there was soup to make and a quilt to carry and the whole north beach waiting.

The descent took three hours and eleven minutes and Annabeth counted every one of them, because counting was how she stayed awake on the way down.

Coming down a mountain is worse than going up and nobody tells you. Going up, your legs are doing the work and you get to stop when you like; going down, your legs are doing the work and gravity is doing everything else, and the whole time you are looking at your feet, which means you are looking at a thousand small stones and not at any of the four or five things you walked up four hours for.

They went down the shelves in the same line they had gone up, and Annabeth read the numbers out loud as she walked, which was the arrangement Thalia had set at the top: every station, the fall, the width, the number of the pin, and Thalia behind her saying *yes* or *no* or, once, at station nine, *say that again*, which Annabeth did, and which turned out to be a transposition of two digits and which she corrected in the book at the bottom of the shelf with a small neat circle and the word CORR and her initials, because a survey with an uncorrected error in it was not a survey, it was a rumor.

At the third shelf they found the light leaving. At the second shelf Olive asked for the last of the bread and got it. At the first shelf, where the scrub started again, Percy came up from the back of the line and walked beside Annabeth for a while and did not say anything at all, which was the correct behavior for the fourth hour of a nine-hour day.

They came off the terrace at twenty past five with the light going gold along the top of the pines and the whole camp laid out below them the way it had been at half past seven in the morning, except that this time it was lit: the amber lamps coming on down the west path, one at the boathouse turn, two at the brake, the odd one at the curve, all the way down to a tenth one that Annabeth could see from the top of the fence line, on a hook by a door at the bottom of the hill.

She stopped at the fence and got out the field book and turned to the top-right corner of the last fair sheet, and wrote under the morning's entry: 17:25. OFF THE TERRACE. WIND W 14. 16 PINS RECOVERED. NO DATA LOST. And under it, in the same small hand, because it was the same record: *Five up, five down. That is the whole of the arithmetic that matters today.*

The crew went to the pavilion for soup and bread because that was the arrangement, and Annabeth did not go with them. She went to the Big House, because there is a particular kind of tired that cannot be put down until the work is off the paper and on the wall, and she had been carrying a blank under a ridge line since August.

The parlor was cold and the lamp took two matches. She put the fair sheets out on the drafting table in order and weighted them with the field book and the pencil and the awl, and stood in front of the map wall with her sleeves rolled, and did the last work of the day.

She took the heading down.

NORTH SPRING — SURVEY PENDING had been on that wall since Sunday night. She rubbed it out with the soft end of the eraser and blew the crumbs off the sheet with the side of her hand, and then she drew the thing itself: the shelf, the plate of granite, the trough, the bowl, the lip. She drew it at the same scale as the rest of the camp, which meant it was small, which meant the whole north spring of Camp Half-Blood ended up about the size of her thumb on a wall two miles away from it. She drew the flow line out from under the slab, running down the trough, over the lip, and down through the granite in a dotted line with a small arrow at the end of it, and the dotted line came out, three contours lower, at the wet corner of the north path — the exact place where Percy Jackson had stood in the mud on a Monday in September and said the water was coming from somewhere uphill that wasn't on the map.

Then she wrote, in the small neat letters that did the camp's records:

NORTH SPRING. FEEDS N PATH DRAIN. FALL 31 FT/300 YDS. FLOW: SEEP, RUNNING.
FOUND 14 SEPT (T. GRACE). SURVEYED 7 OCT.
CREW: GRACE A. GRACE T. NAKAMURA O. BECKER M. JACKSON P.

She looked at it for a while.

Five names on a map wall, in a hand that did the camp's records, in a blank that had been blank since August. The spring had been there for ten thousand years and would be there in ten thousand more, and what had changed at four o'clock on a Saturday in October was that the camp now knew the shape of it, and knew it because five people had walked up a mountain with a tape and a rod and a piece of cake and had written down what was actually there instead of what they hoped was there.

Annabeth stood in the cold parlor in front of the wall and permitted herself about eleven seconds of it, which was her whole budget, and then put the chair back, and blew out the lamp, and went down the hill in the dark toward the north beach, where she could see, from the top of the path, a small orange light at the fire ring and a blue something moving beside it in the wind.

By the time Percy got down to the north beach it was full dark, and the fire ring had a fire in it, and the quilt was on the poles.

It looked right. That was the first thing, and he stood at the top of the sand for a second and took it in before he went down, because he had not expected a thing made out of other people's old clothes on two iron poles to look like it belonged on a beach. The quilt hung from the poles at the top of the sand above the fire, stretched along the whole ring like a sail, and the firelight came through it from behind, and every square of it showed: the boathouse roof with UNVERIFIED under it, the fish, the axe, the leaf, the crossed hammer and tongs, the lightning bolt in copper thread, the trident with the initial in the corner, the corner of a map, and, at the end, a soft grey square with four upright strokes and one across.

Clarisse had it staked out and tied off and was standing in front of it with her arms crossed, inspecting it, and did not look at Percy when he came down onto the sand, which meant she was pleased.

"It's hung wrong," she said.

"It's hung fine."

"The left corner's an inch lower than the right. I can see it from here."

"Nobody on this beach can see it but you, and you can't see it either, and you're going to leave it up, and you're going to deny saying anything about it in about four minutes."

"I'm re-hanging it in the morning," Clarisse said, and did not re-hang it, then or ever, and it stayed up an inch off for the whole of October.

Thalia was on the flat rock with her boot in her hand, looking at the heel of it, which was her way of being tired. Marcus had come down because Clarisse had gone up the hill and told him to, and was sitting on the sand with a bowl of soup and his back to the fire and his hands wrapped round it, entirely content, saying nothing. Olive was there too, with a blanket over her head like a tent, asleep before soup, which was the correct outcome for a ten-year-old who had drawn a spring and walked nine hours for it.

And at the edge of the firelight, in his coat with a can of lantern oil in one hand, Charlie Beckendorf was filling the little brass lamp that Silena had hung on the hook at the top of the beach for exactly the twenty minutes it took him to do it.

He had come at six, as promised, with oil, and had filled the lantern and trimmed the wick and lit it, and then had been handed a bowl of soup by Silena Beauregard in front of seven people, and had taken it, and had sat down on the flat stone at the edge of the light with it, and had not left, and it was now an hour and ten minutes later.

Percy watched the two of them for a while, sideways, without being obvious about it, because he had learned in five months that the most useful thing a little brother could do in a cabin was to know what was happening without ever being the person who said it out loud.

Silena had taken the far end of the quilt, the end with the fish in it, and she was sitting on the sand under it with her knees up and her bowl on her knee, and Beckendorf was four feet along the same edge of the quilt, on the sand, with an empty bowl beside him and his hands on his knees, and the two of them were talking about the hall. Not about anything else. The hall: the pump strainer, the guttering, the tile, the fact that the skylight leaked in the north corner in a storm and that a person with a ladder and a tube of sealant could fix it in an afternoon, and the fact that Clarisse had a rule about the deep end and that a rule about the deep end, in Beckendorf's professional opinion, was the best rule in the camp.

It was the least romantic conversation Percy had ever heard in his life, and he had never in his life seen two people more obviously sitting in the same hour together on purpose.

Then Beckendorf handed Silena the little wrench he had been using on the lantern, without looking, the way a person hands a tool to somebody who is standing nearer to the toolbox, and she took it, and their hands overlapped on it for about a second and a half, and neither of them said anything, and the fire moved, and Percy turned his head away and looked at the lake and took a very long drink of his soup and decided, on the whole, that he was going to remember this evening for a long time and was never once going to mention it to anybody.

Annabeth came down the path at ten past seven with her sleeves down and her hair full of the ridge and the field book under her arm, and stopped at the top of the sand, and looked at the quilt on the poles with the firelight coming through it, and did not say anything for a second, because she had spent four months making that map and had never once thought of it as something that would hang on a beach.

"It's up," she said.

"It's up," Clarisse said. "Left corner's off."

"Left corner's off," Annabeth agreed, and came down the sand, and Percy got up and made room and she sat down on the flat rock beside him under the wool blanket, with her boots off, and put her feet against his shin, which was the arrangement, and nobody had ever once had to discuss the arrangement.

"Done?" he said.

"Done. It's on the wall."

"The blank?"

"The blank has a spring in it, and a flow line, and five names." Annabeth drank his soup, which was hers now, and did not apologize. "And I got the fall wrong by four tenths of a foot on the first two stations and corrected it on the sheet, and the seep is running, and it comes out under a slab that's moved a foot since June, and the water goes down into the terrace and comes out at the wet corner of the north path, which is the thing you stood in the mud and told me about in September, and I have now written that down on a wall, and it has your name on it in brackets, and it says *load, enthusiasm*, and I am never going to change it."

"You could put *rod*," Percy said. "I held a tape for a hundred minutes."

"You stood in the water for a hundred minutes in October and never once mentioned it, which is worth more than the tape, and the brackets stay." She ate the rest of the soup and set the bowl down and leaned into him, shoulder to shoulder, and after a while her head came to rest against his, and the two of them sat on a rock at the top of the north beach under a quilt that a cabin had built out of its own dead shirts, and watched a fire go down, and said nothing at all for the length of a whole song that somebody was playing badly up at the pavilion.

Olive Nakamura woke up at half past seven under her blanket tent, ate two bowls of soup in the order she was given them, told everybody in the firelight that the fall over thirty-one feet in three hundred yards was, quote, *steeper than a roof*, and went back to sleep against Marcus's knee, which Marcus permitted by doing nothing at all about it.

Beckendorf stood up at twenty to eight and said he had a lamp to fill and a bench at six.

"There's a bench at six every day," Silena said.

"There is," he agreed, which was not an argument and was not meant to be, and he put his empty bowl in the basket the way a person did when the basket's owner was standing right there and they had noticed which basket it was, and then he went up the sand and up the path with the oil can in his hand, and the lantern he had filled and trimmed and lit went on burning at the top of the beach behind him.

Silena Beauregard watched him go up, and then sat back down under the end of the quilt with the fish in it, and pulled the corner over her knees, and did not explain a single thing to anybody, and Thalia Grace, from the flat rock, said, without any particular emphasis and without looking at any particular person, "It's a good quilt. Whoever sewed the fish should get a medal," and the color of the whole evening went on being completely normal.

Annabeth, under the wool blanket with her boots off and her feet against Percy's shin, opened the field book on her knees and showed him the last page in the firelight: the numbers, the stations, the flow line, the temperature at three feet into the bowl, the two corrected readings with CORR in the margin and her initials.

"That's the whole mountain," Percy said.

"That's the whole mountain," she agreed. "Thirty-one feet of fall and a hundred and four numbers and a plate of rock that moved a foot in four months, and half the camp is going to walk past that wall on Monday and not look at any of it."

"I'll look at it."

"You'll look at the brackets."

"I'll look at the brackets," Percy said, "and I'll look at the flow line, and I'll check whether the dotted line actually comes out at the wet corner of the north path, because I stood in that mud and I've got a personal interest."

Annabeth closed the field book and leaned into him and did not say the sentence, which was that she had already gone back and checked the dotted line against the drawn contours twice before she inked it, because she had known that he would, and because that was the whole reason the number was worth a hundred and four of anything.

Clarisse counted at eight: one, two, three, four, five, and then, after a pause, six, seven, eight, because there were three extra people on the beach and that was the new rule now and she had written it herself on a slate on a Tuesday.

"Eight," she said, out loud, to nobody. "All good."

Nobody answered her. Nobody had to. The fire went down to its deep coals and the wind came off the north ridge and moved the blue rag on a cairn two miles away, and nobody on the beach could see it and everybody on the beach knew exactly where it was, and the first night of the storm quilt on the north beach went on being the sort of evening that a person does not know is one until about four years later.

Annabeth Grace walked back up the hill at half past nine and did the whole day over again in her head, which is a thing she did on the nights that mattered, because a day you do not review is a day you have not finished with.

The fire had gone to coals. Clarisse had put the quilt back on the poles because it was going to stay there now, on the beach, tied to two iron poles above the fire ring where the whole camp could see it, and she had tied the knots herself in the dark with the flat efficiency of a woman roping a load onto a truck, and had said, out loud, in front of everybody, "Nobody takes it home. It lives here," which was the last plank in the whole structure and the one nobody had thought of.

Silena and Beckendorf had gone up the hill last, and had gone up separately, which Annabeth had watched with the enormous discipline of a girl who never mentions anything, and which she had decided was, in its own way, more of a statement than going up together would have been.

The day, in order: the fence line at first light with five of them in a row looking at the sunrise and not one of them saying anything about it. The long terrace in the frost. The cake at half past nine in a hollow above the camp. The sound of water before she could see it. Thirty-one feet of fall in three hundred yards. Marcus's rod, which had never moved once in four stations except when it was supposed to. Olive's drawing, at one inch to two feet, better than Annabeth could have done it in twice the time. Percy standing in an October stream for a hundred minutes and calling it warmer than the lake. Her sister, on a slab, tying a hemmed blue rag to six stones and standing back with her hand still on the knot.

And then the wall, and the five names.

She went up past the cabins with her hands in her pockets and the field book under her arm and the smell of woodsmoke coming off her own coat, and at the bottom of the Cabin One steps she stopped, because Percy was sitting on the third one, in his coat, with his hands in his pockets, being a lighthouse.

"You were supposed to go to bed," she said.

"I've been sent to bed and I've ignored it."

"By whom."

"By Clarisse. She told me she wasn't my mother and then told me when to sleep, which is legally the same thing, and Silena backed her up with a towel." He stood up and brushed the step off out of habit. "I walked you home. That's all. It's a Friday night thing and today was Saturday and I decided not to let it fall through the cracks."

Annabeth looked at him for a moment in the porch light.

"Your feet are still cold," she said. "From the trough."

"My feet have been cold since July. It's a documented condition."

"It's a documented condition that you stood in running water for a hundred minutes and then walked me home instead of going to bed, and your socks are in the bag, and you're going to get a cold on Monday, and I'll have to file it."

"File it under *facts*," Percy said.

"Everything goes under facts," Annabeth said, and then, because the porch light was on and the camp was asleep and the day had been the best one she had had in a year, she closed the distance and kissed him properly, standing on the second step so that she was nearly his height, with both hands in the front of his coat and her field book pressed flat against his chest between them, being the only thing in the whole camp still holding the shape of the day.

It went on long enough that the porch light, which was on a timer, went off.

They stayed where they were in the dark with their foreheads together, breathing, and the cold came up the hill off the lake and moved the pines, and somewhere down at the bottom of the slope a fire ring held a bed of coals under a quilt with a fish in it, and two miles up the shoulder of the north ridge, under a plate of granite the size of a cabin door, water came out of the ground at the same rate it had come out of the ground in September and would come out of the ground in the spring, and went down through a shelf of stone and out at the wet corner of the north path, where nobody had known it went until five people walked up a mountain and looked.

"Nine hours and forty minutes," Percy said, into her hair.

"What?"

"Yesterday you said twenty-two hours. This is the twenty-two hours. It's the same thing. It's the day, and it's over, and it's in the pile and it's in the wall, and I'm counting up."

Annabeth breathed out something that was almost a laugh and did not let go of his coat.

"You're a menace," she said.

"I'm roped," Percy said. "Roped menaces are safe."

"That was the lake."

"It's all the lake," he said. "Annabeth, it's all the same lake. The spring's the lake, the hall's the lake, the quilt's the lake. It's all just water that's been in a lot of places and came back." He put his arms around her and held her, and it was the ordinary kind of hold, the kind with no performance in it, two people on a set of steps at the end of a Saturday, and after a while he said, quietly, "Go to bed. You've got a wall to look at in the morning and you're going to want to go and look at it."

"I'm going to want to go and look at it," she admitted.

"I know. I'll stand there and be smug about the brackets."

"The brackets stay," Annabeth said, and went up the steps, and turned at the top with her hand on the door and looked down at him standing in the dark on the second step of somebody else's cabin, deliberately, for about four seconds, which was the budget and which she spent the whole of; and then she went in, and the lamp in the window went out a minute later, and the whole camp lay down for the night under a sky with more stars in it than anybody had names for.

Clarisse La Rue did the last thing she did every night, which was to go out onto the porch and look at the hill.

The lamps went up the west path one at a time and the tenth one was on the hook by her own door and the porch smelled of the cold and of soup and of eight people's coats, and the winter list was inside on the table with the last line of it written at the bottom in her blocky hand.

She had written, under the row of entries, at about half past nine, with a cup of tea going cold at her elbow and a fire dying in the stove behind her and four people breathing in her cabin and three more asleep up the hill:

RIDGE — DONE. WATER FOUND AGAIN BY BETTER PEOPLE THAN ME. ON THE WALL. FIVE NAMES.
QUILT — HUNG AT THE RING. LEFT CORNER OFF AN INCH. LEAVE IT.
LANTERNS — 9 UP, 10TH OURS.

And under that, in the very last space on the page, where she had been writing the same thing every night since a Sunday morning in a frost:

FIVE.

She stood at the rail a while with her arms crossed, listening to the stove tick down, and then she did the actual arithmetic, which she only ever did once, at night, on a porch, where nobody could see her face while she did it. Five in the cabin. Two in the cabin up the hill, plus a brother at the other camp who wrote, plus a girl who came down the hill with a bow and a bad heel and ate whatever was put in front of her. A half-brother somewhere south who made bolts and mended tote hooks. A mother under the sea who had raised a boy who was not hers and had never once said so.

That was not a number, that was a fence, and Clarisse La Rue was, more than anything else she had ever been in her life, the person who kept the gate.

She went inside and shut the door on the cold and left the tenth lantern burning on its hook, so that anybody walking up the west path late, in the dark, in October, would come around the cedar brake and see a light at the bottom of the hill and think, without knowing why, that somebody was home.
