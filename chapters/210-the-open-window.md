## Chapter 210: The Open Window

Annabeth started with the paper strip from the morning before. The lower latch had been measured and marked, but the note was still waiting for Chiron’s review. She laid it beside the window and checked the catch without pressing it down.

The sash moved a little in its frame. The latch did not meet the keeper.

She wrote the position on a new page. The old map of Cabin One’s window was folded under the measurement sheet, its corners softened from being opened and closed. She had drawn the whole frame three times over the past week, each version meant to show a different thing: where the water entered, how the sash shifted, how much space the latch needed.

None of them told her what was loose.

Thalia came in with her cane. She set it against the table rather than leaning it on the wall.

“You’re measuring again?” she asked.

“I’m checking whether yesterday’s mark moved.”

“Did it?”

“I don’t know.”

Thalia looked at the paper, then at the latch. “Could be the catch.”

“Could be the frame.”

“Do you want me to hold it?”

“I don’t know yet.”

Thalia sat on the bed by the door. She did not move closer. That was a choice Annabeth noticed and did not comment on.

Chiron arrived before breakfast with Beckendorf. Annabeth held the measurement page out to them. Chiron checked the notes from the rain, then watched her test the sash.

“Where did the water reach?” he asked.

“Along the lower edge, near the latch. It didn’t travel past the sill.”

“Did you see water on the wood inside the frame?”

“Only on the paint line.”

Beckendorf crouched by the latch. He tested the keeper with one finger. It shifted, then held when he lifted his hand.

“The screws are loose,” he said. “The frame feels sound. I’d still check it from the outside before we put weight on it.”

“I can do that,” Annabeth said.

“Not from the sill.”

“I know.”

Chiron studied her page. “If you want to take the repair, choose what you can reach safely. We can secure the sash, check the frame, then decide whether the keeper needs a new screw hole or only a tighter fit.”

Annabeth looked at the two pencil marks. “I want to test the keeper before we move it.”

“That is reasonable,” Beckendorf said. “If the wood is stripped, tightening it won’t help.”

Thalia rose from the bed. “I can hold the sash while you check.”

Annabeth took the note and stepped to the table. “Not until I know whether it moves.”

“I’m not trying to rush you.”

“I know.”

“I can keep it steady.”

“I need to see how far it shifts.”

Thalia paused, then lowered her hands. “All right.”

Annabeth felt an answer gathering in her mouth, too sharp to be useful. She wanted to say that this was her task, that she did not want Thalia to take a tool away from her simply because she was slower or more careful. But Thalia had not taken anything. She had offered.

“Can you hold the lamp?” Annabeth asked. “I need both hands for the gauge.”

Thalia reached for it. “Yes.”

The word was simple. It did not make the offer a promise of authority.

Percy came by after Chiron and Beckendorf left to prepare breakfast. He had a small tool roll and a folded cloth.

“Chiron said you might want these.”

“Did you ask him which ones?”

“He gave me the list.”

Annabeth smiled. “That’s good.”

Percy set the roll on the floor instead of on her paper. She slid one hand into his and squeezed his fingers once.

“Do you want me to stay?” he asked.

“Nearby would be good. I may need you to bring the strip from the shed.”

“Tell me when.”

He kissed her temple. She leaned against his shoulder for a heartbeat, then returned to the window. Their closeness did not change who would hold the tool.

Thalia lifted the lamp as Annabeth asked. Annabeth pressed the wooden gauge against the sash and moved it only far enough to see the gap. The keeper sat almost level, but the screw at its lower corner had backed out.

“There,” Annabeth said. “It’s moving at the screw.”

“Can I keep the lamp higher?” Thalia asked.

“Yes.”

Thalia adjusted the light without touching the frame. Annabeth traced the screw hole with a blunt pencil. She could try tightening it, but if the wood had split, the catch would loosen again under weather. She needed to know whether the original hole held.

“Can you pass me the small driver?” she asked.

Thalia looked to the tool roll. “Which one?”

“Flat head, narrow handle.”

Thalia found it and held it out by the grip. Annabeth took it. She eased the screw backward a quarter-turn and stopped. The keeper shifted under her thumb.

“Don’t pull it yet,” Beckendorf had said. She remembered the direction, and kept the screw where it was.

The wood around the hole had not torn. There was no fresh split. The lower screw was simply loose.

Annabeth rested the driver on the sill. “I think I can tighten it.”

“You think?” Thalia said.

“I’m not certain.”

“Could check the outside.”

“Chiron said he’d check it with Beckendorf.”

“Then wait for them?”

Annabeth looked at the screw again. The keeper had moved, but the hole looked intact. “I want to show Chiron what I found before I turn it.”

“All right.”

Thalia set the lamp on the floor. “Do you want me to sit?”

“Here, please.”

Thalia took the chair by the door and kept the cane beside her knee. She did not leave. Annabeth looked through the gap in the sash. The morning air smelled damp and clean. The window was open just enough to admit it.



Chiron came back with Beckendorf ten minutes later. Neither of them touched the keeper. Beckendorf looked at the pencil mark and the screw.

“The wood is intact,” he said. “The screw backed out. If you tighten it carefully, you won’t need a new hole.”

“How tight?” Annabeth asked.

“Enough that the keeper stops shifting. Don’t torque it past that.”

“What about the gap along the lower edge?”

“That’s a separate question,” Chiron said. “The catch may bring the sash closer. We’ll see after it holds.”

Annabeth checked the note she had made. “If I tighten the keeper and it still leaks, I want to fit a thin felt strip. Removable, no glue in the frame.”

“Reasonable,” Beckendorf said. “But only after we test the close.”

Thalia shifted in the chair. “I can test it from outside.”

Chiron looked at her cane, then at the window. He did not comment on the brace. “The outside check can wait until the ground near the sill is dry. We need a second person on the ground, not on the ladder.”

Thalia leaned back. “I didn’t say I was climbing.”

“I know,” Chiron said. “I am making the limit clear for all of us.”

Annabeth turned toward the window. “I want to do the repair from inside.”

“Then you can,” Chiron said. “I’ll stay here while you do it.”

He did not take the driver from her. Beckendorf set the thin felt strip on the table and stepped back.

Annabeth was glad for the space. She was also aware of Thalia watching her hands.

“Can you hold the lamp a little closer?” she asked.

Thalia moved it nearer the keeper.

“Too close.”

“Sorry.”

“Could you keep it where it was?”

“Yes.”

The lamp moved back. Annabeth placed the driver in the screw head. The first turn met resistance, then the keeper slid into line. She stopped and checked the wood. No crack. She tightened the screw a quarter-turn more.

“Hold it there,” she said.

Thalia held the lamp steady. “Do you want me to check if it moves?”

“Not yet.”

Thalia nodded. She stayed beside the chair, cane against her knee, free hand resting on the table.

Annabeth leaned the sash closed. It met the keeper with a soft click. She opened it again, then closed it a second time. The catch held. She pressed lightly against the frame, not enough to force it, then checked whether the keeper shifted.

It didn’t.

“Do you want to test it?” Chiron asked.

“Yes.”

“Your test or mine?”

“Mine.”

She took the narrow paper strip and placed it at the lower edge. Then she closed the sash and tried to pull the strip free. It resisted, but came away in one piece.

“Still a small gap,” she said.

“Where?” Beckendorf asked.

She pointed to the lower corner nearest the latch. “Here.”

“Would felt close it without making the sash difficult to open?”

“I think so. I want to check the thickness first.”

Chiron nodded. “That is your call. We can wait and see how the latch behaves in the next rain, or use removable felt now and inspect it afterward.”

Annabeth looked at the window, then at the strip. “I want to use the felt. If it makes the sash drag, I can take it off.”

“Then that’s the repair you’ve chosen.”

Thalia set the lamp down. “Can I help place it?”

Annabeth looked at her. “Will you hold the end while I mark the length?”

“Yes.”

“Not pull it tight. Just keep it from curling.”

“I can do that.”

Annabeth handed her one end of the felt. Thalia held it lightly while Annabeth measured the lower edge. They did not touch the screw or the sash together. The repair had one pair of hands doing the work and another keeping the material straight.



Annabeth trimmed the felt with the small scissors. She left the backing on for now and held the strip beneath the sill, checking how the edge lined up with the latch.

“Does it need to go all the way across?” Thalia asked.

“I don’t know. I want to see what happens if I cover only the lower gap.”

“If it leaves a second leak—”

“Then I’ll add another piece.”

Thalia’s mouth tightened. Annabeth looked at her.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That’s not nothing.”

“I thought we were trying to decide together.”

“We are.”

“You keep saying ‘I’ll’.”

Annabeth set down the scissors. “It’s my window.”

“It’s our cabin.”

“I know.”

“And I’m not trying to take it from you.”

“I know that too.”

“Do you?”

The question held more than the latch. Annabeth picked up the paper strip. The pale edge had a pencil mark where the water had reached. “I don’t want to make this into something you have to fix for me.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You keep asking how I want it done.”

“That’s not the same as doing it.”

“I know.”

Thalia shifted her brace under the table. “You can choose the repair. I can still want to help.”

Annabeth rubbed the pencil mark with her thumb. “I know. I just don’t want you to decide that helping means taking the tool.”

“I’m not going to.”

“You did that last time.”

“Last time I thought you needed me.”

“I didn’t.”

“I know that now.”

Annabeth had meant to say it cleanly. It came out like a verdict. She looked at Thalia, who sat with both hands resting on the cane handle.

“I’m sorry,” Annabeth said. “You were trying to be there.”

“I was trying to be useful.”

“You can be here without fixing it.”

“I’m learning that.”

Annabeth turned the felt strip over and peeled back a short length of adhesive backing. “Can you hold the lamp again?”

“Yes.”

“Not the strip.”

“Just the lamp.”

“Right.”

Thalia stood and positioned the light. Annabeth placed the felt along the lower edge, stopping it short of the keeper. She pressed one section at a time and checked that the adhesive did not fold. The line was straight enough to seal without dragging against the moving sash.

“Could you pass me the paper strip?” she asked.

Thalia handed it to her.

Annabeth closed the window. It caught with a soft click. She checked the gap again, then opened it and closed it a second time. The strip moved smoothly.

The repair looked modest. No part of the frame had changed. The latch met the keeper; the felt filled the space where water had entered. The window could still open.

Annabeth held out the paper strip. “Would you test it?”

Thalia took it. She pressed the thin edge against the lower corner and slid it along the sill, stopping where the felt began. It stayed in place. She pulled once, gently. The strip came away without tearing.

“It holds,” she said.

“Does it catch?”

“Yes.”

“Can you open it?”

Thalia tried the handle. The sash lifted without resistance.

Annabeth watched the latch, not Thalia’s face. “All right.”

Thalia closed it again. The sound was quieter this time. The room no longer let the rain through at the corner.

Chiron looked at the result and made no declaration beyond, “We’ll check it after the next rain.”

“I’ll write that down,” Annabeth said.

“Good.”

He stepped away from the window. Beckendorf took the leftover felt and scissors back to the tool roll. Neither told Annabeth the repair was perfect. She had not expected them to.

Thalia lowered the lamp. “Do you want me to stay while you make the note?”

“Yes.”

“Okay.”

Annabeth wrote the repair in the margin: *lower keeper tightened in existing hole; removable felt strip added; test passed today; recheck after rain.* The window remained on the list until the next weather check. The note beside it did not say the job was permanent.



They cleaned the tools in the covered porch because Chiron had asked them to return the screwdriver dry. The rain had softened to a mist, turning the practice field the color of old slate. Thalia sat on the bench with the lamp between her boots while Annabeth checked each tool for water and set it on a towel.

“You don’t have to stay until I finish this,” Annabeth said.

“I know.”

“You can go inside.”

“I know that too.”

Annabeth looked up. Thalia was watching the path, not her. The cane leaned against the bench within easy reach. “Then why are you waiting?”

“Because you asked me to stay while you wrote the note.”

“I did. That’s finished.”

“Do you want me to go?”

The question had no trap in it. Annabeth considered the clean answers and the answer she wanted. “No.”

“Then I’ll wait.”

Annabeth rubbed the screwdriver head with the towel. She had the instinct to explain why the note mattered, how the window had let water through, why she had decided against replacing the keeper. Instead she set the tool beside the others.

“I don’t know how to make help sound like help when I’m annoyed,” she said.

“Most people don’t.”

“That’s not a useful answer.”

“It wasn’t meant to be. You asked how.”

Annabeth slid the screwdriver into its slot. “I keep hearing ‘I’ll’ come out of my mouth. I’ll tighten it. I’ll check it. I’ll take care of it. When you asked to help, I heard someone deciding the repair for me.”

“I wasn’t deciding it.”

“I know that now.”

“Do you?”

She looked at Thalia. “I know you were holding the lamp.”

Thalia smiled, small and tired. “That’s a start.”

The rain ticked against the porch roof. A few campers crossed the yard, heads ducked beneath jackets. The repaired window sat out of sight behind the cabin wall. Annabeth could still picture the gap: thin, uneven, easy to miss until rain pushed through it.

“I don’t want needing a hand to turn into owing someone the whole job,” Annabeth said.

“It doesn’t have to.”

“And I don’t want refusing a hand to turn into proving I don’t need anyone.”

Thalia rested her palms on the cane handle. “Those are both different problems.”

“I know.”

“Do you want to talk about them?”

“No.”

“Then we don’t have to.”

Annabeth finished drying the last wrench. She was grateful Thalia did not ask her to turn the moment into a promise.

The Big House door opened. Percy came out carrying two mugs, one balanced on top of the other. He had taken his jacket off and held it folded over one arm.

“I heard the tools had a porch meeting,” he said. “I brought tea as a bribe.”

“Who are you bribing?” Annabeth asked.

“Whichever person looks most likely to throw a screwdriver at me.”

“Then you should have brought something stronger.”

He handed her one mug. Their fingers brushed. The tea smelled like mint and honey. He had remembered she would take honey only when the weather turned cold, not when she was already warm from work. A small thing, precisely noticed.

“Did it hold?” he asked.

“We won’t know until it rains again.”

“So it’s a successful maybe.”

“It’s a measured maybe.”

“Even better.”

He took the second mug to Thalia and sat at the far end of the bench, leaving room for her cane. No one asked Annabeth if she wanted a report. She drank and let the warmth spread into her hands.

Percy leaned closer. “You were right to make me stay outside the cabin.”

“I know.”

“I did not go in.”

“I know that too.”

“Would you like a certificate?”

“Only if it says you followed one instruction.”

He looked solemn. “I can have it engraved.”

Thalia hid a laugh in her tea. Annabeth put her shoulder against Percy’s for one quiet breath. He did not ask what she had decided. His hand found hers between the mugs, and she held it until the cold made her fingers stiff.

When the tools were dry, Thalia walked back toward the Big House beside them. She kept her pace. Percy matched it without making a show of slowing down. Annabeth carried the lamp, which was heavier than it looked and still had a warm rim from the window work.

At the porch steps, Thalia stopped. “I’m going to get out of this damp shirt.”

“Good idea,” Annabeth said.

Thalia’s gaze flicked to her face, then away. “I’ll see you at dinner.”

“See you then.”

Annabeth watched her go inside. The exchange did not repair everything. It was not supposed to.

Percy reached for the lamp. “Want me to take it?”

“No.”

“Okay.”

She waited for him to make a joke, then realized he had left the choice where she put it. She adjusted the handle in her palm. “Thanks for asking.”

“Anytime.”

They went in together.

The dining room had filled before they reached it. Rain gave everyone an excuse to stay inside, so the benches were crowded and the aisle between them had narrowed to one person at a time. Annabeth set the lamp on the counter and found her place beside Percy. Thalia took the seat across from them, where she could stretch her leg without blocking the aisle.

Ianthe was already there, sorting the bread by size.

“That one is broken,” she told the younger camper at the end of the bench. “Take the other half.”

“I wanted this one.”

“It’s smaller.”

“I know.”

Ianthe reached for it anyway. Annabeth saw the child pull his hand back. For a beat, Ianthe looked as if she might insist on the arrangement she had invented. Then she stopped, fingers hovering above the plate.

“Fine,” she said. “Keep it.”

She moved the intact piece to the center of the basket as if the decision had been hers all along. The boy returned to his bread, shoulders still raised.

Annabeth took her seat and let the moment settle. It was a small correction, but not an apology. Ianthe looked over, caught Annabeth watching, and lifted her chin.

“What?” she asked.

“Nothing.”

“That means something.”

“It means I’m trying to eat dinner.”

Ianthe sat back. “You always say that when you don’t want to answer.”

Annabeth tore a piece of bread. “Then you already know my answer.”

Across the table, Thalia kept her attention on her plate. She did not step in. That was her choice, and Annabeth did not make it for her.

Percy passed the bowl of roasted vegetables. “These are good.”

“They’re carrots,” Annabeth said.

“Good carrots.”

The group laughed, and the air loosened. Conversation returned to the day’s work: the coils drying under shelter, the canvas seam, the line of flags that would need checking when the wind changed. Beckendorf said he would inspect the rack loop tomorrow if the humidity eased. Ianthe asked if he could compare her notes at the same time.

“If the coils are dry and tagged, yes,” Beckendorf said. “Not before.”

“I can do both.”

“You can help with both. I’m still checking the splice.”

Ianthe frowned at her plate. “It’s only a loop.”

“It held more weight than you thought it would,” Annabeth said.

“I know what it held.”

“Then you know why he’s checking it.”

Ianthe set down her fork. “I wasn’t going to cut it.”

“No one said you were.”

“You all talk as if I’m going to wreck something.”

The words landed harder than the room deserved. Annabeth saw Thalia’s gaze rise, then pause. She looked at Ianthe, whose jaw had gone rigid.

“People are waiting for checks,” Annabeth said. “That includes your wind notes. It doesn’t mean they think you’re going to wreck something.”

“Maybe it means you don’t trust me.”

“Trust isn’t the same as skipping the work.”

“Easy to say when you’re already in charge of every list.”

Annabeth set her fork down. The table noise thinned around them, though no one turned outright. She could have said she was not in charge of every list. She could have explained how responsibility had come to her by the unglamorous route of noticing what everyone else had not recorded. Both would have been true and would not have answered Ianthe.

“I’m not deciding whether you get a room or a cabin bed,” Annabeth said. “And I’m not deciding whether your notes are good. Beckendorf will check the source and the comparison. Those are separate things.”

Ianthe’s mouth tightened at “room.” She glanced toward the far door, where the guest hall began. “You keep saying that as if it helps.”

“It doesn’t decide anything. It tells you what is still open.”

“What if everything stays open until no one has to say no?”

“Then we’ll still have to make the decision.”

“And if the answer is no?”

Annabeth did not answer at once. The question could mean a bed, Zeus’s return, or the hundred smaller invitations that people made when they did not know where to put a newcomer. Annabeth knew better than to pretend she could answer all of them.

“If the answer is no, you should hear it clearly,” she said. “I don’t know yet what the answer is.”

Ianthe looked toward Thalia, but Thalia did not rescue her with a promise. Her hands stayed around her mug. “You don’t know,” Ianthe repeated.

“No.”

“That is the only honest thing you’ve said.”

“It’s not the only honest thing.”

Ianthe turned back to her plate. Annabeth felt the urge to win the sentence back. She swallowed it. The food had cooled while they talked. She picked up her fork.

After a moment, Ianthe said, “The window still leaks?”

“We tightened the keeper and put in a temporary strip. We need another rain to check it.”

“You did it today?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because it was letting rain in.”

“You could have asked someone else to do it.”

“I asked Thalia to hold the lamp.”

“That isn’t what I meant.”

“I know.”

Ianthe pushed a carrot around the plate. “I mean you don’t have to do every repair yourself.”

“I didn’t.”

“You made the plan.”

“I did.”

“So you did it yourself.”

Annabeth looked at her, hearing the familiar shape of a criticism that pretended to be practical. “Planning the work is part of the repair.”

“Not always.”

“No. Not always.”

Ianthe appeared surprised that Annabeth agreed. The next words came slower. “You make it hard to help.”

“That may be true.”

“Thalia said you kept saying ‘I’ll.’”

Annabeth’s fork stopped halfway to her mouth. She looked at Thalia. Thalia’s face closed, not with anger but with a question: whether Annabeth wanted her to correct the claim. Annabeth could have asked when they had talked. She could have asked how much Thalia had told her. Instead she recognized the likely shape of the moment. Ianthe had noticed what she noticed; Thalia had not shared anything private.

“She heard me say it,” Annabeth said. “I did.”

“I’m not wrong, then.”

“No. But being right doesn’t make your way of saying it helpful.”

Ianthe’s eyes narrowed. “You don’t have to make every conversation a lesson.”

“You asked if you were wrong.”

“I asked if you make it hard to help.”

“Sometimes I do.”

The younger camper at the end of the bench reached for the bread basket. Ianthe moved it toward him before he had to stretch, then glanced at Annabeth as if to see whether the gesture counted for anything. Annabeth kept eating. A small better choice could be a small better choice without turning into evidence.

Percy reached over and took the last carrot from Annabeth’s plate. She bumped his knee with hers.

“You said good carrots,” she whispered.

“I meant the ones I ate.”

“That was the last one.”

“I know.”

“You could have asked.”

“I could have.” He set the carrot back on her plate, smiling. “May I steal the last carrot?”

“No.”

“May I trade you for half my bread?”

“Maybe.”

He broke the bread cleanly in two and placed the larger piece beside her fork. The ordinary exchange gave her a place to put her attention. They had learned to be together without turning every quiet moment into a test. She leaned toward him, and he kissed her hairline before returning to his dinner.

At the far side of the table, Thalia was listening to a story about a spilled bucket. Her mouth softened at the punchline. Ianthe listened too, even though she kept pretending not to. The room continued around the difficult sentence they had left unfinished.

When dinner ended, people carried dishes to the wash station. Annabeth stacked plates while Percy rinsed the bowls. Ianthe lifted the bread basket, paused, then asked the camper at the end of the table if he wanted to carry it. He said yes. She let him.

The change was barely visible. Annabeth saw it because she had been watching for it.

“Don’t,” Ianthe said without looking at her.

“Don’t what?”

“Make that face.”

“I don’t know what face I’m making.”

“The one where you decide I did one thing right and now I’m improving.”

Annabeth passed a plate to Percy. “I wasn’t deciding anything.”

“You do that too.”

“Do what?”

“Keep score in your head.”

“I’m not keeping a score.”

“Then stop looking like I passed.”

Annabeth put the plate down. “You’re right. I shouldn’t turn one moment into a verdict.”

Ianthe seemed to have expected an argument. Her shoulders lowered a fraction, then rose again. “You could say that without making it sound like you’re the only one who has to change.”

“I’m not.”

“Good.”

The bread basket reached the wash station. The younger camper set it down with an exaggerated bow. Ianthe rolled her eyes. “You’re ridiculous.”

He grinned. “You asked me to help.”

“I did not ask you to be ridiculous.”

“You didn’t specify.”

He left with the other campers. Ianthe watched him go, then wiped a crumb off the counter that was already clean. Annabeth wanted to tell her she had caught herself. She wanted to name the earlier reach for the smaller piece of bread. She wanted to ask if the argument had been about the bread or being watched.

She did none of those things. Some observations were not gifts if they were handed over at the wrong time.



Annabeth returned to the cabin after the dining room had emptied. The mist had thinned; water dripped from the eaves in slow, separate taps. She carried a notebook, the leftover felt, and a small ruler. The window no longer rattled when the breeze shifted. The quiet made the repair feel more finished than it was.

She opened the sash and checked the strip once more. It lifted cleanly. When she closed it, the keeper caught. She wrote down the time and added a note to test the lower edge after rain. Then she stopped herself from adding *repair complete*.

It was not complete. It was usable and still under observation.

The distinction mattered, though she could not have explained to anyone why the exact words made her feel steadier. She copied the measurement into the cabin maintenance page and put a small mark beside the day, not a check. The check would come when there was weather to test it.

A voice behind her said, “You really do write everything down.”

Annabeth turned. Ianthe stood just inside the doorway, arms crossed. She had taken off her damp sweater and changed into a camp shirt. Her hair, still darkened from the mist, had been brushed back.

“Not everything.”

“Just everything that can turn into a problem later.”

“That’s one way to use a notebook.”

“Is there another?”

“Remembering where we put things.”

“Same thing.”

“It’s not.”

Ianthe moved toward the window but stopped a step away. She looked at the keeper. “This is the lower room, right?”

“Yes.”

“Where I’d sleep if I stayed here.”

“If you stayed here, we’d still need to work out the room plan. This window is one part of that.”

“So you are considering it.”

Annabeth held the pencil over the page. “I’m considering the facts we have.”

“That sounds like a yes with more steps.”

“It isn’t a yes.”

Ianthe’s expression sharpened. “You’re allowed to say that.”

“I know.”

“Then why not?”

“Because I don’t know yet.”

“Do you always need to know everything before you answer?”

“No. But I need to know what I’m answering.”

Ianthe leaned her shoulder against the wall. “You think I’m asking for the room.”

“Aren’t you?”

“I’m asking if you’re going to keep me in the guest hall until someone decides I’m too much trouble.”

“That isn’t the plan.”

“Then what is?”

Annabeth set the pencil down. She did not want to supply a comforting answer that would become a promise before anyone had agreed. “You have a guest room while we figure out a stable arrangement. We haven’t decided where you’ll sleep after that. The cabin beds have to be counted. The window and cot need checking. People who already sleep here need a say.”

“People who already sleep here get a say in whether I belong?”

“They get a say in the room they share.”

“That sounds a lot like belonging.”

“It’s a real difference.”

“Maybe to you.”

Annabeth could hear the question under the accusation. She had heard versions of it from every person who arrived after a door had already been built. The difference between being welcomed and being accommodated could be paper-thin; the person waiting outside did not care how carefully she named the line.

“It doesn’t mean you don’t belong,” Annabeth said. “It means I can’t make a room decision for everyone and call it kindness.”

“You can make a decision for yourself.”

“I did. I want you here.”

Ianthe watched her, wary. “You do?”

“Yes.”

“Even when I’m bossy?”

“I don’t like being ordered around.”

“That’s not the same as wanting me gone.”

“No.”

“I know how to be useful.”

“I know.”

“You say that like you don’t believe it.”

“I believe you can be useful. I don’t think you have to earn a bed by proving it.”

The answer landed somewhere between them. Ianthe looked away first, studying the rain-darkened sill. “Dad likes when I can show him something.”

Annabeth did not ask what he had said. Thalia had not told her anything about the conversation, and whatever Ianthe wanted to repeat belonged to her. “It’s good to have something you’re proud of.”

“It’s better if he notices.”

“Yes.”

“He noticed the notes.”

“That matters to you.”

“It should matter to you too. They could help.”

“They might. Beckendorf will check them against the flags when the coils are ready.”

“You keep saying that. He could do it tomorrow.”

“He said he could do it when the coils are dry.”

“Maybe he could move the schedule.”

Annabeth felt the familiar tug: solve it, find the faster path, make the answer less disappointing. She held still. “The schedule is his.”

Ianthe’s mouth tightened. “You could ask.”

“I could. I’m not going to ask him to put wet equipment aside so the test happens sooner.”

“Why not?”

“Because the test is supposed to tell us whether your notes match what happened. If the conditions are wrong, the comparison won’t mean much.”

“Maybe they’re not wrong.”

“Then it can wait until we can tell.”

Ianthe looked at the notebook. “You and Beckendorf act like waiting is neutral.”

“It isn’t neutral. It’s still the right thing here.”

“Who says?”

“He does. I agree.”

Ianthe’s brow lifted. “At least that one was clear.”

Annabeth almost smiled. “I can try to be clearer.”

“You could write it on a card.”

“I could.”

“Then you’d have a list of things you can’t decide.”

“That sounds like a very boring card.”

“It would be useful.”

“I don’t need your approval to write it.”

“I didn’t say you did.”

“You said I should.”

“I said you could.”

Annabeth breathed out. The distinction was familiar; it was also irritating. “You can make a suggestion without turning it into an instruction.”

“And you can hear one without acting like I’m taking over.”

“That’s true.”

Ianthe folded her arms tighter. “You’re doing that thing again.”

“What thing?”

“Agreeing too fast so the other person has to be unreasonable if they keep talking.”

Annabeth sat with the observation. It stung because it was accurate. She sometimes used calm as a wall: if she conceded in a measured voice, the other person had to shout to stay in the conversation.

“I don’t want you to stop talking,” she said.

“Then don’t say things like you’ve filed the whole argument.”

“I’ll try.”

“You don’t have to say ‘I’ll’ every time.”

Annabeth glanced at the notebook. “You noticed that too.”

“It’s hard not to.”

“I’m still working on it.”

“Okay.” Ianthe pushed away from the wall. She looked as if she wanted to say more, but had not found the sentence. “If I help with the room count, I’m not trying to make people give me a bed.”

“I didn’t think you were.”

“Maybe you do.”

“I don’t.”

“Good.”

She turned toward the door.

“Could you help me check the storage list tomorrow?” Annabeth asked.

Ianthe paused. “Why?”

“We’ll need to know which supplies can stay in the guest hall and what can move if we adjust the beds. You’re good at seeing what’s missing.”

“I am.”

“I’m asking because it would help. You can say no.”

Ianthe weighed the request, visibly resisting the urge to answer for both of them. “I can check it.”

“Thanks.”

“But I’m not moving anyone’s things.”

“I won’t ask you to.”

“Good.”

She left the notebook on the desk and stepped into the hall. Annabeth did not call after her. The answer was a start, not a room assignment.

The next morning, the sky had cleared without becoming bright. Wet leaves stuck to the cabin steps, and the air smelled like rainwater and pine. Annabeth took the room inventory sheet to the guest hall after breakfast. She had not written a list of what Ianthe could keep. She had written a list of what the room contained, who owned each item, and what needed checking before anything moved.

Ianthe stood beside the narrow table, reading the sheet upside down.

“You’ve listed the spare blankets by owner,” she said.

“Some belong to the cabin. Some are camp stock.”

“Does it matter?”

“If they move, yes.”

“They’re blankets.”

“People use them.”

“Then put down who uses them.”

“That’s what I’m doing.”

Ianthe straightened. “You have a name for every pillow?”

“Not every pillow.”

“How do you know which ones are camp stock?”

“Inventory marks.”

“That’s ridiculous.”

“It keeps us from assigning something that belongs to someone else.”

“People leave things everywhere. You can’t keep track of every object in camp.”

“No. But we can keep track of what we move.”

Ianthe reached toward a folded quilt on the shelf. “This can go.”

“Let’s check the tag.”

“It’s a quilt.”

“Which belongs to the Apollo cabin.”

Ianthe pulled her hand back. “It was on the shelf.”

“Because we’re storing it until their roof dries.”

“That was not on the list.”

“It’s on the second page.”

“You have a second page?”

Annabeth handed it over. Ianthe scanned the lines and frowned. “You could combine them.”

“I could.”

“They’re about the same room.”

“They’re different owners.”

“They’re still all here.”

Annabeth waited. Ianthe had the paper, and she could see the logic as clearly as Annabeth could. She did not have to enjoy it. “What would you combine?”

“The items that need to move first. You’ve grouped by ownership, not by what’s in the way.”

“That’s a fair point.”

Ianthe blinked. “It is?”

“Yes. We can add a ‘check before moving’ mark for things that block the bed plan.”

“Then put it at the top.”

“I will.”

“You could do it now.”

Annabeth took the pencil out of her pocket. “I can do it now.”

Ianthe sat on the end of the bed and watched as Annabeth drew a new column. “You really do let the paper decide when you don’t want to answer.”

Annabeth paused with the pencil against the page. “You think I’m hiding behind it.”

“You hide behind it and behind other people. Beckendorf will check the wind notes. Thalia will decide if she wants me nearby. The cabin will decide where I sleep. You make it sound fair because no one can point to one person and say no.”

“That’s not what I’m trying to do.”

“I know what you’re trying to do. I’m telling you how it feels.”

Annabeth put down the pencil. She wanted to tell Ianthe that other people’s consent was not a shield for herself. That was true, but the words felt defensive. “I should say what I can decide.”

“Yes.”

“I want you to stay at camp. I want you to have a place here.”

“But you don’t know where.”

“I don’t know where.”

“And if the cabin says there’s no bed?”

“Then we figure out the next option with you.”

“More paper.”

“Probably.”

Ianthe rubbed the edge of the sheet. “You sound like Dad when he wants a meeting to be over.”

Annabeth’s throat tightened, though she kept her face still. She knew Zeus as a presence that could turn weather into pressure and rooms into stages. She knew him as a father in the way that any child knew the stories that had been repeated about him, plus the handful of direct moments she had lived. She did not know whether Ianthe meant it as an insult or a recognition.

“Maybe I’m more like him than I want to be,” Annabeth said.

Ianthe studied her. “You really think that?”

“I think I can make people feel dismissed when I’m trying to be reasonable.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No. It isn’t.”

Ianthe nodded as if storing the difference for later. “I think I’m like him too.”

Annabeth turned the sheet around so the columns faced them both. “What part?”

“I want people to notice when I do something well. I tell them what to do before I ask. If someone gets in my way, I decide they’re being stupid.” She said it quickly, like a list she had memorized for an exam. “Sometimes they are.”

“Sometimes they are.”

Ianthe looked at her.

“Sometimes people make bad choices,” Annabeth said. “That doesn’t mean you’re the only person who can see it.”

“That sounds like you’re calling me bossy again.”

“I’m saying you can be bossy. I can be controlling. Those are things we do, not everything we are.”

Ianthe wrinkled her nose. “You sound like Thalia.”

“That might be worse.”

“It is.”

They both smiled, but Ianthe’s smile left first. “She tells me to choose for myself, then looks like she’s waiting to see what I choose.”

“Does she?”

“Sometimes. She watches me too closely.”

Annabeth remembered how often Thalia had stepped between the room and Ianthe, as if closeness could keep the girl from being disappointed. “I can’t answer for her.”

“I know.”

“I can tell her what you said if you want.”

“No.”

“Okay.”

“Don’t tell her.”

“I won’t.”

Ianthe took the inventory page and found a line she wanted to cross out. “This can stay.”

“Which one?”

“The small stool. It’s not blocking anything.”

“It belongs to the Big House.”

“Then ask Chiron.”

“We can ask.”

Ianthe put a check beside the stool instead of a strike-through. “There. I helped.”

“You did.”

“Don’t make it a lesson.”

“I won’t.”

They finished the first pass. Nothing moved. The list now had two columns and three items that required someone else’s permission. The room plan remained no closer to settled. It was still useful to know what could stay without turning the guess into a promise.

Outside, the path had begun to dry. Annabeth folded the inventory sheet and put it on the table where Chiron could see it. Ianthe remained seated, her eyes on the narrow bed.

“Does it feel like being kept here?” Annabeth asked.

“I’m not sure.”

“You can say you’re not sure.”

“I am.”

“All right.”

“Sometimes I think if Dad came back and said the word, everyone would stop making lists.”

Annabeth leaned against the doorframe. “Would you want that?”

“I want him to come back.”

“That’s not the same question.”

Ianthe’s gaze shifted up. “No. It’s not.”

Annabeth waited, but she did not add the question again. Ianthe picked up the paper and folded its edge into a sharp crease, then smoothed it out.

“Maybe I’d like it if he said he wanted me there,” she said.

“He could say that without deciding the bed.”

“He could.”

“Do you want to ask him?”

Ianthe’s fingers stopped on the crease. “I already asked for him to come back.”

Annabeth thought of all the ways that sentence could mean more than its words. She did not ask when she had asked, or what he had answered. “Okay.”

Ianthe stood. “You’re not going to tell me what to do?”

“I could tell you some things.”

“I know.”

“But not about that.”

“You’re learning.”

“So are you.”

“Don’t make it sound like we’re the same.”

“I didn’t.”

“Good.”

Ianthe walked ahead into the hall. Annabeth followed after a moment, leaving the sheet on the table. She would bring it to Chiron when the room was empty and ask about the stool. Ianthe had said she was willing to help, not that she wanted every decision handed back to her. Those were different things.

Annabeth found Thalia later in the covered archery lane, where the targets had been brought in to dry. Thalia was helping a pair of younger campers sort arrows by condition. One had a feather bent sideways; another had a shaft that needed to be marked for repair. Thalia crouched to show them how to check the fletching without twisting it. She did not touch the bow resting beside the bench.

“Don’t pull it straight,” she said. “You’ll make it worse.”

The younger camper released the feather. “It looked crooked.”

“It is crooked. That’s why it’s getting set aside.”

“Can I use another one?”

“You can ask Beckendorf when he’s done with the rack.”

The child nodded and returned to sorting. Thalia let them do it without correction until they asked. Annabeth stayed by the doorway, watching the rain beads on the target frames.

When the campers left with the box, Thalia looked up. “Were you waiting for them to be gone?”

“I was waiting for you to be free.”

“That’s a kinder version.”

“Ianthe says you watch her too closely.”

Thalia’s face changed. “Did she tell you that?”

“Yes.”

“What else did she say?”

“Nothing I’m going to repeat without asking.”

Thalia lowered the arrow she had been holding. “You can ask.”

“Do you want me to?”

“No.”

“Okay.”

“Was it about the guest room?”

“Some of it.”

Thalia rubbed the arrow shaft between her fingers. “I’ve been trying not to make her feel like she’s being evaluated.”

“Do you think you are?”

“Sometimes.” She met Annabeth’s eyes. “I can’t tell whether I’m checking if she’s okay or checking if she’s going to be a problem.”

“That’s honest.”

“It’s also ugly.”

“It can be both.”

Thalia placed the arrow in the repair box. “I don’t want her to think she has to act like me so I’ll accept her.”

“Have you said that?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because I don’t know if I can mean it every day.”

Annabeth waited.

“I know,” Thalia said. “That’s not how you offer something. I’m not going to promise I’ll never be annoyed. I get annoyed with you.”

“I know.”

“And Percy.”

“Mostly with Percy.”

“Mostly.” She tilted her head. “I don’t want Ianthe thinking there’s a test she can pass. There isn’t. She’s my sister whether I like what she says or not.”

“Have you told her that?”

“Not in those words.”

“Could you?”

Thalia rubbed the heel of her hand over one eye. “Maybe.”

“I think you can say it without adding instructions.”

“I wasn’t going to add instructions.”

“You usually do.”

“Do I?”

“Yes.”

Thalia looked down. “I’ve been doing that with you too.”

“Sometimes.”

“I keep trying to help before you ask. Then you get sharp, and I act like you made me leave.”

Annabeth thought of the porch, the lamp, the tools between them. “Today I asked you to stay.”

“You did.”

“And you stayed.”

“I did.”

“It felt good.”

Thalia turned the arrow in her fingers. “I wanted to take the screwdriver.”

“I know.”

“You had the angle wrong.”

“I did not.”

“You did.”

“I adjusted it.”

“After I told you.”

“You did not.”

“I was going to.”

“Maybe.”

Their argument was ridiculous and comforting in its old shape. Thalia put the arrow away. “I’m sorry I watched every time you reached for the window.”

“Thank you.”

“I wanted to know you weren’t going to hurt yourself.”

“I know.”

“That doesn’t mean you wanted me standing over you.”

“No.”

Thalia leaned back against the support beam. “How do I stay close without acting like I’m keeping you safe?”

“Ask what kind of help I want. Hear the answer.”

“Sometimes you say no.”

“Then you can stay nearby.”

“Like today.”

“Like today.”

The simplicity seemed to irritate Thalia more than a harder answer. She looked out through the open end of the lane. “I can do that.”

“Some days.”

“Some days.”

Annabeth leaned beside her. The damp air carried a faint smell of sawdust and wet rope. They could see the Cabin One window from the far edge of the path, small beneath the eave. Its glass was gray with cloud reflection.

“You said Ianthe asked you to stop watching her,” Annabeth said.

“She did.”

“What did you say?”

“That I’d try.”

“Did you?”

“Today.”

Annabeth nodded. “I don’t want her to have to guess whether you’re disappointed.”

“I’m not her father.”

“No.”

“I can’t fix what he says.”

“No.”

Thalia’s mouth pressed into a line. “You’re agreeing with me a lot.”

“You don’t need me to tell you what to do.”

“I might.”

“Then you can ask.”

Thalia gave a short laugh. “That’s irritating.”

“It’s a useful answer.”

“Maybe.”

She pushed off the beam and set the remaining arrows into the box. “I’ll tell her I’m her sister and I’m still figuring out how to be one.”

“That sounds right.”

“Don’t tell her I said that.”

“I won’t.”

“Let me say it.”

“I will.”

Thalia took hold of the box and carried it toward the equipment shed. Annabeth walked beside her. She did not reach for the weight. At the door, Thalia set it down and lifted the latch with her free hand.

“Thanks for coming to find me,” Thalia said.

“Thanks for hearing me.”

“Don’t start making another list.”

“I wouldn’t.”

“You would.”

“I might.”

“There you are.”

The smile that passed between them did not erase the years of being sisters, nor did it decide what the next year would look like. Annabeth could live with a thing that did not pretend to be finished.

The afternoon stayed overcast. Clouds gathered beyond the tree line, then broke apart without rain. Annabeth checked the cabin window once from the path. The felt strip held; the latch sat flush. There was no weather to test it against, so she did not touch it.

Percy found her at the Big House table with the maintenance page and the inventory sheet. He set down a plate with two pieces of toast.

“Is this lunch or an argument?” he asked.

“Both.”

“Then I brought backup.”

She moved the paper away from the crumbs. “You don’t have to feed me every time I make a list.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

He sat across from her. The table was wide enough for both papers and the plate. “What’s still open?”

“Whether the latch stays dry after rain. Whether the stool can stay in the guest room. Where Ianthe sleeps after we finish counting the beds.”

“That’s a lot of open things.”

“It is.”

“Do you want me to help with any?”

Annabeth looked up from the page. “I need to talk to Chiron about the stool. I need you to tell me if Cabin Three has any spare blankets that can move.”

“Okay.”

“And I don’t need you to make the room decision for me.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“I know. I’m saying it because I need to hear myself say it.”

Percy reached across the table and turned the toast plate so it sat between them. “Then I’m listening.”

She broke off one corner and ate it. “Ianthe thinks I use the lists to avoid saying no.”

“Do you?”

“Sometimes I use them to avoid saying a thing before I know what it means.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No. But from her side, it may feel the same.”

Percy took the other piece of toast. “Do you want her to stay?”

“Yes.”

“Do you know where?”

“No.”

“Do you want to decide tonight?”

“No.”

“Then you’re allowed not to.”

Annabeth's hand stilled against the paper. “I don’t want her to think I’m making her earn a place.”

“Then tell her she has a place while you figure out the bed.”

“I did.”

“Did she believe you?”

“I don’t know.”

“Maybe she doesn’t have to believe it all at once.”

Annabeth studied him. “That’s annoyingly reasonable.”

“I can be unreasonable if you’d prefer.”

“Not now.”

“Later?”

“Maybe.”

He smiled. She leaned forward over the table and kissed him. It was brief and familiar, made easier by the absence of anyone watching for a declaration. When she drew back, he touched his forehead to hers.

“You don’t have to solve the whole family before dinner,” he said.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I’m working on it.”

“Good.”

They stayed at the table another minute. Percy told her that Beckendorf had a dry corner set aside for the coils if the weather shifted. Annabeth wrote it down with a question mark beside it, then crossed the question mark out when Percy confirmed Beckendorf had said so. She did not add a note about the room.

When they rose, Annabeth took the papers to Chiron. He read the inventory and agreed that the stool could remain in place until the room was assigned. He did not promise that it would stay after. The bed plan needed the cabin residents’ input, plus a check of the guest hall. Chiron would speak with the people responsible for both spaces before anything moved.

Annabeth wrote that down. “Do you know when?”

“Tomorrow, if the morning schedule allows.”

“Then I’ll leave the sheet here.”

“That is probably best.”

She almost asked if he thought Ianthe would feel welcome. She stopped before the question became a request for certainty Chiron could not give. Instead, she folded the page once and placed it beneath the logbook.

At the doorway, Ianthe waited with a small stack of dry linens. “I was going to put these in the room.”

“Those are camp stock,” Annabeth said. “They can go on the shelf.”

“I know. I checked the mark.”

“Thanks.”

Ianthe looked at the door to the guest hall, then back at Annabeth. “Chiron said the stool can stay?”

“For now. We haven’t decided the bed plan.”

“I heard.”

“Okay.”

“I wanted to ask if you’d help me sort the shelf tomorrow.”

Annabeth was surprised by the request. “Yes.”

“Not because I want to be in charge of the room.”

“I understand.”

“I mean it. I just want to know where things are.”

“We can do that.”

“And I want to put the linens away myself.”

“All right.”

Ianthe hugged the folded stack against her chest. “You could have asked if I wanted to carry them.”

“I could have.”

“Instead you told me where to put them.”

“You’re right.”

“I know.”

The answer came too quickly, but Ianthe heard it too. Her mouth twitched, and for a second Annabeth saw a version of her who might have been easy to like if no one was hurt or waiting. Then she turned toward the shelf.

“Which one is camp stock?” Annabeth asked.

“The middle shelf. The blue marks.”

“Thanks.”

“I already told you I checked.”

“I know. I’m asking because I want to make sure you’re right.”

Ianthe looked over her shoulder. “That’s the closest you’ve come to testing me.”

“Then I’ll be clearer next time.”

“You’re supposed to trust me.”

“Trust is not the same as pretending I didn’t hear you.”

“That sounds like something a judge says.”

“Maybe.”

She placed the linens on the marked shelf, keeping the stack square. Nothing had been decided about the bed. No one had claimed the stool. The inventory page remained folded under the logbook, where Chiron would see it in the morning. Annabeth did not turn a temporary place into a promise, but she did not take the invitation back.



After dinner, Annabeth went back to the cabin with the logbook. The wind had shifted from the west, pushing a thin branch against the outer wall. It made the window tap once, then fall quiet.

She checked the latch. It held. She did not press the sash harder than she needed to. She wrote the direction of the wind beside the note about the rain check. If the next storm came from the same side, they would know whether the felt was doing its job.

Thalia came in as Annabeth closed the logbook. “Chiron says we’ll talk about the room tomorrow.”

“That’s what he told me.”

“Ianthe asked him if she could move the blue linens.”

“Did he say yes?”

“He said she could put them on the marked shelf.”

“Good.”

“She wanted to know if she could pick a blanket.”

“Those are camp stock. She can pick one.”

“Chiron told her that.”

Annabeth nodded. “Then it’s handled.”

Thalia lowered herself onto the low stool by the doorway. “You don’t have to make every sentence sound like a report.”

“I’m not.”

“You just said ‘handled.’”

“It is.”

“Does that mean you’re okay?”

“No.”

Thalia’s eyes lifted. “What’s not okay?”

“I don’t know if I’m doing this well.”

“With the window?”

“With her.”

“You don’t have to know yet.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Annabeth sat on the edge of the bed. The old mattress dipped under her weight. “I keep trying to make the room feel fair before anyone can say they feel unwelcome.”

“That might be hard.”

“It is.”

“You don’t have to fix that feeling for her.”

“I know.”

“And you don’t have to wait until you have the perfect arrangement to tell her you want her here.”

“I did tell her.”

“Then that’s something.”

Annabeth watched Thalia rub the cane handle with her thumb. “You told her you were still figuring out how to be a sister.”

Thalia looked up quickly. “I said that?”

“You said you wanted to tell her.”

“Did I tell her?”

“Not that I know.”

“Then don’t make it sound like I did.”

“I’m not.”

“Good.”

Annabeth rested her hands on her knees. “You can still say it.”

“Maybe I will.”

“I won’t ask again.”

“I know.”

The wind returned, tapping the branch twice against the wall. Annabeth glanced toward the window. “I’ll move that tomorrow.”

“I can move it.”

“I know.”

“Do you want to move it?”

“Tomorrow.”

Thalia stood. “Good answer.”

“That wasn’t an answer.”

“It was a choice.”

They stepped outside together. The branch had fallen away from the wall. The yard was darker now, but the clouds had opened enough to show a small stretch of evening sky. Ianthe stood at the end of the path with a borrowed jacket looped over her arm.

“Are you going somewhere?” Thalia asked.

“Just past the archery shed.”

“Do you want company?”

Ianthe looked toward the shed, then back at her sister. “You can walk with me if you don’t tell me every three seconds where to step.”

Thalia smiled. “I can try.”

“Try quietly.”

“Fine.”

Ianthe started down the path. Thalia waited long enough for her to choose the pace, then walked beside her. Annabeth remained on the porch. She could not hear what they said after the trees hid them. She did not need to.

She went back inside, marked the window check for after rain, and closed the book.

Outside, late sunlight fell briefly through a break in the clouds. It caught the repaired window and made the glass look open. Annabeth could see the pale line of felt only because she knew where to look. The sash stayed closed, waiting for weather.

Rain began before dawn. Annabeth woke to the soft tick of water on the sill and lay still long enough to tell the difference between the roof and the window. The first few drops were light. Then wind swept across the cabin, and the tapping became a steady hiss.

She got up, pulled on a sweater and crossed the room. The sash was closed. She ran her fingers beneath the lower edge, checking for dampness. The felt was cool but dry. She watched the keeper through another gust, then pressed a strip of paper against the corner and drew it back. No water marked it.

The latch held.

She wrote the time in the maintenance book, then paused before adding anything else. It had held through one rain, with the wind from the west. The next test should be a longer storm. She wrote that instead of *fixed*.

A floorboard creaked behind her. Percy stood at the doorway, hair rumpled, one hand rubbing sleep from his eyes.

“Is the room flooding?”

“No.”

“Are you sure?”

“Pretty sure.”

“That sounds less sure than last night.”

“I’m checking it.”

“Would you like me to hold the lamp?”

“There’s enough light.”

He stepped closer, not past the threshold. “Do you want company?”

Annabeth looked at the window. She wanted to finish the check herself. She also wanted him to stay. “You can stand there.”

“Happy to.”

He leaned against the doorframe and watched her test the lower edge. She opened the sash a fraction, shut it again and listened for the keeper. It caught. She used the paper strip once more, then set it on the sill.

“Dry,” she said.

“Good.”

“One rain isn’t enough.”

“No.”

“I need a longer one.”

“Then we’ll wait for one.”

She looked at him. “We?”

“If you want.”

“Okay.”

He came in and sat on the edge of the bed while she put the paper away. Neither of them went back to sleep right away. The rain filled the quiet between them, and the cabin gave small sounds as it cooled: a beam settling, a drip from the roof edge, a distant door closing somewhere in the hall.

“You heard what Ianthe said about the room,” Annabeth said.

“Some of it.”

“She thinks I hide behind other people.”

“Do you?”

“Sometimes.”

“Do you think she’s wrong?”

“Not entirely.”

Percy watched the rain on the glass. “You can decide what you want without deciding for everyone else.”

“I know.”

“You can say you want her here.”

“I did.”

“And let the bed question take as long as it takes.”

“That’s the part I hate.”

“I know.”

“You don’t get to solve this for me.”

“I know that too.”

She sat beside him. Their shoulders touched. He rested his cheek briefly against the side of her head, then pulled away before the damp ends of her hair could make his shirt wet.

“Very romantic,” she said.

“I’m preserving our blankets.”

“You’re preserving your shirt.”

“That too.”

She smiled and took his hand. It was warm from sleep. She did not ask him to take the notebook or tell her what to write. He did not offer. The quiet between them felt roomy enough for the things neither of them had solved.

By the time the rain eased, the cabin windows were gray with morning. Annabeth added *first rain check: dry at lower edge; keeper caught; test again after longer storm* to the log. The problem had not disappeared. It had changed shape: from a leak they had seen to a repair with one test behind it and another ahead.

When she opened the door, the hall smelled of wet wool. Ianthe stood there with a folded towel in her hands.

“Was the window okay?” she asked.

“It stayed dry.”

“Did you check the paper?”

“Yes.”

“Did it catch the wind?”

“It caught.”

Ianthe studied the latch. “You should test it in a longer storm.”

“I wrote that down.”

“You don’t have to write every single thing.”

“I know.”

“Maybe you could let someone else remember.”

“Maybe.”

Ianthe shifted the towel from one arm to the other. “I can check it with you next time.”

“If you want.”

“I said I can.”

“I heard you.”

“Good.”

She held the towel out. “For the sill. It’s dry, but you touched it.”

Annabeth took it. “Thanks.”

Ianthe left without waiting for praise. Annabeth dried the sill anyway, though no water had gathered there. Then she set the towel aside and added the next weather check to the list, leaving the word *we* out of the note until she knew who would be there.
