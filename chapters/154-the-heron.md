## Chapter 154: The Heron

The evening started with the skiffs rocking.

Percy noticed it from the top of the boat ramp, where he had stopped to shove his fists down into his sneakers and set them right, and had, for the space of one ordinary second, watched the two green hulls knock gently against the pilings in a wind that was not there.

The flag on the Big House pole hung straight down against its rope. The willows along the cove end of the lake stood the loose way they stood when the day was letting go of itself, all their long leaves dropped low and dripping nothing. The lake lay so flat that the far shore came down and stood in it like a town under glass. And the skiffs rocked — one and then the other, one and then the other, the cord creaking against the cleat, the sound of somebody breathing in a room they thought nobody else was in.

"You're doing the face," Annabeth said, behind him on the path.

"I'm not doing a face."

"You're doing the face where you look at the water like it cheated at a game you didn't know you were playing."

Percy pointed. "It's rocking."

"Boats rock."

"Not without wind."

Annabeth came down the path beside him with her sleeves shoved up past her elbows and her camp shirt untucked on one side — the gray one with the blind-faded hem she had been wearing for three days running because it had real pockets — and she looked at the lake with the flat, evaluating expression she used on half-repaired walls and small dishonest children.

Then she looked harder, the way she did when something on one of her own maps had the nerve to be wrong.

"It's rocking," she agreed.

They stood there. The skiffs rocked. Somewhere up the hill the dinner bell was being argued over by somebody's younger sibling, and the smell of the pavilion's evening fish was coming down with the first of the cool, and a loon called out on the far side in the voice of a bird that had never in its life been in a hurry.

"Wind sets the surface, not the hulls," Percy said. "And then there's the rings."

"What rings?"

She followed him down the ramp — past her skiff, the good one, with the cord she always tied because he always forgot — and he put his palm flat on the wet post and pointed.

Out past the buoys, the lake had rings on it. Round, slow, expanding rings, four of them, crossing each other with no wind to push them and no fish under them and no reed edge where they might have started. They moved out of the middle of the swimming area as if something had once dropped from the exact center of nothing, and as they watched, the outermost one reached the dock pilings and spread along them and did not break up, and the next one came, and the next.

"Subsurface feed," Annabeth said.

"There's nothing feeding."

"Then something jostled."

"There's nothing here."

"Then something under something." She was already pulling her sleeves back down, squinting at the water with her head tipped back — the pose she hit when the world was being recalcitrant and she intended to recalcitrate back. "Percy. Is it doing this every night?"

"I don't know," he said. "I haven't been down here in five days."

She looked at him sidelong. "Where have you been?"

"Doing inventory in the boathouse with Clarisse. Explaining to Chiron why the second skiff is now officially hull-good, rope-good, fine."

"That's a real classification."

"I know. I made him write it down." He grinned at her, and she refused the grin the way she always did, which was to catch it and keep it, and he felt the catch of it all the way down his arms. "Come on. Take the boat out with me."

"The second skiff?"

"The water's doing something," Percy said. "It started at the cove end. And you can't fix a thing you won't look at."

"That's my rule."

"Sector twelve, map revision rule. You made me write it on my hand in marker."

"I made you write it on the clipboard like an adult." She pulled the thin canvas paddle out of the bottom of the second skiff and used the butt of it to shove them off the ramp. "Also because I have been inside a dark room for a week drawing the north path drainage, and I have not been on the water since Thursday, and the water is where I'm supposed to be."

"Your sister would say you're not supposed to be on the water with a boy."

"My sister," Annabeth said, stepping down into the skiff with the easy care she used on anything that floated, "is at the archery range until nine, and does not need to hear my schedule. Sit down. Row."

---

The light was at that hour when the gold has gone out of the west but the west is still warm, and the trees stand up against it in black cut-paper, and the whole lake cools from the bottom up instead of from the outside in. The air off the water had begun to smell like evening: wet stone, the green rot of leaf-fall, the faint mineral breath that comes up when a freshwater lake lets go of the day's heat. The frogs had started in the marsh behind the boathouse, one confident voice and then fifty, the way they did — as if the first frog had raised a hand and the rest had followed.

Percy rowed. He was aware, distantly, of doing the thing Thalia said he did, the rowing-with-the-wrists thing, and he did not care, because the pattern of the lake at this hour was not a thing he missed often and he did not want to miss it now.

Annabeth sat in the bow with her knees drawn up and her cuffs showing bare ankle and the collar of her shirt up against her neck, and she watched the water the way she watched everything — in sectors, in lines, in the order things were and the order things were not.

They passed the canoe launch. They passed the swimming buoys, which is where the rings had been, and the rings were still there, slower now, as if whatever had made them was making them economically.

"The rings didn't stop," Annabeth said.

"I noticed."

"You have an interpretation."

"I have a feeling."

"Feelings are data," she said. "Sit on it."

He sat on it, because the truth was that he didn't have an interpretation, he had an itch, the particular one that lived at the base of his skull and had saved his life more times than anything he could name on purpose. The lake was saying something in the low register he was tuned to, the register under sound, the way a big animal will breathe at the far end of a field. It wasn't a warning. It wasn't the hiss of a monster in the deep or the pressure-drop of a storm coming. It was a question. It was the sound the lake made when it was checking something over.

They came up on the boundary rock, the small gray stone that sat out in the water like a period at the end of a sentence, the last legal thing before you were, technically, outside camp. The white X on it was new. Still smelled like paint if you were close enough.

They went past the rock at four miles an hour, slow, the loons still calling, and the lake took the sound of the oars and kept it, and then they were in the green.

The willow cove was not what the rest of the lake was. On the bright open water the evening light came down all at once and lay on everything; in the cove the light had to come through the willow crowns first, and they got it slow, in long broken sheets of green-gold that moved with every leaf. The air was cooler under the crowns and smelled of mud and willow bark and the sweet, overlapping rot of a shoreline that had been eating itself politely for three hundred years. The shallows at the mouth gave up their heat as they entered — warm at the top, cool underneath — and the skiff slid into the shade like a hand going into a pocket.

Annabeth put her hand on the gunwale and felt the hull. "We're drifting backward," she said.

"No we're not. I'm rowing."

"We're drifting backward."

He stopped rowing. The skiff did not stop. It went backward, gently, the way a boat goes backward when the current is moving and it is not, nose swinging two degrees out of line and settling on a new line. Percy looked over the side. The water against the hull was moving outward from the cove — a slow, even push of it, cool against his wrist — going out toward the open lake against the wind.

"There's no current in this lake," Annabeth said quietly.

"There's tide, maybe. A little."

"Not in a lake."

"Not in this one," he agreed, and put the oar down and stood up in the boat, and put his fingertips on the surface, and felt the seam.

That was the only thing he could think to call it. It ran across the mouth of the cove, invisible except that the water changed on the other side of it — the temperature, the smell, the small sound it made against the leaf litter at the reed edge. On the cove side it was cold-shadow cold, still, glassy, with the willow light lying in pieces on top of it. On the lake side it was the ordinary evening water, the open water, the water of buoys and skiffs and loons. And between them ran a line, soft as a change in the weather, where the two kinds of water met and did not mix. The rings he had seen from the ramp — four slow circles — spread out and touched the seam and stopped, flattened against it, and did not cross.

Annabeth, standing now beside him with one hand on his shoulder for balance, said, "The surface's warm out there. Under it's cold in here. Where does the cold come from? There's no spring in the cove. There's no drain. I've been over this shore twenty times."

"Maybe deep water."

"It's a lake, Percy. It's deep everywhere." She was looking down the cove, past the pebble wreck with its flat top, past the point where the reeds closed in and the water got too shallow for the skiffs, to the narrow head of the cove that everyone called the arrow because that was what it looked like on her map, a gray-green point at the end of a long green shape, drawn with her own two hands.

"There," Percy said, and it wasn't a word, it was a letting-go of a word he had been holding since the ramp, since the rings.

The heron was standing on the bow of the sunken skiff.

It stood at the very tip of the half-sunk bow, at the place where the hull had given up and gone under, gray and patient and utterly still, with the dusk green showing in its wings and the last of the light lying along its back like dust. It had been there at the end of the patrol a week ago, he remembered — or something like it — a heron on that boat, in the dark, standing where a boat should have a heron and did not. He had not thought of it since. He had not known he was going to think of it again.

It did not fly when they came in.

It did not fly. It stood. It looked at them with one yellow eye and then, slowly, the other, and the willows dripped, and the frogs went on, and the heron shifted its weight once along the rotted bow and settled, and it looked, if a bird could look like anything but a bird, like somebody who had been waiting at a table for a long time and had stopped expecting the meal and had decided to sit with the waiting instead.

Percy felt the temperature of Annabeth's hand on his shoulder change, the way it did when something had happened to her that she was not going to admit out loud.

"That's the same bird," she said.

"You can't know that."

"I can know that," Annabeth said. "Look at it. It's not scared of us. Herons aren't predators, they're not stupid, they don't stand on sunken boats in the dark holding the end of a cove. It's waiting."

"For what?"

"For us," she said, "I think."

Percy let the oars dip and pull them in under the willows, slow, no wake, the way you approach an animal you don't intend to chase. The cove was quiet in a way that was not the same quiet as the lake outside it. Outside, the lake was quiet the way a room is quiet when someone has gone out of it. In here, the quiet was the kind that had content in it — the drip of the willow leaves, the tiny knock of wet rot against wet wood, the frogs, and under all of it a low, steady pulse that Percy felt in the gunwale against his forearms rather than heard.

He knew every sound this lake made. He had made his bones in it — in this life and the other one, on the breakwater and in the boathouse and under the dock, in the water that was somehow the same freshwater and somehow a different life. This was not one of those sounds.

"It's humming," Annabeth said quietly.

"Yeah."

"Say that again."

"It's humming."

She looked at him. She looked at the water. She put her fingertips on the surface the way he had, and held them there, and her face did the thing it did when she was reading — not a book, not a map, a situation — and the willow light moved over her in pieces and she sat very still with her hand on the lake as if taking a statement.

"How deep is it here?" Percy asked.

"Eight, nine feet. The flat rock's at three in the middle. The arrow shallows out at the very end, goes down to a sunken root shelf and then it's all reed." She recited it the way she recited the cove now, from the map on the Big House wall, from her own training as cartographer — the cove was on the map now. She had put it there herself in the last week, her hand drawing it in official ink, the cove and the arrow and the flat rock, all corrected from the old surveys, all hers.

"That's the thing," Percy said. He pulled one oar, let the skiff turn, and brought them alongside the sunken boat. "You've had a week in that map. Did the arrow move?"

"No."

"Look at it now."

They looked at it now. The sunken skiff lay at the head of the cove the way it had lain all summer, half in, half out, the stern still riding above the surface at the angle of a boat that had been pushed down at the bow by something patient. The wood had gone the color of old tea and was freckled with the argyle of soft rot. Mud had grown a garden on the port side. And the reeds had come up around the hull and claimed it the way reeds claim everything that stops moving.

And the arrow — the narrow head of the cove, the green point where the water went shallow and the reeds closed in — sat a little to the left of where it had been on the map. Or the skiff sat a little to the right. Either way, one of them was off, and Annabeth, beside him, had gone very still in the way she went when the world disagreed with her notes.

"Show me on your map," Percy said.

She already had the notebook out, the one with the blue cover and the torn first page, and she flipped past three pages of the north-path drainage survey to a sheet in her small square hand: the cove. The willows drawn as a dashed border of little leaf shapes. The flat rock marked with a small triangle and the words FLAT ROCK—BEST SCREENING, underlined. The arrow drawn as a point. And the sunken skiff marked with a tiny oblong and a note beside it in the capital letters she used for anything important: HOLD PLACE.

"HOLD PLACE," Percy read. "Is that a thing cartographers do?"

"It's a thing I do," Annabeth said. "It means the feature is there and it has not moved since the last survey and you can trust it to be in the same place next time. It's—" She stopped. She turned the notebook and held it up against the actual cove, the way she had held it against the wall crack and the ramp crack, aligning drawing to world by eye. "It's off."

"How much?"

"Eleven feet. Maybe twelve." Her voice had gone into the register she used for things that had stopped being fun. "The hull. The bow. Where the line of the arrow meets the hull is wrong. I surveyed this cove four weeks ago, Percy. I stood on the flat rock to do it."

"And?"

"And," she said, "a sunken skiff doesn't walk twelve feet in a month unless the lake moves it, and the lake doesn't move things in this cove because there's no current, and you just showed me there is current, and the current is cold, and the seam doesn't mix, and the rings—" She stopped herself, which for Annabeth was a physical act, a hand coming up flat, fingers pressing the air down. "I'm spiraling. I'm going to stop."

Percy rowed them the last two strokes and reached out and took hold of the rotted gunwale of the sunken skiff, and the wood squelched against his palm in a way that no boat should do, and the heron watched him do it, and did not move.

"Okay," he said. "So we go look at it."

"We're going to get wet."

"We always get wet. That's the deal. That's why they let us bring boats."

Annabeth looked down at her cuffs — already rolled, already practical — and at her sneakers, white and scuffed and tied with a double knot, and then she took off her shirt, folded it twice, and put it in his hands before he could say anything clever, and stood on the bench of their skiff in her faded camp tank top with her arms already bare and useful and her hair coming down out of its ponytail.

"Hold it above the water," she said.

He held it above the water.

Then she put one hand on the rotted bow of the sunken skiff and one foot on the gunwale, and the heron finally moved, exactly one step, up the spine of the hull to the place where the mast would have been if the boat had ever had one, and watched her from there.

---

The cove was different from inside the sunken skiff, and it made sense to Annabeth within about four seconds.

She shifted her weight until the boat would accept it and sat on the stern bench — miraculously, alone of all the benches in the cove, this one was dry, the wood pale and worn smooth and just barely tilted toward the water. She sat with her knees drawn up under her chin and her hair down and the dusk green coming through the willow crowns in slow coins. Mud smell. Wet wood, from the inside, the sweet brown smell of a tree that had gone back to being a tree.

Percy stayed in the water at the bow, one hand on each side of the hull, holding the old boat steady against the slow outward push of the cold current that should not have been there. The water came up to his chest, which put his shoulders and his flattening hair and the wet line of his rolled-up sleeves in the coins of light, and every now and then a willow leaf came down and landed on him, and he did not brush them off because there were four already, one in his hair, one on his shoulder, and he was being stubborn about them.

"You've got a leaf," Annabeth said.

"Keep it. It's mine."

"You've got four."

"I'm collecting them."

"For what?"

"I'm going to press them in your notebook," he said, "and label them WILLOW COVE: A COLLECTION, and then when you're famous for cartography you'll have to explain why a boy's leaf collection is in your professional papers."

"I'm not going to be famous for cartography. I'm going to be the best," Annabeth said. "There's a difference. Famous is a thing that happens to you. Best is a thing you do."

"Chase," he called across the boat, using the made-up title the way he always did when she was being too much of a person, "this is your daily reminder that you are a lot."

"I am a productive amount."

"You're a lot of productivity."

She was smiling. She did not deny it. She was aware, the way she was always aware, of the shape of the moment they were in: her dry on the bench of a dead boat in the green dark, him wet and steady in the water at the bow, the heron standing above them like a witness, the frogs and the drips and the hum under everything that was not any sound she could name. And under the moment there was the week, the whole week, laid out like an account she had been keeping without permission — five days of the north path drainage with Olive Nakamura's careful little notes, three evenings of chalk-line revisions in the Big House with Chiron's hooves going back and forth past the door.

And no him, mostly. Which was new, and the newness of it had been its own kind of ache, small and bright and ridiculous, the ache of having a thing in your week that did not happen.

He was watching her with the flat, easy attention he gave her when he thought she wasn't logging it. She had learned to log it anyway.

"Your hands are cold," he said.

"Your nose is cold."

"That's a fact about both of us, then." He shifted his grip on the hull, the squelch of the rotted wood moving under his palms, and looked up at her with his face doing the thing where it said he was about to say something and had decided either to be brave about it or to be casual about being brave, and both of those looked the same on Percy, which was a thing she had learned about him in more than one life. "I missed you," he said.

"I know."

"I mean I actually missed you. Not the—" He gestured with one hand, out toward the lake, toward the week, toward everything. "I got it. I do. The map's the biggest thing you've ever built and I would not be anywhere else while you build it. But there's a difference between being proud of somebody and being—" He stopped, and started again, which he almost never did. "Being with the somebody. I got four days of with."

Annabeth sat very still on the bench of the sunken skiff with the green coins moving on her knees. She thought about the question that had lived in her all week, the small private one that had nothing to do with maps: not whether she loved him — she had never once, not in any of her lives, seriously doubted the answer — but whether she was allowed to want a whole week of a person when the week was what let her become herself, and whether wanting that made her the sort of girl who cost people things.

"I got four days of missing you," she said. "It's not the same data."

"It is when it comes out the same."

"Does it?"

He looked at her. The heron looked at them both.

"Come here," she said.

He came up out of the water onto the stern seat, careful of the tilt, and made himself sit at the other end of the bench with his feet braced and his dripping side facing her, and she moved along the seat toward him, which took almost no time at all, and put her arms around his shoulders and her cheek down against the top of his head, and his wet forehead found her collarbone, and they sat like that in the green light with the heron standing over them and the cold water pushing out of the cove in the dark.

His arms went around her the way they always did, one around her back and one finding her hand, and it was a long time before either of them wanted to move, and the long time was not wasted, which is the rarest thing about waiting.

"Percy," she said, into his hair. It was the voice she used for plans and for truths and for the one in between, and he went still under it, waiting.

"I don't want a whole week of you," she said. "I want all of your life, and I want my own, and I want them to be the same life. That's not the same as wanting a week, and I was afraid it was, and it isn't."

He turned his head, just enough to look at her sideways, still held, still dripping, the coins of light moving on his shoulders and her wrists.

"That's the most terrifying sentence anyone has ever said about scheduling," he said.

"Shut up."

"I'm shutting up. Say it again."

"I can't say it again, it was very expensive." But she was laughing, the small close laugh that lived in the collarbone, and she felt it against her side because his hand had come to rest there, flat and open, the way he held her when he was not thinking about where his hands went because his hands already knew.

"Say a different part of it, then," he said. "Say the part about my life."

"The part about your life," Annabeth said, "is that I like it. I like being in it. I like being the person who is in it. And I would like—" She hesitated, which she never did, and then went anyway, because hesitation was for maps. "I would like it to be allowed."

"It is allowed," Percy said.

"You don't make the rules."

"I make some of them."

She pulled back far enough to look at him, and her eyes had the thing in them that he only got the end of — she had already been through all of it and was meeting him in the middle, and he took her face in both his wet cold hands, and her hands came up over his, and he kissed her.

It was a heron witness of a kiss, if kisses have witnesses: slow, patient, unhurried, the kind where you go slowly because there is no bell to beat and no watch to read and no one above the willow crowns who cared what two fourteen-year-olds did in the shade. He tasted like the lake and like the fish they had eaten at dinner and like the metal cold of wet skin. She had one hand in his hair, and he had his thumb at her temple, and the willow leaves came down around them like small applause, and the cold seam of the current pushed at the hull and did not reach them.

When they broke apart they did not break apart. They stayed with their foreheads together, breathing the same cooled air off the water, and she put her hand flat over the place where his heartbeat lived under his shirt and felt it going the way it went, and thought, with the clarity she saved for architecture and for him, that she had known this boy across a war and a city and an airplane and a kitchen, and that the lake had decided to teach him to her all over again in the dark green at the end of a cove, and that she was willing to be taught.

"You're wet," she said, against his forehead.

"You're dry."

"That's the best thing that happened to me today."

"You're very welcome." He sat back, which was a thing she let him do because he needed to see her face when he said the next thing, and she let him. "Annabeth. There's something I want to look at. In the boat. Under the bench."

"Why?"

"Because it's glowing," he said, "and because your lake just walked a skiff twelve feet, and because the bird won't leave. Look."

Annabeth looked.

It was under the stern bench, down where the bench boards met the hull's inner frame, and it was not glowing — not exactly, not in the way fire glows, not in the way her sister's static glowed. It was the other kind of glowing: the kind where the dark has a place in it that the dark has not reached. A line of green-gray light, thin as a hair, running along the seam where two boards of the old boat had swollen together, and it pulsed. Once. Twice. Slowly, the way the rings pulsed, the way the hum under the water pulsed. Out with the current, in with the willow light, out with the current.

She put her hand near it and felt the air change — warmer. Under the boat, where the wood was always cold, was a small pocket of air that was warmer than the lake and warmer than the evening and warmer than the air itself.

"It's a seam," she said.

"Like the one on the surface?"

"It's the same seam. Same pulse." She sat up straight on the bench, the green light lying on her wrists, and did the thing she did — counted, measured, laid it out. "There's a seam in the water across the mouth of the cove, and there's a seam in the hull of this boat, and both of them pulse at the same time, and the current pushes outward, and the arrow is twelve feet off. Percy."

"Yeah."

"What's under the boat?"

He slid off the bench into the cold, came up under the hull with his hair flattened and his eyes adjusted, and looked down into the green. The water inside the sunken skiff was darker than the cove and colder and it moved, not fast, but decisively, down and out, as if something under the keel were drawing it.

"There's a hole," he said, coming up. "Not a hole from rot. There's a hole that was cut."

"Cut."

"Cut with a tool. The edges are smooth. There's a flat board — a panel — set over it, and the panel has the same seam of light on its edge." He was holding the gunwale, and his voice had changed, not to fear — to the careful, water-tuned attention he used in deep water. "Annabeth. There's writing on the panel."

"Writing where? Under the water?"

"On the underside. I can feel them. They're incised — not painted, cut into the wood, and they're—" His hand moved along the wetted edge, feeling the shape of them, the way you read a letter with your fingertips, and his face did something complicated. "It's not old. That's the thing. It feels new. The board's warm."

She was already moving, already shifting her weight along the bench to where the panel was, and she stopped him with the flat of her hand before she went in, because she was the cartographer and there was a method, and the method was: you record it first, you always record it first, and then you touch it.

"Don't pull it out," she said. "Not yet. Where exactly is it? Show me on the boat, from above."

He showed her on the boat, from above: along the starboard run of the hull, a third of the way back from the crushed bow, where the boards came together in a long seam, and there was a panel there — the shape of it hidden by the slope of the hull, the same wood, the same rot, the same tea color. If you walked past the sunken skiff, you would never see it. If you looked for it, you would have to know what you were looking at: a line of the hull just slightly more even than the wood around it, and a pulse of cold-green light along its edge, and the small, warm breath of air coming out from under it.

She got the notebook out. She drew the skiff. She drew the panel. She drew the seam in the water and marked it NOT MIXING, and wrote beside it, in her square hand, with the pencil in her left hand because her right had gone back to the gunwale, to his hand: THE LAKE IS RINGING. WHEN DID IT START.

"I want you to write the time," Percy said, treading water beside the hull with his chin at the waterline. "Not for you. For whoever reads it next. If somebody did this deliberately, they recorded it deliberately. There'll be a rhythm. Times, dates, how often. If we ever get two of them, we have a pattern, and if we have a pattern—"

"—we know it's a someone and not a something," Annabeth said quietly.

"Yeah."

"That's what scares you."

"That's what scares the lake," he said. "Look at it. It doesn't know what to do with this spot. It's the one place the lake doesn't remember." He said it slowly, feeling the shape of the thing as he said it, the way he did. "Everything here, it keeps. The whole lake. The dock, the pilings, every fish that ever jumped — that's all in there, somewhere, it's the one thing water does for me, the one thing it's always done. And this cove is a hole in the remembering. It's like somebody cut a piece out of the water and left the edges."

The heron moved.

Annabeth watched it come down the length of the hull — careful, deliberate, its claws folding and unfolding on the wet wood — until it stood at the point on the hull where the panel was, where the light was, and it put its head down and touched its beak, once, to the seam, and then it raised its head and looked at the arrow end of the cove, and began to walk along the gunwale in that direction, away from them, one slow step at a time, until it stopped. It looked back.

"It wants us to follow," she said.

"That's my read."

"Birds don't do that."

"This one does," Percy said. "This bird's not here for the fish. Look at the fish. There aren't any."

She looked. There weren't. The cove had always been full of fish at this hour — minnows at the edges, the occasional bass sliding out of the shade, a perch or two working the shallows — and there weren't any. The water was empty of fish the way a room is empty after a party, gently, as if they had left politely some time ago and had not been replaced.

The heron waited. Then, with a sound like a door opening, it took the air and went up over the willows in one long gray stroke and was gone, out over the lake toward the dark, and Annabeth stood up in the sunken skiff and put her hand on Percy's shoulder for balance and looked after it and said, into the green, "It's not an answer."

"It's a direction," Percy said, and put his hand flat on the hull. He had known that tone in her voice for a long time now — it was the one that meant she was scared and pretending not to be, and that the way out of a thing she could not see was to walk down it. "The arrow's thirty yards up. On the way there's the flat rock. That's where the light's strongest."

"I'm going to get the panel free," Annabeth said.

"Can you? It's under three feet of water."

She was already wriggling along the bench to the stern, her sneakers off and tucked in her lap, fingers exploring where the wood had been cut. It was strange work. The fitting was not the work of a boat-builder. It was the work of someone who had measured the hull, cut a rectangle to fit it, and set the rectangle in from the outside with the same patience as everything else about the piece. No nails. No iron. The wood fit together with seams you could not see from the outside — unless you knew where they were, and were looking for them, in the right light, while carrying the right reason.

She got her fingertips under the lip at the stern end and pulled.

It came up an inch, and the cold breath blew out under the hull and up into her face, and the light in the seam flared once and steadied, and she got a better hold and lifted, and the panel came free, wet and heavy and warped secretly along its length.

"It's new," she said, holding it. The underside was pale where it had lain against the cut. The age of the wood was young — not old, not reclaimed from the hull — and where it was pale it was covered in marks.

"Which?" Percy said, from the water at the side of the boat, ready to take it.

"Not letters. Not one I can read." She turned the board in the coins of light, holding it flat in both hands, and the heron's absence from the cove felt like a held breath, and the frogs went on. "They're notches. Tally marks. Cut in groups of four, and then a deeper cut through the whole group, and then more groups." She counted, running the tip of her pencil down the rows of notches. "That's nine groups of four with a slash through each, and the last group only has three in it."

"Thirty-nine," Percy said, from the water. "Nine fours, plus three."

"I can count, Percy."

"Then say thirty-nine."

"Thirty-nine," Annabeth said quietly.

"I don't know." She looked at the underside of the panel, and then at the cut edges of the hull where the panel had sat, and her voice went very quiet. "Percy. The notches match the seams. Where the notches are cut in, the wood under them is warm. It's like—" She searched for the word, found the closest one, and gave it to him. "It's like they're reading positions. Thirty-nine positions."

He stopped treading water, which was the first time she had ever seen him do that. His head stayed up, but his hands stopped, and the water took him, and he stood, out of his depth, in the cold, with his head just above the surface and his eyes on the board in her hands.

"Positions," he repeated.

"The tide lines," she said. "Percy, there's no tide in this lake. But there are — there are rings. And the seam. And the current. This is thirty-nine readings of something. Someone came here thirty-nine times and counted something and wrote the count into a panel under a sunken skiff."

"Someone came and counted the lake," he said.

"Yes."

"Thirty-nine times," he said.

"Yes." She turned the board over in her hands and looked at the outer face of it, the aged face, the face that had pressed against the inside of the hull, and then she reached down and gave him her notebook and her pencil because her hands were full, and he took them, because that was the other thing they did — they had divided the labor between them long ago, the way water and stone divide a shore. "Write it down," she said. "Date, time, what we saw. Where it was. The seam, the panel, the skiff's position, the heron, thirty-nine. All of it."

He wrote it down, sitting in the water up to his chest with the notebook balanced on the gunwale of the sunken skiff and the pencil in his wet right hand, and she watched him write and knew exactly what he was putting under the last line, because he put it under everything they wrote down now, in the same neat capitals: WILLOW COVE. WATER IS WARM. THE CURRENT GOES OUT.

"The arrow," Annabeth said, when he had finished. "I want to see the arrow."

---

The arrow was not a place you went. It was a place you went when you had a reason and were willing to come out with your cuffs ruined and the willows dripping on your head and the smell of it in your clothes for a day.

Up close, it was reeds. It was the green point where the water got too shallow for the skiffs and the reeds closed in, and nobody got in — not because there was a fence or a wall, but because the reed stems grew too thick and too close together for a boat to pass or for a person to shove through without the whole stand of them thrashing. From out on the flat rock it was a gentle point of green. From here it was a wall, and it breathed out cold water at them the way a sleeping animal breathes out.

Percy went ahead of her, of course. He went the way he went through water, which was not through it — his body accepted it and the water accepted him and there was no argument, and the cold current ran out against him the whole way and did not slow him, because it was his and he let it do what it wanted. He went in up to the waist on the flat rock's side, and turned, and put his hand back for her without looking, because he knew exactly where she was, and said, "Give me the panel."

She gave him the panel. He carried it ahead of him clear of the reeds, and she came up and sat down on the flat rock's dry top with her cuffs dark to the elbow and her hair down in her face, and they sat together in the dusk with the panel between them and looked at the arrow from up close for the first time.

And under the reeds, the light was wrong. It was the green-gray of the seam's glow, coming up through the roots and the stems and lying in thin, wavering bands on the underside of everything, and Annabeth sat on the flat rock and understood that they were in a room where somebody had left a light on under the floor.

The water pushed out from it. Cold. Even. Patient, the way everything in the cove had been patient since the sunken skiff had said hello.

"The panel came from in there," she said.

"You can't know that."

"No," she agreed. "But the water smells like it."

"What does water under a light smell like?"

"Ozone," she said, picking it up out of the air, where it had been hiding under the mud-smell all along, faint and almost sweet. "But there's no lightning. There's no machine. It smells like—" She stopped, because she could not say the rest of it, which was that it smelled like the underside of a sky in the moments before her sister was careless with her power, and that was a smell that had been following her for eleven weeks and that she did not have a name for, and she was not going to give it a name on a rock in the dark with a boy and a heron as witnesses. "It smells like a storm that didn't happen," she said instead.

Percy, who had his fingers in the water, and who had been very quiet since the panel, lifted them and looked at them, and then looked at her, and said, "Annabeth. Do you smell it?"

"I do."

"Not like weather. Like—" He turned his wet hand over, and the water on his fingers was not cold where it should have been cold, and did not warm at the warmth of his skin, because it already had a temperature of its own. "It reminds me of the underside of the water in Atlantis," he said. "The way the light would look on a wall, from inside a wall. The way a room sounds under water."

She went still. In the boats, earlier, he had told her things about Atlantis — always with the guard up, always careful, because Atlantis was the place where his grown-up life had been built and the place that, in this one, was a letter he opened slowly.

"Your sea," she said.

"My lake," he said, "when it remembers being one."

She put her hand in his. The heron was gone; the water went out from the reeds into the cove; the willows dripped; the frogs kept count. And in the reeds, at the edge of where the light was wrong, a band of water moved — not a current, a band, a stripe, a line of water that moved the way water moves when something under it makes space. It ran a slow rectangle along the reed wall and came back the way it had come, like something pacing.

It was not a fish. It was the size of a panel.

Annabeth did not ask what it was. She had learned — from Tartarus, from war, from a city that fell and a sea that did not — that there is a kind of thing you meet in the dark that you do not meet by naming. You meet it by standing very still and paying attention.

So they stood very still, on the flat rock by the arrow, and paid attention, and the shape in the reeds paced its rectangle once, and twice, and then it went out of sight among the stems, and the light under the water dimmed, and then — after a while, after a long while — it came back, and it paced its rectangle again, and that time, where it crossed the pale band of the seam's glow, it was not a shape. It was a shadow. A flat shadow of a person in a doorway, walking a corridor that only had one direction.

When it had done that three times, Percy said, very quietly, "Show me the panel."

She showed him the panel. He took it in both hands and laid it flat on their knees between them, the notches up, in the willow light, and Annabeth laid her palm over the notches, and Percy laid his over her hand, and they sat on the rock and let the water come out of the cove past their toes and did not say anything at all for the length of a minute.

Then he said, "It's counting. Whatever it is. It's counting thirty-nine of something, and it's pacing the reed wall, and it's not here for us. It's here for the lake."

"It came here," Annabeth said, "and it set a hole in the lake's remembering, and then it went away, and then it kept coming back to count things."

"That's a guess."

"It's a map," she said. "That's the difference. A map is a guess with measurements taken."

---

They went back out by the flat rock, and Percy took the long way around the shallow shelf instead of the straight line, because the long way was easier on Annabeth's knees in the dark, and because he was not going to let her walk home through the cold current in wet cuffs if he could row them instead. Annabeth followed at his shoulder with the panel carried between them like a third person, her hand over the notches and his hand over hers, and the cold water went out of the cove against them all the way, indifferent, the way a clock is indifferent.

At the sunken skiff they climbed back in, and Percy looked down at the hole in the hull that the panel had been covering, and the hole looked back at him.

"It wants us to leave it," he said.

"Possibly."

"It doesn't want us to leave it. It just doesn't have a concept of leaving things." The water in the hole pushed gently in, then out, like breathing, and the green light inside it was steady. Percy put his palm flat on the gunwale and let the lake tell him what it could, which was not much. He got the shape of the cold seam across the cove mouth. He got the measure of the outward current — stronger than the lake should have been able to make against a flat calm evening, as if a hand had pushed its way through a wall and left the hole open behind it. And he got the fish: they were not in the cove at all. They were massed out past the boundary rock in a single tight school, keeping the distance animals keep from a thing they cannot classify.

"We're going to have to tell someone," Annabeth said.

"Clarisse."

"And then Chiron," she agreed. "And the map."

"And Thalia."

"Thalia." She sighed, and he didn't say it either, because he was thinking it, and from the set of her shoulders she was too. "Tell Clarisse tonight," Annabeth said. "It's her shore. She'll say I made it up, and then she'll say she never said I made it up, and then she'll look at the panel for six minutes and write on her clipboard and go to bed, and tomorrow at breakfast she'll have a plan."

"That's Clarisse," Percy agreed, and cut their skiff loose from the sunken hull and took them out by the long way, past the flat rock and the reed wall, and the thing in the reeds paced its rectangle one more time, and he did not look at it as long as he could help it, and then he looked, and the water in the reeds was only water, only light, only cold.

At the boundary rock he stopped the skiff. The white X was still new paint on the gray stone.

"Hold on," Annabeth said, and got the panel out from between them and held it on her knees while he brought the boat level and let the drifting stop.

Then he stood up in the boat, and put both hands into the water, and pushed his will at the lake, not to command it, because you never command water — only ask. The cold seam across the mouth of the cove was a question. He asked the seam what it was, and the lake answered the way it always answered, which was not in words.

It answered: not now.

"Then when," he said, in his head, to the lake.

It answered: when you're ready.

He knew that answer. It was the lake's whole temperament. It was a lake that did not hurry anything, that would wait for a year or a century, that had been holding a shoreline since before any of them had names. He took his hands out of the water and stood dripping in the skiff in the dusk and looked down the cove — the green half-mile of shade, the arrow that sat twelve feet off, the sunken skiff lying in the dark with its panel gone and its hole breathing.

"Okay," he said.

"What did it say?" Annabeth asked.

"It said not yet."

"That's all?"

"That's all —" he searched for the way to translate it into words she could use, which was hard, because the lake's answers came in a language that had no words in it at all. "It said this isn't the first time somebody's asked. It said there have been other questions, in other years, at other shores, and they weren't answered either, and it said that's all right. It said it's been keeping things a very long time."

Annabeth looked at him, with the light going out of the west and the first star coming on over the pavilion, and the willows dripping, and the frogs keeping count.

"And the architect?" she said. "Did it say anything about — who?"

"It didn't say. It said the sea remembers and the sky forgets. The lake is neither." He stopped. He was hearing the thing himself as he said it, and it was not a thing he had ever heard before — not in all the time he had known this lake, not in the water of Atlantis, not anywhere. "The lake is the door between them," he said. "That's how it kept it safe. And everything I know about doors says somebody built this one."

Annabeth set the panel down in the bottom of their skiff, carefully, notched side up, and laid her folded camp shirt over it to keep the wind out of it, and took his wet hand in both of hers.

"Then we go through the door," she said quietly, "when we're ready. Not tonight."

"Not tonight," he agreed.

"And we don't ask who built it," she said. "We ask what's on the other side."

"That," Percy said, and shipped the oars and took them home — past the boundary rock with its white X, past the buoys, under the boathouse, up the ramp, into the smell of camp at night: pine and smoke and the far-off smell of somebody's grill — "is a good plan."

"That's not a plan," Annabeth said. "That's a map."

"It's a map," he admitted. "Maps are better plans."

"Obviously."

---

Annabeth sat down on the last plank of the dock, where the boards were still warm from a hot day, and spread the blanket over her legs, and pulled Percy down beside her, and they sat without words, watching the water do what it did.

The lake at night was the thing it had always been, which was a mirror turned the other way: not the sky above, but the sky below — the town lights, the low crescent, the star-shimmer, all of it doubled in the water. From up here, the cove was only a darker shade of dark. From up here, there was no seam, no panel, no arrow, no hole in a sunken hull breathing in the dark. Just the lake, holding the sky it had decided to keep.

"The panel stays with me tonight," Annabeth said. "Cabin One, under my bed, and then Chiron's office in the morning before anyone gives it away as firewood." She looked down at the folded shirt in her lap. "You know what the worst part is?"

"What?"

"Somebody went down there in eight feet of freshwater and cut a rect—" She stopped. She had said a thing, and she heard it as she said it, the way she heard things when her body was running ahead of her common sense. "Eight feet," she repeated.

Percy turned his head. "How did you know it was eight feet?"

"I surveyed it."

"When?"

"I don't know." Her hand had gone to her pocket, which was where her hands went when they wanted a pencil, and her fingers found the pencil, and she turned it over once out of habit. "Percy. When I came here, in this life, before anyone had told me the lake was fresh—"

"You told me it was fresh."

"—I knew. I told you it was fresh, and I'd have invented the smell of it and the cold of it and the way the bottom of a lake feels different from the bottom of a sea. And I've said that before. I said it in the cove, weeks ago, to Thalia. It's not a memory." Her voice had gone into the register it went for things that were not map-shaped. "This is different. It's in my hands. My hands know the shape of this cove, and I didn't learn it here, and I can't tell you when I did."

He was quiet for a while. Then he said, "If it's somebody else's — then you take it back."

"Take it back how?"

"By using it on purpose," he said. "Every time. That's the only way anything that isn't yours stays yours. You fight for it, you choose it, and after a while the fight is the choice." He shrugged, and the shrug was the one that meant this was the only kind of wisdom he had and he was spending all of it. "That's how I did it. Everyone thinks I came here knowing all this camp stuff. I know some of it. The rest of it — the cabin, the sisters, you — I chose. I choose it every day. It joins you after a while."

Annabeth looked at him. The light from the far shore came across the water and touched the dock and moved on.

"It's why I like you," she said.

"You told me that once. In a boat. You said that's why you like me."

"And I said it in a cove," Annabeth said. "It's a phrase I save. You don't spend it twice unless you've had two coves."

He laughed — low, breathless, the laugh that lived in the ribs — and she put her head down against his shoulder, and they sat in the dark with the doubled sky under the dock, and the lake went on with its keeping, and neither of them was in a hurry to spend the night on anything else.

After a while Annabeth sat up straight, because she had one more thing, and she was going to say it, and there was no reason in the world to be coy with it, because coyness was for people who had not been through a war and a war and a lake.

"Percy," she said. "If it's in the lake, then it isn't in the sky. And it isn't Thalia. It's never been Thalia."

He nodded, slowly. "There was a storm. The first week I got here. Over the whole camp, the whole valley. I thought it was weather."

"There was."

"The lake never rang," he said. "Not once, all year. And now—" He looked out at the dark, at the place where the cove lay, at the seam, and did not finish the sentence, because she had already finished it with him, the way they finished each other now, in the places where they had stopped being two people who remembered the same future and had become two people building a different one. "It rings now. It rings at a cove. There's a panel counting thirty-nine of something in my bag. The arrow walked twelve feet. And the heron will be standing on the bow of the sunken skiff all night, patient, holding the boat's place, in case in the morning somebody comes back to build something."

Behind them the camp was dark, the cabins shut, the path lamps burned down to their last coals, a single window lit up at the top of the Big House — someone's late mapmaking, somebody's late paperwork, the ordinary watchfulness of a camp that had not yet learned there was something on the lake to watch.

"Okay," Annabeth said, and stood, and brushed the willow-fluff off her sleeves, because the sleeves were borrowed and she wanted to give them back. "Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," Percy agreed.

---

Percy stood too, and shoved his wet hair flat with one hand, and she took his elbow as they went up the path, not for anything, just to be holding on, and the path was warm pine under them and the camp was asleep under the stars, and the lake lay out behind them with its whole sky doubled in it.

Halfway up, Annabeth stopped, and turned, and looked back at the dark water, and said it — to the lake, and to the cove, and to whoever had laid a door under a sunken skiff in her lake:

"I'm going to map you," she said. "I'm the cartographer. You don't get a door in my lake without going on my map."

Percy watched her say it, standing in the dark with his hair still flat from the lake and her pencil behind his ear and his heart somewhere in the vicinity of his throat, and thought, with the certainty of a boy who had been carried out of a storm once and had then, at the age of fourteen, rowed into one on purpose on a Tuesday evening:

Whatever that is. Whatever is on the other side of that door. It came with her.

And then, because he was Percy, and because the day had used itself up and the not-saying was nearly over, he added the other half out loud, against her hair, where no one at camp would ever hear it:

"And I'm going with her. That's the whole plan. That's the map."

She reached back without turning, found his hand in the dark the way she always found it, and squeezed, once, hard.

"Obviously," Annabeth said.

And they went up the hill, and the lake kept their footsteps the way it kept everything, patient, doubled, its whole second sky lying quiet on the water, waiting for morning like a measuring line about to be drawn.
