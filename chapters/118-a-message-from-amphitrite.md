## Chapter 118: A Message from Amphitrite

All morning the sea had behaved, which Percy found suspicious. Beyond camp boundaries it stretched in an unruffled blue sheet under late-morning sun, gulls stippling white overhead, their cries thin and far. No waves slapped the shingle. Even the breeze stayed mild, threading salt and a distant promise of lunch — grilled fish, lemon, warm bread — through the pine scent from the dunes. Light lay flat and bright on the water, almost too still to trust.

Perched on the low stone wall near the canoe lake, lichen rough under his palms and sun warming the rock through his jeans, Percy watched sunlight lay paths across the water, each dissolving the instant he tracked it, bright shards breaking and reforming. Lake water lapped softly at the dock pilings with a hollow wooden knock, and a dragonfly hovered, wings a blur, before darting off.

Behind him, Clarisse litigated with Silena whether the laundry line constituted a strategic hazard.

“Strung at face height,” Clarisse argued. “Ambush.”

“Clothesline.”

“That's what they want you to think.”

“They who?”

“People who don't want me seeing the line.”

With the patience of someone who'd accepted logic as decorative in Clarisse's arguments, Silena sighed. “Could move it instead of declaring war on laundry.”

“War moves things.”

Their voices belonged — part of camp weather. Percy hadn't grasped how much a place reshaped around people until this one did.

“Supposed to be sharpening,” Clarisse called.

“Inspecting.”

“Inspecting the same spot ten minutes.”

“Complicated spot.”

Dropping onto the wall beside him, sun-warmed rock shifting slightly, Silena said, “He's thinking.”

“Worse,” Clarisse said. “Gets ideas.”

Percy opened his mouth to defend his excellent ideas when the canoe lake turned gold.

Not sunlight. Color welled from below, pooling bright and round where the water deepened, gold leafing the ripples. Air cooled against Percy's cheeks, dampening his skin with fine mist, and the smell sharpened — ozone and deep water, kelp and cold stone. Every lakeside sound softened — paddles pausing mid-stroke, talk hushing, a rope creaking as someone stopped coiling it — as if the world had stepped back to yield room, holding its breath.

Standing, Percy let his sword slip to the grass with a dull thud.

“Iris-message?” Silena asked, voice hushed.

“Probably,” Percy said, quieter than intended, pulse quickening.

Clarisse closed ranks, hand finding her hilt. She didn't draw. Percy had learned to read that restraint as its own language.

The golden circle widened. A rainbow arched over the lake, narrow color-bridge from water to air, mist beading his forearms. Percy raised his hand. The lake rose in a shining curtain, droplets hanging luminous, and the message gathered inside.

Amphitrite appeared beneath vaulted pale-blue stone, light filtering down in slow columns, a garden swaying behind her — fronds and blossoms stirring in a slow current, tiny silver fish threading through like needles. Her deep-water robe caught light at every fold, shifting green to blue; a silver circlet rested at her brow, cool against her dark hair. Less dressed to impress than interrupted midday for something that mattered, sleeves still pushed back as if she'd set down work to come.

Percy's throat tightened before permission.

“Percy,” she said, smile warming, “good to see you.”

“Hi,” he managed, ears hot. “Good to see you, too.”

Too small, the words. Right anyway.

Her gaze traveled past him, encompassing Clarisse and Silena. “Poseidon's daughters, Clarisse and Silena. Hope I find you well.”

Eyes widening, Silena remembered herself and dipped a curtsy, knee knocking the wall's edge. “Your Majesty.”

Short nod from Clarisse. “My lady.”

Glancing between them, Percy wavered between pride at their manners and worry the lake had turned courtroom.

“No need to stand at attention for a message, Clarisse,” Amphitrite said gently. “Glad to meet you properly, even through rainbow.”

“Met,” Clarisse said. “Briefly.”

“Then glad to see you again.”

Clarisse's shoulders eased a fraction less square, breath audibly leaving her. Percy wisely said nothing, though relief flickered through him warm as sun on water, and he focused wholly deliberately on the ripple marks lapping the dock.

“I've been thinking of you, Percy. Of all of you,” Amphitrite continued, water chiming faintly behind her words. “I'd like to invite you to Atlantis for a short visit, soon, if willing. A few days, perhaps. No ceremony planned, no obligation beyond letting us feed you. Bring your camp family — those you want beside you. Clarisse and Silena are welcome, of course. And anyone else you choose.”

Water shimmered, light fracturing gold across their faces. The words landed clear as if spoken lakeside, each syllable distinct over the soft lapping. *A few days. Atlantis. Home.* Percy's breath caught; the dock's rough grain pressed into his soles through worn sandals.

For one bright second, blue palace ceilings from his recent visits arched overhead in memory, mother-of-pearl catching light, the great hall curving away in cool shadow, Triton laughing at his own joke with a snort that echoed off stone, sea wrapping around him — not boundary, but place that knew his name, brine and cool marble vivid on his tongue, current combing through his hair like fingers.

Then camp overlaid it: cabins sun-warmed and pine-scented, Clarisse tossing him a practice spear with a barked correction, Silena humming over dinner while steam curled off the serving bowls, Annabeth's shoulder against his, solid and familiar. The life he'd grown here — steady, imperfect, his — woodsmoke and cut grass grounding him, gravel underfoot and laughter ringing off bronze.

“A few days?” he asked, easiest question first.

“As many as suit you,” Amphitrite said. “Know you hold a life at camp. This invitation claims none of your time.”

That should've eased everything. It did, a little, loosening the knot between his shoulders. It also sharpened the ache behind his ribs, tender as a fresh bruise, because she named exactly what he feared — that wanting both places might mean betraying one — and forgave it before he'd confessed.

Clarisse's attention flicked toward him. Silena's smile already bloomed, bright as sunrise on surf.

“We'd need to check camp schedule,” Percy said, toes scuffing stone, grit grinding softly under his sandal. Spray had dried salt-tight on his forearms. “And work transport. Currents, timing, who's on watch while we're gone.”

“Of course. I'll send route and possible dates, tide charts and all,” Amphitrite said, water chiming faintly behind her words like distant glass. “Decide together, unhurried. If the answer is not now, it's not now — the sea keeps.” A pause, kind eyes steady, light moving across her circlet. “I'd love for you to see home again. I'd also love to meet the people who've become home to you, whose names fill your letters.”

Percy swallowed, salt thick in his throat. “Okay.”

“Okay you'll consider it?”

Attempted smile. “Okay I want to come. Details to work.”

“Then I'll await word.” Affection without hooks behind it filled her look. “And Percy?”

“Yeah?”

“You needn't arrive as anyone but yourself.”

Before he could answer, the message thinned. Rainbow collapsed to gold ripple; lake settled blue, lapping ordinary against stone. Warmth returned to the air.

For several seconds, all three stood silent, breathing in recovered birdsong.

Then Silena burst between gasp and laugh. “We're going to Atlantis.”

“Haven't said yes,” Clarisse countered.

“Percy did.”

“Said he wants to.”

“Saying yes while details pend.”

Turning to Percy, Clarisse asked, “Do you want to?”

Eyes on the water's innocent blue, he said, “Yeah. Do.”

“Then we'll go,” Silena declared, as if highest authority had ruled. Delight lit her, but she didn't bounce or clap. She'd learned when Percy needed a moment's slack.

Staring at the lake, Clarisse admitted, “Unsure about guesting in a palace.”

“Can guest without loving palaces,” Silena said.

“Not how palaces operate.”

“How do you know?”

“Read things.”

“What things?”

“Things.”

Retrieving his sword and propping it on the wall, bronze cool and familiar in his grip, Percy wiped damp palms on jeans, leaving faint handprints that faded quickly in the sun. “She said no ceremony.” His voice sounded steadier than his pulse.

“She's a queen,” Clarisse said. “Ceremony might be her word for breakfast.”

Leaning forward, Silena asked, “What's Atlantis breakfast?”

“Bread. Fruit. Sometimes fish.”

“Sounds like breakfast.”

“Plus silver trays and people fluent in spoons.”

“How many spoons?”

“Dunno. Plenty.”

“First useful intel,” Clarisse muttered.

Laughter escaped Percy despite himself. It helped. A little. Breath came easier.

“You don't have to be nervous,” he told her. Catching her look, he amended quickly, “Or you can be. Bad at ordering people not to feel.”

“Not nervous.”

Silena's eyebrow climbed.

“Cautious,” Clarisse corrected. “Difference.”

“There is,” Percy said. “Makes sense.”

Face turning toward him, sun edging her profile, she said, “Quit sounding like I need permission.”

“Don't think that. Only mean—” He groped for phrasing that didn't reduce her to a problem. “New for all of us. Know the place, but not guesting there. Not exactly.”

“Lived there.”

“They remember me as a kid there. I've been learning it again. And going back differs from bringing people you love to visit.” Meeting both sisters' eyes, he finished, “Want you there. Want you knowing it. Don't want either feeling you must prove belonging.”

Reaching over, Silena squeezed his forearm, fingers warm. “Couldn't prove belonging better than arriving with my siblings.”

Mouth pressing thin, Clarisse looked away first. “We'll discuss with the others.”

“Yeah,” Percy said. “Will.”

---

Annabeth intercepted them before the cabins, rolled map under one arm, its paper warm from the sun, pencil behind ear, cheeks flushed from drafting and hair frizzing at her temples. Thalia trailed, mid-debate with Chiron over whether the western path needed another ward, dust on her boots and pine needles clinging to her cuffs, gesturing with half an apple.

Her gaze sweeping trio to water, Annabeth asked, “Iris-message?”

“Amphitrite,” Percy said.

Halting, she searched his face. “Everything okay?”

“Yeah. Invited us to Atlantis.”

“Us?” Annabeth repeated, grip tightening on the map tube.

Nodding, Percy said, “Me, Clarisse, Silena. Said I could bring camp family. Anyone I want beside me.”

Surprise crossed her expression, then calculation, then something held close, unreadable. He refused to rush her toward response.

“What happened?” Thalia asked, arriving.

“Atlantis invite,” Clarisse said crisply. “Few days. Soon. No ceremony, allegedly.”

“Allegedly?” Thalia asked.

“Palaces hide ceremony in furniture,” Clarisse said.

“Plausible,” Thalia conceded.

Eyes shining, Silena breathed, “Might see the gardens.”

“Will,” Percy said. “Pretty.”

“What grows?” Annabeth asked.

“Sea lilies, ribbon-kelp, little blue blossoms that shut in shadow. Path with carved fish that swim if you linger.”

“Not what *carved* means.”

“Underwater, is.”

Her smile flickered toward serious. “Would you want me there?”

Percy blinked. “Of course.”

“I mean as your chosen person,” Annabeth said, fingers whitening around the map tube, knuckles pale. Voice steady, vulnerable underneath, eyes bright and unwavering. “Not because Thalia and I are sisters — daughters of Zeus, both — or because Amphitrite said camp family and I happen to stand here, dusty from measuring. Want to come because you want me, specifically, for me.”

Warm, painful twist in his chest, breath hitching. He had spent so long fretting whether wanting two homes was allowed, turning it over sleepless on his bunk while waves knocked the shore, that he'd missed how the invitation might land on the girl he loved — whether she'd hear herself ranked, counted, assumed. Stepping closer on the dusty path, careful to leave air between them, pine needles crunching softly underfoot, he said, “Want you because you're you. The person I want alongside when I see Atlantis again. To show you what I remember, hear your take, have you meet the people I miss. Then come back here with you.”

Holding his eyes, she said, “Good reasons.”

“Can list them.”

“Already did.”

“Then that's the list.”

Map settling against her hip, she said, “I'd like to go.”

“Good.”

“But I won't fake understanding everything because another life left memories.”

“Won't ask you to.”

“And if I ask about the parts you've had to learn again, we can admit what we don't know.”

“Can do that.”

“Good.” Her hand found his, chaste and sure. “Then I'd like to come as your chosen person.”

Fingers threading through hers, callused and sun-warm, he said, “That's what I want. You, there, with me — seeing what I see.” Her hand fit his as it always had, pencil callus to sword callus.

Glancing at their joined hands, then the lake, Thalia granted them all of one second's sentiment. “Assume I'm invited?”

“If you want,” Percy said. “Family too.”

“Know.” Voice dropping a shade, she bumped Annabeth's shoulder. “Checking the guest list before Clarisse wages war on tableware.”

“Don't wage war on tableware,” Clarisse said.

“Threatened the laundry line this morning,” Silena reminded her.

“Line started it.”

Shaking her head, Thalia said, “Come if camp spares me. Must ask Chiron.”

Chiron, evidently having heard enough, approached up the path, hooves muffled on pine needles. “Meant to ask whether I might accompany you, Percy.”

“Want to go?” Percy asked.

“If invitation allows. Useful to discuss travel with Amphitrite. And some time since I visited Atlantis.”

Delighted, Silena asked, “Been there?”

“Once or twice.”

“Gardens?”

“Quite beautiful. Cool light, quiet water.”

“Food?”

“Excellent.”

“Spoons?”

Smile creasing his eyes. “Depends on the meal.”

Clarisse groaned. “Doomed.”

“Nobody's doomed,” Annabeth said. “Planning.”

“Worse.”

“Often is,” Annabeth agreed, lips twitching.

---

They carried the question to the Big House, where Chiron cleared the long table's end and spread paper, sunlight striping oak in bright bars and dust turning slowly above it. The room smelled of old books and lemon polish, with coffee cooling in a forgotten mug. Nothing trip-shaped yet: no date, no route, ink and blank space. Percy welcomed those unfinished edges. They let him hold the thought without stepping inside, turning it over like a shell found on sand.

Annabeth drew her chair near his, knee brushing his. Opposite, Silena rotated blank paper sideways for listing. Clarisse held the wall until Chiron pointed at a chair. She took it as if granting the chair a favor.

Thalia propped a hip on the table. “First: who's going?”

“Percy, me, Silena, Clarisse,” Annabeth said. “Thalia, if coverage allows. Chiron, if invited.”

“I'll reply confirming Percy's hoped-for party,” Chiron said, “then check availability and camp needs during absence.”

“Shouldn't drain camp at once,” Thalia said. “Cabins need coverage.”

“Not bringing all camp,” Percy said.

“Good. Long swim.”

“Could sail,” Chiron said. “Amphitrite will send the route. Expect one of Poseidon's currents.”

“Bring gear?” Clarisse asked.

“What kind?”

“Kind ensuring I'm not defenseless if trouble surfaces.”

“Palace posts guards,” Percy said.

“Doesn't answer.”

“No,” he admitted. “Doesn't.”

Folding weathered hands, Chiron said, “We'll ask palace expectations. No one travels defenseless, though protected rooms may restrict entry.”

“If weapons wait at the door, want notice before arrival,” Clarisse said, jaw set, arms folded tight across her chest, boots planted. Dust drifted through the light bar above the table. “Dislike discovering the line after someone else rings me with it, like a tripwire found with my shins.”

“Fair question,” Annabeth said, noting swiftly. “Weapons, security, guest bounds.”

“And whether we're guests or something else,” Clarisse said.

Silena asked, “Meaning?”

“Poseidon's daughters. Percy's sisters. Never part of palace life as he was. Don't want strangers deciding what we are before knowing us.” Eyes to Percy. “Or molding us into accessories for him.”

Fingers curling around the table's edge, wood smooth-worn under his grip, Percy understood viscerally. Kinship to power could title a person before anyone learned their name, could turn a sister into a footnote. He'd watched it happen in Atlantis corridors, whispers trailing Triton.

“Tell Amphitrite you're my sisters,” he said firmly. “Not roles or court puzzles. You.”

Studying him, Clarisse said, “Know what you mean.”

“And can ask how she'll introduce us.”

“Matters.” Annabeth logged names, titles, introductions, pencil whispering.

Leaning over, Silena added, “And what to bring.”

“Questions first,” Annabeth said. “No outfit planning before route or dress code.”

“Underwater, clothes aren't trouble,” Percy said. “Current wraps you — breathe, stay dry.”

“How?”

“Magic.”

Margin note from Annabeth: “Accurate, unhelpful.”

Hand lifting, Silena said, “Want to bring Amphitrite a gift.”

“Yes,” Chiron said. “Small welcome token customary.”

“What would she like?” Silena asked Percy.

He pictured pearl corridors glowing softly, gardens swaying in filtered light, her shelves crowded with surface and deep artifacts — driftwood polished by centuries, Roman coins green with age — nacre gleaming at every turn. “Owns plenty beautiful already. Vaults of it.”

“Not something from us,” Silena said.

Nod from Clarisse. “Bring what says who we are. Not expensive.”

“What?” Annabeth asked.

“Something camp-made,” Percy said. “Maybe strawberry harvest jar. Amphitrite likes sweet things, honeyed figs especially.”

Brightening, Silena asked, “Jam from last month?”

“Good jar,” Clarisse stipulated. “Not Percy's open one.”

“Closed it.”

“After ants convened.”

“Quality inspectors.”

“They ate it.”

“One review method.”

Laughing, Annabeth inked *strawberry jam*. “One jar, plus something individual apiece.”

“Could sketch camp,” Silena offered. “Garden, perhaps. Somewhere showing where we come from, salt air and all.”

“She'd like that,” Percy said.

Shifting, Clarisse said, “Could bring camp token. Maybe blue tournament ribbon.”

“Keeping it?”

“Have spares.”

“Then maybe.”

Claiming the pencil, Silena said, “Listing possibilities, not finals.”

“Good,” Annabeth said. “Must ask what's appropriate, whether food crosses, whether camp goods might misread.”

“Nobody mistakes jam for insult,” Silena said.

“Hospitality customs vary,” Chiron reminded her, kindly.

“Xenia,” Percy said. “Guest-friendship. Host offers welcome; guest honors the house.”

“And neither weaponizes it,” Chiron added. “Ideally.”

“*Ideally* worries me,” Clarisse said.

“Worries me too,” Percy said. “But Amphitrite promised no strings. We verify by asking plainly.”

“That's a question,” Annabeth said, adding it, underline firm.

They catalogued dates, travel duration, current mechanics, dress, address forms, protocol around Poseidon, sleeping, early-return options.

“Bow?” Silena asked, fingers twisting her skirt hem, eyes wide and earnest.

“Doubt it,” Percy said, rubbing his neck where salt had dried itchy. “But guest rules unknown — palace currents shift.”

Annabeth asked, “Grew up there — how'd you greet her?”

“Triton says I said, ‘Hi, Amphitrite.’”

Horrified, Clarisse stared, spoon halting halfway to her mouth. “Called the queen by name?” Her voice cracked slightly on *queen*, disbelief and reluctant amusement warring across her face.

“Little.”

“Nobody horrified?”

“Not that he mentioned.”

“Too little to notice.”

“Possibly.” Grin. “Doubt she expects formal bows. We'll ask.”

“What if bow's wrong?” Silena fretted, chewing her lip, hands fluttering. “Too deep, shallow, wrong direction? Wrong god entirely?”

“Could bow at the wrong god,” Thalia said. “Blame the spear rack.”

Small laugh from Clarisse. “Not bowing to Ares.”

“No,” Thalia agreed evenly. “You're not.”

Brief pause followed — not long, a heartbeat or two, cutlery ticking faintly in the background — but Percy caught it. Clarisse's face carried the old flicker that surfaced whenever Ares arose and people expected a spear in her hand to make her one of his, a shadow crossing quickly and gone. She was Poseidon's daughter. Percy and Silena's sister. Settled truth, yet old stories left marks, like tide lines on rock that show even at high water.

Knuckles tapping Clarisse's hand, Silena said, “Nobody assigns you family over dinner.”

“Can handle dinner.”

“Know. Reminding dinner.”

Something in Percy eased. Here at camp, room existed for each to be exactly themselves — sea-smell, spear-calluses, ink-stained fingers and all. He refused to let Atlantis recast his sisters as extras in his story.

Glancing at the sprawling list, Annabeth said, “We can ask what helps us honor her home.”

“Better,” Percy said. “I'll answer what I know, you flag what I miss.”

“Will,” Clarisse promised.

“Eagerly,” Silena added. “Especially spoons.”

“Spoons for everyone's safety,” Annabeth said, writing it with a flourish.

---

By late afternoon, the list filled two pages, edges curling in heat, ink smudged where Silena's sleeve had dragged. Chiron would write to Amphitrite with Percy dictating, stumbling occasionally over phrasing and backtracking; Annabeth would review with her precise pencil; Silena would curate gifts, turning each possibility over like shells. Clarisse would query weapons and security personally, arms crossed, chin high. She trusted her own phrasing over softened versions, and nobody argued.

Practical work had steadied Percy. It lent the invitation edges to grip: date, route, bunks, packing, each written in a different hand around the table. Yet when the others scattered to tasks — paper rustling, chairs scraping stone, footsteps fading down the hallway — the tangled undertow returned, cold seeping back around his ankles. Longing braided with worry, neither loosening.

He found Annabeth on the canoe dock, boots hooked over the edge, planks warm beneath and creaking faintly with each shift, sun low and honeyed. Copper light sheeted the water around her in broken ripples, dragonflies stitching air with their dry-wing hum, pine shadows reaching long across the lake. Sitting alongside, denim whispering on wood, he left the space she'd taught him to leave until invited, hands resting on his knees, lake lapping a slow rhythm against the pilings.

Eyeing the list in his hand, she said, “Kept it.”

“Didn't want to lose it.”

“Could've requested a copy.”

“Know. But this one's my handwriting.”

Scanning, she said, “Improving.”

“Generous.”

“True. Early-return question nearly legible.”

“Added that one.”

“Know.” Folding the paper, she returned it, fingers lingering. “Still worried?”

Watching reflections shiver, he admitted, “Yeah. Want to see them. Want you all with me. Scared I'll arrive and feel I must be the Percy who never left.”

“Needn't be.”

“Know what Amphitrite said.”

“Believe her?”

He recalled warmth threading her voice, *not a claim on your time*. “Do.”

“Then what scares you?”

“Missing camp while there. Or missing Atlantis here. That merging everyone spotlights my split, and someone feels they've lost.”

No accusation of overthinking. No instant fix, no brisk reassurance. Shoulder to his, cotton warm from sun, she let the words stand unshrunk, lake lapping the pilings in slow rhythm, a fish jumping far out with a soft plop. Wind combed the reeds, carrying pine and distant kitchen smells.

“Don't want to force you choosing,” she said.

“Not you.”

“Know. Still want to witness it with you. Meet the people you miss, walk the places you mention. Understand more of your life. Not anchoring you here.”

Palm up on sun-warmed boards, grain pressing his wrist, nails still rimmed with paint from the morning's work, he offered his hand. Annabeth set hers inside without hesitation, calluses familiar, fingers cool from trailing in lake water.

“Want you there,” he said. “Not to camp-ify Atlantis. Not to diminish camp. Because it's you.”

“That's my *chosen person*.”

“Glad you asked.”

“Glad you said yes.”

They sat while light shifted gold to rose, water breathing beneath the dock in slow swells, shadows lengthening across their legs. Across camp, the dinner conch sounded — brassy, imperfect, a little cracked on the high note — summoning through pines with its familiar wobble. It layered, in Percy's ear, with palace bells' deeper resonance through water, low and pure as struck glass. Neither canceled the other; both rang true, braided together like currents meeting.

“What should we bring?” he asked.

“Gift that says us. Something our hands all touch. Camp map we build together,” Annabeth said, hair lifting in the breeze and sticking briefly to her lip gloss, eyes bright with planning. “Silena draws garden and pavilion with all her flowers, you lake and shore where you know every ripple, Clarisse arena with her exacting lines.”

“And laundry line,” Percy said. “Marked hazard.”

“Help with the map,” Annabeth offered. “You know camp water better.”

“Solid gift.”

“Finishable before wardrobe panic.”

“Clothes are fine.”

“Unproven.”

Laughing, tension sloughing off, he said, “Missed planning with you.”

Fingers lacing through his, she said, “Then we'll plan together.”

---

That evening they gathered beneath the pavilion roof after dinner, crickets sawing in the dark beyond the torches, leftover honey and woodsmoke clinging to the air, marble still holding the day's warmth under their sandals. No date decided. Chiron would message Amphitrite; camp's calendar would dictate departure, its crowded columns of drills and watches. The visit remained unhappened. Only possibility had reshaped, like light shifting across water.

Silena had already itemized gifts in her looping script — jam with its gingham cover, camp map with curled edges, garden posy still dewy — while torchlight gilded the paper. Clarisse had block-lettered “ask about weapons” and underlined twice, pencil nearly tearing paper, jaw set. Annabeth's column covered current, access, formal expectations, clear exit if anyone needed, each line numbered with crisp efficiency.

“Bring the good jam,” Silena said.

“Good jam belongs to camp,” Clarisse said. “Then Percy eats the last jar.”

“Ate one jar.”

“One and a half.”

Tapping the page, Annabeth ruled, “One jar, sealed. If Amphitrite likes it, she keeps camp sweetness. If not, Percy eats it homeward.”

“Most practical gift strategy,” Thalia said. “Zero risk.”

“Risk,” Clarisse countered. “Ants may invite themselves.”

“They can't. Not camp family,” Percy said.

“Eating our food months. Earned table seats.”

Giggling, Silena promised, “Keeping them off the guest list.”

Pale parchment in hand, its edge soft with age, Chiron asked, voice resonant in the cooling air, “Writing tomorrow. Any question demanding direct asking? Anything that can't wait for currents and tides?” Lanterns swayed overhead, moths batting softly against glass.

Straightening, Clarisse said, “Do. Want her expectations on arrival. Not court's. Hers.”

“Phrased so.”

“And whether we may bear arms, else what guard covers us.”

“Noted.”

Annabeth added, “How to address her, rooms to avoid.”

Silena said, “Whether guests bring gifts or awkward.”

Thalia raised a hand. “Transit time, plus solo early return without recalling everyone.”

Percy looked around. “Whether we can walk the gardens. And whether Triton attends.”

“He will,” Chiron assured him. “Knowing you may come, he'll make himself available.”

“He'll want to meet you all,” Percy said. “Subtlety isn't his strength.”

“Family trait?” Annabeth asked, arch.

“Not mine.”

Skeptical noise from Clarisse.

“Fine,” Percy conceded. “Bit.”

Settling beside them, bench groaning, Chiron asked, “Else?”

“Tell her some of us are excited,” Silena said, glancing at Clarisse, “to meet her.”

“Willing to meet her,” Clarisse amended. “Excitement unpromised.”

“Fair,” Silena said.

Surveying the circle, Percy catalogued them fondly: Annabeth close at his side, warmth along his arm, Thalia behind her with a brief hand on Annabeth's shoulder, fingers lingering; Silena making space for Clarisse's hard questions, sliding the paper closer; Clarisse staying despite discomfort, jaw set but present; Chiron treating invitation as choice, not summons, voice even and unhurried. Separate reasons to go, none required to mirror his, each valid as a different colored thread.

“We decide together,” Percy said, voice steady despite the tightness in his throat, meeting each gaze in turn. “Nobody comes merely because I want it. Only because you want it too — that's the whole point of family choosing.”

“Want to,” Silena said promptly, bright as sunrise, hands clasped tight with delight, citrus and paint still fragrant on her sleeves.

Exhaling through her nose, misting faintly in the cooling air, Clarisse said, “Want to see it, curious despite myself. Also want rules before walking blind into them, no surprises sprung mid-hall.”

“Want to accompany you,” Annabeth said softly, fingers tightening around his, eyes steady and bright in torchlight. “As your chosen person. Not plus-one. Yours.”

“And want to ensure royalty doesn't swallow my sister whole,” Thalia added dryly, jerking a thumb at Annabeth. “Come if camp spares me. Someone's got to guard the architecture and the girl.”

Nodding, Chiron said, “Accompany you if Amphitrite welcomes me and duties allow,” hooves shifting softly on stone, tail swishing at evening flies. His eyes, ancient and kind, moved across each face in turn.

Worry hadn't evaporated, but it no longer sat solely with him — distributed among warm hands and steady voices, lighter for sharing, like a heavy pack divided trail-wise. The dock's warmth seeped through his jeans as he breathed easier.

Atlantis still ached, camp still extended its hand. He'd imagined those pulls as rival tides with no shore between, grinding and cold. Now, amid debate over jam with its sweet strawberry perfume, weaponry oil-sharp, and royal bows endlessly rehearsed, the image shifted, softened, warmed — family voices braided through it.

Atlantis needn't steal him from camp. Camp needn't bar him from Atlantis. He could escort beloved people, salt and sun on their skin, to the missed place with its cool blue halls, letting camp-home with its pine smoke and laughter meet before-home with its gardens and bells, introductions awkward and wonderful.

Nobody competing, nobody abandoned for the other to count, no ledger of loves. For the first time, both homes coexisted without shoving, tide meeting shore instead of fighting it, foam bright on sand.

Eyes on the question list, its edges fluttering in the pavilion breeze, Annabeth's hand beside his, thumb resting lightly on his knuckle, Clarisse and Silena softly sparring over jam with relish, their shoulders bumping, Percy smiled, chest looser than all day, breathing honeyed evening air deep.

“So,” he said, looking from face to beloved face around the lantern-lit table, “we'll need more than one jar. Possibly a crate. Definitely a plan for keeping Percy away from it till we land.” Laughter broke warm across the pavilion, torches fluttering in the sea breeze.

“Not inviting the ants,” Clarisse said sternly, though her lips twitched against laughter.

“Wasn't meaning ants,” Percy protested, hand to heart. “Scout's honor. Mostly.”

“Good,” she said warmly. “Then we surely stand a real chance together now, slim but ours.”
