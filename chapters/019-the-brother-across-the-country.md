## Chapter 19: The Brother Across the Country


Thalia woke before the first conch because static was gathering around Cabin One's window latch. It wasn't prophecy. She had never dreamed in green mist or seen futures in her sleep; her body simply noticed the pressure before a storm. When she sat up, she realized Annabeth had been drawing for almost an hour. Pencil scratched across graph paper with a rhythm Thalia remembered from years when her sister covered the backs of motel receipts in sketches of imaginary houses.

Thalia had watched Annabeth grow up through missed meals, Beryl's broken promises and finally the safety of camp. Annabeth believed those years were real when Thalia told her, but could not retrieve them herself. Their brother Jason was even farther away. He had been too little when they separated to remember much of the household at all. Chiron had worn a careful expression when Thalia delivered her patrol report the previous night. She couldn't stop wondering whether it meant news about Jason.

"You've been up for an hour," Thalia said, her voice rough with sleep.

"Forty minutes," Annabeth corrected without looking up. She was sitting cross-legged on her bunk, her blanket tucked around her legs even though the morning was warm. Her blond hair was in a messy braid over one shoulder, coming loose. There were smudges of graphite on her left cheek and the side of her hand. "Sorry. Did I wake you?"

"No. Storm sense woke me."

Annabeth glanced up at that. "Is there a storm coming? The sky was clear at dawn."

"Not that kind." Thalia sat up, swung her legs over the side of the top bunk, and dropped down. The floor was cool under her bare feet. "The kind where Chiron has that face."

"What face?"

"The 'I have news and I'm not sure if it's good news so I'm going to over-explain it at breakfast' face. He had it last night when I brought in the patrol report."

Annabeth frowned, tapping her pencil against her notebook. "He was normal at dinner. He asked Percy about his sword forms and asked Silena about the strawberry harvest."

"Exactly. Polite. Chiron is never polite when everything is normal. When everything is normal he tells Clarisse her spear grip is sloppy and tells the Stolls to stop putting honey in the milk pitcher."

Annabeth almost smiled. "Okay. So something's happening."

"Something's happening," Thalia agreed. She crossed the cabin to the single small window and pushed the shutter open. Camp Half-Blood spread out below in early morning gold. The strawberry fields were already bright green and red. Mist hung low over the lake. Someone — probably Percy, because no one else was that relentless — was already doing laps, a dark speck cutting back and forth across the water. "You ready to find out what?"

Annabeth closed her notebook. On the open page Thalia caught a glimpse of columns and measurements, a half-drawn temple with notes in the margins. Annabeth had been designing buildings since she was five. Thalia remembered sitting on the cracked linoleum floor of their first apartment with a box of crayons, Annabeth on her lap, both of them drawing houses with too many windows because Annabeth insisted every room needed a way to see the sky.

"You drew yesterday too," Thalia said lightly. "The Parthenon again?"

"Something like that." Annabeth slid the notebook under her pillow. "Let's go to breakfast before Percy eats all the pancakes and then tells us he saved us some."

---

The dining pavilion buzzed in a way it only buzzed when gossip was moving faster than the food.

Thalia felt it the second she and Annabeth walked up the steps with the rest of Cabin One — which was just the two of them, plus the faint smell of ozone that always clung to their bunks. Heads turned. Not in a bad way. Just in a curious way. Katie Gardner from Demeter waved them over with wide eyes. Travis Stoll outright pointed.

Percy was already at the Poseidon table — which he shared with Clarisse and Silena — but he'd dragged his plate over to the edge closest to the Zeus table, which was technically against the rules and which Mr. D hadn't enforced in months because it was easier than watching Percy sulk. He had a stack of pancakes going cold in front of him and a look on his face like he'd swallowed a secret whole and it was trying to crawl back up.

"Tell me," Thalia said, dropping onto the bench across from him. "You know."

"I know nothing," Percy said immediately. "I am innocent. I am a perfect innocent son of the sea who was just swimming."

"You were eavesdropping on the naiads," Annabeth said, sitting beside Thalia. She stole one of Percy's pancakes without asking and he let her, which told Thalia more than anything else that he was distracted. Normally he'd at least pretend to defend them. "What did they say?"

Percy leaned in, lowering his voice. The pavilion was loud — clatter of plates, laughter from the Apollo table, Clarisse arguing with someone about spear practice — but he still looked around like he thought the wind might be listening. "Okay, so I wasn't eavesdropping on purpose. I was swimming and they were talking by the dock and I have excellent hearing underwater, which is not my fault. They said Chiron got a message from Lupa's people. Like, from the west coast."

Thalia's fork stopped halfway to her mouth.

The west coast meant one thing. It only ever meant one thing when Chiron went quiet and polite and strange.

"Camp Jupiter," Annabeth said quietly.

Percy nodded. He glanced at Thalia, then at Annabeth, his green eyes worried. "They said it's a relay. Like, an Iris-message chain. Because direct messages across the country almost never work with the gods being—" He waved a hand. "You know. Weird and static-y. So they have to bounce it through three or four rainbows. It takes forever to set up and it only lasts a few minutes. Chiron's been working on it for weeks with— I don't know, with whoever is in charge over there. Reyna? No, she's too young still, right? Someone else."

Thalia put her fork down very carefully. Her hand wasn't shaking. She made sure of it. "When?"

"Tonight, after dinner, I think. He was going to announce it at breakfast."

As if on cue, Chiron clopped his hooves against the marble floor at the head of the pavilion. The sound rang out, and the chatter died in ripples.

"Good morning," Chiron said. He smiled, and Thalia recognized the face she'd described to Annabeth. Over-explaining face. Nervous-centaur face. "I have a brief announcement before we begin our day. Many of you know that communication with our counterparts on the west coast is— difficult. Rare. The gods do not make it easy, and distance makes it harder. However. Through considerable effort on both sides, we have arranged a brief Iris-message relay this evening with Camp Jupiter."

A murmur ran through the pavilion. Most of the younger campers looked confused. Camp Jupiter was a rumor to them, a story counselors told to explain why the gods seemed stretched thin. The older campers — Luke, Clarisse, Silena, Beckendorf — sat up straighter. Luke caught Thalia's eye from the Hermes table and gave her a small, steady nod.

Chiron continued, "This is a privilege, not entertainment. I will invite a small group to attend the relay in the Big House rec room. Thalia and Annabeth Grace —" He paused, and Thalia heard half the pavilion turn to look at them. "I would very much like you both to be there."

Thalia couldn't breathe for a second. Then Annabeth's hand found hers under the table and squeezed hard, and she remembered how to inhale.

"Yes," Thalia said, too loud. She cleared her throat. "Yes, sir. Thank you."

"Good." Chiron's eyes were kind. "After dinner. Come straight to the Big House. And —" He hesitated. "It may be brief. The connection is fragile. I want you to be prepared for that."

He moved on to announcements about chariot maintenance and archery rotations, but Thalia didn't hear any of it. Percy was staring at her, his pancakes forgotten. Annabeth's hand was still gripping hers, cold and tight.

"Thalia," Percy said softly. "Is it— Do you think he'll be—"

"I don't know," Thalia said. Her voice came out thinner than she wanted. "They never let us see him before."

---


Annabeth had a set of memories and a set of stories about little brothers, and they did not fit together, and today they were grinding against each other like tectonic plates.

That was how she thought of it. Tectonic plates. She'd read about them in a geology book when she was seven and liked the idea that the ground itself could be in pieces, moving so slowly you didn't notice until everything shook.

In the memories she actually owned — the older life, clear enough to hurt — Bobby and Matthew were her little half-brothers. Frederick's sons with Helen; Annabeth's mother in that life had been Athena. She remembered Frederick's house in California with too much sun, his worried face, a baby crying in another room while she read a book about Greek temples and pretended she couldn't hear. She remembered leaving. She remembered thinking the boys wouldn't remember her anyway. That childhood was hers, however distant its house had become. Jason had been Thalia's younger brother in that life, a friend to Annabeth when they grew older, not another child of Frederick's household.

In the stories true for this life — the ones Thalia told over photographs and too-thin old blankets — Jason was her baby brother. Full brother. Same mom, same dad, same cracked apartment. Thalia described his fists, tiny and red, clutching her finger. She described blowing raspberries on his belly until all three of them laughed at his high, hiccuping giggle. Annabeth wanted those moments to open inside her head. They did not. She had her sister's voice telling her about the morning he was gone.

She had been four then. Thalia had been eight. Their mom — Beryl, beautiful and bright and broken, lightning in a woman's shape — had been pacing for days, talking about omens and wolves and destiny. That was how Thalia told it. Details blurred even for the sister who remembered: adults arriving, an empty crib, the blanket with little embroidered eagles still folded in place. Thalia had screamed, shaken their mother by the shoulders and demanded where he'd gone. Beryl had cried and laughed and said he'd be safe, he'd be strong, he'd be Roman, the wolves would take him where he belonged. Annabeth could repeat every sentence. She could not honestly say she remembered waking to it.

Thalia had not forgiven her. Annabeth would not forget how her sister's voice shook when she described crying that night, trying to be quiet so the little girl beside her wouldn't hear.

Now Annabeth sat in the arena with a practice sword in her hand and couldn't remember any of her forms.

"Grace! Heads up!" Clarisse barked.

Annabeth ducked on instinct as a padded training spear whistled over her head. She rolled, came up, and parried Clarisse's next thrust more by muscle memory than thought. The clash rang up her arms.

"Sorry," Annabeth panted. "Again."

Clarisse lowered her spear, studying her. Clarisse La Rue at thirteen was all sharp edges and loud opinions, but she wasn't stupid. She'd been at camp long enough to know when someone was somewhere else.

"You okay?" Clarisse asked, quieter. "You look like you ate bad ambrosia."

"I'm fine." Annabeth wiped sweat from her forehead. "Just — distracted. Big night."

"Yeah." Clarisse twirled her spear, then planted it. "So. Chiron invited you to talk to the Romans. That's huge. Is it true they have an actual city? Like with plumbing? Travis said they have plumbing and a whole legion and eagles made of gold."

"I don't know," Annabeth said honestly. "We've never— I mean, no one's talked to them in years. Not like this."

Clarisse hesitated, which was rare enough that Annabeth gave her her full attention. "Can I ask something? You don't have to answer."

"Okay."

"Chiron said Grace. Both of you. So — is it family over there?"

Annabeth's throat closed. She'd known this question would come. She'd known it since breakfast, since the heads turning. Camp was small. Everyone knew Thalia had run away with a little sister. No one had ever asked much about before that, and Thalia had never offered.

"Our brother," Annabeth said. The words felt strange in her mouth. Too big. "Our little brother, Jason. He— He was taken when he was really small. Two, almost three. The wolves— Lupa took him to Camp Jupiter. We haven't seen him since."

Clarisse blinked. For a moment all the bravado dropped away and she was just a kid. "You have a little brother? Like, a real little brother?"

"Yeah."

"Huh." Clarisse frowned at her spear tip. "What's that like? I have brothers but they're— older. And mortal. And idiots. They put gum in my hair when I was seven and I punched one of them and got grounded for a month. What's it like having a little brother who's— you know, gone?"

Annabeth opened her mouth and found she didn't have an answer that fit in a sentence. How did you explain missing someone whose first years you knew only through your sister's stories? How did you explain loving brothers from a house the world no longer held, and wanting to love the brother waiting in this one, without treating either love as a replacement?

"It's like—" She stopped. "It's like knowing there's a room in your house you've never been allowed into. You know it's there. You can hear someone moving around in it sometimes. But you can't open the door."

Clarisse nodded slowly, like that made sense. "That sucks."

"Yeah," Annabeth said, and laughed a little because Clarisse always made things simple in a way that helped. "It sucks."

"Is he older than me? Younger?"

"He's ten now. Two years younger than me."

"Oh." Clarisse's face softened. "Little."

"Yeah. Little." Annabeth's eyes stung, and she blamed sweat. "Thalia says he was such a funny toddler. He used to try to eat Thalia's hair when she braided it. He thought lightning was funny. When it thundered he'd clap."

Clarisse grinned. "Seriously? Most kids cry."

"Not Jason. Thalia would hold him up to the window and say, 'Listen, Dad's bowling,' and he'd shriek laughing." The story made a picture so clear Annabeth could almost smell rain on hot pavement. Eight-year-old Thalia, skinny and fierce, holding a giggling toddler on her hip like she'd been born to it. A picture was not a recovered memory. "He couldn't say Annabeth. He called me Beth. Or Beff. With an F. Beff!"

Clarisse laughed, loud and surprised. "Beff! That's amazing. I'm calling you Beff forever now."

"Don't you dare."

"Beff." Clarisse dodged Annabeth's swipe, cackling. "Okay, okay. Sorry. That's — that's cute, though. I get why you're distracted."

Annabeth smiled, but her chest ached. She wondered what Jason called himself now. Did he remember being called Jay-bird by Thalia? Did he remember her at all? Thalia hoped he might have a flash of a girl with blond hair reading to him, a bigger girl singing. Four and eight and two were so young. Memory was so thin at that age, like tissue paper. It tore if you held it too hard.

Practice ended and she went to find Percy, because when her head got too loud, Percy was quiet in the right way.

---


Percy found Annabeth by the lake, doing what she always did when she was thinking too much — throwing pebbles and muttering about architecture.

"Hey," he said, sitting down beside her. He didn't bring food, which showed growth as a person, in his opinion. "You skipped lunch."

"I wasn't hungry." She threw another pebble. Plip. "Did you eat my lunch too?"

"I saved you an apple. It's in my pocket. It's a little warm now. And maybe a little bruised. But it's the thought that counts."

Annabeth almost smiled. "Thanks, Seaweed Brain."

"Anytime, Beff."

She whipped her head around. "Clarisse told you?"

"Clarisse tells everyone everything within six minutes. It's her gift." Percy bumped his shoulder against hers. "I like it, though. Beff. It's cute."

"It's embarrassing."

"It's cute because it's from Jason. Everything from when he was two is automatically cute. That's the rule."

Annabeth was quiet for a moment, watching the water. Percy watched her. He'd gotten good at watching Annabeth — not in a creepy way, just in the way you learned someone you cared about. The way her eyebrows pulled together when she was working through a problem. The way she picked at her braid when she was upset. Right now she was doing both.

"Percy?" she said. "Can I tell you something weird?"

"Always."

"I remember my little brothers in Frederick's house," she said. "Bobby and Matthew. Me leaving. And then I hear about Jason as ours. Thalia's and mine. Beryl giving him up. Thalia waking up and finding him gone." She pulled her knees up to her chest. "One childhood is mine and the other is everybody else's. I love the stories she tells me, but I can't turn them into memories. Tonight — I don't know how to feel about a little brother who's supposed to have known me all his life."

Percy thought about that. He remembered Sally's kitchen and their original camp and every year with Annabeth, sharply, plainly. He did not remember the Atlantis childhood Amphitrite and Triton described. They loved a boy whose old mornings he could not find, and he was trying to meet that love with the years he did have. He and Annabeth talked about it quietly whenever the mismatch got too large to carry alone.

"I think," Percy said slowly, "maybe you don't have to make the stories into memories. You can love the brothers you remember and get to know Jason now. That's real too, right?"

Annabeth stared at him. "When did you get smart?"

"I've always been smart. I'm just humble about it." He handed her the warm bruised apple. "Eat. You need brain food for— whatever architecture you're muttering about."

"I was thinking about a gift," she admitted, taking a bite. "If we get to talk to him. Even for a minute. I want to send something back with the relay. Chiron said messages can carry small things sometimes, if the Mist is stable. Like a picture. Something he can keep."

"That's a good idea." Percy brightened. "We could draw something. I'm bad at drawing but I'm excellent at coloring inside the lines. Mostly. Sometimes."

Annabeth laughed for real this time, and Percy's chest felt warm. "You can help me and Thalia make it. Something that shows him camp. And us. So he knows what we look like now."

"He probably doesn't remember what you look like," Percy said, then winced. "Sorry. That was—"

"No, you're right." Annabeth threw another pebble, harder. "He was two. He won't remember. Thalia's scared of that. She hasn't said it, but I can tell. She's scared he'll look at us and feel nothing."

Percy thought of Sally in his remembered life, of the way she'd held his face the first time he came home from camp and said, "You got taller and I missed it and I'm mad about that." He thought about how Thalia looked at breakfast like someone holding her breath underwater.

"Then we'll make sure he feels something new," Percy said. "Even if he doesn't remember the old stuff. We'll be — I don't know. Memorable. In a good way. Not in a Clarisse way."

Annabeth leaned her head on his shoulder, just for a second. She only did that when she was really tired or really upset, and Percy sat very still so he wouldn't mess it up.

"Thanks, Percy," she murmured.

"Anytime, Beff."

"Stop it."

"Never."

---

Thalia spent the last hour before the call in the Zeus cabin, trying not to make Annabeth nervous.

That was difficult because Thalia herself was nervous enough to polish a spear she'd already cleaned twice. Annabeth had spread three sheets of paper on the desk and was deciding which drawings to show Jason. One depicted the cabin. Another showed a small bridge she'd designed for the lake path. The third was a family portrait based on Thalia's descriptions, with spaces where she hadn't known enough to draw the faces.

"Do you think he'll recognize us?" Annabeth asked.

"I don't know."

"Would it help if you told him a story?"

"Maybe."

Annabeth looked up. She had grown accustomed to Thalia offering definite answers to questions about their childhood. The uncertainty made her sister seem younger for a moment, which was both painful and reassuring.

"I remember when he was small," Thalia said. "But I don't know what Lupa told him afterward. I don't know whether he still uses the names he had then."

"Then maybe ask what he wants us to call him."

Thalia gave her a brief smile. "That's not a bad idea."

They looked at the family drawing together. Annabeth had given Jason a Roman-style cloak because she knew he'd been raised at Camp Jupiter. She hadn't drawn him as a baby or pretended to remember a boy she'd never met in this history.

"Can I show him this?" she asked.

"It's yours. Of course."

"I mean, will it upset him that I don't know what he looked like when he was little?"

Thalia looked at the paper. She had spent so many years wanting Annabeth to remember their family that she sometimes forgot her sister was trying to learn a family she didn't remember. A drawing of three children together could be a promise of affection, not a claim about a photograph that never existed.

"You can tell him how you made it," Thalia said. "That's more honest than pretending it came out of a memory."

Annabeth nodded and rolled the page carefully.

A knock came at the cabin door. Chiron stood outside with the communications authorization. He had received confirmation from the Roman intermediary that a young trainee named Jason could appear for a supervised call. The connection would last only as long as the two camps could maintain the rainbow, and neither side was authorized to share restricted military information.

Thalia was grateful for the permission and infuriated by how formal it sounded. Jason was her brother. She shouldn't need a diplomatic procedure to ask whether he'd eaten breakfast.

"What if we don't have time to ask everything?" she asked.

"Then you prioritize what matters to you and tell him you would like another chance to speak."

"Can we record it?"

"Not without permission from the Roman camp. The connection is private, and Jason should not have to worry that every sentence will be preserved for people he hasn't met."

Annabeth looked at her notebook. She had been planning to write down everything Jason said so she wouldn't lose the details. She hadn't thought of it as recording a child whose permission mattered.

"Could I take notes afterward?" she asked.

"Yes. You can each remember the call in your own way. You simply shouldn't turn the conversation into a formal interview."

Thalia nodded. She was glad he'd made the distinction before they entered the room.

Percy arrived a few minutes later. He had come from Cabin Three with a folded handkerchief, a clean shirt and an expression that suggested Silena had insisted he stop running up the hill in muddy shoes.

"Am I invited?" he asked.

"Yes," Thalia said. "Jason knows you as the friend Annabeth keeps talking about."

Percy smiled. "That's reassuring."

"You can help if he gets nervous."

"Or if you do?"

Thalia looked at him. She might once have snapped that she didn't get nervous. Instead she said, "Both."

The admission made Percy stop joking. He had watched her defend Annabeth in front of Zeus and risk injury during the eclipse. He understood that being able to fight didn't make a person immune to a difficult family conversation.

"What do you need me to do?" he asked.

"Let us talk. If we get stuck, ask him something normal. About school or what he eats or whether he likes the weather there. Just don't treat him like a clue in a prophecy."

Percy nodded. He hadn't planned to, but he appreciated being told what help actually looked like.

Thalia took the spear from its rack and put it away. She wasn't going to meet her younger brother as though preparing for a patrol. Annabeth folded the drawings into a tube and left the most complicated one behind.

At the Big House they found the communications room ready. The lamp had been adjusted to catch a steady band of light, and Chiron stood beside the basin with his hand on the authorization. He told them there would be an adult at the Roman end and that they could stop the call if Jason became overwhelmed.

"Will he know it's all right to stop?" Annabeth asked.

"I've asked the supervising aide to tell him. He deserves the same choice you do."

Thalia stood in front of the basin. It struck her that she had spent years waiting for somebody to give her access to her brother, and now the person she most wanted to see might be equally frightened by the prospect. She didn't want to become another adult who made choices for him because she'd been waiting longer.

"When he appears," she said, "let him speak first if he wants to."

Percy and Annabeth agreed. Thalia knew she'd probably forget the plan the moment she saw his face. She was going to try anyway.



---

At Camp Jupiter, Jason was counting the minutes until the messenger arrived.

He had been told there would be a supervised Iris-message from Camp Half-Blood, and that his sisters might be on the other end. The invitation was written in ordinary language on a folded sheet of paper, which made it more difficult to believe than if it had come with a seal from the praetors.

He had known he had sisters for years. Lupa had told him enough that he recognized their names, and sometimes he received a short note or a drawing forwarded through adults who treated the exchange as a delicate exception to a rule nobody would explain. But knowing a person existed was different from standing in front of a rainbow and trying to decide what to say to her.

Jason was ten. He could recite the basic formations of the Fifth Cohort without help. He knew which hand to raise when a centurion inspected his shield. He had no idea whether he was supposed to call Thalia *Lia*, as he had when he was little, or use the name on the official message.

His supervising aide, a Roman woman named Valeria, had watched him try to polish the same piece of armor three times.

"That shield is clean," she told him.

"There's a mark by the rim."

"That's where it was repaired. Polishing won't remove it."

Jason put down the cloth. He had hoped a spotless shield would make him look older, as though his sisters might believe he'd been doing well if he looked like the other trainees.

"Do I have to wear it?" he asked.

"No. The call is private. You're not attending a review."

"Then why did they tell me to prepare?"

"Because you asked for time to think about what you wanted to say."

He looked at her. She was right. That was annoying.

Valeria set a chair beside the basin where the rainbow would be formed. There was a small table with water and a box of tissues, which Jason considered unnecessary until he remembered the one time he'd asked an adult about his sisters and been unable to finish the question without crying.

"Can I ask what they remember?" he said.

"Of course."

"What if they don't remember me?"

"Then they'll have to tell you that. You may not know what either of them remembers. Neither of you has to pretend."

Jason took out the page on which he'd written questions. He had prepared seven. The first concerned whether Thalia still liked lightning. The second asked whether Annabeth still built little cities. The third was whether their mother had been sending letters. He crossed out the third, then wrote it again. He wanted the answer even if it was difficult.

Valeria watched without interrupting.

"Do they know I've been training?" he asked.

"Some of it. We told them you were safe and learning."

"Safe is not the same as good."

"No," Valeria replied. "It isn't."

He liked her more for that answer. Adults often used *safe* to end a conversation about something else. Jason was safe at Camp Jupiter. He was also lonely sometimes. He was proud of the shield he'd been learning to carry and frustrated when older children treated him as though he should already know every rule. He missed a family he could barely picture. It was possible for all of those things to be true.

The messenger knocked. Valeria stood, checked the authorization and asked Jason whether he was ready.

"No," he admitted.

"Do you want to postpone?"

He looked at the folded note. He'd spent years waiting for an opportunity that never seemed to arrive at a convenient time. If he postponed it because he was frightened, he might spend another month wondering how frightened he would be the next time.

"No. Let's do it."

They went to the communications room. It had a shallow basin set beneath an archway and a wall decorated with the insignia of the Roman camp. Jason had practiced using a rainbow for routine messages, but he'd never stood in this room with a request about his own family.

The attendant adjusted the light, checked the coin and waited for Valeria's signal. Jason kept his paper folded in his hand. He had decided to ask the first question without reading it. If he couldn't remember the words when he saw Thalia, he could look down.

"One more thing," he said to Valeria.

"Yes?"

"What if I don't look like they remember?"

She considered before answering. "Then they get to see what you look like now."

Jason nodded. He liked that answer better than being told he'd be recognized immediately. He didn't know whether recognition was something people could choose. He hoped affection might be.

The light in the basin spread into a wavering arc. Valeria gave him a moment to move closer. He took one step, then another, and tried to remember to breathe normally.

There were shapes on the other side, indistinct at first. A tall girl with dark hair, a smaller girl with blond hair, a boy with sea-green eyes. Jason's hands went cold. He gripped his page tightly enough to crease it.

He heard someone say his name.

For a moment he forgot every prepared question.

Then the rainbow steadied, and the faces became clear.



---

The Big House rec room had never been so quiet.

Thalia counted heads as they filed in. Herself. Annabeth, clutching a rolled-up piece of paper like a lifeline. Percy, who Chiron had invited with a look that said he knew Percy would sneak in anyway so he might as well be invited. Chiron himself. Mr. D, surprisingly present and surprisingly sober, leaning against the wall with an expression that was almost soft. A few others — Luke, there for moral support; Silena, because she wouldn't let Annabeth go through this alone; Clarisse, because she'd asked Chiron point-blank if she could come and Chiron had been too surprised to say no.

"Remember," Chiron said, pouring a measure of golden drachmas into a pouch, "the connection will be brief and unstable. Speak clearly. Do not touch the image. And be prepared — time moves differently in the relays. What feels like minutes to us may have taken hours to arrange for them."

He tossed a drachma into the mist he'd sprayed from a bottle, and said, "Iris, goddess of the rainbow, show us Camp Jupiter. Show us the wolves' hearth."

The mist shimmered.

For a long moment there was nothing but static — gray and gold swirling, voices half-heard, a flash of marble, a flash of purple. Thalia's heart hammered. Annabeth grabbed her hand again. Percy leaned forward so far Thalia thought he'd fall into the mist.

Then it cleared.

A room came into focus. Stone walls. A banner with SPQR and a golden eagle. A hearth with a small fire. And a cluster of people — adults in purple, a tall woman with a stern face, a centurion with a feathered crest — and in front of them, half-hidden behind the woman's cloak, a small boy.

Thalia stopped breathing.

Jason.

He was ten, all knees and elbows and big blue eyes. His blond hair was cut short in a Roman style, but it stuck up in the back the way it always had when he slept funny. He wore a plain tunic that was a little too big, belted with a cord, and he clutched a small wooden sword like a lifeline. He stared into the mist with his mouth slightly open.

"Thalia?" Chiron said gently. "Annabeth? Go ahead."

Thalia tried to speak and nothing came out. Eight years. Eight years of wondering if he was alive, if he was warm, if anyone sang to him. Eight years of being the big sister who couldn't protect the smallest one. Her throat closed like a fist.

Annabeth stepped forward instead, brave brilliant Annabeth, her voice shaking but clear. "Hi," she said. "Hi, Jason. I'm Annabeth. This is Thalia. And Percy. We're— We're your sisters. And our— our friend."

Jason blinked. He looked at the tall woman, who nodded encouragingly, then back at the mist. "Hi," he said. His voice was high and clear, with a faint Californian lilt underneath the crisp Latin training. "Are you from the Greek place?"

"Yes!" Annabeth laughed, wet and startled. "Camp Half-Blood. On Long Island. It's— It's really green. We have strawberries."

"I like strawberries," Jason offered. He took a half-step closer. The woman's hand stayed on his shoulder. "Are you really my sisters? Lupa said I had sisters. But I don't remember."

Thalia found her voice. It tore coming up. "You were little," Thalia said. "I'm Thalia. I remember carrying you when you got tired. You called me Lia because the first part of my name was too difficult." She made herself stop before asking whether that meant anything to him. "You don't have to prove you remember it. I just wanted you to know I do."

Jason frowned, thinking hard. "Lia," he repeated, testing it. Then, shyly, "I like that."

"I like you," Thalia blurted, and everyone laughed, and Jason smiled, a gap-toothed smile — he'd lost a tooth recently, Thalia saw with a pang — and the static crackled but held.

Percy waved. "I'm Percy! I'm not your brother, but I'm Annabeth's boyfriend and best friend, which is a different kind of family. I live in the Poseidon cabin with my sisters Clarisse and Silena and I can breathe underwater. Can you breathe underwater?"

"No," Jason said, giggling. "Romans can't breathe underwater. We build bridges."

"Good point. Bridges are cool too."

Annabeth unrolled the paper with shaking hands and held it up to the mist. It was a drawing — Annabeth's clean architectural lines showing the Big House and the cabins and the lake, Thalia and Annabeth and Percy as stick figures with labels and too-big smiles, and in careful block letters at the top: FOR JASON — FROM THALIA AND BEFF. Percy had colored the lake blue with such enthusiasm he'd torn the paper in one spot and they'd taped it.

"We made you this," Annabeth said. "So you know what we look like. That's me with the blond hair, that's Thalia with the black hair and the punk bracelet, that's Percy with the stupid hair. We wanted to send it through, if— if the Mist lets us."

The stern woman — someone murmured she was a praetor's aide, or a legacy — stepped forward. "We will try. Hold it to the light."

Annabeth held the drawing toward the rainbow shimmer. For a heart-stopping second nothing happened. Then the colors bled at the edges, gold and gray wrapping around the paper, and the copy in Annabeth's hands went pale and thin while something — a shimmer, a ghost of paper — appeared on the other side. The aide caught it, solidifying it with practiced hands, and handed it to Jason, who held it like treasure.

"I can see it!" Jason breathed. He pointed at each figure. "That's you, that's Lia, that's Percy with stupid hair."

"Hey!" Percy protested, delighted.

The mist flickered. Chiron stepped forward, concerned. "We are losing the connection. Quickly, children."

Thalia's heart lurched. Already? It had been — what, three minutes? Four? She had eight years of things to say and no words for any of them.

"Jason—" She leaned in. "Are you okay? Are they good to you? Do you eat enough? Do you—"

"I'm okay," Jason said earnestly. He held up his wooden sword. "I'm training. I'm in the wolves' group. I can do ten push-ups. And I have a friend named Reyna and she shares her bread when mine burns. And I miss—" He stopped. His lower lip trembled. "Lupa says missing people is okay even if you don't remember them. Is that true?"

Thalia cried. She couldn't help it. Tears spilled over before she could stop them, hot and fast. "Yes," she said fiercely. "Yes, it's true. You can miss us and we'll miss you enough for all of us. We'll miss you so much you'll never run out."

Jason nodded, tears in his own eyes but Roman-straight posture, little shoulders back. "Okay. I can do that."

"Thirty seconds," Chiron murmured.

Annabeth pressed her hand to the edge of the mist, not quite touching. "We love you, Jason. Even across the country. Even if you don't remember. Okay?"

"Okay, Beff," Jason said, trying the nickname Annabeth had explained in a rush. He smiled through tears. "I love you too. Lia. Beff. Percy-with-stupid-hair."

The mist collapsed in a swirl of gold.

Silence.

At Camp Jupiter, Jason stood in front of the empty basin with the folded page still in his hand.

He had used none of his prepared questions. The first thing he'd said when the picture cleared was almost embarrassing in its simplicity: he wanted to know whether the girls were really his sisters. They had answered, and then they'd spent the next few minutes giving him a collection of ordinary facts he hadn't realized he'd been missing.

Thalia liked the sound of the rain against the Zeus cabin. Annabeth built models and corrected the measurements when the drawings were too small. Percy made jokes about his own hair and had sisters who argued with him about chores. The camp had a lake, a strawberry field and a centaur named Chiron who sounded considerably more patient than Jason's drill instructors.

He wanted to ask them everything again.

Valeria came to stand beside him. She didn't tell him to move away from the empty rainbow. She waited until he folded his paper.

"How do you feel?" she asked.

"I don't know."

"That seems reasonable."

He looked at her. "They said I could call them when the camps approve it."

"They did. We can make a request. I can't tell you when it will be granted."

"Will they forget?"

Valeria took a breath before answering. "I don't believe so. They were asking about you long before this conversation happened. But you may write to them if you'd like, and I can help you find out whether there's a safe route for the letter."

He considered the offer. A message would not carry a face or a voice, but it would give him time to choose the words without having to think of everything before a rainbow vanished.

"Can I show them my drawing of the aqueduct?"

"You can ask permission to send a copy. I wouldn't send the original until you're certain it won't get lost."

Jason looked down at his page. He'd drawn a canal route in the margin, trying to imagine what he would show Thalia if she asked whether he liked being Roman. He had been afraid she'd be disappointed that he didn't remember a childhood with her.

She hadn't sounded disappointed. She'd sounded astonished to see him.

"They remembered things I didn't," he said.

"That must have been strange."

"It was. They weren't angry at me."

Valeria looked at him carefully. "Why would they be angry?"

"Because I don't remember calling her Lia. She said I did when I was little."

"Lots of people don't remember things from when they were two."

"I know. But she looked as though it mattered."

"Then it mattered to her. You aren't required to produce the same memory in order to care about her."

Jason turned that over. It sounded like something he'd heard Chiron say through the rainbow. He wondered whether adults on both sides of the country had been trying to tell the siblings a similar thing in different words.

He took a clean sheet from the communications desk and wrote *Dear Lia and Beff* at the top. He hesitated, uncertain whether Annabeth truly wanted to be called by a nickname she didn't remember hearing from him.

"Should I use her actual name?" he asked.

"You can ask what she prefers in the letter."

He wrote the question beneath the greeting.

The first paragraph was awkward. He told them he had seen the rainbow, which was obvious, and that he liked strawberries, which they'd already discussed. Then he started describing the week he'd spent learning to measure distances without relying on a centurion to correct him. The words came more easily because he knew those details for himself.

Valeria offered to take the page for review. He wasn't ready to send it yet.

"Could I finish after supper?" he asked.

"Of course."

He folded it carefully and went to the dining hall. The other trainees asked whether the call had been successful, and he told them he'd spoken to his sisters. He didn't tell them everything they'd said. Some of the conversation belonged to the four people who'd been there.

That evening he returned to the letter. He drew the aqueduct from memory, adding a little bridge where the canal bent toward the training grounds. He labeled the supports so Annabeth could see which part he was proud of. Then he added a question for Thalia about the last time she'd seen a real thunderstorm at camp.

He gave the finished page to Valeria for the authorized mail request. She checked the address and told him the Roman intermediary would have to approve the route. He understood. He was ten, not a senator. He still wanted to know why two camps with children who loved the same people needed so many adults to agree before a letter could travel.

For now, he had spoken to his sisters. They had looked at him as if he mattered. That was more than he'd had that morning, and he intended to remember the sound of their voices.

Thalia walked out of the Big House and kept walking until the night air could hold her.

She ended up behind the cabins, near the edge of the woods where the boundary hummed faint and the pine needles were soft under her boots. She sat down with her back against a tree and cried the way she hadn't cried since she was eight years old and woke up to an empty crib — ugly, shaking, hands over her mouth to keep quiet because she was sixteen and a lieutenant-in-training and big sisters didn't fall apart where anyone could see.

He was alive. He was small and brave and polite to strangers and missing a tooth. He shared bread with a girl named Reyna. He could do ten push-ups. He didn't remember her.

That last one hooked in her ribs and wouldn't come out. She'd told him he didn't have to remember, and she meant it, but gods, it hurt. She'd carried him everywhere for two years. She'd fed him crackers when their mom forgot to shop. She'd slept with him curled against her chest when thunderstorms rattled the windows. And he looked at her like a kind stranger.

Footsteps crunched on pine needles. Thalia wiped her face fast, but it was Annabeth — of course it was Annabeth, who always knew where to find her — with Percy hovering a few feet behind, holding a box of tissues he'd clearly stolen from the Big House and looking awkward and determined.

"Go away," Thalia said, but there was no force in it.

"No," Annabeth said simply, sitting down beside her. Percy sat on her other side after a moment, setting the tissues between them like an offering. "You're crying. I'm staying."

"I'm fine."

"You're lying."

Thalia laughed, wet and broken. "When did you get so bossy?"

"When you taught me to be." Annabeth leaned against her shoulder. She was twelve and too thin and too smart and the best thing Thalia had ever done. "He liked the drawing. He said our names. He smiled with the tooth gone — did you see? He lost a tooth and we missed it. We'll miss all of it. First sword, first— whatever Romans do. Toga day."

"Toga day is not a thing," Percy offered. "I think. Is it?"

"It should be," Annabeth said, and Thalia laughed again, crying at the same time, which felt ridiculous and human.

"He was so small, Annie," Thalia whispered. "He's supposed to be— I keep thinking he'll be two forever in my head. Chubby cheeks and Beff with an F. And then he was just— this kid. This brave little Roman kid who doesn't need me."

"He needs you," Annabeth said fiercely. "He just needs you differently. He needs to know we're here. That someone across the country knows his real laugh, not his soldier face. Did you see how straight he stood? He's ten and he already stands like that. Someone needs to tell him it's okay to be silly sometimes. That's us."

Percy nodded. "And he definitely needs to know the story about him eating your hair. That's critical intelligence."

Thalia wiped her eyes. "He ate your sock once. The whole sock. Mom freaked out and you cried because you thought he'd die and then he spit it out and laughed."

Annabeth stared. "What? I don't remember that."

"You were three. You'd just learned to match socks and you were so proud and he gummed up your favorite one." Thalia smiled at the memory, clear as glass. "You drew him a new sock. With crayons. On paper. So he'd have a spare."

Annabeth laughed through tears. "That's the dumbest sweetest thing I've ever done."

"You were a dumb sweet kid. You still are."

"Hey!"

Percy raised his hand. "I have a story too. It's about Jason. It's from tonight. It's about how Jason told me Romans build bridges and I realized he's smarter than me at ten. That's my Jason story. I'm keeping it."

Thalia looked at him, this sea-green-eyed boy who'd wedged himself into their family with pancakes and stupid jokes and absolute loyalty, and felt her heart crack open in a good way.

"That's a good story," she said softly.

They sat there until the crickets got loud and the tissues were mostly used and the tears stopped. Above them the sky was huge and starry, no clouds, the gods conspicuously absent and the night entirely theirs.

The next morning, the drawing table in Cabin One was covered in paper.

Silena had brought colored pencils from the Aphrodite cabin ("We have the best art supplies, it's a fact, don't argue"). Clarisse had brought a slightly squashed box of crackers ("For brain power. My brothers always wanted snacks when they did homework. Shut up, it's relevant."). Travis and Connor had contributed glitter glue they swore they'd obtained legally. Even Mr. D had wandered by, looked at the mess, and left a fresh pack of cardstock without a word.

Annabeth, Thalia, and Percy sat in the middle of it, making a second picture to send whenever the next relay happened — Chiron had promised to try again in a month, maybe two, if the gods cooperated.

This one was bigger. Annabeth drew Camp Half-Blood in careful detail — the Big House with its bronze eagle weather vane, the cabins in a horseshoe, the lake with waves. Thalia drew three figures in front, much better than stick people this time — herself with her spiky black hair and silver bracelet, Annabeth with her gray eyes and Yankees cap, Percy with his messy black hair and Riptide. Percy drew Jason in the corner from memory, gap-toothed smile and wooden sword, with a speech bubble that said HI! And Silena, with an artist's flourish, added strawberries everywhere, big and red and cheerful.

Clarisse leaned over, frowning in concentration. "So he's going to be a soldier? Like, a real Roman soldier with armor and stuff?"

"Eventually," Thalia said. "If he stays. If he survives the training."

"That's messed up," Clarisse said bluntly. "He's ten."

"I know." Thalia's voice was tight, but she kept coloring.

Clarisse was quiet for a moment, then said, "My brothers are idiots, but if someone took them when they were two I'd burn the world down. Just so you know. You're handling this better than I would."

Thalia looked up, surprised. "Thanks, Clarisse."

"Yeah, well." Clarisse cleared her throat, embarrassed. "Can I— Can I add something? To the picture?"

Annabeth handed her a pencil. Clarisse bent over the corner and drew, tongue out, a small spear next to Jason's sword. Underneath, in blocky letters: FOR PROTECTION. FROM CLARISSE.

"He doesn't know me," Clarisse muttered, "but if he's your little brother he's camp's little brother. Tell him that next time. Tell him he has backup."

Silena added a flowing banner across the top: WE MISS YOU, JASON! COME VISIT WHEN YOU'RE TALLER THAN THALIA! Thalia protested that wouldn't take long, everyone laughed, and for a moment Cabin One felt too small to hold all the warmth in it.

Percy watched Annabeth work, her face calm and focused, graphite on her cheek again. He thought about missing childhoods and tectonic plates and rooms you couldn't open. He didn't have answers for that. He was twelve. He was bad at drawing and okay at sword-fighting and excellent at caring about people.

But he could do this. He could sit here and color the lake blue without tearing the paper this time and make sure Annabeth ate lunch and remind Thalia to breathe. He could remember Jason's gap-toothed smile and keep that story safe.

Later, when the picture was done and propped up to dry, Annabeth walked down to the lake with Percy. The sun was setting, turning the water gold and pink. Somewhere behind them, dinner was starting, laughter drifting from the pavilion.

"Do you think he'll remember next time?" Annabeth asked quietly. "Not the old stuff. Just — us from last night. Do you think ten-year-olds remember Iris-messages?"

"I remember things from when I was seven," Percy said. "I remember Sally making blue cookies when I was sad in my original childhood. I remember the exact smell of the ocean at Montauk. I remember deciding I hated olives." He grinned. "He'll remember. How could he forget Percy-with-stupid-hair?"

Annabeth smiled, leaning against him. "Thank you for being there. For both of us."

"Always," Percy said. It was a big word for a twelve-year-old, but he meant it with his whole chest. "You're my family too. You and Thalia. And Jason-by-extension. That's how it works."

Annabeth was quiet, and Percy thought she might be crying again, but when she looked up her gray eyes were clear.

"In my memories, I left Bobby and Matthew," she said softly. "In this childhood, Jason was taken and Thalia couldn't stop it. Different boys, different losses. But last night — when he said my name — it felt like maybe I get to find my brother here. Slowly. Drawing by drawing."

"You will," Percy promised. "We'll send so many drawings the Romans will think it's an invasion. A paper invasion. They won't know what hit them."

Annabeth laughed, and the sound carried across the water, bright and young and hopeful. Above them, the sky deepened to violet. Somewhere across the country, under the same sky but different stars, a ten-year-old boy with a gap-toothed smile had a drawing of three kids and a lake and strawberries everywhere, learning the names Lia and Beff by heart.

Annabeth watched Thalia write **LIA** in large letters beneath the drawing, in case Jason had forgotten the nickname by the next call. Then she wrote **BEFF** beside it, deliberately misspelled.

"You're really keeping that?" Percy asked.

"Obviously."

They left the picture drying on the desk overnight. In the morning Chiron would help them find out whether another copy could reach the Roman camp without a rainbow.

At the mail office, Thalia learned that there was a difference between sending a drawing to Camp Jupiter and getting permission for the Roman camp to receive it.

The distinction had not seemed important when she was sitting at the cabin desk imagining Jason holding the picture. It mattered now, with the courier explaining which routes were open and which were only proposed.

"We can submit a request," the steward said. "Chiron will certify who the senders are. But the Roman intermediary must approve it. I won't tell you it's delivered until we have confirmation."

"How long will that take?"

"I don't know."

Thalia wanted to ask for a faster route, then stopped. She had spent months treating every delay as proof the people in charge didn't understand what it was like to be separated from family. Some probably didn't. That wasn't a reason to turn an uncertain promise into a false one.

"Then can I keep the original?" she asked.

"Yes. Send a copy. The original is yours."

Annabeth stood beside her with the rolled drawing. She had been planning to send the original because the lines looked better on the first page, and now she was relieved not to risk losing it in transit.

"We can copy it with the same scale," she said. "The paint might look different, but Jason will see the design."

Thalia nodded. The steward gave them a blank form and asked them to return it when they'd made the copy.

Back in Cabin One, Annabeth spread the paper across the desk and started tracing the outlines. Thalia helped hold the edges flat. She had told Jason she'd write soon. She hadn't decided what to say beyond that.

"Do you think he needs a letter from me too?" she asked.

Annabeth looked up. "I think he'd like one. But you don't need to describe a whole childhood before you can say hello."

Thalia considered the suggestion. She had wanted Jason to know how much she'd missed him. In the rainbow he'd looked like a boy who was proud of his Roman training and nervous about whether his sisters liked him. Perhaps he'd rather hear about the camp's ordinary activities than receive a list of years they couldn't get back.

She began with the weather.

*Dear Jason, It rained yesterday, and Annabeth tried to tell me how the cabin roofs should be built so the water doesn't collect by the gutters. She was right, which is annoying. Percy helped make a new sign for the lake path. Clarisse says you should learn a good shield grip because Roman shields are too heavy if you hold them like a flag.*

Annabeth read the last sentence and laughed.

"Clarisse didn't say that."

"She said something similar."

"That doesn't mean you can make her quote more sensible."

Thalia crossed out the attribution and wrote the shield comment as a suggestion from the older campers. She didn't want Jason to believe every letter from home was a formal report. She also didn't want to turn other people's words into things they'd never said.

She finished with a question: *What's your favorite class? You can tell us about something you're proud of. It doesn't have to be anything to do with Zeus.*

Annabeth looked at that line for a long moment.

"Keep it," she said.

"Why?"

"Because I know what it's like when people only want to know what your powers do."

Thalia knew the feeling. Their father had a talent for turning every ordinary achievement into proof that a child resembled him. She didn't want to do that to Jason.

They signed the letter separately. Annabeth added a short note about the drawing and asked whether he had a scale he used when sketching the aqueducts. She was careful not to write as if they had already agreed to a visit. They hadn't. They could ask, and they could hope, but Jason was living in a camp with rules and people whose consent mattered too.

When they returned to the office, the steward recorded both the picture and the letter as pending dispatch. Thalia checked the receipt and made sure the copied drawing would be returned if the route were refused.

"That's everything," she said.

"Everything we can do today," Annabeth corrected.

Thalia looked at her sister and almost argued. Then she smiled.

"Yes. Everything we can do today."

They left with the original picture under Annabeth's arm and a copy of the receipt in Thalia's jacket. The uncertainty followed them. It no longer made every step feel like another opportunity they'd missed.

At the dining pavilion, Percy was waiting with three plates. He didn't ask whether the letter had gone through. He asked whether the steward had accepted the packet for processing, a distinction he'd learned while writing to Amphitrite.

"Yes," Thalia said. "Accepted. Not delivered."

"Good. Then we have a record."

She sat down and began eating. Somewhere across the country, Jason was living through an ordinary morning, perhaps doing lessons or arguing with a centurion about the weight of a shield. Thalia wanted to be there. She couldn't. She could still write him a letter that described something true, and she had.

After lunch she passed the arena where the younger campers were practicing basic shield forms. She stopped at the fence. A small boy was having trouble holding his shield level, and the instructor had offered him a lighter one while he learned the movement. Thalia imagined Jason in the same position, determined not to ask for help because he wanted the older trainees to think he belonged.

She had told herself for years that her job was to protect her little brother. At this distance, the only protection she could offer was knowing that somebody was looking after him, asking whether he had the things he needed, and making sure her own hopes didn't become orders for him to follow.

The boy at the fence caught her watching.

"Am I doing it right?" he asked.

"You're moving your feet before the shield," she said. "The instructor will show you how to keep both together."

He tried again. Thalia watched until he made the correction, then continued toward the cabin. She would probably think about Jason every time she saw a younger camper practicing. Today she'd spoken to him directly and learned he had Roman teachers who corrected his stance before letting him carry heavier shields. The pending drawing and letters still needed dispatch authorization, but they were no longer an answer to a question about whether he was even alive. That wasn't something she needed to stop feeling. She could think about him without turning the memory into another reason to demand an answer the Roman camp wasn't ready to give. She could write again next week, ask Chiron what he had learned, and make sure Annabeth had room to build her own relationship with their brother.
