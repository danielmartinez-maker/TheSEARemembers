## Chapter 194: A Place at the Table

Percy washed the last of Sunday's dishes before breakfast on Monday. The job did not need a plan.

He found himself at Cabin Three’s washbasin with his sleeves rolled twice above his wrists, a towel over one shoulder and cold water running down the backs of his hands. There was a stack of bowls beside him, three plates on the drying board, and a cup turned upside down in a patch of weak morning sun. Someone had left a spoon in the cocoa pot. He scraped the last of the dark sweetness into the sink, rinsed the pot, and heard Clarisse behind him.

“You’re doing that wrong.”

He stopped with the sponge halfway across the bottom. “I’m washing a pot.”

“You’re wearing your good shirt.”

“It’s a shirt.”

“It’s the only one you don’t smell like the stove in.” She picked up the towel and dropped it across his back. “You’re going to be in the laundry again.”

“Good morning to you too.”

“Good morning. Put your elbows in.”

She could have done it herself. Clarisse could have taken the pot from his hands and scoured it clean in half the time. She did not. Percy watched her cross the cabin to where the list lay on the table, where Silena had copied its lines onto a fresh sheet after the original became soft at the corners. Bent fence. Soil banking. Awning. Stool. Rail. Frost check. The words were small and neat, but there was no neatness in the work they named.

Clarisse drew a mark beside “rail.”

“Rhys brought tools,” Percy said. “He said he’d fix it today.”

“He said he’d bring somebody with a screwdriver.”

“Did you hear him say that?”

“No.” She ran her finger under the line. “He is somebody with a screwdriver.”

“That’s true.”

She turned the paper so the list faced him. “You’re on the fence with me after breakfast. Silena’s doing soil banking if the ground’s still soft enough. You can wash your shirt tonight.”

“I can do two things.”

“You can, but you’ll do one properly if I give you one.”

“Have you considered that I might be capable of laundry and fence repair on the same day?”

“I have. I decided the fence is more important.”

At the far end of the cabin, Silena was tying her hair back in front of the small mirror nailed to the post. She had changed into an old green sweater, and the sleeves hung past her knuckles. “If he ruins the shirt, he gets your old one.”

“I don’t have an old one.”

“You have a blue one with a hole under the arm.”

“That’s not old. It’s a shirt with a hole under the arm.”

Clarisse looked at him. “You want to argue about fabric all morning?”

“No.”

“Then eat when I say it’s ready.”

Percy rinsed the pot until the water ran clear. The cabin was warm at the stove and cold beside the door. At some point someone had laid a folded blanket over the crack in the bottom frame, and the heat held in a little better than it had last week. He was still learning which places in this life were familiar to everyone else and new to him. Cabin Three smelled like laundry soap, iron, and the shoreline after rain. His body knew how to tie his boots without looking, and his mind still noticed that the boots were arranged by a family who expected him to come back at night.

He set the pot upside down on the board.

“Where’s Annabeth?” Silena asked.

“Cabin One. She said she was going to help Thalia write down what Chiron told her about the splint.”

Clarisse’s attention flicked toward him. “You’re going to apologize today.”

It wasn’t a question. Percy wiped his hands on the towel. “Yeah.”

“Good.”

He looked at her. “That’s all?”

“What else do you want?”

“I don’t know. A speech? A demonstration?”

“I’m not your moral instructor.” She folded the list once and put it back on the table. “You embarrassed her. You know it. Say that. Don’t explain what you thought you were doing until she asks.”

Silena had gone still at the mirror. She met his eyes in its reflection. “And don’t ask her to make you feel better afterward.”

“I’m not going to.”

“You say that now.”

“I mean it.”

“Then you’ll be fine.” Silena reached for the door. “We’re leaving in ten minutes. If the fence is worse than it looked yesterday, I want somebody to fetch Chiron before we touch it.”

Clarisse nodded. “I’ll look first.”

“I can do that,” Percy said.

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because I’ll look first.”

“Sure.”

“Do you need a chart?”

“I could use one.”

Clarisse left the cabin. Silena followed after giving Percy a look that meant she had heard the joke and would not be rewarding it. He hung the towel on its nail, pulled his cuffs down and found the blue shirt with the hole under the arm. He did not put it on. The green wool itched at his throat, but he kept it.

Across the path, Cabin One had a loose porch rail and a small square of empty space where the blanket had not quite covered the window. Percy carried the field book Annabeth had left in Cabin Three the day before. It was tucked beneath a folded cloth and had a graphite smudge across the cover. He had not meant to bring it; he had meant to put it on the table and then had picked it up when he saw her name.

The rail shifted under his hand when he touched it. It had not become safer in the night.

Annabeth opened the door before he could knock.

“You’re early,” she said.

“I brought your book.”

“You could have left it with Silena.”

“I could have.”

She took it, looked at the cover as if checking it still belonged to her, and tucked it under one arm. “How did you sleep?”

“Fine.”

“That’s the word you use when you don’t want to tell me.”

“I slept okay.”

“Better.”

“Did you?”

“Mostly.” She looked past him toward the path, where Clarisse was waiting by the flagstones. “Thalia’s already had breakfast. Rhys is going to bring someone to look at the rail. She has the foot up.”

“Is it worse?”

“Still hurts. It was a bad idea to try to walk to the door before she got the splint on.”

“I’m not going to—”

“I know.” Annabeth’s voice softened, but she did not smile. “You can ask her how she’s doing. You don’t get to make a plan for her before she answers.”

“I know that too.”

“Then we’re both learning something.”

She shifted the field book beneath her arm. Percy glanced at the porch, then back at her. “Can I talk to her now?”

“That’s up to her.”

“I figured.”

“I’ll ask.”

Annabeth stepped inside and left the door open. Percy waited where he was. He could hear a pencil tap twice against wood, then Thalia’s voice, flat with morning.

“If he comes up here to apologize, I’ll hear him. If he comes up here to inspect my ankle, he can go back to Cabin Three.”

“He said he wants to talk,” Annabeth replied.

There was a pause.

“Then he can come in.”

The permission did not make the path shorter. Percy went up the porch steps slowly and stepped around the loose rail. Thalia sat at the table with her heel resting on a folded blanket atop a chair. The splint had a clean wrap around it. Her face looked tired in the way it did when she had spent half the night trying to ignore the part of herself that knew she should stop. Annabeth sat beside the window with the book open to a page of measurements. Neither of them moved to make room for him.

Percy stayed near the door. “Is now still okay?”

Thalia tilted her head. “You’re in the room.”

“I mean, do you want me to say it now?”

“I said I’d hear it.”

“Okay.” He put his hands in the pockets of his trousers. There was a pencil in the left one and a small piece of rope in the right. He took neither out. “I’m sorry I put you on the spot at the shooting line. I saw you draw and decided I had to stop you, but I did it in front of everybody. I made you answer for your foot when you’d already been taking care of it and following Chiron’s instructions. I was worried. That doesn’t give me the right to make you prove anything to me.”

Thalia watched him. She did not nod.

“And I shouldn’t have let Annabeth’s worry turn into me deciding I was allowed to step in. She said she was scared for you. She didn’t ask me to corner you in public.”

Annabeth turned one page in the book. “I did say I wanted you to talk to her.”

“I know,” Percy said. “I was already wrong before you said that. You didn’t tell me to do it that way.”

“Good distinction.”

He looked back at Thalia. “I’m sorry.”

The words were smaller than the room. They did not carry an argument behind them or a request for a verdict. He had rehearsed other versions on the walk up the hill, and all of them had come with the hidden hope that Thalia would say he had been trying to help. This one had nothing useful to offer her except the part that was true.

Thalia leaned back in the chair. Her fingers were wrapped around a mug that had gone cold. “You’re not wrong that I shouldn’t have been trying to shoot from that angle.”

“I know.”

“You’re not wrong that the heel hurt.”

“I know.”

“And I did tell everyone I could manage it when I couldn’t.”

“You don’t have to justify the way you handled that to make me apologize for what I did.”

“I’m not justifying it.” Her eyes narrowed. “I’m telling you why it bothered me when you said it. If you want to be my friend, you don’t get to make me the lesson in front of a crowd and then call it concern.”

“I won’t.”

“You might. You’re stubborn.”

“I’ll try not to.”

“That’s more honest.”

The door stayed open behind him. A draft pressed at his back, smelling of cold leaves and wood smoke. He wanted to ask whether she forgave him. He wanted to ask whether she had slept, whether the heel throbbed, whether the splint was comfortable. He wanted the familiar motion of solving something together. Instead he waited.

After a while, Thalia let her hands settle in her lap. “I appreciate the apology.”

“Okay.”

“That isn’t the same thing as saying I’m over it.”

“I understand.”

“I don’t know when I will be.”

“You don’t owe me a schedule.”

“Good. Because I don’t have one.”

He nodded. A different sort of relief moved through him, one that did not feel like permission. “If you need anything from Cabin Three—”

“Ask me first.”

“Right.”

“And don’t send Annabeth to tell me you’re worried.”

Annabeth looked up. “He didn’t.”

“Not this time.” Thalia shifted the mug. “I’m making it a rule.”

“That’s fair,” Percy said.

“Also, I decide Tuesday. You don’t ask me whether I’m swimming every time you see me.”

“I won’t ask.”

“Good.”

He looked at the splint once, then made himself look at her face. “I’ll leave you alone.”

“You can still say good morning when you see me.”

“I think I can manage that.”

“Barely.”

“Barely is where I do my best work.”

“That explains a lot.”

A corner of her mouth moved. It wasn’t forgiveness; it wasn’t a rejection either. Percy knew better now than to name it for her. He backed toward the porch. Annabeth closed the field book.

“Are you going to the fence?” she asked.

“Clarisse says I’m assigned.”

“I’m sure she’s open to negotiation.”

“She isn’t.”

“That’s what I thought.”

Percy looked at Thalia once more. “Good morning.”

“Morning, Seaweed Brain.”

He stepped outside.

Annabeth followed him to the porch and put the field book under her arm again. “That was good.”

“I didn’t ask for a grade.”

“You didn’t ask if she forgave you either.”

“I was tempted.”

“I know.” She gave him a small, crooked smile. “I’m glad you didn’t.”

“Are you going to keep the book?”

“I’m going to use it. That’s what it’s for.”

He reached for her hand. She gave it to him, and he held it for the length of one breath. There was no audience, no lesson, no urgent problem that needed to be settled before breakfast. The porch rail shifted a fraction beneath his palm.

“You know,” he said, “I could fix that today.”

“You could also ask Rhys before stealing his job.”

“It’s a rail.”

“It’s his rail today.”

“Fine.”

She squeezed his fingers and let go. “Go help Clarisse.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“Don’t start.”

He left before she could throw the pencil at him.

Clarisse was at the fence by the time Percy reached the slope. She had brought two mallets, a coil of wire and a short stack of boards. The boards were warped at one end from the rain, and the fence looked worse in daylight. The middle rail bowed inward where the pine butt had struck it. Two posts leaned toward the path. Someone had tied a bright strip of cloth to the closest one so no one would mistake the sag for a gap they could step through.

Clarisse crouched and pressed a thumb against the nearer post.

“Still moving,” Percy said.

“I have eyes.”

“I know.”

“Then why did you say it?”

“Conversation.”

“I don't need conversation from you. I need you to hold this board.”

He took the board. The end caught against his sleeve. “Where?”

“Here.” She tapped the fence line. “Not on the old rail. Against it. We're bracing until Chiron checks whether the post's split.”

“I thought we were repairing it.”

“We're inspecting it. If we pull the wrong thing loose, the whole side comes down.”

Clarisse stood and tested the post with her shoulder, gently. The ground around it had softened overnight, and the wood moved in a small, alarming way. Percy set the board where she had indicated.

“Do you want me to get Chiron now?” he asked.

“I want you to stop asking me what to do after I told you what to do.”

“Got it.”

She took the mallet from his hand and held it out to him by the handle. “You can knock the temporary brace in.”

He lowered the mallet carefully. It was cold and heavier than it looked. Clarisse stood back far enough that he wouldn't hit her. Percy swung once and the board shuddered against the dirt. The second blow sank it a little farther.

“Not hard,” Clarisse said. “Straight.”

“I know how to hit a stake.”

“You know how to hit things. It's not the same.”

“There's no need to be so encouraging.”

She checked the angle, then put two fingers against the board. “Again.”

Percy lifted the mallet. He had fought monsters with a sword, wrestled with Titans, and watched a sea god pick up the world. The temporary brace did not care. If he gave it too much force, the wooden end would split. He tried again, keeping the swing low. The mallet landed where it should.

“Better,” she said.

“High praise.”

“Don't get used to it.”

The work went slowly. They inspected the other post, found one nail pulled partly out and left it alone, then walked the line to see whether the fence had shifted elsewhere. Clarisse measured the bend with the width of her hand and made a mark in the dirt. Percy held the board, brought her the wire, and did not suggest a solution until she asked. He got used to the silence between instructions. The fence did not need either of them to explain why it mattered. The open space on the north edge was steep, the ground slick. A child could see the lake and mistake the slope for a shortcut.

Silena crossed from the greenhouse with mud on her boots. She had a small wooden crate balanced against her hip. It was full of folded burlap and a jar with a red lid.

“Do you need something?” she asked.

“Chiron,” Clarisse said. “The post's shifting. I don't want us to move the rail until he sees it.”

“I'll get him.” Silena put the crate down. “The soil is still soft enough. I think I'll bank it this morning before the rain comes.”

“Did Chiron say we were getting rain?”

“No. The air smells wet.”

“That isn't the same thing.”

“I know. That's why I said I think.”

Clarisse nodded. Silena picked up the crate and turned toward the Big House, then stopped at the sound of a path lantern swinging. From the hill above them came the irregular knock of a walking stick and a second sound, lighter, like someone walking in boots that had not been made for mud.

The satyr came into view first. He was young enough that his horns still looked smooth at the base, with a green knit cap pulled over his curls and a brown coat open at the chest. A messenger bag bounced at his side. Beside him walked a boy Percy had never seen.

The boy was tall, perhaps a little taller than Percy by a head, with dark hair tied at the nape and a navy coat buttoned to the throat. A canvas duffel hung from one shoulder. He wore clean boots and a salt-stiff scarf. His face had the pale, controlled look of someone trying not to show how tired he was. He took in the slope, the broken fence, Clarisse's mallet and the mud on Silena's boots.

His eyes stayed longest on the fence.

“This is Camp Half-Blood,” the satyr said, though they were already standing inside its boundary. “I'm sorry for bringing you in the long way. The south trail was blocked by a fallen branch.”

“You said there was a gate,” the boy replied.

“There is.”

“Then why didn't we use it?”

“The gate is on the south side.”

“That's a strange place for a gate.”

“It was there before I got here.”

The satyr spotted Percy and Clarisse, and his shoulders lowered with recognition. “Clarisse. Percy. This is Leandros. He needs to see Chiron.”

Clarisse glanced once at the fence and then at the boy. “He can wait. The post is moving.”

The satyr blinked. “Of course. I just thought—”

“It's safe here,” Percy said. “We have to keep the rail from falling.”

“I can wait,” Leandros said. His voice had the clipped certainty of someone offering a favor instead of taking one. He leaned his duffel against the fence, then looked at the sagging rail. “You could wedge the bottom of that post.”

“We have,” Clarisse said.

“Not enough.”

“You've been here for thirty seconds.”

“I can see the base is moving.”

“So can we.”

He looked from her to Percy. “Then why are you hammering a brace onto the rail instead of sinking a second post?”

“We don't know whether the old one is split,” Clarisse said. “A second post might pull against it.”

“That sounds like a reason to check.”

“It is. We are checking.”

“By hammering another board into it?”

“By holding the weight until Chiron gets here.”

Leandros lifted his chin. “If you know what you're doing.”

Clarisse straightened. “I do.”

The satyr looked down at his hooves. “Leandros, they’re helping.”

“I didn't say they weren't.”

“You said they don't know what they're doing.”

“I said if.”

“That's not better,” Percy said.

Leandros turned toward him. “You always speak for her?”

“No.”

“You did.”

Clarisse reached for the mallet. “Percy, the wire.”

He handed it over. Leandros watched the exchange, a faint frown settling between his brows as if he had expected Percy to keep talking. Clarisse looped the wire around the brace and twisted it tight.

“Who are you?” he asked Percy.

“Percy.”

“Poseidon's son?”

Percy's fingers closed around the end of the wire. “Yeah.”

“And she?”

“Clarisse,” she said. “Also Poseidon's daughter.”

“Right.” He studied her again, though there had been no information in his first look he hadn't already seen. “I thought the camp's Poseidon children would be… different.”

“Different from what?”

“Better equipped.”

Clarisse tugged the wire until it stopped slipping. “You think we look poor?”

“I think you're fixing a fence with scrap wood.”

“That's what the fence needs.”

“Not what I meant.”

“It is what you said.”

The satyr reached for the duffel. “I'll carry that for you.”

“I can carry my own bag.”

“You've been walking since sunrise.”

“I can still carry it.”

Percy recognized the way Leandros's shoulders stayed tight as a drawn bow. He had seen it in himself when people tried to carry his gear or answer for him. The resemblance made it harder to decide whether to be annoyed or to give him room.

Silena looked at the mud on her boots and then at Leandros's clean ones. “Do you know the trail from the road?”

“Callum brought me.”

“I meant before the trail.”

“No.”

“Then you don't know what the mud is like in the rain.”

“I can see what mud is.”

“Can you?” Clarisse asked.

“Don't start,” Percy said.

“I'm not starting anything. He said we aren't equipped. The ground's not a fancy shoe test.”

“It's all right,” Leandros said, but he looked as if it wasn't. “I don't need you to defend me.”

“I wasn't.”

The satyr tapped the side of his bag. “I'll go get Chiron. Leandros, stay here. Please.”

Leandros looked at the fence once more. “I can stay.”

Callum trotted up the slope toward the Big House. The boy leaned against the duffel but did not take off his coat. Percy could see the tension in his jaw when he swallowed. He had a small cut across one knuckle, dried blood under the nail.

“Do you want to sit?” Percy asked.

“No.”

“There's a bench by the path.”

“I see it.”

“You've been walking since sunrise.”

“I can stand.”

“You don't have to prove you can.”

Leandros's eyes narrowed. “Is that how people here talk to each other? By deciding what the other person is trying to prove?”

“Sometimes.”

“Then I think I’d rather stand.”

“Okay.”

Clarisse worked the wire into a tight twist. The fence groaned in the wind and held. She picked up the mallet again.

“You should stop while we wait,” Percy said.

“Why?”

“Because Chiron has to look at it anyway.”

“I can keep pressure on this side.”

“It's already braced.”

“Then I can check the next post.”

“You told me to hold this board.”

“And now I’m not telling you that.”

Percy stepped aside. Clarisse moved down the fence line, leaving him with Leandros and the duffel. Silena picked up her crate again.

“Come see the greenhouse when you're done with Chiron,” she said to Leandros. “The path's dry on the west side.”

“I won't be here long.”

“No one said you would.”

She set off without waiting for his answer. He watched her go, then faced Percy.

“Do you all live in the same cabin?”

“Me, Clarisse and Silena do.”

“And you take orders from her?”

“Sometimes.”

“She’s older?”

“No.”

“Then why—”

“Because she knows the fence.”

“Is she in charge?”

“Not in a way you're going to like.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means if you need an answer, ask her. If you need a task, Chiron gives those out. If you want to argue about who belongs at the table, you can save it.”

“I didn't say anything about a table.”

“Not yet.”

He looked away. In the wind, the scarf moved at the edge of his coat. His hands were red around the fingers. Percy noticed that the duffel had been patched twice with careful stitches, one strip of fabric in a different shade of blue. The bag did not match the coat. The coat did not match the boots. Not rich, then, or not in the simple way Percy had first assumed. Still, Leandros stood with the posture of someone who would rather be disliked than pitied.

“You said Poseidon’s your father,” Percy said.

“I asked if you were his son.”

“You knew his name?”

“My mother told me.”

“What did she say?”

“Enough.”

“Enough to send you here?”

“She didn't send me. Callum said I had to leave.”

“I meant your mom.”

“She wanted me safe. She packed the bag.” He nudged it with the toe of his boot. “She told me what this place was. It’s easier to leave if you know what you’re leaving for.”

“Did you have to leave fast?”

Leandros looked at him. “Do you ask everybody questions like this?”

“Only when they’re bleeding.”

“I’m not bleeding.”

“You've got blood on your hand.”

“It's a cut.”

“Okay.”

“I don't need help.”

“Okay.”

A bell rang in the distance, and Leandros turned toward it. It wasn't time for breakfast; the sound came from the Big House, one long, low note. Chiron used it when he wanted the senior campers to gather.

Clarisse came back along the fence and passed the mallet to Percy. “Don't move the rail.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

She gave him a look and left toward the hill. Callum returned with Chiron a few moments later. The centaur came down the path, his shirt collar fastened, a wool wrap around his shoulders. His gaze went first to the fence, then to the new boy.

“Leandros,” he said. “Welcome. I’m Chiron.”

“I know who you are.”

“How old are you?”

“Sixteen.”

“Your mother has written to me.”

Leandros’s expression shifted, a small crack in his certainty. “She did?”

“She asked that you be kept safe and that you be allowed to write to her as often as you wish. I will keep the letter private.”

“She wrote all that?”

“She was thorough.”

Leandros looked down at his bandaged knuckle. “The monster followed me to the school marina twice. You can tell them that. Not what it looked like.”

“I will respect that.”

“My mother drove me to the bus station. She waited until Callum was sure the trail was clear.”

“That was a sensible choice.”

“She didn't want me to leave.”

“But she chose your safety.”

“I'm sixteen. I can choose too.”

“You can. She made sure you could choose safely.”

“Did she say anything about me?”

“She said you are resourceful and stubborn.”

“Those aren't the same thing.”

“No,” Chiron said. “But they can appear in the same person.”

Leandros looked toward the Big House, then at Percy. “Do you need to tell me about this place?”

“We can do so over food,” Chiron said. “You’ve had breakfast?”

“No.”

“Then we’ll start there.” Chiron turned to Clarisse. “Can you leave the fence for now?”

“If we leave it unbraced, the next gust might pull it apart.”

“You’ve braced it well. I’ll examine the post and take over.”

Clarisse handed him the list, not the mallet. “The whole run is bowed. We didn't touch the rail.”

“Good judgment.”

“Percy did what I said.”

“Congratulations,” Chiron said.

“Don’t congratulate him yet. He'll hear it.”

“I heard it,” Percy said.

Clarisse started up the hill. At the last post, she glanced back. Her eyes met Leandros’s. He gave her a tight nod, as if the conversation between them had been concluded by someone else. She didn’t nod back.

Chiron examined the brace while Leandros stood with his duffel. Percy picked up the bag. Leandros reached for it at once.

“I can carry it.”

“I'm not taking it. I'm bringing it uphill.”

“I can bring it.”

“I know.”

“Then give it back.”

Percy held it out. Leandros accepted the strap. The canvas smelled faintly of salt and cedar.

Chiron kept pace with them up the hill, not asking Percy to carry the bag again. Leandros held it himself, though the strap seemed to cut into his shoulder. At the top, the camp opened around them: the dining pavilion, the cabins, the line of the lake beyond the trees. A few campers were gathering in the grass with straw practice swords. One boy had a brace of arrows on his back and was attempting to keep them from tangling in a length of wool scarf.

Leandros glanced at the blue cabins first. “Which one is Poseidon’s?”

“Cabin Three,” Percy said. “Near the lake.”

“Of course.”

“What does that mean?”

“Nothing.”

“You said it like it means something.”

“I meant it’s near water.”

“Most things at camp are near water.”

“Then it’s a bad use of space.”

Percy didn’t know why the comment irritated him. It was a cabin. It sat where someone had put it. He had no memory of choosing the room or the path or the angle of the porch toward the lake. Still, the place was his in a way that made it hard to hear someone describe it as a mistake.

“Maybe you should see it before you decide that,” Chiron said.

Leandros shifted the bag higher. “I thought we were going to eat.”

“We are.”

The dining pavilion stood open on all sides. The roof was dark from last night’s damp, and the tables were arranged so the center aisle faced the hearth. There were bowls on the serving counter, a basket of bread, and a pan of roasted vegetables wrapped in a clean cloth to keep the steam in. On another table, someone had set out a small crock of apple butter and a plate of flat cakes left from breakfast. The meal was warm but not grand: broth with barley, carrots, a little shredded chicken, and herbs that made Percy think of the winter kitchen at home, though he could not remember having eaten this exact soup before.

That was the trick of this life. Something could feel familiar without being remembered.

A girl with a yellow scarf set out three bowls. “Chiron, the pot’s still hot.”

“Thank you, Katie.”

“Do you need another place?”

“We do,” Chiron said.

The girl brought a fourth bowl without asking who it was for. Leandros watched her cross to the table.

“Is there a separate section for newcomers?” he asked.

“No,” Chiron said. “Choose a seat.”

“Is that how it works?”

“Yes.”

“Do you let everyone pick where they want?”

“We try.”

Leandros looked over the room. Campers sat by cabin group and by whoever they had been speaking to that morning. There were no name cards or assigned chairs. He pointed to a table near the hearth. “That one.”

“Fine,” Chiron said.

He sat with the bag beside his leg and folded his hands on the table. The newcomers’ bench was empty, so the empty place he chose was next to one of the older campers who had been listening. The boy shifted to make room, said his name was Connor, and returned to his bread.

Percy sat opposite Leandros. Clarisse came in a moment later, her palms red from the mallet handle. She dropped into the place to Percy’s right and passed him a tin of balm without looking at him.

“Your hands,” she said.

He checked them. There was a small red mark on his thumb where he had held the wire too tightly. “Thanks.”

“Not the cut. The raw place.”

“I see it.”

“Use it before it splits.”

Percy rubbed a little of the balm into his skin. Across the table, Leandros watched them.

“You give each other chores?” he asked.

“She gave me a task,” Percy said.

“Because you can’t decide what to do?”

“Because we were fixing the fence.”

“Wouldn’t the person who knows the most decide?”

“Clarisse did.”

“Why?”

“She was here.”

Clarisse looked at him. “You want to keep going?”

“No.”

“I didn't think so.”

Silena arrived with a bowl in each hand. She set one in front of Clarisse and one by Percy, then sat between her sisters, leaving Leandros across the table from the three of them. “The greenhouse is banked enough for today. The soil was colder than I expected.”

“Did you get the awning?” Percy asked.

“No. It’s on the list for tomorrow.”

Clarisse took her bowl. “The soil’s not the awning.”

“I know. I meant I didn't have time for both.”

“You don’t need to finish everything today.”

“I know that too.”

Leandros watched the two of them, then looked at Percy. “So she organizes the whole cabin.”

“No,” Silena said.

“I didn't ask you.”

“I know. You asked him about me like I wasn't at the table.”

He looked between them. “I was talking to Percy.”

“You were talking about me.”

The girl with the yellow scarf brought a small loaf and set it at the center. Steam lifted from the soup. Chiron returned with his own plate and took the end seat. “You have a chance to meet more of your cabin after the meal, Leandros. For now, you should eat.”

“I’m not hungry.”

“You can keep the bowl until you are.”

Leandros accepted it but did not lift the spoon. The other campers ate, talked, argued about archery scores and whether the library had enough blankets. Percy tried to follow a conversation about the greenhouse. Silena asked him if he had checked the north step. He had not. Clarisse said she could test it after dinner. Annabeth would glue the stool when she had time; the rail might need another screw. The list was something the three of them could pass around without needing to explain what each word meant.

Leandros leaned forward. “Is this how you decide everything? One at a time at meals?”

“No,” Clarisse said. “Sometimes we decide it while we’re working.”

“And who decides that?”

“Chiron decides what needs doing. People choose how to do it if they know the work.”

“Then there’s no order.”

“There’s a list.”

“That isn't the same.”

“Good.”

“You don’t have a leader?”

“We have Chiron.”

“He can’t supervise every detail.”

“He doesn't.”

“Then someone should.”

“Maybe that’s you,” Clarisse said. “You can write it down.”

“I could.”

“Great. Write a list for where you’re sleeping.”

He pressed his lips together. “I thought there were cabins for the gods.”

“There are,” Percy said. “Poseidon’s is Cabin Three.”

“So I sleep there.”

“You can sleep there,” Clarisse said. “That’s not the same as walking in and rearranging us.”

“We haven’t even been introduced.”

“Clarisse,” Silena said, “he’s tired.”

“He’s still talking.”

“I’m here,” Leandros said.

Silena met his eyes. “I know.”

The bowl warmed his fingers. He lifted the spoon, drank once, then set it down again. “If I’m Poseidon’s son, I’m not a guest.”

“No,” Clarisse said. “You’re family. That doesn’t mean we owe you command.”

“Who asked for command?”

“You did.”

“I said someone should lead.”

“Then we agree. Someone should. It’s just not you.”

“That’s because I just arrived.”

“Now you understand.”

Percy felt the conversation close around the bowl. He tried to make it lighter. “You can lead the soup.”

“What?”

“The soup. We need somebody to decide if it’s good.”

“Does that actually happen here?”

“Sometimes.”

“Only if you want to be the one with the ladle,” Clarisse said.

Leandros looked at his spoon. “I can cook.”

“What?” Percy said.

“My mother taught me. On a boat, if the sea was calm enough.”

“That’s useful,” Silena said. “What do you make?”

“Fish. Rice, if it’s fresh. Bread on a skillet.”

“Which fish?”

“Whatever we caught.”

Silena nodded toward the soup. “Then you can tell us what you think of it.”

He tasted another spoonful, more carefully. “It’s not bad.”

“Thank you,” Chiron said.

“I said it wasn’t bad.”

“We’ll take it.”

The boy’s gaze lifted to the rafters, where the wind tapped the roof. A note of static ran along the metal supports, then faded. He glanced toward the open side of the pavilion.

“Storm coming?” he asked.

“Perhaps,” said Chiron. “The weather has not decided.”

“Wind's east.”

“It is.”

“There's a shift under it.” Leandros turned his head toward the lake, listening. “The surface wind's coming in from the east, but the lower current should be pushing west.”

Percy had never learned to read currents like that. He could feel the lake under his skin sometimes, but it did not come with diagrams. “How can you tell?”

“Look at the treetops.” He pointed with the spoon. “They lean one way. The water’s moving another.”

“Is that a power?”

“No. It’s paying attention.”

It was the first thing he had said that sounded like he might be glad to share. Percy leaned toward the open wall and watched the water. The top of the lake shivered in small lines, but he could not tell which way the deeper movement ran.

“Does your dad do that?” he asked.

Leandros set the spoon down. “My father is Poseidon. I don't know him well enough to know what he does.”

No one spoke for a moment.

“Does he know you?” Percy asked.

“My mother told him.” He looked at the bowl rather than at Percy. “He came once when I was little. I remember the tide coming in around the pilings. My mother says he came again after I started sailing. He left a compass. It doesn't point north.”

“What does it point to?”

“Where the water is deepest.”

“That's kind of amazing.”

“It’s mostly annoying.”

“Why?”

“Because no one else knows how to use it.”

“Do you?”

“I do.”

Percy waited for more. Leandros did not offer it. Silena broke off a piece of bread and dipped it in her soup. “That sounds like a good thing to keep.”

“It’s a compass,” Leandros said. “Not a person.”

“Maybe both can be useful.”

Clarisse gave Silena a sideways look. “Don't recruit him into your metaphors yet.”

“I’m not recruiting anybody.”

“You do it without noticing.”

“I do not.”

“You did it to me on Thursday.”

“That was different.”

“How?”

“You were mad and I wanted to help.”

“I was mad and wanted to be mad.”

Silena’s mouth softened. She did not apologize for offering. “I’ll ask next time.”

Clarisse gave a small nod. “Good.”

Leandros watched them in silence. Percy could feel his own shoulders drawing toward his ears. It was hard to see how someone could arrive at a table and call it a family before he had learned how the people at it fought.

A pale blue shimmer appeared above Leandros’s head. It was small at first, like light beneath a wave. Then it sharpened into the shape of a trident, three points of cold radiance, its shaft lowering toward his dark hair. The soup bowl trembled. Every camper in the pavilion noticed.

Leandros pushed back from the table. “What is that?”

Chiron stood. “The sign of Poseidon.”

The glow hovered, blue and unmistakable. It lit the side of Leandros’s face and the rims of the bowls. His mouth had gone tight.

“My mother said he would claim me,” he said.

“It appears he has.”

The trident brightened once, then dissolved into a fine spray of light that fell nowhere. Leandros kept staring at the place above his head after it was gone. No one clapped. A few campers nodded to him; one younger boy whispered something to another and was quickly hushed. Percy recognized the weight of being looked at as a fact before being known as a person.

“Does it mean he’s sending for me?” Leandros asked.

“No,” Chiron said. “It means your parentage is confirmed. Nothing more should be assumed.”

“Nothing more?”

“Nothing more.”

The uncertainty seemed to disappoint him more than the answer. He sat again, though he left one hand on the strap of the duffel. Percy watched the pale mark where the trident had been and felt something complicated move through him. The symbol had once been an answer to a question he had carried in another life. In this one, it was a truth everyone else had already arranged around him before he could remember choosing it.

“You’re Poseidon’s son,” Leandros said to Percy. “So am I.”

“Yeah.”

“Do you live in Atlantis?”

Percy set his spoon down. He had seen the word on letters, heard it in stories, felt it in the pressure of a room when people expected him to know what a palace sounded like. He had lived there. “I was raised there.”

“What was it like?”

“That's private.”

Leandros blinked. “You don't want to tell me?”

“Not yet.”

“Fine.” He took a drink of water. “You can ask me anything you want about the sea.”

“That isn't how that works,” Clarisse said.

“It might be.”

“It isn't.”

Chiron set down his spoon. “You will learn what your siblings choose to share with you, Leandros. The same is true in the other direction. Being family does not make another person's history yours.”

Leandros’s ears had gone red. “I didn’t mean to pry.”

“Then listen,” Clarisse said.

He turned to her. “You don't have to keep correcting everything I say.”

“I do if you keep saying things you don't know.”

“I’ve been here an hour.”

“Exactly.”

Percy stared at the table. He wanted to say there were parts of Atlantis he could speak about and parts he could not put into words. He also wanted to leave the meal. Instead he tore the bread into two pieces and slid one across the table to Silena, who had not asked for it but took it because she knew he was buying time.

“Let's start again,” Chiron said. “The cabin assignment is straightforward. Leandros will stay in Cabin Three for now. We’ll find a place for the duffel and set expectations after he has had a chance to rest.”

“Is there enough room?” Leandros asked.

“There is.”

“I mean a real place. Not under a window with the blankets.”

Percy glanced at him. “What’s wrong with the blankets?”

“I've never slept under a blanket pinned to the glass.”

“Then you’ll learn.”

“Do you always answer for him?” Clarisse asked Percy.

“No.”

“Then stop.”

“Stop what?”

“Being annoyed on his behalf when he hasn’t said anything.”

Leandros’s face closed. Percy felt the correction hit both of them. “Okay,” he said.

Clarisse pushed back from the table. “I’m checking the north step.”

“I’ll come,” Percy said.

“No, you’ll finish your soup.”

“I’m done.”

“You’ve had three bites.”

“I had more.”

“You’re going anyway?”

“Yeah.”

“Then carry the list.”

She placed it in his hand. The paper was folded at the corner where her thumb had pressed it. Percy looked at the list, then at Leandros.

“Chiron will show you the cabin,” he said. “You can bring your bag when you want.”

“I can carry it now.”

“Okay.”

They stood. Chiron gathered the empty bowls. Clarisse walked out first. Percy followed her into the wind, the list pressed flat against his palm. He had not finished apologizing to Thalia, but at least he had stopped asking what the apology was supposed to earn. He had to learn to do that with everyone, though no one had asked him to become good at it by dinner.

By the time lunch ended, the sky had settled into a flat, dull grey. The breeze came down the hill in little pushes and did not carry rain with it yet. Clarisse crossed toward the north path with the list in one hand, and Percy fell into step beside her.

“You don't have to come,” she said.

“I know.”

“Then why are you coming?”

“Because I said I would help with the step.”

“You said you'd help with the fence.”

“I did that too.”

“You've already had an eventful morning.”

“Are you trying to send me away?”

“I’m trying to figure out why you keep offering.”

He looked at the folded list. “It says the step needs checking.”

“That isn't an answer.”

“It's the one I have.”

Clarisse kept walking. The path narrowed where the laurel pressed in. The first-hard-frost check was a small task, but it had been written there on Sunday because the recent repair had to be tested under the conditions it was meant to hold. The granite step sat at the turn above the lake. Annabeth had placed it flush, and Chiron had tamped the earth underneath. It had held on Sunday, but the nights were colder now.

The stone was dry on top. A thin line of damp had gathered in the joint beneath it.

Clarisse crouched and pressed her fingertips to the seam. “It’s not loose.”

“Want to test it?”

“I am.”

She leaned forward, put her weight on one side, then stepped across the center. The stone did not shift. Percy did the same. It held, though the damp line darkened under his boot.

“Write that down,” she said.

He unfolded the paper and found the pencil that had been in his pocket. Under the line, he wrote “holds in dry cold; check again after freeze.”

Clarisse leaned over his shoulder. “Don't write that it holds in a freeze if we haven't had one.”

“I wrote after freeze.”

“Good.”

“Was that a compliment?”

“It was an instruction.”

“You told me not to expect praise.”

“Then you’re learning.”

They walked back toward camp. Leandros stood outside Cabin Three with Chiron, listening as the centaur explained where he could put his things.

“That's not a bed,” Leandros said.

“It's a cot,” Chiron replied. “We’ll see if you need anything else.”

“I need a mattress.”

“The cabin has one.”

“Then why am I looking at a cot?”

“It is the available place.”

“Is it temporary?”

“Yes.”

“How temporary?”

“Until we see how you settle in.”

“I don't like not knowing where I'm supposed to be.”

“Many people don’t.”

The two had not noticed Percy and Clarisse arrive. Leandros turned and looked at the cabin behind him. The windows were covered with blankets and pinned cloth; the doorframe had been stuffed with rolled fabric to keep out the draft. A strip of green curtain hung crooked on one nail. The porch looked sturdy, though one board had been patched with a piece of rough lumber.

“Does it always look like that?” he asked.

“It looks warmer than it did on Saturday,” Percy said.

“I can see that.”

“Then you know what it looks like.”

“It's not an answer.”

“It’s the answer I have.”

Clarisse gave him a look. “You can sleep in there or not.”

“Am I supposed to decide now?”

“You can decide after you’ve eaten.”

“Do people here talk like they don’t care whether I stay?”

“Do you want us to tell you you’re the most important person we’ve ever met?”

“No.”

“Then you get what we have.”

Chiron took the bag from the ground and set it just inside the door. “You may come with me to the infirmary. We can clean your knuckle and get you something dry to wear.”

“I can do that myself.”

“I'm sure you can. The infirmary still has what you need.”

Leandros looked at Percy. “Is she always like this?”

“Clarisse?”

“No, the other one.”

“Who?”

“Silena.”

“I don't know what you mean.”

“You all keep telling each other what to do.”

“We do?”

“It's like a test. Every sentence ends with an order.”

“That isn't true,” Clarisse said.

“Then why did you tell me I could sleep in there or not?”

“Because you asked what you were supposed to decide.”

He shook his head, as if the answer proved his point. “I don’t take orders from girls.”

The air changed before any of them answered. Not the sky; the little space between them.

Clarisse folded her arms. “You don't have to.”

“I mean—”

“You don't have to take orders from anyone. You do have to follow Chiron's camp rules if you want to stay here.”

“I didn’t say I wouldn’t.”

“You said girls.”

“I meant—”

“You meant what you said.”

Leandros’s eyes hardened. “You’re younger than me.”

“I am.”

“So why are you acting like you have authority over me?”

“I’m not.” Clarisse pointed to the cot inside the cabin. “That is where your stuff can go. I told you the choice was yours.”

“You told me where the bag goes.”

“Because you asked where you were supposed to be.”

“That's not the same.”

“No. It’s not.”

Chiron waited until their voices lowered. “Leandros, there are no cabin commanders. Your age gives you no authority over your siblings, and Clarisse’s time at camp gives her no authority to make your private choices. Both facts can be true. The rules are mine to set, not hers. You may reject the cot after dinner, but you may not enter another cabin without permission or take someone else’s bed.”

Leandros was silent for a moment. “I didn’t ask to come here.”

“No,” Chiron said. “And no one expects you to be grateful for being safe.”

“Then why does it feel like everyone wants me to be?”

“Because people are often clumsy around new arrivals.”

“I’m not new.”

“You are here.”

“I've been alive sixteen years.”

“That is not the same thing.”

Percy felt the question inside the answer, the soft opening for Leandros to say what he had left behind. Leandros took the invitation and made it narrower.

“I had a life before this place.”

“Of course you did.”

“My mother didn't send me here so people could decide where I belong.”

“She asked that you be protected and that she be allowed to hear from you.”

“That's not the same as asking you to make decisions for me.”

“No,” Chiron said. “It is not.”

Leandros took the duffel through the door. He set it on the cot and unzipped it. Inside were two folded sweaters, a wool cap, a small canvas pouch, a roll of charts in a wooden tube and a flat tin with a blue lid. The other clothes were neatly stacked. At the bottom lay a thick envelope with his name written across it in a round, firm hand. He shut the bag before the letter could be seen for long.

Clarisse stayed outside. “You don't have to stay in our cabin if you don't like it.”

Chiron looked at her. She shrugged.

“I mean it,” she said. “You can go to the Big House or ask Chiron about a different place. But don't walk in there and start telling us how to live.”

Leandros held the bag closed with his hand. “I didn't.”

“You did.”

“I said I don't take orders from girls.”

“Yeah. I heard.”

“I’m not apologizing for the words if they're true.”

“Then don’t.”

Percy felt his jaw move. He stopped himself from speaking. Clarisse had not asked him to take over. She was not waiting for a defense. He could feel the old reflex that wanted to turn a hard conversation into one he could fix.

Clarisse met Leandros’s eyes. “If you want to tell me where you sleep, you can do it without saying my being a girl matters.”

“I don't need a cot.”

“That's your choice.”

“And if I want to keep my bag with me?”

“Then keep it with you.”

“There. That wasn’t so hard.”

“You made it hard.”

He looked down at the bag, then at the bunk beyond the cot. “I’ll sleep here tonight.”

“Fine.”

“That's all?”

“You expect a song?”

“No.”

“Then don't act surprised when people give you a straight answer.”

Clarisse walked away toward Cabin Three’s other door. Percy waited. Chiron remained with Leandros, but did not move inside the room.

“Do you want me to show you where the infirmary is?” Percy asked.

“I know where it is.”

“Okay.”

“I don’t need a guide.”

“Okay.”

“What do you do when someone says they don't need something?”

“I believe them.”

Leandros’s expression flickered. “That sounds like a bad way to live.”

“It's better than the other way.”

“What’s the other way?”

“Deciding you know better.”

Leandros looked past him toward the hill where the path dropped to the lake. Wind moved through the trees. The branches shifted, but the water below barely moved. “The current’s turning.”

“Is that what your compass tells you?”

“I don’t need the compass.”

“Can I see it?”

“No.”

“Okay.”

The answer appeared to take some of the fight out of his face. Not much. A fraction. “I’ll come to dinner,” he said.

“Good.”

“I’m not promising I’ll like it.”

“Neither do I.”

That almost made him smile. He looked down before Percy could be sure.

Chiron stepped back from the cabin. “The infirmary is behind the Big House. I will see you after dinner, when you have had time to rest.”

“I don't need rest.”

“You have been walking since sunrise.”

“I already told Percy I can stand.”

“He did not ask you to lie down.”

Percy put up both hands. “I didn't.”

Leandros turned away. “I heard.”

He carried the duffel farther inside. Chiron lowered his voice. “You do not need to win every conversation today.”

“Neither do they.”

“No. They do not.”

The centaur moved toward the Big House. Percy and Clarisse took the path toward the lake.

“You were going to say something,” Clarisse said.

“Yeah.”

“Why didn’t you?”

“You didn't need me to.”

“You’re learning.”

“Another compliment?”

“Don’t push it.”

“Was he wrong about the cot?”

“No. It’s uncomfortable.”

“Should we get him the other mattress?”

“He can ask.”

“Okay.”

She walked a few steps before adding, “You can still bring it up if he does.”

“I know.”

“No, I mean if he asks you. You can help him. I don’t want you deciding for him because you think he’s miserable.”

“Understood.”

“Good.”

The word did not make the day easier. But it gave Percy a way to stand next to his sister without needing to be her voice.

After the afternoon’s work, Percy went back to Cabin One to drop off the revised list. Annabeth had left the porch rail alone; the screws would wait for Rhys and his helper. The north step had held. The fence was braced, not repaired. He made a note of each one before the pencil could go missing.

Thalia was at the table again, ankle elevated on the chair. She had changed into a red sweater and pulled her hair into a knot. A book lay open across her lap, but her eyes were on Annabeth’s field notes.

“Did you talk to her?” Annabeth asked.

“I did.”

“Did you ask if she forgives you?”

“No.”

“Did you want to?”

“Yes.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

She smiled. “That’s progress.”

“Are you keeping a list?”

“I could.”

“Please don’t.”

“Then don’t give me reasons.”

Thalia shifted the blanket under her heel. “He apologized.”

Annabeth closed the book. “How do you feel about it?”

“I feel like he said something I wanted to hear.”

“That’s good.”

“I don't feel done being angry.”

“That’s allowed.”

“I know.” Thalia looked at Percy. “You’re still allowed to come up here. Just don’t turn it into a whole thing.”

“I won’t.”

“You turned the apology into a whole thing last time.”

“I was trying to make it right.”

“I know.”

“I thought if I told you what I was thinking—”

“I know that too. You don’t have to explain it again.”

Percy nodded. “Okay.”

She studied his face. “I’m not made of glass.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I’m trying to.”

“That’s different.”

“Yeah.”

“Good.”

Annabeth reached for the list and held it up. “Which tasks are done?”

“Fence is braced. North step held, but needs another check after a real freeze. Silena banked the greenhouse soil. Awning is tomorrow. The stool and rail need people with glue and screws.”

“Who’s looking at the fence post?”

“Chiron.”

“Good.”

Percy glanced at Thalia’s foot. He was aware of the question he wanted to ask. He did not ask it.

She noticed. “You can say it.”

“Say what?”

“How my heel is.”

“I wasn't going to ask.”

“Sure.”

“I wasn't.”

“Then tell me what you were thinking.”

“That the day was long.”

“Not about my foot?”

“Not until you asked.”

“Fine.” She turned a page in her book. “It still hurts. The splint helps. I’m going to see Chiron on Tuesday, and I’ll decide what I’m doing at the hall after that.”

“Okay.”

“If I sit outside the water, I’m not going to need you to ask whether I’m going in.”

“I won’t.”

“Good.”

Annabeth set the list down between them. “I’m coming back to Cabin Three after supper. I have glue for the stool.”

“Does that mean you’re sleeping there?” Thalia asked.

“I sleep here.”

“Right. I forgot I was going to move out so your boyfriend could have my bed.”

Percy nearly choked on air. “That’s not how that conversation went.”

“I know,” Thalia said. “I’m teasing you.”

“Could have said so first.”

“Then it wouldn’t be as good.”

Annabeth leaned back in her chair. “He’s moving into Cabin Three, not taking your bed.”

“Leandros can have the old cot,” Percy said.

“Why do you get to decide?”

“I don't.”

“You said he can have the cot.”

“I meant that's the place Chiron told him about.”

“And if he doesn't want it?”

“He can choose something else.”

“Do you believe he can?”

Percy considered it. “He can decide if he stays. I think he can ask for a different place.”

“Then say that.”

“Okay.”

Thalia rested her chin against her hand. “You don’t need to make him feel like he’s welcome before he figures out whether he wants to be.”

“I know.”

Annabeth gave him a look.

“I am learning,” he said.

“You are.”

“I could ask him questions,” he said. “I want to know what his life was like.”

“That’s your choice. But he gets to choose too.”

“I can do that.”

“You can. You might not like it.”

“I don't like a lot of things I can do.”

“That doesn't stop you.”

She was right. He smiled at the familiar shape of her criticism, the way she used it to bring him nearer instead of pushing him away. He reached for her hand. Annabeth gave it. He bent to kiss her knuckles and she turned the gesture into a kiss on his cheek, brief and warm.

“Go eat,” she said.

“Yes, Wise Girl.”

“Don't call me that when you want me to agree with you.”

“I'm not asking you to agree.”

“Good.”

He rose. Thalia gave him a lazy salute with two fingers. “Tell Silena to save me one of the honey cakes if there are any.”

“I thought you were teasing me.”

“I am.”

“Okay.”

“And Percy?”

“Yeah?”

“Thank you for saying it.”

He stopped at the door. “You’re welcome.”

She did not add anything. Neither did he. He went down the porch steps without touching the loose rail.

Cabin Three smelled like warm wood and apple peel when he got back. Silena had set a pot near the stove with sliced apples, a little honey and cinnamon. The heat had made the whole room smell sweet. Clarisse was at the table sorting the pieces of wire into a shallow tin. She had set two bowls out but not a third. A third bowl waited on the shelf near the door.

“Is the extra one for him?” Percy asked.

“Maybe,” Clarisse said.

Silena stirred the pot. “I thought we could eat here.”

“Chiron said dinner at the pavilion.”

“He can eat there if he wants. I was making something for after.”

“Is it for us?”

“Mostly.”

“Does that mean I can have some?”

“Probably.”

Leandros stood near the door with his duffel at his feet. His coat was gone. He wore a dark sweater with frayed cuffs and had the cut cleaned and bandaged. He held a folded page in one hand.

“You came back,” Percy said.

“I said I would come to dinner.”

“Right.”

Leandros’s eyes moved to the pot. “What is that?”

“Apples.”

“With honey?”

“And cinnamon.”

“Does it go with dinner?”

“It goes after dinner.”

“Then why is it in a pot?”

“Because it gets warm.”

“Seems like a lot of effort for apples.”

Silena stirred again. “It’s not a lot.”

“I didn’t say it was too much.”

“You said it seemed like a lot.”

“I meant—”

“You said what you meant,” Clarisse told him.

Leandros looked at her. “Do you always interpret everyone’s words for them?”

“No. Only when people try to take them back.”

Silena set the spoon down. “I like making this. My mother used to make it when the weather got cold. I don’t need anybody to like it. But I don’t want you to talk about it like it’s silly.”

The room held a quiet that felt more serious than the apples. Leandros lowered his folded page. “I don’t think it’s silly.”

“Okay.”

“I thought it would be something you eat with dinner.”

“You can decide if you want some later.”

He nodded once. No apology, but he had not argued. Silena picked the spoon back up.

Clarisse reached for the paper in Percy’s hand. “What’s the status?”

“Fence braced; Chiron’s checking the post. Step held, but needs a check after frost. Soil banking is done. Awning tomorrow.”

“Write the frost check in the box.”

Percy handed her the page and watched her mark it. Leandros looked at the list, then at Clarisse. “You make all the decisions?”

“No.”

“Who does?”

“Whoever is doing the job.”

“What if they disagree?”

“We talk.”

“Then who wins?”

“Nobody. We decide what needs doing.”

“And if you can’t?”

“Then Chiron helps.”

“Does he have to help every time?”

“No.”

“Then there should be one person who gets to settle it.”

Clarisse set down the pencil. “Why?”

“Because if people keep arguing, you lose time.”

“You also lose time if someone makes a bad call.”

“I can make a good call.”

“You don’t know this place.”

“I know the sea.”

“Good. The fence isn't in it.”

He pressed his lips together. Percy felt the conversation turning toward the same ledge they had reached at lunch. He did not step in. Silena put a small plate on the counter beside the pot.

“Tomorrow there’s an awning to look at,” she said. “If you want work, tell Chiron in the morning. He’ll decide where you fit.”

“I can look at the awning.”

“Tell Chiron.”

“I can tell him.”

“Good.”

“Do you all follow his orders?”

“When it matters,” Clarisse said. “And sometimes when it doesn't.”

“So there is a leader.”

“There’s an adult responsible for the camp. That doesn’t make him our father.”

Leandros looked at the paper in Clarisse’s hand. “Poseidon’s supposed to be.”

“He is our father.”

“Then why is Chiron deciding where we work?”

“Because Poseidon is not here.”

“Would you want him to be?”

Clarisse's eyes narrowed. “That’s not a question you get to ask me today.”

“Why not?”

“Because you don’t know what happened.”

“I know he didn’t come here.”

“You know he came to you.”

“He came twice.”

“And that means?”

“It means he knew I existed.”

“Congratulations.”

Percy looked up. Silena stopped stirring. Clarisse had not raised her voice, but the hurt in the word had been sharp enough to cut. Leandros understood it then, maybe not fully, but enough to go quiet.

“You’re right,” he said.

Clarisse crossed her arms. “I know.”

“I don’t know what happened.”

“No.”

“I’m not asking you to explain.”

“Good.”

The pot on the stove clicked softly as the apples settled. Silena moved it away from the heat. “We’re going to the pavilion. If you want to come with us, you can.”

Leandros looked at the bowl on the shelf. “Is that mine?”

“Maybe.”

“Do I have to decide now?”

“No,” Percy said.

Leandros considered the room, then the open doorway behind him. The night had come down early, and the blue-grey outside made the cabin seem warmer than it had been at noon. The blankets on the windows held. The cot was still uncomfortable. The third bowl was still on the shelf.

“I’ll come,” he said.

They walked together toward the pavilion. Clarisse went first. Silena stayed beside her. Percy waited so Leandros could decide whether to walk with him or behind. The boy chose the space at his right, but kept a hand on the bag strap. They did not tell him what that meant.

At the table, Percy sat between his sisters. Leandros took the place across from him, close enough to reach the bread but far enough from Clarisse to avoid brushing elbows. Silena set the warm pot near the center, and the sweetness rose in a soft cloud. Clarisse took one slice. Percy took two. Leandros waited until the others had chosen, then placed a single piece on his plate.

“It’s good,” he said.

Silena looked at him. “You can have another if you want.”

“I know.”

“Okay.”

He took a second.

No one called it an apology. No one called it a welcome. Percy ate the apple while the wind scraped the pavilion roof and the family at the table learned how much space one more person could take.
