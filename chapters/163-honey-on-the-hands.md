## Chapter 163: Honey on the Hands

Silena Beauregard woke before the bell because the kitchen had asked her to.

It was not a sound, exactly. Cabin Three in the early morning had its own weather: the pool at the end of Tempest Hall breathing low through the channels, the tile cool underfoot, the high windows letting in a wash of pale September light that laid itself across the mosaic hippocampus and made the water look lit from underneath. Silena lay in her bunk with the quilt pulled to her chin and listened to Clarisse breathing in the bunk across the narrow aisle — deep, even, faintly snoring on the inhale — and to Percy turned toward the wall in the third bunk, one arm thrown over his eyes against the light, hair sticking up in four directions.

Thursday. Thalia out on the boundary line until tomorrow. The empty seat at breakfast would be empty one more day, and then Friday she would walk back down the trail with bark in her boots and opinions about granite, and Annabeth would stop checking the tree line.

Silena sat up and swung her feet to the cold tile. She dressed in the half-light without bothering the others: the faded orange camp shirt with the trident washed nearly white, sleeves pushed to the elbow, hem soft from a hundred washes, tied once at the waist because mornings in the kitchen ran hot. Sturdy shorts with flour already in the pocket seams from last week, though she had shaken them out twice. Worn sandals she could kick off and hose down, hair pulled into a haphazard bun with a pencil stuck through it for later lists. In her pockets: a hair tie, a twist of wax paper, a short stub of honey stick saved from Tuesday.

The rule about clothing at camp was simple and hers: it had to work, it had to wash, and it had to let you lift a sack of flour without thinking about it. Anything else was decoration, and decoration came second to feeding people.

She padded down the hall past the pool. The water lay flat and green-clear, warm at the top where the light touched it, colder underneath where the channels came up from somewhere deep. Salt and rain smell, lemon oil from yesterday's oiling still hanging faint. The visitor towels hung on their hooks by the back bunks — two, folded, blue-striped, for Tyson and Triton, who visited and did not live here, who had a sea and forges and better libraries. Tyson had gone back Tuesday after the farewell on the beach, with peanut butter on his thumb and a promise to return in two weeks. Triton came the way Triton came, formally and briefly. The towels stayed. That was the whole argument, and Silena made the bunks withhospital corners anyway, because hope liked clean lines.

In the kitchen corner of the cabin — really just a counter, a cold box, a shelf of tins, and a two-burner plate Chiron pretended not to know about — Silena stood with her hands on her hips and took inventory.

Flour. Honey from the apiary jar, two-thirds full, with the wax seal cracked. Lemons, three, from the greenhouse, heavy for their size. Peaches, four, the last of September, fragrant enough to perfume the whole shelf. Butter. Eggs from the pavilion, if she asked nice. Salt. Baking powder. A tin of chopped walnuts somebody's mother had sent in August. A jar of dried figs. Cinnamon that smelled like winter when you opened it.

She had been turning the idea over since Tuesday night, while the canoe paint dried and the letters went back and forth. Not a cake. Cakes were for birthdays and apologies. Not cookies, though cookies had their place. Something elaborate and ridiculous, the kind of thing you only made when you had a whole day and a whole cabin and a reason that was not a reason. A honey-lemon tower: thin layers of olive-oil cake brushed with lemon syrup, stacked with whipped honey and sliced peaches, crowned with figs and walnuts and a drizzle that would run down the sides on purpose. Tall enough to need a strategy. Messy enough to require tasting at every stage. The kind of thing Clarisse would call structural unsoundness and then eat three slices of standing up.

Silena pulled the pencil from her bun and wrote on the back of a grocery slip in her fast round hand: LAYERS — 3. SYRUP — LEMON + HONEY. FILLING — WHIPPED HONEY. FRUIT — PEACHES. TOP — FIGS + WALNUTS + DRIZZLE. TASTERS — EVERYONE. DISHES — PERCY.

She smiled at the last line. Then she crossed it out and wrote DISHES — ALL OF US, because Thursday was for practice, not punishment.

The pavilion at seven was the best hour: breakfast plates cleared, lunch prep not yet started, long tables sitting in sunlight with crumbs like glitter. Silena went behind the counter with the crate from Cabin Three under one arm and nodded to Ms. O'Fee, the harpy on morning duty, who was folding a stack of dish towels with her wings half-furled and her eyes sharp.

"You," Ms. O'Fee said.

"Me," Silena agreed. "Baking day. Cabin Three plus one. I need eggs and the big bowl and the oven from nine to eleven, and I will wash everything twice."

"The big bowl is for pavilion use."

"The big bowl is for whoever sings for it. That's precedent. I checked with the afternoon harpy."

Ms. O'Fee folded another towel. Slap, fold, stack. "You checked with Euphemia. Euphemia lets you get away with larceny because you harmonize."

"I harmonize and I wash twice. Also I brought you a peach." Silena set the peach on the counter, the best-shaped one, blushing red-gold. "Not a bribe. A tasting note. For later comparison."

The harpy looked at the peach. Looked at Silena. Picked up the peach and sniffed it with the expertise of a creature who had eaten three thousand camp summers. "Sing," she said. "Quiet. No vibrato before coffee."

So Silena sang, soft, into the empty pavilion with the dust in the light shafts and the smell of coffee gone cold on the burner — a little sailing song with no words, the one she used for dough and for paint and for waiting. Ms. O'Fee's wings shivered once. She set the peach aside where it would not bruise and pushed the big steel bowl across the counter without another word, followed by a dozen eggs in a carton and a folded stack of side towels.

"Two hours," she said. "You burn it, you scrape it. You break my bowl, you buy me a new camp."

"Deal," Silena said, and hefted the bowl, which was nearly as wide as she was, and carried it back up the hill with the eggs balanced on top, singing under her breath all the way, because some days the whole world was a kitchen and she was allowed to be glad about it.

Clarisse was up when she got back, sitting on the edge of her bunk lacing her boots with the thoroughness she gave to everything with laces, knots, or consequences. Her dark braid hung over one shoulder, damp from a quick dunk in the pool. Same faded orange camp shirt, sleeves cut short with the edges rolling, cargo pants with the knees blown out and patched with sailcloth Silena had sewn crooked on purpose. Pencil behind her ear already.

"You're baking," Clarisse said, without looking up. It was not a question.

"I'm baking," Silena said. "Elaborate and ridiculous. Three layers. Lemon syrup. Whipped honey. Peaches. You will be required to taste and to have opinions."

"I have opinions without tasting."

"You have louder opinions with tasting. It's science." Silena set the bowl down on the counter with a soft bong. "Percy gets the flour job. You get the peach job, because you have the best knife hands."

Clarisse finished her laces, double-knotted, stood, rolled her shoulders. "I have the best everything hands."

"Modesty will be served at eleven with the cake."

Percy emerged from the bunk alcove at that moment, hair flat on one side and wild on the other, pulling his camp shirt straight. The faded orange with the trident washed white, shorts that had been his in April and were mostly string by September, sneakers still damp from yesterday, laces knotted until the bow sat sideways. In his pockets, she knew without checking: a hair tie she had pressed on him, a smooth white pebble from the canoe lake, a pencil stub.

"What did I do to deserve flour job," he said, yawning.

"You were born into this cabin," Clarisse said. "Fluor — flour — is inheritance."

"It's flour," Percy said. "Good morning to you too."

"It is a baking day," Silena said, clapping her hands once. "Rules: wash hands. Tie hair. Taste when I tell you. No tasting when I don't tell you, which will be difficult for one of you. No sparring with utensils. No feeding batter to the pool, it clogs the channels. Annabeth comes at nine when the map kids are done, and if we are lucky Olive will stay five minutes and tell us everything she knows about drainage."

Percy went to the basin and washed to the elbow, the way Silena had taught him in June. Clarisse washed with the efficiency of someone defusing something. Silena tied Percy's hair back with the spare tie from her pocket before he could protest, and he stood still for it, which was new in August and ordinary now.

"Three layers," she said, laying out tins. "We bake them one at a time in the little oven, so we rotate. While one bakes, we make syrup. While syrup cools, we whip honey. While honey chills, we slice peaches. Tasting at every stage. This is not dessert. This is practice."

"Practice for what," Percy said.

"For feeding people on purpose," Silena said. "Now. Flour."

He measured flour the way she had taught him — spoon into the cup, level with a knife, no packing — and the kitchen corner filled with the soft white dust that got on everything and made the light look thicker. Clarisse sliced peaches with her fighting knife, washed three times, on the wooden board, each slice even enough to measure, juice running over her knuckles. Silena cracked eggs one-handed and let Percy separate the yolk scare with his fingers, and he made a face, and she laughed, and Clarisse said, "Don't be precious, Jackson, it's an egg," and he said, "It's the texture, it knows I'm afraid," and Silena flicked flour at both of them, and the day began the way good days began: with work and noise and somebody already needing a side towel.

By eight the first layer was in the oven and the cabin smelled like warm olive oil and lemon zest, and Percy stood at the counter with flour on his forearms and on his nose and said, "I think I inhaled cake," and Silena said, "That's the idea," and Clarisse ate a peach slice off the end of her knife and said, grudgingly, "Good peach," as if the peach had earned it.

Percy Jackson had learned in one summer that there were two kinds of mornings at Camp Half-Blood.

There were patrol mornings, which started with a clipboard and ended with somebody bleeding on purpose. And there were kitchen mornings, which started with Silena humming and ended with everybody sticky and nobody sorry.

Thursday was the second kind, and he was glad in a way that made him suspicious of himself.

He stood at the Cabin Three counter in his faded orange camp shirt, now dusted white at the front, swim shorts, damp sneakers kicked off by the door because Silena had declared flour plus sneakers a safety hazard, bare feet on the cool tile. His hair was tied back with her spare tie, which was slipping. Flour sat in the creases of his knuckles and along his forearms. The little oven ticked heat into the room. The pool breathed at the end of the hall. Through the high windows the light had gone from pale to gold.

Clarisse stood elbow to elbow with him, slicing the second peach, her braid swung forward over her shoulder to keep it out of the juice. She worked fast, no waste, each slice falling in an even fan. When a slice tried to stick to the blade she flicked it free with a practiced snap and did not look up.

"Stop hovering," she said, without looking up. "You're hovering with your whole body."

"I'm waiting for instructions," Percy said. "I'm an eager apprentice."

"You're flour in human form. Go stir the syrup before it decides to be candy."

He went to the burner where the lemon syrup simmered — honey, water, lemon peel in long curls, a squeeze of juice — and stirred with a wooden spoon, watching the bubbles come slow and glossy. The smell rose, bright and sweet, lemon oil sharp under the honey. It fogged his glasses-less eyes; he blinked. Somewhere in the cabin a timer Silena had borrowed from the pavilion ticked down the first layer.

Silena moved between them the way she moved when she was happiest, bare feet silent, bun slipping sideways, camp shirt tied at the waist, a smear of olive oil on one cuff. She checked the oven with a quick open-close, nodded, pulled the pencil from her bun and made a mark on her slip.

"Syrup needs two more minutes," Percy reported. "It's doing the thick-coat thing on the spoon."

"Good eyes," Silena said. "Pull it at nappe. That's the word. It means it coats and doesn't run. Say it."

"Nappe," Percy said, trying it. "Sounds like a nap for food."

"It's French for don't walk away," Clarisse said. "Watch the pot, Jackson."

The door banged. Olive Nakamura arrived the way Olive always arrived, like weather: ten years old, small for ten, red notebook clutched to her chest, camp shirt two sizes too big with sleeves rolled to the elbow, shorts white at the seams, sneakers with mismatched laces, one blue, one white, hair in two uneven braids with flour already on one cheek though she had been in the room eleven seconds.

"I finished drainage!" she announced. "Annabeth said I could have five minutes if I finished drainage and Marcus said to bring the rope sample and I brought it and also my pencil!" Behind her, Marcus, fifteen and tall and quiet, ducked through the doorway with a short length of whipped rope in one hand and a canvas bag in the other. His shirt, faded to dishwater, sleeves cut clean with a knife, cargo shorts, boots soft at the ankle. He nodded once at Clarisse, who nodded back.

"Five minutes," Marcus confirmed. "Then wall."

"Wall?" Percy said.

"Big House wall," Olive said importantly. "Annabeth's map. We're doing the north shore. Marcus does rope scale. I do noticing." She stared at the counter. "Is that cake? It smells like cake and lemons had a baby."

"It's a tower," Silena said. "Three layers. You are exactly in time for tasting stage one. Wash hands. Both of you. Then one spoon each, no double-dipping, tell me what it needs."

Olive washed so fast water hit the floor. Marcus washed the way he did everything, thorough and silent. Silena ladled a spoonful of warm syrup into two small cups and handed them over with the gravity of a priestess.

Olive sipped. Her eyes went huge. "It's — it's sour and then it's sweet and then it's warm! It tastes like sunshine that got lost in a lemon!"

Marcus sipped. Considered. "More lemon," he said. "Peel. Not juice. Peel."

Silena stared at him. Then she laughed, delighted. "Yes! Peel! Olive, write that down for the margin. Marcus is about rope and also about peel."

Olive fumbled her notebook open, pencil already out. MARCUS SAYS MORE PEEL, she wrote in round letters, and underlined it. Percy leaned over her shoulder to check the spelling and she showed him proudly, and he said, "That's good noticing," and she glowed.

Clarisse, still slicing, said, without looking up, "Kid's right. Syrup's sweet-heavy. Add peel."

"Two votes for peel," Silena said, grating more zest into the pot with quick flicks. "Democracy in action. Percy, stir."

He stirred, and the bright oil burst out of the zest and the whole corner smelled like a grove. Olive stood on tiptoe to watch, narrating under her breath — "stir, stir, peel, stir" — and Marcus stood behind her with his hands in his pockets, watching the oven timer like it owed him money.

The timer dinged. Silena opened the oven with a towel, and the first layer came out golden and domed, smelling of olive oil and honey, and everybody leaned in at once.

"Oh," Olive breathed. "It's beautiful. Can I touch it?"

"Not yet," Silena said. "It has to cool. Hot cake lies about texture. That's a margin lesson."

"Hot cake lies," Olive repeated, writing. "Marcus, write that down."

Marcus pulled his folded square of paper from his pocket and wrote it down, ears pink.

Percy brushed crumbs off the layer's edge with his fingertip and popped them in his mouth before Silena could stop him. She swatted him with a side towel, laughing.

"That's stage-one tasting and you know it," she said. "What does it need?"

He chewed, considering, flour on his nose. "It's good. It's — olive-oily in the good way, like bread that decided to be cake. Needs syrup. Lots of syrup."

"Correct," Silena said. "Brush, not pour. Clarisse, show him the brushing."

Clarisse took the pastry brush, dabbed it in the warm syrup, and showed him in three strokes: light, even, to the edge but not over. "Like painting," she said. "Thin coats. Annabeth would tell you two thin coats beats one thick."

"Annabeth tells everyone that," Percy said, grinning. "It's why her map has eleven students."

"It's true every time," said a voice from the doorway, precise and amused. "That's why it works."

Annabeth Grace stood in the doorway with her notebook under her arm and sawdust on her shoes from the Big House wall. Fourteen, same as Percy, hair in a ponytail coming loose, gray camp shirt with real pockets she had been living in all week, sleeves shoved past the elbows, hem untucked on one side, shorts with a white dust line at the hem, white sneakers double-knotted. A pencil sat behind her ear. Flour dust hung in the air and settled on her shoulders as she stepped in.

"Map kids," she said. "Five minutes is up in two."

"Two!" Olive said. "We have two! Annabeth, taste the syrup! Marcus said peel and Clarisse agreed and Silena did democracy!"

Annabeth washed her hands, took the spoon Silena offered, tasted. Her face did the precise thing it did when she was measuring. "Marcus is right. Peel, not juice. Good call." She nodded at Marcus, who went pinker. "Olive, drainage?"

"Done! I drew the culvert and the puddle and the way the water goes around the rock!" Olive flipped her notebook to a page of wavy blue lines and brown squares. "See? The puddle lies. The rock tells truth."

Annabeth studied it, head tipped. "Good noticing. Put it on the wall this afternoon. Credit yourself. Age ten and a half, north path survey."

Olive glowed so hard Percy thought she might lift off. Marcus picked up the rope sample and the canvas bag. "Wall," he said.

"Wall," Annabeth agreed. "Go on. I'll catch up."

They went, Olive walking backward to watch the cake until Clarisse said, "Walk forward, Nakamura," and Olive turned and ran, braids bouncing, notebook clutched tight. Marcus followed, steady, and the doorway emptied.

Annabeth stayed. She set her notebook on the clean end of the counter, away from flour, and looked at the layer, the syrup, the peaches in their even fan, the figs and walnuts waiting in tins.

"You started without me," she said.

"We started the boring part without you," Silena said. "We saved you the stacking. Also the tasting. Also Percy inhaled cake."

"I inhaled cake," Percy confirmed. "It's a condition."

Annabeth smiled, the small precise smile that was hers, and bumped her shoulder against his as she moved past to wash a peach knife, the way sisters-in-law might, the way people did when the kitchen was small and the day was long. Her fingers were cool from the walk. His arm was warm from the oven. The touch lasted a second.

"Thalia's trail?" Silena asked, quieter, grating lemon.

"Checked this morning," Annabeth said. "No dust yet. She comes tomorrow. Four days of trees." She dried the knife, set it parallel to the board edge. "Her bunk's made. I aired her blanket. Put granite on the pillow as a joke. She'll throw it at me."

"She'll be glad to be thrown at," Clarisse said, blunt, not looking up from her peaches. "Empty seat's been loud."

Annabeth glanced at her, surprised into honesty. "Yes. Loud."

Silena handed Annabeth a peach slice. "Taste. Tell me if they're worth stacking or if we eat them now and use figs."

Annabeth bit, juice running. She chewed, considering. "Stack. They're perfect. Sweet-heavy, but the syrup's sour now. Balance."

"Official cartographer approves balance," Percy said. "Write that down."

"I will," Annabeth said, and did, in her blue notebook, in small neat letters, and Silena laughed and flicked flour at her too, and Annabeth stood there in the flour light with a peach in one hand and a pencil in the other and let herself be floury, which was rare and worth seeing.

Second layer went in. While it baked, Silena set them to whipping honey.

It was ridiculous work. Heavy cream in the big steel bowl, set over a basin of cool water from the pool channel — Silena insisted cold water made better peaks, and nobody argued because she was right about dairy the way Annabeth was right about contours. Honey drizzled in a thin stream while Percy whisked, then Clarisse took over when his arm gave out, then Annabeth took over when Clarisse declared whisking a peasant's labor and then kept whisking anyway to prove she could.

"Peaks," Silena instructed, peering over Annabeth's shoulder. "Soft first, then firm. Stop before butter. Butter is defeat."

"Butter is a different project," Annabeth said, wrist moving in steady circles. The cream thickened, glossed, began to hold lines. "There. Soft."

"Keep going," Silena said. "Percy, taste."

Percy dipped a clean spoon, tasted. Honey and cream, cold and bright. "It's — it tastes like cold honey decided to be a cloud."

"Poetic," Clarisse said. "Whip faster, cloud boy."

He took the bowl back, whisked until his shoulders burned, until the cream stood in firm peaks that held when he lifted the whisk. Silena clapped. Annabeth nodded approval, the small sharp nod she gave to good triangulation.

"Now we stack," Silena said. "Clear the counter. Towels down. Everybody washes the peach off their hands."

They cleared and washed and laid side towels. The first layer, cooled on a rack, was brushed generously with syrup until it glistened. Silena spread whipped honey in a thick even coat with a flat knife, working to the edge in smooth strokes. Then the peaches, arranged in overlapping rings, each slice placed with care. Percy laid three slices, crooked. Clarisse fixed them without comment, nudging each into place with the tip of her finger. Annabeth placed the next ring, precise, edges aligned like contour lines, and Silena smiled to see it.

"You're all hired," Silena said. "Second layer does the same."

Second layer came out domed higher on one side. Clarisse eyed it, took her knife, and leveled it with two clean cuts, the way she cut everything — no hesitation, no sawing. The scraps went onto a plate for tasting. Percy ate his scrap in one bite, crumbs on his chin. Annabeth ate hers in small bites, considering the crumb.

"Tender," she said. "Good gluten. You didn't overmix."

"I overmixed the first time I made this," Silena said. "Eleven years old. Made a doorstop. Clarisse used it to prop open Tempest Hall for a week."

"It was a good doorstop," Clarisse said. "Sturdy. Honest."

They stacked the second layer, syrup, honey, peaches. The tower wobbled. Percy held his breath. Silena slid two thin skewers down through the center to steady it, the way she had learned from watching the pavilion harpies stack for feasts.

"Third layer," she said. "Then figs, walnuts, drizzle. Then we clean and we carry it to the pavilion to chill, and then tonight we eat it with everybody."

"That's a lot of honey," Annabeth observed, looking at the jar, now nearly empty.

"It's a lot of people," Silena said. "Love as daily practice. That's what my mother used to say — no, not my mother. My grandmother. She said you don't prove love once. You feed people every day until they believe it."

Percy looked at her, whisk in hand, cream on his wrist. "That's good," he said quietly. "That's — can I write that down?"

"You can live it," Silena said, and bumped his hip with hers. "Now. Flour off your nose before you drip in the honey."

By ten-thirty the kitchen corner looked like a storm had passed through a mill. Flour on the tile, on the counter, on three camp shirts and one gray one. Peach juice on the board, on Clarisse's knuckles, on the side towel Percy kept using as a cape. Lemon peels in a fragrant pile. The bowl with its traces of whipped honey, scraped nearly clean by tasting spoons. The oven ticking as the third layer baked, filling the cabin with the smell of warm cake and caramelizing honey.

Silena stood in the middle of it with her hands on her hips, bun half-collapsed, pencil gone, flour on her cheek, and felt the particular fullness of a plan working.

"Clean," she announced. "Ten minutes. Then stacking and figs. Percy, you sweep. Clarisse, you do the bowl. Annabeth, you — you can copy the recipe for the wall margin if you want. Every good map needs something sweet in the corner."

Annabeth looked up from where she was wiping peach juice off the board, surprised and pleased. "Can I?"

"Of course. But wash first. Maps hate sticky."

Annabeth washed, dried on a clean towel, opened her blue notebook to a fresh page, and wrote in her neat hand while Percy swept flour into a pile and Clarisse scoured the steel bowl with hot water and a rag, arms to the elbow in suds. The cabin settled into the rhythm of cleaning: broom hush, water slap, the oven tick, Silena humming the sailing song with no words.

Percy swept flour toward the door, sneezing twice. "How does it get everywhere? It's flour. It doesn't have legs."

"It migrates," Clarisse said, rinsing the bowl. "Like Stolls to unattended dessert."

"Speaking of," Percy said, leaning on his broom. "Are we sharing this tower or defending it?"

"Sharing," Silena said firmly. "It's too big for Cabin Three. That's the point. If we made it just for us, we'd be sick and sorry. If we make it for everybody, it's devotion."

"Devotion with figs," Clarisse said, drying the bowl with a snap of the towel. "Fine. But I get to cut it. Somebody has to be the authority with the knife or Connor will take the whole top."

"You can be the authority," Silena said. "You were born the authority."

Third layer came out perfect — golden, level, fragrant. They stacked it, brushed it with the last of the syrup, spread the last of the honey, arranged the figs in a ring, scattered walnuts, drizzled honey in thin lines that ran down the sides on purpose. It stood crooked and glorious on the wooden board, three layers high, smelling of lemon and honey and September peaches, wobbling slightly when Percy breathed near it.

They stood around it, four of them in a flour-dusted kitchen with the pool breathing at the end of the hall and the light gold on the tile, and nobody said anything for a moment, because some things did not need a vote.

"It's ridiculous," Annabeth said at last.

"It's elaborate," Silena corrected. "Ridiculous is a kind of elaborate that worked."

"It's beautiful," Percy said. "It looks like it should have a flag."

Clarisse reached out, straightened a fig with one fingertip, stepped back. "It's good," she said, which from her was a parade. "Wrap it. We chill it at the pavilion. Then dinner."

Silena covered the tower with a clean cloth, tied it loosely. Percy swept the last of the flour. Annabeth copied the recipe in full, with Silena dictating — olive oil, not butter, honey, not sugar, lemon, not vanilla — and titled it in the margin: CABIN THREE TOWER, THURSDAY, with a small sketch of three stacked layers and a flag on top that Percy had suggested and Annabeth had drawn to humor him.

"Okay," Silena said, hands on her hips, looking at her sticky, floury, beloved crew. "Change shirts. Wash faces. Carry the tower down like it's a baby dragon. Everybody gets to hold one corner of the board."

Clarisse La Rue did not mend things where people could see her doing it.

It was a rule she had made at eleven, when Silena had cried over a torn quilt and Clarisse had fixed it at midnight with stitches so crooked they looked like lightning, and Silena had cried harder because somebody had tried. Since then she mended at odd hours, in odd places: spear straps in the arena loft, sail patches in the boathouse after hours, hems in Tempest Hall with the door open so the tile would not sweat.

Thursday afternoon, with the tower chilling at the pavilion under Ms. O'Fee's suspicious guard and the second coat of canoe paint tacking in the sun, she sat on the end of Percy's bunk with his camp shirt in her lap and a needle in her hand, and she was glad the cabin was empty.

The shirt was the faded orange one with the trident washed white, left sleeve hem coming undone in a long loose curl. He had been meaning to fix it for weeks. He had mentioned it twice, the way fourteen-year-old boys mentioned things they did not know how to do — sideways, as jokes. The hem flapped when he ran. It caught on his spear strap. It made him look, Clarisse thought, like somebody nobody was looking after, which was false and therefore intolerable.

She had washed it first, because mending dirty cloth was disrespect. Now she threaded the needle — blue thread, because the spare blue shirt thread was what she had, and because blue on orange was honest, not hidden — and began with small even stitches, the way the forge matron had taught her when she was nine and furious about a torn sleeve.

The tile was cool through her cargo pants. The pool breathed at the end of the hall. Through the high windows the afternoon light came in long shafts full of dust. The visitor towels hung on their hooks. Percy's bunk was made with hospital corners Silena had taught him in June, pillow fluffed, quilt folded back. On the pillow sat the folded wax-paper wrapper that still smelled faintly of honey, which he kept for reasons nobody asked about. Beside the bunk, his damp sneakers sat paired, bows sideways.

Clarisse stitched. In, out, even, small. Her hands knew the work. Her braid hung over her shoulder, tied off with a strip of sailcloth. Her own camp shirt, sleeves cut short, had a fresh flour handprint on the shoulder where Silena had grabbed her during the stacking. She had not washed it off.

Footsteps in the hall. She did not look up. She knew the walk — bare feet, polite to the ground, pausing at the pool edge out of habit.

Percy stopped in the doorway. He wore the spare blue shirt she kept rolled at the bottom of her satchel, sleeves cut jagged, hem frayed, too big in the shoulders and right everywhere else. She had given it to him at the north beach weeks ago for the wind, and he kept wearing it over the orange one when the evenings turned cool, and she had never asked for it back, which was the entire point and also a secret.

He looked at her sitting on his bunk with his shirt in her lap and went very still.

"What are you doing," he said.

"Mending," Clarisse said, without looking up. "Your hem's coming undone. You walk around like a scarecrow and people think Cabin Three doesn't own needles."

He came in slowly, sat on Silena's bunk across the aisle so he would not jostle her hands. "You didn't have to — I was going to — Silena said she'd show me —"

"Silena shows you everything. I'm showing you this." She tied off a stitch, bit the thread — no, cut it with her knife, because biting thread was for people with good teeth and patience — and held the hem up to check. Even. Small. Blue on orange, visible if you looked. "There. Don't pull it. Wash cold. If it goes again, bring it to me, not the laundry. Laundry eats shirts."

He took the shirt, looked at the blue stitches, ran his thumb over them. He was quiet for a moment, which on him meant the words were coming from further down than usual.

"Thank you," he said.

"Don't make it weird."

"I'm not making it weird. I'm noticing it." He smiled, a little crooked. "Blue thread. That's — that's on purpose, right?"

"It's what I had," Clarisse said, which was true and also not the whole truth. She stood, rolled the needle into a scrap of cloth, tucked it in her pocket. "Also it's Cabin Three colors. Orange and blue. So people know whose scarecrow you are."

He laughed, surprised, and pulled the spare blue shirt straight where it hung over his knees. "I was going to give this back. After dinner. Washed."

"Keep it," she said, too fast. Then, leveler, "It's the spare. That's what it's for. Wind turns, you get cold, you get sniffly, I'm not nursing a sniffly brother through sparring. Everybody knows that."

"Everybody knows," he agreed. He folded the mended shirt carefully, set it on his bunk. "Clarisse."

"What."

"Did you — when you were nine — did anybody mend for you?"

The question landed in the middle of the cabin and sat there. The pool breathed. Dust turned in the light shafts. Clarisse stood with her hands in her pockets, looking at the visitor towels, at the mosaic hippocampus glinting through water, at anything that was not him.

"No," she said at last, blunt as a hammer. "Storm over the lake. Trident in the sand. Welcome to Cabin Three. Then nothing. I learned to sew from the forge matron because I tore my sleeve on a spear rack and bled on it and she said I was too old to bleed on my own clothes." She shrugged, small. "It's fine. I learned."

"It's not fine," Percy said quietly. "But — thank you for learning. Because now I know."

She looked at him then, fifteen to fourteen, sister to brother, soldier to sea kid who swam like water was letting him win. Her face did the thing it did when she had not cleared an expression with anybody — the softening, the letdown, the small private undoing.

"You're not going anywhere tonight," she said. It was not a question, but it wanted an answer.

"I'm not going anywhere tonight," he said. "I'm here. Rule, right? If I go somewhere, I come back and tell you where I went."

"That's the rule," she said. "Not the ledger. Just that." She picked up her clipboard from the footlocker, more for something to hold than for business. "Now get your shoes. We're supposed to check the canoe cover before dinner and you're coming because you hold adequate and somebody has to carry the rope bucket."

"I hold adequate," he said, standing, grinning. "That's my epitaph."

"It's your performance review," she said, and cuffed him lightly on the shoulder as they went out, the Clarisse version of everything, and he let himself be cuffed, which was the Percy version of everything back.

Behind them the mended shirt lay on the bunk with its blue stitches bright on orange, and the spare blue shirt went out the door on his back, and the cabin kept both, the way it kept towels and quilts and third bunks, patient, in trust.

Annabeth Grace checked the north trail at four o'clock because checking was a kind of company.

She stood at the bend where the path came out alongside the water, where the pines opened and you could see down the whole length of the canoe lake to where the willows closed over the cove. The afternoon light lay low and gold across the freshwater, turning the small chop to beaten silver-green. The air smelled of pine and sun-warmed rock and the faint sweet rot of late September. Her gray camp shirt, washed hastily after the baking to get the flour off, still held a green thumbprint on the pocket flap from yesterday's canoe paint and a fresh honey stickiness at one cuff she had missed. Shorts, white sneakers double-knotted, ponytail re-tied high and neat. Her blue notebook rode under her arm, pencil behind her ear.

No dust on the trail. No loud shoes arguing with the ground. Thalia was still out on the boundary line, day three of four, past the old quarry by now, probably yelling at a cairn and winning. She would walk back tomorrow, Friday, with bark in her boots and a sealed report and four days of trail mix crumbs in a sock, and the empty seat at breakfast would fill, and Annabeth would stop looking up to be checked.

Annabeth had told her to go. That was the whole story and it was allowed to be boring. The panel sat in Chiron's office in the flat drawer where the old surveys lived, logged in his neat hand, where the siblings could get to it if they needed to. Nobody was re-litigating it. Tyson's letter sat folded in Silena's pocket. The canoe slept under its tarp in the boathouse. Today was for hands and honey. Tomorrow was for sisters coming home.

Still, she checked. Thirty-one times a day had become zero times a day, and zero left a shape.

She turned back toward camp, notebook tapping her thigh, and took the long way past the Zeus cabin to do the sisterly thing she had planned that morning while whisking cream.

The Zeus cabin stood blue-trimmed and crackling faintly at the eaves, the way it always did, smelling of ozone and clean linen. Inside, Thalia's bunk sat made — Annabeth had made it that morning, hospital corners, blanket aired over the rail in the sun, pillow fluffed — and the joke granite sat centered on the pillow: a smooth gray stone the size of a hen's egg, with GRANITE DOESN'T MOVE written on it in Annabeth's small capitals, a quote from Thalia's own pack list. Beside it, folded, lay a fresh camp shirt, a pair of thick socks, and a square of wax paper holding two figs and a wedge of honey cake scrap saved from the leveling, because sisters who walked four days of trees came home hungry and too proud to say so.

Annabeth stood in the empty cabin and adjusted the stone a quarter inch to center, the way she adjusted everything, and then, feeling watched by nobody, adjusted it back, because Thalia liked things a little off-center, to prove she was not her sister.

"You'll throw it at me," Annabeth said aloud, to the bunk, to the stone, to the afternoon light coming through the high window. "And I'll deserve it. And then you'll eat the figs and pretend you weren't hungry."

The cabin did not answer. Cabins never did, until people. She sat on the edge of Thalia's bunk for a moment, feeling the mattress give, looking at the pair of boots by the footlocker, caked with old mud, waiting for fresh mud. Eighteen years old, her sister. Dry and teasing and protective in a way that sounded like insult and landed like shelter. Thalia who had taught her to sight along a rule with one eye closed. Thalia who checked her thirty-one times a day until Annabeth, precise and fourteen and tired of being measured, had told her to stop, and Thalia had stopped, because that was what love did when asked clearly.

Annabeth pulled her notebook open and flipped past the north-shore survey, past Olive's culvert sketch copied in careful lines, past the canoe margin with O.N. initialed in the corner, to a fresh page. She wrote, fast, in pencil: FOR THALIA — FRIDAY. 1. Trail dust check a.m. 2. Blanket back on bunk p.m. 3. Tower slice saved — hide from Stolls. 4. Ask about cairn. Listen, don't fix. 5. Tell her about peel. She stared at the last line, smiled despite herself, added: 6. Let her check. It was a list, because she was Annabeth, and lists were how she held things she could not hold with hands.

She tore the page out, folded it once, tucked it under the granite stone where Thalia would find it when she threw the stone. Then she stood, straightened the blanket, touched the boots once for luck, and went out, pulling the door to behind her.

Outside, the light had tipped toward gold-green through the willows. The dinner bell was being argued over up the hill. Olive ran past with her red notebook, shouting about a culvert that told truth, Marcus following with rope over one shoulder, steady as weather. Annabeth fell in behind them for a stretch, hands in her pockets, notebook under her arm, letting herself be one of the map kids' cartographer for a while instead of a sister missing her sister, which was also practice.

At the Big House wall, she stopped to check the day's work: the north shore in clean blue pencil, Olive's culvert in wavy lines, Marcus's rope scale in neat ticks, the small canoe in the margin with O. Nakamura credited. Beside it, in her own hand from that morning, the recipe title: CABIN THREE TOWER, THURSDAY. She touched the canoe once with her fingertip, lightly, the way Silena had touched the tarp over the new tooth.

"Good wall," she said to Olive, who glowed, and to Marcus, who nodded, and then she headed down the reed path toward the north beach, because Silena had said the tower needed ten minutes to chill and Percy had said the paint needed ten minutes to tack and Annabeth had ten minutes of her own to spend, and she knew exactly where she wanted to spend them.

Percy Jackson knew the sound of Annabeth's shoes on sand before he saw her.

He sat on the flat stone at the north beach with his knees drawn up, shoes off, toes in warm sand, wearing the spare blue shirt over his swim shorts because the wind had turned off the freshwater and the afternoon was cooling toward evening. The canoe lake lay out beyond the shallows, gold-green and riffled, hissing in small clear rushes. The air smelled of sun-warmed rock and late summer. Up the hill the forge hammered in short bursts, stopped, hammered again. The private spot — everybody knew it and nobody went there without asking — held only him, and a gull-feather, and the tower's honey smell still on his hands from the stacking.

He had come down to check what Silena called the lake's mood, which was a joke and also not a joke. The panel sat in Chiron's drawer. The canoe slept under its tarp. Tyson was gone two weeks out, returning in two weeks, visiting not living, forge and mother and nine hundred brothers. Thalia was out until tomorrow. The day had been flour and tasting and teasing, and his shoulders ached from whisking, and his heart felt full in a way that made him want to sit quiet beside water for ten minutes.

Annabeth came around the willow point with her notebook under her arm and sand already in her sneakers. She wore the gray shirt with the green thumbprint, sleeves shoved past the elbows, honey at one cuff. Her ponytail had come loose again. Her pencil was behind her ear. She looked tired the way she got when she had been precise all morning, and glad the way she got when precision had worked.

"Ten minutes," she said, by way of hello, sitting beside him on the warm stone, shoulders touching. "The pavilion has the tower. Ms. O'Fee has the oven. Olive has the wall. Nobody needs us for ten minutes."

"Yeah," he said. "I was just — checking the lake's mood."

"And?"

"Behaving," he said, echoing Tyson's letter question without meaning to. "Green and clean. Sweet water. Treats canvas kind."

She smiled, leaned her head against his shoulder for a moment, just the weight of it. Her hair smelled of lemon zest and honey. His hand found hers on the warm stone, paint-flecked fingers lacing without hurry, flour still white in his creases, honey sticky on her cuff. They sat like that while the water hissed in, withdrew.

"You did good today," she said at last, looking out at the water. "With the tower. With Olive. With — the rule. Clarisse told me about the rule at lunch. Coming back and telling her. She said it like it was her idea, which means it's yours and she adopted it."

He laughed, surprised. "She said that at lunch?"

"She said it while stealing my peach. Quote: Jackson holds adequate and eats bread with sawdust in it, which is why the rule." Annabeth turned her hand over, palm to palm, thumb along his knuckles. "She's scared you'll leave. Not to the sea. Just — grow past. Same fear Thalia has, different shoes. Thalia's scared I'll stop needing her."

"Are you going to?" he asked. "Stop needing her?"

"No. I'm going to need her differently. Ask first. Walk next to me. That's free forever." She said it precise, then softened. "I made her bunk. Put granite on the pillow. Saved her figs. Wrote her a list so she'd know I missed her without having to say it sideways."

"That's sisterly," he said. "That's — that's good, Annabeth."

"It's practice," she said. "Silena's right. You don't prove it once." She looked at him then, directly, gray eyes clear. "You choose it. Every day. The cabin. The sisters. Me. You told me that in the cove. I believe you on land too."

"I choose it," he said. "Every day." He put his arm around her, carefully, the way you put your arm around someone on a warm stone with the whole lake watching and no one watching at all. His chin rested against her hair. Her hand stayed in his. It was not a kiss. It was the thing before and after kisses, the holding that made kisses mean something.

After a while she sat up, though she left her hand where it was, and said, "Kiss me before we go back. The tower needs us, but not yet."

So he did. A quiet kiss, sun-warm and honey-smelling, brief and certain. Her hand came up to his cheek, cool fingers, flour-dusted. His thumb rested at her temple the way it had in the cove. The lake hissed on sand. Then they broke apart and stayed with foreheads together, and she smiled with her eyes closed.

"Okay," she said. "Now the tower needs us."

"Obviously," he said, and she laughed, and they stood, brushed sand off, picked up shoes, and went back up the reed path hand in hand until the pavilion came in sight, when they let go by mutual unspoken agreement, because Clarisse would throw something, and because some things were theirs to keep for ten minutes before sharing.

Behind them the north beach kept their footprints beside Silena's morning ones, and the lake took the shallows and smoothed them, patient, the way it kept everything.

Silena felt dinner coming the way she felt everything worth feeling, in her hands first.

The pavilion at six held the particular gold-green light of late September, slanting through the pines and laying long bars across the tables. The air smelled of evening fish and warm bread and the faint pine smoke from the braziers being lit early against the cool. She stood behind the serving counter in a clean camp shirt — same faded orange, hastily changed after the baking, flour washed from her face but still white in her eyebrows where Percy had pointed and laughed — with the tower on its wooden board in front of her, cloth just lifted, and felt her palms tingle the way they did before a launch.

The tower had chilled. The honey had set. The drizzle held. Three layers, straight enough to be proud of, crooked enough to be theirs. Figs glistening, walnuts toasted hastily in the pavilion oven and smelling like autumn, peaches peeking between layers like secrets.

Clarisse stood beside her with her arms crossed, clipboard nowhere, knife in hand — the good one, washed three times — ready to be the authority. She had changed too, into the other cargo pants without the sailcloth patch, braid re-done tight, flour handprint finally brushed off her shoulder, though Silena had seen her look at it twice before brushing, as if deciding whether to keep it.

Percy came up the hill with Annabeth and the map kids, carrying the board's spare stand, wearing the mended orange shirt now — blue stitches bright on the hem — with the spare blue shirt tied around his waist for the evening cool. His hair was damp from a quick rinse in the pool, tied back, flour gone from his nose. Annabeth walked beside him with her notebook closed, pencil down, hair re-tied, honey still at one cuff because some things survived washing, and Olive bounced ahead with her red notebook, announcing to anyone who would listen that there was cake that was a tower and syrup that was democracy.

The empty seat at the Zeus table sat empty. Argus glanced at it, noted, moved on. Connor told a new kid Thalia had joined the circus, and the new kid believed him, and Annabeth, passing, said, "She's on the boundary line until tomorrow. She walks like she wins," and the new kid wrote that down, and Connor looked abashed, which was rare and worth seeing.

Chiron came through on his slow circuit, hooves quiet on the pavilion stone, and paused at the counter. He looked at the tower, at Silena's floury eyebrows, at Clarisse's knife held like a scepter, and smiled the deep lined smile he kept for days that had a beginning and an end.

"Thursday," he said. "Cabin Three?"

"Cabin Three plus one," Silena said. "Plus eleven, if Olive counts the wall."

"I count the wall," Olive said solemnly. "I count drainage and peel and teeth."

Chiron nodded gravely. "Then it is a large plus. The drawer holds what it holds" — he glanced, just briefly, toward the Big House, toward his office where the panel sat logged in his neat hand beside the tally copy and the old surveys, settled, not for tonight — "and tonight holds what it holds. May I have a slice when the cutting allows?"

"You get the first slice that isn't ours," Clarisse said, blunt, which from her was honor. "Structural integrity requires testing."

"All structures require testing," Chiron agreed, and moved on, and Silena felt her shoulders drop a full inch, because permission had been given and background could stay background.

Dinner ran the way Thursday dinners ran when Thalia was gone: a little louder, a little looser, Annabeth checking the trailhead between courses without meaning to, Percy saving the peach story for later, Clarisse eating with her knife across her plate like a border.

Then Silena stepped forward with the tower, and Clarisse said, in her best carrying voice, "Cabin Three tower. Three layers. Lemon syrup. Whipped honey. Peaches. Figs. If you take the top fig, I'm taking your dessert tomorrow," and the pavilion laughed, and the cutting began.

It was glorious chaos. Clarisse cut with the authority of a general dividing territory, each slice tall and wobbling, honey running, peaches sliding, figs toppling. Percy held plates, Annabeth called names, Silena drizzled extra honey where asked and where not asked. Olive got a slice with extra peach because she had noticed peel. Marcus got a slice with extra honey because he had said nothing and carried everything sharp. The Stolls got smaller slices because Clarisse was the authority and authorities remembered. Chiron got the testing slice, even and dignified, and pronounced it structurally unsound and delicious, and Ms. O'Fee, wings furled, accepted a sliver on a side towel and ate it behind the counter with her eyes closed, and said nothing, which was praise.

Silena moved through it all with a towel over one arm, refilling, wiping, watching. She watched Percy eat his slice standing up, crumbs on the blue stitches, eyes closed on the first bite. She watched Annabeth eat hers in small precise bites, considering the crumb the way she considered paint, then closing her notebook to eat the last two bites with her fingers, which was rare. She watched Clarisse eat hers last, standing behind the counter, leaning against the post, taking big unhurried bites and licking honey off her thumb without apology, and when Olive asked if the tower was going on the map, Clarisse said, "It's going in our stomachs, Nakamura," and Olive wrote that down too.

This was the purpose. Not the drawer. Not the seam or the rings or the tally. Those sat where the siblings could get to them, settled. Today was for the thing you could hold: flour and honey and lemon and heat, made sweet by hands that loved the people who would eat it.

Silena thought about her grandmother's line — you don't prove love once, you feed people every day until they believe it — and looked at her brother with honey on his chin and her sister with honey on her thumb and her sister-in-all-but-blood with honey at her cuff, and thought: belief was coming along nicely.

When the tower was gone to crumbs and the board held only smears and two walnuts, Silena wrapped the walnuts in wax paper and tucked them in her pocket for Thalia tomorrow, with the figs already saved. For the sister coming home. Practice included the absent.

"Okay," she said, clapping flourless hands. "Dishes. All of us."

And they did — Percy washing, Annabeth drying, Clarisse stacking, Olive carrying nothing sharp, Marcus carrying everything sharp — while the pavilion lanterns ticked on and the lake went rose at the edges beyond the pines.

Percy carried the quilt down to the dock because somebody had to, and because he had lost the race in August and winning had never rescinded the duty.

Evening had come cool and clear. The pavilion lanterns burned gold up the hill. The canoe lake lay flat and doubled, freshwater lapping at the pilings, smelling green and cold and clean. He wore the mended orange camp shirt with its blue stitches, swim shorts, bare feet now because the dock demanded it, the spare blue shirt over the top because the wind had turned and Clarisse had looked at his arms once and said nothing, which meant everything. In his pockets: the hair tie, the white pebble, the pencil stub, and the folded square of notebook paper Annabeth had slipped him yesterday at the boathouse — a love note, read last night under the quilt with a flashlight, read again this morning, kept because it still smelled faintly of paint.

Clarisse came down with the tin cup and the satchel, boots in one hand, braid over one shoulder, cargo pants rolled to the knee. Silena came with the quilt over her shoulders like a cape and the wax-paper walnuts in her pocket and her hair down for once, falling over her shoulders, catching the lantern light. Annabeth came last, notebook finally left on the Big House wall shelf, pencil behind her ear anyway out of habit, gray shirt with the green thumbprint, honey at the cuff washed at last, sneakers in one hand, bare feet quiet on the ramp.

No Thalia. One more night. The empty seat had been loud at dinner and was loud here too, in the space at the end of the dock where four people did not sit. Annabeth glanced up the trail once, out of habit, then sat, letting the habit be what it was: love checking.

They sat at the end of the dock with their feet in the water, the quilt over their shoulders, all four under one quilt, which was the only way to share a quilt and the way Cabin Three had always done it, plus one who was Cabin Three by choice and map. The water was warm on their feet from the day and cool underneath, the way the lake held its summer deep down and gave it back slowly.

Nobody said anything for a while. The loon called once from the cove and did not call again. Up the hill somebody was tuning a guitar badly. The heron stood on the sunken skiff in the willow shade, grey and still, one leg folded, watching or sleeping, nobody knew which, and Percy felt the familiar attention, as though the lake had posted a witness, and let it be.

Silena leaned her head against Clarisse's shoulder, and Clarisse let her, and Annabeth leaned against Percy's side, head on his shoulder for three seconds, then six, then stayed, because Thalia was out past the quarry and the Grace-sister lean could be borrowed. Percy put his arm around her, carefully, the way you did on a dock with the whole lake watching and no one watching at all. His hand found hers under the quilt, paint-flecked fingers lacing, honey-warm.

"Float," Silena said softly, after a while. "Ears under. One true thing. In the lake. Because it's Thursday and the tower worked and we're here."

Clarisse looked at the water, dark gold after bright gold, September cold at the edges. "It's September, Silena."

"It's still warm. It was warm at midnight at the end of summer."

"It's October in two weeks."

"Then we've got two weeks," Percy said, echoing himself from the north beach weeks ago, grinning when Clarisse glanced at him. "What? It's tradition. You taught me. Group Float. Somebody floats, somebody holds the ankle, somebody says one true thing, nobody asks."

Annabeth sat up, considering the water with a cartographer's eye. "Ten minutes," she said. "The night doesn't need us for ten minutes."

"Ten minutes per occurrence," Clarisse said, to the lake, in her best carrying voice. "Retroactive. Free for Poseidon cabin. Always was. Plus one."

"Plus one," Silena echoed.

"Plus one," Percy echoed, and Annabeth smiled with her eyes closed and said it too, quiet, precise, glad: "Plus one."

They went in off the end of the dock, four of them, in a spread of ripples that ran the whole width of the light, and the freshwater came up over knees and waists and chests, and Percy lay back, and the lake took him, colder, cleaner, more honest than salt, the way a room is more honest than a palace.

They made their line. Percy on the outside, Silena in the middle, Clarisse closest to the dock, Annabeth beside Clarisse with a hand on the piling to steady, and each holding the ankle of the one in front, and the water moving gently under them all, and the sky going violet above the pines, and the first stars coming out over the far shore.

Silena went first. She took a breath and went under, hair spreading in the green light, said the true thing with her ears full of lake, stayed down long, came up shining, no explanation, because that was the rule.

Clarisse went next. She put her head under without drama, came straight back up, shook her hair out, said, "One," did not explain it. Her fingers tightened once on Silena's ankle, then let go.

Annabeth went next. She went under neatly, the way she did everything, came up with water streaming, said nothing at all, which was allowed — floating and listening was most of it — and squeezed Percy's ankle once, hard, the way sisters squeezed when words were elsewhere, out on a boundary line, walking home tomorrow.

Percy went last. He filled his lungs, went down, let the lake close over him, dock posts going up like pillars, warm and cold layers dividing near his ribs, and in the green roar he said it, one true thing, the ticket price, into water that would not tell anybody. He came up. He did not explain it.

They floated a while in the line, four heads and a spread of hands, held by a thing smaller than any of them. The sea had taught Percy survival. The lake had taught him how to be held. Thursday had taught him the rest: love as daily practice, feeding people until they believed it, mending hems with blue thread, saving figs for sisters coming home, checking trails without apology.

He said that part out loud, and then he explained it, because Silena was already crying a little, quietly, in the way she had, and because Clarisse was looking at him with an expression doing three things at once, and because Annabeth's hand was warm in his under the water, and because the rule about not explaining was tradition, not law.

"It's a good lake," he said. "That's all. It's a good lake, and you gave it to me, and the tower was good, and the thread is blue, and tomorrow Thalia comes home, and I don't know how to say thank you for a thing like that, so I'm saying this instead."

"You're a freak," Clarisse said, thickly.

"I'm your freak."

"You're absolutely my freak." She splashed him, flat-handed, freshwater in his ear and down his neck, and he splashed her back, and Silena came up in the middle laughing her big round water laugh, and Annabeth, precise even in splashing, got them both with one efficient handful, and for a minute the four of them thrashed in the shallows like children, like siblings, like people given a lake who had decided, on purpose, to be grateful.

They crawled back onto the dock, dripping, sat with feet in the water, quilt over shoulders, four under one quilt. Percy put the spare blue shirt back on over his head, dry where folded, smelling of lemon oil and lake and canvas satchel, said nothing about it, neither did Clarisse, which was how gifts worked in Cabin Three.

Silena was the first to notice that the towel beneath them was still damp. She had been pretending not to think about tomorrow's washing, and the damp patch was evidence that pretending had accomplished nothing.

"Everybody up for one minute," she said.

Clarisse groaned. "We just sat down."

"Then you won't have far to go."

They stood with their feet on the boards while Silena wrung the towel into the lake. Percy held the quilt out of her way. Annabeth fetched the clothespins from the satchel, which was surprising only until she admitted she'd brought them for the canoe tarp. The towel went on the line stretched between two posts, crooked but secure.

"Does this count as another kitchen operation?" Percy asked.

"It counts as not leaving mildew for tomorrow," Silena said.

Clarisse moved one peg a few inches to keep the fabric from touching a rough knot in the rope. She did it without comment, which was her usual method of announcing that she agreed.

Back on the dock, Silena opened the wax paper that had held the saved walnuts. She had promised them to Thalia, who was due back on the trail, but the packet was marked with greasy honey fingerprints and the paper had begun to tear. She found a clean envelope in her bag and wrote THALIA on the front in block letters, then slipped the nuts inside.

"Don't eat those," she warned Percy.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were looking."

"I look at most things."

He watched her tuck the envelope away. The empty place in their little group was not something the others needed to fill tonight. It had a name on a packet and a promise attached to it. Silena could carry that upstairs, put it where she wouldn't forget, and greet Thalia with the walnuts tomorrow. They stayed another ten minutes, until Clarisse declared the towel dry enough to retrieve and Percy lost the argument about who would fold it.
