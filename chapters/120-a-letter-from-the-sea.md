## Chapter 120: A Letter from the Sea

The sea had brought Percy mail before, but never with quite so much ceremony from the shoreline.
The bucket of apples had been Silena's idea. The orchard had more fruit than the kitchen could use at once, and she wanted to pick the firm ones before the next rain bruised them against the branches. Clarisse had volunteered to carry the bucket, then Percy had taken the handle because it was heavy. Neither sister had let him carry it alone for long.

“Those on top are for the kitchen,” Clarisse said, checking the apples as they walked. “The bruised ones go in the other bag.”

“They're all for the kitchen,” Silena said. “Some are just better for sauce.”

“Then why are they in the same bucket?”

“Because we haven't sorted them yet.”

Percy adjusted the handle against his palm. It pinched where the wicker had worn smooth. “I can take the heavier side.”

“You are taking both sides,” Clarisse said.

“It's a two-person handle.”

“It's a bucket.”

Silena reached for it. “Give me the other end.”

He let her take the handle. The weight evened out, and the bucket stopped tilting toward his leg. One apple rolled over the rim and dropped to the path. Clarisse caught it before it reached the grass, inspected the skin, and put it back at the top.

“That one is bruised,” she said.

“It's a tiny mark,” Silena replied.

“It's still bruised.”

“It can still be eaten.”

“I didn't say it couldn't. Put it where we can use it first.”

Silena shifted the fruit so the marked apple sat near the rim. Percy watched them arrange the rest by firmness, then took the bucket only after Clarisse moved her hand to the far side. He didn't need to prove he could carry every apple by himself. It was a small thing, but the weight stopped pulling at his shoulder.

They followed the path toward the shore, the orchard's green shade falling behind them. The bucket smelled like leaves and sweet skin. The sea's salt smell grew stronger as the trees opened, and wind moved through the apples, making the loose ones knock softly against the wicker.

He and his sisters were carrying a bucket of apples back from the orchard when the tide reached up the beach, stopped shy of their shoes, and left a green glass bottle on the sand. A thin loop of silver cord held its stopper in place. No message in the water, no rainbow, no voice coming from the waves. Only the bottle, shining in the sun as if someone had polished it for the occasion.

Clarisse eyed it from a careful distance. “If it bites, you’re the one with the water powers.”

“I thought you were the one with the spear,” Percy said.

“I’m carrying apples.”

“Then it’s lucky I’m carrying the bucket.”

Silena set her end of the handle down. “Maybe it’s a present.”

“People don’t put presents in bottles,” Clarisse said.

“They do if the present is a letter.”

“Who puts a letter in a bottle when they can use an Iris-message?”

Percy had already crouched to pick it up. The bottle was cool, though it had been sitting in the sun. He knew the silver cord: fine as sewing thread, braided with tiny knots to keep it from slipping loose underwater. A pale shell seal covered the stopper. Amphitrite’s mark curved across it in dark blue wax.

His breath caught. Afternoon light slanted across them, the bucket between them, the orchard behind and the sea beyond the dry sand. For a moment, he could hear the palace corridors in the bottle’s silence—the distant hum of currents through stone, a little bell announcing someone’s arrival, his mother’s voice from another room.

“Is it from Amphitrite?” Silena asked, softly now.

Percy nodded. “Looks like it.”

Clarisse set the apples aside. Her face had gone still in the particular way that meant she was trying not to let a feeling make the first move. “Open it.”

The seal gave way with a small crack. Inside was a single sheet of thick cream paper folded in thirds. A few drops of seawater clung to the glass, but the paper was perfectly dry. Percy turned it over. The handwriting on the front was unmistakable: neat at first, then leaning more sharply to the right as if the words had become impatient to reach him.

He knew her handwriting from letters since his arrival, recipes with tiny drawings of fish in the margins. Triton had told him she once tucked notes into his schoolbooks, when every day away from home was an expedition. He could picture the gesture without remembering those mornings. Once, homesick at camp, he had tried to make his own letters look like hers. The result had been a page of crooked loops and Clarisse asking whether the ink had survived a shipwreck.

“You can read it to us,” Silena said. She was smiling, but her fingers had closed around the edge of her sleeve.

“Yeah.” Percy unfolded the paper. “I’ll read it out loud.”

Clarisse leaned against the nearest driftwood log. “Good. I don’t trust the bottle to summarize.”

With both hands, Percy held the letter. The paper smelled faintly of salt and crushed flowers he couldn’t name. The handwriting filled the page, with one line added in the margin and a small ink blot near the signature.

“Dear Percy, Clarisse, and Silena,” he read.

Silena glanced at Clarisse, then back to the page. Clarisse’s arms remained folded, but she did not look away.

“The message I sent you was spoken in a hurry, between one task and the next. I wanted the invitation to arrive in a form you could keep, and to say more plainly what I meant.

"I would be delighted to welcome you to Atlantis, whenever the three of you are ready to choose a time. Clarisse and Silena, this invitation is yours as much as Percy’s. You are not being asked to attend as witnesses to his homecoming. I would like to know you as yourselves, if you wish to let me.

“You may bring the friends you choose to have beside you. I will not set a date for you or ask you to hurry your answer. No ceremony is planned. I would rather have an ordinary meal with you than a perfect reception. If there are parts of the palace you would rather not visit, say so. If you need a quiet hour, ask. If you would prefer the garden to the great hall, we will go to the garden.

“Percy, I miss hearing you come down the west stairs too quickly. Clarisse, Chiron sent your questions, and I would be glad to learn what else would help you feel prepared. Silena, Percy told me about your painted shells; I would love to see your work. I have been thinking of you all.

“Bring nothing you do not want to bring. A gift is never required between people who are glad to see each other. But if you decide to bring a piece of your life at camp, I would be honored to receive it.

“Take your time. There is no wrong way to come home, and no wrong answer if the time is not right.

“With all my love,

“Amphitrite.”

For a little while, the only sound was the water easing back down the beach, shells clinking softly as the foam pulled them.

Percy lowered the page. He had known what the invitation would say, in a general sense. The message had already made the offer. The letter did not change the facts, and still it changed something. It put the words in ink. It gave them corners, a fold, a place to rest. The invitation could now be read again without asking the lake to turn gold.
The line about the west stairs came back to him. When he pictured them, he pictured the palace as he had seen it on recent visits: the turn in the hall, the change in the current near the landing, the sound his feet made when he ran too quickly. He did not remember the school mornings Amphitrite was missing. That was something Triton had told him, a story about his mother trying to tuck notes into his books. He could feel the affection in the handwriting without pretending the old mornings belonged to his memory.

The distinction sat quietly inside him. Amphitrite's affection was real whether or not he could remember every place it had lived. The paper did not ask him to manufacture a childhood. It let his mother miss a habit and let him hear that she did.

And her letter had come to Clarisse and Silena by name. They could answer it in their own voices, if they chose. He didn't have to explain the invitation before either sister had the chance to read it for herself.

Silena was the first to speak. “You told her about the shells.”

Her smile had gone watery. Percy couldn’t tell if she was about to laugh or cry, so he said nothing that would force her to pick one.

“The purple one,” he said.

“It was supposed to be a sunset.”

“It looked like a grape with a crown.”

“It was a regal sunset, obviously.” Silena wiped under one eye with her knuckle, salt sharp on her skin. “You told her all that.”

“Of course I did,” Percy said. He could picture a shell on a shelf in the palace, beside some polished treasure or another, still purple under the blue light. “She asks what you make, not just how training went.”

“Tell her about that awful fish-shaped cushion here,” Clarisse said. “Maybe she knows how to get rid of it.”

“You like that cushion,” Silena said.

“I like throwing it at Percy.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

Clarisse’s gaze moved to the letter. “She wrote my name.”

It was not a question. Percy looked down at the line where Amphitrite had mentioned the questions Chiron sent. “She did.”

“Not ‘your sister Clarisse.’ Not ‘the other girl.’ Just Clarisse.”

“Yeah.”

Clarisse nodded, once. The nod cost her visible effort. Then she reached toward the page and stopped short of touching it. “Read the part about my questions again.”

Percy glanced at her. “You want the whole letter again?”

“Just that part.”

He found the sentence and read it once more. Clarisse’s mouth twitched.

“Chiron sent the questions I gave him,” she said. “I want her to have them before we talk about going.”

“That makes sense,” Percy said.

Silena glanced at her. “Is that why you were on shore watch arguing with the tide clock?”

“The clock wasn’t slow,” Clarisse said. “It was wrong.”

“You stood in the watch hut and argued with a clock.”

“It was wasting everybody’s time.”

“The clock was measuring time.”

“Badly.”

“I forgot the clock,” Clarisse said. “I didn’t forget getting called into the Big House because I’d pulled it off the wall.”

“It wasn’t bolted properly.”

“You pulled a clock off the wall?”

“It was a small clock.”

“You were on a stool,” Silena said.

“That’s not a measurement.”

The argument had the familiar comfort of a blanket dragged across a room. Percy joined in, asking why nobody had ever told him the story, and Silena accused Clarisse of editing out the parts where she cried because the clock had fallen on her foot. Clarisse insisted she had been angry, not hurt. Silena insisted that the distinction had been difficult to observe while Clarisse was shouting at an instrument of time.
Clarisse reached for another apple, then changed her mind and leaned back against the log. “It was coming loose before I touched it. I didn't pull it off the wall because the clock had offended me.”

“You were on a stool,” Silena said.

“I was trying to get the clock down without letting it fall.”

“It fell.”

“Because it wasn't bolted properly. That was the entire point.”

Percy looked from one sister to the other. “What made you notice it was wrong in the first place?”

Clarisse shrugged. “It was wrong.”

“That isn't an answer.”

“It's the answer I have.” She rubbed the heel of her hand against her palm, as if an old sting might be there. “I was on shore watch. I knew what the clock said, and I knew it didn't match what I was seeing. I said so. Nobody thought it was worth stopping to check until the clock came loose.”

“Did you prove it?” Percy asked.

“I proved it was loose.”

Silena laughed, but not at her. “You also proved that stools are not good ladders.”

“Stools are fine if the clock is small.”

“Not if you're trying to catch it.”

“I didn't say it was a good plan.”

The admission was so flat that Percy couldn't tell whether she was joking. Then Clarisse glanced at the letter again. “I don't want this to become a story about me being difficult. I was trying to get someone to look at a thing that needed looking at.”

“I don't think she wrote it that way,” Silena said.

“Maybe not.” Clarisse's fingers found the paper's edge. “I just don't want a palace invitation to turn me into the girl who came along because Percy asked. If she wants to know me, fine. But I get to decide what she learns.”

“You do,” Percy said.

She gave him a look. “Don't make it a speech.”

“I wasn't.”

“You were about to.”

He closed his mouth. Clarisse's correction had a familiar shape now. He could listen without adding another reassurance to prove he had heard her.

“Good,” she said, and reached for the apple after all.

When the laughter faded, Clarisse finally took the letter. She read the lines with her own name, her expression severe enough to suggest she was examining evidence in a trial. Her thumb rested at the bottom corner.

“She says we can come as ourselves,” Silena said.

“She says she wants to know us,” Clarisse replied. “That’s different.”

“Different how?”

“It means she doesn’t already know us just because she knows Percy.” Clarisse handed the letter back. “That’s good. It also means we get to decide how much we tell her.”

Percy felt the old reflex rise in him: to explain that Amphitrite was kind, that she would be glad to see them, that the palace would be safe. He let the reflex pass before it became a speech. The letter had not promised them a perfect visit. Neither should he.

“You don’t have to decide today,” he said.

Clarisse’s eyebrows drew together. “I know.”

“I’m not trying to talk you into it.”

“I know that too.” She nudged the apple bucket with the side of her boot. “I want to go. I also want to know what I’m walking into before I get there.”

“That seems reasonable.”

“Don’t make it sound like I need permission to be careful.”

“I’m not.” He adjusted his grip on the folded page. “I mean, I get it.”

Clarisse studied him, then gave a short nod. “Good.”

Silena looped her arm through Clarisse’s. “I’m ready to go now.”

“You’re ready to go everywhere now,” Clarisse said.

“That’s because I have a curious spirit.”

“You have a packed bag under your bed.”

“A curious spirit can be prepared.”

“You packed a comb, two ribbons, and three pairs of socks.”

“I had room.”

Percy tucked the letter into the inner pocket of his camp shirt. “We can show Chiron she sent it. He can acknowledge it when we’re ready to answer.”

“And maybe there won’t,” Clarisse said. “We don’t need to turn every sentence into a rule.”

Silena squeezed her arm. “We can do both. Ask what matters and leave the rest for later.”

Clarisse grunted. In Clarisse language, this meant she was considering it.

They picked up the bucket and walked back to camp, still disagreeing about whether a queen’s invitation could count as a summons. Silena said the letter had the softest edges imaginable. Clarisse replied that paper could still issue orders. Percy decided this was a question for Chiron, who had probably been summoned by at least three kinds of paper.

By the time they reached Cabin Three, the afternoon had settled into an easy golden hour. A little breeze came through the open windows. Inside, their three beds were unmade in three different styles: Percy’s blankets had slid to the floor, Silena’s quilt was smoothed in a way that suggested she had made it before breakfast, and Clarisse’s pillow had been folded into a shape that looked designed to withstand impact.

They set the apples on the table, their skins warm from the sun and sweet-tart on the air. Percy took the letter from his pocket and placed it at the center, weighted with a smooth stone so the breeze would not lift it.

Percy looked between them. Silena’s openness and Clarisse’s caution had never fit together neatly, but lately he’d noticed how they made room for each other. Silena gave Clarisse permission to feel more than one thing. Clarisse made sure Silena did not give away more than she meant to. They did not always agree. They stayed close anyway.

“I’m glad she wrote to all of us,” he said.

Clarisse took a bite of apple. “You sound surprised.”

“I thought she might write to me and say I could bring you.”

“That would’ve been bad,” Silena said. “I don’t like being brought anywhere. I can walk.”

“You can swim there,” Percy said.

“I can also walk on land.”

“For now,” Clarisse said. “The basket of socks suggests otherwise.”

Silena read the paragraph with her name again. “I know Atlantis in pieces,” she said. “From your stories, not like somewhere I can walk around without thinking.”

“Some parts feel like that to me too,” Percy said. “I know the west stairs from visiting, but I still have to learn the corners everyone expects me to know.”

“If we go, you can show us the way,” Silena said.

“Unless I get lost in my own bedroom again.”

“The stool was under the desk,” Clarisse said.

“It had moved.”

“It had not.”

Percy smiled. The room was warm with the smell of apples, clean cotton, and the faint salt that followed him even when he had not been near the shore. He touched the paper’s edge. “I keep thinking about what it’ll be like for you when we get there. You know the stories, but seeing it won’t be the same as hearing about it.”

“We don’t have to know the place the same way,” Silena said. “We’re still your sisters.”

Clarisse’s eyes moved to the letter, then to him. “You don’t have to take care of every feeling we have about the place.”

He looked down. “I know.”

“Do you?”

“I’m trying to.”

She seemed satisfied by the honesty. “Good. I can take care of myself. If I want help, I’ll ask.”

“And I’ll try not to answer before you finish asking.”

“That would be new.”

“I’m practicing.”

Silena rested her chin in one hand. “I might need help finding where things are. Or I might get there and know my way from the map. I don’t know which.”

“Either way, you can ask me,” Percy said. “But I might not know the answer.”

She smiled. “I know that. You once got lost in your own bedroom.”

“The furniture had moved.”

“It had not.”

“The stool had.”

“The stool was under the desk.”

“Exactly.”

The cabin door opened without a knock. Thalia appeared, then immediately backed up and knocked on the frame. “Sorry. I heard the part about furniture moving.”

Annabeth stepped into view behind her. “That’s all it takes to get into this conversation?”

“That and Clarisse threatening a chair.”

“I never threatened the chair,” Clarisse said.

“You called it a coward.”

“It collapsed under me.”

“It was a folding chair,” Silena said. “That was its job.”

Thalia came in with a small stack of paper. Annabeth carried a tin of pencils and her sketchbook. Percy moved the apples away from the middle of the table to make room.

“You got a letter,” Annabeth said, spotting the page.

“From Amphitrite,” Percy replied. “Handwritten. She sent it in a bottle.”

Annabeth set her sketchbook down with care. “May I read it?”

“Yeah.” Percy passed it to her. “She addressed all three of us.”

Annabeth’s eyes lifted to his for a moment, then went back to the first line. She read the letter quietly. Watching her expression shift through the invitations and the open-ended date, Percy couldn’t guess her thoughts from her face alone. He’d learned not to turn every pause into a question.

When she finished, she folded the page along its existing creases. “That sounds like her.”

“You sound sure,” Clarisse said. “Do you think the palace will feel like her visit?”

“I remember that visit,” Annabeth said. “She fussed over Percy's bunk and gave me a bracelet when I hadn't asked for anything.” She tapped the edge of the letter. “She seems to understand that an invitation can be generous without being a demand.”

“She’s good at that,” Percy said.

Annabeth gave him the letter back. “I still want to go.”

“Okay.”

She looked at his face, perhaps checking whether the answer was only politeness. “I do. I want to see the gardens and the palace and the places you keep describing. I want to meet the people who made up that part of your life. But there’s something I’m not sure how to say without making it sound like I don’t trust you.”

“You can say it anyway.”

Thalia moved toward the window. Clarisse busied herself with sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. Silena picked up an apple and began slicing it into quarters. They stayed, but they offered the conversation a little room.

Annabeth rested both hands on the sketchbook. “I’m afraid I’ll be an outsider there.”

Percy did not answer at once. He could have told her she would not be. It was what he wanted to say. It was also a thing he could not guarantee. She might feel like a stranger when people used names he did not know, when the palace sounded different from camp, when everybody else remembered her as Percy’s guest before they knew her as Annabeth. He could not make a place familiar by insisting it was.

“I understand why you might,” he said.

She tilted her head. “You do?”

“I think so. You’d be walking into a home where I know the corners and the people. I might remember things without explaining them. Everyone might be happy to see me and forget that you’re there too. Not because they don’t care. Because they’ll be excited.”

“That’s what I’m worried about,” she said. “Not that they’ll be mean. I think they’ll be kind. Kindness can still make you feel like a guest. Especially when everyone is trying to make sure you feel welcome.”

“Yeah.” He traced the fold in the letter. “I can’t promise you won’t feel like an outsider. I don’t know what it’ll feel like for you. I don’t want to tell you what the place is supposed to mean before you get there.”

Annabeth’s shoulders eased a little. “Thank you.”

“I can show you things. I can explain the family jokes when I remember to. I can ask people to slow down if you need that. But I might miss it. And you might have a bad moment even if nobody does anything wrong.”

“That’s the part I didn’t want you to think you had to fix.”

“I can’t fix it for you.” He glanced up. “I can be next to you while it’s happening.”

Her fingers moved over the sketchbook’s cover, tracing the worn edge. “I want to be next to you there. I want to be honest that I might need time to find my own place in it.”

“You don’t have to earn one. But you can take the time you need.”

“And I might still ask a lot of questions.”

“That’s probably the safest way to get around Atlantis.”

“Even if the questions are about things you think you’ve already explained?”

“Especially then. I’d rather answer you than pretend you know.”

She watched him for a few breaths. “Okay.”

“Okay?”

“I’m still nervous. I still want to go. Those can happen at the same time.”

“Yeah.” Percy turned his hand palm up on the table. Annabeth put her fingers in his. “I want you there. I want to see the place with you, not have you act like you’ve already seen it because I have.”

“That helps.”

“It doesn’t make the nervous part go away.”

“No.” She smiled a little. “But it makes it easier to carry.”

He leaned over and kissed her, brief and warm. When they parted, she let her forehead rest against his for a moment. Percy kept hold of her hand. He did not try to turn the moment into a promise about how the visit would go.

“I’ll tell you when I get lost,” she said.

“I’ll try not to give you directions to the wrong room.”

“You got lost in your bedroom.”

“The stool was involved.”

“The stool has an alibi,” Thalia said from the window.

Annabeth laughed. It was quiet, but it made the room feel less crowded.

Silena set a plate of apple slices on the table. “Since we’re all here, we could start the gift basket.”

“We are not going to Atlantis yet,” Clarisse said.

“I know. We can pack it and keep it here.”

“That sounds like packing for a trip that isn’t happening.”

“It’s packing a gift basket for a trip we hope will happen.”

“That’s a lot of words to say we’re putting jam in a basket.”

“Then let’s put jam in a basket.”

Chiron appeared in the doorway, hooves soft on the wood, arriving in time to hear the conclusion. “If you are preparing a gift to take to Atlantis, I can find a small basket that will travel safely. The palace’s wards can protect a few surface items from the water.”

“Do we need to send it ahead?” Annabeth asked.

“No. We can keep it here until a date is settled.” Chiron glanced at the letter. “Amphitrite sent the invitation in writing.”

“She sent a handwritten invitation,” Percy said. “We haven’t picked a date.”

“Nor do you need to today.” Chiron’s voice was calm and easy. “A visit can be real before it is scheduled.”

Clarisse pointed at him. “That’s suspiciously sensible.”

“I have my moments.”

Chiron glanced at the letter on the table. “The earlier acknowledgment draft is still with the original invitation in the Big House. If you want to revisit it after reading this, I can bring it over.”

Percy looked at the folded page. He remembered setting down his first attempt before Amphitrite had written directly to his sisters as well. He had wanted to say thank you. He had also wanted to answer the practical questions, though most of those questions had not been settled.

“Not yet,” he said. “It was just for her. This letter is for all three of us.”

Clarisse tapped the edge of the paper. “And we haven't decided what we want to say.”

“Right.”

Silena's gaze moved between Percy and the letter. “We can be glad she wrote and still wait until we know what we want to answer.”

“We can,” Chiron said. “The draft will stay where it is. Nothing has been sent.”

Percy felt his shoulders loosen. The draft had not become a task that the new letter had made urgent. He didn't have to write for all of them because he happened to be the person who knew Amphitrite longest, or because his handwriting was already on the page.

“Thanks,” he said.

“Of course.” Chiron left the paper where it was and went to fetch the basket.
Thalia glanced at the stack of papers she'd carried in. The top sheet was the camp duty board, copied in her handwriting, with several spaces still blank.

“I haven't checked whether camp can spare me,” she said. “I might want to go. I might not be able to. Those are separate questions.”

Percy nodded. “We haven't picked a date, so there's nothing to check yet.”

“Right. I don't want anyone writing my name on the map because I was standing here when we drew it.”

“It's still blank,” Annabeth said. “And it stays blank until you decide.”

Thalia's shoulders eased. “Good.” She folded the schedule page and tucked it back with the others. “I'll ask Chiron when there are actual dates to compare. Until then, I'm not on the list.”

“Understood,” Percy said.

Clarisse looked at the blank corner of the map. “The map already has room for that answer.”

It did. The empty space didn't ask Thalia to turn a possible visit into a promise. The picture was theirs to finish only if they all chose to.


He returned with a low reed basket from the Big House pantry. It was wide enough to hold a jar and a folded piece of parchment, but shallow enough that nothing would be buried beneath anything else. Silena inspected the handles and announced that they would need a blue ribbon. Clarisse said the basket did not need to be dressed for a parade. Silena found a compromise in a narrow silver cord tied into a single neat knot.

“No bows?” Annabeth asked.

“One bow,” Silena said. “A modest one.”

“How does a bow become immodest?” Clarisse asked.

“Size. Number. Emotional intent.”

“I’m not asking.”

They carried the basket to the worktable outside the cabin, where the afternoon sun warmed the boards. Annabeth unrolled the portrait map she and Percy had started at the Big House. It already showed the pavilion, lake, strawberry fields and arena as places that mattered to the people who lived here, with no exact route between them. The original had dried enough to handle, though the blank corner for names stayed empty. Percy helped her check the curve of the shore and the inlet that flooded at high tide; he pointed out where the water reached without turning the drawing into directions.

Annabeth kept the portrait map open while they checked the drawing against the camp they knew. The existing color marks crossed the page; one green line around the strawberries looked like a river, and the little wave by the lake leaned toward the arena as if it were trying to get there first.

“That's the lake,” Percy said, pointing at the wave.

“It's also the sea,” Silena replied. “I drew a current between them.”

“The current is wider than the lake.”

“It's a feeling, not a measurement.”

“Then it can be whatever size it needs.” Percy moved his finger away from the page. “I was only checking.”

Annabeth traced the shore with her pencil. “The difference is clear if we keep the lakeside wall separate from the ocean. The drawing doesn't need a north arrow or a scale. It's a portrait, not a route.”

Clarisse studied the map from the other side of the table. “The arena marker is still too close to the cabin roofs.”

“We can move it,” Annabeth said. “But the original should stay here. This is our only copy of the drawing so far.”

“You can copy it onto the water-resistant sheet,” Percy said. “Keep this one safe.”

“Yes.” Annabeth put a blank piece of paper beside the map and checked its size.


Clarisse leaned over the original and pointed to a pale line near the edge. “This isn't one of ours.”

Annabeth followed her finger. It was a faint graphite guide from the early layout, not part of the portrait. She erased it before putting the water-resistant sheet over the map. “Good catch.”

“It's barely visible.”

“Barely is enough to become a route if I trace it.”

“I can transfer the outline, then ask Silena to add the colors. We don't need to finish the original today.”

Silena's fingers tightened around her brush. “I can repaint the flowers on both.”

“You don't have to redo it,” Annabeth said. “The original stays here. It already belongs to us.”

Silena looked at the small garden border. The flowers were soft blue and yellow, with one purple blossom that leaned too far into the path. “I want to redo the purple one. It got smudged.”

“That's your choice.”

“Good. I don't want it to look like I abandoned it.”

Clarisse gave the map another careful look. “Nothing about that map looks abandoned.”

The words came out blunt, almost like an assessment, but Silena heard the reassurance in them. She set the brush down beside the paint cup and turned the water-resistant paper so Annabeth could reach it.

“Do you want help lining it up?” Percy asked.

“Hold the edge so it doesn't slide. Don't pull it taut; the corners curl.”

He held the bottom corner between two fingers. Annabeth laid the original over the new paper, then transferred only the landmark outlines. The ink on the original had set, but its color could still be damaged by too much pressure. She marked the lines through the thin sheet, pausing at the shoreline to compare it with Percy's memory of the camp coast.

“This bends in toward the canoe lake,” he said. “The ocean edge is further out.”

Annabeth adjusted the curve. “There?”

“That's closer.”

“Is the inlet right?”

“It's not a place I can judge from an underwater map.” Percy tapped his own chest with the pencil. “I can tell you the water level. I don't know how it'll look from above.”

“Then we leave the curve simple,” Annabeth said. “We can show that there is water without pretending we have a survey.”

The exchange was practical. Percy didn't have to force a precise answer out of a memory that did not include one. Annabeth didn't treat his uncertainty as a flaw. She kept the shoreline loose and moved on to the pavilion roof.

The wind slipped beneath the paper. Percy pressed down harder than he meant to, and the corner creased under his thumb.

“Sorry.” He lifted his hand at once.

“It isn't damaged,” Annabeth said. She smoothed the fold with the side of her pencil, then turned the page. “It may actually help keep it from sliding, if we crease the same edge.”

“Do you want me to crease it?”

“Not this one. Hold it there while I mark the rest.”

He did. The task was simple, and he could feel his own impulse to keep asking if she was sure. He made himself watch the corner instead. Annabeth finished the linework, including the target marker inside the arena. The map had no gate, boundary, ward or path that a stranger could follow into a protected place. It had enough landmarks to show the shape of their life without opening the whole camp like a diagram.

When she lifted the original, the new page held a faint outline. The canoe lake sat near the center. The pavilion and arena were recognizable, and a curl of blue remained unfinished at the shore.

“That's the shape,” Annabeth said. “Now we can add the color.”

Silena stirred a little blue into the water cup. “Should the lake be lighter than the sea?”

“It is freshwater,” Percy said. “It looks blue when the sky does.”

“So it can be a different blue without making it a different kind of water.” Silena tested a stripe in the margin. “This one is paler.”

“Good,” Annabeth said. “Keep the sea at the edge darker. It should be clear that they're separate bodies of water.”

Silena painted a thin wash over the lake, leaving the pavilion roof bare. Her first stroke bled slightly beyond the pencil line. She stopped, lifted the brush, and touched the spill with a folded scrap of cloth before it could spread.

“Does that ruin it?” Percy asked.

“No,” Silena said. “It means I need a smaller brush.” She chose one with a narrower tip. “I can keep the edge soft. It doesn't need to be exact.”

Clarisse picked up the water-resistant sheet by the corners. “Does the ink run?”

“Not when it's dry,” Annabeth said. “But we'll test a corner before we put the map in the basket.”

“Good.” Clarisse set it down. “I want to know if the paper holds before the jam gets near it.”

“Fair.” Annabeth marked a tiny test square in the lower margin, outside the map border. She wrote the letters *lake* with the waterproof ink, then let the line sit while Silena added the garden colors. No one hurried the drying. They worked around the map, leaving the open signature space blank and the tiny test mark separate from the picture.

Percy watched the new page take on color. The ink and water drew him back to the earlier message, still folded with the unsent acknowledgment at the Big House. This letter came later and addressed his sisters, too. It did not answer the questions they still had, but it gave them more room to ask.

Silena brought out a sealed jar of camp strawberry jam. “Harvest batch, no ants. We can say it came from the camp garden.”

Clarisse checked the lid. “This is decent. Not too big.”

“And it tastes good,” Percy said.

“You ate half a jar,” Clarisse replied.

“I tasted it a lot.”

Silena added a tiny strawberry beside the date on the label. “We’ll leave out the quality inspector’s report.”

Thalia brought a strip of blue cloth left over from the Cabin One banner repair. Annabeth asked if she minded giving it up; Thalia said there was plenty more. They lined the basket with the scrap, its blue a close match for the ribbon around the handle.

“The basket needs something from the arena,” Percy said.

Clarisse hesitated, then offered a short leather cord from her spear grip. “It’s ordinary.”

“It’s yours,” Silena said, weaving the cord around one handle to secure the lining.

When it caught, Clarisse showed her how to free the loop without taking the work from her. Percy watched them finish side by side, their hands briefly overlapping on the same knot.

Chiron supplied water-resistant paper. Annabeth copied the sketch in waterproof ink, while Silena painted a small shell with a crescent of gold and a thin blue line. She left one edge imperfect. It was not meant to copy anything in the palace; it was a piece of camp instead.


Silena set the shell on a folded cloth while the paint settled. Its little grooves caught the blue pigment more heavily on one side, making the line curve away from the gold crescent. She hovered the brush over the edge, then set it down without correcting the mark.

“Is that the part you want imperfect?” Percy asked.

“Not exactly. I want it to look like something I painted, not something copied from a picture of what I think a queen should like.” She turned the shell toward the window. “The line is uneven because I tried to follow the groove. I could cover it, but then I'd be making the same shell twice.”

Clarisse leaned over, careful not to touch the wet paint. “It looks like water crossing the crescent.”

Silena glanced at her. “You think so?”

“I think that’s what it looks like.” Clarisse straightened. “If you want it to look different, change it. But you don't have to fix it because it's uneven.”

Silena looked down at the shell again. A thin patch of gold had gathered near the tip. The paint made the crescent thicker there, almost like sunlight breaking through cloud. She could still scrape it away. She could start again on a clean piece. The jar of paint stood open, and a second brush rested in the cup.

“I want to keep this one,” she said.

Percy almost told her it was good. He stopped before the words became a verdict she had not asked him to give. “What do you want it to say?” he asked instead.

“Not say. Show.” Silena turned the shell once more. “That this came from us. A sea-shell, but it has a camp color on it. That I made it here, with everyone else doing something around me. Not a copy of a palace thing.”

“Then I think the blue should stay,” Percy said.

“I want it to.” She dipped the brush into the water, blotted it, and cleaned the stray gold from the shell's edge. The crescent remained uneven. The blue line crossed it without hiding the darker place where the groove had taken more paint.

Annabeth looked up from the map. “The shell doesn't need to match the drawing.”

“Good. I don't have enough gold paint for another map.”

“The map's supposed to be blue and green.”

“I'm aware. I was trying to make the shell feel included.”

“You could paint it green.”

Silena lifted her eyebrows. “Then it would look like a vegetable.”

Clarisse nodded. “A regal vegetable.”

“I am not giving Amphitrite a vegetable shell.”

They laughed, and Silena's shoulders loosened. She moved the shell away from the open jar to dry. Its mark would not be perfectly smooth, but the line held together. The tiny sleeve could keep it from rubbing against anything once the paint had dried.

“If she wants to see more of my work, I can show her more later,” Silena said. “This one can be a gift without becoming the whole story of what I make.”

“That's right,” Percy said.

“She said she’d be honored to receive a piece of our life at camp. A piece. Not proof that I can make the perfect present.”

“Exactly.”

Silena looked at him. “You almost said something else.”

“I did.”

“What?”

“That it was good. I thought you might not need me to say it.”

“I like when you think my things are good.”

“Okay.”

“But I don't need it to be good before I can give it.” She smiled, then nudged his arm with her elbow. “You can still tell me when you like something.”

“I like it.”

“Thank you.”
Copying the drawing took longer than the first map had. The old page carried its faint folds from the worktable at the Big House, and Annabeth had to keep matching each line by eye instead of pressing through the paper. She transferred the pavilion roof, then the edge of the lake, then the little flower border. Percy sat across from her with one hand on the new page and the other holding the old sheet clear of the ink.

“That's not a straight line,” he said when she reached the arena marker.

“It isn't supposed to be straight.”

“I know. It looked straighter before.”

“It looked straighter because it was pencil.” Annabeth turned the page toward him. “The outline follows the practice circle. I changed the angle so the gate doesn't show.”

“Good.”

“You didn't notice before?”

“I noticed. I was waiting to see if you wanted me to say anything.”

She looked up at him. “That's a new strategy.”

“I've been trying it.”

“Keep it.”

He smiled and returned to holding the paper.

Silena brought over the jar of water for the paint. “This paper can get wet, but I don't think the paint should.”

“Correct,” Annabeth said. “The ward protects a few items from the water. It doesn't make every color permanent.”

“So if the basket tips, the map survives, but my shell might look like a sunset in the rain.”

Clarisse checked the knot on the handle. “We can keep the shell out of the water.”

“Not if we're visiting underwater,” Percy said. “The ward protects the items.”

“It still has to be packed so it doesn't hit the jar,” Silena said. “I can make a small sleeve.”

She reached for a piece of the banner cloth, then stopped. “Is this one available?”

Thalia held up the remaining length. “There's enough for the lining and a sleeve. If we use the narrow end, it won't change the map.”

Silena cut a strip just wide enough to fold over the shell. She stitched the sides with the same waxed thread Thalia had used on the banner, leaving one end open. The first seam was crooked. She pulled it apart and tried again. The second held when she slid the shell inside and shook the little sleeve once.

“Will it come out?” Percy asked.

“If we turn the top down.” Silena folded the cloth over and tied it with a short length of cord. “The paint can dry before we close it.”

“That's good,” Annabeth said. “We don't want to trap moisture against it.”

Clarisse tested the tiny sleeve between finger and thumb. “It won't protect it if the basket falls.”

“It will keep the shell from rubbing on the jar.”

“That's what we need.” Clarisse handed it back without taking it apart.

Once the map's ink had set, Annabeth marked the test square with a damp corner of cloth. The line held. A faint shine remained on the paper, but no black came away on the cloth. Silena waited until the color had dried before touching the lake wash. Her first instinct was to add a darker blue to the sea, then she stopped and compared it to the map's earlier colors.

“Too much?” Percy asked.

“Maybe. The old map has a softer edge.” Silena dipped the brush in water and blotted it on a clean rag. “I want this to look like the same drawing, not an announcement that we made another one.”

Annabeth compared the original and the copy. “The lake is a little darker here.”

“I can lighten it.”

“You can. But the paper is different. It may dry lighter.”

Silena looked at the sample mark, then at the page. “I'll wait.”

That was the part of making things Percy found hardest: stopping while the next step still felt possible. Silena set the brush aside and moved the shell to the far edge of the table. She didn't hide it. She gave the paint time to become what it was going to be.

The map stayed open beside them as the afternoon shifted. Nobody signed the blank corner. Nobody wrote a date there. The paper showed the camp they had chosen to share, and the open space stayed available for names only if the people involved later wanted them there.

“What if she doesn’t like it?” Silena asked, quiet enough that the others might not have heard.

Percy did. “Then she’ll still be glad you made it.”

Silena pressed the brush between her fingers. “You don’t know that.”

“No,” he said. “I don’t.”

She looked at him. He could feel Clarisse looking too, waiting to see if he would reach for a more comfortable answer. He held still.

“I think she’ll like it,” Percy added. “But it’s yours either way.”

Silena nodded and continued painting. Clarisse’s expression softened by a fraction; Percy chose not to point it out.

Once everything had dried, they arranged the basket together. The jam sat in the center, Annabeth’s map was rolled beside it, and Silena’s shell rested at the front. Clarisse’s cord secured Thalia’s blue banner scrap along one side.

It was small, modest, and a little crooked. No one had made it alone. That seemed important.

“It needs a card,” Silena said.

Clarisse looked at her. “No paragraph.”

“I know.” Silena took a small square of card and wrote: *From our camp to yours. We look forward to visiting you in Atlantis.* She paused, then added, *When we’re ready to come.*

“That last part sounds like we’re threatening to make her wait,” Clarisse said.

“It’s honest,” Annabeth replied.

“It sounds polite,” Chiron said. “And clear.”

Clarisse made a dissatisfied noise but did not object further.

Percy read the card once. “Seeing you again in Atlantis,” he said. “That’s right.”

“It means the palace will be new,” Silena said, “not Amphitrite.”

Annabeth nodded. “And the last line keeps the visit conditional.”

“No names,” Clarisse said. “Nobody has agreed to a date.”

“Then it stays unsigned,” Silena said. She slid the card beneath the silver cord, leaving its blank space open. The message was warm, but it did not turn their invitation into a promise.

“A basket can wait?” Thalia asked.

“It’s patience itself,” Silena said. “It has been sitting empty this whole time.”

“Heroic,” Percy said.

Before they carried the gift basket back, Clarisse asked to test it with the jam and rolled map in place. Percy lifted it by the handle. The load leaned left, and the jar bumped the reed side.

“Which piece moved?” Annabeth asked.

“The jar.” Clarisse lowered the basket. “It can knock the map if we carry it far.”

Silena reached for the ribbon. “I can tie the jar in place.”

“That could crush the label,” Percy said.

Thalia suggested a cloth ring around the jar’s base. Annabeth measured it while Clarisse steadied the basket. Silena folded a strip of leftover blue cloth into a shallow band; the first sat too high, so they flattened the fold and tried again. The ring held the jar without covering its label.

“Let's check the repair,” Clarisse said.

Percy carried the basket from the table to the doorway and back. The handle stayed straight, the jar stopped knocking, and the rolled map held against the far side. They had checked only that the contents would not damage each other when lifted, not that the basket was ready for a journey.

Silena checked the shell sleeve. The paint still needed to dry, so she set the shell beside the basket instead of packing it. Chiron offered to store the gift in the Big House, but Percy wanted it where he and his sisters would see it.

They carried it back to Cabin Three and placed it on the shelf above the table, within easy reach and clear of the bunks and spear rack. Percy turned the letter so Amphitrite's three names faced the room, then set the smooth stone beside it instead of weighing down the page. Clarisse checked the shelf was level and nudged the basket away from the edge. It could stay where all three of them could see it without asking them to decide anything tonight.
There were still apples on the table. Percy took the bucket down from the shelf beneath it and set it on the floor. The fruit had been picked for the kitchen, and the basket project had not turned the afternoon into a reason to forget the original errand.

“Should we take these over before dinner?” he asked.

“That's what they're for,” Clarisse said. She began sorting the fruit into the kitchen basket Silena had brought back from the orchard. “Firm ones together. The bruised ones on top so they get used first.”

“Somebody will think the bruised ones aren't good,” Silena said.

“Then write that they're for sauce.”

“On the fruit?” Percy asked.

“On the basket.”

Silena found a scrap of paper and wrote *Use these first* in broad blue letters. Clarisse checked the spelling, then tied it to the handle with the leftover thread. The note swung once when she lifted the basket. She shortened the knot so it wouldn't catch on the pavilion doorway.

Percy took the basket after Silena shifted it so the heavier apples sat near the bottom. This time Clarisse let him carry it without reaching for the other handle. The weight sat evenly against his palm. They walked to the pavilion by the broad camp path, keeping the basket level as they stepped around the branches and loose stakes that still waited to be sorted.

At the kitchen entrance, the cook looked at the apples, read the note, and nodded. “Good. We'll use these for sauce tonight.”

“That was the plan,” Silena said. Clarisse set the basket down without blocking the doorway and pointed out the bruised fruit. The cook transferred those apples to a shallow pan and left the firm ones in the basket, with the note tied on for the next shift.

The cook checked the shallow pan, then the basket. “These firm ones can stay by the back counter. If they go on the serving table, they’ll disappear before anyone has a chance to use them for tomorrow’s breakfast.”

“Then the note should say that,” Clarisse said. She untied the scrap and turned it over. “Use these first is clear about the bruised ones. It says nothing about the others.”

The cook handed her a stub of pencil. “Write what you need. I’ll leave it with the basket.”

Clarisse wrote *Firm apples for breakfast* beneath the first line, then held the note up for Silena to read. Silena nodded, approving the short words. Percy shifted aside to let a camper carrying a tray pass through the doorway. The kitchen smelled of warm bread and onions; behind the cook, someone stirred a pot and called for another spoon.

“There,” Clarisse said. “They can use the soft ones tonight, and the others won’t end up in the sauce by accident.”

“That helps,” the cook said. They set the note against the basket handle and returned to the counter. “Thank you for bringing them in before dinner.”

Silena tucked the loose thread under the knot so it would not snag on the doorway. Clarisse tested the handle once, then let it go. Their part was done; the kitchen could decide when to use the fruit. Percy followed his sisters back toward Cabin Three with the orchard basket empty behind them, while the cook’s pencil marks stayed beside the apples.

The cook paused to separate two apples that had rolled against the side. One was only scuffed; the other had a soft place near its stem. Clarisse pointed out the difference without taking the fruit from them. “That one can wait. The soft one goes in the pan.”

“We’ll cut around the bruise,” the cook said. “The rest is good.”

Silena watched until the pan held the apples meant for sauce and the basket kept the firm ones. The note now named both uses, in Clarisse’s square handwriting. It was a small correction, but the cook could act on it without guessing what the sisters meant.

They left the empty wicker basket by the kitchen door and returned to Cabin Three. Percy washed the orchard dust from his hands. The letter and gift were still on the shelf, waiting without asking them to decide anything tonight.

Clarisse stopped beside the table instead of taking her place. “Do we have a clean scrap?”

Annabeth slid one toward her, blank side up. “What are you writing?”

“A question for Mother.” Clarisse took a pencil from the tin. “Not sending it. I want to know what the palace expects us to do with our weapons.”

Percy reached for the paper, then left his hands on the table. “You can write it.”

“I know.” She pressed the pencil down hard enough to score the paper. *What are the rules about weapons?* She read it, made a face, and drew one line through *rules*. “That sounds like I’m asking for a list.”

“What do you need to know?” Silena asked.

Clarisse held the pencil above the page. “If we bring anything, where can we keep it? Who decides when we have to leave it somewhere? What happens if there’s an alarm and we’re in a room where we aren’t supposed to carry it?”

“Those are three questions,” Thalia said from the doorway. “And the last one depends on what counts as an alarm.”

Clarisse looked up. “Exactly.”

She crossed out the first line and started again, slower: *If we visit, what would the palace expect from us about carrying and storing weapons, and who would explain those expectations before we arrived? If something changed while we were there, who could we ask?* She turned the paper so the others could read it.

Percy started to say that some halls had guards and others had practice rooms, but those facts did not answer what Clarisse was asking. “I don’t know how they handle a visitor’s weapon,” he said. “I can ask, but I shouldn’t guess.”

“That’s why I want the answer before we’re standing in the doorway,” Clarisse said. She drew a small mark beside the question, not a check or a yes, just a place to find it again. “I don’t want the first time we hear the rule to be when someone takes a spear out of our hands.”

“That’s clearer,” Percy said. He kept his voice even, though the word *if* sat in the middle of the question like a gate he was glad she had left closed.

“It doesn’t ask for special treatment,” Clarisse said. “Just the information before I have to guess.”

Annabeth tapped the bottom edge. “You could add that no one has agreed to a date.”

“No. The question already says if.” Clarisse folded the scrap once, then unfolded it and smoothed the crease. “And we’re not putting it in the bottle tonight. I want to think whether there are other things I need to ask.”

“We can keep it with the letter,” Silena said.

Clarisse shook her head. “Keep it with me.” She tucked it into the pocket of her jacket. The invitation remained where it was, and the question went with the person who had chosen to ask it.

The sun slid lower. Campers drifted toward dinner, voices thinning in the evening air. Outside the window, the sea glimmered between the trees, a narrow strip of blue with evening light caught on its surface.

“You sound disappointed,” Silena said.

“I was hoping it would fall apart before we had to decide what to do with it.”

“You could still decide not to go.”

Clarisse looked at the letter beneath the basket. “I know.”

“And you can decide to go without liking every part of it,” Percy said.

“I know that too.” She turned toward him. “You can stop checking every five minutes whether I’m comfortable.”

He closed his mouth. “Okay.”

“I’ll tell you if I need anything. You can ask once in a while. Not every time my face changes.”

“That’s fair.”

Silena tied a narrow blue ribbon over the basket’s silver cord. Clarisse inspected the knot and said it would hold. Percy laughed, and Annabeth’s hand found his on the edge of the table. For a moment the room held the sisters’ easy bickering, Thalia’s quiet amusement, the letter from the sea and the little basket made by camp hands.

“I want to hear more about that tide clock,” Percy said to Clarisse.

“You will, if we go.”

“What if we don’t?” Silena asked.

Clarisse reached over and straightened the blue ribbon. “Then I’ll tell him here.”

The answer came out brisk, but it landed gently. Neither sister had to make the invitation mean the same thing Percy did or be ready at the same pace. The letter did not insist on an answer by morning.

Annabeth squeezed his hand. “You don’t have to decide tonight either.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

He glanced at the letter. A thin line of Amphitrite’s handwriting showed beneath the basket. The page waited, patient as the tide. So did they.

For the first time since the invitation had arrived, Percy let himself stop imagining the journey. He did not have to picture the path beneath the water or the palace doors opening. He could stay where he was: among his sisters, with Annabeth’s hand in his, camp outside the window and the sea beyond it.

The map still held an unsigned corner, and the card carried no date. No one had to turn the kindness in the letter into a choice they had not made.

The visit could wait. Their wanting it did not have to.
