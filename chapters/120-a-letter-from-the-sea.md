## Chapter 120: A Letter from the Sea

The sea had brought Percy mail before, but never with quite so much ceremony from the shoreline.

He and his sisters were carrying a bucket of apples back from the orchard when the tide reached up the beach, stopped shy of their shoes, and left a green glass bottle on the sand. A thin loop of silver cord held its stopper in place. No message in the water, no rainbow, no voice coming from the waves. Only the bottle, shining in the sun as if someone had polished it for the occasion.

Clarisse eyed it from a careful distance. “If it bites, you’re the one with the water powers.”

“I thought you were the one with the spear,” Percy said.

“I’m carrying apples.”

“Then it’s lucky I’m carrying the bucket.”

Silena set her end of the handle down. “Maybe it’s a present.”

“People don’t put presents in bottles,” Clarisse said.

“They do if the present is a letter.”

“Who puts a letter in a bottle when they can use an Iris-message?”

Percy had already crouched to pick it up. The bottle was cool, though it had been sitting in the sun. He knew the silver cord: fine as sewing thread, braided with tiny knots to keep it from slipping loose underwater. A pale shell seal covered the stopper. Amphitrite’s mark curved across it in dark blue wax.

His breath caught. Afternoon light slanted across them, the bucket between them, the orchard behind and the sea beyond the dry sand. For a moment, he could hear the palace corridors in the bottle’s silence—the distant hum of currents through stone, a little bell announcing someone’s arrival, his mother’s voice from another room.

“Is it from Amphitrite?” Silena asked, softly now.

Percy nodded. “Looks like it.”

Clarisse set the apples aside. Her face had gone still in the particular way that meant she was trying not to let a feeling make the first move. “Open it.”

The seal gave way with a small crack. Inside was a single sheet of thick cream paper folded in thirds. A few drops of seawater clung to the glass, but the paper was perfectly dry. Percy turned it over. The handwriting on the front was unmistakable: neat at first, then leaning more sharply to the right as if the words had become impatient to reach him.

He knew her handwriting from letters since his arrival, recipes with tiny drawings of fish in the margins. Triton had told him she once tucked notes into his schoolbooks, when every day away from home was an expedition. He could picture the gesture without remembering those mornings. Once, homesick at camp, he had tried to make his own letters look like hers. The result had been a page of crooked loops and Clarisse asking whether the ink had survived a shipwreck.

“You can read it to us,” Silena said. She was smiling, but her fingers had closed around the edge of her sleeve.

“Yeah.” Percy unfolded the paper. “I’ll read it out loud.”

Clarisse leaned against the nearest driftwood log. “Good. I don’t trust the bottle to summarize.”

With both hands, Percy held the letter. The paper smelled faintly of salt and crushed flowers he couldn’t name. The handwriting filled the page, with one line added in the margin and a small ink blot near the signature.

“Dear Percy, Clarisse, and Silena,” he read.

Silena glanced at Clarisse, then back to the page. Clarisse’s arms remained folded, but she did not look away.

“The message I sent you was spoken in a hurry, between one task and the next. I wanted the invitation to arrive in a form you could keep, and to say more plainly what I meant.

“I would be delighted to welcome you to Atlantis, whenever the three of you are ready to choose a time. Clarisse and Silena, this invitation is yours as much as Percy’s. You are not being asked to attend as witnesses to his homecoming. I would like to know you as yourselves, if you wish to let me.

“You may bring the friends you choose to have beside you. I will not set a date for you or ask you to hurry your answer. No ceremony is planned. I would rather have an ordinary meal with you than a perfect reception. If there are parts of the palace you would rather not visit, say so. If you need a quiet hour, ask. If you would prefer the garden to the great hall, we will go to the garden.

“Percy, I miss hearing you come down the west stairs too quickly. Clarisse, Chiron sent your questions, and Percy wrote about the tide clock you challenged at camp. Silena, he described your painted shells; I would love to see your work. I have been thinking of you all.

“Bring nothing you do not want to bring. A gift is never required between people who are glad to see each other. But if you decide to bring a piece of your life at camp, I would be honored to receive it.

“Take your time. There is no wrong way to come home, and no wrong answer if the time is not right.

“With all my love,

“Amphitrite.”

For a little while, the only sound was the water easing back down the beach, shells clinking softly as the foam pulled them.

Percy lowered the page. He had known what the invitation would say, in a general sense. The message had already made the offer. The letter did not change the facts, and still it changed something. It put the words in ink. It gave them corners, a fold, a place to rest. The invitation could now be read again without asking the lake to turn gold.

Silena was the first to speak. “You told her about the shells.”

Her smile had gone watery. Percy couldn’t tell if she was about to laugh or cry, so he said nothing that would force her to pick one.

“The purple one,” he said.

“It was supposed to be a sunset.”

“It looked like a grape with a crown.”

“It was a regal sunset, obviously.” Silena wiped under one eye with her knuckle, salt sharp on her skin. “You told her all that.”

“Of course I did,” Percy said. He could picture a shell on a shelf in the palace, beside some polished treasure or another, still purple under the blue light. “She asks what you make, not just how training went.”

“Tell her about that awful fish-shaped cushion here,” Clarisse said. “Maybe she knows how to get rid of it.”

“You like that cushion,” Silena said.

“I like throwing it at Percy.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

Clarisse’s gaze moved to the letter. “She wrote my name.”

It was not a question. Percy looked down at the line where Amphitrite had described the tide clock. “She did.”

“Not ‘your sister Clarisse.’ Not ‘the other girl.’ Just Clarisse.”

“Yeah.”

Clarisse nodded, once. The nod cost her visible effort. Then she reached toward the page and stopped short of touching it. “Read the part about my questions again.”

Percy glanced at her. “You want the whole letter again?”

“Just that part.”

He found the sentence and read it once more. Clarisse’s mouth twitched.

“And the clock wasn’t slow,” she said. “It was wrong.”

“You were on shore watch,” Silena said.

“That doesn’t make it less wrong.”

“You stood in the watch hut and argued with a clock.”

“It was wasting everybody’s time.”

“The clock was measuring time.”

“Badly.”

Silena laughed. “I thought you’d forgotten that.”

“I forgot the clock,” Clarisse said. “I didn’t forget getting called into the Big House because I’d pulled it off the wall.”

“You did what?” Percy asked.

“It wasn’t bolted properly.”

“You pulled a clock off the wall?”

“It was a small clock.”

“You were on a stool,” Silena said.

“That’s not a measurement.”

The argument had the familiar comfort of a blanket dragged across a room. Percy joined in, asking why nobody had ever told him the story, and Silena accused Clarisse of editing out the parts where she cried because the clock had fallen on her foot. Clarisse insisted she had been angry, not hurt. Silena insisted that the distinction had been difficult to observe while Clarisse was shouting at an instrument of time.

When the laughter faded, Clarisse finally took the letter. She read the lines with her own name, her expression severe enough to suggest she was examining evidence in a trial. Her thumb rested at the bottom corner.

“She says we can come as ourselves,” Silena said.

“She says she wants to know us,” Clarisse replied. “That’s different.”

“Different how?”

“It means she doesn’t already know us just because she knows Percy.” Clarisse handed the letter back. “That’s good. It also means we get to decide how much we tell her.”

Percy felt the old reflex rise in him: to explain that Amphitrite was kind, that she would be glad to see them, that the palace would be safe. He let the reflex pass before it became a speech. The letter had not promised them a perfect visit. Neither should he.

“You don’t have to decide today,” he said.

Clarisse’s eyebrows drew together. “I know.”

“I’m not trying to talk you into it.”

“I know that too.” She nudged the apple bucket with the side of her boot. “I want to go. I also want to know what I’m walking into before I get there.”

“That seems reasonable.”

“Don’t make it sound like I need permission to be careful.”

“I’m not.” He adjusted his grip on the folded page. “I mean, I get it.”

Clarisse studied him, then gave a short nod. “Good.”

Silena looped her arm through Clarisse’s. “I’m ready to go now.”

“You’re ready to go everywhere now,” Clarisse said.

“That’s because I have a curious spirit.”

“You have a packed bag under your bed.”

“A curious spirit can be prepared.”

“You packed a comb, two ribbons, and three pairs of socks.”

“I had room.”

Percy tucked the letter into the inner pocket of his camp shirt. “We can show Chiron she sent it. He can acknowledge it when we’re ready to answer.”

“And maybe there won’t,” Clarisse said. “We don’t need to turn every sentence into a rule.”

Silena squeezed her arm. “We can do both. Ask what matters and leave the rest for later.”

Clarisse grunted. In Clarisse language, this meant she was considering it.

They picked up the bucket and walked back to camp, still disagreeing about whether a queen’s invitation could count as a summons. Silena said the letter had the softest edges imaginable. Clarisse replied that paper could still issue orders. Percy decided this was a question for Chiron, who had probably been summoned by at least three kinds of paper.

By the time they reached Cabin Three, the afternoon had settled into an easy golden hour. A little breeze came through the open windows. Inside, their three beds were unmade in three different styles: Percy’s blankets had slid to the floor, Silena’s quilt was smoothed in a way that suggested she had made it before breakfast, and Clarisse’s pillow had been folded into a shape that looked designed to withstand impact.

They set the apples on the table, their skins warm from the sun and sweet-tart on the air. Percy took the letter from his pocket and placed it at the center, weighted with a smooth stone so the breeze would not lift it.

Percy looked between them. Silena’s openness and Clarisse’s caution had never fit together neatly, but lately he’d noticed how they made room for each other. Silena gave Clarisse permission to feel more than one thing. Clarisse made sure Silena did not give away more than she meant to. They did not always agree. They stayed close anyway.

“I’m glad she wrote to all of us,” he said.

Clarisse took a bite of apple. “You sound surprised.”

“I thought she might write to me and say I could bring you.”

“That would’ve been bad,” Silena said. “I don’t like being brought anywhere. I can walk.”

“You can swim there,” Percy said.

“I can also walk on land.”

“For now,” Clarisse said. “The basket of socks suggests otherwise.”

Silena read the paragraph with her name again. “I know Atlantis in pieces,” she said. “From your stories, not like somewhere I can walk around without thinking.”

“Some parts feel like that to me too,” Percy said. “I know the west stairs from visiting, but I still have to learn the corners everyone expects me to know.”

“If we go, you can show us the way,” Silena said.

“Unless I get lost in my own bedroom again.”

“The stool was under the desk,” Clarisse said.

“It had moved.”

“It had not.”

Percy smiled. The room was warm with the smell of apples, clean cotton, and the faint salt that followed him even when he had not been near the shore. He touched the paper’s edge. “I keep thinking about what it’ll be like for you when we get there. You know the stories, but seeing it won’t be the same as hearing about it.”

“We don’t have to know the place the same way,” Silena said. “We’re still your sisters.”

Clarisse’s eyes moved to the letter, then to him. “You don’t have to take care of every feeling we have about the place.”

He looked down. “I know.”

“Do you?”

“I’m trying to.”

She seemed satisfied by the honesty. “Good. I can take care of myself. If I want help, I’ll ask.”

“And I’ll try not to answer before you finish asking.”

“That would be new.”

“I’m practicing.”

Silena rested her chin in one hand. “I might need help finding where things are. Or I might get there and know my way from the map. I don’t know which.”

“Either way, you can ask me,” Percy said. “But I might not know the answer.”

She smiled. “I know that. You once got lost in your own bedroom.”

“The furniture had moved.”

“It had not.”

“The stool had.”

“The stool was under the desk.”

“Exactly.”

The cabin door opened without a knock. Thalia appeared, then immediately backed up and knocked on the frame. “Sorry. I heard the part about furniture moving.”

Annabeth stepped into view behind her. “That’s all it takes to get into this conversation?”

“That and Clarisse threatening a chair.”

“I never threatened the chair,” Clarisse said.

“You called it a coward.”

“It collapsed under me.”

“It was a folding chair,” Silena said. “That was its job.”

Thalia came in with a small stack of paper. Annabeth carried a tin of pencils and her sketchbook. Percy moved the apples away from the middle of the table to make room.

“You got a letter,” Annabeth said, spotting the page.

“From Amphitrite,” Percy replied. “Handwritten. She sent it in a bottle.”

Annabeth set her sketchbook down with care. “May I read it?”

“Yeah.” Percy passed it to her. “She addressed all three of us.”

Annabeth’s eyes lifted to his for a moment, then went back to the first line. She read the letter quietly. Watching her expression shift through the invitations and the open-ended date, Percy couldn’t guess her thoughts from her face alone. He’d learned not to turn every pause into a question.

When she finished, she folded the page along its existing creases. “That sounds like her.”

“You’ve never met her,” Clarisse said.

“No, but Percy talks about her.” Annabeth tapped the edge of the letter. “She seems to understand that an invitation can be generous without being a demand.”

“She’s good at that,” Percy said.

Annabeth gave him the letter back. “I still want to go.”

“Okay.”

She looked at his face, perhaps checking whether the answer was only politeness. “I do. I want to see the gardens and the palace and the places you keep describing. I want to meet the people who made up that part of your life. But there’s something I’m not sure how to say without making it sound like I don’t trust you.”

“You can say it anyway.”

Thalia moved toward the window. Clarisse busied herself with sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. Silena picked up an apple and began slicing it into quarters. They stayed, but they offered the conversation a little room.

Annabeth rested both hands on the sketchbook. “I’m afraid I’ll be an outsider there.”

Percy did not answer at once. He could have told her she would not be. It was what he wanted to say. It was also a thing he could not guarantee. She might feel like a stranger when people used names he did not know, when the palace sounded different from camp, when everybody else remembered her as Percy’s guest before they knew her as Annabeth. He could not make a place familiar by insisting it was.

“I understand why you might,” he said.

She tilted her head. “You do?”

“I think so. You’d be walking into a home where I know the corners and the people. I might remember things without explaining them. Everyone might be happy to see me and forget that you’re there too. Not because they don’t care. Because they’ll be excited.”

“That’s what I’m worried about,” she said. “Not that they’ll be mean. I think they’ll be kind. Kindness can still make you feel like a guest. Especially when everyone is trying to make sure you feel welcome.”

“Yeah.” He traced the fold in the letter. “I can’t promise you won’t feel like an outsider. I don’t know what it’ll feel like for you. I don’t want to tell you what the place is supposed to mean before you get there.”

Annabeth’s shoulders eased a little. “Thank you.”

“I can show you things. I can explain the family jokes when I remember to. I can ask people to slow down if you need that. But I might miss it. And you might have a bad moment even if nobody does anything wrong.”

“That’s the part I didn’t want you to think you had to fix.”

“I can’t fix it for you.” He glanced up. “I can be next to you while it’s happening.”

Her fingers moved over the sketchbook’s cover, tracing the worn edge. “I want to be next to you there. I want to be honest that I might need time to find my own place in it.”

“You don’t have to earn one. But you can take the time you need.”

“And I might still ask a lot of questions.”

“That’s probably the safest way to get around Atlantis.”

“Even if the questions are about things you think you’ve already explained?”

“Especially then. I’d rather answer you than pretend you know.”

She watched him for a few breaths. “Okay.”

“Okay?”

“I’m still nervous. I still want to go. Those can happen at the same time.”

“Yeah.” Percy turned his hand palm up on the table. Annabeth put her fingers in his. “I want you there. I want to see the place with you, not have you act like you’ve already seen it because I have.”

“That helps.”

“It doesn’t make the nervous part go away.”

“No.” She smiled a little. “But it makes it easier to carry.”

He leaned over and kissed her, brief and warm. When they parted, she let her forehead rest against his for a moment. Percy kept hold of her hand. He did not try to turn the moment into a promise about how the visit would go.

“I’ll tell you when I get lost,” she said.

“I’ll try not to give you directions to the wrong room.”

“You got lost in your bedroom.”

“The stool was involved.”

“The stool has an alibi,” Thalia said from the window.

Annabeth laughed. It was quiet, but it made the room feel less crowded.

Silena set a plate of apple slices on the table. “Since we’re all here, we could start the gift basket.”

“We are not going to Atlantis yet,” Clarisse said.

“I know. We can pack it and keep it here.”

“That sounds like packing for a trip that isn’t happening.”

“It’s packing a gift basket for a trip we hope will happen.”

“That’s a lot of words to say we’re putting jam in a basket.”

“Then let’s put jam in a basket.”

Chiron appeared in the doorway, hooves soft on the wood, arriving in time to hear the conclusion. “If you are preparing a gift to take to Atlantis, I can find a small basket that will travel safely. The palace’s wards can protect a few surface items from the water.”

“Do we need to send it ahead?” Annabeth asked.

“No. We can keep it here until a date is settled.” Chiron glanced at the letter. “Amphitrite sent the invitation in writing.”

“She sent a handwritten invitation,” Percy said. “We haven’t picked a date.”

“Nor do you need to today.” Chiron’s voice was calm and easy. “A visit can be real before it is scheduled.”

Clarisse pointed at him. “That’s suspiciously sensible.”

“I have my moments.”

He brought them a low reed basket from the Big House pantry. It was wide enough to hold a jar and a folded piece of parchment, but shallow enough that nothing would be buried beneath anything else. Silena inspected the handles and announced that they would need a blue ribbon. Clarisse said the basket did not need to be dressed for a parade. Silena found a compromise in a narrow silver cord tied into a single neat knot.

“No bows?” Annabeth asked.

“One bow,” Silena said. “A modest one.”

“How does a bow become immodest?” Clarisse asked.

“Size. Number. Emotional intent.”

“I’m not asking.”

They carried the basket to the worktable outside the cabin, where the afternoon sun warmed the boards. Annabeth sketched a simple map of camp: cabins, pavilion, arena, and the path to the canoe lake. Percy helped her place the shore and the inlet that flooded at high tide.

“You left out the laundry line,” Clarisse said.

“It’s a map, not a hazard report,” Annabeth replied.

“Those can overlap,” Clarisse said, but let the subject drop.

Silena brought out a sealed jar of camp strawberry jam. “Harvest batch, no ants. We can say it came from the camp garden.”

Clarisse checked the lid. “This is decent. Not too big.”

“And it tastes good,” Percy said.

“You ate half a jar,” Clarisse replied.

“I tasted it a lot.”

Silena added a tiny strawberry beside the date on the label. “We’ll leave out the quality inspector’s report.”

Thalia brought a strip of blue cloth left over from the Cabin One banner repair. Annabeth asked if she minded giving it up; Thalia said there was plenty more. They lined the basket with the scrap, its blue a close match for the ribbon around the handle.

“The basket needs something from the arena,” Percy said.

Clarisse hesitated, then offered a short leather cord from her spear grip. “It’s ordinary.”

“It’s yours,” Silena said, weaving the cord around one handle to secure the lining.

When it caught, Clarisse showed her how to free the loop without taking the work from her. Percy watched them finish side by side, their hands briefly overlapping on the same knot.

Chiron supplied water-resistant paper. Annabeth copied the sketch in waterproof ink, while Silena painted a small shell with a crescent of gold and a thin blue line. She left one edge imperfect. It was not meant to copy anything in the palace; it was a piece of camp instead.

“What if she doesn’t like it?” Silena asked, quiet enough that the others might not have heard.

Percy did. “Then she’ll still be glad you made it.”

Silena pressed the brush between her fingers. “You don’t know that.”

“No,” he said. “I don’t.”

She looked at him. He could feel Clarisse looking too, waiting to see if he would reach for a more comfortable answer. He held still.

“I think she’ll like it,” Percy added. “But it’s yours either way.”

Silena nodded and continued painting. Clarisse’s expression softened by a fraction; Percy chose not to point it out.

Once everything had dried, they arranged the basket together. The jam sat in the center, Annabeth’s map was rolled beside it, and Silena’s shell rested at the front. Clarisse’s cord secured Thalia’s blue banner scrap along one side.

It was small, modest, and a little crooked. No one had made it alone. That seemed important.

“It needs a card,” Silena said.

Clarisse looked at her. “No paragraph.”

“I know.” Silena took a small square of card and wrote: *From our camp to yours. We look forward to meeting you.* She paused, then added, *When we’re ready to come.*

“That last part sounds like we’re threatening to make her wait,” Clarisse said.

“It’s honest,” Annabeth replied.

“It sounds polite,” Chiron said. “And clear.”

Clarisse made a dissatisfied noise but did not object further.

Percy read the card twice. The words were true. He wanted to go. They all did, though in different ways and at different speeds. They could be excited without pretending they had settled the date. They could prepare a gift without confusing preparation with a promise.

“Keep it,” he said. “We can add something if we decide we want to.”

Silena laid the card beneath the silver cord. “Then it’s ready to wait.”

“A basket can wait?” Thalia asked.

“It’s patience itself,” Silena said. “It has been sitting empty this whole time.”

“Heroic,” Percy said.

They carried it back to Cabin Three and placed it on the shelf above the table, out of the reach of accidental elbows and the occasional spear. Chiron offered to store it in the Big House, but Percy wanted to keep it where he and his sisters would see it. The letter went beneath the basket, protected by the blue lining. It did not disappear there. A corner of the page stayed visible.

The sun slid lower. Campers drifted toward dinner, voices thinning in the evening air. Outside the window, the sea glimmered between the trees, a narrow strip of blue with evening light caught on its surface.

Clarisse tested the basket’s handle once. “It’ll hold.”

“You sound disappointed,” Silena said.

“I was hoping it would fall apart before we had to decide what to do with it.”

“You could still decide not to go.”

Clarisse looked at the letter beneath the basket. “I know.”

“And you can decide to go without liking every part of it,” Percy said.

“I know that too.” She turned toward him. “You can stop checking every five minutes whether I’m comfortable.”

He closed his mouth. “Okay.”

“I’ll tell you if I need anything. You can ask once in a while. Not every time my face changes.”

“That’s fair.”

Silena tied a narrow blue ribbon over the basket’s silver cord. Clarisse inspected the knot and said it would hold. Percy laughed, and Annabeth’s hand found his on the edge of the table. For a moment the room held the sisters’ easy bickering, Thalia’s quiet amusement, the letter from the sea and the little basket made by camp hands.

“I want to hear more about that tide clock,” Percy said to Clarisse.

“You will, if we go.”

“What if we don’t?” Silena asked.

Clarisse reached over and straightened the blue ribbon. “Then I’ll tell him here.”

The answer came out brisk, but it landed gently. Neither sister had to make the invitation mean the same thing Percy did or be ready at the same pace. The letter did not insist on an answer by morning.

Annabeth squeezed his hand. “You don’t have to decide tonight either.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

He glanced at the letter. A thin line of Amphitrite’s handwriting showed beneath the basket. The page waited, patient as the tide. So did they.

For the first time since the invitation had arrived, Percy let himself stop imagining the journey. He did not have to picture the path beneath the water or the palace doors opening. He could stay where he was: among his sisters, with Annabeth’s hand in his, camp outside the window and the sea beyond it.

The visit could wait. Their wanting it did not have to.
