## Chapter 73: The Play

The notice appeared on the Big House porch on a Tuesday morning in April, written in Will Solace's neat healer's handwriting on the back of an old infirmary intake sheet, which should have been the first warning sign.

It read:

*APOLLO CABIN PRESENTS: GODS! A Very Accurate And Totally Respectful Retelling Of Our Parents (Please Do Not Smite Us) — AUDITIONS TODAY AT THE AMPHITHEATER. ALL CABINS WELCOME. NO EXPERIENCE NECESSARY. SNACKS PROVIDED.*

Underneath, in smaller, different handwriting that was almost certainly Cecil Markowitz's, someone had added: *Snacks are just Apollo cabin's leftover cookies. Come anyway.*

Percy Jackson stopped in front of it with a mouthful of toast and read it twice.

"No," he said.

Annabeth Grace, coming up the porch steps behind him with blueprints tucked under one arm and a smudge of charcoal on her cheek, read over his shoulder. "Oh, absolutely yes."

"No," Percy said again, more firmly. "Last time Apollo cabin did theater it was that musical about the Trojan War and I had to be a horse for two hours."

"You were a great horse," Annabeth said solemnly. "You had real hindquarter energy."

"I had cramps for a week."

Thalia Grace pushed past them both, sparks crackling faintly off her leather jacket from the morning patrol. She read the sign, snorted, and kept walking. "Hard pass. I don't do community theater."

By dinner, all three of them had been cast.

---

The amphitheater at Camp Half-Blood in late afternoon was golden and half-empty, smelling of hot stone and cut grass. The Apollo kids had dragged out a rack of costumes that looked like they'd been assembled from three different Halloween stores, a lost-and-found bin, and, inexplicably, a toga party at a college none of them attended.

Will Solace stood center stage with a clipboard and the bright, doomed optimism of a director who had not yet realized what he had volunteered for. Kayla Knowles sat beside him with a guitar across her knees. Austin Lake was fiddling with the sound system, which currently only played static and, for some reason, whale noises.

"Okay!" Will called, clapping his hands. "Thank you all for coming! This is going to be great. The idea is simple. A funny, heartwarming play about the gods, written by us — mostly by me last night at 2 a.m. — performed for the whole camp on Friday night as a morale booster before capture the flag this weekend. Mr. D approved it as long as we don't, quote, 'make Dionysus look like an idiot.'"

"So no accuracy, then," Connor Stoll whispered loudly from the second row.

"We've pre-cast some key roles based on divine parentage and temperament for comedic and thematic resonance," Will continued, ignoring him. "Don't worry, it's all in good fun."

Percy sank lower in his stone seat. He could feel it coming the way he could feel a wave building before it broke.

"Percy Jackson," Will said, beaming at him. "Poseidon. Lord of the Seas. Your father. It had to be you."

The Apollo cabin applauded. The rest of the camp turned to look at him.

Percy closed his eyes. "Do I have to wear —"

"A beautiful sea-green chiton, a trident, and a beard," Will said. "The beard is fake."

"I hate this."

"You'll be wonderful," Will said.

"Annabeth Grace," Will went on, "Athena. Goddess of Wisdom. I mean, obviously."

Annabeth tilted her head. "Casting by temperament, then. My father is already on the list."

"Typecasting but make it a family argument," Travis Stoll said.

"Thalia plays our father and I get to argue with him," Annabeth said automatically, then frowned. "Actually, that part sounds familiar. I'll do it, but if you make Athena say 'girlboss' in the script I'm rewriting it."

"She says it twice," Kayla said apologetically.

"I'm rewriting it."

"Thalia Grace — Zeus! King of the Gods!"

Thalia, who had only come because she was supposed to meet Percy and Annabeth afterward to go over border patrol rotations, looked up from picking at her nails. "Absolutely not."

"Come on," Will pleaded. "You're the daughter of Zeus with the loudest stage voice! You have the gravitas! You have the electric personality! Literally!"

"No wig," Thalia said flatly.

"What?"

"If I do this, I'm not wearing a wig. Or a fake beard. Stage Zeus gets black spiky hair and an attitude problem. Take it or leave it."

Will scribbled frantically. "We can work with no wig!"

"Clarisse La Rue — Ares! God of War!"

Clarisse, arms crossed in the back row, grinned like a shark. "Finally, a role that understands me."

"You're going to be great, but please remember stage combat is *simulated* —"

"I make no promises," Clarisse said. Percy and her friends from the Ares cabin cheered.

"Grover Underwood — Satyr Chorus Leader! Narrator! Comic relief!"

Grover, sitting cross-legged on the stage steps munching a tin can, blinked. "Isn't that just... me?"

"Exactly!" Will said. "You're a natural!"

Grover looked pleased and slightly insulted at the same time. "I'll have my people talk to your people about tin can compensation."

Other roles filled out quickly. Piper McLean, visiting for the week between her road trips with her dad, got Aphrodite after she pointed out that no one else could deliver the line "War is ugly, darling, have you tried moisturizer?" with a straight face. Frank Zhang, visiting from Camp Jupiter with Hazel for an exchange week, got drafted as Hephaestus and looked quietly thrilled about getting to build the set. Hazel Levesque got Persephone and immediately started taking it very seriously. Nico di Angelo was cast as Hades and tried to leave, was physically blocked by Cecil, and accepted with the resignation of a man attending his own funeral. Twice.

Jason Grace, visiting under the same supervised Camp Jupiter exchange, got Apollo. "Because you're blond and the spotlight loves you," Kayla explained. Jason nodded like this made perfect sense.

Leo Valdez got Hermes "because you never stop talking and you stole my shoes during auditions." Leo bowed.

By the end of the hour, half the camp had a costume piece and a script stapled crookedly together.

Percy flipped through his. Page one: *POSEIDON enters riding a wave (blue bedsheet carried by two Apollo kids). He shakes his TRIDENT (plastic, lights up).*

"My trident lights up?" Percy said.

"It has three settings," Austin said proudly. "Steady, strobe, and dramatic."

Annabeth was already marking up her script with a pencil. "My first line is 'Father, your meeting about fish is in five minutes.' Who wrote this?"

"I did," Will said. "At 2 a.m. I thought fish meetings were peak comedy."

"They're not *not* funny," Grover offered.

Thalia held her script between two fingers like it was contagious. "My first line is 'I AM ZEUS! TREMBLE BEFORE ME!'"

"Great projection opportunity," Will said encouragingly.

"I don't need projection. I have actual lightning." A tiny arc crackled between her fingers for emphasis. The Apollo kids in the front row leaned back.

"Please don't smite the set," Frank said from where he was already sketching a collapsible Mount Olympus on graph paper. "I'm using real hinges."

Annabeth collected her script after auditions and found Thalia waiting in the passage behind the amphitheater. Her older sister had folded the costume sheet into a square so small that the printed instructions were no longer visible.

"You could just tell Will no," Annabeth said.

"I did."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because apparently no is a suggestion at Camp Half-Blood when there are free cookies."

Annabeth sat on the low stone wall beside the costume rack. The afternoon sun made the bronze fittings too warm to touch. She turned the first page of her script and read the opening dialogue again.

*ATHENA: Father, your meeting about fish is in five minutes.*

It was a stupid line. It was supposed to be stupid. Will had written the whole thing in one sleep-deprived night, and Percy had already laughed at it twice. Annabeth wasn't sure why the words made her stomach feel hollow.

Thalia watched her without asking.

"What?" Annabeth said.

"Nothing."

"You're doing that face."

"I have one face."

"You have at least six. That one means you know something is wrong and you're deciding whether I'll bite you if you ask."

"You do bite."

"Only when provoked."

Thalia came to sit beside her, boots planted in the dust. She unfolded the sheet and read her own opening line.

"Zeus enters. Zeus demands obedience. Zeus threatens everybody with lightning. Gods, Will has done more character research than he realizes."

Annabeth looked at her sister. "You don't have to play him."

"I know."

"Do you?"

Thalia's gaze went to the amphitheater, where Will was arguing with Frank about whether a cardboard column could survive a swordfight. A group of campers passed laughing, carrying costume boxes. Neither girl spoke until the passage was empty again.

"I hate how easy it would be," Thalia said.

"Easy to do what?"

"Stand up there and sound like him."

Annabeth held the script on her knees. She had heard Zeus rage in the present timeline, in hallways that smelled of rain and ozone. She had learned from Thalia how rarely their father came, and how a visit could leave more silence afterward than his absence had. In the old life she remembered, Zeus had been distant, terrifying and almost entirely someone else's father. Here he had a claim on her body and her history that she still didn't know how to inhabit.

"I don't want people laughing at you for playing him," she said.

"They won't."

"They might laugh at him and think they're laughing at you."

"Then I'll find out which is which."

Annabeth considered that. Thalia wasn't pretending the role would be harmless. She was refusing to let it own the meaning of the evening.

"Why did you say yes after you said no?" Annabeth asked.

Thalia snorted. "Because Jason laughed when he heard. And then he said a Roman would give a better performance of Jupiter, which was an act of war."

"That sounds like Jason."

"He had a very smug face."

Annabeth smiled, then looked back down at her script. On the second page, Athena was supposed to tell Poseidon to stop flooding the council floor. On the third, she argued with Ares about strategy. She had memorized dozens of arguments like those in a different life. Back then Athena hadn't just been a character in a myth. She'd been the mother Annabeth spent years trying to impress.

Thalia touched the edge of her script.

"You're allowed to find it strange," she said.

"It's a play."

"That's not what I meant."

Annabeth closed the pages. "I used to call Athena Mom. I know that sounds obvious, but I mean in my head. Even when she wasn't there, every hard decision felt like there was an invisible person grading it. I thought if I could be clever enough she'd approve."

"I remember you telling me."

"No, you remember me *telling* you," Annabeth said softly. "That's different."

Thalia didn't flinch. "You're right."

It was one of the things Annabeth loved about her now. Thalia had enough pride to argue all day about a broken shoelace, but she had learned not to claim events she hadn't witnessed.

"I know you were there for me when I was little here," Annabeth said. "You took me to school. You put me to bed. You argued with our father. I don't remember those things from inside. It isn't that I don't believe you."

"I know."

"But playing Athena and having to call Zeus 'Father'..." She tried to smile at the ridiculousness of it. "Will accidentally wrote me a biography in a joke."

Thalia rested her forearms on her knees. "Then don't play the biography."

"What do I play?"

"Someone who thinks she knows everything and gets to be wrong occasionally. You're good at that."

Annabeth nudged her with a shoulder. "I hate you."

"Deeply mutual."

They sat in the warm passage while the amphitheater emptied. Annabeth could hear a drill running in the workshop and Kayla tuning a guitar, testing the same phrase until it stopped sounding like a complaint.

"Would you want me to tell Will?" Thalia asked.

"That I used to have a different mother?"

"No. That one line is uncomfortable. You don't owe him the whole reason."

Annabeth opened the script again. She stared at the fish-meeting sentence. It wasn't the line itself, she realized. It was the careless assumption that Athena, daughter of Zeus, would call for her father's attention and actually receive it. Zeus could command a room by appearing in it. His daughters spent days guessing whether he would appear at all.

"I can say it," she decided. "But I want to change what Athena does afterward."

"Such as?"

"Maybe she doesn't wait for his answer. Maybe she solves the problem herself, even if he hates it."

Thalia gave her a look of approval so fierce that Annabeth almost laughed.

"That," Thalia said, "sounds like someone I'd watch a play about."

Annabeth took her pencil from behind her ear and wrote a note in the margin: *Athena does not beg for permission. She proposes, gets interrupted, acts anyway.*

"Will wrote the play," Thalia reminded her.

"I'll ask him."

"And if he says no?"

"I'll decide whether I still want the part."

"Good."

They heard footsteps at the end of the passage. Percy appeared carrying a bag of costume odds and ends, his expression suspicious.

"Why are you two looking so serious?" he asked.

"Research," Thalia said.

"On fish meetings?"

"On how Zeus should deliver a line without sounding like a malfunctioning weather forecast."

Percy considered. "Thalia can manage that."

"Thanks, Kelp Head."

He handed Annabeth a dented colander. "They want to know if this passes for a helmet."

Annabeth turned it over. One handle had been repaired with wire and the other was missing.

"It's an excellent example of why we don't buy armor at a kitchen supply shop."

"That's the spirit."

She put the colander on her head. It slipped over one eye. Percy laughed, not at her discomfort but at the ridiculous object perched on her curls.

"Don't," she warned.

"I didn't say anything."

"Your face did."

She took the helmet off. Percy set the bag down and reached for her hand, quiet now.

"You okay?" he asked.

"Getting there."

He didn't ask her to explain in front of Thalia. He didn't reach for a memory to prove that he understood. He just squeezed once and waited.

Annabeth loved him for the waiting.

Thalia stood up. "I'm going to tell Jason he has to stop doing that beagle poem in the hallways. It counts as noise pollution."

"Good luck," Percy said.

"I don't need luck. I'm Zeus."

She took three steps toward the amphitheater, paused and looked back at Annabeth.

"One thing," she said. "If Will insists on making Zeus a perfect father at the end, I'm out."

Annabeth held up her script with the new pencil note. "I think we can convince him not to."

Thalia nodded and disappeared into the sunlight.

Percy sat beside Annabeth. She put the colander between them and showed him the note. He read it, then took the pencil and added a line of his own beneath her careful handwriting.

*POSEIDON: This meeting could have been a seashell.*

Annabeth laughed so hard she had to lean against him.

"That's terrible," she said.

"I know."

"Keep it."

They went back inside to ask Will what could change.

---

Rehearsal day one was chaos in the purest, most Camp Half-Blood sense of the word, which is to say someone was on fire within twenty minutes and it wasn't even Clarisse's fault for once.

They started with the opening number, an ensemble song titled "Twelve (Well, Mostly Twelve) Olympians!" with choreography by Terpsichore cabin, which meant it involved a lot of spinning and one lift that no one could do.

"From the top!" Will called from the front row, script in one hand, megaphone in the other that he'd borrowed from the Ares cabin and that was slightly dented from being used as a weapon. "Grover, you start as narrator!"

Grover trotted onstage in his usual jeans and a hastily added laurel wreath that kept slipping over one of his horns — he'd glamoured them mostly invisible for the visiting mortals' news crew that wasn't there, but the wreath kept catching. "Uh, hi! I'm a satyr! Which means I'm wise and noble and—"

"Typecast!" Leo shouted from the wings, dressed in winged sneakers and a mailbag.

"—and I'm here to tell you the story of the gods, who are powerful and wise and definitely not petty at all—" An offstage thunder rumble played, three seconds too late. Grover paused. "Okay, they're a little petty."

"Enter Zeus!" Will hissed, waving frantically.

Thalia stomped onstage in jeans, her black leather jacket, and a white bedsheet toga draped over one shoulder that she'd knotted herself and refused to let anyone adjust. She had a lightning bolt made of cardboard covered in aluminum foil tucked under one arm like a skateboard.

She looked out at the empty amphitheater seats and deadpanned: "I AM ZEUS. TREMBLE BEFORE ME."

"More kingly!" Will called.

"I AM ZEUS," Thalia said, slightly louder, with exactly the same flat expression. "Tremble. Before me. Please."

Annabeth snorted from the wings. Percy elbowed her. She elbowed him back harder.

"Great! Good enough! Energy later!" Will said quickly. "Poseidon and Athena, you're on!"

Percy walked onstage in his sea-green chiton over his Camp Half-Blood t-shirt and shorts because there was no way he was doing this in just a sheet. His plastic trident was set to steady blue glow. His fake beard itched horribly.

Annabeth followed in a gray chiton over leggings, a toy owl perched on her shoulder held on with a hair clip, and a war helmet made from a colander that she'd already spray-painted bronze and actually made look good because she couldn't help herself.

"Father," Annabeth said, reading from her script with the crisp disdain she remembered from Athena in their original life. "Your meeting about fish is in five minutes."

Percy squinted at his cue card. "Tell the fish... I am busy brooding about... the sea?" He looked up. "Why am I brooding about the sea? I *am* the sea. That's like brooding about yourself."

"It's character depth," Will said.

"It's stupid," Percy said, but he was smiling despite himself because Annabeth's owl was slowly sliding off her shoulder and she kept tilting her head to keep it up without using her hands, which made her look like a curious bird herself.

"You're supposed to argue here," Kayla prompted. "Athena thinks Poseidon is too dramatic. Poseidon thinks Athena is a know-it-all."

"We don't even have to act," Annabeth muttered.

"Hey," Percy said.

"You once cried because you couldn't remember if sea urchins have faces."

"They looked sad!"

"Places!" Will shouted. "Clarisse, entrance!"

The doors at the back of the amphitheater slammed open. Clarisse La Rue marched down the center aisle in full Ares armor assembled from actual armor, red cape billowing, spear in hand, war paint across her cheeks that she had absolutely applied herself and refused to let the makeup crew touch up.

"I AM ARES!" she bellowed, leaping onto the stage in a way that shook the plywood Olympus. "GOD OF WAR! COWARDICE IS FOR ATHENA!"

"I'm right here," Annabeth said.

"EXACTLY! FIGHT ME, OWL GIRL!"

"Clarisse," Will said nervously. "The line is 'Greetings, family, let us discuss this matter calmly.'"

Clarisse stared at him. "Ares would never say that."

"It's a comedy about family therapy!"

"Ares doesn't do therapy. Ares does war." She turned to Percy and pointed her spear at his glowing trident. "Nice night-light, Fish Boy."

"Method acting," Percy whispered to Annabeth. "She's not even breaking character."

"She's not *in* character," Annabeth whispered back. "This is just Tuesday for her."

They tried the fight choreography. It was supposed to be a stylized slow-motion quarrel where Ares lunged, Athena sidestepped wisely, and Poseidon intervened with a wave. What actually happened was Clarisse lunged at full speed, Annabeth sidestepped on pure reflex honed from five years of sparring, grabbed Clarisse's spear shaft, twisted, and both of them went tumbling into Frank's half-built Olympus. A hinge broke. Frank caught a falling column with one hand without looking up from his drill.

"Hold!" Will shouted. "Hold! Are you okay?"

Clarisse sat up in the wreckage, grinning wildly. "Again!"

Annabeth sat up beside her, colander helmet dented, owl now hanging upside down. She started laughing — startled, breathless laughter. "Gods, your footwork's gotten better."

"Yours too, smart girl," Clarisse said, and offered her a hand up. For a moment they just grinned at each other, old rivals turned something like sisters-in-arms, dusty and undignified.

Percy, still standing center stage with his glowing trident, looked at Annabeth laughing in the rubble with her helmet crooked and felt that familiar, helpless lift in his chest. She caught his eye and stuck her tongue out at him. He mouthed *fish meeting* at her and she lost it all over again.

From the side, Piper, already in a pink Aphrodite wig, leaned toward Hazel and whispered, "Those two are disgusting. I love it."

Hazel nodded sagely. "They hold hands when they think no one's looking."

"We're *all* looking," Piper said. "That's the point."

---

By Wednesday rehearsal, things had developed a routine, which at Camp Half-Blood meant a rotating schedule of disasters.

Nico as Hades refused to come out from backstage for his entrance because, in his words, "Hades doesn't do matinees, or evenings, or plays." Will eventually coaxed him out by promising he could stay in shadow the whole time and that his helm of darkness could just be a hoodie pulled low. Nico delivered his line — "I am Hades. I am brooding in the Underworld. Do not perceive me." — in such a perfect deadpan that the Apollo kids howled and wrote him three more lines on the spot, which he hated and secretly memorized immediately.

Jason as Apollo kept accidentally glowing during his solo. Leo had wired little lamps into his costume, and static from Jason's nervous fingers kept triggering them. "Sorry!" he'd say, fumbling for the switch while the stage lights flickered sympathetically. "Still learning how this thing works."

"It's great!" Will insisted. "Method! You're literally radiant!"

Leo as Hermes spent most of rehearsals running actual messages across the stage at superhuman camp-counselor speed because someone had to, and ad-libbing so fast Will gave up trying to script him and just wrote *[LEO TALKS UNTIL SOMEONE STOPS HIM]* in the script.

Frank's set was genuinely incredible — three-tiered Olympus with painted clouds, a working fountain for Percy's wave entrance that used actual water bent very carefully and only slightly smugly by Percy when Will wasn't looking, and a throne for Zeus made from bleachers and gold spray paint that Thalia actually deigned to sit on once, pronounced "acceptable," and then never sat on again because sitting still wasn't her thing.

Piper kept rewriting Aphrodite's lines to be less ditzy and more sharp. "Aphrodite isn't stupid," she told Will firmly. "She's the most politically savvy person on Olympus. She just lets everyone underestimate her." Will, wisely, let her. Her new monologue about love being the only power Zeus couldn't thunderbolt into obedience gave even Clarisse chills in rehearsal.

Hazel as Persephone stole every scene she was in without trying, delivering lines about spring and the Underworld with such quiet conviction that Nico, acting opposite her, actually looked up from under his hoodie and acted back despite himself. After one run-through, he muttered, "You're good," and Hazel beamed for an hour.

And at the center of it, Percy and Annabeth bickered through every scene as Poseidon and Athena, which was easy because the script had them bickering about everything from who invented the chariot to whose city was better — Athens, obviously, and Percy had to say the line "Fine! Your city has better olives! Happy?" through gritted teeth while Annabeth tried not to laugh.

"You *are* enjoying this," Percy accused her on the second day, backstage in the makeshift costume tent while he tried to fix his beard with spirit gum that was definitely older than he was.

"I'm enjoying watching you suffer," Annabeth corrected, reaching up without thinking to help him. Her fingers were cool against his jaw as she smoothed the fake beard down. "Hold still. You're doing it wrong."

Percy went very still. The tent was cramped and hot, smelling of canvas and face paint and sawdust. Somewhere outside, Leo was yelling about winged shoes and someone — probably Clarisse — was yelling back. Annabeth's brow was furrowed in concentration, her gray eyes flicking over his face, her owl clip glinting in her hair. She'd left the colander helmet off for once and her curls were escaping everywhere.

"There," she said softly, patting the beard. "Distinguished. Very godly. Very—"

"Fishy?" he suggested.

"Don't ruin the moment, Seaweed Brain."

He caught her wrist before she could pull away, not hard, just enough to keep her close a second longer. "Hi," he said stupidly.

"Hi," she said back, equally stupidly, and smiled like sunrise.

From outside, Thalia's voice cut through: "If you two are making out in the beard tent, I'm telling Chiron and also I'm leaving!"

They sprang apart. Percy nearly ripped his beard off. Annabeth nearly swallowed her pencil.

"We're not!" they shouted in unison.

Thalia stuck her head in, took one look at Percy's red ears and Annabeth's flushed cheeks, and smirked. "Sure. Zeus sees all, you know. Tremble before me, etc." She tossed Percy a juice box. "Hydrate, Poseidon. You look faint."

"I hate this play," Percy muttered, but he was holding Annabeth's hand under the costume table where Thalia pretended not to see, and he didn't let go until Will called places.

Will called places for the council scene and handed Annabeth a revised page.

"I took your note," he said. "Athena doesn't ask permission at the end. She makes a proposal and Zeus ignores her, so she goes and does it."

"That's better," Annabeth replied.

"I thought so too." He hesitated. "But Thalia says Zeus shouldn't apologize at the end."

Annabeth looked up.

Will glanced toward the throne. "I wrote the apology because it's a family play. People like it when the dad learns something. It's the end of the show. We need a laugh and then everybody gets cake."

"People like plenty of things," Thalia said from the steps. She had heard him. Her costume was draped over one shoulder, and she held the cardboard lightning bolt by its handle.

"That's my entire job tonight," Will said. "Give people something they like."

"Then give them something they recognize."

He opened the script. "I could change 'I'm sorry' to 'I see your point.'"

"That sounds even less like Zeus."

Piper came down from the wing with her revised Aphrodite monologue tucked under her arm. "What are you arguing about?"

"Whether Zeus can learn something in under five minutes," Will said.

Piper looked at Thalia and Annabeth, then at the page. "Can he?"

"Not in my experience," Thalia replied.

Will rubbed his forehead. It was only Wednesday, but his directing clipboard already looked as if it had survived a war.

"I don't want this to be a show about everybody's worst parent," he said.

"It doesn't have to be," Annabeth answered. "But you can't make a father kind just because a joke needs a neat ending. That's not what makes people feel better."

"Then what does?"

She thought of Milo and Lucy and Bea, three new campers who'd spent their first day discovering that camp had beautiful things and frightening ones. Those children might be watching on Friday. So might dozens of others whose gods had never appeared to them at all. Will wanted them laughing, which wasn't wrong. He wanted the show to mean the world could be kinder than it was, and Annabeth wanted that too.

"Let the people in the scene decide what to do," she said. "Athena doesn't need Zeus to approve. Poseidon doesn't need to win. Ares doesn't need to stop being angry to be loyal. The ending can be about the council making a choice."

Will looked unconvinced. "A committee decision. Theater audiences love those."

"Give it an actual problem," Piper said.

"Such as?"

"Something the gods have spent five pages arguing about. Someone's missing. The gods are too busy proving which parent gets credit to notice."

Percy, who'd been standing in the wings practicing the least convincing trident stance in human history, stepped forward.

"Make it about the tide," he offered. "The harbor's flooding because the council can't agree which god owns the shore. Poseidon can solve the water part, Athena can find a route, Hermes can get messages out, and Ares can organize people. They have to work together or the town gets washed out."

Will considered that. "We'd need a way to show a town."

Frank raised a hand without looking up from the hinge he was tightening. "Cardboard village. Three panels. I can make one that comes apart when the water reaches it."

"No actual water against the cardboard," Annabeth said instantly.

"I know."

"You said that about the first mountain too."

"The first mountain was experimental."

Clarisse leaned her spear on the rack. "If I'm playing Ares, he gets to be useful. He doesn't have to be a nice guy. He can still hold the street while civilians get out."

"That could work," Will said, sketching a note.

"And he doesn't get to stab anybody for comic relief," Clarisse added. "That's not a strategy."

Percy glanced at her. "You feeling okay?"

"Shut up, Fish Boy."

Will was already rewriting the last two pages. He paused, pencil hovering.

"What does Zeus do?"

Thalia looked at Annabeth. There was no single answer that would fix a real father's absence. She could play a king who was difficult and still let the play end with someone doing a good thing.

"He can give an order," she said. "A very loud order. Nobody listens because they're already busy solving the problem."

Will's mouth fell open into a grin. "That's good."

"And when it's over," Thalia continued, "he can take credit."

"That's even better."

She didn't smile. "It would be funny."

Annabeth met her eyes. The joke had a sharp edge, but Thalia had chosen it herself.

They spent the next hour rehearsing the new ending.

Leo, as Hermes, ran between two cardboard houses shouting evacuation instructions that nobody could hear because Austin's sound system kept making a noise like a submarine sinking. Frank adjusted the volume. Nico, as Hades, appeared from behind a painted rock to provide a suitably bleak assessment of everyone's life expectancy, then disappeared again before Will could give him another line. Hazel played a villager who refused to leave her garden until someone promised her plants would be replanted. She said it so earnestly that Clarisse stopped joking long enough to promise, in character, to carry the pots.

Percy reached the part where Poseidon was meant to divert the water. He lifted his trident, and the pump behind the stage clicked. A few droplets rose through the fountain's nozzle. Nothing else happened.

"Cut," Will called. "Why didn't it work?"

"The valve isn't open," Frank said.

"Can Percy just move it with his powers?"

Percy shook his head. "Not as a shortcut. If there's plumbing, use the plumbing. We're doing a play, not a demonstration of what I can do if everyone else messes up."

Frank checked the valve. Its handle had been left halfway closed after the earlier test. He opened it, then moved everyone back while Percy took his place again.

This time the fountain made an arc of water that struck the shallow catch basin and stayed there. Leo declared the imaginary harbor saved. Clarisse took a bow for carrying Hazel's garden. Will called for Zeus's final line.

Thalia stepped onto the throne and raised the cardboard bolt.

"I HAVE SAVED YOU ALL," she announced.

The others, busy moving the cardboard houses out of the way, did not look up.

She waited.

No one moved.

Thalia lowered the bolt slightly. "I said I have saved you all."

Piper, still holding a potted fake rose, replied without turning around, "Great. Could you grab the other end of this?"

The rehearsal broke into laughter. Thalia maintained the kingly stare for three more seconds before she began laughing too.

Will raised both hands. "Keep that. That's the ending."

They ran it again. The second time the timing worked better, because everyone knew what had to happen before the joke. Athena pointed out the evacuation route. Hermes carried word to the harbor. Ares defended the bridge. Poseidon moved the water through a safe channel. Zeus appeared last and took credit for a result he hadn't caused.

In the wings afterward, Will sat beside Thalia and offered her a cup of water.

"I'm sorry I put you in a bad position," he said.

She looked at him. "You didn't know."

"I still could've asked before I cast you."

"Yeah."

"Do you want to stay?"

Thalia looked at the damp stage, the cheap throne, the other campers carefully putting scenery away. She had spent the afternoon playing someone who had made her feel small in real life, and for once she'd had room to decide what he said and what he didn't get to control.

"I do," she said. "But if you put an apology back into my mouth, I quit."

"Understood."

She finished her water and went to help Frank carry the throne into storage, still wearing the cardboard crown crooked on her head.

Annabeth watched from a few steps away. She wanted to run after her sister and ask whether she was really all right. She didn't. Thalia had answered Will's question clearly. Annabeth would ask her own later, somewhere private, when an answer wouldn't become another performance.

---

Thursday's dress rehearsal was, in Will's words afterward, "a complete disaster in the best way," which meant everything that could go wrong did, but no one got permanently injured.

The fountain leaked. Austin's soundscape played whale noises during Hades' brooding monologue, which actually made it funnier and Nico refused to admit he laughed but his shoulders shook. Thalia's cardboard lightning bolt caught fire — briefly — when she got annoyed during the Zeus-Poseidon argument scene and sparked for real. Percy, on instinct, doused it with a wave of fountain water, soaking Thalia, himself, Annabeth, and the front row of Apollo kids.

There was a moment of stunned silence. Water dripped off Thalia's spiky hair. Percy's beard hung limp and soggy. Annabeth's chiton clung to her leggings and her owl was soaked.

Then Thalia started laughing — sharp, surprised barking laughter. Annabeth followed. Percy bowed deeply, water streaming off his trident, and announced in his best godly voice: "BEHOLD! POSEIDON HAS BLESSED THIS PRODUCTION!"

The whole amphitheater erupted. Will, soaked to the knees with his clipboard wilting, laughed until he cried. "Keep it! Keep all of it! The wave is now part of the show!"

Clarisse, who had been waiting in the wings for her fight cue, strode on early, spear raised, water flicking off her armor. "YOU DARE MAKE WAVES IN MY THEATER OF WAR?" she roared, completely off-script, and tackled Percy straight into the fountain. They both went under to massive cheers. Annabeth waded in after them to "mediate as Athena" and ended up sitting on the fountain edge, kicking water at both of them while delivering her Athens-is-better speech with perfect timing.

Grover, thinking fast, grabbed the narrator mic and improvised: "And so the gods, in their infinite wisdom, discovered... splash fights! The end! Just kidding, there's a second act!"

Even Mr. D, who had wandered by to complain about the noise and stayed because no one told him he couldn't, was seen to smile faintly into his Diet Coke. "Three out of five stars," he told Chiron. "The fish boy has presence. Don't tell him I said that."

Afterward, damp and exhausted, the cast sprawled across the amphitheater steps sharing towels and stolen dining pavilion cookies. Percy sat with his arm dripping around Annabeth's shoulders, both wrapped in a single Apollo cabin blanket that was too small. Thalia lay across three steps with her jacket over her face. Grover was wringing out his shirt. Clarisse was already planning how to make the fountain tackle look intentional tomorrow.

"Tomorrow's the real thing," Will said, sitting down heavily beside them, hair plastered to his forehead. "Full camp. Chiron. Mr. D. Possibly some dryads if Austin fixes the invitations. Are we... are we actually ready?"

"No," said half the cast in unison.

"We're ready enough," Annabeth said. She'd pulled her damp blueprints out of her bag and was sketching reinforcement braces for Olympus on the back of her script. "Frank's set will hold if we don't tackle it again. Leo knows his cues if someone stands in the wings with a hook. Nico will show up if Hazel asks him nicely."

"I always show up," Nico muttered from under a towel.

"You hid in a prop cloud yesterday," Hazel pointed out gently.

"It was good cover."

Percy nudged Will's knee with his own. "Hey. It's going to be good. It's funny, it's ours. Even if we mess up, everyone will laugh *with* us. Mostly."

Will smiled tiredly. "Thanks, Percy. How's Poseidon feeling about tomorrow?"

"Terrified. Beard itches. Trident has a strobe setting." Percy glanced at Annabeth. "But Athena's got my back, right?"

Annabeth bumped his shoulder with hers. "Always, Seaweed Brain. Someone has to make sure you don't drown on dry land."

Thalia lifted her jacket off her face just enough to peer at them. "If you two do the cute backstage pep talk tomorrow, do it where I can't see. I have a reputation."

"You cried during Piper's monologue yesterday," Percy said.

"I had water in my eyes. From the fountain. That *you* caused."

"Sure, Zeus."

---

Performance night arrived warm and clear, with frogs calling down by the lake and the amphitheater filling row by row as the sun went down. Someone — Leo, probably, with Frank's help — had strung fairy lights and paper stars above the stage. The fountain glinted. Olympus gleamed gold and white under the lights. Backstage smelled of sweat, hairspray, and the particular adrenaline of twenty demigods about to embarrass themselves in front of everyone they knew.

Percy stood in the wings in his now mostly dry chiton, beard freshly glued by Annabeth with much more care and much more lingering than strictly necessary, trident set firmly to *steady* after Will physically removed the strobe option. His hands were shaking.

He'd fought monsters. He'd held up the sky. He'd said no to immortality. None of it felt as exposed as this.

"You good?" Annabeth appeared beside him, already in costume, owl secure, colander helmet shining. She'd lined her eyes with dark kohl for Athena and it made her gray eyes look huge and fierce and, to Percy, completely beautiful.

"No," Percy said honestly. "There's like two hundred people out there. Including my brother. Tyson's in the front row with a foam finger that says GO POSEIDON. He made it himself."

Annabeth laughed, then squeezed his arm. "Hey. Look at me." He did. "You're going to be great. You *are* Poseidon, in the ways that matter. Stubborn, loyal, dramatic about the sea. And if you forget your lines, just point the trident and say something fishy. No one will know."

"Fish meeting in five minutes," Percy said weakly.

"Exactly." She hesitated, glancing around. The wings were crowded — Thalia pacing and cracking her knuckles, Clarisse doing lunges with her spear, Grover hyperventilating into a paper bag while Piper talked him down, Nico lurking in a prop cloud even though it wasn't his cue. No one was looking directly at them, but at camp, everyone was always peripherally looking.

Annabeth held out her hand, palm up, small and steady. "For luck?"

Percy stared at her hand for half a heartbeat, then laced his fingers through hers, tight and warm. Backstage hand-hold, hidden in the folds of their costumes, trident pressed against owl-feather cloak.

It was such a small thing. They'd held hands a hundred times — on quests, falling, running, sleeping on airplanes. But this was different. This was chosen, calm, deliberate. Not survival. Just *luck*. Just *I'm here*.

"For luck," he echoed.

They stood like that while Will gave his frantic last-minute speech — "Have fun, remember your cues, Clarisse please don't actually stab anyone, Leo please *do* stop talking at some point, Thalia your bolt is fireproof now, Grover breathe, Percy point don't strobe, Annabeth you're brilliant, let's go!" — and while the house lights dimmed and the crowd hushed and Austin cued the opening music, slightly off-key lyre and crickets.

Annabeth squeezed once, hard, then let go to take her entrance position. Her fingers trailed against his palm as she stepped away. Percy lifted his trident, adjusted his itchy beard, and thought *I can do this*.

"Break a leg," Thalia muttered as she stalked past him toward her own entrance, cardboard bolt under her arm, no wig, hair spiked defiantly. "Or I will break it for you. Zeus has spoken."

"Tremble before you, got it," Percy said.

Thalia almost smiled.

---

The play, against all odds and several prophecies, was a triumph.

Grover opened to warm applause, wreath miraculously staying on, delivering his narrator bit with earnest charm: "Welcome, mortals and demigods and that one satyr in the back eating the program — that was me, sorry — to the story of the gods!" He got a laugh and visibly relaxed, hooves tapping happily on the stage.

Thalia as Zeus stomped on to cheers from the Hunters in the audience — they'd come for the weekend and whistled sharply — and delivered "I AM ZEUS! TREMBLE BEFORE ME!" with such reluctant, thunderous charisma that the crowd roared. She threw her foil bolt into the air, sparked real lightning off her fingertips to make it flash — Frank had grounded the set for exactly this — and sat sideways on her throne like it was a motorcycle. Someone in the back shouted "NO WIG, NO PROBLEM!" Thalia flipped them off with affection.

Percy's entrance got the biggest cheer of the night so far. Two Apollo kids carried in the blue bedsheet wave, Percy rode it with his glowing trident held high, fountain arcing behind him on cue — Frank had timed the pump perfectly — and Tyson stood up in the front row waving his foam finger and shouting "THAT'S MY BROTHER! HE LIVES IN THE SEA!" Percy, beet red under his beard, pointed his trident at Tyson and mouthed *sit down*, which made everyone laugh harder.

He nailed his brooding-about-the-sea bit by playing it completely sincere, which Annabeth later told him was the funniest possible choice. When he declared, "The tides... they call to me... because I am... also the tide," even Chiron chuckled.

Annabeth as Athena was perfect in a way that made Percy's chest ache. She was sharp and funny and just vulnerable enough in the scene where Athena admits she wanted her father's respect more than his throne. She'd rewritten the dialogue herself the night before, cross-legged on her bunk with Percy half-asleep beside her offering unhelpful suggestions like "add more owls." The new lines landed quietly after all the slapstick: "Wisdom isn't knowing everything, Father. It's knowing when to listen." Thalia, caught off guard, answered Zeus's scripted dismissive line a beat too late and a shade too gently, and for a second they weren't acting at all — just two daughters of Zeus looking at each other across a painted stage, understanding something about their difficult divine father.

Then Clarisse crashed through Olympus — on purpose this time, through the breakaway panel Frank had built — screaming "ENOUGH TALK! WAR!" and the moment broke into delighted chaos. Her fight with Annabeth was clean and rehearsed this time, sidestep and twist and slow-motion grapple while Leo as Hermes commentated at double speed: "And-Athena-ducks-Ares-swings-she-counters-with-logic-he-counters-with-a-spear-oh-this-is-better-than-the-actual-myths-folks!"

Piper's Aphrodite monologue earned real silence, then thunderous applause. Hazel's Persephone song — Kayla had written her a soft spring melody on guitar — made half the Demeter cabin cry. Nico's Hades got a standing ovation from the Hades table, which consisted of Nico, Hazel, and an empty chair they insisted was occupied by "Dad, watching proudly," which may or may not have been a joke. Jason's Apollo solo glowed — literally — and he held the last note while radiant. The crowd went wild.

The second-act splash fight, now fully choreographed, brought the house down. On cue, Percy raised his trident, Thalia sparked, Annabeth shouted her Athens line — "Fine! Your city has better fish! Mine has better *everything else*!" — Clarisse tackled Percy into the fountain, Annabeth cannonballed in after them fully intentional this time, Grover narrated "And thus harmony was restored to Olympus through mutual drowning!" while getting soaked himself. Water arced glittering under the fairy lights. The front rows shrieked and leaned in instead of away.

For the finale, the whole cast assembled dripping on Olympus, arms around each other, and sang the closing reprise off-key but loud: "We're dysfunctional but divine! We're your family through time!" Even Thalia sang, flat and loud. Even Nico mouthed some words from under his hoodie. Percy had water in his beard and Annabeth's wet hand in his — this time openly, center stage, fingers interlocked where everyone could see — and when they bowed, Tyson threw his foam finger onstage and Percy caught it one-handed with his trident in the other.

The applause was deafening. Someone started chanting "ENCORE! ENCORE!" Someone else — Connor Stoll — started chanting "FISH MEETING! FISH MEETING!" which caught on alarmingly fast.

Backstage afterward was sweaty, joyful pandemonium. Will cried happy tears and hugged everyone, clipboard abandoned. Kayla and Austin blasted whale noises in celebration. Frank accepted compliments on his set with modest nods while Hazel showed off her Persephone flower crown to Piper. Leo tried to sell Hermes delivery services for kiss-grams. Jason glowed and apologized. Nico escaped to the shadows with Hazel and hot chocolate. Tyson barrelled backstage, lifted Percy clean off the ground in a hug that nearly removed the beard permanently, and declared, "YOU WERE THE BEST POSEIDON! EVEN BETTER THAN DAD! DON'T TELL DAD!"

"I won't," Percy wheezed.

Clarisse found Annabeth by the costume rack, both still damp, Athena's owl drooping and Ares' war paint smeared. For a second they just looked at each other.

"Good fight," Clarisse said gruffly.

"Good fight," Annabeth said.

Clarisse nodded once, then, awkwardly, stuck out her hand. Annabeth shook it. "Next time, no choreography," Clarisse said. "Real spar. You and me. No audience."

"You're on," Annabeth said, grinning. "Loser cleans the arena."

"Deal, Owl Head."

Thalia found Percy and Annabeth a few minutes later, out of her toga and back in her jacket, bolt tucked under her arm like a trophy. She looked at their joined hands — they'd barely let go since the finale bow — and rolled her eyes so hard it was practically affectionate.

"Disgusting," she declared. "Adorable. I'm telling everyone you were holding hands *before* the show too for luck. That's the kind of propaganda the Apollo cabin lives for."

"You wouldn't," Percy said.

"I would. I am Zeus. Tremble before me." But she slung an arm around each of their shoulders, pulling them into a brief, fierce hug for her sister and her sister's boyfriend that smelled of rain and stage smoke. "You were good, Kelp Head. Both of you. Don't let it go to your heads."

"Too late," Annabeth said, leaning into her. "My head is already huge. Athena, you know."

Thalia snorted. Percy laughed. For a moment the noise of camp faded — the cheering crowd, the bickering cast, the creaking set — and it was just the three of them backstage under paper stars, damp and tired and happy.

Later, after costumes were shed and makeup wiped off and Will had finally stopped crying, Percy and Annabeth walked back to their cabins together the long way, around the lake where the lights from the amphitheater reflected wavering on the water. Percy carried his plastic trident because Tyson wanted him to keep it. Annabeth still had her owl clip in her hair. Neither mentioned that they were holding hands again, swinging slightly between them.

"So," Percy said. "Poseidon. Embarrassing. Never again."

"You loved it," Annabeth said.

"I hated 80% of it."

"And the other 20%?"

Percy thought about her fingers finding his in the wings, her laugh in the fountain wreckage, her hand in his under the stage lights where everyone could see and no one minded. He swung their hands once.

"The other 20% was you," he said simply.

Annabeth stopped walking. Turned to face him in the dark by the lake, gray eyes catching moonlight. "Percy Jackson, was that a line from the play?"

"No," he said. "Will didn't write anything that good at 2 a.m."

She smiled, slow and bright, and kissed him — quick, sure, tasting faintly of fountain water and stage makeup and cookies. Somewhere across camp, someone — Leo, probably — set off celebratory fireworks that crackled green and gold over the lake.

"For luck?" Percy asked when they parted, forehead resting against hers.

"For success," Annabeth corrected. "We already had the luck."

Hand in hand, Poseidon and Athena — Percy and Annabeth — walked the rest of the way home under paper stars and real ones, while behind them Camp Half-Blood buzzed and laughed and rehashed every splash and flubbed line of the night they'd all made together.

The playbill, pinned to the Big House porch the next morning with a soggy foam finger taped beside it, said it best in Will's exhausted, happy handwriting:

*GODS! — SOLD OUT! NO SMITINGS! THANK YOU! SEQUEL NEVER! (MAYBE.)*
