## Chapter 193: The Heel

Percy Jackson woke on Sunday morning in a cabin that had decided, overnight, to be warm, and lay still under the blanket with his eyes open, doing the slow arithmetic of a room he had slept in for five months and had never once woken up warm in.

The difference was not the stove. The stove had been lit at six the night before the way it was lit every night, and it had gone down to the state it always went down to, which was the state of a thing that keeps a low opinion of the dark and holds the opinion until morning. The difference was the windows. On Saturday, in teams, cabin by cabin, the camp had put winter between the glass and the room: blankets hung over the frames and pinned at the corners, cloth rolled and pushed down into the sills, mud packed hard along the bottom of the doors. Clarisse had taken Cabin Three for herself and Percy for carrying, and had stood in the middle of the room afterwards with her arms folded, looking at the work of a woman who had been the entire crew available.

The room had held. That was the whole of it. He could feel the heat coming off the far wall in a way he had only ever felt coming off a stove.

He got up. The boards were cold and the air was warm, which was a combination he had not met before and did not entirely trust. Two pairs of socks, one drawn inside the other; trousers; the wool shirt that still smelled faintly of the dryers after four washes; the heavy coat off the hook, which he did not put on. Outside the shutter the light was the flat grey-white of a late October morning over water, and the tenth lantern on its hook by the door had gone out hours ago and left its small smell of cold brass and burnt wax coming in around the frame.

Down at the bottom of the hill the lake lay closed and grey and enormous, doing nothing. Above it the whole slope was quiet in a way that a hill is quiet the morning after a day of work: no axe, no barrow, no argument about rope. Everybody on it had earned the right to still be in bed, and the four kids in the far bunks were taking it, one of them snoring in a register that Percy had privately filed under *structural*.

"You're making the stove noise," Silena said, from her bunk, without opening her eyes. "You've been standing there for a minute and a half doing nothing. Either sit down or go outside."

"I'm appreciating the warmth."

"You're appreciating it in my light. Sit down and I'll tell you what's for breakfast."

"It's Sunday."

"It's Sunday," Silena agreed, and sat up with her hair in the state it went into and her blanket around her shoulders, and looked at the room the way she looked at a kitchen, which was as a set of things that would shortly be in the wrong place if nobody moved them. "It's Sunday and you are not being the eggs."

"Sunday's me. That's the law. I've been the eggs since September."

"You were the eggs on a Sunday when the cabin was freezing and there was nothing else to do." She was already up, already at the stove, already doing the thing where she was warm before anybody else. "Today you're being fed. There's a difference and the difference is batter."

Percy sat down on the bench by the window with his hands in his sleeves and watched his middle sister run a morning, and thought that there was probably a whole branch of learning somewhere that had never once tried to describe what a girl with a braid and a wooden spoon could do to a room at twenty past seven.

The batter had been Saturday night's, left to sit on the cool shelf behind the door with a cloth over it, and it had done whatever batter does overnight, which is to become more itself. Silena cooked the cakes on the stove top in the flat iron pan, one at a time, and the pan was the right temperature because she had her palm over it and had not needed to look. Honey butter out of the crock. The last of the apple butter from the press shed, in a jar with a paper lid. A pot of cocoa that was already made and already going cold, which was how Silena told you she had made it early and had not mentioned it.

"Clarisse," Percy said. "She'll want to know the list moved."

"The list's on the table," Clarisse said, from the doorway, coming in out of the cold with her coat open and her hair done badly, which meant she had been out on the porch and had been out on the porch for a while. "The list's been up since six. Some of us don't need an invitation to read a page."

"You've read it four times since I've been sitting here."

"Five." She put the pencil behind her ear, took the mug off the stove without waiting to be offered it, and sat down at the head of the table with the winter list flat under her hand, which was the position she had been in since Friday. "Cakes. Good."

"They're batter cakes. They've got a name."

"They've got butter on them is what they've got."

"They've got honey butter."

"They've got honey butter," Clarisse agreed, which was the whole of the concession she was prepared to make before eight in the morning, and she turned the list a quarter round so it faced her properly and went on reading it.

Percy ate two cakes standing up, because sitting down for a second helping was a thing that invited a comment, and looked at the page under his sister's thumb. It had been on the shelf in this cabin since a Sunday morning in a frost, and it had acquired a life of its own since: TEMPEST HALL, TUESDAYS + THURSDAYS, 7PM, CABIN THREE, AND WHOEVER. STORM QUILT. LANTERNS. RIDGE. And at the bottom, where the three oldest jobs had sat all month waiting for a warm day, something had changed.

GREENHOUSE BANK. TORN AWNING. The two of them were still there, still open, still with the blank line beside them that Clarisse did not fill in because filling a line in meant the job was done and the job was not done. But the third line had grown a second line under it, in the same blocky hand, written since Saturday night:

FALLEN PINE. LIMBED TO THE CLEARING. THRESHOLDS PACKED, THREE. SAW CREW THURSDAY. NOT DONE.

"Not done," Percy said.

"Not done." Clarisse did not look up. "The pine's mine. It came down in September and it's been lying there ever since looking at me, and on Saturday I got the branches off it and the mud in the doors, and that is not the same thing as finished, and anybody who says it is can come and saw it themselves."

"I'm not saying it is."

"You're thinking it. I can hear you thinking it. It's a specific noise." She turned the page over, face down, which in this cabin meant the subject had been tabled and would not be tabled again before noon. Under it, Percy had caught, upside down, the edge of a different page with a heading on it in a different pen, and four lines under the heading in a hand that was not Clarisse's blocky one and not Silena's round one either. He had seen it on Friday. He had not asked about it. There were things in this family that you did not ask about, and a page in a different pen was one of them.

"Calder's off the hill," Clarisse said, to her cocoa.

Percy stopped with the pot in his hand. "Since when?"

"Since six. He went past the porch with his bag on his shoulder and his bow cased, and looked at the lantern, and kept going. Didn't say where." She drank. "Chiron knows. Chiron knows everything and says a quarter of it."

"Thalia know?"

"She wasn't up." Clarisse set the mug down and looked at the shutter a moment with the flat expression she wore when she was reporting a thing sideways, which was the only kind of reporting she did. "You'll pass the field on your way up the hill. You can look at the empty if you want to look at the empty."

"I wasn't going up the hill."

"You were going up the hill at ten to nine with a pan in your hands because Silena asked you to, and you've been going up that hill at ten to nine every Sunday since the start of the month, and one of these days you're going to admit you've got a schedule." She reached across the table and took a third cake off the stack without asking, which was a privilege of the oldest, and bit into it, and spoke around it with the total absence of manners that only a person completely at home can afford. "Good cake."

"It's batter."

"It's batter," Clarisse agreed.

In the corner by the stove, on the good chair with the mending basket beside it, Silena had a project going, and the project had been going since Saturday night. It was a snake. It was not an animal. It was a tube of stiff woollen cloth, seamed up the long side and turned back on itself with the seam inside where nobody would catch a heel on it, and it was as long as a windowsill and as thick as a wrist, and it was meant to lie along the bottom of a window frame and stop the cold from coming in under the blanket where the blanket did not quite meet the wood.

She had made four on Saturday night after the work party, on the theory that a cabin with four windows wanted four snakes, and had filled them with sand out of the forge yard because sand was heavy and sand stayed where it was put.

"That's a snake," Percy said.

"That's a draught snake."

"That's a snake that you've made out of my old shirt."

"That's a draught snake that I've made out of your old shirt, and the shirt had a hole in the elbow, and the elbow is now on the inside of the snake where it is doing more good than it ever did on your arm." She did not look up from the fifth one. Her needle went through the wool with the small decisive sound she made everything with. "Cabin Three has four windows and one door and I want the whole place so well sealed that Clarisse has to go and sit on the porch to read, because she can't stand a warm room."

"I can hear you," Clarisse said.

"You're meant to. I'm not being subtle. I'm being a sister." Silena bit the thread off and held the fifth snake up against the frame to check its length against the sill, and then set it down and reached for the sixth, and her hand went, on the way past, to the pocket of the apron, and touched something in it, flat, once, the way a person checks a door they have already locked. Then it went back to the wool, and nobody said anything about it, because nobody in this cabin remarked on the pockets of the middle sister.

Percy poured the last of the cocoa into Clarisse's mug without being asked, which was the other half of the arrangement, and sat down on the end of the bench with his hands around a cup he had not made, in a room that was warm, on a Sunday, and let the whole of it arrive: the stove ticking, the four kids snoring in the far bunks, the snake growing in the corner out of his dead shirts, his oldest sister reading a page she had already read five times because the page was the shape of a thing she did not have a word for yet.

"You're doing the face," Clarisse said.

"What face."

"The face you do when you're being happy and you don't want anybody to know you've noticed it's Sunday."

"I don't have a face."

"You've got about nine. I've catalogued them. They're all stupid and this is the stupidest." She drained the mug and set it down and looked, for a second, at the warm wall the way a person looks at something they have made and are not going to talk about. "The room held," she said. "That's all. The blankets did the job. The mud did the job. It's not a miracle, it's just a room with winter on the outside of it."

"It's warm," Percy said.

"It's warm," Clarisse agreed, and got up, and took the list with her to the porch, because the porch was hers on a morning like this one and everybody in the family knew it.

"Percy." Silena did not look up from the sixth snake. "There's a second pan under the cloth by the door. Two cakes, the small ones, honey butter on the side. Take it up the hill at ten to nine."

"Annabeth's coming down. She comes down on Sundays. She came down twice last week."

"She came down twice last week because it was cold in that cabin and the toast was here and she has got the survival instincts of a person who was raised by my sister." Silena squared the snake against her thumb and judged it. "She'll come down once more today. Take the pan anyway. If she doesn't want them she'll eat them standing up on the step, and you'll have had a walk, and you never go up that hill with nothing in your hands."

"I've got hands."

"You've got hands and a girl and no sense," Silena said, with enormous affection, and turned the sixth snake inside out with a pencil the way a person turns a thing that has been made to be turned. "Go on. Ten to nine. It's a good hour to be out."

It was a small sentence and she said it the way a person says a sentence that has been living in their pocket a while, and Percy, who had spent five months learning that the most useful thing a younger brother could do in a cabin was to carry a pan and ask nothing, put his cup down and went to get his coat.

"Percy," Silena said, behind him, with the needle going. "If you pass the field, don't stop."

"Nobody said anything about the field."

"Nobody's saying anything about a lot of fields," Silena said, and sewed.

---

The hour before nine was the hour when the camp belonged to the people who were already awake. The lake lay flat and grey under a sky that had not decided about the sun, and the frost had come in the night and gone again and left the grass stiff and silver at the edges, and the whole hill had the held-breath feel of a cold morning that was waiting to see what kind of day it was going to be.

Percy went out with the pan under his cloth and his hands in his pockets and his breath going up, and he did not stop at the field, because Silena had told him not to, and because the field was a thing that was going to be there when he came back and the cakes were a thing that were going to be warm for exactly as long as it took to carry them up the hill.

The path up the hill was quiet. The cabins were doing their morning noises behind their doors — a stove being poked, a braid being argued with, somebody's boots on a threshold — and the cold came up off the lake and moved the pines, and the tenth lantern had burned out on its hook at the bottom of the hill and left a ring of frost on the wood where the heat had been, and Percy walked up the hill with the pan under his arm and the cloth coming loose at the corner and the particular pleasure of being the person who carries the warm thing.

He met Annabeth halfway. She was coming down the path with her field book under her arm and her hair in a braid that had already started to lose, and she had on the coat with the mended cuff and the boots with the good laces, and she stopped when she saw him and looked at the pan and then at him and then at the pan again.

"Ten to nine," she said.

"Ten to nine."

"Silena said ten to nine."

"Silena said ten to nine and here I am at ten to nine with a pan." He lifted the cloth. "Two cakes. Small ones. Honey butter on the side."

"She said two cakes. She said small." Annabeth came closer and looked at the pan the way she looked at drawings, with her head on one side and her eyebrows doing something, and then she reached out and took the corner of the cloth and lifted it an edge and looked at the cakes, and the cakes were small and warm and had come out of the oven with their tops cracked in the good way, and she let the cloth fall and stepped back. "They're good cakes."

"They're Silena's cakes."

"They're good cakes and they're Silena's and you carried them up the hill at ten to nine." She was looking at him with the face she wore when she had noticed a thing and was deciding whether to say it. "You're not stopping at the field."

"I'm not stopping at the field."

"Silena told you not to stop at the field."

"Silena told me not to stop at the field and I am not stopping at the field." He shifted the pan to his other arm. "She also told you'd come down twice last week because it was cold in the cabin and the toast was here."

"The toast is always here. The toast is a permanent condition of this cabin." Annabeth fell into step beside him and they went up the hill together with the pan between them and the cold on their faces and the path quiet under their boots, and she said, after a while, "It's cold in the cabin."

"It's cold in every cabin. It's October."

"It's cold in the cabin and the toast is here and I have got the field book and the pencil and the whole morning with nothing in it but the wall." She was not looking at him. She was looking at the path. "The wall's done. The survey's done. The step's in. The door's flat. The greenhouse bank's done. The list is down to two things and one of them's the awning and the awning wants a dry afternoon and it's not going to get one. So the morning's got nothing in it."

"There's the hall Tuesday."

"There's the hall Tuesday and it's Sunday." She stopped and turned to him and there was the face, the one she wore when she was about to say a thing she had been carrying, and she said it. "Come up the hill. Come up and have the cakes and the toast and sit on the step and don't go in. Just come up the hill and be warm."

"I was going to come up the hill."

"I know you were going to come up the hill. I'm asking you to come up the hill." She took the pan from him, because she was Annabeth and took things, and she started up the path, and he followed her, and they went up the hill together with the pan under her arm and the cold on their faces and the morning opening up in front of them like a door.

---

They sat on the step. It was the step he and Annabeth had set the day before, the two halves of the broken stone with the quartz vein running through them like a sentence that had been broken in the middle and put back together, and it was level and solid and the best step on the path, and they sat on it with the pan between them and the honey butter on the side and the toast from the Cabin Three kitchen and the morning doing its slow cold business around them.

Annabeth ate a cake standing up on the step, the way Silena had said she would, and then she sat down properly and ate the second one, and Percy ate two pieces of toast and the honey butter and watched her do the thing she did on a Sunday morning, which was to be quiet and warm and not say anything about being quiet and warm, and the morning went on being cold and bright and full of the sound of the lake going grey at the edges.

"The heel," Annabeth said, after a while.

"What about the heel."

"Thalia's heel. She's been favoring it since the survey and she's not been saying anything about it and this morning at the shooting line she favored it again and I watched her do it and she saw me watching and she looked away." Annabeth set the plate down on the step and looked at him with the face she wore when she was right and knew it and was going to say it anyway. "Somebody has to say it. Somebody who's not her."

"Somebody who's not her."

"Somebody who's not her and who she can't deflect. She deflects me. She deflects Clarisse. She deflects Chiron. She deflects Rhys, and that's a whole thing we're not going to talk about." Annabeth picked up the plate and stood up and brushed the crumbs off her coat. "You're going to say it."

"I'm going to say it."

"You're going to say it because you're her brother and because you saw it on the step yesterday and because you're the only person in this camp she can't win an argument with about her own body, and you're going to say it today, and you're going to say it at the shooting line, and you're going to say it in front of Rhys if you have to, and you're going to say it until she agrees to get it looked at."

Percy looked at her. She looked back. The morning was cold and bright and the lake was grey at the edges and the tenth lantern had burned out on its hook and left a ring of frost on the wood where the heat had been.

"She's not going to agree to get it looked at."

"She's going to agree to get it looked at because you're going to say it in front of Rhys and she's going to be so busy being annoyed about Rhys that she'll agree to anything." Annabeth picked up the pan and held it out to him. "Take this back down the hill. Tell Silena the cakes were good. Tell Clarisse the list is down to two things. And then go to the shooting line at eight and say the thing."

"It's not eight yet."

"It's ten to eight. You've got ten minutes." She put the pan in his hands and turned him around by the shoulders and gave him a push down the path, and he went, and he did not stop at the field, because Silena had told him not to, and because the field was a thing that was going to be there when he came back and the heel was a thing that was not going to fix itself.

---

The shooting line was already awake. The frost had made the ground hard. The flags on the butts had gone stiff in the night. The cold came off the lake in a flat sheet. Thalia was at the line with her bow half-drawn. Her heel was doing the thing it did. The favoring. The downhill shift. The small careful lie her body told every time she set her foot.

Rhys stood beside her. His bow was unstrung. His hands were in his pockets. His eyes were on the target. He was not looking at her. That was its own kind of looking. Percy stood at the end of the line. He had the pan under his arm. His breath was going up. He said it.

"Your heel."

Thalia's bow came down. She did not turn round. "Don't."

"Your heel. It's been three weeks. You favor it going downhill. You did it yesterday on the step. You did it on the survey. You did it at the fire ring. You're doing it right now."

"I'm doing nothing. I'm standing at a shooting line."

"You're standing at a shooting line with a bad heel. You're going to get it looked at." Percy set the pan down on the bench. He came to stand beside her. He looked at her the way he looked at things that were true. "Annabeth says you deflect her. You deflect Clarisse. You deflect Chiron. You deflect Rhys. I'm not deflecting. I'm your brother. I'm telling you. Get it looked at. Today."

The line went quiet. The targets stood in the cold. The flags cracked. Rhys did not look at her. His hand came out of his pocket. It found hers. It held it. He did not say anything. That was the whole of what there was to say.

Thalia stood there. Her bow was in her hand. Her heel was on the ground. Her brother was in front of her. Her boy was beside her. The whole camp was watching. She did the thing she did when she was caught. She was angry about it for exactly as long as it took to stop being angry.

"Fine. I'll get it looked at."

"Today."

"Today. Not tomorrow. Not next week. Not in the spring. Today." She turned to Rhys. She looked at him with the face she wore when she was annoyed and grateful. "You knew."

"I knew."

"You knew and you didn't say anything."

"I knew and I didn't say anything because I'm not your brother. I don't get to say things like that." He put his hand at her waist. "But I'm going to start."

"You're going to start and I'm going to let you. That's the whole of the announcement." She turned back to the line. She set her foot. She drew the bow. The arrow went out. It hit the target an inch low and left. That was where it always went when her heel was bad. She looked at it. "That's the heel. That's not me. That's the heel."

"That's the heel," Percy said.

"That's the heel and I'm going to get it looked at." She unstrung the bow. She handed it to Rhys. She looked at Percy. Her head was on her side. Her hands were on her hips. "The hall's Tuesday. I'll be there."

"You'll be there with a splint."

"I'll be there with a splint and a bad attitude and a heel that's getting better." She picked up the pan from the bench. She handed it to him. "Take this back down the hill. Tell Silena the cakes were good. Tell Annabeth the heel's getting looked at. She'll want to write it in the field book."

"I'll tell her."

"I'll tell her and I'll tell her you said it in front of sixteen people."

"I said it in front of the shooting line."

"You said it in front of the shooting line and there were more than six people on it." She turned. She walked off the line. Her heel did its thing. Her head was up. Her bow was over her shoulder. Percy watched her go. Rhys watched her go. The line went back to being a line. The morning went on being cold and bright. The heel was getting looked at.

---

They went to the Big House at ten. Chiron was the closest thing the camp had to a doctor. Thalia had agreed to it. Percy was not going to let her un-agree. Chiron's office had the wall with the five names. It had the running seep. It had the blank that was not blank. It had the panel with the thirty-nine tallies. The fortieth space was still open. The slate by the door had the small jobs written on it. Thalia sat in the chair across from the desk. Her boot was off. Her heel was in Chiron's hand. Her face was like a woman tolerating a thing she had already agreed to.

Chiron turned her foot this way and that in the cold light from the window. He pressed. She did not flinch. That was its own kind of flinching.

"How long?"

"Three weeks."

"Three weeks and you've been shooting on it."

"Three weeks and I've been shooting on it and running on it and walking on it and standing on it. I haven't said anything about it because I didn't want to say anything about it."

"And now you're saying something about it."

"And now I'm saying something about it because my brother said it in front of the shooting line. Because my boy was there. Because the whole camp was watching. Because I'm tired of favoring it going downhill." She looked at Chiron. She wore the face she wore when she was right and knew it. "I want it better by Tuesday. The hall's Tuesday."

Chiron pressed again. She flinched, once. He let go. He sat back. He looked at her the way he looked at things that were going to be all right.

"You'll rest it. You'll ice it. You'll wrap it. You'll wear the splint. You'll shoot left-footed for a week. That will make you worse before it makes you better. You'll do it anyway."

"I'll do it anyway."

"You'll do it anyway and you'll be there Tuesday. You'll be better by the winter." Chiron made a note on the slate. "I'll write it in the book. The heel's getting looked at. That's the entry."

"That's the entry. That's the whole of it. The heel's getting looked at. I'm resting it. I'm shooting left-footed. I'm going to be better by the winter."

Chiron looked at Percy. Percy was standing by the door. The pan was under his arm. His breath was going up. "You did well."

"I said the thing."

"You said the thing in front of the right people at the right time. That's the whole of the skill." Chiron made another note. "Take the pan back down the hill. Tell Silena the cakes were good. Tell Annabeth the heel's getting looked at. Tell Clarisse the list is down to two things. One of them's the awning. The other one's the pine. The pine wants a saw and a Saturday."

"I'll tell them."

"I'll tell them and I'll tell them you said it. That's the whole of the conversation." Percy went to the door. He stopped. He turned back. Thalia had her boot off. Her heel was on the desk. Her face was like a woman who had won a thing. "Tuesday," he said.

"Tuesday. Not Wednesday. Not next week. Tuesday."

He went out into the cold. The pan was under his arm. The morning was bright. The heel was getting looked at. The hall was on Tuesday. The winter was coming. He went down the path. His breath went up. The tenth lantern had burned out on its hook. The frost was silver. The lake was grey at the edges. The week was turning. The heel was getting looked at. It was going to be all right.

---

He did not go straight down the path. He went to the lake. The dock was empty. The water was flat and grey and cold. The frost had come in the night and gone again and left the boards silver at the edges. Annabeth was already there. She was sitting on the end of the dock with her feet hanging over the water and the field book closed on the plank beside her and her braid losing and her breath going up. She had the second cake. It was on a napkin beside her. She had brought it down for him.

"You took the long way," she said.

"I went to the Big House."

"I know. I wrote it in the book. The heel's getting looked at. That's the entry." She patted the plank beside her. "Sit down. Your cake's getting cold."

"It's never getting cold. It's a cake."

"It's a cake and it's getting cold and you're going to eat it sitting on a dock in the cold and that's the whole of it." She moved over. He sat down. He took the cake. He ate it. The dock was cold. The water was grey. The frost was silver. The sky was doing the thing it did on a Sunday, which was to be huge and empty and full of light.

"Your heel," she said, after a while. "Is it going to be all right?"

"It's going to be all right. Chiron says another week left-footed. Then I'll be right."

"Chiron says another week and you'll be right and you'll be better by the winter." She looked at him. Her head was on one side. "You said the thing."

"I said the thing."

"You said it in front of the right people at the right time. That's the whole of the skill." She put her hand flat on the plank between them. He put his hand over it. Her fingers were cold. He warmed them. The water lapped at the posts. The frost cracked. The sky went on being huge and empty and full of light.

"I'm glad you said it," she said.

"I'm glad I said it too."

"I'm glad you said it and I'm glad she's going to be all right and I'm glad the heel's getting looked at and I'm glad the list is down to two things and one of them's the awning and the other one's the pine and the pine wants a saw and a Saturday." She turned his hand over in hers. She looked at the palm. "Your hands are cold."

"My hands have been cold since July."

"It's November. It's a documented condition." She put his hand flat on the plank and put her hand over it and held it there. "We should go in. Clarisse'll be doing the count."

"Clarisse'll be doing the count and she'll be right and we'll be in it."

"Clarisse'll be doing the count and she'll be right and we'll be in it and she won't say anything and that'll be the whole of it." Annabeth stood up. She picked up the field book. She held out her other hand. He took it. She pulled him up. They stood on the dock with the water below them and the frost around them and the sky above them and the camp doing its Sunday behind them.

"Come on," she said. "The hall's Tuesday."

"The hall's Tuesday."

"The hall's Tuesday and you'll be there and I'll be there and Clarisse'll be doing the count and the heel's getting looked at and the winter's coming." She started up the path. He followed. They went up the dock together. Their hands were linked. The frost was silver. The lake was grey. The tenth lantern came on at the bottom of the hill. The night came down. The week was turning. The heel was getting looked at. The hall was on Tuesday. The winter was coming. It was going to be all right.

---

The cabin was warm when they got back. The stove was going. The soup was on. Clarisse had the list under her plate. She was at the head of the first table. Her boots were off under the bench. Silena was moving between the tables with the bread basket. Her face was like a hostess at a thing she had organised. Thalia was at the second table. Her hair was still wet from the hall. Her boot was off. Her heel was wrapped in the splint Chiron had given her. She was not favoring it. She was sitting on it, the way you sit on a thing you have decided to be done with.

Percy sat between his sisters. That was where he always sat. He ate two bowls of soup. He ate a heel of bread. He ate a piece of the honey cake Silena had made. She had not told anybody about it. The room was loud. The fire cracked. The tenth lantern came on at the bottom of the hill.

Evening came the way it came on a Sunday. It came slow and cold. It came full of the sound of the camp going to its cabins. The lamps went up the west path one at a time. Five in the cabin. Two in the cabin up the hill. Plus a girl who got a heel looked at. Plus a girl who was going to be better by the winter. A half-brother somewhere south who made bolts and mended tote hooks. A mother under the sea who had raised a boy who was not hers. She had never once said so. That was not a number. That was a fence. Clarisse La Rue was, more than anything else she had ever been in her life, the person who kept the gate.

She went inside. She shut the door on the cold. She left the tenth lantern burning on its hook. Anybody walking up the west path late, in the dark, in October, would come around the cedar brake. They would see a light at the bottom of the hill. They would think, without knowing why, that somebody was home.

The heel was getting looked at. The hall was on Tuesday. The winter was coming. The week was turning. The camp went to sleep under a sky with more stars in it than anybody had names for. It was going to be all right.
