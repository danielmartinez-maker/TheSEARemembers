## Chapter 93: Movie Night

The sun went down behind the climbing wall on a Friday in late August and Camp Half-Blood exhaled.

It had been a long week of drills and patrols and heat that stuck to the back of everyone's neck, and by dinner in the pavilion the whole camp had that tired, sunburned, end-of-summer looseness. Percy Jackson sat with the Poseidon table — where Clarisse and Silena had saved seats for him and Tyson, who was visiting for the weekend — and watched the Apollo table three tables over getting louder and louder.

Will Solace was standing on the bench. Kayla Knowles was standing next to him holding a bedsheet like a flag. Austin Lake was holding a dented projector he had very clearly borrowed from somewhere without asking.

"Listen up!" Will shouted, and because he was Will, and because he could project his voice to the back of a battlefield without trying, everyone actually listened. "Apollo cabin announcement!"

Dionysus, at the head table, didn't look up from his wine magazine. "No."

"Movie night!" Will said, louder. "Tonight! On the green south of the cabins! We got the projector working!"

There was a cheer, immediate and massive, that rattled the marble tables. Connor Stoll stood up and whistled with two fingers. Clarisse La Rue yelled, "If it's one of your musical documentaries I'm leaving!" The Demeter kids started chanting movie-night, movie-night, and Grover Underwood, sitting cross-legged by the satyr table with a plate of mashed potatoes, bleated happily and clapped his hooves against the bench.

Chiron trotted forward, his white stallion half catching the torchlight. "What film, Will? And where did you get a projector?"

"We traded the Hephaestus cabin three hours of archery duty and a promise not to sing at breakfast for a week," Kayla said proudly.

"We fixed it up," Austin added. "It works. Mostly. And we hung the sheet last period. You gotta come. We're doing myth movies."

Annabeth Grace, sitting at the Zeus table with her chin in her hand, turned and caught Percy's eye across the pavilion. She lifted her eyebrows. Percy grinned.

Myth movies at Camp Half-Blood were a very specific kind of suffering. Hollywood loved Greek myths and almost never got them right. The last time someone had tried this, two years ago, according to Will, the showing of a famous Titans movie had to be stopped after forty minutes because the Ares cabin started throwing folding chairs at the screen when the actor playing Ares showed up wearing a leather skirt and talking about love.

Which was exactly why it was going to be fun.

Dionysus sighed. "Fine. But if anyone summons anything, curses anything, or burns down my sheet—"

"It's our sheet," Kayla said.

"My hill," Dionysus said. "My rules. No monsters. No quests during the movie. Davies, no selling ambrosia popcorn balls for three drachmas."

"Five drachmas!" Connor shouted. "Inflation!"

Chiron smiled into his beard. "Bring blankets, everyone. It will be cool by the lake. And bring good humor. The gods in film are rarely as they are."

That was all it took. Dinner dissolved into planning.

---

By nine o'clock the south green had turned into something Percy had only ever seen at Camp Jupiter in pictures his friend Frank had sent him — a sprawling patchwork of blankets and sleeping bags and overturned logs and camp chairs, all facing a huge white sheet strung between two tall pines.

The Apollo kids had done it right. They'd washed the sheet until it glowed. They'd run a long orange extension cord from the Big House all the way across the grass, taped down with silver duct tape that the Hephaestus kids had blessed, probably literally, so no one would trip. The projector sat on a picnic table in the middle of the crowd under a little tent of mosquito netting, humming. Austin crouched behind it with a laptop and a nest of cables and the intense focus of a surgeon.

Fireflies drifted over the grass. The lake glittered black beyond the trees. Someone had strung globe lights from branch to branch and they swayed gently, gold and warm. The smell of popcorn hung over everything, thick and buttery, mixed with pine and lake water and citronella.

Percy came down the hill with a blanket over his shoulder and two cans of Coke in his hands. He was wearing a faded Camp Jupiter T-shirt he'd stolen from Frank and gym shorts and his beat-up sneakers. His hair was still damp from a quick shower.

Annabeth was already there saving them a spot.

She'd claimed a prime piece of real estate — center, slightly left, far enough from the projector that the fan wouldn't blow in their ears, close enough that they wouldn't have to squint. She'd spread out her old gray camp blanket and anchored the corners with her sneakers and a copy of a fat architecture book she carried everywhere. She was wearing a soft yellow sweater over shorts despite the warm night, her blonde hair pulled back in a loose ponytail, her camp beads around her neck.

"You brought drinks," she said as he sat down, pleased.

"I brought drinks," Percy confirmed, handing her one. "Ice cold. I had to fight Connor for them."

"You fought Connor Stoll for soda."

"I won," Percy said. "Mostly because he got distracted by popcorn."

Annabeth laughed and bumped her shoulder against his. Around them the green was filling fast. Tyson arrived with Ella the harpy tucked under one arm like he was carrying a football, which Ella seemed to enjoy, and set them both down three blankets over with a happy rumble. "Tyson brought extra feathers for cold!" he announced. Ella, small and red-feathered and wide-eyed, clutched a tiny pillow embroidered with books and nodded furiously.

"Ella is excited about moving pictures," Ella said in her quick, quoting voice. "'And now, for the first time, she saw the great world outside.' Ella is excited and also nervous about loud battle noises."

Tyson patted her head gently. "No loud battle. Ella can cover ears if loud."

Next to them, the Stolls were setting up what could only be described as a concession empire. Travis and Connor had dragged down two coolers, three boxes of candy they'd clearly smuggled from the camp store, and a popcorn machine that smoked faintly and smelled like a fire hazard in the best way. A hand-painted sign read POPCORN 5 DRACHMAS — NO REFUNDS — NO, CLARISSE, YOU CAN'T HAVE IT FOR FREE.

Clarisse arrived five minutes later in full armor except for the helmet, which she carried under her arm, her dark hair pulled back tight. Silena Beauregard arrived two minutes after that in a pink cardigan and jeans, her hair perfect despite the humidity, carrying a huge striped bowl.

They both made a beeline for the same spot on the Stolls' blanket.

"No," Clarisse said.

"Yes," Silena said sweetly, sitting down.

That spot, Percy realized, was next to the popcorn bowl.

"Get your own bowl," Clarisse said, dropping her helmet with a thunk and sitting down hard enough to bounce the blanket.

"I did bring my own bowl," Silena said, holding up her huge striped bowl. "This is it. But yours looks butterier."

"Because I paid for extra butter."

"Sharing is caring, Clarisse."

"I will shank you with a straw."

"You wouldn't. Not in front of the harpy."

Ella, overhearing her name, looked over. "Ella likes popcorn. 'Popcorn is the bacon of the snack world.'"

Grover plopped down on Percy's other side with a sigh of pure bliss, his goat legs folded under him, a flower crown slightly askew on his curly hair. He had a paper grocery bag full of tin cans, pinecones, apple cores, and one very suspicious enchilada. "Movie snacks," he explained proudly.

Thalia Grace vaulted over the log behind them, landed in a crouch, and sat down next to Grover in one smooth motion. Her black hair was spiky with static, her eyeliner sharp enough to kill, her leather jacket covered in pins. She had a soda in one hand and a sour gummy worm hanging out of her mouth.

"Evening, Kelp Head," she said. "Wise Girl."

"Hey, Pinecone Face," Percy said.

Annabeth didn't look up from arranging the blanket corners. "Hey, Thals. Nice landing. Seven out of ten. Deducted points for showing off."

"Please. That was at least an eight-five."

Grover offered Thalia the grocery bag. "Enchilada?"

"Is that from lunch?"

"Maybe."

"Absolutely," Thalia said, taking it.

Will Solace jogged past with a clipboard, counting heads. "Okay! Head count! Poseidon — three plus visiting Cyclops plus harpy plus satyr plus two Grace guests — check! Ares — present, but sulking — check! Athena — present and already correcting the IMDb trivia — check!"

"That page is wrong!" Malcolm Pace shouted from the Athena blanket. "Odysseus did not invent the Trojan Horse in that movie! Epeius did!"

"Tell them, Malcolm!" Annabeth shouted back.

Nico di Angelo melted out of the shadows at the edge of the lights with Will's little sister Kayla, both of them carrying blankets. Nico sat stiffly, like he wasn't sure he was allowed to relax, until Kayla elbowed him and he almost smiled. Hazel Levesque and Frank Zhang, on a supervised Roman exchange visit with Jason, claimed a log behind them, Frank with a truly enormous bag of popcorn, Hazel with a knitted blanket covered in stars. Piper McLean and Jason Grace spread a blanket to the right, Piper already braiding little glow-sticks into her hair.

Chiron settled himself on a large, comfortable-looking hill of cushions near the back where he could see everyone. Mr. D conjured himself a lounge chair and a blanket that said DO NOT DISTURB in purple letters.

"Places!" Austin shouted from the projector. "Places! First feature in five!"

A cheer went up. Someone dimmed the globe lights. The sheet glowed white in the dark, rippling faintly in the breeze.

Percy shook out his blanket — blue, frayed at the edges, smelling faintly of salt — and spread it over the camp blanket so they overlapped. Annabeth helped without being asked, smoothing the corner. They sat shoulder to shoulder underneath it, knees up, sodas tucked between them.

They settled shoulder to shoulder, Annabeth's hand resting on the blanket between them where Percy's hand could find it without making a show. Percy felt his whole nervous system settle. This was his favorite thing in the world, he thought. Not fighting monsters. Not quests. This. Sitting next to Annabeth with popcorn smell in the air and Grover crunching pinecones next to him and the lake making its soft shushing sound through the trees.

Annabeth leaned her head briefly against his shoulder. "You good?"

"Yeah," Percy said quietly. "You?"

"Yeah. This is nice."

"Very nice," Percy said, and meant it.

On their other side, Clarisse and Silena's war was escalating.

---

"That is my popcorn," Clarisse said, very clearly, holding the Stolls' metal bowl on her lap with both arms around it like a football.

"You offered me some," Silena said.

"I did not."

"You said, and I quote, 'take some before I change my mind,' and I am taking some."

"That was ten minutes ago! You've taken like half the bowl!"

Silena popped another handful into her mouth, batting her eyes. "I have a fast metabolism. Excellent popcorn training."

"Bullshit. You're just greedy."

"Clarisse!" Piper called from two blankets over, delighted. "Be nice!"

"I am nice! I'm letting her live!"

Travis Stoll leaned over, grinning. "Ladies, ladies, we can sell you a second bowl for the low, low price of—"

"Shut up, Travis," Clarisse and Silena said in unison, without looking at him.

Connor, filming on his phone, whispered, "This is better than the movie."

Annabeth snorted into her Coke. Percy put his arm along the back of the blanket, behind her but not quite around her, so she could lean back if she wanted. She did, just slightly, her ponytail brushing his arm.

"Projection in thirty seconds!" Austin yelled. "Everybody shut your heckling mouths until at least the opening credits!"

"Boo!" the whole camp yelled happily.

"Yeah, yeah," Austin said. "Shut up. Kayla, lights!"

Kayla killed the last string of globe lights. Darkness folded over the green, soft and complete except for the fireflies and the stars pricking through the pine branches and the glowing white sheet. A hush fell, the good kind, anticipatory and warm, a hundred kids holding their breath together.

Then the projector clattered, whirred, and threw a wobbly rectangle of light onto the sheet. The Apollo cabin logo — a hand-drawn sun with sunglasses — filled the screen to massive cheers.

"Apollo Pictures presents!" Will narrated in a booming voice. "A totally accurate and respectful retelling of Perseus!"

The title card slammed onto the sheet in dripping gold letters: CLASH OF THE IMMORTALS: PERSEUS RISING.

Grover gasped. Thalia sat bolt upright. "Oh no."

"Oh yes," Travis said.

Percy felt Annabeth go still beside him. "Oh gods," she whispered. "Not this one."

"What?" Percy whispered back. "Is it bad?"

"It's historically catastrophic."

"It's gonna be great," Thalia said, cracking her knuckles. "I brought heckles."

The movie started with a shirtless actor standing on a cliff in fake wind, staring meaningfully at a rubber sea monster.

"Is that supposed to be Argos?" Malcolm shouted. "That's not what a Greek coastline looks like! Those are Dover cliffs!"

"That's Perseus?" Clarisse shouted. "He looks like he moisturizes!"

"He's pretty," Silena said thoughtfully, eating more of Clarisse's popcorn. "I'd give him a seven."

"Silena!" Clarisse said. "You're supposed to hate him on principle!"

"I can hate him and acknowledge cheekbones. I'm multidimensional."

On screen, Perseus drew a glowing sword and shouted, "For honor!"

The entire Ares cabin erupted. "BOO! Honor's Athena's thing! Read a book!"

"Yeah!" Frank shouted, surprising everyone, and then blushed when Hazel laughed and squeezed his arm. "Sorry. Got into it."

"It's okay," Hazel whispered, leaning against him. "Shout."

Percy settled deeper under the blanket, laughing so hard his soda sloshed. Annabeth was already in full lecture mode, but the happy kind, where her eyes were bright and she wasn't actually mad, just delighted to be right.

"That's not how the Graeae work!" she called at the screen when three witches with one CGI eye started arguing. "They share an eye and a tooth! And Perseus steals them to find Medusa, not to find a map to Hades!"

"Tell 'em, Wise Girl!" Percy said, bumping her shoulder.

"And Medusa was a priestess of Athena! She wasn't just a snake lady in a cave with lava! There was no lava in Libya!"

"LAVA!" Connor screamed. "DRINK!" The Stolls had invented a drinking game — soda only, Chiron had been very clear — where everyone sipped every time the movie got something wrong. Everyone was already very hydrated.

Grover, munching an apple core, bleated loudly at the screen when the movie's version of Pegasus appeared — a white horse with obviously glued-on feathers that shed with every flap. "As a friend of horses and nature, I am offended! That's animal cruelty and bad puppetry!"

"Preach!" Juniper, his girlfriend, a dryad who'd come down from her tree for the night, shouted from the next blanket, throwing a pinecone at the screen and missing by ten feet. "Justice for puppet horses!"

Thalia stood up, pointed her enchilada at the screen like a sword, and shouted, "THAT'S NOT HOW LIGHTNING WORKS! I WOULD KNOW! I AM LIGHTNING-ADJACENT!"

"You tell them!" Percy shouted, laughing.

"I am the daughter of Zeus and even I think this Zeus is overacting!"

On screen, Movie Zeus, wearing a toga made of what looked like shower curtains and a beard that was visibly peeling off, thundered, "RELEASE THE KRAKEN!"

The entire camp lost it.

"WRONG MYTHOLOGY!" everyone screamed in unison, including Chiron, who then looked embarrassed and hid behind his blanket.

"THE KRAKEN IS NORSE!" Annabeth yelled, half standing, blanket slipping off her shoulders. Percy caught it and pulled it back up around her. "IT'S SCANDINAVIAN FOLKLORE! IT HAS NOTHING TO DO WITH PERSEUS! THE MONSTER WAS CETUS!"

"She's right!" Malcolm yelled. "Cetus! Sent by Poseidon!"

Percy pumped his fist. "Hey! Dad got one right! My dad sent the monster! Go Dad!"

Tyson clapped enormously. "GOOD BROTHER PERSEUS! FIGHT SEA COW!"

Ella flapped her wings in alarm as Movie Perseus rode the shedding Pegasus straight at the rubber Kraken. "Ella is scared! 'And then the sea monster opened its jaws!' Ella does not like jaws!"

"It's okay, Ella," Hazel called softly. "He wins. It's a movie. He always wins."

"But will the horse be okay?" Ella whispered. "Ella worries about the horse."

Grover, who had excellent harpy-calming instincts from years of practice, turned around and said gently, "The horse is fine, Ella. Look, see? He's flying. The feathers grow back."

Ella peeked through her feathers. "Okay. Ella will allow horse to live."

Clarisse, who had been heckling steadily — "Swing higher! My grandmother swings higher and she's dead!" — suddenly realized Silena had eaten all but the burnt kernels at the bottom of her bowl.

"Silena!"

"What?" Silena said innocently, licking salt off her fingers.

"That was mine! You ate the whole thing!"

"You weren't protecting it. You were yelling about swords. Snacks left unguarded are snacks surrendered. It's camp law."

"I'll show you camp law—"

"Girls!" Drew Tanaka from the Aphrodite cabin called. "There's more popcorn at the machine!"

"Stay out of this, Drew!" they both yelled.

Travis held up a fresh bowl like a referee. "Rematch bowl! Five drachmas!"

Clarisse dug in her pocket, slapped a drachma and three buttons and a bronze spear tip into his hand. "Keep the change. And give her the burnt ones."

"I heard that," Silena said.

"You were meant to."

They settled back down shoulder to shoulder, sharing the new bowl with elaborate hostility that fooled absolutely no one, since Silena tucked the blanket around Clarisse's armored shoulders without thinking and Clarisse let her.

Percy watched them and smiled into his Coke. That was the thing about camp. Even the fights were cozy if you looked close.

Annabeth leaned over to whisper to him, her breath warm against his ear, her hair brushing his cheek. "You see that? She saved her the corner spot."

"I know," Percy whispered back. "Don't tell them. They'll deny it."

"Our secret."

On screen, Perseus kissed Andromeda while the Kraken dissolved into paper-mache chunks behind them.

"She just watched her city get destroyed and he's kissing her?" Piper shouted, sitting up, her advocacy instincts fully triggered. "Read the room, Perseus! Consent and grief counseling!"

Jason put his arm around her. "Do you want me to pause it so you can lecture the screen?"

"No, I'm good," Piper said, but she kept muttering. "Unrealistic expectations..."

The movie ended with an eagle soaring into a sunset that was clearly a painting. The projector clattered to a stop. For a second there was silence.

Then the entire camp erupted in boos, cheers, thrown popcorn, and applause.

"ZERO STARS!" Connor yelled. "WOULD GET EATEN BY CETUS AGAIN!"

"Two stars for cheekbones!" Silena yelled.

"Minus five for lava!" Annabeth yelled.

Will stood up in front of the sheet, backlit, arms wide. "Thank you! Thank you! That was art! Intermission! Fifteen minutes! Bathrooms, snacks, stretch your heckling vocal cords! Next up — audience choice tearjerker!"

Austin flipped the house lights — the globe lights — back on. Everyone blinked, laughed, stood, stretched. The green turned into a milling, chatting, blanket-shaking crowd.

---

Intermission at a Camp Half-Blood movie night, Percy decided, was almost better than the movie.

He and Annabeth stayed on their blanket while the crowd swirled around them, content to watch. Grover lay back with his hands behind his head, mid-story to Thalia about the time he'd tried to eat the scenery in a school play. "...and the teacher said, 'Grover, that's a papier-mache cactus,' and I said, 'Not anymore.'"

Thalia snorted Coke out of her nose. "You're an idiot. You're my favorite idiot, but you're an idiot."

"Love you too, Pinecone Face," Grover said placidly.

"Don't call me that. Only Kelp Head can call me that."

"Rude. Accurate, but rude."

Juniper crawled over and curled up against Grover's side, her green dress smelling like fresh leaves. Grover immediately offered her the good pinecone. Thalia pretended to gag.

"Get a room, you two."

"We have a tree," Juniper said sweetly. "You're welcome anytime."

Annabeth had pulled her architecture book into her lap and was sketching the projector setup on the back of a popcorn receipt, labeling the angles. Percy watched her pencil move, quick and sure.

"What are you doing?" he asked.

"Figuring out how they got the image that flat on a windy sheet. They've got it tensioned really well. See the bungees? If I—"

She stopped when she realized Percy was just smiling at her.

"What?"

"Nothing," Percy said. "You're cute when you engineer."

"I am not cute. I am formidable."

"You're formidably cute."

"Percy Jackson, there are a hundred people here."

"And I'm whispering," Percy said, still smiling. He reached over and tucked a loose strand of hair behind her ear, his fingers lingering just a second. Chaste, easy, warm. "Better?"

Annabeth rolled her eyes, but she was blushing, and she leaned into his hand for half a second before pulling back. "You're lucky you're pretty."

"Silena gave that guy a seven. What do I get?"

"Six-point-five. You moisturize too."

Percy clutched his chest. "Wounded."

She laughed and shoved his shoulder, and he caught her hand and held it for a moment, thumb brushing her knuckles, before letting go because Malcolm was watching them with a knowing smirk and making kissy faces.

"Malcolm, I will redesign your bunk into a triangle," Annabeth said without missing a beat.

"You wouldn't dare. The Athena cabin has structural integrity standards."

Three blankets over, Ella was having the time of her life. Tyson had built her a nest out of blankets and spare hoodies, and she sat in the middle of it with her tiny pillow and a juice box, reciting movie quotes back to herself in a happy murmur. "'Release the Kraken. Release the Kraken. Release—' Ella likes saying Kraken. Kraken is a fun word. Kraken."

"It is a fun word," Tyson agreed seriously. "Kraken. Kraken. Kraken."

Frank and Hazel came back from the popcorn machine with a fresh bag and sat close on their log. Frank was telling Hazel about the first movie he'd ever seen at Camp Jupiter — a grainy black-and-white thing about Romulus projected on a barracks wall — and Hazel was listening with her chin on her knees, smiling.

"It's nice," Hazel said quietly, looking around at all the blankets, all the laughing kids, the fireflies. "Like everyone breathes together."

"Yeah," Frank said. "I like it here."

Nico, who had been sitting stiff and silent through the whole first movie, had slowly, imperceptibly, relaxed. Kayla had stolen his soda and he'd let her. Will had come over during intermission, ruffled his hair, handed him a box of Sour Patch Kids without a word, and Nico had taken them. Now he was sitting with his knees pulled up, watching the crowd with something almost like peace.

Piper had pulled out her phone and was taking pictures — not posed ones, just the sprawl: Connor mid-yell, Tyson laughing with popcorn in his teeth, Chiron trying to untangle himself from his cushions, Mr. D asleep with his magazine over his face.

"For the yearbook?" Jason asked.

"For me," Piper said. "I want to remember this."

Clarisse and Silena returned from the popcorn machine with two separate bowls, a truce.

"I got you the extra butter," Silena said, handing Clarisse a bowl. "Because I'm nice."

"You put extra salt on yours because you're going to steal mine anyway," Clarisse said, but she took the bowl.

"Correct. I'm glad we understand each other."

They sat back down hip to hip. Silena pulled the corner of Clarisse's blanket over her own legs. Clarisse pretended not to notice.

"Places!" Austin yelled. "Second feature! As voted by cabin representatives! The myths got it wrong last time, so this time we picked something with feelings!"

Will held up the DVD case. Even from the middle of the crowd Percy could see the cartoon cover — a girl with wild red hair, a bear, standing stones.

Grover gasped. "Oh no. Oh, I love this one."

Thalia groaned. "No. No crying movies. I forbid crying movies."

"You cried at the last one," Grover pointed out.

"I had allergies!"

"You cried because the horse lived."

"I'M ALLERGIC TO HAPPY HORSES."

Annabeth pulled the blue blanket back up over both their laps as the lights went down again. The air had cooled. The lake breeze lifted the edge of the sheet. Percy could see goosebumps on her arms where her sweater sleeves were pushed up, so he shifted, just slightly, so their shoulders pressed together under the blanket, sharing warmth.

She glanced at him, grateful, and settled against him. Not dramatic. Just there. Her head near his shoulder, her knee bumping his under the blanket. Percy felt that familiar, fizzy calm spread through his chest.

"Comfy?" he whispered.

"Mm-hm," she hummed. "Stay."

"Staying."

Grover leaned over and whispered loudly, "You two are disgustingly cute. Just so you know. As your best friend it's my duty to inform you."

"Thank you for your service," Percy whispered back.

"Anytime."

The projector whirred. The second movie began.

---

The second movie was, objectively, a better film. Even Annabeth couldn't heckle the architecture — "Actually those standing stones are pretty accurate, the lichen growth is right" — and even Clarisse went quiet after the first ten minutes, her popcorn forgotten in her lap.

It was the story of a stubborn princess and her stubborn mother, a spell gone wrong, a bear in the woods, and all the things you don't say until you might lose your chance to say them.

The camp watched differently this time. The heckles got softer, then stopped. Someone's baby sibling from the Hermes cabin fell asleep on a pile of jackets. The fireflies thickened. The globe lights swayed.

Percy felt Annabeth's breathing slow against his shoulder. He didn't move, didn't want to break it. He watched the girl on screen argue with her mother and thought about Sally in his original life: the apartment in Manhattan, blue pancakes, and how she had cried when he first left for camp, proud of him even while she worried. He thought about Poseidon, who tried in his own weird god way, who'd sent a seahorse with a note last week that just said PROUD. He thought about how lucky he was to be here, under a blanket, with his best girl leaning on him and his best goat snoring softly next to him.

Next to Grover, Thalia was doing her best tough-girl impression and failing spectacularly.

"I'm not crying," she whispered fiercely, wiping her eyes with the back of her hand. "It's the projector dust. Apollo cabin has dusty projection."

"Uh-huh," Grover said, handing her a napkin without looking away from the screen. He was crying freely, tears tracking through the dust on his face. "Here. Blow."

"I'm not—" Thalia blew her nose loudly. "Shut up, goat."

"Love you too," Grover said. He put his arm around her shoulders, briefly, goat-style, and she let him, leaning her spiky head against his shoulder for a second before sitting back up and pretending it hadn't happened.

Juniper, on his other side, was full-on sobbing into a leaf. "She just wanted her mom to listen!"

"I know," Grover said, patting her back. "I know."

Three blankets over, Ella was utterly absorbed. She'd stopped quoting. She sat forward in her nest, wings wrapped around her knees, eyes huge and shining in the projector light, juice box forgotten.

When the princess realized the bear was her mother, Ella gasped. "Oh! Oh no! 'And her heart broke in two!' Ella understands! Ella—"

Tyson wrapped one huge arm around her gently. "It's okay, little Ella. Mother bear is strong. Like you."

"But she didn't mean it!" Ella whispered, tears spilling over. "She didn't mean to be unkind! She just wanted— she just wanted to be heard! Ella sometimes is loud because Ella wants to be heard!"

Tyson nodded solemnly. "Tyson hears Ella. Always."

Ella started crying in earnest — small, hiccuping, feather-fluffing sobs. Not scared sobs. The other kind. The kind where a story cracks you open in a good way.

Hazel heard and immediately crawled over from her log with Frank's star blanket, tucking it around Ella's shoulders. "Here, sweetie," she whispered. "It's a sad-good part. It's okay to cry."

"Ella is crying," Ella announced through tears, as if confirming it for the record. "'Tears are words that need to be written.' Ella read that. Ella feels many words."

Frank handed her a clean napkin the size of a postage stamp in her talons. She took it gratefully and blew her beak.

"Ella thanks Frank Zhang, son of Mars," she said formally, then hiccuped. "Ella will remember this kindness."

On the Stoll blanket, the popcorn war had called a ceasefire. Clarisse was watching the screen with her arms crossed, her jaw tight, blinking fast. Silena was crying openly, beautifully even, tears sliding down her perfect cheeks.

"Don't," Clarisse muttered.

"Don't what?" Silena whispered.

"Don't cry. You'll make me—" Clarisse's voice cracked. "Dammit, Beauregard."

Silena, without a word, reached over and took Clarisse's hand — the one with the calluses and the scar across the knuckles — and held it. Clarisse didn't pull away. She squeezed back, hard, and kept watching the screen.

"You ever think—" Clarisse started, then stopped.

"What?"

"Nothing. Just. My mom. You know?"

Silena nodded, her head resting briefly against Clarisse's armored shoulder. "I know. Mine too."

Travis and Connor, who had been filming, quietly put the phone down.

Annabeth was crying too, silent tears Percy only noticed because one landed on his hand where it rested on the blanket. He looked over. Her gray eyes were bright and wet, fixed on the screen where the mother and daughter were stitching the tapestry back together.

"Hey," he whispered, worried. "You okay?"

She nodded, wiping her cheek with her sweater sleeve, embarrassed and not embarrassed. "Yeah. It's just—" She laughed wetly. "She's trying so hard to fix it. With the tapestry. Like if she just sews it right her mom will come back. I get that. I always thought if I was just smart enough, good enough—"

Percy didn't say anything stupid like don't cry or it's just a movie. He just shifted so she could lean more fully against him, his arm now properly around her shoulders under the blanket, warm and solid. She tucked her head into the curve of his neck, her ponytail soft against his cheek.

"I've got you," he murmured, so low only she could hear. "You're good."

She breathed out, shaky, and relaxed. "I know. Stay?"

"Always."

They watched the rest like that — Annabeth tucked against Percy's side, his arm around her, blanket over both of them, her tears drying on his T-shirt. Sweet and steady, the way they had learned to be. No one stared. At camp, this was normal. This was Percy and Annabeth. The kids who remembered holding the sky and kept choosing each other and now shared a blanket and a soda on a summer night like any other teenagers who'd survived the world.

The movie built to its ending — the sunrise, the stones, the mother returning, the daughter saying I'm sorry and meaning it. The music swelled.

Ella sobbed loudly. "MOTHER CAME BACK! ELLA KNEW SHE WOULD! 'And they lived with love ever after!'"

The whole green laughed through their own tears, fond and wet-eyed. Even Mr. D sat up and pretended he had something in his eye.

"Stupid pollen," Thalia muttered, wiping her face fiercely. "Stupid... mother-daughter... tapestry..."

Grover handed her another napkin. "Allergies, huh?"

"Shut up and give me the enchilada bag to cry into."

"That's the spirit."

The credits rolled over a painting of the highlands. The projector whirred. The sheet glowed white again.

Nobody moved for a moment. Nobody wanted to break it.

Then Will stood up, his own eyes suspiciously bright, and said into the quiet, "Well. That was emotionally devastating. Who voted for that?"

"The Demeter cabin!" Katie Gardner shouted, crying and laughing. "Sorry! We thought it was about bears!"

"It was about bears!" Travis shouted. "Emotionally devastating bears!"

Everyone laughed, shaky and real, and the spell broke gently into warmth.

The lights came back up but low, just the globe lights and the stars. Nobody rushed to leave. That was the best part of camp movie nights — the slow dissolve.

Austin left the projector running so the white sheet glowed like a nightlight. Kayla put on a playlist from her phone — soft acoustic stuff, Apollo-approved, no bagpipes after the Incident. Kids folded blankets slowly, shared the last of the popcorn, flopped down in piles.

Percy and Annabeth stayed put. They had no reason to move. Annabeth had recovered, her cheeks pink, her eyes bright, and now she was laughing at herself.

"I can't believe I cried at a cartoon bear movie," she said, hiding her face in her hands. "Daughter of Zeus. Strategist. Cried."

"It was a good bear movie," Percy said. "I almost cried."

"You did cry."

"I had allergies," Percy said solemnly, echoing Thalia, and Annabeth laughed so hard she snorted.

Grover had fallen asleep sitting up, his head tipped back, mouth open, flower crown over one eye. Juniper had fallen asleep on his shoulder. Thalia had covered them both with her leather jacket and was now sitting guard over them like a gargoyle, sipping the last of her soda.

"Don't wake them," she mouthed at Percy, jerking her thumb at the sleeping satyr and dryad. "They're cute. Disgusting, but cute."

Percy gave her a thumbs up.

Ella, cried out and happy, had curled up in her nest with Tyson's huge hand resting gently on her back like a blanket. She was murmuring sleepily, "'And so they watched the lights in the sky. The end. Ella liked the end. Ella likes happy ends.'"

Tyson rumbled, already half asleep himself. "Tyson likes happy ends too."

Hazel and Frank sat on their log, sharing the last handful of popcorn kernel by kernel, talking quietly about nothing. Piper had fallen asleep with her head in Jason's lap; Jason was braiding a glow-stick into her hair the way she'd taught him, tongue sticking out in concentration.

Nico had fallen asleep too, slumped sideways against Kayla's shoulder. Kayla looked terrified to move. Will came over, saw them, and mouthed sorry, then draped his own jacket over both of them and sat down at their feet to keep watch.

Clarisse and Silena were still holding hands. They'd both fallen asleep sitting up, heads tipped toward each other but not quite touching, the empty popcorn bowls in their laps. Travis took a picture. Connor whispered, "They're going to kill you." Travis whispered back, "Worth it."

Chiron trotted slowly through the blankets, checking on everyone, pulling an extra blanket over a shivering Hermes kid, extinguishing a citronella candle that had burned too low. He stopped by Percy and Annabeth's blanket and smiled down at them.

"Good night, you two," he said softly. "Good movie?"

"The first one was terrible," Annabeth whispered, smiling. "The second one was perfect."

"As is often the case," Chiron said. "Terrible myths, perfect night. Sleep well. No quests until morning."

"We promise," Percy said.

Chiron moved on, his hooves soft on the grass.

Annabeth yawned, huge and sudden, and leaned her head back on Percy's shoulder. Percy yawned in sympathy. The blanket was warm. The night was cool. The globe lights swayed. The sheet flapped softly in the breeze.

"We should go back," Annabeth murmured, not moving. "Curfew. Malcolm will mark us late."

"In a minute," Percy murmured, not moving either.

"Okay. One minute."

They sat in the buzzing, sleepy quiet. Percy could feel Annabeth's heartbeat where her shoulder pressed into his side. He could hear Grover snoring, Thalia shushing everyone who walked past too loud, Ella mumbling about Krakens in her sleep, the lake lapping, the pines sighing.

He thought about how many nights like this they'd missed — years of summers interrupted by wars and prophecies and funerals. How many times they'd sat like this in the middle of danger, holding their breath, waiting for the next attack. How rare and precious this was. Just kids. Just a blanket. Just a bad movie and a good movie and popcorn and friends.

Annabeth's hand found his under the blanket, fingers threading through his, squeezing once.

"Hey, Seaweed Brain?" she whispered, half asleep.

"Yeah, Wise Girl?"

"Thanks for the blanket."

"Anytime," Percy whispered, squeezing back. "Anytime."

The projector clicked off. The sheet went dark. The stars came out brighter. And on the south green at Camp Half-Blood, under a sky full of real constellations far more accurate than any movie, a hundred demigods fell asleep in piles like puppies, safe for one more night, dreaming of bears and Krakens and home.
