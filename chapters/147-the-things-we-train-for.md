## Chapter 147: The Things We Train For

The morning came in with the smell of cut grass and the sound of somebody's alarm going off in the Hermes cabin and being immediately silenced by a pillow.

Percy woke to the kind of silence that meant he'd beaten the sun up but only just. The cabin was still dark, the bunks around him empty or occupied by shapes under blankets, and the window showed him a sky that was more gray than blue, with the first pale line of dawn just starting to show at the trees.

He lay still for a moment, listening. The cabin had its own sounds at this hour — the fountain in the corner trickling, the shells in the walls settling, the distant lap of the lake against the shore. He knew these sounds the way he knew his own breathing. They were the sounds of a place that had decided to be his.

He got up. Dressed in the dark. Found his camp shirt on the hook by the door, his jeans on the chair, his sneakers by the bunk. Tied the laces quiet. Went outside.

The camp at this hour was a different animal. The paths were empty, the pavilion was dark, the cabins were all closed up and sleeping. The lake was a flat gray mirror with the first light just starting to touch it, and the air was cold enough that he could see his breath, and the whole place felt like it was holding its breath, waiting for the day to start.

Percy walked down to the dock.

He wasn't the only one.

Clarisse was already there, sitting on the end with her spear across her knees and her boots off and her feet in the water, and she didn't turn when he came down the ramp, which meant she'd heard him coming from the top and had decided not to comment on it.

"You're early," she said.

"So are you."

"I'm always early."

"That's not what Silena says."

"Silena's a late sleeper. Silena's opinions are compromised."

Percy sat down beside her. The dock was cold under him, and the water was colder, and the sky was getting lighter by degrees, and the lake was doing its morning thing — the slow silvering, the first ripples, the way it went from mirror to water when the wind found it.

"Clarisse," he said.

"What."

"What are we doing?"

She looked at him. "Sitting on a dock."

"Before that."

"Training."

"Before that."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "I couldn't sleep."

"Me neither."

"You're fourteen. You're supposed to sleep like the dead."

"You're fifteen. You're supposed to sleep like a normal person."

"I don't sleep like a normal person. I sleep like a soldier."

"That's not better."

"I know."

The sky got lighter. The lake got brighter. A bird called from somewhere in the trees, and another bird answered, and the whole camp was still asleep except for the two of them on the dock and the spear across Clarisse's knees and the water moving under them.

"Percy," Clarisse said.

"Yeah."

"Do you ever think about the other life? The one where we weren't sisters?"

"All the time."

"Me too." She turned the spear in her hands, slowly, the way she did when she was thinking. "In that life, I was Ares's daughter. I was mean. I was angry. I picked on you because that's what I did to people who were different."

"You were the meanest girl at camp."

"I was the meanest girl at camp," she agreed. "And you were the new kid with no cabin and no friends and a big mouth, and I made your life hell because that's what I did."

Percy looked at the lake. "You were terrifying."

"I was."

"You tried to drown me in the bathroom."

"That was a misunderstanding."

"You tried to drown me in the bathroom on purpose."

"That was a misunderstanding with intent."

He laughed, and the laugh carried across the water, and Clarisse's mouth did the thing where it almost smiled and then decided not to, and she said, "In this life, I'm Poseidon's daughter. I'm your sister. And I still want to drown you sometimes, but it's different."

"How is it different?"

"In the other life, I wanted to drown you because you were weak. In this life, I want to drown you because you're my little brother and you're an idiot and I love you."

Percy was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "That's the nicest thing you've ever said to me."

"Don't get used to it."

"I won't."

They sat. The sun came up. The lake turned from silver to blue, and the camp started to wake up around them — a door, a voice, the first clang of the pavilion kitchen.

"Percy," Clarisse said.

"Yeah."

"In the other life, were we ever friends?"

He thought about it. Really thought about it, the way you think about something that matters. "No," he said. "In the other life, we were never friends. We were enemies who became allies who became people who could stand to be in the same room."

"And in this life?"

"In this life, we're sisters."

"Yeah," she said. "We're sisters."

---

The training arena at seven in the morning was the kind of empty that made you feel like you were the only person alive.

The sand was still cool from the night, and the weapons racks were full, and the dummies were waiting with their usual expression of mild surprise, and the whole place smelled like leather and metal and the faint ozone smell of the boundary.

Percy and Clarisse had the arena to themselves. They'd been warming up for twenty minutes — stretches, footwork, the basic forms that Clarisse drilled into him with the patience of a stonemason and the subtlety of a brick — and they were both starting to sweat, and the sun was fully up now, and the camp was awake, and the world was happening around them while they did the oldest thing in the world.

"Again," Clarisse said.

Percy reset. Raised his sword — Riptide, the pen that was a sword, the thing that had been with him through two lives and a timeline change — and set his feet in the stance she'd taught him.

"Lower."

He lowered.

"Again."

He went through the form again. The strike, the pivot, the recovery. The way the weight moved through the blade. The way the body followed the sword instead of leading it.

"Better," Clarisse said. "Again."

He went through it again. And again. And again. And each time she corrected something — his grip, his foot, the angle of his wrist — and each time he did it better, and each time she said "again" with the same flat patience that meant she wasn't going to stop until he got it right.

They worked through the morning. The sun climbed. The sand got warm. Other campers started to arrive — some for their own training, some to watch, some just because the arena was where you went when you had energy and nowhere to put it.

Silena arrived with a jug of water and a plate of biscuits and the kind of look that meant she'd been up for hours and was going to pretend she hadn't been.

She sat on the bleachers and watched them work, and when they broke for water she said, "You've been at it for two hours."

"We have," Clarisse said.

"You're going to wear him out."

"He needs the work."

"He needs food."

"He needs both."

Silena handed down the jug and the plate, and Percy drank and ate and sat on the edge of the arena with the sand under his feet and the sun on his back and the two of them on either side of him, and it was the kind of morning that felt like it had been planned by somebody who knew exactly what they were doing.

"Clarisse," Silena said.

"What."

"You're teaching him the Atlas."

Clarisse was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Yeah."

"The Atlas is advanced."

"He's ready."

"The Atlas is advanced and he's fourteen."

"He's fourteen and his body knows things his mind doesn't. The Atlas works with what's already there."

Percy was quiet. She was right. His body did remember — not the specific memory of holding the sky, but the shape of it, the way the weight had pressed down and the way he'd pushed back, the way his whole self had become a column between the earth and the fall. He remembered that the way you remember a dream, or a song in a language you used to speak.

"Okay," he said. "Teach me the Atlas."

---

The Atlas, it turned out, was less a fighting form and more a philosophy.

It was about standing. About being the thing that holds. About taking the weight that's given to you and deciding, with your whole body, that you're not going to move.

Clarisse taught it in pieces. The feet first — where they went, how they held. Then the legs — how they locked, how they became pillars. Then the spine — how it straightened, how it became an axis. Then the arms — how they raised, how they held the weight. Then the breath — how it slowed, how it became the rhythm of the thing.

"You're not fighting it," she said. "You're accepting it. You're saying, yes, this is heavy, and yes, I'm going to hold it anyway, and yes, this is what I was made for."

Percy stood in the middle of the arena with his arms up and his feet set and his spine straight and his breath slow, and he held. The weight wasn't there — there was no sky, no ocean, no Titan's curse — but the shape of it was, and his body knew the shape, and he held it because holding was what he did.

"Good," Clarisse said. "Now hold it for a minute."

He held it.

"Two minutes."

He held it.

"Five."

He held it, and his arms shook, and his legs burned, and his breath came harder, and he held it anyway, because this was the thing — not the holding, but the deciding to hold, the choice you made with your whole self to be the thing that doesn't move.

"Ten minutes," Clarisse said.

He held it. The sun moved. The camp happened around him. His body screamed. His mind went quiet. And in the quiet, he found the place — the still center, the axis, the column. The place where the weight was heavy but he was heavier.

"Time," Clarisse said.

He lowered his arms. His whole body was shaking. His shirt was soaked. His breath was ragged. And he felt — light. Light and clean and like he'd been carved out and put back together.

"Good," Clarisse said. "That's the first time."

"The first time for what?"

"The first time you've done it without almost dying."

Percy laughed. It came out as a cough. He laughed again. "That's a low bar."

"It's the bar we've got."

Silena appeared with more water and the kind of look that meant she'd been watching the whole time and had opinions about it. "You held it for ten minutes," she said.

"I held it for ten minutes," Percy said.

"You held it for ten minutes and you're smiling."

"I'm smiling because I didn't die."

"That's the bar we've got," Clarisse said again, and Silena laughed, and the three of them sat in the arena with the sun high and the sand warm and the morning used up, and it was good, and it was enough, and it was theirs.

---

The sun had climbed past pleasant and into work.

Percy was in the middle of the arena going through the Atlas again — alone this time, because Clarisse had decided he needed to find it without her voice counting him through it, and because Silena had asked if she could watch from the bleachers with her notebook and a jug of water and the kind of quiet that meant she wasn't going anywhere.

So they sat together. Clarisse on the bottom bleacher, arms crossed, eyes on Percy like he was a problem she was solving. Silena two rows up, pencil moving across the page, hair coming loose from its tie in the heat, the sun on the side of her face.

"You're watching him like he's going to break," Silena said.

"He's not going to break."

"I didn't say he was going to break. I said you're watching him like he is."

Clarisse was quiet for a moment. Then: "The Atlas is the thing that's supposed to hold the sky. If he can't find the shape of it on his own —"

"He found it yesterday."

"Yesterday I was counting. Today he has to find it himself."

Silena's pencil stopped. She looked down at Clarisse — really looked, the way she did when she was seeing something she'd been not-seeing for a while. "You're scared."

"I'm not scared."

"You're scared he'll get hurt and it'll be your fault because you taught him."

Clarisse's jaw tightened. "He's not going to get hurt. It's a training form."

"It's a training form that's named after a Titan who got crushed by the actual sky."

"That's why it's called the Atlas. You don't name things after what they can survive. You name them after what they can't."

Silena considered this. "That's very Ares."

"That's very true."

They watched Percy. The sun was fully up now, and the sand was doing its thing — catching the light, holding the heat, making the whole arena glow. Percy stood in the middle of it with his feet set and his arms raised and his breath slow, and the shape of the weight was there even though the weight wasn't, and his body knew what to do with it.

"In the other life," Clarisse said, "I wouldn't have cared."

"Wouldn't have?"

"I would've watched him stand out there and thought, good. Let him. That's what weak people do — they hold things that crush them."

Silena's pencil was still now. She looked at Clarisse like she was reading something in a language she was still learning.

"And now?" she asked.

Clarisse watched Percy. The line of his spine. The set of his shoulders. The way his breath came slow and steady and his whole body was doing the thing she'd taught him, the thing that was more philosophy than form, the thing that said: this is heavy and I'm going to hold it anyway.

"Now I want to go out there and tell him to stop," she said. "Now I want to tell him he's done enough. Now I want to bring him water and make sure he eats something and tell him he did good even if he didn't, because he's my brother and he's fourteen and he's out there holding the shape of the sky because I taught him to, and I don't know if that's the bravest thing I've ever seen or the most terrifying."

Silena climbed down from the bleachers. She sat beside Clarisse. She didn't say anything for a moment. Then she put her hand on Clarisse's arm, just above the elbow, and left it there.

"It's both," she said. "That's what makes it love."

They sat together and watched Percy hold the sky. A pair of Apollo kids came into the arena and started setting up for archery practice on the far side. Somebody's whistle went off. A ball of dust kicked up in the wind and settled. And in the middle of it all, Percy held, and held, and held, because this was the thing — not the holding, but the deciding to hold, the choice you made with your whole self to be the thing that doesn't move.

"He's been at it for twenty minutes," Silena said.

"He's finding it."

"He's been finding it for twenty minutes."

"That's the point."

Silena was quiet. Then: "In the other life, were we like this?"

Clarisse thought about it. "In the other life, we were cordial. We were allies. We were people who could be in the same room without it being a whole thing."

"And now?"

"Now we're sisters who sit on bleachers and watch our little brother hold the sky and argue about whether he's going to break."

"That's better."

"That's so much better."

The sun moved. Percy held. And when he finally lowered his arms and stood there shaking and soaking wet with sweat and grinning like an idiot, Clarisse was already on her feet with the water jug and the kind of look that meant she'd been worried the whole time and was going to deny it in court.

---

The afternoon brought Thalia.

She came down from the Zeus cabin with her spear on her shoulder and her hair in its usual state of rebellion and a look on her face that meant she'd heard about the morning's training and had decided to have opinions about it.

"Heard you did the Atlas," she said.

"Word travels fast."

"Camp's small. Word travels at the speed of gossip, which is faster than light."

Percy was sitting on the bleachers with his shirt off and a towel around his neck and his breath mostly back. Clarisse was coiling the practice weapons onto the rack with the efficiency of someone who'd been doing it for years. Silena was in the shallow end with her feet in the water and her notebook out, drawing something that might have been the arena or might have been a building or might have been both.

"He did the Atlas," Clarisse said.

"And he lived."

"And he lived."

"Good," Thalia said. "I was going to be annoyed if he died before I got to teach him the lightning catch."

Percy looked at her. "The lightning catch."

"You're dating a Zeus kid. You're going to need to know what to do when she accidentally zaps you."

"She doesn't accidentally zap me."

"She accidentally zapped you last week."

"That was a static shock."

"That was a lightning bolt that she threw at you because you surprised her."

"That was a small lightning bolt."

"It left a mark."

"It left a tiny mark."

"It left a mark and you had to go to the infirmary."

"I went to the infirmary because I wanted juice."

"You went to the infirmary because your arm was numb."

Percy looked at Clarisse. Clarisse shrugged. "She's got a point," she said.

"She's got a point," Thalia agreed. "So. Lightning catch. After dinner. Bring your girlfriend."

---

The lightning catch was exactly as terrifying as it sounded.

They met at the arena after dinner — Percy, Annabeth, Thalia, Clarisse, and Silena, because Silena had heard about it and had decided that watching people get zapped was her new favorite thing, and because Clarisse had heard about it and had decided that if anyone was going to get hurt it was going to be in her arena where there was sand to land on.

"The concept is simple," Thalia said. She was standing in the middle of the arena with a ball of lightning in her hand — not a real one, not a dangerous one, just a small controlled spark that she was shaping and unshaping the way you'd shape a piece of clay. "Annabeth throws lightning. You catch it."

"That's not simple," Percy said.

"It's simple if you know what you're doing."

"And what am I doing?"

"You're not the lightning's enemy. You're its conductor. You're the thing it wants to go through, so you make yourself the thing it can go through."

"That sounds like the opposite of safety."

"It's not about safety. It's about physics." She tossed the spark up and caught it. "Lightning wants to go to ground. You're not the ground. You're the path to the ground. So you offer yourself as the path, and you let it through, and you don't grab it and you don't fight it and you don't try to hold it, because holding it is what kills you."

"Great," Percy said. "Love that."

"Annabeth," Thalia said. "Show him."

Annabeth stepped into the arena. She looked at Percy, and Percy looked at her, and the look that passed between them was the kind that meant they were both thinking about the same thing — the week before, the static shock, the mark on his arm, the way she'd cried after because she'd hurt him and he'd told her it was fine and she'd said it wasn't and he'd said it was and she'd said it wasn't and they'd both been right.

"Okay," Annabeth said. "Okay. Small one."

"Small one," Thalia agreed.

Annabeth raised her hand. The air around her fingers crackled — just a little, just the faintest blue-white shimmer, the kind of thing you might have been imagining if you hadn't seen it a hundred times before. And then she let it go, and the spark came across the arena at Percy, and Percy did what Thalia had said — he offered himself as the path, he didn't grab, he didn't fight, he let it through — and the spark went through him and into the sand, and the sand hissed, and Percy stood there with his hair standing up and his skin tingling and his heart doing something complicated in his chest.

"Again," Thalia said.

Annabeth did it again. And again. And again. And each time the spark got a little bigger, and each time Percy got a little better at letting it through, and each time Annabeth got a little more confident that she wasn't going to kill him, and by the tenth time the spark was the size of his fist and it went through him clean and he didn't even flinch.

"Good," Thalia said. "Now do it with the real thing."

"There's no real thing," Annabeth said.

"There's always a real thing. That's the point."

Annabeth looked at Percy. Percy looked at Annabeth. The kind of look that meant they were both remembering the same thing — the week before, the mark, the crying, the argument that had ended in a kiss and a promise and the kind of understanding that comes from knowing someone's worst fear and deciding to face it together.

"Okay," Annabeth said. "Okay. Real thing."

She raised her hand. The air around her crackled — more this time, more real, the blue-white shimmer becoming a glow becoming a light becoming something that had weight and heat and the smell of ozone. And then she let it go, and the lightning came across the arena like it had been waiting for her, and Percy stood in its path and offered himself and the lightning went through him and into the sand and the sand exploded and Percy stood there with his whole body singing with the force of it and his eyes wide and his heart pounding and the taste of copper and ozone in his mouth.

The arena was silent.

Then Thalia said, "Told you."

Clarisse said, "That was bigger than I expected."

Silena said, "Is he okay?"

Annabeth was already running across the arena, and Percy was already turning to her, and they met in the middle with their hands on each other and their eyes asking the same question — are you okay, are you okay, are you okay — and the answer was yes, yes, yes, and it was true, and it was also true that they were both shaking, and it was also true that they were both laughing, and it was also true that the sand was smoking where the lightning had hit and the whole arena smelled like a storm.

"I'm okay," Percy said.

"I'm sorry," Annabeth said.

"Don't be sorry."

"I hurt you."

"You didn't hurt me. You went through me. There's a difference."

"There's not a difference."

"There's a difference and I'm the one who went through it."

She laughed. It was wet and shaky and real. "You're an idiot."

"I'm an idiot who just caught lightning."

"You didn't catch it. You conducted it."

"Same thing."

"It's not the same thing."

"It's the same thing and I'm the one who did it."

Thalia, still standing in the middle of the arena, said, "You two are disgusting and I'm happy for you and I want it noted for the record that this was a good training exercise and nobody died."

"Noted," Annabeth said.

"Noted," Percy said.

Clarisse appeared with water and the kind of look that meant she'd been worried and was going to deny it. "Drink," she said.

Percy drank. Annabeth drank. The two of them sat on the edge of the arena with their shoulders touching and the water cool in their hands and the smell of ozone still in the air, and the camp was going through its evening rituals around them — voices, doors, the distant sound of somebody's music — and the sky was doing its thing, the slow darkening, the first stars, the whole world putting itself to bed.

"Percy," Annabeth said.

"Yeah."

"That was terrifying."

"Yeah."

"I thought I was going to kill you."

"You didn't kill me."

"I thought I was going to."

"But you didn't."

She leaned her head on his shoulder. He put his arm around her. The water cooled in their hands. The stars came out.

"Again tomorrow?" Thalia asked.

"Again tomorrow," Percy said.

"Again tomorrow," Annabeth agreed.

And they did.

---

Afterward, when the smell of ozone had faded and the sand had stopped smoking, Percy and Clarisse went down to the lake to wash off. The water was cold and the sun was low and the whole evening was doing its slow gold-to-purple thing, and they walked side by side with their shoes in their hands and their feet on the warm dust of the path.

Thalia and Annabeth stayed on the bleachers.

They sat together in the kind of quiet that didn't need filling. Annabeth had her notebook out, but she wasn't drawing — just holding it, the pencil loose in her fingers, the pages open to a sketch of the arena that she'd been working on all week. Thalia was beside her with her arm around her sister's shoulders and her eyes on the lake.

"He did good today," Annabeth said.

"He did good today," Thalia agreed.

"He's getting better at the lightning."

"He's getting better at everything."

Annabeth was quiet for a moment. Then: "In the other life, were we like this?"

Thalia thought about it. In the other life, she'd been a tree, then a hunter, then a girl with a spear and a mission and a sister she'd been separated from for years. In the other life, they'd found each other late and fought hard and loved each other in the space between battles.

"In the other life," Thalia said, "we were soldiers who happened to be sisters."

"And now?"

"Now we're sisters who happen to be training." She squeezed Annabeth's shoulder. "It's better. It's so much better."

Annabeth leaned into her. "It's better," she agreed.

They sat. The lake was doing its evening thing — the surface going from blue to copper to dark, the first stars starting to show through the haze, the whole camp settling into its night rhythms. Down on the shore, Percy splashed Clarisse and Clarisse told him he was dead and Percy splashed her again and neither of them was going to win that war.

"Percy told me something," Annabeth said. "Last week. He said that in the other life, the lake was the first big water he loved."

Thalia looked at her sister. "And?"

"And I think —" Annabeth stopped. Started again. "I think he loves it more now because it's not just a lake anymore. It's ours. It's the place where we're all together. Where we train and fight and sit on docks and fix walls and —"

"And get zapped with lightning," Thalia added.

"And get zapped with lightning," Annabeth agreed, and the two of them laughed, and the laugh carried down to the lake, and Percy looked up from his water fight and waved, and Annabeth waved back, and the whole evening was theirs.

"Annabeth," Thalia said.

"Yeah."

"I'm glad we're sisters."

Annabeth put her hand over Thalia's where it rested on her shoulder. "Me too," she said. "Even when you're annoying."

"I'm never annoying. I'm energetically helpful."

"You told Percy his hair was on fire during the lightning catch."

"It was on fire. I was being helpful."

"You told him with a straight face."

"It was a very straight face. The straightest."

Annabeth laughed again, and Thalia laughed, and down on the shore Percy said something to Clarisse that made her throw a handful of water at him, and the lake took the sound and kept it, and the stars came out, and the whole camp was settling into the kind of evening that felt like it was going to last forever.

---

The next morning, Percy woke to the sound of Clarisse's alarm going off at five in the morning and the sound of Clarisse immediately getting out of bed and the sound of the cabin door opening and closing and the sound of the fountain in the corner trickling in the dark.

He lay still for a moment, listening. The cabin was quiet. The bunks around him were empty or occupied by shapes under blankets. The window showed him a sky that was still dark, with the first gray just starting to show at the trees.

He got up. Dressed in the dark. Found his camp shirt on the hook by the door, his jeans on the chair, his sneakers by the bunk. Tied the laces quiet. Went outside.

The camp at this hour was the kind of empty that made you feel like you were the only person alive. The paths were dark, the pavilion was dark, the cabins were all closed up and sleeping. The lake was a black mirror with the first light just starting to touch it, and the air was cold enough that he could see his breath, and the whole place felt like it was holding its breath, waiting for the day to start.

Percy walked down to the dock.

Clarisse was already there. She was doing push-ups at the end of the dock, her spear on the planks beside her, her breath coming in white clouds in the cold air, and she didn't stop when he came down the ramp, which meant she'd heard him coming and had decided not to comment on it.

He sat down beside her spear and watched her work. She did twenty push-ups, then twenty more, then twenty more, and by the time she was done the sky was lighter and her breath was coming harder and she was sitting on the dock with her legs in the water and her hands on her knees and her face doing the thing where it was trying not to smile.

"Again?" she said.

"Again," he said.

They went to the arena. The sand was cold and the weapons racks were full and the dummies were waiting and the whole place smelled like leather and metal and the faint ozone smell of the boundary. They warmed up. They drilled. They worked through the forms that Clarisse had been teaching him, and each time she corrected something and each time he did it better and each time she said "again" with the same flat patience that meant she wasn't going to stop until he got it right.

The Atlas came after an hour. Percy stood in the middle of the arena with his arms up and his feet set and his spine straight and his breath slow, and he held. The weight wasn't there, but the shape of it was, and his body knew the shape, and he held it because holding was what he did.

"Ten minutes," Clarisse said.

He held it. The sun came up. The camp woke up around them. His body screamed. His mind went quiet. And in the quiet, he found the place — the still center, the axis, the column.

"Time," Clarisse said.

He lowered his arms. His whole body was shaking. His shirt was soaked. His breath was ragged. And he felt light.

"Good," Clarisse said. "That's two days."

"Two days of what?"

"Two days of doing it without almost dying. That's a streak."

"That's a low bar."

"It's the bar we've got."

They worked through the morning. The sun climbed. The sand got warm. Other campers arrived. Silena came with water and biscuits and the kind of look that meant she'd been up for hours and was going to pretend she hadn't been. Thalia came with her spear on her shoulder and her hair in its usual state of rebellion and a look on her face that meant she'd heard about the morning's training and had decided to have opinions about it.

"He did the Atlas again," Clarisse said.

"And he lived again," Thalia said.

"And he lived again."

"Good," Thalia said. "I was going to be annoyed if he died before I got to teach him the lightning catch again."

"The lightning catch again?" Percy said.

"You think one day was enough? You think you're going to get zapped once and never again?"

"I was hoping."

"Hope is not a strategy. Hope is what you have when you don't have a strategy."

"That's very Clarisse."

"I learned from the best."

They trained through the afternoon. The lightning catch came after dinner, and it was exactly as terrifying as it had been the day before, and exactly as necessary, and exactly as much a part of the routine as the Atlas and the footwork and the forms. Annabeth threw lightning. Percy conducted it. Thalia supervised. Clarisse provided water. Silena provided commentary.

And the next day they did it again. And the day after that. And the day after that. And each day the lightning got a little bigger and Percy got a little better and Annabeth got a little more confident and the whole thing got a little less terrifying and a little more like the thing they did, the way you do the thing you've decided to be good at.

---

A week into the training, something shifted.

It was a Thursday. The sun was high and the sand was warm and the arena was full of the sounds of other people's training — the clash of practice weapons, the thud of feet on sand, the occasional shout of encouragement or correction. Percy and Clarisse were working through the advanced forms, and Thalia was supervising the lightning catch, and Silena was in the shallow end with her notebook out, drawing something that might have been the arena or might have been a building or might have been both.

Percy was in the middle of a pivot when he felt it — a shift in the air, a change in the pressure, the kind of thing you feel in your bones before you feel it anywhere else. He stopped. Clarisse stopped. Thalia stopped. Silena looked up from her notebook.

"What?" Clarisse said.

Percy didn't answer. He was listening. The arena was still full of noise — the clash of weapons, the thud of feet, the shouts — but underneath it, there was something else. A sound. A frequency. A vibration that he felt in his teeth and his sternum and the soles of his feet.

"Do you hear that?" he said.

"Hear what?" Thalia said.

"That."

They listened. The arena noise continued. And underneath it, the vibration — low, steady, rhythmic, like a heartbeat, like a drum, like the pulse of something big and old and patient.

"The lake," Percy said.

"What about the lake?" Clarisse said.

"It's —" He stopped. Listened again. "It's calling."

The four of them looked at each other. Then they looked at the lake, which was visible from the arena as a strip of blue between the trees, and the lake looked back at them with its surface and its depth and its keeping.

"Calling how?" Thalia said.

"I don't know. Just — calling."

Clarisse was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "The west shore. The crack in the retaining wall."

"What about it?"

"It's been getting worse. I've been checking it every day. The crack's spreading."

"Spreading how fast?"

"Fast enough to be a problem. Fast enough that if it goes, the wall goes, and if the wall goes, the bank goes, and if the bank goes —"

"The lake comes in," Silena said.

"The lake comes in," Clarisse agreed.

They stood in the arena with the sun on their backs and the sand under their feet and the vibration in their bones, and the lake called, and the lake kept calling, and the lake was patient, because lakes are always patient, because lakes have all the time in the world.

"We need to tell Chiron," Thalia said.

"We need to tell Chiron," Clarisse agreed.

"We need to tell Chiron," Silena said.

"We need to tell Chiron," Percy said.

And they did.

---

Chiron listened.

He sat in his office in the Big House with his reading glasses on and his tail twitching slightly and his hands folded on his desk, and he listened to Clarisse explain about the crack and Silena show him the measurements and Thalia describe the vibration and Percy say "the lake is calling" with the kind of seriousness that meant he wasn't being poetic.

When they finished, Chiron was quiet for a long moment. Then he took off his glasses, cleaned them with his tie, put them back on, and said, "The lake is not calling. The lake is a lake. It doesn't call."

"But I heard it," Percy said.

"You felt it," Chiron said. "There's a difference. What you felt was the water moving through the crack. What you felt was the pressure changing. What you felt was the lake being a lake, and your body being a son of Poseidon, and the two of them speaking a language you remember better than you know."

"So it's not a problem?" Clarisse said.

"It's a problem," Chiron said. "The crack is real. The pressure is real. If the wall goes, the bank goes, and if the bank goes, we have a situation. But it's not magic. It's not a call. It's engineering."

"Engineering," Silena repeated.

"Engineering," Chiron confirmed. "The wall was built by somebody's grandfather and maintained by the camp's general fund of 'we'll get to it eventually.' Eventually is here. We need to repair the wall before the next hard rain."

"Can we do it?" Thalia asked.

"You can help," Chiron said. "The actual repair will be done by the Hephaestus cabin and the camp's maintenance crew and possibly a dryad or two. But you can help with the preparation. You can help with the planning. You can help with the —" He paused. "Actually, you can help with everything. We're short on hands and long on need."

---

The next day, the four of them plus Annabeth and a dozen other campers descended on the west shore with tools and materials and the kind of organized chaos that happened when Chiron said "we need to fix a wall" and the camp said "we're on it."

The Hephaestus cabin had sent Jake Mason and two of the older kids with a cart full of tools and a set of plans that looked like they'd been drawn by someone who'd done this before and enjoyed it. The dryads had sent a representative — a thick-limbed oak dryad named Myrna who looked like she'd been a tree yesterday and a woman for a hundred years before that and was not impressed by any of it. And the camp had sent everybody else, because when Chiron said "we need to fix a wall," everybody showed up.

They worked through the morning. The Hephaestus kids measured the crack and marked the repair points and cut the stone that would fill the gap. Myrna and the dryads handled the bank, their fingers digging into the earth and their voices speaking the language of roots and soil, and the bank listened, because the bank always listened to dryads.

Percy and Clarisse hauled stone. It was the kind of work that was hard and hot and satisfying — lifting the cut blocks, carrying them down the bank, fitting them into the gap with mortar and patience and the kind of precision that Clarisse brought to everything she did. Silena mixed mortar and kept the water coming and made sure everybody ate lunch and stayed hydrated and didn't lose fingers. Thalia supervised and organized and occasionally lifted something heavy just to prove she could.

Annabeth directed. She stood at the top of the bank with her notebook out and her pencil moving, and she called down instructions and corrections and the occasional "that's perfect, hold it right there," and the workmen listened, because when Annabeth directed, you listened.

The wall came together. Stone by stone, gap by gap, the crack filled and the repair made and the wall made whole again. It wasn't the original wall — you could see where the new stone met the old, the color slightly different, the cut slightly cleaner — but it was strong, and it was solid, and it would hold.

By midafternoon, they were done.

Myrna ran her hand along the repaired wall and nodded, once, the way you nod at something that meets your standards. Jake Mason measured the last joint and said, "That's clean." Chiron, who had been watching from the shore with his wheelchair on the grass and his tail swishing gently, said, "Well done, all of you."

They stood on the west shore with the sun on their backs and the wall behind them and the lake in front of them, and the lake was still, and the lake was patient, and the lake was not calling, because the lake had never been calling, because the lake was a lake, and what Percy had heard was the lake being a lake and his body being a son of Poseidon and the two of them speaking a language older than words.

"Percy," Clarisse said.

"Yeah."

"You heard it calling."

"I heard it calling."

"And Chiron said it wasn't calling."

"Chiron said it wasn't calling."

"And?"

"And I think —" He stopped. Looked at the lake. "I think it was calling. Not in the way Chiron meant. Not in the way that means magic or destiny or any of that. But in the way that — in the way that a thing can call when it needs you. When it needs something from you. When it's asking you to pay attention."

Clarisse was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "That's the most Percy thing you've ever said."

"I know."

"It's very you."

"I know."

"It's why we keep you around."

"I know."

She bumped her shoulder against his. "You're still an idiot."

"I'm an idiot who heard the lake."

"You're an idiot who heard the lake," she agreed, "and fixed the wall anyway."

---

That night, the camp built a fire.

Not the ordinary campfire that happened every evening in the fire pit near the pavilion — the one where they roasted marshmallows and told stories and the younger kids tried to stay up past their bedtime. This was a different kind of fire. The kind that got built when something had been finished. When something had been saved.

Chiron had announced it after dinner, standing at the head of the pavilion with his reading glasses on and his tail swishing gently and the kind of smile that meant he was about to say something everybody was going to like.

"The west shore retaining wall has been repaired," he said, and the pavilion went quiet. "Thanks to the efforts of Clarisse La Rue, Silena Beauregard, Thalia Grace, Annabeth Grace, and Percy Jackson, along with Jake Mason, Myrna the dryad, and the dozen other campers who gave up their afternoon to make sure the lake stayed where it belongs."

A cheer went up. The kind of cheer that started in the back and rolled forward and got bigger as it came. The Ares table banged their fists. The Apollo table threw bread. The Hermes table started a chant that didn't have words yet but was going to.

Chiron raised his hand. The cheer died down. "I'm told," he said, "that the lake was calling."

Percy, sitting between Clarisse and Annabeth at the Poseidon table, felt his ears go red.

"I'm told," Chiron continued, "that Percy Jackson heard it. That he felt the crack in the wall the way a son of Poseidon feels the water moving. That he brought it to our attention before it became a crisis."

"He's a hero," Clarisse said, loud enough for the whole pavilion to hear, and the Ares table banged their fists again, and Chiron's smile got bigger.

"He's a hero," Chiron agreed. "As are all of you. And heroes get to sit by the fire first."

The fire pit was moved to the beach.

Somebody's older sibling had dragged the logs down from the woodpile and arranged them in the shape of a proper fire — not just a pile, but a structure, a thing with intention. The Hephaestus kids had built a fire ring out of stones from the west shore, and the dryads had cleared the sand around it, and the whole beach had been transformed into something that looked like it had been planned by somebody who knew what they were doing.

The fire caught. The flames went up. The smoke drifted out over the lake, and the lake took it, the way it took everything — sounds and smoke and the smell of burning wood and the laughter of people who'd spent the day doing hard work and were going to spend the evening doing the opposite.

They sat around the fire in the arrangement that had become theirs. Clarisse on a log with her spear across her knees and her boots off and her feet in the sand. Silena beside her with her sketchbook and a stick she was using to draw in the ash. Thalia on the ground with her back against a log and her arms behind her head and her eyes on the fire. Annabeth next to Thalia with her notebook closed on her knees. Percy in the middle of all of them, because Percy was always in the middle of all of them.

"To the wall," Silena said, raising her stick like a glass.

"To the wall," they echoed.

"And to the lake," Thalia added, "for giving us something to do."

"And to the lake," they said.

"And to Percy," Clarisse said, "for hearing it call."

"And to Percy," they said, and Percy's ears went red again, and Annabeth bumped her shoulder against his, and the fire crackled, and the lake moved, and the whole camp was around them — the other tables, the other fires, the other stories being told in the dark.

Jake Mason came by with a plate of cookies that the Hephaestus cabin had made — actual cookies, with chocolate chips, because the Hephaestus cabin had an oven and wasn't afraid to use it. Myrna came by and sat on the edge of the fire ring and didn't say anything but nodded at each of them in turn, the way you nod at people who've done something right.

The Apollo kids started a song. It was the kind of song that didn't have a melody so much as a rhythm — clapping and stomping and words that were half-made-up and half-remembered and wholly the kind of thing that got sung at campfires when the fire was high and the night was warm and the whole camp was feeling like a family.

Percy listened. The fire was warm on his face. The lake was dark behind him. Annabeth's hand was in his. Clarisse was arguing with Thalia about something. Silena was drawing in the ash and not looking at what she was drawing and not caring.

"We did good today," Annabeth said, quietly, so only he could hear.

"We did good today," Percy agreed.

"We saved the lake."

"We helped save the lake."

"Same thing."

"Same thing," he said, and she smiled, and the fire crackled, and the song went on, and the night was theirs.

---

That evening, they sat on the dock.

All five of them — Percy, Annabeth, Thalia, Clarisse, Silena — in the arrangement they'd fallen into over weeks of practice: Clarisse on the edge with her feet in the water, Silena on the planks with her legs dangling, Thalia on her back with her hands behind her head, Annabeth sitting with her knees up and her notebook closed on her shins, Percy in the middle of all of them.

The lake was doing its evening thing. The sun was going down behind the trees and the light was going golden and the water was going from blue to copper to dark, and the camp was doing its evening rituals behind them — voices, doors, the distant sound of somebody's music — and the sky was doing its thing, the slow darkening, the first stars, the whole world putting itself to bed.

"We fixed a wall today," Clarisse said.

"We fixed a wall today," Silena repeated.

"We fixed a wall today," Thalia said.

"We fixed a wall today," Annabeth said.

"We fixed a wall today," Percy said.

"And the lake stopped calling," Clarisse said.

"The lake stopped calling," Silena said.

"The lake stopped calling," Thalia said.

"The lake stopped calling," Annabeth said.

"The lake stopped calling," Percy said.

"And tomorrow we train again," Clarisse said.

"And tomorrow we train again," Silena said.

"And tomorrow we train again," Thalia said.

"And tomorrow we train again," Annabeth said.

"And tomorrow we train again," Percy said.

The lake moved under the dock. The stars came out. The camp went quiet. And the five of them sat in the arrangement they'd earned, with the wall behind them and the lake in front of them and the sky above them and the whole summer stretching out ahead of them like a promise they intended to keep.

---

Later — much later, after the others had gone up the hill and the dock was empty and the lake was doing its night thing under the stars — Percy sat with his back against a piling and his feet in the water and thought about the day.

He thought about the wall. About the crack and the repair and the way the lake had called and the way he'd answered. He thought about Clarisse's patience and Silena's precision and Thalia's supervision and Annabeth's direction. He thought about the way they'd all worked together — not because they had to, but because they wanted to, because this was their camp and their lake and their wall and their summer.

He thought about the other life. The one where he'd been alone. The one where he'd had no sisters and no wall to fix and no lake that called. The one where he'd been a different person in a different world with different problems.

He didn't miss it.

He thought about this life. The one where he had sisters and a wall and a lake and a girlfriend who threw lightning at him on purpose. The one where he was fourteen and training and working and sitting on docks in the dark with the water moving under him and the stars above him and the whole world arranged around him like it had been waiting for him to show up.

He didn't miss the other one.

The lake moved. The stars turned. The camp slept. And Percy sat on the dock in the dark with his feet in the water and his family behind him and his whole life in front of him, and he thought: this. This is the thing. This is the thing I crossed two timelines for. This is the thing I'd cross a thousand more for.

He didn't say it out loud. He didn't need to. The lake already knew.

---

Much later, when the dock had been empty for an hour and the stars were fully out and the lake had gone from copper to black, Annabeth came down the ramp with a blanket under one arm and a thermos in the other hand and the kind of quiet that meant she'd been waiting for him to be done thinking.

Percy looked up. "Hey."

"Hey," she said.

She sat beside him. Spread the blanket over both their legs. Poured two cups of cocoa from the thermos — the good kind, with marshmallows, because Annabeth had a system and the system included cocoa when the day had been the kind of day that deserved cocoa.

They sat. The lake moved under the dock. The stars turned overhead. The camp was asleep behind them, all the cabins dark, all the voices stilled, the whole place resting the way it rested when the day had used itself up.

"Percy," Annabeth said.

"Yeah."

"Do you ever think about the other life? Not the way you did this morning with Clarisse. The way I think about it."

He looked at her. "How do you think about it?"

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "In the other life, I was older. I'd been to war. I'd lost things. I'd built things. I was a different person — not worse, not better, just different. And I found you in that life the way you find a landmark — something that was always there, something you navigate by."

Percy waited.

"And in this life," she said, "I'm fourteen. I haven't been to war. I haven't lost those things. I haven't built those things. And I found you the way you find something new — not because it wasn't there before, but because you're finally ready to see it."

She looked at him. The starlight was on her face, and her eyes were the color of the deep water under the dock, and her hand was warm around the cup of cocoa.

"I think," she said, "that the other life taught me what to look for. And this life is letting me find it."

Percy didn't say anything for a moment. Then he put his arm around her, and she leaned into him, and they sat on the dock in the dark with the blanket over their legs and the cocoa cooling in their hands and the lake moving under them and the whole world arranged around them like it had been waiting for exactly this.

"The other life was good," Annabeth said. "But this one is better."

"This one is better," Percy agreed.

"Why?"

"Because I'm fourteen and I have sisters and a girlfriend who brings me cocoa and a lake that calls and a wall we fixed and a whole summer ahead of me that's mine."

She smiled. "That's a good reason."

"It's the best reason."

They sat until the cocoa was gone and the stars had moved and the lake had done its whole night cycle — the dark, the deep dark, the darker still. And when they finally stood up and folded the blanket and walked up the ramp together, hand in hand, the camp was still asleep and the lake was still patient and the whole world was still theirs.

Percy looked back once. The dock was empty. The lake was dark. The stars were reflected in it, one by one, like a promise being kept.

He followed Annabeth up the hill, and the door of the Poseidon cabin closed behind them, and the camp slept, and the lake kept its own counsel, and the night was good, and the morning would come, and they would train again.

Because that was the thing. That was the thing they'd crossed timelines for. The training and the lake and the sisters and the wall and the cocoa and the quiet and the dark and the whole long summer stretching out ahead of them like a road they intended to walk together.

The lake already knew.

And tomorrow, it would call again.
