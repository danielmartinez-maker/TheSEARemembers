## Chapter 214: The Heel’s Measure

More than a week had passed since Chiron’s last formal examination of Thalia’s heel. The camp had moved on around the injury in the small, uneven way camp moved on around everything. Two storm-damp coils still sat on their spacers, waiting for their centers to dry. The repaired canvas seam was dry, though Beckendorf had not load-tested the separate loop Leandros had spliced. The fence board remained temporary, the lower cut stayed closed, and the footing still needed its proper inspection. The wet season had made the paths shine in the mornings and leave a thin grit along the edges by afternoon.

Thalia had learned which parts of the day the heel disliked.

It was better when she woke. It was better after she had been sitting. It was worse after she forgot to notice the slope under her feet. A sudden turn could pull at the back of the heel, and the longer downhill stretch past the pavilion still left a deep, dull ache that followed her up to the door. The splint had become part of the way she dressed to go outside. She could loosen it when she sat, the way Chiron had shown her, so long as the wrap stayed in place and the heel did not press against the strap.

She had not gone back to the archery line.

That had been her choice after the second arrow tugged. No one had banned her from touching a bow. No one had demanded that she prove she was ready either. The distinction was simple in words and less simple in her body. She wanted to be back on the line so badly that every ordinary step felt like a question someone had asked without permission.

This morning, she wanted to ask Chiron a question herself.

Annabeth was at the Cabin One table, sorting the papers she had used to measure the window latch. The last heavy rain had not come yet, so the repair still needed a longer storm check. She had put the ruler on top of the notes, not because the notes were going anywhere but because she liked the table clear enough to work on.

Thalia stopped by the chair and watched her sibling align the page corners.

“I want to see Chiron today.”

Annabeth looked up. “For the heel?”

“Unless he’s started keeping a second office for people who want to complain about the dining hall porridge.”

“Not that I know.”

“I haven’t had him look since the last check.”

“I know.”

Thalia waited. Annabeth did not add that she should have gone sooner or that she should have gone later. She left space for the answer.

“I think there’s been enough change to tell him,” Thalia said. “And enough that hasn’t changed.”

“Do you want me with you?”

“In the room. If that’s still all right.”

“Of course.”

“You don’t answer for me.”

“I won’t.”

“And if he asks what the pain feels like, you don’t translate my face.”

Annabeth lowered the paper. “I won’t do that either.”

Thalia watched her a moment longer. “You can say something if I ask you to.”

“I can.”

That was the whole agreement. It did not make Annabeth less her sister, and it did not make Thalia less capable of choosing the route down the hill. Annabeth folded the measurement sheet and slid it under the ruler.

“Do you want to go now?”

“After I put on the splint.”

“Do you want me to get it?”

“No.”

“Okay.”

The answer stayed simple. Annabeth picked up her own jacket. She did not reach toward the shelf where Thalia kept the wrap.

Thalia went to her bed and sat. The leather strap had dried cleanly overnight. She wrapped the heel, not too tight, smoothing the cloth beneath the edge so it would not rub. The movement was familiar enough now that she could do it without staring at her hands, but she still checked the fit before she pulled on the boot. The splint gave a soft click as she settled it around the heel.

Annabeth waited at the door.

“Do you want to use the main path?” she asked.

“I do.”

“It’s firmer than the lower cut.”

“I know.”

“I’m asking, not deciding.”

Thalia tugged the boot laces into a loose knot. “Main path. I don’t want the lower cut.”

“Got it.”

They left together.

The air smelled like wet leaves and woodsmoke. Water shone in the shallow grooves beside the path, and the morning sun was catching the edges of the puddles without warming the ground. Thalia chose the firm strip closer to the cabins. Annabeth walked beside her, matching pace without looking down each time Thalia stepped.

At the first fork, a pair of younger campers hurried toward the pavilion carrying a rolled practice mat between them. One of them glanced at Thalia’s splint, then at her face. He looked away before she had to decide whether to glare.

“Everybody’s getting better at pretending they don’t see it,” she said.

Annabeth kept her eyes ahead. “That sounds like an improvement.”

“It’s a low bar.”

“Most useful improvements start there.”

Thalia let out a breath that almost became a laugh. “You and your lists.”

“You make lists.”

“I make lists that work.”

“So do I.”

“Not always.”

“Neither do you.”

“That’s why mine are more interesting.”

The ground sloped gently toward the Big House. Thalia felt the familiar tug begin low at the heel. She shortened her stride before the pain sharpened. At the edge of the path, she paused and waited for it to settle.

Annabeth stopped too, but she did not ask what happened.

“I’m fine,” Thalia said. “That part was expected.”

“Do you want to turn around?”

“No.”

“Then we keep going when you’re ready.”

Thalia waited another few breaths. The tug eased into the dull tenderness she had been carrying for days. She started again, slower. She was aware of Annabeth beside her and of the fact that Annabeth had not tried to steer her toward the flat path that led around the kitchen. That mattered. The main route was the one Thalia had chosen. It was also the route that went downhill.

By the time they reached the Big House porch, the ache had not worsened. It had not disappeared either. Thalia took the last step up carefully and stopped under the eaves.

“Do you want to go in now?” Annabeth asked.

“Yes.”

Inside, the house was quiet. Chiron had left the office door open. The examination table had a folded towel at its foot, and his ledger sat on the desk beside a cup of tea. He was reading when they came in, but he set the book down as soon as he saw them.

“Thalia,” he said. “Annabeth.”

“I want a follow-up,” Thalia told him. “I’ve had more time on the plan, and I want to know what’s changed.”

“Good. How would you like to begin?”

“Talking first.”

“Then we’ll do that.”

Annabeth chose the chair by the door. Thalia stayed standing until Chiron asked whether she preferred the chair or the table. She chose the chair. It was low enough that her foot rested on the floor without being forced to point.

Chiron sat opposite her, his hands folded loosely. “Tell me about the last several days.”

Thalia had expected the question. She had still not prepared a perfect answer.

“It aches after the long downhill stretch. Not every time, but most of the time. The first few steps in the morning don’t pull like they did. It’s stiff, not sharp. Turning fast still hurts, so I don’t turn fast.”

“How has the swelling been?”

“Lower. I haven’t seen it come back.”

“Any pain when you’re sitting or lying down?”

“No.”

“Any new fall or twist?”

“No.”

“Have you used the splint?”

“When I’m moving around camp. I loosen it when I’m sitting, and I keep it dry.”

“Have you tried archery?”

“No.”

Chiron gave her time before asking the next question. “Was that because you were waiting for the tug to settle, or because you decided you did not want to try yet?”

“Both. It tugged on the second arrow last time. I didn’t want to make every morning into a test.”

“That makes sense.”

“It does?”

“It does to me.” He did not turn the answer into praise. “The instruction was to stop if the tug returned. You stopped. You have not been required to try again on a schedule.”

Thalia looked at the table. “I know.”

“Knowing it and hearing it are not always the same.”

“No.”

“Would you like to go on to an examination?”

“Yes.”

“Is Annabeth staying?”

Thalia looked toward the door. Annabeth was sitting with her hands around a closed book. “Yes. She can stay.”

“Would you like her to be quiet, or would you like her involved?”

“Quiet unless I ask.”

Annabeth nodded once. “I can do that.”

Chiron set the ledger aside. “All right. You set the pace. If you need to stop, tell me.”

“I will.”

“I know.”

He brought the towel closer and gave her room to move her boot without reaching for it himself. Thalia untied the laces, eased the leather free, and checked that the splint had not shifted. Her heel was still wrapped. She rested the foot on the towel and waited while Chiron looked at it.

The skin was clear around the edges. The swelling was less visible than it had been at the last examination, though the heel still looked fuller along one side than she remembered it looking before the survey. Chiron compared both heels without touching first.

“Does the wrap need to come off?”

“Yes.”

“Would you rather remove it yourself?”

“I would.”

He handed her the end of the wrap. The cloth unwound in a clean spiral, leaving a faint line where it had been snug. She folded it over her knee.

Chiron looked at the back of the heel. “Tell me if this is uncomfortable.”

He pressed lightly beside the bone, not on the sorest part. Thalia felt pressure, then a small, familiar tenderness.

“Here?”

“Not much.”

He moved a little lower. “Here?”

“More.”

“Same as before, or different?”

“Less than last time.”

“Does it feel tender on the other side?”

“No.”

He asked before he moved her foot, then waited for her answer before he did. She let him flex it a little. The movement was not painful, but she felt a pull when he angled the heel too far. He stopped as soon as she said so.

“Thank you,” he said.

“Why are you thanking me?”

“For telling me where the limit is.”

“That’s your job to find out.”

“It is. You still have to tell me what you feel.”

She sat with that for a moment. “It’s better.”

“Yes.”

“It still hurts downhill.”

“Yes.”

“I can walk. I just can’t pretend the slope isn’t there.”

“That’s a good description.”

“You’re not going to write that in the book.”

“Not in those exact words.”

“Good.”

He checked the wrap marks, then asked her to move her toes and flex the foot once more. Thalia did, slowly. The heel felt stiff at the end of the motion, not worse than it had before. She stopped at the point where the tug began.

Chiron lowered his hand. “The swelling is down. The tenderness has reduced. The heel is still sensitive at the back, and the downhill pull is real. That is improvement with a limit. It is not full recovery.”

“Can I go back to archery?”

“What would you like to know about returning?”

“Whether one short attempt would be reasonable.”

“That depends on what you mean by short, and what happens when you try.”

“One stance. One arrow. I don’t want a whole lesson.”

“Would you prefer to test it here, or at the line?”

“The line.”

“Why?”

“Because I know the ground there. And if I’m going to shoot, I want to be at the place I shoot.”

“That is sensible. We can go together and look at the surface first. If it is wet or uneven, we stop before the bow comes out. If the stance is comfortable, you may take one shot. If the heel tugs, you stop. If it does not, you still stop after one today.”

“You’re making the limit smaller than I asked.”

“I am. You asked for one short attempt. One arrow gives us information without turning the morning into a full lesson.”

“And if it feels fine?”

“Then it felt fine for one arrow.”

“That’s all?”

“That is all we will know.”

Thalia thought about arguing. She wanted to turn the word fine into a doorway. She wanted one shot to mean she could come back tomorrow and stand at the line until the sky went dark. It did not mean that. She knew that too.

“One arrow,” she said.

“One arrow, only if the ground is dry and level enough.”

“I can tell you if it isn’t.”

“I expect you to.”

Chiron wrote a brief note in the medical ledger, turning the page so she could see it: swelling reduced; tenderness improved; downhill discomfort persists; no new injury; no strenuous-training clearance; one voluntary flat-ground arrow permitted today if the footing is safe, stop if pain returns. He paused before the final line.

“Do you want that recorded?”

“Yes. But only in the medical book.”

“Only there.”

He set the pencil down and waited while she rewrapped the heel. The cloth did not feel like a verdict. She made it snug enough to stay and loose enough not to press. Then she fitted the splint around the heel and tied the boot.

Annabeth had not spoken. She met Thalia’s eyes only when Thalia looked over.

“Ready?” she asked.

“For the line. Not for an audience.”

“We can use the far end.”

“Can we?”

“Rhys is teaching the younger group on the near side. The far target is empty. I saw it when we came past.”

“You did see it.”

“I’m not checking the ground for you. I’m telling you what I noticed. You can decide if it helps.”

Thalia lifted her foot from the towel. “It helps.”

They left the office with Chiron carrying the ledger and Thalia carrying her own bow.

The walk to the range was longer than the walk back to the cabins. She chose the main path, not the shortcut through the soft grass. There were two places where the path dipped. She slowed before each one. She did not look at Annabeth to see whether she had noticed.

At the second dip, Chiron asked, “Would you like to stop?”

“Not yet.”

“All right.”

She took three more steps, then paused. The heel had tugged once, briefly. The ache did not sharpen, but she felt it.

“I want a minute,” she said.

“Take one,” Chiron replied.

Annabeth stayed beside her. The morning had warmed slightly, but damp leaves still clung to the edge of the path. Thalia watched one slide along a stone until the breeze moved it into the grass.

“Are you all right?” Annabeth asked.

“I’m not hurt again.”

“I didn’t say you were.”

“I know.”

“Do you want to keep going?”

“I do.”

“Then I’ll keep walking with you.”

Thalia stepped forward when the tug eased. The range appeared through the trees, bright with target faces and dull with the last of the rain. Rhys was speaking to a row of younger campers, showing them how to set their feet without twisting their knees. He looked up when they came in, saw Chiron, then saw Thalia’s splint. He did not call her over.

“Good,” she murmured.

“What?” Annabeth asked.

“He didn’t call me over.”

Annabeth’s mouth softened. “You told him you weren’t ready.”

“I didn’t tell him.”

“You told me.”

“That isn’t the same.”

“No,” Annabeth said. “It isn’t.”

Thalia shifted her bow from one hand to the other. The strap brushed her wrist. “I’ll tell him.”

“Whenever you want.”

The younger campers finished their stance practice and collected arrows. Rhys sent them to refill the water jug and turned toward Chiron.

“Recheck?” he asked.

“Yes,” Chiron said. “We’re using the far target if the footing passes.”

Rhys nodded. “The ground is level there, but the left edge is damp. I’d keep right of the marker.”

“Thank you,” Thalia said.

He looked at her, not at the splint. “You can have the far target for as long as you need.”

“I need one arrow.”

“All right.”

She did not like how quickly the words brought her back to the day of the injury, to the moment when the line had gone quiet and everyone had looked. But today the campers were busy, Rhys was already turning back to his lesson, and Chiron had said she could stop before the bow came out. No one had asked her to demonstrate strength. No one had promised that a clean shot would settle anything.

Chiron walked to the far target and inspected the ground. He crouched to test the soil at the right side of the line, then stood.

“The footing is firm here,” he said. “The edge is not. Stay on this side.”

“I can do that.”

“Do you want to try the stance first without the bow?”

“No. I know the stance.”

“Then tell me when you are ready.”

Thalia took her place behind the marker. The ground was level. She planted her left foot, let the right foot settle, then adjusted her weight until the heel did not pinch beneath the splint. It was not the stance she had used before the injury; Chiron had helped her alter it after the assessment. She could feel that her body was working around the sore place, but the foot held.

“Comfortable?” Chiron asked.

“Not uncomfortable.”

“That is not the same answer.”

“It’s the honest one.”

“Then that’s what we record.”

She lifted the bow. The leather grip was familiar. Her fingers found the string without thinking. She did not draw yet.

“Do you want to stop?” Annabeth asked from behind the line.

Thalia looked back. “No.”

“Okay.”

The bowstring came to her cheek. She drew only as far as she could without shifting her heel. The arrow settled against the rest. She aimed at the center of the target, then moved the sightline a little higher. The heel was quiet. It was not silent; she could feel the presence of it inside the boot, the same way she could feel the support of the splint. But there was no sharp pull.

She released.

The arrow struck low, just outside the painted ring.

It was not a perfect shot. It was not a disaster. It was one arrow.

Thalia held her stance for a heartbeat, then lowered the bow.

“How is the heel?” Chiron asked.

“Quiet.”

“Any tug?”

“No.”

“Do you want another?”

“No.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yes.”

She put the bow down on the bench and turned toward him. “I said one.”

“You did.”

“I want the record to say it felt quiet for one arrow. I don’t want it to say I’m back.”

“It will.”

He wrote the note on the small card he had brought from the office. Thalia watched him include the miss as well as the symptom. She almost asked whether the shot mattered, then decided it did not. It had gone where it went. The point was not that the heel had stayed quiet only if the arrow hit the center.

“How long before I try again?” she asked.

“I would wait until tomorrow. If there is no new ache after this, you may try one more brief session on level ground, with no more than three arrows and a rest between them. Stop if the tug returns. No full lesson yet. No running, patrols, hauling, or steep routes. Keep using the splint when you are moving around camp until our next check.”

“So I can be on the line again tomorrow.”

“You can try a brief session tomorrow if today stays quiet and tomorrow starts without increased pain.”

“And if it doesn’t?”

“Then you stop and tell me.”

“Not go back and test it anyway.”

“Correct.”

The answer sounded obvious. It was also a boundary she wanted someone else to say aloud so she could feel the edge of it.

“When do we check again?”

“In a week.”

“If the tenderness stalls before then?”

“Come see me earlier. If it worsens or stops improving, I’ll ask Will to take a look.”

Thalia nodded. “I’ll come back in a week.”

“Your choice, but I recommend it.”

“I’m choosing it.”

“Then I’ll be here.”

Chiron put the card away. “Would you like help carrying the bow?”

“No.”

“Do you want to take the same path back?”

“Yes.”

The walk down from the range was not a test. She kept the pace she had chosen and used the main path. At the first dip, she slowed. At the second, she did not feel the tug again, though the tenderness remained in the background.

When they passed the pavilion, one of the younger campers ran by with a quiver over his shoulder. He looked at Thalia, then at the bow in her hand. “Did you get to shoot?”

“One arrow.”

“Did you hit the middle?”

“No.”

“Oh.”

“I was aiming for the target.”

He considered that, then ran to catch up with his group. Annabeth smiled after him.

“He expected a more dramatic answer,” she said.

“Everybody does.”

“You didn’t owe him one.”

“I know.”

They reached the Big House porch. Thalia sat on the bench under the eaves and loosened the boot without removing it. The ache was faint. The heel had not worsened. She wanted to stand up and walk in a circle just to see if it would stay that way. She also wanted to go to the archery line and take another shot while the first one was still fresh in her mind.

Instead, she sat.

Annabeth sat beside her. “Do you want to tell me what Chiron said?”

“Better. Still tender. One arrow today, up to three tomorrow if there’s no increase. No full practice. No running, patrols, hauling, steep routes. Check again in a week.”

“Do you want my opinion?”

“Not yet.”

“Okay.”

The answer was immediate, without a sigh. Thalia watched the path below the porch. It had dried slightly where the sun reached it.

“I wanted you there because I wanted you in the room,” she said. “Not because I needed you to agree with him.”

“I understood.”

“Did you?”

“I did. You asked me to stay quiet. I stayed quiet.”

“That’s the easy part.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Annabeth turned her palms upward. “I know I sometimes decide that helping you means anticipating what you need. I’m trying to stop doing that.”

Thalia did not want to make her sister prove it with a speech. “You stayed beside me. You didn’t watch every step.”

“I wanted to.”

“I could tell.”

“Would you rather I hide that?”

“I’d rather you notice it and choose not to turn it into an instruction.”

Annabeth thought about that. “I can do that.”

“I know you can.”

A quiet settled between them. Thalia rested her hands on her knees. The bow lay across her lap, unstrung. It was a strange feeling, to carry the thing she had used for only a few seconds, and to know the brief attempt had counted because she had chosen it, even though it did not count as a return.

The Big House door opened. Chiron came out with the ledger tucked under one arm.

“I’m going to the dining hall,” he said. “Would you like me to take the bow back to the range?”

“No. I’ll bring it when I’m done here.”

“All right.”

He did not suggest that she should go inside, or that she should rest, or that she had done enough. He let her decide what enough meant.

Annabeth looked toward the open door. “I was going to see Silena.”

“Go ahead.”

“Do you want me to come back?”

“Not as a check-up.”

“I can come back because I’m coming back to the cabin.”

“That’s different.”

“It is.”

“Then yes.”

Annabeth stood. “I’ll see you later.”

“See you.”

She went down the porch steps without looking back. Thalia stayed where she was, listening to the small sounds of the camp in the wet morning: a bucket set down, a door closing, the soft thud of arrows into a target. The sound reached her without asking her to join.

She was still sore. She was not secretly failing because the heel had not become new again. She was also not finished. The next week had a plan in it, and the plan had room for her answer.

When she stood, she did it slowly. The heel held. She lifted the bow and walked toward the dining hall on the path she had chosen. The next shot could wait until tomorrow.
---

The dining hall was louder at lunch than it had been on the walk back. The rain had kept people inside for most of the morning, and the damp jackets were all hanging near the door. Thalia found an empty place at one of the long tables. She set her bow beside her, not on the floor, and settled the heel on the rung of the bench.

Silena brought over a bowl of soup and two slices of bread. “You’re sitting like the chair insulted you.”

“The chair started it.”

“Did it?”

“It’s leaning.”

“It’s a bench.”

“Then it’s doing a very good impression.”

Silena glanced at the splint and then back to Thalia’s face. “Do you want the soup on the table or here?”

“The table.”

She placed it where Thalia could reach. She did not move the bow or the bread. “Did Chiron change the plan?”

“Not much.”

“That sounds like a careful answer.”

“It’s a careful plan. One arrow today. If it stays quiet, a short try tomorrow. If it tugs, stop.”

“And how did the arrow go?”

“Low.”

“Did the heel hurt?”

“No.”

“Then the arrow can be low by itself.”

Thalia took a spoonful. The soup was hot and thick with potatoes. She had expected questions from everyone who had seen her leave the Big House with Chiron. Silena had asked two, and both had been about the thing Thalia had chosen to share.

“I don’t want the whole table to know what he wrote,” she said.

“They won’t hear it from me.”

“I know.”

Silena leaned against the far side of the table. “I’m making another batch of rosemary bread after the afternoon chores. You don’t have to help.”

“Was I supposed to?”

“No. I was telling you because I’m making it.”

“Good. I’m not moving sacks of flour with this thing.”

“You weren’t invited to move sacks.”

“Then this is going well.”

Silena smiled, then carried the empty soup pot back to the kitchen.

Thalia finished half the bowl before Ianthe came in from the porch. She had a folded sheet of paper in one hand and a pencil tucked behind her ear. She walked straight toward the table, saw Thalia’s bow, and stopped a few feet away.

“Can I ask how it went?” she said.

“You can ask.”

“How did it go?”

“One arrow. It didn’t tug.”

Ianthe’s expression brightened, but she did not say that meant Thalia was back. “That sounds useful.”

“Useful isn’t the same as finished.”

“I know.”

Thalia watched the sheet in Ianthe’s hand. “What’s that?”

“A list for the paths. The main route is firm now, but the lower cut is still soft after rain. I was going to mark the places where people shouldn’t carry heavy things until Beckendorf signs off.”

“Are you asking me to help?”

“I was going to ask if you want to tell me which path you use.”

Thalia put down her spoon. “You’re going to ask me about the path I use so you can mark it?”

“I can leave your name off.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

Ianthe looked down at the sheet. “I thought knowing which part pulls at your heel would help make the route safer.”

“For me?”

“For anyone.”

“My heel isn’t a route report.”

“I didn’t say it was.”

“You were going to put the route on a list.”

“The path conditions are already on a list.”

“And now you want my body on the same page.”

Ianthe’s jaw tightened. “I’m trying to keep people from having to choose between the safe path and the fast one.”

“Then ask Beckendorf about the ground. Don’t use me as a measurement.”

“I was asking you.”

“You were asking me to give you information so you could decide what everybody else should do with it.”

Ianthe opened her mouth, then closed it. She looked at the paper as if the words might rearrange themselves into a better answer.

“I thought if I knew where it hurt, I could keep the route from making it worse.”

“I didn’t ask you to keep it from making anything worse.”

“That’s what a sister would do.”

“Would it?”

The question landed more sharply than Thalia intended. Ianthe set the paper on the table but kept her hand on its corner.

“I’m not saying I get to be in charge of you,” she said. “I’m saying I thought I could help.”

“Then ask what help I want. Don’t make a plan first and offer me the finished version.”

Ianthe’s fingers curled around the edge of the page. “You make it sound easy.”

“It isn’t. I still want to tell you what to do when I’m worried.”

“So do I.”

“You do?”

“Of course. I can see you favor the left side when you come down the hill.”

Thalia’s shoulder went stiff.

“I’m not tracking you,” Ianthe added quickly. “I just—”

“You can see me. I don’t want you keeping notes.”

“I didn’t write it down.”

“That isn’t what I meant.”

“I know.” Ianthe pulled her hand away from the paper. “I noticed it because you were right in front of me. I didn’t write it down. I’m not keeping a record.”

“Then leave it there.”

Ianthe took a breath. “You’re right.”

Thalia hadn’t expected the words to come so quickly. She still didn’t trust them to mean the end of the argument.

“I don’t want to be a line in one of your plans,” she said. “I know you want to be useful. I know our father liked hearing about your weather notes. But I’m not a problem you need to organize so someone will keep asking you questions.”

Ianthe went quiet.

Thalia immediately wished she had not brought Zeus into it. The words had found the part that hurt and pressed hard. That did not make it fair to use it.

“I shouldn’t have said that like I know why you do it,” she said.

“You don’t know.”

“No.”

“And I didn’t write down your heel.”

“I heard you.”

“I wrote down where the path stays soft because the ground is soft. That’s all.”

“Then the path list can stay a path list.”

“It was going to.”

“I know. I still don’t want to be part of it.”

Ianthe nodded, but the paper stayed under her hand. “All right.”

Thalia picked up her spoon again. The soup had cooled. She knew she should have asked why Ianthe had wanted to make a route list before deciding that she was trying to manage her. She also knew Ianthe had been about to treat Thalia’s pain as useful information without asking what Thalia wanted done with it. Both things could be true.

Ianthe took the sheet and folded it once. “Do you want me to get out of your way?”

“You’re not in my way.”

“Do you want me to sit?”

“I don’t know.”

“That’s an answer.”

“It’s an answer for right now.”

Ianthe nodded again. She moved to the end of the table, far enough away to leave Thalia room, and sat with her own cup. The two of them ate without talking.

After a while, Thalia said, “I don’t want you to decide that I don’t want you near me.”

“I’m not deciding that.”

“I’m telling you I want to know you. I don’t want you managing me.”

“Those sound like different things.”

“They are.”

“I’m still learning that.”

“Me too.”

Ianthe’s attention shifted to the folded list. “Would you rather I show you the route sheet once Beckendorf checks it? You can say no now.”

“No.”

Ianthe’s mouth set.

“I don’t want to review the route sheet. That doesn’t mean you can’t make it.”

“I understand.”

“You can still show it to Beckendorf.”

“That was the plan.”

“Then do that.”

The answer was not warm, but it was not a rejection of her whole place at camp. Thalia tried to make the distinction visible in her voice. She was not sure she succeeded.

Ianthe pushed her cup away. “I’ll take it to him.”

“Good.”

Ianthe looked toward the porch, where the map would be easier to keep dry. “When Father asked me about the wind, I liked it.”

Thalia kept her spoon still. “I know.”

“I’m not saying he always asked, or that it was enough. I’m saying I liked it when he did. He wanted to know what I had noticed. It mattered to me.”

“That makes sense.”

“I don’t want you to think I only make plans because I want him to praise them.”

“I don’t know why you make plans.”

“You just told me.”

“I said I know you want to be useful. I don’t know what all of that means to you.”

Ianthe leaned back. The paper rested between her hands. “When he asked, it felt as if I had a place beside him. If I knew what the pressure was doing, I could tell him what the clouds would do. He listened. Sometimes he remembered the next time.”

Thalia let that sit. “That sounds good.”

“It was good.”

“And then he compared you with us.”

“He said I was more disciplined. He said I was thinking ahead. I liked hearing it until he made the compliment into a way to correct you.”

“You don’t have to stop liking that he paid attention.”

Ianthe glanced at her, uncertain whether she was being offered comfort or a warning.

“I’m not telling you what to feel,” Thalia said. “I’m saying I don’t want to be the reason you have to pretend the good part didn’t happen.”

“I thought you would.”

“Why?”

“Because when he compared us, you looked angry.”

“I was angry at him.”

“You were angry with me too.”

“I was angry that you let him use us like measurements. I wasn’t angry that you were glad he asked about your notes.”

Ianthe’s fingers smoothed the crease in the paper. “I didn’t know there was a difference.”

“There is.”

“Did you want him to ask you about something?”

“I wanted him to ask about me without using one of us to tell the other how to be better.”

“That isn’t a simple thing.”

“No.”

The paper made a dry sound when Ianthe folded it again. “I keep thinking if I can make the right plan, people will see that I belong here.”

“Do you think a map decides that?”

“I think people pay attention when the map is useful.”

“They also pay attention when you leave a job undone or get one wrong.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I’ve been wrong.”

“That’s not what I mean.”

Ianthe looked down. “I don’t know what people would ask me about if I stopped bringing them plans.”

“Maybe the weather.”

“Maybe.”

“Maybe something else.”

“What would you ask?”

Thalia thought about it. “I don’t know you well enough to ask the right thing every time.”

“That’s honest.”

“I could ask what you want to eat. Or whether you want to sit with us. Or if you want me to leave you alone.”

“Those are small things.”

“They count.”

Ianthe gave a humorless laugh. “You say that as if you believe it.”

“I do. I just don’t think small things fix what Father did.”

“They don’t.”

“And you’re still allowed to like him asking about your notes.”

Ianthe folded the page into a smaller square. “I still like it.”

“You don’t have to give that up to sit here.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I’m trying to.”

The conversation did not make the route sheet a family object. It did not let Ianthe put Thalia’s heel on the map. It did give them both a reason to stop pretending the paper was the only thing between them.

Thalia took another spoonful of soup. “You can take the path list to Beckendorf.”

“I will.”

“You can come back to the table after.”

“If you still want me here.”

“I’ll tell you if I don’t.”

Ianthe nodded. The answer did not satisfy every question she had. Thalia could see that in the way she folded the paper too precisely. She also saw that Ianthe was not asking her to take the question back.

She left with the folded paper. Thalia watched the door swing closed and did not ask whether Ianthe would be all right. That was not her job. It was also not the same as not caring.

The afternoon stayed quiet. Thalia rested on the cabin porch and read the same page of a book twice. Once, she heard a shout from the lower field and started to get up. The heel pulled when she turned. She stopped, waited for the ache to settle, then decided the shout sounded like a younger camper who had lost a ball, not anyone in danger.

She sat again.

That choice felt smaller than shooting one arrow. It also felt like part of the same plan.

Near supper, Percy came up the path carrying a tin cup. He paused before the step.

“Are you sitting because you want to, or because the heel asked for it?”

“Both.”

“Fair.”

“Did Annabeth send you to check?”

“No.”

“Did she tell you what Chiron said?”

“No.”

“Did you ask?”

“I thought about it.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

“Good.”

He leaned against the porch post, leaving the bench clear. “Do you want to tell me?”

“One arrow. No tug. The path still pulls downhill. I’m going to try again tomorrow if the morning feels the same.”

“Okay.”

“Not ‘that sounds safe’?”

“I don’t know if it’s safe. Chiron does.”

“Good answer.”

“Do you need anything?”

“No.”

“Then I’ll stop standing here like a question.”

“Thank you.”

He started down the steps, then looked back. “I’m glad you saw him.”

Thalia caught the edge of her reply before it turned into a challenge. “I wanted to.”

“I know.”

He didn’t say she had done the right thing, or that she should keep doing it, or that he had worried. He left her with the cup of tea and the quiet evening. The space he gave her was not distance. It was just space.

---

The next morning was clear but cold. Thalia woke without the heel aching. The first few steps to the washbasin felt stiff, then settled. She kept the splint on while she crossed the cabin floor and sat to tie her boot. She remembered Chiron’s limit: one brief session, no more than three arrows, rest between them. The session was optional. She had asked to try it.

Annabeth was still asleep in the other bed. Thalia did not wake her. She took her bow and went out alone.

The practice field was quiet. Rhys had set the targets for an afternoon lesson, and the far end was empty. Thalia checked the ground, stayed on the dry side of the marker, and put her bow on the bench while she tested the stance without drawing.

Her heel did not hurt.

She picked up the bow.

The first arrow flew a little right of the center ring. The heel stayed quiet. Thalia lowered the bow, rested, and waited. The cold bit the tips of her fingers. She rubbed them together and watched the target instead of staring at her foot.

The second arrow landed closer to the center. As she lowered the bow, the heel tugged.

It was mild. It was familiar. It was enough.

She set the bow on the bench and stood still. She could have told herself it was nothing. She could have taken one more shot, proved it had passed, returned to the cabin and left the whole morning out of the record.

Instead, she went to find Chiron.

He was in the Big House office, writing a note. When she came in, he set the quill down.

“The tug came back after the second arrow,” Thalia said. “I stopped.”

“Did it hurt on the first?”

“No.”

“Has it settled now?”

“It’s still there.”

“Then let’s look before you decide what comes next.”

“Okay.”

Annabeth came in a minute later. She had seen the bow on the bench and guessed where Thalia had gone. She stopped at the door.

“Do you want me here?” she asked.

“Yes. Quiet, unless I ask.”

“Understood.”

Chiron examined the heel again. The swelling had not returned. The tenderness was unchanged from the previous morning. The tug was gone now, replaced by the dull awareness she had learned to recognize.

“You stopped when the tug returned,” he said. “That was the plan.”

“I know. I still hate that it happened.”

“I know.”

“Does it mean it’s getting worse?”

“Not by itself. You had one quiet arrow, then a tug after the second. There is no new swelling, no sharp pain, and no pain while sitting. That tells me we should not add more shooting today. It does not tell me the heel has gone backward.”

“So I try again later?”

“Not tomorrow. Let the tenderness settle. You can walk around camp as you have been, with the splint. Avoid the long downhill route if there’s a way around. If the pain increases during ordinary walking or swelling returns, come back before the scheduled review. Otherwise, we’ll see each other next week as planned.”

“Could I use the line after two days?”

“If the heel returns to its usual level, you can try one arrow on flat ground. If that stays quiet, stop there. We’ll decide about another session after that.”

“It’s smaller than yesterday’s plan.”

“Yesterday’s plan was based on how it felt yesterday.”

“I know.”

“We change the plan when the information changes.”

Thalia glanced at the heel. She wanted the word “information” to mean a clean line of progress. It meant a record that could hold both the quiet first arrow and the tug after the second. She didn’t like the second part. She liked even less that hiding it would make the next choice worse.

Chiron asked before pressing the heel. This time it was only mildly tender. She told him the difference without trying to make it sound smaller.

“Would you like me to write this down?” he asked.

“Yes. Same place.”

“Medical book only.”

He made the entry with the page turned so she could read it: first arrow tolerated; mild tug after second; stopped; no swelling increase; hold archery until tenderness returns to baseline; one arrow after two days if comfortable; reassess next week. He left off the shot placement and the frustration in her voice.

Thalia looked at the line. “That’s accurate.”

“Do you want anything changed?”

“No.”

“Then the plan is set.”

It was not a medal. It was not a punishment. It was a small, annoying plan that left her room to choose again.

Chiron checked the fit of the wrap and gave her a clean strip to replace the one that had loosened. She tied it herself. Annabeth watched without reaching for the roll.

“Do you want to walk back together?” Annabeth asked.

“Yes.”

They took the main path. The heel did not tug on the flat stretch. At the slope beside the pavilion, Thalia slowed before she reached the angle. She looked at Annabeth.

“Don’t ask if I want to turn around.”

“I won’t.”

“Ask if I want to pause.”

“Do you want to pause?”

“Not yet.”

“All right.”

The path leveled. Thalia continued.

At the next turn, a pair of campers rolled a barrel toward the kitchen. The barrel bumped against a stone and one of them looked over.

“Need help?” he called.

“I’m good.”

He nodded and went back to the barrel. The offer did not turn into a lecture about what she should or should not carry. Thalia could say no without proving she was fine.

Annabeth walked beside her. “I wanted to ask whether you wanted me to tell the others.”

“About the heel?”

“Yes.”

“No.”

“All right.”

“If it affects something they’re planning, I’ll tell them what they need to know.”

“Okay.”

“Not the whole medical book.”

“Of course.”

They reached the cabin and sat in the doorway to take off their boots. Thalia loosened the splint while she rested. The ache eased. She watched Annabeth wipe the damp edge of her boot with a cloth.

“I’m sorry I said you translate my face,” Thalia said.

“You were right that I sometimes try.”

“I know. I could’ve said it less sharply.”

“You could have. I still heard what you meant.”

Thalia flexed her toes inside the wrap. “I don’t want you checking me every time I stand.”

“I won’t.”

“I also don’t want you to pretend you don’t see it.”

“I can ask when you want help.”

“Once.”

“Once.”

“And then you listen.”

“I’ll listen.”

“I don’t want it to become a set of rules you have to memorize.”

Annabeth smiled faintly. “You’re giving me a list while telling me not to make a list.”

“I’m explaining.”

“Then I’m listening.”

They stayed on the floor a while, each with a boot in hand. Thalia was glad Annabeth had come to the office. She was also glad Annabeth had not spoken when she wanted to answer. The two things fit together.

At lunch, Thalia told Clarisse only that Chiron had adjusted the archery plan. Clarisse asked if she wanted company later. Thalia said she might. Clarisse said she would be in the armory, then left the decision open. No one asked for the details.

Ianthe arrived at the table with a clean copy of her route map. She handed it to Beckendorf first, then took a seat opposite Thalia. The map had paths, soft ground, and the locations of two temporary barriers. There were no names beside the lines.

“Beckendorf checked the north path,” she said. “He said the dry portion can carry a light bundle, but he wants the lower cut closed until the fence footing is secure.”

“Good.”

“I’m not asking you to review it.”

“Good.”

“I wanted to tell you that I took your name out of the notes.”

“I didn’t give you my name.”

“I know. I had written it in my own draft.”

Thalia considered what to say. Ianthe had made the same mistake she’d just been accused of making: turning someone else’s body into a planning detail. She had also removed it before the map went anywhere.

“Thanks for taking it out,” Thalia said.

Ianthe nodded. “I wanted to ask if you still want me to sit here.”

“You can sit.”

“That’s not the same as wanting me to.”

“No. But I’m answering the question you asked.”

Ianthe studied her face and seemed to decide not to look for a second answer under it. “All right.”

They ate quietly. At the end of the meal, Ianthe showed the map to Clarisse, who checked the symbols and pointed to a place where the dry strip ended sooner than Ianthe had marked. Ianthe erased the line and asked where Clarisse had seen the ground change. Clarisse told her. No one made a ceremony of the correction.

Thalia watched Ianthe make the change, then looked away. It was not her task to decide whether Ianthe had learned something. She could notice that the map was better and let that be enough for the meal.

---

Two days later, Thalia woke with no increase in stiffness. She took the main path to the range after breakfast, carrying her bow and nothing else. The far end was dry. She checked the ground for herself and set the bow on the bench.

Chiron was on the porch outside the Big House. He saw her but did not call out. She appreciated that he let her come to him.

“One arrow,” she said.

“Do you feel ready?”

“I feel like trying one.”

“That’s a different answer.”

“It’s the one I have.”

“Then one arrow.”

Rhys was setting up the near targets. He glanced over, lifted a hand in greeting, and went back to the lesson he was preparing. The younger campers were busy arguing about which target was hardest to hit. Thalia took her place at the dry marker.

She drew, released, and watched the arrow land on the outer ring. Her heel stayed quiet. She lowered the bow and put it back on the bench.

Chiron waited a moment before asking. “How does it feel?”

“Quiet.”

“Anything else?”

“Stiff. Not painful.”

“Do you want another?”

“No.”

“Then you’re done.”

She liked that he did not ask whether she was certain. The plan had been one arrow. She had done one.

At the edge of the range, the younger camper who had asked her about the target a few days ago was sorting arrows. He looked up and saw her set the bow down.

“Only one?”

“Only one.”

“Why?”

“Because that’s what I chose.”

He accepted that more easily than she expected. “Okay.”

A girl beside him asked Rhys how many arrows she had to shoot before he would let her move to the farther target. Rhys asked whether her feet felt steady. The girl said yes, but she was scared of missing. Rhys told her that was a different problem from being ready.

Thalia smiled despite herself. The answer worked for both of them.

She took the path back to the Big House. It was not a race against the heel. She kept an even pace, and the splint supported the foot. By the time she reached the porch, the familiar soreness had returned but not sharpened. She sat, loosened the boot, and made a note for herself: one arrow, no tug; stiffness after; ordinary soreness, not worse.

She did not put the note on a camp board or hand it to Annabeth. It was hers to carry to Chiron.

That evening, Clarisse sat beside her at the fire ring. The distance between them was comfortable enough that neither had to talk over the flames.

“Did you shoot?” Clarisse asked.

“One arrow.”

“How was it?”

“Fine.”

Clarisse glanced at her. “Do you mean fine, or do you mean you don’t want to talk about it?”

“Fine enough for one arrow.”

“Got it.”

They watched sparks rise and disappear. Across the ring, Leandros was helping Percy gather the cups from the bench. He started to carry the whole stack in one hand. Percy told him to split it. Leandros rolled his eyes, then put half on the other side of the tray.

“He’s still impatient,” Clarisse said.

“Everyone is.”

“Not everyone.”

“You’re impatient too.”

“I’m allowed.”

“Because you’re you?”

“Because I know when I’m doing it.”

“That isn’t a reason.”

Clarisse gave her a sideways look. “You’re using your heel as an excuse to win arguments.”

“I’m using my heel as an excuse to sit down.”

“That’s different.”

“It is.”

Clarisse leaned back. “Do you want to go inside?”

“No.”

“Do you want me to stop asking?”

“Not yet.”

They stayed another ten minutes. When Thalia decided to go, she stood at her own pace. Clarisse stood too, but did not put out a hand.

“Main path?” Clarisse asked.

“Yes.”

They walked there together. When the route sloped, Thalia slowed. Clarisse did not move ahead or turn it into an escort. She matched the slower pace and talked about the kitchen bread, which had come out too dense in the middle.

“Silena says the oven ran cold.”

“Did it?”

“I don’t know. I don’t know anything about bread.”

“You could ask her.”

“I could.”

“Would you?”

“Maybe.”

“That’s very brave.”

“I’m not afraid of bread.”

“Good. It can tell.”

Clarisse snorted. The heel stayed sore, and the conversation stayed ordinary. The two facts did not cancel each other out.

On the fourth day after the second arrow, Thalia chose to try two. The first was quiet. She waited the length of a slow breath, then took the second. There was no tug, only stiffness that eased when she sat.

She stopped anyway.

She did not need the pain to return in order to obey the plan. The plan was not a challenge to see whether she could endure more.

---

At the end of the week, Thalia went to Chiron’s office again. This time she went alone. She had asked Annabeth not to come, and Annabeth had said all right.

Chiron invited her to sit before he asked what had changed.

“The stiffness is shorter,” Thalia said. “No pain when I’m sitting. The downhill path still aches, but less than last week. I’ve tried one arrow, then one arrow two days later, then two after that. No tug. The soreness is ordinary, and it settles after I rest.”

“How has the swelling been?”

“I haven’t seen any.”

“Any sharp pain?”

“No.”

“Any twist or fall?”

“No.”

He examined the heel. The swelling had gone down further. The tenderness was still there if he pressed at the same point, but it was fainter and did not make her pull her foot away.

“Does this feel different from last week?”

“Yes. Less.”

“Would you call the improvement steady?”

“Mostly. The slope is the thing that’s taking longer.”

“That is useful to know.”

“Does that mean I can go back?”

“What do you want to go back to?”

“Archery practice.”

“All of it?”

“Not at once.”

“Good. The heel is improving. It is not ready for a full lesson or strenuous training. You can try up to three arrows on level ground, every other day, with a rest between them. Stop if the tug returns. Keep the splint for uneven paths and longer walks. We’ll reassess in two weeks. If the swelling returns or the pain worsens, come back sooner. If the tenderness stops improving, I’ll ask Will to look at it.”

“Can I use the field with Rhys?”

“If he can keep the ground dry and give you space for a short session.”

“I can ask him.”

“You can.”

She sat back. The answer was less than she wanted and more than she had had last week.

“Do you want me to write it in the medical book?” Chiron asked.

“Yes.”

He wrote it down in the same place. Thalia read the note when he turned the ledger around. The words were plain: swelling further reduced; focal tenderness persists but improved; downhill ache less frequent; three arrows on level ground every other day, stop for return of tug; splint on uneven ground and longer walks; reassess in two weeks; Will consult if stalled or worse.

“Accurate?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Anything you want changed?”

“No.”

“Then you have the plan.”

“I have it.”

She left the office and walked back to the cabins alone. She chose the main path. The slope beside the pavilion still caught at the heel, and she slowed before reaching it. The ache came, but it did not pull sharply. She paused at the top, then went down at her own pace.

The day was cool and bright. At the range, Rhys had not yet begun his lesson. Thalia found him near the rack.

“Chiron cleared me for up to three arrows every other day, on level ground,” she said. “No full lesson. I have to stop if the tug comes back.”

“Do you want to try it with me tomorrow?”

“If the surface stays dry.”

“I can check the ground before the lesson. Do you want me to save the far target?”

“Yes.”

“I’ll do that.”

“Don’t tell the younger group I’m back.”

“I won’t.”

“Don’t tell them I’m almost back either.”

“I won’t.”

“Just leave the target open.”

Rhys nodded. “I can do that.”

She looked at the field. The far marker was the one she had used for the one-arrow test. Nothing about it had changed. Tomorrow, she might use it again. She might not. The decision would belong to the version of her who woke with the heel and the weather in front of her.

On the way to the dining hall, she passed Ianthe near the Big House porch. Ianthe had a clean map tucked under one arm and was waiting for Beckendorf to come out.

“Did Chiron see you?” Ianthe asked.

“He did.”

“Do you want to tell me what he said?”

Thalia considered the question. Ianthe had asked, and the answer was hers to choose.

“Better. Still not done. Three arrows on level ground, every other day. I stop if it tugs.”

Ianthe nodded. “Do you want me to keep the path map clear near the range?”

“No. Beckendorf’s handling the path. My plan is with Chiron.”

“All right.”

“You can ask me about it again if I bring it up. Don’t check in every morning.”

“I won’t.”

“I’m not saying you can’t care.”

“I know.”

“I’m saying care doesn’t make you the person who tracks it.”

Ianthe glanced down at the map. “I understand.”

“Do you?”

“I think so. I’ll probably have to remember when I want to make a plan.”

“That’s more honest.”

“I can ask once.”

“You can ask once.”

“And you can say no.”

“Yes.”

Ianthe held the map closer to her side. “Then I’m going to wait for Beckendorf.”

“Good.”

Thalia kept walking. She did not stay to see whether Ianthe looked disappointed. It was not a test she needed to administer. The map was Ianthe’s responsibility, the heel was Thalia’s, and the path belonged to the people who inspected and used it together.

At supper, Percy sat beside Annabeth. Clarisse and Silena were already at the table. Leandros came in a little later, paused to ask whether the open seat was for him, then sat when Clarisse said it was. No one spoke about Thalia’s appointment until she chose to.

“Chiron said I can do three arrows every other day,” Thalia said. “On level ground. If the tug returns, I stop.”

“Good,” Percy said, then caught himself. “That sounds like your plan.”

“It is.”

“I’m glad you have one.”

“Better.”

Annabeth passed her the bread. “Do you want to tell me more about the appointment later?”

“Maybe.”

“All right.”

The conversation moved to the weather, the repaired fence, and the bread Silena planned to make. “The dry seam is repaired, and the loop is done,” Leandros said. “It only needs Beckendorf to load-test it.”

“If it needs a load test, it isn’t done,” Clarisse said.

Leandros looked annoyed. “The seam is repaired. The loop is spliced, not approved for use yet.”

Thalia listened without making herself the judge. The new siblings were learning in different places and at different speeds. That did not make the lesson hers to assign.

After the meal, she walked back to Cabin One with Annabeth. The dusk had gone cold enough that the main path was drying under the lamps. Her heel ached mildly on the slope. She slowed and kept moving.

At the cabin, she loosened the splint and set the boot beneath her bed. Tomorrow’s arrows were not promised. The next appointment was in two weeks. The tenderness remained; the plan had changed because the facts had changed. None of it made her less herself.

Annabeth set the window notes on the table. “Do you want help taking off the wrap?”

“No. I can do it.”

“All right.”

Thalia unwound the cloth, folded it, and set it beside the splint. Then she looked up at her sister.

“Thanks for coming with me the first time.”

“You’re welcome.”

“And thanks for letting me go alone today.”

“You asked to go alone.”

“I did.”

Annabeth sat on the edge of her bed. “Do you want to talk about the list?”

“No.”

“Do you want me to read?”

“Maybe in a minute.”

“Okay.”

Thalia picked up the book she had left on the chair and opened it to the page she had been reading before. The fire snapped softly in the stove. Outside, the wind ran over the roof and past the window that still needed a longer storm check. The heel had a measure now: the slope, the tug, the ordinary ache, the few arrows she could try before she stopped. It was not a verdict about what she could do forever. It was information for tomorrow.

For tonight, she read one page. Then she turned to the next.
