## Chapter 30: Amphitrite Visits

Percy Jackson knew something was happening before Chiron even stood up, because the morning felt wrong in a good way.

Breakfast in the dining pavilion was usually loud in a predictable rhythm — clatter of plates, Clarisse arguing with a Stoll about bacon theft, the Apollo kids tuning a lyre badly on purpose, Annabeth's pencil scratching against a notebook beside his elbow while she stole his strawberries when she thought he wasn't looking. He was looking. He always let her have them anyway.

But that bright Friday morning, the air coming off the Sound was saltier. Sharper. The breeze didn't just ruffle the blue tablecloths on the godly tables up the hill — it lifted them, snapped them, like something large was moving out in the water and pushing air ahead of it. The lake past the canoe dock was flat and bright, too bright, reflecting the sky back so hard it hurt to stare.

And the naiads at the drinks table kept whispering and giggling and looking at him.

"Okay, what's going on?" Percy asked, leaning around Annabeth to stare at Thalia. Thalia was at the Zeus table with her feet up on the bench, trying to look like she wasn't also watching him. "Why does everyone keep looking at me? Do I have syrup on my face?"

"You always have syrup on your face," Annabeth said, without looking up from her notebook. She reached over anyway and wiped his chin with her thumb. Her fingers left a warm spot. "There. Presentable."

"You're deflecting," Percy said. "Both of you are deflecting."

Thalia grinned around her orange juice. "Maybe we just like looking at you, Kelp Head. Ever think of that? You're very lookable."

"Thalia—" Annabeth warned.

"What? It's true. He's got that whole lost-puppy prince thing going."

Percy opened his mouth to complain, and then Chiron's hooves clopped on the marble floor of the pavilion, and Mr. D lowered his diet soda, and the whole camp went quiet in that ripple-out way it did when news was coming.

Chiron smiled. It was a real smile, the kind that crinkled the corners of his eyes.

"If I could have your attention," he said. "A brief announcement before activities. Queen Amphitrite of Atlantis, wife of Poseidon, has formally requested — and been granted — permission to visit Camp Half-Blood today, as a parent."

A beat of silence. Then chaos.

The Poseidon table erupted. Silena actually shrieked and knocked over her goblet. Clarisse choked on her eggs and had to be pounded on the back by a passing Ares kid. Half the camp turned to stare at Percy all at once.

Percy sat very still.

"Me?" he said stupidly. "She's — she's coming for — me?"

Annabeth's hand found his under the table and squeezed hard. "Breathe, Seaweed Brain."

"But — she's never — " Percy stopped. His throat had gone tight. "I've seen her in Atlantis, sure. At feasts and councils and — she braided my hair once when I was seven and I had kelp tangled in it and Dad was useless. But she's never come *here*. To *my* mortal camp. Gods don't do that. They don't — do parent visits. Right? That's not a thing."

"It is today," Chiron said gently, having trotted over while Percy wasn't looking. The centaur rested a large, warm hand on Percy's shoulder. "She wrote to me herself, Percy. And to Mr. D, and to the boundary wards. Very properly. She wishes to see where you live during the summer. To meet your friends. To bring lunch."

"To bring lunch?" Silena repeated, eyes shining. "The Queen is bringing lunch?"

Clarisse was still coughing. Her face was red, and not just from choking. "She's coming to inspect the cabin," she muttered. "Oh gods. Oh gods, my bunk is a disaster. My spear socks are everywhere. She's going to think we're slobs."

"She's not inspecting for the military, Clarisse," Silena said, but she was already patting her own dark braid flat and checking her Camp necklace like she was preparing for a parade.

Percy barely heard them. His heart was thudding against his ribs like a conch drum.

Amphitrite was coming. His stepmother. His queen. The lady Triton said had held his hand when he was seven and terrified at his first Atlantean court ball and whispered, *Stay by me, little prince, and no shark will dare bite.* The lady who sent him tide-pool sweets in a sealed coral box every Saturnalia with a note in looping sea-green ink: *For Percy — eat before your father steals them. — A.*

The lady he had never quite known what to call. Mother still meant Sally Jackson in the Manhattan life he remembered, with her blue cookies and her laugh and her apartment that smelled like paint. Here Sally had died when he was two, and Amphitrite had raised him. But not *Queen Amphitrite* either, not in his head. In his head she was just — Amphitrite. Kind eyes. Cool hands. Someone who had chosen to be kind to a boy who was proof her husband loved someone else.

And now she was coming to camp. To his bunk. To meet Annabeth.

"Oh no," Percy said out loud.

Annabeth blinked. "Oh no?"

"She's going to meet you," Percy said. "She's going to do the — the parent look. The *is this girl good enough for my son* look."

Annabeth raised one blonde eyebrow. "Percy, I'm the daughter of Zeus. In the life I remember I stared down a hellhound when I was seven. I think I can survive your stepmom."

"That's different," Percy said miserably. "Hellhounds don't want you to make a good impression."

Thalia leaned over from the next table, nearly knocking her tray off. "Are you kidding? This is great. A real, functioning divine parent, showing up with lunch and hugs? I want to take notes. For science. For proof it can happen."

There was a little edge under her joke. Percy caught it, and Annabeth caught it, and Annabeth's expression softened.

But before anyone could answer, a horn blew from the beach — not the camp conch for assembly, but something deeper, older, a sound that rolled up from the water and through the wooden bones of the pavilion.

Chiron straightened. "Ah. That will be her now."

Every single camper stood up at once and stampeded for the hill.

---

Percy had seen Atlantean arrivals before. Processions with dolphin guards and glowing banners and his dad in full trident-and-armor mode looking like a tidal wave in sandals.

This was different. This was small on purpose.

The lake — not the Sound, the canoe lake, because the Queen had asked to come quietly through fresh water so as not to alarm the borders — bulged upward in the center like a dome. Light shone through it from inside, green and gold. Then it parted, and out rose two hippocampi, cream-white and sea-green, manes braided with pearls, pulling a low open chariot made of shell and coral.

And standing in the chariot, holding the reins loosely in one hand and waving with the other like any mortal mom arriving for visiting day, was Amphitrite.

Percy forgot how to breathe.

She was tall and regal even in simple clothes — for her, simple meant a dress of deep sea-green silk that moved like water, a shawl of pale foam-colored netting, and a small golden crown nestled in her dark hair, which was pinned up with coral combs but already coming loose in the lake breeze. Her skin glowed faintly, the way all sea gods glowed when they were happy. Her eyes were the color of a harbor at dawn.

When she saw the crowd of kids on the grass, her whole face lit up.

And when she saw Percy, standing frozen between Annabeth and Grover with his orange CAMP HALF-BLOOD shirt half-untucked and syrup probably still on his ear, she laughed out loud — a bright, startled, delighted laugh — and stepped straight out of the chariot into knee-deep water and held out her arms.

"Percy!"

It wasn't dignified. It wasn't queenly. It was perfect.

Percy ran.

He splashed straight into the lake in his sneakers, backpack bouncing, and hit her arms so hard she staggered back a step and the hippocampi snorted. She smelled like salt and jasmine and cold clean depths. Her arms were strong — goddess-strong — and she held him tight enough to lift him half off his feet, rocking him side to side.

"Hi," Percy mumbled into her shoulder, ears burning because the entire camp was watching. "Hi."

"Hello, my darling," Amphitrite said, and kissed the top of his head, and held his face in both her cool hands to look at him. "Let me see you. Oh. Oh, look at you. You've grown half a foot since the spring rites. And you're brown as a seal pup. Are you eating enough? You look thin."

"I'm not thin, I—"

"You are thin. Chiron, is he eating enough?" She looked up at Chiron over Percy's head, mock-severe. "I hold you personally responsible."

Chiron bowed, eyes twinkling. "Welcome to Camp Half-Blood, Your Majesty. He eats like three teenage boys. I assure you."

"Good. Then he can eat a fourth lunch with me." She kept one arm firmly around Percy's shoulders and turned to the crowd. Dozens of demigods stared back, wide-eyed. "Well! Don't be shy. Which one of you lives with this scamp?"

Clarisse stepped forward before Silena could push her. She stood straight, chin up, spear-calluses showing on her hands, trying very hard to look like a veteran and not like a thirteen-year-old girl meeting her queen.

"Clarisse La Rue, ma'am," she said. "Daughter of Poseidon. Cabin Three."

"Silena Beauregard, daughter of Poseidon," Silena added quickly, elbowing her sister. "Cabin Three also. Your Majesty. Welcome."

Amphitrite's expression changed. Percy felt it — the slight tightening of her arm around him, the way her smile went softer and more careful.

It struck Percy again how unfair his upbringing had been to Clarisse and Silena. They weren't only his cabinmates. They were Poseidon's daughters by mortal women, and they had not grown up receiving what he had. Which made Amphitrite their stepmother too. In the old stories, that never ended well. Hera and Heracles. All those terrible stepmother tales the bards loved.

Amphitrite stepped forward and took both girls' hands.

"Clarisse. Silena." She said their names like she had practiced them. Like they mattered. "I know you. Clarisse, you took your first prize in spear drill when you were nine, and your father bragged about it for a month. Silena, you sang the reef-lullaby at the summer tide so sweetly the dolphins repeated it back. I've heard such good things."

Clarisse's mouth fell open. "You — you know that?"

"Of course I know that," Amphitrite said gently. "Do you think I don't ask after my husband's children? Your mothers raised brave, kind girls. I've always been grateful."

Silena's eyes filled instantly. Clarisse looked like someone had hit her lightly on the back of the head with a pillow — stunned, off-balance, not unhappy.

"I — thank you," Clarisse managed. "Ma'am. Uh. Your Majesty."

"Amphitrite, please," the queen said. "At least today. When I'm fussing over bunk beds, titles only get in the way."

She turned then, and her gaze landed on Annabeth, who was standing very straight with her hands clasped behind her back, trying to look calm and mostly succeeding except for the pink climbing her ears.

"And you must be Annabeth," Amphitrite said.

---

Amphitrite had been a queen for three thousand years. She could read a court in a glance — who was angry, who might be hiding embarrassment, who needed a kind word and who needed a sharp one.

She could read a twelve-year-old girl in half a glance.

This one — this slender blonde daughter of Zeus in an orange camp shirt and a bronze knife at her belt and a storm-gray gaze that didn't waver — was nervous. Amphitrite saw it in the way the girl's fingers twisted behind her back, in the way she stood slightly in front of Percy, as if ready to step between him and any danger, even social danger.

*Oh, Poseidon,* Amphitrite thought, amused and touched all at once. *He's found his shield-maiden already. And she doesn't even know she's doing it.*

"Annabeth Grace," the girl said, lifting her chin. "Daughter of Zeus, ma'am. It's an honor to meet you."

"Come here, child," Amphitrite said, and opened her free arm.

Annabeth hesitated for half a heartbeat — Zeus children were not generally hugged by foreign queens without protocol — and then stepped in. Amphitrite embraced her warmly, kissing her cheek in the Atlantean way, and felt the girl relax by slow degrees, surprised at herself.

"Percy writes about you," Amphitrite murmured, just loud enough for Annabeth to hear. "In every letter. *Annabeth figured the maze. Annabeth knew the constellation. Annabeth says hi.* Three pages about heroic deeds and then *Annabeth says hi* like an afterthought, as if I wouldn't notice."

Annabeth pulled back, bright red. "He writes home about camp?"

"Every week. On seashell paper that dissolves if I don't read it fast enough." Amphitrite tucked a stray blonde curl behind Annabeth's ear, motherly and unhurried. "Thank you for being his friend. He was lonely before you, even surrounded by palaces. I could tell."

"He's not lonely now," Annabeth said quietly, glancing at Percy. Her gray eyes were fierce with something old and protective. "Not while I'm around."

"I can see that," Amphitrite said, and meant it. She had met many children of Zeus in her long life — proud, stormy, often lonely in their brilliance. This one had a steadiness to her, a loyalty that reminded Amphitrite of the deep currents that never changed direction. "Will you sit with us at lunch? I brought far too much, and Percy will need someone clever to stop him eating the decorative seaweed."

Annabeth laughed, startled. "Yes. Yes, ma'am. I'd like that."

"Good." Amphitrite looked past her to the tall dark-haired girl hovering at the edge of the crowd, arms crossed tight, jaw set. Thalia, daughter of Zeus. The runaway. The one who had brought her little sister safely through years of road and fear, in stories Amphitrite had heard even in the deep sea. "And you, dear? Will you join us?"

Thalia blinked like she'd been slapped. "Me?"

"You've got your father's lightning in your bracelet," Amphitrite said mildly. "And your sister's expression when she's worried about you. Both are easy to see. I'd be honored if you came."

Thalia stared. Then she shrugged, too quickly. "Sure. Free food's free food."

Amphitrite recognized that shrug. She had seen it on Triton's face when he was twelve and trying not to care. She let it pass with a smile and turned back to Chiron.

"May I see his cabin first? I promised his father I would check for leaks and monsters and insufficient blankets. Fathers worry about such things but never remember to check themselves."

Laughter rippled through the campers. Percy groaned.

"Amphitrite—"

"Hush. Let me be a mother." She linked her arm through his and started up the hill, the whole camp trailing behind like ducklings. "Lead the way, Prince Percy. Show me where you sleep when you're not under my sea."

---

The Poseidon cabin was cool and dim and smelled like salt and cedar when they crowded in — which is to say, when Amphitrite, Percy, Clarisse, Silena, Annabeth, Thalia, Grover, and about twenty curious onlookers tried to fit into a room built for four bunks.

"Out," Clarisse barked, instantly in cabin-leader mode. "Everyone out except Cabin Three plus guests. You — Stoll — I see you. Out!"

Grumbling, the crowd thinned to a knot of heads in the doorway.

Amphitrite stood in the center of the cabin and turned slowly, taking in everything: the walls of pale blue-green stone, the shell floor, the four bunks with their blue blankets, the small window looking toward the sea, the clutter of armor and books and half-finished craft projects.

Percy's bunk was immediately obvious. The blanket was half on the floor. There was a copy of *The Odyssey* with seaweed used as a bookmark. A pair of sneakers, one upright, one on its side. A sketch Annabeth had done of a hippocampus, taped crookedly above the pillow. A conch from Atlantis on the nightstand, next to a photo in a waterproof sleeve — Sally Jackson, smiling in front of a candy shop, waving.

Amphitrite went straight to that photo first.

"Oh," she said softly. "She sent you a new one."

"Triton sent me the copy last week," Percy said, hovering beside her. "An old one. I wish I could tell her about today. About you. And that the blue cookies worked with your recipe tweak."

"I wish we could tell her that her son is thriving." Amphitrite touched the photo with one fingertip, tenderly. She had never met Sally Jackson in person, but before Sally died they had exchanged a few brief letters through Triton as a discreet courier, about tides and a little boy's health and what frightened him. Now Percy described the kitchen from the other life, a home Amphitrite had never known. She listened because Sally mattered to him, and because raising him had never made that love disappear. "I'm glad you have this picture."

Then she turned to the bunk and clapped her hands. "Right. This will not do."

Percy knew that tone. Every Atlantean servant knew that tone. It was the Queen-Is-Going-To-Fix-It tone.

"Amphitrite, it's fine, it's just—"

"It's lumpy." She stripped the blanket back, fluffed the pillow with a sharp snap, and ran her hand along the mattress. A ripple of seawater followed her palm, smoothing and plumping. The sheets tightened themselves. The blanket floated up, shook itself, and resettled, corners crisp. "There. And you need another blanket. The nights get cold this close to the surface. I brought you one."

From the woven kelp basket she had carried up from the lake — which everyone had assumed held lunch — she pulled a folded blanket of deep blue sea-silk, so fine it poured like water, embroidered around the edges with tiny silver waves and dolphins.

The doorway crowd gasped.

"Whoa," Grover breathed. "Is that—"

"Sea-silk," Amphitrite confirmed. "Woven by the Nereids. Warm in winter, cool in summer, and it never gets wet." She tucked it around Percy's bunk with brisk, practiced hands, smoothing it flat. "And this — for the draft from that window."

She set a small coral on the nightstand. It glowed faintly pink-gold, pulsing like a heartbeat. A nightlight.

Percy stared at it, throat tight. "You — you didn't have to—"

"I wanted to." She cupped his cheek, her thumb brushing under his eye. "You sleep under strange stars here, far from my waters. I want you to have something from home."

Clarisse made a small, strangled sound. Amphitrite looked up.

"Oh, my dears, I haven't forgotten you." From the basket she produced two more bundles — smaller, but clearly made with equal care. For Silena, a shawl of rose-pink sea-silk, light as foam, that shimmered when it moved. For Clarisse, a heavy cloak, dark blue-black like deep water, lined with sharkskin-soft gray, with a bronze clasp shaped like a spearhead. "Silena, I heard you feel the cold in the mornings. This should help. Clarisse — your father said you stand watch on the porch when it rains and refuse to come inside. At least be warm while you're being stubborn."

Silena pressed the shawl to her face, eyes shining. "It's beautiful. Thank you. Thank you so much."

Clarisse held the cloak like it might bite. Her hands — big, scarred, capable hands that could string a bow in the dark — trembled just slightly. "You didn't — I mean. You didn't have to bring us anything. We're not — we're not yours. Not really."

The cabin went very quiet. Annabeth looked down at her shoes. Percy held his breath.

Amphitrite sat down on the edge of Clarisse's bunk — uninvited, unhurried — and patted the mattress beside her.

Clarisse, after a moment, sat.

"You're right," Amphitrite said calmly. "You have a mother who loves you, and I would never try to take her place. Your father loves you too, in his clumsy, tidal way. I'm not here to replace anyone."

Clarisse stared at her knees.

"But I am the Queen of his sea," Amphitrite went on. "And you are his daughters, brave and bright, living far from his waters. That makes you — if you'll allow it — under my care. Not my daughters. My charges. My honored guests, whenever you wish to visit. That's all this is." She nudged the cloak. "A warm cloak. From someone who worries, a little, from far away."

Clarisse's jaw worked. "My mom — my mom says you're — she says you're kind. She said if I ever met you, to mind my manners and not be a brat."

Amphitrite laughed, delighted. "Your mother is wise. And you're minding your manners beautifully."

"It's itchy being polite," Clarisse muttered, but she was smiling now, small and real. She swung the cloak around her shoulders. It fit perfectly. "Whoa. It's warm. Like — instantly warm."

"Sharkskin lining," Amphitrite said proudly. "Triton tested it by sleeping in the snow. Don't tell him I told you."

Silena giggled. Percy grinned. The tightness in the room loosened like a knot coming undone.

"Okay," Clarisse said gruffly, standing and adjusting the cloak. "Okay. Thanks. Uh — Amphitrite."

"You're very welcome, Clarisse."

---

Clarisse La Rue had prepared for a lot of things at Camp Half-Blood.

She'd prepared for capture-the-flag with a broken spear and no backup. She'd prepared for a hydra in the woods and a fury in the laundry room and a whole cabin of Ares kids chanting her name like a war drum. She'd prepared for her dad showing up as a giant glowing dude in the middle of a war council and embarrassing her in front of everyone.

She had not prepared for her stepmother tucking in her blanket.

She stood in the doorway of Cabin Three with her new cloak around her shoulders, watching the Queen of Atlantis fuss over Percy's trunk — refolding his shirts, lining up his miniatures, confiscating three suspicious sandwiches — and felt like the floor had tilted three degrees to the left.

*She knows my spear record. She knows Silena's song. She asked after our moms.*

In all the stories, the goddess wife hated the mortal kids. Hera threw snakes into cribs. Hera drove heroes mad. Hera — well, Hera was the prime example, and every demigod knew it. Clarisse had grown up braced for coldness. Her own mom, earthy and blunt and wonderful, had told her straight when she was eight: *Your daddy's wife is a queen, baby. She won't hurt you, but don't expect birthday cards.*

And then the queen shows up with a cloak that fit like it was measured, and calls her brave, and thanks her mother.

Clarisse didn't know what to do with that. It sat in her chest like a hot coal, warming and uncomfortable at once.

"You're making a face," Silena whispered, nudging her. Silena had wrapped herself in the pink shawl and looked softer somehow, younger. Happier. "Stop making a face. You'll scare her off."

"I'm not making a face. This is my normal face."

"This is your *feelings* face. I know the difference."

"Shut up."

Silena grinned and linked arms with her. They watched together as Amphitrite finally finished with Percy's trunk, conjured a small mesh bag for his dirty socks with a flick of her wrist ("For laundry, darling. Even princes do laundry."), and stood, dusting her hands.

"There," the queen declared. "Habitable."

"It's a cabin, not a palace," Percy protested, ears pink while Annabeth smothered a laugh behind her hand. "It's supposed to be messy."

"Nonsense. Even soldiers in Atlantis keep their bunks tidy. Your father once kept his trident polished so bright it blinded the guards." Amphitrite winked at Clarisse. "Don't tell him I told you that either."

"I like her," Grover whispered loudly to Thalia. "Can we keep her?"

"She's not a stray goat, G-man," Thalia said, but she was smiling — a real smile, crooked and surprised.

Clarisse noticed Thalia's smile fade at the edges when Amphitrite hugged Percy again, adjusting his camp necklace straight. Thalia looked away, toward the Zeus cabin visible through the window, white and empty and far too big for two girls.

Clarisse knew that look. She'd worn it every time her dad visited someone else's cabin first. Every time the trident glowed over someone else's head at the campfire. Being a big-three kid meant sharing your parent with the whole ocean or sky. It meant watching other kids get claimed while you waited.

She elbowed Silena. "Come on."

"What? Where—"

"Lunch duty. Queen brought lunch, someone's gotta carry it." Clarisse raised her voice. "Hey! Your Majesty! Where's this lunch you promised? Percy's stomach is eating itself. I can hear it."

Percy threw a sock at her. She caught it one-handed.

Amphitrite laughed. "In the basket! Or rather — " She tapped the kelp basket, which was clearly too small to hold lunch for ten people, and it unfolded like a flower, expanding outward into a massive picnic hamper lined with mother-of-pearl. Inside, nestled in cool sea-mist, were rows of parcels wrapped in giant leaves. "Atlantean picnic. If we carry it down to the meadow, there's enough for whoever wants some."

A cheer went up from the doorway crowd. In seconds, campers were grabbing parcels, Grover was levitating two with his teeth, and the whole procession spilled out into the sunlight toward the strawberry fields.

Clarisse hung back a moment, folding her new cloak carefully — she wasn't going to drag it through strawberry mud, no way — and found Amphitrite beside her, waiting.

"Did I get it right?" the queen asked quietly. Not queenly at all. Just a woman, worried. "With you and your sister? I never know. I rehearse, and then I see you, and you're so grown, and I—"

Clarisse stared. "You rehearse?"

"Every time. For every visit. For Triton too, and he's my own." Amphitrite smiled wryly. "Parenthood doesn't come with a script, even after millennia. Especially with children who have every reason to be wary of you."

Clarisse thought about her mom's voice on the phone last winter: *Be open, baby. Not every story ends like Hera.* She thought about the cloak, warm as a hug.

"You did okay," she said gruffly. "Silena's gonna cry into that shawl tonight, just so you know. Happy crying. And — thanks. For not pretending our moms don't exist. Some — some people do that."

Amphitrite's eyes shimmered, just for a second, like sunlight on water. "Your mothers gave Poseidon great gifts — you. The least I can do is honor them."

Clarisse nodded, throat tight, and looked away. "Come on. Before Percy eats all the good stuff. He does that."

They walked out into the sun together.

---

The meadow picnic was chaos in the best way.

Amphitrite had outdone herself. There were kelp-wrapped rice cakes with crab and mango, cold shrimp skewers glazed with citrus and honey, sea-grape buns that popped sweet-salty on the tongue, abalone fritters crisp as autumn leaves, and for dessert, honeyed moon-jelly tarts that glowed faintly blue and tasted like starlight and sugar. There were bottles of sparkling tidewater that fizzed green-gold and made Grover's eyes go wide. There were pickled sea beans for the satyrs and extra-spicy fire-coral salsa that Clarisse dared Percy to try and then had to pound his back when he turned red.

Percy ate like he'd never seen food. Amphitrite watched him with open delight, constantly pushing another parcel toward him, toward Annabeth, toward anyone within reach.

"Eat, eat. You — tall girl with the sparks — Thalia, yes? Have a tart. You look like you survive on lightning and stubbornness. Grover, dear, more beans? Silena, try this one, it's mild."

Thalia accepted the tart warily, like it might be a trick. She bit in. Her eyebrows shot up. "Whoa. Okay. That's — that's really good."

"My cook's own recipe," Amphitrite said proudly. "I'll send him the mortal measurements. He'll be thrilled. He loves a challenge."

Annabeth was sitting cross-legged beside Percy, a half-eaten rice cake in one hand, her notebook forgotten in the grass. Amphitrite watched the two of them share looks and crumbs and quiet jokes, and felt something loosen behind her ribs.

*He's anchored,* she thought. *Thank the Fates. My wild little seal pup has found his rock, and she loves him back. Even Zeus's brood can see it.*

She caught Annabeth's eye and beckoned her closer while Percy was distracted arguing with Clarisse about salsa.

"Annabeth," she said softly, unclasping something from her own wrist — a bracelet of small black pearls interspersed with tiny gold dolphins. "May I?"

Annabeth held out her wrist, puzzled, and Amphitrite fastened it there. The pearls were cool and smooth.

"When I was young — younger," Amphitrite corrected with a smile, "a wise woman gave me this and told me: *for clear sight in murky water.* You have a strategist's mind, child. You see paths others miss. I want you to have it. Not as a prize. As — a token. From Percy's family to someone who keeps him safe."

Annabeth stared at the bracelet. Her lips parted. "I — I can't accept this. It's too — you're a queen, this must be—"

"It's a gift," Amphitrite said firmly, covering the girl's hand with her own. "And queens give gifts when they please. Besides—" she lowered her voice conspiratorially, "—it matches your eyes when you're thinking. Stormy and bright. Percy told me about that look. He says you get it before you save his life."

Annabeth laughed wetly. "He exaggerates."

"He adores," Amphitrite corrected gently. "There's a difference. Hold his hand when the water gets rough, won't you? He pretends to be braver than he is."

"I will," Annabeth whispered. "I promise."

Amphitrite hugged her again, and this time Annabeth hugged back hard, like she hadn't been hugged by a mother in a long time.

Over Annabeth's shoulder, Amphitrite met Thalia's gaze. The older girl looked away quickly, picking at her tart, jaw tight.

*Ah,* Amphitrite thought, her heart aching. *There's a wound. Zeus, you fool. Come down from your mountain and see your daughters.*

But she said nothing yet. There would be time.

---

After lunch, Amphitrite stood, brushed crumbs from her dress, and held out her hand to Percy.

"Walk with me?" she said. "Just us. I want to see your lake."

Percy's stomach flipped. The whole camp watched them go — Clarisse giving him a thumbs-up, Silena mouthing *be good*, Annabeth smiling encouragingly, Grover waving a shrimp skewer like a flag.

They walked down to the canoe lake in comfortable silence, Amphitrite's shawl trailing, Percy's sneakers squelching (he'd never dried from his run into the water that morning). Dragonflies stitched the air. A pegasus whinnied far off.

At the dock, Amphitrite kicked off her sandals — golden, absurdly fancy — and sat on the edge, feet in the water.

"Your father told me you can stay underwater as long as you like now," she said. "And that you healed that cut on your arm in minutes last week. You're coming into your own."

"Yeah," Percy said, sitting beside her. "It's — it's cool. And weird. I don't get wet unless I want to. Annabeth thinks it's hilarious. She splashes me and I just — stay dry. She calls it cheating."

Amphitrite laughed. "It *is* cheating. Enjoy it." She bumped his shoulder. "Race you to the bottom?"

Percy stared. "What?"

"You heard me." The queen stood, and with a twist of her wrist her dress shimmered and shortened into a sleek swimming tunic the color of deep water. Her crown dissolved into light and reformed as a simple coral clip. She looked younger suddenly — not a queen, just a woman in her twenties, barefoot and grinning. "Last one down has to clean hippocampus stalls for a week. Come on, little prince. Show me what you've learned."

She dove — clean, silent, barely a ripple.

Percy yelped and dove after her.

Underwater, the lake was green-gold and full of light. Weeds swayed. Bass darted. A pair of naiads waved from behind a rock, giggling.

And Amphitrite swam like — well, like the sea herself. She didn't kick or stroke so much as *flow*, her body a ribbon of motion, her hair streaming. Percy pushed hard, arms burning, legs pumping, using every trick his dad had taught him and every current-sense he'd figured out on his own. He caught up at the thermocline, where cold water met warm, and Amphitrite turned, eyes sparkling, and mouthed *faster!*

They hit the muddy bottom together, laughing bubbles, and sat cross-legged in the silt like two kids in a blanket fort. Sound was muffled. Light rippled. No one could see them. No one could interrupt.

*Better?* Amphitrite signed in Atlantean hand-speech, and Percy nodded, grinning.

She took his hands. Her fingers were cool. Her eyes were serious now, tender.

*I missed you,* she signed, slow and clear. *The palace is quiet without you. Your father paces. Triton pretends not to miss you but he polished your old practice trident. Twice.*

Percy felt his throat close. He squeezed her hands. *Missed you too. Camp's great, but — different. Loud. No currents at night.*

*Are you happy here? Truly?* She searched his face. *Not just brave. Happy.*

Percy thought about Annabeth's laugh when he stole her pencil. About Clarisse yelling at him to keep his elbows off the table and then saving him the last roll. About Silena humming while she mended his armor strap. About Grover's terrible jokes and Thalia's electric high-fives and Chiron's stories by the fire.

He nodded, hard. *Yeah. Really happy.*

*Good.* Amphitrite's smile was sunrise through water. *Then I'm happy. But remember — you always have a home beneath the waves. No matter who you become here, no matter what name they call you, there is a room with blue walls and a window looking over the gardens, and it is yours. No one else sleeps there. The fish clean it. They complain, but they do it.*

Percy laughed, bubbles bursting from his nose. He threw his arms around her, and she held him, rocking gently while minnows darted around them like confetti.

She signed that she had taught him bubble pictures when he was seven. Percy knew only what she was telling him, but he tried to copy the movement by watching her hands. When the first bubble collapsed, she demonstrated again without making him explain why he needed the lesson. Now, without thinking, he blew a small, perfect bubble and shaped it: a woman with a crown, holding hands with a small boy.

Amphitrite stared. Then she pressed her hand to her mouth, eyes bright.

*Oh, Percy,* she signed, and pulled him close again, holding the bubble gently between them until it wobbled and popped into sparkles. *My sweet boy. My brave boy.*

They stayed down there a long time, until Percy's fingers pruned (a mortal habit he hadn't lost) and Amphitrite finally pointed up.

They surfaced to find Annabeth sitting on the dock with her feet in the water, waiting, the pearl bracelet glinting on her wrist. She didn't look surprised to see them dripping and grinning.

"Have fun?" she asked dryly.

"The queen cheats," Percy declared, hauling himself up. "She *is* water. How am I supposed to race water?"

"You almost beat me," Amphitrite said, wringing out her hair. Water droplets hung in the air around her like diamonds before falling. "Another year, and I fear for my reputation."

Annabeth handed Percy a towel. Their fingers brushed. "You were down a while. Everything okay?"

Percy glanced at Amphitrite, who smiled and looked away tactfully, giving them space.

"Yeah," Percy said softly. "Everything's — yeah. Really good."

Annabeth studied his face, then nodded, satisfied. She turned to Amphitrite. "Thank you for letting me — for sharing him for a bit. I know time is short."

"Oh, my dear," Amphitrite said, sitting beside her and slipping her sandals back on. "Time is never short when it's spent well. Besides, I got to meet you. That alone was worth the journey through three currents and a very grumpy border ward."

Annabeth blushed. Percy flopped down on her other side, shoulder to shoulder, dripping lake water all over her towel. She shoved him. He shoved back. Amphitrite watched them, chin in her hand, utterly content.

From the tree line, half-hidden, Thalia watched too.

---

Clarisse found Thalia by the archery range, shooting arrows into a target with more force than necessary. Each shaft hit dead center with a crack that made the straw dummy shudder. Sparks crawled along Thalia's bracelet. The air smelled faintly like rain.

"Nice grouping," Clarisse said, leaning on the fence. "Trying to kill it or marry it?"

Thalia snorted, but didn't smile. "Just practicing."

"Bullshit." Clarisse had never been good at gentle. Her mom called it *direct*. Her cabinmates called it *being Clarisse*. "You're brooding. You're doing the Zeus-kid brood where you stare at happy people like they personally offended you."

Thalia lowered her bow. "I'm not — I don't — " She sighed. "It's stupid."

"Try me. I'm great at stupid."

Thalia was quiet for a long moment. Down the hill, laughter drifted from the lake — Percy's laugh, bright and unguarded, and Amphitrite's lighter echo, and Annabeth saying something that made them both laugh harder.

"She's great," Thalia said finally. "Your — Amphitrite. She's — she fussed over his bunk and brought food and swam with him and gave Annie jewelry and — " Her voice caught. She stabbed another arrow into the quiver. "It's just. Nice. You know? To see. A godly parent who actually shows up. Who knows their kids' birthdays and brings blankets."

Clarisse felt that hot coal in her chest again. "Yeah," she said quietly. "It's — yeah."

"Zeus has never visited," Thalia said, staring at the target. "Not me, not Annabeth. He sends lightning. He sends omens. He sent a damn electric toothbrush for Saturnalia with a note that said *BRUSH WELL.* That's it. That's the parenting." She laughed, harsh. "And Hera — don't even get me started. She looks at me like I'm a stain on her marriage. Which — fair. I am. We are."

"Hey." Clarisse pushed off the fence. "Don't. Don't do that."

"Do what? Tell the truth?"

"Talk about yourself like you're a stain. You're Thalia Grace. You punched a cyclops in the eye last week. You're not a stain, you're a — a storm. A pain in my ass, but — " Clarisse shrugged awkwardly. "Look. I get it. Okay? My dad — Poseidon — he's great, but he's not *here*. He sends dreams and glowy tridents and sometimes shows up when the world is ending. Amphitrite — today was the first time she's ever visited. Ever. In four years at camp. And she's not even my real mom."

"But she came," Thalia said. "And she knew stuff. And she was kind."

"Yeah," Clarisse admitted. "She was. And it messed me up too, if it helps. I didn't know where to put my hands. Silena cried. Percy looked like he was gonna explode from happy. It's a lot."

Thalia nodded, eyes bright and angry. "Annabeth's over the moon. Did you see her bracelet? She keeps turning it like — like it's proof. Proof someone's family claims her. And I'm happy for her, I am, she's my sister, but also — " She swallowed. "Also I want to throw something."

"So throw something," Clarisse said simply. "At the target. Not at people."

Thalia laughed wetly and let fly. Bullseye.

They stood in silence while the lake laughter drifted up again.

"You know what she did?" Clarisse said after a while. "Amphitrite. She pulled me aside and asked if she got it right. Like — she was nervous. The Queen of the Entire Ocean was nervous about meeting *me*. Me, who once set Percy's bed on fire by accident—"

"By accident?" Thalia raised an eyebrow.

"Mostly by accident." Clarisse grinned. "Point is — functional divine parents are still people. Still awkward. Still trying. Maybe — maybe Zeus is trying in his weird sky-dad way. Toothbrush and all."

Thalia stared at her. "Did you just defend Zeus?"

"No. Absolutely not. Strike that from the record." Clarisse held up her hands. "I'm just saying — today showed me it's possible. To show up and not screw it up completely. So maybe — maybe yours will. Someday."

Thalia was quiet. Then she shouldered her bow. "Come on. They're probably doing dinner soon. And if I miss Atlantean dessert because I was moping at archery, I'm going to be furious with myself."

Clarisse fell in beside her as they walked down. "That's the spirit. Fury as motivation. Very Ares of you."

"I'm Zeus. Our motivation is spite and thunder."

"Same thing, prettier packaging."

Thalia actually laughed — a real one — and shoved Clarisse's shoulder. The sparks from her bracelet tingled, friendly.

---

Family dinner was Amphitrite's idea.

"Not the pavilion," she said when Chiron offered to seat her at the head table. "Please. May I sit with my children? Just tonight. Just us."

So they pushed two tables together on the Poseidon cabin's little porch — the cabin that faced the sea, with wind chimes made of shells and a view of the sunset turning the water to hammered copper. Amphitrite sat in the middle, Percy on one side, Annabeth on the other (by Percy's insistent tugging), Clarisse and Silena across, with Grover, Thalia, and even Tyson — visiting from the undersea forges with Chiron's permission, who had arrived late, breathless, carrying a rock he'd painted like a hippocampus as a gift — squeezed in wherever they fit.

Chiron stopped by to bless the meal. Mr. D waved from a distance with his soda, which for him was practically a hug.

The food was a mix — camp stew and fresh bread from the kitchens, plus the last of the Atlantean picnic parcels Amphitrite had saved back. Tyson ate three tarts in one bite and cried a little. Grover ate sea beans until his eyes watered. Silena braided Amphitrite's hair while they ate, her fingers quick and gentle, and Amphitrite let her, eyes half-closed in bliss.

"Tell us a story," Silena begged. "About the sea. About when Percy was little."

"Oh, I have many," Amphitrite said, eyes gleaming. "Did he ever tell you about the time he tried to ride a sea cucumber and it — "

"NO," Percy shouted, covering his face while everyone howled. "No. Absolutely not. Royal secrets. Treason to repeat."

"He cried," Amphitrite told Annabeth solemnly. "And the sea cucumber looked very apologetic."

Annabeth was laughing so hard she had to lean on Percy's shoulder. Percy pretended to be mortified, but his ears were red and his grin was huge, and he didn't move away.

Clarisse told a story about Percy flooding the bathroom on his first day. Silena told one about Clarisse sleep-talking orders to her spear. Thalia, emboldened by food and sunset, told a story about Annabeth at age seven trying to organize the border-patrol crew into a filing system. Annabeth threw a bread roll at her. Thalia caught it with lightning reflexes and ate it.

Amphitrite listened to all of it, her arm around Percy's shoulders, her other hand resting near Annabeth's, like she was memorizing them.

When the sun touched the water, she stood and raised her cup of sparkling tidewater.

"A toast," she said. "To my children — by blood, by marriage, by choice, by camp. To Clarisse, whose courage guards the shore. To Silena, whose kindness calms the waves. To Percy, my brave little prince, who carries two homes in one heart and loves enough for both."

Percy stared at his plate, blinking hard. Annabeth squeezed his knee under the table.

"And to their friends," Amphitrite went on, her gaze sweeping over Annabeth, Thalia and Grover, then resting warmly on the visiting Cyclops brother. "Who make them stronger. Annabeth — clear sight in murky water. Thalia — " She paused, and her voice went very gentle. "Thalia, who watches storms from the outside and still stands tall. You are welcome in my waters anytime. Any of you. The sea does not check parentage before it buoys you up."

Thalia stared down at her cup. Her knuckles were white. She nodded once, sharply, not trusting her voice.

"To family," Amphitrite finished. "However we find it."

"To family," they echoed, clinking cups and goblets and one painted rock.

After dinner, when the torches were lit and the dishes cleared by helpful naiads, it was time to go. The hippocampi waited in the shallows, champing and steaming in the cool evening air. The sky was violet, first stars pricking through.

Amphitrite hugged each of them in turn. Silena got a long, rocking hug and a kiss on the forehead. Clarisse got a firm, warrior's hug — clasped forearms first, then a real embrace that lifted her half off her feet — and a whispered, *Write to me. About spear drill. I want to hear.*

Tyson got a pat on the head and a promise of a tour of the forges. Grover got a bag of sea beans for the road. Thalia got a long look and a small shell, white and unbroken.

"For courage," Amphitrite said, pressing it into her palm. "When the sky is loud, listen to the sea inside it. You're stronger than you know, daughter of Zeus."

Thalia closed her fingers around it. "Thank you," she whispered. "For — for today. For letting me — come."

"You are always invited, Thalia," Amphitrite said simply.

Annabeth got another hug, and a murmured, *Take care of him. And let him take care of you.*

And Percy — Percy got last, and longest.

He clung to her, face buried in her shawl, not caring who saw. She held him, rocking, one hand cradling the back of his head like he was seven again.

"Do you have to go?" he mumbled.

"I do, my love. The tide turns, and queens must turn with it." She tilted his face up. Her eyes were bright, but she was smiling. "But I'll write. And you'll write back on that dissolving paper. And your father sends his love and his embarrassment that he couldn't come — Zeus is being difficult about summit meetings, you know how it is."

Percy laughed wetly. "Tell him I said hi. And — tell him the cabin doesn't leak."

"I will." She kissed his forehead. "I'm so proud of you, Percy. Not for quests or powers. For this." She gestured at the porch, the friends, the glowing coral nightlight visible through the cabin window. "For building a home wherever you go. For loving people well. That's rarer than heroism."

Percy swallowed. "Love you," he whispered. It was the first time he'd ever said it out loud to her — not *thank you, Your Majesty*, not *bye, Lady Amphitrite*, but that. "You know? Like — you're family. Real family."

Amphitrite went very still. Then she gathered him in again, fiercely, her crown poking his cheek and neither of them caring.

"Oh, my darling," she breathed. "I know. I love you too. Always have."

She stepped back, climbed into her chariot, and took the reins. The hippocampi tossed their heads.

"Goodnight, Camp Half-Blood!" she called, queenly and bright once more. "Be good to my children! Especially this one — he needs extra dessert!"

Laughter and cheers chased her as the chariot sank beneath the waves in a swirl of foam and light, leaving only ripples and the scent of jasmine.

Percy stood on the beach with Annabeth's hand in his, Clarisse's cloak draped over his shoulders (she'd thrown it at him when the evening breeze picked up — "You're shivering, idiot, take it"), Silena humming on his other side, Thalia turning her new shell over and over in her fingers with a thoughtful look.

The coral nightlight glowed through the Poseidon cabin window up the hill, pink-gold and steady.

"Well," Grover said, breaking the happy silence. "That was the best parent visit ever. No offense to my mom, who is great but cannot summon dessert from seawater."

"None taken," Percy said, grinning so wide his face hurt. "Come on. Let's go look at my new blanket. It's really soft. And Amphitrite said I have to show everyone the sock bag."

As they trudged up the hill together, Annabeth leaned her head against his shoulder.

"She loves you," she said quietly. "Really loves you."

"I know," Percy said. He looked back once at the lake, now dark and calm, and then forward at the warm lights of camp, his friends, his cabin. Two homes. One heart. Just like she'd said. "I'm lucky."

"You are," Clarisse said from behind him, ruffling his hair hard enough to hurt. "Don't forget it, Barnacle Breath."

"I won't," Percy promised, and meant it, all the way up the hill and into the glowing cabin and into dreams of clear water and family dinners that tasted like honey and salt.
