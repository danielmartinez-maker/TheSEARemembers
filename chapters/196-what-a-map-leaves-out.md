## Chapter 196: What a Map Leaves Out

The stool leaned toward its broken leg as if it meant to leave the workbench.

Annabeth nudged it upright with her knee. It stayed for a breath, then listed again, the split in the joint opening like a mouth. She had set the glue, two clamps, a rag, and a small stack of paper on the workbench in Cabin One. Outside, the sky was the color of old pewter. Wind nosed the shutters without making them rattle.

Percy stood across from her, hands held away from the wood. “Where do you want me?”

“Here.” She pointed to the crossbar beneath the seat. “Hold it level while I fit the joint.”

“I can do that.”

“I know.”

He looked at her face, not the stool. “You sound like you think I’m about to do something else.”

“I’m checking.”

“Checking what?”

“Whether you’re going to grab it and decide you can fix the whole thing.”

“I was going to ask if I should hold the leg.”

“You just did.”

“Good. I’m learning.”

Annabeth let the joke stay between them. She turned the stool on its side and examined the split. The wood had pulled away from the dowel, but the dowel itself looked intact. She had noticed that yesterday, then decided she wanted to inspect the joint again in daylight before taking it apart. The stool had been waiting long enough that one more morning did not matter.

“Keep it level,” she said.

Percy set his hands on the crossbar. He did not move until she nodded. She slid the dowel back into its socket, then used the rag to wipe away a bead of glue.

“Not too much,” he said.

“I know.”

“Right. Sorry.”

She kept her attention on the joint. “You can say what you see. Just don’t make it sound like the decision is yours.”

“I wasn’t trying to.”

“I know.”

He smiled a little. “You keep saying that.”

“Because you keep asking.”

“Maybe I want to hear it.”

She looked up. The softness in his expression made something in her chest loosen. She leaned her shoulder against his for a moment, then moved back before the stool shifted. He stayed where she had put him.

“That’s the crossbar,” she said.

“I’m holding it.”

“Good.”

“I’m also holding the silence.”

“You’re doing both.”

He laughed under his breath.

When the glue had taken, she fixed the first clamp and checked the angle. Percy’s thumb hovered just above the wood. He looked as though he wanted to help and was working hard not to turn wanting into movement.

“Can you get the second clamp?” she asked.

He reached for it only after she pointed. He set it in her hand, then returned both palms to the crossbar.

“Thanks.”

“Any time.”

“That’s not a license to take over.”

“I know.”

She looked at him again.

“I heard you,” he said. “I’m not asking you to thank me for a clamp.”

The words were small. They mattered because yesterday she had made a choice about the stool, and he had accepted it without claiming he had been the one to give her permission. The difference did not make him perfect. It made the work easier.

The door opened behind them. Chiron stepped inside, carrying a rolled sheet of brown paper and a tin of drawing charcoal. He paused when he saw the stool held between them.

“Have I interrupted?”

“No,” Annabeth said. “We’re waiting for the glue.”

“Then I’ll be brief.” He put the paper on the clear end of the workbench. “The camp map by the pavilion is older than the north path. We have a new camper learning where things are, and the old one is not useful. Would you mark the paths and the places a newcomer needs to find?”

Annabeth wiped her fingers on the rag. “Which places?”

“Cabins, infirmary, dining pavilion, Big House, training fields, lake, the main gates. Enough to keep someone from taking the long way around the grounds.”

“Do you want a map for Leandros, or do you want me to make a map everybody can use?”

“Both, if you think it can serve both purposes.”

“Does he know you asked me?”

“I’ll tell him after breakfast.”

“Did he say that?”

“Nearly.”

Annabeth looked at the blank paper. “I can do it.”

Percy tipped his chin toward her. “You’ll know where everything is.”

She glanced at him. “That’s why he asked me.”

“Right.”

Chiron watched the two of them, neither intruding nor pretending not to notice the exchange. “There is no deadline. We can take the old one off the board and hang the new one when you are satisfied.”

“When will he see it?” Percy asked.

“After breakfast.”

“He may have suggestions about the lake paths,” Chiron said. “His practical current-reading is narrow, but useful for the shoreline.”

“Because he reads currents,” Percy said.

“Because he has spent time near water,” Chiron corrected. “The map is yours to draw. His local knowledge is his to offer.”

Annabeth turned the charcoal tin over in her hands. Its lid was dented. “I’ll ask him what he knows.”

“Good. And Annabeth, this is a map of places. It is not a map of people’s private histories.”

“I know.”

Chiron’s eyes moved to Percy, then back to her. “I thought you would.”

He left the paper and went back outside.

Percy shifted his grip on the stool. “That seems straightforward.”

“It is.”

“Except he’s going to have opinions.”

“Then he can show me where he thinks the path is.”

“You’re not worried he’ll make another assumption?”

“I’m sure he will.”

Percy drew a careful breath. “I was going to say that when he does, you should—”

“Percy.”

He stopped.

“Don’t finish the sentence for me.”

“I wasn’t trying to tell you what to do.”

“You were about to tell me what I should say to him.”

“I was going to give you an option.”

“You can ask if I want one.”

He lowered his eyes to the stool. “You’re right.”

She waited, though the easy thing would have been to make the conversation smaller. He was not trying to hurt her. He was not entitled to decide what his intent meant.

“I was about to do it again,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Because I think I can head it off before he says something that makes the whole day worse.”

“I know.”

“And because I don’t want you to have to take the hit for all of us.”

“I’m not asking to take a hit. I’m asking you not to step between me and a conversation I’m having.”

He nodded, once. “I can do that.”

“Can you ask first?”

“Yes.”

“Do you want to tell me what you were trying to protect?”

“I thought maybe he’d decide you were on Clarisse’s side before he’d heard anything you said. I wanted him to know you were trying to help.”

“I can tell him that myself.”

“I know.”

“You don’t have to translate me.”

“I’m sorry.”

She searched his face for the familiar urge to explain the apology, to make sure she understood the good intention behind it. He did not add anything.

“Thank you,” she said.

He shifted his hands, careful not to let the stool tilt. “I’m still worried.”

“You can be worried.”

“I can tell you I’m worried.”

“Yes.”

“Can I tell you the options I see?”

“If you ask.”

He glanced at her. “Do you want them?”

“Not yet.”

“All right.”

Annabeth leaned in and pressed the second clamp into place. She checked the pressure, then sat back on her heels. “Hold it for another minute.”

“I’ve got it.”

“I know.”

This time he smiled before she did.

They stayed in the same position until the glue settled enough to take the stool’s weight. When Annabeth straightened, Percy’s wrist brushed hers. She turned her hand and caught his fingers for a second, giving them a squeeze. His expression softened, and she let go before the stool shifted.

“After breakfast,” she said, “I’ll meet Leandros. I’ll show him where the buildings and paths are. I won’t explain what Clarisse meant last night.”

“You don’t have to.”

“If he asks?”

“Tell him to ask her.”

“You don’t have to give me the right answer.”

“I’m practicing.”

“Then ask me if I want help before you give it.”

He swallowed the rest of what he might have said. “Do you want me to come with you?”

She considered it. “For the first part. We can show him the main paths together. If he asks about the cabin, I’ll let Clarisse answer.”

“Okay.”

“And if I want you to step back, I’ll tell you.”

“And I’ll step back.”

“Good.”

The stool held. Annabeth set it upright and waited to see whether it leaned. It stood straight.

Percy looked pleased, but he did not say he had helped her fix it.

Annabeth sat on it once, slowly. The joint creaked, then settled beneath her weight. She stood again.

“It’s holding,” he said.

“It is.”

“Do you want to sit again?”

“No. The glue needs more time.”

He nodded.

She put the clamps in the drawer and put the repair list beside the map paper. Percy picked up the charcoal tin, but did not open it.

“Will you need this?”

“I will.”

He placed it on the table. “Then I’ll leave it here.”

“Thank you.”

He reached for her hand only after she offered it. Their fingers met between the workbench and the door. There was nothing in the gesture that needed explaining.

---

Annabeth found Leandros near the dining pavilion, where the old map had been pinned beneath a calendar with last week’s rain marked in blue chalk. He stood with one shoulder angled toward the lake, listening to the loose canvas overhead. Percy came with her, carrying the roll of paper and a thin board to keep it stiff. He had asked once whether she wanted company. She had said yes.

Leandros looked at the paper. “You’re making a map?”

“I’m marking the paths and buildings.”

“Why?”

“Because the old one is wrong.”

“It’s mostly right.”

“The north path changed. It stops before the split. The infirmary is marked on the wrong side of the hill.”

“It’s not the wrong side. It’s just farther around.”

“Those are different ways of saying the same thing.”

“Not if you’re going to the lake.”

“Then you can show me the lake path.”

He gave her an appraising look. “You want me to tell you where the water is?”

“I want to know where you’d go from here.”

“You’ve already decided the map is wrong.”

“I know what is wrong on the map. I don’t know what you know about the shoreline.”

Percy touched the board with his fingertips. “That’s a fair distinction.”

Leandros looked at him. “You’re going to keep explaining for her?”

“No,” Annabeth said.

Percy blinked, then nodded. “I won’t.”

The pause was brief. Annabeth unrolled the paper across the low pavilion table and held its corners down with four smooth stones. She had drawn the cabins and the Big House first, using the old map as a guide and correcting the places she knew. The north path curved around the hill. The infirmary was marked where it stood now. She had left the shore in a dotted line.

“Here’s where the map ends,” she said. “Show me where you would put the usable path.”

Leandros leaned over the paper but kept his hands to himself. “The boathouse is here.”

“Not quite. The path to the boathouse starts here, but it doesn’t follow the shore.”

“Where’s the canoe rack?”

“Past the birches.”

“That’s farther west.”

“Then mark it.”

He took the charcoal and drew a short line near the western edge. “The path follows the water.”

“It doesn’t. The path turns through the trees.”

“If you go through the trees, you have to take the bend around the rock.”

“How do you know?”

“Because I looked.”

“At the water?”

“And the shore.”

“Then show me.”

He set the charcoal down. “You want me to walk you there?”

“I want to check the map.”

“Then we walk.”

Percy picked up the paper. “I can stay and keep the map open.”

“I can carry the board,” Annabeth said.

“I know.”

“Can you ask?”

He shifted the board toward her. “Do you want me to carry it?”

“Yes. Thanks.”

They followed the central path past the cabins. Leandros walked several paces ahead, then stopped when he saw that neither Annabeth nor Percy had followed him into the narrow track toward the lake.

“This way,” he said.

“Why?” Annabeth asked.

“The boathouse.”

“The path on the map goes left.”

“The path to the boathouse is not on the map yet.”

“The main path is.”

“Do you want to go to the boathouse?”

“I want to see where the path goes.”

“Then you don’t need the main path.”

“I do if I’m drawing the main path.”

He looked back at the route she had drawn. “You could take this shortcut and connect them.”

“That isn’t a shortcut for someone who doesn’t know the ground.”

“It’s not hard to find.”

“Then it can be a secondary trail.”

“Why make it secondary if it gets there faster?”

“Because a map for a newcomer has to work when the person holding it doesn’t already know where they are.”

“You’re making it complicated.”

“I’m making it legible.”

He muttered something too low for her to hear. Percy glanced at Annabeth. He did not say anything.

The path narrowed under the trees. The ground there was soft from old rain, but not muddy. Leandros moved confidently around the low rock, and the path bent west. Annabeth held the paper board against her side. Percy kept pace behind them, not reaching for it.

At the first clearing, Leandros pointed toward the water. “There.”

The boathouse came into view beyond the birches, low against the shore. The path bent toward it and stopped where the ground widened beside the water. Annabeth compared it with the mark on her paper.

“You were right about the direction.”

“I know.”

“You’re still a little west of the boathouse.”

“Because the path bends with the shore.”

“Then the map should show the bend.”

“Now you’re getting it.”

Annabeth took the charcoal back. “Show me where you want the path to meet the boathouse.”

He pointed again, but this time he waited for her to move the paper closer. She studied the shoreline. The water looked calm from there, but the reeds shifted in one direction while the surface farther out moved in another.

“What are you looking at?” she asked.

“Wind on the surface. The current close to shore runs the other way.”

“How can you tell?”

“The reeds are bending toward the west. The floating leaves are going east.”

Annabeth followed the movement. A yellow leaf drifted out from the shore and moved against the wind, slow but steady.

“Would that change the route?”

“If you’re bringing a boat around the shore.”

“Would it change where the path should be?”

“No.”

“Then I’ll mark the shoreline. We can add a note about the current if Chiron wants it.”

“He doesn’t need a note for the water.”

“Why not?”

“People can see it.”

“People who know what to look for.”

He crossed his arms. “It’s obvious.”

“To you.”

He lifted his chin. “I learned it from doing it.”

“And I’m learning it by asking.”

For the first time, he seemed unsure what to do with her answer. She expected him to make it into a contest. Instead he pointed to a low stretch between the path and the shore.

“That part goes under in a hard rain.”

“How often?”

“Depends on the water.”

“Do you know how high it gets?”

“No.”

“Then I’ll mark it as low ground, not a flood warning.”

“I said it goes under.”

“You said you don’t know how high. That’s not enough for a warning.”

He stared at the rise. “I could check next time it rains.”

“You could. If you want to.”

“I might.”

“Then tell Chiron before you go back to the water.”

“I’m allowed to walk by the lake.”

“I didn’t say you weren’t. I said tell him where you’re going.”

“Why?”

“Because camp has boundaries, and because you’re new enough that people won’t know where you are.”

“That sounds like an order.”

“It’s a safety rule.”

“Same thing.”

“Not always.”

“Who decides?”

“Chiron decides the camp boundaries. I can show them to you. You can ask him about the rule.”

He picked at a loose thread on his sleeve. “You could have said that first.”

“I said the map was for a newcomer.”

“You didn’t say the water had boundaries.”

“You’re right. I should have.”

He looked at her as if he had expected her to deny it. “So the map leaves out the parts you don’t know?”

“The map leaves out what I can’t verify.”

“Then it’s incomplete.”

“Yes.”

“Why make it?”

“Because something useful can be incomplete.”

He looked back toward the path. The light had shifted; a strip of cloud covered the sun, and the shore darkened at the edge. He seemed to be listening to it again, but did not announce what it was saying.

Annabeth drew the boathouse, the bend in the path, and the low ground near the shore. She wrote a short note beside the shoreline: “Current may run against wind.” She showed it to him.

“That’s true,” he said.

“It’s also incomplete.”

“Everything’s incomplete.”

“Some things can still help.”

He did not answer. Percy held the board while she added a dotted line to the narrow shortcut. She marked it secondary and wrote “visible path only.” Leandros read the words but did not cross them out.

They walked back along the main path. This time he let Annabeth choose the turns. When they reached the pavilion, she compared the route against the buildings. The old map showed a direct line from the Big House to the lake that cut through a grove and past a patch of soft ground. The new one would mark the path campers actually used.

“Is that the way you came yesterday?” she asked.

“No.”

“Then you walked a different route.”

“I was looking for the cabin.”

“And you found it.”

“Eventually.”

She took the charcoal from the board and drew the route he had taken in a thinner line. “This one ends at Cabin Three?”

“Near it.”

“Do you want to mark it?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because I know where it is now.”

“That’s not the only person who needs the map.”

“I know.”

He said it too quickly. She could see him resisting the next argument, the one about whether a map should be made for people who had already arrived or the ones who would come later.

“Is this going to be posted for everyone?” he asked.

“If Chiron says it’s useful.”

“What if people follow it to the wrong place?”

“Then I correct it.”

“And if you’re not here?”

“Then someone else can use the landmarks.”

“Or they could ask.”

“They can do both.”

“Maps make people think they don’t need to ask.”

“Sometimes they help people know what to ask.”

He looked at the line she had drawn to the boathouse. “You put the trees on it.”

“Some of them.”

“Why?”

“So a person can tell where the path splits.”

“There are more trees than that.”

“There are more cabins than I can draw at full size.”

“So it’s not exact.”

“It’s not a painting.”

He shook his head. “I thought you liked exact.”

“I like knowing what the map is for.”

The dining pavilion filled around them with campers coming in and out. A pair of younger boys passed the table chasing a folded paper glider. One of them clipped the edge of the map board. The stones held the paper in place, but one rolled to the floor.

Leandros caught it before it reached the walkway. He handed it back to Annabeth rather than putting it down wherever he stood.

“Thanks,” she said.

“It would’ve blown away.”

“It might have.”

“Do you always thank people when they stop something from happening?”

“When they help.”

He looked as if he had a question, but the younger boy had returned for the glider. Leandros shifted the board out of the way and gave him space. The child took the paper glider and ran back to his friend.

For a minute, Annabeth watched the pavilion breathe around the map: bowls carried from the serving counter, a pair of campers leaning over a game, Chiron answering a question beside the doorway. None of the marks on her paper showed how many conversations could happen in one place. None needed to.

Leandros tapped the charcoal against his palm. “You should mark that low stretch as off-limits until it dries.”

“Is it off-limits?”

“It gets wet in hard rain.”

“Did Chiron close it?”

“No.”

“Then I can’t mark it off-limits.”

“You can mark it as a hazard.”

“Did someone inspect it?”

“I watched the water go over it.”

“That’s not an inspection.”

“It’s loose.”

“Then tell Chiron. He can check it and decide what to put on the map.”

“Why does everything have to be decided by someone else?”

“Because seeing water cross the path doesn’t make you responsible for closing it.”

“It could make me responsible for telling someone.”

“That part, yes.”

He looked toward the cabins. “You’re making a list now.”

“No. I’m telling you what you can do with the information.”

“Are you going to tell Clarisse?”

“If it’s a Cabin Three issue, yes.”

“Will you tell Clarisse?”

“I’ll tell Chiron about the low ground. If you need Clarisse to know the path, you can show her. If you need her to understand what you’re feeling, ask her yourself.”

“I could tell him.”

“You could.”

“Then I will.”

He held the charcoal still. “Does Clarisse always talk to people like they’re already in trouble?”

Annabeth did not look toward Percy. “You can ask Clarisse.”

“I’m asking you.”

“I’m not going to explain your sister for you.”

“She’s your friend.”

“She’s also her own person.”

Percy shifted the board in his hands. “Clarisse is direct, but she cares about keeping people safe. She’s the one who remembers every loose rail and fence and—”

“Stop,” Annabeth said.

Percy closed his mouth.

“I’m not defending him,” he said after a moment.

“I know. You’re explaining why he shouldn’t take it personally before he decides what it meant. That still smooths it over.”

“I thought it might help him understand her.”

“If she wants him to understand, she can explain it. Don’t turn what she said into a lesson for him.”

Leandros’s gaze moved between them. “I didn’t ask you to defend me.”

“I know,” Annabeth said. “I’m not defending either of you. I’m saying you’re both here. You can speak to each other directly.”

“I asked a question.”

“And I answered it.”

He gave her a thin, skeptical smile. “That’s a bad map.”

“To get you from one place to another?” she said. “It’s enough for today.”

“It might be a good first one.”

---

They took the map to the Big House, where Chiron could check the marked paths against the camp’s current boundaries. Annabeth held the paper by the top corners. Percy carried the board. The wind at the pavilion tried to lift one edge, but the stones kept it in place until they rolled it back up.

Chiron examined the path to the boathouse and the note about the shore current. “You have distinguished what you know from what needs checking.”

“I still need to confirm the low ground after rain,” Annabeth said.

“And the shortcut near the hill?”

“I marked it as secondary. It’s visible if you already know the turn.”

“Good. We can walk it together later. Do not publish a route as safe unless we have checked it.”

Leandros stood a few feet away, arms folded. “A map that says nothing about whether the ground holds isn’t much use.”

“Then we should find out whether it holds,” Chiron said. “You can help me check it after the rain. Until then, Annabeth has marked what she has seen.”

Leandros looked at Annabeth. “You make a lot of rules for a map.”

“I’m trying to make it useful.”

“You make it useful for people who don’t know anything.”

“That’s who it’s for.”

“People can ask.”

“Some people don’t know what to ask.”

He dropped his folded arms. “You’re saying they don’t know enough to come here.”

“No. I’m saying a map can help them find the infirmary without having to ask someone they’ve never met.”

“Then the map is more important than the person.”

“No.”

“Seems like it.”

Annabeth held the rolled paper against her side. “You can disagree with the map.”

“I do.”

“Then tell Chiron which part needs checking.”

Leandros glanced at Chiron, who waited without rescuing either side. “I said the ground goes under.”

“Then we’ll check it,” Chiron said. “Thank you for reporting it.”

The reply took some wind out of Leandros. He nodded, almost like he had expected a different answer.

“Will you help?” Chiron asked.

“I’ll see.”

“That is an answer.”

Leandros left without taking the paper. Annabeth watched him go. He had not apologized for calling her work incomplete, but he had given Chiron the information. That was something. It was not everything.

Percy put the board on the bench. “You did well.”

She turned to him. “Do you mean the map?”

“I meant both.”

“What does that mean?”

“I thought you handled it.”

“I asked about the route. I didn’t ask you to evaluate me.”

He withdrew his hand from the board. “Right.”

“You did the same thing with the stool this morning.”

“I thought I was telling you I believed in you.”

“I know. I heard you.” She tried to keep the edge out of her voice and didn’t quite manage. “You keep saying you know what I mean. You keep answering before I’ve asked for the answer.”

He looked down at his hands. “I’m sorry.”

“Because?”

He took a breath. “Because I tried to explain Clarisse to Leandros when she wasn’t there. And when you stopped me, I was more focused on proving I wasn’t defending him than hearing what you were telling me.”

Annabeth let the rolled map rest on the bench. He had not waited for her to fill in his words. That mattered.

“And why were you explaining Clarisse?” she asked.

“Because I wanted him to understand the rules weren’t there to keep him out. Because I thought if I gave him a reason for how she talked, he wouldn’t treat it like a fight.”

“You made the reason up.”

“I guessed.”

“You guessed what Clarisse meant.”

“I’ve seen her work. I thought I knew.”

“You know some things about her. You don’t know what she wants to explain to him.”

He nodded. “That’s true.”

“And you were trying to make him comfortable before he’d decided whether he was willing to listen.”

“I was.”

“Why?”

“Because he’s new. He arrived with a bag and a cut hand and no one he knows. I saw him waiting for us to tell him what kind of family this was. I wanted to make it easier.”

“You explained Clarisse as if she were a difficult part of the camp he needed instructions for.”

“I wasn’t saying she was wrong.”

“You made what she said sound like a misunderstanding.”

“Maybe I thought it was.”

“Maybe it was. But she gets to decide what she meant. You made the choice for her before she could answer.”

He looked at the map paper. “I heard you tell him to ask Clarisse.”

“After you started.”

“I stopped.”

“You did.”

She could have left it there. The acknowledgment did not erase what had happened, but it mattered that he had stopped when she asked. Annabeth knew she could make a correction sharp enough that the other person heard only the cut. “I’m still annoyed,” she said. “I’m also glad you stopped.”

“I can hold both.”

“Can you?”

“I think so.”

“I don’t always make it easy to know what I want. Sometimes I tell you not to speak for me after I’ve already got angry that you didn’t notice.”

“I don’t think that’s what happened today.”

“It wasn’t all of it. But I know I can do that.”

He glanced at her. “You don’t have to make every correction about what you did wrong too.”

“I’m not balancing the list. I’m telling you I know I can be hard to answer when I’m hurt.”

“I can tell you when I don’t know what you want.”

“You can. And I can tell you without making you guess.”

They stood beside the bench, close enough for their sleeves to brush. The map rolled between them, lines hidden inside the paper.

“You can want that,” she said again. “You can want to make it easier. That doesn’t mean you get to choose what somebody else has to hear.”

“I can want it and still not do it?”

“Yes.”

“I can want it and still not do it?”

“Yes.”

He gave a small, rueful nod. “I’m still figuring that part out.”

“So am I.”

“I don’t want you to think I’m trying to make you small.”

“I don’t.”

“I don’t want you to think I see you as someone who needs a translator.”

“I don’t think that either.”

“What do you think?”

“I think you’re afraid people will get hurt if you stop arranging the conversation.”

He looked toward the path outside the Big House. “Sometimes people do get hurt.”

“Sometimes they do. And sometimes they get to say what happened to them without you managing the answer.”

“I know that in theory.”

“You knew it this morning.”

“I said I knew it. I didn’t say I understood it.”

The admission came without a joke. Annabeth felt her anger ease, not disappear.

“I don’t want you to stop worrying,” she said. “I don’t want you to stop noticing when someone is uncomfortable. I want you to ask whether they want help before you speak for them.”

“And when you say no?”

“Then I want you to let me handle it.”

“Okay.”

“You don’t need to agree with everything I do.”

“I don’t.”

“Good.”

“You can still be wrong.”

“So can you.”

“I know.”

He held her eyes. “I am sorry I tried to smooth it over. It wasn’t mine to do.”

She believed he meant it. She did not have to pretend she liked what he had done in order to forgive the apology. She didn’t say she forgave him; there was nothing to punish him with. He had tried to help and had got in the way. They could talk about that without making their relationship another thing he had to fix.

“I’m glad you said that,” she said.

He let his shoulders settle. “Do you want to keep walking the map with me?”

“Not right now.”

“All right.”

“Can you carry this back to Cabin One?”

“Yeah.”

“Don’t make it mean that I’m asking you to lead.”

“I won’t.”

She put the board in his hands. He took it with both hands, and they walked away from the Big House side by side.

---

The map stayed rolled until after lunch. Annabeth had not wanted to hang it before Chiron checked the path, and Chiron had gone to the north edge to meet with two campers about the fence. She left the board on the table in Cabin One, then took the long route to Cabin Three after Percy asked whether Clarisse would want to see it.

“Ask her,” Annabeth said.

“I was going to.”

“Then go.”

“I didn’t say you had to come.”

“You asked whether she’d want to see it.”

“I was asking your opinion.”

“Then you have it.”

He accepted that. He walked beside her, leaving the rolled map under her arm.

Clarisse sat at the Cabin Three table with a whetstone and a dull practice blade. Silena sorted a basket of clean cloths near the open window. Leandros sat on the cot with his duffel between his feet. He looked up when Percy and Annabeth came in.

“Chiron wants to see the map before it goes on the wall,” Annabeth said. “I marked the boathouse route and the low ground. Leandros showed me where the path turns.”

Clarisse set down the blade. “Did Chiron check the shore?”

“It’s the low ground, not the shoreline,” Leandros said.

“I asked Annabeth.”

“He said it went under in hard rain,” Annabeth said. “I marked it as low ground until Chiron can check.”

“Good.”

Leandros’s expression tightened. “You believe her.”

“I believe she wrote down what you told her.”

“I told her it was unsafe.”

“You told her water crosses it in hard rain,” Annabeth said. “You didn’t know how high it gets.”

“That’s unsafe.”

“It can be. Chiron will check.”

“You could have marked it that way.”

“I could have, if I knew.”

Clarisse picked up the practice blade again. “Did you want to see the map?”

“I’ve seen it.”

“Then why are you looking at it?”

“I’m trying to understand why everyone needs a label before doing anything.”

“Because labels can tell people what to expect.”

“Or make them afraid to go somewhere.”

“Some places are worth being careful around.”

“What if you don’t know whether they are?”

“Then you say that.”

He leaned back against the wall. “You and Annabeth agree on everything?”

“No.”

“Could have fooled me.”

“We’re not agreeing on everything,” Annabeth said. “We’re agreeing that Chiron should check the ground before we mark it closed.”

Leandros’s attention shifted to her. “Is that what you think I asked?”

“I don’t know what you meant.”

“You think I wanted you to put a warning up because I wanted to decide something.”

“I think you were certain before you had all the information.”

“Maybe I had enough.”

“Maybe.”

“Why didn’t you mark it?”

“Because it’s not my job to decide what the camp should close. I can show what I’ve seen.”

“You can draw a map but you can’t draw a warning.”

“I can draw a warning when I know it’s right.”

He rubbed his palm over one knee. “What if it rains before Chiron checks?”

“Then you stay on the path.”

“That’s your answer to everything?”

“No. It’s the answer to this.”

Clarisse’s whetstone scraped once along the blade. “You asked about the map. You got the answer.”

“I didn’t ask you.”

“You asked if I always talk to people like they’re in trouble.”

Leandros looked at her. “I asked Annabeth.”

“And she said to ask me.”

“I didn’t want to start another fight.”

“Then don’t start one.”

“That’s what I mean. You hear everything like a challenge.”

Clarisse laid the blade across her knees. She did not raise her voice. “You asked me a question yesterday and I answered it. You told me you didn’t take orders from girls. I told you that doesn’t make you leader. I didn’t say you had to like me.”

“You don’t give me much of a chance.”

“You haven’t given me much reason to.”

Silena stopped folding the cloth, but she did not step closer. Annabeth kept the map rolled beneath her arm. Percy stayed beside her, quiet.

Leandros stood. His knees hit the edge of the cot. “I apologized.”

“You said you knew what you did.”

“I said I used it because I didn’t like you telling me no.”

“Then you asked me to trust you?”

“No.”

“Good.”

He looked at the floor, then at Clarisse. “Do you want me to keep apologizing?”

“I want you to stop making the same choice.”

“What if I make another one?”

“Then own that one.”

He seemed to wait for more. Clarisse returned to the blade and drew the stone down it again.

“I don’t know what you want from me,” he said.

“Neither do I.”

“You said I was family.”

“I did.”

“Then what does that mean?”

“It means you’re not a guest.”

“Yesterday you said family didn’t mean I had authority.”

“It doesn’t.”

“Then what do I get?”

Annabeth felt the question more sharply than he had asked it. It was not only about rules. It was about whether “family” was a place, or a word said to someone who had already arrived.

Clarisse paused with the stone in her hand. “You get a bed tonight. You get a place at the table. You get to tell us what you need. You get to say when something’s wrong.”

“Do I get to be heard?”

“If you say it like you expect us to answer, yes.”

“I don’t know what that means.”

“Ask without deciding what we’re going to say.”

He gave a laugh that held no humor. “You’ve all got the same advice.”

“Maybe you keep asking us the same thing.”

“I’m asking what I’m supposed to do.”

“You’re not supposed to do anything.”

“That’s useless.”

“It’s not a test.”

“You keep setting boundaries like there’s a correct answer.”

“There are correct answers to some things. Don’t take somebody’s gear. Don’t tell people they can’t give you directions because they’re girls. Don’t grab a balanced ladder. But whether we trust you, whether you want to stay here, whether you like us, those aren’t questions with one answer.”

He stared at her. “Do you want me here?”

“I don’t know you well enough to know what I want yet.”

“Would it matter if I left?”

“Yes,” Silena said before Clarisse could speak. “You’re our brother. It would matter. That doesn’t mean we can make you stay.”

Leandros turned to Silena. “You barely know me.”

“That doesn’t mean it wouldn’t matter.”

The answer seemed to land in him. His shoulders lowered a little, then came back up.

“And if I stay?” he asked.

“Then you keep learning where things go,” Clarisse said. “The same way the rest of us did.”

“Did you have someone showing you?”

“Sometimes.”

“Did you ask?”

“Sometimes.”

“Did it work?”

“Not always.”

“I don’t want to be the person who keeps asking and never gets an answer.”

“Then ask the person who knows.”

He glanced toward Annabeth. “That’s what I did.”

“About the camp map,” she said. “Not about Clarisse.”

“I didn’t know whether she’d answer.”

Clarisse looked up. “I’m answering now.”

He took a breath. “Are you still angry about what I said?”

“Yes.”

“Do you think I meant it?”

“I think you meant it when you said it.”

“That’s not the same as what I believe.”

“No,” Clarisse said. “But it’s what I have to work with.”

“I don’t think girls shouldn’t give orders.”

“You said you didn’t take them.”

“I was mad.”

“That doesn’t make it not yours.”

He looked at her, and then at his hands. He did not tell her she was wrong. He did not ask whether she forgave him. He did not apologize again.

Annabeth unrolled the map and spread it across the table. “Chiron will check the low ground. Until he does, this stays a draft.”

Leandros leaned over the paper. “You’ve drawn the path narrower than it is.”

“Where?”

“Here, beside the boathouse.”

“Show me.”

He pointed to the path on the map rather than reaching for the charcoal. “There’s room for two people. It widens before the turn.”

“Then I’ll add that.”

“Will you write my name next to the current note?”

“Why?”

“So people know who showed you.”

Annabeth considered that. “Would you rather I put your name, or say the shoreline needs checking?”

“Both.”

“Why?”

“Because I don’t want the information getting separated from where it came from.”

That was sensible. “I can write ‘Leandros observed the current moving against the wind; verify on arrival.’”

“Don’t say ‘observed.’ I read it.”

“Then we should use the word that means that without making it sound permanent.”

He looked at the note again. “Current can change.”

“Exactly.”

She wrote: “Leandros read the current against the wind on Wednesday; confirm at the shore.” The date in the sentence was a weekday, not a calendar mark. She left the low-ground note separate.

“Why put when you saw it?” he asked.

“So someone knows how old the information is.”

“That’s useful.”

“It’s also a map.”

Silena rose to put the cloth basket away. “When Chiron says you can hang it, people will use it.”

“If they want to,” Annabeth said.

Leandros tapped the table once with his knuckle. “If you hang it, put it where people can reach it.”

“I was planning to.”

“Good.”

Clarisse lifted the practice blade. “You going to tell me anything else?”

He looked at her, then glanced down at the map. “I think the route to the boathouse needs another marker.”

“Tell Chiron.”

“I can tell him.”

“Then do.”

He nodded. It was a little stiff, but he did not argue about who owned the decision.

When Annabeth rolled the map again, Leandros stepped back to clear the table. He had not asked her to explain Clarisse. He had not answered the whole family question either. She could not tell whether he had heard the difference or whether he had decided that one conversation was enough. Either way, it was not a reason to congratulate him.

Percy held the board while she laid the map across it. His eyes flicked to hers, checking whether she wanted him to speak.

She gave him a small nod. Not now. He understood.

---

The old map remained on the pavilion wall until Chiron had checked Annabeth’s draft. By late afternoon, he had returned from the north side with damp cuffs and had written two notes on its edge: one low path needed another look after rain; the route to the boathouse could be shown as a footpath, but not as a safe place to launch a boat.

“Can we put it up?” Annabeth asked.

“As a draft,” Chiron said. “Mark that it is under review.”

“People won’t know what that means.”

“They will if you write it in plain words.”

She printed: “Working map. Ask Chiron before using the shore path in heavy rain.” The words felt too large for the blank margin. She added, “Main paths checked; shore edge needs review.”

Leandros stood at the other end of the table, reading the note. “That’s too much writing.”

“It’s information.”

“People won’t read it.”

“Then they should ask.”

He looked at her. “I thought maps were supposed to keep people from asking.”

“Maps can help people ask better questions.”

“Then you’re making signs, not a map.”

“Maybe the camp needs both.”

Chiron gave Annabeth permission to take the draft to the wall. She pinned it over the old map, leaving enough space to see the lower corners. The draft bent slightly where the paper had been rolled. Percy held the edge while she fastened the last pin. She made sure he could let go before she nodded.

“Good?” he asked.

“Good.”

He stepped back. “It’s easier to read than the old one.”

“It’s larger.”

“That too.”

Leandros remained at the table. “You put the infirmary next to the Big House.”

“Because it is.”

“The path looks like it goes behind the cabins.”

“That’s the path.”

“Then it’s wrong.”

“Which part?”

“The line makes it look like the path goes straight.”

“Doesn’t it?”

“No. It bends.”

“How far?”

“Not much.”

“Then I’ll curve it.”

“Do you need to curve every path?”

“I need the map to match what people can see.”

He looked from the page to the route outside. “Could you mark the low ground in red?”

“Chiron hasn’t checked it.”

“You already wrote a warning.”

“I wrote that it needs review.”

“Then red would make it clearer.”

“It might make it look closed.”

“Maybe it should be.”

“Maybe. We don’t know yet.”

He rested one finger beside the note, not on the paper. “You could put an X.”

“That means closed.”

“What if you put a small one?”

“Then it means the same thing, just smaller.”

Chiron moved to the map, reading the notes. “For now, use a dotted line where we have not checked the path. That tells people it is uncertain.”

Annabeth drew the dotted line. “Like this?”

“Yes.”

Leandros leaned over the map. “That looks like it ends.”

“It does until the path has been checked.”

“But if people want to go there?”

“They can ask.”

He looked at her, then glanced at the sign. He did not repeat that people could ask. He said, “Then add who to ask.”

“Chiron?”

“Or Beckendorf, if it’s a repair.”

Chiron looked at him. “That is a useful distinction. We can add both.”

Annabeth wrote beneath the map title: “For route questions, ask Chiron. For repairs, ask Beckendorf.” She did not include anyone’s name beside the shore note.

“What about you?” Leandros said.

“What about me?”

“You drew it.”

“I’m not the one who decides whether a path is safe.”

“You know where the paths go.”

“I know the paths I’ve walked.”

“Then someone can ask you.”

“If I’m there.”

He looked back at the map. “You keep leaving yourself off.”

“I’m not a landmark.”

The younger campers gathered near the pavilion wall to see what she had drawn. One, a small girl with a red scarf, pointed to a line that led toward the infirmary.

“Does this go there?”

“Yes,” Annabeth said.

“Can I take it with my friend?”

“Yes. Stay on the marked path and go with someone.”

Leandros stepped forward. “You can go the short way by the water.”

Annabeth looked at the map. The low ground was marked as dotted, and the direct path to the infirmary was a solid line. “That path hasn’t been checked.”

“It’s dry now.”

“It might rain before they come back.”

“It won’t.”

The girl in the scarf stared at them. Her friend moved closer to her shoulder.

Annabeth turned to Leandros. “You don’t know that.”

He opened his mouth, then closed it. His hand had lifted toward the map. He lowered it.

“The main path is checked,” he said to the girl. “Use that one.”

The girl took her friend’s hand. “Okay.”

They followed the solid line past the table, then turned to look at the wall once more. Annabeth did not tell them not to be afraid. There was nothing to be afraid of. They had a clear path.

Leandros watched them go. “You made the map for them.”

“I made it for anyone who needs it.”

“They could have asked.”

“They did.”

He took in the empty stretch between the table and the pavilion wall. “They asked because they didn’t know.”

“Yes.”

He picked up the charcoal tin and looked to Annabeth before opening it. “Can I write the shore note in larger letters?”

“Not until Chiron says what it should say.”

“Can I make a suggestion?”

“Yes. On the scrap paper.”

She handed him a strip. He wrote three words: “Ask before you go.”

Annabeth read them. “That could sound like nobody is allowed to walk there.”

“Then change it.”

“What do you mean?”

“Add who they should ask. Or when.”

“Good.”

He turned the paper toward her. “You can write it.”

“It’s your suggestion.”

“But it’s your map.”

“Camp’s map.”

“It’s yours to make.”

“It isn’t mine to control after it’s on the wall.”

He held the scrap between his hands. “I think people should know before they go near water.”

“That’s a useful idea.”

“Then put it on the map.”

“I’ll ask Chiron.”

“Why does he have to decide every word?”

“He’s responsible for where campers can go.”

“I’m a camper.”

“You’re new here.”

“That doesn’t mean I don’t know anything.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You keep saying to ask Chiron.”

“Because it is his job to know the camp rules. It’s your job to tell him what you’ve seen.”

He held the paper tightly enough that it bent. “I know what water does.”

“You know some of what it does. You said the current changes. You said the low ground goes under in hard rain, but you don’t know how high. You can be right about what you see and still not know what the camp should do.”

He stared at the small strip of paper. “That sounds like the same thing.”

“It isn’t.”

“Then what’s the difference?”

“Knowing a thing happened is different from deciding what everyone should do about it.”

He looked toward the map, where the dotted route ended just short of the shore. “So you can be right and not get to decide.”

“Yes.”

“That’s a bad rule.”

“Maybe.”

“Does Chiron get to decide because he’s older?”

“Because he’s responsible for the camp.”

“Who made him responsible?”

“Everyone who agreed to follow him.”

“And if they were wrong?”

“Then they can tell him.”

Leandros glanced at the bunk where his duffel rested. “Do you always believe people will listen?”

“No.”

“Then why do you draw a map?”

“Because someone might.”

He turned the strip of paper over and smoothed it. “Could this say ‘Check with Chiron before using the shore path in rain’?”

“That’s clearer.”

“Then it’s my suggestion.”

“I’ll say so.”

He looked at her as if waiting for the name beside the map. “You can write Leandros under it.”

“On the draft, I’ll note who suggested it. Chiron decides if it stays.”

He handed her the paper. “Fine.”

“Thank you.”

He blinked at the word but did not answer.

Chiron took the strip and read it. “That is more precise. I’ll add it when we check the shore.”

The map stayed on the wall, its central paths marked solid and the uncertain ground dotted. Annabeth folded the scrap with the suggestion and tucked it inside the charcoal tin. It was not a route yet. It was an instruction waiting to be checked.

Leandros returned the tin to her instead of leaving it beside the map. “You’re going to keep it?”

“Yes.”

“Don’t lose it.”

“I won’t.”

He studied the map once more. “The shortcut near the hill should still be on it.”

“It is.”

“People will think it’s hard to find.”

“It is.”

“It’s not that hard.”

“For you.”

He pressed his lips together. “You say that a lot.”

“Because you know more about a few things than most people here. That doesn’t mean you know what every other person needs.”

He shifted his gaze to the younger campers, now returning from the infirmary path with a small bundle of cloth. “They made it back.”

“They did.”

“Could have used the shortcut.”

“They used the route they could follow.”

He nodded once. It was not agreement exactly. It was the first time he had let her answer sit without asking for a different one.

Annabeth rolled the map only after Chiron said it could stay on the wall until supper. She left the dotted line visible. If the wind shifted and pulled at the corners, she would fasten them again.

Percy came beside her. “Do you want help with the pin?”

“Yes.”

He held the bottom edge while she pressed the paper flat. They did not need to talk about the earlier argument again. He had listened when she asked him to stop, and she had asked when she wanted him beside her.

Their hands met briefly on the edge of the paper. When the pin held, Percy stepped away, leaving the map hers to finish.

---

That evening, the map stayed up through the supper hour. Chiron had added a small note by the shore path: “Check with Chiron before using in heavy rain.” Annabeth wanted to argue that it sounded like a rule, not a route note. Then she read the map as someone who had never been to the camp and understood the point.

A younger camper paused in front of it with a bowl in both hands. She traced the line from the cabins to the infirmary and back to the dining pavilion.

“Is the Big House that building?” she asked Annabeth.

“Yes.”

“And this is the way to Cabin Three?”

“Yes. Follow the main path until the split.”

The camper looked at the paper, then at the path beyond the open side of the pavilion. “I can find it.”

“You can.”

She turned toward the cabins, bowl still steady in her hands. She glanced back once at the map, then kept going.

Percy was at the serving counter, waiting for the last tray to come out. He had saved Annabeth a place near the end of the table. When she sat down, he shifted the bowl toward her and asked, “Do you want the bread?”

“Yes.”

He passed it without comment. Their shoulders touched under the edge of the table. Annabeth let them stay there.

Leandros arrived with Clarisse and Silena a little later. He had a folded scrap of paper in one hand. He stopped in front of the map and read the note Chiron had added.

“You did not write who checked the path,” he said.

“Chiron checked the map, not the shore,” Annabeth said. “The shore is still dotted.”

“So he has not checked it.”

“That’s right.”

“He told people to ask him before going there.”

“He told them what to do in heavy rain.”

“He hasn’t seen it.”

“He can still tell people what the camp rules are.”

Leandros turned the paper over. “That’s the difference, then?”

“Between what?”

“Knowing the rule and knowing the place.”

“Sometimes.”

He studied her, then stepped aside as Clarisse approached the map. She had a plate in one hand and a small stack of repair notes in the other.

“You’re in front of it,” she said.

Leandros moved without comment.

Clarisse read the route to the boathouse. “This turn is too close to the hill.”

“Annabeth marked it.”

“I’m asking her.”

“The old map shows the north path straight,” Annabeth said. “This is the route people use now.”

“It’s right,” Clarisse said. “It just needs the bend.”

Annabeth picked up the charcoal tin from the table. “Can you show me?”

Clarisse set her plate down and pointed to the map. She did not touch the paper. “Here. The path goes past the split, then bends around the tree line.”

“Which tree line?”

“The one with the split trunk.”

Annabeth drew a small mark. “This one?”

“Yes.”

“Good. I’ll add it.”

Leandros leaned nearer, then stopped himself before he reached across the map. “There’s another way.”

Clarisse looked at him. “Where?”

“From the low ground.”

“It’s not checked.”

“I know.”

“Then it’s not a route yet.”

“Would you show me the way from here?”

Clarisse glanced down at the work list. “Not tonight.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Ask me tomorrow.”

“You don’t know?”

“I don’t know what the morning will look like.”

“You could say you don’t want to.”

“I could.”

“Do you?”

“Not tonight.”

The words did not land like a rejection of his existence. They were only the answer to a question. Annabeth saw Leandros take that in. His shoulders tensed, and then he nodded.

“All right.”

Clarisse’s eyes stayed on him. “If you want to know where the boathouse is, follow the solid line. If you want to check the low ground, ask Chiron. If you want me to walk it with you, ask me when I’m not carrying a repair list.”

“I can do that.”

“Good.”

Leandros folded the paper again. “I thought if you were going to say no, you’d say it before I asked.”

“I did.”

“You said not tonight.”

“That’s a no for tonight.”

“Not for tomorrow.”

“I don’t know about tomorrow.”

He looked at the list in her hand. “What’s on it?”

“Things that need fixing.”

“Could I see?”

“Why?”

“Because I want to know what’s left.”

Clarisse considered him. “You can read it.”

He took the paper only after she offered it. His eyes moved down the lines: fence inspection, awning bracket, stool glue, loose rail, frost check. He stopped at the awning.

“The bracket came in?”

“Beckendorf measured it. It’s waiting on the right weather.”

“Can I help when you put it on?”

“Ask Beckendorf.”

“I’m asking you.”

“Then you can ask him tomorrow.”

He returned the list. “You could just tell me.”

“I could. I’m not the one who knows whether you can help with the bracket.”

Leandros’s mouth tightened. He did not say that he knew metal. He did not say he had fixed boats with his mother. He looked back at the map instead.

“Where does this put the fence?” he asked.

“North side.”

“You should draw it.”

“Maybe.”

“Then I could see where the work is.”

“Maybe,” Clarisse repeated. “I’ll ask Chiron whether he wants all the repair places on it.”

“Why does he decide everything?”

“Because he’s responsible for the camp.”

“Everyone says that.”

“Because it’s true.”

“What if he makes the wrong decision?”

“Then we tell him.”

“And if he doesn’t listen?”

“Then we keep telling him until he does, or we find someone else who can fix it.”

He considered that. “You actually do that?”

“Yes.”

“Has it worked?”

“Sometimes.”

“Is that enough?”

“Sometimes.”

Leandros shifted his weight. “You don’t make it sound easy.”

“It isn’t.”

“Maybe that’s why you talk like every question is a fight.”

“Maybe I’m tired of answering the same one.”

“I haven’t asked the same one.”

“You have. You keep asking whether being family means you can decide for us.”

“I didn’t ask that.”

“You asked what you get.”

“I was asking if I belong.”

Clarisse glanced at the folded list. “Those aren’t the same question.”

“I thought they were.”

“They aren’t.”

For a few seconds, the pavilion filled in the silence for them: plates clinked, someone laughed near the serving counter, and the wind moved through the open wall. No one came over to help them finish the conversation. No one needed to.

Leandros finally said, “I don’t know how to ask to belong.”

“You don’t have to ask that in one sentence.”

“What do I do instead?”

“Show up. Ask the next question. Let us answer.”

“And if you don’t?”

“Then you ask what happened.”

“What if I don’t like the answer?”

“You can dislike it.”

He watched her face for a long moment. “You’re still angry.”

“Yes.”

“I thought maybe you’d stopped.”

“No.”

“Because of the apology?”

“Because it’s been one day.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

He did not answer. The words could have become a new argument. He folded the paper again, carefully, keeping the edges aligned.

“Will you show me tomorrow?” he asked.

“I don’t know.”

He nodded. “I’ll ask.”

“That’s all.”

Clarisse picked up her plate. “I’m eating.”

Leandros stepped away from the wall. He did not follow her to the table until she had chosen a seat. Annabeth watched him take the place opposite Silena, the one he had used the night before. He did not move Percy’s bowl or the bread basket. He waited while Silena sat down, then passed the basket when she asked.

The map behind them marked a route to the boathouse and a path to the infirmary. It did not mark who would sit beside whom, or how many evenings a new brother had to stay before someone stopped noticing where he put his hands.

Annabeth knew the map could not answer that. She had drawn it anyway.

When supper ended, she and Percy carried the map board back to the wall. The paper had lifted at one corner. Percy held it while she pressed the pin into place, and she gave him a small nod when the edge lay flat.

“Do you want to walk back together?” he asked.

“Yes.”

He offered his hand. She took it.

The paths to Cabin One were marked in solid lines. They walked them side by side.

At the fork below the hill, Percy glanced toward the wider path and then at her. He did not turn either way.

“Which route?” he asked.

“The short one.”

“It has more roots.”

“I know.”

“Do you want my arm?”

“Do you want me to take it?”

“I’m asking.”

She considered him. “Not this time.”

“All right.”

They took the narrower path. Annabeth had walked it enough to know where the roots lifted through the soil. Percy matched her pace, board tucked under one arm. When she slowed at the steep part, he slowed with her; when she reached the flat ground, he kept beside her.

At the Cabin One steps, she looked back toward the pavilion. The map was too far away to see the words. It showed where the path split and where the solid line went. It did not show why she had chosen one route over another. Percy had asked, and she had answered. That was enough for now.
