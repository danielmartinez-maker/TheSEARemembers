## Chapter 95: Annabeth Sick

The fever came on during sword practice and nobody noticed at first because Annabeth Grace was too stubborn to admit she was sick.

It was a bright late morning at Camp Half-Blood. The strawberry fields were heavy. The lake glittered. In the arena, the air rang with wood on wood and Clarisse shouting cadence.

Percy Jackson knew something was wrong by the third round.

He was sparring with Annabeth, as he always did on Tuesdays, because Tuesdays were theirs. No powers, no tricks, just dull practice blades and footwork. Annabeth was usually faster than him in the first minute. Her mind worked three moves ahead, and her feet knew it. She would tap his wrist, tap his knee, grin that sharp grin, and say, You telegraph with your left shoulder, Seaweed Brain.

That morning she was slow. Not dramatically slow. Just half a beat off. Her parry came late. Her lunge fell short. Sweat stuck her blonde curls to her temples in a way that had nothing to do with the heat, and there were two bright spots of color high on her cheeks that Percy had learned to read the way sailors read clouds.

“You okay?” he asked, lowering his sword.

Annabeth scowled, which was answer enough that she was not. “I’m fine. Again.”

“You missed a block you never miss.”

“I blinked.”

“You never blink.”

Behind them, Thalia Grace looked up from where she was re-lacing her combat boot on the bench. Thalia missed nothing when it came to her little sister. She had that older-sister radar, honed over years of listening for coughs in the night. Her blue eyes narrowed. Her nose wrinkled.

“Hey, Wise Girl,” Thalia said. “Come here a sec.”

“I’m in the middle of kicking Percy’s butt,” Annabeth said, but her voice sounded thin.

Thalia stood up, all tall and black-haired and crackling with latent static even on a clear day, and walked over and put the back of her hand against Annabeth’s forehead the way their mother had taught her to do a lifetime ago. Just like that. No asking.

Thalia’s expression changed.

“Oh, baby,” she said softly. “You’re burning up.”

Annabeth batted her hand away. “I’m warm from sparring. Everyone’s warm.”

“No,” Percy said. He knew what warm-from-sparring felt like, and he knew what this was. He stepped closer and touched her cheek with his knuckles, gentle. Her skin was hot and dry under the sweat. Her gray eyes were too bright, glassy at the edges. “Annie, you’re hot.”

“I said I’m fine,” Annabeth muttered, but she swayed just slightly when she said it, and Percy’s hand shot out to steady her elbow. Thalia was already there on the other side.

Clarisse, who had been watching from the next ring with her helmet under her arm, frowned and came over. “Grace? You look like crap.”

“Thanks,” Annabeth said. “Very helpful.”

“I mean it nicely,” Clarisse said, which for Clarisse was true. She pressed her own wrist to Annabeth’s forehead, professional as any medic, and whistled. “Yeah. That’s a fever. No more swords. Infirmary. Now.”

Annabeth opened her mouth to argue, and the world tilted. Percy felt it through her arm before he saw it, that sudden give in her knees. He caught her fully around the waist as her practice sword clattered to the packed dirt.

“Whoa, whoa, I got you,” he said.

Thalia was already crouching to pick up the fallen sword. Her face had gone pale under her tan. “Okay. That’s it. No arguments. Will. We need Will.”

Silena, who had been braiding a younger Aphrodite kid’s hair on the sidelines, was already running for the infirmary, her skirt flying. Within minutes Will Solace arrived at a jog with his medical bag, his blond hair rumpled, his manner calm in the way that made even Ares kids listen.

He took one look at Annabeth leaning heavily against Percy, her eyes half-closed, her breathing quick and shallow, and nodded toward the shade.

“Bench,” he said. “Let me see her.”

---

Percy sat with Annabeth on the bench while Will worked, and tried not to hover too obviously and failed completely.

Will was quick and thorough. Thermometer under the tongue. Fingers on the pulse at wrist and throat. Eyes, ears, throat with the little light. Hands pressing gently on lymph nodes. Questions asked in a low, steady voice.

“When did this start, Annabeth?”

“This morning,” Annabeth mumbled. “I woke up hot. I thought it was the cabin. Zeus cabin gets stuffy.”

“Headache?”

“A little.”

“Sore throat? Cough? Stomach?”

“No. Just… achy. And cold, then hot. My teeth were chattering at breakfast and then I was sweating through my shirt.”

Will glanced at Thalia. “She push herself yesterday?”

Thalia snorted. “When does she not? She was up late working on the border calculations after the storm. She grounded two lightning strikes with me to bleed off the ridge. She didn’t eat enough dinner. Silena had to remind her to drink water twice.”

“I drank water,” Annabeth protested weakly.

“You drank half a glass and called it hydration,” Percy said. He was still holding her hand. He had not let go since she swayed. Her fingers were hot in his. “You also skipped lunch yesterday to fix the ballista.”

Annabeth gave him a look that would have been withering if her eyes had been able to focus properly. “Traitor.”

“Boyfriend,” Percy corrected. “It’s my job to rat you out when you’re sick.”

Will sat back on his heels. “It’s fever. Pretty high. A hundred and three point two. No obvious infection in ears or throat. My guess is a mix — exhaustion, dehydration, divine overload from channeling too much sky current, plus something viral going around the Hermes cabin. Three of them were in this morning with the same glassy eyes.”

“Is she going to be okay?” Thalia asked. Her voice was tight. Older sisters tried to sound casual and never managed it.

Will gave her a reassuring smile. “Yeah. She’s strong. But she needs rest, fluids, cool, and watching. Fevers like this in demigods, especially sky kids, can spike fast. And when sky kids spike, their powers get… leaky.”

As if to prove his point, Annabeth shivered hard all at once, and a faint crackle lifted the fine hairs on her arms. A tiny blue spark snapped between her fingertips and Percy’s knuckle.

Percy flinched but did not let go. “Ow.”

“Sorry,” Annabeth whispered. Tears stood in her eyes, humiliated. “Sorry, Percy, I didn’t mean —”

“Hey, no,” Percy said immediately, squeezing her hand tighter. “It’s okay. Barely felt it. You’ve shocked me worse with static from the trampoline.”

Will stood and started packing up. “Let’s get her to Cabin Three. It’s cooler down by the water, and the sea air will help her breathe. I want cold compresses, sips of water every fifteen minutes, and someone with her at all times until the fever breaks. If it goes over a hundred and four, or if she starts vomiting or gets confused, come get me immediately. I’ll check back in two hours.”

“I’ll take her,” Percy said at once.

“I’ll take her,” Thalia said at the same time.

They looked at each other.

“We’ll both take her,” Clarisse said dryly. “Gods. You two.”

Silena returned, breathless, with a water bottle and a damp bandana she had already soaked in the water fountain. She pressed the bandana to Annabeth’s neck without ceremony. “I told Katie to air out the Zeus cabin but not to let anyone in to fuss. Cabin Three guest bunk is made up with clean sheets. Chiron says Annabeth is excused from all duties until Will clears her.”

Annabeth groaned. “I don’t need to be excused. I have a wall to reinforce on the north trail and —”

“You have a fever to sleep off,” Thalia said firmly, bending down so Annabeth could put her arm around her shoulders. “Up. Percy’s got your other side. On three.”

Annabeth grumbled all the way across the green, which Percy took as a good sign. Sick people who still complained had fight left. It was when they went quiet that he worried.

The walk to Cabin Three was slow. Annabeth leaned more heavily with every step, her head dropping toward Percy’s shoulder. By the time they reached the low gray sea-stone building with its fountain and shell trim, her cheeks were flushed deep red and her curls were damp with sweat.

Inside, the cabin was dim and cool and smelled like salt and clean linen. The Poseidon cabin was always five degrees cooler than the rest of camp. The walls sweated faintly with condensation. The fountain in the corner trickled endlessly. Someone — Silena, probably — had already pulled back the covers on the spare bunk by the window and set a folded towel and an extra pillow there.

Percy and Thalia eased Annabeth down onto the mattress like she was made of glass.

Annabeth sighed as the cool sheet touched her overheated skin. “Oh. Oh, that’s nice.”

“Good,” Thalia said, pulling off Annabeth’s shoes. “Stay there.”

“I’m not going anywhere,” Annabeth mumbled. Her eyes were already closing. “Room’s spinning.”

Percy knelt by the bed and untied the leather cord holding her ponytail, loosening her curls so air could reach her neck. He knew how she liked it. He had done this on their remembered quests and sick days, when fevers and colds and monster-flu had gone through camp like wildfire.

“Cold cloth coming,” Silena said, wringing out a washcloth in the fountain basin. She folded it neatly and laid it across Annabeth’s forehead. Annabeth hissed at the cold, then sighed.

“Better?” Percy asked.

“Mm.”

Thalia pulled the light blanket up to Annabeth’s waist, not higher, the way Will had instructed. Fevers needed to breathe. “We’ve got you, baby girl. Sleep if you want. We’re right here.”

Annabeth was asleep within minutes, her breathing fast and shallow, one hot hand curled tightly around Percy’s fingers. She did not let go even in sleep.

Thalia looked at Percy over the bed, her expression soft and worried. “You good to stay first? I need to tell Chiron what Will said and grab her pillow and her blanket from Zeus cabin. She sleeps better with her own stuff.”

“Yeah,” Percy said. “I’m not moving.”

“Every fifteen minutes, sips,” Thalia reminded him, pointing at the water bottle.

“I know the drill, Thals.”

“I know you do.” Thalia hesitated at the door, looking back at her little sister flushed and restless in a strange bunk, her curls spread dark with sweat on the pillow. “Perc? If she spikes —”

“I’ll yell. I promise.”

Thalia nodded and slipped out.

---

The first hour was quiet in the way that sickroom quiet is never really quiet.

Percy sat on the stool pulled up to Annabeth’s bunk, holding her hand, watching the rise and fall of her chest, listening to the fountain and the distant sounds of camp going on without them. Shouts from the volleyball court. The conch for lunch. Gulls.

Annabeth slept restlessly. She kicked at the blanket. She turned her head from side to side. The cold cloth warmed within minutes, and Percy replaced it again and again, rinsing it in the fountain basin, wringing it out, folding it, laying it gently across her forehead and cheeks and the back of her neck.

He coaxed sips when she surfaced half-awake. “Hey, Annie. Drink.”

She drank without opening her eyes, two small swallows, then turned away. Her skin was still furnace-hot.

Around noon, the sparks started.

Percy first noticed it as a prickling on his own arm, the way the air felt before lightning. The fine hairs on Annabeth’s arms stood up. Her curls lifted slightly from the pillow, crackling faintly. When Percy brushed a curl back from her forehead, a thread of pale blue light ran from her temple to his fingertip with a soft snap.

He jerked back. “Ow.”

Annabeth whimpered in her sleep and curled tighter. Another spark jumped from her knuckle to the metal frame of the bunk with a pop. Then another, from her elbow to the damp washcloth, sizzling faintly where it touched the wet fabric.

Percy stared. He had seen Thalia do this in her sleep when she was overtired, during a recent cold night when the group had taken shelter together after patrol. Sky children leaked when their control slipped. Fever burned through control like sun through fog.

He did what Sally had taught him when he was small and scared of storms in his original life. He kept his voice low and steady and kept contact, so she would know she was not alone.

“It’s okay,” he murmured, taking her hand again despite the sting. Small shocks bit at his palm, pins and needles, but he did not let go. “It’s just static, Annie. You’re okay. I’m here. It can’t hurt me. I’m waterproof. Remember? Son of Poseidon. Human grounding rod for grumpy sky girls.”

Annabeth’s breathing hitched. Her fingers tightened around his.

Thalia slipped back in just then with Annabeth’s pillow and her soft gray blanket from home, and stopped dead at the sight of blue light crawling over her sister’s skin.

“Oh, gods,” she breathed. “She’s arcing.”

“She started a few minutes ago,” Percy said quietly. “Small stuff. Like carpet shocks.”

Thalia set the things down and came to kneel on the other side of the bunk. She did not flinch at the static. If anything, the sparks leaned toward her, recognizing kin. A thin filament reached from Annabeth’s shoulder to Thalia’s bracelet and grounded with a tick.

“She does this when she’s sick,” Thalia said softly, brushing Annabeth’s curls back with a practiced hand that knew exactly how to touch without getting zapped. “Ever since she was little. Mom used to call her our little storm cloud.”

Percy looked up. “She did this as a baby too?”

Thalia nodded, her eyes never leaving Annabeth’s flushed face. “Mm-hm. First time she got a real fever, she was maybe two. Barely walking. She lit up the whole crib like a night-light. Scared Mom half to death. Dad — Zeus — he was so worried he sat on the roof all night to keep the clouds away so she could sleep. No thunder. Just quiet rain.”

Annabeth tossed her head, muttering something unintelligible. Another flurry of sparks danced across the sheet.

Thalia laid her cool palm on Annabeth’s cheek. “Shh, baby. Shh. Thalia’s here. Percy’s here. No storm. Just us.”

The sparks eased slightly at her voice.

Percy watched, filing every detail away. How Thalia’s touch grounded her.

They settled into the watch.

Thalia took the cloth and re-cooled it. Percy held the water bottle and coaxed sips whenever Annabeth stirred. Together they changed her pillow for her own, tucked the familiar gray blanket loosely around her hips, pulled the curtain half-closed to dim the light.

When Annabeth’s temperature seemed to climb again around one, her cheeks scarlet, her lips dry, Thalia pressed her lips to her sister’s forehead to gauge it the old-fashioned way and winced.

“She’s hotter,” she said. “We need more than washcloths.”

As if summoned, the door creaked open and Clarisse and Silena came in carrying supplies like reinforcements arriving with siege equipment.

Clarisse had a big wooden bucket sloshing with cold water. “Water from the spring behind the Big House,” she announced in a low voice. “Coldest on camp. Chiron says it’s got nymph blessing in it. Also, before you ask, yes, it was heavy, and no, I didn’t let Travis carry it because he’d spill half of it showing off.”

Silena followed with a folded towel over one arm and a small clay bowl in her hands. Inside the bowl was a green, salt-smelling poultice. “Sea herbs,” she whispered. “Kelp and sea lavender and mint from the tide pools, ground up with a little ambrosia honey. Amphitrite sent the recipe through an Iris-message to Will. It’s a Poseidon remedy for fever. The sea draws heat out. Our dad — well, you know, the sea god — the old stories say cold seawater can pull fever from the blood.”

Percy stared at them, overwhelmed with gratitude. “You guys —”

“Shut up,” Clarisse said, but kindly. “She’s our sister too. Camp siblings count. Scoot over.”

What followed was efficient and gentle, the way girls who had nursed each other through quests and stomach bugs and heartbreaks knew how to be.

Silena soaked two clean cloths in the spring water, wrung them until they were just damp and icy, and showed Percy how to wrap them around Annabeth’s wrists and ankles where the blood ran close to the skin. Clarisse, surprisingly tender for someone who could bench-press a hellhound, dabbed the sea-herb mixture onto a thin strip of linen and laid it across the back of Annabeth’s neck.

“It smells like the beach,” Annabeth mumbled, half-waking, her nose wrinkling. “Like low tide.”

“That’s the kelp,” Silena said softly, stroking her hair. “It’s going to pull the hot out, sweetie. Just breathe.”

“Cold,” Annabeth complained, shivering hard. Sparks scattered with the shiver, lighting the dim cabin for an instant.

“I know, baby, I know,” Thalia murmured, holding her hand while Percy held the other. “Cold is good. Cold means the fever’s leaving. You’re doing great.”

Clarisse crouched by the bucket and wrung out another cloth, her strong hands careful. “When I had lung fever at eight, my mom did this,” she said gruffly. “Cold water on wrists. Said Poseidon kids run hot when they fight infection because our blood wants to boil the bad stuff out. Gotta give the heat somewhere to go or it cooks the brain. You gotta respect the sea’s way. Cool, steady, patient.”

Percy nodded. He knew that feeling. When he pushed his powers too far, his own blood felt like it hummed with pressure. Cold water had always saved him.

He pressed the fresh cold cloth to Annabeth’s forehead and felt her lean into it with a soft, pitiful sound that twisted his chest.

“Percy?” she whispered, eyes still closed. “Don’t go.”

“I’m not going anywhere,” he promised. “I’m right here. I’m holding on.”

“I’m here too, baby girl,” Thalia said, squeezing her other hand. “Both of us. All of us.”

Silena pulled a chair up to the foot of the bed and began to fan gently with a folded map, moving air over Annabeth’s overheated body. Clarisse settled on the floor with her back against the bunk, standing guard in the way she always did, solid and unmovable.

For a while, the fever seemed to ease. Annabeth’s breathing slowed. The sparks faded to occasional ticks. Her skin was still hot but damp now with sweat, which Will had said was good. Sweat meant her body was trying to cool.

Percy allowed himself to hope.

Then the dream came.

---

It started with whimpering.

Annabeth had been sleeping more quietly, her hands loose in Percy’s and Thalia’s, her face a little less flushed. Percy had just started to think about asking Silena to fetch more water when Annabeth’s fingers suddenly clamped down hard on his hand.

Her back arched slightly. Her head turned sharply to the side. A thin, high sound escaped her throat.

“No,” she whispered. “No, no, no —”

Percy was instantly alert. “Annie? Hey. Annie, you’re okay. You’re in Cabin Three. You’re sick, but you’re safe.”

She did not hear him. Her eyes moved rapidly under closed lids. Sweat broke out fresh on her forehead. Her whole body tensed, and a burst of sparks crackled across the blanket, brighter than before.

“Bob?” she gasped. “Bob, the doors — hold the doors — Percy, don’t look down —”

Percy’s stomach dropped. He knew that name. He knew that tone. Tartarus.

Thalia’s head snapped up. “What’s she saying?”

“She’s dreaming about Tartarus,” Percy said quietly. His own throat was tight. He remembered it too, in flashes that still woke him sweating. The endless red dark. The screaming wind. The pit that pulled at your soul. Annabeth had fallen with him. She had walked beside him through that hell and come out with scars no one could see.

Annabeth thrashed weakly, her voice rising. “No! Damasen, get back! Percy, run! The blood — it’s rising — don’t let go, don’t let go —”

Her hand tore from Thalia’s as she reached blindly into the air. Sparks showered from her fingertips, snapping and popping.

“Annabeth!” Thalia said sharply, catching her wrist. “Baby, wake up. You’re safe. You’re at camp. No pit. No giants. Just me. Just Thalia.”

But Annabeth was deep in it, fever and memory tangled together. Tears leaked from under her closed lids and tracked through the sweat on her temples. “Please,” she sobbed. “Please don’t let me fall. Percy, please —”

Percy’s heart broke clean in two. He leaned in close, putting his face near hers, his voice steady and low, the way he had spoken to her in the dark down there when there was nothing else to hold onto but each other.

“I’ve got you,” he said. “Annie, I’ve got you. I’m here. I’m holding your hand. Feel that? That’s me. I’m not letting go. Not in Tartarus, not here, not ever. You hear me?”

He laced his fingers fully through hers and held on, palm to palm, the way they had held on while crossing the Acheron, while running from arai, while standing before the Doors of Death with Bob holding the button and smiling sadly.

“I’m holding on,” he repeated. “You hold on too. Squeeze if you can hear me.”

For a terrible second, nothing. Then Annabeth’s fingers squeezed, weakly but deliberately.

Percy exhaled shakily. “Good girl. Good. Keep squeezing. I’m here. You’re not falling. The ground is solid. Cabin Three. Cool sheets. Silena’s fanning you. Clarisse is guarding the door. Thalia’s right here. No pit.”

Thalia took up the rhythm, stroking Annabeth’s hair back, over and over, her large hand cool and steady. “No pit,” she echoed. “No monsters. Just camp. Smell the salt? That’s home. That’s the Sound. You’re home, baby.”

Clarisse stood and came to the head of the bed, her shadow falling protectively over them. She placed her heavy, calloused hand on Annabeth’s shoulder, grounding weight. “I’ve got the door, Grace,” she said gruffly. “Nothing’s getting past me. Not even a dream. You rest.”

Silena, tears in her own eyes, kept fanning steadily and began to hum very softly, an old lullaby Percy had taught her, one Sally Jackson used to sing in the original life he remembered. Percy recognized it instantly. So did Annabeth, somewhere deep.

Her thrashing eased. Her breathing was still fast and ragged, but the desperate arch left her spine. She turned toward Percy’s voice, toward his hand, like a flower toward sun.

“Percy?” she whispered, still asleep but reaching for him.

“Right here,” he said. He pressed his forehead lightly to her temple, careful of the heat and the sparks, letting her feel his closeness. “Right here, Annie. I’ve got you.”

“Don’t let go,” she breathed.

“Never,” he promised. It was a vow he had made a hundred times, in a hundred places, and meant more each time. “I love you. I’m staying with you.”

Annabeth sighed, a small, broken sound, and settled. The sparks that had been showering faded to a faint fizz, then to nothing. Her hand stayed locked in his.

Thalia met Percy’s eyes over the bed. Her own eyes were wet. She mouthed, Thank you.

Percy shook his head slightly. No thanks needed. This was what family did.

The cabin fell quiet again, except for Annabeth’s harsh breathing and the trickle of the fountain. The crisis had passed, but the fever still burned. Her skin was almost too hot to touch.

Thalia checked the water bucket, found it lukewarm, and made a face. “We need fresh cold. Silena, can you —”

“On it,” Silena said, already standing with the bucket. Clarisse stood too.

“I’ll come. It’s heavy when full.”

They slipped out together, leaving Percy and Thalia alone with Annabeth.

Thalia settled back on her stool, still holding Annabeth’s left hand, still stroking her hair with the other. She looked exhausted, dark circles under her eyes, her own hair limp with sweat and static.

“You should rest,” Percy said quietly. “You’ve been up since dawn patrol.”

“So have you,” Thalia said. “We’ll take turns. That’s what Will said. Someone awake always. You take first sleep when the girls get back. I’ll watch.”

Percy shook his head. “I’m not tired.”

“You’re lying.”

“I’m her boyfriend. I’m allowed to be stubborn about sleep when she’s sick.”

Thalia smiled tiredly. “Yeah. Yeah, you are.” She looked down at Annabeth, so small and flushed in the big bunk, her curls stuck to her cheeks, her mouth slightly open as she breathed. “Gods, she looks like she did when she was four.”

Percy adjusted the cloth on Annabeth’s forehead. “Tell me about then? Distract me. I’m going to fall asleep if I just stare at the fountain.”

Thalia was quiet for a moment, gathering memory the way she gathered electricity, carefully. Then she began to speak, her voice low and rough and tender, meant for only the two of them and the sleeping girl between them.

---

“You weren’t there,” Thalia said. “This was before we met you at camp. When Annabeth was small. Mom — Beryl — she tried, in her way, but she wasn’t good with sick kids. She panicked. Everything was drama. So it was mostly me.”

Percy nodded. He knew bits of this story, in fragments. Thalia rarely told it straight through.

“Annabeth was just past two,” Thalia continued. “Tiny. You think she’s stubborn now? You should’ve seen her at two. Blonde curls down to here, gray eyes huge, following me everywhere. Thally, Thally, Thally. She couldn’t say Thalia yet.”

A faint smile touched Percy’s mouth. “Thally.”

“Yeah. Thally.” Thalia’s own smile was sad and fond. “She caught this bug going around daycare. Fever spiked in the middle of the night. I woke up because the whole room smelled like ozone. Like right before lightning hits. I thought Dad was angry about something.”

Annabeth stirred faintly at the mention of Dad, her brow furrowing. Thalia smoothed it with her thumb without pausing.

“I sat up and Annabeth was standing in her crib, holding the rails, crying. Her face was red as a tomato. She was burning up. And every time she cried, these little sparks would pop off the metal rails. Pop, pop, pop. Like tiny fireworks. She’d cry harder because it startled her, which made more sparks, which scared her more.”

Percy winced. “Poor kid.”

“I was six,” Thalia said. “Mom was passed out. Dad was distant. So I just did what felt right. I climbed into the crib with her.”

“You climbed into the crib?” Percy whispered, trying not to laugh.

“Hey, I was skinny. It was a big crib. I climbed in and pulled her into my lap and wrapped us both in her blanket and just held her while she cried and sparked all over me. It stung like hell. My arms were red with little zaps in the morning. But I didn’t let go. I sang that song Mom used to sing when she was lucid. You know the one. The one about the moon and the sea.”

Percy nodded. He knew the tune. Sally had sung it in his old life too.

“I sang and I rocked her and I got a washcloth from the bathroom — I had to keep getting up to re-wet it because she was so hot it would go warm in like a minute — and I just kept telling her, Cool down, baby girl, cool down, Thally’s here, no storm tonight. And eventually…” Thalia’s voice softened. “Eventually the sparks slowed. She fell asleep on my chest, drooling all over my pajamas, still hot but breathing easier. And outside, for the first time all week, it rained. Soft rain. No thunder. Just… Dad listening, I think. Keeping it gentle for her.”

Percy looked at Annabeth, young again and brilliant, remembering her work for Olympus and the horror of Tartarus, reduced by fever to something small and vulnerable again. He tried to picture her as a sparking toddler in a crib, and his chest ached.

“You’ve been cooling her down for a long time,” he said.

Thalia shrugged, embarrassed. “She’s my baby sister. It’s my job. Then we had camp healers to help — cool water, sea air, patience — and it got easier. You remember that cold spell here when we all felt awful after patrol? Will put us in the Big House?”

Percy grinned. “Will set up cots in the living room of the Big House and wouldn’t let us out of his sight. He made us drink that disgusting seaweed broth.”

“And we all threw up in the same bucket and laughed about it,” Thalia said, laughing wetly. “Gods, we were gross kids.”

“We’re still gross,” Percy said. “Just taller.”

Annabeth, as if she heard them laughing and wanted in, mumbled in her sleep, “‘S not gross… ‘s immunity…”

Percy and Thalia both froze, then burst into quiet laughter.

“She’s still in there,” Percy whispered, relief flooding him. “Wise Girl’s still arguing in her sleep.”

“Always,” Thalia said. She leaned down and kissed Annabeth’s hot temple, unconcerned with sparks or sweat. “You hear that, baby? You’re still the smartest person in this room, even with a fever of a hundred and three. Rest now. Let us be smart for a little while.”

Annabeth sighed and turned her face into Thalia’s hand.

The door opened softly. Clarisse and Silena returned with fresh cold water, Will Solace right behind them with his bag.

“Report,” Will said quietly, already reaching for Annabeth’s wrist.

Percy gave it straight, the way Annabeth had taught him to give reports. “Fever still high. Sparks started around noon, small but frequent. Bad dream around one-fifteen, Tartarus memory, thrashed and cried, calmed with contact and talking. Sips every fifteen minutes, maybe two ounces each time. Sweating now. Cold compresses on forehead, wrists, ankles. Sea herbs on neck.”

Will raised his eyebrows, impressed. “Good. Really good. You two are naturals.” He checked temperature, pulse, breathing. “A hundred and three point six. Up a little, but that’s expected in the afternoon. Lungs clear. Pulse fast but strong. The sweat is good. Keep doing what you’re doing.”

He replaced the sea-herb linen with a fresh one, showed Silena how to dilute the poultice if Annabeth’s skin reddened, and left two more doses of nectar-spiked electrolyte drink.

“Alternate watches tonight,” he instructed. “No one stays up all night. If she wakes clear-headed and wants food, give her crackers and broth only.”

“Already learned that one,” Percy said.

Will grinned. “Son of Poseidon versus daughter of Zeus. The eternal struggle.” He squeezed Percy’s shoulder. “You’re doing great, Percy. She’s lucky to have you.”

“We’re lucky to have her,” Percy said simply.

---

Evening came soft and gold to Camp Half-Blood.

The light through Cabin Three’s window turned the color of honey. The fountain sang. Outside, the dinner conch blew, and campers laughed on their way to the mess hall, but inside the little sea-stone cabin, time moved slow as syrup.

Thalia took first watch as ordered. Percy dozed on the next bunk over, one arm hanging off so his fingers could stay tangled with Annabeth’s toes through the blanket.

Clarisse and Silena brought dinner — plain broth for Annabeth, and bread and cheese for the watchers.

Annabeth slept through dinner, her fever holding steady. She woke once around sunset, glassy-eyed and confused, and Thalia coaxed her up enough to sip broth and the nectar drink. She swallowed two spoonfuls, coughed weakly, and fell back asleep with her head on Thalia’s arm.

“She called me Mom,” Thalia whispered to Percy, her voice breaking just slightly. “When she woke up. She said, Mom, cold. Broke my heart.”

Percy reached over and squeezed Thalia’s shoulder. “You are Mom right now. That’s okay. Sally used to say sick kids always called for whoever feels safest.”

Thalia blinked hard. “Yeah?”

“Yeah. You’re safe, Thals.”

Around nine, Percy woke fully to take his turn. Thalia protested — of course she did — but Percy herded her to the spare bunk with a blanket and a pillow and stood over her until she lay down.

“Sleep,” he ordered, using his best stern voice, even though Thalia was older. “That’s an order, Scout.”

Thalia stuck her tongue out at him but closed her eyes. Within minutes her breathing evened out, one hand still stretched toward Annabeth’s bunk even in sleep.

Percy settled into the watch with a book he did not read, a cold cloth he changed religiously, and Annabeth’s hot, dry hand in his.

The night watch was the hardest. The camp went quiet. The crickets sang. The fountain seemed louder. Annabeth’s fever climbed with the dark, as fevers do, and the sparks came back stronger.

Percy watched blue light crawl over her knuckles and felt helpless in the way loving someone can make you feel helpless — wanting to take the sickness into his own body, to carry it for her the way he carried water, to filter it clean through himself and give her back only cool.

He did what he could. Cold cloths. Sips when she stirred.

He told her about the lake. How cold it was at the bottom even in August. How the tide would come in tonight and wash everything clean.

Annabeth’s breathing slowed as he talked. The sparks eased.

Around midnight, she dreamed again. Not as bad this time — a whimper, a flinch, a whispered, “Don’t fall —”

Percy was ready. He leaned close, held her hand to his cheek so she could feel his skin, warm and alive and there.

“I’ve got you,” he murmured. “Feel that? That’s my hand. No pit. No falling. Just Cabin Three. Just Percy. I’m not letting go.”

Annabeth squeezed, sighed, settled. Her fingers were still furnace-hot, but they stayed locked with his.

Thalia woke around two and wordlessly took the cloth from Percy’s tired hands and re-cooled it. She did not tell him to sleep. She just sat on the other side of the bed and shared the watch, shoulder to shoulder with him in the lamplight, both holding on.

“She’s cooler,” Thalia whispered after a while, pressing her cheek to Annabeth’s forehead. “Feel that? She’s damp all over. Fever’s breaking.”

Percy felt. Annabeth’s skin was still warm, but no longer scorching. Sweat stood on her upper lip and forehead. Her curls were soaked. The air around her no longer prickled with static.

“Oh, thank gods,” he breathed. Tears pricked his eyes and he did not bother to hide them. Thalia was crying too, silent tracks through the tired grime on her face.

They changed her damp shirt for a dry one with practiced modesty — turning away, holding up the blanket, Silena had left a clean camp shirt folded just for this — changed the sweat-soaked sheets for dry ones, re-tucked the familiar gray blanket around her, and settled her back with fresh cold cloths that she barely needed now.

Annabeth slept on, deeply now, truly restfully for the first time all day, her mouth soft, her hands loose, no sparks, no thrashing.

Percy and Thalia sat on either side of her in the dim cabin, holding her hands, too relieved to sleep.

“She’s going to be okay,” Thalia whispered.

“Yeah,” Percy whispered back. “She is.”

---

Morning came pale and cool through the window, with the smell of rain and strawberries.

Percy woke with a crick in his neck and his cheek stuck to the edge of Annabeth’s mattress. He had not meant to fall asleep. He had meant to watch all night. But sometime before dawn, relief had pulled him under.

He sat up with a jerk. “Annie?”

Annabeth was awake.

She was propped up on her own pillow, pale and washed-out and thin-looking, her curls a damp tangle, dark circles under her gray eyes — but her eyes were clear. Clear and annoyed and fond all at once. The fever-flush was gone. Her skin was cool and slightly clammy to the touch. No sparks lifted her hair. No static snapped when she moved.

She was holding a mug of broth in both hands and smiling weakly at him over the rim.

“Hi, Seaweed Brain,” she croaked.

Percy stared at her for a full three seconds before his brain caught up. Then he made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and carefully — so carefully, because she was still fragile — gathered her into his arms, mug and all.

“Hey, don’t — broth —” Annabeth protested, laughing weakly, but she leaned into him anyway, resting her forehead against his shoulder. She was cool. Wonderfully, blessedly cool. “I’m all sweaty and gross.”

“You’re cool,” Percy mumbled into her hair. “You’re cool and awake and talking. Gods, Annie, you scared us.”

Thalia woke at the noise and sat bolt upright, hair wild, eyes wild. “What — is she —?”

“She’s awake,” Percy said. “Fever broke.”

Thalia was across the room in two strides. She knelt by the bunk and cupped Annabeth’s face in both hands, studying her like she was a map. Then she pressed her forehead hard to Annabeth’s, the way she had done since they were small, checking temperature skin-to-skin.

“Cool,” Thalia whispered. Tears spilled over. “Oh, baby girl, you’re cool.”

Annabeth reached out with one broth-warmed hand and wiped her sister’s cheek. “Don’t cry, Thally. You’ll make me cry.”

“I’m not crying,” Thalia said, crying. “It’s allergies. To you being stubborn.”

Will Solace arrived ten minutes later, summoned by a shrieking Silena who had poked her head in with fresh water and found Annabeth awake and promptly burst into tears and ran for the infirmary.

Will checked temperature — ninety-nine point one and falling — pulse, lungs, throat, and pronounced himself satisfied.

“Fever broke around four, I’d guess,” he said. “Classic. Sweat it out in the night watches. You three did perfect. Cold compresses, fluids, rest, contact. Especially contact. Fevers like this — the body fights harder when it knows it’s safe.”

“So I can get up?” Annabeth asked hopefully.

“Absolutely not,” Will, Thalia, Percy, Clarisse, and Silena said in unison.

Annabeth groaned and flopped back onto her pillow. “I knew you were going to say that.”

“Twenty-four hours bunk rest,” Will said firmly. “Cabin Three or Zeus, your choice, but supervised. Fluids, bland food, no training, no strategy, no lightning. If fever stays down, you can go to meals tomorrow.”

Annabeth pouted, but she was too weak to argue hard. Percy took that as proof she was still recovering.

Clarisse thumped her lightly on the shoulder. “Don’t look so tragic, Grace. We saved you some breakfast. Silena threatened the Stolls with death if they ate your bacon.”

Silena presented a plate with toast, scrambled eggs, and the promised bacon, plus a fresh glass of the nectar drink. “Small bites,” she instructed. “And then you have to tell us if your throat hurts or your head hurts or anything feels weird.”

Annabeth ate obediently. Color came back into her cheeks with each bite, healthy pink instead of fever red. Her hands were steady. No sparks.

Between bites, she looked around at them — Thalia rumpled and red-eyed on one side, Percy rumpled and stubbly on the other, Clarisse looming with arms crossed, Silena perched on the foot of the bed — and her eyes filled.

“You guys stayed?” she whispered.

“Of course we stayed, idiot,” Clarisse said gruffly, looking away so no one would see her expression. “You’re family. Where else would we be?”

“We took turns,” Silena said softly. “Thalia and Percy never left. Clarisse and I ran water. Will checked. You were never alone.”

Annabeth looked at Percy. “You held my hand? I dreamed you held my hand in… in the dark. And you didn’t let go.”

Percy’s throat closed. He held up their hands, still linked — he had not realized he had taken her hand again. “I held on. I promised I wouldn’t let go. Not ever.”

Annabeth squeezed, tears slipping down her pale cheeks. “Thank you.”

Thalia leaned in and kissed the top of Annabeth’s head. “You sparked all over the place, baby girl. Just like when you were two. Lit up the whole cabin. Percy got zapped like ten times and didn’t let go once.”

“I got zapped?” Annabeth said, horrified. “Percy, I’m sorry —”

“Worth it,” Percy said immediately. “Barely felt it. I’m waterproof, remember?”

Annabeth laughed wetly and leaned her head against his shoulder, too tired to hold herself up anymore. Thalia leaned against her other side, and for a moment the three of them just sat there in the narrow bunk, shoulder to shoulder, breathing together.

Silena quietly pulled the gray blanket up over Annabeth’s lap. Clarisse refilled the water bottle and set it within reach.

Outside, Camp Half-Blood woke fully. The bell rang for morning activities.

Inside Cabin Three, Annabeth Grace fell asleep again — this time cool and quiet and spark-free — with her big sister’s arm around her shoulders and her boyfriend’s hand in hers.

Thalia looked at Percy over Annabeth’s bowed head and mouthed, Thank you, again.

Percy shook his head, smiling tiredly, and mouthed back, Always.

He stayed until Annabeth’s breathing deepened into true sleep, until Will nodded that he could rest, until Thalia gently pried his fingers loose and promised to hold on in his place.

Even then, he slept on the next bunk over, close enough to hear her breathe, close enough to reach her if she dreamed of falling.

He did not let go for long.

He never did.
