## Chapter 95: Annabeth Sick

By the third round, Percy knew Annabeth was sick.

She had told him before they left Cabin One that she'd woken cold and then too hot. She blamed the stuffy cabin and insisted she could still practice. Will had cleared Percy to return to ordinary drills after a full day without fever; he was not about to mistake a fever for the heat of the arena again.

It was Tuesday at Camp Half-Blood. The strawberry fields were heavy with fruit, and the arena rang with wooden blades and Clarisse calling cadence. Percy and Annabeth had chosen footwork and blunt practice swords: no powers, no full-speed strikes, and either of them could call a stop. The arrangement was Annabeth's idea. She wanted to test a new way of reading his shoulder before he moved.

She had explained the drill before they picked up the swords. Percy would give her the beginning of a move, not the whole attack; she would call the direction she thought he meant to go. If she was wrong, he could show her the shoulder or hip that had given him away. If he was right, she would change her stance and make him try again. They had agreed that a raised hand meant stop, no questions about whether the reason was pain, a bad step, or simply enough practice for one morning.

“You keep telling me my shoulder gives it away,” Percy said after the second pass. “I think you just read my mind.”

“Your mind is not that difficult to read.”

“That's hurtful.”

“It's useful.” She moved back to the chalk line, eyes fixed on his left side. “Again. Don't make it obvious this time.”

He tried. Annabeth named the feint before he had moved his sword. She tapped his shoulder with the flat of her blade, light enough to keep the drill honest, and he had to laugh at himself. Her expression sharpened with the satisfaction of finding a pattern she could test. Percy liked seeing that look. He also noticed how carefully she placed her feet before the next pass, as if she were budgeting effort rather than spending it.

“We can stop after this round,” he said.

“We can stop when I call it.”

“Or when I do.”

“Fine. Either of us.” She lifted her practice sword. “You first.”

Percy stepped into the chalk square and gave her a low feint. Annabeth shifted toward his sword, then caught herself before she crossed the line. He had changed the direction at the last moment. She studied his stance instead of pretending she'd called it.

“Your left foot tells me more than your shoulder,” she said.

“My left foot is minding its own business.”

“Your toes point where you're about to go.” She nudged the dirt with her shoe to show him the angle. “If you want to hide the move, turn your hips later.”

He tried the feint again, this time changing the order. Annabeth blocked it, but not by much. “Better,” she said. The grin that followed belonged to the training, to finding a new answer and making him work for the next one. Percy liked being corrected when she could show him exactly why.

Clarisse's cadence rolled across the rings. A pair of younger campers finished their pass and stepped aside. Annabeth waited until the lane was clear before they reset. She was still the one setting the pace, still asking for another attempt. That was why her stumble on the next exchange caught him: it came between the decision and the movement, not because she had missed the pattern.

She was usually quicker in the first minute. This morning her parry arrived late and her next step landed short. Sweat darkened the curls at her temples despite the breeze. Percy held back for one exchange, then saw her set her jaw at him.

“Don't do that,” she said.

“Do what?”

“Decide I can't finish a drill because I missed one beat.”

“I didn't decide that.” Percy lowered his blade. “I decided to ask if you're okay.”

Annabeth scowled, which was not an answer. “I'm fine. Again.”

Behind them, Thalia Grace looked up from the bench. Her right palm remained under a dry wrap, held close against her thigh while she worked the bootlace with her left hand. She watched Annabeth reset her feet, then watched her pause to breathe through her mouth. Her eyes narrowed.

“Hey, Wise Girl,” Thalia said. “Come here a sec.”

“I’m in the middle of kicking Percy’s butt,” Annabeth said, but her voice sounded thin.

Thalia got up. “Can I check your forehead?”

Annabeth looked at her. “You can ask first.”

“I just did.”

“Then yes. One second.”

Thalia used her left hand, careful not to brush Annabeth's shoulder with the wrapped palm. She touched the back of her fingers to Annabeth's forehead. The worry settled into her face before she pulled away.

“Oh, baby,” she said softly. “You’re burning up.”

Annabeth batted her hand away. “I’m warm from sparring. Everyone’s warm.”

“No,” Percy said. He knew what warm-from-sparring felt like, and he knew what this was. He stepped closer and touched her cheek with his knuckles, gentle. Her skin was hot and dry under the sweat. Her gray eyes were too bright, glassy at the edges. “Annie, you’re hot.”

“I said I’m fine,” Annabeth muttered, but she swayed just slightly when she said it, and Percy’s hand shot out to steady her elbow. Thalia was already there on the other side.

Clarisse had been watching from the next ring with her helmet under her arm. She came over, took in Annabeth's unsteady stance, and frowned. “Grace, you're done for today.”

“Thanks,” Annabeth said. “Very helpful.”

“I mean it helpfully,” Clarisse said. She left the diagnosis to Will. “No more swords. Shade, then infirmary.”

Annabeth opened her mouth to argue, and the world tilted. Percy felt it through her arm before he saw it, that sudden give in her knees. He caught her fully around the waist as her practice sword clattered to the packed dirt.

“Whoa, whoa, I got you,” he said.

Thalia was already crouching to pick up the fallen sword. Her face had gone pale under her tan. “Okay. That’s it. No arguments. Will. We need Will.”

Silena had been braiding a younger camper's hair on the sidelines. She set the comb down, asked the girl to wait with a counselor, and ran for Will. He arrived with his medical bag and directed them to the shaded bench.

He took one look at Annabeth leaning heavily against Percy, her eyes half-closed, her breathing quick and shallow, and nodded toward the shade.

“Bench,” he said. “Let me see her.”

---

Percy sat with Annabeth on the bench while Will worked, and tried not to hover too obviously and failed completely.

Will checked her temperature, pulse and breathing, then looked in her eyes, ears and throat. He asked before touching her neck and paused when she flinched. Annabeth answered as clearly as she could; the others stayed quiet unless he asked them something.

“When did this start, Annabeth?”

“This morning,” Annabeth mumbled. “I woke up hot. I thought it was the cabin. Zeus cabin gets stuffy.”

“Headache?”

“A little.”

“Sore throat? Cough? Stomach?”

“No. Just… achy. And cold, then hot. My teeth were chattering at breakfast and then I was sweating through my shirt.”

Will glanced at Thalia. “Has she eaten and had water today? Any new symptoms before practice?”

“She said she woke up cold, then hot,” Thalia said. “She had water at breakfast. I don't know how much. Yesterday she stayed up over the border calculations after the storm and helped me ground two strikes. She skipped most of dinner.”

“I drank water,” Annabeth protested weakly.

"You drank half a glass and called it hydration," Percy said. He was still holding her hand. He had not let go since she swayed. Her fingers were hot in his. “You also skipped lunch yesterday to fix the ballista.”

Annabeth gave him a look that would have been withering if her eyes had been able to focus properly. “Traitor.”

“Boyfriend,” Percy corrected. “It’s my job to rat you out when you’re sick.”

Will read the thermometer twice. “One hundred and three point two. That's high. I don't see an obvious source in her ears or throat, and the exam doesn't tell me what's causing it. There have been sick campers in Hermes, but that doesn't prove this is the same thing. The work yesterday might have left her tired; it doesn't explain the fever by itself.”

“Is she going to be okay?” Thalia asked. Her voice was tight. Older sisters tried to sound casual and never managed it.

Will looked at Annabeth, not Thalia. “You need rest and fluids. I also saw the spark. I don't know whether the fever caused it or whether it's a separate power flare. We'll keep metal and water away from your hands if it happens again. If you feel confused while you're awake, start vomiting, have trouble breathing, or the fever rises further, call for me immediately.”

As Will finished speaking, Annabeth shivered. A tiny blue spark jumped between her fingertips and Percy's knuckle.

Percy pulled his hand clear. “Ow.”

“Sorry,” Annabeth whispered. Tears stood in her eyes, humiliated. “Sorry, Percy, I didn’t mean —”

“Hey, you're okay,” Percy said. He eased his hand back when another tiny spark ticked between their fingers. “I'll stay close. I don't want you trying to hold on through a shock.”

Will put the thermometer away. “We can keep you here in the shade while I contact Chiron, or move you to the infirmary. If you stay in a cabin, you need someone nearby and regular checks. Drink when you wake and feel able; nobody should wake you just to force water. A light sheet is enough. If a cool cloth feels good, use it for comfort, but don't ice or wrap you in cold water. I'll come back within the hour, sooner if you call.”

Annabeth swallowed. Her eyes were clearer than they had been on her feet. “Cabin Three, if you'll let me. I don't want the infirmary full of people asking questions.”

“You can choose that,” Will said, “as long as you stay alert and we keep checking you. If that changes, I'll move you to the infirmary.”

“I’ll take her,” Percy said at once.

“I’ll take her,” Thalia said at the same time.

They looked at each other.

“We'll both help,” Clarisse said. “Gods. You two.”

Silena returned with a water bottle and a clean cloth. “Chiron says you're excused from duties today. Katie will keep the cabin quiet and bring your bag over if you want it.”

Annabeth groaned. “I don’t need to be excused. I have a wall to reinforce on the north trail and —”

“You have a fever and the room's moving,” Thalia said. “Can you stand if we take one side each?”

“Yes. Give me a second.” Annabeth looked at Percy. “Don't pull me up. Just be there if I tip.”

“Got it.” Percy offered his arm. Thalia supported her with her left, keeping her wrapped hand clear. Clarisse walked ahead to open the Cabin Three door.

Annabeth complained once about the distance and twice about the fact that Clarisse was carrying her practice sword. Percy let her keep both complaints; she was still answering them.

The walk to Cabin Three was slow. Annabeth leaned more heavily with every step, her head dropping toward Percy’s shoulder. By the time they reached the low gray sea-stone building with its fountain and shell trim, her cheeks were flushed deep red and her curls were damp with sweat.

Percy kept his arm where she had asked him to keep it, ready to steady her if she tipped but not hauling her forward. His first instinct had been to carry her the moment she swayed. The correction was harder than it should have been. She could be ill and still decide how she moved; he could be frightened without turning that fear into another pair of hands on her.

Clarisse walked a few paces ahead and cleared the path without making a show of it. Thalia stayed on Annabeth's other side, her wrapped palm tucked against her body. Once, when Annabeth's shoe caught on a rut, Percy and Thalia shifted toward her. She shook her head before either could grab her.

“I have it,” she said.

Percy held still. Annabeth planted her foot, found her balance and continued. Only when she leaned back into his arm did he give her more support. He remembered the way Annabeth had asked what he wanted during his own sick day; this was the answer she was giving him now, in the same plain language. He could help by paying attention, not by deciding the next move for her.

Inside, Cabin Three smelled of salt and clean linen. Its fountain trickled in the corner. Someone had made up the guest bunk near the window with a clean sheet and a folded towel.

Percy and Thalia lowered Annabeth onto the mattress slowly, giving her time to settle against the pillow.

Annabeth sighed as the cool sheet touched her overheated skin. “Oh. Oh, that’s nice.”

“Good,” Thalia said, pulling off Annabeth’s shoes. “Stay there.”

“I’m not going anywhere,” Annabeth mumbled. Her eyes were already closing. “Room’s spinning.”

Percy knelt by the bed and untied the leather cord holding her ponytail, loosening her curls so they would not pull at her neck. He set the cord beside the pillow where she could find it when she woke.

“Cool cloth, if you want it,” Silena said, wringing out a washcloth in the fountain basin. She held it up for Annabeth to see. Annabeth nodded, and Silena laid it across her forehead. Annabeth hissed at the cold, then sighed.

“Better?” Percy asked.

“Mm.”

Thalia pulled the light blanket up to Annabeth’s waist, not higher, the way Will had instructed. Will had asked them to keep the cover light, and Annabeth could move it if she wanted. “We’ve got you, baby girl. Sleep if you want. We’re right here.”

Annabeth drifted off with one hand resting over Percy's on top of the sheet. Her breathing was quick but even. Will's chart and the water bottle stood on the small table where a watcher could reach them without leaning over her.

Thalia looked at Percy over the bed, her expression soft and worried. “You good to stay first? I need to tell Chiron what Will said and grab her pillow and her blanket from Zeus cabin. She sleeps better with her own stuff.”

“Yeah,” Percy said. “I’m not moving.”

"Keep the water where she can reach it when she wakes," Thalia said, pointing at the bottle.

“I know the drill, Thals.”

“I know you do.” Thalia hesitated at the door, looking back at her little sister flushed and restless in a strange bunk, her curls spread dark with sweat on the pillow. “Perc? If she spikes —”

“I’ll yell. I promise.”

Thalia nodded and slipped out.

---

The cabin settled around the small sounds: the fountain, a ball bouncing somewhere near the court, the lunch conch. Percy watched Annabeth's breathing and left the water within reach. When she woke enough to ask for a drink, he passed it over and waited while she chose how much to take. She drank, then asked him to set the bottle back on the table.

He did not try to cool her with the wet cloth. She had said the cool sheet felt good, and that was enough. Will's chart sat beside the thermometer; Percy recorded the check and the few sips without turning them into proof of anything. The fever stayed high. The cabin's ordinary afternoon continued beyond the door.

Near noon, an electric snap broke through the fountain's steady trickle.

Percy had leaned toward Annabeth to ask whether she wanted the curtain closed. A pale blue arc jumped from her fingertips to the iron bed rail. He pulled his hand back before it could cross the space between them. Silena had moved the cloth to the table when Annabeth fell asleep, so it no longer touched her skin. It slipped from the edge; Percy left it where it had fallen until the spark faded.

Annabeth whimpered in her sleep and curled toward the pillow. A second arc ticked against the rail. Percy moved the water bottle and metal tray beyond the reach of the bed, then stepped back. He had seen Thalia's power flare when she was overtired, but that gave him no rule for Annabeth's fever. He called for Silena rather than guessing.

His mind went to Sally, to the calm voice she had used when storms frightened him in the life he remembered. Percy kept his voice low and spoke from the doorway, where the sparks could not reach him.

“Annie, it's Percy. You're in Cabin Three. I'm here. You don't have to wake up.”

Her fingers unclenched. The next arc died before reaching the rail.

Thalia returned carrying Annabeth's pillow and gray blanket. She kept the wrapped right palm close and used her left hand to set them down. At the sight of the pale flashes, she stopped short.

“This happened when she was little,” she said. “I don't know whether this is the same thing. Let me tell Will before anyone touches the bed.”

Percy looked up. “She did this when she was small?”

Thalia nodded, her eyes never leaving Annabeth’s flushed face. “Mm-hm. First time she got a real fever, she was maybe two. Barely walking. She lit up the whole crib like a night-light. Scared Mom half to death. Dad — Zeus — he was so worried he sat on the roof all night to keep the clouds away so she could sleep. No thunder. Just quiet rain.”

Annabeth tossed her head, muttering something unintelligible. Another flurry of sparks danced across the sheet.

Thalia kept her left hand on the blanket, not Annabeth's skin. “I'm here, baby girl. Percy is too. No storm in this room.”

The next spark snapped against the rail and stopped. Her voice might have helped; Percy could not tell. He watched the rise and fall of Annabeth's chest and waited for Will.

Thalia called Will from the porch while Clarisse and Silena came with fresh drinking water, clean towels and a spare dry sheet. Clarisse had carried the heavy jug herself. She set it on the far side of the room and looked at Percy.

“I brought water, not a cure,” Clarisse said. “Will said quiet room and checks. That's what we're doing.”

Silena placed the clean towel on the table. “The spring is cold. I can keep the bottle filled. If she wants a cloth on her forehead, we can ask first.”

“That's our plan,” Percy said. “No one puts anything on her while she's asleep.”

Clarisse nodded once. “Good.” She moved the metal tray and the cup beyond the bed's reach, then left the room for a moment to ask the cabin counselor to keep the doorway clear. There was no raised voice, no spectacle; she simply made the space less crowded.

Silena stayed long enough to set the water within Annabeth's reach for when she woke, then checked the time on Will's chart. “I'll take the next check. After that I need to go to dinner shift. Katie can bring food here if Annabeth asks, but no parade.”

“Thanks,” Percy said.

“Save it for later. I'm not cancelling my shift.” She smiled at him to make sure he understood. “I'll be back after dinner if she wants me.”

When Annabeth woke, it was to Thalia beside the bunk and Percy in the chair. She looked at the dry cloth on the table, then at the water bottle.

“Could I have a little water?”

Percy handed her the bottle, and she drank two swallows. “And take that cloth away. It made me shiver.”

Thalia removed it at once. “Done.”

“I want the curtain half closed, not all the way. I don't want to wake up and think it's night.”

Silena adjusted it to the mark Annabeth pointed out. Annabeth watched until the line of daylight fell where she wanted it, then let her head rest again. The fever had not gone; the room was simply arranged to her liking.

Clarisse came back in and leaned against the doorframe, arms folded. “You need anything else, Grace?”

“For five minutes without another question.”

Clarisse's mouth quirked. “I can do that.”

“Thank you.”

“Don't get used to me being polite.”

Annabeth's answer was a tired but unmistakable smile.

She watched Percy move the water bottle a little farther from the bed. “Is the north-trail wall still on the schedule?”

“Chiron knows you're out today,” Clarisse said. She leaned back into the doorway. “The crew won't touch the damaged section until someone checks the braces.”

“Who is covering the inspection?”

“Chiron assigned two counselors. I don't know which ones yet.”

Annabeth frowned. “I don't want them climbing over the loose side.”

“They won't,” Clarisse said. “I told the counselor on the way in. The trail is closed until the braces are checked.”

Annabeth let out a breath. “All right. Tell Chiron I can give him the old sketch when I'm awake enough to read it. No one needs to guess where the weak post is.”

“I'll tell him,” Percy said.

“Not now,” she added. “Now I want to close my eyes.”

“Now you close your eyes,” Thalia said. Her voice stayed gentle. “We'll pass on the note.”

Annabeth turned toward the pillow. For once, she did not make them prove that the work could wait. Clarisse stayed by the door until her breathing evened out, then stepped outside to relay the closure to the counselor. Percy wrote “north trail closed” in the margin of the chart, not in the temperature column.

The note seemed too small for a problem that had kept Annabeth at the arena after she knew she felt wrong. She had wanted the wall reinforced because a weak section could put somebody in danger; staying in bed did not make that concern foolish. It did mean the work had to pass to someone else. Percy tried not to turn her worry into another instruction she had to manage.

“When Katie brings my bag, can you find the green folder?” Annabeth asked from the pillow. Her eyes were still closed. “The north-trail sketch is in the front pocket. I want to mark the post I told Chiron about.”

“If you wake up and still want it,” Percy said.

“I do.”

He asked Silena to send Katie for the bag. When it arrived, Percy set it on the small table and waited. Annabeth woke enough to ask him to open the folder, then pointed to the pencil line at the northern wall. The sketch did not answer whether the brace was sound; it only showed where she had seen the trouble and what needed inspection before anyone climbed near it.

“The trail stays closed until they check the supports,” she said. “I don't want someone taking this as permission to start fixing it.”

“I'll make that clear,” Clarisse said. “Chiron already has the closure. I'll take him the sketch and tell him it's for the inspection, not a work order.”

Annabeth looked from the paper to Clarisse. “Please.”

Clarisse folded the page along its old crease without covering the marked post. “Done.”

Percy watched Annabeth follow the pencil mark with one finger. Her hand paused at the bottom edge of the page. “I should check the measure again.”

“You can check it when Will says you're up to it,” he said. He kept his voice even; the answer belonged to Will, not to Percy.

“I know.” Annabeth let the folder close. “I just don't want them to treat my sketch like a guess.”

“Then the note can say that. You don't have to stand beside the wall to make it true.”

She studied him for a moment, then nodded. “Write that the north trail stays closed until the braces are inspected. Chiron can ask me about the measurement another day.”

Clarisse took the folder to the porch. She returned a few minutes later with the paper still in her hand. Chiron had received it, she said, and the two counselors assigned to the inspection would not enter the closed section until the supports were checked. He would keep the sketch for the inspection and ask Annabeth about the measurement another day.

Annabeth's shoulders lowered. She didn't say the work no longer mattered. She let the people who were well enough take the next step.

When he sat again, Annabeth had already drifted off.

Then the dream came.

---

It started with whimpering.

Annabeth had been sleeping more quietly, one hand resting across Percy's on the sheet and her other loose beside Thalia's chair. Her face looked a little less flushed. Percy had just started to think about asking Silena to fetch more water when Annabeth’s fingers suddenly clamped down hard on his hand.

Her back arched slightly. Her head turned sharply to the side. A thin, high sound escaped her throat.

“No,” she whispered. “No, no, no —”

Percy was instantly alert. “Annie? Hey. Annie, you’re okay. You’re in Cabin Three. You’re sick, but you’re safe.”

She did not hear him. Her eyes moved rapidly under closed lids. Sweat broke out fresh on her forehead. Her whole body tensed, and a burst of sparks crackled across the blanket, brighter than before.

“Bob?” she gasped. “Bob, the doors — hold the doors — Percy, don’t look down —”

Percy's stomach dropped. The names and the plea belonged to a memory only he and Annabeth carried from the life they remembered. It was not his story to explain over her sleeping body.

Thalia's head snapped up. “What is it?”

“A nightmare,” he said. His throat tightened around the word. “I know the place she's afraid of, but she can tell you about it herself if she wants to.”

Annabeth thrashed weakly, her voice rising. “No! Damasen, get back! Percy, run! The blood — it’s rising — don’t let go, don’t let go —”

Her hand tore from Thalia’s as she reached blindly into the air. Sparks showered from her fingertips, snapping and popping.

"Annabeth!" Thalia said, then stopped before her hand reached the sparking blanket. “You're safe. You're at camp. No falling. Just me, Percy, and the cabin.”

But Annabeth was deep in it, fever and memory tangled together. Tears leaked from under her closed lids and tracked through the sweat on her temples. “Please,” she sobbed. “Please don’t let me fall. Percy, please —”

Percy's heart clenched. He stayed at the bedside but out of the arc's reach, speaking in the low, steady voice he had used with her in the dark they both remembered.

“I'm here, Annie. Cabin Three. You can hear my voice. I love you. You don't have to answer.”

The blue flashes stopped for several breaths. Percy put his hand on the sheet beside hers, not on her skin, and waited. When Annabeth's searching fingers found his, he let her close them around two of his. He remembered the pressure of her hand in the life they shared, but this was a new room, this fever, this choice to reach for him.

“I'm right here,” he said. “If you can hear me, squeeze once.”

For a terrible second, nothing. Then Annabeth’s fingers squeezed, weakly but deliberately.

Percy exhaled shakily. “I felt that. You're in Cabin Three. The bed is under you. Thalia's here. Silena's here. Clarisse is by the door. You're not falling.”

Thalia kept her wrapped hand in her lap and matched Percy's slow cadence. “The cabin is quiet. The fountain's running. You're on your bed, little sister. We're staying close.”

Clarisse stood at the door, giving the bed room. “I've got this side of the cabin, Grace. Nobody's coming in to crowd you.”

Silena began the sailor song her mother had hummed while braiding hair. Percy knew it from the cove drill and from the night he had heard her sing it in Cabin Three. He did not confuse her mother's song with his own memories of Sally.

Her thrashing eased. Her breathing stayed fast, but the desperate arch left her spine. She turned toward Percy's voice. The static had quieted; he kept his hand where she could find it.

“Percy?” she whispered, still asleep but reaching for him.

“Right here,” he said. He did not touch her face while the last small sparks continued. “I'm staying in the room.”

“Don’t let go,” she breathed.

“I love you,” he said. “I'm here now.”

Annabeth sighed and settled. The sparks faded to a faint fizz, then stopped. Her hand stayed around his fingers.

Thalia met Percy’s eyes over the bed. Her own eyes were wet. She mouthed, Thank you.

Percy shook his head slightly. Silena had kept her shift; Clarisse had cleared the doorway; Thalia had stayed with her sister; he had not had to carry the watch alone.

The cabin fell quiet again, except for Annabeth's breathing and the fountain. The nightmare had passed. Her fever had not.

Thalia checked Will's chart and the clock. “He's due back soon. Clarisse, can you refill the drinking bottle? Silena, you can go to dinner. I'll call if Annabeth wants you.”

“I can carry the jug,” Clarisse said. “The path's clear.”

Silena glanced at Annabeth, then at Thalia. “I'll finish my shift and come back after. You don't have to keep me here.”

“I know,” Thalia said. “Thanks.”

They went together. Thalia stayed on her stool, the wrap resting in her lap and her left hand clear of Annabeth in case the sparks returned.

“You should rest,” Percy said quietly. “You were on dawn patrol.”

“So were you.”

“Will said we take turns.”

“I heard him.” Thalia rubbed her left thumb along the seam of her jeans. “I'm still getting used to not being the only one who can tell when she needs something.”

Percy looked at Annabeth's face, turned toward the window. “She told us what she wanted at the bench. Then she asked for the curtain. She's still doing that.”

“I know.” Thalia's voice lowered. “I see her little and I start answering for her. It's not fair to her.”

“You caught yourself.”

“After she had to remind me.” Thalia watched the lamp's reflection in the fountain. “Can you stay while I get a drink? Five minutes. I want to be back before Will.”

“Yeah.”

She stood carefully, keeping her right hand close, and left the door open. Percy stayed near the bunk but outside the arc's reach. Annabeth's breathing stayed quick but regular. He wrote the time on Will's chart and left the diagnosis column blank.

Thalia came back with a cup of water and sat down without drinking. She looked at her sister for a long moment.

“Can I tell you a story about her?” she asked Percy. “It's one of mine. She may not want it told while she's awake.”

“You can tell me. I'll keep it yours unless she asks.”

Thalia nodded. She gathered the memory slowly, not like a warning, but as something she had carried for years and rarely set down. Her voice was low enough that it wouldn't wake Annabeth.

---

“You weren’t there,” Thalia said. “This was before we met you at camp. When Annabeth was small. Mom — Beryl — she tried, in her way, but she wasn’t good with sick kids. She panicked. Everything was drama. So it was mostly me.”

Percy nodded. He knew bits of this story, in fragments. Thalia rarely told it straight through.

“Annabeth was just past two,” Thalia continued. “Tiny. You think she’s stubborn now? You should’ve seen her at two. Blonde curls down to here, gray eyes huge, following me everywhere. Thally, Thally, Thally. She couldn’t say Thalia yet.”

A faint smile touched Percy’s mouth. “Thally.”

“Yeah. Thally.” Thalia’s own smile was sad and fond. “She caught this bug going around daycare. Fever spiked in the middle of the night. I woke up because the whole room smelled like ozone. Like right before lightning hits. I thought Dad was angry about something.”

Annabeth stirred faintly at the mention of Dad, her brow furrowing. Thalia smoothed it with her left thumb without pausing.

“I sat up and Annabeth was standing in her crib, holding the rails, crying. Her face was red as a tomato. She was burning up. And every time she cried, these little sparks would pop off the metal rails. Pop, pop, pop. Like tiny fireworks. She’d cry harder because it startled her, which made more sparks, which scared her more.”

Percy winced. “Poor kid.”

“I was six,” Thalia said. “Mom was passed out. Dad was distant. I didn't know what the fever meant. I knew Annabeth was scared, and she stopped crying for half a breath when I stood beside the crib. So I climbed in.”

“You climbed into the crib?” Percy whispered, trying not to laugh.

“Hey, I was skinny. It was a big crib. I pulled her into my lap and wrapped the blanket around us. She sparked against the rails and my sleeves. It stung, but I didn't want her to be alone with it.” Thalia paused. “I sang the song Mom used when she was lucid. The one about the moon and the sea.”

Percy listened. The tune touched the edge of a song Sally had hummed in the life he remembered, but he could not tell whether it was the same melody. He didn't claim it as his own to Thalia.

“I sat with her until the night passed. I remember having a washcloth in my hand and thinking it warmed up too fast. I don't know that it changed the fever. She was calmer when she could hear me.” Thalia took a breath. “In the morning there was rain, soft rain without thunder. I wanted to believe Dad had kept it gentle for her. I don't know what he did. I only know she wasn't crying anymore.”

Thalia rubbed her left thumb along the rim of the cup. “I remember being scared the sparks would hurt her. I don't remember knowing what to do with the fever. Those aren't the same thing, but at six I thought if I could get her to stop crying, I had fixed it.”

“Did you think you had to do it by yourself?” Percy asked.

“I thought I was the person awake.” She gave a small, tired shrug. “That's what I knew. I don't remember what Mom did when she woke up, or whether Dad really changed the weather. I remember the crib rail under my arm and her little hand grabbing my shirt. The rest gets fuzzy if I try to make it into a clean story.”

Percy let the pause stand. He knew what it was to want the missing pieces to explain why a memory had stayed. He also knew better than to fill in the part Thalia could not remember.

“I was six too,” she said. “I can see that now. Back then I thought leaving her alone would be the same as letting something happen.”

“You don't have to prove you care by keeping every watch.”

“I know.” Thalia looked toward Annabeth. “Knowing doesn't always get there before the fear does.”

“No,” Percy said. “It doesn't.”

She gave him a quick look, as if she had expected a smarter answer and was relieved he hadn't offered one. Then she set the cup down and listened for Annabeth's next breath, not reaching for her unless she woke and asked.

Percy tried to picture the toddler Thalia described. That was Thalia's memory, not his. The person in the bunk was the Annabeth he knew now: stubborn about a practice she had chosen, exact about the curtain, and still able to ask for what she needed through the fever.

“You've been beside her a long time,” he said.

Thalia shrugged. “She's my sister. I can be beside her without taking the whole watch.” She looked over at Percy. “I watched Annabeth do that for you during your sick day. She took her breaks, asked what you wanted and trusted Will's chart. It looked simple until I tried it.”

“It wasn't simple,” Percy said. “She had to keep asking because I was doing a bad job of saying what I needed.”

Thalia gave a tired smile. “You told Clarisse the soup was too salty.”

“I didn't want to lie.”

“Exactly.” Thalia glanced toward the bunk. “I need to do that with Annabeth. Let her tell me what helps.”

Annabeth shifted under the gray blanket. Her eyes stayed shut, but her mouth moved. “You can both stop arguing. It's loud.”

Percy and Thalia looked at each other. He lowered his voice. “Yes, ma'am.”

“Thank you,” Annabeth mumbled.


Clarisse and Silena returned with a refilled water bottle and a folded dry sheet. Will came behind them. He stopped at the foot of the bunk instead of reaching for Annabeth at once.

“Give me the short version,” he said.

Percy used the chart. “One hundred and three point two at the arena. No new reading since then. A few sparks jumped to the rail. We moved the metal tray and water away. She asked us to take the cool cloth off because it made her shiver. The dream scared her. She squeezed my fingers once, then settled.”

“Any trouble breathing? Vomiting?”

“No.”

Will put the thermometer where Annabeth could see it. “Annabeth, I need one more check. Is that okay?”

Her eyelids opened. She looked first at the light through the curtain, then at Thalia. “I'm in Cabin Three. It's Tuesday. My head hurts.”

“Can I take your temperature?”

“Yes. No cloth, though. It was too cold.”

“Understood.” Will checked her, then waited until she was ready before asking about the pulse. “One hundred and three point six. That's higher than before. I'm going to stay while we check whether the rest of you is steady.”

Annabeth swallowed. “Did I say anything?”

“You had a frightening dream,” Percy said. He kept his voice low. “You asked us to stay. I didn't tell them what it was about.”

She looked at the others. Thalia's face was worried, but she did not fill the silence. Annabeth nodded once. “Thank you. I don't want to talk about it while I'm sick.”

“Then we won't,” Clarisse said. She stayed by the doorway.

Will checked Annabeth's breathing and asked her to name where she was, who was in the room, and what she remembered from the last hour. She answered without hesitation, though she closed her eyes between questions. Her hands shook once; no spark followed. He told the group that a dream during sleep was different from being unable to orient while awake, but if she became confused again or had trouble breathing, they should bring her to the infirmary immediately and call for him. He could not identify the cause of the fever or say whether it had anything to do with the sparks.

“Keep water nearby for when she wants it. Let her sleep. A light sheet, a comfortable room, and checks on her through the night. No cold-water wrapping, no poultice, no nectar unless I specifically prescribe it. Nothing here tells us the fever's source.”

Annabeth opened one eye. “Can the people in this cabin take turns being quiet?”

“They can,” Will said.

“Good. I don't want a crowd when I wake up.”

Clarisse nodded. “I'll keep the doorway clear.”

Silena placed the water where Annabeth had pointed and pulled her own chair away from the bunk. “I'll finish dinner shift, then ask whether you want company. If you say no, I'll go back to my cabin.”

“That sounds good,” Annabeth said.

Will recorded the temperature and the time, then left the chart on the table. “I'll come back in an hour. If anything changes before then, send someone directly to me.”

Percy watched him go, then looked at the numbers on the page. He did not add a cause or a prediction. He wrote down the time and the reading, exactly as Will had given them.

Thalia rested her left hand against the edge of her chair. Her wrapped palm stayed in her lap. Clarisse set the spare sheet within reach. Silena washed her hands at the basin before leaving for dinner. Each person had a next step; no one had to guess.

When Annabeth closed her eyes again, Percy stayed by the chair. He wanted to hold her hand, but he waited until the static had stopped and she reached toward him. Then he let her rest her fingers across his.
---

Evening settled over the camp while Will made the first return check. Annabeth's fever remained high, but she could answer him and breathe without strain. He wrote the reading on the chart, asked whether she wanted him to stay, and waited for her answer.

“Can you come back in an hour?” she asked. “I don't want to answer questions every five minutes.”

“I can do that. Thalia or Percy will call if something changes.”

“Thank you.”

When Will had gone, Percy moved the chart beside the lamp so he could read it without lifting it over her. The page held the facts they knew: her temperature, the time, what she had drunk, and whether she had been awake. He left the space marked cause blank. The empty line bothered him more than he expected. He wanted a name he could blame and something he could fix. The chart offered neither.

Clarisse and Silena brought the evening meal in separate covered bowls. Annabeth had slept through the dinner conch. When she woke, she asked what time it was and whether she had missed the whole meal. Percy told her the sun was down and that the kitchen had saved broth and toast.

“Broth, please. No honey. I don't want anything sweet.”

Silena set the small bowl on the table and put a spoon beside it. “You can try a little, then stop.”

Annabeth managed a few spoonfuls. She paused to swallow, then handed the spoon back. “That's enough.”

“Got it.” Silena closed the bowl and took it away.

Clarisse leaned on the wall with her arms crossed. “You don't have to win a prize for eating.”

“I wasn't trying to.”

“Good. I don't have one.”

Annabeth's mouth twitched. She looked past Clarisse to the door. “Can you keep people from coming in just to look at me?”

“Already started,” Clarisse said. “They can ask how you are from outside.”

“Thanks.”

Silena checked the time. “I need to get back to dinner shift. I'll be in the pavilion until the dishes are done. If you want me after, tell Thalia.”

“Go,” Annabeth said. “I know you have work.”

Silena smiled, then went. She did not apologize for leaving.

Thalia took the chair by the bunk after Clarisse stepped into the doorway to turn away a pair of curious campers. Her bandaged right hand remained in her lap. Annabeth studied the wrap.

“Did Will check your palm today?”

“No. I haven't asked him. It still hurts if I press it.”

“Then don't press it.” Annabeth's eyes drifted shut and opened again. “You don't have to stay up because I'm sick.”

“I'm on watch.”

“You can take a turn. You don't have to take all of them.”

Thalia's first response came quickly. “I know.”

Annabeth waited.

Thalia glanced down at her bandaged hand. “I'm working on it.”

“Okay.” Annabeth shifted her pillow a fraction. “I want you here. I also want you to sleep before you fall over. Both things can be true.”

Thalia let out a breath through her nose. “You sound like Chiron.”

“That's awful.”

“It is.”

Percy tried to hide a laugh and failed. Annabeth's eyes found him.

“You too,” she said. “You had a fever recently. You need sleep.”

“Will cleared me for practice.”

“That isn't the same as a full night's sleep.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

He looked at the chart, then back at her. She was tired, but she was tracking the room. “I can take the first watch and sleep after Will checks you.”

“You can take the first hour,” she said. “Then Thalia takes one. Then Clarisse can take one while Silena finishes work. No one has to sit here all night. Will is close.”

“And you?” Thalia asked. “You haven't slept since dawn.”

“I'll sleep when you take your turn.”

“The chart has the order,” Annabeth said. “Percy takes this hour. Then you. Clarisse can keep the doorway clear until Silena is back from dinner.”

“I'm not playing healer,” Clarisse said. She had come back with the watch chart Will left. “If Annabeth wakes, I can ask if she wants water, keep people out, and call Will. I'm not guessing at symptoms.”

“Good,” Annabeth said. “Don't guess.”

Clarisse set the chart where Percy could reach it. “And if someone asks, I'll tell them you're resting. They don't need your readings.”

“Thank you.”

“They won't get anything else from me.” Clarisse glanced at Percy. “You heard that too.”

“I did.”

Thalia studied the rotation as if she were about to rewrite it. Annabeth's eyes remained closed, but she knew her sister well enough to catch the pause.

“You take your hour after Percy,” she said. “Then you sleep. You don't have to check every turn yourself.”

Thalia exhaled through her nose. “All right. My hour, then sleep.”

“That's the plan,” Annabeth murmured. “Not a dare.”

The others looked at each other. Clarisse gave a small nod. “That works.”

“Good,” Annabeth said. Her eyelids lowered. “I don't want to wake up and see four people staring at me.”

“We can arrange that,” Percy said.

“Please do.”

Clarisse and Silena were outside the door when Annabeth fell asleep again. Thalia stayed by the chair until the next time check, then stood carefully.

“I'll get a drink and wash my face,” she said. “I'll be back before Will.”

Percy nodded. She left the door open, as she had promised. The wrapped palm stayed close to her side.

The cabin felt different with fewer people in it. Percy could hear the conch from the dining pavilion, then the clatter of dishes being stacked. Beyond the porch, someone laughed and was shushed. Annabeth's breathing was quick but regular. The lamp lit one corner of the chart; the rest of the room stayed dim.

Percy read the temperature column without trying to predict the next entry. He could not make the number fall by watching it. He had learned that in the days of battles and quests: keeping watch was not the same as controlling what happened. It still took effort to sit still.

When Will returned, Percy stepped back and gave him room. Annabeth woke for the check and agreed to the thermometer. The reading was still high. Will recorded it and asked whether she knew where she was.

“Cabin Three,” she said. “The fountain is making that little clicking noise again.”

“Do you know who I am?”

“Will.”

“Good. Any vomiting? Trouble breathing?”

“No. My head hurts. I'm thirsty.”

Will checked her breathing, then passed the bottle to Percy for Annabeth to hold herself. She drank enough to wet her mouth and returned it.

“Do you want me to stay longer?”

“No. But come back when you said.”

“I will.”

After he left, Percy sat down again. Annabeth gave him a tired look. “You can read something if you want.”

“I don't have a book.”

“Then tell me something.”

“What kind of something?”

“Something ordinary. Not a battle.”

Percy thought of the lake and a summer evening when he had been twelve. The story he chose did not need to be about the lake's magic or an old quest. It was about a blue cereal box and Sally letting him think she hated the burnt edges of his cookies. He kept the details in the life he remembered, not in a childhood he had not lived here.

Annabeth listened with her eyes shut. “Did you know she was doing it?”

“Not at first.”

“What did you think?”

“That she hated the burnt parts.”

“And she let you think that?”

“For a while. Then one day I saw her scrape the dark edge off hers and put it on my plate when she thought I wasn't looking.” Percy smiled at the memory. “She used to read the maze on the cereal box wrong on purpose. I'd correct her, and she'd act like I'd solved something impossible.”

Annabeth's mouth tilted. “You enjoyed being right.”

“I was twelve. I enjoyed being right about everything.”

“That sounds familiar.”

“I have improved.”

“A little.”

The joke made it easier to keep talking. The story belonged to the life Percy remembered, and he didn't try to make it stand in for a childhood he couldn't recall. “I miss the small things more than I expected,” he said. “Not just the big moments. Her leaving the kitchen light on when I was supposed to be asleep. The noise of her putting dishes away.”

Annabeth didn't tell him that he should feel better or that the memory proved something. She was quiet for a while, then asked, “Do you want to tell me another one?”

“Maybe tomorrow.”

“Do you want me to ask, or leave it alone until you bring it up?”

Percy looked at the blue line of the night-light across the floor. The people in this life remembered the little boy who had lost Sally when he was two. He remembered the mother who had packed lunches, burned cookies and left the kitchen lamp on. Both truths sat in him at once, and sometimes he hated how easily other people assumed they knew which one he meant.

“You can ask,” he said. “Just don't act like telling you fixes it.”

“I won't.” Annabeth shifted the sheet against her wrist. “And you can say no if you don't want to answer.”

“I know.” He set the memory down between them without pretending it was a cure. “I wanted to tell you this one.”

“Then I'm glad you did.” She blinked slowly. “I wanted to know what you meant by ordinary. Now I do.”

Annabeth breathed out something close to a laugh.

“I thought you wanted ordinary.”

“I did.” She opened her eyes long enough to look at him. “You can tell me the rest when I'm not trying to stay awake.”

“Deal.”

She reached for the edge of the sheet, searching. Percy waited until the static had stopped before placing his hand on top of the blanket beside hers. Annabeth curled her fingers around two of his. When she drifted off, he kept his hand still and his eyes on the chart.

Thalia returned after washing. She took the first scheduled watch from Percy, and he lay down on the next bunk with his shoes still on. The small distance between them felt like a task he had been given: rest until the alarm, then come back if needed. He did not like leaving Annabeth, but she had asked him to take the turn. He let that be enough.

Clarisse took the next watch. She sat by the open door with a cup of water and a paperback she never opened. When Annabeth stirred, Clarisse said her name once and asked whether she wanted the bottle. Annabeth shook her head and went back to sleep. Clarisse marked the time without touching her.

Silena came back when the dinner shift ended. She washed her hands at the basin, asked Thalia whether Annabeth had been awake, and sat down only after Thalia said the room was quiet. She did not bring another remedy or a second helping. She brought the sailor song her mother used to hum, but waited until Annabeth's breathing deepened before she sang it under her breath.

The melody moved through the small room without changing the temperature or promising a cure. Percy, awake now, recognized the tune from the cove drill. It belonged to Silena and the story she had told him about her mother. In his mind, a different song from Sally remained separate. He let both stay where they belonged.

Near midnight Annabeth whimpered. Her hand closed on the blanket, and a blue spark jumped from her fingertips toward the bed rail. Silena stopped singing. Clarisse moved the metal cup farther away. Percy spoke from the chair, keeping his hands clear until the spark faded.

“Annie. It's Percy. You're in Cabin Three. The window is open a crack. Thalia's asleep on the cot. Nobody is asking you to get up.”

Annabeth's eyelids fluttered. “Don't let me fall.”

“I hear you. You're lying down. The mattress is under you.”

“Stay?”

“I can stay in the chair.”

She nodded once without opening her eyes. The spark did not return. Percy waited, then offered his hand. She took it. The warmth of her fingers steadied him, though he knew it could not lower the fever.

When she began to breathe more evenly, Percy loosened his grip so she could move away without waking him. He did not promise to hold her all night. He promised only what he could do: stay in the chair until Thalia woke for the next shift.

Thalia woke before the alarm and saw him still sitting there. She checked the chart before asking how Annabeth was. Percy gave her the reading and the time; he left interpretation to Will.

“Did you sleep at all?” she asked.

“An hour.”

“That's something.”

“It's not enough.”

“No,” she said. She poured herself water and took a drink. “You can lie down. I'll take the rest of this turn.”

“Annabeth wanted me to sleep.”

“Then do what she asked.”

Percy eased his fingers from Annabeth's. A tiny spark touched the blanket and went dark. He stood back, gave her space, and crossed to the next bunk. Thalia sat in the chair and kept the chart beside her, not her hand on Annabeth's face. When Annabeth stirred, Thalia asked before moving the blanket.

The hours passed with small, separate actions. A check. A sip when Annabeth asked. The room kept its night sounds: fountain, crickets, distant footsteps on the porch. Once, Annabeth woke clear enough to ask whether it was morning. It was not. Thalia told her that, and Annabeth let her eyes close again.

Will returned once more before dawn. Annabeth was asleep, but he checked her breathing and temperature without waking her. He wrote the result in the chart and told Thalia it had begun to come down. He did not claim the illness was over. If Annabeth woke confused, started vomiting or had trouble breathing, they were to bring her directly to the infirmary.

Thalia read the instructions back to him. Percy listened from the bunk. When Will asked if she understood, she said yes and repeated the signs that meant they should call. He left the chart on the table.

The fever did not vanish at once. Annabeth woke once more before the sky lightened, drank a few sips, and said her head still hurt. Percy offered to get Will. She shook her head, then looked at Thalia. Thalia asked if she was sure. Annabeth answered that she was clear, only tired, and wanted the room quiet. Thalia stayed within reach and gave her time.

At dawn the window showed a pale strip of sky. Percy had slept in pieces, never long enough to lose the shape of the room. Thalia's wrap remained dry; she had not used her right hand. Silena had gone back to her bunk after her shift. Clarisse was outside with the first counselor patrol, leaving the door unblocked.

Annabeth woke with her own pillow under her head and her gray blanket over her legs. Her eyes were clear. She looked at Percy and gave him a tired half smile.

“Hi, Seaweed Brain.”

“Hi, Wise Girl.”

He did not grab her. He waited. Annabeth set the mug of broth on the table, then held out her hand. Percy moved closer and took it.

“Did I say anything in my sleep?” she asked.

“You had a nightmare,” he said. “You asked me not to leave. I stayed close.”

“What did I say?”

He considered the question. “There were names and pieces of the old memory. I can tell you the words if you want.”

Annabeth was quiet for a moment. “Not while I'm this tired. And please don't repeat them to the camp.”

“I won't.”

Thalia sat up from the cot. “I told Percy a story about when you were two. It's my memory. I didn't mean to speak for you.”

Annabeth looked at her sister. “You can tell me later if you want. I don't remember the crib. I don't want either of us to pretend I do.”

Thalia nodded. “I know.”

“And the camp can know I was sick,” Annabeth said. “It doesn't need the dream.”

“Understood,” Clarisse called from the porch. She had come back in only far enough to leave the door open. “I can stop people from collecting details.”

Annabeth gave her a faint smile. “That would help.”

Will arrived with the thermometer and a clean page for the chart. He asked permission to check Annabeth, and she agreed. The reading was ninety-nine point one. Her pulse had slowed, and she could answer his questions without losing the thread. He said the fever had come down overnight; he could not name the exact hour or its cause.

Then he asked Thalia if she would let him look at her palm. Thalia turned her wrist so he could inspect the wrap without using the injured hand. The dressing was still dry. The burn remained tender, so Will left it protected and reminded her that she was not cleared to spar or brace with that hand. He wanted another check before she returned to training.

“Tomorrow morning?” Thalia asked.

“Tomorrow morning,” Will confirmed. “If it gets wetter, more painful, or numb before then, come find me.”

“Got it.” Thalia tucked the hand back into her lap.

“I'm glad it's lower,” he said. “You're still sick. Stay in the cabin today. You can choose Cabin Three or Zeus, but don't go back to practice until you've been fever-free for a full day and I clear you. If the confusion returns, or you have new trouble breathing or can't keep fluids down, come to the infirmary.”

“Cabin Three,” Annabeth said. “For today. I'll decide about tonight after you check me again.”

“Good.”

“Can I sit up to eat?”

“If you feel steady. Ask someone to help you the first time.”

Annabeth took the mug from the table and had a few careful sips. When she was done, she asked Silena to set it aside. She did not need the others to finish the breakfast for her or praise each bite. Silena did what she asked and went back to her shift.

Thalia's eyes were wet. She used her left hand to wipe them, keeping the bandaged palm tucked in her lap.

She took a small Iris-message from the shelf. “Do you want Grover to know you're awake?”

“Tell him the fever is down and I'm staying in Cabin Three today. You can say I'm hungry enough to ask for toast.” Annabeth looked toward the door. “No dream details.”

“Only that,” Thalia said. She sent the message, then set the device aside without waiting for a response.

“You stayed,” Annabeth said.

“I did.”

“I'm glad. But you can sleep now.”

Thalia tried to object. Annabeth raised an eyebrow.

“I'm listening,” Thalia said. She stood, checked the wrap on her palm without pressing it, and asked Percy to take the next quiet hour while she slept in the next bunk.

Percy agreed. Annabeth rested her hand on the blanket between them, close enough to reach and far enough that she could move it whenever she wanted.

The cabin began to wake. The fountain clicked once, then ran smoothly. Someone crossed the porch carrying breakfast dishes. Annabeth closed her eyes, then opened them again when Percy shifted.

“Still there?”

“Still here.”

“Good. I'm going to sleep now.”

“I'll be here when you wake.”

That was enough. Annabeth settled into the pillow. Thalia lay down on the other bunk with her injured hand protected. Percy took the chair beside Annabeth, not the role of sole guard, just his turn. The fever had eased; the cause remained unknown. Morning would bring another check and another decision, and Annabeth would have a say in both.
