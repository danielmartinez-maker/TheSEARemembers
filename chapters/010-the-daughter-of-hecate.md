## Chapter 10: The Daughter of Hecate

Zoe Nightshade had been awake since before first light, looking for the ledger someone had moved from the Hecate cabin without signing the borrowing sheet.

She found it beneath a stack of old field guides in the library's east alcove. The book was bound in cracked green cloth, and its corners were soft from being carried in damp bags by campers who had been told not to get it wet. The name on the inside cover belonged to an assistant who had worked on the Hall's original foundations. Someone had crossed it out and written a newer name beneath, with a date that made Zoe suspicious enough to fetch another volume for comparison.

The handwriting matched. That was interesting, and not nearly enough.

She took both books to the long table under the window and copied the dates into a clean notebook. Three years ago, the Hall's custodians had logged an inspection after a minor ward surge. The report included a sketch of a mark under the northern column. In the margin, a later hand had written *repair complete*. No signature followed. The same mark, or something close to it, appeared in an older drawing associated with the Big House attic. Neither entry identified the person who had installed the bronze.

Zoe knew what Percy would do when he saw the drawings. He'd ask whether they proved a connection. He'd look for a person to blame, and she'd have to remind him that a matching symbol was not a signature. She was tired of being the person who repeated that distinction. It was still her job to make it.

A knock came against the library's inner door. Nico stood there holding a folder with the same careful grip he used for his most valuable Mythomagic cards.

"Chiron said you'd want these."

"What are they?"

"Photocopies of the maintenance transfer sheets. I found the requests, but not the completed returns."

Zoe took them. "Did you check the catalog?"

"I checked three times. The archivist said if I check a fourth time she'll make me file the entire old correspondence series."

"She knows you would enjoy that."

Nico considered the accusation. "Probably."

He set the folder beside the ledger. Zoe moved her mug out of the way and made space for him to sit.

"The seal's drawing is only evidence that somebody recorded the design," she said. "If we want to learn who installed it, we need an actual work order or the testimony of someone who was there. If we want to know what it does now, we need an inspection we can repeat safely."

Nico opened the first folder. "Do you think the eclipse will make it worse?"

"I think the notation from the last eclipse is worth taking seriously. That doesn't mean the same thing will happen."

"That's what Chiron said."

"Then he and I agree on one thing."

Nico glanced at the chalk circle she'd drawn on a scrap of black paper. "Could you test the pattern without going near the Hall?"

"A simplified pattern, perhaps. Not the actual ward. I don't know what it's connected to, and anything that holds pressure may have been designed to react when it's copied."

His eyes widened. "It can tell when you draw it?"

"Some can. So we won't draw a complete working version. I want to compare the geometry with diagrams that are known to be inactive."

Nico leaned closer. He had a surprisingly good eye for differences in handwriting and proportion, although he still tended to think every unfinished task could be solved by keeping a better list.

"Then I'll find the older notebooks."

"You can find them with the archivist present," Zoe said. "You've already been warned twice about borrowing the restricted ledgers."

"Technically I was warned once and asked a pointed question the second time."

"You're not making a persuasive case."

He grinned and went. Zoe watched him leave with the photocopies folded beneath one arm, then turned back to the table.

The Hecate cabin had always been full of people who could make a small room feel large. One girl could darken a lamp without touching it. A boy named Tomas could make a reflected image appear half a second later than it should. Zoe's own talent lay in recognizing when someone had built a spell out of ordinary pieces and hidden the joins under ornament. It was useful for repairs and much less impressive at demonstrations, where everyone wanted to see a door open into nowhere.

She had once hated that difference. Her mother was a goddess of crossroads and magic. Surely Zoe ought to have been able to do something that made younger campers gasp. Instead she could spend half an afternoon studying a cracked stone and tell everyone the repair had been done in the wrong order.

After the storm she was grateful for the talent, even when it left her with more questions than answers.

At the far end of the table, an unopened letter lay beneath her field notebook. It was from an older cousin who'd offered to let her spend the winter outside camp. Zoe had been planning to answer before the Hall disaster. She wanted to go. She also had a responsibility to the younger Hecate campers who trusted her explanations, and now Chiron was asking for her expertise almost every day. She didn't know whether she could leave until the ward was secure.

The thought irritated her, partly because she'd always been quick to tell Percy and Annabeth that they didn't have to carry every problem alone.

She unfolded the letter and began an answer. She was halfway through the first paragraph when she heard Percy's footsteps outside. He moved like a boy trying to reach an answer before somebody shut a door, and she guessed he was carrying the same copied mark he'd shown Chiron.

Zoe set the letter aside. She would finish it after lunch, even if nobody stopped asking questions before then. She refused to spend another season being useful enough that she forgot she'd meant to have a life beyond the library.

---

Percy had noticed Zoe's empty place at the Hecate table before he had noticed his own cold porridge.

Now he sat opposite her in the library, with the mark from the Oracle's chair drawn between them.

The room smelled of old vellum and rain through an open window. Zoe's folio lay open to a diagram of the Hall of the Gods, its columns reduced to circles and its mosaics to careful squares. A chipped mug stood outside the drawing's edge. She had moved it there when he leaned over the table.

"This is a maintenance record," she said. "Not a spellbook."

"It has spell-shaped problems."

"Most buildings do, if a god has opinions about the foundations."

She pointed to a notation beneath the northern column. Three hooked strokes met around a small circle. One was scratched out.

"The man who repaired this floor noted it three years ago. He thought it belonged to the drainage wards. He couldn't find it on the earlier plan."

"Someone added it."

"Probably. The earlier plan might be incomplete."

Percy leaned back. Every time he thought they had reached an answer, somebody put another possibility beside it.

"You don't think it matters?"

"I think it matters enough to be accurate." Zoe turned a page. "Hecate is my mother. That lets me recognize some kinds of spellwork. It does not let me read an erased memory, much less identify someone from an inscription I haven't seen in person."

He nodded.

"Can you remove it?"

"Not safely yet."

Beyond the window a cart rattled over the path. Zoe waited for the noise to pass.

"Some wards join distant points," she said. "The strip under the Oracle's chair and the Hall floor may be connected. If one is a restraint and the other is holding pressure, cutting the wrong piece could send that pressure somewhere else."

"Into the camp."

"Or into whoever is standing nearest."

He thought of Annabeth sitting on the dusty attic floor, her hand shaking over the copied prophecy.

"Chiron wants the Hall empty."

"Good."

"There's an eclipse."

"Two days away." Zoe drew a line beneath a date in the ledger. "The last unexplained surge recorded here also happened during an eclipse. Different year, same notation afterward."

"So it happens again."

"It may. We prepare because it may, not because we've solved the person behind it."

Zoe laid three tracing sheets on top of the maintenance plan. She had marked the northern column on each but used different colors for the routes that ran beneath the Hall.

"What am I looking for?" Percy asked.

"Tell me what changes between the first and the third sheet."

He leaned forward. The first showed a drain leading outside the western wall. The second had a narrower channel branching toward the center. On the third, a protective inscription sat over the junction, drawn with the hooked strokes he'd seen in the attic.

"The drain is still there," he said, "but somebody built over the place where the channels meet."

"That's what the drawings suggest. We need to know whether the work was actually done that way." Zoe put her finger against the center of the sheet. "If the new ward was installed above an existing one, the later layer might interfere with a function the builders didn't understand. It might be an error. It might be deliberate. The failure still has to be repaired."

She asked him to take the top page away. He did, exposing the older survey.

"Now find the safest access to the original channel, without entering the Hall."

Percy traced the boundary of the building with his eyes. The western side had a narrow run of stone between the foundation and the hill. He knew it flooded during heavy rain because he'd helped keep the water off the lower path. There was a maintenance hatch near the wall, but the note beside it was partly obscured by a stain.

"Here," he said. "If the hatch still opens, the crew can inspect the drain from the outside."

"Good. What would you want them to check before they open it?"

"Whether the water's moving, whether anything inside is carrying a charge, and whether the hatch itself is part of the protection."

Zoe nodded. "That's what I told Chiron."

"You already told him?"

"This morning. I wanted to know whether you'd arrive at the same question without me giving you the answer."

Percy sat back. "You could have just asked if I was paying attention."

"I did. You gave me three answers."

She smiled briefly, and he found himself smiling back. Zoe had been so careful with the frightening parts of the investigation that he'd begun to forget she enjoyed a problem even when it wasn't dangerous.

A Hephaestus camper named Mara came through the library doors carrying a wooden sample box. Zoe waved her over and made room at the table. Mara put down three bolts, each tagged with a date and the name of the workshop that had inspected it.

"These aren't from the Hall," she said before Percy could ask. "They're unused stock from the same order. The smithy checked them for cracks and heat damage. They're ordinary bronze fittings."

Zoe examined the tags. "And the bolts removed from the Hall?"

"Still under Chiron's seal. Nobody's letting me cut them open until the protection team is certain they won't react."

Percy asked, "Why would an ordinary bolt react?"

"It might not," Mara replied. "But it's been holding a piece of a magical structure together for three years. I can't promise the metal is ordinary anymore because the paperwork says it started that way."

Zoe pointed at her. "That is the correct answer."

Mara looked at Percy. "She made me repeat it twice before she let me take the samples out of storage."

"Only because you kept saying 'probably safe,'" Zoe said.

"I was describing a range of possibilities."

"You were hoping you'd be allowed to hurry."

Mara chose not to dispute that. She handed Zoe a list of the inspection team's requirements: dry tools, a grounding frame provided by the forge, protective screens around the access hatch, and a clear route to the infirmary in case someone was hurt.

Percy read the list. "This is what happens when you let people who know what they're doing make the plan."

"You sound disappointed," Mara said.

"I'm mostly impressed they can get everybody to agree on the same list."

"They haven't," Zoe said. "The Hall custodian still thinks we're overreacting. He wants the exhibit reopened before the festival preparations."

"Which festival?" Percy asked.

"The one his budget depends on," Mara said. "Every year the Hall hosts visiting patrons. If it stays closed, he has to explain why his repairs haven't worked."

Percy considered the unsigned transfer receipt Nico had shown them. There were good reasons to want the Hall open that had nothing to do with secret prophecies. Embarrassment, money, habit, pride. None of those made a person innocent or guilty. They did make the investigation more complicated than accusing somebody whose handwriting looked strange.

Zoe asked Mara whether the custodian had authorized the new fittings three years ago.

"That's the problem," Mara answered. "He signed a receipt acknowledging delivery, but the installation record has a different seal. Nobody knows who actually supervised the work."

"Can you find the workers?" Percy asked.

"Some have left camp. One's in New York. Another was a temporary hire who gave a name the stores keeper doesn't recognize. We're checking. Until then, we don't put a suspect's face on a missing signature."

Mara took back the sample box. She had to return the bolts before noon, and the labels were too important to leave in a room full of curious campers. Zoe promised to send Chiron their revised sketch of the drainage junction.

When Mara left, Percy looked at the three sheets again. The drawings were ordinary enough that he could imagine bringing them to a mortal building inspector. The danger lay in the gap between what they knew about the structure and what the structure might do when the eclipse changed the conditions around it.

"Is there anything we can actually help with?" he asked.

"Two things. First, make sure the route maps at the pavilion still match the locations of the barriers. Second, stop passing around conclusions that aren't supported by the measurements."

"The first is easy."

"The second isn't impossible."

He thought about the expression on Thalia's face when he'd looked at her as though she were hiding something. Zoe had not named her. She hadn't needed to.

"I'll try," he said.

"That isn't a result."

"It's the only promise I can give before I've done the work."

She accepted it. He spent the next half hour helping her label the diagrams, then copied the maintenance hatch location onto a public map for the repair crew. It wasn't glamorous work. It would matter if somebody needed to get a person out of the Hall without stepping across the central floor.


---

Percy studied the drawing.

The Hall was not a building he remembered from his first childhood at camp. It was large, expensive, and placed as if everyone had always agreed it belonged there. Most campers had never questioned that.

He had treated their certainty as evidence of someone else's careful plan. But a building could shelter people and still hide something dangerous in its floor. Those facts did not identify the builder, or the hand that had altered their lives.

"Could a Hecate child do it?" he asked.

"Add a ward? Yes. Some of us."

"Change someone's memories?"

Zoe's expression changed.

"I've seen enchantments that hide an afternoon," she said. "Not anything that replaces years of a person's life."

"You must have theories."

"I have enough theories to fill that shelf. None would justify accusing someone." She tapped the drawing. "We can see this. We can compare its dates. We can tell Chiron where the dangerous part may be. Start with what your hands can hold."

He looked at his hands, still faintly sore from the storm.

"There are people here who would suffer if we changed anything back," he said. "Children who didn't write a prophecy or ask the gods to put wards under the Hall."

"Including you," Zoe said. 

"Including me."

She waited.

"What if whoever did it thought they were helping?"

"Then we still need to know what they did, and whether they intend to do more."

He thought of Silena leaning over her mending, alive and impatient with a tangled thread. Of Clarisse saving him a practice weapon that fitted his smaller grip. Of Thalia catching Annabeth by the elbow before she tripped over a loose board, then pretending it had been an accident.

Wanting to keep them did not tell him who had changed them.

"I need Annabeth," he said.

"Yes. Bring her the diagram." Zoe closed the folio. "I will take the ledger to Chiron myself. You shouldn't have to carry every explanation."

He stood, then hesitated.

"I asked if you did it."

"You did."

"It wasn't fair."

"It was a question." Her gaze held his. "Questions can be answered. Deciding the answer before you ask is where they become dangerous."

---

Zoe carried her drawings to the old forge annex after the lunch bell. It was the smallest workroom that had a proper stone floor, a sand bucket and enough ventilation to make Chiron comfortable with a supervised demonstration. He'd agreed to let her use it on the condition that the test would be entirely separate from the Hall's live wards.

"Separate," he'd repeated, while examining her proposal. "No salvaged metal. No piece taken from the Oracle's chair. Nothing that could establish a sympathetic link to either installation."

"I've written that down," Zoe had told him.

"Write it twice."

She had.

Mara met her at the annex with a wooden tray of ordinary copper strips and a canvas roll of tools. She was about two years older than Zoe and had the kind of patience that came from years spent explaining why a furnace could not be asked to work faster because somebody was in a hurry.

"Everything's clean," Mara said. "No hall metal. No inscriptions other than the ones you supplied for the control test."

Zoe checked the tags anyway. Three strips had been cut from the same piece of copper, then polished and cleaned. One carried a simple closed boundary symbol; another had the same pattern with its outer line broken. The third was blank. Each had been prepared using a harmless training sequence Zoe knew well enough to undo under Chiron's supervision.

She had spent the previous week staring at drawings of an object she could not safely touch. It felt almost indulgent to work with something whose origin she could verify.

"What's the question?" Mara asked, laying out the tools.

"If we see a hooked mark on the Hall and another beneath the Oracle's chair, are we justified in thinking they do the same thing?"

"Probably not."

"Exactly. So I want to show the inspection team how different two marks can behave when they're drawn from the same pattern."

She began with the control strip. The little boundary charm made a line of flour stand on its edge. A bead of water rolled toward it and stopped, not because Zoe was commanding the water, but because the strip carried a minor repulsion spell. There was no strain. She could feel the enchantment settle and remain quiet.

Mara watched the bead. "So it stops matter."

"Only this kind of matter, at this distance. If you throw something heavy enough, it passes right over. Watch."

She set a small stone against the boundary. It crossed. The water bead remained behind.

"That's useful," Mara said. "You can see the limit."

"Now the broken line."

Zoe replaced the strip. The bead crossed the flour, but its path curved slightly before it stopped. The charm had not failed completely. It directed a tiny amount of pressure sideways. The difference was small enough that a casual observer might have described both marks as *containment*. It was precisely the kind of casual description Zoe was trying to get the maintenance team to avoid.

She wrote the results down, including the fact that a heavier object had not been affected by either strip. A design that contained one thing might freely permit another. If the Hall's inscription had been repurposed from an older protection, a person interpreting the image alone might be badly wrong about what it held.

Chiron arrived while she was putting the strips away. He examined the control sheet and asked her to repeat the second test with the stone instead of the bead. The stone crossed the boundary again. He nodded, then gestured toward the diagrams on the table.

"What can you conclude?"

"That these training marks have different effects despite looking similar. It doesn't tell us what either live mark does."

"Good."

"What I want to test next is the completed drain layout. Not an active inscription. The physical path of the water."

"That is a matter for the engineers and the repair crew. If they approve it, you may observe."

Zoe accepted the limit, although her first instinct was to argue. She was tired of waiting for somebody else to sign off on measurements she felt capable of taking herself. She also remembered what had happened when Percy had treated the attic as a problem he could investigate without an adult. Being right about the existence of a danger had not made the visit harmless.

Mara put a lid over the copper tray. "I'd like a second person to watch the outlet. If the water goes where the plan says it should, the next thing we need is a way to prevent a surge reaching the evacuation path."

Zoe turned to Chiron. "Could we put another barrier outside the western wall before the eclipse? One that isn't connected to the existing ward?"

"I will discuss it with the repair team," he said. "You will need to demonstrate that the barrier cannot feed charge back into the Hall."

"Then we should make the prototype away from the site and bring only the approved pieces."

He smiled faintly. "You have just described the process I have been asking everyone to follow."

"It's easier to describe from inside a workshop."

"It's easier to obey once you understand why it exists," Chiron said.

When he left, Zoe stared at the blank strip. She had expected the test to produce a clear revelation about the mark under the Hall. Instead it had established why she should be skeptical of any claim based only on a copied design. That was useful, but it would not impress campers who wanted her to wave a hand and fix the Oracle.

Mara noticed her expression.

"You look disappointed."

"I wanted the control to tell me more."

"It told us something about the method. That's a result."

"You're starting to sound like me."

"I'm trying to keep you from spending the next two days doing everybody else's work." Mara picked up the tray. "Did you answer your cousin?"

Zoe glanced at the letter tucked into her satchel. "Not yet."

"Then do that before dinner. Chiron has a dozen people who can carry a barrier. Your cousin only has one person she's written to."

The comment was inconvenient, which made it effective. Zoe washed the copper dust from her hands and went back to the library to finish the letter. She wrote that she was needed at camp for the eclipse preparations but would send an answer about the winter visit after the inspection. It was a small act of refusing to disappear into her own usefulness.

She sealed the page before anyone came looking for her. Then she brought the control results to Chiron, marking clearly what the test demonstrated and what remained unknown.

---

Lake water slapped the dock posts. Annabeth sat at the end with bare feet skimming the surface and her notebook open on her knees.

"Chiron?" Percy asked.

"Took copies. Asked us to stay out of the attic. Asked me twice."

"Did he know anything?"

"He knew the dates disagreed. He hadn't found the earlier wording we copied." She closed the notebook. "He looked tired. Not surprised enough for my liking."

Percy sat down and told her what Zoe had shown him.

At the mention of the eclipse, her toes stopped moving.

"Two days."

"A possible surge. That's all we know."

She drew the Hall's drainage channel on a fresh page, using the diagram he had brought.

"Three days is enough to put barriers around the building. Move the training groups. Check the path to the infirmary."

"You sound like Chiron."

"He was taking notes while I suggested it."

"How did that feel?"

"Very satisfying. Until he suggested I remain behind the barriers myself."

Percy looked at her.

"He's right about that part."

"I know. I dislike how many people are right before lunch."

A dragonfly settled between them, then lifted away when she moved the paper.

"I keep looking at Thalia," Percy admitted.

Annabeth's pencil paused.

"As a suspect."

"I don't mean to."

"You mean to enough that I notice."

He could have defended himself. Instead he watched a duck pick its way between the reeds.

"She has things she didn't have before."

"So do you."

"I know."

"She's frightened because I don't know our own childhood. Not because she knows the childhood I do remember."

He nodded.

Annabeth folded the diagram along its old crease.

"Tell her what we found. Tell her what we don't know, too. If we need her help, we can't begin by making her defend herself against a story she never lived."

---

That afternoon, Annabeth met the evacuation team at the lower path with two copied maps and a headache she refused to acknowledge.

Bea had set three wooden stakes along the edge of the drainage ditch. Clarisse stood beside the first with a measuring rope, while a pair of Hephaestus campers worked on a portable footbridge made from salvaged practice-yard planks. Thalia was checking the western route with a group of counselors and had left instructions that nobody was to cross the ditch until the bridge had been tested.

Annabeth arrived in time to hear one of the builders announce that the bridge was finished.

"It isn't," she said.

The boy turned. "We tightened every bolt."

"That's not the same as testing it under weight."

"We stood on it."

"One of you stood in the middle for three seconds. What happens when eight frightened campers cross at once and the board underneath them is wet?"

He looked irritated, which Annabeth understood. She hated having someone correct work she had spent hours on. That didn't make the question less useful.

Bea intervened. "We're here to test it. Nobody's saying the bridge is bad. We just need to know what it can carry."

They ran a trial with sacks of sand instead of children. Clarisse and the builders carried the first four across; Annabeth timed the crossing and checked whether the rails moved. When they added a fifth sack, one of the posts shifted a little in the mud. It would have been easy to miss if Annabeth hadn't been measuring against a stake they'd fixed in the ground.

"Stop," she said.

The builders halted. Bea checked the distance. The bridge itself hadn't broken, but the support on the far bank had begun to settle.

"That will be worse if there's more rain," Annabeth said.

"We can put another plank under the foot," suggested one of the builders.

"Or shift the bridge upstream where the ground's firmer," Bea replied.

Clarisse looked toward the Hall. "Moving it adds another turn. If the campers come around the west side they'll lose sight of the pavilion. That's where people went wrong during the storm."

Annabeth unfolded her second map. The original plan had shown a narrower footpath behind the archery shed. She had dismissed it because it was too steep for a large group, but it might work if they used it for a smaller group and kept the bridge for the remaining campers.

"Could we divide them by cabin?" she asked. "Not so everybody has to make the same crossing at once. A leader takes the youngest by the gentler route. Everyone else uses the upper bank."

"That only works if each counselor knows which route their cabin has," Clarisse said.

"We can put the assignments on the board before the eclipse. And we can practice once with the counselors, not the younger campers."

Bea ran a hand down the length of the railing. "We'd need an extra pair of people at the bend."

"I can take that," Clarisse said. "If you make the route clear enough I don't have to argue with anyone about it."

Annabeth wrote the change down and looked at the little bridge. She still wanted it repaired rather than discarded. The builders had put real work into it, and they'd be needed elsewhere if the Ward team demanded additional barriers.

"Can you reinforce the footing and test again tomorrow morning?" she asked.

"Yes," said the boy she'd corrected. After a moment he added, "You could have said the bank was soft instead of making it sound like we were idiots."

Annabeth felt heat rise in her face. "You're right. I was worried somebody would rush across it before we finished checking. I didn't mean to dismiss your work."

He shrugged, not entirely appeased, and started taking measurements. She was glad he hadn't pretended the apology resolved the irritation. He would be the one working on the bridge after everyone else went to dinner.

The rest of the trial took almost an hour. Annabeth marked timings for each route, listed where the signs could be confusing and crossed out the assumption that every child would be able to run the same distance. She asked Bea to time the route at walking speed, and they discovered the gentler path took longer than the drawing suggested.

At the last turn, Percy arrived with the revised supply list from Silena. He had a bundle of marked straps over one shoulder and the expression he wore when somebody had given him a task and told him not to improvise.

"You have a map emergency?" he asked.

"We have a bridge that needs another footing."

"I brought straps."

"Excellent. You can assist the engineers without making a suggestion about water."

"I was going to suggest we carry the ditch away."

Clarisse took the straps from him. "You can carry sand. That might actually help."

Percy went with her without arguing. Annabeth watched him move toward the builders and noticed that he wasn't reaching to steady himself on every post he passed. His forearm was healing, and he had been careful about using it for heavier work. The change relieved her, though she didn't say so.

Thalia returned when the last trial ended. She stood beside Annabeth and looked at the two maps spread across a wooden crate.

"You changed the assignments."

"We found a weak bank. I don't want everybody using the same bridge."

Thalia checked the annotated timings. "Good. I'll tell the counselors. Have you told Chiron?"

"Not yet. Bea is taking him the revised plan."

"Then we're finished here."

Annabeth looked toward the work crew. "They still need a hand reinforcing the bank."

"They do. You need supper."

"I'm not a child."

"You're twelve. So are some of the people testing that bridge. Everyone needs supper."

Annabeth could have argued. Instead she folded the map and let her sister carry one end of the measuring rope back toward the pavilion. They were supposed to test routes because an eclipse might make the Hall dangerous. It occurred to her that a plan was only useful if the people who made it had enough judgment left to follow it.

Over dinner she rewrote the assignments in larger handwriting so the younger counselors could read them without squinting. Thalia ate beside her, offering observations about the steeper path and correcting one direction Annabeth had marked from memory rather than measurement. The corrections did not feel like an accusation. They felt like work they were doing together.

By the time the plates were cleared, Annabeth had an updated map she trusted and a list of questions for Chiron that no longer began with the Oracle. The questions about the Oracle were still there. She put them in a separate notebook before returning to Cabin One.

---

Sleep would not come that night.

Clarisse breathed roughly from her bunk across Cabin Three. Silena had turned her lamp low, leaving a warm square on the blanket tucked around her knees. She looked up when Percy reached for his shoes.

"Going somewhere?"

"For air."

She glanced at the folded diagram in his hand.

"Air with homework."

"Something like that."

"Stay where somebody can find you."

He nodded and slipped outside.

The path smelled of cooling pine needles. Near the armory he found Clarisse waiting on a stump, one boot in her hands.

"You were snoring," he said.

"Practice. Discourages conversation."

"You've fooled generations."

She pulled the boot on and eyed his paper.

"Silena saw that at dinner. Said you looked ready to eat it."

He sat beside her and explained the mark, the ledger, the eclipse. Clarisse listened without interrupting.

"What needs doing?" she asked when he finished.

"Chiron's moving people away from the Hall. Zoe's checking the ward."

"And you?"

"Trying not to make up the missing parts."

"Good project."

He gave her a tired look.

"You turned up last month and now you're my brother," she said. "That doesn't make me a clue in somebody's prophecy. And it doesn't mean Silena and I were waiting around for you to solve our lives."

"I didn't say—"

"I know. But you're watching all of us like you expect a confession. It's starting to itch."

He rubbed the edge of the paper between his fingers.

"I don't think you did it."

"Why?"

"Because you tell me when something's wrong. Even when you wish I'd stop asking."

"Reasonable." She stood. "And if you need someone to carry sandbags, ask. I'm better at that than mysterious prophecy."

He stood with her.

"Thanks."

"Sleep first. You look like a drowned sock."

"Very specific."

"I've met a lot of drowned socks. You're brothers."

He laughed despite himself. She bumped his shoulder on the walk back, then took the diagram long enough to work out which path Chiron meant to close.

---

Talking with Thalia took until the following afternoon.

She came from patrol with mud up one boot and a loose thread hanging from her jacket cuff. Percy found her by the spear racks and asked for five minutes.

"Does Annabeth know you're asking?"

"She told me to."

"Excellent. A conspiracy with paperwork."

He showed her the drawing.

Thalia stopped joking when he explained the mark beneath the Oracle's chair.

"Annabeth was there?"

"With me."

"Did it touch her?"

"No."

"Did it try?"

He told her about the black thread of smoke. Her fingers tightened around the spear she had been cleaning.

"And you're telling me now."

"Chiron has the notes. Zoe has the ledger. I should have spoken to you sooner."

"Yes."

The answer was so plain he could not retreat into an argument.

He went through the precautions. As he spoke, her gaze kept moving from him to the drawing and back.

"You think my father has something to do with it," she said.

He hesitated.

"He's one possibility."

"Because he changes his face, breaks promises, and makes us miserable?"

"Because his children seem tied to it. Poseidon's, too."

"We're tied to everything Olympus does. That isn't proof."

"No."

"Then say that when you speak about him."

A woodpecker worked at a dead branch beyond the fence. Percy let its steady tapping fill the pause.

"I'm scared," he said.

Thalia looked away from the drawing.

"I know what he can be like," he continued. "And I keep thinking if I name the person, I can stop watching every doorway."

"I do that with monsters." She set her spear back in the rack. "It doesn't work on fathers."

He nodded.

"He's cold. He's careless. Sometimes he's kind enough to make me forget why I was angry." Her voice became quieter. "But I won't decide he caused whatever's happening to Annabeth just because I'm angry with him."

"Neither will I."

"Good." She returned his paper. "And keep me informed about the Hall. I can help with a sky surge. I can't help with something everyone has decided I mustn't hear."

---

That night Annabeth waited on the Zeus cabin roof. She had permission to be there until the evening conch, a detail she supplied before Percy could ask.

The shingles held the day's warmth. Below, cabin windows shone in small amber squares. Silena and Clarisse were arguing across the path about whose turn it was to fetch clean towels.

"How did it go?" Annabeth asked.

"I got told off."

"Good."

"I apologized."

"Better."

He settled beside her. She leaned her shoulder against his.

"She asked me what I remember," Annabeth said.

"What did you tell her?"

"Some things that don't fit the childhood she remembers. That I remember you before you arrived. That there were wars. She looked at me as if I had spoken a different language."

"Did she believe you?"

"She believed I was frightened. She also asked whether this was another nightmare after the fever." Annabeth rubbed a thumb over the edge of her notebook. "I told her I didn't know how to prove it. I don't."

"What did she say?"

"That she'd rather hear something difficult than spend another month guessing. Then she asked whether I still wanted her to braid my hair tomorrow."

He watched her fingers pick at a rough shingle.

"How did she take it?"

A small smile moved across her mouth. "As if that were the hardest question."

"Maybe it was."

Annabeth rested her head against his shoulder. He could feel how tired she was from the way she let it stay there.

"We're keeping the barriers," she said. "Even if nothing happens during the eclipse."

"We can look magnificently foolish behind them."

"I'll draw a commemorative diagram."

"Label my good side."

"You have two. Very inconvenient for planning."

He laughed, and she looked up long enough to kiss him. They knew each other's awkwardness in this smaller present; they did not have to mistake it for a first beginning.

When the conch sounded, he helped her down from the roof. Thalia stood in the cabin doorway with a coil of cord and two folded jackets.

"Tomorrow we check the route to the infirmary," she said. "All three of us. Nobody improvises alone."

Annabeth took a jacket.

"That includes you."

Thalia's mouth twitched.

"Apparently."

Percy walked back to Cabin Three with the diagram in his pocket. Two days had become one. The danger still had no name, but the people helping him did.
