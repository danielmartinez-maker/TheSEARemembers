## Chapter 205: The Shape of No

The bed plan had begun as a rectangle.

Annabeth had drawn it on the back of an old supply sheet, then erased it when the table needed more room. In the morning light, the pencil marks still showed through: two parallel lines, a narrow gap, the corner of the window. She could see where she had pressed hard enough to leave a groove.

The stool stood on its side beside the wall. The glue along its repaired leg had dried clear at the edge and cloudy in the middle. Chiron said to let it sit another day. Annabeth had moved it herself last night and left it where no one would bump the joint.

Thalia sat at the table with her boot unfastened and her foot resting on a folded blanket. The splint was visible beneath her cuff. She had not tried the heel on the floor yet.

“Are you going to the hall tonight?” Annabeth asked.

“I haven’t decided.”

“You said you’d stay dry if you went.”

“I did.”

“Do you still want to?”

Thalia’s fingers moved once along the side of the blanket, then stopped. “I don’t know if I want to go enough to deal with the floor.”

“Then you can decide later.”

“I can decide now.”

“Do you want to?”

Thalia looked at her. “No. I’m not going tonight.”

“Okay.”

“That’s it?”

“What do you want me to add?”

“Nothing.” Thalia flexed her toes inside the sock. “I’m just checking whether you’ve started making a list.”

Annabeth smiled. “I left my book closed.”

“Very restrained.”

“I’m practicing.”

The room held the quiet for a moment. A curl of steam rose from Annabeth’s tea and blurred the bottom edge of the window. Outside, the camp had the muted gray look of a morning that might turn to rain or clear without warning. The firepit smelled faintly of ash.

A knock sounded at the cabin door.

Thalia glanced at Annabeth. “You expecting someone?”

“No.”

“Then we’re even.”

Annabeth crossed to the door. “Who is it?”

“Ianthe.”

Annabeth opened it.

Ianthe stood on the porch with a rolled sheet under one arm and the winter list in her hand. Her coat was buttoned wrong at the collar; one side sat higher than the other. She had a smear of graphite on her thumb and a determined line between her eyebrows.

“Can I come in?” she asked.

“You can.”

Ianthe entered, careful not to step on the threshold rug. Her gaze moved to the stool, the table, the window, then to Thalia’s foot.

“I brought another drawing.”

“Is it the bed plan?” Annabeth asked.

“It’s a room plan. I measured the wall and the table.” She put the list down beside Annabeth’s tea, then unrolled the paper across the cleared part of the table. “This version gives the window side more space. The bed fits if the stool moves over here.”

Annabeth stayed standing. She could see the plan without leaning over it. The third bed was drawn beside the window, just as before. A narrow rectangle at the table’s end represented the stool. The route from the door looked clear. The cupboard door was not drawn open.

“The cot still hasn’t been checked,” Annabeth said.

“I know. Chiron said the carpenter would look at it.”

“He hasn’t.”

“Which is why it’s a plan.”

“It has your bed on it.”

“I’m trying to find out if there’s room before the cot gets here.”

“I said we could look at another drawing.”

“You did.”

“That doesn’t mean we agreed to move anything.”

Ianthe’s face tightened, just slightly. “I’m not moving the bed. I’m showing you that the room can work.”

Annabeth leaned over the page. She traced the line from the door to the table without touching the paper. The drawing was careful. The measurements were written in the margins in small, even numbers. Ianthe had drawn the table to scale this time. She had also put the stool exactly where the cupboard would open.

“You’ve moved the stool into the cupboard’s swing,” Annabeth said.

“I accounted for the door.”

“It opens out.”

“I measured it.”

“Then the drawing is wrong.”

Ianthe looked down at the page. “The measurement is right.”

“The arc isn’t.”

“It’s the same width.”

“It isn’t the same movement.”

A pale line appeared between Ianthe’s eyebrows. She pulled the pencil from her sleeve and drew the cupboard’s door arc in a smooth semicircle. The curved line crossed the stool.

She stopped.

Annabeth waited for her to erase the mark. Ianthe did not. She lifted her pencil and shifted the stool shape a little farther toward the wall.

“That still leaves room,” she said.

“Not with the actual stool there.”

“I know it’s curing.”

“Then leave it where it is.”

“I was planning to.”

“Your drawing moves it.”

“It moves it on paper.”

“That doesn’t mean you get to move it in the room.”

“I didn’t say I was going to.”

“I said not to move it.”

“I heard you.”

The words were small, but Annabeth felt the old pressure in them: the certainty that her answer was a problem to solve rather than a limit to respect. Ianthe looked at the plan and began measuring the space between the table and wall with her eyes.

“I could shift this,” she said. “That would make enough room for the stool to stay here.”

“You can’t shift the table by yourself.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Did you ask?”

Ianthe looked up. “Why would I ask about the table if we’re only planning?”

“Because it’s the table we use.”

“We could put it in the Big House until the cot comes.”

“No.”

“I’m trying to make space.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you acting like I’m trying to take something?”

Annabeth moved between the drawing and the stool, not touching either. “Because you keep telling me what you’ll move before asking whether I want it moved.”

“I’m not asking you to move your things.”

“You just told me where my table should go.”

“I’m saying where it fits.”

“On a drawing.”

“That is what drawings are for.”

Thalia shifted in her chair. Annabeth heard the chair leg scrape over the floor.

Ianthe glanced at her. “I’m not trying to make this difficult.”

“I know,” Annabeth said.

“You don’t sound like you know.”

“I know what you’re trying to do. I still don’t agree.”

Ianthe reached for the stool. “Let me show you the distance. I can hold it while you see—”

“Don’t move it,” Annabeth said.

The stool’s front leg lifted a fraction from the floor.

Thalia pushed back from the table.

“Put it down,” she said.

It came out with an edge that seemed to arrive before the words. Annabeth turned toward her. Thalia was already on her feet, her injured heel angled away from the boards. Her hands were open at her sides.

Ianthe froze with one hand on the stool.

Annabeth felt both sisters waiting for her to choose which one to answer.

“Thalia,” she said. “I can answer.”

“I know.”

“Then let me.”

Thalia did not sit. “I’m letting you.”

“You already answered for me.”

“I told her not to move the stool.”

“I had already told her that.”

The space between them tightened. Thalia’s hand closed, then opened again.

“I heard you,” she said.

“Then let the answer be mine.”

Annabeth turned back to Ianthe. “Put the stool down. Carefully.”

Ianthe lowered it. The repaired leg touched the floor with a faint click. The joint shifted a little, the glue line bending enough for Annabeth to see the movement.

“Stop,” she said.

Ianthe kept her hand on the seat. “It moved.”

“Don’t adjust it.”

“I’m holding it.”

“Put your hand away.”

Ianthe lifted both hands. The stool stayed upright, but the leg sat a hair off square. Annabeth did not reach for it. If she touched it while the glue was fresh, the joint might slip further.

“I’m sorry,” Ianthe said, looking at the stool rather than at her. “I thought I could move it without—”

“You heard me say not to move it.”

“I thought you meant not to use it.”

“You decided what I meant after I told you.”

Thalia made a small sound. Annabeth kept her eyes on Ianthe.

“The stool was curing. You moved it anyway. I need it to stay exactly where it is.”

Ianthe drew her hands close to her chest. “I said I’m sorry.”

“I heard you.”

“What else do you want me to say?”

“Nothing right now. I need you to step back.”

Ianthe looked at the ruler, then at the mark on the floor where the stool had been. She took one step away from it.

Annabeth pointed to the paper. “The drawing can stay on the table. The stool stays put. No one moves the table or my things without asking me, and a no ends the question.”

“I wasn’t moving your things.”

“You were about to.”

“I was trying to show you a plan.”

“You started moving the stool after I told you not to.”

Ianthe’s jaw shifted. “Fine.”

“Don’t say fine if you don’t mean it.”

“Then I don’t know what to say.”

“You can say you’re angry.”

“I’m not angry.”

Annabeth looked at her.

“I’m frustrated,” Ianthe said. “I made the drawing better and now the actual room is in the way.”

“The actual room is the part we live in.”

“Right.”

The apology had not gone anywhere. Neither had the plan. Annabeth put a finger on the paper’s corner, holding it in place without smoothing the whole sheet flat.

“Give the glue another day,” she said. “Don’t touch the stool.”

“I said I would.”

“You said you were planning to.”

Ianthe flinched as if Annabeth had pushed her, though Annabeth had not moved. “I can leave.”

“You can.”

She gathered the winter list from beside the tea. Her eyes caught on the pencil mark at the cupboard arc. “Do you want me to take the drawing?”

“No. You can leave it.”

“Why?”

“Because you brought it. It’s yours.”

Ianthe rolled the corners inward, then stopped. She laid the paper flat again.

“Can I take the measurements tomorrow?” she asked.

“Ask me tomorrow.”

“I’m asking now.”

“Tomorrow’s different.”

Ianthe pressed her lips together. “All right.”

Thalia still stood by the table. Annabeth heard her shift her weight and did not turn toward her yet.

Ianthe picked up the list. “I’ll tell Chiron the cot still hasn’t been checked.”

“That’s fine.”

She walked to the door, then paused with her hand on the latch. “I didn’t mean to break it.”

“It’s not broken.”

“But the leg moved.”

“It did.”

“Then I made it worse.”

“Yes.”

The word sat between them.

Ianthe nodded once. “I’ll come back tomorrow.”

“Ask first.”

“I will.”

She left, closing the door behind her with more care than she had opened it.

Annabeth looked at the stool. The leg had shifted only a little. It might settle if left alone. She would need to check it with Chiron before adding glue. The repair had lost a day, perhaps more.

Thalia’s shadow fell across the floor, but she did not come closer.

“Do you want me to sit down?” Thalia asked.

“Do you want to?”

“No.”

“Then you don’t have to.”

Thalia let out a breath. “I’m sorry.”

“For what?”

“For stepping in before you needed me.”

“You thought I needed you.”

“I did.”

“You were wrong.”

“Yes.”

Annabeth heard how quickly she said it, and how much work had gone into not adding a reason after it.

“I had already said no,” Annabeth said. “I needed her to hear it. I didn’t need you to make it louder.”

“I thought she was going to hurt the stool.”

“She did move it. That’s why I needed you to let me tell her.”

Thalia glanced at the stool. “I should’ve waited.”

“Yes.”

“I don’t like waiting when someone is about to ignore you.”

“I know.”

“Do you want me to leave?”

“No.”

“Do you want me to sit?”

“Yes.”

Thalia eased herself back into the chair and adjusted the blanket beneath her foot. She did it slowly, keeping weight off the heel. Annabeth lowered herself to the bench opposite her.

The drawing remained spread on the table between them. The window bed fit in the pencil room. The real room had not agreed to it.



---

Annabeth waited until the door had stopped vibrating in its frame.

Thalia sat down again, both hands resting on the edge of the table. The folded blanket had slipped away from her heel. She adjusted it, then checked the splint strap with one finger.

“Does it hurt?” Annabeth asked.

“No.”

“Do you want me to get Chiron?”

“No.”

Annabeth nodded. She looked at the stool without touching it. “I should tell him the joint shifted.”

“I can get him.”

“Thalia.”

“I said I could.”

“I can ask him.”

Thalia’s eyes lowered to the blanket. “Right.”

Annabeth pulled the chair opposite her and sat. The room felt different with the drawing spread between them and the stool pressed back against the wall. Nothing had been rearranged, but Ianthe’s pencil marks had turned the space into a question someone else expected Annabeth to solve.

“You can still go to her,” Thalia said.

“Go where?”

“To the door. Tell her she can bring the drawing back tomorrow.”

“I don’t want to.”

“Okay.”

“I don’t want you to do it either.”

“I heard you.”

“You heard me after you’d already stood up.”

Thalia’s hands flattened on the table. “I saw her lift the stool.”

“So did I.”

“You’d said not to.”

“I had.”

“She did it anyway.”

“Yes.”

“I wanted to stop it.”

“I know.”

Annabeth waited for Thalia to defend herself. She could see the words gathering in her face: the stool was curing, Annabeth was right, Ianthe shouldn’t have ignored her. Those things were true. They were also not the whole of the conversation Annabeth was having with her sister.

“I want you to understand something,” Annabeth said. “When she ignored me, I needed her to hear my answer. When you spoke first, I had to stop both of you.”

“I wasn’t trying to make you choose.”

“You made me.”

Thalia turned the mug between her palms. “I thought I was backing you up.”

“You were.”

The answer seemed to surprise her.

“I did need you to know I meant no,” Annabeth said. “I did not need you to give the no for me. There’s a difference.”

“I know the difference.”

“Do you?”

Thalia looked up.

Annabeth kept her voice level. She did not want to make the point sharp enough that it became a punishment. “You had to tell Percy yesterday that being worried didn’t make the choice his. This morning you saw Ianthe ignore me, and you took the choice anyway.”

Thalia’s gaze flicked to the stool. “That’s fair.”

“I’m not comparing them exactly.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I do. Percy was worried about your foot. Ianthe was trying to move the stool. They’re not the same thing.”

“Good.”

“I still shouldn’t have answered for you.”

“No.”

Thalia let out a slow breath. “I can try to wait next time.”

“I’d like that.”

“I can’t promise I’ll always get it right.”

“I’m not asking you to.”

“Would you want a signal?”

“Like what?”

“If you want me to step in, say my name. If you don’t, I wait.”

Annabeth considered. She liked the intention, though not the idea of turning every disagreement into a code. “You can ask me quietly if I want help. Once.”

“And if you say no?”

“I finish what I’m saying.”

“Even if I still think she’s being unfair?”

“Yes.”

Thalia nodded. “Okay.”

“If I say I need you, then you can be there.”

“I’m already here.”

“That’s different.”

“I know.”

The words did not fix the moment. They gave it a place to go. Thalia stayed seated instead of trying to make up for what she had done with a grand gesture. Annabeth had not asked for one.

She glanced at the window. “I don’t want to go to the hall tonight.”

“Because of the floor?”

“Because I don’t want to spend the evening deciding whether it’s safe enough to sit in a room.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I know. I’m telling you what I want.”

“All right.”

“I wanted to go yesterday.”

“I know.”

“Now I don’t.”

“Okay.”

Thalia leaned back. “You’re very calm about it.”

“I’m trying to let your answer be the whole answer.”

“Does that work?”

“Sometimes.”

“That’s better than pretending it always does.”

Annabeth picked up the drawing’s loose corner, then let it fall. “I’m going to tell Chiron about the stool. I’ll ask him what to do. I don’t want you to get up.”

“I can get up.”

“I know you can.”

“I’m not a porcelain jar.”

“I didn’t say you were.”

“You looked at my heel.”

“Because the splint shifted.”

“It didn’t.”

“You checked it.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No,” Annabeth said. “It isn’t.”

Thalia looked at her, then gave a small nod. “You can go.”

“Do you want me to close the door?”

“No. I want the room quiet.”

Annabeth stood. She left the drawing where it was and the stool where it had been. At the threshold, she turned back.

“Will you be all right here?”

Thalia’s expression sharpened. “Do you hear yourself?”

“I do.”

“I’m going to sit at the table and not touch the stool.”

“Right.”

“Go ask Chiron.”

“I will.”

“And don’t tell him I made it worse.”

“You didn’t.”

“Tell him the joint moved.”

“I will.”

Annabeth stepped out. She left the door unlatched, open the width of her hand.



---

The Big House workshop smelled of cedar, iron filings, and wet leather.

Annabeth found Chiron sorting old hinges into a shallow tray. He had a pair of spring clamps on the table beside him and a paper cup of tea near the window. One of the clamps had a blue-painted handle, its color worn off at the pressure points.

“Did you bring the stool?” he asked.

“I left it in Cabin One.”

“Good. I can look at the joint without moving it again.”

“It shifted when it was lifted.”

“Did the leg come loose?”

“Not all the way.”

“Then we can reset it. The glue needs another full day.”

Annabeth looked at the clamps. “Can I use one?”

“Of course.”

“I want to do it.”

“I thought you might.”

He took the clamps from the table and placed them in her hands. She tested the spring and checked the screw head. The blue handle squeaked.

“Is this one all right?”

“It holds. The squeak is only the handle.”

She set it down. “I can check the joint and decide where to clamp it.”

“You can.”

“Are you asking whether I want you to watch?”

“I was going to ask whether you want me to help.”

“I don’t.”

“All right.”

Chiron picked up his tea. He did not ask who had moved the stool. Annabeth liked that he waited for her to decide whether the answer mattered.

“Ianthe touched it,” she said. “After I told her not to move it.”

Chiron set his cup down. “Did the joint shift?”

“Yes.”

“Then we’ll check it.”

“I don’t need you to speak to her.”

“I wasn’t planning to.”

She nodded. The answer let her keep the conversation where she wanted it.

A second set of footsteps crossed the porch. Ianthe appeared in the doorway with the drawing rolled under her arm. She stopped when she saw Annabeth and the clamps.

“I thought the stool was in the cabin.”

“It is,” Annabeth said.

“Are you taking a clamp?”

“I am.”

“I can carry it.”

“I can carry it.”

Ianthe looked at the blue-handled clamp. “I know you can.”

Annabeth waited.

“I was offering,” Ianthe added.

“I don’t want help with the clamp.”

“Okay.”

Chiron moved a hinge into a different pile. His attention seemed to be on the tray, but he was close enough to hear. Annabeth chose not to lower her voice.

“Did you come to talk about the drawing?” she asked.

“I came to show Chiron the measurements.”

“I don’t want to look at it right now.”

“I didn’t ask you to.”

“Good.”

Ianthe unrolled the paper and laid it on the table, keeping it on her side. “The cot measurements are still missing. I know.”

“Then the plan has a blank.”

“It has a provisional shape.”

“Which isn’t the same as a measurement.”

“I know that too.”

“Okay.”

Ianthe’s hand rested on the edge of the paper. “I’m trying not to make it sound like I expect to move in today.”

“Do you?”

She lifted her eyes. “I expect to know if I can have a place there.”

Annabeth looked at the drawing. The bed was still beside the window. The small square for Annabeth’s stool sat to the right, far enough from the cupboard in this version. The chair had been moved toward the foot of Thalia’s bed. The rug was not shown.

“I can’t answer that today,” Annabeth said.

“I know about the cot.”

“It isn’t only the cot.”

“What else?”

“We all have to agree on the room.”

“You and Thalia.”

“And you.”

Ianthe gave a short, doubtful laugh. “I’m the one asking if I’m allowed to live there.”

“You’re the one who would live there.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

Annabeth set the clamp down. “I want you in the cabin.”

Ianthe looked at her, still waiting.

“I also won’t decide where Thalia sleeps or where her chair goes. I won’t decide what happens to my work. And I don’t know where the bed fits until we measure the room with the right cot.”

“Then you can’t say you want me there.”

“I can say I want you there. I can’t promise what the room will look like.”

Ianthe turned the drawing so it faced herself. “You have a bed. Thalia has a bed. You both have a table and the window and all the time to decide. I have a guest room with a door that closes.”

The words came out clipped. Annabeth could hear the hurt under them. She could also hear the accusation: that she and Thalia had used the room’s uncertainty to keep Ianthe outside.

“The guest room is where you’ve been sleeping,” Annabeth said. “It isn’t a verdict on whether you’re family.”

“It feels like one.”

“I believe you.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I do.”

Ianthe’s fingers pressed against the paper. “Then tell me where I’m supposed to go.”

“I can’t.”

“That’s convenient.”

“It isn’t.”

“You can say no because you already have a place.”

“I haven’t said no.”

“You haven’t said yes either.”

“No.”

The answer landed cleanly. Annabeth did not try to make it gentler by changing what it meant.

Ianthe let go of the paper. “You told me we could look at another plan.”

“We can.”

“You told me you’d be here.”

“I am.”

“And now you’re saying you can’t decide.”

“I said we’d look. I didn’t say I could decide for everyone.”

A line formed above Ianthe’s nose. “You could at least tell me whether this version is better.”

“I don’t want to talk about the drawing right now.”

Ianthe’s mouth opened. Annabeth saw the question arrive—the objection, the correction, the next solution. Ianthe closed her mouth.

“All right,” she said.

It wasn’t agreement. It was obedience to the boundary. Annabeth noticed the difference.

“I’m going to reset the stool,” she said. “I don’t want help. I don’t want it moved again.”

“I won’t touch it.”

“Thank you.”

“I’m sorry I did it.”

“You already said that.”

“I know. I wanted to say it when you could hear it.”

Annabeth considered the apology. “I heard you.”

“Are you still angry?”

“Yes.”

Ianthe dropped her gaze. “All right.”

“That doesn’t mean I don’t want you in the cabin.”

“You already said that.”

“I know.”

“So you want me there, but not enough to move your stuff.”

“I want you there enough to ask what you want. I don’t want you there so much that I stop asking.”

Ianthe’s face changed; not a smile, but a loosening around the eyes. “That’s a strange answer.”

“It’s the one I have.”

“Fine.”

“Do you mean fine?”

“I mean I heard you.”

Annabeth picked up the clamp. “I’m going back.”

“Can I come?”

“Not right now.”

“Okay.”

Ianthe rolled the drawing inward, keeping the paper flat between her palms. Annabeth waited for her to follow. She did not.

Chiron slid the tray of hinges toward the wall, clearing a path to the door. He did not offer to carry the clamp.

“Thank you,” Annabeth said.

“You’re welcome.”

She crossed the porch alone. The gray sky had started to lift, and a stripe of sunlight showed between two clouds. Annabeth held the clamp by its handle, thinking about the stool joint and the bed drawn beside the window.

A plan could be clear and still belong to no one yet.



---

Thalia was still at the table when Annabeth returned.

The folded blanket had been moved closer to the chair, and the splint strap sat flat beneath her trouser cuff. She had poured tea into two cups. One was near the empty chair Annabeth usually used; the other was beside Thalia’s hand.

“Did Chiron check it?” she asked.

“He said the joint can be reset. It needs another full day.”

“Does the whole stool need another day?”

“The glue does.”

“Do you want me to hold it?”

“No.”

“All right.”

Annabeth set the clamp on the workbench and cleared the area. She did not touch the stool until she had put down a folded cloth to protect the floor. The leg had shifted by less than a finger’s width. The glue had not failed, but the joint was no longer square.

She remembered how much work it had taken to get the leg even. The stool had been one of the small tasks she could finish without anyone asking whether the room had room for her new sister. It was a stool. It still mattered.

“Can you hand me the jar?” she asked.

Thalia picked it up from the shelf and passed it over. She did not stand.

“Thanks.”

Annabeth loosened the clamp, wiped away the old glue, and reset the leg against the mark she had drawn on the underside. It was satisfying to put the parts in the right place. Not because she had to prove she could repair it, but because the stool needed to be repaired and she knew how.

Thalia watched from the chair. “Do you want me to talk to Ianthe later?”

“About what?”

“About the drawing. About the stool.”

“No.”

“Okay.”

Annabeth pressed the joint together and held it until the clamp caught. “Do you want to?”

“I think she needs to hear that you meant what you said.”

“She heard me.”

“She heard me too.”

“That’s what I’m saying.”

Thalia considered the table. “I can tell her I shouldn’t have spoken for you.”

“I don’t want you to do that while you’re also explaining what I meant.”

“Right.”

Annabeth adjusted the screw until the clamp held the leg firm. “If you want to apologize for your part, you can. I don’t want you to carry mine.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“Yes.” Thalia put both hands around her cup. “I don’t always know where help turns into taking over.”

“Neither do I.”

“You’re better at it.”

“I make different mistakes.”

“Like deciding you can fix the whole cabin if you draw enough lines?”

“Exactly.”

Thalia smiled. “That one is impressive.”

“I’m glad you appreciate the scale of it.”

The tea smelled of mint. Annabeth drank a little and set the cup back where she had found it. “I felt like she was deciding the room while I was standing there.”

“She was.”

“And then you decided to stop her.”

“I did.”

“I know why.”

“Still.”

“Still.”

Thalia shifted her heel on the blanket. “I watched her lift the stool and I thought, if I wait, she’ll move it before you can say anything.”

“I had already said something.”

“I heard you.”

“Then I need you to trust the answer.”

Thalia looked at the cloudy glue at the joint. “I can try.”

“I’m not asking you to stop caring.”

“I know.”

“I’m asking you to let me speak before you decide I can’t.”

“I can do that.”

“Sometimes.”

“Sometimes,” she agreed.

The stool stood in the clamp, small and steady, its repaired leg pressed into the exact place Annabeth had set. She could leave it for a day. It would hold if nobody touched it.

“I don’t want her to think I hate her,” Annabeth said.

“I don’t think she does.”

“She might.”

“She might think you don’t want her in the cabin.”

“I do want her.”

“Then tell her.”

“I did.”

“Maybe she needs to hear it more than once.”

“Maybe. I still can’t say yes to the bed.”

“You shouldn’t if you don’t know.”

“The cot isn’t here. The room isn’t ready. I’m not ready.”

“That’s three reasons.”

“It’s also the answer.”

Thalia nodded. She seemed relieved to hear Annabeth say it plainly, even if it did not resolve what Ianthe wanted.

“Would you like me to ask her to come back with the drawing later?” Thalia asked.

“No.”

“Okay.”

“Don’t ask me again.”

“I won’t.”

Annabeth placed the used rag in the water basin. The glue jar went back on the shelf. She made a note on the edge of the paper: *reset; clamp; cure another day*. She kept the line brief. It was her project log, and she did not owe it to anyone else.

Thalia looked at the note. “Do you want to tell Chiron it’s done?”

“I want to tell him it’s clamped.”

“Right.”

“Do you want to tell Silena you’re not going to the hall?”

“Later.”

“Do you want to go for a walk?”

“No.”

“Do you want me to stop asking questions?”

“Yes.”

“Okay.”

Annabeth took the chair beside the table and sat down. She did not make a joke about lists. The day’s questions could wait.



---

Annabeth waited until the glue had set enough for the clamp to hold without her checking it.

Then she put on her coat and went to the Big House.

The west hall was warmer than Cabin One. Someone had left a kettle on the stove and the windows cracked an inch to keep the steam from fogging the glass. The guest-room door was open. Ianthe sat at the small desk inside, bent over a page covered in measurements. Her coat lay folded at the foot of the bed. She had taken out the same drawing Annabeth had left on the table.

“Can I talk to you?” Annabeth asked from the doorway.

Ianthe turned. “About the cot?”

“About the drawing.”

“You said not right now.”

“I did.”

“Is now different?”

“Yes.”

Ianthe set the pencil down. “All right.”

“I want to look at it on paper. I don’t want to move anything in the cabin, and the stool has to stay where it is.”

“I won’t touch it.”

“If you want, we can go through the measurements together. You can point things out. I’ll say what I think.”

“Together?”

“Together.”

Ianthe studied her face. “Are you doing this because you feel bad?”

“No.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yes. I don’t want you to think the room is already decided. I don’t want to agree to something we haven’t measured. I can do both.”

Ianthe leaned back in the chair. “You can do a lot of things.”

“I can.”

“You don’t have to say that like it’s a challenge.”

“I wasn’t.”

“Okay.”

Annabeth let the silence settle. “Do you want to bring the drawing?”

Ianthe picked up the page. “I’ll bring it.”

“Good.”

They walked back toward Cabin One together. Ianthe kept the drawing rolled beneath her arm. Annabeth did not ask why she had gone back to measuring after the argument. She could guess, but she didn’t know. The page had been hers before it became a conflict.

At the cabin, Thalia sat by the table with a book open in front of her. The splint was under her trouser cuff; her foot rested on the folded blanket. The stool remained in the clamp beside the wall.

Annabeth stopped just inside the door. “Ianthe’s going to show me the drawing. We’re keeping everything on paper.”

Thalia looked up. “Okay.”

“You can join if you want.”

“I want to.”

Annabeth pulled three chairs around the table, leaving the stool alone. “Does that work for you?”

Thalia nodded. “Yes.”

Ianthe unrolled the paper in front of her. “This version leaves a wider path from the door. The bed is here, the table turns this way, and the chair goes beside the footboard.”

“Whose chair?” Thalia asked.

“Yours.”

“Did you ask me where I want it?”

Ianthe’s fingers went still on the paper. “No.”

“Then ask.”

“Where do you want it?”

“By the table, where it is now.”

“That leaves less room.”

“It also lets me sit at the table.”

“Your heel is injured. You should sit by the bed.”

“I know where I want to sit.”

Annabeth watched Ianthe look at Thalia. She did not answer for her. She did not translate the choice into a practical detail. Thalia’s face stayed calm; the answer did not need backup.

“All right,” Ianthe said. “The chair stays by the table.”

“Thank you.”

“Then I have less room for the bed.”

“Maybe.”

Ianthe looked back at the drawing. “I could shorten the table by moving it closer to the wall.”

Annabeth turned the scale ruler over. “How much shorter?”

“Enough to make the path clear.”

“We need the door arc first.”

“I drew it.”

“Your cupboard arc is wrong.”

“I fixed that.”

“On this page?”

Ianthe tapped the pencil mark near the corner. “Here.”

Annabeth placed the ruler along the cupboard edge and traced the arc on a separate sheet. “The door swings out from this hinge. It needs this much space.”

Ianthe leaned close to see. “That’s wider than the door.”

“It has to open past the handle.”

“Right.”

Thalia turned a page in her book. The paper rustled. She didn’t move the chair or shift the blanket.

Annabeth drew the table at scale. “The table needs space on both sides if two people sit here.”

“I can eat in the Big House.”

“That’s not the point.”

“Isn’t it?”

“No. We use the table for other things.”

“I know.”

“Then the table stays.”

“Does it have to be this size?”

“It has to fit the work we do.”

Ianthe looked at the line. “You could use the Big House table for drawings.”

“I could. I don’t want to move every time I need to use a pencil.”

“Then you want the table.”

“Yes.”

Ianthe set the pencil down again. “I keep thinking if I can fit a bed, I’ll stop being a guest.”

Annabeth held the ruler in place. “That’s not what decides whether you’re our sister.”

“It decides where I sleep.”

“It does.”

“Those things are connected.”

“They are. They’re not the same.”

Ianthe’s eyes narrowed. “You can say that because you’re not the one waiting.”

Annabeth could have argued that she had waited for things too. She could have told Ianthe about the years before camp, about the beds she had shared and the rooms that had changed around her. But those histories did not belong to the current question, and Ianthe had not asked for them.

“You’re right that I’m not waiting for this bed,” she said. “I don’t know what that feels like for you.”

“Then don’t tell me they’re not connected.”

“I said they’re not the same. I didn’t say they don’t matter.”

Ianthe turned the pencil once between her fingers. “All right.”

“What do you want us to know about the plan?”

“That I want the window.”

“Why?”

“I like the air there. I can tell when the wind changes before the weather board catches it.”

“You want the window because you can read the wind?”

“I want it because I like it. The wind is a reason I can explain.”

Annabeth smiled, almost despite herself. “That sounds like you.”

“It sounds practical.”

“It can be both.”

“Can you write that down?” Ianthe asked, then caught the command in her own voice. “Could you? If you want.”

Annabeth picked up the pencil. “I’ll write: ‘Ianthe prefers the window because she likes the air and can read the wind.’”

“Not ‘prefers.’ Wants.”

Annabeth glanced at her.

Ianthe took a breath. “Can you write ‘wants’?”

“Sure.”

The correction was small, but Annabeth noticed it. She wrote the word as asked.

Thalia looked up from her book. “The window opens only a little.”

“I know.”

“It rattles.”

“I can fix it.”

“Have you asked Chiron?”

“No.”

“Then don’t write that the window is fixed.”

Ianthe’s mouth tightened. “I meant I could ask.”

“Then ask him,” Thalia said. “The rattle is from the loose latch.”

Annabeth kept her eyes on the drawing. The conversation was theirs. She didn't need to intervene.

Ianthe marked the window in a corner of the page, then paused. “Can I ask Chiron to look at the latch?”

“You can,” Thalia said.

“Thank you.”

They turned the sheet so each of them could reach it. Annabeth used one pencil for the existing room and another for proposed changes. Ianthe began to draw a line where the bed might go. She stopped before it reached the chair.

“What?” Annabeth asked.

“I was going to move your chair on the plan.”

“You can draw an option.”

“I don’t know if you want it moved.”

“I don’t.”

“Then I won’t draw it as a choice you’ve already made.”

Annabeth slid the second pencil toward her. “You can draw it as a question mark.”

Ianthe added a small dotted square near the table and wrote *ask Thalia* beside it.

Thalia leaned over the paper as far as she could without shifting her foot. “I’ve answered.”

Ianthe crossed out the note. “Right. Sorry.”

“It’s okay.”

“Do you want me to leave it where you said?”

“Yes.”

Ianthe moved the square back. “Like this?”

“Yes.”

The line between the beds was narrow. The window bed might fit only if the table moved, but neither Annabeth nor Thalia wanted it to. The bed could be shortened, but the cot’s measurements were unknown. They could continue drawing options; they could not make the space larger than it was.

Annabeth wrote in the margin: *Cot inspection pending; no furniture moved.*

Ianthe watched her. “You’re making sure I can’t pretend it’s final.”

“I’m making sure we know what is still unknown.”

“I know what a question mark means.”

“I know you do.”

Ianthe looked back at the drawing. “Then keep it.”

Annabeth left the note where it was.



---

They kept working on the drawing until the window light changed.

Annabeth laid the ruler over the first version and used a clean sheet for the measurements. The door opening took more space than Ianthe had drawn. The rug could not be compressed just because the lines on the page were crowded. She marked the path with a blue pencil, from the threshold to the table, then from the table to the window.

“Do you need this much clearance?” Ianthe asked.

“I do.”

“For the door?”

“For the door and the cupboard. And so two people can pass without turning sideways.”

“I can walk sideways.”

“I know.”

“Then why draw it that wide?”

“Because it isn’t only for you.”

Ianthe folded her arms. “I wasn’t planning to walk through it alone.”

Annabeth looked up. “I know.”

The answer quieted her. Ianthe pulled her arms apart and leaned over the drawing.

“The window bed would go here,” she said. “The table could move to this side.”

“The table is used by both of us.”

“It would still be in the room.”

“It would block the cupboard if it turned that way.”

“Not if it moved lower.”

“Then the chair won’t fit.”

“Thalia can use the bed.”

“I can use the table,” Thalia said.

She had been sitting at the other end, hands around her tea. Her heel remained on the blanket. She had not leaned over the paper again, but she was listening.

Ianthe glanced at her. “Would you rather have the chair in the corner?”

“I’d rather keep it by the table.”

“Can I ask why?”

“Because I use it there.”

“Okay.”

Annabeth watched Ianthe draw a small chair beside the table. She did not move it toward the bed.

“The table is staying where it is for now,” Annabeth said. “We can draw a version where it moves. We cannot move it today.”

“I understand.”

“Do you want to draw the version?”

Ianthe took a breath through her nose. “Yes.”

“Then it stays on paper.”

“Yes.”

Annabeth passed her the ruler. She waited until Ianthe took it before letting go. The exchange felt almost formal; that did not make it a bad one.

Ianthe measured the table and marked two positions. The second made the cupboard door overlap the edge by half an inch.

“That won’t work,” Annabeth said.

“It would if the cupboard didn’t open all the way.”

“It needs to open.”

“Why?”

“Because our clothes are in it.”

“We can keep clothes in the Big House.”

“No.”

“I meant if we—”

“I don’t want to move our clothes out of the cabin.”

“All right.”

Thalia sipped her tea. “You don’t have to solve everything today.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Ianthe looked at her. “No. I’m trying.”

Thalia nodded. “That counts.”

Annabeth wrote the measurements at the side. It was useful work. It was also tiring to keep every sentence from sliding into a decision no one had made.

A thin strip of paper lay beside the window. The draft from the frame lifted its corner, then let it settle.

Ianthe noticed. She stood, moved toward the window, and stopped before touching the latch. “Can I open it a little?”

Annabeth glanced at Thalia. It was Thalia’s chair near the draft. She made herself not answer.

“Ask her,” Annabeth said.

Ianthe turned to Thalia. “Can I open the window a little?”

“Not while my foot’s by it. You can wait.”

“Okay.”

Ianthe returned to the table. She sat on her hands for a moment, then let them rest beside the page.

“You could have said yes,” Thalia said.

“I could have.”

“Do you want to?”

“Not until we move the chair.”

Thalia shifted her blanket. “It’s fine.”

“I’m not moving your chair.”

“I know.”

Ianthe looked at the window. “I wanted the bed there because I like the air. If it’s always going to be closed, I don’t know if it’s worth it.”

“It doesn’t have to be always closed,” Thalia said. “The latch needs repair. It rattles when the wind comes from the north.”

“I can fix the latch.”

“Can you?”

“I can look at it.”

“That’s different.”

“Right. I can ask Chiron to look at it.”

Annabeth added a note: *window latch — inspect before assigning repair*. Ianthe read it.

“Are you keeping a record of what I say?”

“I’m recording what needs to happen to the room.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“No.”

Ianthe watched the pencil move. “Can you write that I asked?”

“I can.”

“Not that I’m assigned.”

“I won’t.”

“Okay.”

The word held a little less distance than before. Not much. Enough to continue.

They made another version of the layout. The bed stayed beside the window. The table stayed where it was. The chair stayed by the table. They left a rectangle for the stool, unmarked because the real stool remained in the clamp.

The room on paper had a narrow clear path, a cupboard that opened, and a window bed that might fit if the cot was small enough. It did not tell them whether the cot was safe, whether the frame could hold it, or whether Thalia wanted to share the room with Ianthe.

Annabeth wrote *provisional* at the top.

Ianthe read the word. “Does that mean you don’t want me to sleep there?”

“It means we don’t know yet.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It is the answer I have.”

Ianthe tapped her pencil on the table. “You have a lot of answers that sound like waiting.”

“Because some things are waiting.”

“Like the cot.”

“Yes.”

“Like whether I belong.”

Annabeth set the pencil down. “That isn’t waiting on the cot.”

“Then what is it waiting on?”

“Nothing. You already belong to the family.”

“But not this room.”

“The room has two people living in it now. You don’t become less our sister because it’s not ready.”

“You can say that from your bed.”

“I know.”

Ianthe’s eyes moved to the window. “If I leave the drawing here, can you keep it flat?”

“Yes.”

“Can you keep it on the table, not fold it?”

“Yes.”

“Thank you.”

“You’re welcome.”

Ianthe sat back. Her fingers were smudged with graphite. She rubbed them together, leaving a gray line across her palm.

“Do you want me to help clean the pencil off?” Annabeth asked.

“No. I can wash my hands.”

“Okay.”

“I’m not helpless.”

“I didn’t say you were.”

“I know.”

Thalia smiled faintly into her cup. “Sounds like a morning we’ve all had.”

Nobody argued.



---

The plan stayed on the table after the window light began to thin.

Annabeth had stopped drawing new lines. The ones on the page were enough to show the problem: the bed by the window fit only if the desk moved, and the desk could not move without narrowing the path from the door. Ianthe had not asked to change that path. She had asked to see what the room looked like if her bed were in the place she wanted it.

The drawing could show the request. It could not make the room bigger.

Thalia shifted her leg against the folded blanket. The movement was small, but Annabeth noticed the careful pause afterward.

“Does it hurt?” she asked.

“A little.”

“Do you want the pack?”

“Not yet.”

“Okay.”

Thalia rubbed her thumb over the edge of the paper. “Does Ianthe know you want her here?”

Annabeth looked up. “Here in the cabin?”

“Here. With us. Around us. Whatever word she’s using for it.”

The question had more than one answer. Annabeth could say she wanted Ianthe to feel welcome. She could say she did not want to make the room into a reward for being easy to live with. Both were true. Neither was the whole thing.

“I want her to have a place,” she said. “I don’t know if I’m ready to share this room with her.”

Thalia nodded once. She did not look offended. “That’s different from wanting her gone.”

“Yes.”

“Do you think she knows the difference?”

“I don’t know.”

“She might hear ‘not yet’ as ‘you’re a guest until you stop being difficult.’”

Annabeth looked again at Ianthe’s careful measurements. “I don’t want her to hear that.”

“Wanting her not to hear it isn’t the same as making sure she doesn’t.”

“I know.”

Thalia watched her. “Do you?”

Annabeth capped the pencil. “I’m trying to answer you. I want her to be here. I don’t want her to think I can promise her the window or the bed. And I don’t want to promise I’m ready to live with someone because she’s family.”

“Good.”

“Good?”

“Those can all be true at once.”

The easy agreement made Annabeth pause. “You’re not worried about what she’ll think?”

“I’m worried about it. I’m still not going to tell you what you want to hear so she’ll like me.”

Annabeth understood the line of thought and disliked how familiar it felt. “Do you want her to like you?”

“Yeah.”

“Do you want her to be your sister?”

Thalia looked down at her heel beneath the blanket. “She is my sister.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

“No.” Thalia took a breath. “I want to know her. I want to see what she’s like when she isn’t trying to prove she deserves the best place in the room.”

“She’s trying to make herself useful.”

“Maybe. She also keeps correcting people like that’s the same thing.”

Annabeth glanced at the doorway. Ianthe had gone to the hall. The drawing lay flat between the stones, the penciled bed still inside the too-small rectangle.

“She thinks if she finds a better arrangement, then nobody can say she doesn’t belong,” Annabeth said.

“Maybe.”

“I think she knows she’s doing it.”

“I think she knows what she wants. I don’t think she knows what it does to people when she tells them their plan is wrong before they’ve finished explaining it.”

Annabeth turned the pencil between her fingers. It was the same distinction she had tried to make with the stool: intention did not soften the effect enough to skip naming it. Still, there was something tender in the way Thalia said it. She was not dismissing Ianthe. She was refusing to excuse her.

“Does it feel like she expects you to decide whether she belongs?” Annabeth asked.

“Sometimes.”

“Do you?”

“No.” Thalia shifted, and the blanket slid a little. She pulled it back with her free hand. “I don’t want a job deciding if she’s family enough. She’s my sister. That’s where we start. What happens next is between us.”

Annabeth thought of Ianthe waiting at the door for one of them to change the answer about the hall. “She may think being your sister means you’ll make room for her.”

“It might mean I let her be mad at me without leaving.”

“Would you?”

“I don’t know. I haven’t met all of her yet.”

That answer held more room than a promise. Thalia did not owe Ianthe an instant closeness, any more than Annabeth owed her a bed. The thought sounded obvious until the need for comfort pressed against it.

“I want the room to work,” Annabeth said. “I’m worried if she thinks I’m deciding, every time I say no she’ll think I’m deciding if she’s family.”

“You’re allowed to say no to a bed.”

“I know.”

“Are you?”

Annabeth met her eyes. “I’m allowed to say no to a bed.”

“Good.” Thalia rested her palm on the table, away from the drawing. “And if she acts like that means you don’t want her, she can be wrong about you. You don’t have to buy your way out of the misunderstanding.”

Annabeth felt the words land. She had spent the morning setting a boundary, then spent the afternoon trying to shape it so Ianthe would not hurt. There was care in that, but there was also a small, ugly wish to be understood as the good person in the room.

“I don’t want to make this into a test,” Annabeth said.

“Then don’t.”

“People make tests out of it anyway.”

“Sometimes.”

Thalia did not reach for the drawing. She let the paper remain between them, even where the line for the window bed ran close to the wall. Annabeth took the blue pencil and erased a mark that had drifted into the door swing.

“What did you think when she told us she wanted the window?” Annabeth asked.

“I thought she wanted it.”

“And?”

“And I thought she’d decided wanting it was a reason to get it.”

Annabeth let out a quiet breath. “That was my thought too.”

“Did you want to give it to her?”

“I wanted her to stop looking like she expected the answer to be no.”

“Those aren’t the same thing.”

“No.”

“I didn’t want you to give it to her because you felt bad.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Annabeth set the pencil down. “I do now.”

Thalia’s mouth moved as if she might smile, but she didn’t. “I’m not saying she shouldn’t get the window. I’m saying I want to be asked what I think before somebody gives it away because they think I don’t need it.”

“You do get a say.”

“I know. I just don’t want my say to show up after the decision.”

“You’re right.”

“And if I say I don’t want to share with her yet, I don’t want that to turn into a story about me being mean to my sister.”

“I won’t tell that story.”

“You might not be the one telling it.”

“I can’t control what she says to herself.”

“No. But you can tell her what you meant.”

Annabeth looked at the drawing. “I can tell her that no one’s decided the bed, and that the guest room isn’t a verdict.”

“If that’s what you mean.”

“It is.”

“Then tell her that. Don’t make it sound like you’re asking her to be patient so she earns it.”

Annabeth looked back at Thalia. “You’re better at this than I am.”

“At telling people what they don’t want to hear?”

“At knowing where a boundary stops and a punishment starts.”

Thalia shrugged. “I’ve gotten them confused before.”

“Me too.”

“We can still get them confused tomorrow.”

Annabeth smiled faintly. “That’s reassuring.”

“It shouldn’t be.”

“It is, a little.”

Thalia leaned back against the chair. The movement made her wince, and this time Annabeth waited. Thalia took a breath and let it out.

“Cold pack?” Annabeth asked.

“Now, yes.”

Annabeth got up and brought it from the cooler, wrapped in the towel. She held it out, not reaching for Thalia’s heel.

“Beside it?” she asked.

“Beside it.”

Thalia shifted the blanket, placed the pack herself, then settled her leg again. Annabeth returned to her chair.

“You think Ianthe needs a clear yes about something,” Thalia said.

“Maybe.”

“Not the bed.”

“No. She needs to know we heard that she wants a place.”

“She knows we heard. We’ve been talking about it all day.”

“Maybe she needs to hear it without a floor plan attached.”

Thalia considered that. “Could you say it without making it sound like the bed will happen?”

“I can try.”

“Don’t say ‘we’ll find something.’ That sounds like a promise.”

“I won’t.”

“Don’t say ‘you’ll fit.’ The room might not.”

“I won’t.”

“Just tell her you want her here.”

Annabeth folded her hands. “And if she asks where?”

“Then we tell her what we know. Guest room tonight. Cot inspection pending. No decision after that.”

“You’ve thought about this.”

“I’ve had a lot of time to think today.”

“About Ianthe?”

“About her. About you. About the heel.” Thalia shifted the cold pack. “Mostly the heel.”

Annabeth didn’t ask what she meant by mostly. Thalia would say more if she wanted to.

A breeze moved the edge of the drawing. Annabeth put a stone back on the corner. The bed stayed where Ianthe had drawn it, neither erased nor approved.

“Do you want me there when you talk to her?” Annabeth asked.

“No.”

“Okay.”

“I want to decide when I talk to her. If she asks me something, I’ll answer. If I don’t know, I’ll say I don’t know.”

“That seems fair.”

“It isn’t a promise that I’ll be warm about it.”

“I didn’t think it was.”

“Good.”

Annabeth glanced at the long line from the bed to the door. “When you do want to talk to her, I can leave.”

“Maybe. You can ask me then.”

“Okay.”

Thalia looked at her as if checking whether she could accept the answer without making it into a larger agreement. Annabeth let the silence remain silence.

The room grew cooler. She folded her coat over the chair and brought the stool closer with her foot, then remembered the joint and stopped before it could catch. The clamp held. She moved the chair instead.

Thalia watched the careful adjustment. “You don’t have to treat the stool like it’s going to explode.”

“I don’t. I’m treating it like it needs another day.”

“Fair.”

Annabeth ran her thumb over the blue line on the plan. “I’m sorry I kept trying to make the conversation easier for her.”

“Why are you sorry to me?”

“Because I was talking like I could promise the room for both of us.”

“You weren’t.”

“I was thinking it.”

“That’s not the same as doing it.”

“It can become the same thing pretty fast.”

Thalia nodded. “Then just remember to ask.”

“I will.”

Thalia’s gaze sharpened slightly.

“I’ll try to remember to ask,” Annabeth amended.

“Better.”

“Not perfect?”

“Not required.”

The evening light continued to fade across the paper. They could see the room plan without agreeing to it. Annabeth did not cross out the window bed. She did not darken its outline. It remained one possibility, cramped and unworkable as drawn, waiting for measurements neither of them had.

“I’m glad she came,” Thalia said.

“Me too.”

“I’m glad she’s here.”

Annabeth heard the distinction. “Even if she keeps being annoying?”

“Especially if she keeps being annoying. It means she hasn’t figured out how to behave yet.”

“That’s one way to look at it.”

“It’s not the only way.”

“No.”

Thalia moved the cold pack a little higher and closed her eyes for a moment. Annabeth returned to the plan, not to change it, but to keep the page from curling.



---

By the time the bell for the evening hall rang, the drawing had dried flat.

Thalia’s heel was still under the folded blanket. She had moved her foot once to get the splint comfortable, then settled it again. The day had not made the ache worse. It had not made it disappear.

“I’m not going tonight,” she said.

Annabeth looked up from the room plan. “Okay.”

“You don’t have to stay because I am.”

“I know.”

“Do you want to go?”

“No.”

“Then stay because you want to.”

Annabeth folded the corner of the paper to mark the line for the cupboard. She let it go and smoothed it open again. “I want to keep looking at this.”

“You don’t have to finish.”

“I know.”

Thalia smiled. “That is a good answer.”

A knock came from the porch.

Ianthe stood outside with her coat on and the drawing rolled beneath her arm. She waited for Annabeth to open the door.

“Can I come in?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“I’m going to the hall.”

“Okay.”

“I wanted to ask if I can leave the drawing here.”

“On the table?”

“If that’s all right.”

“It is.”

Annabeth cleared a space beside the scale plan. Ianthe set the drawing down and unrolled it. She weighted the corners with the two smooth stones from the sill.

“Do you want me to leave it open?” she asked.

“You can.”

“Okay.”

Ianthe looked at the clamp by the wall. “Did the stool hold?”

“It’s clamped. It needs another day.”

“Because I moved it.”

“Because the joint shifted when it moved.”

“I moved it after you told me not to.”

“Yes.”

“I wanted to see if the space worked.”

“I know.”

“I should have asked.”

“Yes.”

Ianthe nodded. She did not ask Annabeth to tell her the stool was fine or that the apology fixed the room.

“I’m sorry,” she said. “I heard you say no, and I kept treating it like there was a better answer if I explained the plan.”

Annabeth studied her face. “Thank you for saying that.”

“I still want the bed.”

“You can want it.”

“I still want the window.”

“You can want that too.”

“I don’t know if I can wait without getting angry.”

“You don’t have to like waiting.”

“That doesn’t mean I get to move things.”

“No.”

Ianthe’s shoulders dropped a little. “I know.”

Annabeth looked at the paper plan. “We can look again after Chiron measures the cot.”

“Will you?”

“If you ask.”

“I’m asking.”

“Then yes.”

Ianthe pulled a small fold of the drawing free, then stopped. “Can I leave this corner folded?”

“Why?”

“So the cupboard arc stays visible. It covers the note.”

“Leave it open.”

“Okay.”

She flattened it carefully. Annabeth noticed that she kept the paper away from the stool and the clamp.

“Are you coming to the hall?” Ianthe asked Thalia.

“No.”

“You can come and stay dry.”

“I know.”

“It would still be—” Ianthe stopped. Her fingers curled around the edge of her coat pocket. “I hope you have a good evening here.”

Thalia looked at her. “Thanks.”

Ianthe shifted her eyes to Annabeth. “Do you want to come?”

“No.”

“All right.”

She stood by the door for another moment, waiting as if one of them might change the answer. Neither did.

“Have a good hall,” Annabeth said.

“Thank you.”

Ianthe stepped onto the porch. The cold air moved through the open door and lifted the edge of the plan. Annabeth held it down with one hand until the latch clicked.

The quiet came back slowly.

Thalia looked at the paper, then at Annabeth. “Do you want me to apologize again?”

“For what?”

“For speaking before you could.”

“You already did.”

“I’m not asking if you heard me.”

Annabeth set the two stones back on the plan. “I know you were worried she’d move the stool. I know you wanted to help.”

“That doesn’t make it okay.”

“No.”

“I’m sorry.”

“Thank you.”

“Are you angry?”

“Yes.”

Thalia’s mouth flattened. “Okay.”

“I’m also glad you’re here.”

“I don’t know what to do with both of those.”

“You don’t have to do anything with them.”

Thalia looked at the folded blanket under her foot. “I can leave you alone.”

“You could.”

“Do you want me to?”

“No.”

“Then I’ll stay.”

Annabeth returned to the drawing. She had not made a promise about a bed. She had not lost the table. The stool had one more day to cure, and the window latch still needed an inspection. Ianthe had left her plan with permission.

None of those things had become easy. They had become clearer.
