## Chapter 4: The Hall of Gods

The Hall of the Gods stood at the center of Camp Half-Blood like a marble accusation.

It was beautiful — there was no denying that. Twelve columns of white stone supported a roof carved with scenes from the Trojan War, and the floor was a mosaic of Olympus itself, each tile placed with impossible precision. But to Percy, who remembered the original camp's simpler layout, the building felt like an intrusion. A reminder that the divine had stamped its authority on this place in a way it never had before.

Cold marble leaching through his shoes, he stood at the entrance, staring up at the carved lintel while wind hissed pine-cold off the hill. The inscription read: *Know Thyself, Know Thy Father, Know Thy Fate.*

"Noble, isn't it?"

Percy turned. A girl stood behind him — maybe fifteen, with dark skin and close-cropped hair, wearing a camp necklace with more beads than seemed possible. She was smiling, but her eyes were assessing.

"It's new," Percy said carefully.

"Three years old. I'm Zoe. Daughter of Hecate. And I'm telling you this because I think you're going to figure it out eventually anyway: the camp isn't what it seems. The rules are different here. The gods are closer. And there are things happening that nobody talks about."

"Like what?"

Zoe glanced around, then lowered her voice. "Like the fact that three years ago, someone broke into the Big House and stole a prophecy from the attic. Nobody was ever caught. Chiron won't even talk about it."

Percy's blood ran cold. "A prophecy?"

"A big one. The kind that makes the gods nervous. I don't know what it said — but I know it changed things. After that night, the Hall of the Gods went up. The rules about big three kids shifted. And the gods started visiting more often." She met his eyes. "Something happened here, Percy. Something big. And I don't think it's over."

She walked away, leaving Percy standing in the shadow of the Hall of the Gods, his mind racing.

A stolen prophecy. Changes to the camp's divine infrastructure. Rules rewritten. It all pointed to someone with power — not just mortal power, but divine power. Someone who could manipulate the boundaries between gods and mortals.

Someone who had been at this camp three years ago.

He needed to find Annabeth.

---

With dust and brine thick in the still air, he found her in the library — or rather, in the small building that passed for a library, which was more of a study hall with delusions of grandeur. She was surrounded by books, her brow furrowed in concentration, and she didn't notice him until he sat down across from her.

"Hey," he said.

She looked up. "Hey. What's wrong?"

"Everything. But specifically — did you know someone stole a prophecy from the Big House three years ago?"

Annabeth's expression went statue-still. "No."

"A daughter of Hecate told me. She said it was a big one — the kind that makes gods nervous. And she said it changed things. The camp, the rules about big three kids, the divine visitations — all of it happened after this prophecy was stolen."

"Percy, that's..." Annabeth trailed off, her mind working. "If someone stole a prophecy, they knew what it said. They knew what was coming. And they used that knowledge to prepare — to change things in advance."

"That's what I'm thinking. The question is: who? And why?"

"Someone with access to the Big House. Someone who could get past Chiron and Mr. D. Someone who understands prophecies well enough to know which one to take."

"That's not a short list."

"No. It's not."

They stared at each other while cold lamplight pooled on the table, the mystery pressing around them like deep water.

"We need to talk to Chiron," Annabeth said, shifting his weight against the cold. "Carefully. We can't just ask him about a stolen prophecy — that would raise too many questions."

"Agreed. But there might be another way. The attic of the Big House — if the prophecy was taken, there might be records. Notes. Something."

"You want to break into the Big House."

"I want to *investigate* the Big House. Breaking in is just a method."

Annabeth smiled — small and conspiratorial. "When?"

"Tonight."

---

That night, they crept through the darkness toward the Big House.

The camp was quiet, pine needles soft underfoot and cold starlight prickling the skin. The cabins were dark, the dining pavilion's bonfire had burned down to embers, and the only sound was the distant crash of waves against the shore. Percy moved with the practiced stealth of someone who'd spent years infiltrating enemy territory — which, technically, he had.

Annabeth moved like a shadow. She was twelve years old and small and quiet, and she navigated the darkness with a precision that spoke of training Percy didn't fully understand. Her balance occasionally surprised him: an adjustment this body made before her conscious plan caught up. He recognized the old Annabeth's stealth and could only guess what Thalia had added to her training here.

The Big House was dark and still. They circled it once, looking for signs of occupancy, and found nothing. The windows were black, the porch empty, the door locked.

"Window?" Annabeth whispered.

Percy nodded. They found a side window that was slightly open — probably for ventilation — and Percy boosted Annabeth through, then followed her inside.

The interior was dark and smelled like old books and brine, dust-dry paper over cold stone and cheaper wine. Mr. D's domain, but empty. They moved through the ground floor, checking rooms, finding nothing but dusty furniture and empty chairs.

The attic was upstairs. They climbed the narrow staircase, their footsteps careful and silent, and pushed open the attic door.

The room was exactly as Percy remembered — low-ceilinged, cluttered, smelling of old paper and divine energy. The Oracle's mist hung in the corners, shimmering faintly in the moonlight that filtered through the small window.

"Over there," Annabeth whispered, pointing to a desk in the corner.

They crossed the room. The desk was covered in papers — notes, transcripts, records of past prophecies. Annabeth rifled through them while Percy kept watch.

"Here," she said softly. "Look at this."

Percy leaned over her shoulder. The paper was dated three years ago, and it was a transcript of a prophecy — one that had been crossed out and rewritten multiple times, the ink blotted and smudged.

*The oath shall break, the king shall fall,*
*The prince of seas shall answer the call.*
*Three thrones shaken, two sisters bound,*
*In blood and fire, the truth is found.*

Percy read it twice. Then a third time. "What does it mean?"

"I don't know. But look — the original text was different. Someone crossed it out and rewrote it. See?"

She pointed to the crossed-out lines beneath the new version. Percy squinted, trying to make out the older text.

*The oath shall break, the king shall fall,*
*The son of Poseidon shall face the call.*
*One throne shaken, one sister bound,*
*In darkness and fear, the truth is found.*

The differences were subtle but significant. "Prince" instead of "son." "Three thrones" instead of "one." "Two sisters" instead of "one sister." And the final line had been changed from "darkness and fear" to "blood and fire."

"Someone altered a prophecy," Annabeth said, her voice barely above a whisper. "They rewrote it."

"But how? You can't just rewrite a prophecy — they're divine utterances. They come from the Oracle. They're—"

"They're messages from the gods. And gods can be manipulated. Or bribed. Or threatened."

Percy's stomach turned. "Who would have that kind of power?"

"I don't know. But whoever it's, they've been planning this for years. The changes to the camp, the altered rules, the increased number of big three kids — it's all connected to this prophecy. To whatever it says."

"And we're in it," Percy said, reading the lines again. "The prince of seas. That's me."

"And 'two sisters bound' — that's Annabeth and Thalia. Or maybe—" She stopped. "Or maybe it's not about the people we think it's about. Maybe it's metaphorical."

"Or maybe it's literal, and someone's trying to make it come true."

They stared at each other in the darkness, the altered prophecy hanging between them like a blade.

"We need to take this," Annabeth said, shifting his weight against the cold.

"If we take it, they'll know someone was here."

"If we leave it, we lose our best clue."

Percy hesitated. Then he folded the paper and slipped it into his pocket. "We'll put it back when we're done. For now, we need to understand what it means."

They left the way they'd come — through the window, down the side of the house, back into the darkness. They didn't speak until they were safely away from the Big House, hidden in the trees near the lake.

"This changes everything," Annabeth said, shifting his weight against the cold.

"I know."

"Someone's been rewriting fate. And we're part of it."

"I know."

"Percy, we have to be careful. If they realize we know—"

"I know."

They stood in the darkness, the stolen prophecy burning a hole in Percy's pocket, and the night felt suddenly much colder.

Somewhere in the distance, thunder rumbled, low through the soles of their feet, ozone-sharp on the tongue.

The storm was coming.

---

The next day, Percy was summoned to Chiron's office.

He found the centaur in his wheelchair form, but his expression was grave. Beside him, Mr. D stood with his arms crossed, looking even more irritated than usual.

"Percy," Chiron said. "Thank you for coming."

"Of course. What is it?"

Mr. D answered before Chiron could. "You've been asking questions. The daughter of Hecate told me you've been poking around the Hall of the Gods, asking about the changes. Asking about the prophecy."

Percy kept his expression neutral. "I was curious."

"Curiosity gets people killed." Mr. D's voice was sharp. "Especially curiosity about things they shouldn't know about."

"I don't think I'm the only one who's noticed the changes," Percy said carefully. "The camp is different. The rules are different. People have been talking about it."

"People talk too much." Mr. D's eyes were hard. "And people who talk too much tend to have accidents."

"Mr. D," Chiron said quietly. "Perhaps that's enough."

The god of wine — who looked nothing like a god of wine, with sharp eyes and an even sharper tongue — studied Percy for a long moment. Then he said, "I don't like this one, Chiron. Too much curiosity. Too much backbone. He'll get himself killed, or worse, get someone else killed."

"With respect, Mr. D," Percy said, "I've been at camp for two weeks. I've been nothing but polite. I've done everything I've been asked to do. Why would I get myself killed?"

"Because you're a child of the Big Three in a time when the Big Three are very important. That makes you either very useful or very dangerous. Depending on who's doing the asking." Mr. D turned away. "Watch yourself, Jackson. Some questions aren't worth the answers."

He swept out of the office, leaving the air heavy with unspoken warnings.

Chiron watched him go, then turned back to Percy with an expression that seemed almost... apologetic.

"He's not always so harsh," the centaur said.

"I know. He's worried."

"More worried than usual. Something is coming, Percy. Something that has the gods on edge and my old friend Dionysus reaching for his thyrsus before noon." Chiron leaned forward. "I don't know what it is. But I know that the answers to some questions are more dangerous than the questions themselves. And I think you need to decide, very carefully, which ones you're willing to die for."

Percy met the centaur's ancient eyes. "I'm already willing to die for the answers. The question is whether I'm willing to let other people die because I don't have them yet."

Chiron was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "That's the question, isn't it? And I don't have an answer for you. I'm sorry."

Percy stood and walked out of the office, and behind him, Chiron watched with eyes that had seen too much and understood too little and could do nothing to change what was coming.

---

That evening, Percy found Annabeth at their spot by the lake. He told her everything — the meeting with Chiron and Mr. D, the warnings, the sense that they were being watched.

"They're putting you on notice," Annabeth said, her expression dark. "Which means they know you were in the attic."

"Or Zoe told them. Or Mr. D did some checking. Or—"

"Or they're just being cautious because big three kids are targets and they don't trust strangers."

"Maybe."

They sat in silence, watching the water. Then Percy pulled out the prophecy — the one he'd taken — and showed it to Annabeth.

She read it three times. Then she pulled out her notebook and compared it with something she'd found in the library.

"There are more versions," she said quietly. "Three, at least. I found two in the records, and this is the third."

She showed him. The second version was different from both the original and the altered text:

*The oath shall break, the king shall fall,*
*The child of storm and sea shall heed the call.*
*Two thrones shaken, one sister bound,*
*In light and shadow, the truth is found.*

"Three versions," Percy said, shifting his weight against the cold. "Three different prophecies?"

"Or three different recordings of the same prophecy. Or three different attempts to capture what the Oracle actually said." Annabeth's eyes were bright with the thrill of discovery, even in this moment of danger. "Whoever altered the original didn't destroy it. They layered over it. And underneath, the older versions are still there."

"Can we find more?"

"Maybe. There might be other records in the attic. Other transcripts. If someone went through the trouble of altering one version, there might be traces of the alteration process. Evidence."

"You want to go back."

"I want to go back. Tonight. While they think they've scared us off."

Percy looked at her. This girl — this fierce, brilliant, reckless girl — was everything he remembered and nothing he expected. The original Annabeth had been strategic, careful, always three steps ahead. This Annabeth was all of that and more. She was brave in a way that bordered on reckless, and she was looking at him with an expression that said she knew this was dangerous and she'd do it anyway.

"Okay," he said. "Tonight."

---

The second trip to the attic was harder. They'd added security — new wards on the windows, a tripwire across the back entrance, something that hummed in the air near the front door like a low, constant note.

Percy felt it first. The hum was a pressure against his skin, a warning that made the hair on his arms stand up.

"New ward," he whispered. "Someone reinforced it after last time."

"Then we use the old entrance. The vent by the kitchen."

"There is no vent by the kitchen."

"There's always a vent. Every building has ventilation. Chiron's is just better hidden than most." Annabeth's smile was thin. "I spent two days mapping the airflow patterns in this building. I know where every gap is."

Of course she did.

They moved through the shadows, following Annabeth's map, and twenty minutes later they were in the attic again. The Oracle's mist swirled around them, thicker than before, as if responding to their presence.

"Records," Annabeth said, already moving. "Prophecy transcripts. Look for anything dated three years ago or earlier."

Percy searched the desk, the shelves, the boxes stacked in the corners. He found old journals, training logs, camp rosters. He found a collection of poetry that made him smile — it was Clarisse's, he realized. She wrote poems. She was secretly a poet.

But nothing about prophecies. Nothing dated three years ago.

"Annabeth," he said. "I can't find anything."

"Keep looking. The alteration would have been done carefully — they wouldn't leave notes about it. But there might be traces. Ink differences. Paper age. Something."

They searched for another twenty minutes. Then Annabeth found it.

A piece of parchment, tucked between two old journals. It was covered in handwriting that Percy didn't recognize — cramped, precise, the letters cramped together like they were trying not to take up too much space.

"It's a note," Annabeth said, scanning it. "Dated three years ago. It says—" She stopped. Her face went pale.

"What? What does it say?"

"It says: 'First change successful. The prince's thread adjusted. The sisters bound as predicted. Next: establish the Hall of the Gods. —A.'"

The words hung in the air like smoke.

"Someone made the changes," Percy said, shifting his weight against the cold. "They documented it. They—" He looked at the note again. "A. That's a signature."

"One letter." Annabeth's voice was barely above a whisper. "It's not enough to identify anyone."

"It's enough to know we're on the right track."

"Percy, this changes things. Whoever did this — they were deliberate. They had a plan. And they signed their name." She folded the note carefully. "We're taking this too. Everything we find, we're taking."

They left the attic the way they'd come, slipping through the vent and dropping into the shadows. They didn't speak until they were back at the lake, their prize secured.

"A," Percy said, shifting his weight against the cold. "Someone whose name starts with A altered the timeline, built the Hall of the Gods, and documented the whole process."

"Someone with access to divine power. Someone who understood prophecies. Someone patient enough to work in secret for years." Annabeth's eyes were fierce. "We're going to find them, Percy. And we're going to stop them."

"Together."

"Together."

And somewhere above them, the storm gathered — patient and dark and inevitable.

---

The next morning, Clarisse found Percy at the training grounds.

"You've been busy," she said, sitting on the bench beside him. "Nighttime excursions. Stolen records. Very adventurous of you."

Percy's heart stopped. "How long have you been following me?"

"Since the night you snuck into the Big House the first time. You're not as subtle as you think, Jackson." She was smiling, but her eyes were serious. "I followed you to the attic. Saw what you and the Athena girl were doing up there. Didn't interfere because I wanted to know what you were after."

"And now you know."

"Now I know you're investigating something big. Something dangerous. Something that got Mr. D's attention." She leaned forward. "I also know you're not the only one investigating. There are others — older campers, a few counselors — who've been quietly digging into the changes. We've all noticed the same things. The Hall of the Gods. The prophecy theft. The way the rules changed."

"You mean there's a group."

"Let's call it a conspiracy. We meet in secret. Compare notes. Try to uncover who's really behind all of this." Clarisse's smile was bitter. "We're all children of the Big Three, if you haven't noticed. Poseidon's kids, Zeus's kids, one of Hades. And we're all asking the same questions: why does the world suddenly care so much about us? Why are the gods watching? What changed three years ago, and why doesn't anyone want to tell us?"

Percy stared at her. "How many?"

"Five, including you and your girlfriend. And I'm guessing you two have the same suspicions we all do — that this prophecy theft, whatever it means, it's connected to us. To the Big Three kids specifically."

"It is. We're in the prophecy. Both of us."

"Then we need to tell the others. Tonight. There's a meeting at the amphitheater, midnight. Come if you want answers."

She stood and walked away, leaving Percy standing at the training grounds with his heart pounding and his world tilting sideways.

Not simply them. Other Big Three kids. Other investigations. Other people who had noticed the cracks in the world and started digging.

He wasn't alone.

Percy had never been less alone in his life.

---

The midnight meeting in the amphitheater was small and tense and revelatory.

Five people, as Clarisse had said. Percy and Annabeth. Clarisse and Silena. And a boy Percy hadn't met before — Nico, the young son of Hades, who sat in the shadows like he was afraid of being seen.

"You're the new one," Nico said, without looking at Percy. "Poseidon's kid. The one who grew up in Atlantis."

"That's me."

"I've heard about you. Everyone has." Nico's voice was flat, emotionless. "The prince. The sheltered kid. The one who beat Thalia in a sparring match on his first day."

"That was luck."

"It wasn't. Don't be humble. It's boring." Nico looked up, and his eyes were ancient in a child's face. "I know what you and the Athena girl have been doing. The attic visits. The stolen records. The note with the signature."

Percy's blood ran cold. "How do you know about that?"

"Because I've been doing the same thing. For longer than you have. For longer than any of us have." Nico stood, and for a breath his posture shifted — older, harder, carrying the weight of someone who'd seen too much. "I've been investigating the changes to this camp for three years. I've been trying to uncover who altered the prophecy and why. And I've almost found out."

"Almost?"

"I know who changed the timeline. I just can't prove it yet." His eyes swept across the group. "And I'm not sure I want to."

The silence that followed was absolute.

"How old are you?" Annabeth asked. "Really?"

"Does it matter? What matters is that I've been here longer than any of you. I remember what this camp was before the changes. I remember the world before the changes." Nico's voice cracked, just slightly. "And I know it's coming back. The thing that was changed three years ago — it's going to come back. And when it does..."

He didn't finish the sentence. He didn't need to.

Percy felt the press of it — the impossibility of it, the danger of it, the sheer terrifying scope of what Nico was describing. Someone had changed the timeline. Someone was still changing it. And somehow, in three years of quiet, careful investigation, this boy had gotten closer to the truth than anyone else.

"Who did it?" Clarisse demanded. "Who changed the timeline?"

Nico looked at her. Then at Silena. Then at Annabeth. Then at Percy.

"Someone we all trust," he said. "Someone with reason to want the changes. Someone who thought they were saving us."

"Who?"

But Nico shook his head. "Not yet. Not until I'm sure. Because if I'm wrong — if I accuse an innocent person — everything falls apart. The investigation, the conspiracy, all of it. And whoever really did it will know we're close."

"Then what do we do?"

"We keep digging. We compare notes. We work together instead of separately. And when I've proof — when I can show everyone what I found — we confront them together."

He looked at Percy with those ancient eyes. "You're the prince. Poseidon's son. You've access and authority that none of us have. Use it. Carefully. But use it."

"Why should we trust you?" Silena asked. "You've been alone on this for three years. Why would you trust us with what you know?"

Nico was quiet for a heartbeat. Then he said, "Because I'm tired. I'm still a kid, and I've been carrying this alone for three years, and I'm tired. And I think — I hope — that together we can do what I couldn't do alone."

He looked at Annabeth. At Percy. At Clarisse and Silena.

"I think we might actually be able to stop this."

The amphitheater was silent. Five kids, sitting in the moonlight, shouldering the press of a conspiracy and a mystery and the fading hope that they might be able to fix what had been broken.

"Together, then," Percy said, shifting his weight against the cold.

"Together," they echoed.

And somewhere in the darkness, the storm gathered — patient and dark and inevitable.

And they waited for it to break.
