## Chapter 90: Storm and Sea

By the time Percy stepped onto Cabin Three's porch, the morning sky had gone the color of an old bronze shield. He watched the clouds gather over Long Island Sound, each bank folding over the one beneath it, until the horizon disappeared. The wind came in short, contradictory turns: north against his cheek, east through the porch rail, then straight down hard enough to flatten the mess-hall flags against their poles. It never settled on a direction long enough for him to blame the weather on one shore.

The sea announced itself first. Pressure gathered behind Percy's molars; copper spread across his tongue. Down at the beach, waves struck one another sideways and broke into white foam. The canoes rattled in their rack. The naiads had already shut their shutters. Beneath the noise, he felt too much water moving too fast in too many directions.

Clarisse came out onto the porch behind him, barefoot, hair still damp from the shower, frowning at the sky.

"That's not Zeus," she said.

Percy glanced at her. "What?"

"That's not just Zeus," Clarisse clarified. She jerked her chin toward the clouds. "Zeus storms smell like ozone and hot metal. Like right before he smites something. This smells like —" She inhaled, nose wrinkling. "Salt. Old salt. Deep water. Something's stirring the sea up from underneath and the sky's reacting to it."

Silena pushed open the screen door with a mug in each hand. She gave one to Percy without asking. The air was humid enough to swim through, but the warm cup steadied his fingers, and Silena looked calmer now that she had something useful to carry.

"Border patrol just doubled," Silena said quietly. "Chiron's calling all senior campers to the Big House in twenty. Annabeth just Iris-messaged me. She said Thalia's nose is bleeding."

Percy went still. "Thalia's nose is bleeding?"

"From the static," Silena said. "She says every time lightning hits within a mile she feels it in her sinuses. Jason's here on an exchange visit approved by both camps; he's grounding her as best he can, but there's too much charge in the air. It's building faster than they can bleed it off."

Clarisse cursed under her breath, vivid and sailor-worthy and entirely Poseidon. "So it's both. Sea and sky fighting each other."

Percy looked out at the churning Sound, then up at the churning sky, and felt the familiar pull in two directions. His father in the deep. His uncle in the high air. And Camp Half-Blood caught in the middle, a thin green valley with magical borders that were never designed to hold against both at once.

He drank the hot chocolate in three gulps and set the mug down.

"Then we'd better get to the Big House," he said.

---

The Big House living room was packed shoulder to shoulder and smelled like rain and sunscreen and fear.

Chiron stood in his wheelchair at the center, his face grim. Mr. D was actually present for once, no longer pretending to read his wine magazine, his eyes fixed on the windows where rain had just started to lash sideways. Annabeth stood by the Ping-Pong table with a huge map of Camp Half-Blood spread out, weighted down at the corners with spare arrowheads. Thalia stood beside her, pale, a smear of dried blood under one nostril, her black hair standing out from her head in a staticky halo. Jason Grace stood just behind his sister, one hand hovering near her shoulder without quite touching, his own hair lifting.

Katie Gardner, Travis Stoll, Malcolm, Will Solace, Nico di Angelo — all the senior counselors, all talking at once until Chiron stamped a hoof against the floor.

"Enough," Chiron said. "Annabeth. Report."

Annabeth stepped forward, and Percy felt that familiar flip in his stomach, pride and worry tangled together. She was in full strategist mode, hair pulled back, gray eyes sharp, a piece of charcoal in her hand.

"The borders are holding for now, but they're stressed on two axes," she said, drawing a rough oval on the map. "Peitho and the dragon — Peleus — are maintaining the magical perimeter, but the storm is hitting them with storm surge from below and electrical overload from above. It's like —" She glanced at Percy. "It's like the quest. The one at the reservoir."

A murmur went through the room. Percy felt Clarisse and Silena straighten on either side of him.

He remembered. Of course he remembered.

At the reservoir, a dam spirit had turned a fight between a Nereid and a wind nymph into a hazard for the camp's water supply. The runoff carried more than silt; the spirit had called lightning down to defend itself, and each element kept feeding the other. Percy, Annabeth, Thalia, Clarisse and Silena had gone out to break that loop. They had stopped trying to hold the flood in place. The Poseidon siblings gave it a moving spiral and a clear outlet; Annabeth worked out the angles, and Thalia directed the strike into the current so it could carry the charge away. Percy could still remember the first instinct—to brace against the water as if strength alone could make it harmless—and how close that had come to making the problem worse.

Water to carry. Sky to release. Mind to aim.

It had worked, though no one had mistaken the outcome for a clean victory. Clarisse had shouted for Percy to let go before his arms locked; Silena had checked his face when steam hid him from view; Annabeth had called angles from the dam with rain plastering her hair to her cheeks. Thalia had planted her spear in the spillway and laughed once, wild with relief, when the current took the charge. They had returned singed, shaking and more careful about what each of them could ask the others to carry.

Thalia remembered it too. She touched her nose self-consciously and nodded at Annabeth.

"Tell them the part about the timing," Thalia said hoarsely. Her voice crackled faintly, as if her throat was full of static. "Tell them why it has to be moving."

Annabeth nodded and drew three quick arrows on the map, all converging on the cannon. "At the reservoir, the first two strikes failed because Percy tried to hold the water still like a shield. The charge pooled and backwashed up his arms. Third time, Silena kept the water circulating — a loop, not a wall — and Clarisse forced the inflow to match the outflow so the level never rose. The lightning rode the current down and out instead of sitting in it. Flow rate matters more than volume."

Silena raised a hand shyly, as if still in class. "The water has to want to leave. If it wants to stay and flood, it holds the charge and gets angry. If it has somewhere to go — if we promise it the sea — it carries the charge with it happily."

"Water psychology," Travis whispered to Katie. "Got it."

"It's true," Percy said, serious. "They listen better if you give them a destination."

Jason leaned forward, his blue eyes narrowed in thought. He looked exhausted already, dark circles under his eyes from bleeding off charge all morning. "And from the sky side? At the reservoir, Thalia almost stopped her own heart on the second draw. What changed on the third?"

Thalia and Annabeth exchanged a look, the look of two sisters who had argued for an hour in a van and come out trusting each other more.

"Grounding through contact, not through air," Annabeth said. "Thalia didn't let the bolt jump to her. She met it halfway with the spear tip and kept the butt already in the water before the strike landed. No gap, no arc flash. And I called the timing. She can't watch the cloud and the water at the same time. I watch the cloud. She trusts me."

"You trust me to be your eyes," Thalia corrected, bumping Annabeth's shoulder with her own. Static snapped between them. "Still do?"

Annabeth bumped her back, unfazed. "Always."

Chiron watched the exchange with something like relief crossing his ancient face. "Then we replicate it. But children, understand scale. The reservoir was a pond. This is a hurricane with divine fingerprints. You will not hold one strike. You will hold dozens."

Annabeth tapped the map now. "Same principle, bigger scale. The sea is surging against the border from the outside — waves ten feet above normal, pushing monsters and debris ahead of them. The sky is overloading it from the top — lightning seeking the tallest conductive points, which right now are Half-Blood Hill, the Big House lightning rod, and any demigod holding a metal weapon. If a bolt hits the border while it's saturated with salt water, the feedback could shatter the ward for minutes at a time."

"Minutes is all it takes," Chiron said quietly. "There are already scorpion nests and hellhounds gathering at the property line. They can feel the weakness."

"What do you need?" Percy asked.

Annabeth looked at him, then at Clarisse and Silena, then at Thalia and Jason. Her expression softened for just a second — the strategist letting the friend through.

"I need Storm and Sea to stop fighting each other and work together," she said. "Poseidon kids take the water. Zeus kids take the sky. I coordinate."

Thalia wiped at her nose and grinned, sharp and wild. "You had me at work together, Grace."

Mr. D sighed. "I suppose this means I actually have to protect the wine — I mean, the campers. Very well. The Zeus children will not be struck down on my watch if I can help it. Chiron, assign the infirmary and the young ones to me."

It was as close to caring as Mr. D ever got, and everyone knew it.

Chiron nodded. "Poseidon cabin — you will hold the shoreline and the creek. Prevent flooding inside the borders. Channel all excess water out through the cannon and back to the Sound in a controlled flow. Do not let it pool. Pooled water conducts."

"Understood," Clarisse said. She was already rolling her shoulders, settling into her fighter's stance.

"Zeus cabin — you will ground the lightning before it strikes the wards. Controlled draws, as at the reservoir, into prepared grounding points. No free strikes."

Jason nodded. "We can set grounding rods — celestial bronze spears driven into wet sand, connected —"

"No," Annabeth interrupted. "Not sand. The channel has to keep moving. Percy, hold the runoff on its way to the cannon. Thalia can put the strike into that current at the mouth and drive it beyond the border, into the Sound. We do not pull seawater through camp."

Percy followed the route with one finger. "The creek and the lake stay fresh. The outlet carries the charge away; it doesn't reverse the flow. Same idea as the reservoir, only the current has farther to travel."

"Much bigger," Silena murmured, but she was smiling a little. "Okay. Okay, we can do that."

"Annabeth will be at the command post on Half-Blood Hill with a mirror and a runner system," Chiron said. "Thalia, you will be with the Poseidon team at the shore for the actual grounding. Jason, you will hold the hill and bleed off the ambient charge so the hill ward doesn't overload."

Jason opened his mouth like he wanted to argue — no one liked being split from their sibling in a fight — but Thalia clapped him on the shoulder.

"He's right, Jase. You're better at steady bleed. I'm better at big dramatic strikes. It's the whole daredevil versus electrician thing."

"You're both electricians," Travis muttered, and Katie elbowed him.

Chiron raised a hand. "All other cabins to defensive positions. Archers to the designated indoor shelter with their equipment secured. Healers to the infirmary with Mr. D. Every younger camper moves with a counselor to the approved interior shelter rather than remaining alone in an exposed cabin. Go. The next wave hits in forty minutes."

The room exploded into motion.

Percy caught Annabeth's hand for half a second as everyone pushed toward the doors. Her fingers were cold.

"Hey," he said quietly. "Reservoir rules?"

She squeezed back. "Reservoir rules. You hold. I aim. Nobody plays hero."

"Promise?"

"Promise," she said. She was gone into the crowd, calling for copper cable and the hill's grounding points, while Percy turned to find his two sisters waiting for him.

Clarisse cracked her knuckles. Silena tied her hair back with a piece of seaweed ribbon she kept in her pocket for luck.

"Storm and Sea, huh?" Clarisse said.

Percy grinned, despite the thunder rolling hard enough to rattle the windows. "Storm and Sea."

---

The first thing Percy did at the beach was stop the team from running into the water.

Clarisse had five volunteers and two rescue lines ready to go. The Sound was already rising against the dunes. A broken mast floated beyond the surf, and a small group of younger campers had begun gathering near the road, looking frightened but curious.

"Everybody back to the upper marker," Percy said.

Clarisse looked at him. "We've got a line."

"I know. We need to check where the water is moving before anyone gets in it."

She paused, then nodded. The siblings had spent a week learning to obey the same stop signals, and Percy was using one now instead of assuming he could hold every wave himself.

Silena led the younger campers toward the shelter. She asked the counselors to count them by name rather than by cabin, because several children had been visiting friends when the storm warning sounded. Bea had her stuffed seal and wanted to know whether the magical tide pools from the spring beach day were safe.

"They went home," Silena reminded her. "Remember? Their nursery is in Atlantis. The beach is not holding them here."

Bea nodded, relieved. Lucy helped her walk behind the counselor, and Milo kept track of the names on the emergency list without asking which children were frightened. His job was to check attendance, not explain how everyone felt.

Silena stopped beneath the Big House overhang to compare Milo's count with the roster. The first tally had one extra child in Cabin Eleven and none in Cabin Seven; the second showed a missing name that turned out to belong to a camper who had been carrying blankets to the infirmary. She made the counselors start again from the bottom of the list rather than guess which entry had gone wrong. Nobody was allowed to run back to the cabins to prove they were safe. If a name could not be checked, a counselor reported the last place they'd seen that camper, and Chiron's adults handled the search.

Percy wanted to stay until every name had a mark beside it. Silena handed him the roster instead of letting him hover over her shoulder. "Can you take this to Chiron and ask him to compare it with the infirmary sheet? I'll keep the shelter count here."

It was a small job, but it gave him somewhere to put his hands. He read each name aloud while Chiron checked the second sheet. One Apollo camper had been sent to fetch a blanket and had not made it back before the door was barred; Will confirmed she was safe in the infirmary, waiting with a cup of water and a counselor. Percy marked her location instead of crossing her off. The distinction seemed obvious when he said it out loud. He had not thought to make it before Silena asked.

When he returned, Malcolm was at the upper marker, sheltering the flag set under his jacket. The hill team had been sending signals to the shoreline, but rain and wind had begun to tear at the cloth. Annabeth couldn't shout far enough for Percy to hear her; Malcolm could relay a short flag code from the hill, and the shoreline crew could repeat it by hand signal. It was slower than a voice, but no one had to guess at a sentence lost in the gusts.

"How many flags can you see from there?" Percy asked.

"One at a time if I hold them above the marker," Malcolm said. His teeth were chattering, but his hands were steady. "Two if the wind lets them hang."

Percy took one of the spare signal cloths from the kit and gave him the driest one. "Then we use one at a time. If you lose sight of the hill, you come down to the Big House. Don't try to make a message up."

Malcolm looked as if he might argue that he could do more. Then he nodded and tucked the cloth beneath his coat. He was part of the plan, Percy realized, but he was also allowed to leave it. Percy had once thought asking someone to stay safe meant doubting them. The new rule was easier to live with: make the job clear, then make the way out just as clear.

Percy watched until they passed the protected turn. Then he turned back to the Sound.

Annabeth's map had separated the water into two problems, and Percy held on to that distinction as he walked the upper path. The Sound's salt surge was striking the outside of the border. Rain and runoff were collecting inside camp, carrying soil off the strawberry rows and pushing the creek toward the canoe lake. The lake was fresh water; the storm had not opened a route from the Sound into it, and nobody was to turn the two systems into one just because the beach channel offered an outlet. They would move inland runoff out through the cannon, then check the lake's banks and the creek's direction before reopening any path beside it.

At the marker, Annabeth had weighted the map with four bronze arrowheads and drawn the freshwater route in charcoal. The map was already smearing at the edges. She had asked Malcolm to report only three things: whether the signal was visible, whether the lightning was building, and whether a path had flooded. No one needed him to interpret the storm.

"If the creek reverses, close the path beside it," Percy said, following her line with one finger. "Keep the canoe lake out of the sea channel."

Annabeth nodded. "And the salt surge stays on the far side of the ward. We move only the rainwater and the runoff already inside the border. If that changes, we abandon the cannon route and get people uphill."

Percy studied the little marks she had made for the lake, the strawberry fields and the beach. It was a familiar feeling to see her turn a problem into a map. He knew enough now not to mistake the map for the problem itself. "I'll check the creek from the high side before Clarisse and I touch the channel."

"Good." Annabeth tapped the route she had drawn. "And no one goes into the lake to check it. We can see the bank from the trail."

"I wasn't going to—"

Her look stopped him. He amended the sentence. "I won't."

She reached out and squeezed his wrist, the quick private reassurance they used when there were too many people around for a longer conversation. "I know you can help. I need you to tell me what you see before you decide what it means."

"I can do that."

It was a promise about the next ten minutes, not a vow to be fearless or perfectly sensible. Percy could keep that one.

Before the shoreline crew moved, Annabeth asked Malcolm to take the signal cloth to the sheltered marker and show them the code from there. The first red flag vanished against the dark cloud behind him. He tried again lower, where the pale cloth showed against the Big House roof. Percy repeated the signal with his arm; Clarisse repeated it from the beach. The chain worked while everyone kept the same sightline.

They rehearsed the signal chain before anyone moved: a held red flag meant stop; a held white flag meant the channel was clear; a raised yellow flag meant irregular wind and an automatic hold; and a single drop of white meant the strike was authorized. Thalia would raise two fingers to say she was ready. Malcolm would repeat each hill signal, Percy would repeat it at the shore, and Thalia would answer before the strike. If the cloth disappeared or the message changed, they would hold position. Annabeth tested the sequence twice. On the second try, Silena raised her hand too soon and Percy corrected her before Clarisse started moving water. It felt fussy. It also took less than a minute to prevent them from acting on different versions of the plan.

"The mirror gives me the beach, but it doesn't give me your voice," Annabeth told Percy through Malcolm's relay. "If I lose the flags, assume nothing has changed."

Percy lifted two fingers in answer. *Understood.*

She held his gaze in the small Mist-glass for a moment before turning back to the map. Percy knew the look; she was already measuring every line again, though the first version had been hers. It mattered that she kept checking. It mattered more that she let them see what she was unsure about.

Clarisse came beside him to inspect the lower rescue line. "You made her test the signals."

"She asked us to test them."

"Good." Clarisse tugged the rope once, then frowned at the loosened anchor. "A plan is only useful if everybody gets the same one."

That was all the approval she offered. Percy found he liked it better than being told he'd done something heroic.

The rescue line was long enough for the marked practice area, not for waves that were now breaking across the entire beach. Clarisse examined the anchors. One was set in ground that had begun to soften. If they tied a person to it and the sand gave way, the line could drag them into the surf rather than pull them out.

"We need to move it," she said.

Percy looked at the higher rock shelf. "That one?"

"Maybe. Check the attachment point."

They called the trained shore crew rather than improvising a knot around a half-buried driftwood post. The crew tested the rock anchor and approved it for the rescue line. A second line was laid out for the lower walkway, in case anyone needed to move equipment away from the beach. Nobody entered the surf.

Silena returned with the evacuation count. Every camper assigned to the beach group was accounted for, but there was one problem: the canvas kit containing the spare dry bandages had been left in the shoreline shed. The shed was near the old tide mark and already taking spray through the door.

"I can get it," Percy said.

"Not alone," Clarisse replied.

"I can move water."

"You can move water, and you're also thirteen. That kit is not worth putting you in the surf."

He looked at her. The memory of Will checking his hands after the storm kissed the back of his mind. He didn't like hearing it. He knew it was true.

"Then can we leave the kit?" he asked.

Silena looked toward the shed. "Yes. Will has supplies at the infirmary. The dry bandages aren't worth somebody getting trapped."

Percy felt an odd relief. A month ago he would have tried to save the kit because it was there. Today he could let it go.

They marked the shed as unsafe and told the maintenance crew not to enter until the water receded. The decision left the beach team with less equipment, but it kept everybody in a position they could defend.

"All right," Clarisse said. "Now what do you need?"

Percy looked at the approaching wave. He was beginning to feel the water pulling against his senses, as if it wanted to ask him a question in a language he understood only partly.

"I need you to keep the line crew back until I say I can hold a channel," he replied. "If I call retreat, everybody goes. No debate."

Clarisse nodded. "Agreed."

"And Silena, don't come down if someone gets a scrape. Let Will decide who needs help."

She smiled grimly. "Agreed."

Percy took a breath. He didn't feel ready. He felt informed enough to try, with people nearby who could stop him if the plan failed.

The beach was unrecognizable forty minutes later.

Percy had learned this beach through his months at camp, through drills, walks with Annabeth and spring visits with his sisters. His remembered childhood had been spent with Sally in Manhattan; this life's earlier years belonged to Atlantis, not to first-person memories of a Long Island camp beach. He knew every dip and hollow, every place the tide liked to pool.

All of it was gone under gray-green chaos.

The Sound had risen six feet. Waves as tall as a house broke a hundred yards out and surged forward in foaming sheets, carrying kelp and driftwood and once, sickeningly, the mast of a mortal sailboat, barnacle-crusted and snapping like a twig. The wind screamed straight off the water, flinging spray so hard it stung like needles. Rain fell sideways, then upside down, then sideways again. Thunder never stopped — it just rolled continuously, a low growl punctuated by cracks so loud Percy's ribs vibrated.

And the lightning. Gods, the lightning.

It came down in forks and sheets and once in a perfect, terrifying spiral over the eastern hills, striking Half-Blood Hill three times in ten seconds. Each time, the air went white-blue and the smell of ozone burned Percy's nose, and on the hilltop he could see a small figure — Jason, spear raised — shuddering with the effort of pulling the charge sideways into the grounding cables Annabeth had rigged.

"PERCY!" Clarisse shouted over the roar. She was ankle-deep in surf, her spear planted, her other arm extended. Water already spiraled around her forearms in thick braids, clear and glowing faintly green. "WE'RE LOSING THE CREEK!"

He turned. The creek that fed the canoe lake had burst its banks. Brown floodwater poured across the strawberry fields, ripping out rows of plants, heading straight for the cabins. If it pooled around the cabins, the next lightning strike would travel through every puddle at once.

"Silena! Left flank!" Percy shouted, running.

Silena was already there. She stood knee-deep in the flood, eyes closed, humming the shanty she'd taught them during the cove drills. The steady chorus gave her hands a rhythm. Water slowed around her, listened, curled. She raised both palms, and the flood lifted with them into a trembling wall.

Percy splashed in beside her and added his strength. He felt the water like an extension of his own blood — cold, panicked, wanting to spread, to sink, to drown. He spoke to it the way Poseidon had taught him, not in words but in pressure and intent. *Not here. This way. Follow me. I've got a better path.*

The water hesitated, then turned.

Clarisse joined them, breathing hard, and added her pull. Three Poseidon children, shoulder to shoulder in the rising flood, three wills braided together. Percy in the middle, Clarisse on the right with her brute-force control, Silena on the left with her gentle coaxing. It was how they trained in the cabin — Percy the anchor, Clarisse the hammer, Silena the hands.

Together they bent the flood into a curve.

"Channel! To the cannon!" Percy yelled. He looked uphill for Malcolm's flag rather than wasting breath on a message Annabeth could not hear.

Up on Half-Blood Hill, half a mile away, Annabeth stood under a hastily rigged tarp with a pair of bronze binoculars and a Mist-mirror linked to the beach. A runner — Malcolm, soaked to the bone — relayed her signals with red flags.

Malcolm waved: *HOLD. THALIA INBOUND. CLEAR THE CHANNEL.*

"Clear it!" Percy relayed. "Silena, narrow the walls! Clarisse, push faster!"

They ran, half-wading, half-surfing on the water they controlled, herding a living river across camp. The water-channel glowed faintly, ten feet wide, three feet deep, rushing from the flooded fields toward the border at the cannon, where the boundary dropped straight into the Sound. Campers scrambled out of the way, staring. A couple of Apollo kids cheered.

Percy could feel both his siblings in the water with him, as clearly as if they were holding his hands. Clarisse was all force and edges, shoving the water forward with her will, keeping the current fast enough to carry charge. Her control was not delicate, but it was relentless. She had learned to water-wrestle in the deep trenches with killer whales, and she fought the flood the same way she fought everything — head-on, refusing to yield an inch. Where the channel tried to bulge and spill, Clarisse slammed it back into line.

Silena was the opposite. Where Clarisse pushed, Silena persuaded. Percy felt her power as a cool, steady humming along the edges of the flow, smoothing turbulence, coaxing eddies to rejoin the main current. She spoke to frightened water the way she spoke to frightened new campers, softly and without condescension. Under her touch, the muddy floodwater ran clear, dropping its silt, calming. Percy had once asked her how she did it. She had shrugged and said she just remembered what it felt like to be small and swept along, and promised the water it would not be alone.

And Percy held the middle. That was his job, then and now. The anchor. He took Clarisse's hammering force in one hand and Silena's gentle shaping in the other and blended them, keeping the channel alive but contained. His father's power sat heavy and bright in his chest, and he poured it out steadily, breath by breath. Inhale, gather. Exhale, release downstream. He could feel every rock and root the water passed over, every dip where it wanted to pool. He whispered to it constantly, promises and directions. *Almost there. Keep moving. The sea is waiting.*

Thalia met them at the cannon, soaked, laughing wildly, her camp spear crackling in her hand. Lightning crawled up and down the shaft in blue-white arcs. Her eyes reflected every flash.

"Percy Jackson!" she shouted joyfully. "Tell me you've got a river for me!"

"Right here!" Percy shouted back, holding the channel with everything he had. His arms shook. Clarisse grabbed his shoulder to steady him, pouring her strength into his. Silena kept the walls smooth, her face white with concentration. "It's live! It's moving! Can you feed it?"

Thalia looked up at the sky, where a massive anvil cloud pulsed with inner light. Another bolt was coming — Percy could feel it building, the hairs on his arms standing straight up. It wanted to hit the hill. It wanted to hit the Big House. It wanted to hit *them*.

Thalia raised two fingers toward the hill—the signal they'd agreed meant she was ready. Malcolm repeated her gesture from the marker. Annabeth held white to confirm the channel was clear, then dropped it once when the bolt was ready. Malcolm repeated the drop; Percy repeated it to Thalia.

*Now.*

Thalia thrust her spear straight up and *called*.

There was no other word for it. She didn't wait for lightning. She invited it. She reached up with her father's power and said *here, here, I can take it,* and the sky answered.

The bolt came down like the fist of a god.

White. Soundless for one impossible second. Then sound so huge Percy felt it in his marrow.

It struck Thalia's spear — and Thalia, screaming with effort and exhilaration, *wrenched* it sideways and drove the butt of the spear straight down into the center of Percy's moving river.

The world went green-white.

Percy felt the lightning hit the water — felt it as heat and pressure and a high singing note that went through his bones. For a heartbeat he was terrified they had miscalculated, that the charge would travel up the water and into their bodies, that they would all fry together in a perfect circuit.

The water was moving. That was the lesson they'd kept from the reservoir: don't give the charge a still pool to gather in.

The river *took* the lightning and ran with it, glowing from within like a vein of light, rushing, rushing toward the border, through the cannon's mouth, out over the cliff and into the Sound where it grounded with a hiss and a plume of steam visible even through the rain.

The thunder arrived a second later and knocked everyone flat.

Percy sat up, coughing, ears ringing, staring at his hands. They tingled, but they worked. Clarisse lay on her back in the mud, laughing breathlessly. Silena was on her hands and knees, soaked hair plastered to her face, grinning like a maniac. Thalia stood over them all, spear smoking, hair standing straight up, nose bleeding again, triumphant.

"ONE!" she shouted, holding up a finger. "Who's counting?"

Malcolm, from the hill, waved frantically: *TWO MORE BUILDING. NORTHWEST. HOLD POSITION.*

Percy groaned and got to his feet, pulling Silena up. "You heard the man! Channel's still open! Clarisse —"

"On it," Clarisse growled, already rebuilding the walls where the surge had collapsed them. "Silena, I need smooth on the bend by the volleyball court, it's eddying —"

"I've got it, I've got it — Percy, the lake's overtopping, can you —"

"I've got the lake, you've got the field, Clarisse holds the middle — Thalia, talk to me about timing —"

The canoe lake was swelling under rain and runoff, not the Sound. Percy could feel the difference in the water: the lake's familiar cool weight against the harder, salt-heavy push at the cannon. He kept the outer surge beyond the boundary and drew a separate run of freshwater away from the lake's overflowing edge, folding it into the camp-side channel only after it had cleared the cabins. The current did not need to become seawater to move toward the outlet. It needed a route that did not turn the lake into a reservoir for the next bolt.

"The lake stays fresh," Percy called, more to the team than to the storm. "No one pulls from the Sound."

Clarisse glanced at the rising bank, then back at him. "I have the wall. Don't split yourself three ways."

"I need two."

"Then tell me which one you want."

Percy pointed to the lower edge where runoff was beginning to curl back toward the cabins. Clarisse shifted her pressure there, holding the bend while Percy pulled the overflow out. Silena kept the field water moving. It took longer than his first instinct wanted, but the brown sheet stopped advancing. The lake continued to rise, yet the water nearest the path fell a few inches. He could feel the margin return under his feet.

The lowest strawberry strip was already flat where the runoff had crossed it. Katie stood on the higher path, lifting seed trays onto a cart while two younger campers carried them uphill. She looked toward the cabins, then pointed to the damaged strip and shook her head. The choice was plain even through the rain: saving the plants would turn the water toward the walkways.

"Let that strip go," Clarisse said. She held the bend with both arms out, face tight from the effort. "We can keep the rest if we don't pull too hard."

The words landed badly. Percy could feel the plants bending in the mud, their roots loosening one by one. He wanted to lift every row clear, divert every puddle and keep every camper dry. The water kept asking for a path, and Katie had marked one that led away from the cabins.

He waited for her next signal. She had stopped the younger campers and sent them toward the Big House with the remaining trays. Then she held up her palm: enough. She had counted what they could save from where she stood. Percy followed her decision.

"All right. We protect the path," he called. "Silena, keep the runoff in the lower furrow. Clarisse, hold this bend. I'll draw it away from the lake."

Silena kept the field water moving; Percy lifted the shallow overflow; Clarisse took the force where the ground narrowed. Mud and broken leaves went toward the cannon with the runoff. The cabins stayed clear. Most of the strawberry rows remained upright, and the lower strip could be replanted after the ground dried. Percy could not rescue the field by himself. He could help the people who knew it make the next decision.

The second strike nearly broke them.

It built faster than Annabeth expected, a low, flat-topped cloud to the northwest that pulsed three times in quick succession. Malcolm raised yellow: *IRREGULAR WIND. HOLD.* A gust snapped the cloth down before he could replace it with the white channel-clear signal. Annabeth saw the cloud change and dropped her own flag on the old count. Thalia read the falling cloth as the release and began to move before Malcolm could repeat it or Percy could relay confirmation. The bolt came down wide of the spear, striking the edge of the channel instead of the center.

The water exploded.

A geyser of steam and spray shot thirty feet into the air. The shockwave knocked Silena backward into Percy, and Percy lost his grip on the left wall. Floodwater burst through the gap, rushing toward the cabins in a brown sheet. Clarisse roared and threw herself into the breach, planting both feet and both hands, forcing the water back with sheer will. Her arms shook. Blood vessels stood out in her temples. The water resisted, panicked by the heat and noise, wanting to scatter.

"Silena!" Percy shouted, hauling his sister upright. She was gasping, eyes wide, hair full of mud. "Talk to it! It's scared!"

Silena nodded, wiped water from her face, and plunged both hands back into the flood. She did not push. She sang. The same sea shanty, louder now, cracked and breathless but steady. Percy felt her power flare, warm and insistent, wrapping around the frightened water like arms around a crying child. *Hush. Hush. I know it hurt. Come back. Come back to the path.*

Slowly, the spill slowed. Turned. Rejoined.

Clarisse grinned at Silena through dripping hair, fierce and proud. "Good. Good, little sister. Hold it there — Percy, seal it!"

Percy sealed it, braiding his will over theirs, and the channel snapped back into shape, narrower but faster.

Thalia, meanwhile, had been knocked to one knee, her spear humming angrily. Her right glove smoked. She tore it off with her teeth and flung it aside, revealing a red, blistered palm. Percy felt his stomach clench. Will would later call it a superficial flash burn, but that did not make it nothing.

"Thalia!" he shouted. "Status!"

She held up her bare hand, wiggling her fingers. "Still sexy! Still conductive! Annabeth — I need a cleaner call! That one was early!"

On the hill, Annabeth cupped her hands around her mouth. Percy could not hear a word over the wind. Malcolm relayed her flag: *EARLY CALL. CROSSWIND. I'LL RESET THE COUNT.*

Thalia studied the signal, then reset her stance instead of rushing back into the strike. "Understood, Grace. I'll wait for the second flag."

The next signal came clean. Malcolm kept the yellow flag raised until the gust passed, then relayed the clear channel with white. Thalia answered with two fingers. Annabeth waited for the stepped leader, dropped white once, and Malcolm repeated it before Percy relayed it to the shoreline. Thalia waited for the full chain. She had shifted the spear into her left hand, keeping her blistered right palm off the shaft. The bolt entered the channel at its mouth, traveled outward, and broke into a burst of steam beyond the ward. Thalia let the spear drop only after the current had carried the last of the charge away.

Annabeth watched the clouds the way only she could, tracking wind layers and charge differentials and the subtle green tint that meant a strike was thirty seconds out. She counted under her breath, dropped her flag exactly when the stepped leader began its descent, and Thalia called exactly on the mark. Spear up. Bolt down. Wrench sideways into the water. The river lit from within like a glow stick, carried the fire to the sea, and vented it in a towering plume of white steam that the wind tore away.

"THREE!" Thalia shouted, holding up three fingers, rain streaming down her face. "THAT'S THREE!"

Jason answered from the hill with a distant, rolling peal of his own, a smaller bolt drawn into his cables to keep the hill ward from overloading. Brother and sister, trading thunder across half a mile. Percy felt the hair on his neck rise every time Jason worked, a sympathetic resonance through the storm.

Between strikes, there were moments — brief, gasping lulls where the three Poseidon siblings stood knee-deep in their own river, panting, checking each other. Clarisse would grab Silena's shoulder to steady her. Silena would wipe mud off Percy's cheek without thinking. Percy would adjust their spacing with a nudge, keeping them in triangle formation so their fields overlapped. No words needed. They had trained for this in the cove behind the cabin on calm summer mornings, making water slides and whirlpools for fun, never imagining the games would become this.

"You good?" Percy asked during one lull, looking at each of them in turn.

Clarisse rolled her neck until it cracked. "Bored. Wish the storm would try harder."

Silena snorted, exhausted. "Liar. You're terrified."

"Damn right I'm terrified," Clarisse said cheerfully. "Keeps me sharp."

Silena leaned her head briefly against Percy's shoulder, just for a second, borrowing his balance. "Don't let go," she murmured, so quietly only he heard.

"I'm here," Percy said. "If my grip changes, I'll tell you before I drop the wall. You tell me too."

Clarisse nodded once. "We stop when someone says stop. No one gets to decide for the rest of us."

The next lull lasted long enough for Percy to notice the prickling in his right hand had returned. He closed and opened his fingers, then raised two fingers toward Malcolm's marker: *hold*. He did not wait to see if he could push through it. Clarisse took his side of the channel while Silena kept the left wall from buckling. Percy stepped clear of the current and pressed both hands against his shirt until the numbness passed.

Annabeth sent a white flag from the hill. She had seen the signal and was waiting. It cost them almost a minute before Percy gave the ready sign again. When he stepped back into place, Clarisse shifted over without a remark, leaving him room to take the flow in stages. The current held while he was gone. The camp did not need every part of him at once.

For the rest of the storm, the work changed but did not ease: a surge shoved the creek toward the cabins; the three Poseidon siblings bent the runoff toward the cannon; Thalia waited for Annabeth's confirmed signal; Jason drew the lesser strikes away from the hill. Each time the current narrowed, they had to decide whether it was still safe to hold or safer to let the water spread into a field already cleared of campers. Percy stopped treating every break as a failure. A wider channel in the empty field cost him power; a narrow one near the cabins cost the camp its margin.

Once, a drakon shouldered up beyond the border, its head rising over the breakwater as the ward flexed. Percy felt the creature's weight through the outer water. Clarisse started to turn toward it, but he called her back to the channel. If she pulled away, the runoff would spill toward the infirmary path. Thalia and Jason handled the threat from their positions: Thalia drew a strike into the moving water at the cannon's mouth, and Jason caught the echo before it could climb the hill ward. The drakon recoiled from the electrified spray outside the boundary and dropped beneath the surf. Nobody crossed the border to chase it.

Later, storm spirits came low over the western fence, rattling the protective charms. Percy saw them only when a flash lit the trees. Jason raised one hand from the hill, and Thalia looked for Annabeth's signal before she moved. The timing held this time: Jason bled off the charge building over the hill while Thalia pulled the next bolt away from the fence. The spirits broke apart in the crosswind and vanished beyond the treeline. Percy could not tell whether they had been driven off or had chosen to leave. He only knew the ward stopped shuddering, and Malcolm's next report showed no breach.

The camp's work continued around them. At one point Silena called the beach team to pause while she checked the roster against the counselor count; a younger camper had been moved from the Big House to the infirmary, and the two lists were a page apart. She found the mismatch without sending anyone back into the rain. Katie's crew confirmed that the lowest strawberry strip had taken the runoff and marked it for daylight inspection. Percy kept the water moving while the campers followed the jobs their counselors had assigned.

The infirmary had one sprained ankle, Thalia's flash burn, and several campers who needed dry clothes and a quiet place to stop shaking. Percy had tingling in both hands but could grip Will's fingers evenly; the healer told him to keep reporting if that changed. Travis Stoll had been struck by a beach umbrella and was already arguing that it belonged on the injury list. Will put him on the bench with a blanket and honey tea while he checked the ankle and dressed Thalia's palm. The injuries were minor, but they still got names, treatment and a note to check again.

But the borders held.

At hour four, the wind shifted one final time — from chaos to west, clean and cold off the Sound — and the clouds tore open like wet paper. Sunlight poured through in solid golden shafts. The rain stopped mid-drop, as if someone had turned off a faucet. Thunder rumbled once, distantly, moving out to sea, and then was gone.

Percy stood in the middle of a muddy field, holding a river that was suddenly just a puddle, and watched steam rise off Thalia's spear.

It was over.

Percy did not let the water go all at once. The current still had enough force to tear through the softened bank if he dropped it, and he could feel the strain in his wrists each time the channel pressed against its walls. He looked uphill for the signal. Malcolm raised one white flag and held it steady. A second appeared beside it: no more lightning building toward the hill. Annabeth added the dropped-flag signal they'd agreed on, and only then did Percy ease the current down in stages.

Clarisse took the western bend while Silena guided the last of the muddy runoff toward the cannon. Percy stayed near the creek mouth, where the fresh water could still be diverted before it spread across the lower path. The canoe lake had risen, but its surface remained the same dark green as before the storm; there was no salt sheen, no wave coming in from the Sound. He could not tell from sight alone whether the runoff had carried anything into it. That would be a morning check for Chiron and the naiads, not a reason to send anyone into the water now.

At the cannon, the current thinned to a clear stream, then stopped. The Sound was still rough outside the boundary, but it no longer shoved against the same section of ward. Percy let his hands lower. A tremor traveled from his fingers into his elbows, and he caught the stone lip before his knees could make the decision for him.

"Sit," Clarisse said.

"I'm fine."

She gave him a flat look. "You can tell Will that."

The rescue line crew had already begun coiling rope. One anchor had shifted in the softened ground; the higher rock held. Clarisse had the crew mark the failed spot with a strip of orange cloth and leave it alone until the ground could be inspected in daylight. Percy watched them work and felt the old pull to take the rope from their hands, move the marker himself, and keep checking until every knot had been retied. He stayed where he was.

Silena reached them with her roster folded inside her jacket. The shelter count and infirmary count matched now. She pointed Percy toward the upper path, where a counselor was helping the camper with the sprained ankle sit on a dry blanket. No one was missing from the beach group. Travis was still insisting the umbrella had attacked him on purpose.

"I'll check the other cabins," Percy said.

"You can report to Will first," Silena replied. "Then you can tell Chiron the creek is no longer rising. Katie is already checking the fields."

He nearly argued. Instead he nodded and went to Will. The tingling in his hands had faded enough that he could button his coat, but Will tested each finger and made him name any numbness that remained. Percy did not call it nothing. He did not call it serious either. Will wrote down what he found and told him to come back if the feeling returned or his grip weakened.

Annabeth reached the shore after the last cable was grounded. Rain had flattened her hair and streaked charcoal across the heel of her hand. She looked first at Percy, then at the closed route on the map Malcolm carried.

"The first call came early," she said. Her voice had gone rough from shouting uphill. "The gust bent the flag before Malcolm could repeat it. I saw the cloud change and acted on the old count."

Percy thought of Silena hitting him, the brown water rushing toward the cabins, Thalia on her knee with the glove smoking. "It broke the left wall."

"I know." Annabeth did not look away. "Next time, no strike on a single dropped flag. Malcolm repeats it, and Thalia confirms she's ready. If I can't see both, we wait."

"That gives the storm longer to build."

"Yes." She looked out toward the Sound, calculating rather than defending herself. "But one more minute is better than sending a bolt into the wrong part of the channel."

Percy took her hand. Her knuckles were cold, and the charcoal had marked his palm. "You caught it. We adjusted. I'm not going to pretend it didn't cost anything, but I trust you to fix the call with us."

Annabeth squeezed his fingers. "And I trust you to tell me when the water is doing something I can't see from the hill."

It was enough for the moment. She copied the revised signal rule onto the map's dry corner, then gave it to Malcolm to carry back under his coat. Percy had expected the end of the storm to feel like a finish. Instead there was a list: check the lake, inspect the soft bank, replace the frayed flag, count the dry blankets, repair the outer ward. They could do it in the morning. For now, the names were accounted for, the channel had stopped, and every person he'd promised to keep in sight was still on camp ground.

Chiron came down from the upper marker after the grounding cables had been disconnected. Mud streaked his coat; he had stayed with the ward team through the last strike. Percy gave him the sequence as plainly as he could: two misfires at the channel, the creek overtopping, the drakon and storm spirits outside the border, no confirmed breach. He told him the Sound had never entered the canoe lake and that no one had checked the lake's water after the runoff.

"Good distinction," Chiron said. "We know what the storm did here. We do not yet know what called it or whether it was meant to reach the camp. We will inspect the ward and water in daylight, after everyone has been counted and treated."

Percy had wanted a reason since the first flash over the hill. A reason might have told them whether this could happen again. Chiron did not pretend to have one. He assigned the next checks to counselors who could do them with the sun up, then sent the remaining shoreline crew to the shelter. Percy went with them instead of asking to stay for one more look at the Sound.

The Poseidon cabin smelled like salt and chocolate and wet wool.

Someone — probably Silena, bless her — had dragged every blanket in the cabin into a nest on the floor in front of the fireplace, which Percy had lit with shaking hands. All his clothes were soaked. All everyone's clothes were soaked. There was a pile of steaming socks by the door that smelled like low tide. No one cared.

Getting the hot chocolate had itself been an operation. Percy had stood in the tiny kitchenette with water dripping off his nose into the pot, hands trembling so badly he could barely open the cocoa tin. Clarisse had wordlessly taken it from him, her own hands steadier through sheer stubbornness, and measured out spoonfuls while Silena found the milk and the marshmallows shaped like shells that she saved for emergencies. Thalia had leaned against the counter, still crackling faintly every time she moved, and stolen three marshmallows straight from the bag. Annabeth had caught her and stolen one back, and for a moment they had stood there like ordinary teenagers, bickering over marshmallows while thunder died outside.

Now they were all on the floor. Clarisse sat with her back against the couch, a huge mug clasped in both hands, hair dripping. Silena lay on her stomach with her chin on a pillow, also holding a mug, eyes half-closed. Percy sat between them, shoulder to shoulder, too tired to move, holding his own mug like a lifeline. Thalia had commandeered the armchair, feet tucked under her, wrapped in a blanket that was technically Percy's but now smelled like pine and ozone. Jason had stopped by just long enough to confirm his sister was alive, ruffle her already disastrous hair, and collapse in the Zeus cabin. Annabeth sat on the floor next to Percy, close enough that their shoulders touched, her own blanket around her shoulders, her hair damp and curling.

For a while they compared what the storm had left behind. Clarisse showed off the bruise on her shin from the flying oar and called it beautiful. Silena held up her pruned fingers, insisting they looked like raisins until Clarisse laughed cocoa through her nose. Thalia kept her bandaged right hand tucked against her chest. When Percy started to applaud the clean strike, she pointed at the dressing with her left hand.

"It burns," she said. "That's all. You don't have to clap for me hurting myself."

Annabeth shifted toward her sister. "Do you want help with your sleeve?"

Thalia eyed the wet fabric caught at the edge of the bandage, then nodded. Annabeth eased it free without tugging the blistered skin. Thalia's jaw tightened once, but she let her sister finish and then tested her left-hand grip on the mug. Will had told her to keep the dressing dry and not spar until he'd checked it again. She did not call the burn nothing. She also did not surrender the whole evening to it.

Percy put his hands out for Annabeth to check. She pressed each fingertip in turn and asked him to squeeze against her fingers. The tingling had faded, though he could still feel it when he flexed. "Tell Will if it comes back," she said.

"I did. He wrote it down."

That answer made her shoulders lower. Percy understood why: he had not tried to decide by himself that a symptom didn't count. She closed his fingers around hers, one at a time, then rested their joined hands between them on the blanket.

Will had come by with dry socks and honey tea and strict orders to drink, sleep and stop calling every injury an inconvenience. Katie had reported that most of the strawberry field could be saved; the damaged rows would wait for daylight. Malcolm had relayed that the hill ward was holding at ninety percent. Chiron and the naiads still had to inspect the canoe lake in the morning, and the soft anchor near the shore had been marked for repair. The dragon was snoring. The camp was safe for the night, with work still waiting.

The practical list settled them better than a speech would have. Silena asked Clarisse to remember where the ankle had turned so Will could check the path. Clarisse complained, then described the patch of mud exactly. Thalia asked Annabeth to repeat the revised signal rule; Annabeth gave it once, and Thalia repeated it back without teasing. Percy listened, the hot mug warming his knuckles, and felt the room become smaller around the people he knew well enough to be tired beside.

Finally Silena lifted her head. "Say it," she mumbled.

"Say what?" Percy asked.

"The old promise," Silena said. "The one we made in the van."

Clarisse snorted into her mug, but her eyes were soft. "She means the corny thing."

"It's not corny," Silena protested weakly. "It's important."

Percy knew what she meant. After the reservoir, they had sat soaked and shaking in the camp van while Annabeth drove too fast and Thalia tried to laugh through the blood on her sleeve. Silena had held out her pinky and asked them to promise that the next argument, parent or prophecy would not make them leave one another alone. Storm and Sea and Mind, she had called them. No one fights alone.

They had promised. They had kept it today.

Percy held out his pinky. It trembled with exhaustion. "Still here," he said.

Clarisse linked her pinky through his immediately, strong and sure. "Obviously."

Silena added hers, smiling sleepily. "All three, then."

From the armchair, Thalia extended her pinky without getting up. "And Sky, you idiots. Don't leave out Sky."

Annabeth leaned her head against Percy's shoulder — just for a second, just to reach — and linked her pinky through the knot of theirs. Her hand was warm from the mug.

"I know," Annabeth murmured. "I'm here."

They sat like that for a moment, five pinkies tangled together over a cooling mug of hot chocolate, while the fire popped and the last of the storm dripped off the eaves outside.

"Okay," Silena said, satisfied, and flopped back onto her pillow. "Now we can sleep for a year."

Clarisse drained her mug and set it down with a clunk. "I'm too tired to make it to my bunk. I'm sleeping here. If Dad floods the cabin in my dreams, I'm killing him."

"He wouldn't dare," Percy said. "Not tonight. Tonight we saved his shoreline."

Silena was already half-asleep, her mug tipping dangerously. Percy gently took it from her hands and set it aside, tucking the blanket up around her shoulders. She mumbled something that sounded like *love you, stupid fish boys — and girls —* and went still.

Clarisse's eyes drifted shut. Within minutes her breathing went deep and even, her head tipped back against the couch, her hand still resting against Percy's ankle where she had left it for balance during the promise. Sister. Anchor. Hammer.

Percy let his own eyes close. He was so tired his bones ached, his palms burned, his ears still rang faintly. But underneath the exhaustion was something warm and steady, like the hearth fire. They had done it. Not by being stronger than the storm, but by giving it somewhere to go. Together.

He felt Annabeth's head grow heavier on his shoulder.

He looked down. She was asleep. Really asleep — mouth slightly open, eyelashes damp against her cheeks, gray eyes hidden, strategist finally, finally off duty. Her blanket had slipped. Her fingers were still curled loosely around nothing where their pinkies had linked.

Percy went very still, afraid to wake her. His heart did something complicated and tender in his chest.

Thalia watched from the armchair, and her usual teasing grin softened into something tender toward her sister and her sister's boyfriend. She stood quietly, wincing at her own stiffness, and pulled the spare blanket — the soft blue one Silena knitted last winter — from the back of the chair.

She draped it gently over both of them, tucking it around Annabeth's shoulders and Percy's arm where it rested against the floor for support.

"Reservoir rules," Thalia whispered, brushing Annabeth's damp hair back from her forehead with surprising gentleness. "You hold. She aims. Nobody plays hero."

Percy met her eyes over Annabeth's sleeping head. "Thanks," he mouthed.

Thalia winked, her own eyes already drooping. She curled back into the armchair, pulling the corner of Percy's stolen blanket up to her chin. "Still here, Kelp Head," she murmured, half-asleep. "Don't make me say it twice."

Outside, the Sound lapped gently against the restored shore. The borders hummed, steady and strong. Peleus the dragon snored somewhere on the hill, smoke curling from his nostrils in peaceful rhythm.

Inside Cabin Three, the fire burned down to a red seam beneath the logs. Percy listened until the rain stopped ticking from the roof and the people around him settled into sleep. The creek would need checking in daylight; the injured camper would need Will's second look; the flag code would have to survive a calmer test. For now, the mugs were empty, Annabeth's hand was warm in his, and everyone had made it back inside.
