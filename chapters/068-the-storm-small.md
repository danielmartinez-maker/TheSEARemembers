## Chapter 68: The Storm (Small)

The morning at Camp Half-Blood started the way all good warm April mornings started, with bronze on bronze and Percy Jackson getting knocked flat on his back in the arena.

Annabeth watched from the second row of the bleachers with her chin in her palm and a schematics notebook open and completely ignored in her lap. The sun was already high enough to turn the strawberry fields gold at the edges and to make the mist off the Long Island Sound burn away in thin strips. The air smelled like cut grass and hot metal and sunscreen. Somewhere near the Arts and Crafts cabin someone was laughing too loud. A conch horn blew down by the canoe lake.

In the center of the ring, Percy spat out dust, rolled his shoulders, and grinned up at Clarisse La Rue like getting speared was the best part of his day.

“Again?” he said.

Clarisse twirled her spear. “Gods, Jackson. You ask like you think the result’s gonna change.”

“It might,” Percy said. “Character growth.”

Annabeth snorted before she could stop herself. Three months of relative peace had made Percy softer around the edges. Not weaker. Just less braced. He laughed easier. He slept through the night more often than not.

Sherman Yang, leaning against the arena rail beside her, glanced over. “Your boyfriend’s about to eat dirt again.”

“He’s working on his left-side guard,” Annabeth answered, trying to keep her smile out of it. “Clarisse keeps exploiting it.”

“Yeah, well,” Sherman said. “She’s Clarisse.”

In the ring Percy tried to pivot left and Clarisse caught him clean behind the knee with the butt of her spear and sent him sprawling for the second time in four minutes. The Ares kids cheered. The Hermes kids who’d bet on Percy booed.

Percy just lay there for a second staring up at the blue sky. Then he lifted one hand and gave Annabeth a thumbs-up without looking at her, because he knew exactly where she was sitting.

Annabeth felt her face go warm. She lifted her notebook to hide it.

It was stupid how safe she felt in moments like that. After everything — after Tartarus, after the war with Gaea, after the months she’d spent that winter learning how to carry the strange, humming spark she had inherited from Zeus — safety still felt fragile.

The spark shifted under her sternum at the thought, like a fish turning over in dark water.

Annabeth went still.

Easy, she told herself. It’s fine. It’s quiet. Leave it quiet.

It had been almost six weeks since the last surge. Six weeks of breathing drills and grounding exercises and Chiron’s patient voice saying, Progress, child, not perfection.

She pressed her palm flat against the bleacher wood, feeling the grain, the heat of the sun in it. Five things I can see, she recited silently. Sun on bronze. Dust in the air. Percy’s stupid curls stuck to his forehead. Clarisse’s red plume. Sherman’s busted sandal strap.

The spark settled.

Annabeth let out a breath and forced her attention back to her notebook, where she’d been sketching storm shutters for the Athena cabin.

Across the arena, Percy got to his feet, dusted off his jeans, and bowed deeply to Clarisse. “Thank you for your wisdom, O mighty barbeque master.”

“I swear on the Styx—” Clarisse started, but she was fighting a smile.

It was, Annabeth thought, going to be a good day.

She had no idea how fast a good day could turn.

---

At the end of sparring, Annabeth expected Percy to come straight to the bleachers. Instead he stayed with Clarisse to argue about the angle of his last failed block, using two practice swords to demonstrate where his arm should have been.

"He'd rather get knocked down again than admit I'm right," Clarisse told Annabeth.

"He's admitting you're right. He's trying to understand why."

"Same annoying habit."

Percy looked up. "You can hear me, you know."

"That's the idea."

Thalia arrived carrying a small slate and a piece of chalk. She'd promised Annabeth a practical drill before lunch, but when she saw the scorched mark in an old training ring, her attention shifted.

"Someone used lightning here?"

"Not today," Annabeth said.

"I know. I meant before."

"An Apollo camper was testing a reflected-flash signal yesterday. No storm magic involved."

Thalia relaxed. "Good."

Annabeth noticed. Her sister did not ordinarily ask such careful questions. In the months since Annabeth's powers had surfaced, Thalia had learned that every new burn mark at camp could start a rumor about which Grace sister had lost control.

"Let's go somewhere quiet," Annabeth suggested.

They used the dry strip beyond the arena where the instructors practiced silent commands with flags. Percy offered to come, but Annabeth shook her head.

"Finish your sparring. It's not your shift."

He looked disappointed for half a second, then nodded. "See you at lunch."

Thalia waited until they were clear of the last practice post before handing Annabeth the slate.

"Today's exercise doesn't involve any electricity," she said.

"Then why did you bring me a target?"

"It's not a target. It's a list."

Written on the slate were three statements.

SOMEONE INSULTS YOU.

SOMEONE INSULTS PERCY.

SOMEONE SAYS YOU ARE DANGEROUS.

Annabeth stared at the second line.

"That's not a weather drill."

"No. Last week you told me that you can stay calm when a storm starts naturally, but you struggle when you feel trapped in an argument. So we're practicing arguments."

"With chalk?"

"Unless you'd prefer I call Clarisse over and have her shout at you."

"Chalk is fine."

Thalia put the slate between them. "What do you do first?"

"Check my breathing."

"What else?"

"Move away from anything conductive."

"And?"

"Tell someone I'm reaching my limit."

"That's the part you keep forgetting."

Annabeth frowned. "Because if I say it aloud, everyone will look."

"If you don't say it aloud, we won't know you need space."

Thalia let her argue for a while, not because she was enjoying it, but because Annabeth needed to discover where the argument would get her. Annabeth knew the breathing pattern. She could recite the five-senses exercise backward. Neither of those guaranteed she'd choose to stop talking when an insult made her angry.

Finally she took the chalk.

Under the first statement she wrote: STEP BACK. She left room for a second action, then added ASK SOMEONE TO COVER THE CONVERSATION.

"Who?" Thalia asked.

"Whoever's nearby and knows the situation. You. Chiron. Percy."

"Percy might be the person they're attacking."

"Then I shouldn't make him responsible for rescuing me."

"Exactly."

Annabeth wrote another line. If Percy was the subject of an argument, she could protect him by refusing to help the argument become a spectacle. She could ask the other person to stop once, clearly, then leave if they refused.

"That's harder than making a speech," she said.

"Yes."

"Or making lightning."

"Much harder."

They practiced aloud. Thalia played an obnoxious camper who accused Percy of receiving special treatment. Annabeth said the accusation was unfair, then started to explain why. Thalia kept arguing, repeating the same claim until Annabeth felt heat rise into her cheeks.

"Stop," Annabeth said.

Thalia immediately fell silent.

Annabeth blinked. She had not expected it to work.

"That's the point of a drill," Thalia said. "You get to stop it before you learn that your body won't."

"What if the real person doesn't stop?"

"Then we leave or get an instructor. The drill isn't about teaching other people to be decent. It's about giving you a plan when they aren't."

Annabeth erased one part of her answer and rewrote it more simply.

"You're not going to tell me I handled that perfectly?" she asked.

"No. You spent two minutes debating with somebody who was never going to listen."

"I noticed eventually."

"Eventually is progress. Let's try again."

By the time the conch sounded for lunch, Annabeth had practiced the first step three times. She'd managed to ask for space aloud without apologizing for taking it. Thalia put the slate in her hand, but Annabeth declined to carry it to the pavilion.

"I know what's written there," she said.

"I know you do. Knowing and doing aren't the same."

Annabeth handed it back. "I'll practice again tomorrow."

"Good. And eat something now. Your stomach is making decisions for both of us."

They walked toward lunch, Annabeth rehearsing the words in her head. The plan felt sensible. She had no reason yet to suspect she'd need it before the hour was over.

---

By noon the good day had turned hot and sticky and loud in the way that meant everyone was overtired and two seconds from snapping at each other.

Lunch in the pavilion was a crush of noise. At the Poseidon table Percy was trying to eat one-handed while sketching something on a napkin.

“Is that a boat?” Annabeth asked, sliding in beside him with her plate.

“It’s a trireme,” Percy said with dignity. “For your pond model.”

“My harbor defense model. And that’s a canoe with a sail.”

“It’s conceptual.”

“It’s terrible.” She bumped his shoulder with hers and stole his apple slice. “Keep your day job, Seaweed Brain.”

“Rude. My day job is getting stabbed by Clarisse. I’m excellent at it.”

“You were late on the pivot again.”

“I was distracted by your overwhelming beauty in the stands.”

“You were distracted by Sherman making faces at you.”

“That too.” Percy grinned at her, quick and crooked and unguarded, and then his expression softened into something quieter. Under the table, his hand found hers. His fingers were callused and warm and a little sticky from nectar. “You okay? You look tired.”

Annabeth hesitated. Percy knew her tells now. “Spark’s restless,” she admitted quietly. “Since this morning. Just… awake.”

Percy glanced up at the sky. It was still blue, but there was a line of white cloud building far out over the water, tall and anvil-headed. Sailors’ clouds.

“Thalia say anything?” he asked.

“She says there’s a front coming tonight. She can feel it in her teeth. She wanted to run drills after lunch.” Annabeth picked at her corn. “I told her I’d be there.”

“Good.” Percy squeezed her hand. “Want me to come?”

“You have canoe duty with the new kids.”

“I can drown them later.”

Annabeth laughed despite herself. “Might be boring. Breathing and counting.”

“Your favorite.”

“My favorite,” she agreed.

That was when the bench across from them scraped loudly and a tray slammed down.

Annabeth looked up.

Bryce Lawrence was a new arrival. Seventeen, son of Ares, three weeks at camp and already alienated from most of his own cabin.

He’d decided he hated Percy Jackson. Maybe it was jealousy. Maybe it was rivalry. Whatever it was, he’d been needling Percy for days.

Now he sat down uninvited across from them with two of his half-brothers flanking him like backup singers, and smiled a smile that had nothing friendly in it.

“Well, well,” Bryce said. “If it isn’t the great Percy Jackson. How’s the dirt taste?”

Percy didn’t look up from his napkin trireme. “Salty. With notes of Clarisse’s foot.”

One of the Ares boys snickered. Bryce’s smile tightened.

“Funny,” Bryce said. “You’re funny. That’s what they tell the kids, right? Oh, Percy’s so funny, Percy’s so brave. Meanwhile my brother Sherman told me you got your whole squad killed last winter on that retrieval job. That true?”

The pavilion noise seemed to dip around them, just a little. Or maybe that was Annabeth’s blood suddenly roaring in her ears.

Percy’s pen stopped moving. “That’s not what happened.”

“No?” Bryce leaned forward. “Because word is you panicked. Word is you froze up and left a twelve-year-old to hold a bridge while you cried about your girlfriend. And then you all voted to give you a medal anyway because Daddy Poseidon pulls strings on Olympus.”

Annabeth’s hand went cold in Percy’s.

Everyone at camp knew about the Mill Creek bridge. A routine retrieval that turned into an ambush. Percy had carried an injured satyr across under harpy fire and gone back for Louisa from Demeter. He’d gotten a commendation and nightmares for two months.

Now Percy’s face did something very still and very controlled. “You don’t know what you’re talking about.”

“I know enough,” Bryce said. “I know real heroes don’t hide behind their girlfriends’ skirts. I know if you were in my unit back at school—”

“Bryce,” Annabeth said. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears. Too calm. Too flat. “Stop.”

Bryce turned that sneer on her. “Oh, I’m sorry. Is the princess going to defend him? That’s cute. The Zeus girl playing guard dog for Fish Boy. Tell me, does she do your thinking for you too, Jackson? Or just your fighting?”

“Don’t,” Percy said quietly. He was looking at Annabeth now, not Bryce, and there was something urgent in his eyes. “Annabeth. Hey. Look at me.”

She couldn’t. Her vision had gone strange at the edges, too bright, too sharp. She could hear every fork clink at the Apollo table. She could smell ozone, faint and metallic, under the smell of pizza and sunscreen.

The spark in her chest was no longer flickering. It was rising. It happened like this sometimes. Not with monsters — monsters were simple. It happened with hot shame and helpless anger on someone else’s behalf. The hard-won clarity she remembered from her old life drowned in unfamiliar Zeus noise.

“Bryce, I’m serious,” Percy was saying. He’d put himself half between Annabeth and Bryce without seeming to move. “Drop it. You don’t want to do this.”

Bryce laughed. “Or what? She’ll cry at me? Gods, look at her shaking. Are you scared, Princess? You gonna—”

The sky cracked.

It wasn’t big. Later, everyone would agree on that. It wasn’t the kind of bolt that split trees or set the Big House roof on fire the way Thalia could do when she really lost it. It was small. A white-blue flicker, no thicker than Annabeth’s wrist, that jumped from somewhere above the pavilion’s open roof with a sound like a bullwhip and struck the empty bronze brazier three tables over.

The brazier rang like a bell. Sparks showered. A scorch mark bloomed black across the marble floor. A half-dozen campers yelped and dove. A harpy shrieked and dropped a plate.

Silence fell over the pavilion, total and ringing.

Annabeth stared at her own hands in horror. Blue-white static crawled between her fingers. Her hair was lifting. Every light bulb in the pavilion was buzzing.

No, she thought. No no no not again not here please not here.

“Annabeth,” Percy said. His voice was very steady. He was still sitting, which meant he was looking up at her, and he had one hand extended palm-up on the table. He wasn’t flinching from the static. He wasn’t even looking at the scorch mark. “Hey. I’m here.”

“I didn’t—” Her voice broke. “I didn’t mean—”

“I know,” Percy said. “I know you didn’t. Can I hold your hand?”

Around them, no one moved. Bryce was frozen mid-sneer, his face white. One of his brothers had his hands half up like he wasn’t sure if he should run or duck.

Annabeth stared at Percy’s outstretched hand. The static on her own skin hissed and spat. If she touched him, would it hurt him? She could kill him. Gods, she could actually hurt him. The thought looped and the static brightened.

“Don’t,” she whispered. “Percy, don’t, you’ll get—”

“You won’t hurt me,” Percy said, with absolute, ridiculous, Percy Jackson certainty. “We've practiced this. Come on. Grounding, right? Like Thalia taught us. Water grounds sky. I’m basically a human puddle.”

A choked sound escaped her that might have been a laugh or a sob.

“Percy—”

“Hand,” he said gently. “Please.”

She gave it to him. His hand was cool and damp. She felt the static rush toward him and vanish the way lightning vanishes into the sea. He hissed once and didn’t let go.

“Breathe with me,” he said quietly. “In — two — three — four. Like we practiced.”

Annabeth breathed. The air tasted like burnt metal and pizza cheese. In for four. Hold for four. Out for six. Percy counted under his breath, thumb stroking slow circles on the back of her hand. Thalia Grace arrived at a jog, took one look, and went into commander mode. “Okay, everybody, show’s over. Eyes on me, not her.”

Thalia came around the table and stood where Annabeth could see her without turning her head. She didn’t touch Annabeth yet. She knew better.

“Hey, Owl Eyes,” Thalia said softly. “There you are. Can you hear the rain?”

It was their code. There was no rain. There was a cloudless blue sky except for that anvil head far out over the Sound. But when Thalia talked Annabeth down, she always started with imagining rain — steady, ordinary, garden rain, not storm rain.

Annabeth tried to speak and only managed a nod. The static on her arms was fading, sinking from lace to faint blue threads to nothing. Her hair settled back onto her shoulders. The buzzing in the lights died.

“Good,” Thalia said. “What does it sound like on the Athena cabin roof?”

“Pinging,” Annabeth whispered. “Like — like pennies.”

“Yeah. Like pennies. What does it smell like?”

“Pine. Wet stone.”

“Good girl. Keep breathing. Percy’s got you, I’ve got the room. Nothing to fix right now. Just rain.”

Annabeth breathed. In for four. Hold for four. Out for six. Percy’s hand in hers. Thalia’s steady blue eyes on her face. The distant clink of the harpies resuming their rounds. The slow return of normal pavilion murmur as campers, reassured by Thalia’s calm, turned back to their food and pretended very hard not to stare.

The last of the static guttered out. Annabeth’s knees buckled.

Percy caught her before she hit the floor and eased her back into her righted chair. Thalia was there on her other side, a solid leather-jacketed wall.

“It’s okay,” Percy murmured, close to her ear. “You’re okay. Nobody got hurt. You pulled it back.”

Annabeth looked at the scorch mark on the marble. Black, ugly, unmistakable. Her doing. Her loss of control, in the middle of lunch, in front of the whole camp. Because some idiot boy with a grudge had called Percy a coward and she hadn’t been able to take it.

Shame flooded her so fast and hot it nearly brought the spark back. She shoved her hands under her thighs to hide their shaking.

“I’m sorry,” she said, too loudly, to no one in particular and to everyone. “I’m — I’m sorry, I didn’t mean to—”

“Hey,” Thalia said firmly. “No apologies right now. Later. Right now, water. Percy, give her your goblet.”

Percy pressed his water into Annabeth’s hands. She drank because Thalia told her to, because her body knew how to obey when her mind was spinning. The water was cold and tasted faintly of the sea. It helped.

Across the table, Bryce was still sitting there. His bravado had drained away, leaving something young and shaken. He kept glancing at the scorch mark and then at Annabeth’s face and then away.

“Is she—” he started, and his voice cracked. “Is she okay? I didn’t— I wasn’t trying to—”

Thalia turned her head and gave him a look that had made full-grown werewolves reconsider their life choices. “Not now, Ares.”

Bryce shut his mouth.

Chiron arrived then, his wheelchair’s wheels whispering on the marble. He took in the scene with one sweeping glance — the brazier, the scorch, Annabeth pale and shaking between Percy and Thalia — and his kind, ancient eyes were sad but not shocked.

“Annabeth,” he said gently. “My dear. Would you like to come with me to the Big House for a little while?”

Annabeth nodded, not trusting her voice. She couldn’t look at him. She couldn’t look at anyone.

Percy started to stand. “I’ll come—”

Chiron shook his head, kindly but firmly. “Thalia will accompany her. You, Percy, should stay and finish your lunch and then report to canoe duty as scheduled. Routine will help the camp settle. And it will help you settle as well, I think.”

Percy looked torn. He looked at Annabeth, and Annabeth tried to give him a reassuring nod and probably failed.

“I’ll be okay,” she whispered. “Go drown the new kids.”

He managed a weak smile. He squeezed her hand once more before letting go, reluctantly, like unwrapping a bandage. “Come find me after? Porch?”

“Porch,” she promised.

Thalia offered her arm like they were going to a ball instead of a disciplinary chat. Annabeth took it because her legs still felt like wet rope. Together they walked out of the pavilion with Chiron wheeling beside them, into the brutal bright noon sun.

Behind them, Annabeth could feel every eye on her back.

---

They made it past the pavilion steps before Annabeth realized she was still gripping Thalia's arm hard enough to leave crescent marks.

"Sorry," she said, loosening her fingers.

"Don't apologize for needing help walking. Just let me know when you don't need it anymore."

Annabeth could hear the pavilion behind them. Plates were being collected. Someone was arguing about who would clear the overturned chairs. The noises were ordinary, which made the silence they'd left behind feel even more unreal.

She looked back once.

A younger Hermes camper stood at the edge of the doorway, clutching a cup with both hands. He was no older than eight, and his mouth was open as though he'd wanted to say something and forgotten what. When he saw Annabeth watching, he stepped back.

She stopped.

Chiron stopped with her. "What is it?"

"The little boy."

Thalia followed her gaze. "You don't have to talk to anyone right now."

"I know. But he looked scared."

"That's why you don't have to."

Annabeth stood uncertainly between them. In the pavilion she'd felt the power rising and had forgotten every word of the exit drill she'd practiced that morning. Now she knew the words and couldn't think how to use them.

"Could I ask him whether he wants to say something?" she said.

"Yes," Chiron answered. "And he may say no."

Annabeth approached only as far as the path. She kept her hands at her sides, fingers visible, although she realized afterward that doing so probably made her look more formal than she intended.

"Hi," she said.

The boy nodded. His cup rattled slightly against his teeth.

"I'm sorry I frightened you."

He looked over his shoulder, checking whether one of the counselors was listening. Then he looked back at her.

"Was that your lightning?"

"Yes."

"Does it happen when you get mad?"

"Sometimes. I'm learning to control it."

"Can it hurt people?"

The question was so direct that Annabeth almost told him everything was fine. She wanted him to stop looking at her like a storm cloud had learned to speak. She wanted to reassure herself as much as him.

"It could," she said. "That's why I train with Chiron and Thalia. Nobody was injured today, but I still shouldn't have lost control in the dining room."

He thought about that for a long time.

"My brother said you did it on purpose."

"I didn't."

"Bryce said it was because Percy can't fight for himself."

Annabeth closed her eyes for one second. This was the line on Thalia's slate. She could feel anger rise with humiliating speed. She could also feel Thalia just behind her, close enough to help and far enough to let her decide what to do.

"Bryce was wrong about Percy," she said. "But lightning didn't make him wrong. It only frightened other people. I should've stopped arguing and stepped away."

The boy stared at her.

"Would you rather I leave you alone?" she asked.

He nodded.

"All right."

She went back to Thalia without adding another sentence. It was a small success, almost invisible. She'd wanted to stay until the boy smiled, or tell him a funny story, or make him promise not to fear her. Instead she'd asked what he needed and done it.

Chiron watched the boy return to his table.

"That was difficult," he said.

"It was awful."

"Yes."

Thalia was rubbing her arm where Annabeth had gripped it. "You stopped talking when he asked."

"He didn't exactly ask."

"He nodded when you offered to leave. That's an answer."

Annabeth looked at the marks on her sister's skin.

"Did I hurt you?"

"No. You held on because you were unsteady. I would've told you if I needed you to let go."

They started walking again. The Big House stood a little way uphill, its windows bright in the sun. Annabeth had not fixed anything by talking to the boy. She had taken responsibility for one small part of what had happened and left the rest where it belonged.

At the porch steps Chiron opened the door and gave her room to go in first.

She went.

---

The Big House living room was cool and dim and smelled like lemon polish. Annabeth sat on the couch with a blanket Thalia had thrown around her shoulders. Chiron poured tea with his own hands.

“He was saying things about Percy,” she said. “About Mill Creek. That he froze. Percy told him to stop and I told him to stop and he called me a princess playing guard dog.”

Chiron nodded slowly. “And what do you think you did wrong?”

Annabeth looked up, startled. “Everything? I lost control in the middle of the pavilion at lunch. I could have hurt someone. I did — I scorched the floor, I scared the Apollo kids, I—” Her throat closed. “Six weeks, Chiron. Six weeks clean and I threw it away because I couldn’t handle someone being mean.”

“Ah,” Chiron said. “I see. Thalia, would you tell her what you told me last winter, after the stables?”

Thalia sighed and stretched her legs out, boots crossed at the ankle. The silver bracelets on her wrist clinked. “I told him I was a failure and should go live on a rock so I didn’t fry anyone.”

“You did,” Chiron agreed. “And what did I tell you?”

“That I was an idiot,” Thalia said promptly, and Annabeth startled out a wet laugh. “No, okay, the PG version was that control is progress, not perfection. That I’d gone from leveling a shed to singing the grass. That that counted even if I still slipped.”

Chiron turned his gentle gaze on Annabeth. “Do you remember what your surge looked like in December, my dear?”

Annabeth nodded miserably. In December three bolts had hit the lake and boiled it to steam. She’d woken up in the infirmary with a nosebleed.

“And January?” Chiron prompted.

“The archery range,” Annabeth whispered. “When the new girl called Percy dead weight. I — the bowstring snapped. The target caught fire.”

“February?”

“Behind the stables,” Thalia supplied when Annabeth didn’t answer. “Argument with the Stolls about the prank on Percy’s bunk. You blew out every light in the stable and gave Blackjack a fright. No fire. No bolt. Just static and lights.”

“And today?” Chiron asked softly.

Annabeth glanced at him, confused. “Today I — a small bolt. One. No injuries. No fire except — except the scorch. I pulled it back in under a minute.”

“With help,” Chiron said. “Which you asked for by allowing Percy and Thalia to help you, instead of running as you once did. Do you see?”

Annabeth stared at him. “You’re saying this was… better? But I still lost control.”

“I am saying,” Chiron said, “that you are learning to lose control less catastrophically, with greater awareness and faster recovery, and with trust in others. That is progress. Perfection is not on the menu. Progress is.”

“But I scared them,” she whispered, dabbing at her eyes. “The little kids. The new kids. They’ll think I’m — a monster. Unstable.”

“You startled them,” Chiron corrected. “There is a difference. And how you repair that startle matters more than the fact that it happened. What do you think should happen next?”

Annabeth thought about it. Really thought, the way she thought about bridge loads and siege lines. Cause, effect, repair.

“I need to apologize,” she said slowly. “Own it. To the camp, and to Bryce. And I need to keep practicing. I got complacent.”

Thalia winced. “Yeah. The boring stuff works, unfortunately.”

Chiron smiled. “A wise plan. I will speak with Bryce about his behavior. But your apology must be your own. At dinner? Briefly. Just truth.”

Annabeth nodded. “Yes. Please.”

“Good.” Chiron patted her hand. “You are not your worst moment, child.”

Afterward Thalia took her down to the lake for grounding before dinner.

At the lake, Thalia kicked off her boots, rolled her jeans, and waded in to her ankles. Annabeth followed. The water was cool and lapped softly. The sky reflected in broken blue pieces.

“Okay,” Thalia said. “You know the drill. Feet in the mud. Feel it. Sky’s up there, you’re down here. You’re the rod, not the cloud. Let it pass through, don’t hold it.”

Annabeth closed her eyes. She felt mud squish between her toes. She felt the lake water tug. She felt the ember in her chest lift curiously toward the coming front, toward Thalia’s own vast storm-presence beside her like a mountain with weather on it.

“Rain on the Athena roof,” Thalia murmured. “Pennies. Pine. Wet stone.”

“Pennies,” Annabeth repeated. “Pine. Wet stone.”

They stood there while the breeze picked up and ruffled the lake. Annabeth breathed in for four, held for four, out for six. She visualized it the way Thalia had taught her: herself as a riverbed, the sky-energy as water running over, not pooling, not damming. Let it run to the sea. Let Percy’s sea take it. That visualization always helped, though she’d never told Percy that. He’d get a big head.

After a while the ember dimmed to almost nothing. Annabeth opened her eyes. Thalia was watching her with a small, proud smile.

“Sixty seconds today,” Thalia said. “December you couldn’t stand near water without steaming it. That’s progress, Owl Eyes.”

Annabeth felt something tight in her throat loosen. “Chiron told you to say that.”

“Nope. This one’s free.” Thalia bumped her shoulder. “You did good today, you know. You pulled it. You didn’t run. You let Fish Face hold your hand even though you were scared you’d zap him. That’s the hardest part.”

“I was scared,” Annabeth admitted quietly. The water moved around her ankles. “I’m always scared I’ll hurt him. He just — holds on anyway. It’s terrifying.”

Thalia’s expression softened into something rare and unguarded. For all her tough-lieutenant armor, Thalia loved Percy like a little brother, and Annabeth like a little sister, and she worried about both of them in the way she never worried about herself.

“He’s a Seaweed Brain,” Thalia said. “But he’s our Seaweed Brain. He knows what you are. He’s not going anywhere.”

Annabeth nodded, blinking fast. “I know.”

“Good. Now get out of the lake before the leeches stage an uprising. You’ve got an apology to make and I’ve got to go scare an Ares kid straight.”

---

Dinner was the hardest part.

Annabeth had faced down Arachne, had fallen to Tartarus and climbed out, had stood before the gods on Olympus and told them they were doing it wrong. None of that felt as exposed as standing up in the pavilion with every eye on her and her hands empty at her sides.

Chiron gave her the nod. The murmur died. Percy, at the Poseidon table, gave her a small, steady look that said I’m here. Thalia, at their Zeus table where she always sat, gave her a fist-bump to the chest that meant you’ve got this.

Annabeth took a breath.

“I’m going to keep this short,” she said. Her voice carried, a little shaky at first, then stronger. “At lunch today I lost control of… of the sky power I inherited from Zeus. I got angry about something cruel that was said about Percy, and instead of handling it with words, I discharged. It was small, and thank the gods no one was hurt, but I scared a lot of you, especially the younger campers, and I’m sorry for that. You trust me to be steady, and today I wasn’t.”

She looked down at her hands, then forced herself to look back up.

“I’m learning to control this. I’ve been learning for months, with Thalia and Chiron’s help, and I’m better than I was. But I’m not perfect yet. If I scare you again, I’m sorry in advance, and I’ll keep working until I don’t. If anyone has questions, or if you were scared and want to talk, my door’s open. Cabin One, Annabeth’s bunk. I’m usually drawing.” A nervous ripple of laughter. “Thank you for — for giving me room to get there.”

She sat down quickly. Her face was burning. Under the table Percy’s hand found hers immediately and held on tight.

Around the pavilion, the response wasn’t applause — camp didn’t applaud apologies — but something better. Nods. A few murmured It’s okays. Louisa from Demeter, three tables over, lifted her cup in a toast. The little new kid from Hermes, eight years old and wide-eyed, gave her a gap-toothed smile and mouthed it’s cool. Even Clarisse gave her a curt, respectful nod that meant more than words.

And Bryce — Bryce stood up.

He looked miserable. Scrubbed raw. Clarisse had clearly already gotten to him, and Chiron, and probably his own cabin. He held his cap in his hands and twisted it.

“I owe apologies too,” he said stiffly, staring at the floor. “What I said about Percy Jackson was lies. I heard rumors and I repeated them because I wanted to look big. Percy didn’t freeze at Mill Creek. Louisa told me what really happened. I’m sorry, Percy. And — Annabeth — I’m sorry I baited you. It wasn’t — I didn’t know about the — anyway. I’m sorry I scared you into that. I’ll muck stables and I’ll do better.”

He sat down to scattered, surprised murmurs. His ears were red. One of his brothers clapped him on the shoulder.

Percy stood. Annabeth tugged at his hand — sit down, don’t make it a thing — but Percy just smiled at her and stood anyway.

“Apology accepted,” he said simply to Bryce. “We’re good. Anyone who volunteers to muck stables for a week is all right in my book. Just — don’t talk about my girlfriend like that again. Or about Louisa. Or about anyone’s service. We’ve all bled for this camp.”

Bryce nodded jerkily. “Yes, sir.”

“Don’t call me sir,” Percy said, and the pavilion laughed, and the tension broke like a wave.

Dinner resumed. Normal noise. Normal chaos. The harpies confiscated a contraband brownie. Someone started a salt-pass chant at Apollo. Annabeth’s shoulders slowly came down from around her ears.

Under the table, Percy leaned close and murmured, “You were amazing.”

“I was nauseous,” Annabeth murmured back.

“Amazing and nauseous. My favorite combo.”

She laughed, shaky but real, and leaned her head briefly against his shoulder. He smelled like lake water and sunscreen and lunch pizza. Safe.

After dinner, as promised, Chiron had a quiet word with Bryce that left the boy looking chastened but not crushed, and Clarisse had a louder word that left him looking like he’d rather muck stables for a year than face her again. Annabeth watched from a distance and felt no satisfaction, only tired relief. He was just a kid. They were all just kids, trying to be brave in a world that kept giving them wars.

The campfire that night was subdued but warm. They sang the usual songs. Rachel told a terrible ghost story that made the Hermes kids scream and then demand an encore. The anvil cloud that had been building all day finally reached them around nine, and the sky opened into steady, ordinary rain — garden rain, not storm rain. Everyone crowded under the pavilion awning and the Big House porch to watch it come down in silver sheets, smelling of pine and wet stone and lake.

Exactly like Annabeth had imagined that afternoon.

She stood on the Big House porch with Percy, shoulder to shoulder, watching the rain hammer the grass. Thalia was a few feet away, face tipped up, eyes closed, drinking it in the way only a sky child could. Lightning flickered far out over the Sound, too far to matter, pretty as a thought.

Percy’s hand found Annabeth’s on the porch rail. His fingers laced through hers, sure and easy.

“How’s the spark?” he asked quietly, so only she could hear under the rain noise.

Annabeth checked. It was there, a low ember, humming faintly in tune with the real storm out over the water. Not pushing. Just listening. Like a dog with its head on its paws during thunder — alert, but not panicked.

“Quiet,” she said. “Tired. Like me.”

“Good.” Percy was quiet for a moment, watching the rain. Then he said, “Can I tell you something? And you have to promise not to architect it to death.”

“I make no promises.”

He smiled, but his eyes were serious. He turned to face her, rain misting his hair, his shirt damp at the shoulders.

“I’m not scared of your power,” he said.

Annabeth blinked. “Percy—”

“No, listen. I need you to hear this.” He held her hand tighter, both hands now, like an anchor. “Today at lunch, when you lit up — I wasn’t scared. Not for a second. I was worried about you, because you looked scared, and I hated that you were scared in front of everyone. But I wasn’t scared of you. You could throw lightning around the pavilion every day for a year and I still wouldn’t be scared of you.”

“Percy, I could hurt you,” Annabeth whispered. The old fear, the one that lived under all the drills and breathing counts. “Today was small. What if next time isn’t? What if I—”

“What if I flood the cabins in my sleep?” he countered gently. “What if I earthquake the Big House during a nightmare? We all live with what-ifs, Annabeth. You think I don’t know you could level me if you really lost it? You’re terrifying. You’ve always been terrifying. You took on Arachne with a knife and a plan. You outsmarted gods. The lightning’s just — new jewelry. It doesn’t change who you are.”

Annabeth stared at him, throat tight. Rain drummed on the porch roof. Behind them, someone laughed around the fire.

“I love you,” Percy said simply. “All of you. Including the parts that spark. I’m not going anywhere, and I’m not going to flinch. So you don’t have to be scared that I’m scared. Okay?”

Annabeth felt the ember in her chest flare — not with anger, not with shame, but with something warm and bright and entirely hers. Her eyes stung. She didn’t blink it away this time.

“Okay,” she whispered.

He pulled her in, rain be damned, and wrapped his arms around her. She pressed her face into his shoulder and breathed him in — sea and rain and Percy — and let herself be held. His heartbeat was steady under her cheek. Her spark settled against it like it had finally found its grounding wire.

Not caged, Chiron had said. Trained. Trusted.

Progress, not perfection.

Thalia, pretending very hard not to eavesdrop a few feet away, caught Annabeth’s eye over Percy’s shoulder and mouthed, You good?

Annabeth mouthed back, Yeah. She smiled, wet-eyed and real.

Thalia grinned and gave her a thumbs-up and turned back to the rain, giving them privacy the only way Thalia knew how — by loudly announcing, “IF ANYONE NEEDS ME I’LL BE STANDING IN THE RAIN LIKE A DRAMATIC WEIRDO,” and striding out into it with her arms spread while the younger campers cheered.

Percy laughed against Annabeth’s hair. “Your sister’s an idiot.”

“She’s my sister,” Annabeth said, and the words came easier now. The blood relationship still belonged to a childhood she could not remember, but the shared powers, late-night talks, and someone who knew exactly what it felt like to have sky in your blood belonged to the months they had lived together. “Full sister. Full-time idiot.”

“Full-time,” Percy agreed. He tilted her chin up so he could see her face. Rain mist sparkled on her eyelashes. “Hey. You pulled it back today, Wise Girl. One bolt. No injuries. Sixty seconds with Thalia. Apology like a leader. That’s pretty badass.”

“It was still a storm,” Annabeth said.

“Yeah,” Percy said. “But a small one. And small storms pass.” He kissed her forehead, lightning-quick and gentle. “Come on. Rachel saved us seats by the fire. And I promised Louisa I’d help her roast the perfect marshmallow to celebrate her not having to hear Bryce lie about her anymore.”

Annabeth took his hand and let him lead her back toward the firelight, toward the noise and the songs and the smell of smoke and sugar. The rain fell steady and clean on the porch roof. The scorch mark in the pavilion would fade. The story of the day would shrink in retelling until it was just another camp story: Remember when Annabeth zapped the brazier because that Ares kid mouthed off? Small. Survivable. Human.

Halfway to the fire, Annabeth stopped and looked back at the storm over the Sound. Lightning flickered again, silent and far away. She felt the answering ember in her chest flicker once, quietly, and then settle.

She wasn’t afraid of it anymore. Not the way she had been. It was part of her now — wild, divine, difficult. Something to train, not to hate. Something Percy held space for without flinching. Something Thalia understood without explaining. Something Chiron saw clearly and still called progress.

She turned back to Percy, who was waiting for her with infinite patience and a marshmallow stick he’d stolen from somewhere.

“Ready, Wise Girl?” he asked.

Annabeth laced her fingers through his — warm, dry, steady, hers — and walked with him into the light.

“Ready,” she said.
