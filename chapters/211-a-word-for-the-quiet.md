## Chapter 211: A Word for the Quiet

Silena waited for the blue wash to dry without touching it.

The page lay on the end of the cabin table, held flat beneath two smooth stones. She had painted the water line loosely, then left the shore white. If she added another color, the whole thing might turn into a map. She did not want a map. She wanted to see what the water did when it had no marked destination.

Across the table, Clarisse sharpened a practice spear. She had chosen the seat near the door, where she could see the yard. Percy and Annabeth had stopped by to return the bowl from breakfast. They were arguing about whether the camp's new rain barrel should sit beside the shed or closer to the kitchen.

“If it’s by the shed, people have to carry water farther,” Percy said.

“If it’s by the kitchen, people will block the path.”

“We can put it on the other side.”

“Then the runoff won’t reach it.”

“Maybe we could measure it.”

Annabeth looked at him. “You’re proposing a measurement?”

“I have learned from the best.”

“Then carry the tape.”

Percy did. He kissed her cheek on the way out, and she handed him the rolled map without looking up from the bowl. Their disagreement stayed small enough to fit beside the morning’s ordinary work. Silena watched them disappear into the yard, close and unhurried.

Clarisse turned the spear once in her hand. “Are you going to paint that line?”

“I don’t know.”

“You have paint.”

“That doesn’t mean I have to use it.”

Clarisse nodded like that answer made sense. She returned to the spear. They had learned not to make every silence an emergency.

A knock came on the open doorframe. Chiron stood just outside the cabin, a message card in his hand.

“Silena, Amphitrite is at the Big House for a short visit. She asked whether you might like to sit with her for a little while. If not, she understands.”

Silena looked at the water line. The blue had dried to a dusty shade, pale at the edge.

“Did she say what she wanted?”

“She asked if you would have time to talk. She left the subject to you.”

“Is she expecting an answer now?”

“No. I can tell her you’ll decide when you’re ready.”

Clarisse set down the spear. Her face had gone carefully neutral.

“You don’t have to go,” she said.

“I know.”

“If you do, I can keep the page out of the way.”

Silena glanced toward the stones pinning the paper. “You can leave it right there.”

“Okay.”

Chiron waited.

“I’ll go,” Silena said. “For a little while.”

“Would you like me to tell her where to meet you?”

“The garden bench. The one by the low wall.”

“I’ll pass that along.”

Chiron left the card on the sill. It held no added instruction, no reason Silena had to accept. She looked at the card once before putting it beneath the water jar. She had not needed a note to know Amphitrite was at the Big House. Still, seeing the invitation written down made it easier to tell that it did not contain a demand.

Clarisse drew the whetstone along the spear’s edge. “Do you want me to walk with you?”

“No. I want to go by myself.”

“All right.”

Silena held her gaze a moment, then smiled. “Thanks for asking.”

“Don’t make it weird.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You were about to.”

Silena laughed once, quietly. Clarisse lifted the spear again, and the cabin returned to its familiar sounds: whetstone, brush, a cup set down near the sink. The blue wash continued drying without her.

She took her jacket from the peg and went alone.

---

The Big House garden smelled of damp earth and bruised herbs. The morning rain had left a shallow shine on the path, and the sun had not yet found the space between the clouds. Amphitrite sat on the bench by the low wall, hands folded in her lap. She wore a pale green dress and a dark shawl, both simple enough that Silena only noticed them because the colors matched the rosemary beds.

She stood when Silena approached, then stopped herself as if remembering that the movement might feel like ceremony.

“You don’t need to stand,” Silena said.

“I can sit if you prefer.”

“I don’t mind either way.”

“Would you like the bench to yourself?”

“No. You can sit.”

Amphitrite waited for Silena to choose a side, then settled at the opposite end. There was enough space between them for another person, and neither filled it with a bag or a basket.

“Thank you for coming,” Amphitrite said.

“I had time.”

“How was the painting?”

“Still wet.”

“That’s a respectable stage.”

“Do you want to see it?”

“Only if you want me to.”

Silena looked out toward the herb beds. “I don’t have it with me.”

“Then I’ll wait until another time.”

The answer held no disappointment she needed to manage. Silena rested her hands on her knees.

“Were you hoping to talk about something specific?” she asked.

“No. I thought it might be good to see you, if that suited you. I can leave if you’d rather be alone.”

“Do you have other things to do?”

“I do.”

“Do you need to get back?”

“Not immediately.”

Silena considered the opening, the space for a no. “We can sit here for a while.”

“Then I’ll be glad to.”

They listened to the garden. Water fell from the eaves into a shallow basin. Somewhere behind the Big House, a camper called for a missing boot. A bee entered one rosemary flower and left for another.

“You don’t have to keep coming just because I said I liked your company,” Silena said.

“I know.”

“I mean it. I don’t want you to take that as me promising something.”

“I didn’t.”

“Good.”

“I like your company too,” Amphitrite said. “That doesn’t make you responsible for mine.”

Silena glanced at her. “You remember that?”

“I remember what you tell me.”

The words were simple. No claim of knowing Silena better than she knew herself; no reminder of everything that could be offered to her. Just a statement about what Amphitrite had heard.

Silena let it rest.

After some time, Amphitrite asked whether Silena wanted to hear about the herbs in the bed. Silena said yes. Amphitrite pointed out the rosemary, mint, and late thyme, but did not name every plant before Silena had a chance to ask. When she guessed the mint, Amphitrite confirmed it. When she called one leaf oregano and was wrong, Amphitrite smiled without turning the mistake into a lesson.

“Do you cook with these?” Silena asked.

“Sometimes. I grow more than I can use.”

“Why?”

“Because I like seeing them grow.”

“That’s a good reason.”

“I think so.”

They watched the bee come back to the rosemary. Silena began to ask what the basin was for, then stopped. It did not matter. Quiet could be enough without being a blank that needed filling.


Amphitrite did not ask how long Silena wanted to stay. She let the quiet stretch until Silena decided to break it.

“Why did you come back to camp?” Silena asked.

“I had a few things to discuss with Chiron. I also wanted to ask you if another visit would feel all right.”

Silena kept her eyes on the garden. “You could have asked that in the note.”

“I could have. I didn't want it to sound as if I was making a plan and inviting you to fit into it.”

“That’s good.”

“I’m glad.”

The answer was not an argument for why Amphitrite had done the right thing. She left it there.

“Would you come again?” Silena asked.

“If you wanted me to.”

Silena pressed her thumb against the seam of her jacket. “That isn’t what I asked.”

“No. I would like to come again. But I won’t unless you want that too.”

Silena turned slightly. “How often do you usually visit?”

“It depends on the season, and on what is happening in Atlantis. I cannot promise a schedule.”

“I don’t need a schedule.”

“Then we don’t need to make one.”

The distinction mattered. A promise could be broken; a plan could become a test. Silena did not want to count the weeks between visits and decide what each delay meant.

“I might want to see you again,” she said.

“I would like that.”

“I don’t know when.”

“That’s all right.”

“Maybe I’ll change my mind.”

“You can.”

Silena watched the bee move toward the thyme. “You really don’t want me to say something specific.”

“I want you to say what you mean. Specific or not.”

“You can’t be certain I mean it if I don’t use the exact words.”

“No. I can only listen to the words you choose.”

“That sounds difficult.”

“It is.”

“Are you used to people agreeing with you?”

Amphitrite considered. “People often agree because I am their queen. I do not always know what the agreement means.”

“Do you ask?”

“I should ask more.”

Silena was not sure if that answer was an invitation to praise her honesty. It did not sound like one. She let it be what it was.

“What do you do when you don’t know?” she asked.

“I wait. Sometimes I ask. Sometimes I make the wrong guess and need to apologize.”

“Do you?”

“Apologize?”

“Make the wrong guess.”

A faint smile appeared. “Often enough.”

Silena smiled back, then let it go. The whole morning had been made of things she did not need to keep. It felt possible to enjoy Amphitrite’s company without deciding what it meant.

“Can I ask you something?” Amphitrite said.

“You can ask.”

“Would you rather I speak to you as one of your parents’ friends, or as a member of your family?”

Silena had not expected the question. Her hand closed around the edge of the bench. She could feel the rough grain beneath her fingertips.

“I don’t know.”

“Then I’ll leave it open.”

“Are you okay with that?”

“Yes.”

“Do you want me to decide?”

“I don’t want you to feel that you need to.”

Silena studied Amphitrite’s face, looking for the reaction that might follow. Nothing hardened. No disappointment became a demand.

“I don’t want to talk about my mother today,” Silena said.

“Then we won’t.”

“I might want to later.”

“Then I will listen later, if you want me to.”

The letter to Maria was still folded inside the flat pocket of Silena's portfolio. It had been there long enough that its edges had softened. She had written three versions of the first line and kept the one that sounded least like a request for a reply. She had not sent it. She did not know whether Maria had received the last one or whether she would answer. Amphitrite did not know that. Silena had not told her, and she was not ready to.

She was glad the quiet could contain a thing Amphitrite did not know.

The bell from the kitchen rang once. Lunch was not ready; it was only the signal for the first group to bring in the covered trays. Amphitrite looked toward the house.

“I should go,” she said. “Chiron is expecting me to talk through the harbor schedule.”

“Do you want to?”

“It would be easier to go now.”

“That's not what I asked.”

“No.” Amphitrite’s smile returned. “I don’t especially want to discuss the harbor schedule.”

“Then why go?”

“Because I told him I would.”

Silena recognized the answer. It was a reason that belonged to Amphitrite, not a request for Silena to excuse it.

“You can stay until the next bell,” Silena said.

“Would you like me to?”

“Yes.”

“Then I will.”

They stayed. Amphitrite told her one small thing about the herb bed—how the thyme had come back after an early frost—then let Silena decide whether to ask more. Silena asked about the rosemary. They talked about drying leaves and which stems could survive a careless hand. When the bell rang again, Amphitrite rose.

“Thank you for letting me sit with you,” she said.

“I liked it.”

“Would you like a hug?”

Silena considered the distance, then the words. “Not today.”

“All right.”

Amphitrite did not reach for her. Silena stood too, and they walked together to the porch. At the steps, Amphitrite turned toward the Big House while Silena took the path back to Cabin Three.

No one had asked her to decide what the visit meant. That helped. It did not settle anything.


When Silena reached Cabin Three, Clarisse had moved from the spear to the practice shield. Leandros sat at the worktable with a length of cord and a scrap of canvas. Beckendorf stood beside him, checking the new splice.

“Does it hold?” Silena asked.

“Not yet,” Beckendorf said. “It’s laid evenly. I still need to test how it sits under the cover’s weight.”

Leandros’s hands were flat on the table. “I didn’t ask her to report it to Clarisse.”

“No one said you did,” Silena replied.

“I was explaining.”

“I know.”

He leaned back, annoyed. “You always hear the worst version.”

“You asked me to carry something to Clarisse before.”

“I didn’t this time.”

“Then we agree.”

Clarisse glanced up from the shield. “What are you two talking about?”

“Nothing,” Leandros said.

“His splice isn’t tested yet,” Silena said. “Beckendorf is checking it.”

“I didn’t ask you to say anything,” Leandros said.

“Then don’t ask me to answer.”

The sentence came out sharper than she meant. She had no energy to soften it into a lesson. Leandros looked at her, then turned back to the cord.

“I asked Beckendorf to check it,” he said. “Not you.”

“Good.”

Clarisse shifted the shield against her knee. “The board still has the tag that says not to use it.”

“It’s there,” Beckendorf said. “No one is putting the cover up until the test passes.”

Leandros picked up the loose end of cord, then put it down again. “I know.”

No one praised him. No one scolded him. Beckendorf returned to the check, Clarisse to the shield, and Silena to the portfolio she had left open on her bunk. The blue line had dried to a pale edge. It still needed another wash.

Clarisse came to stand beside her. “How was it?”

“Fine.”

“Do you want to tell me more?”

“Not right now.”

“Okay.”

Silena slipped the painting between two sheets of clean paper. She had not told Clarisse what she and Amphitrite had discussed. She had not decided to keep it secret forever; she simply wanted one thing to remain hers until she knew what it was.

Clarisse picked up the practice shield. “I’m going to the field.”

“Have a good session.”

“Do you want to come?”

“No. I want to finish this.”

Clarisse nodded, without asking what this meant. She went out the door, passing Leandros without speaking. He watched the table.

Silena kept her eyes on the painting. She was not going to translate the silence between them either.

---

A few days later, Percy and Annabeth found Silena on the back steps, trimming the loose edge of a watercolor sheet. Percy sat beside her with a bowl of cut fruit. Annabeth leaned against the post, a folded list tucked into her pocket.

“Do you need a knife?” Percy asked.

“I have scissors.”

“Those are not for fruit.”

“I’m not cutting fruit.”

“Then I have a bowl of fruit you may look at.”

Silena snipped the paper cleanly. “How generous.”

Annabeth opened the list. “We’re checking the roof runoff after the next storm.”

“Has the Cabin One window stayed dry?”

“The first rain check passed,” Annabeth said. “We still need a longer storm.”

“Ianthe wants to help,” Percy added.

“She offered,” Annabeth corrected. “We haven’t made a full plan.”

“Right.”

Silena turned the page over. “It sounds like you both know how to leave something unfinished.”

“Not how to,” Annabeth said. “More like we’re getting used to it.”

Percy took a grape from the bowl. “It’s a skill.”

“Is that why you keep eating the fruit before dinner?” Silena asked.

“That’s a different skill.”

Annabeth nudged his arm with hers. “It’s not a skill.”

“Then I’m naturally talented.”

Silena laughed. The ordinary sound sat between them without turning the morning into a milestone. Percy told her that Amphitrite had visited the camp again, and he saw her in the Big House garden. “Chiron said you spoke with her,” he added. “You don’t have to tell me what about.”

“I’m glad you said that.”

“I figured you’d tell me if you wanted to.”

“I had a nice time.”

“Good.”

“It doesn’t mean I know what to call her.”

Percy looked at her, then at Annabeth. “You don’t owe anyone the same answer.”

“I know.”

Annabeth took the fruit bowl from his lap before he could tip it. “You can decide what the word means for you.”

Silena heard no pressure in the sentence. Annabeth did not say what she should do, or how she would feel if the answer changed. She simply held the bowl steady and let Percy choose another piece of fruit.

“I have a letter to my mother I still haven’t sent,” Silena said.

Percy nodded. “I know.”

“I don’t know if she’ll answer.”

“I know.”

“I don’t want saying something to Amphitrite to mean I forgot Maria.”

“You haven’t forgotten her,” he said. “You don’t have to prove that.”

Silena held the paper between her fingers. The letter had nothing to do with Amphitrite, except that it sat in the same pocket where Silena kept things she had not decided to do. “I might still not say anything.”

“Okay.”

“I might say it and then take it back.”

“That’s yours too.”

Annabeth folded the list and put it away. “It can be private for as long as you want.”

Silena looked from her to Percy. They had not asked what she planned to say. They had not asked when. Their care left space for an answer that might be different tomorrow.

“Thanks,” she said.

Percy nudged her knee with his. “Anytime, sister.”

She nudged him back. “Go eat the rest of the fruit before Annabeth confiscates it.”

“I would never,” Annabeth said.

“You absolutely would,” Percy replied.

“I would preserve it for dinner.”

“That’s what confiscation means.”

Their voices overlapped, warm and familiar. Silena watched them argue over the bowl and returned to the edge of the watercolor page.

In the days that followed, Silena finished the first wash and started a second. She kept the page in her portfolio, not because she planned to show it to Amphitrite, but because she wanted it safe from splashes in the cabin. The loose folds of the paper caught at the corners. She smoothed them each evening before closing the cover.

She did not send a message to the sea.

Amphitrite did not ask for one. She came to camp once more on a day when Chiron had a meeting with her. Silena learned it only when she reached the Big House to return a pair of kitchen shears. Chiron mentioned the visit in the same tone he used to say the stable roof would be checked after lunch.

“Amphitrite asked whether you might like to see her before she leaves,” he said. “I told her I would ask you. There is no need to decide if you don't want to.”

Silena set the shears on the counter. “Where is she?”

“The garden.”

“Is she alone?”

“She is waiting by the same bench.”

“Did you tell her I might say no?”

“I did.”

“Then I’ll go for a little while.”

Chiron did not ask why. He only nodded.

This time the bench was dry. Amphitrite had not brought the watercolor paper or anything she thought Silena might like. She sat with both hands around an empty cup of tea.

“Did Chiron tell you why I’m here?” she asked.

“He said you had a meeting.”

“That is true.”

“What is the other reason?”

“I wanted to see you.”

Silena sat at the opposite end of the bench. “How long do you have?”

“Until the meeting.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

Amphitrite considered. “I can stay for twenty minutes. Then I need to go upstairs.”

“Okay.”

“Would you prefer tea?”

“No, thanks.”

“Would you like me to wait until you ask a question?”

Silena had not expected the offer. “Yes.”

Amphitrite nodded. She did not fill the silence. Silena listened to the small scrape of a gardener’s rake in the next bed, the fountain’s thin thread of water, and the wind moving through the laurel leaves. A minute passed. Maybe more.

“I want to show you the next painting,” Silena said.

“Would you like to bring it now?”

“No. It’s still at the cabin.”

“Then I can look another time, if you decide.”

Silena’s shoulders loosened. “I don’t want you to think I’m making a gift for you.”

“I haven’t thought that.”

“I’m painting for myself.”

“I’d be glad to see it. That doesn’t change who it belongs to.”

“You’re sure?”

“Yes.”

Silena pressed her fingertips to the bench. “I don’t want to make you happy by giving you something I wanted to keep.”

“Then keep it.”

“Even if you want it?”

“Wanting to see it doesn't give me a right to it.”

Silena looked at Amphitrite’s cup. The tea had cooled enough that the steam no longer showed. “Do you always know what to say?”

“No. I’m not sure I knew what to say just now.”

“That seemed like a good answer.”

“I’m glad.”

Silena tilted her head. “You can’t say that every time I agree with you.”

“I can try not to.”

“Please.”

“All right.”

Silena enjoyed the way the word sounded: not because it was a new promise, but because it ended the subject without making her defend it. Amphitrite glanced at the garden gate.

“Would you like me to leave now?” she asked.

“Not yet.”

They spent the remaining minutes talking about the garden. Amphitrite asked whether the late thyme had gone to seed; Silena said she did not know and asked how to tell. Amphitrite pointed, and Silena leaned over the low bed to look. Her sleeve brushed the rosemary. Amphitrite did not reach to move her arm, only watched as Silena found the dried stalk.

“Could I take one?” Silena asked.

“Of course. There are more.”

Silena clipped a short stem from the outer edge. She put it in her pocket, where it would leave a little scent on the lining. It was not a gift. It was a piece she had asked to take, and Amphitrite had let her choose.

When Amphitrite rose to leave, Silena stood too. “Will you come back?”

“If you want me to.”

“I might.”

“Then you can tell Chiron, or send word through him.”

“Okay.”

“Would you like a hug?”

Silena let the question sit a moment. “Not this time.”

“Thank you for telling me.”

Amphitrite turned toward the steps. Silena watched her go, then took the other path to the cabin. No one waited to ask what had happened. She was grateful for that.

---

That evening, Clarisse found the rosemary stem tucked into the edge of Silena’s portfolio.

“Did you pick that from the garden?”

“Amphitrite showed me where the thyme had gone to seed. I took rosemary.”

“Why?”

“I liked it.”

Clarisse lifted a brow. “All right.”

Silena rubbed the stem between her thumb and forefinger. The scent rose clean and sharp. “You don’t have to ask more.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“I know.”

Clarisse leaned against the table. “Do you like seeing her?”

“Yes.”

“Do you like her?”

“I think so.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No.”

“Are you still not sure?”

“I’m still not sure about what it means.”

Clarisse nodded, then picked up the repaired spear to check its balance. “You can like someone and not know what they are to you.”

“I know.”

“And it doesn’t change what she is to Percy.”

“No.”

Clarisse set the spear down. “I don’t want you thinking you have to decide for all of us.”

“I don’t.”

“Good.”

“I’m not doing it for you.”

“I know that.”

“Then why did you ask?”

“Because I wanted to know how you were doing.”

Silena looked at her sister. Clarisse had asked the question and then left it open. “I’m okay. I like spending time with her. I don’t know if I want to call her anything.”

“You don’t have to tell me the answer.”

“I know.”

“Then don’t.”

Clarisse returned to the spear. Silena folded the rosemary into a scrap of paper and placed it beside her paintbrush. The stem would be dry by morning. She could use it in the bread if she wanted, or put it in a sketch. It did not need to become a symbol just because she had chosen it.


The next afternoon, Silena opened the drawer beneath her bunk and took out the letter to Maria.

The paper had been folded in thirds, then folded again. The first version was too long; the second sounded like a report. The one she had kept began with *I am doing all right*. It said nothing about whether Maria had received the last letter. It did not ask whether she was coming back. Silena had not decided what she wanted the answer to be.

She put the letter on the table and did not open it. The name at the top was still clear.

Amphitrite was not Maria. Calling her something else could not change that. Silena did not need to choose one mother against another, or one history over another. Even if she used a word that fit, the letter would still be there. The unanswered question would still be hers.

She returned the letter to the drawer.

Leandros came in with a short length of cord looped around his fingers. “Beckendorf said the rack splice can be tested tomorrow.”

“Good.”

“I thought you might want to know.”

“I do.”

He waited, the cord swinging slightly. “Are you going to the Big House again?”

Silena set her paintbrush in the water cup. “Why?”

“Because I saw you and her in the garden.”

“You saw us from the path.”

“I wasn’t trying to spy.”

“I didn’t say you were.”

“Clarisse asked too.”

“What did you tell her?”

“Nothing. She didn’t ask me.” He looked down at the loop. “I thought you might be going because she’s a queen.”

Silena studied him. He had drawn the cord into a tight circle without noticing. “I’m not going because she’s a queen.”

“I know you don’t like being told what you’re doing.”

“Then why tell me?”

“I’m trying to understand.”

“Ask a question. Don’t explain me to myself.”

He released the cord. It loosened against his palm. “Are you going because you want to see her?”

“Yes.”

“That’s it?”

“That’s enough.”

He was quiet, looking as if he had expected a longer answer he could take apart. “I thought there might be more.”

“There may be later. You don’t get to decide what the more is.”

“I’m not trying to decide.”

“Then stop asking like you’re waiting for a verdict.”

Leandros tucked the cord into his pocket. “You always make it sound like I’m trying to control you.”

“You often try to control what people say about you.”

“That’s different.”

“It feels the same from here.”

He looked at the floor. “I don’t want you to have to explain to Clarisse what I’m doing.”

“I’m not going to.”

“I know that.”

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

“What do you want from me?”

“Nothing.” He rubbed his thumb over a rough place in the cord. “I wanted to know.”

“Then you know I want to see her.”

He nodded, but did not leave. “Does it bother you that Percy already calls her Mom?”

Silena felt the question press against the letter in the drawer. “No.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yes.”

“Because it would be strange if he called her something else.”

“His word is his.”

“Then yours can be different.”

“It can.”

He shifted his weight, the cord now still. “I don’t know what I would call my father if he came here.”

“You don’t have to decide.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I’m asking you, not giving you a lecture.”

“And I’m telling you the answer.”

A pause opened between them. Leandros folded his arms, then unfolded them. “Fine.”

“Fine what?”

“I don’t need to know yet.”

Silena let the answer stand. It was the first time he had asked about her choices without also trying to tell her what those choices should mean. It did not erase the way he had treated her as a messenger, or the untested splice, or any other thing. It was one better question.

“Thanks for asking,” she said.

He blinked. “You’re thanking me?”

“For asking.”

“Not for the rest?”

“No.”

A reluctant laugh left him. “Fair.”

He went back to the worktable. Silena picked up her brush. The quiet returned without either of them claiming it.

---

That evening, Clarisse sat on the cabin steps while Silena sorted the dried rosemary into a small jar. The air had cooled. A thin line of cloud crossed the moon, and the porch lantern made the stone pale around their shoes.

“Leandros asked if I was going back to see Amphitrite,” Silena said.

Clarisse twisted the end of a loose thread on her sleeve. “Did he tell you what to do?”

“No.”

“Did he try to?”

“Only a little.”

“What did you say?”

“That I wanted to see her.”

“Good.”

Silena pressed the rosemary into the jar. “I don’t know what I’d call her.”

Clarisse looked toward the yard. “You don’t have to know.”

“I know.”

“Then what do you need from me?”

“Nothing. I wanted to say it out loud.”

Clarisse’s expression softened, barely. “You can.”

“I don’t want it to become a thing everybody discusses.”

“It won’t.”

“If I decide to call her something, I don’t want it to mean you have to decide too.”

“I’m not you.”

“I know.”

“I might not want anything from her.”

“That’s yours.”

“Good.”

Clarisse took the jar from Silena and checked the lid. “This one’s loose.”

Silena tightened it. “Better?”

“Better.”

They sat for a few minutes without talking. In the yard, somebody laughed on the way back from the pavilion. The laugh faded behind the cabin wall.

“Would you be angry if I wanted something you didn’t?” Silena asked.

Clarisse turned the jar once in her hands. “I might be angry about other things.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“I don’t think I’d be angry at you for choosing your own word.”

Silena nodded. It was not a promise about what Clarisse might someday want. It was a promise not to make Silena’s choice into a demand.

Clarisse stood. “I’m going to sleep.”

“Good night.”

“Don’t leave that jar where Percy can find it.”

“He’ll find it anyway.”

“Then put it somewhere harder.”

Silena took the rosemary inside. She placed the jar in the upper cupboard, where Percy would have to use a stool, and put the stool back where it belonged. The choice was small, almost funny. She thought she might bake with the leaves later. She did not know yet.


Another handful of days passed. Silena painted the second wash, folded the rosemary stem into a scrap of paper, and put it away when she decided she did not need it in the picture. She was learning that a thing could matter without having to be used.

When Chiron next said Amphitrite would be at camp for a short meeting, Silena chose to ask for a little time. She did not ask him to make it sound important. She asked him to tell Amphitrite she would like to sit together after the meeting, if Amphitrite had time.

“She said she would be glad to,” Chiron told her. “Would you like to meet in the garden again?”

“Could we sit in the Big House studio?”

“The windows are open, and there will be no one else using it.”

“That’s where I’d like to go.”

Chiron passed the choice along. Silena did not send the painting ahead. She carried it there herself in a flat folder.

Amphitrite was already in the studio when Silena arrived, standing by the open windows. She had not touched the watercolors or the paper stacked in a tray. She stepped away from the table so Silena could set her things down.

“Is the studio all right?” Amphitrite asked.

“Yes.”

“Would you like me to sit, or would you rather I stand?”

Silena smiled faintly. “You can sit.”

Amphitrite took the chair across from her. Silena opened the folder but kept the page inside.

“Would you like me to look?” Amphitrite asked.

“Not yet.”

“Okay.”

Silena drew out the picture and rested it facedown on the table. “I might show you when I’m ready.”

“I’ll wait.”

“You don’t have to wait for me to decide.”

“I don’t mind waiting.”

Silena looked at the closed back of the paper. The line she had painted was not more beautiful than the last one. It was looser, rougher at the left edge, with a place where the blue had bled into gray. She liked the mistake. It looked like water breaking at a shore.

“What do you usually do when you’re waiting?” she asked.

“Depends on what I’m waiting for.”

“Do you get impatient?”

“Yes.”

“What then?”

“I try to remember that the person who needs time isn’t responsible for making me comfortable.”

Silena tilted her head. “Does that work?”

“Not every time.”

“What do you do when it doesn’t?”

“I apologize and begin again.”

“What if the person doesn’t want you to begin again?”

“Then I give them that choice.”

She moved the folder closer to herself. “You don’t have to give every answer to me like it’s a rule.”

“I’m sorry.”

Silena looked up. Amphitrite’s face had gone still, waiting to see whether Silena wanted more. She had not demanded that the apology count. She had not explained that she was trying to help.

“It’s okay,” Silena said.

“I’ll be more careful.”

They sat with the page between them. The open window let in the smell of leaves and damp stone. Somewhere downstairs, a chair scraped across the kitchen floor. Silena heard the first note of a song from the dining pavilion, cut off when the singer forgot the words.

“I don’t know what being a mother is supposed to look like when someone is already sixteen,” Silena said.

“Neither do I.”

“Does that worry you?”

“Some.”

“Are you supposed to know?”

“I have other children. That doesn’t mean I know you.”

Silena appreciated that answer more than any promise that Amphitrite would understand everything. “I don’t want to be treated like a little kid.”

“I won’t choose that for you.”

“You might do it by accident.”

“Then you can tell me.”

“I might not.”

“Then I’ll pay attention and ask.”

“What if I don’t answer?”

“I will wait.”

Silena took the page from the folder and turned it over. The colors had settled. “You can look now.”

Amphitrite leaned forward slightly, then stopped. “May I?”

“Yes.”

She looked at the painting for a few seconds. “The gray is beautiful.”

Silena waited for the rest: the explanation, the suggestion, the way to improve it. Amphitrite only looked at the page.

“You don’t think it needs something?”

“I don’t know what you want it to be.”

“I don’t either.”

“Then it can stay as it is until you do.”

Silena glanced from the page to Amphitrite. “You’re not going to tell me what it could be?”

“Would you like an idea?”

“Not yet.”

“Then I won’t.”

Silena turned the page toward herself. “I like the part where the blue runs.”

“I do too.”

“That’s enough.”

Amphitrite sat back. She did not reach to touch the corner or keep the picture in front of her. The small care mattered because it did not ask for a response.

A few minutes later, Amphitrite asked if Silena wanted to continue or end the visit.

“Can we stay until the kitchen song is over?”

“If you like.”

The song began again downstairs. This time the singer remembered the words. Silena listened while she packed the watercolor away. Amphitrite listened too. When the song ended, Silena closed the folder.

“That’s my time,” she said.

“All right.”

“I liked this.”

“So did I.”

Silena stood. At the door, Amphitrite paused and let Silena choose whether to walk ahead. Silena went first. They parted at the stairwell without a hug. The visit had not produced a gift, a promise, or a new title. It had been an afternoon in which Silena decided how much of herself she wanted to show.


The rosemary bread took longer to rise than Silena expected.

She checked the bowl twice, then stopped checking. The dough would not rise faster because she watched it. She used the time to set the table and finish the list of ingredients she had copied into her recipe notebook. She added a note that the rosemary should be crushed before it went in. It would not help anyone else until they asked to borrow the recipe.

Clarisse came in with Percy and Annabeth. “I thought you were making a small loaf,” Clarisse said.

“I am.”

“That’s not small.”

“It is for this bowl.”

Percy peered into it. “How many people is ‘this bowl’?”

“Four.”

“There are five of us.”

“Then someone can have a smaller piece.”

“I volunteer Clarisse,” Percy said.

Clarisse elbowed him. He flinched theatrically, and Annabeth caught his sleeve before he knocked the flour off the counter.

“Don’t injure yourself before dinner,” Annabeth said.

“I wasn’t the one who started it.”

“You were the one standing there.”

Silena slid the bowl closer to herself. “I can finish the dough.”

“Do you want help?” Annabeth asked.

“Can you oil the pan?”

“Sure.”

“Percy, could you carry it to the oven when I’m ready?”

“Absolutely.”

Clarisse reached for the rosemary jar. Silena stopped her with a look.

“I wasn’t going to add it,” Clarisse said.

“You were opening it.”

“To smell.”

“You can smell it without taking the lid off.”

“I can?”

“Now you know.”

Clarisse smiled, put the jar down, and washed her hands. No one tried to take the dough away. Silena worked the herbs in, folded the edges, and transferred the loaf to the pan. Annabeth oiled the sides; Percy carried it when Silena asked.

Leandros appeared just as the loaf went into the oven. He had dried canvas dust on one cuff and the pale groove of cord across his thumb.

“Is that the rosemary recipe?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Can I have some when it’s done?”

“If you want.”

“I do.”

“Then you can.”

He paused as if he had expected a condition. “Should it have more salt?”

“It has enough.”

“Have you tasted it?”

“I know what I put in.”

“That doesn’t mean—”

“It’s my recipe.”

The words stopped him. Clarisse looked down at the table. Percy occupied himself with a spoon. Annabeth did not rescue the moment.

Leandros studied the loaf through the oven window. “I’m not trying to change it.”

“Then don’t.”

“I was asking.”

“You were telling me what it needed before it was baked.”

He exhaled. “Fine. I’ll taste it first.”

“That would be better.”

He went to wash his hands. Silena turned back to the table and set out five plates. She did not soften the answer for him. He had asked to eat; she had said yes. That was what she meant.

When the bread came out, the crust had browned unevenly along one side. The center held, and the rosemary smelled bright without overpowering the dough. Silena let it cool before cutting.

Percy took a slice. “This is excellent.”

“Is it?” Annabeth asked.

“Very.”

“You say that about everything she cooks.”

“I say it because it’s true.”

Clarisse tried a piece and nodded. “Good.”

Leandros waited until Silena set down the knife before taking a slice. He tasted it without comment, then added a little salt only to his own piece.

“Better?” Silena asked.

“Different.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s my piece.”

She laughed. “Fair.”

They ate at the table while the kitchen emptied around them. Percy asked for another piece. Clarisse pushed the plate to him, but not before taking the last slice she wanted. Annabeth took a small piece and wrapped the remainder for later. Leandros did not ask Silena to set aside a loaf for anyone. He did not ask whether she had told Clarisse about his splice. He remained at the table after his plate was clean, listening more than he spoke.

When Clarisse stood to put the dishes away, Silena kept the recipe notebook by her place.

“Want me to wash those?” Leandros asked.

“You can dry them.”

“Okay.”

She passed him a towel. He dried the plates as Clarisse washed. No one announced that he had improved. The work had to be done, and he did it.

After the last dish, he nodded toward the portfolio on the shelf. “Are you going to show her the painting?”

“I don’t know.”

“Okay.”

“You don’t need to ask again.”

“I wasn’t.”

“Good.”

He glanced toward the doorway. “You don’t owe me the details.”

“No.”

“I know.”

He left the room before she could decide whether to say anything else. Silena shut the recipe notebook. The bread had belonged to her even when other people ate it.


Silena did not use the word that night.

She used no word at breakfast either. She sat beside Clarisse, traded the heel of her bread for a slice of apple, and listened to Percy explain the rain barrel map. Annabeth corrected one line and Percy let her. A messenger from the Big House arrived with a list of deliveries for the infirmary. Clarisse said she could take the first basket; Silena said she would take the second one after she finished her inventory.

The day made room for other things.

At lunch, Chiron mentioned that Amphitrite would be returning to Atlantis before evening. Silena nodded and passed the bread to Percy, because that was the sort of thing she did when she had no idea what to say and didn't want anybody to notice. The news wasn't a surprise. Amphitrite had councils to attend and a whole city beneath the sea that kept running whether she was at camp or not. Silena knew all of that. She had known it every time Amphitrite visited. It did nothing to explain why the rest of the meal seemed to go on without her, voices rising and falling around the table while she worried at a loose thread on her cuff.

She spent the early afternoon helping with the Big House linen, partly because the basket was full and partly because the work required two hands. She could fold a sheet so its corners met even when her head was full of other things. Her painting was drying in its portfolio back at Cabin Three, and the letter to Maria was still in her drawer, addressed but unsent. Silena had read it so often that she knew which sentences were trying too hard. She'd spent an entire page telling her mother what the camp looked like in winter without writing the one thing she actually wanted to ask: whether Maria missed her. Now there was another woman she wanted to see, and that desire sat badly beside the letter, as though one ought to cancel the other.

Amphitrite was by the garden gate when Silena came out with the clean cloths. She was speaking with a naiad whose hands were wet to the wrists. Whatever had been under discussion ended with a brief laugh and a gesture toward the lake; then the naiad slipped away, leaving Amphitrite alone among the bare stems. The queen wore the same dark-blue cloak she'd worn at breakfast, but the wind had pulled a strand of hair loose behind her ear. For a strange instant Silena could imagine her coming to the porch on an ordinary Tuesday, bringing nothing more ceremonial than the wrong kind of bread and asking whether there was tea.

“That's an impressive mountain of laundry,” Amphitrite said when she noticed the basket. “I was about to offer to help, but I think I might make matters worse.”

Silena glanced down. A towel was trailing over the rim and threatening to fall into the mud. “Only if you pick it up by that corner. Chiron keeps saying the linen cupboard is overflowing, and then somebody produces six more cloths from nowhere.”

“Palaces have a similar problem. Everyone believes clean linen appears because somebody asked politely.” Amphitrite rescued the dangling towel without touching the rest of the basket. “Where are you taking this?”

“Upstairs. It's not heavy.” Silena adjusted her grip, then regretted the defensiveness in her voice. Amphitrite hadn't suggested she was weak. She hadn't taken over the painting either, or laughed when Silena decided a color was wrong after spending an hour mixing it. She kept waiting for the moment that patience would turn out to have a price.

Amphitrite took her hands away from the basket. “I believe you.”

They stood there with the garden between them, neither in much danger of saying something useful. Silena could hear campers shouting over a game by the cabins, and the normality of it irritated her. Somebody was probably winning at something ridiculous while she stood here trying to work out how to ask a woman whether leaving today meant she might not come back for months. At last she set the basket on the wall, careful of the damp moss, and brushed her palms against her skirt.

“Chiron said you're going back this evening.”

“I am. There's a harbor council I ought to have attended yesterday, and I've put it off as long as I can.” Amphitrite smiled faintly. “Triton has been very gracious about it, which is how I know he's annoyed.”

Silena laughed before she could stop herself. “He does that with Percy. He's polite right up until Percy realizes he's in trouble.”

“He learned it from me, I'm afraid.” The smile faded into something more attentive. “Were you hoping we would have more time?”

Silena almost said no. She had been good at that answer for years, good enough that even Clarisse sometimes accepted it before looking twice. This time she picked up the loose end of her cuff and wound it around one finger, then unwound it. “I think so. I don't know. We had time. It's not as if you didn't spend it with me.”

“I liked spending it with you,” Amphitrite said. “And I'd have liked more.”

There was no clever reply to that. Silena had expected reassurance, a promise about the next visit, perhaps one of the lovely courtly speeches Amphitrite could make when she wanted a room to listen. Instead, she'd been given something small and unadorned, and it hurt more than it should have. She picked the basket up again before her face could give her away.

“Would you walk with me to the linen room?” she asked. “I can carry this, but I wouldn't mind the company.”

“I would love to.”

They took the upper path together. Amphitrite didn't hurry her, even when a gust of wind caught the cloak against her knees, and Silena found herself talking about the absurdly uneven stack of sheets on the landing. She told her about the pale blue wash she'd been trying to save in her painting, about how Clarisse had asked whether the rosemary belonged in stew, about Percy stealing crust from a loaf that had barely come out of the oven. The details weren't important to anybody outside the cabin. Amphitrite listened as if they were, and when Silena forgot what she was saying halfway through a sentence, she didn't fill in the rest.

At the Big House steps Silena stopped to shift the basket. “Could you take that side for a moment? The handle's cutting into my fingers.”

Amphitrite took the handle and followed her up. It was such a simple thing that Silena resented herself for noticing. Maria had carried things for her. Clarisse had too, and Percy, often while complaining that someone had packed stones into them. Nothing about sharing a heavy basket made Amphitrite a mother. Silena knew that; she wasn't a child who confused being helped with being loved. Yet as they made their awkward way around the turn in the stair and nearly caught the basket on the railing, she felt suddenly and painfully happy, the kind of happiness that made her afraid to move in case it vanished.

They left the linen outside the cupboard. The first towel had come unfolded during the climb, and Silena fixed it because otherwise she'd think about it all night. Amphitrite lingered by the doorway, looking down at the neat stack. She didn't ask if Silena wanted anything else. Silena was grateful, and then she was angry at being grateful, and then she could hardly remember why she'd stopped walking in the first place.

“Can I tell you something?” Silena asked.

Amphitrite looked up. “Of course.”

“I've been wanting to say it for a while. Which makes this a terrible way to start, because now it's going to sound like a proclamation.” Her laugh came out thin. “And you're already a queen. You probably hear enough proclamations.”

“More than enough.”

“I'm serious.”

“So am I.” Amphitrite stood a little straighter, her hands loose at her sides. “I'm listening.”

Silena looked toward the landing. Nobody was there. She wished there had been a reason to delay, a misplaced sheet or a door that needed shutting, something useful she could do with her hands. In Cabin Three she knew where she belonged; she could be angry with Clarisse, tease Percy, put her painting aside for a day, and still find the same people at breakfast. This felt different because there was no name for it that she'd been willing to say aloud, and because naming it might make it harder to bear when Amphitrite went back beneath the sea.

“Mom?” she said, so quietly that she almost hoped the wind had swallowed it.

Amphitrite's expression changed. Not dramatically, not all at once. Her lips parted, and for the first time that afternoon she seemed to forget about speaking like a queen. She took a breath, then let it out. “Yes, sweetheart?”

Silena's eyes filled before she could decide whether to be embarrassed. “I don't mean all the time. I mean, I don't know what I mean exactly. Maria is my mother. I haven't even sent her the letter I wrote, and I don't want this to be some horrible way of giving up on her.”

“Oh, Silena.” Amphitrite's voice had roughened. She started to reach for her, then stopped, leaving Silena room to move. “Loving me doesn't take a single thing away from her.”

“I know that in my head.” Silena rubbed at her cheek and was annoyed to find her fingers wet. “I just wanted it to be true somewhere else, too.”

“It can be.” Amphitrite swallowed, and Silena saw with a shock that there were tears in her eyes as well. “You don't have to work out the rules for it today.”

That was nearly enough to undo her. Silena stepped forward and rested her forehead briefly against Amphitrite's shoulder. She was trembling, which felt ridiculous because nothing frightening had happened, but Amphitrite held still until Silena lifted her head. She smelled faintly of salt and something floral Silena could never identify properly. It wasn't Maria's scent. The difference no longer felt like a betrayal.

“I don't want everybody to know yet,” Silena said, wiping her face with the back of her sleeve. “Especially Clarisse. She's still figuring out what she wants from you, and I don't want her thinking I've made some decision for the cabin.”

“You haven't.” Amphitrite touched the cuff Silena had been twisting, then withdrew her hand. “I won't tell her. Or Percy. This can be ours until you want otherwise.”

Silena let herself breathe. “You'll be back?”

“I intend to. There are ways to write, and ways to ask Chiron to send a message when you want one. I'm not going to promise a day I can't be certain of.” She gave a crooked little smile. “I've spent far too many years watching other people make promises to children they couldn't keep.”

Silena knew who some of those people were. She wasn't going to ask Amphitrite to explain them now. She reached out, gave her a quick, fierce hug, and was startled by how readily the older woman hugged her back. It lasted only a moment. Neither seemed eager to be the one who ended it.

“You really are going to be late for that council,” Silena murmured against her shoulder.

“Probably. Triton will survive.”

“That's not what I meant.”

“I know.” Amphitrite pulled back enough to look at her. “I'm glad you told me.”

Silena nodded, unable to trust her voice. She stayed on the landing while Amphitrite descended the stairs. At the bottom, the queen looked back once. Silena raised her hand, then laughed at herself and waved properly, like someone seeing a parent off at the door. Amphitrite returned the wave and passed through the garden gate without summoning a procession or making the farewell into something people would ask about later.

For several minutes Silena stood beside the linen room with her hands empty. She ought to have felt lighter. Instead, she felt tired and pleased and bereft all at once, and somewhere beneath it was the absurd wish that she could run after Amphitrite and say the word again just to hear her answer. She wasn't going to do that. The basket still needed putting away, and Clarisse would wonder why she'd missed the start of supper. Silena gathered up the clean cloths, carried them into the cupboard, and found that her hands had stopped shaking by the time the last shelf was straight.

By the time Silena returned to Cabin Three, the table was already set for dinner. Clarisse had placed the bowls in a line. Percy and Annabeth were arguing over whether the bread should sit near the soup or at the far end.

“If it’s by the soup, people will reach across it,” Annabeth said.

“If it’s at the far end, Percy will eat it before everyone sits,” Clarisse said.

“I can wait,” Percy said.

“You have never waited for bread,” Annabeth replied.

“I have waited for bread.”

“You stood beside the oven.”

“That’s waiting.”

Silena took the loaf from the cooling board and moved it to the center. “There. You can all reach it.”

Percy gave her a grateful look. Clarisse rolled her eyes. Annabeth passed the serving spoon to Silena. The ordinary exchange made room for all of them without asking anyone to settle anything else.

Leandros arrived just before they started eating. He carried no cord this time. Beckendorf had told him the splice would be tested the next morning, not at dinner.

“Are we waiting for anyone?” he asked.

“No,” Clarisse said.

Leandros took the open place. He glanced at the bread, then at Silena. “Is it the rosemary loaf?”

“Yes.”

“Can I take a piece?”

“You can.”

He cut one. He did not ask what went into it or whether she had written the recipe down. He sat and ate.

Halfway through dinner, Clarisse asked if the spice mix should go into the stew the next time she made it. Silena said she could share the list if Clarisse wanted it. Clarisse asked for it. Silena told her where the notebook was, then continued eating. She did not leave the table to fetch it.

Percy told them the rain barrel had been moved. Annabeth said the new position might block the path and would have to be checked after another delivery. Percy said he could shift it if it did. She answered that they could decide after seeing it in place. He put another piece of bread on her plate, and she handed him the bowl without looking up.

No one made a speech about family. They ate, listened to a camper describe a runaway chicken, and asked whether the next morning’s rain would last. The table was crowded, the soup was warm, and Silena did not have to take anyone aside to make the evening work.

When Clarisse stood, Silena rose with her to carry the dishes. “Do you want me to get the notebook?” Clarisse asked.

“I’ll bring it tomorrow.”

“Okay.”

Clarisse did not ask about Amphitrite. She had asked before, and Silena had said she was okay. That answer had been enough. Silena did not need to add more to prove that she trusted her.

At the sink, Percy reached over to steal a piece of crust. Annabeth caught his wrist.

“Not the last one,” she said.

“I wasn’t going to take the last one.”

“You were reaching for it.”

“I was reaching near it.”

“That’s not how hands work.”

Silena set down the bowls, smiling. Percy made a show of surrendering the crust. Annabeth gave him half anyway. He bumped his shoulder against hers. She let it stay there as they rinsed the dishes.

Silena went to her bunk after cleanup. The letter to Maria stayed in the drawer. The recipe notebook stayed on the shelf. The rosemary jar had left a trace of scent on the paper around it.

She took out the watercolor she had begun before the visits and looked at the broken edge of the blue. She did not add anything. Not tonight.

The word she had chosen was not written on the painting, the list, or the recipe page. It did not alter the shape of the table or the people sitting around it. It belonged to the conversation where she had said it. She could keep it there until she decided otherwise.


The next morning, Silena took the spare towels to the bathhouse. When she returned, Clarisse was sitting on the cabin steps, rewrapping the leather at the end of her practice spear.

“Do you want the awl?” Silena asked.

“It’s on the porch.”

“Then I’ll bring it.”

Clarisse shifted the spear across her knees. “You don’t have to.”

“I know. I was going to get water anyway.”

Silena picked up the awl and a cup from the porch table. She set the tool within Clarisse’s reach and sat down one step lower. The sun had come out, but the stones still held the morning chill.

“Are you going to see her again?” Clarisse asked.

“Maybe.”

“You like being around her.”

“I do.”

“Do you have to know what that makes you?”

“No.”

Clarisse pressed the leather down with her thumb. “Good.”

Silena watched the new wrap take shape, one even turn at a time. “You don’t have to know either.”

“I know.”

“I mean it.”

“I know that too.”

Clarisse looked at her. “You’re not going to try to make my answer match yours?”

“No.”

“Even if I don’t answer?”

“Especially then.”

Clarisse returned to the spear. “What if I decide I don’t want to be close to her?”

“You can decide that.”

“What if I change my mind?”

“You can do that too.”

Clarisse frowned at the leather. “You make it sound like there’s no consequence.”

“There might be. It would still be your choice.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No. It isn’t.”

They sat for another minute, the awl between them. Clarisse kept her gaze on the wrap. Silena could see she wanted to say more, or wanted not to. She left the space open.

Finally, Clarisse said, “I don’t want you to start watching me because you did something first.”

“I won’t.”

“You can’t promise that.”

Silena considered. “I can promise I’ll ask if I’m not sure.”

“You can try.”

“I can.”

Clarisse tightened the end of the leather and pressed it flat. “That’s better.”

“Is it?”

“It’s a better promise.”

Silena nodded. The distinction did not need a conclusion.

Clarisse stood, testing the grip. “Want to go to the field?”

“Not yet.”

“Okay.”

“Maybe after lunch.”

“Maybe.”

Clarisse headed toward the porch. Silena stayed on the step. The morning did not ask her to prove anything by following.

At lunch, they sat together at the long table. Percy had saved her a seat, and Annabeth moved a basket so Clarisse could set her spear out of the aisle. Leandros arrived with Beckendorf, carrying the tested rack loop between them. Beckendorf set it on the far table and explained that the splice had held a controlled pull. It still needed a check on the cover before it could be returned to service.

Leandros did not smile. “It’s only a loop.”

“It carries the weight of the cover,” Beckendorf said. “That’s the check.”

“I know.”

Silena took a spoonful of soup. She did not answer for him, or for Beckendorf. Clarisse asked whether the loop was ready to go up. Beckendorf said not yet.

“Then it stays here,” Clarisse said.

Leandros pressed his lips together. For a moment, Silena thought he might argue. Then he carried the loop to the shelf and laid it flat. He did not ask anyone to notice.

After lunch, the group divided into ordinary tasks. Percy and Annabeth went to check the rain barrel’s path. Clarisse headed to the field. Silena stayed at the table to write down the new loop status so it would not be lost among the other repairs.

Leandros came back with the work list. “You can put ‘controlled pull passed; on-cover test pending.’”

“I know what to write.”

“I wasn’t correcting you.”

“You brought me the phrase.”

“Because Beckendorf said it.”

“Then he can write it.”

“He’s carrying the cover.”

Silena looked at the sheet. The work list did need the status. She wrote the phrase as Beckendorf had given it, then placed the page beside the loop.

“Thanks,” Leandros said.

“I wrote it because it needed to be there.”

“I know.”

She put the pencil away. The repair had advanced one step. It had not become a reason for him to ask for a report, a favor, or forgiveness. It was one task, with another check left open.


That evening, the second page rested on the table. Its edges had finally dried. Silena moved the stones and lifted it carefully. The blue had settled into a pale strip with gray where the pigment had run. She tilted the page toward the window, then back toward the lamp. The line changed with the light.

She opened the folder and placed the page in its own sleeve, apart from the study she had shown Amphitrite. It was still hers. She could show it someday or keep it folded away. Either choice could wait.

The letter to Maria remained in the drawer. Silena took it out once, checked that the fold had not torn, and put it back without changing a word. She did not have to send it to make it matter. She did not have to answer it herself before anyone else could care about her.

Across the cabin, Clarisse put the practice spear away. She did not ask Silena what she was thinking. Percy called from the path that Annabeth had found a better place for the rain barrel, and Annabeth called back that the position still needed a full check after rain. Their voices moved farther from the cabin and then disappeared.

Silena closed the drawer.

The quiet word did not solve the distance between Silena and Amphitrite. It did not make Maria’s letter less important, or give Clarisse an answer, or change Percy’s place in the family. It was not a promise for every day. It was the word Silena had chosen in one private moment, and the choice could remain small without becoming less real.

She put the watercolor folder beside her pillow and turned out the lamp.

For a while, she listened to the cabin settle. The boards creaked when the wind moved against them. A cup clicked softly in the kitchen, then stopped. Someone crossed the porch and went down the steps. Silena could have let sleep come. Instead, she sat up and took the folder back from beside her bed.

The painting had dried all the way through. The blue line stayed pale at its edge and darker where it had pooled. She held it by the corners and studied the places where the pigment had drifted. Nothing in it looked like the garden. Nothing in it looked like a person.

She went outside to the porch with the page, leaving the portfolio open on her blanket. Clarisse sat on the bottom step, unwrapping the leather from her spear. The night was clear now, though the stones still held the day's damp.

“Can’t sleep?” Clarisse asked.

“I can. I just want to see this in the lantern light.”

Clarisse looked at the page in Silena’s hands. “Do you want me to look?”

“Not yet.”

“Okay.”

Silena stood beside her for a moment, letting the page catch the warm light. The wash became almost gray. She had thought it would look empty. Instead, the empty part gave the blue somewhere to stop.

“Are you going to finish it?” Clarisse asked.

“Maybe.”

“Does it need finishing?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then maybe you can wait.”

Silena smiled. “That sounds familiar.”

“I can give good advice.”

“Sometimes.”

Clarisse wrapped the leather around the spear grip. “Do you want me to sit with you?”

“Not tonight.”

“Okay.”

No hurt crossed Clarisse’s face. She tightened the last turn and tucked the end beneath the wrap. Silena watched her hands work. Clarisse did not ask whether Silena had decided anything, and Silena did not tell her about the word she had chosen.

“I’m glad you asked,” Silena said.

“About sitting?”

“About looking.”

Clarisse nodded. “It’s your painting.”

“Yes.”

“And your time.”

“Yes.”

The answer was not a new rule between them. It was an ordinary truth they had both managed to remember.

Silena sat on the step above Clarisse and laid the page across her knees. She used the blunt end of her pencil to mark a second line beneath the wash, faint enough that the color still seemed to lead. It was not a shoreline. It did not point to a place or ask anyone to go there.

Clarisse glanced up. “Is that the finish?”

“No.”

“Is it part of it?”

“Yes.”

“Then why isn’t it finished?”

“Because I’m not finished looking at it.”

Clarisse considered this and went back to her spear. “Fair.”

Silena kept the pencil moving. She drew a few small, broken marks at the edge of the gray, then stopped before the page became crowded. The painting held both the place where the water gathered and the blank where it had not reached. She did not know what the blank meant. She did not need to decide.

When she finally lifted the page, the lantern light shifted and the pale line almost vanished. In daylight it would return. The painting could change without becoming a different story.

She held it a little longer, watching the colors soften at the edge. A pale bloom had dried near the bottom corner. She could cover it, but the paper had curled there. Instead, she left the mark alone, a small change the water had made while she was not watching.

Clarisse finished wrapping the spear and set it against the wall. “You’ll ruin that if you stay out here all night.”

“I won’t.”

“Do you need anything?”

“No.”

“All right.”

Clarisse went inside. Silena stayed on the step for one more breath, listening to the night wind pass through the pines. The word she had chosen remained private. The painting remained unfinished. Both could be true at the same time.

Silena carried the page back inside. She did not write a title on it. She slipped it into the folder beside the earlier wash, then put both pages away. The letter to Maria stayed in the drawer, folded the same way.

Tomorrow she might paint again. Tomorrow she might decide to show someone the page, or keep it. Tomorrow she might use the word she had chosen, or not. The choice was not waiting for her to prove anything.

She closed the portfolio and turned out the lamp.

The cabin grew quiet.
