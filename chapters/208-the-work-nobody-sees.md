## Chapter 208: The Work Nobody Sees

The gray wash had dried lighter than Silena expected. She lifted the practice page by its corners and held it toward the window, where the morning light showed the uneven edge of the water line. It was not wrong. It was not the shape she wanted.

She wrote “too pale at the base” on the back, then set the page beside the unfinished three-wave copy. The copy belonged in her portfolio, where she could decide what happened to it. It did not belong to the table just because people had begun to recognize the drawing.

A little space on the side of the page remained blank. She could paint the next blue there, or wait until she had enough light to try it in the afternoon. Silena shut the portfolio.

She was not going to spend the whole morning chasing one color.

Outside, the work list had been taken down and brought back to the lower shed. The storm repairs still sat beside the older jobs. The two coils were on the rack, dry at the surface and damp in the center. The canvas strip had passed the first check, but Beckendorf had not signed it off. The drainage board had not moved.

“Chiron said the ground might be ready to inspect tomorrow,” Percy said from the doorway.

Silena looked up. He carried a mug in both hands, one thumb pressed over the chip in the rim. Annabeth followed him with a small roll of paper tucked beneath her arm.

“Did he say it would be ready?” Silena asked.

“He said might be.”

“Then it’s not ready.”

“That’s what I told Leandros.”

“Did he listen?”

“He heard me.”

Annabeth put the paper on the shelf and took Percy’s mug away before he could spill it over her map. “You were walking and explaining at the same time.”

“I can do both.”

“You can walk. You can explain. We haven’t confirmed you can do both with hot tea.”

“Fair.”

She handed the mug back after he shifted his grip. Their fingers touched around the handle. Percy leaned briefly against her shoulder while he took a drink, and Annabeth let him, though she nudged him upright when he started to tip the mug.

Silena smiled and returned to the portfolio. They did not need her to witness every private thing between them; they had only brought her the news about the inspection.

“Beckendorf’s checking the canvas later,” Annabeth said. “He asked if you could bring him the inventory page.”

“I can.”

“Good. He has the old measurements too.”

Silena pulled the page from the portfolio. It had the three replacement tags listed in their correct order, with a note beside the light line and a time for the next drying check. She could take it over before lunch. She could also carry the portfolio if she wanted to work near the shed. She did not have to turn one errand into the other.

A knock came at the open door. Chiron stood outside with his cane angled carefully around the soft ground.

“Silena, do you have a moment?”

She tucked the practice sheet beneath the portfolio flap. “Yes.”

“Amphitrite asked if you would like to join her for some quiet time at the pavilion table later. She’ll be here through tomorrow morning.”

He said it as information, not an invitation she had to accept. That mattered. Silena put the inventory page on top of her portfolio.

“What time?”

“She said she would be there after lunch.”

“I’ll see how the work goes.”

“Of course.”

Chiron waited as though she might ask more. When she didn’t, he nodded and turned toward the lower shed. No one told her she should make time. No one said Amphitrite had come all this way for her.

Percy and Annabeth had already gone. Their voices moved down the path together, close but not joined at the hip. Percy said something that made Annabeth laugh; then she corrected his route.

Silena slid the portfolio into the bag, leaving the practice sheet inside. She put the inventory page under her arm and tied the bag shut.

She could join Amphitrite after the work. Or not. The afternoon had room for either answer.

---

The lower shed was warmer than the path, but only because the door had been pulled nearly shut. Beckendorf had unrolled the canvas strip across two sawhorses. The edge looked sound until Silena leaned closer. One corner had a run of loose stitches, dark with old rain.

“Is that from the storm?” she asked.

“Maybe,” Beckendorf said. He ran two fingers along the seam, not pulling at it. “Could have been starting before. The wet made it easier to see.”

Leandros stood beside the table with a short length of waxed thread. “I can close the seam.”

“After I mark the weak section,” Beckendorf said. “Then you can take the approved repair length and stitch through the existing holes. Don’t widen them. Don’t add a patch unless the fiber check says we need one.”

Leandros nodded. “I know how to mend canvas.”

“Good. Then you know to wait for me to finish marking it.”

Beckendorf turned to the inventory page. Silena gave it to him and pointed out the time she had recorded for the coils. He checked the tag order and wrote a note for the next drying inspection.

The conversation at the table took less than a minute. Beckendorf had work to do elsewhere, so he set the canvas aside and went to speak with Chiron. Leandros stayed at the bench. He did not unroll the thread or move the canvas.

Silena could have taken the page back with her, but Beckendorf had asked her to keep the inventory together until he added the check. She stood near the door and waited.

“You have a minute?” Leandros asked.

“For what?”

“I need to tell Clarisse something.”

“Then tell her.”

“She’s been with Chiron all morning.”

“She’ll be back.”

“I don’t want to wait until the next time she’s by herself.”

“Why not?”

“Because I don’t want to have this conversation in front of everyone.”

“Then ask her when she has a minute.”

He tapped the thread against the table. “Can you tell her I’m going to work on the canvas, and that I’m not trying to get out of the board inspection?”

Silena looked at him. “No.”

“It’s not a complicated message.”

“It’s still yours to give her.”

“She knows I’m supposed to wait for Chiron.”

“Then she doesn’t need me to tell her that.”

“I asked you because you talk to her.”

“I talk to her because she’s my sister. I’m not a path you can use to get around her.”

“I’m not trying to get around her.”

“You want me to tell her something so you don’t have to.”

“That is not what I said.”

“It’s what you asked.”

He lowered the thread. “You make everything sound like I’m using you.”

“I’m telling you how it feels.”

“I thought you liked helping.”

“I do like helping. I don’t like when you decide helping means I should carry words you don’t want to say.”

His face tightened. “You always know how to make it sound simple.”

“It isn’t simple.”

“I have been here a week, and every time I try to do something, I’m told to wait, ask, or leave it alone.”

“Sometimes that’s the right answer.”

“You don’t have to say it like I’m stupid.”

“I didn’t.”

“You didn’t have to.”

She felt the familiar pull to soften her voice, to reassure him that he was capable, that nobody thought he was stupid. It would have been true. It also would not answer the question he had asked. Silena let the urge pass.

“I won’t tell Clarisse for you,” she said. “You can wait until she has time. You can tell her yourself. You can choose not to say it. I’m not going to decide which one.”

Leandros looked down at the canvas. “Fine.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s what I have.”

“Then keep it.”

He picked up the thread and laid it across the marked seam, but he did not start stitching. He waited until Beckendorf returned and pointed to the exact places where the fabric needed to hold.

Silena took the inventory page to the wall shelf. When she turned back, Leandros had put the thread through the first two holes and pulled it snug. His hands were steady. He did not look toward her.

---

Clarisse was outside the shed, where the gravel path split toward the kitchen and the fence. She had the work list folded in her pocket and a pencil behind one ear. Percy had passed her on his way to the pavilion, but now she was alone.

“Did he ask you to say something to me?” Clarisse asked.

Silena set the inventory page back in the box. “He wanted me to tell you he was starting the canvas seam.”

“And?”

“I said no.”

Clarisse shifted the pencil. “Good.”

“He asked me not to make it sound like he was using me.”

“Was he?”

“He wanted me to carry the message because he didn’t want to wait for you. He also wanted me to tell you he wasn’t trying to get out of the board inspection.”

Clarisse looked toward the fence. “I know he’s waiting for the inspection.”

“I thought so.”

“Then why does he keep asking?”

“Because he wants you to tell him something he likes.”

Clarisse gave a short, humorless laugh. “That part I didn’t know.”

Silena leaned against the dry side of the shed. “He asked me last night too.”

“To carry another message?”

“To tell you he understood what you said.”

“I’m glad you didn’t.”

“I didn’t.”

Clarisse rolled the pencil between her fingers. “I’m not mad at you.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Silena watched her sister’s hand move the pencil back and forth. Clarisse was good at making a question sound like a challenge, even when she meant it honestly.

“I know you want to decide what you say to him yourself,” Silena said. “I also know I don’t want to be the only person who can make everyone talk.”

Clarisse set the pencil down on the wall. “I do that to you sometimes.”

“Sometimes.”

“I thought you were good at it.”

“I am good at it.”

“That’s not the same as wanting to do it.”

“No.”

Clarisse nodded. She did not apologize for something Silena had not asked her to apologize for. “I don’t want you carrying my answer for him.”

“Good.”

“And I don’t want him thinking you can tell him whether I’m going to stay angry.”

“He already asked.”

“What did you say?”

“That I don’t know what you want. I don’t get to decide.”

“Right answer.”

“It’s the only one I have.”

Clarisse picked the pencil up again. “You going to see Amphitrite?”

“After the inventory.”

“You don’t have to go because Chiron asked.”

“Chiron told me the time. He didn’t ask me to go.”

“Good.”

Silena waited. Clarisse might have wanted to say more. She did not.

“You could come,” Silena said. “If you want.”

“I don’t.”

“Okay.”

“I’m not asking you to stay here.”

“I know.”

“I might not be ready to spend the afternoon with her.”

Silena nodded. The answer did not hurt. It did not have to mean anything about her own choice.

“I can go by myself,” she said.

“Do.”

Clarisse stepped closer and pulled the inventory page from the box. “You left the time blank.”

“Beckendorf is checking it after lunch.”

“You should write that.”

“I didn’t want to guess.”

“Then write ‘after lunch, pending Beckendorf.’”

Silena took the pencil and wrote it exactly. The mark fit beside the three tags.

“Better?” she asked.

“Clearer.”

Clarisse handed the page back. Her fingers brushed Silena’s wrist, brief and ordinary. “You coming to the pavilion after?”

“I’ll see.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It’s what I have.”

This time Clarisse smiled, just a little. “Fine.”

They walked together toward the kitchen, matching pace until the path split. Clarisse went toward the cabin. Silena went back inside for her portfolio. Neither of them tried to turn the afternoon into a test of which sister cared more.

---

After lunch, Silena went to the pavilion table with her portfolio under one arm. The floorboards had dried near the windows, though the outside steps were still slick. Amphitrite sat at the far end with a cup of tea and a small stack of letters. She had not placed anything on Silena’s side of the table.

“Can I sit here?” Silena asked.

Amphitrite looked up. “Yes. I’m glad you came.”

Silena set her portfolio down, then pulled the chair out. “I said I’d see how the work went.”

“You did.”

There was no disappointment in her voice. Silena opened the portfolio and took out the pale gray wash. “I wanted to try this one in a different light.”

“Would you like me to move the cup?”

“No, it’s fine.”

“Would you like company, or would you like me to look?”

Silena smiled. “Company first.”

Amphitrite nodded and returned to her letters. She did not turn them over so Silena could read the addresses. Silena dipped her brush and let it rest against the water jar until the bristles had softened.

She had meant to return to the three-wave copy, but the page seemed too important to practice on. She set it aside and drew a fresh row of three narrow rectangles. One would hold a gray wash, one a darker blue, and the last one she left blank. The blank space was not a mistake. She wanted to keep it for a color she had not chosen yet.

Amphitrite folded the letter she had been reading and kept it in her hand. “Are you working on the same sample from last time?”

“Some of it. I still have the strip you painted.”

“Do you want me to see it?”

“Not today.”

“Okay.”

Silena expected a question about whether the color was still useful or whether she had copied it. Amphitrite only put the folded letter beside her cup and returned to the page in front of her.

The quiet made it easier to paint. Silena tested the gray in the first rectangle, then changed the angle of the paper so the water ran toward the lower edge. She wanted the color to gather there, like a tide line. The gray spread too evenly.

She blotted the edge with a cloth.

“Do you want a second opinion?” Amphitrite asked.

“Not yet.”

“Then I’ll keep reading.”

Silena painted another line. It spread farther than the first. She lifted the brush and waited, keeping both hands on her own side of the table. The wash dried with a narrow darker mark at the bottom.

“That’s closer,” she said.

Amphitrite did not look up. “Good.”

Silena added the note to the back of the sheet: “angled wash; leave more water at lower edge.” She let the page rest. The work had taken only a few minutes, but she had changed her mind twice and kept the blank rectangle blank.

She drew the three-wave copy toward her. The top line bent too sharply. The lower line was softer. The middle wave had a space where she had not decided whether to put a highlight.

“You said you wanted company,” Amphitrite said. “May I ask about this one?”

“Yes.”

“Is it part of the copy you were making with your siblings?”

“It’s a copy of the drawing we made near the tide pools.” Silena touched the paper’s edge, not the blue. “I’m still deciding what I want the colors to do.”

“It looks like a careful decision.”

“It’s not done.”

“I didn’t say it was.”

Silena looked at her. Amphitrite’s voice held no suggestion that she ought to finish it or give it to someone. She had not called the sketch a gift, or asked whether Percy was waiting for it.

“I might keep it,” Silena said.

“You may.”

The simple answer made her shoulders ease. Silena leaned forward again and placed a thin stroke in the empty middle wave. The blue was darker than the other two. It made the three shapes look less like one image and more like separate pieces.

She set the brush down.

“I don't like that.”

“Do you want to change it?”

“I’m not sure.”

“Then you can leave it until you are.”

Silena glanced at the practice strip she had given Amphitrite in the earlier visit. The strip was nowhere on the table. “Did you keep the one I gave you?”

“Yes. I put it between two sheets so it wouldn’t bend.”

“Can I see it?”

“Of course.”

Amphitrite opened a small folder in her bag and showed Silena the paper, flat and dry. The gray-blue looked a little different in the pavilion light. The dark ring had held at the center.

Silena did not ask for it back. She did not ask where it would go. It had been a practice piece, not a promise. She traced the air above it without touching the paint.

“Thank you for keeping it flat.”

“I remember that you asked me to.”

Silena closed the folder and handed it back. “Could I have your thought on the middle wave now?”

Amphitrite took a moment to look. “I might make the edge lighter so the dark stroke stays inside the shape.”

Silena stared at the drawing. She imagined the brush moving along the edge. “I can try that on the scrap first.”

“That sounds sensible.”

She made a small test on the spare piece. The dark blue softened when she pulled a little water across it. On the main page, she used less pigment and more space. The middle wave still looked different from the other two, but it no longer pulled the eye away.

“Better,” she said.

“Do you like it?”

“More.”

“That’s a good answer.”

They returned to their separate work. Amphitrite read two letters. Silena painted another strip and recorded how long it took to dry. Once, she asked Amphitrite to hold the page steady while she tested a clean stroke. Amphitrite put two fingers on the far corner and kept her hand there until Silena lifted the brush.

When the line was finished, Silena reached for the paper herself. Amphitrite released it at once.

Silena washed the brush and set it across the jar. Amphitrite had reached the last page of her letters. She folded each one back into its envelope and lined them up in the same order they had been stacked.

“You have a system,” Silena said.

“I do.”

“Is it important?”

“Yes.”

“Then I’ll leave them alone.”

Amphitrite smiled. “Thank you.”

Silena looked at the portfolio. There was enough light for the three-wave copy now. The middle line still felt too dark, but she could not fix it by staring.

“Leandros asked me to carry another message to Clarisse,” she said.

Amphitrite’s hand paused over the envelopes. “Would you like me to speak with him?”

“No.”

“Would you like me to speak with Clarisse?”

“No.”

“Would you like me to say nothing?”

Silena laughed softly. “Yes.”

“Then I will.”

She had not expected Amphitrite to ask three different questions. In the past, Silena might have told someone what they wanted to hear and then spent the rest of the day arranging the next conversation. It could feel easier to be the person who knew how to get everyone through a meal without anyone leaving. The easier choice did not mean she wanted it every time.

“He thinks I can tell him what she wants,” Silena said. “And sometimes I can guess. That doesn’t make it mine to say.”

“No.”

“I don’t want to punish him by refusing. I just don’t want him to use my answer as if it came from Clarisse.”

“That distinction matters.”

“I know.”

“Would you like me to remember it?”

Silena looked at her. “Yes.”

Amphitrite nodded. “I’ll remember.”

She did not say that Leandros should understand. She did not say Clarisse should hear him out. She did not tell Silena that her kindness was a gift or that she should keep being the bridge. She picked up the first envelope and placed it in her bag.

Silena returned to the page. She tested a pale blue along the edge of the middle wave and waited for it to settle.

“Do you want the same table tomorrow?” Amphitrite asked. “I can be here after breakfast, if you want.”

“I don’t know what I’ll be working on.”

“That’s all right.”

“Maybe I’ll bring the gray page.”

“I’d like that.”

Silena left the blue to dry. The offer did not become a promise she had to keep. She could decide again tomorrow.

---

The next morning, Silena came back to the shed with the gray page in her portfolio. She had not decided whether to paint on it or keep it as a record of the first attempt.

Leandros was at the canvas bench. Beckendorf stood across from him, holding one end of the strip flat. The thread crossed the seam in short, even stitches.

“Looser,” Beckendorf said.

Leandros stopped. “It’s not pulling the canvas.”

“It is at the corner.”

He leaned closer. One section had puckered where the seam curved. The rest was straight.

“I can take it out.”

“Take out the last three stitches, not the whole row. Keep the edge flat as you come around.”

Leandros set down the needle and unpicked the stitches with a thin awl. It took longer than making them had. He did not complain. When he tried again, the third stitch drew the cloth tighter.

“Ease off,” Beckendorf said.

“I did.”

“You’re still pulling.”

Leandros looked up. “The fabric’s uneven.”

“Then match the uneven fabric. Don’t force it into a line that isn’t there.”

For a second, Leandros held the thread stretched between his hands. Then he let it slacken. He took out the last stitch and started again.

Silena placed the inventory sheet on the shelf. “The coil check is after lunch.”

“I know,” Leandros said without looking up.

“You asked yesterday.”

“I’m not asking now.”

She nodded. “Okay.”

Beckendorf let go of his side of the canvas and walked to the tool cabinet. Leandros kept the strip flat by himself. There was no one to watch him except Silena, and he did not look up to see whether she was impressed. He counted the holes in the seam under his breath, then threaded the needle through the next one.

Silena set her bag on the shelf and checked the tags. The labels were still in the right order. The ink had dried. The new board edge was smooth beneath her thumb.

“You can go,” Leandros said.

“I’m checking the tags.”

“You’ve checked them.”

“Then I’m checking them again.”

He looked at her. “You don’t need to stay because I’m here.”

“I didn’t say I did.”

“Fine.”

The word came out with more edge than the one she had given him yesterday. He lowered his eyes to the canvas again.

Silena checked the light-line tag against the inventory and found it still matched. She checked the two coils by touch, careful not to disturb the spacer. The outer turns were cool and nearly dry. The center held damp.

When she was done, she picked up her portfolio. The gray practice page was still tucked under the flap.

“Your seam’s pulling,” she said.

Leandros stopped. He looked toward the corner.

“Not the new one,” she said. “The one beside it.”

He checked it with his fingers. “It’s fine.”

“Beckendorf said to match the fabric.”

“I know what he said.”

She left the shed without arguing. Behind her, the needle moved through the cloth again.

---

Silena returned to the pavilion after the inventory check. The table was clear except for the folded page from her portfolio and the jar of water she had brought from the cabin. She meant to paint one last test before the afternoon light shifted.

Percy and Annabeth came back with a bowl of apples from the kitchen. Percy set it at the far end, away from the paint.

“Is that the drawing you made with Clarisse?” he asked.

“It’s the copy.”

“Can I see?”

Silena turned the portfolio toward him. “You can look. Don’t touch the blue.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

Annabeth glanced at his fingers. A faint gray smudge crossed his knuckle. “You say that every time.”

“I know where my hands are.”

“Not always.”

He lifted both hands, palms out. Annabeth leaned over to examine the drawing, keeping the bowl of apples between herself and the wet jar. She pointed to the blank space under the third wave.

“Are you leaving that empty?”

“I haven’t decided.”

“It could be a place for the date.”

“Then it would stop being empty.”

Annabeth gave her a small nod. “Fair.”

Percy took a step back. “I like it.”

“Which part?”

“The middle wave. It looks like it’s about to turn over.”

“That’s the part I wasn’t sure about.”

“Keep it.”

Silena lifted an eyebrow. “That’s not the same as deciding.”

“I know. It’s just my vote.”

Annabeth rested her hand on the back of Percy’s chair. “You don’t have to finish it today.”

“Everyone keeps telling me that,” Silena said.

“Because you keep trying to finish it today,” Percy answered.

She laughed. “I didn’t know I was that obvious.”

“You made three versions of the same wave yesterday.”

“They were different.”

“They were,” Annabeth said. “The second one had a stronger lower curve.”

Silena looked at her. “You remember the shapes?”

“I remember what I looked at.”

The answer pleased her. It was not a judgment about what the drawing should become. She kept the paper open and set a narrow scrap beside it.

“Would either of you hold this?” she asked. “I want to see if the darker line reads better when the sheet is tilted.”

Percy picked up the scrap, but Annabeth reached over and took it from him. “You’re holding it too close to the wet edge.”

“I can hold a piece of paper.”

“You can. I’m holding it now.”

She angled it until the late light caught the blue. Silena watched the color gather at the bottom, then loosen at the corner. It looked more like a wave when the shadow crossed it.

“That’s better,” Silena said.

Annabeth lowered the page. Percy leaned in and kissed her temple, quick and familiar. She rested her shoulder against his for a beat, then returned her attention to the paper.

Silena did not have to make their affection part of her drawing. She let them stand beside her, each with an apple, while she adjusted the wash.

The pavilion door swung open. Leandros entered carrying a small tin of waxed thread. He paused when he saw the art supplies.

“Are you making another set of tags?” he asked.

“No,” Silena said. “I’m painting.”

“It looks like the same wave on three papers.”

“Some of them are practice strips.”

“You could make the middle one clearer.”

“I’m working on it.”

“It’s too dark at the base.”

Silena set down the brush. “I didn’t ask for notes.”

“I was only saying what I saw.”

“I know what you saw. I’m deciding what to do.”

He looked at the page again. “It would be easier to see if you made all three waves the same shade.”

“I don’t want them the same.”

“Then why ask for opinions?”

“I asked Percy and Annabeth to hold the paper. I didn’t ask you to redesign it.”

Leandros lifted the thread. “I’m not trying to run your project.”

“You don’t have to mean to. You’re still telling me what the answer should be.”

His mouth flattened. “I was trying to help.”

“Then ask if I want help.”

He set the tin on the edge of the table. “Do you want it?”

“No.”

“All right.”

He moved away before she could decide whether he had accepted the answer or only stopped talking. Percy looked at the thread tin, then at the doorway.

“Do you want me to bring that back to him?” he asked.

“No. He can remember it.”

Annabeth turned the paper back to Silena. “Do you want the light this way or from the side?”

“From the side.”

Annabeth shifted the scrap. Percy picked up the apples. They stayed long enough for Silena to finish one more wash, then left together with the bowl.

---

Beckendorf had gone to check the other rack when Silena returned to the shed. The canvas lay flat, with the seam mended from the first marked hole to the curved corner. Leandros had clipped the loose thread but left the cloth where it was.

“Not ready to use yet,” he said. “Beckendorf needs to check it.”

“I know.”

He looked at the tin of thread. “I thought you’d tell Clarisse.”

“Tell her what?”

“That I fixed the seam.”

“I didn’t say I would.”

“I thought she might want to know.”

“Then tell her.”

Leandros’s brow furrowed. “You don’t have to make everything difficult.”

“I’m not making it difficult. I’m not your messenger.”

“I’m not asking you to carry an apology.”

“You’re still asking me to carry something.”

He picked up the thread tin, then set it down again. “I’m not going to ask twice.”

“Good.”

Clarisse entered from the yard with wet gravel clinging to the bottom of her boots. She had a small notebook tucked beneath one arm. Her eyes went to the canvas, but she did not step closer.

“Is it ready?” she asked.

“Beckendorf has to check it,” Leandros replied.

“Then it’s not ready.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

She turned to Silena. “Did Chiron leave the inspection note?”

“On the desk by the map.”

Clarisse went past them. She did not look back at the seam. Leandros watched her disappear into the passage.

“I did what I was supposed to do,” he said.

“Beckendorf hasn’t checked it.”

“I mean I waited. I followed the directions.”

“That’s what you were supposed to do.”

“I’m not asking for a medal.”

“No one said you were.”

He looked at her as if her answer had ignored something important. “I thought she might at least acknowledge I’m trying.”

Silena put her portfolio on the shelf. “She might. She might not. The work still needs checking.”

“That is all anyone can say.”

“It’s what I can say.”

“Can’t you tell me what she thinks?”

“No.”

“Not even a guess?”

“No.”

He pressed his lips together, then moved the thread tin to the cabinet. He did not slam it or leave. His hands were careful when he set it down, as if the small action could be done correctly even when the conversation could not.

Clarisse returned with the inspection note. She read it once and placed it beside the canvas. “Beckendorf wants to see the seam before he checks the coils.”

Leandros stepped away from the bench. “It’s here.”

“Then leave it flat until he gets back.”

“I wasn’t going to move it.”

“I didn’t say you were.”

“You didn’t have to.”

Clarisse stopped. “I’m not going to pretend yesterday didn’t happen because you fixed one seam.”

“I didn’t ask you to.”

“You asked if we were done.”

“That was before.”

“It was yesterday.”

He looked at the canvas. “I’m allowed to do something right without it being used against me.”

Clarisse shifted the note under her hand. “I’m not using it against you. I’m waiting for the person who knows what the repair needs to check it.”

“I know how to sew canvas.”

“Beckendorf still has to check it.”

He looked at her, then nodded. “Fine.”

Clarisse picked up the inventory page. She made a mark beside the canvas: “repair completed; inspection pending.” The list remained open. She did not write Leandros’s name beside it.

He saw that, but he did not ask her to.

---

At supper, Clarisse sat with the work list folded beside her bowl. The paper had a clean crease down the middle where she had tucked it into her pocket. The canvas seam was still marked “inspection pending.”

Silena had brought the portfolio back to Cabin Three. She set it on the shelf above her bunk, where the paint would stay away from the soup.

Leandros came in after the others. He carried no tools, but the tin of thread had left a faint waxy smell on his fingers. He stopped at the table and looked at the empty place between Silena and Percy.

“Is anyone sitting there?” he asked.

“No,” Percy said.

Leandros sat.

Clarisse moved the work list farther from the bowl. “That can stay there. It’s damp.”

“I wasn’t going to move it.”

“I know.”

The words did not soften the room. They did not make it worse, either. Clarisse broke a piece of bread and dipped it into her soup. Silena tasted the broth and added a little water to her own bowl; it was too salty for her.

Leandros tried his. “It’s fine.”

“You can say you like it,” Silena said.

“I like it.”

“Good.”

They ate. The table’s uneven leg tapped the floor whenever Percy leaned his elbow down. Annabeth, who had come by to return the rolled paper from earlier, wedged a folded napkin under it. Percy tested the table again.

“Better?” she asked.

“Almost.”

“Is that your final inspection?”

“For now.”

She rolled her eyes. He smiled and reached for the bread basket. Their hands met above it. Annabeth let him take the slice, then rested her palm on his shoulder while she leaned across him for her own. Percy turned his head and kissed her knuckles before she sat back. Nobody at the table had to stop eating to make room for the moment.

Silena kept her attention on her soup. Percy and Annabeth were like that: comfortable with one another, not asking the room to approve it.

Leandros looked from them to Clarisse. “I know the canvas still needs checking.”

“Beckendorf will check it.”

“I know.”

“Then we don’t need to discuss it.”

He set the spoon down. “I was going to say I’m not asking you to praise me for the seam.”

Clarisse held his gaze. “I didn’t.”

“I know.”

“Then why say it?”

He rubbed his thumb along the side of his bowl. “Because I keep thinking you’re waiting for me to fail again.”

“I’m waiting for Beckendorf to check the canvas.”

“That’s not what I mean.”

“I know.”

“Do you think I’m trying to buy forgiveness?”

Clarisse put her bread down. “I think you want an answer today.”

“And you don’t have one.”

“I have one. I’m angry.”

“That’s all?”

“That’s what I have right now.”

He glanced at Silena, then stopped himself from asking her. “I’m trying to do the work.”

“Good.”

“It doesn’t change anything?”

“It changes the work.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“You asked if I’m done being angry. I’m not.”

He leaned back. “I don’t know how long you expect me to keep waiting.”

“I’m not setting a schedule.”

“Everything has a schedule here.”

“Not this.”

“Then I’m supposed to just keep going without knowing?”

“Yes.”

The answer landed harder than Clarisse seemed to intend. Her fingers curled once around the spoon. She looked down and took another bite, not taking the sentence back.

Leandros studied the table. “I can do that.”

Clarisse did not nod or thank him. “Then do.”

He went back to eating. Silena did not turn the silence into a softer subject. She did not need to find a second conversation for them.

---

In the morning, Silena took the gray page back to the pavilion. The outer edge had dried in a thin line. She could still see the spot where she had blotted the water too early.

Amphitrite was there, standing by the window with her cup untouched. She turned when Silena entered but did not come toward her.

“Good morning,” she said.

“Morning.”

“Would you like to paint?”

“I brought the page.”

Amphitrite smiled, then waited.

Silena sat at the same end of the table. She set the portfolio beside her and took out the gray page, the three-wave copy, and the blank strip she had saved the day before. “I’m not sure what I want to do with the lower edge.”

“Would you like my opinion?”

“Not yet.”

“All right.”

Silena mixed a small amount of blue into the gray and tested it on the blank strip. The color darkened too quickly. She rinsed the brush, took a smaller amount, and tried again. This time the blue only cooled the gray, keeping it from turning brown.

She wrote the mixture on the back of the strip and set it beside the drawing.

“Could I ask you something?” Amphitrite said.

“Sure.”

“Would you like me to keep the practice strip from yesterday, or would you rather have it back?”

Silena looked up. “You can keep it.”

“Thank you.”

“I don’t need it anymore.”

“Then I’ll keep it flat.”

Silena made one small mark on the gray page, a line that followed the curve instead of closing it. The blank space under the third wave still remained blank. It had stopped feeling like a missing piece.

“Do you want to leave that open?” Amphitrite asked.

“Yes.”

“Then I won’t suggest a date.”

Silena smiled. She had not expected that to be the question. “Thanks.”

“You were clear about the space.”

“Sometimes people think they’re helping by finishing it for me.”

“I understand that.”

“Do you?”

“I have done it.”

The honesty sat between them without demanding an answer. Silena ran a clean brush along the edge of the new wash. The line softened. It was not the same as the older practice strip, and she did not need it to be.

Amphitrite glanced at the clock above the pavilion door. “Chiron said you might be going to the shed after this.”

“I am.”

“Would you like me to walk with you?”

“No. I need to stop at Cabin Three first.”

“Then I’ll stay here.”

She did not ask Silena to take her with her or explain the visit’s schedule. Silena packed the pages in their order and closed the portfolio.

“I’ll see you later,” she said.

“If you want to.”

“I do.”

She stood. Amphitrite did not reach for the bag or offer to carry it. Silena left with the portfolio tucked under her arm. The choice of it was hers to make, and she did not feel like she had to make it quickly.

---

The lower shed was quiet when Silena returned. The canvas was folded lengthwise, but the seam sat open along the bench. Leandros had clipped the extra thread and tied the end beneath the hem. Beckendorf checked the whole length with the back of his hand, then turned it over to inspect the opposite side.

Silena set the inventory page beside him.

“The line is still drying,” Beckendorf said. “The canvas can go back on the rack after I finish.”

“Did it hold?” Leandros asked.

Beckendorf pulled the seam gently at the repaired corner. The stitches stayed flat. He ran the same check farther along, where the cloth had no new thread.

“It holds.”

Leandros nodded. He did not look toward Silena or Clarisse, who had come in behind her.

“The corner isn’t as straight as the others,” Beckendorf added. “That’s cosmetic. It will take the strain.”

“I can even it out.”

“Not if you have to pull the seam tighter to do it.”

Leandros glanced at the line of stitches. “I can leave it.”

“Good.”

Beckendorf wrote “fiber check passed; seam repaired” beside the canvas entry. The pencil moved down the page to the damp coils and stopped.

“Those still need another check at lunch,” he said.

“Could finish today,” Leandros replied.

“If the cores are dry.”

“They should be by then.”

“We’ll see.”

Leandros’s hands curled around the edge of the bench. “And the board?”

“Still waiting for the ground.”

“I can set the brace before we shift it.”

“Not until Chiron and I inspect the footing.”

“I could make the brace.”

“You can ask Chiron if there’s prep work. You can’t decide it yourself.”

Leandros drew a breath, then turned toward Clarisse. His mouth opened. Silena saw the question forming before he spoke: would she tell Chiron, would she say he was ready, would she translate the answer after.

He looked at her. Then he looked back at Clarisse.

“Can I ask Chiron whether I can cut the brace lumber before the inspection?” he said.

Clarisse had not taken the pencil from Beckendorf. She folded her arms. “Ask him.”

“Will you be there?”

“If I’m needed.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the answer I have.”

He nodded once and left the shed. His boots crossed the gravel carefully. Nobody called after him.

Beckendorf slid the canvas into a dry crate. “You can return that to the rack after lunch.”

“I’ll mark it,” Silena said.

Clarisse read the line he had written. “Does it need another check?”

“Not unless the seam takes a load.”

“Then it stays off the rack until the next time we need the cover.”

Beckendorf agreed. The canvas was repaired, but there was no reason to put it under strain before it was needed. The work list stayed open for the coils and the fence.

Silena wrote the condition in the margin: “mended; store dry; inspect before use.” The words fit the space.



The path from the shed to Cabin Three was slick where the pine needles had gathered. Silena walked slowly enough to keep the inventory page from bending in the wind. She had finished the work she agreed to do. The rest could wait for Beckendorf's next check.

Leandros was at the cabin steps when she arrived, sitting with a narrow board balanced across his knees. He held a pencil against one edge and kept measuring the same line.

“Chiron said I can cut the brace pieces once Beckendorf confirms the measurements,” he said. “I have to bring it back before I touch the ground.”

“That sounds like a clear answer.”

“It’s half an answer.”

“It’s the half he gave you.”

“He could have told me whether the board will be ready tomorrow.”

“He doesn’t know.”

Leandros lowered the pencil. “You all say that like not knowing is useful.”

“Sometimes it is.”

“It leaves everything open.”

Silena let the paper rest on her lap. “You keep asking people to close things before they’re ready.”

His eyes narrowed. “I’m asking what’s happening.”

“You ask if the work will be finished. Then you ask if Clarisse is done being angry. Then you ask me to explain what she means.”

“I asked you to carry a message.”

“You asked me because you wanted to know what she would say before you had to ask her.”

“That isn’t the same thing.”

“It felt the same to me.”

He looked toward the shed. “I apologized.”

“You did.”

“And you heard me.”

“Yes.”

“So what else am I supposed to do?”

“Ask Clarisse what she thinks. Ask me what I think. Wait for answers that belong to each of us.”

“I did ask you what you think.”

“Not when you wanted to know what Clarisse thought.”

“I thought you might understand her better.”

“I might. I’m not going to package her anger for you.”

The pencil rolled against his knee. He caught it before it fell.

“You’re good at talking,” he said. “I figured you could make it simpler.”

“That’s the problem.”

“What?”

“You decided that being good at talking means I’m here to do it for everyone.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You didn’t have to.”

Silena set the inventory page beside her. There was a loose corner near the fold. She smoothed it down with one finger. The crease had started before the paper got wet; the storm had only made it visible.

Leandros studied her face as if he were waiting for the rest of the explanation. She did not add one.

“I didn’t mean to make you do extra work,” he said.

“You made extra work. I already told you.”

“I apologized for that.”

“I heard it.”

“And now you’re saying the same thing again.”

“I’m saying I heard you. That doesn’t mean I’m finished being angry.”

His mouth tightened. “How long?”

Silena looked up. “That’s another question you don’t get to set for me.”

“I’m trying to understand the rules.”

“There isn’t a rule that makes my anger expire on a schedule.”

“Clarisse said that too.”

“Then you’ve heard it from both of us.”

He looked away. The board rested across his knees, cleanly marked and still uncut. “You want me to stay?”

“That’s not the question you asked.”

“Do you?”

Silena thought of the blurred tags and the extra inventory. Of how quickly he had decided what work mattered, and how easily he had asked her to repair the space between him and Clarisse. Of the bread she had made for herself, which he had tried only after asking.

“I want you to stop acting like every answer is a test of whether we want you here,” she said. “Sometimes I’m just telling you what I think.”

“That doesn’t answer.”

“It’s the answer I have.”

He tapped the pencil against the board once and stopped. “So I can’t ask?”

“You can ask. I can answer. I can also say I don’t want to explain Clarisse. That’s not the same as telling you to leave.”

He held her gaze. “I don’t know what the difference is yet.”

“Then you’ll have to learn it.”

For a second, she thought he might argue. Instead he turned the board over and checked the other side. The mark lined up with the grain.

“I’ll ask her,” he said.

“You should.”

“I did.”

“Then listen to what she said.”

He nodded, but it was a tight, impatient motion. Silena did not mistake it for agreement. She gathered the inventory page and carried it inside. The conversation had not settled anything. It had made one boundary visible, which was its own kind of work.



Silena left the page on the table and went out to wash the paint from her hands. By the time she came back, the light had shifted from the window and the gray looked warmer than it had that morning.

Amphitrite stood just inside the doorway with a small cup of tea. She did not step toward the painting.

“Chiron said you might still be working,” she said. “Would you like company?”

“Maybe. I want to finish this edge first.”

“Then I can wait.”

“You can sit down. Just don’t look over my shoulder yet.”

Amphitrite took the chair nearest the window and turned it slightly toward the garden. She did not ask what Silena was painting or how long she thought it would take. The quiet held.

Silena dipped her brush into the water and drew a thin blue curve where the gray met the empty paper. The color spread farther than she wanted. She lifted the brush. A bead of pigment slid toward the lower edge.

She pressed a clean cloth against the corner before it ran.

“Do you want the paper held?” Amphitrite asked.

“No, I have it.”

“Okay.”

The answer came without another offer. Silena turned the page so its wet corner faced the open window. The blue began to dry in a narrow line. She let it.

“Do you want to see it now?” she asked.

Amphitrite shifted her chair back toward the table. “If you do.”

Silena lifted the page and placed it between them. “It’s a study. I haven’t decided what I’ll do with the middle wave.”

Amphitrite looked where she pointed, not at the rest of the portfolio. “The middle line changes direction.”

“I wanted to see if it could turn without closing.”

“It can.”

“That’s all I know so far.”

“Then I’ll stop there.”

Silena glanced at her. “You don’t have to stop talking.”

“I don’t have to finish the thought for you, either.”

She touched the lower corner of the page, then lifted her hand before it reached the paper. “May I?”

Silena thought about it. “Yes. Just hold the edge.”

Amphitrite took the corner carefully. The paper no longer curled. Silena adjusted the board beneath it and brushed one more line into the wash.

“You kept the first strip I gave you,” Silena said.

“I did.”

“Where is it?”

“In my room. Between two pages so it stays flat.”

“You didn’t paint on it?”

“No.”

“Or write anything?”

“No.”

Silena nodded. That answer mattered more than a compliment would have. The strip was still the thing she had given, not a space Amphitrite had filled in for her.

She worked in silence for several minutes. Then she set the brush down. “Sometimes I want you to ask me about the painting.”

“I can do that.”

“Not every time. If I don’t bring it over, I may not want to talk about it.”

“I can ask whether you want to talk.”

Silena considered the difference. “You can ask. I can say not today.”

“Yes.”

“And you won’t look hurt?”

“I might feel disappointed. I can take care of that without making you responsible for it.”

Silena studied her face. Amphitrite did not sound as if she had rehearsed the sentence. She sounded as if she knew she could fail at it.

“I don’t know you very well yet,” Silena said.

“I know.”

“But I like when you sit here.”

Amphitrite’s smile was small. “I like sitting here too.”

Silena did not reach for the word that might have made the moment easier to name. There was no need to call a beginning finished.

She gathered the test strips and sorted them into the portfolio. The page with the crooked blue edge went in the front pocket so it could dry overnight.

“Will you be here in the morning?” she asked.

“Until after breakfast.”

“Maybe I’ll show you the page when it’s dry.”

“Maybe,” Amphitrite said. “I’ll be here if you want to.”

The answer left the choice where it belonged. Silena tied the portfolio shut and carried it back to her cabin.

---

The next morning the lower shed smelled of wet hemp and cold wood. Silena came with the inventory ledger and a small bag of clean tags. She had agreed to check the written list, not to make decisions about where the coils went.

Beckendorf was at the rack, testing the air against the center of one coil. Leandros stood by the open tool box, sorting the needles and cord into separate piles.

“Still damp?” Silena asked.

“Center is,” Beckendorf said. “Outer line’s ready to use. We’ll check again this afternoon.”

Silena wrote the time in the ledger. The two coils remained on their spacers.

One canvas loop on the side of the drying rack had stretched until its knot slipped each time the wind tugged the cover. It was separate from the storm-damaged strip; the rack still held, but the loose loop meant the cover had to be tied to the post. Beckendorf pointed it out to Leandros.

“Don’t use that loop to hold the canvas,” he said. “It’ll pull loose. We can replace it once the coils are clear.”

Leandros pinched the frayed end between two fingers. “I can put in a new splice.”

“After lunch. We’ll take the weight off it first.”

“Could be done now.”

“It could. We’re not moving the coils until the core dries.”

“I’m not touching the coils.”

“You’re reaching across the rack. Wait until I’m here to unload the cover.”

Leandros let go. The loop fell against the post.

Silena finished checking the written order of the tags. She added no note about the loop because Beckendorf had already marked it in his maintenance book. Her work was done, but she stayed long enough to make sure the ink on the new tags had dried before returning them to their case.

When she stepped out, Leandros was still sorting the cord. He had not asked her to tell Clarisse about the extra repair. He had not asked whether she thought it counted.

She carried the empty tag case to Cabin Three. Near the porch, Clarisse was tightening the temporary cord on the window blanket.

“Beckendorf says the canvas seam passed its check,” Silena said. “He’s storing it dry until it’s needed.”

“Good. What about the coils?”

“Still damp in the center. He’ll check this afternoon.”

Clarisse pulled the knot tight and tested it with her palm. “And the board?”

“Chiron hasn’t cleared the ground.”

“I know.”

The cord held. Clarisse let her hand fall.

Silena nearly mentioned that Leandros had asked Chiron about cutting the brace lumber. She stopped before the words left her mouth. It wasn’t her news to deliver. If Clarisse wanted to know, she could ask him. If he wanted her to know, he could tell her.

Clarisse noticed the pause. “What?”

“Nothing I need to carry.”

Clarisse studied her for a second, then nodded. “Okay.”

Silena went inside. She set the ledger on the shelf and took out her portfolio. The blue line had dried with a thin dark center, just as she had intended in the smaller study. The middle wave still turned without closing. It did not need a border yet.

She placed the page where morning light could reach it. There was room in the day to paint, and room to leave the work alone.



Amphitrite was at the Big House table when Silena brought the dry page over. She had folded a blue scarf around a few small things and set them in her bag. Her chair was pulled out from the table as if she had only stepped away for a minute.

Silena held out the page. “It dried.”

Amphitrite took it by the edges. “Would you like to tell me about it?”

“I want to show you. I don’t know what to say about it yet.”

“Then I’ll look.”

Silena sat opposite her. The page held the pale gray wash and the narrow blue curve. The color had dried darker at the center. Amphitrite looked without leaning in or taking the page closer.

“Do you want to hear what I notice?” she asked.

“Just what you see.”

“The line has a place where it changes direction. The empty space around it feels deliberate.”

“It is.”

“I like that you left it open.”

Silena took back the page. “I did too.”

She put it into the portfolio, behind the older study and in front of the unfinished copy. The three pages made a progression she could see, though no one else had to read them that way.

“Are you leaving after breakfast?” she asked.

“Yes. I have work in the palace, and Chiron only offered the room through this morning.”

“Will you come back?”

“If you want me to.”

Silena paused. The question could have felt like a promise she was expected to make. Amphitrite let it remain one she could answer honestly.

“I’d like that. I don’t know when.”

“Then we can leave the date open.”

Silena nodded. She noticed the scarf and bag, but did not offer to help. Amphitrite did not ask her to keep anything safe or deliver a message to Percy. They sat in the morning light while the kitchen moved around them. Someone set out plates. Chiron asked for a fresh cup. A camper ran back for a forgotten book.

When it was time to go, Amphitrite stood and waited. “Would you like a hug?”

“Not this morning.”

“Of course.”

“Maybe next time.”

“Maybe,” Amphitrite said.

Silena put the page away. She was not sad that Amphitrite was leaving; she was not glad either. The visit had been real, and it could end without being turned into a test of whether either of them cared enough.

She watched Amphitrite leave the Big House with Chiron. Then she carried the portfolio back to Cabin Three.

---

After lunch, the lower shed door stood open. Beckendorf and Chiron were outside, checking the ground by the fence. The wet soil still yielded under their boots, so the temporary board stayed where it was.

Silena had come to return the ledger. The coils were still on their spacers, their cores cool and damp. She wrote the time beside the drying check and closed the book.

Inside, Leandros was alone at the sawhorse. The rack cover lay flat across it, weight removed. The stretched loop was cut away. He had a short lacing cord, a blunt needle and a piece of scrap canvas to test the spacing. He fed the cord through the first opening, then stopped and pulled the slack back out.

He did not see Silena at the doorway.

The first pass had crossed too close to the frayed edge. He reset it. This time he left a finger’s width between the edge and the stitch line. He drew the cord through, kept the tension even, and began a narrow splice that would hold the loop without pulling the cover into a fold.

The work was slow. He had to undo one side twice. When the cord twisted, he flattened it between his thumb and the bench before continuing.

Silena put the ledger on the shelf. She could have left without saying anything. Instead, she waited until the needle was laid down.

“Beckendorf said you could do that?” she asked.

“After the coils came off the rack. He checked the cover would stay supported here.”

“Then it’s approved.”

“For the loop. Not to put the cover back.”

“Right.”

Leandros glanced toward the door. “I thought I’d get it started before he came back.”

There was no pleased look on his face. No hand lifted to show the new line. The repair was small enough that, if she had not come to return the ledger, she might not have known he had begun it.

Silena looked at the cut loop in the scrap bowl. “The old one stretched.”

“That’s why I cut it off.”

“I know.”

He watched her as if waiting for a judgment. She did not give him one.

“You don’t have to tell Clarisse,” he said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

He looked down at the stitch line. “I know you weren’t.”

“I’m not going to carry news about your work either.”

“I didn’t ask.”

“You were waiting.”

His shoulders tightened. “Maybe I wanted someone to notice.”

“That’s different from asking me to report it.”

“Is it?”

“Yes. You can want people to see what you did. You just can’t make that my job.”

He set the needle down. “I’m trying to do the thing without asking for credit.”

“Then finish it because the loop needs fixing.”

“I am.”

She looked at the new splice. Beckendorf still had to check it before it could safely carry the cover again. “The loop still has to hold the cover,” she said.

His expression shifted, almost a smile, then stopped. “I know.”

She picked up the ledger. “Beckendorf still has to check it before it goes back under weight.”

“I know that too.”

Silena returned to the path. Behind her, the needle moved through the canvas again. She did not turn around to see whether he had watched her leave.



At dinner, Clarisse asked whether the coils had dried. Silena told her their centers were still damp. Leandros did not add an estimate. He ate from the bowl nearest him and left the work list beside Beckendorf’s place.

No one mentioned the rack loop. It remained laid flat on the bench, the new splice complete but not tested. Beckendorf would check it before the cover went back up. The canvas seam stayed stored dry. The board stayed where it was until the soil held.

Silena took her portfolio to bed. She left the blue-gray page open on top, where she could see the middle wave turning into blank paper. The line had not solved anything. It was only a line she had chosen to make.

For tonight, that was enough.
