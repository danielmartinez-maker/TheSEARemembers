## Chapter 141: The Canoe Race

The morning dock smelled of hot pine sap and old rope and the faint copper tang of lake water left overnight in aluminum canoes, and Percy Jackson stood at the end of the planks with his sneakers already damp and his shoulders already warm under the sun. Light came down in flat white sheets through the pines and broke into green-gold flecks where it hit the ripples, and the boards under his feet thrummed with every footstep from the crowd gathering behind him. Somewhere near the boathouse a screen door banged, and a cooler lid scraped, and a paddle knocked hollowly against a hull, and gulls argued far out over the deep water.
Percy wore his camp tee washed so many times the orange had gone pale as peach skin at the shoulders and the trident print had cracked into fine white lines, the cotton thin and soft and sun-bleached across the back where the sun always caught him when he swam. Over it he had a too-big orange life vest with frayed nylon straps and a buckle that stuck, the foam lumpy under the fabric cover and the edges salt-stiff. His khaki cargo shorts were faded at the pockets and damp at the hem, the stitching loose, and his calves were dusted with sand. His black hair stuck up in damp tufts where he had dunked his head to cool off, droplets catching light at the temples, and a smear of sunscreen shone on his nose.
Clarisse La Rue shoved past him with a canoe paddle balanced on one shoulder, her presence announced by the slap of her sandals and the creak of her vest. She wore a camp tee a size too small that pulled tight across her shoulders, the orange darker than Percy's, sweat-darkened at the collar and frayed at the sleeves where she had cut them off with a knife, the raw edges curling. Over it her life vest was cinched so tight the straps dug grooves into the tee, the navy-blue nylon scuffed white at the buckles, one strap repaired with orange duct tape. Her brown cargo shorts were stiff with old lake mud at the knees, the pockets bulging with a coil of spare cord, and her calves showed pale scrapes half-healed. Her dark brown hair was pulled back into a low stubby ponytail, flyaways frizzed by humidity and stuck to her neck, the elastic straining.
"Move, Jackson," Clarisse said, grinning edge-first. "Or are you guarding the dock for the pigeons now?"
"Good morning to you too," Percy said. "Nice bedhead. Very warlord chic."
"Nice shirt," Clarisse shot back. "Did the sun eat it or did you lose a fight with bleach?"
Silena Beauregard arrived next with a woven picnic basket hooked over one arm and a paddle in the other hand, stepping light to avoid the splinters. She wore a white camp tee with the orange Pegasus small over the heart, the cotton smooth and clean, the sleeves neatly cuffed once, tucked into high-waisted denim shorts washed to a soft sky blue, the hems rolled and fraying into white threads against her thighs. Over the tee she wore an open orange life vest, unbuckled at the top, the fabric bright and new-smelling, the straps hanging loose. On her head sat a wide straw hat with a black grosgrain ribbon, the brim bent from being crushed in a bunk trunk, straw fibers rough and sun-warmed and smelling faintly of coconut sunscreen. Her long black hair fell in loose waves past her shoulders, glossy where the light hit, a few strands stuck to her lip gloss. Her sandals were thin brown leather, the straps dark with water spots, and a tiny silver dolphin anklet flashed at her ankle.
"Do not start without me, siblings," Silena said, setting the basket down with care. "Percy, your vest is crooked. Clarisse, your mouth is crooked."
"My mouth is perfect," Clarisse said. "Ask anyone I have yelled at."
"Your mouth is famous," Silena said sweetly. "Like a foghorn. A very handsome foghorn."
Thalia Grace came down the hill with Annabeth Grace at her side, both carrying paddles, both already arguing in the way sisters argued when no one else was allowed to join. Thalia wore a black camp tee with the orange print cracked almost entirely away, the cotton heavy and soft with age, the neck stretched loose and the hem uneven where she had tied it and untied it a hundred times. Over it she wore a faded red life vest, the nylon sun-faded to brick pink at the shoulders, the buckles scratched, worn open over her chest and cinched only at the waist. Her black cargo pants had been cut off into ragged shorts, the denim stiff and dark, frayed threads hanging at the thighs, the pockets safety-pinned shut. Her long black hair was pulled into a single thick braid over one shoulder, bound every few inches with thin blue cord that matched the electric sting of her eyes, the tail end frizzy and wind-lofted, a few short strands plastered to her forehead with sweat. Her boots were scuffed black leather hikers unlaced at the top, mud caked in the treads.
Annabeth wore a clean camp tee in bright orange, the fabric crisp despite the heat, the sleeves neatly hemmed, tucked into khaki shorts with sharp creases still visible at the front, the cotton twill smooth and new, a small ink stain like a constellation on the right pocket where a pen had leaked. Over it she wore a yellow life vest, neatly buckled top to bottom, the straps adjusted to exact even lengths, the nylon still stiff and smelling faintly of plastic. Her blonde hair was pulled into a high ponytail, curls tight with humidity at the nape, flyaways haloed gold in the sun, a thin braided strand behind her ear tied with gray thread. Her white socks were already gray with dust inside her battered white sneakers, the laces double-knotted, the rubber soles squeaking on the wet boards. A pencil stuck behind her ear, and a waterproof notebook bulged in her vest pocket.
"Absolutely not," Thalia was saying. "You are not bringing star charts in a canoe."
"I am bringing one laminated card," Annabeth said. "For science."
"For sinking," Thalia said. "You, me, and science are going to tip over because you wanted to read."
Grover Underwood trotted down after them with a life vest clutched in both hands and a look of deep moral suffering. He wore a camp tee in faded orange stretched over his thin chest, the cotton pilled under the arms, grass stains smeared green across the front, the hem chewed by goats at some point in the distant past. Over it he wrestled with an oversized green life vest, the straps tangled around his elbows, the foam bulky over his narrow shoulders, smelling of mildew and old granola. His baggy brown shorts hung to his knobby knees, the corduroy worn shiny at the thighs, crumbs in the pockets. His curly brown hair stuck out in damp clumps under a crooked Camp Half-Blood cap, the brim bent, goat-ear holes cut ragged in the sides. His legs were shaggy goat-furred from mid-thigh down, the hair damp and smelling of sweet hay and lake mud, his hooves clacking and slipping on the planks. A reed pipe hung on a cord around his neck, knocking against the vest.
Ella the harpy fluttered down last, wings stirring hot air that smelled of feathers and old paper. She wore a camp tee altered with two large slits for her wings, the orange cotton thin and patched at the shoulder with blue stitching, the hem uneven and chewed. Over that she wore a child-sized orange life vest rigged backward so it did not crush her wing joints, the straps crossed and knotted with red yarn, the foam dented where her feathers pressed. Her lower body was feathered in rust-red and gray, the feathers soft and dusty and smelling of sun-warmed dust and lake wind, her bird talons clicking delicately on the wood. Her straggly black hair hung around her pale face, tangled and stuck with a leaf, her eyes bright and darting. A canvas satchel of books thumped against her hip, the strap worn velvety.
Tyson, visiting from the undersea forges for the afternoon, stood at the dock post like a mountain in a vest, holding a starting flag made from a bedsheet tied to an oar. He wore a huge camp tee that barely stretched across his single eye-wide shoulders, the orange fabric thin at the seams and damp with sweat, the letters CRONCHED, the hem riding up over blue swim trunks patterned with white sharks, the polyester shiny and dripping where he had already waded in. Over it all he strained a XXXL blue life vest, the buckles barely meeting, the nylon groaning, smelling of rubber and peanut butter. His brown hair was cropped short and stuck flat with water, droplets running down his broad face, his single eye bright with excitement. His huge feet were bare, toes sunk in mud at the dock edge, the skin gritty with sand.
Triton had come up from Atlantis with him and leaned against a piling with his arms crossed, dry where everyone else was damp. He wore no camp tee but a fitted teal rash guard with scale-patterned seams, the Lycra smooth and gleaming, water beading and rolling off without soaking, the cuffs tight at the wrists. Over one shoulder he had slung an unbuckled black life vest he clearly had no intention of wearing, the strap dragging in the water. His green board shorts were tailored and crisp, the fabric heavy and quick-dry, a gold trident embroidered small at the thigh. His dark hair was slicked back wet and perfect despite the heat, a few strands curling at the nape, his face tanned and bored and handsome in a way that made Percy want to push him in. His feet were bare and clean, toenails trimmed, balanced on the barnacled wood without slipping.
"Rules," Triton drawled, loud enough to carry over the slap of water against the pilings and the creak of rope. "Two canoes. Far shore and back. No drowning. Try not to embarrass Father."
"No powers," Thalia said immediately, pointing her paddle at Percy. "I mean it, Kelp Head."
"No lightning," Percy said. "I mean it, Pinecone Face."
"My face is majestic," Thalia said.
"Your face is sunburned," Annabeth said, nudging her sister's arm. "Your nose is peeling."
"Your nose is a know-it-all nose," Thalia said, bumping Annabeth's shoulder with her own. "You got sunscreen in my braid and now I smell like coconuts."
"You always smell like thunderstorm and old socks," Annabeth said. "Now you smell like tropical thunderstorm and old socks. Improvement."
Clarisse snorted. "Gods, the Grace sisters fight like seagulls over a fry."
"Says the girl who once cried because Silena braided her hair too tight," Percy said.
"I did not cry," Clarisse said. "My eyes sweated from pain. Silena has iron hands."
"My hands are delicate," Silena said, examining her pink nails. "Your scalp is dramatic. Percy, tell her."
"Clarisse is dramatic about everything except actual danger," Percy said. "Then she laughs."
"Then I win," Clarisse said, shoving his vest strap so the buckle rattled. "Remember that when you are eating our wake, brother."
"Brother who will save you when you tip," Percy said, shoving back.
"Brother who tips first," Silena said, flicking water from her fingers at both of them. "Behave, boys. Or no picnic."
The taste of sunscreen and lake spray sat on Percy's tongue, salty and chemical and faintly sweet from Silena's nearby coconut lotion, while the vest foam pressed warm against his collarbones and the sun pressed hotter. The light glared white off the aluminum hulls and made him squint until tears pricked. A fish jumped out beyond the buoys with a flat smack, and the pine smell sharpened as the breeze shifted.
Tyson raised the flag. "Ready! Set! Paddle nice! No bonking!"
---
The start was chaos of splashing and shouting and aluminum scraping wood as both canoes shoved off, and Percy felt the familiar lurch in his stomach as the hull found water. The lake spread wide and glittering, light shattering into a thousand white needles across the chop, wind pushing small whitecaps that slapped the bow. The smell changed instantly away from the dock, cleaner and wilder, algae and cold depth and sun-heated metal, and the sound opened up, paddles chunking, water hissing along the hulls, Thalia whooping, Grover bleating in panic.
Percy knelt in the bow of the Poseidon canoe, paddle dug deep, his sun-bleached camp tee already sticking to his shoulder blades under the vest, the cotton translucent with sweat at the seams, the vest riding up and chafing his neck raw where the nylon edge rubbed. His cargo shorts soaked through at the thighs from kneel-water sloshing in the bottom, the fabric dark and heavy, sand grains grinding under his knees against the ribbed aluminum, cold and ridged. His hair whipped wet across his forehead, salt-sting in his eyes, sunscreen running bitter into the corner of his mouth when he licked his lips.
Clarisse knelt mid-ship, powering the stroke with her whole back, her cut-off camp tee sleeves flapping, sweat patches spreading under her vest where the navy nylon trapped heat, the duct-taped strap flapping loose and slapping her ribs with each pull. Her cargo shorts rode up as she braced, mud softening and smearing across the aluminum, her ponytail whipping and shedding droplets, her jaw clenched, a vein standing in her temple. Her paddle dripped arcs of glittering water across her arms, beading on sun-browned skin.
Silena knelt in the stern, steering with neat J-strokes, her straw hat jammed low now with the ribbon tied under her chin, the straw rough against her jaw, shadow striping her face gold and brown as light filtered through the weave. Her white tee clung at the waist where spray hit, the cotton cool and damp, her open vest flapping, the bright orange fabric slapping her sides. Her denim shorts darkened with water spots, the frayed hems dripping, her leather sandals sliding on the wet hull, toes curled for grip, the silver anklet cold and tight. Her hair tangled in the wind, strands whipping across her glossed lips, tasting of strawberry and salt.
In the other canoe Thalia knelt in the bow like a figurehead, braid flying straight back, blue cords snapping, her black tee plastered to her back under the faded red vest, sweat gleaming at her stretched collar, the heavy cotton dark with water. Her cut-off denim shorts soaked at the frayed edges, threads dripping, her hiker boots braced wide, leather darkening with lake water, laces trailing and soaking. Her eyes narrowed against glare, teeth bared in effort, the tendons in her forearms standing out.
Annabeth knelt mid-ship with her laminated card tucked stupidly safe inside her vest, paddling with efficient clipped strokes, her bright orange tee still impossibly neat except for a spray of droplets across the chest, the cotton dotted dark, her yellow vest snug and squeaking with each twist, plastic smell sharp in the heat. Her khaki shorts stayed crisp except where her knees pressed the hull, twill creased and damp, her sneakers squelching, socks soaked gray, ponytail bouncing, the gray-threaded braid strand stuck to her wet cheek, pencil still miraculously behind her ear.
Grover huddled in the stern trying to steer and mostly praying, his pilled camp tee soaked through and clinging to his ribs, green vest riding up around his ears, mildew smell stronger wet, corduroy shorts heavy with water, goat fur plastered dark and dripping, hooves slipping with soft thuds, cap backward now, curls plastered to his forehead, reed pipes bouncing and tooting a mournful accidental note each time the hull rocked.
Ella perched on the center thwart with a paddle far too big for her, wings half-open for balance, her slit camp tee flapping, backward vest slipping, yarn knots dripping, feathers ruffled and spiked with water, smelling stronger of wet bird and dust, talons gripping the aluminum gunwale with tiny metallic ticks, hair plastered to her skull, eyes huge.
The Poseidon canoe shot ahead on raw muscle, Clarisse grunting with each pull.
"Eat it, Graces!" Clarisse yelled, spray flying from her blade. "Eat our wake! Eat lakeweed!"
"Your wake is wobbly!" Thalia yelled back, her voice cracking with laughter and wind. "Your steering is wobbly! Your brother is wobbly!"
"My brother is ballast!" Clarisse yelled. "Pretty ballast!"
"Hey!" Percy said, catching a faceful of cold spray that tasted of minerals and sun. "I am hydrodynamic."
"You are hydro-dramatic," Silena called from the stern, laughing, flicking her paddle to shower him. "Hold still, little brother, you are rocking us."
"I am not rocking," Percy said. "The lake is rocking."
"The lake is flat, baby brother," Clarisse said, reaching forward to ruffle his wet hair hard enough to sting. "You are making excuses. Fourteen and already making old-man excuses."
"Fourteen and already carrying you," Percy said, shaking water from his eyes. "Paddle, old woman. Fifteen is practically thirty."
"Fifteen is practically champion," Clarisse said. "Watch and weep."
She leaned hard over the side and dug her paddle deep and dragged, throwing a deliberate wall of water at the Zeus canoe, then rocked her hips to send a wake sloshing sideways. The aluminum rang as water slapped. The texture of the splash was cold needles on Percy's arms, gritty with stirred silt, and the light through the airborne droplets broke into brief rainbows.
"Clarisse!" Silena scolded, laughing despite herself. "No splashing! We talked about sportsmanship!"
"Sportsmanship is for people losing," Clarisse said, doing it again, bigger. Water sheeted over Grover, who yelped.
The Zeus canoe wobbled, Grover bleating, Ella squawking and flapping, wings shedding spray that glittered.
"Hey!" Annabeth shouted, wiping her face, her ponytail dripping, droplets hanging on her eyelashes. "That was intentional! Foul!"
"Prove it in canoe court!" Clarisse shouted, grinning wickedly, water running off her chin.
Thalia narrowed her eyes, braid whipping. She lifted her paddle and tapped it once on the gunwale, and a tiny spark of static crackled blue-white at the tip, sharp as a camera flash, the smell of ozone cutting sudden through lake air, metallic and burnt.
"Thalia," Annabeth warned. "No."
"What?" Thalia said innocently, too innocently. "I am just stretching. Just a little weather. Just a tiny startle."
She flicked the spark toward the water near the Poseidon bow. It cracked with a snap that echoed off the water, light flaring white-blue, and the sound punched Percy's ears. Silena shrieked and jerked her paddle, the straw hat tilting over one eye, her whole body jolting. Percy flinched so hard his paddle clanged the hull, the vibration buzzing up his arms, aluminum hot then cold under his palms.
"Thalia Grace!" Silena gasped, clutching her hat, heart hammering visibly at her throat. "You nearly tipped me into the lake! My hat! My hair!"
"Your hat survived," Thalia called, cackling, wind tearing the sound. "Your scream was excellent! Very musical! Do it again!"
"Do not," Annabeth said, smacking her sister's vest hard enough to thump foam. "You promised no powers. You are cheating."
"I am enhancing morale," Thalia said. "Their morale is now terrified. Our morale is excellent."
"Your morale is getting us disqualified," Annabeth said. "Paddle straight, Thunderhead. Eyes forward."
"Yes, ma'am, Star Chart," Thalia said, but she winked at Percy across the water, her grin white and wild, braid cords dripping. "Scared, Kelp Head?"
"Terrified of your aim," Percy said, forcing a laugh while his pulse hammered. "You missed by a mile, grandma. Eighteen and already losing your eyesight."
"Eighteen and already winning," Thalia said. "Watch the wake, baby."
Percy felt the tug low in his gut as the water around his own canoe chopped steeper, wind-against-tide steep little peaks that grabbed the bow. Without thinking he reached out with that other sense, the one that lived behind his ribs, and smoothed it, pressing the chop flat in a glassy lane under their hull. The canoe surged, quieter, faster, the hiss softening to a whisper, the ride steadying. It felt like running a hand over rumpled sheets and smoothing them.
Instant guilt flooded hot behind his sternum, sour as swallowed lake water. No powers. He had promised. The taste of the promise turned metallic on his tongue. He glanced at Clarisse, who was crowing about speed, and at Silena, who was smiling into the wind, and the smoothness of the water felt suddenly loud to him, obvious, cheating ringing in his ears louder than the paddle splash.
He let go. The chop came back with a slap, the bow thumping, spray kissing his face again, cold and honest. His shoulders burned with the extra effort to hold pace without help, cotton shirt chafing, vest heavy.
"Whoa, what was that?" Clarisse said, frowning at the water. "We just slowed."
"Wind shift," Percy said quickly, too quickly, heat climbing his neck under the vest strap. "Lake stuff. Currents."
Silena gave him a look over the brim of her straw hat, eyes knowing and kind, lip gloss cracked with salt. She said nothing, just adjusted her stroke to cover, her paddle whispering.
Percy stared at his blade dripping, light running liquid down the wood, and swallowed. The smell of ozone still hung faint, mixing with hot aluminum and his own sunscreen sweat.
---
By mid-lake the sun stood hard and white directly overhead, light flattening the water to hammered silver except where the reed beds threw long green shadows near the far shore. The air smelled of hot mud and crushed cattails and distant charcoal from someone's beach grill, and every breath tasted of warm minerals and Silena's coconut drifting back and Grover's sweet hay-sweat from the other boat. Sound carried weirdly flat and close, paddle drips ticking, Ella muttering book titles under her breath like a prayer, a speedboat droning far behind them.
Percy's camp tee now clung completely, sun-bleached orange gone almost translucent at the shoulders, salt lines white at the armpits, cotton gritty with dried spray, the vest over it heavy and hot, foam damp and sour-smelling, straps chafing red lines into his neck. His cargo shorts squelched, pockets full of water, sand grinding between his thighs, his knees aching from the ridged hull, skin dented. His hair had dried into stiff salt spikes, EDGE curling at his forehead, sunscreen long gone except a greasy shine at his temples.
Clarisse had stripped her vest open to vent, the navy nylon hanging loose, duct tape peeling in the heat, her cut-off tee dark with sweat in a V down her back, raw sleeve edges scratchy against her biceps, her cargo shorts stiff then soft then stiff again with mud and water, scrapes on her calves stinging with salt, her ponytail limp and dripping, frizz haloed white in the glare. She paddled with gritted teeth, arms shaking.
Silena had retied her straw hat tighter, ribbon denting her chin, straw hot and prickly where it touched, her white tee now sheer at the shoulders with water, clinging, her open orange vest sun-hot to the touch, plastic buckles burning, denim shorts heavy and cold against her thighs, hems unraveling further, her leather sandals waterlogged and squeaking, anklet caked with silt, her waves tangled into salt knots, gloss gone, lips chapped and tasting of salt and strawberry ghost.
Thalia's black tee steamed, heavy cotton reeking of sweat and lake, red vest faded further to pink-white at the edges, nylon hot enough to sting, her cut-off denims chafing, frayed threads stuck to her wet thighs, boots full of water sloshing audibly, braid damp and heavy, blue cords dark with water, frizz plastered to her neck, sunburn blooming across her nose despite sunscreen, skin tight and hot.
Annabeth's neatness had finally surrendered, her bright tee wilted, cotton sticking in patches, yellow vest squeaking constantly, straps loosened one notch, khaki shorts wrinkled and damp, sneakers sodden, socks bunched, ponytail sagging, curls escaped and stuck to her neck, pencil still behind her ear but soft with water, notebook pages wavy, her cheeks flushed, eyes bright with calculation.
Grover looked half-drowned, his tee plastered, green vest stinking strongly of wet dog and mildew, corduroy shorts dripping, fur matted in points, hooves soft and sore-sounding on aluminum, cap lost and recovered and now perched crooked, curls dripping into his eyes, pipes warbling with water inside.
Ella looked bedraggled and happy, her patched tee soaked, backward vest askew, yarn sagging, feathers clumped and dripping, smelling of wet down and hot dust, talons white-knuckled on the thwart, hair in wet ropes, satchel dark with water at the bottom.
The reed maze loomed, a floating confusion of green stalks and lily pads and half-sunk logs marking the old channel to the far-shore picnic cove. Both canoes entered side by side, stalks whispering and scraping aluminum with a dry hush-hush, dragonflies ticking past, water suddenly dark and still and smelling of rot-sweet mud and lilies, light dappling green-gold through leaves.
"This is where people get lost," Grover moaned, paddle trailing, ripples spreading oily. "Oh, I do not like this. Oh, reeds. Oh, mud. Oh, things touching the boat."
"No one is touching the boat except water," Annabeth said patiently, though her own hands were white on her paddle. "Stay centered, Grover. Ella, stop rocking to look at frogs."
"Frogs are friends. Frogs see. Frogs sing in the dusk with throats like drums," Ella chanted, wings fluttering, showering everyone with droplets that tasted of pond. "Ella sees the way. Ella remembers the book about stars."
Clarisse snorted from the other boat, reeds brushing her arms, leaving thin green scratches. "Great. The bird is navigating. We are saved. Poseidons, straight through, power over pretty."
"We do not know straight through," Silena said, squinting under her hat brim, light speckling her face. "Percy, baby brother, which cut? The water all looks the same."
Percy stared at three identical green tunnels, water black-glass between them, lily pads waxy and cool-looking, yellow flowers waxy-sweet smelling. His gut said left, but his gut had been guilty and loud all race. He opened his mouth.
Annabeth was already pulling the laminated card from her vest, shaking water off it, holding it flat despite the rocking. The card showed a crude hand-drawn sun compass with star overlays, pencil lines blurred by laminate glare.
"Stars in daylight trick," Annabeth announced, voice lifting with nerdy triumph. "You find the sun, you find south, you back-calculate Cassiopeia's height for this latitude and date. Thalia, hold us steady. I need a shadow."
"Oh gods, she is doing math," Thalia groaned, but she jammed her paddle straight down into mud to pole them still, muscles standing out in her forearms, boot soles grinding. "Someone fan me. Genius at work. Do not tip us while you stargaze at noon, little sister."
"Little sister who knows where we are," Annabeth said, sticking her tongue out, sweat beading on her upper lip, tasting salt when she licked it. "Big sister who gets us lost going to the bathrooms."
"I never get lost," Thalia said. "I take scenic detours. With lightning."
"You took us to the boys' bathrooms," Annabeth said. "By smell."
"By destiny," Thalia said. "And I was eight. Let it go."
Percy watched over the gap, reeds ticking against his hull, the texture of a cattail head brushing his bare arm soft then scratchy, pollen dusting his skin yellow and peppery-smelling.
"Need a hand, Wise Girl?" Percy called. "I know a guy who knows water."
"I know a guy who cheats at water," Annabeth called back, but her smile was fond, eyes crinkling, sun flashing on her wet ponytail. "Stay in your boat, Seaweed Brain. Watch how Zeus girls do it without magic."
"Without magic except sparky fingers," Clarisse muttered.
"Without much magic," Annabeth conceded, holding her pencil upright on the card to cast a shadow, squinting at the line. Light glared off laminate and made her tear up. "There. Sun shadow says that cut is north-northwest. Water flow and lily lean says current pushes to the cove that way. Cassiopeia would be low over that treeline tonight, which means that treeline is north. So we go middle-right."
"That was hot," Thalia said blankly. "I understood none of it and I am proud and terrified."
"I understood stargaze and paddle," Grover said. "Which is it?"
"Paddle!" Annabeth and Thalia shouted together, then looked at each other and burst laughing, forehead to forehead for a second, vest foam bumping.
"Show-off Graces," Clarisse grumbled, but she was watching with reluctant respect, paddle dripping. "Fine. We follow them. But if they beach us, I am blaming stars."
"We are not following," Silena said primly, lifting her chin, hat ribbon fluttering. "We are coincidentally choosing the same correct channel because Poseidon children feel currents in their bones. Right, Percy?"
Percy felt the current indeed, a faint cool pull against the hull, silt-sweet, tugging middle-right. He nodded, throat tight with the earlier guilt, not trusting magic, trusting eyes. "Middle-right. I feel it too. No powers. Just eyes."
"Just eyes and big opinions," Clarisse said, shoving his shoulder affectionately, her knuckles rough and wet. "Lead on, Star Girl. Try to keep up, Thunder Butt."
"Try to keep your hat, Straw Hat," Thalia shot back. "Wind is coming. I can smell it."
They paddled into the green tunnel, stalks closing behind with a sigh, light going emerald and cool for a blessed minute, water chuckling under the bows, mud sucking softly at poles, the taste of shade clean after glare.
---
The far shore arrived with a soft crunch of bow on sand and the sudden smell of hot grass and pine needles baking and watermelon rind from coolers already waiting, carried down by Tyson and Triton who had teleported or speed-swum or cheated outrageously and now stood on the beach looking smug. Light slanted gold-green through overhanging oaks, dappling the sand white-hot and cool-black in patches, cicadas screaming in the trees, waves lapping with a gentle shush-shush, sandals slapping as everyone half-fell out of canoes into shin-deep water cold and silky with suspended silt.
Percy splashed out first, hauling the bow, his sun-bleached tee dripping and cold-then-hot against his skin, vest suddenly ten pounds heavier with absorbed water, straps squealing, cargo shorts plastered and full of sand that ground at the seams, feet sinking into wet sand that sucked and released with soft pops, water beading on his sunburned shoulders. His hair dripped into his eyes, salt sharp on his lips when he grinned.
Clarisse vaulted out and landed knee-deep with a splash that soaked them both, her cut-off tee riding up, vest flapping open, duct tape finally giving way and hanging by a thread, cargo shorts heavy and sagging, mud washing off her calves in brown clouds, ponytail flinging water, her laugh loud and startled and real. "Land! Sweet stable land that does not move!"
Silena stepped out daintily and immediately sank to the ankle, shrieking with laughter, her straw hat tumbling forward over her eyes, ribbon yanked, white tee splashed brown at the hem, orange vest bumping, denim shorts soaked through and cold, sandals sucked half-off, anklet gritty, waves tangled higher, sunscreen and sweat and lake drying sticky on her arms, tasting salt when she laughed open-mouthed.
Thalia fell out mostly on purpose, rolling in the shallows with a whoop, her black tee instantly sand-crusted, red vest grinding grit into the fabric, cut-off denims filling with sand that rasped, boots heavy as anchors, braid dragging in water, blue cords trailing silt, sunburned nose wrinkled, eyes squeezed shut, lake water up her nose making her snort. "Oh gods, cold! Cold sand in places sand should never!"
Annabeth waded out careful with her notebook held high, then slipped and sat down hard in six inches of water with a gasp, her bright tee splashed, yellow vest bobbing her up like a cork, khaki shorts instantly brown-wet and gritty, sneakers filling with liquid sand that oozed, ponytail dunked, gray-thread braid dripping, pencil lost then found stuck in her vest strap, cheeks pink with embarrassment and laughter, water tasting of warm sand and sunscreen.
Grover collapsed onto dry sand on hands and knees and kissed it, his pilled tee sandy, green vest twisted, corduroy shorts caked, fur dripping and shedding water in sheets, smelling powerfully of wet goat and joy, cap hanging by one ear-hole, curls matted, pipes full of water tootling flat. "Land! Blessed dirt! Oh, I will never steer again!"
Ella fluttered and hopped and landed on a driftwood log, shaking like a dog, her slit tee spraying, backward vest crooked, yarn dripping, feathers puffing then sleeking, dust-smell blooming wet then drying in sun, talons gripping bark rough and hot, hair wild, satchel thumping wood. The paddle back was slow
 and golden and deliberately together, two canoes lashed side by side with spare cord to make a wobbly catamaran that moved like a sleepy turtle, and nobody minded. Light had gone honey-thick and low, laying long amber paths across water gone glassy-calm, smelling of cooling pines and warm algae and leftover watermelon sour-sweet on everyone's breath. Sound softened to paddle drips and hull creaks and sleepy talk and Ella humming, beeswax-warm, dragonflies late. The taste in Percy's mouth was melon and lake and sunscreen ghost, sweet then mineral.
Percy paddled lazy stern strokes, his sun-bleached camp tee finally dry and stiff with salt and juice, cotton scratchy at the neck, orange pale as sunset itself, vest worn open now and flapping, nylon cool, cargo shorts dry-crackly with sand that sifted out with each shift, skin tight with sun, hair dry and soft again lifted by breeze, feet bare now, shoes kicked under the thwart, soles gritty.
Clarisse trailed her hand in the water, her cut-off tee dry and loose, sweat salt white at the seams, vest open, duct tape gone, cargo shorts sun-hot, scrapes clean, ponytail loose and windblown, flyaways gold-tipped, expression drowsy and content, fingers wrinkling in the cool silk of the wake.

Thalia lay back with her head on Annabeth's lap, braid undone and spread black across Annabeth's knees, blue cords tangled, black tee dry and sandy, red vest pillowed under her shoulders, cut-off denims warm, bare feet crossed and sandy, nose peeling and shiny with fresh aloe Silena had smeared, eyes half-closed against amber light, smile lazy.
