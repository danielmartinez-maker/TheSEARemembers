## Chapter 96: Training Together

At dawn, after Annabeth's temperature had registered lower overnight, Percy woke in Cabin Three to the fountain trickling on the porch and uneven breathing from the other bunks. Will's last reading had been 99.1, but he had said she was still ill. The number did not tell them when her fever began to fall or what had caused it. Sunlight striped the shell-shaped windows. Clarisse snored on the top bunk across the room, one arm over her eyes; Silena was already awake, sitting cross-legged on her bed with her hair in a loose braid, mending an armor strap with small, neat stitches.

Percy sat up and rubbed his eyes. "Is she awake yet?" he whispered.

Silena smiled without looking up. "Annabeth? Thalia told me what she sent Grover: her fever is down, she's staying here today, and she wants toast. That's all Annabeth approved."

Percy felt his chest loosen, though the worry did not leave. He remembered Annabeth swaying in the arena, Will's thermometer, and the watch schedule they had made because she had asked them to. When the cool cloth made her shiver, they had set it aside. He had kept his turn beside her and left only when Thalia took the next one. The message held no promise about when Annabeth would be well enough to leave the cabin.

"Good," he said quietly. "Good."

Clarisse snorted awake, sat bolt upright, and glared at nothing. "Who's dying? What's on fire?"

"Nothing's on fire," Silena said, amused. "Your little brother was worrying."

Clarisse scrubbed her face and looked at Percy. "She'll tell us what she needs when she wakes. That's better than us guessing."

Several days later, Annabeth had chosen to return to Cabin One for the night, and Will had cleared her for gentle training after the required fever-free interval. A knock sounded at the Poseidon cabin door, sharp and precise. Three raps. Percy knew that knock.

He scrambled out of bed so fast he nearly tripped over his own sneakers and yanked the door open.

Annabeth Grace stood on the porch in the morning light, her blonde curls pulled back in a ponytail, her gray eyes clear and bright again, her cheeks pink with health instead of fever. She wore her camp orange shirt and jeans and her Yankees cap tucked in her back pocket, and she had a notebook under one arm and a pencil behind her ear. Behind her stood Thalia Grace, tall and dark-haired with her silver circlet glinting, grinning like she had invented mornings.

"Hi, Seaweed Brain," Annabeth said.

Percy stared at her for a full three seconds and then broke into the biggest grin of his life. "Hi, Wise Girl. You're supposed to be on bed rest."

"Will cleared me," Annabeth said primly. "Light duty. No sparring till tomorrow. Strategy and powers only. And before you ask, yes, I ate breakfast. Thalia watched me eat two eggs and toast to make sure."

"I counted bites," Thalia confirmed solemnly. "Twenty-four bites. Very thorough."

Silena appeared behind Percy and opened her arms. "Oh, thank the gods. Come here, baby."

Annabeth allowed herself to be hugged. Silena squeezed her tight. Clarisse came stomping over, checked her color, nodded, and ruffled her hair.

"Color's back," Clarisse pronounced. "Good. You looked like Hades yesterday."

"Lovely to see you too, Clarisse," Annabeth said dryly, smoothing her hair, but she was smiling.

Thalia leaned against the doorframe and crossed her arms. "So. Chiron wants us. All five. Big Guy's pavilion. Ten minutes."

Silena raised an eyebrow. "Swimsuits and brains? That's oddly specific."

"That's Chiron," Annabeth said. She tapped her notebook. "I bet I know what it is. I've been thinking about it since yesterday. While I was stuck in bed doing nothing."

"You were sleeping," Thalia said.

"I was thinking between sleeping," Annabeth said with dignity. "Come on."

---

Chiron waited for them in the Big House meeting room with his wheelchair in its human disguise, a tray of lemonade on the table, and a large map of Camp Half-Blood unrolled and weighted down with seashells.

Grover Underwood was already there, his satyr legs folded under him, a brand-new stopwatch around his neck that he kept polishing proudly. Beside him, perched on a tall stool with her feet tucked up, was Ella the harpy, with her ruffled red feathers, her bright intelligent eyes, and a stack of wax tablets and styluses piled higher than her head.

"Ella will record," Ella said when they walked in, half to herself. "Ella records everything. Training together. Water and lightning. For remembering."

"Ella volunteered to be our scribe," Grover explained. He beamed at the five demigods filing in. "And I'm the timer! Chiron said joint training needs an impartial judge, and my hooves are very impartial."

Annabeth sat down next to Ella and smiled at her. "Hi, Ella. Did you bring the blue stylus?"

"Ella brought blue and red and green," Ella said, pleased that Annabeth remembered. "Blue for water. Yellow for lightning. Red for mistakes. Ella likes red best. There will be many reds at first."

Clarisse snorted. Thalia laughed. Percy sat beside Annabeth, close enough that their shoulders touched, and felt the familiar calm settle over him that always came when she was near and healthy.

Chiron cleared his throat. "Thank you all for coming. Your families have had reason to distrust one another's powers. Poseidon and Zeus have not always, ah, trained well together."

Thalia muttered, "That's one way to put it. Dad and Uncle P have thrown hurricanes at each other for three thousand years."

"Exactly," Chiron said. "Which is why what I am about to propose requires patience. Individually, you are formidable. Percy can move more water than any child of Poseidon I have taught in decades. Clarisse can hold a line under pressure. Silena can sustain a shield without losing its shape. Thalia can call lightning that would make her father proud. Annabeth, even at thirteen, can read a problem involving electricity with the care of an engineer."

Annabeth blushed to the tips of her ears. Percy nudged her knee with his under the table.

"But together," Chiron continued, "you have never trained as a unit. If you combine your powers carelessly, someone could be hurt. We will not use a person or a body of water as a path for lightning. The Poseidon siblings will shape the water; the Zeus sisters will control a separate strike through open air. Your work meets in the count and the target, not by contact."

He tapped the map. A line ran from the camp beach to the top of Half-Blood Hill, where the old bronze beacon stood, dark and unused since the last war.

"The beacon," Annabeth breathed. Her eyes lit up. "I've read about it. It used to burn with Greek fire to call the camp to arms. It hasn't been lit in years because no one can get enough sustained power to the top without wiring."

"The old beacon is our eventual goal," Chiron said. "Today is a controlled exercise. The Poseidon siblings will shape a water arch toward a marked target. It will stop short of the bronze receiver. The Zeus sisters will practice a separate, low-power spark through open air, aimed at that receiver. The water is not a wire, and no one will use a person or the ground as part of a path. We will not attempt the hill until each part works alone and everyone can stop on the same signal. Grover will time the holds. Ella will record only what you agree is useful to keep."

Silena leaned forward, fascinated. "A water arch beside a lightning strike? Like a river of light?"

"Like the Sea remembers the Sky," Ella whispered, scribbling. "Ella likes that. Sea remembers Sky. Pretty words."

Percy looked at Annabeth. Annabeth looked at Percy. He could see the wheels turning behind her gray eyes, the diagrams already drawing themselves.

"When do we start?" Annabeth asked.

Chiron smiled. "Right after you change. The beach in twenty minutes."

Annabeth kept the map open when the others began to stand. "Before we go, I want the first trial to be water only. Then we can test a spark at the plate after the water is down. If either part fails, we don't combine them."

Chiron studied her sketch and nodded. "Agreed."

Clarisse frowned at the map. "If you want the water held in one shape, don't put me at the end and expect me to aim it. I can keep a boundary from wandering. I can't make three people hold the same line if nobody tells me where it is."

"Then tell us when it moves," Percy said. "I can handle the center."

Silena leaned across the map, her fingers hovering over the blue line. "I'll take the lower curve. It needs a steady pace. If I breathe too fast, it climbs; if I hold my breath, it narrows." She glanced at Clarisse. "You can call the edge. I can keep time with you."

Thalia flexed her left hand. The wrap covered her right palm, and she kept it close to her side. "I can work a small spark with this one. If it starts to hurt, I stop. I don't need the other hand to prove I can aim."

Annabeth wrote the stations in the margin: Percy at the crown, Silena at the lower curve, Clarisse at the far boundary, Thalia at the bronze target, Annabeth watching the gap and calling the correction. She drew a second line between the target and the water. "No one crosses this."

Grover clicked his stopwatch closed. "I'll call the count and watch the lane. I can use my voice; the whistle is only for stopping."

Ella tapped the yellow stylus against the table. "Ella can write names, times and what worked. Ella will not write the dream or the sick things. Ella asks before she draws faces."

Annabeth looked at her. "Thank you. The trial is about the signal and the boundary. That's what we need to keep."

They left the map on the table for Chiron to roll. The exercise had a job now, and each of them had chosen a part they could actually do.

---

The beach glittered white in the late morning sun. Grover had set up a folding table with his stopwatch and whistle. Ella sat in the shade with her tablets in neat rows.

"Attempt one in five minutes," Grover announced importantly, clicking his stopwatch. "Ella, mark the time. Ten forty-two."

"Ella marks ten forty-two," Ella repeated, scratching in blue. "Sun high. Wind west-southwest. Harpy hungry but professional."

Clarisse stood at the water's edge in her black swimsuit and camp shirt tied around her waist, her arms crossed, her dark hair pulled back tight. Silena wore a blue one-piece with a white cover-up, her dark hair braided down her back. Percy wore swim trunks and an old camp shirt, barefoot in the sand, already feeling the pull of the water like a second heartbeat.

Thalia and Annabeth stood a little farther up the beach, in shorts and camp shirts, their feet dry. Thalia's hair crackled faintly even in the sunshine. She rolled her shoulders, and tiny sparks snapped between her fingertips. Annabeth held her notebook, flipping to a fresh page covered in neat sketches of arcs and waves.

"Okay," Annabeth said, all business. "First rule: we don't put a spark into the water. We need a water shape that stops short of the target, and a separate line through the air for the lightning. If either path drifts, we call stop. Nobody stands under the arch or downrange of the bronze plate."

"We need a shape with a clear ending, not a lake," Thalia translated for Clarisse, who had started to glaze over.

"Exactly," Annabeth said. "A contained stream, held clear of the target. Poseidon kids shape the water. Zeus kids aim at the bronze plate through open air. The two parts meet in the timing, not by touching."

Clarisse cracked her knuckles. "So we make a water snake, you ride it with zappy fingers, and we don't die."

"That's the plan," Annabeth said.

"Great plan," Clarisse said. "I love plans where we don't die."

Silena laughed and bumped her younger brother's shoulder. "Percy, you're strongest at lifting. You take the center. I'll take the tail at the surf, keep the intake steady. Clarisse, you take the head, aim it at the target."

"What target?" Percy asked.

Grover blew his whistle. "Target practice first! No beacon till you can hit the dummy!"

He pointed down the beach, where he and Juniper had wrestled a straw training dummy dressed in an old Greek helmet and holding a tin shield. The shield had been painted with concentric circles in red and white.

"Distance: fifty feet," Grover said. "Goal: the water stops before the shield, and a separate spark hits the bronze plate through open air. We keep the lanes clear. Also, no knocking over Ella's umbrella. Or me."

"Ella does not want to be electrocuted," Ella said firmly. "Ella has been electrocuted before. Zero stars."

Percy stayed behind the chalk boundary and reached for the water with the sense that told him where each moving drop lay. The sea answered at once, but he did not pull it up yet. The first job was to keep the three Poseidon siblings out of the channel they were shaping.

"Ready?" he called.

Clarisse took the left station and Silena the right. They checked the chalk marks with Percy, then waited until Grover had cleared the lane. Their powers felt different even before they worked: Clarisse held her focus like a shield braced against a shove; Silena listened for the water's change in weight; Percy watched the shape of the whole inlet.

"On three," Percy said. "One. Two. Three."

They lifted.

A thick rope of seawater rose from the surf with a roar, as wide as Percy's waist, twisting and sparkling in the sun. Silena held the base steady where it met the sea, her face calm with concentration, her free hand smoothing the flow. Percy held the middle, keeping it aloft and moving, his arms raised like a conductor. Clarisse gritted her teeth and aimed the head toward the dummy, her whole body leaning into it.

The water snake wavered, then straightened, humming.

"Whoa," Thalia breathed. "Okay. That's awesome."

"Hold the water," Annabeth called, then looked to Thalia. "No spark on this pass. The channel hasn't held its shape yet."

Thalia lowered her left hand. The wrap on her right palm stayed dry and tucked close to her side. The water rope wavered, split near the middle, and fell in a harmless sheet that soaked the straw dummy and the edge of the marked lane. No one had entered the channel.

"Ella records: water path failed before the target," Ella announced, writing in red. "No strike attempted. Dummy damp, not deceased."

Percy watched the last of the water run back toward the inlet. "Everybody clear?"

Clarisse wrung water from her sleeve. "I'm fine. The shape was too wide. Do it again, but smaller."

Silena brushed a wet strand from her cheek. "The tail held. The middle didn't. I can narrow my side."

Thalia remained behind the chalk line, watching the three siblings check their footing. "I thought we were about to try the spark. I didn't hear the change. Are you all right?"

Annabeth closed her notebook and looked from Percy to Clarisse and Silena. "Everyone all right?"

"Fine," Percy said. "The channel fell apart before it reached the target."

Annabeth nodded once, then wrote the failure down. "Good. No strike happened. That is why we keep the exercises separate until the timing is solid."

"I didn't send anything," Thalia said. "I heard 'hold' and thought the next word was coming. I should have waited for the full signal."

Clarisse clapped Thalia's shoulder, careful of the wrapped hand. "Then we make the call clearer. That's what a first try is for."

"My first water shield collapsed on a naiad's picnic," Silena added cheerfully, wringing out her cover-up. "She still won't talk to me."

Percy stood and shook water from his hair like a dog, spraying Annabeth. She squeaked and batted at him, and they both laughed, the scare dissolving.

Grover retrieved his stopwatch and blew his whistle. "Attempt one: six seconds hold, zero control, one dummy killed in action. Ella, note heroic sacrifice of Straw Bob."

"Ella notes Straw Bob the Second gave his life for science," Ella said solemnly, starting a fresh tablet. "Ella will sing his saga later. In under twelve syllables, as is traditional."

Annabeth retrieved her notebook, now damp at the edges, and flipped to a new page. Her hands were still shaking a little. Percy noticed and gently took the pencil from her.

"Hey," he said quietly, so only she could hear. "We're okay. You kept us safe. You called the mark perfect. We'll just tune it."

Annabeth looked up at him, her gray eyes meeting his green ones. "Promise you'll tell me if it hurts? Even a little tingle? You always try to tough it out."

"I promise," Percy said. "You promise you'll tell me if you're getting tired? You're still getting over being sick."

"I promise," Annabeth said.

They linked pinkies on it, solemn as an oath on the Styx, and Thalia, watching from a few feet away with her arms crossed, smiled a soft older-sister smile that she quickly hid by pretending to stretch.

---

Attempt two was better and worse.

The water rope held longer. Percy called "hold," but Clarisse heard it as a cue to adjust the head; she sneezed at exactly the wrong moment and the shape whipped sideways, drenching Grover, his table, his enchiladas, and half of Ella's tablets. They dropped the water before anyone tried a strike. Once the channel was gone and the lane clear, Thalia sent a small spark toward the separate bronze plate. It hit, neat and bright, while the straw dummy stayed wet and unburned.

"Ella's reds are wet!" Ella wailed, holding up a smeared tablet. "Ella's beautiful reds! Now they are pink! Pink is not science!"

Grover sat in the sand, dripping, his stopwatch ticking gamely on, enchilada foil stuck to his horns. "Eleven seconds! New record! Attempt two: eleven seconds!"

"I'M SO SORRY," Clarisse bellowed, horrified. She ran over and hauled Grover to his feet like he weighed nothing. "Satyr down! I repeat, satyr down! Somebody get him a towel!"

Silena laughed and sat down in the shallows after the stream had fallen. Percy called a pause while Grover and Ella checked their equipment. Nobody started a new attempt until the table was dry and the lane had been marked again.

Clarisse stood in the collapsing foam, blinking seawater from her eyes, and then started laughing too, a great rough bark of laughter. "Okay! Okay! The sea claims its own! Fine!"

Thalia doubled over, clutching her sides. Annabeth tried to stay serious and take notes, but her pencil kept slipping because she was giggling. Even Ella, after rescuing her tablets, made a small chuckling clicking sound deep in her throat.

"Attempt two notes," Ella dictated to herself, "Poseidon anchor strong. Poseidon aim terrible. Satyr moist. Harpy damp but resilient. Morale high."

Grover shook the sand from his stopwatch and raised his whistle. "Before anyone tries again, I want to check that we can stop from every station. Not with water. No sparks. Just the signal."

Clarisse wiped her face with the hem of her shirt. "We just stopped."

"We stopped because the water hit the table and everyone was looking at the mess," Annabeth said. She pointed her pencil at the painted marks in the sand. "That's not the same as noticing a signal when you're watching the channel."

Chiron came down from the viewing path, keeping to the open side of the lane. His expression was kind, but he did not wave the attempt away as a joke. "A rehearsal will take less time than a mistake. Where will each of you be looking when the arch is up?"

Percy looked at the beach. Silena would watch the lower curve; Clarisse would watch its far edge; his attention would be on the crown and the current. Thalia would look toward the bronze. Annabeth would stand on dry ground where she could see the gap. The answer gave him a clear view of the water, but not of every face.

"From the target, I won't see Annabeth's hand if she's behind the plate," Thalia said. She shifted a few paces, found the view blocked by the dummy, then moved back. "And if I'm watching the bronze, I can't watch Percy too. I need the stop sign where it won't disappear behind the arch."

Annabeth took a red card from Ella's tablet case and held it high. From the inlet, Percy saw it above the chalk line. He nodded. Clarisse turned away to check the target; the red vanished behind her shoulder. Annabeth moved to the side until Clarisse could see the card without turning her back on the channel.

Grover tried two sharp whistle blasts. The first carried across the sand. A wave broke just as he blew the second, and its sound vanished under the surf. Clarisse kept her eyes on the target and didn't react. Grover lowered the whistle. "That's not good enough."

"I didn't hear it," Clarisse said. She looked annoyed at herself, not at him. "I was checking my edge. If that's the only warning, I could miss it again."

"Then it won't be the only warning," Annabeth said. She laid the red card on the table and asked Grover to stand at the viewing mark. Percy went to the inlet; Silena and Clarisse took their stations; Thalia stood by the bronze. Annabeth raised the card once. Everyone saw it. She called, "Stop," and every person answered by saying their own name and stepping backward. The movement was awkward and uneven. They tried again, and this time the five answers came before anyone moved.

Grover blew two blasts. Percy heard one and saw the card rise. Silena echoed the call; Clarisse repeated it from the far side. Thalia raised her left hand. The message reached each station, though by a different route. Ella wrote the result in red, then drew the clear sightline in blue so they could tell which part belonged to the setup and which to the signal.

"Anybody can call stop," Chiron said. "The card is a second way to make it visible. The whistle warns the people at the viewing line to step back. If one of those fails, the others still matter."

Clarisse walked to her mark again and checked that the card remained visible from there. "Fine. And when we're working, we don't stare at one thing so hard we miss the rest. I can check the boundary, but if I lose sight of the red, I tell Percy before we lift."

Silena glanced toward Grover's soaked table. "We should move the records upwind, too. Ella can't call a problem if she's busy saving the ink."

Ella gathered the dry tablets under her wing and hopped to a patch of firm sand beyond the lane. "Ella chooses a place with less splash. Ella likes records that can be read afterward."

Percy checked the sightline from each station. The stop card was small against the sea, but the chalk boundary made it easy to see. When the rehearsal ended, the five returned to their marks to review what had gone wrong with Attempt Two before lifting the water again.

Percy waded over to Annabeth and flopped down beside her in the sand. "How bad?"

Annabeth showed him her notes. One sketch tracked the water rope and the moment Clarisse adjusted its end; another showed Thalia's strike to the separate plate. Between Percy's call of "hold" and the water team's release, she had marked a half-second delay. The two paths had never touched, but the delay explained why the water kept moving after the stop call.

"You're holding shape well," she said. "Especially Silena. Her end is steady. The problem is our signal: Clarisse adjusted after I called hold, and I kept the arch up while she was correcting it. We need a count that tells everyone when to hold and when to drop."

"So we need to sync better?" Percy asked.

"We need a count," Annabeth said. "Like music. Or rowing." She looked up the beach toward Grover, who was trying to dry his stopwatch on his shirt. "Grover! Can you call cadence?"

Clarisse set down her helmet and rubbed a thumb across the chalk at her station. "I moved the head after Percy called hold. I thought he wanted a correction, not a freeze. Next time I'll say 'adjusting' before I move it. If the answer is 'drop,' I drop it."

"I laughed and let go of the tail," Silena said. She was smiling still, but her voice had gone steadier. "I didn't mean to make Percy catch the whole thing. I can keep a rhythm without trying to hold everything by myself."

Percy looked at the wet patch between their stations. He had been trying to keep the center, the tip, and the people around him in mind at once. "If I say 'hold,' I mean keep your part where it is. If I say 'drop,' I let the whole shape go. I won't try to fix the edge for you."

Annabeth wrote the two calls beside the diagram. "And anyone can call stop. We don't wait for me or Chiron. If one person says it, everyone ends the attempt."

Thalia touched the dry wrap with her left fingertips. "I'll call stop if the spark feels bigger than the target, even if it's already in the air. Nobody needs to talk me into one more try."

"Nobody is going to," Percy said. He wanted to add a joke, then saw the set of her jaw and left it alone.

Clarisse looked at her, then gave a short nod. "Good. Same rule for us. We all stop on the first call."

The group's line of stations changed on Annabeth's page. There was now a clear gap between the water route and the bronze plate, and another between the plate and the dry spectators' mark. It was a small drawing; Percy could see how much trouble it had saved them from.

Grover brightened instantly. "Can I? I was born to call cadence! I can count to four without rhyming about goats. Usually."

He tried the calls once without any powers. "Ready," he said. "Hold. Adjust. Drop."

Clarisse lifted two fingers. "If I say 'adjusting,' that's information, not a move. I wait until Percy tells me the center is steady."

"And if I say 'hold,' nobody changes anything," Percy said. "If I say 'drop,' I let go. I won't try to rescue the shape while you're moving it."

Silena tapped a quiet rhythm on the side of Grover's table. "Then I can keep the lower curve on the count. If I lose the rhythm, the water comes down."

They tested the calls with a narrow water loop that stayed over the open sand. Percy raised it only to waist height. Silena kept one side even, and Clarisse called "adjusting" before she moved the far edge. Percy waited for her to finish. The loop steadied, then a gust pushed it outside the chalk. Annabeth called "stop" before it could reach the target. They all released at once.

The water fell. No one reached for it after it crossed the line.

"That's the point," Annabeth said. "We don't save a bad shape. We make another one when we're ready."

Ella recorded the calls beside the wet mark. Grover read them back in order until even Clarisse stopped correcting his rhythm.

"Perfect," Annabeth said. She turned to Thalia. "Can you make a spark no larger than the one you just showed me, and keep it pointed at the plate? We can try a separate target after the water exercise."

Thalia frowned and held up her finger. A tiny spark popped. "Like this?"

"That size," Annabeth said. "One count, toward the bronze plate. We don't need a bigger bolt to learn whether the aim is true."

"Stroke, don't punch," Clarisse advised, shaking sand from her boots. "Like with a spear. You don't throw your whole body every time. Just the tip."

Thalia blinked. "That sounded dirty."

"EVERYTHING sounds dirty to you," Clarisse said loftily.

Silena came up the beach, her cover-up clinging, her braid dripping, and slipped her arm around Thalia's shoulders. "You're doing great. Really. That second one was so controlled. Percy said his hair only stood up a little."

"Only a little," Percy confirmed, pointing at his hair, which was sticking up in damp spikes. Annabeth reached up without thinking and smoothed it down, her fingers lingering just a moment on his forehead. Percy went very still and smiled.

Thalia watched Annabeth smooth Percy's hair, and the teasing left her face for a moment. Percy remembered the story she'd told about her little sister hiding in her bunk during storms. He couldn't know what Thalia was remembering now, but for a beat she didn't tease them. Then her mouth tilted.

"You guys are cute," Thalia said abruptly. "Disgustingly cute. Stop it before I puke lightning."

"We're not doing anything!" Annabeth protested, snatching her hand back, her ears turning red.

"You smoothed his hair," Thalia said. "That's practically a marriage proposal in demigod years."

"It was sticking up!" Annabeth said.

Percy grinned and smoothed his own hair down. "I liked it."

"See?" Thalia said to Silena. "Cute. I'm going to tell Mom."

"You do that," Annabeth muttered, burying her face in her notebook. But she was smiling so hard the pencil shook.

---

Attempt three lasted twenty-two seconds and ended when a curious hippocampus surfaced under Silena's intake and startled her so badly she squealed and dropped the whole rope.

No one minded, because the hippocampus, a beautiful dappled mare named Rainbow, stayed to watch, clicking happily and blowing spray from her nostrils while Percy scratched her nose and introduced her around.

"Ella records: hippocampus interference," Ella said, delighted, sketching a horse head in the corner of her tablet. "Rainbow says water was tasty. Rainbow says lightning smells spicy. Rainbow will observe from safe distance."

Grover marked the time. "Twenty-two seconds! You're getting longer every time! That's what she—never mind. Juniper told me not to say that anymore."

"Wise advice," Silena said primly, but her eyes danced.

Annabeth sat cross-legged in the sand with her notebook across her knees, chewing on her pencil, watching the way the water fell back into the sea. Percy flopped down beside her and offered her half his granola bar. She took it without looking, ate it, and only then realized what she had done.

"That was yours," she said.

"We share," Percy said simply. "S'what we do."

Annabeth looked at the sand stuck to his cheek and reached over to brush it away. When she noticed he had gone quiet, she lowered her hand and smiled at him as if she knew exactly why. Percy did not need her to explain what the old memories meant; the warmth between them belonged to this afternoon too.

"Okay," she said, turning back to her notes to hide her face. "New idea. Clarisse, you're muscling the head too much. You're an Ares girl—sorry, Poseidon girl with Ares training. You try to force it. Let Percy shape the tip. You just supply power from behind, like pushing a chariot."

Clarisse considered this, then nodded slowly. "I can do that. I hate aiming anyway. Aiming is for Apollo kids."

"And Silena," Annabeth continued, "your intake is flawless, but you're holding your breath. I can see it. Breathe with the waves. In, out. Grover can call breathing too."

"I can!" Grover said eagerly. "In! Out! I took prenatal breathing classes with Juniper's cousin! Well, I observed!"

"And Percy," Annabeth said, turning to him. "You're doing three jobs. Center, tip, and watching all of us. Let go a little. Trust us. Just hold the middle and trust Clarisse won't drown you."

Percy met her eyes. "I trust you all. Easy."

"And Thalia and me," Annabeth said, tapping the dry ground between the marked lanes. "We work the sky side together. Thalia aims the strike. I watch the target and call the correction. Neither of us touches the water, and we don't try to carry power through it."

Thalia raised an eyebrow. "You can do that? Since when?"

"I've practiced a short pulse on the dry bronze target with Will watching," Annabeth said. "I can keep one count steady. I haven't tested anything through water, and I won't. You aim the spark; I read the line and call if it starts to wander. Sisters."

Thalia stared at her little sister for a long moment, then held out her fist. "Sisters."

Annabeth bumped it. "Sisters."

Clarisse stood and cracked her neck. "Okay, Poseidon kids! Huddle!"

Percy and Silena joined her, arms around each other's shoulders, heads together. "Sea on three," Clarisse said. "One, two, three—"

"SEA!" they shouted.

"Zeus kids! Huddle!" Thalia said, grabbing Annabeth around the neck and pulling her in. "Sky on three! One, two, three—"

"SKY!" they yelled, laughing.

Grover blew his whistle. "Attempt four in one minute! Ella, ready?"

"Ella ready," Ella said, with fresh dry tablets. "Ella predicts less red this time. Ella hopes."

Rainbow the hippocampus whinnied encouragement from the surf.

---

Attempt four was the first time they brought the two exercises together without joining the elements.

Grover called the count. "Ready. Lift. Two. Three. Hold. Two. Three. Steady. Two. Three."

The water rose in a narrow arch and stopped at the painted boundary below the bronze plate. Silena kept the tail even with a slow breath. Clarisse held the far edge from shifting; she stopped adjusting whenever Percy called the shape steady. Percy guided the center with small movements of his hands, keeping the stream clear of the chalked strike lane.

Annabeth watched the gap between water and target. She waited for the arch to settle, then called, "Clear. One count. Aim for the plate."

Thalia lifted her uninjured left hand and sent a short blue-white spark across the open air. It struck the bronze with a clean ping. The suspended drops caught the flash and scattered it into a trembling line of reflected light, but the lightning did not enter the water.

Annabeth counted once, then sent her own smaller spark. It followed the same air gap and met the plate beside Thalia's mark. The water arch held while the metal rang twice. Ella's beak parted in delight. Grover nearly forgot to stop his watch, then looked at Annabeth's card.

"Stop," Annabeth said.

Percy let the stream fall. It returned to the inlet in a soft rush, well away from the marked lane. Thalia lowered her left hand. The wrap on her right palm remained dry. No one had touched the water during either strike.

For a breath, nobody spoke. Then Silena looked at the two faint marks on the plate and laughed. "We made a target ring."

"The sea side held its boundary," Annabeth said, already writing. "The sky side hit the receiver twice. The parts stayed separate. That is a successful test, not permission to skip the next check."

She set the notebook down and flexed her fingers once. The work had tired her, but she was steady on her feet. Percy waited instead of reaching for her. "Do you want to stop here?"

"Yes," she said. "We got the answer we came for. I want to keep the rest of my energy for walking back, not prove I can keep going."

Thalia gave her a look of approval, then caught herself before making it a speech. "Fair. I'll carry the tablets."

Silena brought Annabeth a cup of water. "And I saved the least-soggy sandwich. It's not a medical treatment. It's lunch."

Annabeth took it. "That is exactly the distinction I wanted."

Clarisse checked Grover's stopwatch. "Twenty-eight seconds from lift to stop. The plate rang twice. Put that in the record, not 'we nearly fried everybody.'"

"Ella records: two clear strikes, water untouched, stop called by Annabeth," Ella said, marking the notes in blue and yellow. "Ella will not write 'sea conducted lightning.' That would be wrong. Ella likes being right."

Grover checked the stopwatch. "Twenty-eight point four seconds, including the clear call. Five seconds with the arch steady. Ella, underline that part."

"Ella underlines two times," Ella said. She showed them her drawing: a blue arch ending before a bronze circle, with two thin gold lines reaching the circle through open air. "Separate paths. Same count."

Annabeth ate half the sandwich and drank the water. When she leaned briefly against Percy's shoulder, she chose the contact; Percy kept still until she moved away. "Sorry I kept the others waiting," she said.

"You called stop when you meant it," Percy said. "Nobody was waiting on you."

Annabeth huffed a tired laugh. "I always beat you at Capture the Flag."

"Not always," Percy said. "Once I hid in a bush for an hour and you couldn't find me."

"I let you win," Annabeth said. "You looked sad."

"I did not look sad!"

"You looked like a kicked puppy."

"I did not—"

Thalia and Silena exchanged glances over their heads and simultaneously mouthed, Cute.

Grover called a lunch break. They ate peanut butter sandwiches and apples in the shade, Rainbow dozing in the shallows, Ella reading back her notes aloud in her strange singsong voice that made even mistakes sound like poetry.

"Ella reads Attempt Four," Ella intoned. "Water held outside the strike lane. Two short sparks hit the plate through open air. The water reflected the light; it did not carry it. Annabeth called stop. Harpy dry. Satyr proud."

Everyone applauded. Ella bowed, feathers fluffing with pleasure.

Annabeth rested with her head in Thalia's lap while Thalia braided her hair. Silena rubbed sunscreen into Clarisse's shoulders. Percy and Grover skipped stones, Percy cheating with tiny currents.

After lunch, Annabeth pulled Percy aside, down to the water's edge where the waves whispered. She looked rested, color back, eyes bright, but serious.

"I need to ask you something," she said quietly. "And I need you to be honest, not brave."

"Okay," Percy said.

"When we made the strikes, did the water move? I couldn't tell from where I was standing. And don't answer just to make me feel better."

Percy remembered the promise they had made after the first attempt. "No. The water didn't move when either strike landed. I saw the flash in it, but it was reflection. I didn't feel a current through the stream."

Annabeth nodded slowly and added the answer to her notes. "Good. We keep the same gap at the beacon. No touching, no water-to-metal contact, and no strike until the channel is stable." She hesitated. "Are you scared of the distance? That's a much bigger hold."

Percy looked up the hill, where the bronze beacon glinted. Holding an arch that far would take everything the three of them had.

"A little," he admitted. "But not about getting hurt. About letting you down. If I drop it—"

"You won't," Annabeth said with absolute certainty. "You never drop me."

Percy swallowed. At thirteen, with the sun on the water and his best girl looking at him like he hung the moon, he felt brave enough to do anything.

"Then I'm not scared," he said.

Annabeth smiled, small and fierce and fond. "Good. I have a plan for the beacon. But I'm done with practice today."

She closed the notebook and turned it so Percy could see the page. The water arch ended before the bronze target; the lightning lane remained a separate line through the air. She had crossed out the old version, where she had drawn the elements meeting.

"I thought I could manage a bigger exercise," she said. "I wanted to finish it before we stopped. That's not a good reason to stay until I'm exhausted."

"You said you were done. I heard you." Percy rested his hands in the sand instead of reaching for the notebook. "I was worried you'd try to prove something. I don't want that worry to turn into deciding for you."

Annabeth studied him for a moment. "Ask once. Listen to the answer. If I want to stop, I want you beside me, not carrying me away before I say so."

"I can do that."

"And the camp can celebrate the light if we make it. It doesn't get the story of the sick night." She tapped the edge of her drawing. "I already told you what I wanted shared."

"I remember. The message and the toast. Nothing about the dream."

She nodded, satisfied. Their old memories made some kinds of worry easier to name and others harder to separate from the present. Percy had loved her before this life; he still had to listen to the person sitting beside him now.

---

Annabeth's plan, sketched on a dry page after lunch, gave each power a separate route and one shared count.

"We were thinking of a straight line from the beach to the hill," she said, gathering them around the diagram. "That is long and hard to control. The water can make an arch that marks our direction, but it stops short of the beacon. The lightning goes separately through the air to the bronze receiver. We use the same count to coordinate them; we do not join the paths."

"Like a rainbow," Silena said, delighted.

"Like a signal and its reflection," Thalia said. "The water shows the line. The strike hits its own target."

"One brief spark after the water arch is stable," Annabeth said. "We don't touch the channel. Grover calls the count, and the lightning goes through open air to the bronze receiver. If the water moves into that line or the bolt misses its target, we stop."

Clarisse studied the diagram, then nodded grudgingly. "That's—actually smart, Grace."

"I have my moments," Annabeth said.

"And safety?" Grover asked anxiously. "Chiron said no fried demigods."

"Ella thought of safety," Ella piped up, proud. "Ella wrote: nobody stands below the water arch or in the strike lane. The water and lightning never touch. Grover says 'clear' before anyone starts. One call of 'stop' ends the exercise; two whistle blasts mean everyone steps back. Ella wrote it down."

"Those are clear rules," Annabeth said. "We'll use them, and stop if anything leaves its lane."

Silena re-braided her hair. Clarisse tied her shirt tighter. Thalia kept her wrapped right hand close and chose her left for the short practice spark. Annabeth checked the painted target and the separate dry lane one more time. Percy took three breaths and felt the sea rise to meet him.

They did not lift the water again that afternoon. Chiron folded the plan and brought them to the hill so they could test the walking route with their feet instead of their powers. The beacon stood above the pine, its bronze rim dark in the late light. A worn path curved around the slope; Annabeth marked where it crossed the proposed strike line and crossed the path out of the exercise area.

"The camp trail stays open," she said. "So the water cannot pass over it, and the strike lane cannot point across it. We move the starting station farther east. The arch ends here, short of the receiver."

Chiron asked where the water would go if someone called stop while the arch was still up. The marked return lane led down the open side of the beach, away from the camp trail and the cabins. Percy walked it with Clarisse while Silena checked the lower edge for rocks. They found one loose stone near the chalk and moved the boundary a pace toward the surf.

"If the wind pushes it toward the path, we release toward the inlet," Silena said. "We don't try to steer around anyone."

Percy traced the new fall zone with his toe. It was broad enough to give them room, but only if each person kept to the agreed edge. "I can let the crown go in that direction. If I lose the line, I won't pull it back across the beach."

Clarisse checked the clear route to the water and nodded. "Then nobody has to guess which way the spill goes."

Annabeth changed the blue line in her notebook and marked the return lane with arrows. Chiron checked the drawing against the ground before approving it. They walked the route once without powers, then left the chalk and flags in place for the morning.

Clarisse crouched beside the chalk mark and checked its distance from the beacon. "Then I'm holding this edge. If it creeps toward the path, I let go."

"And I'll keep the tail low enough not to spray anyone," Silena said. She did not volunteer to hold the whole channel. She asked Chiron where he wanted the last support and chose a spot that left her room to step clear.

Percy traced the water's proposed curve in the air with one finger. The arch could be shaped from the inlet without anyone standing beneath it, but he could not make it answer a command he had not practiced. "I'll hold the crown. If I can't keep it inside those marks, I'll drop it."

"Good," Annabeth said. "No saving the shape after the boundary is gone. We stop, and we start again another day."

Thalia checked her wrapped palm with her left hand. Will had looked at it during the morning recheck: the dressing was still dry, but the burn remained tender. He had not cleared her to spar or brace with it. She could use her left for one small strike, or watch from outside the line. She chose the left hand and kept her right arm at her side.

Ella held up the tablet with her notes. "Ella records names and stations, not private sickness. Ella asks before a face goes in the drawing."

Annabeth's expression eased. "Thank you. Put the date as unknown. We don't have one."

The five walked the lane once more, then left the beacon dark. Chiron repeated that the exercise had no deadline. Annabeth sat through dinner with the others, ate what she wanted, and went to bed before the cabin grew loud. Percy did not ask whether she felt well enough to stay longer. He had heard her answer already: she was finished for the day.

The next morning, Will checked Annabeth before she returned to the hill. She had remained fever-free for the required interval, had no dizziness, and had managed an ordinary walk without symptoms. He cleared one brief, low-power exercise under his conditions: no prolonged hold, no sparring, and stop at the first sign of fatigue or a returning symptom. Thalia's palm remained tender; her hand would not be used to brace, and she could leave whenever she wanted.

They returned only after Annabeth agreed. Will stayed with Chiron at the viewing line while Grover walked the route and repeated the calls: "clear" before the arch, "hold" only when every station was ready, and "stop" from anyone at any point. Two whistle blasts meant all powers down and everyone stepping back. Ella checked that she could see both the receiver and the end of the arch from her recording place.

Annabeth stood at the start of the dry lane with her card. "If I can't see the gap, I call stop. If the arch crosses the chalk, I call stop. If anyone feels unsure, they can call it too."

"That is a lot of stopping," Clarisse said, but she checked the line again and moved one shell marker a pace farther out. "Fine. More room means I don't have to guess."

Thalia flexed her left hand once, then let it hang loose. She checked the dry wrap over her right palm but did not touch the target or raise a spark.

"One short strike," she said. "If it hurts, I stop before there's a second one."

"Then we do one," Annabeth said, "and stop after it."

The wind lifted the flags Chiron had placed at the edge of the lane. Percy felt it push at the loose water near the beach. Silena looked up the slope; Clarisse covered the bronze target with one hand to shade it from the moving glare.

Annabeth lowered her card. "Wait. The top flag is moving."

No one argued. Percy let the water at his feet settle back into the inlet. Grover stopped the watch. They waited while Chiron checked the flags and the camp trail. When the gust passed, the flags hung still again. Annabeth did not raise the card until she had asked Will, then each of the four others, whether they were ready.

Percy answered yes. Silena said yes. Clarisse checked the line, then nodded. Thalia looked at Annabeth and waited for her call.

Grover took his position with the whistle and stopwatch. "One short attempt, then we review. Ella, mark the time."

"Ella marks three seventeen in the afternoon," Ella said, stylus poised. "Sun westering. Wind calm. Hippocampus awake. Harpy nervous but ready."

Rainbow whinnied from the surf as if she understood.

The Poseidon siblings took their stations outside the channel, each far enough away that the water could pass without touching them. Thalia and Annabeth stood behind the dry chalk line. Thalia kept her wrapped right hand at her side; Annabeth held the stop card where Grover could see it. The beach below the arch was empty. Chiron had sent the curious campers to a viewing place beyond the marked lane.

Percy looked to Clarisse. She nodded once, fierce and ready. Silena checked the tail and then met his eyes. She had room to step away if the shape crossed its boundary.

Annabeth looked down at the gap between the water's end and the distant bronze receiver. Percy could see her counting the empty space. She waited until the arch path was clear, then gave Grover a small nod.

"Ready," Grover called. "Lift on my mark. Three. Two. One."

They lifted.

The water rose as a smooth arch from the inlet, each section held at its own station. Silena shaped the lower curve. Percy raised the crown. Clarisse kept the far edge from drifting toward the camp trail. The arch ended several feet before the bronze receiver on Half-Blood Hill. It did not touch the beacon, the target lane, or any person.

The span looked improbable from the shore. Percy had to keep his attention on its center, where the water thinned and the wind pressed it sideways. Clarisse called once when the edge shifted; Percy corrected by a handspan, and she did not try to compensate for him. Their separate jobs made the arch steadier than any of the earlier attempts.

Grover checked the stopwatch. "Clear. One count."

Thalia lifted her left hand and sent a narrow spark through open air. Annabeth watched the gap and called, "A little left." Thalia adjusted. The strike hit the bronze receiver with a single bright note.

The water caught the light as reflection. It glittered blue and white, but no lightning entered it and no drop reached the bronze. The pre-measured Greek fire in the beacon's basin took a small, steady flame.

Annabeth raised the stop card. "Stop."

Percy released the crown. Silena let the lower curve fall back toward the inlet. Clarisse stepped clear of her station. Thalia lowered her arm. The water descended on the open side of the lane, well away from the receiver, and returned to the sea. The beacon's flame stayed at the height Chiron had approved.

For five seconds the arch had held while the receiver caught. Then it was gone. Ella had stopped writing. Grover checked the card, then the watch. Rainbow whinnied from the surf. Chiron looked at the flame, then at the clear space between bronze and water.

Clarisse whooped, loud enough to send the gulls up from the path. "We did it. And nobody had to stand in the middle of it."

Silena laughed and pulled Clarisse into a hug. Thalia stepped in for a one-armed hug with her left; Annabeth hugged her back, then stepped away when she was ready. Grover blew his whistle once, the agreed signal that the exercise was over. Ella stood on tiptoe to see the flame above the grass, then checked the page where she had recorded the water boundary and the single strike.

Percy walked toward Annabeth only after the channel had fallen and Chiron had called the lane clear. She met him halfway, smiling through the tiredness.

"You held the arch," she said. "Clarisse caught the drift before it crossed the line. Silena kept the lower curve from spreading."

"You saw the angle," Percy said. "Thalia got the strike where you called it."

Annabeth's fingers brushed his. "We did it together."

He offered his hand. She took it, and they stood for a moment with their shoulders touching, looking up at the flame. Around them, their sisters argued cheerfully about which part had been hardest. There was no need to turn the handhold into a new promise; the familiar warmth between Percy and Annabeth had been there before the beacon and remained after it.

"Hi," Percy said stupidly.

"Hi," Annabeth said back, squeezing his fingers.

Thalia saw it and covered her mouth with both hands to stifle a squeal. Silena saw it and pressed her hands to her heart. Clarisse saw it and pretended to gag, but she was grinning so hard it ruined the effect.

"Twenty-eight seconds from lift to stop," Grover reported, checking the watch. "Five seconds with the arch steady. One clean strike. That's the record for this drill. Ella, underline it."

"Ella underlines twice," Ella said, using her yellow stylus. "Ella draws the gap. Ella draws the water and the strike separately. Ella does not write private things without asking."

They walked back along the open side of the beach, seven campers and one hippocampus, with sand on their shoes and the beacon reflected in the water. Percy and Annabeth kept their hands joined until the group reached the path.

At the viewing place, Chiron waited with lemonade and a clear copy of the safety plan. Campers had gathered beyond the marked lane, where they could see the beacon without crossing the route.

"Well done," Chiron said. "The beacon is lit, the route stayed clear, and everyone stopped when asked. That is what I wanted from this exercise."

A younger camper came as far as the viewing boundary and called to Annabeth, "Are you all better now?"

Annabeth glanced once at Percy, then answered for herself. "Will cleared me for this short exercise. I was sick earlier. That's all I want to share about it."

"Got it," the camper said. Their eyes went back to the flame. "Can Ella show us how the water stopped before the beacon?"

Ella turned to Annabeth before lifting her tablet. Annabeth nodded. "The route and the signals are fine to show. Leave the rest out."

Ella held the page where the viewing group could see the blue arch ending before the bronze circle and the gold line crossing open air. She read the stopping calls, then lowered the tablet. No one asked what Annabeth had dreamed or why she had spent a night in Cabin Three. The story they watched was the one the group had chosen to tell: the beacon, the boundary, and the signal that ended the attempt.

Annabeth leaned her head briefly against Percy's shoulder. He rested his temple against her hair, then let her straighten when she wanted to look at the beacon again.

"Sea remembers," Ella whispered, writing the final line on her tablet as the beacon burned green-gold behind them. "Sky remembers. Camp remembers. Ella remembers."

---

That night they ate dinner together on the Poseidon cabin porch, all five of them plus Grover and Ella, sharing pizza that Silena had sweet-talked from the kitchen naiads and watching the beacon burn steady on the hill. The wind had dropped. Somewhere down the path, campers were still talking about the light, but the cabin porch belonged to their group again.

Clarisse had her feet up on the railing, her arm around Silena, retelling Attempt Two with exaggerated gestures. "And then—SPLASH! Grover's enchiladas are swimming! Ella's reds are pink! Percy's hair is standing straight up like a sea urchin!"

"My hair looked great," Percy protested, with his fingers still laced through Annabeth's under the pizza box where they thought no one could see.

Thalia kicked his shin lightly and winked. "Sure it did, Kelp Head."

"It did," Silena said. "The trouble was that the water line moved while we were laughing. Next time I want to try the lower curve from the other side. I think I can hold it without keeping the same rhythm as Clarisse."

Clarisse nodded, mouth full. "Fine. I can hold the edge. You pick the pace."

Grover had Ella's clean copy on the table beside his plate. "I wrote down the time and the calls. I didn't write that it was a miracle or that one of you saved the others. We all did our jobs."

"Ella's record is useful," Ella said. "Ella's first page was wet. This one says what happened. Ella likes this one better."

Annabeth looked over the copy. "And it doesn't say anything about why I was sick."

"No," Grover said. "That's yours to tell, if you ever choose to."

"I don't need to," she said, then nudged Percy beneath the table with her knee. He didn't ask a follow-up.

Annabeth's eyes began to close against Thalia's side. The day's work had used the strength she had kept in reserve, and she did not try to hide the tiredness.

"Hey, Wise Girl," Thalia murmured. "You okay?"

"Mm-hm," Annabeth said. "Best day. We lit it. Together."

"Yeah, we did," Thalia said softly. "Dad would be so proud he would zap something. We should write Mom too."

Annabeth opened her eyes and looked toward the path to Cabin One. "I want my own bunk tonight. I need to look at the camp map in the morning; one corner still bothers me."

Thalia didn't ask whether she was sure. "Want me to walk with you?"

"Yes. I can carry my notebook."

Percy squeezed her hand once. "I'll see you tomorrow."

"At breakfast," Annabeth said. "And if we write Mom, I decide what goes in the letter. The beacon is a story I want to tell. The sick night stays mine."

Thalia nodded. "Your story, your choice."

Silena folded the leftover paper plates and slid them into a bag. Clarisse, without a word, shifted her chair to block the wind.

Percy watched his sisters and Annabeth's sister give her room to choose what she needed, and felt his throat go tight. This was family in a form he knew and a form he was still learning: they could disagree about the plan, ask before helping, and still be there when the work was done.

Grover raised his lemonade cup. "Toast! To sea and sky!"

"To sea and sky!" they echoed, clinking cups and pizza crusts.

"Ella toasts to remembering!" Ella said, lifting her own cup of birdseed tea. "Ella will write it all down so no one forgets!"

Annabeth sat up, thanked Silena for dinner, and stood without taking anyone's arm. Percy walked beside her and Thalia to the fork. Annabeth squeezed his hand. "Breakfast tomorrow," she said. "And I want to look at my map before we meet."

"I'll walk you to Cabin One," Thalia said. She waited for Annabeth's nod, then went with her down the path. Percy returned to the porch. He had just started stacking plates when Thalia came back and leaned on the rail beside him.

"I saw you wait for her to say stop," Thalia said. "Keep doing that."

"I will," Percy said. "She gets to choose what help looks like."

"Good," Thalia said. "Because if you don't, I will fry you like Straw Bob."

"Fair," Percy said.

Clarisse snorted. "Get in line, Grace. I'm his sister. I get first punch."

"Second punch," Silena corrected sweetly. "I'm eldest. I get first hug, then first punch."

The others laughed while they gathered the plates. The beacon held its small green-gold flame on the hill. Annabeth had her own bunk and a map to finish; what she told their mother would be her choice. Percy watched the light until the fountain's steady splash drew his attention back to the porch.
