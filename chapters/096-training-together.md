## Chapter 96: Training Together

The morning after Annabeth Grace's fever broke dawned blue and clean over Camp Half-Blood, the kind of morning that made the whole valley smell like strawberries and salt.

Percy Jackson woke early in the Poseidon cabin with sunlight striping across his face through the shell-shaped windows. For a moment he just lay there listening to the fountain in the corner trickling away, to the soft breathing of the cabin that was, for once, not empty. His big sister Clarisse snored faintly on the top bunk across the room, one arm thrown over her eyes. His other big sister Silena was already awake, sitting cross-legged on her bed with her hair in a loose braid, mending a strap on her armor with small, neat stitches.

Percy sat up and rubbed his eyes. "Is she awake yet?" he whispered.

Silena smiled without looking up. "Annabeth? Thalia texted Grover on the Iris-message at dawn. Fever's gone. She's cranky and hungry, which means she's better."

Percy felt his whole chest loosen. The day before had scared him more than he wanted to admit, watching Annabeth sway and go pale in the arena and then sleep through the long fever watch in Cabin Three while Will Solace fussed and Thalia refused to leave her side. He had sat on the floor by Annabeth's guest bunk half the night holding a cool cloth, until Thalia had ordered him to bed.

"Good," he said quietly. "Good."

Clarisse snorted awake, sat bolt upright, and glared at nothing. "Who's dying? What's on fire?"

"Nothing's on fire," Silena said, amused. "Your little brother was worrying."

Clarisse scrubbed her face and looked at Percy, her expression softening in the way it only ever did in the cabin, when no one from Ares was watching. "Grace kid's tough. Zeus kids don't stay down. She'll be up and bossing us around by lunch."

Several days later, after Will had cleared Annabeth to resume gentle training, there was a knock on the cabin door, sharp and precise. Three raps. Percy knew that knock.

He scrambled out of bed so fast he nearly tripped over his own sneakers and yanked the door open.

Annabeth Grace stood on the porch in the morning light, her blonde curls pulled back in a ponytail, her gray eyes clear and bright again, her cheeks pink with health instead of fever. She wore her camp orange shirt and jeans and her Yankees cap tucked in her back pocket, and she had a notebook under one arm and a pencil behind her ear. Behind her stood Thalia Grace, tall and dark-haired with her silver circlet glinting, grinning like she had invented mornings.

"Hi, Seaweed Brain," Annabeth said.

Percy stared at her for a full three seconds and then broke into the biggest grin of his life. "Hi, Wise Girl. You're supposed to be on bed rest."

"Will cleared me," Annabeth said primly. "Light duty. No sparring till tomorrow. Strategy and powers only. And before you ask, yes, I ate breakfast. Thalia watched me eat two eggs and toast to make sure."

"I counted bites," Thalia confirmed solemnly. "Twenty-four bites. Very thorough."

Silena appeared behind Percy and opened her arms. "Oh, thank the gods. Come here, baby."

Annabeth allowed herself to be hugged. Silena squeezed her tight. Clarisse came stomping over, checked her color, nodded, and ruffled her hair.

"Color's back," Clarisse pronounced. "Good. You looked like Hades yesterday."

"Lovely to see you too, Clarisse," Annabeth said dryly, smoothing her hair, but she was smiling.

Thalia leaned against the doorframe and crossed her arms. "So. Chiron wants us. All five. Big Guy's pavilion. Ten minutes."

Silena raised an eyebrow. "Swimsuits and brains? That's oddly specific."

"That's Chiron," Annabeth said. She tapped her notebook. "I bet I know what it is. I've been thinking about it since yesterday. While I was stuck in bed doing nothing."

"You were sleeping," Thalia said.

"I was thinking between sleeping," Annabeth said with dignity. "Come on."

---

Chiron waited for them in the Big House meeting room with his wheelchair in its human disguise, a tray of lemonade on the table, and a large map of Camp Half-Blood unrolled and weighted down with seashells.

Grover Underwood was already there, his satyr legs folded under him, a brand-new stopwatch around his neck that he kept polishing proudly. Beside him, perched on a tall stool with her feet tucked up, was Ella the harpy, with her ruffled red feathers, her bright intelligent eyes, and a stack of wax tablets and styluses piled higher than her head.

"Ella will record," Ella said when they walked in, half to herself. "Ella records everything. Training together. Water and lightning. For remembering."

"Ella volunteered to be our scribe," Grover explained. He beamed at the five demigods filing in. "And I'm the timer! Chiron said joint training needs an impartial judge, and my hooves are very impartial."

Annabeth sat down next to Ella and smiled at her. "Hi, Ella. Did you bring the blue stylus?"

"Ella brought blue and red and green," Ella said, pleased that Annabeth remembered. "Blue for water. Yellow for lightning. Red for mistakes. Ella likes red best. There will be many reds at first."

Clarisse snorted. Thalia laughed. Percy sat beside Annabeth, close enough that their shoulders touched, and felt the familiar calm settle over him that always came when she was near and healthy.

Chiron cleared his throat. "Thank you all for coming. As you know, this summer has asked a great deal of our two eldest divine families. Poseidon and Zeus have not always, ah, trained well together."

Thalia muttered, "That's one way to put it. Dad and Uncle P have thrown hurricanes at each other for three thousand years."

"Exactly," Chiron said. "Which is why what I am about to propose is both difficult and necessary. Individually, you are formidable. Percy can move more water than any child of Poseidon I have taught in decades. Clarisse has unmatched control under pressure. Silena has the finest touch for sustained shields. Thalia can call lightning that would make her father proud. Annabeth, even at thirteen, has a tactical mind for electricity that rivals Athena's own children."

Annabeth blushed to the tips of her ears. Percy nudged her knee with his under the table.

"But together," Chiron continued, "you have never trained as a unit. Water conducts lightning. Uncontrolled, that is deadly. Controlled, it could light our beacon. I want the three children of Poseidon to anchor, and the two daughters of Zeus to discharge."

He tapped the map. A line ran from the canoe lake to the top of Half-Blood Hill, where the old bronze beacon stood, dark and unused since the last war.

"The beacon," Annabeth breathed. Her eyes lit up. "I've read about it. It used to burn with Greek fire to call the camp to arms. It hasn't been lit in years because no one can get enough sustained power to the top without wiring."

"No wires needed," Chiron said. "If you can carry a charge through water from the lake to the hill without grounding it out or shocking each other. Percy, Clarisse, and Silena will form a living aqueduct. Thalia and Annabeth will feed lightning into it in pulses. Grover will time your holds. Ella will record every attempt so we can learn."

Silena leaned forward, fascinated. "A water bridge that carries lightning? Like a river of light?"

"Like the Sea remembers the Sky," Ella whispered, scribbling. "Ella likes that. Sea remembers Sky. Pretty words."

Percy looked at Annabeth. Annabeth looked at Percy. He could see the wheels turning behind her gray eyes, the diagrams already drawing themselves.

"When do we start?" Annabeth asked.

Chiron smiled. "Right after you change. Beach in twenty minutes."

---

The beach glittered white in the late morning sun. Grover had set up a folding table with his stopwatch and whistle. Ella sat in the shade with her tablets in neat rows.

"Attempt one in five minutes," Grover announced importantly, clicking his stopwatch. "Ella, mark the time. Ten forty-two."

"Ella marks ten forty-two," Ella repeated, scratching in blue. "Sun high. Wind west-southwest. Harpy hungry but professional."

Clarisse stood at the water's edge in her black swimsuit and camp shirt tied around her waist, her arms crossed, her dark hair pulled back tight. Silena wore a blue one-piece with a white cover-up, her dark hair braided down her back. Percy wore swim trunks and an old camp shirt, barefoot in the sand, already feeling the pull of the water like a second heartbeat.

Thalia and Annabeth stood a little farther up the beach, in shorts and camp shirts, their feet dry. Thalia's hair crackled faintly even in the sunshine. She rolled her shoulders, and tiny sparks snapped between her fingertips. Annabeth held her notebook, flipping to a fresh page covered in neat sketches of arcs and waves.

"Okay," Annabeth said, all business. "Theory first. Water conducts electricity, but salt water conducts better than fresh, and moving water disperses charge faster. If Percy, Clarisse, and Silena just make a big puddle and Thalia zaps it, we'll all get fried. We need structure."

"We need a hose, not a lake," Thalia translated for Clarisse, who had started to glaze over.

"Exactly," Annabeth said. "A contained stream, lifted off the ground, insulated from the sand. Poseidon kids hold the shape. Zeus kids feed in short bursts. Like—like threading a needle with lightning."

Clarisse cracked her knuckles. "So we make a water snake, you ride it with zappy fingers, and we don't die."

"That's the plan," Annabeth said.

"Great plan," Clarisse said. "I love plans where we don't die."

Silena laughed and bumped her younger brother's shoulder. "Percy, you're strongest at lifting. You take the center. I'll take the tail at the lake, keep the intake steady. Clarisse, you take the head, aim it at the target."

"What target?" Percy asked.

Grover blew his whistle. "Target practice first! No beacon till you can hit the dummy!"

He pointed down the beach, where he and Juniper had wrestled a straw training dummy dressed in an old Greek helmet and holding a tin shield. The shield had been painted with concentric circles in red and white.

"Distance: fifty feet," Grover said. "Goal: water stream touches shield, lightning rides stream, shield pings. Without knocking over Ella's umbrella. Or me."

"Ella does not want to be electrocuted," Ella said firmly. "Ella has been electrocuted before. Zero stars."

Percy waded in to his ankles. The water welcomed him, cool fingers curling around his feet. He closed his eyes and reached out with that inner sense he had always had, the one that told him where every drop was. The sea answered instantly, eager, joyful.

"Ready?" he called.

Clarisse stepped in to his left, Silena to his right. The three Poseidon siblings joined hands for a moment, instinctively, the way they always did before big workings. Percy felt his sisters' power settle alongside his own, Clarisse's like iron wrapped in heat, Silena's like silk over steel, his own like deep tide. Together they breathed in.

"On three," Percy said. "One. Two. Three."

They lifted.

A thick rope of seawater rose from the surf with a roar, as wide as Percy's waist, twisting and sparkling in the sun. Silena held the base steady where it met the lake, her face calm with concentration, her free hand smoothing the flow. Percy held the middle, keeping it aloft and moving, his arms raised like a conductor. Clarisse gritted her teeth and aimed the head toward the dummy, her whole body leaning into it.

The water snake wavered, then straightened, humming.

"Whoa," Thalia breathed. "Okay. That's awesome."

"Hold it!" Annabeth shouted, running forward with her notebook forgotten. "Hold it right there! Thalia, low charge, three-second pulse, on my mark! Aim for the middle of the stream, not the kids!"

Thalia raised her hand. The air tasted suddenly of metal and ozone. Her blue eyes went almost white with gathered charge. Sparks crawled up her arm.

"Mark!" Annabeth yelled.

Thalia threw the bolt.

It was too much. Way too much. A jagged white spear slammed into the water rope with a crack like the sky breaking. The water flashed to steam. The shockwave knocked all three Poseidon kids backward into the surf. The dummy exploded into burning straw. Ella's umbrella flipped inside out. Grover's stopwatch flew off his neck and landed in the sand.

"Ella records: too much lightning," Ella announced, scribbling furiously in red. "Very red. Very loud. Dummy deceased. Harpy alive, thank gods."

Percy sat up in the shallows, soaked and ringing, seawater in his ears. "Is everyone okay?"

Clarisse surfaced beside him, spitting water, her hair plastered to her face. "I'm fine! That was AWESOME! Do it again, but less!"

Silena coughed and laughed at the same time, holding her braid out of the water. "My ears are ringing in E major."

Thalia stood frozen with her hand still raised, horrified. "Oh gods. Oh gods, I'm so sorry. Are you—did I hurt you?"

Annabeth was already splashing toward Percy, her notebook abandoned on the sand, her face white. "Percy! Clarisse! Silena! Talk to me!"

Percy grinned up at her and held up a thumb. "We're good, Annie. Just got a bath."

Annabeth stopped knee-deep in the water, her hands on her knees, breathing hard with relief and leftover fear. "Don't grin at me like that. You could have been—Thalia, that was triple what we planned."

"I know," Thalia said miserably, hurrying down to help pull the Poseidon kids to their feet. "It slipped. I get excited and it just—comes out big. Sorry, sorry."

Clarisse clapped Thalia on the back hard enough to stagger her. "Hey. No moping. First try always blows up. That's tradition. My first chariot race ended with a wall on fire."

"My first water shield collapsed on a naiad's picnic," Silena added cheerfully, wringing out her cover-up. "She still won't talk to me."

Percy stood and shook water from his hair like a dog, spraying Annabeth. She squeaked and batted at him, and they both laughed, the scare dissolving.

Grover retrieved his stopwatch and blew his whistle. "Attempt one: six seconds hold, zero control, one dummy killed in action. Ella, note heroic sacrifice of Straw Bob."

"Ella notes Straw Bob the Second gave his life for science," Ella said solemnly, starting a fresh tablet. "Ella will sing his saga later. In under twelve syllables, as is traditional."

Annabeth retrieved her notebook, now damp at the edges, and flipped to a new page. Her hands were still shaking a little. Percy noticed and gently took the pencil from her.

"Hey," he said quietly, so only she could hear. "We're okay. You kept us safe. You called the mark perfect. We'll just tune it."

Annabeth looked up at him, her gray eyes meeting his green ones. "Promise you'll tell me if it hurts? Even a little tingle? You always try to tough it out."

"I promise," Percy said. "You promise you'll tell me if you're getting tired? You're still getting over being sick."

"I promise," Annabeth said.

They linked pinkies on it, solemn as an oath on the Styx, and Thalia, watching from a few feet away with her arms crossed, smiled a soft older-sister smile that she quickly hid by pretending to stretch.

---

Attempt two was better and worse.

Better because Thalia managed a smaller bolt, a neat crackling arc no thicker than her wrist. Worse because Clarisse sneezed at exactly the wrong moment and the head of the water rope whipped sideways and drenched Grover, his table, his enchiladas, and half of Ella's tablets.

"Ella's reds are wet!" Ella wailed, holding up a smeared tablet. "Ella's beautiful reds! Now they are pink! Pink is not science!"

Grover sat in the sand, dripping, his stopwatch ticking gamely on, enchilada foil stuck to his horns. "Eleven seconds! New record! Attempt two: eleven seconds!"

"I'M SO SORRY," Clarisse bellowed, horrified. She ran over and hauled Grover to his feet like he weighed nothing. "Satyr down! I repeat, satyr down! Somebody get him a towel!"

Silena was laughing so hard she had to sit down in the surf, which made the base of the water rope collapse, which made Percy lose the middle, which dumped another hundred gallons directly onto Clarisse.

Clarisse stood in the collapsing foam, blinking seawater from her eyes, and then started laughing too, a great rough bark of laughter. "Okay! Okay! The sea claims its own! Fine!"

Thalia doubled over, clutching her sides. Annabeth tried to stay serious and take notes, but her pencil kept slipping because she was giggling. Even Ella, after rescuing her tablets, made a small chuckling clicking sound deep in her throat.

"Attempt two notes," Ella dictated to herself, "Poseidon anchor strong. Poseidon aim terrible. Satyr moist. Harpy damp but resilient. Morale high."

Percy waded over to Annabeth and flopped down beside her in the sand. "How bad?"

Annabeth showed him her notes. She had drawn the water rope with little arrows for flow, and Thalia's bolt with a jagged line, and where they met she had drawn a starburst and written TOO LATE — 0.5 sec lag.

"You're holding shape great," she said. "Especially Silena. Her intake is perfect. But Thalia's discharge is hitting after Clarisse micro-adjusts. So the charge meets a moving edge and sprays."

"So we need to sync better?" Percy asked.

"We need a count," Annabeth said. "Like music. Or rowing." She looked up the beach toward Grover, who was trying to dry his stopwatch on his shirt. "Grover! Can you call cadence?"

Grover brightened instantly. "Can I? I was born to call cadence! I played reed pipes for the marching band at Camp Jupiter one time! Well, I was kicked out, but still!"

"Perfect," Annabeth said. She turned to Thalia. "Thalia, can you do less than half of what you just did? Like, think about lighting a candle, not smiting a Titan."

Thalia frowned and held up her finger. A tiny spark popped. "Like this?"

"Yes!" Annabeth said. "Do that, but a little bigger, and hold it for two counts instead of throwing it."

"Stroke, don't punch," Clarisse advised, shaking sand from her boots. "Like with a spear. You don't throw your whole body every time. Just the tip."

Thalia blinked. "That sounded dirty."

"EVERYTHING sounds dirty to you," Clarisse said loftily.

Silena came up the beach, her cover-up clinging, her braid dripping, and slipped her arm around Thalia's shoulders. "You're doing great. Really. That second one was so controlled. Percy said his hair only stood up a little."

"Only a little," Percy confirmed, pointing at his hair, which was sticking up in damp spikes. Annabeth reached up without thinking and smoothed it down, her fingers lingering just a moment on his forehead. Percy went very still and smiled.

Thalia watched them and felt something warm and achy in her chest, not jealousy, just that big-sister feeling of watching your baby sister grow up too fast. She had been there when Annabeth was seven and afraid of thunderstorms, hiding in Thalia's bunk while Thalia told her stories about Zeus to make the thunder less scary. Now Annabeth was thirteen and diagramming thunderstorms in a notebook with a pencil behind her ear.

"You guys are cute," Thalia said abruptly. "Disgustingly cute. Stop it before I puke lightning."

"We're not doing anything!" Annabeth protested, snatching her hand back, her ears turning red.

"You smoothed his hair," Thalia said. "That's practically a marriage proposal in demigod years."

"It was sticking up!" Annabeth said.

Percy grinned and smoothed his own hair down. "I liked it."

"See?" Thalia said to Silena. "Cute. I'm going to tell Mom."

"You do that," Annabeth muttered, burying her face in her notebook. But she was smiling so hard the pencil shook.

---

Attempt three lasted twenty-two seconds and ended when a curious hippocampus surfaced under Silena's intake and startled her so badly she squealed and dropped the whole rope.

No one minded, because the hippocampus, a beautiful dappled mare named Rainbow, stayed to watch, clicking happily and blowing spray from her nostrils while Percy scratched her nose and introduced her around.

"Ella records: hippocampus interference," Ella said, delighted, sketching a horse head in the corner of her tablet. "Rainbow says water was tasty. Rainbow says lightning smells spicy. Rainbow will observe from safe distance."

Grover marked the time. "Twenty-two seconds! You're getting longer every time! That's what she—never mind. Juniper told me not to say that anymore."

"Wise advice," Silena said primly, but her eyes danced.

Annabeth sat cross-legged in the sand with her notebook across her knees, chewing on her pencil, watching the way the water fell back into the sea. Percy flopped down beside her and offered her half his granola bar. She took it without looking, ate it, and only then realized what she had done.

"That was yours," she said.

"We share," Percy said simply. "S'what we do."

Annabeth looked at him, at his sunburned nose and his sea-green eyes and the sand stuck to his cheek, and felt that familiar flip in her stomach that had nothing to do with leftover fever. Being thirteen again had not taken away the love she remembered. She still liked sitting next to him more than anywhere else.

"Okay," she said, turning back to her notes to hide her face. "New idea. Clarisse, you're muscling the head too much. You're an Ares girl—sorry, Poseidon girl with Ares training. You try to force it. Let Percy shape the tip. You just supply power from behind, like pushing a chariot."

Clarisse considered this, then nodded slowly. "I can do that. I hate aiming anyway. Aiming is for Apollo kids."

"And Silena," Annabeth continued, "your intake is flawless, but you're holding your breath. I can see it. Breathe with the waves. In, out. Grover can call breathing too."

"I can!" Grover said eagerly. "In! Out! I took prenatal breathing classes with Juniper's cousin! Well, I observed!"

"And Percy," Annabeth said, turning to him. "You're doing three jobs. Center, tip, and watching all of us. Let go a little. Trust us. Just hold the middle and trust Clarisse won't drown you."

Percy met her eyes. "I trust you all. Easy."

"And Thalia and me," Annabeth said, taking a deep breath. "We need to share discharge. Thalia's raw power is too spiky alone. If we both touch the stream at the same time, I can smooth it. Like a resistor."

Thalia raised an eyebrow. "You can do that? Since when?"

"Since the sick watch in Cabin Three when I was bored and Will let me hold his defibrillator paddles to see what they'd feel like," Annabeth said. "Kidding! Kidding! Don't look at me like that. I've been practicing small static holds. I can do a steady hum. You do the spark, I shape it. Sisters."

Thalia stared at her little sister for a long moment, then held out her fist. "Sisters."

Annabeth bumped it. "Sisters."

Clarisse stood and cracked her neck. "Okay, Poseidon kids! Huddle!"

Percy and Silena joined her, arms around each other's shoulders, heads together. "Sea on three," Clarisse said. "One, two, three—"

"SEA!" they shouted.

"Zeus kids! Huddle!" Thalia said, grabbing Annabeth around the neck and pulling her in. "Sky on three! One, two, three—"

"SKY!" they yelled, laughing.

Grover blew his whistle. "Attempt four in one minute! Ella, ready?"

"Ella ready," Ella said, with fresh dry tablets. "Ella predicts less red this time. Ella hopes."

Rainbow the hippocampus whinnied encouragement from the surf.

---

Attempt four was the one they would talk about for weeks, not because it worked, but because it almost did.

Grover called cadence in his clear tenor bleat. "Ready! Lift! Two! Three! Hold! Two! Three! Steady! Two! Three!"

The water rope rose again, smoother this time, humming with steady flow. Silena breathed with the waves, in and out, her face serene. Clarisse fed power from the back, her jaw set, her eyes on Percy's shoulders. Percy shaped the middle and the tip with delicate flicks of his fingers, his whole body relaxed, trusting.

"Now!" Annabeth called. "Thalia, spark! I'm with you!"

The Zeus sisters stepped forward together and placed their hands on the water rope, Thalia's right, Annabeth's left, side by side.

Light ran up the water.

It was the most beautiful thing Percy had ever felt. Not a shock, not a burn, but a warm tingling rush that sang through the stream from base to tip, lighting it from within like a vein of sunlight. The water glowed blue-white, every droplet lit. The air sang. Ella gasped and dropped her stylus. Grover forgot to click his stopwatch.

For five perfect seconds, sea and sky were one river.

Then Annabeth's knees buckled. She was still recovering, still weaker than she admitted, and holding the smoothing field took more out of her than she expected. Her hand slipped. The charge wobbled, grounded through the wet sand with a bang and a flash, and the rope burst into spray.

Everyone was fine. Everyone was laughing and shouting. But Annabeth sat down hard in the sand, pale and shaking, and Percy was there before she hit, his water work forgotten, his arms around her.

"Whoa, hey, I got you," he said. "Breathe. You're okay."

"I'm fine," Annabeth whispered, frustrated tears bright in her eyes. "I ruined it. It was perfect and I ruined it. Stupid fever. Stupid—"

"Hey," Percy said firmly, tilting her chin up so she had to look at him. "No. You made it. That glow? That was you. You did that. We just need to build your stamina back up. That's not ruining. That's step four."

Thalia knelt on Annabeth's other side, her own hand tingling, her heart pounding. "Percy's right, baby. That was the best we've ever done. You held enough charge to light a trireme and you shaped it. I just supplied the juice. You're the artist."

Silena brought Annabeth water and a square of ambrosia, fussing like a mother hen. "Eat. Drink. No arguments. You're still getting well."

Clarisse stood guard over them all, her hands on her hips, seawater dripping from her chin, grinning fiercely. "Twenty-eight seconds! Grover, tell her the time! Tell her!"

Grover checked his stopwatch, which he had finally remembered to stop. "Twenty-eight point four seconds! With full illumination for five point two seconds! Ella, write that down with gold!"

"Ella has no gold stylus," Ella said, but she was beaming, scratching in blue and yellow as fast as she could. "Ella writes big. Ella draws glow. Look, look." She held up the tablet, where she had drawn the water rope with radiating lines and two small stick figures holding hands on it, labeled SEA and SKY.

Annabeth ate the ambrosia and drank the water and let her siblings fuss, which for Annabeth was a miracle of humility. She leaned against Percy's shoulder, just for a minute, because she was tired and he was solid and warm.

"Sorry," she murmured.

"Stop apologizing," Percy murmured back. "Best five seconds of my life. Besides the time you beat me at Capture the Flag."

Annabeth huffed a tired laugh. "I always beat you at Capture the Flag."

"Not always," Percy said. "Once I hid in a bush for an hour and you couldn't find me."

"I let you win," Annabeth said. "You looked sad."

"I did not look sad!"

"You looked like a kicked puppy."

"I did not—"

Thalia and Silena exchanged glances over their heads and simultaneously mouthed, Cute.

Grover called a lunch break. They ate peanut butter sandwiches and apples in the shade, Rainbow dozing in the shallows, Ella reading back her notes aloud in her strange singsong voice that made even mistakes sound like poetry.

"Ella reads Attempt Four," Ella intoned. "Water rose like serpent. Sky touched sea. Sea sang. Sky sang. Sisters held. Siblings held. Glow lasted five heartbeats. No one fried. Straw Bob the Second avenged. Harpy dry. Satyr proud."

Everyone applauded. Ella bowed, feathers fluffing with pleasure.

Annabeth rested with her head in Thalia's lap while Thalia braided her hair. Silena rubbed sunscreen into Clarisse's shoulders. Percy and Grover skipped stones, Percy cheating with tiny currents.

After lunch, Annabeth pulled Percy aside, down to the water's edge where the waves whispered. She looked rested, color back, eyes bright, but serious.

"I need to ask you something," she said quietly. "And I need you to be honest, not brave."

"Okay," Percy said.

"When Thalia and I touched the stream, did it hurt you? Even a little? Truth."

Percy considered lying, then remembered his pinky promise. "Not hurt. It tingled. Like when your foot falls asleep, but nice. Like soda bubbles. I could feel you in it, actually. Your part felt different from Thalia's. Hers was hot and sharp. Yours was—steady. Like humming."

Annabeth nodded slowly, absorbing this. "Good. That means my smoothing works. I was afraid you'd all feel the full spike." She hesitated. "And are you scared? For the beacon? That's way farther. Way more power."

Percy looked up the hill, where the bronze beacon glinted. Holding a water bridge that far would take everything the three of them had.

"A little," he admitted. "But not about getting hurt. About letting you down. If I drop it—"

"You won't," Annabeth said with absolute certainty. "You never drop me."

Percy swallowed. At thirteen, with the sun on the water and his best girl looking at him like he hung the moon, he felt brave enough to do anything.

"Then I'm not scared," he said.

Annabeth smiled, small and fierce and fond. "Good. Because I have a plan for the beacon. And you're going to love it."

---

Annabeth's plan, sketched in damp notebook pages and explained with flying pencil gestures, was insane, brilliant, and utterly Annabeth.

"We've been thinking straight line," she said, gathering them all around her diagram in the sand. "Lake to hill, straight uphill. That's the longest path, maximum gravity, maximum dispersal. Stupid." She drew a swooping curve. "Instead we go arc. Silena launches high from the lake, Percy carries the middle in a high arch like an aqueduct, Clarisse catches and aims down into the beacon's basin from above. Gravity helps on the way down. Shorter hold time, less power needed."

"Like a rainbow," Silena said, delighted.

"Like a circuit," Thalia said, catching on. "Up and down, not just up."

"Exactly," Annabeth said. "And instead of one big discharge, Thalia and I do pulses. Three short touches, not one long hold. Grover calls rhythm. One-two-touch, one-two-touch. Like a heartbeat. That way I don't burn out, and the water doesn't overheat."

Clarisse studied the diagram, then nodded grudgingly. "That's—actually smart, Grace."

"I have my moments," Annabeth said.

"And safety?" Grover asked anxiously. "Chiron said no fried demigods."

"Ella thought of safety," Ella piped up, proud. "Ella thought all morning. Water team wears shoes. No bare feet on wet sand for grounding. Sky team touches only with fingertips, one hand behind back, like lightning safety posters. If anyone yells STOP, everyone lets go and Grover blows whistle twice. Ella wrote it down."

"Ella's safety rules are excellent," Annabeth said warmly. "We'll follow them exactly."

They put on water shoes. Thalia tucked her left hand behind her back with a mock-solemn vow. Annabeth did the same. Silena re-braided her hair. Clarisse tied her shirt tighter. Percy took three deep breaths and felt the sea rise to meet him.

Grover took his position with his whistle and stopwatch, suddenly grave. "This is it, guys. Beacon attempt. Ella, mark time."

"Ella marks three seventeen in the afternoon," Ella said, stylus poised. "Sun westering. Wind calm. Hippocampus awake. Harpy nervous but ready."

Rainbow whinnied from the surf as if she understood.

The three Poseidon kids waded in to their knees and joined hands. The two Zeus sisters stood on dry sand just above the tide line, right hands extended, left hands clasped behind their backs.

Percy looked down the line at his sisters. Clarisse nodded once, fierce and proud. Silena smiled, calm and bright.

Percy looked at Annabeth. Annabeth looked at Percy. She mouthed, Trust me.

He mouthed back, Always.

"Ready!" Grover called. "Lift on my mark! Three! Two! One! LIFT!"

They lifted.

This time the water rose as a smooth arch, glittering. Silena fed it from below, swaying gently. Percy held the crown with both hands high. Clarisse caught the descending arm and aimed it toward the hill.

The arch stretched, and stretched, and reached.

From the beach it looked impossible, a bridge of sea reaching for the sky, thinning as it went but holding, holding.

"It's reaching!" Grover shouted, forgetting cadence. "It's—it can't—it's—"

"CADENCE, GROVER!" Annabeth shouted, laughing despite herself.

"Right! Sorry! Pulse one! Three! Two! One! TOUCH!"

Thalia and Annabeth stepped forward as one and touched the base of the arch with their fingertips.

Lightning ran up the rainbow.

The whole arch lit from within, blue-white-gold, a river of light across the sky. On the hill, the beacon's basin caught the first splash of charged water with a hiss and a sputter.

Not enough. The light flickered.

"Hold!" Percy gasped. Sweat ran down his face. His arms shook. "Hold!"

"Pulse two!" Grover called. "Three! Two! One! TOUCH!"

Again the sisters touched. Again light ran. The beacon sputtered, sparked, caught a small flame that danced and died.

Clarisse growled with effort. "Come on! Come on, you stupid hill! We're right there!"

Silena's breath hitched, but she kept swaying, kept feeding. "Almost! Almost! Percy, a little higher!"

Percy dug deep, deeper than he ever had, calling to the sea not as a master but as a brother. Please, he thought. Please, for Annabeth. For camp. Just a little more.

The sea answered. The arch steadied, brightened.

"Pulse three!" Grover screamed. "THREE! TWO! ONE! TOUCH!"

Thalia and Annabeth placed their hands on the water together and held, pouring everything they had into one final shared pulse. Thalia's wild sky met Annabeth's steady hum and braided together, gold and white, running up the water like dawn.

The beacon caught.

It started as a spark in the basin, then a flame, then a roar of Greek fire green and gold and blue, leaping twenty feet into the air with a whoosh that flattened the grass on Half-Blood Hill. The light hit the water arch and refracted everywhere, turning every droplet into a prism, throwing rainbows across the beach, across the camp, across the faces of five kids staring up in disbelief.

The beacon was lit. Sea and sky had lit it together.

For a full ten seconds, the arch held, blazing, perfect. Ella stood up under her umbrella, stylus forgotten, mouth open. Grover stared at his stopwatch without seeing it. Rainbow reared in the surf and trumpeted joy.

Then, gently, as if by agreement, they all let go. The water fell back into the sea with a soft rush like sighing. The lightning faded. The beacon burned on, steady and bright, a new star on the hill.

Silence.

Then Clarisse screamed so loud the seagulls fled. "WE DID IT! WE LIT THE DAMN THING!"

Silena burst into tears and laughed at the same time, hugging Clarisse so hard they both fell over in the surf. Thalia grabbed Annabeth around the waist and spun her in a circle, both of them shrieking. Grover blew his whistle over and over, hopping on his hooves. Ella danced in place, feathers flying, chanting, "Beacon lit! Beacon lit! Sea remembers Sky! Ella saw it! Ella wrote it!"

Percy stood knee-deep in the water, breathing hard, his whole body trembling with exhaustion and triumph, watching Annabeth spin in her sister's arms with her hair flying and her face lit gold by the beacon. She caught his eye over Thalia's shoulder and mouthed, We did it.

Thalia set Annabeth down. Annabeth ran straight for Percy through the surf, laughing, crying a little, her notebook long abandoned.

"You did it!" she shouted. "Percy, you held it! You held the whole arch!"

"You lit it!" Percy shouted back. "That last pulse was all you!"

They met in the shallows with a splash. Annabeth threw up her hand for a high-five, grinning wide. Percy raised his hand to meet it.

Their palms slapped together, stinging, perfect.

And then, instead of pulling away, their fingers curled. Annabeth's hand slid down and caught Percy's. Percy caught back. They stood there in the foaming water with the beacon burning on the hill and their siblings cheering around them, holding hands, both blushing furiously, neither letting go.

It wasn't a big romantic kiss like in the mortal movies Silena loved. They were thirteen. It was simple and enormous: a high-five that turned into a hand-hold, salty and sandy and shaking with leftover adrenaline.

"Hi," Percy said stupidly.

"Hi," Annabeth said back, squeezing his fingers.

Thalia saw it and covered her mouth with both hands to stifle a squeal. Silena saw it and pressed her hands to her heart. Clarisse saw it and pretended to gag, but she was grinning so hard it ruined the effect.

"Young love," Grover sighed happily, clicking his stopwatch off at last. "One minute twelve seconds total hold. Thirty-one seconds full illumination. New camp record. Ella, underline that."

"Ella underlines three times," Ella said, doing so with her yellow stylus. "Ella draws heart around hand-hold. Ella is romantic. Ella cries a little. Do not tell Ares."

They walked back up the beach together, all seven of them plus one hippocampus, dripping and sandy and exhausted and glowing. Percy and Annabeth held hands the whole way, swinging them just slightly, pretending not to notice that they were doing it.

At the top of the beach, Chiron waited with lemonade and towels and the biggest smile Percy had ever seen on the old centaur's face. Behind him, half the camp had gathered on the path, drawn by the beacon's light, murmuring and pointing.

"Well done," Chiron said simply. "All of you. You have done what gods struggle to do. You have made sea and sky remember each other."

Annabeth leaned her head against Percy's shoulder for just a second, tired and happy. Percy bumped his temple gently against her hair.

"Sea remembers," Ella whispered, writing the final line on her tablet as the beacon burned green-gold behind them. "Sky remembers. Camp remembers. Ella remembers."

---

That night they ate dinner together on the Poseidon cabin porch, all five of them plus Grover and Ella, sharing pizza that Silena had sweet-talked from the kitchen naiads and watching the beacon burn steady on the hill.

Clarisse had her feet up on the railing, her arm around Silena, telling the story of Attempt Two for the third time with exaggerated gestures. "And then—SPLASH! Grover's enchiladas are swimming! Ella's reds are pink! Percy's hair is standing straight up like a sea urchin!"

"My hair looked great," Percy protested, with his fingers still laced through Annabeth's under the pizza box where they thought no one could see.

Thalia, who could see perfectly well, kicked his shin lightly and winked. "Sure it did, Kelp Head."

Annabeth was half-asleep against Thalia's side, her notebook closed for once, her head nodding. The day's work plus yesterday's fever had caught up to her. Thalia wrapped her arm around her and tucked her close.

"Hey, Wise Girl," Thalia murmured. "You okay?"

"Mm-hm," Annabeth mumbled. "Best day. We lit it. Together."

"Yeah, we did," Thalia said softly, kissing her sister's temple. "You were amazing. Dad would be so proud he would zap something. We should write Mom too."

Silena produced a blanket from nowhere and draped it over Annabeth. Clarisse, without a word, shifted to block the wind.

Percy watched his sisters and Annabeth's sister take care of his girl, and felt his throat go tight. This was family, he thought. Not just blood, not just godly parent. Choice. Training together, failing together, lighting beacons together.

Grover raised his lemonade cup. "Toast! To sea and sky!"

"To sea and sky!" they echoed, clinking cups and pizza crusts.

"Ella toasts to remembering!" Ella said, lifting her own cup of birdseed tea. "Ella will write it all down so no one forgets!"

Later, when the pizza was gone and Annabeth was fully asleep with her head on Percy's shoulder and her hand still in his, Thalia leaned over and whispered, "You take good care of her, Percy Jackson."

"I will," Percy whispered back. "Always."

"Good," Thalia said. "Because if you don't, I will fry you like Straw Bob."

"Fair," Percy said.

Clarisse snorted. "Get in line, Grace. I'm his sister. I get first punch."

"Second punch," Silena corrected sweetly. "I'm eldest. I get first hug, then first punch."

They all laughed quietly so as not to wake Annabeth. The beacon burned on, steady and bright, sea-fire and sky-fire braided together, visible all the way to the Sound.

Percy looked at it, then at Annabeth's peaceful face, then at his family sprawled around him on the porch, and squeezed her hand gently in his sleep-warmed fingers.

Training together, he decided, was the best kind of magic. Better than water alone. Better than lightning alone. Best when sea remembered sky, and sky remembered sea, and two thirteen-year-olds held hands under a burning beacon and didn't need to say anything at all.
