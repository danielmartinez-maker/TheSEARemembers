## Chapter 145: The Lake Keeps What It Takes

The boats had been tied down by the time they got back to the dock, but the skiffs rocked at their moorings, two green hulls tied to the cleats with the cord loose enough that Percy could have kicked them loose if he wanted to and did not, because Annabeth had tied the second one and he knew better than to trust his own memory for things she had already done. It was the hour after breakfast, the dock mostly empty, the sun climbing toward the middle of the sky. The lake was lower than it had been in the morning, or maybe the morning had risen away from it; either way the water sat close beneath the planks, dark and patient, full of the kind of quiet that made Percy think, every time, that if he leaned over far enough the lake might ask him what he was still doing here.

He said, "We're supposed to be checking the west mooring."

Annabeth said, "We are. In a minute."

"In a minute where?"

"In one that involves less standing around and more not talking."

He looked at her. She had one hand on the edge of the nearest skiff and the other behind her back, and the set of her shoulders was the look she got when she had decided the world was being unreasonable and was willing to meet it with the only weapon she trusted, which was a very determined small selfishness.

"What are you doing?" he asked.

"Nothing."

"You're hiding your hands."

"I'm not hiding them. I'm holding them."

"In a suspicious way."

"Yes. There's a suspicious way. Isn't it exciting."

He leaned over the dock. "I hate you."

"You don't."

"I do."

"You do not."

"I absolutely do."

"Liar," she said, fond and certain, and let her hands out and held them, palms up, as if she had been hiding treasure and was now offering it back in exchange for something better than treasure. "That's the bad kind of answer," he said.

"It's the honest kind."

"It's the kind that makes one of us start something."

"Maybe we both should."

He did not ask what she meant. She did not explain. She backed away from the skiff until her back was to the dock and the open water, and Percy made the mistake of letting his attention follow her, which was how he missed the plank giving way under her heel and throwing her sideways into the space under the dock with a sound like someone remembering a joke too late.

He went after her.

Not because he was fast. Because he was a son of Poseidon and the dock was old and the lake was close and fear had a way of making people move before they knew they were moving. He dropped through the gap in the boards and landed on the first beam with one knee and one hand and the taste of lake water in his mouth, and Annabeth was there on the next beam down, one hand braced on a joist, hair coming loose from the ponytail in the damp air, her eyes going wide — she had not expected to be caught under the dock, and she had definitely not expected him to drop through the planks after her like a bad idea that had learned to swim.

She said, "Percy."

He said, "You're not supposed to be down here."

"Neither are you."

"Clarisse told us not to."

"Clarisse also tells us not to breathe too loudly. That's not a real prohibition."

"It is if she's looking for an excuse."

"It is absolutely an excuse."

He stopped pretending to be helpful. The space under the dock was narrower than it looked from above and cooler than the water ought to have been, with boards overhead and lake light coming down in broken strips through the cracks, and Annabeth standing in it with her jeans cuffed wet at the ankle and her hair coming undone and her mouth set in that stubborn line she used when she had been caught doing something she had not meant to do and was now deciding whether embarrassment was going to win.

He stepped closer. Not all the way. Just enough to put the world between her and the rest of the dock, just enough to make the lake into a ceiling and the dock into a room and them into the only two people in it who knew how to breathe without making it obvious.

She did not move away. That was the first answer.

He said, "Annabeth."

She looked at him, and the look on her face changed from offended to awake to something quieter, the kind of quiet that meant a conversation had already started and nobody had announced it.

"You're trouble," she said.

"We established that."

"Under the dock too."

"Apparently."

She let her hand fall from the joist. Not all the way. Just enough that he could reach for it, and he did, and her fingers found his because they had been practicing that for a while now, ever since the morning had become the kind of morning where neither of them could be trusted with open daylight.

He said, very low, "Is this a good idea?"

She said, "No."

He said, "Then it's the right kind of idea."

That made her laugh, which made her face go from offended to bright to something else entirely, and he did not let her get away with it. He stepped in and kissed her, and she kissed him back with the same hungry little certainty she had used on him in the mess hall doorway, only here, under the planks with lake light on the boards above them and his thumb pressed to the inside of her wrist, it felt less like a public thing and more like a secret the lake had agreed to keep.

Not a polite kiss. Not a performance. It was the kind that came out of wanting the world smaller than it was and having the luck, for once, to be in a place where nobody could interrupt it without falling through the dock first.

She tasted like the cocoa she had made earlier and like lake water and like the clean cold smell of the cabin she had left on the way out. He kissed her until she stopped pretending to guard herself and kissed him back with both hands, one on his wrist and one on the front of his shirt, and he held her steady against the beam because if he held her any other way she would slide off and they would both go under and the lake was right there waiting to be a problem.

They broke apart too soon because they were fourteen and because the lake had a way of making time feel longer from below than it did from above, and because Thalia, somewhere above them, had started looking for them with the kind of attention that meant she had already decided not to be the sister who failed to notice anything.

Annabeth pressed her forehead to his shoulder for one heartbeat. "You're impossible," she said.

"We already know that."

"You mean me."

"I mean both of us."

"That's worse."

"It's fairer."

She made a small sound that might have been a laugh if it hadn't been so close to a gasp. "Thalia's going to find us."

"Let her."

"Clarisse too."

"Definitely."

"Silena definitely."

"Absolutely."

"Then we'd better make it worth the wait."

He kissed her again, and this time she met him with both hands on the front of his shirt and her back against the beam and the lake light flickering over them in broken pieces, and they stayed like that until the moment had gone past wanting and become something else, something that had lasted long enough to matter and not long enough to regret.

When he pulled back, her hair was coming loose in a way that made her look younger and older at once, and her mouth was pink from the lake air and from his, and she said, with the grave seriousness of someone who had just been caught by the world and was not sorry about any of it, "That was my idea."

"That was ours," he said.

She looked at him like that was an argument she had already lost and was not unhappy about losing.

They climbed out of the dock together, one after the other, with Annabeth brushing lake-lit dust from her jeans and Percy hauling himself up first and then reaching back to help her over the edge, and when they emerged into the daylight Thalia and Clarisse and Silena were all standing at the end of the dock in a row that looked deliberately staged and was probably exactly that, and Thalia had one hand over her mouth and Clarisse had her arms folded and Silena had the kind of grin that meant she had already counted three ways this could go.

Thalia said, "I have never been so glad and so annoyed at the same time."

Clarisse said, "Which is the family specialty."

Silena said, "I'm going to pretend I didn't see the climb."

"You did see the climb," Thalia said.

"I did. I saw the part where Percy dropped like a stone and Annabeth followed him into the dark, which is not what I expected from you two, and I'm keeping it private out of respect for the mystery."

Clarisse said, "There isn't any mystery. There's just Percy being Percy and Annabeth being Annabeth and Percy being Annabeth's problem."

Annabeth looked at her. "I'm not your problem."

Clarisse looked back at her. "No," she said. "You are."

Annabeth's mouth tightened. Then she looked at Percy, and Percy looked at her, and the three of them stood there with the lake behind them and the dock under them and the rest of camp waiting somewhere beyond the trees, and Percy said, very quietly, "That's the wrong answer."

Clarisse said, "I'm the oldest one here. I get to decide."

"You don't."

"I do."

"No you don't."

Clarisse said, after a moment, "No. I don't."

That surprised everyone, including Clarisse. She stood there with her arms still folded and her face carefully blank, and then she said, "Don't make me regret it."

Annabeth said, "I won't."

Clarisse said, "You say that like you're being charitable."

"I am."

Clarisse snorted, and for the first time all morning she looked almost younger than fifteen, which was a look people did not usually get to see and one that Silena made a note of without admitting it.

Thalia said, "If you two are done being disgusting under the dock, we still have a patrol to get to."

Percy said, "We are done."

Annabeth said, "We're always done."

Clarisse said, "You're lying."

"I know," Annabeth said.

Clarisse said, "That's exactly it. You're lying and you're happy about it."

"I am."

Clarisse looked at her, and then at Percy, and then at the boats and the lake and the rest of the dock and the sky that had climbed behind them while they were under the planks, and she said, with the exasperated affection of someone who had decided she liked them despite herself, "Fine. Let's go."

They went. Annabeth with Percy's hand in hers and Thalia pretending not to notice and Clarisse pretending she had not just admitted one thing she had not meant to and Silena pretending she had not been counting the whole time, and the day opened in front of them the way the lake had opened in the morning, slow and deliberate and full of things they had not yet asked for.

---

The dock at the canoe lake was the same dock Percy had sat on a thousand times in his original life, and the same dock he had sat on a hundred times in this one, and the weird thing was that both sets of memories felt true at the same time, as if the wood had two histories folded inside it like a deck of cards.

That flashed through his head now, standing on the same dock in the same morning light, and he let it go, because the present had enough of his attention already.

This morning, it held him and Annabeth at either end, with four feet of sun-warmed planks between them and no one else awake enough to notice what they were doing. He had woken before dawn, which annoyed him and thrilled him in equal measure. The annoyed part was simple: fourteen-year-old bodies did not run on Atlantean time, and his old habits from the seventh-watch guard shifts did not always translate to a surface camp that expected kids to sleep in until breakfast. The thrilled part was that Annabeth sometimes woke at the same hour, and if he got to the lake before she did, he got the first few minutes of the world all to himself, and those minutes belonged to him in a way nothing else in camp quite did.

The sky was still pale. The lake was still black at the edges and only beginning to silver in the middle, where a thin line of wind had opened a seam across the surface. The dock smelled like wet pine and last night's rain and the faint sweet rot of leaves trapped in the pilings. Percy sat with his sneakers off, one bare foot hooked around the edge of a plank, and watched the water do what water did when it was deciding what kind of day it wanted to be. Then her footsteps came up the ramp, and he knew it was her before he turned around, because nobody else walked with that exact set of pauses and weight shifts, as if she were crossing a blueprint in her head and making sure each step landed where it should.

Annabeth came to the end of the dock and stopped.

She had on an old camp hoodie that belonged to Thalia — it was too big for her in the sleeves, and she had rolled them twice so they wouldn't swallow her hands — and her hair was a mess in the way that meant she had not tried to make it neat and had not tried not to either, which was how Percy liked her best. Her sleep mask was bunched in one fist. Her eyes were already awake, already watching him, already doing the thing they did when she had been thinking about him in the dark and had not bothered to hide the evidence.

"Don't look so surprised," she said.

"I'm not," he said, and let her see that he was lying. "I'm just glad you're here."

She went very still at that, the way she always did when a truth hit close enough to bruise. Then she set her sleep mask on the end of the dock, sat down beside him with her knees tucked to one side, and said, "You woke me up."

"I did."

"I heard you. I tried not to."

"Succeeded."

"No."

He grinned, because she had come anyway, because she had dragged herself out of bed on the word of a boy she had loved in another life and loved in this one and was probably still deciding how much of that love she was willing to admit to in the cold gray of morning. He made room for her feet. She put them over his instep, warm through the blanket of camp dust and lake night, and leaned her shoulder against his.

That should have been enough. It was not.

"You're staring," she said.

"Because you're here."

"Because I'm breathing."

"That too."

She narrowed her eyes at him, but she was smiling under the sternness, and Percy let the grin stay until she put her hand flat on his knee and said, quietly, "You're impossible."

"I know."

"And reckless."

"Also yes."

"And apparently incapable of letting a good morning pass without trying to make it ours."

He turned his head to look at her. The lake was brighter now. The seam in the water had widened into a strip of moving silver. Somewhere across the far shore, a bird called twice and then stopped. The hoodie sleeves bunched around her wrists. Her wristwatch — the one Thalia had critically evaluated before letting her keep it — told him she had been up for eleven minutes already, which made her look more awake than she had a right to.

"I dreamed about us," she said.

There it was. No careful lead-in. No, *I had a dream, and it was interesting*, and no performance of casualness. Just the fact of it, laid out where he could see it. Percy felt something in his chest go soft enough to hurt.

"What about?"

"About New Rome. About the apartment. About the list we made on the kitchen whiteboard — the one with the grocery items and the furniture measurements and the stupid argument about whether plants counted as a real hobby."

He laughed, because that list had existed, because it had been ridiculous, because they had built a whole future out of ridiculous little things and then gone to sleep beside each other and woken up to find out the future was no longer theirs to keep.

"I remember the list," he said. "I remember you erasing the plant entry three times before you admitted you were just annoyed with me."

"You were being insufferable."

"I was right."

"You were not."

"I was."

"Percy."

He looked at her. She was looking back at him with that gray eye doing the exact work gray eyes did best — holding a conversation her mouth had not started yet.

"Last night," she said, "I dreamed we went back. Not to that apartment. Not to that list. To before. To when we thought we had years. And I woke up and you were beside me, and for one second I thought that was true, that this was the before, that everything had been put back the way it was."

"You're a terrible person."

"And you're nodding, which is cowardly."

"I'm not nodding."

"You are."

He was. He couldn't help it. The thought of her being seventeen in a bed that had belonged to them both, the thought of her turning over in the dark to find him there and thinking for one impossible second that the world had healed itself — that thought made the sleep-heavy room tilt around him.

"I was there," he said quietly. "For the one second."

And then, before either of them could do anything sensible with that, before Thalia could come down with her impossible radar and Clarisse could holler from the cabin about who was making the fish wake up, Annabeth reached across the dock and tugged the front of his shirt until he looked at her properly, and kissed him.

Not a question. Not a test. A thing that had been waiting all night in the dark between them and only now had found enough daylight to step out.

He kissed her back so instantly she might have thought he'd been planning to. He had been. He had been planning to since the moment she climbed the ramp and did not bother to pretend she had not been thinking of him all the way up.

For three heartbeats, the world shrank to the length of her mouth and the taste of sleep on her lip and the damp weight of her hoodie sleeve under his fingers. Then the first light hit the lake hard enough to flare, and the second set of footsteps came up behind them, and Thalia said in a voice that had the exact right amount of mock horror in it, "You two are disgusting."

They broke apart. Not because they wanted to. Because Thalia had arrived, because Clarisse was visible now on the dock ramp with her arms crossed and a towel over one shoulder, because Silena's laugh floated up from somewhere below them and said, with perfect timing, "Good morning, shameless people."

---

As soon as the kiss broke, Annabeth's first instinct was to pretend it had not happened. Not because she was ashamed. Because she was still the girl who had to wake up every morning and decide how much of the previous night she could afford to own. The dream was one thing. The kiss was another. The kiss belonged to the place where Percy's hand had been under her chin and his thumb had brushed her cheekbone and the whole world had gone small enough to fit between her lips and his, and that place was not the same as the place where she had to stand at breakfast and laugh at Clarisse and pretend she did not wake in the dark wondering whether she was still in love with a boy who had loved her across two different lives.

She sat back on her heels and tried not to look as wrecked as she felt. Tried not to let the sleep-creased corners of her mouth betray her. Tried, and failed, and then gave up when Clarisse said, "Your hair is doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The 'I have been thinking about a boy since midnight' thing."

Annabeth opened her mouth, which was a mistake. Everyone at the Poseidon cabin knew to watch Silena during a reveal; she had timing like a professional and conscience like a saint, which was a dangerous combination.

"I have not," Annabeth said.

"You're doing it right now."

"I am not doing it."

"You literally are," Silena said, pointing at her own mouth, because some truths were worth acting out. "The little pucker. The 'I just remembered I need to be annoyed about this' pucker. It's adorable. It's also a lie. You love it."

Annabeth's hand found Percy's sleeve and held on. "I do not love anything you're describing."

"Sure," Silena said. "That's why you're sitting in a puddle of morning light with your boyfriend and your sister and my sister, all of you pretending this is an innocent lake visit."

"I do not have a boyfriend."

Percy coughed. Thalia said, "Oh, gods, the fog is thick today," and Clarisse said, "Wow. The air is really polluted with denial."

"You're all insufferable," Annabeth said, but she was laughing, and the laugh was the honest kind, the kind that had nothing to do with defense and everything to do with having been caught in something better than she had planned for.

Thalia narrowed her eyes. "What time did you two get here?"

"Before you," Percy said.

"Liar."

"Not a liar. Early. We were already here when I heard you."

Annabeth glanced at him. He was still grinning. That should have annoyed her. It did not. It did the opposite, the opposite of annoyance being a warm and slightly dangerous thing she had spent a very long time learning to recognize as hers.

"And you were?"

"Counting your footsteps," Silena said. "I heard you. You do the thing where you stop counting at the exact moment you're late enough to be embarrassed."

"I do not."

"You do. It's your quiet alarm."

Annabeth turned on her, but Thalia was already there, leaning down over her shoulder with the expression of an older sister who had been up for hours and had opinions about morning people.

"Last night," Thalia said, "you were awake until two."

"So?"

"So I was awake until two as well, because I had to sleep in shifts and couldn't sleep, and I heard you get out of bed, and I heard you come back twelve minutes later, and I am not stupid."

Annabeth's mouth went carefully blank.

"That's not proof of anything," Percy said.

"Oh, thank you," Thalia said. "The boy I do not fully trust is suddenly my alibi."

"It's not an alibi."

"It is if you're refusing to tell me anything, which I am."

Annabeth looked at Percy. He looked at Annabeth. The space between them held all the things they had not yet said, plus a few things that had been said in other lives and were still living in the bones of both of them.

"Thalia," Annabeth said, "you do not have to interrogate us on the dock."

"I'm not interrogating you."

"You literally asked me what time we got here."

"I did that because I know you, Annabeth. I know when you're lying by the shape of your mouth before the words come out. And right now your mouth looks like it's hiding a fight."

She did not have a rebuttal for that, because Thalia was right.

The fight was not dramatic. It was smaller than that and more intimate. It was the ongoing argument between who she had been in the earlier life and who she had become in this one, between the girl who had planned New Rome with Percy and the girl who had to wake up beside Thalia and pretend to remember a childhood she did not have. It was the argument about whether she owed her sister the version of herself that could laugh at old stories and nod at inside jokes and sleep through the night without waking in a cold panic for a name she could not answer with. It was the argument about Percy, and how much of him belonged to the life she had lost and how much belonged to the one she was living now, and whether she was allowed to want both without flinching every time she was honest about it.

Percy could feel all of that from where he sat. He could feel it the way he had learned to feel shifts in water before they became waves.

"You're not hiding a fight," he said to Annabeth. "You're hiding a conversation you haven't finished yet."

She flinched a little. Thalia stiffened a little too.

Then Clarisse said, "About time," and Silena said, "This is my cue to be useful," which was how Clarisse and Silena had somehow turned a confrontation into a breakfast run and a lake supply expedition before Annabeth had even decided whether to answer the question in front of her.

"Thalia," Percy said, "you want coffee, or is this one of those mornings where you think caffeine is a personal insult?"

Thalia opened her mouth, considered, decided against both, and settled for, "I want a container."

A container for what?

"For whatever you're going to bring back from the mess hall that's actually edible."

Annabeth let out a breath she had been holding since Thalia arrived. The breath changed something in her chest — loosened a knot she had not known was there. Percy felt it. Of course he did. He always felt that kind of thing, the same way he felt the hill of camp before he saw it and the shift in the air before a storm.

"You're impossible," Annabeth said.

"So I've heard."

They sat with their shoulders touching, and Percy's hand had come to rest over hers on the dock between them, thumb tracing a slow small circle over her knuckles. He had been doing it since she sat down, and he had not once pretended it was nothing, which was a kind of courage she was still learning how to answer.

"It is the most natural thing in the world," he said. "I'm your boyfriend. I'm allowed to do that."

"You are not my boyfriend."

"This is unfair."

"It is true."

"Annabeth."

"I'm serious, Percy. I am not — I'm not one of the people who gets to pretend we already figured this out. I am the one who has to wake up and decide every morning whether I'm still in love with you or just still kind of haunted by the idea of you."

He took her hand, and she let him, and he turned it over so the back of her palm rested against his and said, "That is not what you are."

"That is exactly what I am," she said, but her voice had gone quiet, and the quiet meant she was listening to him now instead of defending herself.

"Listen to me," he said. "I am not asking you to choose between the person you were and the person you are. I'm asking you to stop punishing yourself for remembering us. You remember me because you loved me, not because you're weak. You remember New Rome because we built it, not because you're stuck. Those things are yours. They don't make you less of Annabeth. They make you more."

She looked at him in the way people looked at things that mattered. The lake behind them had begun to gold at the center. The hoodie sleeve had slipped down. Her wristwatch said it was almost time for breakfast, which meant it was almost time for Clarisse to holler and Silena to filch something sweet and Thalia to pretend she had not been waiting for all of it. None of that erased the moment.

"You're a good liar," she said.

"Yeah," he said. "For the record, I think you do remember. I think you remember enough."

She did not argue. She turned her hand so their fingers touched, then laced them together, then did not let go when Percy said, "Breakfast. Before Clarisse steals all the good bread."

---

Clarisse La Rue did not trust mornings. Mornings were when people were soft and tired and full of lies. Not the big lies. The small ones. The kind that let people get through breakfast without admitting they were afraid of the day.

She had been watching the dock from the cabin steps for seven minutes before she called down, "If either of you is planning to be late enough to make me wash the plates alone, let me know now, because I have opinions about fair labor."

That brought three heads up at once.

"Clarisse," Silena said, already laughing. "Good morning to you too."

"Good morning to me as well," Clarisse said, climbing down the steps with her duffel under one arm and the sort of expression that usually meant she was either about to hit something or about to save something. Today it was the second one.

She stopped on the ramp when she saw Percy and Annabeth and Thalia and Silena all on the dock in a cluster that could only mean trouble, and her eyes moved from face to face with the flat, appraising look she used on everything from spar partners to suspicious adults.

"Are you hiding something," she asked, "or did someone finally decide to tell Annabeth how to make coffee?"

Annabeth, who had been bracing for exactly that question since she was six years old, said, "We do not make coffee the way you mean."

"Then explain why Percy looks like he's been caught in something better than a sunburn."

Percy, who had been waiting for this, said, "I have."

Clarisse stared at him. Then she stared at Annabeth. Then she turned to Silena, who said, "I told you," and Clarisse made a noise that was halfway between a growl and a laugh.

"Of course you told me," she said. "You always tell me the useful bits after the fact."

"I'm working on the timing."

"You're working on the denial," Clarisse corrected. "Which is charming but not especially effective."

Thalia said, "He is not telling you. I am telling you. You two have been impossible since the lake thawed. You have been impossible since yesterday. You are impossible right now."

"Thank you, Thalia," Clarisse said dryly. "Someone had to say it."

Silena hooked her arm through Clarisse's and steered her toward the mess hall ramp. "Come on. If you want to hate them honestly, you'll need caffeine and stolen muffins."

"I do not steal."

"You borrow aggressively."

"Same thing."

"No," Silena said. "Different thing. One of them involves you apologizing later."

Clarisse said, "I never apologize."

Silena said, "That's why you need me."

They climbed the next set of steps together, Clarisse grumbling and Silena answering with something that made Clarisse snort before she could stop herself. Annabeth watched them go and then looked at Percy, and Percy was still looking at Annabeth, and neither of them had the sense to pretend they were not seeing each other.

She said, "My sisters are insufferable."

"They're your sisters," he said. "That's the whole point."

"I did not know that was the point."

"Now you do."

She almost laughed. Then, because she had spent too long being the one everyone expected to be hard, she let herself be, and said, "You're really not going to tell me one thing about last night."

"I already did."

"You did not."

"I did."

"Percy."

He said her name like he meant it. He always did, but more so now, with the morning light on his face and her hand still in his and the lake behind them doing the slow work of becoming day.

"I told you," he said. "I told you I was there. I told you I remembered. I told you I'm not asking you to forget any of it."

"And I told you I'm not going to pretend that memory doesn't hurt."

"It does hurt."

"Yes."

"And it's still yours."

"And I am still," she said, "the one who has to wake up and decide."

"Yes."

"And decide badly."

"No."

She held his gaze while Thalia hauled a basket of bread over the side of the mess hall bench and Clarisse yelled that someone had better be glad she was present because the good yogurt was gone and Silena said, with the calm of a person who had already taken it, "I'm sorry, Clarisse. It was my fault."

"Of course it was," Clarisse said.

Annabeth turned back to Percy. "We should eat."

"We should."

"Before they start."

"Before who starts."

"The rest of camp, if we're lucky."

He stood up so fast he almost knocked her over, but she caught his arm and steadied him, and that small act of steadying became a kind of promise between them, because there it was again, the ordinary miracle of a hand on his arm and a body that knew how to lean and stay.

"You're going to be late," she said.

"I'm already late."

"Then we're both late."

"Excellent."

They walked the last stretch of dock together, and the sun had climbed enough to put gold on the water and silver on the leaves and a clean sharp line across the sky where night was still trying to hold on.

---

Silena Beauregard had opinions about mornings, and most of them were correct.

The first correct opinion was that people who said they did not sleep well the night before were usually the same people who had not tried to sleep well. The second correct opinion was that Clarisse La Rue, who claimed to have zero interest in anyone's feelings, knew Percy Jackson's mood by the set of his shoulders before anyone else in camp did. The third correct opinion was that the Poseidon cabin was better when all four of them were somewhere near the same place doing things that involved food, movement, and at least one person being mildly embarrassed.

Today's embarrassment belonged to Annabeth, who had not managed to hide the fact that she had been awake before sunrise and had not managed to hide the fact that she had been with Percy while she was awake, and had not managed to hide that Silena could tell the difference between her pretending not to be relieved and her actually not being relieved.

That was Silena's gift and her curse: she could read a room the way other people read the water, and she loved Percy because he made Annabeth easier to read and because he made the whole cabin less lonely than it had been before he arrived and because he had the annoying habit of being right about the important things.

She dragged Clarisse toward breakfast by the elbow and said, "If you keep glaring at Annabeth like that, she's going to start believing you actually care."

"I do not."

"You absolutely do."

"I care about her safety."

"You care about her being happy."

Clarisse stopped walking. So did Silena. The mess hall was loud with breakfasts and morning complaints and the clatter of metal chairs, but the two of them paused in the doorway and stood there with the lake visible through the open side wall, where Percy and Annabeth and Thalia were coming down the ramp in a clump that looked suspiciously like a family.

"She is happy," Clarisse said.

"That's not what I asked."

"Same answer."

Silena leaned in. "You're being sentimental."

"I am being practical."

"You are being sentimental and calling it practical."

Clarisse looked at the dock. "She looks like herself."

She did. On the ramp, with Percy's hand at her elbow and Thalia's glare softening into something more like attention, Annabeth looked like the girl Silena had watched grow up in this cabin, with the same sharp mouth and the same guarded eyes and the same careful way of moving through the world as if she expected it to ask something of her and wanted to be ready. The difference was that today the carefulness looked less like fear and more like someone who had been given a reason to stay loose.

Percy noticed, too. Silena could tell. Percy noticed most things worth noticing.

"He's ruining her," Clarisse said.

"No," Silena said. "He's letting her be real."

Clarisse snorted. "That's not the same."

"It is. It's adjacent. It's the same direction."

Clarisse would have argued, but she was staring at Thalia, who was stepping off the ramp and saying, in the small voice that meant she was almost being kind, "I made it. I didn't oversleep. Someone hand me a fork before I change my mind."

Thalia took the fork. Clarisse handed it to her. Silena watched all of it and thought, not for the first time, that the Poseidon cabin had become a place where secrets did not last as long as they used to, and that was probably a good thing, because secrets had a way of sharpening themselves in the dark until they could cut the people who kept them.

Behind them, Percy laughed at something Annabeth said, and the laugh was loud enough to draw Clarisse's glare, and Annabeth laughed back, and Clarisse's expression made a tiny, unwilling shift that Silena would have sworn on everything she owned was the beginning of affection.

"That's not affection," Clarisse muttered.

"It's the start of it."

"No you don't."

"You do."

"No I don't."

"You do not."

Clarisse turned away before Silena could press. "Come on. Before Percy actually does steal all the good bread."

"I don't think that's how stealing works."

"I think it is if he wants it badly enough."

They followed them into the mess hall, into the noise and the smell of coffee and pancakes and damp wool, and Silena thought, as the doors swung shut behind them, that the lake had not taken anything this morning. It had only given them back the day, and that was enough, for now, to be grateful for.

---

The mess hall was chaos in the good way, the way that meant everyone was alive and talking and reaching for things and dragging chairs and complaining about Chiron's early drills. Percy sat beside Annabeth with his back to the wall so he could watch the room and her at the same time, which was not suspicious so much as strategic, and it made Clarisse roll her eyes in a way that said she knew exactly what he was doing and approved of half of it.

"Your napkin's on the floor," Annabeth said without looking down.

"I noticed."

"You're waiting for me to pick it up."

"I always wait for you to pick it up."

"You don't."

"I do."

"Percy."

"I'm joking."

"Somehow."

He reached down and got it anyway, because he liked the small things that made her look up and frown and then glance back at him with the corner of her mouth doing the exact work that mouth did when it was trying not to smile and failing.

Thalia slid into the bench on his other side with a plate the size of a shield and said, "I fixed the oatmeal situation. You're welcome."

"By 'fixed,' " Clarisse said from across the table, "you mean you ruined it in a way none of us can prove."

"I improved it."

"You made it gray."

"It's supposed to be gray. It's oatmeal."

"It's a funeral," Clarisse said.

Silena piled scrambled eggs on Thalia's plate because that was the peace offering she used on mornings when Thalia was being Thalia and Clarisse was being Clarisse and Annabeth was, apparently, being quietly happy in a way that made the whole table feel tamer than it had a right to.

"Eat," Silena said to Thalia. "You've been up since three. You need fuel."

"I am fueled by spite."

"That's not a food group."

"It is if you're me."

Clarisse snorted into her juice. Thalia pretended not to notice. Percy passed Annabeth the basket of bread, and she took it, and their fingers brushed, and the brush of fingers was small enough to be invisible to everyone else but was, to him, the same kind of sign as a lighthouse in fog.

"What were you two doing out there," thundered Clarisse, "before breakfast? Other than plotting world domination with your faces?"

Annabeth looked up. Percy looked up. Thalia looked up. Silena looked up. The whole table had the expression of people who had been waiting for this question and were not sure they wanted the answer.

"Talking," Percy said.

"Talking," Annabeth agreed.

"About what," Clarisse demanded.

"About the lake."

"About the sky."

"About last night."

"About not answering questions," Silena said.

"You don't have to tell us," Thalia said. "I'm not interrogating you."

"You literally just asked."

"I was demonstrating the interrogation you should expect if you ever lie to me."

"That's intimidating," Clarisse said.

"It's efficient."

Annabeth took a piece of bread and turned it in her fingers. "We were not lying."

"No," Percy said. "We were just not volunteering."

Clarisse studied them for a beat, then changed the subject by saying, "Who stole the blueberry pancakes?" and the table relaxed by a degree, because Clarisse had turned a confrontation into breakfast, which was her best skill and Silena's favorite one to exploit.

Silena was already reaching for the plate when Clarisse said, "Not those. I saw them first."

"I saw the table first."

"That's not how it works."

"It is if you want pancakes."

They wrestled over the plate in a way that made Thalia laugh despite herself and Clarisse mutter that siblings were the worst and Annabeth say, quietly, "You're both wrong," and Percy say, "I brought muffins," which made the table go quiet in the way that meant they had caught the good part.

He had brought them from the cabin, wrapped in a towel and carried them down before sunrise, because Annabeth liked them and because he liked the sound of her saying she liked them and because he had decided, sometime in the dark, that he was done pretending he could keep this part of himself neat and small and hidden.

Annabeth took the first one immediately. "You remembered the blueberry."

"I remember everything you like."

"That's excessive."

"It's accurate."

"That's worse."

Thalia watched him with the look that had replaced her suspicion, which was worse for him in some ways and better in others, because now she was paying attention, and attention from Thalia Grace meant she had decided he mattered enough to track.

"Where did you get these?" she asked.

"Cabin kitchen."

"Before the counselors arrived?"

"Before the counselors could pretend they did not exist."

Clarisse pointed at him. "And yet you were not arrested. Suspicious."

"I know people."

"You bribed someone."

"I did not bribe anyone."

"You absolutely bribed someone."

"I reminded someone that Silena likes blueberries and that bribing Silena is the fastest route to disaster."

Silena said, "That's true. Also unfair. Also I want one."

She got one. Clarisse let her have it, which was the same as Clarisse admitting defeat and therefore the same as Clarisse pretending she had never been losing in the first place.

Annabeth leaned into him while she ate. Not all the way. Just enough that her shoulder pressed his and her weight leaned his way and her hand found the edge of his sleeve and held on while she chewed and looked out at the room with the calm, steady look of someone who had decided, at least for this morning, to let herself be here without apologizing for it.

He felt the shift in her. He always did. He felt the moments when she stopped holding herself in two pieces and let them be one piece for a while, and that was the thing he loved most about her and the thing he feared most about her, because love and fear were not opposites for people who remembered a future that had not happened the way they planned.

"You're staring," she said.

"Into your soul."

"Dramatic."

"Accurate."

She laughed. The laugh made Thalia's mouth twitch. Made Clarisse's shoulders drop a fraction. Made Silena look up and grin.

That was the morning Percy wanted. Not perfect. Not safe. Just real, and shared, and loud enough to drown out the parts of the night that still ached.

Thalia rapped the table with her fork. "Okay. Enough of the sentimental nonsense. We've got patrol in an hour, and I'm not waiting for Percy to develop a personality before we leave."

"I have a personality."

"You're developing it right now, in front of us."

"Progress."

"No."

Clarisse said, "He's obviously still working on it."

Annabeth said, "Give him time."

Clarisse said, "I have all the time in the world, and I'm still not sure."

"That's because you refuse to admit when you like something."

"I do not."

"You literally just defended him to Thalia."

"I did not."

"You did."

"You multiple times."

Clarisse shut her mouth. Thalia grinned. Silena stole another piece of pancake. Annabeth rested her head against Percy's shoulder for one long breath and then pulled back before anyone could turn it into a whole conversation, which was how she handled things that were too tender to survive being named out loud.

"Patrol," she said.

"Patrol," Percy agreed.

"On the lake?"

"On the lake."

"Great," Clarisse said. "Because we were all desperate to be near the water before breakfast."

"You love the water."

"I love not drowning."

"Same thing."

"Absolutely not."

They ate in the comfortable noise that followed, and Percy watched Annabeth watch the room and watch him and keep both things going at once, and thought that this was the life he had wanted in the other timeline and the life he had gotten in this one, and that the difference between them was smaller than he had once believed and larger than he could explain to anyone who had not stood where he stood.

When the bell rang, they were already up, already moving, already half out the door before Clarisse could complain about being rushed and Silena could claim she had always been ready and Thalia could announce, as she always did, that she had been ready since midnight and was only being polite.

"I was," she said.

"Of course you were," Annabeth said.

"You're lying."

"I know."

Thalia almost smiled.

Percy caught her wrist on the way out and squeezed once, quick and light, and she let him, and then they were all outside, and the lake was waiting, and the day had not yet decided what it would ask of them, and that was the best kind of morning there was.

---

Clarisse did not intend to lose. She never did. Which was why, when Silena said, with the smug little smile that meant trouble had already arrived, "Everyone to the cabin lounge," Percy knew, before anyone else spoke, that the next hour was going to involve a game and that the game was going to involve him.

The Poseidon cabin lounge was the part of the cabin nobody used for sleeping and everybody used for everything else. It had a low table in the middle, three uncomfortable chairs, one armchair that had once belonged to someone important and was now used by whoever claimed it first, and a window that looked out over the lake in a way that made people think about things they did not want to think about. Right now it held Thalia folded into the armchair with her boots on the coffee table because she was Thalia and comfort was a negotiation she had already won, Clarisse standing with her arms crossed and a look on her face that said she was pretending not to be delighted, Silena already seated cross-legged on the floor because she had never met a game she did not want to join, Annabeth perched on the edge of the low table because she was Annabeth and still not quite sure whether to sit in a chair or surrender, and Percy, who had been dragged into the room before he had time to form an opinion, standing with his hands in the pockets of his camp shirt and trying to remember whether he had promised to participate in this or merely to tolerate it.

"Truth or dare," Silena said.

"Excuse me," Percy said. "I was perfectly comfortable outside."

"You were perfectly annoying outside," Thalia said. "Sit down before I make you."

"Thalia."

"Me."

Clarisse said, "If we're doing this, we're doing it properly. No half-measures. No refusing the first question because you've developed a conscience overnight."

"I have not developed a conscience overnight."

"Then prove it."

Percy looked at Annabeth. She was looking at him with that measured calm she used when she was deciding whether a thing was worth the trouble, and then she said, very mildly, "If you want my opinion, you should dare him first. People are more honest when they're trying to prove they're not scared."

"Annabeth."

"You heard her."

Clarisse's grin was small and sharp. "That settles it."

She pointed at Percy. "Dare."

Percy's mouth tightened. "You drew that out of a bag you brought, didn't you."

"I came prepared," Silena said. "Clarisse has been carrying a folded list in her jacket since breakfast. She's been waiting to use it."

"I have not."

"You have."

"Show it to me."

Clarisse brushed past him and raked a crumpled paper out of her duffel, where it had apparently been folded so many times the creases had gone soft. She slapped it on the table. It was not, in fact, a list. It was a page torn from something and covered with Clarisse's handwriting in the neat, severe way she used when she was trying to look in control of something she was not.

Truth or Dare Rules, it said at the top, in block letters that looked as if they had been pressed into the page with force.

1. No backing out once you answer.
2. No claiming you don't know what you're thinking.
3. If you refuse, you take the dare anyway.
4. If you lie, Clarisse decides.
5. If someone cries, that person has to make cocoa for everyone.

"This is tyranny," Percy said.

"This is organization," Clarisse said. "Sit."

He sat. Not because she had made him. Because Annabeth had said, from where she sat on the table edge, "Sit down, Percy. It's just a game."

That did it. He sat. Annabeth slid off the table and took the chair beside him, close enough that her knee touched his and she did not move away, which ought to have been a normal thing and was, instead, the kind of small treason he had learned to recognize as hers.

"Clarisse," he said, "do we have to go around the circle, or can I go last and think about my life choices first?"

"First," Clarisse said.

"Of course."

She studied him for a beat, then asked, "Who was the first person you kissed at camp who was not Annabeth?"

Silena made a noise of delight. Thalia raised one eyebrow so high it looked painful. Annabeth's hand found Percy's sleeve and pressed, hard.

Percy leaned back in the chair and looked at the ceiling, where the weather was beginning to show through the paint. "That's not a fair question."

"It's a true question," Clarisse said.

"It's a trap."

"It's a dare with extra steps."

"I'm not answering a trap."

"Then you're refusing, which means tell the truth."

"I already am."

Clarisse's eyes narrowed. "You're stalling, which means you're hiding something."

"I'm not hiding anything. There's nothing to hide."

"Then why are you still sitting there looking guilty?"

"I do not look guilty."

"You do."

"He does not," Annabeth said. Her voice had gone flat in the way it got when she was protecting him. "He has no reason to look guilty."

Percy shot her a look. She didn't blink. "You first," Clarisse said to her, "since you're so invested."

Annabeth took one sharp breath. "Truth."

"Favorite thing about Percy at camp."

Silena snorted. Clarisse's mouth tightened, which was Clarisse's answer to almost everything. Annabeth did not seem to notice. She looked at Percy, and for one second her expression went soft enough that even Clarisse looked away from it, and then she said, "That he notices things."

"What kind of things?" Silena asked.

"The things other people miss. The way someone's voice changes before they lie. The way the lake changes before the weather turns. The way you look at me when you think I'm not paying attention."

Silena went very still. Clarisse went very still. Thalia, who had been watching the window, turned around so fast the chair leg scraped the floor.

"That's not," Thalia said slowly, "how you phrase things."

"It's how I phrased them," Annabeth said, and there was steel under it, the kind that had nothing to do with powers and everything to do with the fact that she had just admitted something she had not meant to admit.

Percy's hand moved on its own and touched her wrist. Just there. Under the sleeve. "That's true," he said. "And I notice that too."

Annabeth looked at his hand, then at him, and then, before anyone else could do anything with that, she said in a voice so quiet it almost disappeared into the room, "Stop."

"Stop what?" Clarisse asked, delighted.

"Stop talking about us like we're a puzzle."

"We are a puzzle."

"We're not."

Clarisse said, "Oh, gods, yes you are. You're the most complicated thing at this camp and you both know it."

"I'm not," Percy said.

"You are," Clarisse insisted. "Which is why you're stalling on my question. Answer it."

Percy sighed. "There isn't a first kiss. There are a lot of first kisses. You're asking the wrong question."

"Try again."

He looked at Annabeth. She was looking back at him with that same dangerous calm, and then she said, "Clarisse."

"Yes?"

"If you want the truth, ask him who he thinks about when the lake is quiet at night."

Clarisse blinked. "That's not a dare."

"It's better," Annabeth said. "It's the real one."

Clarisse did not like that. She never liked being out-flanked. She stood with her hands on her hips and looked from Annabeth to Percy to Silena to Thalia, who had gone quiet in the way that meant she had already decided not to interrupt something important.

"Fine," Clarisse said after a moment. "Truth. Who do you think about?"

Percy did not look away. "Annabeth."

The room went quiet. Not Thalia-quiet. Not Clarisse-quiet. The deeper kind. The kind that happened when everyone at once understood that the answer had arrived and had no interest in being polite.

Silena exhaled a little. "Finally."

Clarisse said, "You could have said that three years ago."

"You could have asked," Percy said.

"You could have made it easier."

"You could have not made every conversation a contest."

Clarisse's expression changed. Not much. Just enough to show that she had heard him. "You're seventeen in your head," she said.

"Sometimes."

"And you're fourteen in your body."

"And I'm fourteen in my body."

"Which means," Clarisse said, "you're walking around like you already know how this ends."

"I don't."

"You do."

Annabeth's voice came out low and steady. "He doesn't have to know how it ends to know what matters."

Clarisse looked at her. "That's a very Annabeth thing to say."

"It's a true thing to say."

Silena kicked her feet against the leg of the table and grinned. "Okay. My turn."

"Your turn to fail spectacularly," Clarisse said.

"Dare," Silena said.

Clarisse pointed at her. "Hold Annabeth's hand in front of everyone and don't let go until I say so."

Silena laughed. "Too easy."

"Then do it."

Silena reached across the table and took Annabeth's hand. Not delicately. Not performatively. Just took it, palm to palm, and held on. Annabeth did not pull away. She turned her hand and laced their fingers together, and Clarisse, who had expected Silena to make a joke of it, watched the two of them sit there like that while the room did not know what to do with itself.

"That's it?" Clarisse asked.

"That's the dare," Silena said.

"That was a dare?"

"It was," Silena said. "Clarisse doesn't understand that some things are only brave because they look ordinary."

Clarisse snorted. "I understand plenty."

"Then understand this: you're jealous."

Clarisse went red. "I am not."

"You are."

"No."

"Yes."

"I am not jealous of Silena holding Annabeth's hand."

"Then what are you?"

Clarisse did not answer. She looked at Percy instead. "Your turn."

He took a breath. "Dare."

Clarisse smiled. "Finally."

She said, "Kiss her."

Thalia said, "Oh, absolutely not."

Clarisse said, "Thalia."

"Not happening."

"It is happening."

"Clarisse, no."

"Clarisse, yes," Silena said, delighted.

"Clarisse, one more word and I will put you in the lake," Thalia said.

"Thalia Grace," Annabeth said, "you're being dramatic."

"I'm being reasonable."

Clarisse looked at Percy. "You heard her. Kiss her."

Percy stood up first. Annabeth's hand left the table. "Percy."

"I know what I'm doing."

"Do you?" Clarisse asked. "Prove it."

He did not answer. He crossed the space between them, leaned down in front of her chair, and kissed her. Not hurried. Not theatrical. Just real, the way he had kissed her on the dock, only here, in the cabin lounge with Thalia folded in the armchair and Clarisse pretending not to watch and Silena grinning like she had won a war, it felt different. It felt bolder. It felt like a thing said out loud in front of witnesses.

When he pulled back, Annabeth's face had gone pink from the collar up, and she looked at him the way she did when she had let something happen that she would spend the rest of the day pretending she had not.

"Well," Clarisse said, very slowly, "that answers the question."

Thalia set down her coffee cup with a force that cracked the saucer. "That is exactly what I was afraid of."

Annabeth stood up and brushed past Percy. "That was enough of you."

He followed. "It was only one kiss."

"It was one kiss too many in front of your future sister-in-law," Clarisse said.

"I'm not your sister-in-law."

"Give it time."

Silena said, "I already told Uncle Tyson it was happening. He laughed for five minutes."

"Uncle Tyson did not—"

"He did."

Thalia said, "I hate all of you."

"No you don't," Silena said.

"I do."

"No you don't."

"I do."

Clarisse turned to Percy. "Alright. Back to you."

He sat down so fast he knocked the chair leg against the floor. "Dare again. I'm on a roll."

Clarisse said, "Don't get cocky."

"Too late."

She asked, "What's the thing you do with Annabeth that you hope nobody notices?"

Percy's mouth tightened. He glanced at Annabeth. She was watching him with that same awake-late-at-night look, and then she answered before he could, "He holds my wrist when we cross the street."

Clarisse said, "You don't cross the street."

"At camp we do," Annabeth said. "There are paths and there are shortcuts and there are places where you're supposed to look before you step."

"And you notice him doing it?" Clarisse asked.

"Of course I notice," Annabeth said. "It's one of the things that makes me trust him."

Clarisse's face changed again, just at the corners. "That's it?"

"That's enough."

Silena said, "No it's not. Clarisse is trying to make you say something embarrassing."

"I'm not," Clarisse said.

"You are."

"I'm trying to get the truth."

"Which is embarrassingly sweet anyway."

Percy said, "That's because you've never had anything sweet in your life."

Clarisse said, "That is a lie."

"No," Percy said. "That's observation."

Clarisse stood up so fast she knocked her chair back. "My turn."

Thalia said, "I truly did not expect to be in this room when Clarisse became the referee."

"You should have," Clarisse said. "This is your family."

Thalia looked at her. "That's a mean thing to say."

"It's a true thing to say."

Silena snorted into her elbow. Clarisse ignored her and said, "Truth or dare, Thalia."

Thalia considered the question with the expression of someone who had never considered whether she wanted to participate and had suddenly been invited to anyway. "Truth."

Clarisse's eyes lit up. "Who were you watching on the dock this morning?" she asked.

Thalia's chair scraped the floor again. "You don't get to ask that."

"I do."

"I was not watching them."

"You were."

"Clarisse."

"Don't Thalia me. You were awake until two for a reason. You knew where they were. You knew what they were doing. And then this morning you stood on the dock and you watched the whole thing."

Thalia went very still. Then she said, quietly, "I was scared."

Silena leaned forward. "Of what?"

"Of losing her," Thalia said, and the words came out so raw that even Clarisse stopped grinning. "Of something happening and me not seeing it. Of her slipping away and me being the person who was supposed to stop it and not being enough. Of Percy. Of Annabeth. Of everything."

The room went very quiet. The lake could be heard through the window, a steady low sound like breathing.

Clarisse said, "That's not a game answer."

"It's the truthful one."

Thalia stood up. "That's enough of this. You're all ridiculous."

"We're not," Annabeth said. "We're honest."

Thalia looked at her. Looked at Percy. Looked at Clarisse. Looked at Silena. "That may be," she said. "But it doesn't make this easier."

Annabeth took a step toward her and stopped. "It will get easier."

"Will it?" Thalia asked.

"Yes."

Thalia did not believe her. She did believe her anyway. That was the strange thing about this family, and about Percy and Annabeth especially: they could say things that were not yet true and still make people want to believe them.

Clarisse sat back down. "My turn to dare again."

Percy said, "Clarisse, no."

"Clarisse, yes," Silena said.

Clarisse said, "Annabeth. Dare."

Annabeth said, "No. I'm not doing whatever you're planning."

Clarisse said, "You are."

Annabeth said, "Clarisse."

"Yes."

"If this is about making me kiss Percy again, I will refuse."

Clarisse grinned. "Then you refuse, which means you take the dare anyway."

"That's not how any of this works."

"It is if I wrote the rules."

Annabeth looked at Percy. He was already standing.

"Percy."

"I know."

"Clarisse."

"Yes."

"This is in poor taste."

"It's excellent taste."

"Clarisse."

She said, softly this time, "Kiss her on the cheek. In front of us. And don't let go until you've said why."

Annabeth turned pale. Percy didn't seem to hear her. He crossed the room again, and this time Annabeth did not try to stop him.

He bent beside her chair, and he kissed her cheek, and he said, very low, "Because I remember you." Then, a little louder, "Because I'm still here." Then, with her hand in his and the room watching, "Because I'm not done with you."

When he stood up, Annabeth's hand stayed on his sleeve for one heartbeat too long, and Clarisse looked away from it, and Silena made a small sound that might have been a laugh, and Thalia said, "That's enough psychology for one afternoon."

Clarisse said, "I think I won."

Silena said, "You did."

Clarisse said, "I knew I would."

Thalia said, "No you didn't. You just embarrassed all of us."

Clarisse looked at her. "And you're still here."

Thalia opened her mouth. Closed it. Then she said, "That's not the same thing."

"It is."

"No."

"It is."

Clarisse leaned back in her chair and grinned at the whole of them. "Alright. That's the game. Next time, I'm making you all write letters to each other."

"Absolutely not."

"Clarisse, no."

"Clarisse, yes," Silena said.

Annabeth said, "If you write one to Percy, I will burn it before he reads it."

Percy said, "I would never let you."

Clarisse laughed. It was the best sound anyone in the room had made all day.

Outside, the lake kept moving. Inside, nobody wanted the game to end.

Silena stretched one leg out under the table and kicked the leg of Clarisse's chair lightly, not hard enough to hurt, just hard enough to make her sit a little straighter. "You know you're hoarding the good parts," she said.

Clarisse said, "I am not."

"You are. You've got the list in your jacket and the list in your head and now you're waiting for the next one to happen so you can keep score."

"I do not keep score."

"You absolutely do. I've seen the face you make when someone wins."

Clarisse considered this. Then, very reluctantly, she said, "I make a face because I like winning."

"That's what I said."

Clarisse turned to her. "You're impossible."

"Thank you," Silena said. "Someone finally noticed."

Annabeth laughed, and it was the real kind, the kind she had been saving all morning, and Percy felt it in the chair where he sat and thought, not for the first time, that this was the life he had wanted and the life he had gotten and the distance between those two things had become a place he could actually live in.

Thalia, who had been pretending not to listen, said, "You know this is exactly the kind of thing that gets you in trouble at camp."

"Which is why we're doing it," Clarisse said.

"That makes no sense."

"It does. If we're going to be ridiculous, we should be ridiculous on purpose."

Thalia snorted. That was not quite a laugh. It was close enough.

Percy looked at Annabeth. She looked back at him, and this time there was no hiding the look, the one that said she was still thinking about the kiss and still thinking about the morning and still thinking about the part of her that had let go for one heartbeat too long and was not sorry about any of it.

He said her name quietly. "Are you okay?"

She met his eyes. "Yeah."

"You sure?"

"I'm sure."

He reached across the table and touched her hand again, not like he had on the dock, not like he had in front of everyone a moment ago, but quiet and steady, the way he did when he wanted her to know he was not done, not even now.

She turned her hand over and laced theirs together, and Clarisse, who had been watching from across the table with the face of someone who liked being included and hated admitting it, said, "If you two are finished with the romance hour, I'm going to need everyone to remember the rules."

"We remember the rules," Annabeth said.

"Do you?" Clarisse asked.

"Yes."

"Then remind me."

Annabeth glanced at Percy, then back at Clarisse, and said, "No backing out once you answer."

Clarisse nodded slowly. "Good. Because I'm about to make Thalia play."

Thalia said, "Absolutely not."

Clarisse said, "Clarisse, yes."

Silena said, "Clarisse, do it."

Thalia said, "I hate this family."

"No you don't."

"Yes I do."

"No you don't."

"Yes I do."

Clarisse stood up and pointed at her sister. "Truth or dare."

Thalia stared at her. Then, very slowly, she said, "Dare."

Clarisse's grin returned in full. "Good answer."

She leaned forward and said, "Annabeth's going to ask you something. You're not allowed to dodge it. And you're not allowed to call it a game when it isn't."

Thalia's jaw tightened. "You're making this worse."

"I'm making it honest," Clarisse said. "There's a difference."

Thalia turned to Annabeth. Annabeth turned to her, and for one long beat nobody else moved, because this was the part none of them could make easier and none of them wanted to rush.

Annabeth said, "Thalia, that thing you said back there, about being scared. Do you want help with it?"

Thalia went very still. Then she said, "Not from you."

Clarisse said, "From who, then?"

Thalia looked at Percy. Looked at Clarisse. Looked at Silena. "From whoever is willing," she said quietly, "to show up and not pretend I'm stronger than I feel."

Clarisse looked away first. Silena looked down at her hands. Annabeth's mouth softened.

Percy stood up and crossed the room to the armchair where Thalia had been sitting and pulled it around to face him. "I'm willing," he said.

Thalia held his gaze. "You always are."

"So are you," he said.

Clarisse snorted, but it was the soft kind, the kind that came out when she had run out of replies and had to settle for being human.

Silena said, "Okay. I think that's enough truth for one day."

Clarisse said, "Is it?"

Silena said, "It is. Because if we keep going, Clarisse is going to keep getting better at it, and then she'll start enjoying this and there will be no stopping her."

Clarisse looked at her with genuine horror. "No. You don't."

"I do."

Thalia said, "She already does."

Clarisse took it as an insult. Silena took it as a compliment. Annabeth took it as the truth, which was, as always, the most inconvenient thing in the room.

Percy looked at Annabeth one more time before the moment could slip away. "We should go," he said.

She stood up. "Yeah."

Clarisse muttered, "You're both impossible."

"We know," Annabeth said.

Clarisse said, "Good. That means you'll fit in here."

They left the lounge in a loose knot of bodies, Annabeth with Percy's hand in hers and Thalia pretending not to notice and Clarisse pretending she was not watching them leave and Silena pretending she had not been counting.

Outside, the lake was still there, still waiting, still making room. Percy let Annabeth's fingers slide between his and thought, not for the first time, that the morning had been larger than he had expected it to be and better than he had expected it to be, which was the kind of thing he had learned, in both lives, to trust.

They reached the door just as Clarisse, who had been stalking after them with the determined air of someone who had decided not to be the last one out of a room she had already won, caught up and said, "You're forgetting the cocoa rule."

Annabeth said, "Which one is that?"

"Rule five," Clarisse said. "If someone cries, that person has to make cocoa for everyone. I forgot to mention that before, which means I'm planning ahead, which means"

"It means you're tyrannical," Silena said.

"It means I'm prepared," Clarisse corrected.

Thalia said, "I'm not crying. I was never crying. I was only being dramatic in the approved family way."

Clarisse looked at her for one beat too long, then said, "That counts."

Thalia set down her coffee cup on the table with such force that the saucer jumped. "Clarisse."

"I'm serious. Rule five."

"It was not crying. It was emotional emphasis."

"It was crying."

"It was not."

"It was."

Annabeth looked at Thalia. "You were."

Thalia opened her mouth to deny it and closed it again. Then she said, very stiffly, "Fine. I'm making cocoa for everyone. But only because I choose to, not because you made me."

Clarisse said, "Of course you choose to. I'm not the kind of sister who tells you what to do."

"You are exactly that kind of sister."

"I'm the kind of sister who makes you admit the truth."

Thalia took one step toward her and Clarisse held her ground, which was the closest thing to bravery either of them had ever let the other see, and Silena, who had been watching them with her hand still over her mouth, said, "If you yell at each other in the kitchen, I'm telling Percy."

"Someone has to," Clarisse said.

Thalia gave her a look that could have curdled milk. "You're not the one I'm yelling at."

Clarisse said, "I know. You're the one I'm going to yell at next."

Thalia turned on her heel and marched out of the lounge, Clarisse hot on her heels, and by the time they had disappeared down the hall, the rest of the room had returned to the shape of a family that had just survived one of its better disasters.

Annabeth stood in the doorway with Percy's hand still in hers and laughed, because there was nothing else to do with the fact that Thalia Grace had just been publicly defeated by a cocoa rule she had written herself.

"You're enjoying this," Percy said.

"Immensely," Annabeth said.

Clarisse appeared at her shoulder again, which she had a talent for doing when she had decided she was still part of the conversation. "You heard her. Cocoa."

"You wrote that rule," Annabeth said.

"I did. Which means I get to remind everyone of it. That's how rules work."

"That is not how rules work."

"It is at my table."

Silena said, "She says that about everything. Sleep schedules. Dishes. Who gets to sit nearest the window. It is a Clarisse specialty."

Clarisse said, "I have standards."

"You have opinions," Silena said.

Clarisse paused. Then, much to everyone's surprise, she said, "Maybe."

She looked at Annabeth. "Are you going to help?"

Annabeth said, "If you want me to."

Clarisse said, "I do."

"Then yes."

Clarisse nodded once, as if that were a major concession and not merely a small one, and said, "Good. Because I'm not making four cups of cocoa alone, and I'm not letting Silena make it, and I am not letting Thalia make it without supervision, which means"

"Which means you want Percy," Silena said.

Clarisse said, "I said which means we do it together."

"That means you want Percy," Silena repeated, delighted.

Clarisse said, "I hate you."

"No you don't."

"Yes I do."

"No you don't."

They went down the hall together, Clarisse arguing and Silena answering and Annabeth watching them go before turning to Percy, who was still standing in the doorway with the lake visible through the hall window and her hand still warm in his.

She said, "I think Clarisse made that rule specifically so she could force Thalia into the kitchen."

"That tracks," Percy said.

"It's probably also the first time Clarisse has ever written a rule she planned to break herself."

"You don't know that."

"I know the face she makes when she's planning something mean and the face she makes when she's planning something she's going to enjoy. This was both."

Percy smiled. "You're impossible."

"So I've heard."

She squeezed his hand and said, "Come on. Before Clarisse gets bored and decides the cocoa pot needs supervision from everyone in the cabin."

He let her lead him down the hall. She smelled like lake water and rain and the faint clean smell of the cabin, and he thought, as he had thought before and would think again, that that was the smell of a life he had not known he was allowed to keep.

Clarisse and Silena were already at the kitchen table when they got there, Clarisse staring at a pot and Silena pretending not to know where the spoons were. Thalia was not yet in sight, which meant she was either marching toward the pantry with deliberate purpose or she was sulking in her room and refusing to be part of the family in the dramatic way only Thalia Grace could manage.

"We have hot water," Clarisse announced.

"Here?" Silena said. "How did you get hot water so fast?"

"I have my methods."

"You have one method. You ask Triton."

Clarisse went pale. "I do not ask Triton for hot water."

"You absolutely do," Annabeth said.

Clarisse slammed the pot onto the stove and said, "This is not Triton. This is a kettle."

"That is not where your methods come from."

Clarisse ignored her and reached for a packet of cocoa mix with the expression of someone who had only recently discovered that joy could be measured in powder. "We have cocoa mix."

Silena looked up from under the table. "You kept that from me."

Clarisse said, "I don't keep secrets from you. I keep supplies."

"Same thing."

"No. Different thing."

"Same thing."

Clarisse threw a spoon at her. Silena caught it without looking, because she had been expecting exactly that, and Clarisse made a noise of outrage that sounded, to everyone in the room, suspiciously like laughter.

Thalia appeared in the doorway a moment later, carrying a tin of marshmallows she had apparently liberated from somewhere, and said, "If we're doing cocoa, we're doing it properly."

Clarisse said, "I was about to say that."

Thalia said, "You were too slow."

Clarisse said, "I was waiting for the appropriate moment."

"There is no appropriate moment for marshmallows. There is only now."

Silena said, "Finally, a statement I can agree with."

Clarisse said, "You agreed with nothing I said before that."

"This one was undeniable."

Thalia dumped the marshmallows into the bowl with a toss that was, as always, both reckless and precise. "And now we wait."

Clarisse stood with her arms crossed and looked at the pot. "We're not waiting. We're supervising."

Silena said, "Same thing, if you say it loudly enough."

Clarisse said, "It is not."

"It is."

"No it's not."

"Yes it is."

Thalia leaned against the counter and watched them with the look on her face that Percy had come to recognize as the one she used when she was trying not to smile. "You two are insufferable."

Clarisse said, "We know."

Thalia said, "I'm surrounded by children."

Clarisse said, "We're older than you think."

Thalia said, "By about five years."

Clarisse said, "You're younger than you think."

Thalia made a noise that might have been a laugh or might have been a protest against being punched with words. "Fine. You both win. I'm surrounded by insufferable children who know how to make cocoa."

Clarisse looked at the pot. "It's boiling."

Silena said, "That's the point."

Clarisse said, "That's the danger."

"It's cocoa."

"Still."

Annabeth said, "You're enjoying this."

Clarisse looked at her. "No I'm not."

"Yes you are."

Clarisse said, "I'm doing a job."

"You're having fun."

Clarisse said, "I am not having fun."

"You are."

She held her ground. Thalia said from the counter, "Clarisse, if you don't stop denying everything, I'm going to start charging you for sentences."

Clarisse looked at her. "What do they cost?"

Thalia said, "Three marshmallows a denial."

Clarisse said, "That's extortion."

"It's parenting."

Clarisse muttered, "I'm not your mother."

"No," Thalia said. "You're my sister. Which is somehow worse."

Silena snorted. Annabeth laughed again. Percy watched them all and thought that this was the kind of family he had wanted in the other life and the kind he had gotten in this one, and that the difference between wanting and having was mostly a matter of paying attention long enough to notice the thing was already there.

The pot started to sing. Clarisse reached for it with the caution of a person who had burned herself before and was still paying attention. Thalia set the marshmallows in a neat pile beside the bowls. Silena found the spoons. Annabeth poured the cocoa mix into four cups with the steady hands of someone who had done it enough times to know where not to spill.

Clarisse said, "Don't make me look bad."

Annabeth said, "I'm making you look like a sister."

Clarisse said, "That is a very different thing."

"It is."

Clarisse looked at her for a moment with that unreadable face she used when she was deciding whether to be soft in public or pretend she was not, and then she said, very quietly, "I'm glad you're here."

Annabeth's hand found her sleeve and held on for one heartbeat. "Me too."

Thalia said from the counter, "That was extremely sappy. I'm billing you both."

Clarisse said, "Put it on Percy."

"I don't take payment in marshmallows," Percy said.

"You should," Thalia said.

Silena said, "That's because you don't understand the economy of marshmallows."

"There is no economy of marshmallows."

"There is when Clarisse is around."

Clarisse said, "I am not the central bank of marshmallows."

"Actually," Silena said, "you kind of are."

Clarisse picked up a spoon and looked at it as if it had personally offended her. "We're going to have to stop soon."

"Why?" Thalia asked.

"Because we have a patrol."

"Right. Patrol." Thalia set the spoon down and looked around at all of them. "All of us."

Clarisse said, "Including you."

"Including me."

Annabeth said, "Including Percy and me."

Clarisse said, "Including everyone."

Silena said, "Including the people who made the cocoa."

Clarisse said, "Especially the people who made the cocoa."

Thalia looked at Clarisse. "You really did write that rule for me, didn't you."

Clarisse said, "I wrote it for everyone. You just happened to be the one it caught."

Thalia said, "Sure."

"Sure," Clarisse repeated, and this time there was something in it that sounded almost like affection, or at least the beginning of it.

Thalia held her gaze. "I'm going to keep an eye on you, Clarisse La Rue."

Clarisse said, "You already do."

"That's what I'm saying."

Percy looked at Annabeth. She looked back at him, and this time there was no hiding the smile, not even a little, and he thought that maybe the truth of this family was not that it was easy or safe or anything like that, but that it was real, and that real families were made of things that looked like trouble and turned out, if you stayed long enough, to be the opposite.

Clarisse set the bowls down in a row and said, "Come on. Before Thalia starts billing us for sentences."

"She already has," Silena said.

Clarisse said, "She has not."

"She has. Three marshmallows. I heard her."

Clarisse said, "That's not what she said."

"It is."

Clarisse stalked over to Thalia and made a grab for the marshmallow tin. Thalia pulled it away, and Silena laughed hard enough to make Clarisse snarl and Annabeth laugh twice as hard, and Percy, who had spent a very long time waiting for moments like this, stood in the kitchen with Annabeth's hand still in his and let himself have it.

It was enough. More than enough. It was a good morning and a good family and a good life, and it was theirs.

---

By the time they reached the lake, the sun had burned through the mist and painted the dock with a clean bright line. The water was calm. The distant trees were still and green. The whole place looked like a place that had been waiting for them to come back to it.

Percy took her hand as soon as they stepped off the ramp. No drama. No announcement. Just his fingers sliding between hers and his thumb pressing once against the inside of her wrist, where her pulse was already awake and steady and waiting for him to notice.

"You noticed," she said.

"I always do."

"That's the problem."

"That's the gift."

"No."

"Yes."

He stopped walking. Turned to her. Looked at her the way he had looked at her on the dock before Thalia came, like he was making sure she knew he had not planned to stop loving her just because the timeline had changed.

"Thalia's not the only one who wants answers," he said.

"I know."

"I'm not going to give you a speech about how you should trust me. You don't have to. Not yet. Not while you're still deciding what you owe this version of yourself."

"Percy."

"I mean it. I can wait. I have waited. I'm good at waiting."

She almost hit him. Almost. Then she laughed instead, because laughter was easier than telling him that waiting was the one thing she could not promise him, because she was seventeen years old in memory and fourteen in flesh and had not yet figured out how to live inside both of those without wanting to collapse the distance between them.

"You're terrible at waiting," she said.

"I've improved."

"You have not."

"Guilty."

He kissed her then, and this time she met him halfway without thinking, because the morning was warm enough and the lake was calm enough and Thalia was close enough to call it collusion and Clarisse was close enough to call it trouble and Silena was close enough to call it what it was.

When she pulled back, Thalia said, "Oh, for the love of—"

Clarisse said, "If you two keep this up, I'm going to assume you're married."

Silena said, "I already assume. I gave you a date for the ceremony in my head. It was yesterday. It was lovely."

Annabeth nearly dropped Percy's hand. "You did not."

"I absolutely did."

Clarisse said, "I hate all of you."

"No you don't," Silena said.

"I do."

"You do not."

"I do."

Clarisse turned away before Silena could press. "Come on. Let's go."

Silena said, "Don't mock her."

"I'm not mocking her. I'm respecting her by making fun of her."

"That makes no sense."

"It makes perfect sense."

Thalia ignored them all and started down the dock again, and Percy followed, and Annabeth followed, and Thalia's shoulders were squared and her stride was set and her face was closed, and Annabeth knew, with the certainty that had nothing to do with powers and everything to do with loving someone's sister, that Thalia was afraid, and that Thalia was brave, and that Thalia would be Annabeth's sister in this life whether Annabeth had earned it or not, and that was a thing she would spend a very long time learning how to hold.

They reached the end of the dock, and the water opened in front of them, and Percy said, "Ready?"

Annabeth said, "Ready," and meant the part of her that wanted to, and did not mean the part of her that was still afraid of wanting to, and accepted both parts because that was the only honest way to be with him.

He kissed her once more before they turned back toward the boathouse, and Thalia pretended not to watch, and Clarisse pretended not to notice, and Silena pretended, for everyone's sake, that she had not been keeping score.

Behind them, out in the shallows, a heron folded itself down onto the bow of a sunken, forgotten skiff and settled in, unhurried, holding the boat's place as if the lake had decided something was about to be built there.

It was a good morning. It was not a safe morning. It was theirs.
