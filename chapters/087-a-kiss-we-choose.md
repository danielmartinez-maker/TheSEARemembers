## Chapter 87: A Kiss We Choose

The storm kiss happened on a Thursday.

Percy Jackson remembered that very clearly, because Chiron later wrote it down in the camp incident log in his neat looping handwriting as *Thursday — unseasonal storm cell over canoe lake, contained by P.J. and A.G. with assistance*, which made it sound tidy and small and administrative, and it had not been tidy or small at all.

It had been wind screaming off the Sound, Annabeth shouting rope tension over the roar, Percy holding too much water back at once, and lightning splitting the far pine.

And then it had been done. The rack held. The kids were safe. The wind dropped all at once the way it only did when Percy finally remembered to breathe.

And Annabeth had turned, soaked to the bone, hair plastered to her face, a scratch bleeding down her cheek from a flying splinter, and she had grabbed the front of his rain poncho with both hands and kissed him hard on the mouth for exactly one second.

Then she had let go, said, "You idiot, you could have drowned," and gone straight back to tying knots.

That was it.

Except it wasn't the whole story.

Will had made Annabeth sit on an infirmary bench before she'd finished telling Percy why he'd scared her. The scratch on her cheek wasn't deep, but there was grit in it from the wind and a sliver of wood stuck near the edge. Percy stood by the door, drenched and shaking from the effort of holding the water away from the younger campers.

"You can sit too," Will told him.

"I'm fine."

"Then you can sit fine."

Percy sat.

The storm had been an unseasonal squall, not the first violent weather they'd faced. He hadn't solved it alone. Annabeth had organized the rope team, Clarisse had pulled the children away from the exposed rail, and Chiron had directed the sheltering of the canoe equipment. Percy had pushed the worst of the water back toward the lake long enough for the last camper to clear the path. It had felt like a duel with the whole sky. The report made it sound like a community response, which was closer to the truth.

Will cleaned Annabeth's cheek and removed the splinter. He asked Percy how long he'd been holding the water and whether he'd felt dizzy afterward.

"A while," Percy said.

"Not an answer."

"Maybe six minutes."

Annabeth looked at him sharply. "You told me two."

"I wasn't looking at a clock."

"That's why we have people watching the time," Will said. "You can't judge your limits by how heroic the last minute felt."

Percy wanted to argue. He had held up far more water in the life he remembered. He had survived Tartarus, battles with giants and things he couldn't discuss in the infirmary without raising questions he wasn't ready to answer. But the body he inhabited here was thirteen. He didn't get to assume it would tolerate every feat his old memory said he could perform.

"Chiron wants you to rest today," Will continued. "Both of you. No drills and no experiments with the lake."

Annabeth sighed. "I have a design meeting."

"You can send the notes."

"I need to explain the load failure."

"Then write the explanation."

Percy looked at her. "I can help."

She turned on him. "You can help by sitting still for a minute."

He went quiet.

Will left them a bottle of clean water and went to treat a camper who'd twisted an ankle on the path. The room smelled of antiseptic and damp jackets. Percy could hear the rain easing outside.

"I'm sorry," he said.

Annabeth touched the bandage on her cheek. "For what?"

"For making you worry."

"That's not what I'm angry about."

"Then what?"

"You decided you could hold the whole storm. I was asking you to move with the rope team. You didn't answer."

Percy remembered. He had heard her calling over the wind and assumed she wanted him to hold the water a little longer. He'd been too occupied proving he could help to hear the change in her plan.

"I didn't know you wanted me to stop," he said.

"That is the problem."

He looked at his hands. "I thought if I let go, somebody would get hurt."

"And if you'd passed out, somebody might have."

The words landed. He had experienced versions of himself dying or disappearing in false worlds, and he was tired of being scared. It would have been easier if Annabeth told him she wasn't angry, kissed him again and let the incident fade into a romantic story about bravery.

Instead she asked him to listen.

"I kissed you because I was relieved," she said. "Not because I think you should nearly drown every time you want to make me happy."

Percy looked up. "I know."

"Do you?"

"Yeah. I was relieved too. I love you. I just got stuck thinking the only safe thing was to keep pushing."

Annabeth sat back against the bench. "I want us to be able to say stop and know the other one heard it."

"Then we'll make a signal for when we can't hear."

"We have signals."

"We should check they're the same ones."

She considered that. It was the kind of solution she liked, concrete enough to test and change.

"Okay," she said.

They wrote the emergency gestures on the back of her draft report: stop, retreat, need help, route clear. Percy suggested one he called *water is being rude*, which Annabeth removed because it could describe everything from a wave to a full sea monster.

When Will returned, he checked the list and made them repeat the gestures without looking at the paper. Percy got the retreat signal wrong the first time. Annabeth corrected him, not unkindly. They tried again until both could identify the commands.

"Now don't make this into a secret code only the two of you know," Will said. "If the rope team is going to use it, everybody needs to be trained."

Annabeth nodded. She asked Chiron to review it before the next storm drill, and wrote his name beside the request so she wouldn't forget.

Percy was still shaken. The kiss had been real, the relief had been real, and their love had existed long before the storm. They were allowed to remember the moment tenderly without turning the dangerous part into something they hoped would happen again.

When he rose to leave, Annabeth caught his hand.

"Can we talk about the kiss later?" she asked.

"Sure."

"When you're not trembling."

"I'm not trembling."

She looked at his fingers.

"Fine," he admitted. "A little."

"Rest first," she said.

He nodded. That was one promise he could keep immediately.

 That was the storm kiss, different from their earlier kisses here and from every kiss they remembered.

Everyone at camp had seen it. Half the Poseidon cabin had cheered. Clarisse had laughed so hard she fell off the canoe rack. Grover had fainted sideways into a life jacket bin and then popped back up shouting, "Did I miss it? Tell me I didn't miss it!"

Percy and Annabeth had not talked about it properly for three whole days.

On the fourth day, Sunday, bright and cool and smelling of pine sap, Percy was sitting at the end of the dock with his feet in the water, thinking about that one second until his brain felt sunburned.

He heard Annabeth's boots before he saw her. He always knew her walk. Steady. Purposeful. Left boot scuffing just a little when she was thinking too fast.

She sat down next to him and put her feet in the water too. Her ponytail was damp. She had charcoal on her wrist and wore the silver owl bracelet Thalia had made her.

For a long time they just sat. A dragonfly landed on Percy's knee. The forge clanged up the hill.

"So," Percy said at last to the water.

"So," Annabeth said to the water.

"We kissed."

"We did kiss."

"In front of like, forty people."

"Thirty-seven," Annabeth said automatically. "I counted. For incident report purposes."

Percy snorted, then went quiet again. He kicked a little at the water. "Does it bother you? That it was like that?"

Annabeth was quiet for a beat too long. She picked at a splinter on the dock board. "No. Yes. I don't know. It doesn't bother me that it happened. It bothers me that — "

"That it was desperate?" Percy offered.

She looked at him then, gray eyes direct. "Yeah. It was desperate. You were about to pass out and I thought the rack was going to hit you and I just — did it. I wasn't thinking."

"I liked it," Percy said honestly, and then went bright red to his ears. "I mean — I mean I liked that you — I wasn't complaining — "

Annabeth's mouth twitched. "I know, Seaweed Brain."

"I just mean," Percy said, staring very hard at a cloud, "it wasn't really chosen. It was like, crisis kiss. Reflex kiss. Oh-no-you're-going-to-die-so-I'm-going-to-kiss-you kiss."

"Romantic summary," Annabeth said dryly, but her ears were pink too.

"I'm good with words," Percy said. "That's my thing. Words. Excellent at them."

She bumped her shoulder into his. The nervousness eased a fraction. This was their language. Teasing meant safe.

"Do you wish we'd waited?" she asked quietly.

Percy thought about it seriously. He thought about the rain in his mouth and her hands fisted in his poncho and the way his heart had tried to climb out of his throat. "No," he said. "I'm glad you did it. I'm really glad you did it. I just — "

He stopped.

"You just what?" she asked.

He picked at his shoelace. "I want one that's ours. Not the storm's. Like — on purpose. Where we're not scared someone's about to die. Where we decide."

Annabeth went very still. Then she exhaled slowly, like she'd been holding that exact sentence in her chest for three days and he had just said it for her.

"Me too," she whispered. "I want a proper one."

"Yeah?"

"Yeah. A chosen one. Not a desperate one."

They both stared at the water like it might give them instructions.

"Okay," Percy said. "Okay, good. We're agreed. We want — another proper kiss. A planned proper kiss?"

"A chosen kiss," Annabeth said firmly. "The storm one counts as history, but this one counts as — as us."

"Right. So how do we — " Percy gestured vaguely. "Do we just — schedule it? That sounds weird. Hey, Thursday at six, kiss on the dock, bring snacks?"

Annabeth laughed, a short nervous burst. "Gods, I don't know. I've read about sieges with less planning anxiety than this."

"You've read about sieges for fun."

"They're instructive!"

"Annabeth." Percy turned to face her fully, knees knocking against hers. "Are you nervous?"

"Extremely," she said promptly. "Are you?"

"Extremely."

"Good," she said. "At least we're extremely together."

He took her hand, laced their fingers the way that had become automatic over the last year, and they sat there swinging their feet while the lake lapped at the pilings and the whole enormous terrifying wonderful idea settled around them like sunlight.

"We need advice," Annabeth decided.

Percy blinked. "What?"

"Advice. Data. We have old memories, but apparently that doesn't stop us overthinking a new moment." She was already shifting into planning mode, the mode where her eyes went sharp and her hands started sketching invisible diagrams. "We need to consult experts."

"Experts? Annabeth, we're thirteen. Who's an expert? Aphrodite kids? They'll make it weird."

"Not Aphrodite," Annabeth said. "Family. Your sisters know things."

Percy stared at her. "You want to ask Clarisse La Rue for kissing advice."

"And Silena," Annabeth said. "Clarisse for — practical. Silena for — romantic. Between them we'll get something usable."

"Between them we'll get killed," Percy said.

But he was already standing up, because when Annabeth got that look, resistance was ceremonial.

---

Clarisse La Rue was in the arena hitting a practice dummy with a spear. She was Percy's older half-sister — same father, different mothers. Poseidon's daughter. Her helmet was pushed up and her armor scuffed.

Silena Beauregard, the other Poseidon sister, was sitting on the arena fence with a sketchbook.

Percy and Annabeth hovered in the arena doorway like kids about to ask a teacher for extra homework they didn't want.

Clarisse saw them first. She lowered her spear a fraction. "Jackson. Zeus girl. What do you want? You're interrupting my quality time with Bob." She jerked her chin at the dummy.

"Bob?" Annabeth asked.

"I name them so I feel less bad," Clarisse said. "This is Bob the Fourth. Bob the Third betrayed me."

Silena waved her pencil. "Hi, babies! Come for swordplay or gossip? Please say gossip, my hand is cramping."

Percy and Annabeth exchanged a look. The look said *you say it*. The other look said *no, you say it*.

"We need advice," Annabeth said finally, very fast, like ripping off a bandage. "Relationship advice. Kissing advice specifically."

Silence.

Clarisse blinked. Silena sat bolt upright so fast her sketchbook slid.

"Sorry," Clarisse said. "I thought you said kissing advice. Must have spear trauma to the ear."

"We did say kissing advice," Percy said miserably. "We want — we had a crisis kiss during the storm, and now we want a proper one, on purpose, and we don't know how to — do it right. And Annabeth said to ask you."

Annabeth elbowed him. "I said to ask *both* of you."

"You came to *me*," Clarisse said slowly, "Clarisse La Rue, for *kissing* advice."

"You're our sister," Percy said quickly. "And you're — you're brave about everything. We thought you'd know how to not be scared."

That was, Annabeth thought later, exactly the right thing to say. Clarisse's whole posture changed. The suspicion melted off her face and was replaced by something gruff and almost soft.

"Well," Clarisse said. "Well. Okay. Fine. You're right. I am brave about everything." She planted her spear butt in the sand. "Come here. Lesson one. Kissing is like boarding action."

Percy paled. "It is?"

"Absolutely. Listen up." Clarisse held up fingers and counted. "One: eye contact. Don't wimp out. Look them in the eye like you're about to take their ship. No — not like that, Jackson, that's your constipated face. Like — like you mean it."

"I don't have a constipated face," Percy muttered, while Annabeth bit her lip hard to keep from laughing.

"Two: breathing. Through your nose. Gods help you, through your nose. If you forget to breathe you will pass out and I will never let you live it down and Dad will hear about it because I will pray about it every night just to embarrass you."

"Noted," Annabeth said faintly, taking mental notes because she could not help it. "Nasal respiration. Critical."

"Three: hands. You gotta do something with your hands or you'll stand there like a scarecrow. Put them on shoulders. Or waist if Chiron isn't looking. Or — " Clarisse glanced at Silena. "What did you do with Chris?"

"I held his face," Silena said dreamily. "Like this." She cupped the air tenderly. "Like he was a precious goblet."

"I am NOT holding his face like a goblet," Percy said.

"Why not?" Silena asked. "It's romantic."

"It's weird! His face is right there! He knows where it is!"

Annabeth was turning redder by the second. "Clarisse, what's four?"

"Four: commit. Don't hover. Hovering is for cowards and hummingbirds. You go in, you do it, you pull back. Quick. Clean. Like pulling a tooth. Or stabbing."

"Stabbing," Percy repeated hollowly. "Our kiss should be like stabbing."

"Efficient stabbing," Clarisse clarified. "Friendly stabbing."

Silena put her face in her hands. "Oh my gods. Oh my gods, this is why Chris said you told him to quote-unquote establish dominance before holding hands."

"It worked, didn't it?" Clarisse said defensively. "You're dating!"

"We held hands after he beat you at arm wrestling, Clarisse, that's not the same — " Silena turned to Percy and Annabeth, sliding off the fence. "Okay, babies, ignore her. Ignore everything. Come here, let big sister fix it."

Clarisse crossed her arms. "My advice is solid."

"Your advice is a war crime," Silena said affectionately, and put an arm around Annabeth's shoulders. "Sweetheart. Listen. Clarisse is right about breathing and not hovering, but ignore the stabbing metaphor. Kissing isn't boarding action. It's — it's like dancing you make up as you go."

Percy looked hopeful. "That sounds better."

"It is better," Silena said. "Now, practical tips from someone with actual experience and excellent lips. One: chapstick. Very important. That storm chap — you both looked like prunes. Two: don't eat garlic bread right before. Chris did that once and I still bring it up at every anniversary."

"We don't have anniversaries," Percy said. "Does the dock count — "

"Three," Silena continued loudly, "pick a good spot. Not the arena. Gods, not the arena, Clarisse told Chris the arena and there was a javelin incident — "

"HE DUCKED," Clarisse shouted from behind them. "HEADS UP IS A RULE."

"— pick somewhere quiet and pretty where you won't get interrupted by javelins or satyrs or Mr. D complaining about teenagers being gross near the wine — "

"Mr. D said that?" Annabeth asked.

"He says it about everything," Silena and Clarisse said together.

Silena tapped Annabeth's nose. "Four: talk first. Say something sweet. Not a speech. Just — why you want to. Boys melt for that. Chris cried when I told him his laugh was my favorite sound."

"I did not cry," said a voice from the arena entrance. Chris Rodriguez leaned there with a shield under one arm, grinning. "I got sweat in my eyes from emotion."

"See?" Silena said. "Five: tilt. You gotta tilt or you'll bonk noses. Percy, tilt right. Annabeth, tilt left. Or vice versa. Just — opposite. Practice on an orange if you have to."

"Practice on an orange?" Percy said.

"I practiced on an orange," Chris offered. "Clarisse made me. She drew a face on it. It was humiliating and effective."

"Bob the Orange gave his life for love," Clarisse said solemnly. "Never forget."

Annabeth looked like she was trying to memorize all of this and also set it on fire. "Is there a six?"

"Six," Silena said softly, and her teasing melted into something sincere. She took both their hands, one in each of hers. "Don't worry if it's awkward. Chosen kisses can still be a little awkward, however well you know each other. That's how you know it's real. If it was perfect it would be a movie. You're not a movie. You're Percy and Annabeth, and you're thirteen and you're sweet and you've already survived a storm together. Awkward is fine. Awkward means you're paying attention."

There was a beat of quiet in the arena. Even Clarisse stopped glaring at Bob the Fourth.

Then Percy said, very quietly, "Thanks."

"Yeah," Annabeth said. "Thanks. And please don't tell the rest of camp. We're not inviting an audience."

Clarisse cleared her throat loudly and picked her spear back up. "Yeah, yeah. Don't get sappy. And Jackson — " She pointed the spear at him. "If you hurt her feelings, I will boarding-action *you*. Understood?"

"Understood," Percy said.

"And Annabeth." Clarisse pointed the spear at her too, then lowered it, awkward. "If he does something dumb — he will, he's my brother — just — tell us. We'll handle him. Lovingly. With violence."

Annabeth smiled, watery and bright. "Got it."

"Now scram," Clarisse said, but there was no heat in it. "I have to mourn Bob."

As they left, Silena called after them, "Sunset! Do it at sunset! Everything's prettier at sunset! And chapstick! And no garlic bread!"

Chris called, "Tilt opposite! Don't both go left! Left-left is death!"

Clarisse called, "BREATHE THROUGH YOUR NOSE!"

They spent the rest of Sunday in a state of heightened, ridiculous nerves that Grover Underwood later described as watching two squirrels try to defuse a bomb.

It started at dinner. The pavilion was golden with late light. The Poseidon table was, as always, loud. Percy and Annabeth sat in their usual spot where the Poseidon and Zeus tables almost touched. They tried to act normal. They failed.

Percy put salt in his lemonade. Annabeth buttered her napkin. They both reached for the same roll and jerked back like it was electrified.

Across the table, Malcolm from Athena raised an eyebrow at Annabeth. "Are you okay? You're red."

"I'm sunburned," Annabeth said instantly. "In June. At dinner. Indoors."

"Compelling," Malcolm said.

Grover, who had trotted over with his tray to sit with them, looked from one to the other and stopped himself before asking. "You two have plans, don't you?"

"It's not today," Percy said too quickly. "It's — we're thinking about — it's a general — "

"Then I hope you have a good evening," Grover said, keeping his voice low enough that nobody else could hear.

Half the pavilion turned around.

"No we aren't!" Annabeth squeaked, which fooled no one because Annabeth never squeaked.

"You are!" Silena sang from two tables over. "Sunset! Dock! I told you! Oh, this is perfect, Chris, they're doing sunset dock!"

Clarisse stood up and banged her goblet on the table. "QUIET! All of you! This is a military operation now! No one follows them! No one spies! No one places bets!"

"Too late," Connor Stoll called. "I got five drachmas on nervous laughter before!"

"Ten on someone trips!" Travis added.

"You're betting on us?" Percy demanded, horrified and flattered in equal measure.

"We bet on everything," Travis said. "Last week we bet on whether Chiron would wear the brown vest or the black vest. Love you. No pressure."

Chiron, passing with his tray, paused and gave them a warm, knowing look that made both of them want to melt through the floor. "Percy. Annabeth. Whatever you have planned this evening, the lake is lovely at sunset. Mr. D and I will be ensuring the Big House porch is — very occupied — between seven and eight. For administrative reasons."

"Thank you, Chiron," Annabeth whispered.

"Discretion is a virtue," Chiron said gravely. "So is chapstick. Silena left some on your plate, Percy."

Percy looked down. Sure enough, there was a little tin of strawberry chapstick on his bread plate with a note in Silena's looping script: *FOR THE MISSION. No garlic bread. Love you!!*

He wanted to die. He also wanted to laugh. He did both, internally, at the same time.

After dinner, Annabeth dragged Percy to Cabin One's back porch. On her desk was a half-finished model of the new footbridge and now — incongruously — a single orange with a face drawn on it.

Percy stared at it. "Is that Bob the Second?"

"Silena gave it to me after dinner," Annabeth admitted, pink. "She said, and I quote, tilt practice, honey, trust me. I told her we were beyond oranges and she said nobody is beyond oranges."

Percy picked up the orange. It had big eyes and a smile and the words *YOU GOT THIS* on its forehead. "I love our family," he said. "I say that with complete exhaustion."

Annabeth sat down on the porch steps and pulled her knees up. The sun was starting to slant. Gold pooled on the path. "Percy? Can I tell you something?"

"Always."

"I'm scared it's going to be bad." Her voice was very small. Not weak — Annabeth was never weak — but honest in the way she only was with him. "Not bad like — I don't want to. I do want to. I really do. Just — what if I bonk your nose or I forget to breathe and faint like Grover said his cousin did or I laugh at the wrong second and you think I'm laughing at you — "

"Annabeth." Percy sat down next to her and took her hand. His palm was sweaty. Hers was too. That somehow helped. "What if I'm bad at it? What if I tilt the wrong way and we do the left-left death thing Chris warned about? What if my hands are cold?"

"They're always cold," she said fondly. "You're a sea creature with circulation issues."

"See? Terrible. You should pick someone with warm hands. Like a Hephaestus kid. They're basically space heaters."

She laughed, and some of the tightness left her shoulders. "I don't want warm hands. I want your hands."

"Even if they're clammy with fear right now?"

"Especially then. Means you care."

They sat there while the light turned. Somewhere inside the cabin, someone was playing a lyre badly. A breeze moved through the pines.

"You know what Silena said that stuck with me?" Percy said. "About saying something sweet first. Why you want to. I think — I think I want to do that. Not a speech. Just — "

Annabeth's heart hammered so loud she was sure Cabin One could hear it. "Okay."

"But not here," Percy said quickly. "Down at the dock. Where — where we always go. Is that okay? Sunset, like Silena said?"

Annabeth nodded. She was smiling so wide her cheeks hurt. "Sunset dock. It's perfect. It's ours."

"Seven-thirty?" Percy asked. "Chiron said seven to eight is clear."

"Seven-thirty," Annabeth agreed. "I'll meet you there. I need — I need to go talk to Thalia first."

Percy blinked. "Thalia? Like — permission?"

"No!" Annabeth said quickly, remembering the exact distinction they had agreed on that morning. "No, not permission. I'm thirteen, she's not my mom, even if she acts like it sometimes. Just — she's my sister. She raised me. I want her to know. I want — I don't want her to hear it from Connor Stoll's betting pool."

Percy nodded slowly. That made sense. Thalia Grace was thunder in human form — fierce, loyal, terrifying to freshmen, secretly soft for exactly two people in the world, one of whom was currently sweating through his camp shirt on Cabin One's porch steps. Annabeth's older sister in this timeline, the one who had found her and kept her alive and taught her to be brave before Camp ever did.

"Should I come?" Percy asked.

Annabeth shook her head. "She loves you, but she does her protective static-electricity thing when you're around and I'll never get a word out. I'll find her. You go — get ready. Whatever that means."

"What does that mean?" Percy asked plaintively. "Do I shower? Do I change? Is this a change-shirt situation?"

"Percy, you always need to change your shirt, you have pizza on this one — "

He looked down. She was right. There was pizza on it.

"Seven-thirty," she repeated, standing and pulling him up with her. "Dock. No garlic breath. Breathe through your nose. Tilt opposite."

"Don't hover," Percy recited. "Commit. Friendly stabbing — no, wait, ignore that one."

"Ignore that one," Annabeth agreed, laughing despite herself.

They hugged quickly at the porch steps, tight and warm, and then Annabeth went to find her sister while Percy went to panic in private.

---

Thalia Grace was on Half-Blood Hill, sitting under the pine, watching the sun go down. She heard Annabeth coming up the path two steps at a time.

"Hey, sparkplug," Thalia said without opening her eyes.

"Hey," Annabeth said, slightly out of breath, and flopped down next to her in the grass.

Thalia opened one eye. Her little sister was flushed and picking grass with intense focus. Thalia knew that look.

Thalia sat up. "Okay. Spill. Do I need to kill someone?"

"No! No, nobody — no killing." Annabeth twisted grass around her finger. "It's — it's good. It's a good thing. I think. I hope."

Thalia waited. Thalia was good at waiting. She'd learned it from years of being the older sister, the one who stayed up while Annabeth had nightmares and didn't push, just left the light on and hummed until the breathing evened out.

Annabeth took a deep breath. "Percy and I want an evening that's ours. We already kiss. But after the storm, I want a kiss without anyone in danger or a quest clock ticking. At sunset, on the dock, if we both still want that."

Thalia blinked. Then she grinned, wide and helpless. "Oh, thank the gods. Finally, an actual plan."

"Thalia!"

"What? You two have been mooning at each other since you arrived together. The visiting Hunters have a pool. Phoebe owes me twenty bucks because I said you would plan a quiet date this spring and she said you would keep waiting for summer."

"You BET on us too?" Annabeth demanded, but she was laughing.

"Everyone bets on you, it's camp tradition, get used to it." Thalia bumped her shoulder against Annabeth's. Then her grin softened. "Hey. Hey, look at me. Are you happy? Is this what you want?"

Annabeth nodded, eyes bright. "Yeah. Our other kisses count too. We just want this moment quiet and planned. No danger. Just — us deciding when to stop overthinking it."

Thalia felt something catch in her throat. Her baby sister, who she had carried on her back when they were small, sitting here at thirteen, glowing like sunrise.

"That's beautiful, you little sap," Thalia said gruffly.

"I know," Annabeth whispered. "Don't cry."

"I'm not crying. There's pine pollen. I'm allergic to your cuteness." Thalia swiped at her eyes and missed. "So why are you telling me? You don't need my permission, you know that, right? Your heart's yours."

"I know," Annabeth said quickly. "It's not permission. It's — you're my sister. You raised me. I wanted you to know first. Before the betting pool tells you. And I wanted — " She hesitated. "Are you okay with it? With us? I know Dad's weird about — about things, and I know you worry — "

Thalia's expression turned fierce in an instant. Not angry at Annabeth — never at Annabeth — but at the world that made her sister ask.

"Listen to me," Thalia said, taking Annabeth's face in both hands. "I am more than okay with it. Percy Jackson is a good kid. If he ever hurts you I'll electrocute his shoes, but I trust him with you. I trust *you*. If you choose him, that's enough for me."

Annabeth's eyes filled. "Really?"

"Really. Zeus himself could come down in all his never-wears-the-same-face glory and tell me otherwise and I'd tell him to file a complaint with the pinecone." Thalia hugged her hard, fiercely, the way only big sisters could. "I'm proud of you. Both of you. Doing it right. Choosing it."

"Thanks, Thali," Annabeth mumbled into her shoulder.

"Now go," Thalia said, shoving her lightly. "Go get your sunset. And Annabeth?"

"Yeah?"

"Chapstick," Thalia said solemnly. "Silena told me to tell you. She Iris-messaged me, the traitor. She said if I didn't remind you she'd never forgive me."

Annabeth laughed through tears. "I have it! Percy has it! We all have chapstick!"

"Good. Now scram. I'll be — elsewhere. Definitely not watching. Definitely not hiding in the trees with binoculars like a creep."

Annabeth narrowed her eyes. "Thalia."

"What? I'm going to be on the hill. The hill is far. If I happen to have excellent vision and happen to get emotional, that's my business."

"You're going to watch, aren't you?"

"I'm going to *not* watch in your general vicinity," Thalia corrected with dignity. "It's different. It's sisterly."

Annabeth hugged her once more, hard, then ran down the hill toward the lake, ponytail flying, while the sun sank lower and gold spilled across the grass.

Thalia watched her go, hand pressed to her chest, and whispered to the wind, to whoever was listening, "Be good to her, Seaweed Brain."

Percy Jackson spent the hour before seven-thirty in a state of preparation that could only be described as military-grade panic.

He showered. He changed his shirt — twice, because the first replacement also had pizza on it. He brushed his teeth until Grover, sitting on Percy's bunk, said, "Dude, your enamel's going to complain."

"I need to not have gross breath," Percy said around toothpaste.

"You had eggs for breakfast," Grover said. "You're fine. Here." He tossed Percy the strawberry chapstick. "Silena's orders. Apply liberally. Think of it as armor."

Percy applied it. He looked in the cracked mirror over the sink. His hair was doing its usual thing, which was everything at once. His eyes were too bright. He looked terrified. He looked happy.

"You look like you're about to fight the Minotaur," Grover observed.

"I feel like I'm about to fight the Minotaur. Except the Minotaur never made my stomach do this." Percy pressed a hand to his stomach. "Is it supposed to feel like there are eels in there?"

"Totally normal," Grover said wisely. "When I asked Juniper to the solstice dance I threw up twice and then ate a tin can. Love does that."

"That's reassuring."

"I'm full of wisdom." Grover stood and straightened Percy's collar with surprising gentleness. "Hey. You good? Really?"

Percy met his best friend's eyes in the mirror. Grover, who had been there since the beginning, who had carried them both more times than any of them counted.

"Yeah," Percy said quietly. "I'm good. I'm nervous, but I'm — I'm sure. Is that weird? Being sure and nervous?"

"That's the best kind of sure," Grover said. "The other kind is just cocky. You don't want cocky for this. You want sure-and-nervous. That's the kind that means it."

Percy nodded, throat tight. "Thanks, G-man."

"Anytime, Seaweed Brain." Grover hugged him quickly, hard. "Now go. It's seven twenty-five. Don't be late. Girls hate late. I read that in a book. Or Silena told me. One of those."

Percy grabbed his hoodie and ran.

The path down to the lake at sunset was the most beautiful Percy had ever seen it. Light slanting through the pines. The creek sparkling. Crickets just starting.

His heart hammered the whole way down. *Breathe through your nose. Tilt opposite. Don't hover. Say something sweet. Don't eat garlic bread — well, too late to mess that up, good. Chapstick — check. Hands — shoulders? Waist if Chiron isn't looking — gods, where do hands GO — *

He reached the dock and stopped.

Annabeth was already there.

She was standing at the very end of the dock where they always stood, where they had stood after waking in this changed world, furious and uncertain about their new families, where they'd sat that morning with their feet in the water. The sunset was behind her, turning the lake to fire, turning her hair to gold at the edges. She'd changed too — clean camp shirt, her owl bracelet, her hair down instead of in a ponytail, which she only did for special things. She was twisting the bracelet around and around.

She looked up when she heard his sneakers on the wood. Her face lit — that immediate, involuntary brightening she only ever did for him — and then went nervous again.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi," he said.

They stood there, six feet apart, grinning like idiots.

"You came," Annabeth said.

"Of course I came," Percy said. "Seven-thirty. Dock. Mission."

"Mission," Annabeth repeated, and laughed — a nervous, breathless little laugh that made Percy laugh too, and then they were both laughing, standing on the dock laughing at nothing and everything while the sun sank and the water lapped.

Percy walked forward until they were close. Not touching yet. Close enough to see the freckles across her nose that showed in the warmer spring light, the charcoal still faint on her wrist, the way her gray eyes reflected orange and pink.

"You changed your shirt," Annabeth said.

"You noticed my shirt?" Percy said. "Smooth, Jackson. Real smooth."

"You have pizza on all your shirts, it was a fifty-fifty guess." She reached out and straightened his collar where Grover had already straightened it. Her fingers lingered a second. "You brushed your teeth for like an hour, didn't you?"

"Grover timed me. Four minutes. Felt like an hour." He took her hands. They were cold. His were cold. Perfect. "You let your hair down."

"I thought — Silena said — it would be prettier," Annabeth admitted. "Is it weird? I can put it back — "

"No," Percy said quickly. "No, it's — you look — " He stopped, swallowed. *Say something sweet. Why you want to.* Silena's voice in his head. Clarisse's voice too: *Commit. Don't hover.* "You look like you. That's my favorite. You always look like you."

Annabeth's eyes went soft and shiny. "Percy — "

"Can I — can I say the sweet thing first?" he asked. "Before I forget it? I rehearsed it and if I don't say it now Grover's pretzel speech will push it out."

She laughed wetly. "Say it."

He held both her hands tighter. He looked her in the eye, not the constipated face, the real one. "Okay. So. The storm kiss — I'm glad it happened, but you were scared and I was scared and we didn't choose it. And I like choosing you. I choose you every day anyway. When I save you the blue gummy bears and when I listen to you talk about drainage for twenty minutes and when I — when I wake up and you're the first person I want to tell stuff to. So I want to choose this too. On purpose. While the sun's going down and nobody's dying. I want to remember it like this."

Annabeth stared at him. Her lips parted. No words came out for a second, which for Annabeth was unprecedented.

"That was really good," she whispered finally. "Did Silena write that for you?"

"No! That was all me! I rehearsed it on the way down and a squirrel looked impressed!"

She laughed, crying a little, laughing a lot. "Okay. My turn. My sweet thing." She squeezed his hands. "You — you make me brave in a different way than Thalia does. Thalia makes me brave like — like I can fight. You make me brave like I can — feel things. Like it's okay to want things that aren't plans. I plan everything, Percy. I planned this and I still don't know what I'm doing, and that's — that's because of you. You make not-knowing feel safe. So I choose you too. Every day. Even when you put salt in your lemonade."

"I did that once!"

"You did it tonight!"

"Oh gods, you saw that — "

"I see everything, I'm Athena — I'm Zeus — I'm — whatever, I'm yours, you know that — "

"I know," Percy whispered. "I'm yours too."

The sun touched the far treeline. Gold flooded the dock. The lake went pink and orange and deep blue underneath. A fish jumped somewhere with a soft plop. The wind moved once through the pines and then stilled, like the whole camp was holding its breath.

Percy remembered, distantly, every piece of terrible wonderful advice. *Breathe through your nose. Tilt opposite. Don't hover. Hands on shoulders. Commit.*

He put his hands very gently on her shoulders. She put hers very gently on his waist, fingers curling in the fabric of his hoodie. They both tilted — Percy right, Annabeth left, opposite, thank the gods, no left-left death — and they both laughed at the exact same second because their noses bumped anyway, just a little.

"Hi," Annabeth whispered, so close her breath warmed his cheek, strawberry chapstick and nerves.

"Hi," Percy whispered back.

And then they kissed.

It was brief. It was closed lips, soft and a little clumsy, Percy's hands shaking the tiniest bit on her shoulders, Annabeth's fingers tightening in his hoodie. It lasted maybe three seconds. Maybe four. It tasted like strawberry chapstick and sunset and relief.

It was perfect.

They pulled back just an inch, foreheads almost touching, eyes still closed. Then Annabeth snorted. Then Percy snorted. Then they were both laughing again, foreheads pressed together, hands still holding on like anchors.

"We did the nose bump anyway," Annabeth gasped.

"Left-left death avoided, but nose bump achieved," Percy said solemnly. "We're pioneers."

"Did we breathe?" Annabeth asked. "I can't remember if we breathed."

"I breathed. I think. Through my nose. Clarisse would be proud." Percy opened his eyes. Her gray eyes were right there, shining, happy, a little teary. "Was it — was it okay?"

Annabeth smiled, wide and real and unguarded in a way she rarely let anyone but him see. "It was better than okay. It was chosen."

"Yeah?" His grin spread until his cheeks hurt.

"Yeah. Can we — can we do it again? Now that we know we can? For — for data?"

"For science," Percy agreed gravely.

So they did it again, a second time, still sweet, a little less shaky, a little surer, sunset gold on their closed eyelids. This one lasted a breath longer. This one felt like coming home.

When they pulled back, Annabeth tucked her head under his chin the way she had through their remembered years together, and Percy wrapped his arms around her, and they stood at the end of the dock wrapped up together while the sun sank the rest of the way and the first stars pricked through the eastern sky.

"Do you think the betting pool is satisfied?" Percy murmured into her hair.

"I think Connor owes Travis five drachmas because we definitely had nervous laughter before," Annabeth said. "And someone definitely tripped — did you trip? I felt a trip."

"I tripped on a nail on the way in. Don't tell Clarisse. She'll make it a lesson."

They swayed a little. The dock creaked. The water lapped.

"I love you," Annabeth said quietly, simply, to the sunset and to him. Thirteen and fearless. "You know that, right? Not — not like grown-up movies. Just — you're my person."

Percy's throat closed. He held her tighter. "I love you too. You're my person too. Forever, even if you keep buttering napkins."

"I buttered one napkin!"

"And I'll never forget it."

Up in the treeline above the lake, half-hidden behind a pine with bark digging into her shoulder, Thalia Grace stood with her hands clamped over her mouth and tears running freely down her face.

She had told herself she wouldn't watch. She had told herself she would give them privacy, be cool, be the chill older sister who trusted and waited on the hill.

She had lasted approximately four minutes on the hill before muttering, "I'm just going to make sure no monsters show up, that's a legitimate sister duty," and creeping down through the trees with all the stealth she had learned on camp patrols.

Now she was crying so hard her shoulders shook.

She watched Percy take Annabeth's hands. She watched Annabeth laugh through tears. She watched the nose bump and the laughter and the two brief, sweet kisses backlit gold, and she pressed her fist to her mouth to keep from sobbing out loud and scaring them.

*That's my baby sister,* she thought. *Choosing her person. Safe and happy and thirteen.*

She thought of carrying a six-year-old Annabeth on her back and promising, *I'll keep you safe until we find somewhere safe.* She thought of every thunderstorm they'd huddled through, every night she'd hummed until Annabeth slept. And here was Annabeth, safe, at Camp, at sunset, held gently by a boy with sea-green eyes and clammy hands who loved her with his whole enormous heart.

Thalia cried harder.

A branch cracked behind her. She whirled, hand on her spear — and found Grover, Clarisse, Silena, and Chris crouched in the bushes behind her with a pair of binoculars, a box of tissues, and a bag of popcorn respectively.

"What are you guys doing here?" Thalia hissed. "Clarisse, you said no spying! You made it a military operation!"

"I said no one follows them," Clarisse whispered back, eyes suspiciously bright. "This is overwatch. It's different. It's tactical."

"You have popcorn!" Thalia accused.

"It's emotional support popcorn!" Chris whispered. "Silena, give her a tissue."

Silena, already crying, handed Thalia a tissue with a sob. "They tilted opposite! Did you see? They tilted opposite! Our babies! Our beautiful babies!"

"They held hands the whole time," Grover wept quietly. "Did you hear what he said? He chooses her every day — "

"I heard," Thalia choked. "Shut up, I'm trying to be tough."

"You failed," Clarisse said gruffly, swiping at her own eyes with the back of her hand. "We all failed. They're perfect. If anyone tells Jackson I cried I'll deny it and then I'll kill them."

"Noted," Chris said, putting an arm around her.

Down on the dock, Percy and Annabeth stood wrapped together against the sunset, laughing softly about something none of the watchers could hear. The lake glowed. The stars came out. The world, for once, was gentle.

Thalia stopped at the edge of the marked patrol path, realized the couple had not invited spectators, and turned back toward the cabins. She told the others that the dock belonged to Percy and Annabeth for the evening and there was no reason to watch them. Her love for her sister did not give her a right to spy.

They crept away through the pines together, five unlikely guardians, sniffing and smiling and shushing each other, leaving the dock to the two thirteen-year-olds who had chosen each other — not in desperation, not in a storm, but on purpose, at sunset, with nervous laughter and strawberry chapstick and love.

On the dock, Annabeth tilted her head up. "Do you hear something in the trees?"

Percy listened. Branches. Sniffling. A whispered argument about popcorn.

"Nah," he said, smiling. "Just the wind."

"Just the wind," Annabeth agreed, smiling too, leaning into him.

And they stayed until the last gold faded from the water, holding hands, chosen and choosing, thirteen and brave and just beginning.
