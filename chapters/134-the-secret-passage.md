## Chapter 134: The Secret Passage

By the time Percy heard about the secret passage, it had already explained three things: why Cabin Six's porch light had flickered, where someone had hidden a missing box of drachmas, and how the Ares cabin reached the dining pavilion without passing the arena.

"It's under the cabins," a younger camper told his friend across the breakfast table, oatmeal cooling between them, spoons clinking against ceramic. "All the way from Athena to Poseidon."

"No, it starts at the Big House."

"My counselor said it starts at Cabin Six."

"Maybe it has more than one entrance."

Percy glanced at Annabeth. Her spoon had halted halfway to her mouth.

"Heard of a passage under the cabins?" he asked.

"Not one connecting them," she said. "Old service spaces run under parts of camp. Drainage, water lines, storage. Nothing on the plans connects Cabin Six to Cabin Three."

"That sounds like a maybe."

"A maybe with considerable missing information."

Across the table, Clarisse lifted her head from her toast. "My cabin contains no secret passage."

"The rumor says it starts at Cabin Six," Silena said.

"Then Cabin Six contains no secret passage."

"You haven't checked," Percy said.

"Were a secret passage in my cabin, I would have found it."

"Maybe you weren't looking."

"I look for useful things."

"A hidden route could prove useful."

"For sneaking?"

"For reaching places."

"Places are reachable by walking."

Smiling over her cup, steam warming her face, Silena said, "A passage could hold interest as camp history, even without use for sneaking."

Annabeth set down her spoon. "That interests me. A service corridor might reveal how camp was laid out before the cabins were rebuilt."

Long enough back at camp, Percy recognized the look that meant a question had become a map in her head. "So we ask Chiron?"

"We ask Chiron," Clarisse said. "Nobody crawls beneath a cabin because a rumor grew bored."

"I wasn't planning to crawl under anything."

"Good. I don't want to drag you out by the ankles."

Passing Clarisse the jam, its seeds glinting ruby in the morning light, Silena said, "We can test whether the story holds truth. If only pipes, we'll have learned something about pipes."

"Not my hoped-for outcome," Percy admitted.

Annabeth's eyes met his. "I know."

An old passage linking the cabins offered exactly the right blend of mystery and ordinary camp nonsense, rumor bright as a new coin. Percy wanted to see where it led, to feel cool dark close around his shoulders. He also wanted to watch what Annabeth would notice along the way, the quick narrowing of her eyes before a measurement.

---

The rumor might have stayed a joke if a younger camper hadn't been accused of stealing the drachmas.

Percy heard about it on his way to the Big House. Two Hermes campers stood by the fountain, arguing over a missing box that belonged to their cabin's supply account. The younger one kept saying he'd never seen it. His counselor had already checked the cupboards, but someone had decided that the secret passage must lead to a hiding place beneath the cabin.

"If there's a tunnel, he could have carried it out without anyone seeing," the older boy insisted.

"That's not evidence," Annabeth said.

"It's a possibility."

"So is somebody moving the box because it was left on the wrong shelf."

Percy looked at the younger camper. He was small enough that his camp shirt still hung past his wrists. He had gone very pale.

"Where was the box last seen?" Percy asked.

"At the counting table. I helped put away the coins, and then the counselor said it was gone."

"Did you sign it out?"

"No. I wasn't supposed to. I just counted."

The older camper opened his mouth to object, but Annabeth held up a hand.

"Before we start looking for secret tunnels, let's find out who was responsible for the box," she said.

They went back to the Hermes cabin with permission from its counselor. Percy stayed outside while the counselor checked the storage room; Annabeth read the supply log and asked when the box had last been counted. The record contained two different handwriting styles and a crossed-out entry. It was a clerical mess, not a mystery worthy of a prophecy.

"Here's the transfer," Annabeth said at last. "The box was sent to the Big House for the camp games. Someone initialed it, but didn't write the destination on the front page."

The counselor looked embarrassed. "That was me. I put the note in the wrong column."

The older boy flushed. He stared at the younger camper as though he'd expected the case to be more dramatic.

"So there wasn't a thief?" Percy asked.

"Not unless Chiron stole his own games budget," the counselor replied.

Nobody laughed. The younger camper had been frightened, and the accusation did not vanish just because the record had been corrected. He wanted the older boy to apologize. He had to ask twice before the apology came.

Percy stood beside him while it happened. He didn't tell the boy to be grateful that the others had finally checked the ledger. He knew what it felt like to have people decide who you were based on a story they'd already been telling about you.

Annabeth wrote the correction in the margin and had the counselor initial it. The missing drachmas were accounted for, and the question of the passage was still open.

"That's the trouble with a good rumor," she said as they left. "People use it to explain things they haven't bothered to check."

Percy kicked a pebble along the path. "You sound disappointed that our mystery isn't a crime."

"I'm pleased it isn't a crime. I'd rather not have a camp where the first response to a missing box is to blame somebody younger."

"Then what do we do with the tunnel rumor?"

"We investigate the part that might be real. With Chiron. And we don't let anyone decide what it means before we know where it goes."

They found Silena and Clarisse waiting outside the Big House. The news about the box had spread faster than their walk. Clarisse looked annoyed.

"Someone told the little kid he could crawl out of the cabin and hide under the ground," she said. "That's not even physically plausible from the way the cabins are built."

"Rumors don't need measurements," Percy said.

"They would be improved by them."

Silena had brought a small stack of paper and charcoal. She suggested drawing a simple map of the known service areas once Chiron allowed it, so younger campers could see which parts were maintenance spaces rather than a playground.

"Not a map to the entrance," Annabeth said. "We don't know whether it's safe."

"No. A map of what we know and what we don't."

That sounded like a project Percy could agree to. It would make the mystery less exciting to the people hoping for a secret route beneath the cabins. It might make camp safer. He had been accused of worse things than ruining a good adventure.

They walked toward the Big House together. The younger Hermes camper had gone back to breakfast, though Percy doubted he would enjoy the rest of his oatmeal. Percy resolved to check on him later, not because he was responsible for every quarrel at camp, but because an apology from somebody who had scared you sometimes took longer to believe than the person offering it expected.

From the Big House porch, where he was sorting repair requests, pine boards warm beneath his hooves, Chiron heard their question. The stack included a loose bench, two missing shields, a leaky roof, and a complaint about strawberries that "refused to stay in rows."

"Service passages run beneath sections of camp," he said. "Some carried water. Some granted the maintenance crew access to pipes and foundations. Most were sealed as cabins changed."

"Any between Cabin Three and Cabin Six?" Annabeth asked.

"I can't say. I recall an old plan showing a corridor beneath that part of camp, though it may have been a drain."

"May we look?"

"In daylight, with me present. We won't shift stones or open a sealed panel. Should we find an entrance, the maintenance crew will inspect it before anyone enters."

"Fair," Clarisse said.

Chiron promised to bring the old plan and meet them near Cabin Three, map tube slung across his back. From the equipment shed Percy fetched a lantern, its handle cool and worn, oil sloshing faintly within. Annabeth brought her notebook, two measuring tapes, a roll of chalk. Silena carried cloths for dusting any labels or signs. Clarisse arrived without her spear, which she announced before anyone could ask.

They started along Cabin Three's west wall, morning sun slanting across blue stone veined with salt, lizards skittering from the warmth. Annabeth measured corners and noted where the foundation shifted from blue stone to gray. Clarisse searched the soil for settlement. Silena examined an old rain channel running toward the path. Percy held the lantern while Annabeth knelt to study the masonry, though broad daylight made the flame nearly invisible.

"The porch doesn't belong to the original foundation," Annabeth said. "An older line runs beneath it. The wall was extended later."

"Does that indicate a passage?" Percy asked.

"It indicates the building changed."

"You excel at making things sound less exciting."

"I excel at stating what we know."

At the north corner, beneath a rosemary bush fragrant where Clarisse had brushed it, needles scattering across stone, she found a shallow dip. Dirt had slipped from the lowest stone course, leaving a gap wide enough to glimpse darkness, cool air sighing faintly outward. Without touching the wall, Chiron crouched to examine the opening.

"It could be a drain," he said. "We won't place anything inside."

Lowering the lantern, Percy saw a few inches of dry earth and stone before light stopped, ants threading the crumbled edge. No sound came from beyond except ordinary camp noise overhead: practice swords ringing, a distant shout, dishes clattering back toward the kitchen.

"Nothing living in there," Percy said.

"Beyond the light, we don't know what's there," Clarisse replied. "Hence we keep our hands out."

They checked Cabin Six from outside, cicadas loud in the bordering shrubs. Its gray stone base bore a rectangular patch of newer mortar on the west wall, pale against weathered courses. Annabeth traced its edges with her eyes. Almost hidden by the cabin's shadow, its straight lines mismatched the surrounding blocks.

"An access panel may have stood here," she said. "Covered during a repair."

Chiron unrolled the old plan, parchment crackling, edges soft with handling. Ink had faded to brown. The cabins appeared as simple shapes, scale approximate, but a thin line ran between the foundations of two structures near the present cabins.

"This could mark a water line," Clarisse said.

"It could," Annabeth agreed. "The line turns where the old buildings meet. That might be a corridor."

"So the rumor could hold true," Silena said.

"Partly," Annabeth said. "A service passage may exist. That doesn't mean anyone built it for secret visits."

Leaning close to the patched wall, Clarisse spotted a small curl carved into the mortar at its lower corner. She didn't touch it. "Old Poseidon mark. I've seen the same curl on palace fittings."

Now Percy recognized it: a wave curling into the suggestion of a trident, familiar from old doors and stonework in Atlantis. "So the passage connects to my dad?"

"It's connected to a symbol used in his temples," Annabeth said. "That tells us the mark is old. Not who built the passage."

"Still interesting," Silena said.

"Interesting differs from safe," Clarisse reminded them.

"I know," Silena said. "We can be careful and interested together."

From the cabin's storage room, smelling of cedar shavings and old canvas, canvas tarps cool underfoot, Chiron inspected the patch while the maintenance satyr tapped the masonry with a small wooden mallet. The center returned a hollow note. Years ago a removable panel had been plastered over, though the surrounding frame looked stable.

"We'll open it from this side," the satyr said. "Slowly. Cracked frame, we close again. Bad air, we leave."

The work consumed nearly an hour, plaster dust filming their hands and the sharp scent of disturbed lime in the air. They cleared the storage room and spread a drop cloth. Old plaster came away in pieces, chalky against their fingers. Behind it, the panel lifted from its frame without shifting surrounding stones. Cool, dry air breathed through the opening, carrying cedar and dust.

Near the gap the maintenance satyr checked airflow with a prepared testing charm, then examined the first stretch of floor from the threshold and felt the nearest timber for rot. A second worker recorded the result before anyone was admitted. He looked to Chiron.

"Sound so far. We can look, but nobody touches pipes or beams, and we turn back if anything looks unstable."

"Understood," Chiron said.

Clarisse entered behind him, then Silena. Annabeth ducked through with her notebook. Percy handed her the lantern, but she passed it back.

"You hold it," she said. "I need both hands for notes."

He liked being trusted with work that mattered, even a lantern.

---

The maintenance foreman asked Percy one question before allowing anyone to enter the passage.

"Can you feel the water through the wall?"

Percy stopped at the threshold. "Some of it."

"Tell me what you can sense. Don't try to move anything."

That was a fair distinction. Percy had spent enough time using water to solve problems that his first response to a damp place was to imagine lifting the water out of it. Here that might break an old joint or force a trickle into a wall that had held its weight for decades.

He put one palm against the stone and closed his eyes. The dampness inside the first pipe was faint and sluggish, unlike the open flow of the freshwater canoe lake or the strong saltwater currents at Long Island Sound. It wasn't a route to Atlantis. It was water caught inside a very old building.

"There's something moving along the lower line," he said. "Not fast. I can't tell whether it's supposed to be there."

The foreman compared his report with the maintenance diagram. The drawing showed the line as disconnected. A second worker found a more recent note saying part of it had been reused for the wash station near Cabin Six. The two records disagreed.

"Then we don't touch the joint," the foreman said. "If that line is live, we need to know where it goes before we isolate it."

Percy stepped back. "I could stop the water while you check."

"You could. But if you stop one end without knowing the other, where does the pressure go?"

He didn't have an answer. He could force water to obey him, but he couldn't promise that every neglected pipe would respond like an open channel.

"Right," he said. "No moving it."

The foreman seemed relieved. "Your observation is useful. It doesn't replace our drawings."

Annabeth wrote down the discrepancy and asked whether the wash station had a separate cutoff. They identified the valve on the modern system and tested only the accessible outside fixture, with Chiron's approval. The water pressure changed at the wash station but the movement Percy felt in the lower pipe continued. That suggested the pipe was carrying something else, or that his sense was too imprecise to be used as a measurement.

"Could it be groundwater?" Annabeth asked.

"Could be," the foreman answered. "Or an old line from the hill. We'll document it."

Percy found the uncertainty strangely comforting. The passage wasn't obliged to reveal its whole history on the first day they entered. Their job was to avoid making it worse while they learned.

Silena watched the foreman update the drawing. "You have to keep old records even when they disagree?"

"Especially then," he said. "Someone who repairs the wash station next year needs to know why we left this part alone."

Clarisse examined the exposed valve handles. One had been painted red long ago; the others were rust brown. She asked why the colors didn't match the modern system.

"They were labeled before the cabin was rebuilt," the foreman said. "Don't assume the paint is current."

She nodded. "Then we should warn people before anyone touches it."

He agreed. A temporary tag went on the questionable line and another on the drawing. Annabeth marked the area as unsurveyed, resisting the temptation to sketch a neat corridor beyond the point they could actually inspect.

Percy had hoped for a secret passage that led somewhere thrilling. He was getting old pipes, ambiguous records and a lesson in when his powers weren't the right tool. He liked the work more than he'd expected. It meant being useful without taking control of everybody else's decision.

The foreman did one last check of the support braces. He assigned Percy the lantern, Clarisse the spare rope and Silena the record board, so every person knew what they were carrying and what they were not supposed to touch.

"Ready?" he asked.

Annabeth closed her notebook for a moment and looked at Percy. Her gray eyes had the familiar brightness of a person about to learn something that hadn't yet been written down. He smiled back.

"Ready," he said.

Low enough that Percy bent his neck, the passage stretched ahead, cool air raising gooseflesh on his arms. Its stone floor felt solid underfoot, grit crunching faintly. One wall rose from pale blocks; the other showed brick patches. Along the base ran a rusted pipe on brackets spaced several feet apart, flaking orange beneath dust, cool damp kissing the air near its joints.

"This wasn't built at once," Annabeth said. She indicated a change in the joints. "Older stone runs rougher. Brick marks later repair."

"How much later?" Percy asked.

"Impossible to tell from here."

"A few decades?"

"Maybe. Or longer."

"Considerable maybe."

"Hence the notes."

They advanced at the maintenance satyr's pace, lantern light pooling ahead and hooves scuffing softly on stone. In a shallow recess Silena found soot along the upper stones, likely from an old lamp, its blackening smooth to the eye. Near a bend Clarisse spotted a second curled wave mark, matching the motif at the patched entrance.

"Could it mean the pipe carries seawater?" Percy asked.

"It could name the builders," Annabeth said. "Or a water route. More examples are needed."

"Could be both," Silena said.

"That happens," Annabeth agreed. "A sign can serve more than one purpose."

The old plan suggested the corridor continued east, though drawn without much precision. They followed until the route ended at a brick wall bearing a faded wooden sign. One word remained legible: *STORE*.

"A storage room," Silena said. "Almost disappointingly ordinary."

"Maybe it stores secret things," Percy offered.

"Thus ordinary storage grows suspicious."

The satyr examined the masonry. "We're leaving this closed. The wall bears the ground above. The crew will inspect from the other side."

Annabeth consulted the plan again. "A small building once stood here. The room may belong to its foundation."

"So the rumor got the passage right and the purpose wrong," Percy said.

"It may have," she said. "We haven't seen the whole route."

On the return, Silena noticed a long patch of pale plaster, smoother than surrounding stone. Through its center ran a thin seam. After study, the maintenance satyr said he couldn't determine what lay behind without testing from the other side.

"Something lies beneath the plaster," Percy said.

He had shifted the lantern aside, and in angled light a few faint lines emerged. One sharp zigzag sprouted three short rays. Like a lightning bolt it looked.

Leaning near the wall, Clarisse said, "Could be a mark."

"Or the edge of a larger picture," Annabeth said. Without touching the surface, she drew what was visible in her notebook. "Plaster covers paint. Someone who knows preservation must examine it without damage."

Percy held the lantern steady while she finished the sketch. Except for her pencil's soft scratch and the faint tick of cooling metal from the pipe, the passage stayed quiet. He watched her work: marking dimensions before decoration, noting the arch hidden under plaster, recording light direction so she could recover the lines, breath fogging faintly in the cool passage. He liked how she could render a place understandable without pretending full knowledge.

"That's good," she said when she finished. "Thank you."

"I held a lantern."

"You held it where I needed."

"I can manage that."

A small smile crossed her face. "I know."

Chiron had the entrance closed again while the maintenance crew arranged proper inspection. Outside, to curious campers they reported only an old service corridor, dust still on their shoes and chalk on their fingers. This restraint made the rumor louder. By lunch it had acquired an underground lake, a secret armory, a door that opened only at the right word.

"We should correct them," Clarisse said.

"Once we know what's behind the plaster, we will," Annabeth said. "Until then, we state what we've actually found."

"An old maintenance corridor and a mysterious wall," Percy said.

"A plastered section possibly covering a painting."

"Less catchy."

"More accurate."

The crew agreed to inspect the wall the next afternoon. To examine it, Chiron asked Myra, an older Athena camper who had preserved a painted border in the Big House. After practice Percy found Thalia near the arena and described the faint lightning mark, sweat and dust sharp in the air, leather and hot sand layered beneath.

"You went underground?" she asked.

"With Chiron and maintenance. Safe. Mostly pipes and old stone."

"Mostly?"

"A wall remains unopened."

She gave him the look that meant she would have preferred earlier notice. Percy explained they had followed the rules, and Annabeth had mapped everything. Worry eased in Thalia's face, though it didn't vanish.

"You should have sent someone to tell me," she said.

"We didn't know what we'd found until we were inside."

"You could have asked."

"Fair."

"What did the mark resemble?"

"A zigzag. Like lightning."

Thalia's expression altered. "I'll come tomorrow. I know some old Zeus markings."

"Annabeth thought you might."

"And Annabeth is coming?"

"Of course."

"Good. I want her interpretation of the wall, not merely the rumor."

"She suspects a mural."

Setting her spear back on the rack, Thalia said, "Then we'll learn."

---

That evening the rumors about the passage multiplied. By dinner someone was claiming the old wall showed a route to Olympus. Someone else insisted it must be a prison that Hades had built before the camp was founded. Percy corrected three versions before he decided correcting the fourth was making him part of the entertainment.

Chiron posted a short notice near the dining pavilion: an old maintenance corridor had been located; the inspected section was closed to general use; work would proceed under supervision. He did not mention where the access point lay. Most campers read the notice and went back to their dinner. The most determined rumor collectors called it proof that Chiron was hiding something.

Annabeth took a copy of the first survey to Cabin One. Percy walked with her as far as the fork, carrying the lantern she'd borrowed from the Big House. The oil had been extinguished and the glass was cool. He had expected her to spend the entire walk talking about the plaster. Instead she watched Thalia coming down from the watch hill.

"She'll want to see the zigzag," Annabeth said.

"That was the plan."

"She'll want to decide what it means, too."

Percy considered. "Don't you?"

"I want to find out. That's different."

Thalia joined them, wind rattling the straps on her armor. She asked to see the drawing. Annabeth unfolded it beneath the porch light and showed the small mark at the plaster seam, a crooked line that might have represented lightning or might have been part of a larger figure they'd not yet exposed.

"It could be Zeus," Thalia said.

"It could be a crack in a painted border," Annabeth replied.

Thalia gave her a hard look. "I know what lightning looks like."

"I'm not disputing that. I'm saying we don't know what it belongs to."

The exchange ended with silence, which Percy found more alarming than an argument. Thalia had spent years trying to predict the moods of a father who changed his face when he chose to visit. A mark on a wall could become another thing she felt obliged to understand before it hurt anyone. Annabeth had spent years trying to keep architecture from becoming prophecy. Percy understood both impulses well enough to know that they were about to collide.

"Chiron has asked a conservator to look tomorrow," he said. "Nobody is deciding what it is tonight."

Thalia exhaled. "I want to be there."

"I know."

"Not to stop Annabeth. To understand what we're going to tell the younger campers."

Annabeth looked at the drawing. "Then let Myra lead the inspection. She knows the material."

Thalia nodded, with visible effort. "All right."

Inside Cabin One, while Percy stood at the open doorway, the sisters went through the survey notes. Annabeth had measured the exposed stones and marked the places where later repairs covered older work. Thalia checked the time of the inspection and asked whether Chiron had arranged for the passage to remain closed overnight. That was a useful question. It could be answered, and no one needed to interpret a god's motives to answer it.

When they finished, Annabeth looked at her sister. "Are you going to be upset if the mural shows Zeus?"

"I don't know."

"You can leave if it does."

"So can you."

"I might want to stay."

Thalia's expression tightened. "Then I won't make you come out with me."

Percy watched the promise settle. Thalia was not becoming suddenly comfortable with her father's image. Annabeth wasn't asking her to approve of it. Each had made a little room for the other's response.

He gave the lantern back, kissed Annabeth's cheek and headed toward Cabin Three. She was still reading the survey when he left, already comparing the old floor plan with what the maintenance crew had actually found.

Later, in his own cabin, Clarisse asked whether the old tunnel would ever be made into an escape route.

"Not while we know this little," Percy said.

"Good."

"You were hoping for one?"

"I was considering where a second exit might go if the cabin ever needed it. That's different from wanting everyone crawling around underground."

Silena came in with a cup of tea and asked whether the children who'd accused the Hermes camper had apologized. Percy said yes, though it had taken some insistence. She nodded and then returned to a piece of embroidery she was working on. It was a relief to be surrounded by people who could care about a discovery without treating every unanswered question as an emergency.

On the next morning the maintenance crew checked the air and the bracing before admitting anyone to the mapped corridor. The conservator had requested a temporary work platform, cloths to catch loose plaster and a written list of every tool brought inside. Percy helped carry the supplies to the outer room. He did not try to enter before Myra approved it, even though he'd been among the first to see the old mark.

The next afternoon, Myra inspected the plaster with a small mirror and soft brushes. Without touching it she studied the seam, then showed Chiron where plaster lifted cleanly from paint. Beneath the wall the crew laid cloths and positioned two lanterns, their glow warming the close air and drawing honey tones from old paint.

"We'll expose a narrow section," Myra said. "If paint flakes, we stop. No scraping, scrubbing, or filling what is missing. Each step gets documented."

Annabeth opened her notebook, pencil poised, pages already gridded with faint guidelines. Clarisse stood back, arms folded, boots planted wide against the uneven floor. Silena readied the camera, strap looped twice around her wrist. Thalia watched the faint zigzag beneath the plaster, breath shallow in the close air. Percy held the lantern while Myra lifted the first small fragment.

Color emerged: blue, then green, then the red-brown edge of a painted border, pigments vivid where plaster had sealed them from air. Plaster crumbled in small bits that Myra caught on the cloth. Beneath the zigzag, a pale gold bolt slanted toward the raised hand of a tall figure.

Leaning closer without touching, Thalia said, "Zeus's lightning."

"Certain?" Percy asked.

"I've seen it in old carvings. The fork at the end matches."

Myra uncovered another section. The next figure gripped a trident, its shaft wrapped in a painted crest of blue-green waves. At once Clarisse recognized it.

"Poseidon," she said. "The old tide crest. It marks the palace doors."

Percy stared as more appeared, lantern warmth against his knuckles and damp stone chilling his shoulders. The central figure's face had worn away, but dark hair and sea-colored robe remained. The trident pointed toward the ground, not at Zeus. Beside him stood the lightning-bearer, one hand resting on his shoulder.

At the far left a third figure emerged in a black cloak, folds pooled with painted shadow, helm gleaming faintly at his staff's tip. He held a short staff topped by a helm-like shape. Behind him spread small painted stars.

"Hades," Percy said.

Close together the three figures stood beneath an arched border, shoulders nearly touching beneath flaking gold leaf. Along the mural's bottom clustered a few small cabins and a dining hall in an older arrangement. Upon one cabin's steps sat a child, looking up.

"The Big Three," Silena said. "The siblings."

"From the old camp," Annabeth said. Stepping back to study the whole panel, dust motes drifting through lantern light, she added, "Observe the rooflines. The dining hall stands correctly placed, but the cabins follow an earlier arrangement. This depicts camp before the current buildings."

"So they truly came here?" Percy asked.

"The mural can't prove that," Annabeth said. "It could have been painted later to depict an earlier time."

"But its painter wanted us to see them here," Silena said.

"Yes," Annabeth said. "That much we can claim."

Percy kept the lantern aimed at the panel, oil scent mingling with old dust. These gods didn't resemble the enormous statues he remembered. They looked like people paused mid-conversation, paint catching lanternlight in soft ridges where the brush had passed. Zeus's arm bridged the space between him and Poseidon. Hades stood near enough that their sleeves touched. Neither posed above camp nor apart from it. The child on the steps belonged to the same scene.

"Maybe they wanted the child to know belonging here," Silena said.

Clarisse kept her eyes on the trident. "I'd like to save it."

"So would I," Annabeth said. "But carefully. We shouldn't redraw what is lost. First we map the whole surface and learn which parts hold stable."

"I can photograph each section," Silena offered. "The camera will record as plaster comes off."

"I'll help measure panel and passage," Annabeth said. "We can compare against old camp plans."

Clarisse nodded toward the floor. "I can help the crew clear the passage. Not touch the mural."

"And I'll keep holding the light," Percy said.

"We'll find a hook for the lantern," Myra said. "Nobody must hold it for hours."

Percy's arm had grown heavy with aching, but he kept the beam steady until she finished the first section. Annabeth studied the painted architecture, pencil moving. At her request he shifted the lantern — lower, then left — following direction until carvings sharpened into focus.

He liked watching her perceive the whole mural before full visibility, eyes tracing lines the rest of them needed light to find. She noticed the path's angle, the way old cabins faced the dining hall, the narrow spaces between painted buildings where weeds might once have grown. He admired how gently she treated blank patches. She wanted to know what had stood there, without feigning knowledge.

"The child might be among the first campers," Percy said.

"Possible," Annabeth replied. "Or the painter may have meant any camper."

"Either way, they're in the picture."

"Yes."

Thalia looked from lightning to the smaller figure, throat working briefly before she spoke. "Strange to see Zeus standing as a brother instead of a king, hand resting easy rather than raised."

"And Poseidon as a brother instead of a god," Percy said.

Clarisse's gaze stayed on the trident. "He is both."

Percy understood. The mural neither erased their divinity nor simplified the family. It showed them together in one ordinary place, beside someone who might have needed them.

Myra halted at a crack near the panel's top, its edges darkened by centuries of dust. "We need a preservation plan before continuing. Paint holds stable in the middle, but the arch is fragile. We'll brace the surface and proceed in sections, shoring timber padded so no weight bears on pigment."

"Can we help?" Silena asked.

"With records and photographs, once I've prepared the work area. Supports belong to maintenance."

Annabeth eyed the arch and added another note. "We should preserve cracks as part of the record, not erase them from the drawing."

"Exactly," Myra said. "Restoration doesn't mean pretending time never touched it."

Clarisse nodded. "We can leave worn faces. Only make certain the remainder doesn't crumble."

"That's the plan," Myra said.

Chiron promised the crew would secure the corridor and improve lighting before restoration, stringing caged bulbs so no open flame threatened pigment. Annabeth offered a measured drawing, scale exact to the quarter-inch. Silena would photograph each stage, flash diffused through cloth. Clarisse would move supplies and keep curious campers from crowding. Thalia would search Zeus cabin records for other examples of the lightning symbol.

"And Percy?" Silena asked.

He looked at the lantern in his hand. "I can help where needed."

Clarisse gave him a sidelong look. "Not a job description."

"I can carry things. I can hold the light. I can stay out of the way."

"Three jobs."

"Then I hold a job."

"You can also help carry supplies," Clarisse said. "Only don't volunteer for everything."

"I won't."

"Good."

Undramatic, the agreement stood, sealed with dusty handshakes and Myra's brisk nod. No oath, no promise the mural would look new. They would restore what could be saved, document findings, leave missing parts missing rather than invent them, honoring both the painter's hand and time's passage. Percy liked that. This long the mural had endured without requiring them to pretend full repair was possible.

---

They should have left the chamber once Myra called a pause, but nobody moved at first. The half-revealed painting held their eyes. They could see enough to recognize the three gods and the ancient camp, not enough to know why the painter had put them together.

Percy kept looking at the child on the steps. A pale line ran across the child's shirt, too worn to identify as a design. His first thought had been that it looked like a wave. He didn't say so. He knew how quickly one detail could become a story that everyone repeated as if they'd witnessed the original.

Silena came beside him. She had lowered her camera because Myra was packing away the pigments.

"You want him to be a son of Poseidon," she said quietly.

"I don't know."

"I think you do."

Percy considered denying it, then shrugged. "Maybe. It would be nice."

Clarisse heard them. "It might be any camper. Don't make the painter choose our cabin after a hundred years."

"I'm not."

"You were thinking it."

"That's allowed."

"Sure. Just don't write it on the wall."

Silena smiled, but the exchange stayed with Percy as the crew gathered their tools. The three of them shared a divine father, but the same heritage had given them radically different lives. Percy had been raised in a palace after Sally died in the rewritten childhood; Clarisse and Silena had grown up elsewhere. If the child in the painting had been a son of Poseidon, that did not tell them whether he had lived with a king or been left to find his own shelter.

"My mother would have liked the little camper," Percy said.

He meant Sally, and he felt the old ache as soon as the words left him. In the altered life his sisters knew, Sally had died when he was little. They wouldn't understand how many specific remembered afternoons he carried from a life in which she hadn't. He didn't try to make the comment do more work than it could.

"Mine might have," Silena said. "I don't know. I wish I did."

Clarisse ran a thumb along the grip of her practice dagger without drawing it. "Maybe the painting was meant for the campers, not for the gods. The kid's the one looking up. He'd see them when he came out of the dining hall."

Percy looked again. That was possible. The child's place near the bottom of the mural was not a sign of insignificance. It might be the artist's chosen point of view.

Annabeth joined them with her notebook. "I wish we could date it more precisely."

"Would a date change what the painting means?" Percy asked.

"It could. If we know when the cabins looked like that, we can say whether the artist was painting the present or imagining an older camp. But even then we wouldn't know what they believed."

"Thalia thinks the lightning is certain."

"The symbol probably is. The meaning of the whole scene isn't."

Percy touched the edge of Annabeth's closed notebook, careful not to smear graphite across the page. He liked that she could admit where a measurement stopped and an interpretation began. Her intelligence had often kept them alive in the world they remembered. In this one it could keep their curiosity from erasing other people's work.

At the end of the chamber, Thalia was watching the worn face of the painted thunder god. Her own father sometimes came to her in a stranger's face. There was something painfully fitting about a portrait whose face had been lost while the lightning remained unmistakable.

"Do you wish the painter had shown him differently?" Percy asked.

Thalia took a long time to answer. "I wish he'd been different. That's not the artist's problem."

Annabeth moved close enough to touch her sleeve. Thalia didn't turn away.

"Will you come back when Myra opens the next section?" Annabeth asked.

"Yes. If I'm invited."

"You are."

Percy watched the two of them standing beneath an image of gods who seemed, in paint, much easier with their siblings than the gods had often been in life. He understood why the mural mattered to the camp. He was also glad they were not treating it as an instruction manual for their own family.

Myra called them toward the exit. Nobody had to decide what to believe about the painting before the next section could be examined. They could preserve it first and argue about meaning afterward.

Myra stopped them before they left the mural chamber.

"One more question," she said, looking at Annabeth rather than Percy. "When we open the passage to campers, what are they going to think they're allowed to touch?"

Annabeth had been sketching the arch and the painted figures. She paused. The exposed plaster lay beneath a cloth, and the little area of surviving gold at the edge of the wall had begun to dry in the lantern heat. Myra had asked for the lamps to be moved farther away, but the chamber remained warm.

"We'd need a barrier," Annabeth said.

"A barrier keeps hands back. It doesn't tell anyone why."

"We could explain the paint is fragile."

"Good. And what if they ask to take a piece as a souvenir?"

Percy looked startled. "Who would do that?"

Myra gave him a level look. "Someone who thinks a piece of a wonderful old picture is more valuable in their pocket than on the wall. Usually someone who means no harm."

Clarisse stepped closer to the painted figures. "Put a guard at the entrance."

"For an entire summer?" Chiron asked from the doorway.

Clarisse frowned. "Maybe not."

Silena took out her notebook. "We could schedule short visits with someone trained to guide them. Not everybody at once."

"And who would train them?" Myra asked.

"I can help write the instructions."

Myra nodded. "Then we should start with a condition record. Before people see the painting, we need to know what state it is in today. Otherwise every new chip becomes a mystery about when it happened."

They spent the next hour making the record. Annabeth measured the uncovered portion of the wall and marked the edges of every crack. Percy held a steady lantern at the height Myra requested. Silena wrote the descriptions Myra dictated, including the colors that had survived and the places where plaster was lifting. Clarisse checked the chamber for places a visitor might trip or touch the wall while trying to keep their balance.

"That step is narrower than the one before it," she said. "If a younger camper rushes in, they'll reach for the wall."

"Then the route should avoid it," Annabeth replied.

She drew a safer path along the opposite side and marked where a removable rail could be placed. Chiron approved the idea on the condition that the rail's posts not be drilled into historical stone.

The obstacle was practical. A rail without holes needed to stand on its own, and the passage was too narrow for a heavy base. Annabeth proposed two freestanding supports joined by a cord that could be moved when the maintenance crew worked. Clarisse tested the idea using chairs from the storage room and discovered the cord could be knocked loose by someone carrying a large bag.

"Then visitors leave their bags outside," Silena said.

"That will make them grumpy."

"Better grumpy than scraping an ancient picture."

Myra smiled. "That is the sort of decision restoration often requires."

Percy watched them revise the route a second time. The old mural had survived years behind plaster because almost nobody knew it was there. Revealing it would change that. They couldn't keep it safe simply by admiring it.

Thalia arrived to inspect the temporary barrier. She was impressed by the exposed figures, but her first question was about the air in the chamber.

"Does it need more ventilation?"

"Yes," Myra replied. "But we must not direct a strong draft across the surface before it's stabilized."

"Then that door stays closed until you know."

Chiron agreed. He assigned the passage to maintenance and put a temporary notice at the entrance. It gave no sensational description of the mural. It said the corridor was under restoration and unauthorized entry could damage both the artwork and the visitor.

Percy thought the notice was disappointingly dull. He also thought it would work better than a warning about ghosts or secret treasure.

On the walk back to the cabin, Annabeth kept examining her sketch.

"What bothers you?" he asked.

"I want to see the whole painting."

"So do I."

"But I don't want my impatience to be the reason some of it gets ruined. Myra knows the process. We need to let her set the pace."

"You're very good at setting a pace."

"Which is why it's difficult to let someone else do it."

He took her hand. "You were good at asking today."

"I practiced."

Percy smiled. It was more believable than pretending she'd suddenly become patient. He'd watched her stop at least three times when she wanted to run ahead. At the corridor door, Myra had finally let her see one more narrow section of pigment, on the promise that she would not tell campers it was a completed discovery. She'd kept that promise.

The mural existed. Its age, purpose and missing portions remained uncertain. Percy liked that they could carry the discovery home without pretending the missing parts had revealed a prophecy or a warning. Sometimes the most interesting thing behind a wall was something someone had once made and others had tried to preserve.


---

When they had finished documenting the exposed section, Silena spread her photographs on the worktable. The images looked unreal in the lantern light: blue against gray plaster, a flash of old gold, the painted child so small that a careless crop could make the whole scene seem to belong to the gods alone.

"I want to show people," she said.

Myra looked at the prints. "Eventually."

"Not tonight?"

"Not without captions. These pictures don't show the whole wall, and the restoration process isn't complete."

Silena pressed her lips together. "If we keep everything quiet, the rumors will get worse."

"Then make a statement about the discovery. That doesn't require sending an unfinished photograph into every cabin."

Percy watched his sister consider. She hadn't taken pictures to make herself famous. She wanted campers to feel included in a discovery that belonged to their home. But Myra knew how quickly a picture could become proof of something it didn't show.

"Could I draw a general view instead?" Annabeth asked. "No exact access route, no unverified reconstruction. Just the shape of the exposed area and the objects we can identify."

"Yes, if we label missing sections and uncertainty."

Clarisse looked at the first print. "You can't show the child in a close-up and tell everyone you know whose kid it was."

"We don't," Silena said.

"Then say that."

Silena gave her a sharp look. "I'm aware."

Percy expected the exchange to become an argument. Instead she set the print aside and took a fresh card from her bag. She wrote a draft caption: *Old wall painting in a maintenance corridor at Camp Half-Blood. Partial cleaning reveals three divine figures and a small human figure. Identity and date unconfirmed.*

"How's that?" she asked.

"Better," Myra said.

"It sounds dull."

"Accurate things sometimes do."

Percy laughed. Silena pretended to be offended and then asked Myra to correct the term for the outer pigment layer. The conservator explained it and watched her write the word in full. The photographs would stay in the work archive until the condition record was complete; the public notice could use Annabeth's labeled diagram and a short account of what the crew had found.

Thalia examined the draft too. She asked whether the diagram should identify the lightning bearer as Zeus with certainty. Annabeth pointed to the recognizable symbol and said they could write *likely Zeus, based on lightning attribute*, while keeping the full work's interpretation open. Thalia nodded. She did not demand that the picture present her father in a better light than he deserved.

They carried the draft to Chiron. He read it aloud, changed one sentence about the age of the painting and approved the general description. It was not the thrilling announcement Percy might have made at breakfast. That was probably its greatest virtue.

Later Silena would make a separate set of photographs for the restoration crew, showing the areas each camper had helped preserve. It mattered to her that the work be remembered, not merely the spectacle of discovering it. Percy offered to hold the papers while she sorted them. For once he didn't insist on being the person who carried every box.

The corridor remained quiet while they packed up. Behind the temporary curtain the painting waited, neither restored nor forgotten. Percy could have imagined the lost faces and filled them with all the hopes he held for his own family. He chose to leave them as they were.

Before they could leave, the maintenance foreman asked Annabeth to show him exactly which turn they had followed. The old stone floor was dry now, but a water stain ran up one wall and disappeared beneath a later patch of mortar.

"That section stays closed," he said. "We don't know what's behind it."

Percy looked toward the dark opening. "Could be another room."

"Could be a drain that hasn't been inspected for decades," the foreman replied. "Or a pocket with poor air. Curiosity isn't a permit."

Annabeth drew a line across the route on her plan. She marked the inspected corridor in ink and the sealed branch in pencil, then added the date and the name of the person responsible for opening it. The difference mattered. A map that showed every possible gap as a passable path would be worse than a map with an honest blank.

Clarisse checked the lanterns and counted the group. "Who has the spare?"

Silena lifted her hand. She held a small lamp she hadn't lit. "I didn't need it."

"Keep it until we get out."

They walked single-file through the surveyed section. At the entrance the foreman fixed a temporary barrier across the stairs. He asked that none of them share the precise access point with younger campers until the work had been assessed. Percy agreed. He knew how quickly a rumor about a hidden passage could become a dare.

"We still found something," he said to Annabeth when they were outside.

"We found enough to know what we don't know."

"That's a disappointing treasure."

"It might be the safest one."

The foreman signed the survey and kept the working copy. Annabeth kept a version marked *not for general circulation*, with the closed branch clearly labeled. The discovery belonged to the camp, not to whoever happened to squeeze through the opening first.

The boy from Hermes cabin was waiting at the fork when Percy emerged. He had a towel over one shoulder and a half-eaten apple in his hand. Percy recognized him from the accusation over the missing drachmas. The boy glanced at the others and lowered his voice.

"Did you find the box?" he asked.

"We found where it was supposed to be logged," Percy said. "That's what mattered."

"No, I mean underground. Did you find a room full of stuff?"

"No. A passage and an old painting."

The boy looked excited. "Can I see?"

"Not yet. It's closed while the maintenance crew checks it."

"People say you're hiding treasure."

"People said you were hiding drachmas."

He went still. Percy hadn't meant it as a rebuke, only as a reminder of how quickly the same rumor could be turned against a person. The boy stared at his apple.

"I'm sorry," Percy said. "That sounded harsher than I intended."

"It's okay."

"It isn't, exactly. You asked a fair question."

"Nobody believes me when I say I didn't take anything," the boy admitted. "Even after the counselor fixed the record, one of the others said I probably knew about the tunnel."

Percy felt anger rise. He could have promised to make the older campers stop. He couldn't guarantee it, and the boy had already spent enough time being told what other people supposedly knew.

"Would you like me to go with you to Chiron?" he asked. "The counselor can explain the correction publicly if that's what you want."

The boy considered, then nodded.

Annabeth put her notebook away. She'd been listening from the other side of the path. "The corrected entry has both signatures. It's not just our word."

They walked to the Big House together. Chiron heard the account, called the Hermes counselor and arranged for the corrected supply record to be explained at the next cabin meeting. He offered to speak privately to the campers who had continued the accusation. The younger boy chose to be present for the first part and to leave before the rest. Chiron accepted the choice.

On the porch Percy sat beside him while they waited for his counselor. The boy asked if the underground painting showed a secret door.

"Not that we found," Percy said.

"Then why's everybody excited?"

"Because somebody painted it a long time ago, and somebody else covered it so it survived. We get to learn who worked on it, maybe. That's interesting even if it doesn't lead to a stash of treasure."

The boy looked skeptical. "It's just a picture."

"Sometimes a picture tells you things nobody bothered to write down."

"Like what?"

"What camp used to look like. Which gods somebody wanted to put together. What they thought a kid standing in the middle of it might feel."

The boy took another bite of apple. "Is the kid in the picture like me?"

"We don't know who the kid is."

"That's not what I asked."

Percy looked at him. "I think he might be."

The boy seemed satisfied by that without needing Percy to name the painted figure or claim a history he couldn't verify. He finished the apple and stood when his counselor came.

Chiron stepped into the doorway with the draft notice Silena had written. He asked Percy and Annabeth whether they were comfortable with its wording. Percy read the careful phrases about an old maintenance corridor, a partially exposed painting and work proceeding under supervision. It didn't describe secret treasure or a route from one cabin to another.

"Could we add that the discovery belongs to all of camp?" he asked.

"It is part of our shared history," Chiron said. "But belonging doesn't mean everyone can enter at any time."

"Then say both."

Annabeth added a sentence: *The conservator will arrange supervised viewing when the site is stable.* The words didn't promise a date they couldn't yet know. Silena approved the revision, and Clarisse said she'd personally throw out anyone who tried to find the entrance before the work was finished.

"Or," Chiron said, "you could report them to the supervising counselor."

Clarisse looked wounded by the lack of faith in her judgment. Percy laughed.

They went toward the cabins with the evening light flattening the shadows on the path. The excitement of finding the passage had changed into a quieter kind of commitment. It would take workers, notes, patience and enough restraint not to make a wonderful discovery into a dangerous one.

They left the passage to maintenance and carried notes back to Cabin Three, evening cool settling over the path, pine needles soft underfoot and woodsmoke threading the air. Campers still whispered about the discovery. By dinner the story had transformed again: supposedly the mural showed gods building camp with their own hands. Annabeth corrected one version; Chiron promised an announcement once Myra finished assessing paint.

Inside the cabin, lamplight pooling gold across the table, Annabeth sat at the table and began a clean drawing of the panel. Beside her sketchbook lay the old camp plan, its brown ink fragile at the folds. Silena sorted photographs, and Clarisse studied her image of the trident, worn prongs visible in lantern light. Leaning over Annabeth's shoulder, Thalia traced the painted lightning's line in the air.

"The arch runs wider than it looked inside," Annabeth said. "The corridor angle made it seem narrow."

"You can see better now," Percy said.

"I can think better now."

"That too."

In broad shapes she drew the three figures, leaving worn faces blank, charcoal whispering across fresh paper. Below she added cabins, dining hall, the child seated on steps.

"Do you think the child was real?" Percy asked.

"I don't know," she said. "I hope the painter knew someone worth remembering."

Percy looked at the photograph of his family in Atlantis, still hanging above his bunk, edges softened from handling, glass smudged where fingers had traced familiar faces. Its people were not statues or symbols. They were his father and mother, his brother, his sisters, Annabeth and Thalia. With a much older family the mural did something similar: it granted gods a place to stand, and set a camper in the same picture.

"I hope we can save it," he said.

"We can help," Annabeth answered.

"You think it will take a while?"

"Probably."

"And you still want to?"

"Of course. Good work isn't always quick."

She bent over the drawing again, hair falling forward and brushing the page. From the table Percy lifted the lantern and held it over the page, careful to keep light from her eyes, moths ticking softly against the cabin window beyond. Once she glanced up, smiled, and continued.

"You were good down there," she said.

"I held a lantern."

"You paid attention. You shifted it when I asked, and you left untouched what you shouldn't touch."

"Clarisse was watching."

"Not the reason."

No ready reply came. He liked being noticed for care, warmth spreading through his chest at the simple acknowledgment. More, he liked being trusted enough to hear what she needed and allowed to help, lantern smoke curling faintly upward.

"I liked watching you work," he said. "You saw things everyone else would have passed."

"The passage existed before I found it."

"I know. You still saw it."

Her shoulder rested against his, steady through his shirt, pencil pausing as she leaned. "Glad you were there."

Beside her he sat, holding the lantern over the sketch, its warmth steadying against the cabin's evening cool. Across the room Silena and Clarisse debated whether the trident's painted waves resembled crest or spiral more closely, tapping the photograph for emphasis. Having found a note in camp records, Thalia read it aloud. Familiar, overlapping voices filled the cabin.

The rumor had led to an old maintenance route, not a secret shortcut, its stones cool with groundwater and history. The passage had led to a mural waiting behind plaster while camp changed around it, patient through seasons of footsteps overhead. It proved nothing about every god-story, nor explained the difficult parts of their families still tender and unresolved. Simply something found together and worth caring for, lantern after lantern, note after careful note.

Annabeth finished the outline of the small camper on the steps, pencil lingering on the lifted face. Steady, Percy held the lantern while she shaded the old roofline, lead whispering across paper. For once, a hidden passage had led to a picture of family — not a warning or a weapon, but three siblings standing beside an ancient camp, lanternlight warm across pigment, with room in the scene for someone else.
