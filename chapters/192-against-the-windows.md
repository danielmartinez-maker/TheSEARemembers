## Chapter 192: Against the Windows

The frost let go of the hill in the middle of the night, and Percy Jackson woke at ten past six to the sound of it happening.

It was not one sound. It was the whole small vocabulary of thaw: the eaves over the porch giving up their icicles one at a time, a tick and then a pat on the boards below; the iron of the door frame letting go with a noise like a knuckle cracking; water coming down the inside of the stovepipe in a slow arpeggio that started and stopped and started again; and, off past the brake where the ninth lantern stood, the regular drip-drip of pine trees shaking themselves off like dogs coming out of a lake.

The cabin was warm. That was the first surprise. The night had been cold enough to put Monday's frost to shame and everybody in the cabin had said so at bedtime in the tone of people lodging a complaint. Yet the room stood at the temperature of a held breath, because Silena had banked the stove at eleven with the last two armloads from the shed and damped it down with the flat of the shovel, and the stove had done the thing the stove always did, which was to hold the heat and give it back slowly, all night, without being asked twice.

He lay still and let the cabin tell him who was awake.

Four beds breathing, softly, in the particular rhythm of people who have nowhere to be until at least seven. Two beds empty and made: Clarisse's, squared off the way she squared everything, and the one by the door that belonged to nobody in particular and had had a kid in it since the start of October. Through the window over the sink — the one with the rolled towel tucked into the frame — the grey light came in, and the glass was running with water in three thin streams that had worn their own paths in a month of dirt. The frost that had been on that glass at bedtime had gone back to being water and was finding its way down in the grooves. Somebody would come and do that window properly today. He had been thinking about it since Friday, in the way he thought about most things, which was from the outside.

He got up. Socks two pairs, one drawn inside the other; trousers; the wool shirt; the field jacket off the hook, and he knew the weight in the left pocket before he put it on, the way you know where your own hands are. It had been in that coat since the sixth of October, which made fifteen days by his count, and the count was his own and he had never once said it out loud to anybody except the coat. Gloves, two fingers mended at the knuckle. Boots, which had stood by the stove and had come out of the night stiff as two loaves, as they did, as they would keep doing until April.

In the kitchen part of the room Silena was at the range with her sleeves pushed up and her hair tied back with a piece of string, doing something to a pot of oats that involved the apple butter and a great deal more attention than oats had ever earned from anybody. The four little ones were at the table in their coats with their collars up, eating fast and saying very little, because the room was still cool enough that talking cost breath. A sentence came out of one of them as a small white cloud with words inside it, went about a foot and a half, and stopped existing.

"Your breath's back," Percy said. "That's disappointing. I enjoyed Monday."

"That was Monday," the little one said, and shrugged, which was the whole of the answer and everybody present accepted it.

Clarisse was at the table with her tea and the list, and beside the list, face up, a sheet of brown paper ruled in Silena's square hand with the day's teams written down the middle of it. She had the pencil behind her ear. She had had the pencil behind her ear since Friday, which in this family had come to mean that a thing had been decided and the deciding was only waiting for daylight.

"Morning," Percy said.

"Morning," Clarisse said. "You slept through the drip."

"I woke up at the drip. I've been lying here being smug about it for ten minutes."

"You've been lying here being cold for ten minutes. The drip is just the excuse you use." She turned the brown paper a quarter turn so it faced him, which was the way she handed anything over. "Today's the day."

"Which day."

"The day where we stop the outside from coming in." She said it flat, the way she said things that had cost her something to say. "The fix-projects thing on Friday was the rehearsal. Friday was everybody fixing their own business. Today's the rest of it. Today's the camp, and it's got till dark."

Silena turned round from the stove with the spoon in her hand and the face of a woman who had been waiting all morning to be the one to explain. "The thaw's doing us a favour," she said. "It'll take two days off in the sun for the ground to go the other way and then nothing packs and nothing takes a stake. Right now the mud's soft to the depth of a hand and it'll do what it's told. So: mud today. Panels today. Pipe sweeps today. Anything that's a sewing job waits, because sewing wants dry, and there isn't a dry afternoon coming this side of November."

Percy pulled the chair out and sat down at the end of the table, and Clarisse read the brown paper out in the flat voice she used for inventories, which was the voice she had used on the slate at the deep end on Tuesday and on the survey stakes on Saturday the week before, and which meant she had thought about it more than she intended to admit.

"Teams. One cabin each, nobody swaps without my say-so. Cabin Three's mine and yours." She tapped the page. "The Hermes pair have the boathouse store and the pavilion north end. The forge have the stove-pipes, all of them, every cabin, plus the panel-cutting at the sawhorses. Thalia's got the archers and the pine. Katie takes the small ones and they carry, they do not climb, and if anybody hands one of them an axe I want to know which one of you did it, by name, at dinner."

"By name," Percy repeated.

"By name." She went on down the page. "Marcus and Olive have the ropes and the cart and the string. Chiron's walking it at the end with the lantern, and what Chiron doesn't sign off is not done, whatever Silena's hat says."

Percy looked up from the oats. "Silena's hat?"

Silena set a bowl in front of him with the care of a woman defusing something, and took the grey wool thing off the shelf by the door and put it on the table. It was a hat that had been grey once and was now the colour of a wet afternoon, with a bobble that had been lost in September in an argument with a pine, and everybody at the table looked at it the way you look at a familiar animal.

"It's the arguments hat," Silena said. "Today there's going to be techniques. There is always techniques. Somebody will say the bead sits proud and somebody else will say you tool it flat, and by ten o'clock that's not an opinion any more, it's a border war, and nobody in this camp is getting a border war out of a caulk gun."

"So people write their ruling down and put it in the hat," Percy said.

"People write the ruling down and put it in the hat, and the hat holds them till the fire tonight, and then we empty it out and we see who was right, and whoever was wrong buys the next round of whatever the winner was right about." She folded her arms. "It's a democracy. I've never voted in it and I never intend to. That's what makes it fair."

"How do I know my ruling?"

"You'll know." She took her spoon back. "Also you've got a visitor coming up the path with your name on her face, so eat."

Annabeth Grace came in without knocking, because she had knocked in June and it had been arranged then, between the two of them, without a word, that she would come in. She had the field book under her arm and her field jacket zipped to the chin and her hair doing what it did in weather, which was to agree with the weather. She took the last chair, which was the chair by the stove, which was the best chair, and put her cold hands flat on the table between the bowls and let them warm while she read the brown paper over Clarisse's shoulder.

"Teams," she said.

"Teams," Clarisse said.

"You've put me on Cabin Three."

"You've put yourself on Cabin Three. I just wrote it where you couldn't pretend you didn't see it." Clarisse drank her tea. "You measure. You're the only person at this camp who measures a thing before she touches it, and half of what's happening today is people touching things before they measure them."

"Flattery," Annabeth said, in the tone of a person receiving a shipment and checking it against the invoice.

"It's not flattery. It's arithmetic." Clarisse put the pencil behind her ear again, although it was already behind her ear. "The pine's mine. The bank and the awning stay where they are and nobody's to look at them. If anybody asks me about the bank today, the answer is mud, and if anybody asks me again the answer gets longer."

"What happens if they ask a third time?"

"Then they get to help me with the pine," Clarisse said, and Percy grinned into his oats, and one of the little ones said, "I want the pine," and was told, by four people at once, "You're carrying," and accepted it as the justice it plainly was.

They went out at twenty past eight into a morning that was not cold so much as wet with cold, the way a cloth is wet with water. The frost had come off the grass and left it dark and flat and steaming very slightly, and where the week before there had been white there was now a thin shine of water lying in the tread of last week's footprints. The west path went down the slope between the nine lamps, all of them dark and beaded with melt that had run down inside the glass and dried in streaks, and up at the top of the slope the forge chimney was putting out its thin honest smoke, and the smell of the morning was the smell of a camp that had been rained on inside its own frost: wet cedar, wet earth, woodsmoke, and, from somewhere down by the lake, the flat mineral breath of water that had now been open to the air for a week and was going to stop being open to it shortly.

Half the camp was already moving. Katie's line came down past them four abreast in coats that were too big, arguing about who had promised who the good saw. Marcus and Olive went by with the cart, Olive walking backwards with the string coiled on both hands and Marcus doing the talking, which was the arrangement they had. A boy from the Apollo cabin went past at a trot with a coil of rope on his shoulder and the particular forward lean of somebody who had been given a job by Thalia and intended to keep it.

Cabin Three stood at the bottom of the row with its stovepipe putting up its small straight column into the still air, and the three of them came up the path to it with the tool basket between them, and Annabeth stopped on the bottom step.

"There's a caulk tube on the rail," she said.

"There's two," Clarisse said. "And the door's got fresh mud on it."

They went in. The cabin was warm and it smelled distinctly, strongly, of wet putty. On the floor by the west window, open and with its papers out, was a caulking kit — the good one, out of the Big House store, the one Chiron kept on a shelf with the numbered rules — and beside it, wiped and stacked in a neat pile, four cut lengths of canvas panel, all of them the right size, and a hammer with a new handle, and a bag of glazing sprigs with the top folded down like a sack of grain.

And standing in the middle of the room with the enormous, fragile pride of two boys who had done a good turn before breakfast, was the Hermes pair.

"We got you a head start," the taller one said. "We figured it was the nearest one to the tool shed, and you three are the busiest, so."

"Sunrise," said the smaller one. "Two hours. We did the lot."

Percy set the tool basket down very slowly, the way a person sets a thing down when setting it down fast would be a mistake. He looked at the west window. The rolled towel, the one that had lived in that frame since September, was out. In its place, tacked over the opening, was a panel of canvas, and the canvas had been fastened with, at his count and he was good at counting things that annoyed him, eleven nails, in a pattern that could best be described as hopeful. The bottom edge did not reach the sill. The top edge overlapped the glass by a finger's width and had been nailed flat over the corner of the frame in a way that could only have been deliberate if the boy had hated the frame.

He looked at Clarisse. Clarisse looked at Annabeth.

"Two hours," Annabeth said, in a voice with nothing in it at all.

"Two hours," the Hermes pair said together, and then, as the silence went on, "Is it not—"

"No," Annabeth said.

---

The good thing about a cabin that has been done badly is that it fails in the open, where everybody can see it, and Annabeth Grace had been around bad work her whole life and had never once looked down on it; what she looked down on was bad work that was hiding. So she took the panel down herself, rather than letting Clarisse take it down, because taking it down was going to be a lesson and a boy who is allowed to pull out his own nails learns more than a boy who watches somebody else pull them.

"Corner first," she said. "Not the middle. You always start at a corner, because the corner tells you whether the thing wants to come off straight."

The taller Hermes boy crouched and got a fingernail under the canvas and lifted, and the third nail came away in his hand with a small ragged sound, and he made a face at it.

"Sprigs," Annabeth said. "Not nails. For canvas on a frame it's sprigs, because sprigs go in and they clench and they don't lever the wood open the way a nail does, and in February when the canvas is frozen as hard as board you'll be glad."

"We had the nail tin," the smaller one said, in the voice of somebody who had been sent to the store for the nail tin.

"You were sent to the store for the nail tin." She did not say it unkindly. She said it the way she filed everything, which was in a drawer marked *true*. "Chiron has a shelf with numbered things on it. The numbered things are the ones people got wrong before us. That's not an insult. That's literally what a shelf is for."

They worked the panel off in four minutes, and the window underneath told its own story: the caulking had gone on thick and cheerful and had been tooled dead flat, in one long smear, with a thumb at each end.

The taller boy saw her looking and straightened up, defensive. "We did it inside-out. Like Mrs. D— like it said. Inside-out."

"It does say inside-out. That part's right. You start inside and finish outside." Annabeth put two fingers on the bead and drew them along it, and a small grey worm of caulk came up on her fingertips. "But this has been pressed flat, and pressing it flat makes a lid. And water sits on a lid. And a lid of caulk over a wet sill is not a seal, it's a puddle with a plan, and by Christmas the puddle has a plan for the frame. Neve!" she called, without turning her head, at exactly the volume that carries across a clearing.

Neve came into the cabin backwards, holding the door with one hand and a length of stovepipe in the other, and did not put the pipe down, and looked at the caulk, and said, to the room, in the flat regretful voice of a person who has watched an apprentice ruin a good tube: "It's tooled flat."

"It's tooled flat."

"It's beautiful. It's the flattest caulking I've ever seen and it's going to hold a quarter inch of rain on that sill until the second week of December." Neve set the pipe down. "The bead sits proud. It has to sit proud, and then it skins over, and the skin is the seal. If you press it flat you've made it smooth and you've squeezed the middle out and you've got two edges and no middle." She demonstrated with her thumb, on nothing, in the air, which is the way people who work with their hands demonstrate everything. "Proud. Like a worm on the wood. Then in an hour it's hard, and then it doesn't matter what the weather thinks."

"Do I write that in the hat?" the smaller Hermes boy said, and produced, from a pocket, a folded slip of paper and a pencil stub, and Silena, from the doorway, said, "That's what the hat is for," in a voice of tremendous satisfaction, and he wrote it down and put it in the hat, and the hat, which was on the table between the bowls, took the slip in with the soft sound a hat makes when it is being used for its proper purpose for the first time in years.

Cabin Three, by the time the whole of it had been assessed, had five things wrong with it, and Annabeth wrote them in the field book in her margin hand, because she had opened the book at the table without deciding to and that was how decisions got made in her: 09:52. CABIN 3: DONE TWICE. (1) BEAD TOOLED FLAT. (2) CANVAS NAILED, NOT SPRIGGED. (3) BOTTOM EDGE TWO INCHES SHORT. (4) MUD NOT PACKED AT THRESHOLD. (5) PIPES NOT TOUCHED. UNDERNEATH IT ALL: WORKMANSHIP FINE.

"Underneath it all what," Clarisse said, reading over her shoulder.

"Underneath it all, the workmanship's fine. Your panel's cut two inches short, but it's cut square, and they didn't cheat on the canvas, and I've seen worse done slower." She capped the pencil. "So we keep them."

"Doing what."

"Doing the boathouse store. Where they were assigned." Annabeth looked up. "And we do this one again. It's our cabin. I'm not letting the Hermes cabin paper over our sill with a smile."

The Hermes pair went out backwards, with the good-caulk-kit shame of boys who had discovered that being fast and being helpful were two different skills and had only got the second one right, and Clarisse stood in the middle of her cabin with her hands on her hips and looked at the west window and said, to nobody, "Twice."

"Twice," Percy agreed.

"Everything twice."

"Not everything." He picked up the sprig bag and felt its weight. "The arguing's only once. That's what the hat's for."

Then Annabeth went out to walk the day, because there was a thing she did with her hands when she had a roomful of people working and no part of it was hers, which was to carry a book and be seen to be carrying it, and the book took the edges off the whole business: a person who is writing you down is a person who has agreed that the work counts.

She wrote the margin line first, standing in the doorway with the cold coming up off the threshold mud and the smell of wet putty going out at the same time: SAT 21. 10:14. WORK PARTY. THAW ALL DAY, WIND BACKING N. CABINS IN PROGRESS. And then she went down the row, and the day made sense in the order she walked it.

Cabin One, because it was the one with her name on the door and because Thalia had taken it as a personal insult when the wind had lifted her shutter in the night. The sills were scraped back to bare grey wood, two panes reset with putty gone on thin and even in the flat bead that the ruling called for, and the granite stone still sitting on the sill of the weather window doing whatever it was the stone did, which she had stopped arguing with in September and had started, somewhere, being glad of. The door frame had fresh putty over the hinge screws and a feather of wood shaved out of the swelling so the door would shut without lifting, and Annabeth ran two fingers over the swelling, and found it good, and went on down the row with her chin up.

Cabin Four and Five, the long low stretch where the Hermes and Ares kids had paired into fours and were working two cabins at once like a crew breaking down a boat. She checked their bead with two fingers and found it proud and already skinning, and told them so, and the boy who had been the loudest voice in the morning caulk argument said, "It won?" and she said, "It won. Neve's ruling's in the hat. You can put yours in too," and he said, "Mine said the same thing," and she said, "Then you're buying," and he said "Deal" with his hand out, and she shook it, and wrote: BEAD. PROUD. CONFIRMED, FOUR CABINS, ONE DISSENT (SEE HAT).

At the forge the sawhorses had come out into the open, because a shop that was cutting forty lengths of panel could not have everybody at the bench, and Beckendorf's boy was cutting canvas against a chalk line with a knife, four panels at a time, squaring them on a template cut out of tin that was hung on a nail and was clearly older than she was. Neve had a length of stovepipe across her knees and was running a wire brush down the inside of it in a long spiral, and the sound of it came and went like something drilling at the bottom of a well. Every cabin on the hill was getting the pipe swept, the collar checked, the spark screen reset, and Beckendorf was carrying a card to each one and nailing the card inside the door frame where the wood would take a nail and lose it in the warmth.

"Hand's width," he said, when she stopped to watch, and he said it to the pipe and not to her, which was the only way he ever gave anybody anything. "From any wood. You can lay your hand flat between the pipe and the beam, it stays. You can't, it comes down and goes again."

"Whose rule is that."

"Chiron's. Mine's stickier." He did not look up, and he was, she noticed, standing where the doorway of Cabin Three was in his line of sight, and there was a woman in that doorway with an apron and a clipboard, and he was checking his work against the sound of her voice. Annabeth put that in the pile, the way she put everything, and went on up the hill.

The greenhouse stood on the north slope with its glass in good repair and its bank still nothing but wet earth, and she stood in front of it for a moment and did not touch it, because it was one of the three lines on a page she did not own. A kid from the Ares cabin went past with a shovel and said, hopefully, "The bank?" and Annabeth said, "Ask Clarisse," and the kid said, "I don't want to ask Clarisse," which was the correct answer, and went on anyway.

"Not today," Clarisse said, coming up behind her with two pine branches under one arm, without being asked. "It wants a warm day and a dry soil and this is neither. You bank wet earth and you've built a sponge round the glass, and the sponge freezes, and then you've made the greenhouse worse than it was with its opinions. Ask me in November."

"You'll say the same thing in November."

"I'll say it with more evidence." She went on down the slope.

The awning on the pavilion's west end was still torn, ten feet of canvas hanging with its seam split and one corner folded back where the wind had got in under it, and Silena's ruling was already in the hat and it was the dry afternoon one, and there was no dry afternoon, and everybody who walked past it looked at it and nobody with a needle touched it, which was the whole law of Clarisse's list, written in behaviour instead of ink.

Up past the junction the brake opened, and the pine was down in it the way it had been down in it since the storm, across the tool fence, a great grey dead thing with the bark coming off it in plates where it had hit. The crosscut saw was working on it in a long singing stroke, two of the Apollo kids pulling in turns with a rope on the end for the third pair of hands, and Thalia was at the butt end with her sleeves tied back and her jaw set and a peavey she was using the way she used everything, which was as though it had contradicted her.

"She's doing it wrong," a voice said, and Rhys Calder came past with a coil of rope on his shoulder, and stopped, and looked at Thalia's elbow with the expression of a man who had earned it. "That elbow. She's pushing with it instead of hanging on it. She'll have it in a sling by two."

"Tell her," Annabeth said.

"I told her at the eight o'clock. She told me the archery had corrected it." He shifted the rope and did not go and say anything again, because he had learned in three weeks that a thing said twice to Thalia Grace was a thing she would do wrong on purpose by the third telling. "Your family's odd," he said, without any heat in it.

"Your family's always right," Annabeth said. "We manage."

She stood and watched the saw go through the grey dust and thought, in the part of her that measured things, that the day was a machine with nobody driving it, and that the part she liked best was the pair of ends where the saw came free: one of the Apollo kids pulling, and Thalia at the butt with the peavey, and between them a rope, and beyond them the whole hillside knocking and calling and carrying, and the ninth lantern burning down in its hood on the post in the middle of the brake with Beckendorf's card tied to it and the moths arguing.

Then she went back down the hill to Cabin Three, and found Percy on his knees at the door with the board of mud and the first armful of broken pot, and the west window waiting with its canvas off and its bare glass looking naked and apologetic, and the afternoon still raw and wide open in front of both of them.

---

By half past ten the camp had turned into something that Percy had no word for except *work*, and the word did not fit it, because work in his history had been drills and quotas and the sea's opinion of a boy's mistakes, and this was not that. This was forty people on a hillside making dry rooms for the winter, and the sound of it carried down the whole slope: the crosscut saw at work in the brake, on a long singing stroke, two people pulling it in turns; the flat knock-knock of hammers on sprigs; somebody's voice raised in a technique argument somewhere past the forge; the scrape of the cart; the rattle of a nail tin; and every so often the sound of a person saying *hold that end* and another person saying *I am holding it* and a third person saying *no, the other end*.

Percy and Annabeth did the threshold first, because the threshold was the thing the cabin had been losing its heat through since August and because mud was the thing the thaw was offering and Clarisse had said, in the flat way she said the things that mattered, that whoever did Cabin Three's threshold was to pack it in lifts or not at all.

"Two inches in lifts," Annabeth said, and put her own slip in the hat before she touched the board, because she had a ruling and she intended to be held to it. "Each lift tramped before the next goes on. You want grit in the bottom lift or it shrinks off the wood in the frost and leaves a gap you can put a pencil in."

"What's the grit."

"Broken pot. Sand if you're lucky." She was already at the woodpile, picking through the offcuts for anything with a history of being fired. "Clarisse. Is there a broken pot?"

"There's a whole shelf of them behind the greenhouse," Clarisse said, going past with the pine branches over one shoulder, and Percy took that as a yes and went to find a flat stone to break them on, and when he came back Annabeth had her sleeves rolled another turn and was standing in the doorway with the first armload of mud on a piece of board, holding it level against her chest, and the sun had not come out and would not, but the whole slope had gone the flat silver-grey it went when the day had decided to stay honest and cold and busy.

They packed the threshold in three lifts. It took forty minutes and it was the sort of job where the tool is your boot: you put the mud in and then you stand on it, and then you put more mud in, and then you stand on it again, and the art of it is that you do it slowly and in order and you do not get clever. A boy from the Apollo cabin stopped on his way past with a coil of rope and offered an opinion about how his uncle packed thresholds, which took the form of a rambling story about a turf roof and a pig, and Annabeth listened to the whole of it with the grave attention she gave to anything anyone had found out for themselves, and then said, "Write it in the hat," and he wrote it in the hat, and Percy tramped the last lift flat with the heel of his boot and looked at what they had made, which was a mound of grey-brown clay packed hard as brick across the bottom of the door, with a stray stem of dry grass sticking out of it at an angle, and thought that it was the least beautiful thing he had built all month and would probably still be there in April.

Then there was the window.

The west window was the bad one. It had been the bad one since the storm, when a shutter had worked itself loose and knocked the frame twice in a night, and it had a star in the corner of the lower pane like a small white frost that had got into the glass and stayed, and it had the rolled towel in the top of the frame from September onward, and it was the reason the cabin's west side had been the cold side of his bed for a month.

"We do the pane first," Annabeth said, with her chin on one shoulder, looking at it with the expression she saved for things that had offended her personally. "Not the panel. The panel's a coat. The pane's the wall. You put a coat on a wall with a hole in it and you've just made a cold hole with a coat on it."

"Where's Beckendorf."

"Where he always is. Somewhere behind me being useful." She was already marking the rebate with the rule. "He cut a pane Tuesday for the boathouse and he had one left over and he says it's the same size as yours, which is a thing men say about windows."

"Men who cut glass."

"Men who cut glass," she agreed, and went off to fetch him, and came back with him, and the pane turned out to be two millimetres proud of the rebate, which was a number Annabeth said in a tone of enormous satisfaction, because two millimetres proud was a thing you could fix with a rasp and two millimetres small was a thing you could only fix with a phone call to the sea.

Beckendorf did the glass the way he did everything, kneeling on the floor with his sleeves rolled and the light coming in grey over his hands, putting the pane in on a bed of fresh putty and tapping the sprigs in with the flat of a small hammer, one at a time, talking to nobody. Percy sat back on his heels and watched the man's hands, and thought about Tyson's letter at the bottom of his trunk — the bolt, the hook for a tote handle, the prow of a canoe he had promised to come back and fit — and about how two men with the same trade could be so far apart and still be doing the same thing with their fingers. Then he went and got the panel, because the pane was Beckendorf's half of the job and the panel was his.

The canvas came off the sawhorse where Beckendorf's boy had trimmed it square to the new line, two inches longer than the bad one, and Percy measured it himself while Annabeth watched him do it, which was the same thing as being trusted and he knew it and did not make anything of it, which was the whole trick of being loved by her, he sometimes thought: she let him do it right and she watched him do it and she never once said either of those things out loud.

"Hold the bottom," he said.

"I'm holding the bottom." She was inside the frame with the canvas against the glass and her gloves off, because you cannot feel a thing through a glove and Annabeth Grace had never once in her life accepted a substitute for feeling a thing. "The top. Get the top and then hang your weight on it. No — hang it on the tack, don't push it with your thumb. You'll split the tack line. There."

The sprigs went in with a sound like a cough. One, two, along the bottom, the canvas coming tight against the frame with the flat report of cloth that has decided to behave, and Percy worked along the edge with the little bag loose in his fist and his breath going out level, and there was a point about two thirds of the way up the second side where he became aware that his hands had stopped being cold.

It happened the way it always happened with her, which was gradually and then all at once. She had shifted to reach the far edge without letting the canvas sag, and her shoulder had come to rest against his, and then her hip against his hip, and then the whole long warmth of her side against his side in the small space between the window and the bunk, and somewhere in there the sprig bag had gone from being an object he was holding to being an object neither of them was holding. He could feel the work in her: the day in her forearms, the cold morning still in the collar of her coat, the heat coming off the back of her neck where her hair had been pinned up and had come down again. Her hands had nail scratches across the knuckles and a smear of the same wet grey he had on his own sleeves, and she smelled of mud and canvas and woodsmoke, and she was the only warm thing in a cabin that was otherwise doing nothing but waiting for winter.

"Done," she said, quietly, to the canvas.

"It's done."

"It's straight."

"It's very straight."

She turned her head. That was all it took: she turned her head, and her forehead was right there against his jaw, and her breath went warm on the side of his neck where the collar was, and the distance between two people who have been working side by side for two hours in a cold room is not a distance at all and everybody in the world has known that forever.

Percy put the sprig bag down on the floor, because his hands were not going to be any good for what he wanted them for. Then he put one hand against the cold wall beside her shoulder and turned into the space between her and the window, and she came the rest of the way, and they kissed the way they kissed in the afternoon when neither of them had anywhere to be: unhurried, and with the knowledge in it of a thing they had learned in June and practiced every day since, that a kiss was not a door you went through but a room you stood in.

The first minute was mostly breath. Her mouth was warm from the walk up from the brake and cold at the corners where the wind had got it, and his had been cold all morning and was not cold any more, and the two temperatures argued the way they always did, and he felt her smile against his mouth at the second round, and put his hand up to the back of her head where the pins had come out and let her hair fall the rest of the way down, and she made the small sound she made when he did that, which was not a word and which he had never told her he listened for and which he would have found in the dark at the bottom of the sea.

They broke for air at the kind of distance where you can still feel the other person breathing, and she kept her forehead against his jaw and said nothing, and he kept his hand on the wall and said nothing, and the cabin went on around them: the stove ticking in the wall, the little ones' voices out on the path carrying a plank between them with the deep concentrated silence of children doing a two-person job, and somewhere below them on the slope a hammer, and above it, faint, the long singing stroke of the crosscut saw.

"My hands are filthy," she said.

"I know."

"I'm getting it on your coat."

"I know." He rubbed his thumb along her jawbone, and the wet grey of the morning went onto her in a stripe, and she looked at him out of the sides of her eyes and said nothing at all, which was permission, and he did it again, a little slower. Her hand had come around to the front of his jacket, not pulling, just holding on to it, the way she held on to the field book when she had written something true in it.

Then they were sitting on the floor with their backs against the cold wall under the window they had just made tight, with his arm around her and her legs stretched out and her boots crossed over his, and her head on his shoulder, and the day having gone out of both of them at the same time. The wall was cold enough to feel through his coat and warm enough, an inch in, where their bodies had been lying against it, and he could feel the mud drying on his boots and cracking when he moved his foot, and her hair had come loose from its pins and was doing what it did in weather, and her breath went up out of the top of his collar and into the cold air and came back down.

"I can see the whole hill from the dock," she said, after a while, with her eyes shut. "I told Marcus that. He said that was the point of a dock."

"He's right. That is the point of a dock."

"I'm building up to something."

"Take your time. I've got nowhere to be. I'm a threshold."

She pinched him, without any force in it, at the place where his ribs had a joke in them, and then put her hand flat on his chest and left it there, and he put his hand over hers, and they sat like that and let the wall take the cold, because that was what the wall was for now, that was what they had spent four hours on, and neither of them said it and both of them knew it.

"Percy," Clarisse said, from the doorway, with her coat collar up and a pine branch under one arm. "If you two are going to stand there being the most disgusting people on this hill, I'd appreciate it if the disgust could happen for another twenty minutes, because I need somebody strong at the brake and you're the only one who isn't already carrying something."

"We're sitting down," Percy said. "We're not even being disgusting. We're laboring."

"You're laboring at something. It isn't windows."

"Sister," Annabeth said, with enormous and unembarrassed dignity, "I have caulked a sill today. You have no standing."

For a second Clarisse's mouth did something that on anybody else would have been a smile, and then she was gone down the path with the branch, and Percy put his forehead against the top of Annabeth's head and laughed into her hair, and Annabeth said, into his coat, "Twenty minutes," and he said, "Twenty minutes," and neither of them moved for at least five of them, because the wall was doing its work and the day was still holding and there was a whole winter coming that they were, apparently, going to have to be lived in.

Then Annabeth got up, which she did in stages, the way she did everything that involved standing: one hand flat on the wall, one knee, a pause while the blood came back into a foot that had gone to sleep, and a look at the window on the way past that was pure professional satisfaction.

"Two millimetres," she said.

"Two millimetres," Percy agreed, and got up the same way and worse, and stood with his back to the cold wall while the mud cracked on his boots.

She had the rasp out again. She ran the flat of it along the bead twice, took the grey worm of putty off on her fingertip, showed him the fingertip, and wiped it on her trouser leg, which was the whole of the victory, and she let him watch all of it.

"Right," she said. "Panel."

They finished it in eleven minutes, which he knew only because she told him, standing in the doorway with the field book open on her palm the way other people looked at a watch. The last four sprigs went in along the top, tapped home with the flat of the hammer in the order Beckendorf had shown him at the sawhorse: corners first, then the middles, then back to the corners, because canvas moved, and you gave it the chance to move where it wanted and then took that chance away from it. And then the bead.

The bead was the part Percy had been quietly waiting for since half past nine, and he did not let it show. He loaded the tube, cut the nozzle on the angle, and ran a line of caulk along the seam between the new canvas and the frame, and then he drew the tool through it the way he had watched Neve draw her thumb through the air, which was proud, and left it proud, and then stood back and looked at a worm of grey sitting up on the wood like it had opinions of its own.

"It'll skin over inside an hour," Annabeth said.

"It can skin over whenever it likes."

"It's going to rain on Tuesday."

"Then it'll be a wet worm with a skin on it, and it'll still be doing its job." He set the tool down on the board. "That's what the Hermes pair didn't understand. They made a lid."

"They made a lid," Annabeth agreed, and wrote something in the field book that he was not allowed to see, and then, because she was not a cruel person, turned the page so that he was. 12:41. Silena Beauregard ran the inside of Cabin Three the way she ran everything, which was by standing in the middle of it with her sleeves pushed up and letting the room arrange itself around her ankles.

By the middle of the afternoon the cabin had become a place you walked through rather than a place you were. There was mud on the boots of everybody in it. The hat had gone from being a hat to being an institution, and had taken on, in the course of the day, seven more slips: one in a careful child's hand about canvas that said ONLY TWO NAILS AND ONE OF THEM WAS A HOPE; one in Neve's flat capitals about the bead; one from Marcus, which was a diagram; one from Chiron, which was three words and had such authority in it that it made Silena nervous to look at it; one from the taller Hermes boy, written small, which she suspected was an apology; and two from Clarisse, both of which were simply the words NOT YET in her block capitals, and she had put them in and closed them into the hat without comment because there were arguments and then there was the list, and the list was not an argument.

The little ones went in and out with the seriousness of ferries. Two of them had been given the job of the floor, which meant that every time somebody came in muddy they said "off, off, off," and scraped at the boots with a piece of board, and got in the way exactly as much as helps people. One of them brought her a sprig from the floor with the expression of a cat delivering a small verdict, and she put it in the pocket of her apron because that was what pockets were for, and then spent ten minutes finding that she was, without having decided to, keeping a pocketful of the day.

The stovepipe came out at half past two. The small iron stove in the wall had a collar at the ceiling that nobody had looked at since the spring, and Silena had known it since the first frost and had not said anything, because saying things about stoves was not her trade, and there was a man whose trade it was, and he came in the afternoon because the afternoons were when the shop was quiet.

Charlie Beckendorf did not knock on the door of Cabin Three, because you do not knock on a door you have been fixing for three weeks, but he did stop in the doorway and say, "Dirty," to the room, which meant he wanted to know whether he should take his boots off, and Silena said, "Off, off, off," in a voice that was not hers, and the two of them looked at each other for a half second and then both looked very hard at the floor.

"You're not dirty," she said. "You're just carrying the standard amount."

"I'm carrying the standard amount." He set the tool roll down on the bench by the door, which was where he always set it, which had become a thing. "Where's the collar?"

"Above your head. It's the one with the rag in it that I can't take out."

"You shouldn't take it out. It's supposed to be in." He had the small iron stove open in four minutes, with the ash drawn out into the pail and the grate out and wiped on his cuff, and the room filling up in a good way, the way a room fills when somebody who knows what he is doing has opened the thing that everybody else has been living beside. The pipe came down in two lengths. He ran the brush down it from the top and the soot came out onto a sheet of newspaper, and the sound of it was a dry bristle in metal, and he talked while he worked, to the pipe and to nobody and to her.

"The collar's proud by a finger," he said. "That's good. If it was sunk you'd have a cold back and you'd never know why."

"I'd know why. I'd just be wrong about it."

"You'd be wrong about it in writing. That's worse." He stopped, and looked up at her from the floor with the brush still in his hand, and the look was the one that had arrived the same way all his life and arrived earlier than he had intended. "I wrote something on the back of the card."

"The stovepipe card?"

"The card's got a back." He put the brush down. "It's under the nail on the door frame. I put the nail in crooked on purpose so the card sits proud and you can get your nail behind it."

Silena had stopped in the middle of the floor with a bowl in her hands, and the bowl had stopped making any kind of sense. "Charlie," she said. "If you've written something on the back of a stovepipe card, everybody in this cabin can read the back of a stovepipe card."

"That's why it's under the nail." He went back to the pipe. "Some people read things twice. Some people don't. I put it there for the people who do."

She knew. She went out to the doorway and got her fingernail behind the card with the nail crooked under it and lifted it off, and there on the back, in the same careful hand that had drawn the hook, were four words: SAY THE COLOUR. and under them, smaller: NOT THE ORANGE. THE HONEY.

She stood in the doorway with the card in both hands, and the afternoon was going amber and grey at the same time out past the brake, and the work party was finishing, and she read the back a second time in the doorway light because reading it once had not been enough for her heart to bank, and then she heard him behind her saying, to the pipe, "I'm not standing here while you do that."

"Then sit down."

"If I sit down I'll be here till dark."

"That's never stopped you before," Silena said, and turned round with the card, and he had the spark screen in his hands and he was looking at it with an attention that a spark screen has never in the history of iron been given by any man, and she went back in and stood beside him and looked at the new pane in the west window, which was the amber colour, the honey, the one he could not name and had been trying to find the word for in the shop since the second of October, and she said, quietly, so that the room could hear it: "It's honey."

"I know it's honey."

"I'm saying it to you."

"I heard you say it." He put the spark screen down and sat back on his heels on the floor with his hands on his knees, and the stove was open between them with the smell of clean iron and old ash, and he said, in the way he had of getting a thing said and then putting it down again, "I heard you say it in October. I thought I'd forget. I've been in that shop six afternoons with three panels of glass and a boy who cuts them, and not one of the three has once been that."

"They will be. You just have to be there when the light goes." She sat down on the floor too, on the other side of the stove, with her back against the bunk frame, because standing while he was sitting was making her dizzy in a way she could not afford to be. "You've got a bench. You'll be there."

"I'll be there," Beckendorf said, and put his hand out flat on the floorboards between them without looking at it, which was, she thought, in the language he had, a very long sentence, and she put her own hand down flat next to it, not touching, and the two hands lay there on the boards with an inch of wood between them, and the inch of wood was, at that moment, the busiest object in the camp.

Nothing about it was going to happen fast. She had known that since July and had never once thought it was a problem with it; a thing you have waited five months for you do not ruin in five minutes, and she had Silena's patience, which was not the soft kind people thought it was but the hard kind that outlasts. But the nearness of it was physical, the way weather is physical. She was aware of his hands, and of the smell of the forge that had gone cold on his sleeves since lunch, and of the fact that when he reached past her for the pail his shoulder came within an inch of her hair and stayed there for the length of a sentence about soot. He was clumsy in the small space, which nobody who worked with metal would have believed of him: he knocked the putty tin off the sill with his elbow and caught it against his hip, and then he was laughing at himself and holding up the tin, and she was laughing because he was and because she had knocked the same tin off the same sill three times that morning, and they were two people on a cabin floor with a broken stove and an empty nail on the door frame, laughing at nothing, and it was the closest she had been to anybody since June.

The light went out of the window very slowly. The pipe went back up clean, with the collar checked and the rag set in heat-proof order and the spark screen down, and Beckendorf pulled the card out from under the crooked nail and wrote the date on the front and nailed it back, and then he took the hammer and drove the crooked nail straight, which took him four seconds, and stood looking at the nail he had driven straight with the expression of a man who has just done something to a card on purpose.

"You drove it straight," Silena said.

"I drove it straight." He picked up the tool roll. "It'll hold till January."

"It'd have held till January crooked."

"Everything holds crooked," Beckendorf said, and got his coat off the bench, and stopped in the doorway with his hand on the frame the way people did in this family now, and said, without turning round, in the voice that always came out slightly too early and entirely on purpose: "I'm at the shop at five. And then he went out down the path past the brake with the tool roll under his arm, into the amber end of the afternoon, and did not look back, which was the only unclumsy thing he did all afternoon, and which she was glad of.

Silena stood in her cabin with her hand over her apron pocket, where the pocket had the sprig in it from the little one and the card's note in her memory and the man's "it's warm" going round in her like a kettle coming up, and then she went and did the last thing she had to do, which was to put the hat on the shelf by the door where it could see the whole room, and to count out of the day's work what it had actually bought.

She had seen them, from the path at half past eleven: the two of them inside Cabin Three with the canvas half up, and the way he had put his hand on the wall beside her head, and the way she had turned into it. She had kept walking, which was a decision she had made in about a quarter of a second and had never once regretted, because there are some things you see so that you know the world contains them and not so that you can say anything about them. She had gone on up the hill with the basket of panels and thought about a shop at five, and about the friendliness that was in the world, and how it was not in fact unfair that other people had it easy, and she had got to the sawhorses with her face perfectly straight and nobody had known anything.

At four o'clock Clarisse came down the path with her sleeves still tied back and grey sawdust all down the front of her coat, and put the hat in the middle of the table without picking it up, and said, "Fire at five thirty, food at six, the hat gets emptied by six thirty, and nobody's going home before Chiron's seen the cabin. Silena. Feed them."

"I'm feeding them," Silena said. "Go and be a pine."

"I'm being a pine," Clarisse said, and went back up to be one.

The pine came apart the way pines come apart, which is that you can plan it and you can discuss it, and the moment the saw bites it has opinions.

By four o'clock the brake had three lengths of it lying stacked, and a fourth coming, and the whole clearing smelled of resin and wet sawdust and the cold resin smell that a cedar brake has in October when the sun goes off it. The crosscut saw was in the hands of two of the Apollo kids and Thalia had the butt end with the peavey, and Clarisse stood over the cut with her list in her coat pocket and her sleeves still tied back and gave the word that made them all pull together.

"Straight," she said. "On the line. Not on my line, on the chalk. You said quarters and then you cut eights. Again."

"Again," said Thalia, and set the peavey, and leaned her weight into it in the way she leaned into everything, which was with her whole back and no explanation, and the saw took the chalk dust and went grey with it and the wood began to make the sound that means a section is going.

Clarisse watched the saw and not the girl, because if she watched the girl she would have to say something about the left boot, and Clarisse had learned, the way everybody at this camp had learned things about Thalia Grace, that there was a category of saying that Thalia noticed being done. "Saw's biting high on the pull," she said instead. "Lift the far handle. Not you — the small one. Lift, don't pull."

"Lift, don't pull," said the smaller Apollo kid, and lifted, and the saw came out of the wood in a long sigh with a disc of grey sawdust on the teeth of it, and Thalia straightened up with her hands on her knees and said, "Again," and got her breath, and said nothing about her boot, and nobody said anything about her boot, and the wind moved in the tops of the cedars and took the sawdust away in a thin grey sheet toward the lake.

They cut the pine in the order Clarisse had called it that morning, which was not the order it wanted. The root end first, big and wet and difficult, the wood dark in the middle and heavy as iron; then the sections where the branches had been, which came faster and smelled sweeter; then the long clean run toward the tip that had lain under the others in the fall and had gone silver-grey on the upper side and stayed the colour of tea underneath. Each length came free with a crack and a settle, and the whole length of it tipped and took on the lean it had been promising all afternoon, and Clarisse's stomach went over, the way it always went over with a big thing moving, until the ropes were on it and the cart was loaded and the little ones were hauling on the rope ends with their boots slipping and Katie standing over the whole enterprise saying, "Heave. Heave. Now stop," in the voice of a person who has moved things for a living.

The stack went up under the brake on a pallet of two lengths laid crosswise, bark up, because wood on the ground is wood with the ground in it, and every length was counted and every count was said out loud by the little ones, which resulted in the number four being reported four separate times by four separate people with four separate accents on it, and Clarisse writing them all down as four.

"That's the fence free," she said, at the end, when the last length but one was walking out of the mud on the cart, and the tool fence stood up out from under the grey curve of the fallen thing with a section of it sprung and bent and most of a season's worth of nothing done to it. "It's been under there since the storm. It's had enough."

"Does it go on the list?" Thalia said, wiping her face on the inside of her wrist, in the tone she used when she was going to say a thing before anybody could stop her. "The fence. It's sprung."

"The fence is not on the list. The fence is a Tuesday." Clarisse put the pencil behind her ear and looked up the hill, where the lanterns were not lit yet and the cabins were standing with their new mud on the thresholds and the forge smoke going flat in the wind that had got up at four. "Everything on this hill is a Tuesday. That's what the list is for. It's the four things I won't let be a Tuesday."

The butt length stayed where it was.

It was the root end of the tree, four feet across the flat of it and heavy with the weight of itself and the water of thirty October days, and it lay across the sprung section of the fence in the last of the light with its heartwood showing at the cut like a pale tongue. It was not dangerous. It was not in anybody's way. It was simply the one length that wanted a sledge and a lever and the kind of arms that arrive fresh in the morning, and Clarisse stood over it with her hands in her coat pockets and did her arithmetic, and then did the other arithmetic, which was the one where she weighed the honest pull of a four-foot block of wet pine against the honest pull of a camp at the end of a day's work, and found that the day had already had more than its share.

"Tomorrow," she said, to the butt, and Thalia, passing with the last rope, said, "It'll be there."

"It'll be there. It's a pine. It knows where it is."

Chiron came up the slope as the light went, with the lantern in his hand and his own list on a card, and the two of them went down the row together and looked at each cabin in turn, which was the part of the day that Clarisse had been working toward since a Sunday morning in a frost and had said so to nobody. Cabin One, with the putty proud on the two reset panes and the shutter hanging true and the granite stone on its sill; Chiron wrote something small on his card and did not say anything, which from him was the same as a speech. The forge cabins, with the pipes swept and the collars checked and the spark screens down and a card nailed on every inner frame in the same careful hand. The boathouse store and the pavilion north end, double-hung, the blankets lifted in and out with the air gap between them and the canvas panels tacked over the glass, and the awning still torn on the west end, and Chiron looked at the awning and then looked at Clarisse and then, very deliberately, did not say a single word about it, and she loved him a little for it and would have died before telling him so.

Cabin Three was last. Chiron came up the path and looked at the mud at the door, packed hard as brick with the grit in it and a stem of dry grass sticking out at an angle, and at the west window, with its new pane and its canvas tight, and at the card inside the door frame with the date on it and the crooked nail driven straight, and then he looked at the two Hermes boys, who had come up behind the whole party the way you come up behind a verdict.

"Twice," Chiron said.

"Twice," Clarisse agreed.

"Whose fault do you think that is?"

"Mine," the taller one said. "I read Silena's board wrong."

"Then it's a training fault," Chiron said, "and you'll remember it in February, which is more than can be said for anything I've ever told you about mucking out." He wrote TWICE on his card, and then, under it, in small letters, GOOD SECOND TIME, and then he went on up the hill to his own accounts, and the fork let the party split into its pieces, and the light went off the water all at once, the way it does in October, as though somebody had put a lid on it.

In the cabin at ten past six, with the four beds breathing and the stove ticking down and the little ones asleep under their blankets, Clarisse sat down at the table with the winter list and the last of her tea, and read the page the way she read the page at the end of a day, which was from the top, and then she took the pencil out from behind her ear and wrote on the pine line for the first time since the second week of October.

PINE — SAWN AND STACKED UNDER THE BRAKE. FENCE FREE. BUTT LENGTH ON THE FENCE — MORNING. SLEDGE. FOUR ARMS.

She looked at it, and then, because there was room in the margin and because she was alone, she wrote under that, smaller: WORKMANSHIP — CLARISSE.

And she left the greenhouse bank and the torn awning exactly where they were, and put the pencil back behind her ear, and went to the door to look down at the hill, where the nine lamps were going up one at a time along the west path and the tenth was still dark on its hook by her own porch, waiting for whoever got sent to it last.
