## Chapter 75: Zoe's Story

The fire burned low and steady in the center of the amphitheater, sending a column of smoke straight up into a sky crowded with stars. It was late, later than the younger campers were usually allowed, but no one from the Big House had come to dismiss them. The April night was cool after a mild afternoon, with a breeze moving through the pines and down the hill, carrying the smell of salt from the Sound and woodsmoke from the fire.

Percy Jackson sat on one of the long stone benches with his girlfriend Annabeth leaning against his shoulder, half asleep and pretending she was not. They had claimed a spot near the middle, close enough to feel the heat, far enough to watch everyone. Around them the camp was settled in its familiar spring-evening pattern. The Ares kids took up a whole bench to the right and shared a blanket that looked like it had been stolen from the arts and crafts shed. The Apollo kids were quiet for once, their usual singing done. The Demeter kids smelled faintly of earth and strawberries even at night. Thalia Grace sat a little apart with her legs stretched out, her electric blue eyes catching the firelight, her leather jacket thrown over the back of the bench despite the chill.

And near the fire, on a low stool that no one else ever took, sat Zoe Nightshade.

She was older than most of them, nineteen and holding it like a weight she had learned to carry well. Her dark hair was braided back, her skin bronze in the firelight, her eyes the color of storm clouds before rain. She wore a silver bracelet on her right wrist and a black shawl over her camp shirt, the shawl pinned with a small bronze key and a tiny torch. Everyone knew what cabin she was from even though they almost never said it out loud. Cabin Twenty. Hecate. Magic. Mist. Crossroads. The cabin with only three bunks filled and dust on the rest, the cabin people walked past a little faster after dark.

Chiron had asked her that afternoon if she would speak. It was a tradition, though an irregular one. Older campers near the end of their time told a story if they wanted to. Not a quest report. Not a lesson. Just the truth about how they got here and why they stayed. Zoe had said yes without hesitation, which had surprised Percy. She was private. She helped in sword training and disappeared during free time. She was kind in a distant way, the kind of person who would fix your shield strap without a word and vanish before you could thank her.

Now she sat forward with her hands folded, looking into the flames, and the whole amphitheater went quiet in the way it only did for someone they respected.

"I was asked to tell why I stayed," Zoe said. Her voice was low and clear, with an accent no one could place. "It is a long story. If you fall asleep, I will not be offended. My mother says sleep is also a kind of magic."

A ripple of soft laughter went around the benches. Annabeth lifted her head from Percy's shoulder, suddenly more awake. Percy nudged a corner of his blanket toward her without looking, and she pulled it over her knees.

"My mother is Hecate," Zoe said. "Goddess of magic, of the Mist, of crossroads and ghosts and the moon when it is dark. You know this. What you may not know is that I did not grow up knowing it."

She paused, and the fire popped.

"I grew up in a house with a blue door in a town you have never heard of, with a mother who sang while she cooked and a father who fixed radios for a living. At least, that is what I believed until I was twelve. The woman who raised me was kind. The man who raised me was patient. They were not my parents. Not in blood. They were the people my mother chose for me, because she could not keep me in her own world and keep me safe."

Thalia shifted, her brow furrowing. Percy saw Annabeth glance at Thalia, then back at Zoe. The displaced feeling was already there, settling over the older kids like a shared blanket.

"I was a strange child," Zoe continued. "I saw things. I would point to an empty corner and ask who the man in the hat was. I would know when someone was lying, not because I was clever, but because the air around them went thin, like watered milk. I would dream of roads. Always roads. Three roads meeting under a dead tree. A woman standing there with two torches, waiting. I thought she was a dream until the night she spoke to me awake."

---

The house with the blue door had a kitchen with yellow curtains and a linoleum floor that was always cold in winter. Zoe remembered it perfectly, she said, because memory was the first magic Hecate ever taught her to keep.

She was eight the first time her mother came while she was awake.

It was late October. Rain beat against the windows. The power had gone out, and her parents, the people she thought were her parents, had gone next door to borrow candles. Zoe sat at the kitchen table with a coloring book, a box of crayons worn down to stubs, and a flashlight that flickered every time she moved it.

The flashlight went out completely. She tapped it. Nothing. And then there was light anyway, soft and greenish gold, coming from the corner by the pantry.

A woman stood there. Tall. Dark hair bound up. A long dark cloak damp with rain that did not puddle on the floor. In each hand she held a torch, but the flames did not burn the ceiling. They lit her face from below, kind and tired and impossibly old.

"Hello, little owl," the woman said.

Zoe did not scream. She said later she should have screamed, but she did not. She said, "Are you the lady from the roads?"

The woman smiled. "I am Hecate. I am your mother."

Zoe told the fire that she had laughed. She actually laughed, at eight years old, alone in a dark kitchen, because it was so absurd. "My mother is upstairs looking for batteries," she said.

"Your keeper is upstairs looking for batteries," Hecate corrected gently. "She loves you very much. That part is true. But you grew under my heart first. Do you want me to prove it?"

Zoe nodded before she could be afraid.

Hecate knelt, which Zoe said was strange because goddesses should not have to kneel, their knees should not creak like that. She set one torch floating in the air, it just stayed there, burning. With her free hand she touched the linoleum.

"Watch," she whispered.

The cold floor warmed under Zoe's bare feet. A pattern spread outward from Hecate's fingertips, lines of faint silver light forming three roads meeting at a point right beneath the kitchen table. Zoe gasped and pulled her feet up onto the chair.

"Every life has crossroads," Hecate said. "Most people walk through them without seeing. My children see. You will always see. That is my gift and my burden to you. Close your eyes. Tell me which road is safest."

Zoe closed her eyes. She described it to the campfire exactly as she remembered. With her eyes shut she could still see the three silver lines. One hummed low and steady. One buzzed like angry bees. One was silent, too silent, like a held breath.

"The middle one," she whispered.

"Good," Hecate said. "You chose the steady road. The buzzing road would have led to a fall down the stairs tomorrow. The silent road would have led to nothing at all, which is sometimes worse. You see? You already know how to listen."

The lights came back on. The torches vanished. Hecate was just a woman in a damp cloak, her face pale.

"Why did you leave me?" Zoe asked. It was the question she had carried without knowing she carried it, and it came out small.

Hecate's eyes filled. "Because my world eats soft things," she said. "Because a child of magic with no training shines in the dark, and there are things in the dark that hunt shine. Because I am a Titan-born goddess and old enemies watch my doors. I placed you here, behind wards, with good people who wanted a child and did not ask where you came from. It was the strongest magic I knew. Love without blood."

"Will you stay?" Zoe asked.

Hecate shook her head. "I cannot stay long. The wards I built to hide you work against me too. But I will come when I can. On new moons, when the dark is mine. Listen for dogs in the distance. That will be me, telling the Hounds to keep watch, not to hunt."

And then the front door opened, her keeper-parents came in with candles they did not need, and the kitchen was empty except for Zoe and a faint smell of smoke and herbs.

Zoe stopped there for a moment, looking around the amphitheater. Percy realized he was holding his breath. Next to him, Annabeth had gone very still, her gray eyes fixed on Zoe. He knew what she was thinking because he was thinking it too. The idea of being placed. Of being loved without blood and still wondering where the blood went.

"She came again," Zoe said softly. "On every new moon for four years. Sometimes for an hour. Sometimes for five minutes. She taught me small things. How to hold a memory still so it does not blur. How to tell Mist from truth by the weight in your ears. How to leave an offering at a crossroads. An apple. A coin. A kind word said backward. She never did big magic in front of me. She said big magic is just small magic that learned patience."

Percy smiled at that despite himself. Annabeth elbowed him lightly, but she was smiling too.

"When I was twelve, the wards broke," Zoe said. And her voice changed.

---

She did not dramatize it, which made it worse.

It was a Tuesday in March. Rain again. She was walking home from school with a backpack full of library books about astronomy. She took her usual shortcut through the little crossroads behind the grocery store, where two dirt paths and an alley met under a streetlamp that never worked. She always left something there without knowing why. A hair tie. A pebble. That day, a button.

The air went thin. That was the only warning. The world peeled back like wet paper, and something stepped through.

Zoe described it the way children of Hecate always describe monsters. Not by what they look like, but by what they do to magic. "It drank the light," she said. "The streetlamp tried to flicker on and could not. My ears popped. My teeth ached. It was tall and wrong jointed, with eyes like coals, and it said my name in my mother's voice, which was cruel."

She ran. She said this plainly. "I ran. I dropped my books. I am not ashamed. Running is also magic when you are twelve and alone."

It chased her to the edge of the crossroads. And there, she said, her feet stopped on their own. She felt three roads under the asphalt, humming. Even in a mortal town, the crossroads was still a crossroads. Power lived there.

She remembered what her mother taught her. Close your eyes. Listen for which road is safest.

She closed her eyes while running, which she admitted was stupid, and she fell hard to her knees on the wet pavement. But with her eyes closed she saw it. Three lines. One buzzing with the monster on it. One silent and dead. One low and steady, leading left, toward the laundromat with the buzzing neon sign.

She threw her school button as hard as she could to the right, toward the silent road, and shouted a word her mother had taught her for lighting candles. "Phos."

Light exploded on the silent road. Not much. Just a flash, like a camera. The monster flinched toward it, hungry, confused by shine. Zoe scrambled left, toward the steady road, scraping her knees raw, and ran for the laundromat.

The woman behind the counter took one look at her bleeding knees and called her parents. The monster did not follow past the edge of the crossroads. Later, Zoe would understand. The offering and the spoken word had sealed the boundary for one hour. Small magic with patience.

But that night, Hecate came in daylight, which she had never done, and her face was grave.

"The ward is broken," she said, standing in Zoe's bedroom while Zoe's keeper-mother made soup downstairs, unaware. "Something found the edge and tore it. I can mend it, but it will only hold a little while. You shine brighter every year. You need training. You need a place with walls made for this."

Zoe started crying. "I do not want to leave them."

"I know," Hecate said, and she sat on the bed and held Zoe while she cried, goddess and child, and the bedspread got wet with tears. "And they will not remember you clearly, because that is part of the mercy. If they remembered fully, they would grieve fully, and they are mortal and soft and good. I will leave them the warmth without the wound. They will think you moved to live with a distant aunt for school. They will be sad but not broken. I am sorry. This is the price of my blood."

Zoe looked up at the campfire, her eyes bright but her voice steady. "I hated her for a week for that. I want you to know that. I loved her and I hated her. Both can live in the same house."

Thalia gave a short nod from her bench, like she understood exactly. Clarisse, arms crossed, was frowning, but not in a mean way. More like she was working something out.

"I came to Camp Half-Blood in May," Zoe said. "A driver with three dogs brought me in a black van. The camp looked... small. I had expected palaces. Instead there were cabins and strawberry fields and a big pine tree with something golden in it. I was angry the whole ride. I planned to stay one week and demand to go home."

She smiled a little. "That was seven years ago."

Soft laughter again. Percy felt Annabeth relax against him again, just slightly. He knew that feeling. Arriving angry. Planning to leave. Then the camp getting under your skin like pine sap.

---

Zoe talked about her first summer, and Percy and Annabeth exchanged glances over and over because it was so familiar and so different at the same time.

She was placed in Cabin Twenty, which at the time had one other camper, a boy named Alabaster who left the next year for reasons Zoe did not go into. The cabin was at the edge of the woods, half sunk in shadow even at noon. Inside it smelled of incense and old books. The bunks had curtains. There were jars of herbs and bowls of salt and a basket of keys no one would explain.

"I hated the cabin at first," Zoe admitted. "It was dark. It whispered. The Mist pooled in the corners like water. I told Chiron I wanted to sleep in the Hermes cabin, where it was loud and normal. Chiron told me, 'My dear, normal left the station a long time ago.'"

That got a real laugh. Even Chiron, sitting in his wheelchair at the back with his kind old face, smiled.

"Mr. D told me I would either go mad or become interesting," Zoe said. "I told him those were the same thing. He said, 'Finally, a Hecate child with sense.'"

More laughter. Percy grinned. That sounded exactly like Mr. D.

"But my mother kept visiting," Zoe said, and the laughter faded into listening again. "That is what I want to tell you. People think gods do not visit. Mine did. Not often. Not on schedule. But on new moons, when the camp fire burned low like this, I would feel dogs barking far away in the woods, and I would know to walk to the crossroads by the pine tree."

She described it, and her voice went softer, almost reverent.

There is a place at the edge of camp, past the boundary pine, where the main path splits. One way to the cabins. One way to the woods. One way down to the road mortals never find. A simple wooden post marks it, with three arrows. Campers pass it every day without thinking. But on new moon nights, Zoe would go there with an offering. Sometimes a cake she saved from dinner. Sometimes a drawing. Once, a broken sword hilt from training that she could not bear to throw away.

She would set the offering at the base of the post, light a small candle, and wait.

"She always came from the third road," Zoe said. "The one that is not quite there in daylight. Her torches would appear first, two lights in the dark. Then the dogs, big black Hounds with red eyes, but gentle, tongues lolling. Then her."

What did they talk about? Small things, Zoe said. Her progress with Mist control. How to lock a door without touching it. How to tell if a prophecy was heavy or light. But also mortal things. School. Friends. Whether Zoe was eating enough. Hecate would braid her hair and ask about camp gossip like any mother, and Zoe would tell her about Clarisse stealing the Apollo cabin's sunscreen and filling the bottles with blue paint, and Hecate would laugh until she cried.

"She told me once that magic is not power," Zoe said. "Magic is permission. The world says no. Magic asks, what if yes, just here, just for a moment, just for her? Crossroads magic is the strongest because a crossroads is where the world is already uncertain. Three choices. Three futures. The veil is thin there. If you stand in the center and speak clearly, the world listens because it has not decided yet."

Annabeth sat up straighter. Percy knew that look. That was her I-need-to-write-this-down look. She whispered, "Permission, not power," under her breath, committing it to memory.

Zoe smiled at her. "Yes. You understand, daughter of wisdom."

Annabeth blinked. Percy felt her tense, then ease. She was not a daughter of wisdom in this altered history, though she remembered being one. Here she was Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus, Thalia's younger sister; Percy was Poseidon's son and the boyfriend who had arrived with the same impossible memories. Zoe knew her intelligence, not that erased past. But Zoe's words still fit. Annabeth always understood the shape of ideas.

"I am sorry," Zoe said quickly, catching herself. "A compliment, not a claim. Daughter of the sky, and wise all the same."

"It's okay," Annabeth said quietly, and Percy squeezed her hand.

"My mother taught me the three rules of crossroads magic," Zoe continued. "I will teach you, because they are not secret. They are just forgotten. First, never stand in a crossroads without an offering. You are asking the world to pause. Pay for its time. Second, never choose for someone else. You can light the roads, but they must walk. Third, never look back once you have chosen. Doubt undoes the spell faster than any counterspell."

She let those sit in the firelight.

"I practiced at the camp crossroads. Little things. Helping younger kids choose bunks by lighting the paths in their dreams. Finding lost swords by asking which road they took. Once, I helped a dryad decide whether to move her tree after a storm. I lit three acorns and let her listen. She chose to stay, and her tree lived. That is crossroads magic. Not flashy. Just listening, and lighting, and letting go."

Percy thought about that. He was good at flashy. Waves. Earthquakes he tried not to cause. He was not always good at listening and letting go. He felt Annabeth thinking the same thing about herself, in her own way. They had both arrived displaced, angry, grieving for a life before camp, and they had clung to each other so hard that first summer that Chiron had to gently remind them to make other friends too.

Zoe was still speaking, and her voice had grown quieter.

"Why did I stay? Because the visits got shorter. My mother is a goddess. Wars move. Titans stir. The Mist thins in places it should not. Some years she missed three new moons in a row, and I would sit at the post with my candle burning down to nothing, and the dogs would not come, and I would walk back alone. I realized camp was the crossroads I had chosen. Not my mother's road. Not my keepers' road. Mine. The steady road. Low and humming. I stayed because here, I could be the person who waits at the crossroads for others, the way she waited for me."

The fire crackled. No one spoke.

---

It was Clarisse who broke the silence, and of course it was Clarisse.

She uncrossed her arms, leaned forward, her face harsh in the firelight but her voice not unkind. Just blunt. Clarisse was always blunt. It was her version of honesty.

"So your real mom just... left you with fake parents and then left you here?" she said. "And you are okay with that? You are not pissed? Because I would be pissed."

A few kids inhaled sharply. Percy tensed. Annabeth put a hand on his arm, as if to say, wait.

Zoe did not flinch. She looked at Clarisse with something like fondness.

"Thank you for asking it plainly," she said. "Others wonder it politely, which is harder to answer. Yes. I was pissed. I am sometimes still pissed. I was twelve and I lost a bedroom with glow stars on the ceiling. I lost a father who taught me to fix a bike chain. I lost a mother who cut my toast into triangles. You do not get over that because a goddess says it was necessary. I grieved. I still grieve on birthdays."

Clarisse nodded, satisfied to be taken seriously.

"But here is what I learned," Zoe said. "My keeper-parents were not fake. They were real. Love made them real. My goddess mother was not fake either. Fear and duty made her distant, but love made her come back on new moons with rain in her cloak. I have two mothers. One gave me safety. One gave me sight. Camp gave me a place to use both. Am I angry? Sometimes. Am I only angry? No. Anger is a crossroads too. I can stand in it and choose which road to walk. Most days I choose the steady road. Some days I sit down in the middle and cry for a while. Both are allowed."

Clarisse stared at her for a long moment, then gave a jerky nod and sat back. "Okay," she said. "That makes sense. Sorry about the glow stars."

"It is okay," Zoe said kindly. "I drew new ones on my cabin ceiling. Come see them sometime. They are crooked. Alabaster painted over them once and I almost hexed him into a ferret."

Laughter broke the tension. Clarisse even smiled, a rare real one. Percy saw her wipe her eyes quickly and pretend it was smoke.

Thalia spoke next, her voice rough. "You sat at that post alone a lot, huh?"

Zoe turned to her, and something passed between them, the recognition of older girls who had both waited for absent parents in different ways.

"Often enough," Zoe said.

Thalia nodded slowly. "You should not have to walk back alone. Next new moon, I will walk with you. Not to intrude. Just to the edge. So someone knows where you are."

Zoe's composure wavered for the first time all night. She pressed her lips together, then smiled. "I would like that, daughter of Zeus. I would like that very much."

"It is Thalia," Thalia said.

"It is Zoe," Zoe answered, and they smiled at each other, mutual respect settled and solid, no performance in it.

Percy felt Annabeth's shoulder tremble slightly against his. He looked down. She was crying quietly, trying not to show it. He bumped his forehead lightly against hers, their old signal from the life they remembered, from quests and sleepless nights listening for danger. It meant, I am here. Same road.

Annabeth bumped back, then wiped her face and sat up.

"Zoe?" she said, her voice small but clear. "Do you ever miss them? Your first parents? Even though... they do not remember?"

Zoe's gaze softened completely. "Every day, little owl. Every day. I leave an extra offering for them at the crossroads. An apple cut into triangles, like toast. I ask the world to give them a quiet life, good radios that never break, a garden that grows. Magic is permission. I give the world permission to be kind to them."

Annabeth nodded, tears spilling over. Percy put his arm around her shoulders, steady and familiar, and she leaned into him. He understood. They had their own version of placed and replaced, of homes that did not hold. Camp had held. Barely, at first, then fully. He remembered sitting with her on Cabin Three's porch after they arrived in this altered past, listening to the sea beyond the walls, whispering, we stay together, right? Promise? Promise. Then she had gone to Cabin One, and he had stayed with his sisters.

"I relate to that," Percy said aloud, surprising himself. His voice sounded rough. "The displaced part. Showing up and everyone assumes you know the rules and you do not. Feeling like you have to pick a road fast or get run over."

Zoe nodded at him. "Yes, son of the sea. Exactly. You arrived with salt on your shoes and grief in your pockets, you and Annabeth both. I watched you. You chose each other every time. That is also crossroads magic. You lit the road for each other."

Percy swallowed. Annabeth took his hand and held tight.

"I stayed for her at first," Percy admitted, glancing at Annabeth. "Like, if she left, I left. No offense, camp."

"None taken," Chiron called from the back, to soft laughter.

"But then..." Percy trailed off, looking at the fire. "Then it was the strawberries. And Beckendorf teaching me to whittle. And you fixing my shield strap, Zoe. And Thalia telling me I run like a crab. I stayed because it started feeling like mine too. Not just ours. Mine."

Annabeth nodded. "Mine too. I stayed because Percy stayed, and then I stayed because... I liked who I was here. I was not the weird kid who knew too much. I was just... Annabeth. Who knows too much, but usefully."

That got laughs and a few nods. Zoe smiled warmly at them both.

"Displaced does not mean lost," Zoe said. "It means placed twice. It means you carry two maps. That is heavy, but it also means you never have to tell a new kid, I do not understand. You do understand. Remember that when the new ones come in angry."

Percy and Annabeth nodded together, the way they did after surviving the same quests and hearing Chiron's lectures a hundred times.

Zoe leaned forward and threw a pinch of something into the fire. Lavender and rosemary, by the smell. The flames turned green and gold for a moment, Hecate's colors, and the smoke curled into three paths before dissolving.

"My mother says the Mist is thinning," Zoe said, more serious now. "You have felt it. Monsters finding camp borders faster. Dreams leaking. That is why I tell this story now, while I have you all here. To remind you. When the world feels thin, go to a crossroads. Leave an offering. Light the roads. Listen. Do not choose out of fear. Fear makes all roads buzz. Breathe until one hums steady. Then walk, and do not look back."

She looked around the circle, meeting eyes. Percy sat up straighter without meaning to.

"And if you sit at the post and no one comes," she added quietly, glancing at Thalia, then Clarisse, then Percy and Annabeth, "come find one of us. Older kids. Counselors. Friends. Be the dogs barking far off, telling the traveler they are watched over, not hunted. We can be that for each other. No one should walk back alone."

Thalia's jaw tightened, but she nodded. Clarisse looked down at her hands. Annabeth wiped her face again and laughed wetly.

"Can you teach us?" a small voice piped up from the front. It was a young Demeter kid, maybe nine, hugging his knees. "The listening thing?"

Zoe smiled, the teacher emerging. "Yes. Close your eyes. All of you, if you want. Breathe. Feel the ground. There are always three roads, even here. One is yesterday. It buzzes with what you wish you did. One is tomorrow. It is silent because it has not happened. One is now. It hums, low and steady, like the fire. Listen."

The amphitheater went silent except for the fire and the wind and the distant wash of the sea. Percy closed his eyes. He felt Annabeth's hand in his, Thalia's slow breathing a few benches away, the heat on his face. He tried to listen the way Zoe taught. Yesterday buzzed, all right, loud with mistakes and missed chances. Tomorrow was silent and a little scary. Now... now hummed. Fire. Annabeth. Friends. Smoke and salt. Steady.

When he opened his eyes, Zoe was watching them all with a look so tender it almost hurt.

"Good," she whispered. "You heard it."

She stood then, brushing ash from her shawl, and gave a small bow to the fire, then to Chiron, then to the campers.

"That is my story," she said. "Child of crossroads, raised by kindness, kept by magic, staying by choice. If you remember nothing else, remember the three rules. Bring an offering. Let others choose. Do not look back."

Applause started slowly, then swelled, real and warm. Not the polite clap for a lesson, but the deep appreciation for someone who had given them something true. Clarisse clapped hard, standing up. Thalia stood too, and gave Zoe a warrior's salute, fist to heart, which Zoe returned, eyes shining.

Percy clapped until his hands stung. Annabeth clapped and laughed and cried at the same time.

Chiron wheeled forward, thanking Zoe, dismissing the younger kids to bed with reminders about teeth and curfews that no one heeded. The circle broke slowly. Kids drifted off in twos and threes, talking quietly. The Apollo kids hummed. The fire burned lower.

Percy and Annabeth lingered, as did Thalia and Clarisse. They ended up near the fire with Zoe, not quite a circle, just a cluster, sharing the last of the blanket and a bag of stale marshmallows someone produced.

"Your ceiling stars are really crooked?" Clarisse asked Zoe, a peace offering.

"Terribly crooked," Zoe confirmed solemnly. "You can help me repaint them, if you like. You have a steady hand with a spear. A brush should be easy."

Clarisse snorted. "Fine. But I am not doing pink."

"Silver," Zoe agreed. "Hecate's color. For remembrance."

Thalia bumped Zoe's shoulder with her own. "New moon. I will be there. Edge of the woods. Do not argue."

"I would not dare," Zoe said, bumping back. "Daughter of Zeus argues like thunder."

"Thalia," Thalia corrected, but she was grinning.

Percy looked at Annabeth, who looked back, and some silent, familiar conversation passed. Annabeth nodded.

"Zoe?" Percy said. "Thanks. For... the displaced thing. For saying it out loud. Makes it feel less weird."

Zoe reached out and tucked the blanket more securely around both of them, motherly and sisterly at once. "You are welcome, little heroes. You lit the road for each other when you were small and lost. Now you can hold torches for others. That is how camp survives. Not walls. Torches."

Annabeth leaned her head on Percy's shoulder again, sleepy at last. "Permission, not power," she murmured.

"Exactly," Zoe said.

The fire sank to embers. Chiron called a final goodnight. The wind moved through the pines. Far off in the woods, dogs barked once, twice, then were silent. Zoe lifted her head, listening, a small smile on her face.

"Hounds on watch," she whispered, more to herself than to them. "Not hunting."

Percy squeezed Annabeth's hand. Thalia pulled her jacket tighter. Clarisse threw the last marshmallow into the embers for an offering, because why not.

They walked back together up the hill toward the cabins, a small group of friends, sisters, and sweethearts, torches in the form of flashlights and jokes and quiet company, choosing the steady road home without needing to close their eyes.

And behind them, the campfire glowed low and gold, humming steady in the dark.
