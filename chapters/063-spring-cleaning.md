## Chapter 63: Spring Cleaning

The first real warm morning of March came to Camp Half-Blood sideways, gold light spilling over the hills and turning the morning mist in the valley to something thin and shining. The strawberry fields were already damp with dew. The Big House porch creaked. The bronze dragon at the attic window snored once, shifted, and went still again.

Percy Jackson woke to sunlight through the one salt-stained window of Cabin Three and to the sound of someone knocking on his door like it owed them money.

He rolled over, pulled a pillow over his head, and said, "No."

"Yes," Annabeth Grace said through the door.

Percy peeked out. His hair was flat on one side and sticking straight up on the other. His shirt was inside out. One sock was missing.

Annabeth stood on the step in jeans and a gray Camp Half-Blood sweatshirt with the sleeves pushed up, her blond hair pulled back in a ponytail, a clipboard in one hand and a roll of trash bags in the other. Behind her, the whole camp was already awake in a way it never was on Saturdays. Grover was herding satyrs with wheelbarrows. Connor Stoll was carrying a mattress out of the Hermes cabin by himself while Travis supervised, which meant Travis was sitting on the steps drinking hot chocolate. At the forge, the Hephaestus cabin had already dragged out three workbenches and a pile of scrap that clanked even from here.

Percy blinked. "Is it war?"

"It's spring cleaning," Annabeth said.

Percy stared at her. "That is worse than war."

"It's Chiron's orders. Whole camp. Cabins, shrines, armory, boathouse, Big House attic. Winter mold, storm damage, inventory before the summer campers arrive." She checked her clipboard. "Poseidon cabin is first on my list because you and visiting Tyson somehow generate more mess than the entire Ares cabin."

"Hey," Percy said. "Tyson's at the forges until noon."

"Then you get a head start." She handed him a trash bag, a spray bottle, and a sponge. The spray bottle said VINEGAR in Annabeth's neat block letters. "I have to go help Thalia with the Zeus cabin. I'll check on you at lunch."

Percy took the supplies like they were weapons he didn't know how to use. "You love bossing me around."

"I love checklists," Annabeth corrected. She leaned in and kissed him quickly on the cheek, efficient and warm. "Have fun."

"Wait," Percy said as she turned to go. "Define fun."

She was already walking toward the Zeus cabin, ponytail swinging. "Find something you forgot you loved!"

He watched her go for a second longer than was strictly necessary for spring cleaning morale, then turned back to face Cabin Three.

Cabin Three was beautiful and impossible. Long gray walls like the inside of a seashell, a fountain in the center that gurgled salt water day and night, a saltwater aquarium built into one wall, fishing nets draped over the rafters, and three bunks — Percy's, Clarisse's, and Silena's — with a guest hammock for Tyson when he came from the forges under the sea. It always smelled like low tide and clean laundry, because Amphitrite had come on a visit and taught the boys to fold sheets with hospital corners. His sisters enforced the lesson.

This morning, with the spring sun slanting in, Percy could see exactly how bad winter had been. Dust on the rafters. Sand tracked into every corner. A greenish film on the fountain stones. A pile of wet towels that he had definitely not left there, except he absolutely had. A stack of camp newsletters he meant to read. Three pairs of sneakers, none matching. A Montauk sweatshirt crumpled under his bunk. A box labeled MISC. IMPORTANT?? in Tyson's huge handwriting.

Percy tied the trash bag to his bunk post, opened all the windows, and got to work.

He started with the easy stuff. Trash. Laundry into the big net bag Annabeth had left. He dragged the mattresses off the bunks and propped them against the porch rail to air out. He scrubbed the fountain with vinegar until his hands smelled sharp and the stones were gray-blue again. He siphoned half the aquarium water and refilled it, apologizing to the tiny hippocamp figurines Tyson insisted on arranging like furniture.

"Sorry, guys," he told them. "Renovation."

By ten, the cabin smelled like vinegar and sea air and sounded like gulls. Percy was sweating through his shirt. He had found four drachmas, a waterlogged copy of *The Odyssey* with Annabeth's notes in the margins — *Percy, page 214 is literally you* — a single blue sock, and a granola bar from approximately the Trojan War.

Then, under his bunk, behind the spare blanket and the shoebox of sea glass he and Tyson collected for Amphitrite, his hand hit something hard and plastic.

Percy pulled it out and sat back on his heels.

It was a toy. A little battered plastic playset, the colors half-rubbed off. A domed city with tiny towers, a trident gate, three mermaid figures with chewed heads, and a blue plastic dolphin missing its tail. On the bottom, in faded raised letters: ATLANTIS — BATH TOY SET — AGES 3+.

Percy just stared at it.

He remembered another toy like it so clearly it hurt. Montauk, in the life Sally had raised him. He must have been four, maybe five. Sally had saved for months for that weekend cabin rental, the one with the cracked linoleum and the perfect view. It rained the whole time. They couldn't go to the beach. So his mom filled the bathtub with too many bubbles and gave him this three-dollar Atlantis set from the drugstore by the highway and said, "Well, if we can't go to the ocean, we'll bring the ocean here."

He had played with it for hours. He made the mermaids have quests. He made the dolphin the oracle. He made the trident gate open only for heroes. Sally sat on the closed toilet lid and read him the label off a shampoo bottle in an oracle voice because she'd left her book at home. When he got out, pruny and happy, she wrapped him in a towel that smelled like sun and told him Atlantis was real, you know. Just sleeping. Someday, she'd say, you'll see it.

Percy turned the little dome over in his hands. This one had a cracked tower instead of the melted one he remembered from Gabe's apartment. One mermaid had Annabeth-yellow hair drawn on with marker — he had added that at camp after coming back. Different toy, different household. It still brought Sally's voice into the room so clearly he had to sit down.

He hadn't seen Sally's old toy in years. This different one had somehow traveled from the palace to his camp trunk, quietly tucked among his blankets. He would have to ask Amphitrite about it.

"Hey," he whispered to the empty cabin. "Look at you."

He washed it carefully in the fountain, dried each tiny figure on his shirt, and set the whole city on the windowsill where the sun hit it. The plastic glowed amber and blue. The dolphin with no tail stood guard.

He was still sitting there when the door banged open.

"Brother!" Tyson boomed.

The whole cabin shook. Tyson ducked through the doorway — eight feet tall now, one big brown eye shining, his teeth square and happy — wearing a bronze foundry apron over his camp shirt and carrying a crate of tools and a bag of donuts.

"You started without me!" he said happily. "Good! Chiron said spring cleaning is next to godliness. Hephaestus said spring cleaning is next to not having your bunk blown up by accident."

"Both true," Percy said, getting up to hug him. Tyson hugged back hard enough to lift him off the floor.

Tyson set down the donuts and immediately spotted the Atlantis toy on the windowsill. His eye went wide.

"You kept it!" he said.

"You remember this?" Percy asked.

"Yes! From the bathtub ocean!" Tyson picked up the dolphin with enormous gentleness, between thumb and forefinger. "Amphitrite sent it with your blankets. She said you used to put it in the palace bath. I don't know why you wanted toy towers when you had real towers. But it has a dolphin. Dolphins are good."

Percy's throat went tight. "Yeah. Well. You were better than Atlantis."

Tyson beamed. He set the dolphin down and pulled something from his tool crate — a tiny bronze tail, fresh-cast, still warm, with a loop to fit the broken stump.

"I made this," he said shyly. "Long ago. For when we found him. Can I?"

Percy handed him the dolphin. Tyson fitted the bronze tail with a soft click. It matched perfectly. The dolphin stood whole, bronze tail gleaming against blue plastic.

"Now he can swim again," Tyson declared.

Percy put his arm around his older half-brother's huge shoulders and leaned against him for a second. "Yeah," he said. "Now he can."

They cleaned together after that, fast and easy. Tyson lifted bunks with one hand while Percy swept under them. Percy scrubbed high windows standing on Tyson's shoulders. They found Tyson's lost lucky wrench, Percy's missing sock — it had been inside Tyson's spare helmet — and a colony of dust bunnies under Tyson's bunk that Tyson insisted on relocating outside "humanely."

When Annabeth came to check at lunch, she found them sitting on the aired mattresses on the porch, sharing donuts, with the Atlantis city between them gleaming in the sun.

She stopped, clipboard lowering. "Is that—"

"My bath Atlantis," Percy said. "Amphitrite snuck it in. It looks like one Sally gave me in my old childhood."

Annabeth picked up the mermaid with the yellow marker hair. Her mouth softened. "You drew me as a mermaid."

"I was twelve and subtle," Percy said.

"You were twelve and correct," Annabeth said, and set her down carefully. She looked around the clean cabin through the open door — swept floor, shining fountain, made bunks, windows open to the air — and nodded approvingly. "Good work, boys. Poseidon cabin passes."

"Do we get a prize?" Percy asked.

"You get to help the Ares cabin next," Annabeth said. "They requested muscle."

Tyson flexed happily. Percy groaned into his donut, but he was smiling.

---

The Ares cabin smelled like leather oil, metal polish, and old sweat socks, and it sounded like an argument.

When Percy and Tyson arrived after lunch, lugging buckets and brooms as tribute, the door was propped open and every bunk had been dragged into the yard. Shields and spears were lined up in rows. The red walls were bare — all the posters and warning signs and dented helmets taken down for washing. In the middle of it all stood Clarisse La Rue, hands on her hips, hair pulled back under a red bandana, giving orders like a general.

"No, Trenton, rust goes on the *outside* pile for Hephaestus to melt! Ellis, if you stack those javelins like that again I will stack *you*! Chris, don't just stand there looking cute, grab a—"

Chris Rodriguez, sitting on an overturned bucket with a scrub brush, grinned up at her. "Was that a compliment, babe? In front of the troops?"

"It was a threat with a pretty word in it," Clarisse said, but her ears went pink. Then she saw Percy and Tyson. "Thank the gods. Jackson, Cyclops, you're on mattress duty. Drag them all out, beat them, check for knives."

"Why would there be knives in the mattresses?" Percy asked.

Clarisse gave him a look. "Why wouldn't there be knives in the mattresses?"

Fair enough.

The Ares kids worked hard when Clarisse told them to. Within an hour the cabin floor was swept and mopped, the weapons rack was oiled and inventoried, the helmets were lined up by size, and a mountain of trash bags stood by the door. Someone had found a shrine to Ares under Bunk Four made of offering candy from three Halloweens ago and a note that said *please let me pass math*. They left the candy for Ares and threw away the note after saying a prayer.

Percy was on his hands and knees scrubbing under the training bench Clarisse had loaned the Ares kids, the corner one with the best wall for spear drills — when his sponge pushed against something taped to the underside of the bench frame.

"Uh, Clarisse?" he called. "You've got something—"

"Don't touch my stuff, Jackson!"

"It's taped under your bench. It's paper. It's— kind of falling apart."

Clarisse went still. Then she crossed the cabin fast, knelt down beside him, and peered under.

It was a drawing. Crayon on construction paper, faded and creased, taped with yellowing tape. The paper was soft at the edges from years of hands.

The drawing showed two stick figures. One was tall, with wild brown scribble hair and a red spear that took up half the page. The other was small, with long yellow hair and a pink dress and a huge smile. They were holding hands. Above them, in wobbly capital letters, backward in places: SILENA + CLARISSE BEST FRIENS HEROES.

Clarisse stared at it like it had stabbed her.

"Oh," Chris said quietly from behind them.

Nobody else said anything. Even the youngest Ares kids, ten years old and feral, went quiet.

Clarisse reached in and peeled the tape back with careful fingers, like disarming a trap. She sat back on the dusty floor and held the drawing in her lap.

"She made this," Clarisse said, her voice rough. "Silena. We were— I was seven, she was eight. First summer. I was mean. I was so mean, I tried to be mean so nobody would mess with me. She just— she followed me around with crayons. She said I looked lonely."

Percy sat beside her, not saying anything. Tyson sat on the other side, huge and gentle.

"She drew me as a hero before I was one," Clarisse said. "She drew herself in a pink dress on a battlefield. I told her she'd get blood on it. She said then she'd get a red dress." Her laugh cracked. "I kept it. I told her it was stupid and I kept it under my bench for years."

Chris knelt down next to her and put his hand on her back. "Can I see?"

She showed him. He studied it the way he studied everything Clarisse loved — seriously, like it mattered.

"You're really good with the spear," he said. "Proportions are intimidating."

"Shut up," Clarisse said, but she leaned into him.

One of the little Ares kids — Ellis, nine, missing a front tooth — crawled closer. "Is that Silena? The one who stood up to Zeus?"

"Yeah," Clarisse said.

"She makes ribbons," Ellis said. "I didn't know she was that brave."

Clarisse looked at him sharply, then softened. "Listen up, runts," she said loudly, so the whole cabin could hear. "Silena Beauregard faced Zeus without a weapon. She spoke for the camp. She—" Her voice caught. Chris's hand moved in slow circles on her back. "She draws stupid pictures and she loves pink and she's tougher than any of you. You remember that when she helps you fix your armor later."

"We will," Ellis whispered.

Clarisse stood up, drawing held to her chest. "I'm going to get this framed," she announced. "And if any of you make a joke, I'll kill you."

"No jokes," Trenton said quickly.

"Good." Clarisse looked at Percy. "Jackson. Thanks. For finding—"

"Anytime," Percy said.

She nodded once, brusque, and went to her borrowed supply box to wrap the drawing in a clean T-shirt for safekeeping. Chris followed her, carrying a frame he'd already pulled from the donate pile.

Silena found the drawing before Clarisse had chosen a frame.

She arrived with a box of replacement straps for the training shields and stopped in the doorway when she saw Clarisse sitting on the floor, the faded paper resting on her knees. A younger Ares camper was holding up two frames and asking whether the silver one looked more heroic.

"What's that?" Silena asked.

Clarisse stiffened. "Nothing."

Silena stepped closer. "That's very clearly something."

Percy watched Clarisse prepare a joke, the sort she used to get out of conversations she'd rather fight than finish. Instead she held the paper so Silena could see it properly.

Silena stared at the two figures. Her mouth opened, then closed.

"I thought you threw that away."

"I said I did," Clarisse answered.

"You said it was stupid."

"It was stupid. Look at the size of the spear."

Silena crouched next to her. She didn't take the drawing, although Percy could tell she wanted to touch it.

"I made this when I thought all heroes had to look angry," she said.

"You gave yourself a pink dress."

"I liked the pink dress."

"You said you were going to wear it to battle."

"I would have," Silena replied. "I would've ruined it and complained to everybody, and then I'd have wanted another one."

Clarisse snorted.

Percy had known the two of them as his sisters for long enough that he sometimes forgot their childhood together preceded his place in Cabin Three. In this life they'd been children of Poseidon the whole time, but they hadn't grown up with Percy beside them. The stories they carried did not belong to him just because their father was the same.

Silena examined the crayon lines.

"Did you keep it under the bench all these years?"

"Nobody looked there."

"Because you told everybody not to touch the bench."

"Good policy."

Chris came over with the two frames and asked which one they preferred. Silena wanted the plain wooden one. Clarisse wanted the heavier dark one. They were about to argue when a younger camper suggested hanging it over the weapons rack.

"No," Silena said at once.

Everybody looked at her.

"I drew it," she said. "I didn't know it would be found today. I don't want it hung up for strangers to make jokes about."

Clarisse set down the dark frame. Her expression changed.

"I was going to put it on the mantel," she admitted.

"You can put it wherever you want in your own cabin," Silena said. "But you're borrowing this one. The Ares kids don't need my handwriting on the wall all year."

Percy watched Clarisse think. She'd been proud of the drawing, and the younger campers' attention had pleased her. That didn't mean Silena had agreed to be a story everybody could tell.

"One night?" Clarisse asked. "While we're here, so the kids who helped clean can see it. Then I'll take it back to Cabin Three."

Silena studied her. "Only if I get to choose the frame."

"You're going to choose the boring one."

"Yes."

"Fine."

The younger Ares camper nodded as if he'd witnessed a diplomatic treaty. He fetched the wooden frame, cleaned the glass and helped Clarisse slide the drawing behind it without folding the edges.

Chris found a clean hook above the mantel. He didn't put the frame up until Silena told him the height was right.

"There," she said, standing back. "That's nice."

Clarisse looked at the little drawing on the wall. She did not look comfortable with people watching her face, but she didn't take it down.

"Now can we finish cleaning?" she asked.

"Yes," Silena said. "And you can return those shields after you've checked the straps."

Percy grabbed the replacement straps and helped stack them. He was glad they'd found the drawing. He was also glad Silena had arrived before it became a story everybody believed they could own.

At dinner, a few campers asked about the picture. Clarisse told them it was hers and Silena's and that it would be coming home with them after the feast. The distinction lasted longer than Percy had expected.

That evening, by the time the Ares cabin passed inspection — floors clean enough to eat off, though nobody recommended it — the story had spread. The little drawing of two sisters as heroes was propped on the Ares mantel in a plain wooden frame, next to a freshly polished helmet and a pink ribbon someone from the Aphrodite cabin had left without a word.

Percy thought about it all the way to dinner. How long you could keep love taped under your bed and pretend it wasn't there.

The Zeus cabin was different. It was too big, too clean, too empty.

Thalia Grace stood in the middle of it with her hands in her leather jacket pockets, staring up at the high ceiling where dust moved in the light from the tall windows. The cabin was all white marble and storm-gray banners, with Annabeth's regularly used bunk, Thalia's bunk kept ready for visits, and a guest cot for Jason when a supervised visit was possible. A throne-like chair stood against the wall, unused. A statue of Zeus glowered in the corner.

"It's weird cleaning a place where Beth lives and still feeling like nobody is meant to," Thalia said when Annabeth arrived with vinegar and trash bags. "It's like cleaning a museum to my dad."

"I stay here all year," Annabeth pointed out, opening windows. "Jason has a cot when he can visit, you have your bunk, and about twenty pigeons live in the rafters."

Thalia snorted, but she grabbed a broom. "Chiron says we have to do the storage loft. Nobody's done it since— gods, since we actually cleared it out?"

The loft was a narrow space above the bathroom, reached by a ladder. It was full of camp-issue blankets, broken lightning bolts from the camp store, a box of Roman candles labeled DO NOT USE — JASON, and, in the back, a dented metal box with a faded airline sticker: BERYL G.

Thalia froze when Annabeth pulled it into the light.

"Oh," Thalia said. "No. Nope. We're throwing that away unopened."

"Thalia," Annabeth said gently.

"No, Annabeth. I know what's in there. My mom's stuff. The stuff she left when— when she dumped Jason— when she—" Thalia's hands curled. Her bracelets — the silver skull bracelet, the copper one — clinked. "I don't do mom boxes on cleaning day."

Annabeth sat down cross-legged next to the box and didn't open it. "You don't have to. But I'm here if you want to."

Thalia paced. Lightning flickered faintly at her fingertips, static in the dry loft air. Below, the camp sounded far away — laughter from the Hermes cabin, someone hammering at the forge.

"She kept everything," Thalia said finally, voice low. "Newspapers where she was mentioned. Photos. She was— she was beautiful, you know? Before the drinking got bad. Before Zeus left and came back and left. She used to do this thing where she'd put on old movie star makeup and pretend we were going to premieres. Me and Jason. Jason was tiny. She'd put sunglasses on him."

Annabeth listened.

Thalia sat down hard across from the box. "Open it," she said. "You open it. If there's a bottle, we throw it out."

Annabeth lifted the lid.

No bottle. Just photographs, stacked in envelopes, and a few small things: a dried corsage, a ticket stub for a planetarium, a child's drawing of the sisters sitting under a pine tree, a postcard from Los Angeles unsent.

Annabeth handed Thalia the first envelope.

The top photo was Beryl Grace in her twenties, laughing, dark hair wild, holding a toddler Thalia with fierce blue eyes on her hip. Both of them were sunburned. Behind them was a convertible with the top down.

"That was the desert trip," Thalia whispered. "She packed nothing but chips and told me we were chasing storms. We got stuck in Needles. She sang Elvis the whole time we waited for the tow truck."

The next: Beryl holding a baby in a blue blanket — Jason, tiny and serious — while a small Thalia leaned in to kiss his head, scowling at the camera like someone had told her to smile and she'd refused.

"He cried all the time," Thalia said, and laughed wetly. "I used to tell Mom to take him back. Then one night there was thunder and he got scared and I— I crawled into his crib. Well, I tried. I got stuck. Mom found us like that in the morning and took a picture. I don't know where that one is."

She kept going. Beryl asleep on a couch with all three kids piled on her. Beryl in a waitress uniform making a face. Beryl staring out a window, beautiful and empty, Thalia's small hand on her shoulder, out of focus.

"She tried," Thalia said. "People forget that. She tried so hard it broke her. Loving a god — it's not like the stories. It's just— waiting by a phone that never rings and wondering if you're crazy."

Annabeth's own throat ached. She thought of her dad, her stepmom, the long cold war of her childhood before she ran away. Different, but the same shape. Parents who loved badly.

"You're not her," Annabeth said.

"I know," Thalia said. "I'm the kid who ran away and decided she didn't need any adults. Much healthier." But she smiled, sad and real. She held up a photo of seven-year-old Thalia with missing teeth, holding a sparkler. "Look at this punk."

"Cute punk," Annabeth said.

"Don't tell Percy. He already thinks he's cooler."

"Percy thinks everyone thinks he's cooler. It's his tragic flaw."

Thalia laughed for real that time, wiping her eyes with her sleeve. She carefully put the photos back, keeping the sparkler one out to take to her bunk. She added the pine tree drawing on top.

"Keep the box?" Annabeth asked.

"Yeah," Thalia said. "Clean it up. Put it on the shelf. Maybe— maybe Jason wants to see some of these when he visits. He's got like two memories of her that aren't terrible. He should have this one." She tapped the photo of herself kissing baby Jason's head. "Of his big sister being nice once."

"Once ever," Annabeth agreed solemnly.

"Watch it, shortstack."

They dusted the box, labeled it in Annabeth's neat hand — BERYL GRACE — PHOTOS, KEEP — and set it on the clean shelf beside extra blankets. Thalia stared at it for a long moment, then touched the lid once, lightly.

"Okay, Mom," she murmured. "You get to stay."

Downstairs, the Zeus cabin gleamed. The marble shone. The banners were shaken out. The statue of Zeus had been dusted and, at Thalia's insistence, given a pair of spare sunglasses someone left behind.

"He looks like he needs to chill," Thalia said.

Annabeth laughed until her stomach hurt. "Chiron is going to kill you."

"Worth it."

---

By late afternoon, the whole camp smelled like lemon and vinegar and fresh-cut grass. Mattresses aired on every porch. Trash bags lined the paths like strange parade floats. The Hephaestus cabin had set up a repair station in the plaza — Leo Valdez shouting, "Bring me your broken! Your busted! Your mildly cursed!" while Nyssa tightened hinges and Harley threw away anything that smoked.

Chiron rolled through in his wheelchair, inspecting with a clipboard and a handkerchief pressed to his nose against the dust. "Poseidon — pass! Ares — pass with distinction for the mantel! Zeus — pass, though please remove the sunglasses from my lord's likeness!"

"No!" Thalia shouted from the Zeus porch, to general laughter.

At the bonfire pit, someone had started a share pile — not trash, not donate, but things found during cleaning that needed stories. An old Pegasus bridle. A cracked oracle mug. A single ice skate. A love letter never sent, unsigned. Campers drifted over with brooms and dustpans to look, to laugh, to remember.

Percy found Annabeth there, sitting on a log with dust on her cheek and the sparkler photo Thalia had given her to hold. He sat next to her and bumped her shoulder.

"How was the mom box?" he asked quietly.

Annabeth told him. Percy listened the way he always did — all the way, no fidgeting, sea-green eyes steady.

"She kept the good ones?" he asked.

"Yeah," Annabeth said. "Thalia's going to show Jason."

"Good." Percy was quiet a second. "You okay? I know — dad stuff—"

"I'm okay," Annabeth said, and meant it mostly. "It makes me think. About how we keep things. Even when they're messy."

Percy nodded at the share pile. "Like Clarisse's drawing?"

"You heard?"

"Everyone heard. Ellis from Ares told the whole dining pavilion at snack. With sound effects." Percy smiled. "It was sweet. Don't tell Clarisse I said sweet."

"I wouldn't dare."

They sat while the sun slanted lower. Grover came by with a bag of baby satyrs' lost socks for the share pile. Piper told the story of the ice skate — it was hers, from a mortal school ice show, before her dad got famous-really-famous. Leo told three different stories about the oracle mug, all lies, until Calypso hit him with a broom. Jason, on Iris-message from Camp Jupiter for all of five minutes, saw the photo Annabeth held up and went quiet and said, "Keep that one safe for me," and Thalia, standing behind Annabeth, said, "I will, little brother," and Jason grinned like the sun.

It was that kind of day. Dusty and tender. Everyone finding the kid they used to be under the bunk.

Chiron clapped his hands for attention as dinner approached. "One last task before the feast! The hillside shrines! Athena and Zeus need sweeping, offerings cleared, spring blessings! I need volunteers!"

Percy and Annabeth looked at each other.

"We'll take it," Annabeth said.

"Of course you will," Thalia said, rolling her eyes fondly. "Nerds."

---

The hillside shrines sat above the camp in a grove of young olive trees and storm oaks, where the wind smelled like rain even when the sky was clear. Two small marble altars stood side by side — one for Athena, owl-carved, with an olive branch frieze; one for Zeus, with clouds and lightning. Offerings from winter — dead flowers, rain-soaked notes, a cracked clay owl, coins green with age — littered the steps. Leaves clogged the corners. Moss greened the lettering.

Percy and Annabeth climbed up with a bucket, brushes, fresh cloths, and two new offerings: a small blue glass dolphin from Percy, bronze tail glinting, and a miniature olive-wood owl Annabeth had been carving for weeks in the Athena cabin workshop.

They worked side by side without needing to talk much. Percy hauled water from the spring. Annabeth scrubbed inscriptions with a toothbrush. Percy pulled weeds from between the stones. Annabeth polished the owl eyes with her sleeve. The sun sank lower, turning the marble honey-gold. Below, camp sounds faded — dishes clinking as the dinner feast was set out, someone laughing, the dragon snoring.

"This is nice," Percy said, wringing out a rag. "Just us and the altars. Your dad has a very intimidating cloud frieze."

"Athena did respect you," Annabeth said. "In her terrifying way, in the life we remember. She told me once that you show surprising tactical adaptability for someone who once tried to microwave a burrito still in foil."

"Tell her I said thanks and I'm sorry about the microwave."

Annabeth laughed, flicking soapy water at him. He caught her wrist, and for a second they just grinned at each other, twelve again and dusty and happy, and then they went back to work because the shrine deserved it.

It was Annabeth who found the initials.

She was cleaning the back of Athena's altar, where the frieze met the base, brush moving in small circles. The stone was worn, but under the moss she felt grooves too straight to be natural.

"Percy," she said. "Come here."

He knelt beside her. She brushed away the last moss.

Carved into the marble, small and deep, the letters crisp despite the years:

S.B. + C.L.
M.C. + J.D.
L.V. + C.Y.
P.B. + ?

And dozens more. Initials of demigods, going back decades. Some with dates. Some with little symbols — a trident, an owl, a sun, a dove. Lovers and best friends and siblings who had cleaned this shrine together on days just like this and left a mark where the gods could see but the counselors wouldn't scrub off.

"Oh," Percy breathed. "It's like— a secret yearbook."

Annabeth traced S.B. + C.L. with her fingertip. "Silena and Clarisse," she whispered. "That has to be."

Percy pointed. "L.V. + C.Y. — Leo and Calypso? When did they—"

"Last spring. He told me he was going to do something romantic and I told him if he graffiti'd a shrine I'd kill him." Annabeth smiled. "I'm glad he did it anyway."

They found more. G.U. + ??? Grover, hopeful. T.G. + L.G.? No — Thalia would never. Then, near the bottom, fresh enough to still have sharp edges: J.G. + R.D. — Jason and Reyna? As friends? Percy raised an eyebrow and Annabeth shrugged, smiling. Some stories weren't theirs.

And there, half-hidden under lichen, an older one that made Annabeth go still:

F.C. + S.J.

"Frederick Chase and Sally Jackson?" Percy guessed.

Annabeth shook her head slowly, touching the letters. "Look at the date. Ninety— this is before we were born. F.C. could be Frederick, but it could be somebody we never met. And S.J. could be Sally, or another camper. I can't turn two initials into a memory." Her eyes shone. "I still like imagining them doing something ordinary like this. Before everything."

Percy was quiet. Then he put his hand over hers on the stone. "That's us," he said softly. "People who love each other, doing something ordinary together."

Annabeth leaned her head against his shoulder. "I like that."

They sat there a long time, reading initials, making up stories for the ones they didn't know. The wind moved in the olive leaves. An owl called once from the woods.

Percy pulled his camp knife from his pocket — the small utility one, not Riptide — and held it up. "Can we?"

Annabeth looked at the altar, at Athena's name, at Zeus beside her. At all those small loves carved where they wouldn't hurt anything. At camp rules about defacing sacred property, which she knew by heart and could quote.

She took the knife.

"On the back," she said. "Small. Where it doesn't show from the front. Like the others."

Percy grinned. Together, knees touching in the dirt, heads bent close, they worked. Annabeth did the letters because her carving was neater — she'd had practice on workshop wood. Percy held the stone steady and told her when to press harder.

P.J. + A.G.

Small, neat, deep enough to last. Under it, Annabeth added a tiny owl and Percy added a tiny wave, overlapping, so the owl looked like it was riding the sea.

They sat back.

"There," Annabeth whispered.

Percy put his arm around her. She leaned into him, dusty cheek against his dusty shirt. The shrine gleamed clean in the sunset. Their new offerings stood side by side — blue dolphin, wooden owl — guardians for all those initials.

"We'll come back in ten years," Percy said, "and it'll still be here."

"In twenty," Annabeth said. "With our— with whoever comes after us. We'll show them where to look."

Percy kissed the top of her head. "Promise?"

"Promise," Annabeth said, and held up her pinky, camp-solemn. Percy hooked his pinky through hers.

Below, the dinner horn blew, long and low. Lights came on in the cabins, warm squares in the dusk. Someone — probably Tyson — shouted, "PERCY! ANNABETH! WE SAVED YOU BLUE COOKIES!"

"We should go," Annabeth said, but she didn't move.

"One more minute," Percy said.

So they stayed one more minute, watching the sun go down behind the hills, the clean shrines gold and gray, the initials of a hundred kids who had loved each other through wars and winters safe under moss and stone.

When they finally stood, Annabeth left a spring blessing — olive oil and honey poured in the proper lines — and Percy left a splash of sea water from his flask. They brushed the steps one last time, picked up the bucket, and walked down the hill hand in hand, dirty and tired and full.

The camp was waiting, clean and shining, stories aired out like mattresses in the sun.

At the dining pavilion, Clarisse had hung the framed drawing where everyone could see it for the night. Thalia was showing Jason's Iris-message face the Beryl photos by firelight. Tyson was waving frantically from the Poseidon table, where two blue cookies sat untouched on Percy's plate next to a tiny bronze-tailed dolphin standing guard.

Annabeth squeezed Percy's hand.

"Best spring cleaning ever?" Percy asked.

"Best ever," Annabeth said.

And they went in to feast.
