## Chapter 60: Summer's End

The last week of summer arrived at Camp Half-Blood the way it always did, not with a battle or a prophecy, but with laundry.

It was everywhere. Sheets snapped in the sea wind on the lines behind the cabins. Half-folded shirts spilled out of open trunks. Sandals in mismatched pairs wandered the paths like stray animals. The strawberry fields were heavy and sweet, the last big harvest before the year-rounders took over. The air smelled like sun-warmed grass and pine and salt, and underneath it, faintly, like cedar chests and goodbye.

Percy Jackson woke to sunlight through the salt-sprayed window of Cabin Three and, for a full minute, did not want to move.

The cabin was quiet. Tyson had already gone home to the forges a week early for extra work, leaving his bunk made with military precision and a note in wobbly Greek that said: BROTHER — SAVE ME CAKE AT DINNER. DO NOT LET HORSES EAT MY BLANKET. Tyson had drawn a picture of Mrs. O'Leary wearing the blanket like a cape. Percy had pinned it above his own pillow.

His trunk sat open at the foot of his bed, half-packed and already a mess. Two hoodies. One shoe. Three empty nectar bottles he was definitely not supposed to keep. A piece of Atlantean coral that glowed faintly blue when he touched it. A camp shirt for every year, folded with varying degrees of effort.

Someone knocked on the fish-scale door.

"If you're decent, Seaweed Brain, I'm coming in," Annabeth Grace called.

"Define decent," Percy said, sitting up and rubbing his face.

"Awake," she said, pushing the door open with her hip, her arms full of scrolls and a laundry bag. Her blonde hair was pulled back in a ponytail, curls escaping everywhere from the humidity. She had a smudge of charcoal on her cheek and a pencil behind her ear. "Up. The Big House porch meeting in ten. And you promised you'd help Grover crate his tin cans before lunch."

Percy groaned and flopped back. "I did promise that. Why did I promise that?"

"Because you're noble and self-sacrificing," Annabeth said dryly, dropping her bag on his spare bunk. "Also because he bribed you with enchiladas."

"Right. The enchiladas." Percy sat back up. "Worth it."

Annabeth looked around Cabin Three, at the half-packed trunk and the salt drying in white lines on the windowsill and the sea-shell wind chime Tyson had hung crookedly over the door. Her expression softened.

"Last week," she said quietly.

"Yeah," Percy said. "Weird, huh?"

She nodded. Outside, someone laughed by the volleyball court. A conch horn blew far off, too cheerful for the morning. A Pegasus whinnied over the stables.

"Come on," Annabeth said, nudging his trunk shut with her foot. "If we're late, Chiron will give us the disappointed centaur look."

"You mean the look that makes Clarisse cry?"

"No one makes Clarisse cry," Annabeth said. "She cries on her own schedule and then stabs anyone who notices."

Percy grinned and got up, pulling on a camp shirt and shorts. He grabbed the coral from his trunk and slipped it into his pocket out of habit now. He spent half his nights in Atlantis these days, and he liked having something that worked in both places.

They walked to the Big House together, shoulders brushing, through the bright, busy heart of camp.

---

The porch of the Big House was already crowded when they arrived.

Chiron stood in his wheelchair form, a clipboard balanced on his lap, Mr. D beside him in a Hawaiian shirt that said I SURVIVED ANOTHER SUMMER WITHOUT DYING (BARELY) trying very hard to look bored. Grover Underwood sat on the steps with a notebook, his crutches leaning beside him, his new leg braces glinting. Clarisse La Rue leaned against the railing with her arms crossed, Silena Beauregard beside her braiding and unbraiding a strand of dark hair. Thalia Grace sat on the railing itself, boots swinging, her black hair with its silver streak tied back, her eyes bright and restless.

"Ah, Percy, Annabeth," Chiron said warmly. "Sit. We are merely reviewing departures."

"Departures," Mr. D said. "My favorite word. Less children means less paperwork. And less property damage."

"Last week schedule," Chiron continued, ignoring him. "Cabins will pack and inventory. The lake canoes come out Thursday. The stables Friday. Final campfire Friday night. Buses for the city leave Saturday morning after breakfast. Year-rounders, please see Argus if you are staying so we have a count for the winter stores."

Grover raised his hand. "Do Searchers-in-training get a bus? Or do we just... wander dramatically into the mist?"

A laugh went around the porch. Chiron smiled.

"We will discuss your autumn, Grover," he said. "I have received a letter. We will speak after."

Grover blinked. "A letter? From...?"

"The Council," Chiron said, just that, and moved on before Grover could combust, but Percy caught the look Grover shot him — wide-eyed, hopeful, terrified — and Percy grinned back and mouthed, told you.

Thalia leaned forward. "Any word from Jupiter? I mean, Camp Jupiter?"

Chiron glanced at his clipboard. "Reyna sent an Iris-message yesterday. The Twelfth Legion had a good summer. No major incidents. She asked after you and Annabeth particularly."

Thalia and Annabeth exchanged a look Percy had come to recognize over the past two weeks — the plotting look.

"Did she say anything about—" Thalia started.

"Jason?" Chiron finished gently. "Only that he is well. He led a successful quest to recover a lost eagle standard in July. He is... finding his footing."

Thalia's jaw tightened, but not in anger. In something older and more tired. Longing.

"He's my brother," she said quietly. "I haven't seen him since... gods, since before the quest. Since the statue. I keep thinking—"

"You will see him," Annabeth said firmly. "We will."

Mr. D snorted. "Family reunions. Always tears and lightning."

"Shut up, Mr. D," Thalia and Annabeth said together, and Mr. D held up his hands in surrender while Clarisse snickered.

Chiron cleared his throat to hide a smile. "If you wish to arrange a visit in the school year, I will help with safe travel. Reyna owes me a favor from the Sea of Monsters incident involving three harpies and a hot-air balloon. I intend to collect."

"Yes," Thalia said immediately. "Yes. Annabeth and I were talking— fall break? Or even a weekend? We can take the train partway, Iris-message ahead—"

"We'd need mortal cover," Annabeth said, already shifting into planner mode. "San Francisco Bay Area, field trip for an architecture program, Thalia as my extremely intimidating chaperone—"

"I am extremely intimidating," Thalia agreed.

"You are medium intimidating before coffee," Clarisse said.

"I will push you off this porch, La Rue."

"You can try, Pinecone Face."

"Girls," Chiron said mildly. "Save it for capture the flag tomorrow."

The meeting broke up with assignments and groans. Percy lingered, watching the younger kids chase each other across the green with pillowcases full of dirty clothes, watching the naiads wave from the canoe lake, watching the whole bright machine of camp start to fold itself up for another year.

Annabeth touched his elbow. "You okay?"

"Yeah," he said. "Just... trying to memorize it. So I remember in February when it's snowing here and my tutors are making me do algebra."

She smiled. "You like algebra now. Don't lie."

"I tolerate algebra," Percy corrected. "For my tutors. And for you, because you help me and you make that face when I get it right."

"What face?"

"That proud Wise Girl face. Like, yes, my Seaweed Brain can add fractions."

Annabeth laughed and shoved him lightly. "Come on. Grover's cans won't crate themselves."

---

The packing schedule wasn't the only paper Chiron had for them.

He caught Percy as the porch emptied and asked him to carry a sealed envelope to the satyr office. There were two envelopes, actually: one addressed to the recovery team caring for Ferdinand, and another containing the map of Medusa's garden. Percy recognized Annabeth's numbered handwriting through the thin paper.

"Has there been news?" Percy asked.

"Some," Chiron said. "Enough to pass on privately. Grover knows the first part. I don't want a report about his uncle read aloud at the dinner table."

Percy tucked the envelopes beneath his shirt to keep the ink dry. "Does that mean he's getting better?"

"It means the team has finished identifying two more people from the garden. The spell remains difficult to reverse. We don't yet know what recovery will look like."

For a moment Percy wished Chiron would say something more reassuring. He had heard the same cautious answer often enough that he recognized the shape of it. It was what an adult said when refusing to buy a few seconds of comfort with a promise he couldn't keep.

"I'll take them," Percy said.

"One other thing. Grover's proposed route in the Pacific Northwest will take him away from the recovery center. He deserves the chance to weigh that before the Council expects an answer."

Percy looked out over the green. Grover was arguing with Connor about whether an old soup can counted as a collectible or rubbish. Every few seconds he glanced back toward the Big House as though the Council's letter might appear in the doorway.

"Then you'd better tell him," Percy said.

"I intend to."

The satyr office was in a small building behind the stables. Percy had passed it for years without going inside. He found two younger satyrs inspecting a box of trail maps and an older one organizing correspondence by region. The room smelled of wool coats, hay and paper dried too close to a fire.

Percy handed over the first envelope. The older satyr checked the seal, signed a receipt and placed it in a locked drawer. He wouldn't show Percy the response, which was mildly irritating until Percy remembered that he wasn't its intended reader.

"Grover will receive it," the satyr said. "And the other one?"

"Recovery team. There are people in the garden we couldn't bring out."

The satyr took the map and studied the destination.

"We've marked the site for a search party," he said. "But the land's still dangerous. It'll have to be coordinated with people who can examine the stone before moving it."

Percy nodded. He could almost see Grover kneeling beside Uncle Ferdinand's statue, asking how much of the stone remained alive. He wanted to say they'd done something brave by bringing three people out. He knew that. It just didn't make the others vanish from the list.

When Percy returned to the north strawberry rows, he found Juniper and Grover carrying a young serviceberry tree in a canvas sack. Its leaves looked fragile beside the sturdy maples. The sapling was hardly taller than Grover's chest.

"You got it," Percy said.

Juniper set down her end of the sack. "The nursery had one that was ready to move. The groundskeeper approved the spot near the fence. It's far enough from the irrigation channel that the roots won't interfere."

Grover glanced toward the board they'd repaired before the quest. "I checked twice."

Percy smiled. "I believe you."

He showed Grover the receipt and explained what Chiron had said about the newly identified victims. Grover listened without interrupting. He didn't ask whether his uncle was cured, which was good, because Percy couldn't have answered.

"That's another two names," Grover said.

"Yes."

"Not another two people brought home."

"No."

Grover looked down at the serviceberry. He had said in June that it would be a place for Ferdinand when he came to camp, not a memorial. Percy could tell he was still trying to protect that distinction.

Juniper dug the planting hole while Grover checked the root ball. Percy helped with the earth only after she explained where he could press without damaging roots. By the time the soil was level, his hands were filthy and the sapling stood upright with a small stake beside it.

Grover poured water around the base. Most of it soaked in. A little ran over Percy's shoe.

"Sorry," Grover said.

"That's the least dangerous thing that's happened to my shoes all year."

Juniper smiled. Then she looked at the young branches and frowned.

"You can't leave it like this and hope it survives," she said. "It'll need watering while the weather's hot, protection from deer in winter, and somebody to check the ties as the trunk grows."

Grover was quiet. He'd spent so much time planning the planting that the long months after it hadn't seemed as real.

"I won't be here," he said.

"I will," Juniper answered. "And the younger satyrs can take turns. We just need a schedule that doesn't depend on someone remembering whenever they walk past."

"An Annabeth schedule," Percy said.

"Exactly," Juniper replied.

Grover reached for the bark list he carried everywhere, but Juniper offered him a clean card. Together they wrote the watering routine and the name of the groundskeeper responsible. Grover drew a tiny goat in the corner; Juniper added a leaf.

Afterward they stood back. The tree was crooked by perhaps an inch. Percy pointed this out and immediately regretted it when Juniper made him hold the trunk while she adjusted the stake.

"Better?" she asked.

"Much."

Grover looked at the small tree for a long time. "Ferdinand used to complain that I planted things where the birds couldn't see them."

"Well, they'll see this one," Juniper said. "They'll probably eat all the berries before we get any."

"He'd like that."

Percy didn't try to make the moment grand. The tree needed a dozen ordinary things, not a speech. And Grover had a decision coming from the Council that meant leaving it and his uncle's care in other people's hands.

"Are you going to take the Searcher route?" Percy asked.

Grover made a face. "I haven't even got the letter yet."

"I know. But do you want it?"

Grover looked between the serviceberry and the hills beyond camp.

"I think so," he said. "I don't want to stop being the person who goes to find things just because I found one of the things I was looking for."

Juniper crouched to firm the last of the soil. She didn't tell him to stay. Percy suspected that was harder than it looked.

They left the little tree in the sun and went to help pack the cans. Before Grover left, he came back once, just to check whether the canvas watering bucket had been placed where the younger satyrs could find it.

It had.

---

Grover's collection of tin cans lived behind the Big House in a shed that smelled like goat and apples and old rain.

It was, objectively, the weirdest hoard at camp, and that was saying something in a place where the Ares cabin kept a cursed throne as a coat rack. Dozens of cans, washed and labeled in Grover's neat handwriting: BLUEBERRY (MAINE, 06/12), PEACHES (GEORGIA, AUNT'S), TOMATO SOUP (DO NOT EAT). Each one was a memory from his years searching for Pan, eating out of trash cans and grocery dumpsters while he wandered the wild.

"You don't have to keep all of them, you know," Percy said, holding a crate steady while Grover carefully nested cans in straw.

"I know," Grover said. "But... they remind me. Where I started. I was just a kid with a bad leg and a set of pipes who believed Pan was still out there when everyone said he was gone. And we found him. Well. We said goodbye to him." His voice wobbled slightly. "It feels wrong to just throw that away."

Percy was quiet for a moment, thinking of the wild god's fading voice in the cave, the way the trees had bowed. I await you.

"You won't throw it away," Percy said. "You'll just... archive it. Like Annabeth archives her blueprints."

Grover snorted. "Annabeth burns her bad blueprints in the Zeus brazier and pretends they never existed."

"True. I've seen the ash pile."

They worked in comfortable silence, the summer bees loud in the clover. Finally Grover said, very casually, "So Chiron got a letter from the Council."

"Yep."

"And he said we'll talk after."

"Yep."

"Percy."

"Grover."

"What do you know?"

Percy grinned. "I know that Leneus stopped me after sword-fighting yesterday and asked if you'd been eating your vegetables and sleeping eight hours because, quote, Searchers can't go falling asleep in caves, Jackson, tell your goat friend to drink more water."

Grover froze with a can halfway to the crate. "Searchers? Plural? He said Searchers?"

"He said Searchers can't—"

"That's— that's the license word!" Grover squeaked. "That's what they call licensed Searchers! Oh gods, do you think—? I'm only sixteen, most don't get licensed until twenty—"

"Grover," Percy said. "Breathe. Into the paper bag. Not the tin can."

Grover breathed. "Do you really think the Council would—? After Pan, I thought they'd disband the Searchers. But if they're restarting, if they need someone who heard him—"

"I think," Percy said carefully, "that Chiron wouldn't have mentioned a letter on the porch in front of everyone if it was bad news. He knows you. He was giving you a week to get used to hoping."

Grover sat down hard on an overturned bucket. "Hoping is the worst."

"I know," Percy said, sitting beside him in the grass. "But you're good at it. You're the best hoper I know."

Grover laughed wetly and wiped his eyes. "When did you get wise?"

"Annabeth rubs off. Like sea salt. Impossible to wash out."

They finished crating in better spirits, Grover humming an old satyr traveling song under his breath. When Chiron wheeled up an hour later and asked Grover to walk with him down to the woods, Grover looked back at Percy with wide eyes, and Percy gave him two thumbs up.

Annabeth appeared at Percy's shoulder as they watched them go, Grover talking a mile a minute, Chiron listening patiently.

"Searcher license hint?" she guessed.

"Strong hint," Percy said. "He's going to vibrate out of his hooves."

"Good," Annabeth said softly. "He deserves it. After everything he gave up to help us—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. They both remembered the Labyrinth, the desperation in Grover's voice as Pan faded, the way he'd held his pipes like they might break.

"He'll find something," Percy said. "Even if Pan's gone, the wild is still out there. Someone has to look after it."

Annabeth leaned her head briefly against his shoulder. "Promise me we'll Iris-message him every week when he's on the road. So he doesn't get lonely."

"Promise," Percy said. "Every Sunday. Even if I'm in Atlantis and the connection's all bubbly."

"Even then."

---

Percy's divided summer had become camp legend by August.

He split his weeks between camp and Atlantis, commuting through the water. Connor Stoll still couldn't believe he did dishes here with a palace waiting. The truth was more complicated, and only Annabeth knew all of it. Atlantis was beautiful. It was also a lot.

The second afternoon of the last week, Percy took Annabeth down to the cove to tell her about it properly, away from the noise of packing.

They sat on the warm rocks where they'd sat a hundred times, waves foaming white around the barnacled bases, the Long Island Sound stretching silver-blue to the horizon. Percy's pocket glowed faintly where the coral sat.

"Tell me about yesterday," Annabeth said, pulling her knees up and resting her chin on them. "You came back late. You looked tired."

Percy kicked off his shoes and let his feet dangle in the water. Instantly, the tiredness in his shoulders eased, the salt working into him like medicine.

"It was the tribunal," he said. "Some rogue telkhines off Crete have been melting mortal fishing nets for fun. Dad wanted me there to, quote, observe justice. Which meant sitting on a too-big chair for six hours while old fish lords yelled in Ancient Greek so formal even I could barely follow it."

Annabeth winced sympathetically. "Did you have to decide anything?"

"No. Thank gods. Dad did the whole booming Poseidon thing and exiled them to clean up the Pacific garbage patch for a decade. Which is actually... good? Creative punishment. Triton said it was my idea from last month and Dad stole it."

"You've been giving policy ideas?" Annabeth teased, but her eyes were proud.

"Accidentally. I said, why don't we make polluters clean it up, and Dad went, MY SON SPEAKS WISDOM, and now it's law." Percy shook his head. "It's weird, Annie. In Atlantis, I'm Prince Perseus. They bow. Little mermaid kids ask for my autograph on shells. Generals ask my opinion on patrol routes. And then I come back here and Connor Stoll puts seaweed in my bed."

Annabeth laughed. "Which do you like better?"

"Here," Percy said immediately. Then he hesitated. "But... I like there too. Is that okay? I feel guilty. Like I'm supposed to pick. Mom says I don't have to pick, that I can love both, but when I'm in Atlantis I miss camp, and when I'm at camp I worry I'm letting Dad down, and Triton's trying so hard to be a good brother even though for centuries he was used to ruling the household—"

"Hey," Annabeth said, cutting through his spiral the way she always did, gently but firmly. "Breathe."

He breathed.

"You don't have to pick," she said. "You're allowed to be both. Camp Percy and Atlantis Percy are the same Percy. The one who does dishes and gives good ideas and gets seaweed in his bed because he loves idiots like the Stolls. Your dad knows that. That's why he wants you there — not to be a statue on a chair, but to be you."

Percy looked at her. "When did you get so smart?"

"I've always been smart. You've just finally developed pattern recognition."

He laughed, and some of the weight lifted. The water lapped at his ankles, cool and familiar.

"Dad said I can come and go during the school year," he said quietly. "There's a... there's a current. From the Sound straight to the palace. Like an express train. Only family can use it. He showed me. So I can visit on weekends. And Mom said I can Iris-message every night, but the salt water messes with the image, so I'll probably just look like a talking clam."

"I'd still talk to you," Annabeth said. "Even as a clam."

"Good to know my girlfriend has low standards."

"Girlfriend?" Annabeth raised an eyebrow, but she was smiling.

"If you'll have me in September when we're back to mortal school and I forget my locker combination again."

"I'll consider it," she said loftily. "If you promise to write. Real letters. Not just Iris-messages. Chiron still has our postbox. He checks it every day like it's a quest."

"I promise to write," Percy said solemnly. "Even if my handwriting is terrible."

"It is terrible. I've seen your essays. Chiron cries."

"Hey! I've improved. Poseidon made me practice royal decrees. My cursive P is majestic."

Annabeth leaned her shoulder into his. They sat like that for a while, watching the sailboats drift, listening to camp sounds fade in and out — sword practice, laughter, the distant thump of the climbing wall lava.

"I'm going to miss this," Annabeth whispered. "Even the lava."

"Me too," Percy said. "But we'll be back next summer. Both of us. Promise?"

"Promise."

They spat in their palms and shook on it, the way they'd done since they were twelve, and then wiped their hands on their shorts making disgusted faces, the way they'd done since they were twelve.

---

Thalia found them an hour later, dragging Annabeth off for what she called strategic sister planning and what Percy suspected was mostly gossip and ice cream.

He used the time to check on Tyson's old work corner at the forges, where the younger Cyclopes were already missing him, and then headed for the arena, where Clarisse was running extra drills for the younger Ares kids.

Clarisse La Rue in August was a different creature than Clarisse La Rue in June. Sunburnt nose, hair pulled back in a tight braid, a new thin scar across her left forearm from the summer's last monster skirmish. She barked orders with her usual relish, but Percy noticed she also handed out water bottles herself and adjusted a ten-year-old's grip without mocking him.

"Jackson," she said when she saw him, tossing him a practice sword. "Spar. I need to hit something that hits back before I start crying over packing like Beauregard."

"Silena's crying?" Percy asked, catching the sword.

"Silena's been crying since July," Clarisse said gruffly, but her voice softened. "She cries at sunsets. She cries at pancakes. Yesterday she cried because a Pegasus sneezed and it was cute."

As if summoned, Silena appeared at the arena entrance with a picnic basket and a tearful smile, waving. Her usual brightness was slightly dimmed by a summer cold, but she still looked like she'd stepped out of a fresco — dark hair, warm eyes, the kind of kindness that made even Ares kids sit up straighter.

"Break!" she called. "I brought lemonade and ambrosia squares!"

The Ares kids stampeded. Clarisse muttered about discipline but took two squares.

Percy sat with them in the shade of the weapons rack, lemonade sweating in his hands.

"So," Silena said, dabbing her eyes preemptively. "Atlantis. Tell us everything. Is it as beautiful as they say? Do the windows really sing?"

"They hum," Percy said. "When the current changes. It's like... whale song, but in tune. And yeah. It's beautiful. Gardens made of coral that change color. Libraries carved into whalebone. Tyson's got his own forge down there now, bigger than the camp one, with lava vents and everything."

Clarisse whistled low. "Beckendorf would've loved that."

A brief silence fell, warm and sad. Charlie Beckendorf's name still lived in the forges, in every perfect weld. Silena reached for Clarisse's hand and squeezed it.

"He would have," Silena said softly. "He would have made Tyson a sign that said WORLD'S BEST LITTLE BROTHER in bronze."

Percy swallowed. "He basically did. Tyson kept his old apron. Hung it up in the new forge."

Clarisse cleared her throat loudly. "So. Atlantis. When are you going to invite us, Your Highness? Or do commoners have to grovel?"

Percy grinned. "You have to grovel. It's in the royal charter. Section three: All Ares kids must compliment my hair."

"Your hair looks like seaweed had a baby with a bird's nest," Clarisse said immediately.

"Perfect. Invitation granted." Percy turned to Silena. "Seriously, though. Dad said I can bring friends in the fall. Once a month, if the weather's good and there's no war or anything. The palace has guest rooms. With actual beds that don't try to eat you like the Lotus Casino."

Silena gasped, delighted tears spilling over. "Really? Oh, Percy, really? I've always wanted to see the Siren choirs! And the Aphrodite shrine Louisiana—? The one with the pearl columns?"

"The very one. Mom— Amphitrite, I mean— said she'd show you herself. She likes you. She said you have kind eyes."

Silena pressed both hands to her heart. "Tell her I said thank you and I will bring her the finest mortal chocolate and not cry on her carpet, or I'll try not to—"

"You're crying on the arena dirt right now," Clarisse observed.

"I know! It's just—" Silena sniffled. "This summer was so good. We all made it. After last year, after everything with Luke and the Titan— I was so scared we'd lose more people. And instead we got this. Packing and lemonade and promises."

Clarisse put an arm around her, awkward and fierce. "Yeah, well. Don't get soft, Beauregard. I'll still kill you at capture the flag tomorrow."

"You always say that and I always capture your flag with lip gloss and strategy," Silena said, leaning into her.

Percy watched them, his chest tight in the good way. Clarisse and Silena's friendship had become one of the steadiest things at camp — fire and honey, spear and mirror. He'd seen Clarisse carry Silena's armor when she was tired. He'd seen Silena talk Clarisse down from a rage with a single hand on her wrist.

"Promise me something," Silena said to him suddenly. "Promise you'll Iris-message the minute you get to Atlantis. So we know you got there safe. And promise you'll visit us too. Not just us visiting you. New York isn't that far by hippocamp."

"I promise," Percy said. "Both. I'll even bring seaweed souvenirs that aren't alive."

"Deal," Clarisse said. "And Jackson? If any fish lord gives you crap about being half-mortal, you tell me. I'll come down there and shove a spear up his—"

"Clarisse!" Silena said, laughing through tears.

"—fin," Clarisse finished innocently. "Up his fin."

They shook on it, sticky with lemonade, and Percy thought, I'm going to remember this exactly. The heat. The clank of swords. Silena's laugh. Clarisse pretending not to care while she refilled everyone's cups.

---

That evening, the Hermes cabin hosted the annual Packing Panic and Promise Night, a tradition started by the Stolls and now embraced by the whole camp.

The premise was simple: everyone brought half-packed trunks to the green, traded forgotten items, and made promises to write or Iris-message. Travis and Connor ran the barter table shouting about sandals and chargers.

Percy traded a spare camp shirt for a waterproof notebook (for Atlantis ideas, Annabeth insisted) and a bag of mortal candy for Tyson. Annabeth traded architectural magazines and acquired, mysteriously, a tiny bronze owl that hooted when you wound it.

Thalia sat cross-legged on her trunk, writing addresses in a battered notebook.

"Okay, roll call," she announced as Percy and Annabeth joined her. "Iris-message schedule. Annabeth, you and I: every Friday, no excuses, even if one of us is in Tartarus."

"Even if we're in Tartarus?" Annabeth said. "The reception there is terrible."

"Especially then. We'll need moral support." Thalia scribbled. "Grover: Sundays, when he's on the road. Clarisse and Silena: Wednesdays, joint call, because if we call them separately they'll just be in the same room yelling."

"True," Silena said, appearing with two cups of punch. "We do everything together now. It's more efficient for gossip."

Clarisse nodded, mouth full of cookie. "Also she loses her drachmas. I have to pay."

"I do not lose them! I redistribute them into the couch cushions for future generations!"

Grover trotted up, glowing. He was practically levitating.

"Well?" Percy demanded. "What did Chiron say?"

Grover tried to be cool and failed spectacularly. "Provisional Searcher's license! Extended through the winter! With— with a stipend! And a route! The Council wants me to check the national parks in the Pacific Northwest first, places where the wild is waking up again. If I do well, if I report back, they'll consider a full license in the spring!"

A cheer went up. Grover glowed as Katie hugged him and Thalia punched his shoulder.

"Grover!" Annabeth said, hugging him hard. "That's amazing!"

"I know!" Grover said, breathless and bright-eyed. "I'm— I'm terrified. I'm going to be alone out there, no camp, no dining pavilion, just me and my pipes and junior satyrs who talk too much. But Pan— Pan told us to keep looking, right? Even after he's gone, the wild needs protectors. And Leneus actually smiled at me. Leneus! I thought his face would crack!"

"You earned it," Percy said firmly, clapping him on the back. "Every mile. Every tin can."

Grover's eyes shone. "Promise you'll write? All of you? When I'm out there and it's raining and I can't find a dry bush to sleep under, I want to know you guys are thinking of me."

They promised, solemnly, with spit and handshakes while Silena cried and Clarisse blamed dust.

"Every Sunday," Annabeth said. "Iris-message. Even if Percy looks like a clam."

"Especially if Percy looks like a clam," Thalia said. "I want photographic evidence."

As the night wound down, younger campers drifting off to cabins with arms full of traded treasures, the older group lingered on the green, lying on blankets and watching the stars wheel overhead. Someone had brought a guitar. Someone else had brought ambrosia marshmallows that tasted like whatever you needed most — for Percy, tonight, they tasted like his mom's blue cookies and sea salt.

Thalia rolled onto her stomach, chin in her hands. "So. Jason."

Annabeth nodded, serious. "Reyna's message. Fall break. You think Chiron meant it? He'll really help us get to California?"

"He meant it," Thalia said. "And I need— gods, Annie, I need to see him. He's my little brother. I missed his whole childhood. He was two when Hera took him, and now he's eleven and doing things the legion expects of him. Three days together before the quest weren't enough. I don't even know if he still hates peanut butter."

"He still hates peanut butter," Percy offered. "Iris-message rumor. Reyna told Hazel who told Frank who told me in Atlantis. Apparently he thinks it's a Roman conspiracy."

Thalia laughed, wetly. "Gods, that's so— that's so Jason. Stupid noble Roman dork."

"You'll find him," Silena said gently. "And he'll be so happy to see you. Brothers always are, even when they pretend not to be."

Percy thought of Tyson, of Triton awkwardly trying to braid seaweed into Percy's hair last week because someone told him mortal brothers did that. He thought of the way Jason's name made Thalia's whole face light up and ache at once.

"We'll all go," Annabeth said suddenly. "Not just us. All of us. Fall break. Camp Jupiter. If Reyna invites us. Romans and Greeks, no war, just... family."

"Family reunion with lightning," Thalia said, echoing Mr. D, but smiling.

"Promise?" Grover asked sleepily, half-asleep on his blanket.

"Promise," they all said, and the stars burned steady overhead, indifferent and kind.

---

The next two days blurred into heat and hurry. Thursday's capture the flag ended with Clarisse hiding the flag in a hollow log while Silena charmed the guards, and Friday was canoes and cabins and a last sword lesson.

And Friday evening, before the final campfire, Percy took Annabeth to the beach with a lump in his pocket and his heart hammering harder than it had in any battle.

He'd been carrying the shell for a week.

It wasn't fancy, as Atlantis treasure went. No gold, no pearls. Just a small, perfect conch, spiraled tight, the outside wave-worn smooth and pale, the inside blushing pink like sunrise. He'd found it on the palace beach during low tide, when the water pulled back to reveal sand rippled like desert dunes. He'd picked it up because it reminded him of her — tough outside, soft inside, and if you held it to your ear, you could hear the whole ocean thinking.

Annabeth was waiting by the water, sketchbook in her lap, pencil moving. She'd been sketching all week — the cabins, the Big House, the climbing wall mid-eruption, Grover's earnest face as he tuned his pipes. Trying to keep it all.

"Hey," Percy said, sitting beside her. His hands were sweating. "Whatcha drawing?"

"Us," she said simply, turning the book so he could see.

It was the cove. The rocks. Two small figures with their feet in the water. The horizon wide and bright. In the margin she'd written in neat Greek: Last week — remember the light.

Percy's throat went tight. "Annie, that's—"

"It's for you," she said, tearing the page out carefully along the perforation and folding it once, twice, pressing it into his hands. "So you have something for your trunk. For when it's February and all you can see up here is snow and you forget what summer looks like."

He unfolded it gently, smoothed it. The lines were sure and loving, every barnacle on the rocks, every curl of wave. He could feel the sun in it.

"I love it," he whispered. "Thank you."

"Your turn," Annabeth said, nudging his pocket with her eyes. "You've been fidgeting with that all week, Seaweed Brain. I thought you were hiding candy."

Percy laughed nervously and pulled out the shell. "It's not candy. It's— here."

He placed it in her palm. It fit perfectly, as he'd known it would.

Annabeth stared at it. Turned it over. Held it to her ear and closed her eyes.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, Percy. It's— I can hear Atlantis. And camp. Both."

"I found it where the palace meets the wild water," he said in a rush. "Where the tame beach ends and the real ocean starts. I thought— that's us. Camp and Atlantis. Mortal and godly. You're my wild water, Annie. You keep me honest."

She opened her eyes, and they were bright. "That's the nicest thing you've ever said to me, and you once compared me to a hippocampus with anger issues."

"I've grown as a poet. Royal training."

Annabeth laughed and wiped her eyes quickly. "It's perfect. I'm going to keep it on my desk at school. And when Zeus is being difficult or my math teacher assigns extra homework, I'm going to listen to it and remember that my boyfriend is a prince who does dishes."

"Boyfriend," Percy repeated, grinning. "You said it this time."

"I said it. Don't make me regret it."

"Never," Percy said. Then, quieter, "Promise me next summer? Same cabins? Same cove? No world-ending quests, just... camp?"

Annabeth took his hand, lacing their fingers together, salt and charcoal and sun. "Promise me you'll write lousy letters with majestic P's. Promise you'll Iris-message even when you look like a clam. Promise you'll save me a seat at the campfire and a blue cookie at dinner."

"I promise all of it," Percy said. "Every Sunday to Grover. Wednesdays to Clarisse and Silena. Fridays you and Thalia plot to storm Camp Jupiter. And every day—"

"Every day what?" she whispered.

"Every day I'll think of you," he said simply. "Even when I'm being Prince Perseus with seaweed hair."

The sun was setting, huge and gold over the Sound, turning the water to molten light. The camp behind them was lighting torches, smoke rising sweet from the final campfire. Someone called their names, distant.

Annabeth leaned in and kissed him on the cheek, soft and warm, her lips tasting faintly of salt and lemonade. It wasn't on the lips; tonight the cheek felt tender, a quiet promise suited to the two thirteen-year-olds watching the water. They knew other kisses, other summers. They could still make something special of this one.

Percy went very still, then very red, then very happy.

"Was that—" he started.

"A promise," Annabeth said, her own cheeks pink, but her gray eyes steady. "For next summer. When we're older. When we've written a hundred letters and visited Jason and Grover's a full Searcher and Clarisse and Silena have seen Atlantis. Then we'll have another summer to remember."

"Another summer," Percy echoed, dazed and grinning. "I can look forward to that. I'm good at looking forward. I'm very patient. I'm the most patient—"

"Percy."

"Yeah?"

"Shut up and watch the sunset with me."

He did. They sat shoulder to shoulder as the sky went gold to rose to violet, the first stars pricking through, the shell warm in Annabeth's hands, the sketch warm in Percy's pocket. The tide came in gentle around their feet.

Behind them, camp glowed.

---

The final campfire was the best one Percy could remember.

The whole camp crowded onto the benches, year-rounders and summer kids pressed shoulder to shoulder, the flames leaping high and strange with godly colors. Chiron told the story of the summer — not the battles, but the small things: the Pegasus that learned to fetch, the Demeter cabin's prize-winning giant tomato, the Stolls' failed attempt to flood the bathrooms with strawberry juice. Mr. D, to everyone's shock, said three nice things about the campers and then claimed he'd been possessed.

They sang and roasted marshmallows and burned offerings — Percy threw in coral for Poseidon and whispered, Thank you for both homes.

Grover played a new song on his pipes, wild and sweet, about roads and coming home. Half the camp cried.

Thalia stood and announced, to cheers, that she and Annabeth were planning a fall quest — not a monster quest, a brother quest — to Camp Jupiter to see Jason. "Any Romans listening via Iris-message," she shouted at the rainbow shimmering over the fire, "tell my little brother his big sister is coming and she expects peanut butter-free cookies!"

Annabeth stood beside her, holding up her sketchbook. "And I'm going to draw every mile. So when we're old and gray and running this camp, we'll remember."

Clarisse stood next, dragging Silena up with her. "And we're going to Atlantis!" Clarisse yelled. "To make sure Jackson doesn't go soft eating royal shrimp!"

"And to see the pearl shrine!" Silena added, tearful and beaming. "And to hug Tyson's new forge! Promise, Percy!"

"I promise!" Percy shouted back, standing, Annabeth's hand in his. "Guest rooms! Chocolate for Amphitrite! No man-eating beds!"

The fire crackled. The stars pressed close. Chiron raised his cup.

"To summer's end," he said. "Not an ending, but a turning. Go safely into the mortal world, my heroes. Study. Grow. Write to one another. Iris-message your old centaur, who worries. And return to us next summer, as you always do — braver, kinder, and only slightly taller."

"To summer's end!" the camp roared.

Percy looked around at their faces in the firelight — Grover's hopeful grin, Thalia's fierce joy, Clarisse's reluctant softness, Silena's shining eyes, Annabeth beside him, steady and bright, the shell she'd tucked into her jacket pocket peeking out, the sketch folded safe in his.

He thought of Atlantis waiting blue and patient under the sea. He remembered Sally's apartment in Manhattan, blue cookies in the oven, Paul grading papers at the kitchen table in the life he had lost. He thought of letters and Iris-messages and supervised trips to California and currents to the palace.

Summer was ending, but it didn't feel like loss.

It felt like a promise kept.

Later, when the fire burned low and the younger kids had been herded to bed and the older campers lingered, unwilling to be the first to leave, Percy and Annabeth walked back to the cove one last time.

The moon silvered the water. The shell wind chime in Cabin Three tinkled faintly in the breeze.

"Next summer," Annabeth said.

"Next summer," Percy agreed.

They sat until the moon was high, memorizing the light, and when they finally walked back up the hill hand in hand, the camp sleeping around them, the strawberries sweet on the night air, Percy Jackson, son of Poseidon, prince of Atlantis, dishwasher of Camp Half-Blood, thought his heart might burst with the sheer, hopeful warmth of it all.

Summer's end. And beginning.
