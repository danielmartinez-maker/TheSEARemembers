## Chapter 11: The Eclipse

The first person to knock on Zoe's door that morning was Tomas. He was carrying a clean shirt and a stack of observation cards, and his hair stood up at the crown in a way that suggested he'd been awake longer than he admitted.

"I did the numbers," he said.

"Which numbers?"

"Who watches which route. Nico said somebody had written two people into the same place and nobody into the west crossing."

Zoe took the cards. She had checked the assignments the previous afternoon, but a late request from the infirmary had changed the list. The west crossing was indeed unassigned. She could have found the mistake in another hour of paperwork. She was grateful Tomas had noticed before the eclipse began.

"Well caught," she said. "Did you tell Bea?"

"She was asleep."

"People are allowed to sleep, Tomas."

"So I came here."

Zoe sent him to get breakfast before she would discuss the cards further. He argued that he wasn't hungry, then ate the piece of bread she'd placed beside her notebook while continuing to object. She hadn't expected to spend the morning chasing children into ordinary routines when the Hall might behave unpredictably. That made it exactly the sort of morning she should have expected.

Mara arrived with the forge checklist and a small wooden box containing the sealed test strips. They had no intention of using the strips on the live ward. They were included because the team wanted an intact record of which independent control tests had been completed before any intervention.

"Chiron asked us to check the emergency stop procedure aloud," Mara said.

"We've done it."

"Then we can do it once more."

Zoe stood and joined her by the table. Mara read the steps: isolate the site, establish a clear escape route, keep water off exposed conductive surfaces, inspect from outside, and never disengage a live coupling without checking where its load might go. Zoe repeated each step and pointed out where the procedure depended on information they did not yet have.

"That last part worries me," she said.

"Me too. The frame can take the kind of surge we tested. If the Hall's ward is doing something else, we pull back. We don't pretend a training result is a guarantee."

"Good."

Mara looked toward the window. Camp was waking. Sunlight fell across the pavilion roof and the repaired noticeboard where the public status sheet hung next to the routes Annabeth had marked.

"I heard the custodian tried to get Chiron to open the exhibit wing for the eclipse," Mara said.

"He didn't."

"No. He wanted visitors to see the celestial event reflected in the Hall's dome."

"Then he needs a different plan for his visitors."

Mara laughed, but the sound was strained. They had spent the week making sensible choices in anticipation of a danger they still could not define. Zoe understood the temptation to turn the whole preparation into something dramatic enough to justify the work.

The more worrying possibility was that nothing would happen, and people would decide the precautions had been foolish. Some might be tempted to stop following them before the inspection ended.

Zoe checked the observation board once more. The circle at the center of the Hall's motif resembled the boundary mark she'd demonstrated on the copper training strip. That resemblance didn't prove the inscriptions behaved alike. She had repeated this statement so many times that it had become an argument with herself. Every time she caught herself imagining a neat mechanism linking the Hall to the attic, she wrote down what evidence would be needed to support it.

She closed the ledger when the conch sounded.

Tomas was still sitting near the door with half his bread in his hand. He watched her buckle the case.

"Are you going into the Hall?" he asked.

"Only if Chiron decides the site can be approached safely. You're staying with the Hecate cabin group at the pavilion. If the route changes, you follow the counselor with the marked cord."

"I know."

"Then tell me which cord."

"Two knots for the pavilion path. One for the upper bank. We don't cross the closed fence just because someone says they saw a light inside."

"Good."

He hesitated. "If you're late coming back, do we wait?"

"No. You go to the assembly point with your counselor. If you need to find me, the adults have the route log."

Zoe watched him take in the instruction. It meant she would not necessarily be the person he could run to if he was scared, and that knowledge made her sad. It was also the only way she could help Chiron work on a ward without making her younger cabin mates responsible for protecting her.

"Can you bring my cloak back if I leave it with the observation crew?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Then you've got a job. A small one. Nothing near the Hall."

Tomas took the folded cloak and looked satisfied to be trusted with it.

Outside, the sky was bright and ordinary. Zoe walked to the western path where Mara's team was assembling the grounding frame. The two of them inspected the casing for cracks, checked that the insulated handles were intact and moved the frame away from a patch of wet grass. Their preparation was deliberate and unglamorous. That was the point.

Only when the equipment had been signed back into the observation log did Zoe permit herself to look toward the Hall and wonder whether any of their efforts would be enough.

---

Eclipse morning arrived clear and cold.

Percy woke before the conch. Clarisse had already pulled on her boots; Silena stood beside the door with a bundle of clean towels and the expression she wore when someone was about to make a preventable mess.

"Infirmary supplies," she told him.

"I didn't ask."

"You looked like you would."

He sat up. Outside, counselors called campers toward breakfast. The ordinary sounds had acquired a hurried edge: doors banging, a bucket rolling off a porch, somebody asking for the third time whether looking through colored glass was safe.

Chiron had answered that question the evening before. He was apparently going to answer it again.

Percy dressed and found the two of them inspecting a wooden board propped against the cabin wall. Silena had copied the route assignments from the pavilion onto it and marked the time of each counselor's next check-in. Clarisse was holding a piece of chalk with an expression that made it clear she disliked having to explain to the board what she intended to do.

"What's wrong?" Percy asked.

"Nothing yet," Silena replied. "Clarisse has decided she can be in three places at once."

"I can get from the west crossing to the pavilion faster than anybody else," Clarisse said.

"That doesn't mean you can supervise both at the same time."

"I only need to check the bridge at the beginning."

"And if something happens while you're away?"

Clarisse looked at the assignment sheet. There were not enough senior campers for every route to have a familiar face at both ends. Percy understood the problem. They had built a plan around the idea that everyone would reach the pavilion before the eclipse. If anyone was delayed, the last person on the path might be on their own.

"I can take the western turn," he offered.

Silena shook her head. "You're assigned to the pavilion group. The counselors asked you to help account for the younger swimmers because you know who can handle the water crossings."

"I can still be useful over there."

"That's not the point. The point is that we can't build a route around you and then move you every time a problem appears."

Clarisse set down the chalk. She didn't look pleased, but she didn't argue.

"Fine," she said. "I'll stay at the west crossing. Bea can take the upper path. Percy stays at the pavilion, and the younger campers can be counted before they move."

Silena wrote the changes on the board, then made each of them read the assignment aloud. Percy tried to joke about being tested on a schedule, but she ignored him.

"I want us to know where to find one another if the light changes," she said. "If the Hall starts making noise, we don't go chasing whoever isn't in sight. We report the missing person to Chiron."

Clarisse looked at Percy. "And if you decide to be helpful by running into the building, I will personally make your chores so bad you'll miss the infirmary."

Percy thought of the Hall door in the storm. He had run toward it because Annabeth was inside. He would probably run toward her again if he believed she was hurt. He could promise not to treat his power as a solution to everything. He couldn't honestly promise that fear would never outrun his judgment.

"I'll follow the plan," he said.

Silena took a second look at his face. "That's what I need to hear."

She put the chalk down and checked the spare dressings in her bag. Percy watched her sort them into two pockets so she could find a clean one without taking everything out. It reminded him of Amphitrite checking his departure bag before camp, asking whether he'd packed the things he actually needed rather than the things that made him feel prepared.

"Are you scared?" he asked her.

Silena looked up from the bag. "Yes."

She said it so plainly that Percy was momentarily surprised.

"I know how to do the supply part," she continued. "I know how to get people to the infirmary and make sure the counselors have what they need. I don't know whether the Hall is going to hold through the eclipse."

"Neither do I."

"Then it's better we do the part we can plan."

Clarisse buckled her sword belt and moved toward the door. "I don't care if the Hall does nothing. I'm not spending all morning answering questions about whether the sun's going to fall out of the sky."

"You can tell them not to look at it directly," Silena said. "That's one useful answer."

"They've heard it."

"They'll ask again."

Percy looked from one sister to the other. There were moments when he recognized them almost instinctively as family and moments when he could feel the gap where his memories of their shared childhood ought to be. Today they seemed entirely themselves, impatient and frightened, working at the same problem with different tools. It would have been easier to think of the altered world as a spell if it didn't contain so many people making choices he hadn't expected.

Clarisse handed him the last marked route cord.

"Keep this where we can see it," she said. "You aren't getting out of the assigned route just because you can move water."

"I wasn't planning to."

"Good. Then we've already improved on the last emergency."

She opened the door. Outside, the other cabins were waking. The three of them stepped into the cold morning and went toward the pavilion, each carrying the supplies they'd agreed to bring.



Annabeth waited by the Zeus cabin with her jacket buttoned and her notebook tucked beneath one arm. Thalia was checking the knot on the cord used to close the path to the Hall.

"No gaps," she said.

"You checked it twice," Annabeth replied.

"Then it should survive a third."

Percy looked toward the building beyond the cabins. Its roof shone in the morning sun. Whatever lay beneath the mosaic had not announced itself yet.

---

Near noon, the light began to change.

Chiron gathered the camp at the dining pavilion. He had arranged the benches by cabin so the counselors could count everyone without losing a small camper behind a large one. Mr. D stood near the fire with his arms folded and told anyone who asked that an eclipse was no excuse to burn lunch.

Percy sat with Clarisse and Silena. Across the aisle, Annabeth kept one hand on her notebook and watched Thalia speak with Zoe by the steps.

They were supposed to remain at the pavilion.

The plan had been clear: Zoe and Chiron would inspect the ward from outside the Hall, and the children would stay out of reach.

By the second count, the pavilion was considerably less orderly than it had been when the campers first sat down. Several younger children had traded places to sit beside friends from other cabins, a decision that made sense to them and made the counselors' lists useless. Silena moved between the rows with a clipboard, asking children to return to their assigned groups without treating the mistakes as acts of rebellion.

Percy helped find Milo, who had squeezed behind a bench to retrieve a fallen snack bag. The boy looked up from under the table with an expression of exaggerated guilt.

"I didn't mean to move," he said.

"I know. Come out before Clarisse counts the table as one of her missing campers."

Milo crawled back into view and brushed dust from his knees.

"Is the sky supposed to look green?"

Percy looked out past the roofline. The ordinary bright blue had begun to flatten, and the shadows cast by the pavilion's columns had grown unexpectedly sharp. He remembered another eclipse from a school trip in his first childhood, when Sally had made him use proper viewing glasses and he'd complained because he wanted to see what everyone was talking about. He had not known then how long he would remember her standing beside him, holding the cardboard frames against her own face.

"Different light can make colors look strange," he said. "We still don't stare at the sun. Chiron's showing everyone the safe way to watch."

Milo looked uncertain. "What if the sun doesn't come back?"

"Then Mr. D will be extremely annoyed about having to serve dinner in the dark."

The boy smiled, though Percy suspected the joke was more useful to him than an explanation of orbital mechanics would have been.

Silena arrived beside them. "Milo, Cabin Eleven is at the third bench. I need you there so we know where you are."

He went without protest. Percy watched him count the benches under his breath, then stop beside the right counselor.

"We should have practiced the group count once more," Silena said.

"We've practiced it every day this week."

"I know. I keep thinking about the child we almost missed during the storm."

Percy looked at her. She had been dealing with camp supply requests since dawn, and the worry had left dark shadows under her eyes. He wanted to say something reassuring. Instead he pointed to the clipboard.

"What can I do?"

"Walk the end of the pavilion. If any counselor has an uncertain count, bring them to me before the next check. Don't improvise a new route."

He obeyed. He found two campers who had been counted twice and a girl whose counselor thought she'd gone to the infirmary. She was sitting by the kitchen steps, helping a harpy gather napkins that had blown into the grass. Percy brought her back and corrected the form with the counselor.

The work took a few minutes and mattered more than he'd expected. Every child accounted for was one less person someone would have to search for if the Hall became dangerous.

At the Athena table, Nico had brought a stack of Mythomagic cards and was using them to distract a nervous pair of campers from the changing light. He was explaining why one creature's attack rating ought to be higher when the shadow fell across its miniature.

"That's not how eclipses work," Percy said.

"It's how my tournament rules work."

"That's a separate and more powerful law of nature."

Nico nodded as though Percy had finally grasped something obvious. The younger children laughed, and Percy left them arguing about who should get the next turn.

Across the aisle, Annabeth was copying the final route changes onto a board. She looked up when he passed.

"Any missing?"

"Not anymore. Silena has the list. The north row had an extra camper counted twice."

"Good. The upper-bank bridge is closed for the moment. Bea wants everyone on the pavilion route if they have to move."

"I heard."

Annabeth lowered her voice. "Chiron said the ward team is outside the Hall. Zoe has the observation board, and Mara's carrying the isolation frame. If something happens, we wait for their instructions."

"That's the plan."

"And if I tell you to stay put, don't decide you're helping by getting closer."

Percy looked toward the Hall. "The same goes for you."

She considered that, then nodded. The agreement lacked the grandeur of the promises they'd made in other lives. It would be harder to keep precisely because it depended on what they did in the next hour, not what they believed themselves capable of surviving.

Thalia came down the pavilion steps and checked the last row of cabin identifiers. She paused beside Annabeth and straightened the cord on her jacket.

"Everyone accounted for?" she asked.

"Silena's verifying the final count," Percy said.

"Good." Thalia looked at her sister. "And you?"

"At the pavilion. Where I was assigned."

Thalia seemed to want to say more. Annabeth waited, eyes on the board. In the end her sister touched the corner of the map and pointed to a correction Annabeth had made that morning.

"That's the right crossing," Thalia said. "Bea checked it."

"I know. She signed here."

Their exchange was nothing like the fights they'd had about the Oracle, but Percy could hear the effort both were making not to turn a practical question into an argument. Thalia checked the cord one last time and returned to the steps.

The light dimmed further. A hush moved across the campers, then broke into excited questions. Chiron asked everyone to remain seated while the viewing glasses were passed along. Mr. D complained that he had never seen a generation so determined to injure its eyesight on a perfectly ordinary astronomical schedule.

Percy was about to sit beside Clarisse when the first sign of trouble came. The vibration was slight enough that he thought someone had kicked the bench. Then the metal spoon beside his bowl began to tremble. He put a hand against the table and felt a rhythm beneath the wood that did not match anyone's footsteps.

He caught Annabeth's eye across the pavilion. She had felt it too. She did not rise. She closed the notebook, set it under her arm and turned toward Chiron.

Then a sound came from the far end of camp.

Not thunder. A high metallic note, sustained until the bowls on the table began to tremble.

The water in Percy's cup rose against one side.

He caught it before it spilled.

Annabeth stood. Thalia was already moving down the steps.

"Stay here," Chiron called.

Thalia stopped, but the cord on the Hall path jerked taut. One post tore sideways out of the ground.

A white line flashed across the building's doorway.

Zoe put her hand over the mark on her diagram. "It isn't discharging."

Percy heard her despite the ringing. "It's drawing in."

---

Chiron sent the campers toward the Big House under their counselors. Clarisse took charge of the nearest group without being asked, pointing two younger children toward Silena and ordering the others to follow.

"Jackson," she said. "Don't make me count you missing."

"I'll stay with Chiron."

Clarisse looked at him, then toward Silena, who was guiding three younger campers past a fallen post. "You hear that, Silena? If I have to come looking for him, he's carrying all the sandbags afterward."

"I'll write it on the chore board," Silena called back.

Percy raised his hands. "I'm standing right here."

"Then stay there," Clarisse said, and ran to help move the fallen post. 

He joined Annabeth at the foot of the pavilion steps. Zoe was explaining something to Chiron, her words nearly lost beneath the metallic whine.

"The drain is blocked," Annabeth said.

Percy followed her pencil toward the map. The mark beneath the northern column sat over the channel that should have carried water and excess charge outside.

The Hall's open doors slammed against their stops.

Thalia looked at the doorway, then at the children still crossing the path beyond it.

"Get them farther back."

She ran before Annabeth could catch her sleeve.

Chiron called her name.

Thalia lifted her spear as she reached the Hall. A bright sheet of static spread between the columns, catching a burst that would have struck the path. Her boots slid on the threshold.

For one ugly moment Percy saw her framed by light, arms raised above a mosaic marked with hidden spellwork.

Every suspicion he had tried to put down returned.

Annabeth gripped his wrist.

"She's holding it away from them," she said.

He looked again.

Thalia was not standing at the mark. She was leaning against the doorframe, trying to keep the flare inside.

"I see it," he said.

They ran with Chiron and Zoe behind them.

---

Clarisse didn't see Percy disappear into the Hall. She was too busy trying to keep a line of frightened children moving toward the Big House without letting the leading pair run ahead of everybody else.

The metallic whine had made one of the younger campers drop his marked cord. Clarisse stopped the group long enough to retrieve it, then tied it back around the boy's sleeve with the knot Silena had shown them. If he lost the cord again, she'd have to make sure he remembered the route without it.

"Nobody runs," she said. "We can get there at a walk. Running means we stop counting."

A girl from Hermes cabin pointed toward the Hall. "There's lightning inside."

"I can see that."

"My brother's on the repair crew."

"Then his crew leader knows where he's assigned. We're going to the assembly point, and I'll have someone check the crew log."

The girl wanted to argue, but Clarisse put her hand on the girl's shoulder and walked her back to the group. Panic had a way of making ordinary routes seem impossible. She had been thirteen long enough to know how humiliating it felt when an older camper treated fear as childish. She refused to be gentle in a way that made them feel stupid.

At the bend by the infirmary, they met Silena coming the other way with the revised count. She had been helping a limping camper from Apollo reach the porch and was still carrying an empty water pouch.

"Eleven from your group?" she called.

"Twelve. The Hermes girl was with Bea before the alarm. I picked her up by the steps."

Silena checked the mark beside the girl's name. "Good. Then Bea won't have to look for her. Keep moving. Chiron wants everybody at the Big House."

The path ahead was clear except for a fallen post that had rolled from the lower barrier. Clarisse pushed it aside with another counselor, careful not to let the metal fitting touch the ground where the ward's light was spreading. She couldn't tell whether the post was charged. She could tell that it was blocking the only route that didn't pass the flooded ditch.

"Nobody touches the metal," she told the campers. "Use the dry side."

When the group reached the Big House, the entryway was already crowded. Some children had gone inside; others were clustered on the porch, looking back toward the Hall. The counselors' lists no longer matched the clusters, and Clarisse knew what would happen if everyone began calling names at once.

"Silena," she said. "Can we count by cabin before anybody gets moved again?"

"Already doing it. The Athena kids are on the north side of the porch, Hermes by the steps, Demeter inside the first room. I need you to keep people from walking between the groups."

"That I can do."

Clarisse took the entrance. Her voice carried without the help of a horn. When two older campers tried to slip back down the path toward the Hall to find a friend, she blocked them.

"Chiron has the rescue team," she said. "If you know somebody is missing, tell Silena the name and the last place you saw them."

"We can't just stand here!"

"Then help us count. That's what keeps the rescue team from searching the wrong place."

The taller boy looked at her with open frustration. She could hardly blame him. If Percy had been missing from a list, she would have been tempted to go looking herself. She was already fighting that impulse every time she heard the Hall ring.

She handed him a clipboard. "Check Cabin Nine. If your friend isn't there, write it down. Don't go near the closed path."

He took it reluctantly.

A minute later Milo appeared at the top of the hill with his cabin counselor. He was carrying the clean route map Annabeth had helped him distribute. He held it tightly, though he had already reached the safety of the porch.

"I knew the upper crossing was closed," he told Clarisse. "The map said so."

"Good. Then you did exactly what we needed."

"Is Annabeth coming?"

Clarisse looked toward the Hall. The light around the columns flashed white again, and her stomach tightened.

"She's with Chiron," she said. "We're going to do our part until he brings the team back."

Milo nodded, visibly unsatisfied. She was too.

By the time the third count was complete, every child assigned to the pavilion group had reached the Big House. One forge worker remained at the Hall with Chiron's team, and two counselors were still at the outer barrier. Silena marked their names on a separate adult-duty sheet rather than calling them missing campers.

Clarisse checked the count again. The result was the same. That helped.

She carried the list down to the last safe boundary, stopping where Bea stood with an observation flag. They could hear the ward team calling measurements from inside, and once Clarisse caught a glimpse of Percy's blue shirt near the damaged fountain.

She wanted to shout at him. She wanted to demand why he wasn't at the pavilion where he'd been assigned. But the work inside was already underway, and she would do no good by distracting anyone.

"All campers accounted for," she told Bea. "Make sure Chiron knows."

Bea signaled the result toward the Hall. Clarisse waited for an acknowledgment, then returned to the porch. She had children who needed water and counselors who needed to know which route would reopen after the eclipse. If Percy came back hurt, she could be furious with him then.

For now she was doing the job he hadn't stayed to do. She didn't intend to let him forget that, even if she was grateful when he came home.


---

Inside, the air tasted of hot pennies. A ceremonial cloth had fallen from a column and caught over Thalia's shoulder; she shook it free without taking her hand from the frame.

"Back," she said when she saw Annabeth. "I can hold it."

Another surge rolled through the room. Her knee buckled.

"For how long?" Annabeth asked.

Thalia did not answer.

The mosaic's northern edge glowed white. Between the tiles, something dark moved against the direction of the light.

Percy took a step toward it.

"Don't cross the center," Zoe said.

He stopped.

Chiron braced the doors open while Zoe crouched beside the marked column. She held her lantern near the floor without touching the tiles.

"Added over the drain," she said. "A second ward covering the first."

"Can you interrupt it?" Chiron asked.

"Only if we first isolate the active coupling," Zoe said. She turned toward the doorway. "Mara's portable grounding frame is outside. We tested the frame on an independent training mark. We haven't tested it on this."

"Then we proceed as though it may fail," Chiron said.

Annabeth looked at the diagram in her hands. Percy knew that look. She had found a weakness in the plan and was working out whether there was enough time to explain it.

"Say what you're thinking," he told her.

"The drain is flooded, and the western floor is covered with runoff. If anyone tries to use that water as a discharge path, the charge could spread unpredictably. Percy can move the water *away* from the exposed coupling. The electrical team needs a dry working area and a separate tested route to the grounding frame."

Percy nodded. "I can clear the puddles. I can't promise where lightning goes."

"Neither can I," Annabeth said. She flexed her bandaged hand. "The healer hasn't cleared me for electrical work. I'll watch the boundary and call the stop if the mark spreads."

Thalia was still braced against the frame, trying to keep the flare inside the Hall. Her breath came in hard pulls.

"You haven't done this together," Chiron said.

"No," Annabeth replied. "We haven't. So we stay with what each of us can do safely and let Zoe direct the ward work."

"Good," Chiron said. "No improvising with electrical discharge. Move the campers back another boundary. Mara brings the frame to the outside threshold. I call the stop."

---

Percy knelt beside the broken fountain, careful not to cross the line where the floor had begun to shine. Water had collected along the western wall, spreading toward the mosaic in a thin sheet. If it reached the exposed metal, anybody standing in the room could be caught by an uncontrolled discharge.

He could do something about the water. He did not intend to ask it to carry lightning.

"How much space?" he called to Zoe.

"Clear the western channel and the doorway. Keep the center untouched."

He drew the puddle back from the bright line, gathering it against the basin of the old fountain. The movement was slower than he'd expected; some of the water had mixed with grit and fragments of tile, and the floor sloped the wrong way. He resisted the urge to pull harder. A sudden surge might send water across the very surface he was trying to dry.

Annabeth crouched behind Chiron with the inspection diagram on a board. She followed the luminous line from the northern column to the doorway, marking where it had changed since their first observation.

"The third hook is brightening," she warned. "If it reaches the threshold, the grounding frame will be too close to the active edge."

Mara and another forge worker brought the frame to the outside path. Its bronze contacts were fixed within an insulated stone casing, carried on a sled so nobody had to grab the live metal. Zoe went to them, checked the connections without touching the active ward, and asked for the team to confirm the nearest escape route.

Percy held the water in the basin. His arms ached, but the western path remained dry.

Thalia's spear shook. The light she'd been keeping from the doorway had begun to climb the inside of the frame. She was no longer deflecting a single flare. The Hall's hidden ward was feeding pressure into the same point again and again.

"I can't hold this much longer," she said.

"Then tell us when you need to release," Chiron answered. "You do not have to wait until you fall."

"I need to release now."

"Then back three paces. Let the screen take the next flare."

She obeyed, though it cost her. The first flare struck the protective screen Zoe had placed against the inside of the wall. It rang like an enormous bell and threw a spray of sparks harmlessly into the enclosed portion of the ward. The grounding frame outside shuddered but did not move.

"Stop," Chiron called.

They stopped. Percy let the water settle into the fountain basin without trying to force it farther. Zoe studied the movement of the mark, counting the seconds between pulses. Annabeth checked her drawing against the live boundary.

"It's still drawing pressure toward the north column," she said. "The screen is holding at the threshold, but the mark itself hasn't changed."

"Then we have a moment to choose," Zoe replied.

She looked at the control notes she'd prepared, then at the fresh readings Mara held up through the doorway. The old western drainage connection was separate from the ward coupling. The safe repair procedure called for isolating the coupling before releasing the pressure it contained. They had not been able to make that separation during the storm.

"Chiron, the secondary contact has settled within the test range," Zoe said. "I can disengage the exposed fitting with the insulated tool. If the pressure rises, we stop and retreat."

Chiron checked Mara's signal and nodded.

Zoe moved to the edge of the northern mosaic. She kept one hand on the long insulated handle of the tool, the other free to drop it if the current jumped. Percy held the western floor dry, doing nothing with the charge itself. Annabeth watched the line's progress and counted aloud whenever its edge shifted.

Zoe eased the tip beneath the first hooked stroke. The metal resisted. She adjusted her angle and released a narrow retaining pin.

A black thread rose from the seam, moving against the direction of the light.

It reached toward Annabeth.

"Back," she said, already retreating. She did not try to meet it with lightning.

Thalia moved as though she meant to interpose herself, then stopped when Chiron shouted. She raised her spear but remained behind the screen.

The thread struck the edge of Zoe's containment lantern and curled along its inner surface. It did not enter the room. Zoe kept her grip steady while Mara checked the grounding frame from outside.

"Hold," Zoe said. "The ward is transferring. Don't touch anything."

Annabeth's injured fingers tightened on the board. Percy could see the effort it took her not to reach for power she could not yet control. She pointed instead.

"The circle has a break at the eastern edge. That's the part that wasn't on the original survey."

Zoe examined it without moving the tool. "Chiron, I can lift this coupling away from the active line. I cannot tell you what else was attached to it before today."

"Do only what the inspection permits."

She worked the tool beneath the loose metal. The second retaining pin gave with a brittle click. Pressure escaped into the isolated frame in a series of short pulses, each one quieter than the last. The bright line around the northern column dimmed. When the third pulse passed, the black thread folded in on itself and vanished into the seam.

"Clear," Mara called from outside. "The frame is stable."

"Do not touch the floor," Chiron replied. "Wait for a second reading."

They waited. Percy could hear water settling against the fountain's basin and the rough sound of Thalia trying to get her breath back. Annabeth's hand was shaking, but she held the map where Zoe could read it.

A second reading came. Then a third. The luminous edge retreated toward the exposed stone beneath the column.

Only when Zoe had confirmed the line was no longer active did Chiron allow anyone to move beyond the outer threshold.

Percy let the water drain slowly into the clear western channel, far from the isolated ward. The effort left him dizzy, and he sat down before his legs made the decision for him. Thalia lowered her spear, took one step toward her sister and nearly stumbled. Annabeth went to her, keeping both of them outside the damaged mosaic.

They had interrupted the surge. They had not learned who had installed the coupling, what the black thread had been meant to restrain, or why the Oracle had been silent. The object on the floor was now evidence, not an answer.


---

Light returned in a gray wash.

The Hall had not disappeared. Its columns still stood, the northern mosaic cracked around a circle of exposed stone. Someone had torn the ceremonial cloth. Water ran in ordinary rivulets toward the drain.

Thalia slid down the wall.

Annabeth knelt beside her. Percy could see a red mark across Thalia's palm where the spear had burned her.

"You said you could hold it," Annabeth said.

"I could."

"You were falling."

"I was about to revise my estimate."

The joke came out thin. Annabeth held her sister's wrist carefully, keeping her fingers away from the burn.

"You told me not to improvise alone."

Thalia looked at their joined hands.

"I saw the children on the path."

"I know. I saw them too."

For a moment neither spoke.

"Next time," Thalia said, "I call for help before I decide I'm the help."

Annabeth nodded.

"I can't lose you."

"I'm here."

Percy turned away long enough to bring a dry towel from the doorway. Clarisse had delivered the bundle after the last children reached the Big House; she stood outside the cord now, waiting for Chiron's signal.

Thalia took the towel.

"You looked at me," she said to Percy, "like you thought I knew what was happening."

He could not pretend he had only been checking the light.

"I did."

Her face closed a little.

"For a second. Then I saw what you were doing."

"What did you think before that second?"

He set the other towels down.

"That you might have wanted something different badly enough to ask a god to change things. You don't remember the same history I do. I let that turn into an accusation in my head."

Annabeth went still beside her.

Thalia looked at him for a long time.

"I don't know the life you're talking about," she said. "I know my sister. I know I want her safe. Those are the things I can answer for."

"I was wrong to treat your fear for Annabeth as proof against you," he said.

Thalia folded the towel carefully over her burned palm. "You don't have to like me, Jackson. But don't tell yourself you know my reasons better than I do."

"I won't."

"You don't get to decide that once and expect me to trust you forever."

Percy nodded. "Fair."

She stood, and for a moment he thought she'd walk away. Instead she helped Annabeth up, keeping her uninjured arm around her sister's shoulders. Percy followed them to the infirmary. They had fought on the same side today. That didn't erase the conversation.

---

Chiron made them visit the infirmary before he let them discuss the floor.

Thalia's palm needed dressing. Percy had a shallow burn beside his wrist and muscles that shook whenever he reached for something. Annabeth complained of a headache, then stopped complaining when the healer told her that counting the symptoms aloud was useful.

Silena brought them water.

Clarisse stood at the doorway and counted.

"Three," she said. "Good."

"Four with you," Percy answered.

"I wasn't in the stupid building."

"Five with Silena."

"Drink your water."

Annabeth leaned against Thalia while the dressing was finished. She looked exhausted and unmistakably herself. There was no return of a missing romance, no new childhood suddenly waiting behind her eyes.

When Thalia asked whether she remembered a similar storm from when they were small, Annabeth shook her head.

"I don't," Annabeth said. "I know that's hard to hear."

Thalia looked down at the fresh dressing on her palm. "It is."

Silena pretended to be occupied with arranging the water cups. Clarisse stayed at the doorway.

"If you ever want me to tell you about it again," Thalia said, "I will. You used to ask for the story."

"Maybe tomorrow," Annabeth said.

"Tomorrow's fine."

They did not try to remember it together. Thalia stayed until the healer told her she could leave, and Annabeth waited for her.

---

They met by the lake that evening, after Chiron had assured them that the Hall was closed and the fragment from the drain was locked away.

Zoe had not identified its maker. Breaking the mark had interrupted the surge; it had not explained the altered records, the changed families, or their return to twelve.

Percy sat beside Annabeth at the end of the dock.

"Same hands?" he asked.

She turned hers over in her lap.

"Still sparks if I get careless. Still Zeus."

"Still Grace."

"Still me."

He nodded.

Behind them, Thalia sat on the shore with Clarisse and Silena. Somebody had brought food from the pavilion. They were arguing about whether a sandwich rescued from a closed lunch counted as dinner.

Annabeth listened to her sister laugh.

"She remembers things I don't," she said. "And I remember things she doesn't. That hasn't changed."

"No."

"I don't want to make her love prove which history is right."

Percy looked toward his sisters. Silena was trying to pass Clarisse a second sandwich. Clarisse was protesting while taking it.

"Me neither."

Annabeth rested her head against his shoulder. He put an arm around her and felt the small shift as she settled comfortably, without having to work out who he was.

The lake smelled of damp reeds and cooling pine. A fish rose near the pilings, leaving a circle that widened beneath their feet.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we sleep late."

"An ambitious plan."

"No ancient seals before breakfast."

"Could put that on the cabin door."

She smiled.

They stayed until Thalia called them to eat. Then Annabeth took his hand and pulled him up, careful of his sore wrist. The unknown part of their lives was still unknown. When they reached the others, Clarisse handed Percy one sandwich and informed him that he'd earned exactly half of her forgiveness for missing his assigned place during the evacuation. He offered to carry the empty food basket back to the pavilion. She gave him the heavier one instead.

Silena made room for Annabeth beside Thalia. Annabeth took the place, shoulder touching her sister's, and nobody made a speech about it. There were five of them eating late on the shore. The Hall would still need repairs in the morning.
