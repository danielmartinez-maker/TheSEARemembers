## Chapter 117: A Day Apart

Over toast, Percy and Annabeth hatched the plan — sensible packaging for something that might otherwise swell too important. Steam rose off the toast in thin ribbons, butter pooling gold, and the pavilion's morning din wrapped them in a kind of privacy: cups ringing, benches scraping, the Stolls arguing cheerfully two tables over about who'd claimed whose sausage.

At the Poseidon table, Clarisse debated whether a spear qualified as a breakfast utensil. Silena had already rescued the apple it was meant to spear. Across from Annabeth, Thalia groused that the Zeus cabin's new gutter had developed “a personal grudge against the roof.” Bacon hissed; coffee steamed; benches scraped stone.

“I was gonna ask if you wanted to see the new current rig at Tempest Hall,” Percy said. “Clarisse says it needs testing. Silena says that means she wants someone else wet first.”

“Testing matters,” Clarisse called two seats down.

“Agreed to the second half,” Silena said. “Not the first.”

Glancing up from her sketch, graphite smudging her thumb, Annabeth said, “Promised Chiron I'd finish the east porch study today. Options for stairs and drainage. Library this morning, then measurements with Thalia.”

“Sounds like a banner day for the porch,” Percy said.

“Confusing porch. Deserves attention.”

In the pause, words tumbled out before he could dress them less like a dare. “Maybe we take the day for our own things. Meet at dinner?”

Her spoon stilled on the page edge, metal ticking faintly against ceramic. Percy stopped himself before adding an explanation. The question could stand on its own.

“I'd like that,” she said.

He nodded, letting her answer be enough.

“You would?”

“Yes. I want the porch study, plus time with Thalia. You want time with your sisters. We can do both without holding a referendum on whether we like each other.”

“Like you,” Percy said.

“Know. We can let an ordinary day be ordinary.”

Swiveling on the bench, Clarisse demanded, “You two breaking up?”

“No,” Percy said.

“No,” Annabeth echoed.

“Good. Already had a threat list drafted.”

Silena asked, “How long?”

“Depends on annoyance level.”

“Not a number.”

“Flexible number.” Apparently satisfied, Clarisse returned to her eggs.

Sliding her sketchbook into her bag, Annabeth said, “Dinner at the pavilion. If something actually comes up before then, we can find each other.”

“No hourly check-ins just to confirm the plan?”

“Exactly. Plan isn't law.”

“Can do that.” Her hand settled near his on the scarred wood. “Looking forward to dinner. Also looking forward to this morning.”

“Me too.”

Knuckles brushed — quick pact, no grand vow, skin warm from holding cups. Then Annabeth shouldered her bag, canvas strap rasping, and followed Thalia toward the porch, their footfalls fading into the general clatter. Percy watched her go a beat too long, catching the sway of her ponytail and the sun flashing on her sketch tube; Clarisse nudged his shin under the table, hard enough to rattle the bench.

“Breakfast's cooling,” she said.

“Aware.”

“Then eat. Missing someone's no excuse to punish eggs.”

“Haven't missed her yet.”

Placing the rescued apple on Clarisse's plate, Silena deadpanned, “Deeply convincing.”

“Tempest Hall once we clear up,” Clarisse said. “You're hauling lumber.”

“What lumber?”

“The lumber you're hauling.”

“Enthusiasm dropping.”

“Good. Excitement makes you sloppy.”

Hoisting her basket, Silena added, “You get sloppy excited, tired, or pretending you're not hungry.”

At his look, curiosity and faint defensiveness together, she finished, “Sister. Noticing is the job,” and flicked a crumb off his sleeve with practiced ease, apple-sweet breath warm in the cool hall air.

Standing, Clarisse said, “And I'm your sister. Exploiting it during training is mine.”

Silena glanced toward Cabin Three. “We wrote two to three as quiet hour. If the gate test runs through it, do we shift today?”

“Only if all three agree,” Percy said.

Clarisse considered the slate notes and the damp cuffs of his shirt. “Let's plan for after the test, before dinner. If we're back in time, we use it. If the test runs long, we keep the fast lane closed and reset the hour together.”

“Agreed,” Silena said. “I'll write the one-day change on the board when we stop at the cabin.”

“Agreed,” Percy said. “I want to record what we learned while it's fresh.”

Out Percy followed them, sun already warming the path.

---

Tempest Hall smelled of wet stone, bronze, and Silena's lemon oil, sharp citrus laid over deep mineral damp. Its deep pool joined the Sound through hidden channels, water breathing faintly against tile. Gates and baffles tuned the current for drills; today a loose hinge rattled with each tidal shift, spoon-banging-in-a-pot style, the vibration traveling up through the soles of their sandals. Echoes chased across tile. Chlorine ghosted beneath the salt, and every breath tasted faintly of metal and lemons.

Arms crossed, Clarisse glared at the vibration. “Distracting.”

“Training tool,” Percy said. “Teaching rhythm.”

“Teaching me to hit it with a hammer.”

Setting down her basket of bright cork floats, Silena said, “No hammers on equipment.”

Since their last test, fasteners had worked loose. Chiron wanted gates checked before the next group drill. Clarisse's plan involved bracing the hinge and forbidding Percy to “solve everything by asking the water nicely.”

“Water listens to me,” Percy said.

“Hinge doesn't,” Clarisse replied, slapping a wrench into his palm. Cold steel, slick with condensation.

Inspecting the gate, Percy found the top hinge kicked outward. At his touch, a ripple crossed the pool and current tugged his fingers, insistent as a dog on a leash.

“I'll hold while you reset the pin.”

“Don't let it swing at me.”

Hair tied back, Silena strung bright cork floats across the pool to chart the faster water, colors bobbing orange and white.

Clarisse had brought two lengths of hardwood from the forge and a folded sheet of Chiron's notes. She laid the notes on a dry bench, pinning the corners with the wrench and a smooth stone so the hall's damp air couldn't curl them back. The lumber went on the opposite bench, away from the water. Percy could see her eye the rattling hinge each time the gate shivered.

“Before anybody gets in,” Silena said, “we need to know what we're measuring.”

“Whether the gate stays put,” Clarisse replied.

“That's one thing. What about whether the lane stays predictable? Whether a person can reach the steps? Whether the rope holds? We can't call it safe if only the hinge works.”

Clarisse's fingers tapped twice on the wrench. “Didn't say we could.”

“The last test started with us all wet and ended with the float halfway across the wrong lane.” Silena took out her narrow notebook. “If we do it in stages, we can tell which adjustment caused what.”

Percy ran his thumb over the gate's outer plate. The metal was cold enough to sting. “We can mark the current setting first. Then let the water settle between each change.”

“And nobody goes in while we're moving a gate,” Clarisse said. “You stay on the ledge until I say the pin's locked. If the float crosses the rope, we stop the test. Nobody reaches for it from the fast lane.”

Silena looked from Clarisse to Percy. “What's the stop signal?”

“Hand up,” Percy said. “Or say stop. Doesn't matter who's calling it.”

Clarisse set the wrench down. “If I call it, we stop even if the test looks fine.”

“Yes,” Silena said. “And if the hinge starts rattling harder?”

“Stop. No one tries to catch the pin while the gate is moving.” Clarisse pulled a short length of yellow cord from her basket and threaded it through the side ring. “We can mark the gate's safe range on the rail. It won't hold the gate. It just tells us if the brace slips.”

Percy followed the line of the cord to the bronze ring. “A witness mark.”

“Whatever you call it. It gives us something we can see without putting a hand between the leaves.”

They divided the first job without assigning Silena to record everything. Clarisse checked the bolts and counted the pin holes. Percy stood out of the gate's swing and watched the water, while Silena noted the current's direction from each float before any change. Three orange floats drifted down the near lane at different speeds. The first moved almost imperceptibly; the second caught a side current and rotated; the third passed beneath the tiled arch, where its orange top briefly vanished.

“The arch hides it,” Percy said.

Silena marked a short line on her paper. “We need a marker outside it too. If it disappears, nobody can tell whether it's stuck or just out of sight.”

Clarisse walked the perimeter, trailing two fingers over the coping without leaning into the water. A ladder sat at the far end, its metal rungs slick with mineral film. She checked that it was secured and kicked the lowest rung once with her boot. It rang hollow, then held. She looked at the rope mounts, tested the knot, and moved a cork marker away from the ladder's landing so it couldn't snag a foot coming out.

“Clear access,” she called. “No float past the ladder until we test the gate shut.”

“And no one stands in the opening,” Silena added, making the note.

Clarisse looked back at her. “Write that larger.”

“I will.”

Percy watched the small current pull the floats against their tethers. The movement wasn't dramatic, but it told him enough to start. He could feel the water ask for more, a pressure under the skin of his palm, but he didn't answer it yet. They needed to know what the mechanism did before he made the pool obey him. Otherwise, every mistake could be hidden by his ability to smooth it over.

“Ready to mark the starting point?” Silena asked.

Clarisse pointed to a narrow seam on the gate. “There. If it moves past that, we call it. No one leans in to fix it until the flow is stopped.”

Percy marked the setting on the dry slate with a piece of chalk, then stepped back beyond the painted line on the tile. Clarisse tested the wrench fit on the nearest bolt, then withdrew it. The metal had no room to spin freely while the gate carried pressure. She asked Percy to ease the pool down by a fraction, not to close the passage, only to reduce the force enough for her to seat a temporary brace.

Water pressed against his hand when he reached toward it. He lowered the level of the surge by easing the current toward the deeper channel. The first push resisted, shoulder-tightening and tugging a line of cold along his wrist. Then the surface calmed. The floats kept moving, but their ropes slackened.

“It's stable,” he said.

“Brace first,” Clarisse answered. “Then we'll test whether it stays stable without you holding it there.”

That was why the lumber was here. Clarisse fitted a short brace beneath the hinge plate. Percy held the worklight while she drove two temporary bolts, each turn of the wrench sending a faint vibration up her arm. Silena watched the witness cord from outside the swing and called out when it shifted a finger's width. Clarisse stopped on the spot, backed the bolt off, and changed the brace angle by a few degrees.

“If the cord moves again?” Percy asked.

“We don't tighten it with the gate loaded. We lower the current, pull the pin, then reset it.”

The procedure meant more work than forcing the metal into place. Clarisse chose it anyway. She finished the brace and tapped its edge with the handle of the wrench. The sound came back solid. Nothing shifted at the witness mark.

“Dry test,” she said. “No bodies in the water.”

She stepped back and looked to Percy. He let the current rise to its resting level, then to a modest pulse. The hinge trembled once. The cord moved, but not beyond the chalk. The gate held.

“Again, from the other side,” Silena said.

Clarisse switched to the opposite control and applied pressure in small steps. Percy tracked it with the floats, one hand hovering above the water without touching it. At the second step, the top hinge quivered more sharply. The float by the arch spun in place, then shot forward when the current slipped past the baffle.

“Stop,” Silena called.

Clarisse killed the setting. Percy drew the water back into the deep channel until the float drifted clear. Nobody moved toward it while the flow still pressed. When the pool calmed, Silena retrieved the cork with a pole.

“That was the part we missed last time,” she said. “The near lane looks slow until the current finds the opening. Then the float catches up all at once.”

“Far lane too,” Percy said. “The two baffles are feeding into each other.”

Clarisse crouched by the slot and studied the metal edge. “We need the second adjustment smaller. And the next mark should go beyond the arch, not where we can't see it.”

“That gives us a measurable limit,” Silena said. “If the marker reaches the ladder before the count is over, stop and reset. No one tries to cross the lane.”

Clarisse looked at the chalk on the rail and the three floats waiting at the edge. “Write that.”

Silena did. “Then we test the baffle with the pool empty, then with a float, then with one person at a time. The next person doesn't enter until the first is out and the gate is locked.”

“Good.” Clarisse retrieved the spear and rested it on the ledge, away from the water. “Now we know the sequence.”

“Percy, in,” Clarisse ordered.

He waded out, cold climbing to his waist and stealing breath, tile slick-mossy under his toes. Palm to gate, iron chill biting his skin, he felt the push thrum through bone into his teeth. “Ready.” Water lapped at his ribs, insistent, tugging his shorts, raising gooseflesh along his arms despite the warm air above.

The pin slipped, splashed, and Percy fished it dripping from the depths while Clarisse reset the hinge. Their first baffle shift overshot; a float rocketed across the pool. Easing the current, Percy coaxed it to a steady sweep, shoulders loosening as the water answered.

“Far lane too quick,” Silena called. “Need a hand rope and clear markers.”

The float at the arch passed the first chalk tick before Silena finished counting. She lifted her hand. Percy brought the flow down; Clarisse waited until the gate had settled before reaching for the baffle. The orange cork bumped the inside edge of the ladder and bobbed there, too close to the exit to call that run a success.

“It crossed our stop line,” Silena said. “Even if it didn't reach the ladder.”

“We stopped it,” Percy replied.

“After it crossed.”

Clarisse stood over the drawing, water running from the ends of her braid. “Move the stop line upstream. If it's past the arch, it's already hidden.”

“And if we move the line upstream, the slow lane gets too short,” Percy said. “We need the warning before the arch, but the exit marker needs to sit after it. Two marks.”

Annabeth wasn't here to redraw it, and Percy was glad the paper stayed with Silena. She laid the sketch on the bench and put a second mark past the arch, where it could be seen from the entrance. “This one says ‘slow the gate.’ That one says ‘the lane is closed.’ If the float gets to the second mark, nobody enters until we've reset.”

Clarisse traced the interval with a wet finger. “The first mark can't be only a color. Some of the old cork is orange, some faded yellow. Use shape too.”

Silena pulled out two spare floats. One had a square wooden tag tied to its cord; the other carried a triangular chip. “Square is the caution mark. Triangle is the stop. We can make them visible from both ends.”

“No tag close to the gate where it'll snag,” Clarisse said.

“I'll put them on the wall mounts, not the float line.”

Percy looked along the pool. A thick rescue rope hung on the near wall, coiled beside the ladder. In the draft, they had planned to add a hand rope to the far side. The nearest mount, however, pointed straight across the boundary they needed to keep clear.

“This one can't stay there,” he said. “If somebody reaches for it while a float comes through, their arm's in the lane.”

Clarisse tested the mount with a tug. The metal eye held, but its angle was wrong. “We can move it to the short wall, on the dry side of the ladder. Then the rope runs along the coping instead of across the water.”

“Let's see if it reaches from both steps,” Silena said. She unwound only a few feet, keeping the remainder secured in a coil. “If it doesn't reach, we need another mount, not someone improvising with the loose end.”

They tested the reach with the pool calm. The rope reached the first step with slack to spare, but pulled tight before it reached the lower landing. Clarisse moved the anchor down one tile and secured the tail. On the next check, the rope reached both steps without crossing the lane or catching on the ladder. Percy ran his hand along it from the dry side; the fibers were rough but did not shift at the knot.

“Now the float,” Silena said. “No swimmers.”

Clarisse set the baffle at the lower mark. Percy restored the current one step at a time. The orange float moved through the near lane, hesitated where the two flows met, and drifted toward the wall. It reached the square tag before Silena's third count. She signaled, and Percy lowered the force. The float remained on the safe side of the arch.

“Good stop,” Clarisse said. “Reset it. Do the same setting again.”

“Same setting, not same current,” Percy said. “The Sound's turning.”

Clarisse checked the tide mark beside the service hatch. It had crept down since their first run. “Then we write down the conditions, not just the notch.”

Silena added a second column: gate mark, tide direction, float result. That made the sheet messier, but it let them see why two runs at the same notch could move differently. Percy took a dry slate and copied the stop order where it could be read from the steps: **FLOAT FIRST. SIGNAL. REDUCE FLOW. LOCK GATE. THEN RETRIEVE.**

“What if the person already in the water can't read it?” Silena asked.

“Then one of us gives the signal,” Clarisse said. “No one moves until the gate is locked.”

“Who is watching the gate?” Percy asked.

“Not whoever's doing the drill.” Clarisse marked two places on the coping. “One person operates the gate from here. One watches the ladder and markers. If it's just two of us, we run one at a time and stop before the next person enters.”

“That means more waiting,” Percy said.

“Good.” Clarisse gave him a flat look. “Waiting's cheaper than a rescue.”

He didn't argue. The first test had shown how quickly the far lane turned a float into a problem. The plan now had a visible warning, an exit, a stop point, and a rule for who touched the gate. It was slower, but it let them see the whole pool instead of trusting the fastest person to fix whatever happened.

Clarisse volunteered for mounts. Silena would paint signs; Clarisse countered with a spear-by-hazard proposal.

“A spear is not a sign,” Silena said.

“It points.”

“At people.”

“At the hazard.”

“From which side?” Percy asked. He had found a smear of yellow paint on the edge of the slate and wiped it away with his thumb. “If the point marks the fast lane, it'll look like an invitation from the other entrance.”

Clarisse leaned over the sketch again. “Then we need a sign on both sides. Same symbol.”

Silena flipped to a clean sheet. Her first drawing was a square tag with a wave inside it. It looked elegant, which was not the same as clear. Percy held it at the far end of the pool. From there, the wave became a curl with no meaning. Clarisse's second draft was an enormous spear crossing a circle, unmistakable even from the steps but hard to draw twice the same way.

“Could just say CLOSED,” Clarisse said.

“The entire pool isn't closed,” Silena replied. “Only the fast lane. We still need people to reach the ladder.”

“Then say FAST LANE CLOSED.”

“And an arrow to the safe route,” Percy said. “A warning without a way out isn't helpful.”

Silena cut a wider strip from the spare board and wrote **FAST LANE CLOSED** in block letters, then drew the triangle stop symbol beneath it and an arrow curving toward the ladder. Clarisse took it to the entrance and held it low, where someone approaching from the wet steps would see it. Percy looked from the pool level and called when the arrow disappeared behind a post. Silena raised the strip until the whole design cleared the timber.

“Too high for someone in the water,” Clarisse said.

“Then put a second one at the ladder.” Silena made a smaller copy, leaving space for the triangular tag. “This tells you where not to enter. The tag tells you when to stop.”

Percy carried the first sign to the opposite entrance. The wind from the open hall lifted its corner. Clarisse fetched two bronze weights, and they tested whether the board would stay upright without blocking the path. It leaned once, then held when she moved the base an inch from the tile seam.

“If the water gets high enough to reach this,” Percy said, “the sign might get wet.”

“It'll be painted on both sides,” Silena said. “And sealed after it dries. Today we can use the board and the tags. The permanent lettering can wait.”

“Good,” Clarisse said. “No one treats wet paint like a safety measure.”

Silena's laugh slipped out. “Put that on the board too.”

“No more boards.”

“We already have three.”

“One is a plank, one is a map, one is the sign.”

“Exactly. A family of boards.”

Percy tested the route from the water. The sign was visible from the shallow end and from the step. He could see the triangle and the arrow at both approaches. The hand rope ran along the coping, clear of the lane, and the ladder landing stayed free. When he lifted one arm, Silena repeated the stop call and Clarisse moved to the gate. Their response took a few seconds; not instant, but coordinated and clear.

They checked the cue from the lowest step as well. Percy stood where the spray broke against the tile and lifted his arm once. The post cut the signal in half from Silena's first station; she saw the movement only when he leaned out. She moved one tile toward the ladder and raised her own hand to show him where she could be seen. On the second try, the signal passed cleanly to Clarisse at the gate.

“Watcher stands here if anyone's below the step,” Clarisse said, marking the position on the temporary map. “If they can't see you, the drill doesn't start.”

Silena copied the note onto her test sheet, then added a box for the lowest-step check to the next run. The fast lane remained closed; they had learned how to position the watcher, not that the whole arrangement was ready for use.

“We need the sign closer to the entrance before the next group,” Clarisse said. “And the painted version has to dry before anyone takes the board down.”

Silena wrote the two tasks in her notebook and underlined them once. “I'll bring it back after it dries. Until then, the fast lane stays closed unless the person at the gate and the watcher both say the test is ready.”

“Not just me,” Clarisse said. “Both.”

“Both,” Percy agreed. “And either one can stop it.”

They labored till the gate held: Clarisse bolting rope mounts with sharp metallic clinks, Silena knotting markers with deft salt-rough fingers, Percy metering pressure till his forearms burned pleasantly and water sang against tile. When the tide pulsed, deep and insistent, the gate stayed, shuddering only faintly. Testing the lane spear-first, Clarisse caught current at the hip and spun, spray sheeting off her shoulders in bright arcs.

“Water's fault,” Percy said quickly.

“Know. Again,” Clarisse ordered, wringing her ponytail.

Second attempt she rode with the flow, spray cool on her face, planted with a scrape of sandals, and swept the spear in a clean arc that hissed overhead and threw droplets spinning into sunlight.

Silena clapped. “Acceptable,” Clarisse decreed, smile betraying her. She tossed the spear to Percy, droplets scattering diamonds.

His gaze tracked the bright marker line, orange and white bobbing in the churn. Annabeth would've clocked the pattern and quizzed the angle's effect on flow. The thought tugged; Clarisse's voice still rang, water still sliding around his calves.

“Thinking about Annabeth?” Clarisse asked.

“She'd have a theory about the angle.”

“She'll share at dinner if she wants.” Tapping the spear on the ledge, Clarisse nodded at the water. “Go.”

He went. First pivot swung wide. Current seized his waist and dumped him into the markers, one blue float adhering to his shoulder like a barnacle.

Plucking it free, Silena grinned. “Again, little brother.”

No impatience, only invitation. Resetting his feet on slick tile, Percy tried again. The second pivot landed. He let the current bear him a fraction before pivoting against it, borrowing its force to finish. The spear sliced a bright arc through misted air.

“Better,” Clarisse said — high praise, Percy translated.

Climbing out, he accepted Silena's steadying hand while Clarisse held the salve tin, its herbal bite cutting the chlorine. The scrape on his elbow throbbed.

“Allowed to say it stings,” Silena said.

“Fine.”

“Can sting and still be fine.”

“It stings,” Percy admitted.

“There.” Her fingers, gentle and efficient, smoothed salve over raw skin. Cool relief spread. “Now it can stop.”

Clarisse nursed a blister too; Percy cleaned and wrapped it while Silena supervised. “Spear grip goes slick wet,” he noted.

“Then tell Chiron it's a design flaw,” Clarisse said, extending her hand palm-up. “Not whining.”

“Naturally.”

They shared honey biscuits on the ledge, sugar and thyme on their tongues, crumbs sticking to wet fingers. Stone warmed their backs through damp shirts while pool water ticked softly against tile. Percy offered his for Silena's apple; Clarisse intercepted the apple mid-negotiation. Silena split the spare biscuit three ways. “No treaty required.”

Laughter echoed off tile and returned softened. Annabeth's empty place between them tugged at him; then Clarisse licked honey from her thumb and Silena sorted the supplies into tidy piles, humming as she worked. The ache eased enough for him to notice the argument about the last biscuit.

“Good call, being here,” he said.

Clarisse glanced over. “Fixing the gate?”

“Being here.”

“Obviously.” She repacked cloth and salve. “No sentiment till final test passes.”

“Thought testing finished.”

“Never finished.”

Gathering floats, Silena said, “Sounds invented to keep us longer.”

“Want to leave?”

“No.”

“Then irrelevant.”

They logged settings, flagged the slick grip, and attempted a two-person maneuver.

Anchored in the current, Clarisse held while Percy circled behind; Silena counted timing, voice crisp over splashing. First try they collided, drenching the bench. Second, the turn came clean, water sheeting off shoulders.

“Could work,” Clarisse said.

“Clarisse for *good*,” Percy translated.

“Percy for *stop talking*,” she returned.

They reset baffles and left the Hall cleaner than found, drains gurgling. At the slate, Silena copied the final sequence in order: clear the lane, check the rope and ladder, mark the gate, test with a float, use the stop signal, lock before retrieval, then admit one swimmer at a time. Clarisse crossed out *fast lane open* from the old sheet and wrote **CLOSED PENDING GRIP WRAP AND SEALED SIGNS** beneath it. She initialed the note, then handed the chalk to Percy so he could add the tide direction and the setting they'd used. The temporary test had a result, but not a permission slip for the next group.

“Chiron gets this before anyone uses it,” Clarisse said. “He can inspect the brace and decide whether our marker points are enough.”

“And the grip?” Percy asked.

“Rewrap it dry. Then test it wet before we try the turn again.”

Silena tucked the slate under her arm. “I'll bring the sealed signs when they're ready. Until then, the entrance board stays up.”

Clarisse checked that the main gate sat at neutral, then touched the wrench to each mount once more. No rattle answered. She still didn't call it done.

At the doors, Percy's eyes strayed toward the Big House path. Too early for dinner, too late to start another project, and the day hadn't split to reveal a hidden exam. Probably Annabeth bent over plans with Thalia, bickering about a staircase. He hoped the porch behaved.

He didn't hunt her down. He let the thought remain a thought and trailed his sisters cabinward, wet hair dripping down his neck.

---

Twice Annabeth measured the east porch, because the first pass trusted the old plan. Tape rasped out and snapped back, metal tongue cool against her thumb. Sawdust and warm wood rose from the boards, piney and sun-baked; paint curled where sun struck, revealing grey weathered grain beneath, rough as cat tongue under her fingertip.

The plan lied by three inches at the landing and lied again about the gutter. Somewhere a sensible field adjustment had calcified into official error.

She loved camp — loved growth by improvisation, hands rerouting paths when floods came or trees fell. She also wanted words with whoever inked a rainwater arrow pointing uphill.

“Maybe the arrow's optimistic,” Thalia said, crouched at the gutter with the tape, knees dusty.

“Water doesn't do optimism.”

“That's what the arrow wants you to think.”

Noting carefully in the margin, Annabeth muttered, “Landing narrower than drawn. Widen it, railing moves. Railing moves, steps slide toward the rose bed. Steps slide, the post—”

“—blocks the door,” Thalia finished. “Third briefing.”

“Confirming the problem remains the problem.”

“Changed its mind?”

“Not yet.”

“Good. Hate unpredictable porches. Gutter covers that quota.”

Annabeth unrolled the old plan on the porch boards and pinned one curling corner with a smooth stone. The original landing was drawn square to the door, wide enough for two people to pass without turning. The existing rail sat closer to the hinge side, and the stair run angled toward the rose bed. She set the measuring tape against the threshold and checked the landing again, then made Thalia measure from the other side.

“Same three inches,” Thalia said. “Unless both tapes are conspiring.”

“They don't know each other.”

“Could be a very organized conspiracy.”

Annabeth wrote the revised width in the margin, then shaded the path from the door to the first step. “If we widen the landing back to the drawing, the railing's post has to move. That puts the outside stair close enough to the roses that people will brush the bed when they turn.”

“Could cut the roses back.”

“Not mine to cut.”

“Katie's.” Thalia glanced toward the bed. “We can ask. She might say yes.”

“We can, but there's no reason to assume the porch gets the space just because the old plan says so.” Annabeth rolled the drawing from one side and flattened it again. “Let's test the smaller landing before we take room from the bed.”

Thalia looked around for materials. A stack of spare planks sat beside the work area, leftover from an earlier repair. She laid one across the threshold where a bench might go, then added a second short piece at right angles to mark its end. Annabeth measured the mock edge and marked the outline with chalk. It took less time than drawing an elegant bench, and it made the gap real.

The door opened inward, catching the air and settling with a soft knock against its stop. Its lower corner barely cleared the chalk line. Annabeth stood on the landing with a rolled sketch tube under one arm and a basket balanced against her hip. She tried turning around without shifting the basket into the rail. The first attempt bumped the plank. The second caught the tube's strap on the door handle.

“The mock-up steals the doorway,” Thalia said.

“Because it's too deep, or because the door needs another stop?”

“Both might be true.”

Thalia held the door at its usual open angle. Annabeth tried again, this time stepping past the hinge instead of turning toward the rail. There was room, but not enough to pass someone coming down the stairs. She moved the end plank inward until it sat between the door's sweep and the stair edge. The mock bench shrank by a handspan. Annabeth tried the turn with the basket again. It fit, though she had to pause before stepping down.

“If somebody's sitting here, no one can pass behind them,” Thalia said.

“A bench should invite sitting. It shouldn't make the stairs a one-person queue.” Annabeth sketched the reduced depth and marked the remaining clear strip. “We need to test the route with a person sitting, not only with lumber.”

“I'll sit.”

Thalia lowered herself onto the plank. It flexed and creaked, so she shifted her weight to the porch proper instead of trusting the mock seat. Annabeth took the basket and walked from the door toward the stairs. At the narrowest point, her shoulder brushed the rail. She stopped, backed up, and rotated the basket to her other side. That worked, but the handle caught against the edge of the plank.

“The bench is still too close to the route,” Annabeth said.

“Then shorten it.”

“Or set it along the wall rather than across the landing.”

They moved the plank parallel to the cabin wall. The bench would face outward and leave the main approach clear, but it used the space where someone would naturally pause to remove muddy boots. Thalia pointed at the wet groove behind the step. “The water goes there. So do boots. If we put the bench over it, it'll stay damp.”

Annabeth marked a narrow gap between the wall-side bench and the downspout's route. “We can keep the runoff open and still have a place to sit, as long as the channel doesn't cut across the landing.”

“Can it reach the flower bed?”

“Maybe. Let's see where it goes first.”

From the storeroom they fetched a bucket and filled it at the outdoor spigot. Water sloshed against the sides, cool drops splashing Annabeth's wrist and darkening the dust on her sandal. Thalia tipped only a little along the gutter's outlet while Annabeth watched from the step. The first trickle followed the existing groove. A second, stronger pour split at the cracked corner; part ran behind the step and part spread across the boot-scraping area.

“That's the problem,” Annabeth said. “The gutter isn't sending all the water down. It escapes at the joint.”

Thalia inspected the seam without touching the loose edge. “Could seal it.”

“Possibly. But that would only send more water to the same place unless we give it somewhere to go.” Annabeth drew an arrow on the plan, then crossed it out when the line passed through the stair footing. “This channel would undermine the step.”

“So the first optimistic arrow was uphill. The second was under the stairs.”

“The second one was a draft.”

“Still optimistic.”

They poured another small measure, this time holding a shallow board beneath the joint to keep the water from spreading while they watched the flow. The stream caught the board's edge and redirected toward the rose bed, where the ground dipped and the soil was already dark from the last overflow. It was not enough to know the water could go there; Annabeth needed to avoid sending it directly into the roots or toward the cabin wall.

She sketched a shallow stone channel along the outside edge, curving around the stair footing and ending in the open soil between the roses. No trench, no buried pipe, no cut to the bed. A line that could be adjusted after the next rain. She marked the gutter seam as a separate repair from the channel, then put a question mark beside the channel's end.

“Katie has to approve the outlet,” she said. “Chiron too before we dig. We can show them the flow and the plan, but this doesn't authorize us to start moving dirt.”

“Agreed.” Thalia emptied the rest of the bucket away from the step, into the lawn. “We know more than we did when we came out here.”

Annabeth rolled the wet board upright so it could dry and changed the plan: the landing would remain narrow enough to protect the rose bed; the bench would sit against the wall, with a clear route from the door to the stairs; runoff would get a separate shallow channel only after approval. The gutter joint still needed repair. She wrote each as a question or decision, careful not to make a sketch look like permission.

Modest, the porch: cracked corner rough under her palm, uneven step hollow-sounding underfoot, gutter baptizing the boot-scraping zone with a steady plink. It invited pausing anyway — shade cool on their necks after the glare, wood creaking a welcome. A porch's sole duty. Two sketches proposed divergent cures: widen the landing with a bench where someone could linger with a book, or preserve the footprint while reworking stairs and rails, pencil lines firm and alternative dotted faint. Pencil shavings gathered at her feet.

“Why not pitch both?” Thalia asked.

“One won't fit.”

“Sounds like design.”

They walked the perimeter, checking slopes, flagging measurements, orange survey tape fluttering and snapping in the breeze. Heat pressed on their shoulders; dust puffed up around their sandals with each step, fine and chalky on their tongues. Down by the amphitheater, a flute player missed notes with total confidence, each sour tone hanging in hot air, joined by the far ring of hammer on bronze and the sleepy drone of bees in the rose bed.

Trailing Annabeth's pencil marks, Thalia asked, “This morning, wanting the day to yourself — mean the *whole* day?”

Looking up, sweat prickling her hairline, Annabeth said, “Meant wanting to be here. And wanting you without feeling Percy must join every plan.”

“Not offended asking.” Thalia toed a stake deeper with her boot. “Checking you're doing what you want, not performing for him.”

Annabeth studied her drawing. “I want to enjoy this because I like it, then go back because I want to. I don't want to measure affection in hours spent together.”

“Then don't,” Thalia said, retrieving a toppled stake. “I have plenty of afternoons away from you. Still your sister.”

“And Percy isn't picking them over me. He's picking time with them.”

“I know. Now help me find the lying porch section.”

Annabeth grinned, dust on her fingers. “Excellent plan.”

“Been practicing.”

Behind a post, the downspout disgorged where runoff had grooved the step into a dark damp channel, moss furriest at the edges. Annabeth sketched a shallow stone channel toward the flower bed, pencil whispering, then traced porch and prospective rail with slower, surer lines. She pictured rain drumming the roof: muddy campers stomping, someone reading on the bench with knees tucked up, another holding the door against wind that smelled of wet roses. The design needed to shelter all of them, hinges sighing, boots thumping hollow wood, water chuckling away through stones.

While Thalia fetched water, Annabeth tested a short roof extension, erased it for stealing winter light, and drew a slimmer one, eraser crumbs scattering.

Percy surfaced in her thoughts — less an absence on the porch than a future telling: the uphill arrow, the stubborn downspout. He'd ask if uphill was deliberate, she'd explain it wasn't, he'd suggest optimism regardless. She smiled to herself, pencil pausing.

No drachma reached. No search commenced, though her sandals scuffed restlessly against the porch edge. The urge to report the arrow could rest till dinner, honey still sticky on her fingers, like setting one sketch aside to finish another — not suppression, merely sequencing, paper whispering as she rolled the finished sheet.

Thalia returned with water and two honey biscuits, crumbs already dotting the bag. “Silena approved,” she preempted.

“Wasn't asking.”

“Thinking loudly.”

They perched on the steps to eat, honey sticking faintly to fingers, wood warm through jeans, sun heating the tops of their heads. Thalia brushed a crumb from Annabeth's sleeve and tucked an arm around her shoulders, cotton soft with washing, faintly smelling of pine soap. Familiar affection landed with unusual clarity today, steady as the step beneath them.

“You can engineer scaffolding around any feeling,” Thalia said, brushing a crumb from Annabeth's sleeve.

“Sounds like me.”

“Little, you charted how long you could stay mad at me before it stopped paying.” Cheek to Annabeth's head, Thalia's hair tickled.

“That's an excellent chart.”

“Had categories.”

“Required categories.”

“Eleven, no clue whether I stayed mad or merely couldn't apologize.”

“And then?”

“I apologized. You forgave me.”

“So the chart helped.”

Thalia kissed her hair. Annabeth leaned into her sister.

“From your stories, I can picture how little me might've thought distance invited disaster,” Annabeth said quietly. “Like staying close was a way to keep you safe.”

Thalia bumped her shoulder gently. “I was the one running off. You can miss your sister now, too.”

“Know.”

“And you can miss Percy too. Doesn't mean you can't manage a day without him.”

“You and Clarisse comparing notes?”

“Clarisse? No. She'd prescribe silence and hitting something.”

“Might have a point.”

“Usually does about hitting.” Thalia lifted her cup. “You're here, doing good work, and still looking forward to dinner. That's enough data for me.”

“Think I'm okay,” she said.

“You are.”

“Sound less relieved.”

“Worried since you were a baby. Frown like a tiny judge.”

“Evaluating the room.”

“Drooling on my shirt.”

“Both true.”

Thalia kissed her temple. “There she is.”

They lugged notes to the Big House library, cool and paper-scented after the glare, dust motes turning in the high windows. Annabeth inked designs onto tracing paper while Thalia honed a knife across the table, steel whispering in slow draws, leather strop creaking. They traded quiet remarks about porch and blade, pages rustling softly, a chair groaning as Thalia shifted her weight.

Annabeth set a square grid beneath the clean sheet and transferred the porch measurements from the field notes. Her pencil moved from the threshold to the narrowed landing, then down the two possible stair runs. One preserved the existing steps but turned the last tread toward the roses. The other shifted the lower step outward and brought the railing closer to the post. Neither fit the old drawing. Both could fit the porch as it stood, if she stopped treating the old lines like instructions.

“Which one's easier to use?” Thalia asked, drawing the whetstone down the blade in a slow, even pass.

“Depends what you're carrying.” Annabeth folded a paper rectangle to mark the landing. “A basket can pass on this one if the bench is against the wall. A shield catches the rail if someone comes down while you're carrying it.”

“So people shouldn't pass each other on the stair.”

“Not unless the rail moves.”

“Moving the rail eats into the rose bed.”

“Or widens the landing. That's the trade.” Annabeth moved a small wooden ruler to show the widened option. “If the landing is wider, the first stair has to shift outward. The foundation edge stays here, so that new step rests closer to the roots.”

“How close?”

“Close enough I won't call it safe without checking what's under the soil. And that means digging before we've agreed what we're building.”

Thalia set the knife down on its cloth. “No one asked you to dig today.”

“I know. Still want the draft to show what's possible and what needs another check.” Annabeth turned to a blank margin and made a list: *foundation edge; rose roots; downspout joint; door swing.* “If I draw a pretty answer that ignores one of those, Chiron has to start over when he reviews it.”

“That is why he asked you to do the study.”

“Exactly.”

Thalia lifted the paper template and turned it ninety degrees. “What if the bench goes here, along the wall, but ends before the door's swing? Then someone can sit while the door is closed. When the door's open, nobody has to walk around them.”

Annabeth looked down at the previous mock-up in her notes. She had written **BENCH MAY BLOCK CLEARANCE** beside the first layout. Her sister's angle changed the problem. She cut another rectangle from scrap tracing paper, shorter and shallower than the first, and placed it along the wall. The clear route remained straight from threshold to stair. The end of the bench stopped before the door's arc.

“That might work,” Annabeth said. “But if the bench is too shallow, it's a shelf, not a seat.”

“Ask someone who sits on things.” Thalia dragged the library chair back from the table. Its legs groaned against the floorboards. “You can try it.”

“The chair's not the bench.”

“The chair has a back and the porch bench won't. But it can tell you whether the route stays open when someone's occupying the seat.”

Annabeth frowned at the chair, then at the sketch. “That's fair.”

Thalia positioned the chair parallel to the table as a stand-in. Annabeth sat, then got up with the roll of tracing paper under one arm and a small wooden box in the other. She crossed the mock landing and descended the imaginary stair drawn on the floor in chalk. The first pass was clear. On the return, the box brushed the chair's rear corner. Annabeth shifted her route and found she could pass, but only by turning sideways.

“If someone is sitting with their legs out, it blocks the line,” Thalia said.

“Then the bench can't have a deep seat.” Annabeth measured the chair's projection and marked a shallower rectangle. “It needs enough room for a person to sit upright, feet beneath them. No lounging across the passage.”

“That's a strict bench.”

“It's a porch bench with stairs next to it.”

Thalia tried the same passage with a laundry basket at her hip. She didn't have to turn, but the handle came close to the rail. Annabeth moved the stair line inward by a small amount, keeping the bottom step clear of the flower-bed border. She drew the basket route with a dashed line, then stopped before putting any exact dimension on it.

“The field mark says we have three inches less than the old plan,” she said. “Until Chiron confirms the post and root line, I can show the clearance we tested, but not a final cut list.”

“Then draw the tested clearance. Don't call it a final dimension.” Thalia took the box and walked the route once more, deliberately turning as if the door had opened behind her. “Still works. Just don't ask two people and a basket to pass at once.”

“The porch doesn't have room for a crowd.”

“Which is why the big landing was a crowd solution.”

Annabeth laughed once. “You thought I was designing for a crowd that doesn't exist.”

“Was I wrong?”

“No.”

She placed the shorter bench template against the wall and the narrow stair trace beside it. The design was no longer just a preference between sitting room and standing room. It had a route: door clears the bench; bench ends before the threshold's swing; stair width preserves the rose bed until the ground is checked; the main path stays open; runoff skirts the footing. A separate note showed the joint that still needed repair and a proposed channel that still needed approval.

Thalia leaned over her shoulder. “What goes to Chiron?”

“The measured landing, two stair options, and the narrower bench version. I'll recommend the narrow landing with the bench along the wall, but the channel stays a proposal until Chiron and Katie approve where it ends.”

“Good. What goes to Katie?”

“The outlet point and the chance that we might need to disturb soil near the roses.”

“And the gutter?”

“Separate repair. We can show the leak and ask how it should be handled. The water test didn't fix the joint.”

Annabeth wrote the carry-forward items on a tracing-paper flap, then pinned it beneath the drawing with the whetstone so it would not be lost in the tube. The list was short enough to survive the walk: verify foundation and roots, review the gutter joint, ask about the channel outlet, preserve the door path, keep bench depth within the tested clearance. She did not add a construction date. The work had produced a decision, not permission to start building.

When Thalia tapped the chosen outline with one finger, Annabeth didn't erase it. “This one?”

“This one.”

“You sure?”

“Yes. Narrow landing, wall bench, no crowd fiction.”

Thalia returned to the knife and resumed sharpening, blade whispering over stone. Annabeth traced the selected plan in ink, then shaded the two unapproved areas in pale pencil rather than crossing them out. One decision could be firm while the questions stayed open.

By late afternoon, the drainage route was mapped and two stair options drafted; the walk-through made Annabeth's wall-side bench easier to recommend.

“Still recommending the bench?” Thalia asked.

“Bench, narrow landing. The broad landing stays as the alternative if Chiron finds the footing or roots allow it.”

“Then why keep the second drawing?”

“So he can compare the tradeoff. A study should show the option I rejected too.”

“And you're sure this is your recommendation?”

“Yes.”

“There. Decided.”

“Chiron weighs in still.”

“Present as expert opinion.”

“Am the expert.”

“Exactly.”

Rolling drawings into her tube, Annabeth said, “Good day. Let's show Chiron the drainage question before dinner.”

“And bring Katie in before anyone touches the outlet,” Thalia said, pocketing her whetstone.

Back at Cabin Three, the board held the day's one-time shift in Silena's neat hand: quiet hour after the Tempest Hall test, before dinner. The chalk had smeared at the corner where someone touched it with a wet sleeve, but the words stayed readable. Percy hung the blue cloth from the hook. Clarisse set her spear down and took the far bunk with a whetstone and a strip of leather. Silena sat at the table with the practice grip and a coil of dry wrap.

The first minutes felt awkward. They were all in the cabin, and yet no one had brought the hour's silence with them like a punishment. Silena worked the wrap onto the grip in even turns, testing each layer with her thumb. At the seam she pressed the overlap flat, pulled the wrap hard in both directions, then rolled the grip between her palms to check for a ridge. Clarisse held the spear steady while Silena tried to twist it free. It shifted a fraction, then stopped against the new texture.

“Dry test only,” Clarisse said. “Still need Chiron to see it before we try the wet grip.”

“I'll write that on the sheet,” Percy said, adding a line beneath the gate settings: **DRY WRAP FIT: HOLDS; WET RETEST WITH CHIRON PENDING.**

Silena read it, then set the spear beside the board instead of returning it to the rack. “There. No one grabs it by mistake and thinks it's cleared.”

Clarisse sharpened one edge, stopped when the stone rasped too sharply, and shifted to the porch where the sound wouldn't carry through the room. Percy copied the remaining gate settings onto a clean sheet, leaving space for Chiron's inspection and the follow-up grip test.

He noticed himself listening for a question. None came. Nobody watched to see whether he stayed still, or tried to calculate whether his silence meant something worse. Silena could see him from the corner of her eye, but kept her attention on the wrap. The room stayed shared: paper turning, cloth whispering, sandals crossing the porch once and returning only after the whetstone was put away.

Percy finished the notes before the hour ended. He added a box beside the stop procedure, then wrote **RETEST WITH DRY WRAP** in it. The work was useful, but no one had assigned it to him. He could choose to finish and still leave the last line blank for Chiron.

When the agreed hour passed, Silena lifted the blue cloth from the hook. “How'd that feel?”

“Quiet,” Percy said. “Easy to use.”

“Good. I'll leave the shift marked as done. Tomorrow we go back to the board's usual time unless we all choose something else.”

Clarisse picked up the spear, inspecting its dry grip without squeezing. “And we don't use this one till it's wrapped and tested.”

“Right.” Percy put the notes beside the board, where Chiron could read them when he came by. The first quiet hour had not solved every argument about space. It had given them one stretch of it without anyone stepping over the rule to see what would happen.

Percy spotted Annabeth before reaching the table.

Beside Thalia at the Zeus end, sketch tube against the bench, graphite smearing her thumb in a soft grey crescent, she was mid-sentence, Thalia grinning with an apple core in hand. His face started smiling before consulting him. Roast and rosemary drifted from the serving tables, mingled with char and fresh bread; cups clattered and a breeze lifted the edge of the tablecloth, warm against his shins.

No hurry. No need. Clarisse and Silena flanked him, toting notes on the current rig and litigating whether honey biscuits constituted post-training ration or bribe.

“Both,” Silena said.

“Categories don't work that way,” Clarisse replied.

“They do with enough columns.”

“Annabeth's argument.”

“Still good.”

Nearing the pavilion, Annabeth turned and caught his gaze. Her expression opened. Only a smile — no promise the day had dragged, no signal she'd waited, no reversal. Simply glad.

Sliding in beside her, bench creaking and sun-warmed through his jeans, Percy asked, “How'd the porch go? Tell me it behaved.” His knee knocked hers lightly, familiar as breathing.

“Lied about dimensions. Caught it.”

“Gutter?”

“Gutter and landing. Downspout waters behind the step, plan claims a wider landing than exists.”

“Knew it looked shady.”

“You did not.”

“Suspected. Guilty-looking porch.”

Laughing, she detailed, “Two stair options, drainage channel for Chiron. Possibly a bench.”

“Porch bench: solid.”

“Saying that 'cause you're happy to see me?”

“Happy to see you. Bench still solid.”

Half a second's study, then a nod. “Good.”

Thalia quizzed him on the rig, leaning in with apple-sweet breath. He spread the notes, paper fluttering in the breeze and corners curling from pool damp. Tracing the revised flow with one finger, nail still rimmed with paint, Annabeth suggested the baffle angle explained the far lane's acceleration. Clarisse leaned across to see, braid swinging. Silena added they'd marked a safe route and rewrapped the practice grips, though the wet test was still due.

“So: safer and more complicated,” Thalia said.

“Safer,” Clarisse corrected. “Complication preexisted.”

“I made it safer,” Percy said. “Clarisse assaulted a hinge.”

“Misbehaving hinge.”

Passing bread, its crust crackling, Silena said, “And I made signs. Enormous ones.”

“She wanted a spear as signage,” Annabeth said.

“Would've worked,” Clarisse maintained.

“Terrified every newcomer.”

“They'd remember the hazard.”

Over the bread basket, crust crackling as Thalia tore it, Percy caught Annabeth's eye. A dozen small reports waited — the gate's final shudder, the spear's clean arc, the uphill arrow — and the evening stretched to hold them, lamplight just kindling along the pavilion beams. Nobody rushed. They ate, sauce sharp with tomato on their tongues, covered porch, pool, and the flutist reportedly relocated from amphitheater to outside the Zeus cabin, where he plagued an entirely new audience.

Once, leaning to hear him through clatter and laughter ringing off the rafters, her shoulder met his, cotton to cotton, warmth blooming through. Warmth, uncomplicated pleasure, thyme and bread on their breath. He neither clutched nor withdrew. He let contact be itself, easy as breathing.

Afterward the pavilion thinned gradually. Campers ferried plates to wash, water sloshing in basins, bronze clanking, torches sputtering awake as shadows lengthened. Clarisse and Silena lingered to brief Chiron on equipment, voices low and earnest over the table, while Thalia carried the porch study Big House-ward, tube balanced on her shoulder. Percy and Annabeth slipped outside together, cool air rinsing the roast-smell from their clothes.

They crossed toward the green, late light coppering cabin roofs and gilding the dust above the path, crickets tuning in the lengthening shadows. Warmth rose off the packed earth underfoot. Not holding hands immediately. Steps in, gravel whispering, her fingers grazed his, knuckles light as moth wings. Palm turning up, he received her hand threading through, fingers settling with familiar ease.

“Missed you today,” Percy said.

Squeeze in answer. “Missed you too.”

They walked in the quiet between camp sounds, gravel whispering beneath their steps. Percy told her about the float that crossed the stop mark, how Silena called it and Clarisse waited for the gate to settle before anyone retrieved it. “The fast lane stays closed until both the watcher and the person on the gate say the test is ready. Either one can stop it.”

“Good. What about the ladder?”

“Clear both sides. Rope along the coping, not across the water. We still have to rewrap the grip and get the sign sealed.”

“So you made a safer test, not a finished rig.”

“Exactly.” He glanced at her. “What'd the porch decide?”

“Narrow landing, bench along the wall, separate runoff channel if Chiron and Katie approve the outlet. The old plan is wrong by three inches, and widening the landing eats the rose bed.”

“I suspected the porch.”

“You did not.”

“It looks guilty.”

“I tested the turn with a basket. The bench has to stay shallow so the path to the stairs works.”

“And you're sure?”

“Sure enough to recommend it. Not sure enough to dig without approval.”

“That's a real plan.”

She squeezed his hand once. “So is yours.”

Head to his shoulder, cotton of his shirt sun-warmed and faintly salty against her cheek, she settled; his arm curved around her, familiar and careful, thumb resting lightly on her sleeve seam. They watched campers cross the green, heard a Hermes door slam hollow in the distance, listened to Clarisse order someone not to carry a spear indoors, her bellow fond despite itself. A moth blundered past the brazier flame.

“Tomorrow might want all afternoon with you,” Annabeth murmured, eyes bright despite the gathering dark. “Boring you with porch trivia till you beg for mercy.”

“Tomorrow might want all afternoon at the pool,” Percy countered, grinning. “Dunking till you beg for mercy.”

Lifting her head, eyes narrowing playfully: “Threat?”

“Scheduling possibility.”

“Brave.”

“Have sisters. Accustomed to being outnumbered.”

“Two Cabin Three sisters, plus Thalia defects to their side whenever bossing you appeals — which is frequently,” Annabeth teased, shoulder bumping his. “Poor Percy, gloriously doomed by committee.”

“Doomed, then.”

“Probably.”

Beneath the trees, hands linked, unhurried, leaves whispering overhead and bark rough where Percy's shoulder brushed the trunk. Fireflies stitched the dark between branches. Percy leaned a fraction, asking without words, breath warm in the cool air. Chin lifting, eyes catching lamplight, Annabeth answered. Their kiss stayed warm and gentle — quiet hello after hours in separate corners, honey and salt lingering faintly. His hand rested lightly at her shoulder, thumb against her collarbone. Her fingers curled against his sleeve, fabric soft under her grip. Parting, neither stretched the moment toward tomorrow's promise; they let it rest, complete.

“See you morning?” Percy asked, voice low and hopeful, thumb still tracing her knuckles. “If we both want. Which I will.”

“If we both want.”

“Probably will.”

“Me too.”

One more kiss, brief and certain, then forehead to forehead, breath mingling in the cool air.

From the porch, lamplight spilling gold across the steps, Thalia hollered, cupped hands to mouth, “Annabeth, architecture escaping! Corner's lifting!”

Pulling back, Annabeth said, “Left them on the table.”

“Aware! Saving it!”

“Go,” Percy said. “Porch needs you.”

“And the bench.”

“Especially the bench.”

Squeeze of hands before release. Off she jogged toward her sister, sandals slapping the path, shouting that rolled plans couldn't blow away, laughter bright in her throat. Thalia shouted back she was guarding a corner anyway, arms spread wide over the paper like a gull sheltering chicks.

Watching them go, their figures small against the green's broad gold, then turning Tempest-ward where the hall's damp breath already cooled the path, Percy felt the day settle whole behind his ribs. Their quiet hadn't walled. It had pathed — separate routes rejoining at day's end, footprints converging in dust.
