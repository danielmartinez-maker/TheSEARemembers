## Chapter 174: Everything the Rain Brings In

It rained on Monday the way it had been threatening to rain since the frost: all at once, from a sky that had clearly been holding it in out of politeness.

Percy woke to the sound on the roof of Cabin Three, which was a different sound than rain on any other roof at camp, because Cabin Three's roof was made of green-glazed tile that had been laid by kids who cared, and tile did not drum, tile spoke. He lay in his bunk a while and listened to the whole hall talking, and then he got up, because Monday was Monday, and because Clarisse La Rue considered sleeping in to be a personal insult to the day.

The commons was already awake and already damp. Silena had towels on the floor by the door in a line like stepping stones and a rule about it that nobody had ever been told and everybody followed. Clarisse had two buckets out, one catching a drip from the east window that had been catching that drip since August, and one under the hatch, because the hatch had opinions in weather. Tyson's white rock sat on the corner bunk in its usual place, minding its own business, and somebody had put a folded camp shirt under it so it would not scratch the wood. Nobody mentioned that either.

"Buoys," Clarisse said, by way of good morning. "Chiron wants them in by the middle of the month, which means the rowing part of it is today, because if we wait the lake takes the docks and then we're swimming for hardware in November."

"In the rain," Percy said.

"In the rain. You're a sea kid. You've got a rain exemption."

"I don't have a rain exemption, I have a father."

"Same thing on paper." She was already pulling her jacket on, and she had the look she got the night before a task, which was the look of a person who had slept badly and decided to be useful instead. "Annabeth's coming. She wants the soundings. Silena's doing the pavilion with Katie, so it's you, me, and the girl with the pencil, and if you show up without a coat I'll put you in the lake and claim it was an accident."

Percy showed up with a coat. He showed up with two coats, one of which he did not need, because Silena had handed it to him at the door without comment on the way out, and Silena handing you a thing at a door without comment was how Silena said I love you before nine in the morning.

---

The lake in the rain was the best version of the lake, and only Percy and about four other people at camp seemed to believe that.

It went gray and soft at the same time. The rain came down onto the surface so steadily that the whole sheet of it seemed to be moving and still at once, and the far shore disappeared into a smear of mist with the ridge barely pencilled above it, and the pines on the north side stood in the water's edge looking older than they were. There was no horizon. There was just water going up and becoming weather.

They took the flat-bottomed work boat, the ugly one with the scarred gunwales that Clarisse called the mule and had counted twice, and they went out along the buoy line with a canvas sack of hardware in the bow and the rain running off the brims of their hoods.

Annabeth had a slate she had oiled against the weather and a piece of chalk and a sounding line that she had made herself out of a fishing weight and marked with knots at every fathom, and she sat in the middle of the boat with the slate on her knees and did not once complain about the rain, which was the largest thing Percy had ever known her not to complain about.

"You're enjoying this," he said.

"I'm doing arithmetic," Annabeth said. "The enjoyment is incidental."

"Say it anyway."

"Fine." She looked up, and the rain had got into her eyelashes and she did not wipe it out, and the whole lake was going gray and soft behind her head and her cheeks were pink with the cold and she was, Percy understood with the particular helplessness he had been feeling since June, the most beautiful thing he had ever seen, and she was holding a knotted rope. "It's the first day the lake has belonged to the map instead of to the swimmers. Everything I measure from now until April, nobody will ever wade through. It's all going to be secondhand, and I get to be the one who made it. Yes. I'm enjoying this."

"You're very strange," Percy said.

"I'm very thorough. It's a different thing and it's more useful."

Clarisse, in the stern, said nothing, which was how Clarisse agreed with things she did not want to be seen agreeing with. She brought the boat in against the first buoy with two strokes and no shouting, and Percy leaned out over the cold gray water and hauled the float up by its ring, and the rope came in wet and black and full of lake, and Annabeth wrote the soundings down in small neat chalk numbers while the rain tried to take them off the slate and lost.

They did the line in an hour and a half. Nine buoys. Nine soundings. One buoy whose chain had grown a beard of pondweed that Percy pulled off with his hands while Clarisse told him it was not his business to clean the lake's furniture, and then helped him do it anyway. At the last one, out past the sandbar, the rain thinned all at once and the lake gave one long breath of mist, and for about forty seconds the boundary rock stood clear with its white X and the willows came back out of the gray in a row, and the bottom of the lake showed, far down, brown and gold and perfectly still, the way it must have looked from a boat all summer to people who were not allowed to be in it.

"That's the last one," Annabeth said. She wrote the last number and put the chalk in her pocket and wrapped the slate in the oilcloth with the care of somebody putting a knife to bed. "Nine buoys, four deep, five shallow, all of them where they were in June, which is not what I predicted and I'll be adjusting my method."

"You predicted wrong," Clarisse said.

"I predicted wrong usefully. My wrongness has a shape now." Annabeth tucked the slate under the seat and looked out at the lake, and her shoulders came down a little, which was as close as Annabeth Grace came on a Monday to admitting a thing had been pleasant. "Take us back. I want the numbers on the wall before lunch or they'll sit on the desk for a week and turn into a pile."

They took the boat back to the south dock and tied it off in the rain, and Clarisse went up the hill with the canvas sack over her shoulder and did not help them with the oars, because she had decided on the walk back that she was not going to be seen carrying oars in the rain in October, and because she knew they would do it.

Percy racked the oars. Annabeth shook the water out of the oilcloth and stood under the eave of the canoe shed with her hood down, which she only did when the rain had gone soft enough to be interesting, and looked at the lake with her head tipped, and did not say anything, which meant she was filing something.

He came and stood next to her under the eave, out of the drip, and they watched the gray water the way the two of them watched any water, which was to say without any particular plan, and after a while he took his hand out of his pocket and put it around hers, and her fingers were cold and chalky and closed on his immediately, the way they did, the way that had stopped being an event and had started being the weather.

"You didn't read the note," Annabeth said.

"I didn't write the note," Percy said. "You're the one with the note."

"I'm the one with the note," she agreed. "I was going to read it at breakfast. Then Clarisse needed the boat, and then there was a buoy chain with a beard, and now it's eleven o'clock and I've been carrying it around all day like a person with a library book."

"Read it now."

"Not now."

"Why not now?"

Annabeth looked at the lake and thought about it, which he could tell because she went still in a particular way: not the still of somebody deciding what to say, but the still of somebody measuring a thing they had already decided. "Because the whole morning was work, and I liked the work, and I want to have the note in a different piece of the day," she said at last. "That's all. It's not a mystery. I'm not saving it. I'm just — " she made a small gesture with her free hand, the one that wasn't holding his, opening it and closing it, "-- putting things in order. Work first, then the post, then dinner. It's how I keep a day from turning into confetti."

"I've never once in my life kept a day from turning into confetti."

"I know. It's why I go everywhere with you. Somebody has to know where the days went."

The rain came back then, heavier, shaking the eave, and they stepped in under the shed roof together, which was not much of a step, and which happened to put her shoulder against his arm, and neither of them corrected it.

"Percy," she said, in the new quiet.

"Yeah."

"Last week, after the meal at the north beach, you said something about the pile." She had her eyes on the water and her voice flat and even, the way she read out numbers, and he could hear, under the flatness, the thing she was being even about. "You said nothing that happens gets lost, it goes in the pile, and in February you can go and find it. I've been thinking about that all week and I've decided I have one objection."

"Go ahead."

"The pile doesn't get to have everything," Annabeth said. "I want some of it fresh. I want to be able to take today — the boat, the numbers, the wet oars, you standing under a shed being smug — and carry it around in my pocket and look at it whenever I want, and not have to go into February to go looking for it. That's the objection."

"Okay," Percy said. "So take it."

"That's what I'm saying. I'm taking it." And then she did something he did not expect, which was to turn and put both hands flat on his chest, palms over his coat, and stand there in the doorway of the canoe shed looking up at him with rain running off the roof behind her, and say, in the same even voice she used for soundings, "Which means I'm going to read the note in about four seconds, and the four seconds are going to be slow, and that's all I'm telling you."

He did not say anything. He had learned, over a summer, that the way to be with Annabeth Grace when she was spending something was to hold still and let her spend it.

Four seconds was four seconds. The rain fell off the roof in a line. Somewhere down the lake a gull that should have gone south already gave one flat cry and quit. Then she came up on her toes and kissed him, unhurried, with both hands still flat on his chest, and he put one arm around her back and one hand at the back of her neck under the wet hair and that was the whole of the shelter they had, an eave and a rack of oars and forty feet of gray water, and it was enough, and it lasted long enough that the rain changed twice.

When she stepped back, her face was doing something complicated and she was trying to file it as nothing, and failing, which he enjoyed more than he said out loud.

"There," she said. "That's the day. I've got it. Now dinner."

"Now dinner," Percy agreed, and they walked up the hill in the rain, and she held his hand the whole way, and by the time they got to the cabins the note had been in her fleece pocket for three hours and had not been read once, and she still would not say why, and he had stopped needing her to.

Cabin Three had one law in wet weather, and the law was the towel line.

Silena had laid it down in June after a storm had put four kids in the infirmary with something the Apollo cabin called a complaint and everybody else called being an idiot: towels on the floor inside the door, one per person, no stepping past the towels with wet feet. Two towels if you had been in the lake. Three if you had been in the lake with Clarisse, who did not get out of a boat until she had checked the whole boat, so you could stand in the rain for ten minutes holding an oar while she counted.

By midday the whole commons smelled like wet cotton and lake. Coats hung on the porch rail in a row, dripping onto the boards with a sound like a slow clock. Somebody had dragged the big mat in from the steps and laid it by the stove and somebody else had stood on it, so it was a mess, and Clarisse had opinions about the mat that she was keeping to herself, which was her way of being in a good mood.

Percy came in from the boat shed with his hood down and his hands red with cold and did the towel thing without being told, and then went and stood at the stove for a full minute with his back to the room and let it happen to him, the way you let heat happen to you after water.

"You left the oars out," Clarisse said from the table.

"I racked the oars."

"You racked them wet."

"They were in the lake. There's a whole lake down there. It's basically made of wet." He turned around. "Clarisse, I have a question about the boat."

"No."

"You don't know the question."

"The question was going to be whether we could go out again on Thursday and the answer's no, the buoys are in, there's nothing to go out for." She had the winter list open and was copying again, because she had decided that a list copied once was a draft and a list copied twice was a document. "If you want to be on the water, wait until it freezes and I'll teach you to walk on it."

"That's a different thing."

"It's a better thing. It's got a rope and a spike in it."

Silena was at the end of the table with the bread knife and the whole cold chicken and a bowl of the last of the orchard apples, building the thing she built on wet days, which was a pile of sandwiches so tall that people took two and put one in their pocket. She had a system for it that nobody had ever been able to copy: bread, butter, the thin-cut meat, a slice of apple, honey at the edge where it would not make the middle limp, pepper, close, press, cut on the diagonal, stack, cover with a cloth. She did not look up while she did it and she did not need to.

"Percy, sit down and eat something that isn't a sideways piece of bread."

"I had — "

"You had a corner of the loaf at some point because you always do, and you're going to be hungry in an hour and grumpy at four, and the grumpy at four is mine to manage." She handed him two sandwiches without looking. "Go and sit on the porch and eat them where I don't have to watch you do it standing up."

Percy went and sat on the porch.

The rain had come back, thin and steady, and the camp had gone into its wet Monday shape: nobody outside who could be inside, smoke going up off the pavilion, the archery targets standing alone in the field with their faces wet, the whole lake disappearing and reappearing in sheets behind the pines. Down at the bottom of the hill somebody had put a canoe on the rack crooked and left it, and it was going to drive Clarisse insane by Thursday, and Percy had decided not to mention it because there was a small mean joy in watching her find it herself.

Clarisse and Silena came out after a minute with their own food and sat down on the step below him, and the three of them ate on the porch in the rain without saying anything for a while, which was, Percy had come to understand, the actual sound of a family: not talking, and not having to.

"The list's good," Silena said eventually, around a sandwich. "It's the best list we've had. The greenhouse bank's smart. Nobody ever thinks about the greenhouse in October."

"The greenhouse is a glass box that leaks and the bank's the only thing that ever fixed it." Clarisse chewed. "I put your name on the root cellar."

"You did not."

"I did. Full authority. Chiron approved it, which means Chiron has eaten your apples and has opinions."

Silena's face did something complicated and pleased, and she covered it by eating, which was her whole method. Percy watched her do it and did not say anything about it, and then watched her, thirty seconds later, do the other thing: look up the hill. Just a glance. Up past the climbing wall, to where the forge light would have been in the rain if the forge had been lit during the day, which it was not, and the glance was so short and so ordinary that if a person had not known her for five months they would have filed it as nothing at all.

Percy had known her for five months. He filed it and said nothing, which was, he was learning, the entire discipline of being somebody's little brother.

"You made the tea too sweet last night," he said, instead.

Silena turned her head very slowly and looked at him with an expression of enormous calm.

"I did not."

"The whole cabin smelled like a beehive."

"The whole cabin smells like bread, which is a smell that exists, and if you have something to say about my tea you can say it to my face."

"I've never seen your face from this angle."

"Then don't." She took a bite of her sandwich with the flat menace of a girl who was going to win. "Eat your lunch. You're thirteen years of trouble in a fourteen-year-old coat."

"That's not how numbers work."

"In this cabin it is," said Clarisse, and the three of them sat on the step in the rain and ate the food, and somewhere down the hill a wolf that was not a wolf howled at the mist for no good reason, and the lake went in and out of sight behind the pines, and the day went on being exactly as ordinary as it wanted to be.

An hour later there was a meeting on the porch that lasted four minutes and produced one decision.

Clarisse had written the hall on the bottom of the winter list and she had, in the way of all her useful decisions, decided that the right time to enforce a thing was the moment nobody had time to argue. So she stood at the rail with her arms crossed and said, "Tomorrow. Seven. The hall. Nobody has to like it. Bring a towel and bring the same towel, because I'm not running a laundromat," and then she walked back inside before anyone could answer her, which was the four-minute meeting.

"She wrote 'and whoever' on it," Silena said, to nobody.

"I know."

"She wrote it under the line and then turned the page so we couldn't look at it, and then she told us about it anyway."

"I know," Percy said again. "That's how she says she loves us. If you write it down where people can see, it's not the same."

Silena looked at him for a second, the bread knife still in her hand and rain running off the eave in a line between them, and then she said, in the mildest voice in the world, "You're going to be all right, you know. You're going to be better at this than the rest of us."

"At what?"

"At the boring days," Silena said, and went back inside to finish the sandwiches.

The map wall had a rule above it that Annabeth had not written down anywhere, because writing it down would have made it a policy instead of a law, and the rule was: no guesses.

She stood in front of the wall on Monday afternoon with the oilcloth slate unwrapped on the drafting table and the nine soundings pencilled in down the left margin of the lake sheet, and did the thing she did with new numbers, which was to read them twice and then ignore them for five minutes so that any error would get bored and show itself.

The parlor smelled of wet wool and graphite. Rain went on the screens in long soft hands and off the gutters in one steady rope on the north side, where the downspout had been lashed in June and had held. The whole camp had gone indoors and gone quiet, and a quiet camp in the rain was the best place in the world to draw, because nobody came up the path to tell you anything.

Four deep, five shallow. She ran the numbers again in her head and got the same answer, which was the answer she had not wanted: the lake had not changed depth at all since June. She had predicted eleven inches of silt along the northern third from what the spring had been doing all summer, and there was no silt, and she had written the wrong number in her own field book in August with her own hand and then drawn conclusions on top of it for a month.

She sat with that for a while. It was not a large error and there was no chance at all that it mattered to anybody but her, and it was, she understood clearly, the best thing that had happened to her all week.

Because if she had wanted to be right, she could have been right about nothing and never known it. Nine sounding lines on a slate in the rain was what being right cost, and today it had cost two hours and a wet hood.

She took out her eraser and her rubber shield and lifted the silt shading off the north sheet in a strip about as wide as her thumb, and under it the paper was pale and the contour she had drawn in August sat there like a lie she had told once and then lived with. She redrew it in three minutes flat and pencilled a small note in the margin in her record hand: NO SILT. METHOD SUSPECT. Then, underneath, smaller, because she had been raised by a sister who believed you should be allowed to laugh at your own paperwork: (I WAS WRONG. IT WAS GOOD FOR ME.)

It was noonish when the door banged and Olive Nakamura came in with her hood up and her boots off in her hand and a roll of brown paper under her arm.

"You said Mondays," Olive said.

"I said Mondays."

"I brought my paper. I brought my own paper, so I don't use the wall paper. Because Marcus said wall paper is for wall work, and I'm not on the wall yet."

"Marcus is right, and you're right to say he's right, and you're not on the wall yet." Annabeth pulled the small table away from the window and set the two chairs at it, because the young-mapmaker table was part of the law now and had been since the second week. "Sit. Show me what you've got."

What Olive had was the boathouse, drawn at a scale of one inch to four feet, which was the wrong scale and correctly chosen, because the boathouse was the only building small enough that Olive could walk around it during one afternoon with a tape measure from Silena's sewing basket and a friend to hold the far end. The drawing was good in the places where she had measured and bad in the places where she had guessed, and the bad places were all the places that were hard to reach, which was the most instructive thing about it.

"The roof," Annabeth said.

"I couldn't see the roof from the ground."

"So you drew a guess."

"I drew a *shape*," Olive said, with the enormous dignity of ten.

"You drew a guess wearing a shape's clothes. Look." Annabeth tapped the roof line with her pencil, which was the only pencil that was allowed on the small table, because the rule about pencils on the small table had also become law. "If you had drawn nothing up here, the drawing would be honest and still be useful. There's a whole notation for not knowing a thing. You write UNVERIFIED along the line, and then you're allowed to draw the shape, and everybody who reads it knows exactly what they're standing on. A guess that's labeled is a survey. A guess that isn't is trespassing."

Olive considered this for a long moment, with her elbows on her own paper and her chin on her knuckles, and Annabeth, who had been on the receiving end of that exact sentence at that exact age from Chiron, waited and let it land.

Then Olive wrote UNVERIFIED above the roof and drew the shape anyway, and Annabeth drew a small tick beside it in the margin, which was the highest grade the small table gave.

Marcus arrived twenty minutes later with the weather on him and nothing to say, and took the far chair without being told, and the three of them worked through the wet afternoon: Olive redrawing the boathouse roof, Marcus copying the soundings from the slate onto the fair sheet in a cramped careful hand, Annabeth lettering the lake sheet's margin with the date, the boat, and the weather, because a survey without the weather in it was a claim and a survey with the weather in it was a record. Twice Thalia put her head around the door with her sock feet on the boards and her heel wrapped in a hot cloth, looked at the small table, and went away again without being asked to.

At half past three Thalia came in properly, which was different from the earlier visits, and sat on the edge of the long table with her wrapped heel swinging and a mug in her hand.

"You've got two kids drawing roofs," she said.

"I've got two mapmakers drawing a roof, and one of them has learned the word unverified, which makes this a good Monday."

"Unverified." Thalia tried it out. "That's what I should have written on the whole ridge. Four days of walking and half of what I brought back is a shaped guess."

"Then we'll verify it Saturday." Annabeth set the ruler down. "That's the point of Saturday. You're not allowed to hand me guesses, I'm not allowed to pretend they're numbers, and by dark we'll have one true sheet between us and one more piece of the map that somebody stood on."

"In the mud."

"It rained all day, so yes, in the mud."

"In the mud, in the wet, with a rod." Thalia drank her tea and looked at her sister over the mug, dry and even. "You know what I keep thinking about? The rag. I tied a blue rag to the cairn on the slab and it was raining that afternoon and the rag was the only thing in four days of that ridge that moved. Everything up there is granite and it's been standing there since the world started and it's not going to move for us, and I tied a scrap of Silena's old shirt to it and the wind took it and it did a thing, and that's the only mark I made up there that belonged to me."

"You made a cairn."

"Anybody can stack rock. You stack rock because the manual says stack rock." Thalia pointed her chin at the blank on the wall. "The rag was the part where I said: I was here, and I'll be back, and here's a piece of fabric that says so. That's what a survey is. You go up and you say a thing in the only language a hill understands, and then you come down and you tell people about it, and somebody draws it, and then it's real."

Annabeth was quiet for a moment, because that was the most words Thalia Grace had spent on a feeling in a week and both of them knew it.

"Saturday," she said. "You bring the rope. I'll bring the rag's replacement."

"The rag hasn't fallen off."

"The rag's a scrap of Silena's old shirt, in wind, on a ridge, in October. I'm bringing a replacement."

"Fine," Thalia said, standing up, "but it's staying blue," and went off down the parlor with her mug and one boot on, and Olive Nakamura, who had been drawing her roof with the enormous concentration of a child pretending not to listen, said to her paper, "What color blue?", which nobody answered, and which put a very small and entirely private expression on Annabeth's face for about four seconds.

The note came out at four o'clock, when the rain had settled into a steady drum and the two kids had gone home and Annabeth was alone with the lamp and a fair sheet, and she took it out of her fleece pocket and unfolded it along the creases, and read it standing up, the way you read a thing you have decided not to sit down for.

Percy's handwriting leaned uphill and gave up on the ends of words. There was no heading. There was a small trident in the corner, lopsided and earnest, the way it always was.

*For your pocket. Not the pile.*

*Yesterday you said the pile doesn't get to have everything, and I've been thinking about it, and you're right, so I'm filing an amendment: I want to keep some of it fresh too. Today, for example. The boat was a disaster, the buoy chain had a beard, and you wrote real numbers in the rain with your hood up because you wanted the lake on paper before it went under. I stood in the back of that boat and looked at you and thought: there's my whole life, doing arithmetic in a storm.*

*Here's the thing I actually wanted to say and didn't, because Clarisse was there and she would have charged me per occurrence. You are the only person I've ever met who puts things in order instead of taking them. Everybody else at this camp takes the day and eats it. You save yours. You file it. You're going to be old one time in some house you designed, with a wall full of paper and a drawer full of notes, and you'll know exactly what happened on a Monday in October, and I want to be in the room when you check.*

*So here's the amendment. Fresh, not filed: the way you said four seconds. I'm going to spend the rest of my life trying to get you to count out loud again.*

*— P.*

Annabeth read it twice. Then she read the third sentence of the last paragraph three more times, and by the fourth time her face was doing something she would have hated to be seen doing and there was nobody in the parlor to see it.

She put the note down on the drafting table. She picked it back up. She folded it, and then — which was the thing she would remember about Monday for a long time — she took out a fresh sheet from the fair stack and wrote a note back instead of answering it out loud, because a note could be carried and reread, and because she had learned, this summer, that the family she had was a family that put things in writing, and that this was not weakness, it was infrastructure.

The lettering took her thirty-five minutes, which was too long, and she knew exactly how much of that time she had spent on the first line. It was four words. It went onto the paper in her small upright record hand, the one that did the camp's official marks, the one Chiron would have recognized from across a room.

*You're in the room.*

Under it, because four words alone was a dare and a dare was not a letter, she added: *Four seconds, for the record, was seven. I measured. Don't argue with my method.*

Then she drew the trident back, badly, deliberately, in the corner, so that he would know the loop was closed.

She folded it along the creases, and put it on the corner of the drafting table where he would find it, and then, because she had a whole evening and a wall and no intention of wasting either, she picked up the lamp and carried it to the lake sheet and worked until the rain stopped.

There was a theory in Silena Beauregard's family — mostly her own theory, mostly told to herself — that rain was a recipe and Monday was a good day to use it.

Bread wanted a wet day. That was the whole of it. On a hot day the dough went sour on you while you were looking at it; on a cold wet day it rose slow and even and forgiving, and the cabin filled up with the smell of it, and people came in from the weather and stood around the stove without admitting that was why they had come. Cabin Three on a rainy Monday at five o'clock was, in Silena's personal and unhumble opinion, the best room in the world.

She had the big bowl out on the table and her sleeves rolled and flour in the crease of her wrist. Clarisse was in the big chair with her boots off, going through the ledger-that-wasn't-a-ledger anymore — the loose sheets of the winter list Chiron had given her, which she was copying onto a clean page in her blocky hand because the man's writing was legible but his margins were a scandal. Percy was on the floor with his back against the stove and his feet on the warm stone of the hearth, half asleep, one knee up, holding a mug he had stopped drinking from twenty minutes ago. Annabeth was at the table with a fair sheet and a ruler and her tongue between her teeth, which she would deny.

The two of them had the specific quiet of people who had exchanged something that day and had not told anybody about it. Silena did not need to be told. She had a nose for it the way other people had a nose for smoke. It was in the way Annabeth's pencil kept stopping and starting, and in the way Percy kept looking at the back of her head and then looking away like a man checking that a lamp was still lit.

She punched the dough down, turned it, covered it, and started the second batch. She made two. One for the table, one for the forge.

That was the other thing about rain: the forge ran late. There was always work in the wet, because wet got into everybody's hinges and straps and spear shafts, and the Hephaestus kids did not stop for weather, and Silena had noticed — in June, without meaning to, the way she noticed things — that on rainy days nobody up there ate an actual meal. She had started sending bread up on rainy evenings in July and had never said why and had never been asked.

"You're doing the forge batch," Clarisse said, without looking up from her copying.

"I'm doing the forge batch."

"The forge batch is a supply run and a supply run is a chore and chores go on the rotation."

"It's not a chore, it's baking, and baking has never once been on the rotation because you people eat raw dough like animals and I refuse to be part of it." Silena laid the second loaf on the peel to cool and dusted her hands. "Also I'm going anyway."

Clarisse's pen stopped. She looked up in the flat way she looked up when she was doing arithmetic that had nothing to do with numbers. "You've gone up there Wednesdays and Sundays all summer and now it's Mondays."

"The bread's better on a wet Monday."

"I'm sure it is," said Clarisse, in the voice of a woman who was absolutely sure of no such thing, and went back to her copying, and that was the end of the inquiry, because Clarisse La Rue had a rule about other people's business that she had never written down either, and it was the same as the rule about the boat.

Silena wrapped the loaf in cloth and put it in the flat tin, and put on her coat, and went out into the wet evening with the tin under her arm and her hood up, and did not once say to herself why.

The path to the forge was a long gray shine under the pines. Water came off the branches in irregular handfuls, the way water does when the trees have been holding it and get tired. Halfway up she stepped in a puddle that was deeper than a puddle and soaked her right sock to the ankle, and stood there a moment in the dark being annoyed, and then went on, because the bread was getting cold and because being annoyed at a puddle was, she had decided four years ago, a way of being five years old again for free.

The forge doors stood open the way they always did in rain, because a forge in October was a warm room with a fire in it and a door was a hole you could see out of. The light came out in a long orange plank across the wet ground. Inside there were four of them working and one of them — the smallest, a girl named Neve — sitting on a crate with a chisel and a rag and the entire expression of somebody who had been told to sit down.

The heat hit Silena in the face from ten feet away, the good heat, dry and mineral, the heat that smelled like iron and resin and somebody else's hard day.

"Beauregard," Beckendorf said, from the bench at the back.

He was working on the winter lanterns, the big storm lamps that got hung along the paths from the cabins to the pavilion when the light went early and stayed gone. It was a two-hand job and he had it on the bench in front of him with the glass panel out and the frame clamped, and he had a rag over his shoulder and soot on one knuckle and a pencil behind his ear that he had plainly forgotten about, and his hair was standing up in the heat the way everybody's did in the forge.

"I brought the loaf," Silena said, and put the tin on the end of the bench, out of the sparks, exactly in the spot where she had learned to put it in July. "It's wet out, so it's the kind that holds. I'm not staying, I've got the second batch on the rise."

"You don't have to explain it every time."

"I'm not explaining it, I'm — " She stopped, because she had been about to explain it, and he had known she was about to explain it, and the corner of his mouth had moved. "Fine. I brought the loaf."

"Noted." He set the clamped frame down and wiped his hands on the rag and came over and did the thing he did, which was to look at the loaf for a second like it was a piece of work somebody had handed him, and then open the tin and cut into it with the small knife he kept on the ledge, and hand the first piece to Neve without looking at her, and the second piece to the other boy at the anvil, and only then take a piece himself. Silena had watched him do that with food four times now. He did it without thinking. She had decided, somewhere around the second time, that if he ever did it with her she would have to go outside and stand in the weather for a while.

"The lantern glass," Beckendorf said, around the bread. "You're the one who did the windows in the pavilion. Silena, the amber or the plain?"

Silena blinked. "I did the pavilion curtains. Not the windows."

"You did the pavilion curtains and the two windows by the door, and the plain glass read as cold at night and the amber read as a house." He said it exactly the way he said everything about materials, flat and practical, as if he were reading a gauge. "We've got both. Nine lamps. I've got to hang them this week and I'd rather do it once."

"The amber," Silena said, and then, because that had come out of her mouth much faster and much warmer than she had planned it, and because everyone in the forge was now not watching her, "the amber. It reads as a house. You want to walk to dinner and see the path and think somebody left a light on, not think there's a laboratory on the hill."

"Amber," Beckendorf said, and wrote it in the margin of his bench board in pencil, which meant it was real, which meant it was going to be nine lamps with amber glass hung along the paths in the last week of October by a boy who had asked her opinion once and written down her answer.

Silena stood there with her wet sock and her tin and the second batch rising at home and the heat pouring over her face, and understood, precisely and completely, that she had walked up a hill in the rain to be asked a question about kitchen-glass, and that she had a whole sentence in her throat that she was not going to say, which was: I've been bringing the bread since July and I know you eat it in the order you hand it out, and I know you keep the notes people leave you because there's a wall over the bench with four of them up, and I have never once in my whole life had a reason to be here at this bench and I have been here thirty-one times.

What she said was, "Don't hang them in a line. Stagger them. Two, one, two, one, and put the odd one out at the curve where the path bends, because that's where people feel the dark."

Beckendorf looked at her for a second. Not a long second. Long enough that the fire in the forge moved and the light changed on his face, and long enough that Silena heard her own heart do something unhelpful.

"Staggered," he said. "Left on, right off?"

"Left. Always left. People coming up the hill keep the left and let the right fall away."

"That's not in any manual," he said.

"No," Silena agreed, "but it's true, and you asked." Then she picked up the empty tin and turned around and went out of the forge before she could say anything else, and the rain took her hood and her face in one cold hand, and she stood outside in the dark thirty seconds longer than she needed to, and then went down the hill with the tin swinging and said nothing at all about it, to anybody, for the rest of the night.

---

By nine o'clock the rain had stopped and the whole camp had steamed, which is what a wet camp does in the cold: it puts up mist off every roof and every path and it smells like the inside of a coat.

Clarisse La Rue sat at the long table in Cabin Three with the winter list in front of her and a mug of tea she was not drinking and did the last piece of really useful work of her Monday, which was arithmetic with other people's names on it.

The list had come off Chiron's board and onto her clean page in five columns, one per kid, and under each name she had written what that kid owed the season: canoes, buoys, the pump, the shutters on the west cabins, the greenhouse bank, the hay in the paddock, the firewood stack behind the pavilion. The winter list was a machine and the machine did not care who was tired. Somebody had to hand out the parts.

She did not give any of them the arena. She had noticed that everybody put the arena on the winter list, and that the arena was where the fun went and the fun was a bribe, and she did not bribe her own family with March. The arena went to the Ares cabin, who liked it, and she took the pump and the wood for herself, which is what a person did with the cold jobs, and if anybody asked her why she would say it was because she was better at them, and it would be true and also not the reason.

At the bottom of the page, in the space where the list ran out, she wrote the only line that was not on Chiron's board.

TEMPEST HALL. TUESDAYS + THURSDAYS. 7PM. CABIN THREE. (AND WHOEVER.)

She looked at that for a while.

The lake had closed on Sunday. The lake had been, since June, the place where this cabin put everything it did not know how to say: the float, the dock, the races, the fights that got worked out in canoes where nobody could walk away. Take it out and the cabin lost its second living room, and Clarisse had watched enough families in five years to know what happened when a family lost its second living room in October. People stayed in their bunks. People got quiet. People got through the winter and then did not come back in the spring.

So the winter got a Tuesday and a Thursday and a black-tiled hall full of clean salt water that they already owned and mostly forgot about, and she would drag all of them down there twice a week all the way to March if she had to carry them by the collar.

"You wrote our cabin's name on a hall reservation," Percy said, from the floor. He had woken up at some point and had not moved much past the waking. "In the space where the list runs out."

"It's my list. I can write on the bottom of my own list."

"And whoever." Percy read it again, chin on his knee. "That's the most Clarisse sentence I've ever seen in writing."

"Nobody who lives in this cabin is allowed to swim alone, and nobody who lives in this cabin is going to get through October without it, and a third of the people who wandered into the lake with us this summer don't live in this cabin." She turned the page over so he would stop reading it. "So. And whoever."

"Thalia's going to say it sounds like homework."

"Thalia can say it sounds like homework while she's in the water doing it." Clarisse set her pen down and looked at him properly for the first time that evening, because the second useful thing about Monday at nine o'clock was that it was a good hour to say things in. "You asked me something in September. You asked me to teach you the staying part. I said I'd think about it."

Percy sat up a little. "You did."

"I've been thinking about it for six weeks and I've got one sentence, so I'm going to say it before it turns into a speech." She took the tea up at last and held it in both hands, the way she held the tin cup on the beach, bluff and low. "Staying isn't a feeling. You don't stay because you want to stay. You stay because you showed up on the boring day. Yesterday you slept on a beach, fine, the beach was a whole event, the beach could do the work for you. Today it rained and we pulled nine buoys and you came without being asked and you didn't make it funny, and that's the part that counts, because Tuesday it's going to rain again and the buoys are already in and there's nothing to do and you'll still be here."

"That's a longer sentence than one."

"It's one sentence with things in it." She drank the tea. "Anyway. That's the lesson. There's no certificate. Go to bed."

The screen door banged and Thalia came in out of the mist with her heel wrapped in the cloth and her boots in her hand and her socks wet to the ankle, and looked around the room the way Thalia looked around every room, like a scout doing the count before she would let herself sit down.

"Bread," she said, to the empty board by the stove.

"Gone. It's nine o'clock at night."

"Then Monday is a failure." Thalia sat down at the table, put her wet boots on the floor with a thump, and read Clarisse's list upside down from the wrong side of the table, which meant she had to get up and come around, which she did, without asking, and stood behind Clarisse's shoulder going down the columns with her finger. "Why's the arena not on this."

"Because the arena's a bribe."

"Fair. Why's my name got a rod and a level on it."

"Because you found the spring."

"And why's Olive's name got 'rod holder, Saturday, in the wet,' in your handwriting."

"Because she's ten and she's not carrying a level."

Thalia was quiet a second. Then she said, in a different voice, dry and even, "You've put my sister's survey on your winter list."

"I've put everything on my winter list. That's what the list is. Sit down and drink something, you're dripping on my arithmetic."

Thalia sat down. Silena came in from the washroom with her sleeves down and her hair in a braid and her face still holding whatever it had been holding when she came back from the forge at six, and nobody asked her about it, which was the family rule and the reason the family worked, and she poured tea for all four of them and put the mug in front of Annabeth without being asked, and Annabeth — who had been asleep over the fair sheet at the end of the table with the ruler still in her hand and her cheek on her own forearm, having lost the fight at about half past eight — did not wake up for it.

Clarisse drank her tea and looked down the table at the four of them: Thalia arguing quietly with Silena about whether a wet boot on the hearth was a fire hazard, Silena saying it was a fire hazard and leaving it there anyway; Percy on the floor with his back against the stove, one leg out, watching the end of the table.

He got up after a minute. He did it slowly, and he did it without saying anything, and he took the quilt off the back of the big chair — the wool one, the real one, which had come down to the beach on Saturday and come back up on Sunday in his arms — and he put it over Annabeth's shoulders, and he tucked the end of it in at her elbow so it would stay, and then he stood there a moment with his hand flat on the top of her head, not stroking it, not doing anything, just there, the way you put a hand on a stone that is warm.

Then he came back and sat down on the floor and picked up his mug like nothing had happened.

Nobody said anything. Thalia did not say anything, which for Thalia was a whole speech, and Silena did not say anything and went on pouring, and Clarisse did not say anything, and if asked she would have denied it, but she took the pen up and, under the line about Tempest Hall, in the very last space on the page, she wrote the count.

FIVE.

Then she shut the list and got up and put her mug in the sink, and went to the door to look out at the mist on the lake, which was doing its slow ghost business down the hill, and stood there a minute with her arms crossed because the mist was worth a minute, and because four people were breathing behind her in the warm room and that was the whole of Monday, and it had been a boring day, and every single one of them had shown up.

---

Percy walked her up the path to Cabin One at ten, through mist that had come down off the lake and gone to work on the whole camp.

Annabeth had woken when he touched her shoulder, all at once and with a pencil already in her hand, the way she did, and had looked at the fair sheet, and at the quilt on her shoulders, and at him, and had not said anything about the quilt, and had gathered up her papers and her slate and her ruler with the flat efficiency of a person packing up a workshop, and then had walked out the door into the mist without her hood, because the mist was not rain and Annabeth had standards.

The path up to the Zeus cabin ran through the pines and then out along the grass, and the grass was wet to the ankle and the mist stood in the open places in long soft rooms, so that they walked through one and out into wet clear dark and into another. You could hear the lake but not see it. The lanterns had not gone up on the paths yet — Beckendorf's nine amber lamps were still on the bench — so there was nothing to see by except the smear of the pavilion fire, gone down to coals behind them, and the lamp in Cabin One's window above the hill, which somebody had left on.

"You left your boots by the hearth," Percy said. "They're a fire hazard. Silena's ruling."

"Silena's rulings are for wet boots that belong to other people." Annabeth hiked her slate up under her arm. "Also, they'll be dry by morning, which was the entire point of the fire hazard. You can tell her I said that, and she'll say it's not the same, and I'll agree, and the boots will be dry."

"That's the whole family in four sentences."

"Yes," Annabeth said. "It is."

They went on up. At the last rise, where the path came out of the pines, the mist opened for about twenty feet and the lake showed through below them, all of it, silver-gray and utterly still with the trees standing in it and the boundary rock a soft shape out in the middle with no X visible at all, and they both stopped, because there was no version of either of them that walked past that without stopping.

"Look at it," she said. "It's the same lake and it's a completely different drawing. I don't have a wash that color."

"Then make one."

"I'll make one." She was quiet a second, looking. "It's going to be like this until April. I've never had a thing I could see that far ahead that I was actually looking forward to."

"You're looking forward to a month of fog."

"I'm looking forward to having a month where I know exactly where I'll be standing." She turned and looked at him then, and the mist had put little bright beads of water in her hair. "Percy, the reply's on the corner of the drafting table. At the Big House. Not tonight — the parlor gets locked at ten and I'm not having you climb in a window and turn my note into a crime. Tomorrow, before breakfast."

"You wrote back."

"I wrote back."

"How many words."

"Four," Annabeth said. "And a postscript. And the trident, which I did badly on purpose, so don't stand there looking pleased about it."

"I'm not looking pleased."

"You're a lighthouse," she said. "You've been a lighthouse since the boat."

He kissed her on the last rise above the camp, in the open grass, with the mist moving through on both sides of them and the lake going on being silver down below. She had both arms full of papers and a slate and a ruler, so the kiss was him leaning down and her coming up off her heels into it with her chin and her whole mouth and nothing to hold onto, and it went on long enough that the mist changed hands twice and long enough that she made a small sound into it that she would have denied in a court of law.

When they separated she stayed close, forehead against his jaw, breathing.

"Two minutes," she said. "That's the official measurement. You can file it or you can keep it fresh, but the number's mine and I'm not redoing it."

"Keep it fresh," Percy said.

"Correct answer. Walk me the rest of the way."

Thalia was on the porch of Cabin One with her heel wrapped and her arms crossed and the lamp behind her, and she looked at the two of them coming up the steps with the expression of a border official who had been given no reason to object and resented it.

"Ten twenty," she said.

"Chiron didn't set a curfew."

"Chiron didn't. I did. It's on my list, right under 'hang the lamps.'" Thalia stepped back inside and held the door with one shoulder, and the warm light came out all around her. "Genius, your papers, on the table, and don't leave the slate on the bed, you roll on it and I hear about it for four days."

"Noted."

"Night, Jackson."

"Night."

The door closed, but Annabeth opened it again before Percy reached the path.

"Wait. You forgot the ruler."

"That's yours."

"You carried it all the way up for me. I'm giving it back so you can bring it down tomorrow without bending the paper."

He took it, less because he wanted another errand than because he understood what she was asking. She had both arms full when she kissed him. The ruler would be perfectly safe on her table. She wanted him to have a reason to come back.

"Tomorrow, then," he said.

"After breakfast. And don't put it in your back pocket again. You sat on the last one."

"That was a structural test."

"It failed."

She shut the door before he could argue. He started downhill in the damp, keeping the wooden rule tucked under one arm where it wouldn't catch on his coat. Behind him the cabin's lamp stayed lit while she put her papers away, a small, busy square of light in the mist.

At the fork he met a Hermes camper carrying a lantern and a bundle of dry towels. Percy held the lantern while the boy adjusted the slipping bundle, then took his own hands back when it was secure. The rain had left work for everyone, and it was still leaving more. By the time Percy reached Cabin Three, his ruler was dry and his shoulders were wet. He put the ruler on the shelf beside his boots where he would see it at breakfast.
