## Chapter 35: The Letter

The shoebox had lived under Thalia's bunk for three weeks.

She knew exactly how many letters were inside without opening it because she counted them every night before lights-out, like some other kids counted drachmas or arrowheads or the cracks in the cabin ceiling. Seven. Seven letters from Beryl, all in the same slanted, looping handwriting that got messier toward the bottom of the page, like her mom ran out of patience with neatness before she ran out of things to say.

Thalia hadn't answered any of these seven new letters. They had sent an earlier reply when Beryl first contacted the camp, but that hadn't made the next letters easier.

It wasn't that she didn't want to. She'd started eleven drafts. Annabeth had found three of them crumpled behind the Zeus cabin's trash can and one folded into a paper airplane that Thalia swore she didn't make but absolutely did during a boring orientation lecture on proper harpy deterrents.

"Dramatic," Annabeth had said, unfolding the airplane. "'Dear Mom. Camp is fine. Don't worry. Love, Thalia.' That's it? That's your whole letter?"

"It covers the bases," Thalia had muttered.

Annabeth had stared at her the way only a twelve-year-old sister who thinks she's forty can stare. "You forgot Jason."

"I didn't forget Jason."

"You didn't mention him."

"Because I'd barely figured out how to write to Mom, and I didn't want to get his hopes up about a visit before we could arrange one."

Annabeth had thrown the paper airplane at her head. It had stuck, nose-first, in Thalia's hair.

That was four days ago. Since then, the shoebox had gotten heavier in Thalia's mind, even though she knew, logically, paper didn't gain mass from guilt. She'd carried it around camp in her backpack. She'd shoved it under her pillow. She'd tried to give it to Percy to hold for safekeeping, and Percy — bless him, loyal, sea-brained Percy — had said, "Sure," and then immediately asked, "Wait, what's in it? Is it spiders? If it's spiders I'm going to scream."

It wasn't spiders. It was worse. It was seven unanswered letters and the horrible, sticky feeling that every day she didn't answer, she was proving something unkind about herself.

On Thursday morning after breakfast, Annabeth cornered her behind the Zeus cabin with her hands on her hips and her chin stuck out, which meant she meant business.

"We're doing it today," Annabeth said.

"Doing what today?"

"Writing back. Both of us. Together."

Thalia leaned against the cabin wall and tugged her jacket sleeves down over her hands. It was a cool morning and the air had teeth. The sky above Half-Blood Hill was that brilliant, hard blue that always made her chest ache a little, like looking straight at her dad and getting caught.

"I know we should," Thalia said. "I just — every time I sit down, it sounds stupid."

"So let it sound stupid. First drafts are supposed to sound stupid."

"Says who?"

"Says every writer in history," Annabeth said promptly. "Chiron told me. Athena cabin rule. And you're not allowed to say no because I already told Silena we need the big table in the arts and crafts room and she already put a hold on it and had Beckendorf drag an extra bench over."

Thalia blinked. "You planned a letter-writing party without me?"

"I planned a letter-writing intervention," Annabeth corrected. "There's a difference. One has snacks."

Despite herself, Thalia smiled. "What kind of snacks?"

"Silena said she'd bring strawberry stuff from the Aphrodite cabin stash. And Percy said he'd steal extra blue cookies from the kitchen if you came."

"He bribes me with cookies and it works every time and he knows it," Thalia grumbled, but she was already pushing off the wall. "Fine. Intervention. But if I cry I'm blaming you."

"You won't cry," Annabeth said, with the absolute confidence of someone who had not, in fact, checked with Thalia's tear ducts. "Or if you do, it's okay. I'll pretend I don't see."

Thalia slung an arm around her sister's shoulders and squeezed. Annabeth was getting taller — still small for twelve, still all elbows and knees and flyaway blonde hair she refused to braid properly — but she fit under Thalia's arm like she'd been designed for that spot.

"Lead the way, mastermind," Thalia said.

---

On their way to the arts room, Annabeth stopped at the mail board by the Big House.

"There's something I want to check."

Thalia followed her gaze. The outgoing satchel schedule was posted beneath a notice about the camp's annual supply orders. Letters addressed to the mortal world were collected twice each week. Correspondence to Camp Jupiter followed a different route entirely, one Chiron had been trying to negotiate since the sisters asked him to contact Jason months earlier.

"We can write both letters today," Thalia said. "That doesn't mean they'll leave together."

"I know. I want to make sure we don't write anything we can't actually send."

She found Chiron in his study. He was reading the latest response from an intermediary, and he looked pleased to see the girls arrive together rather than arguing over the doorway.

"What can I do for you?"

"We're going to write to Jason and Mom," Annabeth said. "We need to know which channel can carry each letter, whether anyone will read them first and whether we can ask for them to be returned if delivery isn't possible."

Chiron took off his spectacles. "Those are three sensible questions. The answers differ."

He explained that ordinary mail to Beryl would travel through a mortal address that had already been verified, while Roman correspondence required a request through the camp's authorized intermediary. He could submit it but couldn't promise it would be accepted. If a message was refused, it would be returned unopened. They could choose to keep the contents private from the intermediary, although the address and reason for delivery would necessarily be visible to the courier.

"Does Jason know we're writing?" Thalia asked.

"He knows you want contact. That was established during your Iris-message. I don't know whether this particular letter will reach him."

Annabeth looked at the paper in Chiron's hand. "Then we shouldn't promise him a visit this summer."

"I would advise against promises neither camp has agreed to support."

Thalia nodded. She had spent years wanting to tell Jason that she'd come find him the moment she was old enough. She still wanted to say it. It was harder to accept that promising a visit could make him wait for something she couldn't deliver.

"What about Mom?" she asked. "If we answer, does she get to come here?"

"A letter is not an invitation to visit," Chiron said. "Nor does it change your arrangements at camp. You may say you're glad to hear from her while remaining clear that any visit would need to be discussed separately."

"She's been sober for more than a month," Thalia said.

"That is a real achievement. It is not evidence by itself that every concern has been resolved. Your mother is responsible for her recovery. You are not."

Thalia looked down. Chiron had told her similar things before. She still needed to hear them, which annoyed her.

Annabeth asked whether the letters had to be written together. Chiron looked slightly surprised.

"No. If each of you wants a private reply, you may write separately. If you'd rather write a joint message, that's also your choice."

Thalia turned toward her sister. "I thought you wanted us to write it together."

"I want us to sit down together," Annabeth said. "I don't want to decide what you say to Mom. And I don't want you deciding what I say."

Thalia took a breath. That was different from the plan she'd expected Annabeth to impose after breakfast. Her sister had organized a room full of friends and snacks, but she was leaving the most difficult part to each of them.

"That's fair," Thalia said.

Annabeth nodded. "And the Jason letter?"

"Joint, if you want," Thalia replied. "He should hear from both of us."

They left Chiron with a list of mailing instructions and a better idea of which promises were theirs to make. On the steps, Annabeth hesitated.

"Do you mind everyone being at the table?"

"Sometimes," Thalia admitted. "But I also don't want to sit in the cabin trying to write one sentence while you watch me like a teacher waiting for a test."

"Then we can let the others help with the easy parts. The hard parts belong to us."

Thalia looked at her sister. "You've become very reasonable."

"Don't sound so disappointed."

"I'm suspicious. I'll get used to it."

They continued toward the arts room, carrying the addresses and a few blank pages. Thalia was still nervous. She knew she could tell her friends to leave if she needed privacy, and that made the gathering feel less like an intervention and more like an offer.



---

The arts and crafts room smelled like tempera paint and sawdust and hot glue, and at ten o'clock on a Thursday morning it was empty except for their crew.

Silena Beauregard had claimed the big table in the back by the windows and covered it with a pink tablecloth she must have brought from her cabin. There were stacks of paper — lined, unlined, fancy cream-colored stationery with little Pegasus watermarks in the corner, and one pad of neon green paper that made Thalia's eyes water.

"Options are important," Silena explained when Thalia stared. "The paper sets the tone. Cream says 'I am mature and thoughtful.' Neon green says 'I am fun and possibly unhinged.'"

"I vote unhinged," said Clarisse La Rue from the end of the bench, where she was sharpening a pencil with a hunting knife. Everyone had politely decided not to comment on the knife. "If you're writing to your mom, you might as well be honest."

"Clarisse!" Silena scolded, but she was smiling.

Clarisse shrugged. She'd shown up in full armor except for the helmet, which sat on the table next to a Tupperware of what looked like double-chocolate muffins. Thalia stared at the muffins.

"Did you bake?" she asked.

"No," Clarisse said. "My mom sent them. She sent a note that said 'for your friends, not just you, Clarisse, I mean it.' So I brought them. Don't make it weird."

"That's really sweet," Annabeth said.

"It's logistics," Clarisse said firmly. "If we're going to be here for three hours while you two agonize over commas, we need calories."

Percy Jackson arrived five minutes later, out of breath, with a paper napkin full of blue cookies and a smear of blue frosting on his cheek.

"Got them," he announced triumphantly, sliding onto the bench next to Annabeth. "Mina in the kitchen says hi and also says if Thalia doesn't write her mom back soon, Mina's going to write her herself and tell her Thalia's been eating nothing but pinecones and attitude."

"Tell Mina I eat plenty of attitude," Thalia said, stealing a cookie. "It's low-cal."

"Thalia!" Annabeth protested, but she was laughing.

Grover Underwood trotted in last, his hooves tapping softly on the wooden floor, a battered messenger bag slung across his chest. His old crutches were strapped to the bag for what he called "emotional support"; one bumped the doorframe as he came through, and he ducked back to free it.

"Mail room recon complete," he announced, setting the bag on the table. "Mr. D is in a mood because someone put a 'Pin the Tail on Dionysus' poster in the Big House, so avoid him. The harpies are sorting the morning drop now. If we get letters done by lunch, they'll go out with the afternoon satchel to the mortal post office. After that, nothing till Monday."

"Monday's too late," Annabeth said quickly. "Mom's letters come on Tuesdays. If we miss today, she'll think —" She stopped.

"She'll think we don't want to talk to her," Thalia finished quietly.

Nobody said anything for a second. Silena reached over and squeezed Thalia's wrist. Clarisse suddenly found her pencil-sharpening extremely interesting. Percy nudged Annabeth's foot under the table.

"Okay," Grover said brightly, clapping his hands. "So we write today. I've got stamps, I've got envelopes, I've got the official Camp Half-Blood mail room guide memorized. Rule one: no monsters' names in the address line, it confuses the mortals. Rule two: if you're writing to a mortal parent, keep the god stuff vague. Like, 'archery lessons' not 'archery lessons to defend against hellhounds.'"

"We know, Grover," Annabeth said fondly. "You've told us like six times."

"Seven," Grover said. "This is the seventh time. It's an important rule."

Thalia pulled the shoebox out of her backpack and set it in the middle of the table like an offering. Seven envelopes, already opened, edges soft from rereading. She hadn't let anyone else read them — not even Annabeth, not all the way through — but now, with everyone gathered, the box looked less like a secret and more like a project.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Where do we start?"

Annabeth pulled out her own stack — smaller, just three letters, because Beryl wrote to Thalia more often, longer letters, and they both knew why and didn't talk about it — and laid them side by side.

"We start with Jason," Annabeth declared.

Thalia groaned. "Why Jason first? Jason's harder."

"Jason's easier," Annabeth countered. "Because Jason's ten and he's at Camp Jupiter and he's our brother and we can just tell him stuff. Mom is —" She hesitated. "Mom is Mom."

"Profound," Clarisse said.

"Shut up," Annabeth said without heat. "You know what I mean. With Jason we don't have to explain anything. With Mom we have to explain everything and also nothing."

Silena uncapped a pen. "So Jason first as a warm-up. What's Jason like? I've never met him."

Thalia and Annabeth looked at each other.

"He's..." Thalia started.

"He's very Roman," Annabeth said at the same time.

They both laughed.

"He's very serious," Thalia said. "Like, Annabeth-serious, but without Annabeth's chaos. He irons his tunics. Who irons tunics at camp?"

"He writes lists," Annabeth added. "He numbered his last letter. Point one, point two, point three. Point four was 'tell Thalia her hair looks like a startled porcupine in the photo she sent and I mean that affectionately.'"

"He said that affectionately?" Percy asked, delighted. "I like this kid."

"He's sweet," Thalia said, and her voice went soft in a way that made Silena look up. "He's really sweet. Before he was taken, he used to crawl into my bed during thunderstorms with his red blanket. He was so little he could hardly say my name. Now his letters have Roman emperors in the margins. Nero got a whole paragraph last time because apparently being Roman doesn't mean you have to like all of them."

Annabeth nodded, eyes bright. "He still sends me diagrams. Of the aqueducts at Camp Jupiter. With labels. In Latin."

"Okay, I love him already," Silena declared. "Let's write to Jason."

---

Annabeth had been dreading this and looking forward to this in equal measure for weeks, which was a very Annabeth feeling — two opposite things at full intensity, buzzing in her chest like bees in a jar.

She loved getting letters. She loved Beryl's handwriting, the way her mom dotted her i's with little circles when she was happy and sharp dashes when she was tired. She loved Jason's numbered lists and his terrible drawings of eagles in the margins. She kept every letter in chronological order in a binder with plastic sleeves, because of course she did.

But answering? Answering meant deciding what was true enough to write down. She had no childhood memories of Beryl to lean on. There were Thalia's stories, the letters themselves, and the affection she was building with the woman who called her a brave girl. None of that brought back the missing years.

How did you explain Camp Half-Blood to your mortal mom without scaring her? *Dear Mom, today in Capture the Flag I got speared (non-fatally) by Clarisse and Percy carried me to the infirmary while I yelled strategy at him. Love, Annabeth.* How did you explain that you were happy here, truly happy, without making it sound like you didn't miss home? How did you tell your little brother across the country that you thought about him every night without making him sad?

She'd tried six times. All six drafts were in her binder behind the letters, and they all sounded wrong. Too formal. Too breezy. Too sad. One of them was just a list of facts — *The cabins are arranged in a horseshoe. The lake is blue. The strawberries are good.* — and she'd stared at it and thought, *Who is this for?*

Now, with the big table and the pink tablecloth and her sister across from her chewing on a pen cap, Annabeth felt something loosen.

"Okay," she said, pulling a sheet of cream stationery toward her. "Jason first. Together. We each do a section?"

"Or we do one joint letter?" Thalia suggested. "Like, 'Dear Jason, it's your favorite sisters'?"

"We're his only sisters," Annabeth pointed out.

"Exactly. Favorite by default. It's airtight."

Percy raised his hand. "Can I add a P.S.? I haven't met Jason in this history, but I know we'd get along. I want to tell him about the time Clarisse launched me into the lake with a catapult."

"You tell everyone about that," Clarisse said.

"Because it's a great story!" Percy said. "It's character-building."

"You cried," Clarisse said.

"I had lake water up my nose! Anyone would cry!"

"You two," Silena sighed happily, uncapping a glitter pen. "Okay. Opening line. Ideas?"

"'Dear Jason,'" Annabeth said.

"Too boring," Thalia said. "He gets a hundred letters that start 'Dear Jason.' We need a hook."

"This isn't an essay," Annabeth said.

"Everything's an essay if you try hard enough," Thalia said.

"'Dear Jason, Supreme Praetor of Our Hearts'?" Percy offered.

Thalia pointed at him with her pen. "Yes. That one."

"No!" Annabeth said, but she was giggling. "He'll think we've been possessed. How about 'Dear Jason, Greetings from the Greek side'?"

"Ooh, that's good," Silena said. "Acknowledges the rivalry. Cheeky but respectful."

"It's a letter to her brother, Silena, not a diplomatic summit," Clarisse said.

"It can be both," Silena said primly.

Thalia scribbled *Dear Jason, Greetings from the Greek side (don't barf)* at the top of the page and held it up. Annabeth lunged to grab it.

"Don't barf? Thalia!"

"What? It's funny! He'll laugh!"

"He'll think you're mocking the entire Roman camp!"

"He knows I'm mocking the entire Roman camp! That's our dynamic!"

They wrestled for the paper while Percy narrated like a sports commentator — "And Annabeth goes for the left corner, Thalia defends with the elbow, oh, it's a stalemate, folks" — until Grover intervened, gently extracting the sheet and smoothing it flat.

"How about a compromise?" Grover suggested. "Keep 'Greetings from the Greek side,' lose the barf?"

Thalia and Annabeth stared at each other over the table, breathing hard, hair mussed.

"Fine," Thalia said.

"Fine," Annabeth said.

"Write it neatly," Thalia added. "Your handwriting's better."

Annabeth flushed with pleasure and carefully wrote the line at the top of a fresh sheet. Her letters were small and precise, each one standing up straight like soldiers.

"Okay," she said. "Paragraph one. News. What's happened since we last wrote?"

"Capture the Flag," Percy said immediately. "Tell him about Capture the Flag. Tell him Annabeth took down two Ares kids with nothing but a ladder and spite."

"It was tactics," Annabeth said, but she was grinning. "And a ladder."

"Tell him about the chariot race," Thalia said. "Tell him Clarisse won and Percy came in second because his chariot fell apart and he had to run the last lap carrying a wheel."

"I finished!" Percy protested. "That's what matters! Endurance!"

"Tell him about Grover's music," Silena said softly. "Tell him Grover played for the dryads last Friday and the whole grove went quiet."

Grover ducked his head, ears pink. "It wasn't that good."

"It was that good," Silena said firmly. "Jason should know good things are happening here."

Annabeth wrote, tongue poking out of the corner of her mouth in concentration: *We had Capture the Flag last Friday and Thalia led the defense and nobody got past her. Percy says hi and says Romans wish they had our lake. (His words, not mine, please don't declare war.)*

"Hey!" Percy said, reading over her shoulder. "I didn't say that! Okay, I thought it, but I didn't say it."

"I'm paraphrasing for diplomacy," Annabeth said.

Thalia leaned in. "Add the part about the ladder."

"I'm getting there!" Annabeth said. "You do paragraph two."

Thalia took the pen and stared at the paper. The silliness drained out of her face, replaced by something focused and fragile.

"What do I say?" she murmured. "I haven't written him in a month."

"Just tell him the truth," Percy said quietly. "Tell him you miss him."

Thalia glanced at him. Percy wasn't joking now. He had cookie crumbs on his shirt and lake water still in his hair from morning swim, and he looked at her with those earnest green eyes like missing someone was the simplest thing in the world to admit.

"Okay," Thalia said. She wrote slowly, her handwriting big and slanted: *I miss you, Jase. I miss your dumb lists and your dumb diagrams. Annabeth showed me the aqueduct one and I put it on our wall. Everyone thinks it's cool even if they pretend Romans are weird.*

Annabeth read it and her throat tightened. "That's good," she whispered.

"Yeah?" Thalia said.

"Yeah."

They passed the letter back and forth for the next twenty minutes, arguing over every sentence.

"No, we can't say 'the food is okay,' Mom from the Demeter cabin will cry," Silena said. "Say 'the strawberries are amazing and the rest is edible.'"

"Can we say Clarisse is our friend?" Annabeth asked. "Will Jason know who that is?"

"Say 'Clarisse (Poseidon, scary, secretly nice, do not arm-wrestle her),'" Thalia dictated. "Parentheses are key."

"Do not tell him I cried about the catapult," Percy said.

"I'm telling him you cried about the catapult," Thalia said, writing it in.

"Thalia!"

"It's character development," Thalia said piously. "Jason values growth."

Clarisse snorted so hard she almost inhaled her muffin. "I like you, Grace. You're mean."

"Thank you," Thalia said. "It's a gift."

By the time they'd filled two pages, the letter looked like a battlefield — cross-outs, arrows, Silena's glitter stars in the margins, a small sketch Percy had added of himself waving from a sinking chariot with the caption *still finished!* Grover had contributed a pressed leaf from the grove and a careful note in his round handwriting: *P.S. from Grover — your sisters are brave and kind and everyone here looks out for them. Including me. — G.*

Annabeth read the whole thing aloud, stumbling over Thalia's handwriting in places, and when she got to Grover's P.S., Thalia reached over and squeezed Grover's hand hard.

"That's perfect," Thalia said hoarsely. "Thank you."

"Of course," Grover said, like it was nothing, like he hadn't just said the thing both sisters needed to hear their brother hear.

Silena dabbed at her eyes. "Don't mind me. I'm just emotional. It's the glitter fumes."

"It's a good letter," Clarisse said gruffly. "Jason's going to love it. Seal it before you overthink it."

Annabeth folded the pages with shaking hands and slid them into an envelope. Thalia wrote *JASON GRACE — CAMP JUPITER — DO NOT LOSE THIS OR ELSE* on the front in huge letters, and Annabeth added underneath in smaller script, *Please forward. Thank you! — A.G.*

"One down," Thalia said. "One to go."

The table went quiet.

Because Jason was the warm-up. Everyone knew it. Jason was easy — Jason was lists and aqueducts and *I miss you* scribbled without overthinking. Mom was different.

---

Thalia stared at the blank sheet of cream stationery like it might bite her.

She could hear the clock ticking in the arts and crafts room. Someone had left a half-finished clay hydra on the drying rack and its lopsided heads stared at her judgmentally. Outside the windows, the camp hummed along — sword practice, laughter from the volleyball court, the distant *thwip-thwip* of arrows.

"Okay," she said. "Mom."

Annabeth didn't say anything. She just pulled her chair closer until their shoulders touched, and uncapped her own pen.

"What do we even say?" Thalia asked the table at large. "Last time she wrote, she said —" She flipped open the most recent letter. Beryl's handwriting, tired but warm: *My brave girls, I saw lightning over the city last night and thought of you both. Are you eating enough? Are you sleeping? Thalia, are you wearing that jacket I sent? Annabeth, are you reading too late again? I love you more than all the stars, even the ones you can name. Love, Mom.*

Thalia's eyes burned. She blinked hard.

"She always asks if we're eating enough," Annabeth said softly. "Like that's the main thing."

"She worries," Thalia said. "She tries not to, but she does."

"So tell her we're eating enough," Percy said practically. "Tell her about the blue cookies. Moms like details."

"Tell her about the good stuff," Silena agreed. "Not just 'we're fine.' Tell her what fine looks like."

Thalia picked up her pen. Put it down. Picked it up again.

"I don't know how to start," she admitted. "Every time I try, it sounds like I'm writing a report. 'Dear Mom. Today we did spear drills. The weather is nice. Sincerely, Your Daughter.'"

"So don't start with news," Grover suggested. "Start with something true. Like, what's the first thing you think when you read her letters?"

Thalia looked down at Beryl's letter. The paper was soft at the creases. There was a coffee ring on the corner and a smudge that might have been mascara or paint. Beryl painted whenever she could find work, and her letters often smelled faintly of turpentine, even after weeks in a shoebox. Thalia could never tell from the paint on an envelope whether Beryl was doing better or simply trying to sound as if she were.

"The first thing I think," Thalia said slowly, "is that I can hear her voice. Like, I read it and she's right there. Saying 'my brave girls' in that dumb dramatic way she does."

Annabeth nodded. "And she always underlines 'brave.' Like three times."

"Because she thinks we need reminding," Thalia said.

"Do we?" Percy asked.

Thalia considered. "Sometimes."

Annabeth wrote at the top of her own sheet: *Dear Mom,* and then stopped and looked at Thalia.

"Together?" she said. "Like, we write it together-together? Not sections?"

Thalia swallowed. "Yeah. Together-together. You start a sentence, I finish it? Like we used to do for birthday cards?"

Annabeth's face lit up. "Tell me how it went. I can help finish it this time."

"We wrote 'Happy birthday, you old —'" Thalia started.

"'—magnificent genius, please don't ground us'?" Annabeth guessed. Thalia snorted. Close enough. They both cracked up.

"Okay," Silena said, clapping. "That's the energy. Birthday-card energy. Go."

Annabeth wrote: *Dear Mom,*

Thalia leaned over and added, in bigger letters: *Hi! It's us! Your favorite daughters!*

"Thalia, we're her only daughters," Annabeth said.

"It's a theme today," Thalia said. "Go with it."

Annabeth rolled her eyes but kept writing: *We're writing this together at the big table in arts and crafts with our friends. Silena picked the paper (cream, very fancy) and Percy brought blue cookies and Clarisse brought muffins and Grover is here to make sure we actually mail it.*

"Hey, I do more than that," Grover protested.

"It's true, though," Percy said. "You're the mail enforcer."

"I accept this title," Grover said after a moment. "Mail Enforcer. It sounds cool."

Thalia took the pen: *Camp is good. Like, actually good, not just 'don't worry about us' good. I'm on defense for Capture the Flag and I haven't lost anyone past my line in three games. Annabeth is basically running the strategy for half the camp and Chiron says her plans are scary, which is a compliment.*

"Chiron did not say scary," Annabeth said.

"He said 'formidable,' which means scary but polite," Thalia said.

Annabeth laughed and took the pen back: *I wish I could be there for your pancakes (Thalia says they're always a little burnt on one side), and I miss Jason even though he's far too. Thalia tells me about the apartment smelling like paint. Your letters make it feel closer. But I like it here too. I like that the sky is so big. I like that Thalia sings in the shower even though she's terrible at it.*

"I am not terrible!" Thalia said.

"You are terrible," Annabeth, Percy, Clarisse, Silena, and Grover said in unison.

Thalia pressed a hand to her chest. "Betrayed by my own intervention."

"Keep writing," Silena said, wiping glitter off her fingers. "This is good. This is real."

They kept going, line by line, trading the pen until both their hands were ink-smudged. They argued over wording constantly.

"We can't say 'monsters are everywhere,' she'll freak out," Annabeth said.

"But monsters ARE everywhere," Thalia said. "That's just facts."

"Say 'we have good teachers who keep us safe,'" Grover suggested. "True and reassuring."

"We can't say 'Zeus cabin is lonely with just us,'" Thalia said quietly. "She'll feel guilty."

Annabeth bit her lip. "But it is lonely sometimes."

"Say 'we like having our own space but we wish Jason could visit,'" Silena offered. "True, but hopeful."

"Silena, are you sure you're not a daughter of Athena?" Percy asked.

"I'm a daughter of common sense," Silena said. "Which is rarer."

They wrote about the lake. They wrote about the strawberries. They wrote about Mina in the kitchen who slipped them extra bacon, and Beckendorf who fixed Thalia's shield strap without being asked, and the dryads who braided Annabeth's hair with leaves when she fell asleep under a tree reading.

They wrote: *We're okay. We're more than okay. We're learning stuff. We're making friends. You'd like them. Mom, you'd LOVE Silena. She knows everything about colors and she says your paintings have the best blues.*

Silena made a small, overwhelmed sound. "Stop, I'm going to cry."

"You're already crying," Clarisse observed.

"I'm hydrating my eyes," Silena said with dignity. "It's skincare."

Percy contributed a diagram of the best hiding spots for Capture the Flag, labeled *TOP SECRET — FOR MOM'S EYES ONLY.* Clarisse, after much grumbling, dictated a single sentence: *Your daughters are tough and they watch each other's backs. — C.* Thalia stared at her.

"That's... really nice, Clarisse."

"Don't make it weird," Clarisse said, ears red. "Just write it down."

Annabeth wrote it down, carefully, in her neatest handwriting.

Grover added another P.S.: *Mrs. Grace — they are doing wonderfully. You raised brave girls. — Grover Underwood, satyr, friend.*

Thalia read that line three times and had to look away out the window for a minute, blinking fast. Annabeth pretended to be very interested in capping and uncapping her pen.

"Okay," Thalia said finally. "Okay. We need an ending. How do we end it?"

"'Love, Thalia and Annabeth'?" Percy suggested.

"Too simple," Thalia said. "It needs... I don't know. Something that sounds like us."

Annabeth thought for a moment, then wrote, slowly: *We love you more than all the stars, even the ones we can name. Love, your brave girls, Thalia and Annabeth.*

Thalia stared at the line. It was Beryl's line, thrown back at her like a ball. For a second her chest hurt so much she couldn't breathe.

"That's perfect," she whispered. "That's — yeah."

Silena was openly crying now. Clarisse handed her a napkin without looking, because Clarisse was also blinking very fast and would deny it later. Percy put his head down on the table.

"Guys," Percy mumbled. "You're going to make me cry and then I'll get cookie crumbs in my eyes and it'll burn."

Grover patted his back. "It's okay to cry, Percy."

"I'm not crying! I have crumbs in my eyes!"

Thalia laughed — wet, shaky, real — and picked up the pen to sign her name with a flourish, adding a tiny lightning bolt after the *a*. Annabeth signed underneath, adding a tiny owl.

They sat back and looked at the letter. Three pages, ink-smudged, glittery in one corner where Silena had gotten overenthusiastic, with Percy's diagram on the back and a cookie crumb stuck to page two.

"It's messy," Annabeth said.

"It's us," Thalia said.

---

The letters were done, but Thalia wasn't done.

Annabeth could tell because Thalia kept fiddling with the shoebox, flipping the lid open and shut, not looking at anyone. The happy, floaty feeling from finishing started to curdle around the edges.

"What?" Annabeth asked quietly, so only Thalia could hear. "What's wrong?"

Thalia glanced at the others — Percy was trying to get the cookie crumb off page two without tearing it, Silena was addressing the envelopes in her perfect calligraphy, Clarisse was pretending not to listen while listening intently — and then she pulled something from the very bottom of the shoebox.

A letter. Older than the others. The paper yellowed, the creases worn white, the ink faded to brown.

"What's that?" Annabeth asked. She didn't recognize the envelope. No stamp, and its softened folds looked older than the letters she had received herself. Thalia would have to tell her where it belonged.

"It's the one she sent right after..." Thalia hesitated. "After Dad — after Zeus left. When I was eight and you were four and Jason was two. She was... she wasn't doing well. She was sad all the time. And she wrote this, and I... I've never read it out loud. I've read it like a hundred times in my head, but never out loud."

Annabeth's stomach dropped. "Is it sad?"

Thalia nodded, eyes bright. "Yeah. It's really sad. She talks about how the apartment is too quiet and how she doesn't know how to be a mom and a... and everything. And how she looks at us sleeping and feels like she's failing us."

"Oh," Annabeth said softly.

"I used to read it when I missed her and it would make me cry," Thalia said. "Every time. And I kept thinking, if I read it enough, it would stop hurting. But it never did."

Percy had gone still. Silena had stopped writing. Grover set down his stamps. Clarisse put down her knife. Everyone was listening now, but gently, without staring.

"Do you want to read it now?" Annabeth asked. "Out loud?"

Thalia looked at the letter for a long time. Her hands trembled.

"I think..." she said. "I think I want to. Because I'm tired of it making me cry. I want to see if — if it sounds different now. With you all here."

Annabeth moved her chair even closer and put her head on Thalia's shoulder. "Okay," she said. "We're here."

Thalia unfolded the letter. The paper crackled. She cleared her throat.

"'My darling Thalia,'" she read, voice shaking. "'You are eight today and you asked me why the sky is crying...'" She paused, swallowed. "'And I didn't know what to tell you, so I told you the sky misses your father, and you said, that's stupid, Mom, the sky IS our father, and I laughed until I cried...'"

Her voice cracked. Annabeth tightened her grip on her arm.

Thalia kept reading. "'Annabeth is four and she read a whole book by herself today, a big one, with no pictures, and she came to me so proud and I was too tired to be as proud as she deserved, and I will never forgive myself for that...'"

"She forgave herself," Annabeth whispered. "She wrote about it in one of my letters. She said that day taught her to pay attention."

Thalia glanced at her, surprised, then continued: "'Jason is teething and screaming and I walked around the block at 2 a.m. with him in my arms because it was the only thing that worked, and Mrs. Hernandez from 4B opened her door and said, honey, you look like hell, give me the baby, and I almost kissed her...'"

Percy snorted. Thalia looked up, startled.

"Sorry," Percy said quickly. "It's just — Mrs. Hernandez sounds awesome."

"She was," Thalia said, and smiled a little. "She gave us flan every Sunday."

She kept reading: "'I don't know what I'm doing, baby. I love you so much it scares me. You kids are the only magic I have left and I'm afraid I'm going to mess you up because I'm messy and sad and I paint storms when I should paint sunshine...'"

Thalia's voice wobbled. She took a breath.

"'P.S. Thalia, please stop putting mehr—'" She squinted. "'Please stop putting... what is this word?'"

Annabeth leaned in. "Let me see." She peered at the faded ink. "'Please stop putting mayonnaise on your waffles. It's disgusting and I love you but I will disown you.'"

There was a beat of silence.

Then Percy burst out laughing. "Mayonnaise on waffles? Thalia! That's criminal!"

"I was EIGHT!" Thalia protested, but she was laughing too, laughing through tears. "I thought it was just white syrup!"

"White syrup?" Clarisse repeated, horrified. "Grace, that's — that's a war crime."

"Oh, gods," Silena gasped, clutching her sides. "Mayonnaise! On WAFFLES!"

Grover was bleating with laughter. Annabeth was laughing so hard she slid off her chair onto the floor, dragging Thalia with her, and they ended up in a heap under the table, shoulders shaking, the old letter clutched between them.

"It gets worse," Thalia wheezed, wiping her eyes. "'P.P.S. Annabeth, stop teaching Jason to say 'thunderbutt.' He said it to the pediatrician.'"

"THUNDERBUTT?" Percy howled. "Annabeth! You were FOUR and you were already corrupting your brother!"

"Apparently I was a visionary!" Annabeth protested, laughing so hard her stomach hurt. "Thunderbutt is a great word!"

"Say it to Chiron," Clarisse challenged. "I dare you."

"I will!" Annabeth said. "I'll say it in my next essay! 'Dear Chiron, thunderbutt is a valid tactical term.'"

They laughed until they cried, until their sides ached, until the old, sad letter was damp with tears but the tears felt different — lighter, fizzier, like soda instead of rain.

Thalia held the letter to her chest and leaned back against the bench, breathing hard, smiling so wide her cheeks hurt.

"I didn't only cry," she said wonderingly. "I mean, I cried, but I laughed too. For the first time. It was sad, but it was also... funny. She was funny. Even when she was sad, she was funny."

"She's still funny," Annabeth said, crawling back onto the bench and resting her head on Thalia's shoulder again. "Her last letter had that part about the pigeon that stole her paintbrush."

"And she chased it three blocks," Thalia said. "In slippers."

"In the rain," Annabeth added.

"Iconic," Silena declared. "Beryl Grace has an excellent sense of humor when she wants to use it."

Thalia folded the old letter carefully and tucked it back into the shoebox — not at the bottom, this time, but on top, like it didn't have to hide anymore.

"Thank you," she said quietly, looking around the table. "For... laughing with me. I thought if I read it, everyone would feel sorry for me. But this was better."

"We're not sorry," Clarisse said bluntly. "Your mom sounds cool. Messy, but cool."

"Messy but cool is the highest compliment Clarisse gives," Percy translated. "It means she likes you."

"I know what it means, Percy," Thalia said, but her eyes were soft.

Annabeth picked up the two sealed envelopes — one to Jason, fat with pages and Grover's leaf, one to Beryl, even fatter — and held them like treasure.

"Before we go," Thalia said, "I want one thing clear."

Annabeth lowered the envelopes. Silena stopped arranging the pens.

"We're writing because we want to," Thalia said. "Not because Mom did thirty-eight days and that means everything's different. If she writes back and she's doing badly again, we don't have to drop everything."

Nobody answered immediately. They had spent the afternoon laughing at Beryl's old letter, and Thalia felt as if she had insulted the person who'd written it.

Percy started to speak, then looked at Annabeth and stayed quiet. That was probably the most useful thing he could have done.

"I know," Annabeth said. "I want her to get better. I don't want to have to check on her every morning."

"I used to check every morning," Thalia said. "Even after we left. I'd hear a bottle roll across a floor and think I'd missed something."

Annabeth leaned against the table. "I can't remember those mornings. I believe you when you tell me about them."

Thalia looked at the envelope in her sister's hands. She hadn't asked to be believed. Not out loud. But there were worse things than hearing it anyway.

"We can still send this," Annabeth said. "And if there's a next letter, we can decide then whether to answer it. Together, or separately. However you want."

"Separately is allowed?" Thalia asked.

"Obviously. You're not allowed to edit my spelling if we're writing separately."

"I have never edited your spelling."

"You crossed out 'definately' on my shopping list."

"That word deserved it."

Silena laughed and turned back to the envelopes. Clarisse picked up the pile of unused stationery without making a comment. Thalia could see that she was trying, very hard, not to look tender about any of this.

Thalia put the old shoebox away. It would be under her bunk when she returned. The letters inside would still contain the years she had spent holding the household together when their mother could not. A funny story didn't remove them.

She could send a new letter anyway.

"Mail room?" she said.

"Mail room," Thalia agreed.

---

Before they could seal the letters, Silena took a closer look at the envelopes. She had spent enough afternoons helping with camp correspondence to know that a letter could be beautifully written and still end up in the wrong collection because somebody had assumed the postmaster would recognize the destination.

"Which one is going to the mortal address?" she asked.

Thalia indicated the cream envelope. "Mom."

"And the Roman route?"

"The heavier one. Chiron gave us a routing slip."

Silena spread the papers across an empty table. The request to contact Jason had two signatures, one from each sister. Chiron's acknowledgment was clipped to the back. That meant the letter could be submitted for consideration by the intermediary, not that a Roman courier had agreed to carry it.

"Then we're not going to tell Jason we'll see him next week," Silena said, reading the draft.

Annabeth looked up sharply. "We didn't."

"You wrote *soon*."

"That's not a date."

"To a ten-year-old waiting for news from his sisters, it might sound like one."

Annabeth looked at the page. She had meant soon as a promise that she would keep trying. She hadn't thought about how a younger child might understand it when there were adults between them and every piece of communication.

"What should I write instead?"

"Something true. That you want to visit when the camps approve it, and that you'll keep writing whether or not the visit happens quickly."

Thalia watched her sister erase the word. Annabeth was usually the person reminding everyone to distinguish a wish from a schedule. It was comforting, in a strange way, to see that she could miss the distinction when the wish mattered enough.

They changed the sentence. It now said that they hoped to see Jason when an authorized visit could be arranged, and would write again regardless of the answer. Thalia added a line about the map he had shown them during the Iris-message, asking whether he'd changed the western aqueduct route since the last drawing.

"That's the part he'll answer," Annabeth said.

"He likes correcting things."

"So do you."

Thalia smiled. She could imagine Jason at a desk in a Roman barracks, writing a careful answer with a ruler beside his paper. She didn't know whether that was the room he actually used. She was learning not to turn every guess into a memory.

Clarisse came back from fetching more envelopes and saw the two girls rewriting the last paragraph.

"I thought we were finished," she said.

"We are," Thalia answered. "We just made one part more accurate."

"That sounds like something Chiron would say."

"It was Silena."

"Same disease."

Silena took the joke without looking up. She had spotted another problem. The Beryl envelope contained a page written by both sisters, but Annabeth had added a personal note she didn't necessarily want anyone else to see.

"Do you want that included with the joint letter?" Silena asked her quietly.

Annabeth hesitated. She wanted to tell her mother about a book she'd finished and the way she'd been rebuilding the Parthenon model. She didn't want Thalia to feel obliged to approve every line. Months earlier they'd agreed they could each write personal replies. Today's gathering had become so communal that she'd almost forgotten the boundary.

"Could I send it separately?" she asked.

"Of course."

Thalia looked up. "I thought we were doing one letter."

"We are. I also have something I'd like to send myself."

The older girl's face tightened, then relaxed. It wasn't an easy habit to lose, expecting a younger sister to tell her everything before letting it go into the world. She had made the same mistake when Beryl's first letter arrived.

"All right," she said. "I'm not reading it."

Annabeth smiled. "Thank you."

Silena wrote the address on a second envelope and checked it with the camp's verified record. She could hear Clarisse and Percy arguing about whether the sticker for Jason's letter should be a lightning bolt or an eagle. It was a ridiculous argument, exactly the sort of thing the afternoon was supposed to make possible. But it didn't erase the more difficult decision the sisters were making: to send words they couldn't control once they left camp.

Grover brought the final mailing rules from the post office. He had written them on a card so nobody would have to memorize the difference between the satchel for mortal addresses and the request packet for Roman correspondence.

"Which one gets a stamp?" Percy asked.

"Both, but the Roman request needs Chiron's seal. It doesn't go through the same post office. I checked."

"Good," Silena said. "We should make a copy of what we send, then keep it somewhere the sisters choose, not in the public cabin file."

Thalia was wary. "Why a copy?"

"In case a letter gets lost or returned. You can decide whether to resend it without having to write every sentence from scratch."

Annabeth thought that was sensible. Thalia was less convinced. She disliked the idea of keeping the words in a box where she would read them over and over while she waited for an answer.

"Then I'll keep mine sealed," Thalia said. "If it comes back, I'll decide whether to look at it again."

They made the copies. Annabeth put hers in a labeled folder. Thalia folded hers into an envelope and wrote *only open if the original is returned* on the front. Silena did not try to persuade them to use the same system.

At the mail room, Marge checked the address on each envelope and explained which dispatch would carry it. The mortal letter was ready for that afternoon. The Roman request would be forwarded to Chiron's contact, and it might be days or weeks before they knew whether the channel had accepted it.

Thalia listened to the explanation twice. When Marge stamped the receipt, the sound startled her more than it should have. She had wanted to write for so long that actually sending something felt like giving away control of it.

She took the receipt. Annabeth held the copied address list, careful not to smudge the ink. They had made the decisions they could make. The answer, if there was one, would belong to someone else.



---

The walk to the mail room was a parade.

Percy carried the Jason letter over his head like an Olympic torch. Clarisse carried the Beryl letter tucked inside her breastplate "for safekeeping," which meant no harpy, god, or idiot would dare touch it. Silena carried the shoebox, because Thalia's hands were shaking and Silena just took it without asking. Grover led the way with his Mail Enforcer badge — a sticky note that said *MAIL ENFORCER* stuck to his shirt — and Annabeth walked next to Thalia, swinging their joined hands.

It was lunchtime and camp was bustling. Kids stared as they marched past the cabins, past the dining pavilion, toward the small stone building near the Big House with *CAMP HALF-BLOOD POST* painted over the door in flaking gold letters.

"What's all this?" Beckendorf called from the forge, wiping sweat off his forehead.

"Letter intervention!" Percy shouted back. "We did it! They wrote back!"

Beckendorf whooped. A couple of Hephaestus kids cheered. Someone started chanting "MAIL! MAIL! MAIL!" and by the time they reached the post building, they had a small crowd.

Inside, it was dim and smelled like feathers and old paper. Three harpies perched behind a long counter, sorting envelopes with terrifying efficiency, beaks clicking. The head harpy — an ancient, grizzled creature with one cloudy eye and a nametag that said *Marge* — looked up as they entered.

"Well?" Marge croaked. "You got something or you just here to gawk?"

Grover stepped forward, suddenly all business. He took the envelopes from Percy and Clarisse and laid them on the counter with a flourish.

"Two outgoing," he said. "One to Camp Jupiter, via mortal post plus divine relay. One to Manhattan, mortal post, priority. Both sealed, stamped, and scented against monster interception." He tapped the small sachets of lavender Silena had tucked into each envelope. "Lavender. Monsters hate lavender."

Marge examined the envelopes, turning them over with one talon. She sniffed the Jason letter. Sniffed the Beryl letter. Grunted.

"Grace girls?" she asked, peering at Thalia and Annabeth.

"Yes, ma'am," Thalia said, suddenly nervous.

Marge grunted again. "Your mother writes a lot. Asks about you every time she sends one. Says 'tell my girls the pigeon came back.'" She stamped both envelopes with a heavy *THUNK*. "Good girls, writing back. Mothers worry."

Thalia's throat closed. "She — she asks about us? At the post office?"

"Every Tuesday," Marge said. "Brings the letters herself. Wears paint on her hands. Nice lady. Worries too much." She shoved the envelopes into a canvas satchel marked *OUTGOING — MORTAL*. "They'll go out this afternoon. Anything else?"

Thalia shook her head, unable to speak. Annabeth stepped forward.

"Thank you," Annabeth said earnestly. "Thank you for taking care of her letters."

Marge blinked her cloudy eye. "Eh," she said, but she sounded pleased. "Go on, get out. You're letting the cool air out."

They stumbled back into the sunlight, blinking. The crowd had dispersed, but Beckendorf gave them a thumbs-up from across the yard. Percy was already talking about lunch — "We deserve extra dessert, this was emotional labor" — and Clarisse was threatening to stab him if he took her muffin, and Silena was hugging the shoebox, and Grover was peeling off his Mail Enforcer sticky note and presenting it to Thalia.

"For you," he said. "For being brave."

Thalia took it and stuck it to her jacket. "Mail Enforcer in training?"

"Mail Enforcer emeritus," Grover corrected. "Retired with honors."

Annabeth tugged Thalia's sleeve. "Come on," she said. "Let's go sit by the lake for a minute. Just us?"

Thalia nodded. They told the others they'd meet them at lunch, and Percy promised to save them seats and "not eat all the cookies, just most of them," and Silena blew them kisses, and Clarisse grunted something that was probably affectionate, and Grover unstrapped one crutch and waved it in salute.

The lake was glassy and blue, ringed with cool gold light. Thalia and Annabeth sat on the dock with their feet dangling over the water, shoulders touching, the shoebox of old letters between them.

For a while they didn't say anything. Just breathed. Just watched the water ripple.

"Do you feel lighter?" Annabeth asked finally, in a small voice.

Thalia considered. She thought about the shoebox, still holding the seven letters and the old mayonnaise letter, but no longer a box of unanswered questions. She thought about Beryl at the post office every Tuesday with paint on her hands, asking about her girls. She thought about Jason opening their letter in California, reading about ladders and catapults and *we miss you, Jase.* She thought about laughing under the arts and crafts table until her stomach hurt, and Marge's stamp going *THUNK*, and Percy's diagram, and Clarisse's one gruff sentence, and Silena's glitter stars, and Grover's careful P.S.

"Yeah," she said. "I do. Like... like I put down something heavy I didn't know I was carrying."

Annabeth nodded. "Me too. I kept thinking if I wrote the perfect letter, everything would be perfect. But messy was better. Messy was us."

"Messy but cool," Thalia said.

"Messy but cool," Annabeth agreed.

Thalia put her arm around her sister and pulled her close. Annabeth leaned in, head on her shoulder, hair tickling Thalia's neck. Above them, the sky was huge and blue and streaked with thin clouds. Somewhere over the city, Beryl was probably painting, probably thinking of them. Somewhere across the country, Jason was probably ironing a tunic and making a list.

"She's going to love the letter," Annabeth murmured.

"Jason's going to frame the thunderbutt story," Thalia said.

They laughed, soft and easy, and sat there until Percy's distant voice echoed across the hill — "THALIA! ANNABETH! THEY HAVE PIZZA AND I SWEAR I'LL EAT YOUR SLICES!" — and they stood, brushing dock dust off their pants, picking up the shoebox together.

"Come on, brave girl," Thalia said, holding out her hand.

Annabeth took it. "Coming, brave girl."

They walked back to lunch side by side, lighter, unhurried, sisters who had finally written back.

Thalia put the shoebox back under her bunk when they returned from lunch. The oldest letter was on top. She could still remember every line, and she knew writing back did not guarantee her mother would keep writing.

Annabeth came in with two envelopes from the arts room. They were blank, in case anyone wanted another try later.

"Put them in the box?" she asked.

"Not yet." Thalia set them in her desk drawer. "We'll see what happens."

Annabeth nodded and went to find her book. Thalia closed the drawer, leaving room for her sister to ask about it again.

---

On Friday morning, Annabeth found Thalia at the mail board before breakfast.

The outgoing notice hadn't changed since the previous afternoon. The Roman request was still listed as pending, and the mortal satchel had departed on schedule. Neither status said anything about what their mother or brother thought of the letters. That was the hardest part for Annabeth. She could measure whether a document had left camp. She couldn't calculate whether the person who received it would be happy.

"How long are you going to stand here?" she asked.

Thalia looked over her shoulder. "Not long. I wanted to see whether the Roman intermediary acknowledged the packet."

"He hasn't."

"I can see that."

Annabeth leaned against the porch rail. Chiron had been clear that the reply could take time and that nobody should use an Iris-message to pressure the Roman camp into sending a faster answer. The boundary was reasonable, even when it was frustrating.

"We could go to breakfast," she suggested.

"In a minute."

Annabeth knew her sister well enough to recognize the tone. Thalia wasn't waiting for a particular piece of information. She was waiting because standing at the board made the uncertainty feel like something she was actively managing.

"Would it help if I checked later?" Annabeth asked.

Thalia considered. "You have architecture lessons."

"After lunch. I can stop by once in the afternoon."

"Then I'll check in the morning."

They agreed on it. It was a small arrangement, not an attempt to divide their worry into equal portions. It meant both of them could leave the board for a few hours without imagining the other had forgotten about Jason.

On the way to the pavilion, they passed the Hermes cabin. A younger camper was sitting on the steps, trying to address a letter to a mortal relative. The child had written the surname correctly but left out the street number.

Annabeth stopped to ask whether she wanted help. The girl held out the envelope.

"I don't know the number. I thought maybe the post office would know."

"It probably won't," Annabeth said. "But we can ask the counselor who registered your address when you arrived. You don't need to guess."

They walked her to the cabin office. The counselor checked the verified contact record, filled in the street number and made a copy of the address for the girl's notebook. The child looked relieved.

"Will it get there now?" she asked.

"It has a better chance," Thalia replied. "That's the part we can help with."

Annabeth heard the words and glanced at her. They had spent an entire afternoon writing letters because both wanted to do something about missing family. Helping another camper solve an ordinary mailing problem didn't make their own uncertainty disappear. It reminded them that a letter was a way of reaching someone, not a guarantee that the person would answer in the way they'd hoped.

At breakfast, Percy asked whether they'd checked the board.

"Once," Annabeth said.

"Just once?"

"Thalia's in charge of mornings. I'm doing afternoons."

Percy looked between them, then wisely decided not to make a joke about their schedule. He passed the bread instead.

Thalia took a piece and ate it. She was still thinking about Jason. She was also hungry, had a sparring session at ten, and had promised Bea she'd help review the next day's route. Nothing about writing to her family absolved her of those ordinary plans. She was glad the letter had been sent. She was going to have to learn to keep living while she waited.

After the meal, Chiron met them beside the pavilion with a brief update. The mortal satchel had reached the regional sorting office. The Roman intermediary had acknowledged receiving the delivery request, but not accepted the personal letter yet.

"That isn't a refusal," he said. "It's simply where the matter stands."

Thalia was tempted to ask him whether he could make the process move faster. She didn't. He had already explained which people were responsible and what he could reasonably do. Pushing him would not change the Roman camp's response.

Annabeth wrote the status in their shared correspondence notebook, carefully separating *request received* from *letter delivered*. The distinction seemed pedantic until she imagined having to explain to Jason months later why they'd told him a letter was on the way when it had never left the intermediary's desk.

"Can we send another one while we wait?" she asked.

"You may draft one," Chiron replied. "I suggest waiting to learn whether this route works before submitting several packets to the same channel."

She agreed. Thalia watched her sister put the notebook away. They had learned something useful without getting the answer they wanted.

By the time they left the pavilion, both had appointments they were supposed to keep. Annabeth went to her model-building lesson; Thalia went to the practice yard. The two letters could travel without requiring the girls to follow them in their minds every minute. Neither had forgotten her family. Both had done what they could for that day.
