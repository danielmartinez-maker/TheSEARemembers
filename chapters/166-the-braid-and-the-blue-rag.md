## Chapter 166: The Braid and the Blue Rag

Annabeth woke to the blue-white Sunday light that came through Cabin One's high windows and lay across the floorboards in long patient bars, and she stayed where she was a while, gathering, the way she stayed still at the map wall before she drew.

The cabin breathed around her. Ozone and clean linen, the smell the blue trim gave off, and under it the smell of two girls living: graphite, lemon oil, damp lake clothes on the line by the washroom door, the faint crackle that lived in the eaves and raised the hair on her forearms when she brushed the wall. Outside, the pines were doing their September trick of holding the light and letting the sound through — a gull somewhere downhill, the clank of the pavilion bell on its hook, water moving through a channel under some other cabin's floor.

Her bunk was made. She had made it at first light without deciding to: hospital corners, pillow fluffed, the way Silena had taught her in June. Across the aisle, Thalia's bunk looked like a fox had circled a mark in it and lost interest — blanket on the floor, sheet half off. Annabeth had made that one Friday while her sister was still on the ridge, and Thalia had slept in it exactly one night and unmade it again, because order in somebody else's system was not a thing Thalia Grace could keep for longer than the dark.

On the windowsill, in the stripe of sun, sat the granite stone — smooth gray, the size of a hen's egg, GRANITE DOESN'T MOVE written across it in Annabeth's small capitals. Her joke, planted on Thalia's pillow Thursday, delivered as prophesied: Thalia had walked in the door Friday and thrown it at her head inside ninety seconds, and Annabeth had caught it, and filed it, and the sill was where a stone went when its work was done.

On the bedpost hung the gray flannel. It had been Thalia's since forever — the camp shirt's older brother, sleeves gone soft, the gray taken uneven by a hundred washes and one autumn of woodsmoke. Annabeth had worn it at Friday's homecoming fire and all Saturday, and somewhere around the second of those days it had stopped being a loan and become hers, without a ceremony, the way the important transfers in this family happened.

"Genius." Thalia's voice came out of the fox-den of blankets, thick and rough with sleep. "Is it Sunday?"

"It's Sunday."

"Did the world end?"

"I would have felt it."

"Then the day's ours." The blankets went back. Thalia sat up, black hair wild and full of static, the fine hairs on her arms standing where they'd touched the sheet, her camp shirt twisted around her middle. She looked eighteen and seven at once, the way she had since Annabeth was small enough to know better. "Cabin One to ourselves. Did Clarisse tell you that, or did you learn it from a mole?"

"Nobody told me. I deduced it." Annabeth sat up, hugging her knees under the flannel. "The sea kids claimed the canoe end of the lake. Silena's doing her Sunday bread, Clarisse's got them counting canoe straps, and Percy's under a hull with a wrench. They'll be busy till the bread's done."

"Under a hull." Thalia cackled. "That's where he lives now. We should charge admission."

"They feed us in exchange. It's a sound system."

Thalia swung her feet out of bed, stretched until her back popped, and padded barefoot to the windowsill. She picked up the granite stone, turned it over, read her own quote back to herself, and set it down exactly where it had been, which was a kind of respect.

"That rock has more job security than I do," Thalia said.

"It's load-bearing."

"The stone is load-bearing. I'm the survey crew. I see how it is." Thalia dropped onto the end of Annabeth's bunk, which was the sister version of a hug, and bumped her shoulder with her own. "Okay. It's Sunday. We're alone, the cabin's ours, and I have four days of trees in my pockets I haven't told anybody. I move we steal the whole day."

"I move we steal the whole day," Annabeth agreed. "But I already made a list."

"You already made a list."

"Friday night. On the back of the survey margin." Annabeth pulled the folded paper from under her pillow with the unrepentant air of a girl whose lists had never once failed her. "It's good. It has contingencies."

"Execute it, then."

Annabeth unfolded it and read it aloud, editing as she went, which was how her lists actually worked: they were conversations with herself, revised in front of people she trusted.

"One. Breakfast here, cooked by us, because the sea kids have the pavilion and the day is ours and I want to see you try to cook."

"Try? You burned water in July. It's on the map of grudges I keep."

"Two. The mail, at the Big House, because the Thursday pouch is still hanging on its hook with the whole camp's letters in it, and letters come from people who don't carry phones, which is all the interesting people. Three. We raid the Athena loft for paper and ink, because the north-ridge sheet deserves better than your field-book margins. Four." She paused. "Four is the big one."

"Four sounds domestic."

"Four sounds like you, sitting still for one hour of your life. Your survey sleeve's hanging off you, your boot's leaking, and your hair's been trying to escape since Friday."

Thalia looked down at herself. The left sleeve of her clean camp shirt had a fresh tear at the shoulder seam, thin as a thread — opened by the granite shoulder, by the cairn, by four days of trees that had wanted her jacket more than she'd wanted to give it up. She poked the tear. It spread like a mouth.

"Huh," she said. "That wasn't there yesterday."

"That wasn't there yesterday because yesterday you were wearing the blue shirt. Is the blue shirt retired?"

"The blue shirt is retired. It gave its life at the arena." Thalia considered the tear like a woman betrayed by cloth. "Fine. One hour. Sitting. But I set the terms. All day, you owe me one true thing an hour. Unprompted. Something true you wouldn't have said otherwise. That's the admission price for Sister Sunday."

Annabeth's mouth opened. Closed. "That's extortionate."

"That's the market. Hourly truths are a ritual, and rituals are how families stay invented, genius. Look at your boyfriend and his little folded notes. Look at Clarisse counting straps. Look at Silena's book on the shelf. You don't think those things just happened? We built them. Building them is the job." Thalia ticked them off on her fingers. "You and me, we lost our rituals somewhere between New York and here, and I want them back. Take it or leave it."

It was a good idea wearing a bad disguise, which was how Thalia's best ideas arrived. Annabeth knew better than to say so out loud; Thalia would only charge more.

"One per braid," Annabeth said.

"What?"

"One true thing per braid. You re-braid my hair — properly, the whole thing, parts and everything, the way you used to — and each braid gets one true thing from you, unprompted, no repeats. Not an hourly tax. A fee."

Thalia was quiet a moment. Then her mouth did the crooked thing. "You're negotiating."

"I'm a cartographer. I measure things before I give them away." Annabeth folded the list back along its creases. "Take it or leave it. My counteroffer expires when the kettle boils."

"I hate you."

"You haven't filled the kettle yet. It's in the plan."

---

Thalia found the kitchen corner of Cabin One and assessed it the way she assessed a ridge: confidently, at speed, and with no survey equipment at all.

Cabin One did not cook. In five years of Grace sisters the corner had produced, in total: tea, burned toast, one ambitious soup that Annabeth still refused to discuss, and the eggs Thalia was currently staring down like they had challenged her. There was a two-burner plate Chiron pretended not to know about, a tin kettle, a shelf of tins, and a cool box that mostly held field books nobody wanted to drown. There was no Silena. That was the point of the day, and also, Thalia thought, hefting the eggs, possibly a design flaw.

"Bread," she announced. "Where's bread?"

"There is no bread. Cabin One doesn't own bread. Bread is a Poseidon asset."

"There was bread. Yesterday." Annabeth came in behind her, already dressed in the gray flannel over her camp shirt, olive shorts, sneakers double-knotted, her hair in the same lopsided plait she'd put in Friday night. "Silena sent a loaf with a note. The note said —" She pulled a folded paper from her pocket and read it aloud in her best Silena voice. "Eat before you plan. Love, the authority. The loaf lives on the shelf."

"The loaf lives," Thalia agreed, and found the loaf wrapped in cloth, still good, and set it on the counter with the gravity of a woman placing a bet. "Now. Eggs."

"We're doing eggs?"

"We're doing eggs. You wanted to see me cook."

"I wanted to see you try."

"Same thing."

Thalia cooked the way she did everything: fast, from instinct, with no measurements, and with the absolute conviction that enthusiasm was a substitute for skill. She cracked three eggs into the one skillet with her thumb — Annabeth winced at the shells — turned the heat to its highest notch, because the plate had so many notches and the highest one must exist for serious people, and left them alone, because hovering was for amateurs. She was, she told Annabeth with her hand on her hip, executing a recipe she had invented on a ridge in under two minutes.

"What's the recipe called?"

"The Surveyor's."

"What's in it besides ambition?"

"Eggs. Heat. The belief that I am a capable woman."

Annabeth filled the kettle and did the toast by the book: two slices from the loaf, butter from a wrapped brick in the cool box that was mostly butter now, into the second pan on low, timed with the little sand timer somebody had left in the tin drawer. Her side of the kitchen looked like a drafting board. Thalia's side looked like weather.

"Sand's halfway," Annabeth reported.

"Wait for it."

"Thalia, your eggs are smoking."

"That's the smoke of flavor."

"That's the smoke of carbon."

The toast came out perfect on one side and scorched on the other, because Cabin One's plate had a hot corner that everybody knew about and nobody had ever drawn on a map, which Annabeth found personally offensive. Thalia's eggs came out black at the edges and yellow in the middle, seasoned with exactly two things: salt, and granite dust that was probably still in her fingerprints from the field sheets.

They ate on the front step with the cabin door open behind them, the morning going gold through the pines. The eggs were, Thalia admitted around a mouthful, experimental. They ate all of it anyway, with honey from the tin they'd traded Silena for in July — honey, it turned out, could repair almost anything, which was a recurring theme in this family.

"Okay," Thalia said, licking the plate with the back of her wrist. "That was barely food and I enjoyed it more than the pavilion. That's either true love or protein poisoning. Don't answer that."

"Noted." Annabeth stood, brushing crumbs into the grass. "Mail?"

"Mail."

They walked up the switchback together in the midmorning heat, Thalia barefoot with her boots hanging from two fingers — she refused to wear boots to collect mail, it was a principle — and Annabeth with her notebook under her arm out of pure habit. Camp was doing its Sunday: archers at the range, a volleyball argument somewhere, the forge hammering in short bursts, the lake flashing green-gold downhill whenever the path bent. The other Cabin One kids had claimed the arena for the morning, which meant the big blue-trimmed house at the top of the path was theirs until the bell.

At the Big House, Chiron's office door was shut, and the flat drawer inside it was always locked, and Annabeth walked past it the way you walked past a sleeping horse: aware, respectful, uninterested in waking it. "One of these days the panel gets to come out again and live on the wall," she said.

"When it's ready," Thalia said. "Some things you don't survey till they're ready. Come on. Mail."

The Thursday pouch hung on its hook by the front desk, still hanging there on Sunday morning, because nobody at this camp had ever once collected their own mail on time under a hand-lettered sign that read DO NOT STEAL — BELL, which was a lie, because Bell's whole job was stealing. They took their share: a circular from Chiron, a seed catalog somebody had ordered, and —

"Ah," said Thalia.

A lavender envelope. Not the plain kind, the kind with flowers printed up one corner and a kiss on the flap in lipstick pressed hard enough to leave a real mark. Annabeth's chest did a small complicated thing.

"She wrote you too?" Thalia asked.

"She wrote you too?" Annabeth asked at the same time.

On the back of Annabeth's envelope, in the same loopy hand: ANNIE — SKY. LAUGH TONIGHT. And on Thalia's, visible over her sister's shoulder: TO MY GIRLS, BOTH OF THEM, EVEN THE GRUMPY ONE.

"Both," Thalia said quietly. Her face did the thing it did when joy and fear arrived holding hands. "Okay. Both. Outside. Let's — outside."

They read them on the front step with the door open and the loaf cooling on the counter, the two lavender letters side by side on the boards, the lipstick kiss up. Beryl Grace wrote the way she lived, which was to say that everything happened at once, in loopy ink that wandered uphill and gave up on the margin, with hearts over the i's and entire sentences she had forgotten to finish.

The news was a chip. It was blue. It was, Beryl reported, "not a frog, I asked, Carol says the frog thing is a joke invented by television and she has very strong eyebrows when she says it." The chip had been given to her at the program, which met in the basement of a church on Halsted, where a man talked about "one day at a time," which Beryl had improved upon: "one day more, girls. Every morning I say it. It's from your favorite play. I know you hate when I'm right."

She wrote about the diner where she worked lunch shifts, and the woman who owned it, who had given her a locker, because a locker of your own was a thing nobody told you about when you were young. And she wrote that she had stopped keeping the number in her pocket, on the stone steps, in her head, wherever she used to carry it — "I don't count days anymore, girls. I just put on both shoes in the morning like a person who intends to wear them."

Annabeth read her own letter four times. Halfway down page two was the part that made her stop breathing for a second. "Annie — are you still drawing the whole world? You told me once you were going to draw a map of everything and I said everything wouldn't fit and you said that was because I wasn't trying. You were six years old. You were terrible. I have thought about that every week for eight years. Draw me something small and mean it."

Beside her, Thalia had gone very quiet, the way she went when something had gone in under her armor. Annabeth reached over without looking and took her sister's wrist, thumb on the pulse point, checking, which was the oldest language they had.

"You told her that," Thalia said finally. "You told her everything wouldn't fit."

"Apparently I did."

"You were six."

"I don't remember being six." Annabeth turned the page. "I don't remember any of it. I just have the photograph."

"Photograph's something. It's where the rest of it lives till it comes back."

"She asked about Jason," Annabeth said softly.

She had. On the back page of Thalia's letter, in the same loopy hand with the hearts, careful as a woman stepping over a creek: "Tell me something about your brother. One thing. Anything. If it's small, let it be small. I won't ask again if you tell me to stop. But I'm allowed to miss him, girls. I'm allowed to miss all three of you. I'm allowed to want to be somebody you'd tell first."

Thalia read it twice. Then she folded her letter along the creases, held it a moment against her knee, and looked out at the pines.

"She didn't count," she said.

"The days?"

"She always counts the days. Every letter. It was the only part I trusted, because numbers don't lie." Thalia's jaw worked. "No number. Just shoes. 'Both shoes in the morning like a person who intends to wear them.'" She made a sound that was half a laugh and half something else. "That's worse. That's so much worse."

"How is it worse?"

"Because I can't argue with shoes." Thalia wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way she did everything she refused to call crying. "She used to give me a number so I could hold it at arm's length. Now she's just — trying. In a church basement. With a locker. And the worse part is I want it. I want it so bad I can taste it, and every time I want it this much something goes wrong, Annie. That's the pattern. Want, then rain."

Annabeth thought about lists — her own hands making them over and over, because some things could not be held directly and could only be held in order. She thought about thirty-one checks a day, and what a list really was, down at the bottom: a way of standing at a distance from a hope and calling it a plan.

"She wrote Annie," Annabeth said. "She used the word." She tapped the envelope, and felt the name sit in her chest like a stone in a riverbed that the water had finally accepted. "I'm going to answer her. Today. Me, Annie, in my own hand, with the map work still on my cuffs. If I plan it, I'll plan it till it's small enough to lose."

Thalia looked at her sister then with an expression Annabeth had seen exactly twice before and had earned both times. "Okay," she said. "Okay. We answer today. Both letters. I'll do Jason. You do — you do her. And then we steal the rest of the day, and she gets a letter that's warm and honest and doesn't lie to her about anything except the eggs, which were a war crime."

"Deal." Annabeth stood. "Lunch first. The letter's worth doing right, and I want the whole afternoon for it."

"Look at you," Thalia said, grinning, the wetness tucked away somewhere safe. "Making decisions at ten in the morning. Who allowed you."

"I did. It's on the list."

"Your list is a tyrant."

"Your list is a professional courtesy." Annabeth headed for the cabin door, then paused in the stripe of sun and looked back — her sister, eighteen, barefoot, survey tear hanging off one shoulder, a lavender letter over her heart, pine needles somehow back in her hair since Friday. "Thank you. For saying yes to the letter. Hers isn't — I didn't know if it would be mine to answer."

"It's yours," Thalia said simply. "Nobody else's. Come on. There's a loft to raid."

---

The Athena loft was four flights up a ladder that had been built by somebody with long legs and no sympathy, and Thalia Grace went up it first because going up anything first was a thing she could not help and had stopped trying to help.

"It's a *library*," Annabeth called up, from the bottom, in the voice of a girl who had read the rules of the loft and was prepared to file a complaint about the ladder.

"It's a shed in the sky. Come up."

The loft took the whole head of the Athena cabin under the gable and it had been accumulating for about thirty years, which at a camp that replaced its own cabins every generation was close enough to a geological era. It smelled of paper and dust and old varnish. There were three hundred boxes of things. There were rolls of drafting paper stacked like masts. There was a jar of ink the size of a soup pot with a lid wired on, and a whole wall of drawers labelled in five different hands, and the window at the end had been propped open with a broken awl the way somebody had propped it in 1971.

Annabeth came up the ladder and stood in the middle of it with her hands at her sides and did not say anything for a moment, in the same way she would not have said anything walking into a room where somebody had left a piano.

"Two sheets," Thalia said. "You said two sheets."

"I said two sheets before I came up."

"I know what you said before you came up. That's the whole problem with you."

What actually happened was that Thalia spent forty minutes handing things down off shelves while her sister made a small, intense sound at each one. Heavy paper from a mill that had gone out of business. Three boxes of pencils, cedar, all of them soft from age and all of them still good. The soup-pot ink, which turned out to be perfectly fine and which Annabeth smelled with her eyes shut like a person checking wine. A case of brass dividers in a fitted box, stamped with somebody's initials. And, in the back corner, under a canvas sheet, a rolled tube of the camp's old maps, going back so far that the earliest one had the lake drawn wrong.

Annabeth unrolled the top one on the boards, and the two of them sat over it in the dusty light with their knees up while she read the drawn country line by line: the old boundary, the old boathouse, the pavilion in a place where the pavilion had not been since the 1970s, the ridge line drawn blank above the same point where the ridge line was still blank on the wall in the parlor.

"Somebody else stopped here," Annabeth said.

"Somebody else stopped there in 1962."

"And nobody ever filled it in."

"Sixty-three years," Thalia said, "and then a girl with a pencil and a bad heel walks it in four days." She said it flatly, the way she said things she did not want to be paid for, and got up and went to the window and looked out at the camp because the sentence had left her face doing something.

They took two sheets of the heavy paper, the dividers, and the jar of ink, signed them out in the loft's own ledger in Annabeth's hand, because that was the law of the loft and the ledger had signatures in it going back to before either of them was born, and Annabeth wrote *GRACE, A.* on the next blank line in her small upright letters and then, deliberately, without saying anything, wrote *GRACE, T.* underneath it.

"I'm not carrying a jar of ink down a ladder," Thalia said.

"You're on the ledger."

"I'm on the ledger," Thalia agreed, and carried the jar down the ladder backwards, one-handed, the way you did a thing you had declared yourself incapable of, and did not drop it, and did not mention that either.

They came down into the common room of the Athena cabin, which was empty because it was Sunday and everybody with any sense was outside, and they walked out of it into the cold gold light with their arms full of paper, past a wall of pinned drawings, and at the door Thalia stopped and turned around and looked back into a cabin that was not hers and never would be, at a girl standing in the middle of it in an orange fleece with a jar of ink under her arm, reading somebody's pinned drawing with her whole body leaning toward it.

"Hey," Thalia said.

"Mm."

"One true thing."

Annabeth looked up. "That's not the deal. The deal's one per braid."

"I'm changing the deal. First one's free, because I've had it in my mouth since the ridge and it's going to rot." Thalia stood in the doorway with the papers under her arm and the cold coming in past her and said, in the most ordinary voice she owned, "I was happy up there. Four days, alone, in the rain, with the whole hill under me and nobody to stand in front of. I have never been as happy as I was on the third day on that ridge, and I spent the fourth day walking down because I was afraid of what it meant that I didn't want to come home."

Annabeth did not move for a second. The pinned drawings on the wall behind her did not move either, and outside on the grass a kid shouted about a ball and then stopped shouting.

"And what did it mean," she said, carefully, the way she asked for a measurement.

"It means I'm not the hill," Thalia said. "It means the hill's fine without me and I'm still here, which is a thing you figure out at eighteen if you're lucky and at forty if you're not." She hitched the papers up. "Second one you pay for. Come on, genius, the light's going."

They walked down through the cabins with the paper, and behind them the Athena cabin shut its own door in the wind with a bang that made four kids on the grass look up and then go back to their ball, and Thalia Grace walked the whole way without saying anything else, which was, on a Sunday, the most generous thing she did all week.

---

Cabin One at noon was warm at the top and cold at the floor, which is how a cabin with high windows and one stove always is, and Annabeth Grace sat down on the boards in front of the stove with the gray flannel over her knees and the old quilt folded under her and the day's list, its first four items spent, lying flat on the table like a spent shell.

"Comb," Thalia said.

"You know where the comb is."

"I know where the comb is. I'm announcing it so you know it's starting, because you hate things that start without announcements."

Thalia got the comb out of the tin on the shelf and came and sat behind her on the quilt, and Annabeth took the pins out of her own hair and let it down, and the whole thing took about a minute, and then there was nothing left in the room except the stove ticking and the camp going on outside, one story up and two panes of glass away.

Thalia Grace was not gentle with a comb. She had never been gentle with anything in her life, and her sister had known that since before she could remember, and had decided at some point around the age of nine that gentleness was not what she wanted from a person's hands. What she wanted was sureness. Thalia parted the hair in four strokes with the tail of the comb, sharp and quick and right the first time, and Annabeth, who had spent her whole life being handled by people who were careful with her, sat on the floor of the cabin with her eyes shut and let her sister be sure at her.

"Section one," Thalia said. "A fee. I owe you a true thing. No repeats."

"No repeats."

"I've been standing outside your door at night."

Annabeth's eyes came open. "What?"

"Since June. Not every night. The bad nights." Thalia took up the first section of hair and began to braid, tight and even, three strands moving like a rope being laid. "I get up in the dark and I come down the aisle and I stand in front of your bunk and I listen to you breathe, and I count to ten, and then I go back to bed and I sleep. That's the whole thing. There's no other part to it."

The stove ticked. Somewhere on the grass outside a ball went past the window and did not come back.

"You don't have to do that," Annabeth said.

"I know."

"I'm fourteen and I've been in this camp five months and I sleep in this cabin every night, and I have never once "

"I know," Thalia said again, and went on braiding. "That's not why I'm telling you. I'm telling you because it changed and I noticed, and if I don't say it out loud I'll start lying about it." Her hands did not stop. "I came down the aisle on Tuesday night like always, and I stood at the end of your bunk in the dark, and I listened, and you were breathing, and I had this thought that was so clear it was like a bearing: *she's fine, and you're the one in the dark.* And then I stood there another minute trying to decide whether that was a good thing or a bad thing, and I couldn't, so I went back to bed, and I've been sleeping worse than I did before I started in June, which is, if you're scoring, the wrong direction."

Annabeth sat very still with her sister's hands in her hair.

"It's a good thing," she said, eventually.

"Prove it."

"I can't prove it. It's not a measurement." She thought about it the way she thought about a contour, wanting the true line. "It's good because it means you're not spending the whole night on a watch that ended. It's bad because you liked the watch."

"Huh," Thalia said, which was the sound she made when a sentence landed somewhere and she was going to be angry about it later, in private, while doing something else.

The first braid ran down the back of Annabeth's head and ended in a tail. Thalia tied it with the blue thread off the spool on the shelf, the same spool that held the mending thread, and put her palm flat on the top of her sister's head for a second, checking the shape the way she checked the shape of anything she had made, and then took down the second section.

"Section two," she said.

"You don't have to."

"It's a fee. I set the price. Don't try to underpay me, it's insulting." She parted, and combed, and started. "I wrote her a letter in April. A real one, four pages. About the house, and about what I remembered, and about all the things she did that I've been carrying around in a bag for four years, and the parts where I blamed her, which was most of page three."

Annabeth waited.

"I read it out loud in the loft with the door shut," Thalia said. "Twice. I practiced being angry. I practiced the parts where I'd stop in the middle and she'd ask me to go on. I worked the whole thing out, front to back, like a survey, and I said it to a wall with nobody in the room, and then I put it in the stove."

"Thalia."

"Not because I forgave her." Thalia's hands went on working, tight and even. "Because if I sent it, I'd have to find out what she'd do, and I already know what she'd do. She'd try. She's trying now, in a basement, with a locker and a chip of plastic in her pocket, and the trying is worse than the drinking because I can't argue with it, and if I send her four pages of everything she did in the house I'd have to stand there and watch her try harder, and I'm not doing it. I'm eighteen. I'm allowed to not be ready for a thing."

"You are allowed," Annabeth said, and her voice came out with a crack in it that she had not planned on.

"Don't," Thalia said, very quietly. "It's a fee, not a confession. You're supposed to just sit there and let me work."

So Annabeth sat there and let her sister work, and the second braid went down the crown of her head in a curve like a road, and the blue thread went round the end of it twice and knotted.

"Section three," Thalia said. "Last one."

"Same price?"

"Same price." She started at the front, the little section over the temples that Annabeth could never do herself, small and quick, the part that had to be redone every single day of her life. "The truth is I don't know the address of my own mother. I have it written down. It's in the coat, in the pocket, on a card, with the phone number of the church basement on the back, and I've looked at it every day for two weeks and I still know her by the diner and the woman who owns it, and not by the street. And this morning I read her letter twice, and both times I got to where she says *I don't count days anymore* and I felt the floor go, and I still don't know whether that's a good thing that's happening to her or a thing she's saying so that we'll answer."

Annabeth sat with the last braid going in at her temple and her hands folded on the quilt in front of her.

"Answer anyway," she said.

"I was going to."

"I know. I was just saying the thing out loud so it would be in the room." Annabeth opened her eyes and looked at the light in the window, at the dust going round in it. "My turn. You owe me nothing and I'm paying anyway."

"That's not the deal."

"I'm renegotiating. I do that." She took a breath. "I don't remember her kitchen. Four hours ago I read a letter from my mother telling me about a kitchen in a house I lived in for ten years, and I have nothing to check it against. Not the yellow door, not the window, not the table. It's a photograph and a sentence from a letter and a story you've told me twice." She put her hand up and touched the second braid, blind, the way you check a knot on a load. "What I remember is this. That's the true thing. My whole childhood is your hands doing my hair in a doorway, and I've stopped grieving the rest."

Thalia did not say anything for a while. Her hands stayed in Annabeth's hair with the last section half-braided, and the stove ticked, and outside somebody finally got the ball back and yelled about it.

"That's two fees in one," Thalia said at last, thickly.

"Charge me."

"I'm charging you." She finished the braid, tied it, and put both hands on her sister's shoulders from behind and left them there, hard and flat and warm, for about four seconds. "There. Three braids. Best hair in this cabin."

"It's a terrible braid."

"It's a *functional* braid." Thalia stood up and went to the stove to put the kettle back on, and Annabeth got up off the quilt and looked at herself in the little mirror by the door: three braids, tight, uneven, doing exactly the job a braid was supposed to do, done by the same pair of hands that had been doing her hair since before she could reach the back of her own head.

She did not take them out for three days.

The writing table in Cabin One was a plank the two of them had put on two crates in the second week of June, because the cabin had come with one desk and one chair and an argument, and they had settled the argument by building a second place to work out of scraps, badly, in an afternoon.

It was level. That was Annabeth's only requirement and she had enforced it with a level and a folded paper shim, and Thalia had mocked her for it for five months and always used that table anyway.

They sat at it with the jar of loft ink between them, and the two sheets of heavy paper, and the good pen and the farm pen, and the stove doing its low afternoon thing, and they wrote.

Annabeth went first because she had known what she was going to write since the letter came up the hill, and knowing what she was going to write was not the same as being able to do it. She sat with the pen over the paper for four full minutes while the sun moved off the boards and onto the wall, and Thalia, who was two feet away and could have said something, did not say anything, which was the correct gift and the one nobody ever mentions.

Dear Mom, she wrote, and then stopped again, because the shape of the word on the paper was different from the shape of it in her mouth, and both of them were different from the shape of the woman in the photograph with the yellow door behind her.

What she wrote in the end took forty minutes and was one page. It said: *The chip is blue and I have written the date of your letter on the back of it so it doesn't get lost. The diner sounds loud. I'm glad about the locker. I don't remember the kitchen but I'm glad it wasn't broken in your dream, and I'm not mad that you said it. I am the person who draws this camp now. That means I know where everything is and almost nobody else does, and when somebody needs to find out where the water goes they come and ask me, and I tell them, and then I go and find out. It seems like the kind of thing you'd tell somebody at a table.*

Then she stopped and read it twice, the way she read everything, once for the words and once for whether they were true, and it was true, and it was not enough, and there was one line left that she had known about since four this morning.

*You asked me to draw something small and mean it.*

She took the second sheet of the heavy paper, the good one out of the loft with a watermark in it and a rough edge where it had been torn out of the book, and she drew.

It took eleven minutes. She drew it with the fan-pen and the jar ink in one corner of an otherwise empty page, and no lettering, because lettering would have made it a diagram: six stones stacked, a plate of grey under them going out of the frame at an angle, and the rag. The rag was the whole point, so she spent eight of the eleven minutes on it, and it came out in three strokes and a shadow, blue, going northeast, standing out of the top stone at the angle a thing stands at when a southwest wind has been holding it for four days.

She looked at it for a while. Then she wrote under it, small, in her own hand: *The north ridge, two miles up. I found the man who drew this and I'll find out what the water is for. A. — Sunday.*

"That's the rag," Thalia said, over her shoulder, quietly-ish. "That's my rag. You drew my rag."

"It's a mark on a map. It's public."

"You drew it for her."

"I drew what she asked for," Annabeth said. "She asked for something small. It's small. It's three strokes."

Thalia looked at the page for a while longer, with her chin on her sister's shoulder in a way she would have denied in any court, and then stood up and went back to her own sheet, which she had been working at for twenty minutes, and which Annabeth could see from where she sat and was careful not to read.

What Annabeth could see was that her sister had written *Dear Jason* and then crossed it out and written it again and crossed that out too. There were three starts under the crossings, and a fourth line that had been written out half a sentence and stopped, and Thalia sat over it with the pen in her fist and did the thing she did when a bearing would not close, which was to hold perfectly still and stare at the paper like a person waiting for it to make the first move.

Annabeth inked the date under her own drawing and set both pages aside to dry and did not help.

That was the whole of it. She wanted to help, the way you want to put your hand on a walking thing to steady it, and she had learned in this cabin that a person standing at a page four months deep does not want to be steadied; they want to be allowed to stand there while you happen to be in the room.

"Thursday," Thalia said, at last, out loud, to her own page.

"What?"

"The post goes down Thursdays with the Hermes run. Whatever's in the box by dark goes down the hill Thursday, and that's the trick, that's the thing I was looking for." She sat back. "If I put it in the box it goes Thursday and then it's out of my hands. It's a timing problem. I can solve a timing problem."

"You can solve a timing problem," Annabeth agreed.

"Don't be kind at me, I'll lose my edge." Thalia picked the pen back up and drew a line across the empty bottom of her page, in the field hand she used for boundary marks, and wrote under it: *POST DAY. THURSDAY. DO NOT MISS IT LIKE APRIL.* Then she turned the sheet face down on the table and stood up and stretched until her shoulders cracked, which meant the work was over for the day and also meant she had not written the letter, and both of them knew which of those two things had just happened.

Annabeth's own letter was ready at four o'clock, folded square, the drawing inside it wrapped in a slip of the good heavy paper so the drawing would not crease, and she walked it down to the Big House herself, because she was not going to hand it to anybody else.

The box had a lid with a slot in it and a card on the wall above it in Chiron's hand: US MAIL — THURSDAY SACK. To get a letter to the road and onto a truck and into a state she had never been in, she had to trust a slot, a centaur, and whichever Hermes child had the sack that week.

She stood in the cold hall a second with her hand flat on the lid of the box, which was the same gesture she used on a closed drawer in the office, and then she dropped the letter in and heard it land on the other paper in there, and went out into the afternoon, because the day was three-quarters spent and she had made a rule at breakfast that she was going to steal the rest of it.

Percy Jackson found the Grace sisters at the lake at half past four, and knew before he was within fifty feet of them that something had happened, because they were both in the water and neither of them was swimming.

Thalia was standing in the shallows up to her knees with her arms crossed like a woman at a bus stop, and Annabeth was sitting on the dock with her feet in and her field book closed on her knees, which was the tell, because Annabeth with a closed book in front of her was a person who had stopped working on purpose, and Percy had learned in five months that a girl who stopped working on purpose had something she was keeping.

He had two things with him and he had brought both of them without being asked: half a loaf of Silena's bread in a cloth, and news.

"You're both in the lake," he said, from the sand.

"It's cold," Thalia said, "and it's the last of it, and I'm standing in it out of spite."

"That's the whole relationship you have with water."

"Correct."

He came down the dock and sat on the boards beside Annabeth with the bread between them, and did not kiss her, because her sister was standing twelve feet away in the water watching them both like a border guard, and because they had an arrangement about that which nobody had ever written down and everybody observed.

"There's bread," he said.

"There's always bread."

"There's bread because Silena found out you two were doing a whole day without the pavilion and said, quote, somebody in that cabin has to eat, and it isn't going to be either of the two who own it."

Annabeth took the bread. She tore it and gave half of the half back to him without looking, which was a thing they did now, and which he had noticed she did with exactly one other person in the world and that person was standing in the shallows being cold at them.

"Braids," he said.

"What?"

"Your hair. There are three braids in it." He looked at it properly, the way he looked at things he was going to remember: tight, uneven, four blue threads at the ends, done by hands that did not make pretty things. "It's not the way you do it."

"It's the way she does it."

"It's the way I do it," Thalia said, from the water, "and it is *functional*, and if either of you says one word about the shape of it I'll take it out and you can do it yourself for the rest of the year."

They ate the bread on the dock. The sun came down the hill in a long gold sheet and turned the lake over from blue to something closer to brass, and the whole camp above them was doing its Sunday: the ball game on the grass, the archers on the range, somebody's radio going faintly on the pavilion steps, the smoke off the forge standing up straight in the still air. Down in the willow cove, past the point, a grey shape stood on the bow of the half-sunk skiff with one leg folded and did not care about any of it.

"You see the heron," Percy said.

"I always see the heron."

"Nobody always sees the heron. I look for it every day and I miss it half the time." He tipped his chin at the cove. "That's the thing about her. She's been out there all summer and she's never once been anywhere else in my whole life, and I still miss her half the time, which is a dumb way for a person to be."

Annabeth was quiet a second, looking at the cove.

"You miss her because you look for her in the wrong place," she said. "You look at the water. She's on the boat. She's always on the boat. The boat's the constant, and she's the variable, and if you looked at the boat first you'd see her every time."

Percy thought about that for a while. It sounded like the kind of thing he was going to use in a letter.

"I brought news too," he said. "Chiron's closing the swim dock on the eighteenth so the buoys can come in, and he said it in front of four of us, and Clarisse has already put it on a list."

"Of course she has."

"You're not sad about it."

"I'm extremely sad about it." Annabeth opened the field book — just opened it, with one hand, and closed it again. "I'm sad about it and I've already started doing the arithmetic on it, which is how I do sad, and today I'm not doing any of it. Today I drew a rag on a piece of paper and mailed it to my mother, and my sister braided my hair in three sections and told me three things, and I have not worked since eleven o'clock this morning, and it is now nearly five."

"That's the longest you've been off duty since June."

"It's the first day I've had," Annabeth said. "Somebody told me last month that the ordinary days are the ones that hold, and I've been thinking about it constantly and doing nothing about it, which is the most ordinary thing a person can do."

Thalia waded out of the shallows then, dripping, with the flat expression of a woman who had made her point to the lake, and picked up the rest of the bread and ate it standing over both of them like a gull, and said, to Percy, in the driest voice available, "You two have four minutes. I'm going up to get the fire started and if you're not on the path in five I'm putting the good blanket on the floor in the far corner of the cabin and you can find it yourself."

"That's a lot of information," Percy said.

"It's a *schedule*," Thalia said. "My sister lives on them. Learn."

She went up the sand with her boots in her hand and her trousers rolled and the water going off her in a line, and when she was thirty feet away and not looking, Annabeth took her feet out of the lake and turned and kissed him on the dock in the last of the brass light, unhurried, twelve minutes ahead of the schedule she was supposed to be keeping.

It was a good kiss. It was the kind where her hand goes up and stays on the side of his neck and neither of them listens for anything, and when it was over she stayed close and said, against his jaw, "Three braids and three true things and I've got one left over from this morning and I'm not telling you what it was."

"Was it about me."

"It was about a heron," Annabeth said, which was a lie, and they both knew it was a lie, and it was the kind of lie a person tells when the true thing is too big for a dock at five o'clock on a Sunday.

---

They ended the day at the little beach in the willow cove, because Thalia had gone up the hill to start a fire and had decided, somewhere between the cabin and the woodpile, that the fire was going to be at the water instead.

It was a small fire and it was the last of the season for the little ring by the willows, which nobody said out loud, though everybody knew it: the sunset was going down early now, and the nights were getting long enough that a fire at seven felt like a fire at ten. Clarisse came down with the folding chair and did not carry it for anybody. Silena came down with the tin of honey cake that had two pieces left in it and a rule about who got them, which was that they went to the two people who had done the most work that day, and since nobody could agree on which of the Graces had done the most work, Silena ate one of them herself and gave the other to Percy for carrying the wood, which Percy accepted while looking directly at Annabeth the entire time.

They stayed until the coals went grey at the edges.

Annabeth sat on the flat rock with the wool quilt over her knees and her feet out toward the fire, and Percy sat next to her under the same blanket because there was one blanket and two of them and that was the whole reason for the arrangement and everybody at the ring had given up on pretending otherwise. Thalia was on the sand on the other side of the fire with her boots off, arguing with Clarisse about the difference between a bearing and a heading, which was a fight that neither of them intended to win and both of them intended to have. Silena hummed something with no words in it and worked at the last of the honey cake tin with a cloth, polishing it for no reason, because a tin with a lid was a thing you kept.

"Braids," Clarisse said, in the middle of the argument, without any transition at all.

"What about them," Annabeth said.

"There's three of them. You never do three." She looked over the fire with her flat quartermaster's attention. "Somebody who isn't you did that. It's uneven on the left crown."

"It's *functional*," Thalia said, from the sand.

"It's uneven."

"It's *functional*."

"Both things can be true," Silena said, to the tin.

Annabeth sat with the quilt and the fire and her sister's braids in her hair and the whole small stupid quarrel going on around her and did the thing she had learned to do over five months, which was to notice a moment while it was happening instead of filing it afterward. The fire put everybody at the wrong color. Clarisse's chair was sinking into the sand on the left side because the chair always sank on the left side. Percy's shoulder was warm against hers and he was eating the last of somebody else's cake with an expression of enormous innocence. The lake had gone from brass to slate and the first two or three stars were out over the far shore, and out past the sandbar the boundary rock sat in the dark with its white X, and past the willows there was nothing to see at all.

"Tell them about the rag," Annabeth said.

Thalia looked up. "What?"

"The rag. Tell them."

So Thalia told the fire ring — flat, fast, the way she told everything, with the details in the wrong order because they were the right order to her — about the third day on the ridge and the slab the size of a cabin door and the sound of water before she could see it, and about how she had stood in the rain and put one hand into the seam and gone numb to the wrist, and about the cairn she had built with six stones out of the rubble, and the scrap of blue cloth out of Silena's mending basket that she had carried up there in her survey pocket for four days and tied to the top stone in a southwest wind.

"It's still up there," she said. "It's the blue one. It's been up there since the fourteenth, and it's going to be up there in the spring, and if it isn't, I'm going to have something to say about it to whatever took it."

"Why blue?" Percy asked.

"Because blue's what I had."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the whole answer," Thalia said. "You go up a hill with what's in your pocket, and what's in your pocket is out of somebody's mending basket, and you tie it to a rock so the next person knows something happened here. That's a survey. That's the entire profession. It's a scrap of blue on a stone and a girl who'll come back in April and check."

Silena had stopped polishing the tin and was looking at Thalia across the fire with an expression Annabeth could not entirely read, which happened about once a week and always meant something was being filed away on somebody's behalf.

"That was my shirt," Silena said.

"It was a scrap."

"It was the left shoulder of a shirt I got in March and ruined the elbow of in May, and it's been in the mending basket in that cabin since June, and you took it up a mountain in your pocket." She put the tin down. "I want that noted. At the fire. In front of everybody. It's *my* shirt that's on the ridge."

"You can have it back in April," Thalia said, "when it's grey and it's got frost on it and the rope's frayed, and you can put it in your basket and mend the elbow of something else with it, and I'll take the new one up in May."

"Deal," Silena said, which was the shortest sentence she had said all day, and the fire popped, and nobody looked at anybody for a minute or two, which was how this family did the big ones.

Annabeth had the drawing in her pocket by then — not the drawing, the *idea* of the drawing, which was folded in an envelope in a slot in a box by the door of the Big House — and she sat on the rock in the dark with the quilt over her knees and looked at the ridge, which was not visible at all, and did the arithmetic she had been putting off since four o'clock.

Two miles and eleven hundred feet up, on a shelf of granite in the dark, in September, in a southwest wind, a scrap of blue cloth out of a mending basket was moving back and forth on top of six stacked stones, and had been moving for four days, and nobody in the world was watching it, and that was the entire point of it: a mark is a thing that works when nobody is looking.

In four days it would be in a canvas sack on a hook at the end of the road, and in five a man in a truck would lift the sack down and drive it into town, and a woman in a diner with a locker and a chip of blue plastic in her pocket would open an envelope and find a piece of heavy paper with three strokes and a shadow on it, blue, going northeast, done by a fourteen-year-old who had spent eleven minutes on eight of the strokes and who does not remember your kitchen and is willing to tell you that herself in her own hand.

And Annabeth Grace sat at a fire she had helped lay, under a quilt that belonged to the person next to her, with her sister's terrible functional braid in her hair, and understood, precisely and completely, that she had had a day. Not a Saturday at the beach, not a birthday, not a quest. A Sunday in September with nothing in it: breakfast, hard boiled by a person who cannot cook; a ladder into a dusty loft; three braids and three truths; a page and a drawing and a slot in a box; bread on a dock; her sister lying to her about a heron; a fire going out.

She was going to keep it. She had already decided how, which was the part she would never explain to anybody: she was going to draw the fire ring with the five of them at it, and pin it in the corner of the map where the present ran out, and write the date on it, and that would be the thing that made it real.

"Hey," Percy said, from under the same blanket.

"Mm."

"You're filing something."

"I'm filing everything," Annabeth said. "It's Sunday. It's what I do."

"Then file this," he said. "It was a good day. Say it out loud or it doesn't go in."

Annabeth looked at the fire, and at her sister arguing, and at the tin, and at the stars coming out over the far shore, and said, out loud, in a voice that only the person next to her could hear:

"It was a good day."

"Logged," Percy said, and the fire ate the last of the wood, and in two miles, on a granite shelf, in the dark, the blue rag went northeast in a southwest wind, the way it had every hour since the fourteenth, whether or not anybody up there was looking, whether or not anybody down here knew it was there.
