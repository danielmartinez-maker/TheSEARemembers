## Chapter 128: The Sky in the Sea

Since Atlantis, Thalia had watched Percy carry a different quiet. Not homesickness — steadier than that, like the aftersound of people allowed to miss each other without turning it into debt. At breakfast he sat beside Clarisse, slid her the last piece of toast, its crust crackling faintly, and listened while Silena described the reef garden, her hands shaping blossoms in the air. Back at camp, salt still clung to him, sharp in his hair and on the collar of his shirt when the morning wind shifted.

That should have been simple to be glad about. Instead Thalia kept picturing Annabeth in the underwater garden, surrounded by people who had known Percy since before camp, before her. Annabeth had returned with a sketchbook full of coral and a face that said she'd enjoyed herself. Thalia believed her. Happiness never stopped someone feeling like a guest.

On the fourth morning back, she asked Chiron for a rainbow.

"A short message," she said in the Big House doorway, a drachma balanced between her fingers. "To my father."

Chiron glanced up from paperwork. "Have you decided what to ask him?"

"A ride to the coast. Annabeth and I want to visit Atlantis for the day."

"And if he asks why?"

"I'll tell him."

Mildly Chiron said, "That isn't the same as knowing what you'll say."

"I know. I haven't made it polite yet."

From the table, where she'd been marking a map, Annabeth looked over. "No need to make it polite."

"I know." Thalia glanced at her. "Do you want to go?"

"Yes." Annabeth closed her sketchbook. "Not to prove I belong there. I want to see more of the palace, the parts we haven't drawn yet. And I want to go with you."

That last part loosened something in Thalia. She offered Chiron the drachma. "See? We have a plan."

"A plan beginning with summoning Zeus," Chiron said. "I shall warn camp lightning is expected."

"Tell them it's a small storm."

"I shall try to make the distinction clear."

Above the fountain the rainbow shimmered, mist cool on their faces, smelling of wet stone and summer grass, tiny droplets beading on skin and eyelashes. Thalia tossed the coin and called for Olympus, voice steady despite her hammering heart, the drachma spinning gold before it vanished into color. Thunder answered in her father's voice, rolling off the Big House roof and sending pigeons up in a clatter of wings.

"Thalia. What is it?"

She asked for a day in Atlantis and a way down and back, no escort into the palace. Zeus asked whether Poseidon had agreed. Thalia said Amphitrite had invited them, Percy and his sisters were going ahead for training and would meet them the next morning, and Triton had sent her a charm. Her father went quiet.

"I will take you to the sea gate," he said. "I will not intrude on the visit."

"Good."

"You may want to say thank you."

"I may."

The rainbow thinned. Annabeth set down her pencil. "That went well."

"He agreed not to intrude."

"For Zeus, that's a good start."

Thalia gave a skeptical huff, but Annabeth's humor stayed with her while they packed.

---

An hour before sunrise, Thalia found Beryl's last letter beneath a stack of Annabeth's drawings.

She hadn't lost it. She'd put it there on purpose, because the handwriting made her both grateful and angry. Their mother had written about Jason's latest schoolwork, a kitchen window that needed replacing, and a passing remark Zeus had made about seeing the girls soon. No date. No plan. No acknowledgement that *soon* could mean tomorrow or next winter.

Thalia had read that sentence five times. Now she unfolded the page again, sitting at the table in Cabin One while Annabeth packed a waterproof case.

"You look like you're about to cross-examine the stationery," Annabeth said.

"Letter from Mom."

"I know. You read it after dinner."

"I missed something."

"Did you?"

Thalia put the page down. "Our father told her he might visit. He didn't tell us directly until yesterday."

"He doesn't usually."

"That doesn't make it good."

Annabeth threaded a cord through the case's fastening, tested it, and sat opposite her sister. At fourteen she could be infuriatingly composed about some things and painfully hopeful about others. Thalia remembered her at seven, with scraped knees and a habit of taking apart anything that looked too complicated for a child to understand. She remembered fixing the damage and telling herself that being the older sister meant catching every mistake before anyone got hurt.

"What are you expecting him to do?" Annabeth asked.

"Appear. Make a grand speech. Tell us he's pleased. Then tell us we're doing something wrong."

"He might not."

"He might do all four before breakfast."

Annabeth gave a reluctant smile. "That's possible."

Thalia folded the letter in half. "I don't like him using the visit to Percy as a reason to turn up near us without asking."

"He said he'd respect our plans."

"Yes."

"Do you believe him?"

Thalia could have said no. It would have been easy and partly true. She didn't trust Zeus to remain consistent merely because he had been briefly considerate during a message. But Annabeth had asked for an honest answer, not the one Thalia normally used to make a conversation end.

"I believe he intended it when he said it. I don't know whether he'll still intend it when he's standing in front of us."

Annabeth looked at the waterproof case. "Then we'll decide based on what he actually does."

"You're very calm."

"I'm not calm. I know you can't make him predictable by worrying harder."

That was irritating. It was also something Thalia had spent years trying to teach herself.

She folded their mother's letter again and put it inside the drawer where they kept family correspondence. It didn't belong in the packet for the sea visit. Annabeth didn't need to carry every unfinished family conversation into an underwater palace.

"What are you taking?" Thalia asked.

"A notebook, two pencils, a sealed compass, the route card from the last visit, and a second case for anything the archivist lets me copy."

"One notebook?"

"I have another in my bag."

"Naturally."

Annabeth held up a small metal disk, no bigger than a coin. It was the mysterious birthday gift Zeus had sent, whose silver face sometimes became a bird and sometimes a compass. The Hephaestus cabin hadn't identified the enchantment. She'd kept it on a shelf since the party, apparently waiting for a reason to ask about it.

"Are you bringing that?" Thalia asked.

"Yes. Not to rely on it. I want to ask the observatory technician whether the silver responds to a magnetic field or an enchantment. I won't activate it in the water."

"Good."

"You're not going to take it away?"

"I wasn't planning to."

Annabeth smiled, surprised, and put the disk in a padded pocket.

Thalia felt a little ashamed that her sister was grateful for such an ordinary courtesy. Over the years she had become skilled at anticipating danger. She'd also become skilled at making Annabeth feel watched. It was possible to be good at one without being good at the other.

Outside, the last watch bell sounded near the cabins. Camp was just beginning to wake. Thalia went to the porch with Annabeth and looked toward the hills, where the eastern sky was pale.

A younger camper from Apollo cabin came by carrying a lantern. She stopped when she saw the waterproof cases.

"Are you going on an expedition?"

"A visit," Annabeth said.

"Underwater?"

"Yes."

"Are you scared?"

"Some," Annabeth admitted.

The girl looked startled. "But you're a daughter of Zeus."

"Being a daughter of Zeus doesn't make me unable to be scared."

Thalia watched the girl absorb the idea. She'd grown up at camp with older children who boasted about powers they barely understood. It was easy for a younger camper to think godly parentage meant you never had to stop and check a risk.

"How do you go underwater safely?" the girl asked.

"With an escort and a working charm," Annabeth answered. "And we stop if anything changes."

"Could you just use lightning to make a bubble?"

"No," Thalia said immediately. "Lightning and seawater are a very bad combination. We won't experiment with that."

The girl looked disappointed. Thalia didn't soften the answer. Some facts were too important to turn into encouragement.

They helped her carry the lantern to the Big House, then continued toward the saltwater shore by the upper path. Percy was already waiting with his sisters and Triton, talking about which route the escorts had cleared. Clarisse glanced at Thalia's face and seemed to notice she hadn't slept enough.

"You ready?" Clarisse asked.

"More or less."

"That's an honest answer."

"Don't sound surprised."

Clarisse gave a short laugh. She was not an easy friend, and Thalia liked that she rarely pretended to be one. The two of them had disagreed at the camp docks and the training arena often enough that politeness wasn't the point. Clarisse trusted a person who spoke plainly about what they would and wouldn't do.

Annabeth took Thalia's hand briefly, then went to compare notes with Percy about the return route. Thalia stayed by the shore, looking at the saltwater where the first of the palace escorts had appeared. She could feel a small charge building beneath her skin, a sign of nerves rather than any deliberate use of power. She made herself relax her fingers and let it fade.

A visit was a visit. A father might still become a problem. None of that required Thalia to begin the morning prepared for a fight before anyone had said hello.


---

Just after sunrise Zeus met them at the shore wearing another unfamiliar face, broad-shouldered, gray threading his hair, gold catching in his eyes. Rain on hot stone flavored the air, ozone sharp beneath pine, and the sand still held the night's cool under their shoes. Wet pebbles glinted like scattered coins where the last wave had withdrawn.

"You have the charms?" he asked.

Annabeth touched the sea-glass pendant beneath her collar. Thalia showed the dark blue piece Triton had sent, tied to a cord at her wrist.

"Good. Poseidon has opened a passage at the outer shelf. I will carry you there. The rest is his domain."

"You don't have to come all the way," Thalia said.

"I know." His gaze rested on her a fraction longer than necessary. "I said I would take you."

Almost tender. Thalia nodded, throat tight before she swallowed it down. Zeus lifted a hand, and clouds gathered into a narrow gray bridge, vapor curling cold around their ankles. The three stepped on, the surface giving faintly like packed snow. The shore dropped; the sea opened below, blue deepening to indigo, whitecaps flecking like salt. Annabeth kept her chin up, close enough for Thalia's sleeve to brush hers, the coarse fabric rasping softly. Wind tugged their hair, cold and clean, stinging their cheeks pink.

At the far end the water rose in a clear arch. Inside, a current turned, steady and bright, humming faintly. Where air met sea, Poseidon waited.

Zeus stopped short of the arch. "The passage is yours."

Poseidon inclined his head. "Your daughters are welcome in Atlantis."

Zeus's mouth tightened, as if courtesy left him uncertain what to do with an expected challenge.

"See that they return safely," he said.

"I will."

Centuries stacked in the quiet between the two gods. Thalia hated being its cause.

"We'll be back before dinner," she said.

Zeus looked at her instead of Poseidon. "I will be here."

He stepped onto the cloud path and vanished in a flash, leaving only low rumble across the water and the smell of struck air.

Poseidon turned to the sisters in a deep blue robe, dark hair loose around his shoulders. Up close he looked like a man searching for the right first sentence.

"Annabeth," he said. "Good to see you again."

"Thank you for having us."

His attention moved to Thalia. "Thalia Grace. Daughter of Zeus."

"Just Thalia is fine."

"Of course." He paused. "I know who you are. I didn't want to pretend your family was absent from the room."

"It's usually there whether people mention it or not."

Sharper than she'd meant. Poseidon didn't flinch. He weighed her, not only the words.

"Then I'll remember you're here as yourself," he said. "Both of you are welcome."

The current lapped around their ankles, cool and insistent, tugging at hems and chilling the skin beneath. Beneath her shirt Annabeth's charm brightened, its warmth spreading faintly outward; against Thalia's pulse the blue stone warmed, a slow answering heat that steadied her breathing. Side by side they stepped through the arch, light breaking green-gold across their faces.

Percy waited beyond, hair floating around his face, one hand raised. His smile held more relief than surprise.

"You made it."

"We had an escort," Thalia said. "And a fairly dramatic walkway."

"The walkway's new."

"Your father put it there?" Annabeth asked.

"Yes. Poseidon. He likes the entrance clear." Percy offered Annabeth a hand as the current lifted them. She took it for balance, then kept hold because she wanted to. Thalia made herself study the garden gates instead.

The passage descended alongside pale stone. Percy pointed out terrace walls and lanterns marking the palace way, small flames swaying in glass globes. Annabeth looked everywhere at once; Percy kept turning to catch what she'd noticed, grinning when she beat him to it.

At a bend Clarisse and Silena appeared from a side corridor, lamplight catching wet stone. Silena waved with both hands. Clarisse lifted her chin, coral token at her wrist flashing red and blue.

"You came back," Silena said.

"For one day."

"Enough to learn the palace."

"Took Percy six years," Clarisse said. "He got lost in the pantry."

"The pantry was bigger then."

"It was not."

Silena turned to Thalia, eyes bright. "Nice when they argue about stories they both know."

"It happens at camp too," Annabeth said. "Mostly maps."

"The maps are wrong," Percy said.

"The maps are accurate," Annabeth replied. "You keep moving the landmarks."

Thalia watched them settle into familiar joking. Between his sisters Percy stood without scanning for the moment one might pull away. Clarisse bumped his shoulder; Silena's hand found his arm. No perfect family portrait. A living one, old questions and all.

Thalia caught herself hunting the weak point before she could stop, eyes flicking to exits and hands, old habit prickling at the back of her neck. The lamplight smelled faintly of hot oil and brine, and somewhere water chimed against stone.

Clarisse caught her gaze. "The palace won't collapse because you inspected it."

"I was looking at the support columns."

"That's what I said."

"I can inspect you instead," Thalia said.

Clarisse's smile turned small and challenging. "After breakfast."

---

Amphitrite welcomed them in a family dining room, not the formal hall. Windows opened onto a sheltered lagoon where light rippled green across the ceiling, shifting in slow nets. The table sat low enough nobody faced a far end, its stone cool and smooth under their forearms. Between settings rested a bowl of sea plums, skins dusted violet with a bloom like frost, and warm bread steaming faintly of salt, crust crackling as it cooled.

"You have already been here," Amphitrite told Thalia, "but I'm glad to welcome you in your own right."

"Thank you." Thalia returned the hand-over-heart greeting Triton had taught at camp. "Glad to be here."

"Then you've begun perfectly." Amphitrite's smile warmed without expecting. "Sit where you like."

Thalia chose between Annabeth and Clarisse. Percy sat across beside Silena. Triton had brought a tablet of notes to breakfast.

"You brought work to breakfast?" Percy asked him.

"The work followed me."

"You could've left it in the council room."

"Then it would've been there when I returned."

Clarisse pointed her spoon at Triton. "Worst argument I've ever heard for bringing work to breakfast."

"And yet true."

"Only if you're in charge of something."

Triton glanced at his mother. "I believe I've been insulted."

"You've been invited to eat," Amphitrite said. "The rest is optional."

No speech opened the meal. Steam rose from bread, butter melting to gold, the smell yeasty and rich; sea plums sweated cool juice that stained fingers purple. Thalia had braced to feel conspicuous, the mortal girl at the table where Percy had been a child, cutlery strange and too many glasses glinting in the green light. Instead she felt noticed without being watched, the chairs comfortable against her back, the light kind on her skin. Familiar jokes still reached her a beat late; welcome, not woven into oldest memories. People asked and waited for answers. Silena wanted to know if surface sky looked different from the palace ceiling; Clarisse asked for seconds, then denied it, wiping juice from her thumb with a quick, practiced flick.

Percy started explaining the windows' history, then stopped. "Sorry. You already know this part."

"I remember it differently," Silena said. "Keep going."

Clarisse shrugged. "I remember the windows were cold to touch."

"Always cold," Percy said. "Current runs behind the glass."

"Then tell it your way," Clarisse said. "We'll tell ours."

At Triton’s prompting, Percy retold his brother’s story about counting fish at breakfast and inventing one with a blue stripe to avoid finishing food. Triton insisted it had been a reflection. Percy remained unconvinced, gesturing with bread.

"Triton says they had that argument for years," Silena told Thalia. "Percy likes defending the old version."

Thalia thought of Percy stopping himself from deciding what his sisters should feel. Harder, that restraint, when an unfamiliar room held memories she couldn't share.

Across the table Triton noticed her watching. "Would you like the eastern gallery after breakfast? An observatory sits above it. The roof projects the surface sky."

"A fake sky?"

"An accurate one."

"So a fake sky."

"A sky made from light and water. Accuracy is a virtue."

Annabeth leaned in, bread forgotten. "How does it track the stars?"

"With a great deal of careful design."

"I want to see it."

"Then you shall."

Triton set his tablet aside. Thalia recognized the move — making them comfortable by arranging the day rather than asking what they needed. Older-brother management.

---

Clarisse found her in the practice court after breakfast.

The rounded terrace lay open to the current, sparring ring marked in white stone, water sliding past with a low hush that tugged at balance. Salt and cold metal hung in the air, mixed with sun-warmed rock, and light struck upward from the pale floor in a shimmer that made edges swim. Gulls cried far above, muffled by water to thin, distant needles of sound. Thalia was testing the weapons rack, wood smooth and heavy in her grip, grain worn slick by countless hands, when Clarisse came in, footsteps deliberate on wet stone, each step sending a faint sheet of water over her toes.

"You still want to inspect me?" Clarisse asked.

"I said I could."

"That wasn't an answer."

"Then yes."

Clarisse pulled two training spears from the rack and tossed one over. "Good."

Thalia caught it. The shaft ran heavier than camp weapons, wood slick with seawater. Clarisse tested her own, then stepped into the ring, bare feet gripping stone.

"We're not trying to break each other," she said.

"Thought that was the point."

"Not today."

The first exchange came fast. Clarisse drove her back with a direct strike, feinted high, turned toward Thalia's ribs. Thalia caught the spear and pivoted, forearms stinging. Their weapons locked with a crack. Clarisse's stance sat grounded and precise, knees soft.

"You fight like you expect someone from behind you."

"You fight like you expect someone to try."

"They usually do."

Clarisse came again. Thalia slid aside, but Clarisse's shoulder bumped hers — clean hit, solid and fair. Thalia laughed despite herself, breath short.

"You're enjoying this," Clarisse said.

"A little."

"Allowed."

Next exchange, Clarisse hooked Thalia's spear and knocked it clattering to stone.

"You lead with your shoulder," Clarisse said.

"You lead with your entire personality."

"It works."

Thalia retrieved the spear, fingers tingling. "So does mine."

"Sometimes."

Clarisse showed her a small weight shift making the spear's reach deceptive, standing shoulder to shoulder while Thalia copied it, adjusting her hips. Their next exchange lasted longer, feet slapping wet rock; Thalia tapped Clarisse's shoulder before they stepped out of range.

"There," Clarisse said. "Now it counts."

They rested at the low wall. The current slid around the terrace and lifted the edge of Clarisse's hair, cool fingers combing through the damp strands. Thalia leaned her spear against her knee, chest heaving, sweat cooling in the flow to a clammy sheen, her lungs burning sweetly with effort.

"You watch Percy a lot," Clarisse said.

"He's my friend."

"Not what I asked."

Thalia glanced over. "I watch Annabeth."

"Noticed."

"She's my sister."

"I know." Clarisse turned the coral token at her wrist. "Not asking you to stop."

Shoulders tightening, Thalia said, "Didn't say you were."

"You don't know us yet. Fair. But if you're waiting for one of us to hurt her, say it plain."

Thalia studied pearl-colored stone under her boots. She had come to see where Annabeth had been, and the old instinct had traveled with her. "I don't think you would. I don't know you well enough to be certain."

"Fair." Clarisse rested forearms on knees. "She doesn't need you to decide if this place is safe. She can decide herself."

"I'm trying to," Thalia said.

Clarisse nodded. "Good. I like her. Don't want her taking care of me, or choosing between being your sister and being with Percy."

"She doesn't have to choose. They're careful with each other."

"I can tell. They hold hands and look at each other like they have a secret."

"It's not a secret."

Thalia looked toward the doorway. Beyond, Silena's voice drifted from the corridor, bright with laughter, water echoing it. Percy answered, too low to catch. For a moment the three sounded all in one room, familiar with pauses in one another's sentences.

"You three are close," she said.

"We are." Clarisse picked up her spear. "Not because everything's easy. Because we keep coming back to the same table."

Thalia heard truth under bluntness. Protecting a sibling she knew; trusting others with one proved harder.

"You want another round?" Clarisse asked.

"Yes."

"Good. Maybe you'll stop leading with your shoulder."

"Maybe you'll stop announcing your plans."

"No."

They returned to the ring. Thalia kept shoulders loose and landed a clean strike before Clarisse disarmed her. They called it even; Clarisse insisted spear touching ground meant she'd won.

When they finished, Clarisse held out a hand to pull Thalia up from the mat. Thalia took it, palm rough against hers.

"Better than I expected," Clarisse said.

"You too."

"I know."

The closest Clarisse came to welcome. Thalia accepted it as such.

---

Triton found Thalia by the gallery stairs, wax-sealed papers stacked in his hands, sleeves rolled to the elbow showing ropey forearms.

"You have a council meeting?" Thalia asked.

"I've just left one."

"That why you look like you want to throw those into the sea?"

"The sea would return them."

"So the sea has more sense than the council."

Sideways he gave her a look. "Not what I said."

They walked the gallery toward a balcony where currents separated into different colors, teal bleeding to indigo in slow, silk ribbons. Triton set the papers on a stone shelf, salt flaking from the seals in white crusts, the wax cool and ridged under his palms. With hands free he didn't seem to know what to do with them, flexing his fingers once before letting them fall still.

"Clarisse says you keep an eye on Annabeth," he said.

"Clarisse says a lot of things."

"She does. Usually accurately."

"Is this where you tell me to stop?"

"No. I was going to say I understand."

Thalia leaned on the balcony, cool stone under her elbows, spray kissing her face. "You have younger siblings."

"Percy. Clarisse and Silena."

"Not a small job."

"Wasn't assigned to me."

"Neither was mine."

They let the words rest. Silver fish streamed past the balcony, hundreds, turning as one blade.

"When Percy was little, I taught him the outer channels. Thought knowing every current could keep him safe."

"Did it work?"

"No."

"Learned that with Annabeth and stairs."

"She climbed where she shouldn't?"

"I could shout or climb after her. Neither stopped her being Annabeth."

"Percy got good at swimming," Triton said. "And you still worry."

"I still worry."

Triton nodded. "Easy to mistake worry for responsibility. Stop worrying, you think you've stopped doing your part."

"Keep worrying, sometimes you make it somebody else's job to reassure you."

Thoughtfully he looked at her. "Have you done that?"

"Probably. Have you?"

"I have," Triton said. "Do you apologize?"

"Learning."

"Good. Wisdom's free when you earn it by making mistakes."

They continued toward the observatory, Triton explaining how surface light and mirrored currents built the artificial sky, voice echoing softly off shell-inlaid walls. Thalia let him fill the quiet, content for once not to fill it herself.

At the entry arch he paused. "You needn't be responsible for Annabeth every moment."

"I know."

"She isn't alone here, and that doesn't mean she's leaving you."

Thalia's jaw tightened, brine sharp on her tongue. "Didn't say she was."

"No. But you looked as if afraid the room would decide for you."

She considered him. "More observant than your brother says."

"Percy says I'm observant when he wants me to stop being right."

"Sounds like him."

"I'll tell him you said that."

"You wouldn't."

He opened the door. Beyond, the dome glowed pale and sunlit, a bowl of light built beneath ocean. In the center Annabeth waited, sketchbook open, tracing the false horizon. Overhead the sky held washed morning blue, thin clouds stitched of light.

---

Thalia did not immediately enter the observatory. Beyond the threshold, Annabeth was kneeling beside a brass floor plate, studying the dome's false horizon as though she could take it apart by looking hard enough. Her pencil moved across the paper. Above her, the imitation sky brightened in response to a hidden mechanism.

A technician stood beside the door, adjusting three copper rods on an open rack.

"What are those?" Thalia asked.

"Grounding conductors," he said. "The dome carries light, not weather. We use them to discharge stray energy from the projection machinery into the stone below. The current beyond the wall carries no charge."

She examined the rods. "What happens if lightning enters the room?"

The technician stopped working. "Real lightning?"

"Yes."

"We isolate the dome and evacuate. This room isn't designed to hold a sky storm."

Thalia was relieved. Some palaces treated every problem as an opportunity to impress visitors. Here, somebody had been willing to say there were things the architecture couldn't handle.

"Good. My sister is learning how to carry a charge. I don't want anyone mistaking a lesson for a demonstration."

Annabeth looked up. "I'm not demonstrating anything."

"I know. I'm making sure the adults know too."

She saw Annabeth's expression tighten. Thalia had made it sound like her sister needed supervision, and Annabeth was unlikely to forgive that merely because the intent was good.

"I'm sorry," Thalia said. "I should have asked whether you wanted me to explain."

"I want you to tell people the limits," Annabeth replied. "Not tell them I'm irresponsible."

"Then we'll tell them together."

The technician showed them the switch that isolated the projection machinery. Annabeth asked him to explain the path electricity would take in the event of a fault. His answer was methodical. The copper rods carried a controlled discharge to a protected chamber, but the sea surrounding the observatory made any uncontrolled electrical experiment dangerous. A spark released into the open water could reach people far beyond the room.

"I thought the whole structure was magic," Annabeth said.

"Much of it is," he answered. "Magic still has to go somewhere when it fails."

She wrote that down and drew the conductors. Thalia liked the sentence too, though she would never have admitted it in front of Annabeth.

A chime sounded from the corridor. The technician paused. A household attendant had arrived with a folded note bearing Zeus's seal.

Thalia saw it and felt the small, familiar tightening in her stomach.

"His Majesty requests that both daughters attend him at the upper chamber before the evening meal," the attendant said.

"When did he decide that?" Thalia asked.

"This morning."

"He didn't mention it to us."

"I was instructed to deliver the message here."

Annabeth stood. "We have plans."

"The king expects you."

Thalia could hear the warning in those words. Their father often arrived in a new shape and demanded that everyone behave as if his presence were both a gift and a command. He could be warm for an hour and furious when someone set a boundary. A message to his daughters was never merely a message.

"Tell him we received it," Thalia said. "We haven't agreed."

The attendant looked alarmed. "My lady, he may be displeased."

"He may," Thalia said. "We're not going to rearrange our day before we talk about it."

Annabeth came to stand beside her. "I want to finish this visit. If he asks again, tell him to give us a time and a reason, not an order through a stranger."

The attendant hesitated, then bowed and withdrew. Thalia knew the answer might bring trouble later. She also knew agreeing quickly didn't always prevent trouble. Sometimes it just let their father's mood decide the rest of the day.

"Do you want to see him?" she asked Annabeth.

"I don't know. I want to know why he wants us."

"Then let's find out without walking into his room first."

They sent a short written reply through the steward. It said the sisters were with their hosts and requested an explanation of the meeting. Thalia signed it first, then gave the pencil to Annabeth to sign for herself.

In the minutes that followed, they returned to the observatory. Annabeth demonstrated how the false sky changed with a simple dial, nothing electrical, while Thalia watched her work. The technician showed them how the room could be made dark without frightening guests who needed a clear exit. He said the sea outside would remain the sea regardless of what the dome projected overhead.

Thalia liked that. It meant an imitation sky didn't have to pretend to be the real one to be worth looking at.

The reply from Zeus arrived late. There was no explanation. The note merely said *another time*. Thalia folded it and put it in her pocket. Annabeth looked at it, disappointed and not particularly surprised.

"He'll be angry tomorrow," Annabeth said.

"Maybe. Maybe he'll forget he asked."

"Neither makes me feel better."

"I know."

Thalia put her arm around her sister's shoulders, waiting until Annabeth leaned into it. She didn't promise their father would change. She promised only that they would decide together whether to answer the next summons.

When they finally left the observatory, Annabeth carried two pages of measurements and one question for the technician to answer by letter. The dome stayed lit behind them, its false clouds moving across the ceiling. Thalia had spent enough of her life watching the real sky for warning signs. For a few minutes she'd been able to appreciate one that could be turned off when it wasn't safe.


---

The technician met them at the inner door with a folded sheet of calculations and a problem he had not wanted to raise in front of the visitors.

"The lower contacts held for the demonstration," he told Thalia, "but the west ground went warm. I should shut down the dome until we check it."

Thalia stopped. Beyond the door Annabeth was still sketching the ceiling, delighted by the false horizon. "Did the power come through her?"

"We cannot tell. She was conducting a small current when the contact warmed."

"Then we shut it down," Thalia said.

The technician shifted the sheet between his hands. "The queen was expecting to show the dome to the afternoon guests."

"Tell her what's happened."

"She may ask whether the display can continue with half the lights."

"And you'll tell her the ground went warm. That's the answer."

Thalia could hear herself using the voice she reserved for commanding an arena. The technician was not a recruit. He knew the mechanism better than she did, and he had already decided what he thought was safe. She softened her tone.

"Sorry. You were the one who spotted it. What do you need us to do?"

"Please ask your sister to leave the central plate clear while I isolate the contacts."

Thalia crossed the observatory and found Annabeth kneeling beside the brass panel. Her pencil had worn down almost to the wood.

"They need to check the grounding," Thalia told her. "We have to stop."

Annabeth looked up, disappointed. "Because of me?"

"We don't know. The technician noticed a warm contact. He's going to find out."

Annabeth closed the sketchbook but didn't rise. "If I did something wrong, I want him to tell me what it was."

"He will, if he can work it out."

"Then don't send me away."

Thalia almost told her to get behind the door and leave the professionals alone. Instead she looked toward the technician.

"Can she watch from the viewing alcove?"

He considered. "From behind the glass, yes. No active current until the inspection is complete."

Annabeth stood and brushed chalk from her knee. She followed Thalia to the alcove, watching the lights dim in sequence. Without the artificial sky, the dome became a plain structure of stone and brass. It looked smaller and more honest.

The technician used a testing rod to isolate the western plate. Heat had accumulated where a layer of salt had gathered between two contacts. The charge from Annabeth's demonstration might have aggravated it, but the fault existed before she arrived. He pointed to the residue and showed them how the current had taken a path the designers hadn't intended.

"Was that dangerous?" Annabeth asked through the speaking grille.

"It could have been, if we'd left it running at full power. It wasn't an emergency when I found it."

"So the demonstration should have stopped sooner?"

The technician didn't pretend the question was rude. "I should have checked the ground before asking you to conduct a current near it. That was my error. Your charge was within the limit we agreed."

Thalia felt the delayed unease of what might have happened. She had enjoyed watching Annabeth succeed so much that she hadn't asked what would happen if the machinery itself was unreliable. At camp she'd insisted on safety distances and dry ground for weeks. An elegant palace had made her less suspicious than she should have been.

"We'll put this in the visit report," she said.

Annabeth looked at her. "Both of us?"

"All of us. You can record what you did. He can record what failed."

"And you?"

Thalia exhaled. "I can record that I assumed the equipment was sound because a prince invited us to see it."

Annabeth gave her an approving look. "That's unusually fair."

"Don't get used to it."

The technician shut the western circuit and posted a closure note at the inner door. The queen's other guests could see the architecture but not the artificial sky until the fault was repaired. Nobody was pleased with the inconvenience. The technician looked relieved anyway.

Outside the observatory Poseidon waited beside a shallow fountain, water chuckling over shells. Down the corridor Percy and Silena argued over a shell ornament; Clarisse kept changing sides, enjoying herself.

The sight slowed Thalia. She'd braced for court, but the family scattered in little knots, sandals slapping wet floor. Percy waved her over. She lifted a hand but held her ground. Poseidon turned, robe stirring.

"Did you enjoy the gallery?" he asked.

"Interesting."

"Triton showed you the observatory."

"Explained the entire ceiling."

"That's how he shows affection."

"By explaining ceilings?"

"By making sure nobody has to wonder how they work."

Lightly said, but Poseidon's face stayed uncertain. He looked like he'd rehearsed what came next.

"You can just say it," she told him.

Hands folding behind his back, he said, "You've watched your sister grow up around the gods, and your friend grow up with us. You may have an opinion of me."

"I do."

Thalia looked toward Percy and the others. Clarisse's hand rested on Silena's shoulder; Percy had hung back to let them decide whether to include him. At ease, but not careless.

"You're trying now," Thalia said. "I can see that."

Poseidon lowered his gaze to the fountain's churn. "I should have tried sooner."

"Yes."

No defense. Thalia let breath ease out.

"I don't know what you knew when Clarisse and Silena were little. That's between you and them. They don't owe you a new story because you're here now."

"I know."

"They don't owe forgiveness on schedule or comfort when they're angry."

"I would not—"

"People do, even without meaning to. They explain too much, or get sad until the other person comforts them. Don't make them tend your guilt."

Poseidon watched water circle his wrist and slip away. "You're right."

Agreement came quicker than she'd expected. Down the hall someone laughed — Silena, bright. Poseidon kept his eyes on the fountain. "I've wanted them to know I love them. That doesn't repair what they didn't have. I won't ask them to reassure me."

"Good."

"They may never feel comfortable calling me Father."

"Their choice."

Then he looked at her. "You speak as if you know what it is to have a father not easy to trust."

First instinct: shrug it off. "Mine's complicated."

"So are all fathers, I think."

"Not all of them are Zeus."

Faint, rueful smile from Poseidon. "No."

"I don't need you like him or unlike him," she said. "I need you to let them be angry without turning it into a contest about whether you love them."

"I can do that."

"Then keep doing it."

Poseidon nodded. No reach for her shoulder, no bid for judgment. He let the conversation close when she was ready.

"Glad you came," he said. "Not to speak for them. Glad to know you as Thalia."

She watched him. "That sounded almost practiced."

"I thought about what to say."

"Not the same thing."

"Apparently not."

His mouth lifted at the corner. Thalia found she could return a small smile without making it mean trust. "You can call me Thalia."

"I will."

At the corridor's end Silena had won the argument. Percy started toward Thalia, but she signaled she was fine. He slowed and turned back, reading space as answer. She appreciated it more than she could say.

---

The afternoon passed without ceremony: a tour of upper halls where light fell in green shafts, tea by the lagoon, bitter and hot, and Clarisse's promise to spar again. Percy moved between his sisters and the others, sharing old stories and leaving room for new ones. Thalia saw return-work in small things: Poseidon asking before joining a conversation, Amphitrite leaving Silena's rose token for another day, Triton keeping a chair open without choosing who filled it.

At the observatory she found Annabeth alone beneath the dome.

The artificial sky had shifted to amber sunset, a ring of blue at the horizon deepening to violet, stars pricking through one by one. Salt and warm stone scented the air, the bench holding the day's stored heat through her jeans, solid and reassuring. Faint motes drifted in the projected light like plankton, turning slowly in the still air.

Annabeth sat on a curved bench, sketchbook closed in her lap. She looked up as Thalia entered.

"There you are."

"I was with Percy."

"I know."

"Does that bother you?"

"No." Annabeth looked back at the dome. "I was thinking."

Thalia sat beside her, shoulders touching, bench cool through cloth, its stored warmth fading at the edges. "About?" Her voice dropped low under the dome's hush, and the false starlight moved faintly across their hands.

"The sky. How many times Percy has stood under it and thought of home."

"He didn't have to show us everything."

"No. But he wanted to." Annabeth traced her sketchbook's edge. "Clarisse and Silena needed their own memories. He wasn't keeping anyone out."

"You didn't feel left out?"

"A little. I wanted to see how it felt first. I like it here now. I still feel like a guest sometimes. Allowed."

"I worried you'd feel that and decide you had to be grateful instead of honest."

"You were trying to protect me," Annabeth said.

"From things that hadn't happened yet."

"You don't have to stop caring. Only trust me to tell you when I need you."

Thalia had heard versions of the same from Clarisse and Triton. Harder to accept from Annabeth, the sister she'd once kept safe by watching every door. "I know you're not a little kid. Still feel like if I don't look out for you, nobody will."

Annabeth's hand found hers on the bench, warm and sure. "You looked out for me when I needed it. You're still my sister when you aren't standing between me and a door."

Thalia stared at false dusk until her eyes stopped prickling.

"You were deciding whether they were safe enough for me," Annabeth said.

"I was deciding whether I could trust them with you."

"And?"

"Clarisse is terrifying."

"Not an answer."

"Part of one."

Annabeth squeezed her fingers. "Percy is happy here. So am I, sometimes. I still miss camp."

"You're allowed to be glad for me without feeling like you're losing your place."

Thalia gave her a look. "That sounds suspiciously like something Triton told you."

"Thought of it myself."

"You do that sometimes."

The simulated sunset thinned to blue. Familiar constellations gathered: the Big Dipper, then Cassiopeia, pricking out one by one. Annabeth rested her head against Thalia's shoulder, hair smelling of camp shampoo under sea salt.

"Think this one's accurate?" Annabeth asked.

"Triton says it is."

"And you believe him?"

"Think he wants it to be."

"Not the same thing."

"No. But a good start."

The dome shifted with a quiet shimmer. A faint silver line joined the stars above, a reflection of the current far overhead. Not open sky, and not pretending to be. Atlantis making room for something it could not hold.

Thalia thought of Percy and his sisters, Triton learning to set down old responsibilities, Poseidon trying to show up without asking for praise. She thought of camp pines and the cabin door that always stuck. None of those homes canceled another.

"You don't have to choose them over me," Thalia said.

Annabeth lifted her head. "I wasn't going to."

"I know."

"And you don't have to choose me over them."

"I know that too."

"Good."

Thalia rested her temple against Annabeth's hair. For a while they watched artificial stars brighten against deep water. She couldn't shield her sister from every unfamiliar room, or every complicated family, or every future asking Annabeth to decide what she wanted. She could sit beside her while she decided.

Under the sky the sea had built for itself, Annabeth's hand stayed in hers. Outside the dome the current moved on. Borrowed overhead, imperfect, the stars stayed worth looking at together.
