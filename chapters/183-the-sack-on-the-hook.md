## Chapter 183: The Sack on the Hook

The parlor belonged to the mail on Thursdays, and the camp knew it the way it knew anything chalked on a board by Chiron: by arguing about it at breakfast and obeying it by afternoon. The door stood wedged under the coal scuttle so the draught off the hall could not take the loose sheets. The chest under the window had its lid up. The long table had been slid a foot to the left to catch the light, which was an adjustment nobody remembered deciding on and nobody was permitted to undo, and by half past one the Hermes cabin had finished its shouting on the porch and gone down the hill to find somebody else to charge postage from, and the room had gone quiet in the particular way a room goes quiet when four people in it are concentrating on paper.

Annabeth Grace had the corner with the light. She had held that corner since noon the way she held a survey station, early and completely and with her elbow on the boundary, and in front of her lay a sheet cut square, a jar of water, her pencil, her pen, and her field book, closed, with its spine turned toward her in the exact position she had put it in and then moved twice more. She had opened the back cover once, for the length of one breath, and shut it again. The back cover was not a record. She was about to write to her mother, and the two things were going to have different minutes.

Her mother's letter had come up the hill on Monday in an envelope with the corner softened from handling, four pages, no crossings-out, and a hand that ran even from the date at the top to the last line at the bottom, which was the thing Annabeth had read twice on the Cabin One steps with her knees up and her breath going white in front of her. An even hand. You could hold a letter like that up to a window and read the whole of it without once stopping to work out what a word had been meant to be, and she had put it on the sill above the granite stone where Thalia would find it without being told, because finding out that their mother was well was a thing the two of them still did separately and then mentioned to each other in the tone of people discussing a roof.

Three weeks of Thursday pages sat in the same corner of her memory, and each of them had been harder to start than the last, because the first page of any letter to Beryl Grace had to get past the question of what a fourteen-year-old said to a woman who had asked, in September, to be told something small and to mean it. Annabeth had answered that with a drawing. Drawings were done. Now there was a Thursday in October and the spring had been found and the wall had five names on it and the frost had come down twice, and she had decided at noon, in the light, that this page would be a page of things that were true and finished, because a finished thing was the kindest gift you could post to a woman who was keeping her own list of days.

"Your pen's the bad one," Percy said.

He had come in off the porch with his coat still on and a sheet of paper held flat against his chest so the draught should not get under it, and he was standing at the near end of the table looking at the pen tray the way a person looks at a line-up.

"I know which is the bad one," Annabeth said. "I've assigned it to nobody."

"You've assigned it to the tray, which is a way of assigning it to somebody."

"It's a way of keeping it available." She turned her sheet a quarter round. "Sit down, Percy. You're making a climate."

He sat down at her elbow, which was where he had sat every Thursday since the first one, close enough that the sleeve of his coat lay along the same line as her arm and neither of them had ever moved it, and he put his paper down and took the good pen and then, after a moment, put the good pen back and took the bad one, and she watched him do it and did not say anything about it, because that was their oldest piece of byplay and it was worth more unremarked.

What he was writing was a page for Tyson, and she knew the shape of it before she read any of it, because Percy wrote to his brother the way other people packed a bag: nothing left behind, everything doubled. She watched his hand travel. He wrote with his whole forearm and his tongue slightly caught, and his letters stood up straight and a little too large, as if the page might be read from the back of the room, and when he came to the end of a line he lifted the pen entirely rather than squeezing the last word in, which was a habit she had privately decided was the single most legible thing about him.

"Say it's Thursday," she said, without looking up. "Tyson likes it when the day is in the first line. It's the forge in him. Everything gets stamped where it's made."

"Everything gets stamped where it's made," Percy repeated, and wrote it, and she heard the pen go down on the word *Thursday* with the particular pressure he used for anything his brother had asked for.

They wrote. The stove ticked. Somewhere out on the lawn a first-year was explaining to a second-year that the chest did not need to be shouted at, and the voice came through the open door in the cheerful way of a small animal that has discovered it can be heard. Annabeth got four lines into her page and stopped at the place where a page either becomes an inventory or becomes a letter, and while she was deciding, Percy took one of the cut squares from the stack, wrote on it with the bad pen in his large upright hand, folded it along its creases in the two moves he always made, once down the long way and once across, and then he reached over without looking at her face and opened the back cover of her field book and slid the square in flat against the flap and closed it, and went back to his own page, and the whole operation took about as long as it takes to set a cup down.

Annabeth looked at the field book. Then she looked at the side of his face, which was doing nothing at all, which was how he did everything that mattered.

She waited two minutes, because two minutes was the amount of time that made it a discovery instead of an ambush, and then she opened the cover.

*You have written the same word three times and crossed out two, and both times you went back to the one you crossed out, which means the word is right and the sentence is the thing that isn't ready yet. I've seen it before. It's the best part of watching you work. Take the sentence. I'll be here at the end of it. P.*

She read it once for the words. She read it a second time for the handwriting, which was a thing she had started doing in September and had never once told him about: the way his *t*'s were cut clean through, the way he gave a *y* a tail that went further than it needed to, the way the pressure faded toward the end of a line where his hand had begun to hurry back to her. A folded square of paper could carry a whole afternoon of a person's attention, and his did, and she sat at the lighted corner of a table in the Big House with her mother's letter half written and read it a third time before she was honest with herself about the third one.

Under the table, out of sight of anybody, she put her hand flat on the cover of the book.

Then she took a fresh square from the stack, and wrote in her small upright record hand, the hand that did the camp's marks, and folded the paper along the creases he used, both folds, the long way and the across, and when Percy reached for the water jar she lifted her field book off the table and slid the square into the inside pocket of the coat hanging over the back of his chair, where it lay flat against the lining with the fold toward the opening, and she put the book back and was writing again before his hand came back.

He did not find it at the table. She had known he would not; he never found them where they were put, he only ever found them, later, in the dark or on a set of steps, and the finding was the second half of the gift and she had learned to leave it alone.

Her page went on. She wrote about the frost coming down twice and the stove going lit at six in the mornings and the fact that the lake had not frozen and would not for a month yet, because her mother had asked in September whether the lake froze and had deserved a straight answer from somebody who would go and look. She wrote that Thalia said hello in her own way, which was that she had not said it and had instead sharpened Annabeth's pencil twice and put it back exactly where it had been. She wrote the true and finished things: five people had walked up a ridge with rods and drawing pins and come down with a mountain measured, and the spring was running again where it had run in the spring, and the names were on the wall in the map room, in order, and one of them was hers.

And she wrote the line about Percy, because a page that left him out would be a lie of omission and her mother had asked to be told things that were true:

*There is a boy named Percy. He says his favorite part of the wall is the brackets, which is a position he will defend at length and which I have stopped arguing with, because a person who likes the brackets is a person who likes the part that holds the rest of it up. He walks me home in the dark and then stands on somebody else's step until I've gone in. I am writing it down because it is true and because you asked for true things, and not because you need any persuading.*

She signed it, blotted it, and laid it against the jar to dry, and was reaching for the envelope when the door came in off the hall with a shoulder, which was how Clarisse La Rue opened a door in October.

Clarisse came in, crossed the room, put a single envelope in the chest without any ceremony at all, and looked down at the inside of the chest the way she looked at the boathouse in the morning, counting what was there without appearing to. She had her winter list folded out of sight in her back pocket; Annabeth knew it the way she knew the location of most things that Clarisse La Rue carried, which was by the particular squared set of her shoulders when a page was on her.

"Two-thirds full," Clarisse said.

"It's early yet."

"It's Thursday," Clarisse said, as if that settled the arithmetic, and then she looked at the two of them at the table, Percy with his tongue caught and Annabeth with her blotter, and her face did the small warm thing it did when she had walked into a room and found it arranged exactly as she would have arranged it, and she said, "Read-aloud rule, by the way. Whatever gets read at this table gets read because the writer said so. Nobody's page gets taken off them at dinner."

"Nobody was going to take anybody's page," Percy said.

"I know nobody was. I'm writing it down anyway." Clarisse put her knuckle on the table twice, the way she tapped a rail, and went out with the draught behind her, and the door stayed wedged under the scuttle, because the scuttle had been positioned by somebody who understood that a Thursday door was a working door.

Percy looked at Annabeth.

"Read-aloud rule," he said.

"Read-aloud rule," Annabeth agreed. "She's had it about four minutes and it's already a good one, so it stands."

"And if somebody reads something aloud without saying so?"

"Then Clarisse writes it on a list, and you live in her cabin." She picked up her pen. "Ask me in an hour. I'm at the difficult part of a letter."

"You're always at the difficult part of a letter."

"The difficult part of a letter is where you decide what not to say, and I'm always there because I'm there for both of us, and you can thank me in squares."

Percy laughed, quietly, into his page so as not to disturb the four other people who had begun to drift through the door with envelopes, and Annabeth went back to the place where her letter either became an inventory or became something else, and outside the light went on moving across the lawn in the slow way it did on a clear October afternoon, and the chest under the window filled the rest of the way up past two-thirds without either of them watching it happen.

Percy Jackson went down the hill at three o'clock with his half-finished page under his arm, because letters to brothers were a stove-side business, and the parlor's light, which was excellent for a girl who wrote in a record hand, made his own letters look like a report.

The west path took him past two of the nine lamps, both cold and trimmed, hanging in their brackets like birds that had decided against it, and the smell of the cabin reached him before the door did: woodsmoke, oil, and underneath both, unmistakable and still going strong from the day before, the smell of the first hot bread of the year. Silena had lit the oven at five on Wednesday morning and the cabin had not stopped smelling of it since. Nobody had made a speech about the bread. There had been no need. What there had been, over the whole length of Wednesday, was a division of the loaf conducted by three sets of hands and one butter knife, under the terms of an agreement that all three parties still described differently and none of them would put in writing.

Clarisse was at the long table with the winter list, which she had reached the difficult part of. Percy could tell from the way she was holding her pencil, not writing, resting it on a line the way a person rests a finger on a spot in a map they have already decided not to believe.

"Greenhouse bank's still there," he said, hanging his coat.

"I know where it is."

"I'm not saying you don't know where it is. I'm saying it's still on the list."

"Everything still on a list is on a list." Clarisse turned the page a quarter round. "Bank's not going anywhere. Neither is the awning, and neither is the pine, and all three of them know it, and they're waiting for the cold to make the decision for me. I'm letting them wait."

"You're letting them wait."

"I'm letting them wait until the ground's hard and I can get a bar in without tearing the whole thing out." She looked up. "You want my mail, or you want to supervise mine?"

"I want to finish my own." Percy dropped into the chair at the end of the table and took out his page, and Silena, who had been writing at the near end with her hair pushed back off her face and her tongue caught in the corner of her mouth in the way it did when she was thinking, which Percy had noticed and filed under things a person does not mention, looked up at the sound of paper and said, without any lead-in at all:

"Read it aloud, then."

"I haven't finished it."

"You were always going to read it aloud. You've had that page out since Tuesday and you've been carrying it around like a lantern." Silena put her pen down and reached for the bread knife. "That's the rule as I understand it. Whatever gets read at the table gets read because the writer said so, and you have been looking for a reader since Sunday."

"I have not been looking for a reader. I've been looking for time."

"You've been looking for time in the same place you always find it," Clarisse said, without looking up from the list, "which is five minutes before it's needed, with your boots on."

Percy looked at one sister and then the other, and put his page on the table, and read the first four lines of it out loud in the voice he used for things that were going to be judged, and the four lines were about the bolt, and how he had rolled it across the Cabin Three table on Wednesday night to see whether it was straight, and how it had gone the whole length of the table, and off the end of the table, and into Clarisse's boot, and that this was a compliment to the manufacturing and he wanted it recorded as one.

Clarisse's pencil stopped.

"It went in my boot," she said.

"It went in your boot."

"I was wearing that boot."

"You were wearing that boot at the time, yes."

Clarisse looked at the page for a moment with her face completely level, and then she made a small sound that in any other cabin would have been a laugh and in this one was a formal finding of fact, and she said, "Good. Tell him the boot confirms it," and went back to her list, and Percy wrote, in the margin of his letter, *Clarisse says the boot confirms it*, which he knew Tyson would read four times and enjoy every one.

Silena sliced the bread. She did it the way she did everything with her hands, which was evenly, and she put two pieces on the board and slid one of them across to him with the butter knife laid on top at an angle, and sat down again, and picked up her pen, and did not read anything aloud, and Percy watched her fold a square of paper that she had written on while his back was turned, and fold it once more along a second crease with her thumbnail, so that it sat flat and square and without any spring in it at all.

"That one's got two folds," he said.

"All mine have two folds."

"That's not true. Your letter last week had one fold and a blob of wax."

"That letter had a proper seal and an argument in it and I'd make the same argument again." Silena slid the square to the far side of her blotter, squared it to the edge of the wood, and then, when she thought nobody was looking at her, which was a calculation she had always made badly with him in the room, let her eyes go once to the window and the road beyond it that ran up toward the forge.

Percy ate his bread and said nothing about the window, and the nothing he said about it was a large and deliberate nothing, and he filled it with the next four lines of his letter instead: that the hook had arrived for Silena's mending tote and that Silena had said it was fine, and that Tyson had predicted she would say it was fine, and that the hook was in fact not fine and never had been, and that a hook could be honest about a thing like that and it was a lesson to all of them.

"Whose hook?" Clarisse said.

"Silena's. For the tote. The old hook's sad. It's in the letter."

"The old hook is fine," Silena said.

"The old hook," Percy said, "is in exactly the condition my brother said it would be in, which is why I'm quoting him rather than arguing with him, because he measured it and I haven't."

Silena looked at him for a moment with the particular warm exasperation of a girl who has been quoted accurately, and then she said, "Read the rest of it, then. Out loud. Since the rule's new and everybody's using it."

"Nobody's read anything aloud but me."

"Nobody's read anything aloud but you, which makes you the tradition." She pushed the board toward him. "Go on. What does the letter say about the prow?"

The prow was the one part of the letter Percy had not been able to write, and he sat back with the bread in his hand and looked at the ceiling a second, because the answer to the question was a measurement he had not taken and did not want to take badly. Tyson had asked him, in the careful phrasing he used for anything that might be bad news, to put his foot on the prow piece of the canoe and write and tell him whether it had moved. The canoe was upside down on racks in the boathouse with six others, covered for the season, and the prow piece was under a canvas sheet at the far end, and Percy had walked past it on Tuesday and on Wednesday and had not put his foot on it, because if it had moved he would have to say so, and if it had not moved he would have to say that too, and both of them were going to make the letter heavier than a letter about a prow should be.

"It says I'll put my foot on it this weekend," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's an honest non-answer and I'm going to mail it."

"It'll mail," Clarisse agreed. "Nobody's opened my letters to check whether they answered things."

"Nobody opens anybody's letters to check whether they answered things," Silena said. "That's not what the chest is for. The chest is for going down at dark and coming back with answers on the other side of the year." She stood up with her square in her hand, and the square had gone from the far side of her blotter to her fingers without any visible journey in between, and she said, in a voice with no particular emphasis in it at all, "I'm going up. Whoever's coming, come."

Clarisse held up a hand without looking, which meant she was staying with the list and the three lines that were waiting for the cold. Percy gathered his page, his coat, and the last of the bread, and followed his sister out into a afternoon that had turned the colour of iron.

They went up the path side by side at the pace of people who had been walking it together since the summer, and at the second lamp Silena said, without turning her head, "You can say it."

"Say what?"

"Whatever it is you've been not saying since the window."

"I said nothing. I was extremely nothing about the window. It was the best nothing anybody's done all week."

"It was." She put her hands in her pockets. "Wednesday, at the forge oven. I went over with the tray and he was there with the pans. We talked about bread and work; neither of us said what the rest of it meant. Then I came back with the tray. That's the whole of it, and if you tell Clarisse I'll put salt in your boots."

"I wouldn't tell Clarisse. Clarisse would only have a finding."

"Exactly. A finding." Silena walked two more steps. "It doesn't need one. It's fine. It's a Wednesday."

"It's a Wednesday," Percy agreed, "and it's fine, and I'm only going to say one thing about it, which is that he's been coming up this path at about this time every day this week with a lantern for our porch, and that on the days he doesn't, somebody notices, and that's the whole finding, and I'm not writing it down."

Silena did not answer, and the not answering had a shape to it that he recognised because he made the same one himself, and they came around the cedar brake and up to the Big House with the light going off the windows in long slanting bars and the chest inside the open door showing, as Clarisse had promised, two-thirds of the way to full.

She put her square in herself. Percy stood at the porch rail with his page under his arm and watched her go to the chest, hold the folded paper between thumb and forefinger the way a person carries a stamp, lean in, and slide it under two others so that it lay flat and did not show, and then, because the envelope next to it had gone in crooked, she straightened that too and tapped it flush with the edge of the wood, and stepped back, and the whole business had about it the air of a girl arranging a shelf in a room she did not live in.

"You didn't have to fix the one next to it."

"I didn't have to," Silena agreed. "It was crooked."

The forge road came up the side of the hill in the last of the light, and Percy heard boots on it before he saw anybody, and then Charlie Beckendorf came around the corner of the hedge with his collar up and an envelope in his gloved hand, the kind of envelope that gets addressed at a workbench because there is nowhere else in the world to address it at. He said good evening to Percy. He said good evening to the porch. Silena, who had been standing squarely in the middle of the open door with her arms down, said good evening to a point approximately one foot to the left of his shoulder, and Beckendorf said good evening to the same point, and then he went in and put his letter in the chest and came out again and went back down the forge road, and neither of them had looked at the other one directly at any moment during the entire exchange, and both of them had known exactly where the other one was standing, which Percy knew because he had been standing close enough to see it and had watched the whole thing with the enormous patience of a brother who intends to say nothing whatsoever for the rest of his natural life.

Thalia Grace had been at the table in Cabin One since four o'clock with a sheet in front of her and a pen that wrote in capitals whether she liked it or not, which was the only kind of pen she had ever got on with.

The room was cold in the way that rooms get cold when nobody has bothered to argue with the window: the sash was down tight, the stove had gone to a grey knot of ash, and the two coats in the house were both on hooks by the door because both girls had gone out in them and come back without them. Thalia did not mind. Cold was a straight condition. You were in it or you were out of it and there was nothing to interpret, which was more than could be said for a page of paper at five o'clock on a Thursday.

She had written eleven lines. She read them. They were in block capitals the size of fence posts and they said, in order, that camp was fine, that the frost had come twice, that the lake was closed for swimming as of the first of the month and had moved indoors to a hall that was warm enough to swim in and that Annabeth had designed most of it, and that the west path was lit end to end now with nine lanterns and that somebody had put a tenth on the Poseidon porch so that the bottom of the hill had a light in it too.

Eleven lines was not a letter. Eleven lines was a status report with a greeting attached, and Thalia knew it, and she sat with the pen resting on the last full stop and did not add to it, because the thing she wanted to add was the thing she had not said out loud to anybody in the house since the summer, and letters were the wrong place to say a thing for the first time.

The door came in with Annabeth behind it, coat over her arm, field book flat under her elbow, and the smell of the Big House's ink on her fingers.

"Done," Annabeth said. "Four pages. Mother gets the frost, the hall, the ridge, the five names, and a paragraph about a boy and his brackets that I want it on record I did not need any help writing."

"You always need help with the last paragraph."

"I always need help with the last paragraph and I always end up cutting the help, which is why the last paragraph works." Annabeth dropped her coat on the bunk and came to look at the sheet on the table, and her eyes went down the eleven lines with the slow, unsentimental attention she gave to measurements, and she said, "You've done the housekeeping."

"I've done the housekeeping."

"That's the hard part done, then."

"That's the hard part *described*," Thalia said. "Describing isn't doing. Doing's the bit after the full stop where you say the true thing, and the true thing is four pages long and I've got eleven lines." She put the pen down. "Last week's had the roof seam in it and the words your room in it and I read it out loud twice before it went in, and it went in, and it's gone, and it was the best one I've written. This week I've got frost."

Annabeth pulled out the other chair and sat down with her boots crossed at the ankle and her hands in her sleeves, the way she did when she was about to say something she had already decided not to soften.

"Then write four pages," she said, "or write four lines. But don't sit there being charged for a page you've decided in advance to be honest about. That's the same as not sending it, and not sending it is the thing you told me you were done with."

"I know what I told you."

"You told me at a post, on a county road, in the cold, in front of a sack. I'm holding you to it in a kitchen."

Thalia looked at her sister for a moment. Then she laughed, one breath of it, and picked the pen back up, and said, "Fine. Out loud, then. You'll hear it before he does and you can tell me where it goes wrong."

"You're going to read it out loud as you write it?"

"I'm going to read it out loud as I write it, because writing it in capitals means I can't hear the mistakes until somebody else hears them first." Thalia squared the sheet. "Go on. Sit there. Be an audience."

So Annabeth sat at the other end of the table with her chin in her hand and her field book open on her knees, and Thalia wrote, in capitals, and read each line as it went down, in the flat voice she used for anything she had decided to give away, and the letter went from eleven lines to nineteen, and stopped there, because nineteen lines was the length of a letter that had been written on a Thursday by a person with a normal week in her and not a person performing a week.

It went like this:

*Jason - short one this week. The camp did the ordinary things. The frost came twice and the stove is lit at six. Annabeth is designing a building indoors where the lake used to be and it is warm in there and the whole camp comes in out of the cold and nobody has to be asked twice.*

*We walked up the ridge on Saturday and found water. It comes out of the ground on a shelf, runs through the trough, and disappears under the rock at the lip. Annabeth drew the underground route as a dotted line toward the wet corner of the north path, but we have not confirmed the connection. The spring has probably been running there the whole time and nobody looked. There are five names on the wall in the map room now. Yours is not one of them because you were not here. That is written down so you can argue with it in person.*

*Thalia says hello. Annabeth says hello. We both say it in the same way, which is by writing it and then standing here looking at it.*

*You answer on Sundays. Fine. Thursday to Sunday is four days, and I have counted four days before, and it does not get longer with practice.*

*Here is one thing I need you to know. The lantern at the bottom of the hill stays lit until the cabin's asleep. It is our porch light and it is not for anything but the fact that it is on. You would walk up it. I have never once doubted that.*

*Your sister,*
*Thalia*

"That's the true thing," Annabeth said, from the other end of the table, in a voice that had gone careful in the middle.

"That's nineteen lines of the true thing," Thalia said, and blotted it, and did not read it out loud again, because some things got weaker in the second hearing and this was not one of them.

They sat a minute. The light went off the window in the long slanting bars that meant the sun was nearly under the ridge, and somewhere down the hill the pavilion was laying tables for a dinner that half the camp would miss, because Thursday was a hall night and Thursday was also the night the mail went down, and the two of them had never yet managed both.

"Coming down?" Thalia said.

"I'm coming down. Percy said he'd be at the door by the time the light went."

"Then he'll be at the door before the time the light went, and he'll say something about having arrived at a normal hour." Thalia stood, took her letter off the blotter, and folded it once, along the middle, and then, out of a habit she would have denied under oath, folded it a second time across, so that it sat flat and square and without any spring, and put it in her back pocket.

They went down the west path in the last of it. Two lamps were already burning at the boathouse turn and the third was not yet lit, and the Big House stood at the bottom of the slope with its porch light on and its front door open and the chest by the door visibly past two-thirds and now somebody's problem rather than somebody's project.

Percy was on the porch with the canvas sack in his hands, holding it open by the rim with the patient look of a man who has been given a job involving a container and intends to do the container justice.

"Lamps are going up," he said, by way of hello.

"I can see the lamps."

"You can see two of the lamps. I've been watching the third one. Whoever's on lamp duty is at dinner."

"Whoever's on lamp duty," Thalia said, "is you, in about an hour, and you're doing it early by complaining about it."

The sack stood open and the chest stood open and between them was the whole outgoing week of Camp Half-Blood. Percy tipped it in, letter by letter at first and then in handfuls, and Thalia held the rim with him because holding the rim was the job that kept a person honest, and the envelopes went down in a soft slumped pile: the cream one from the Athena cabin, the one with the leaf pressed under the flap that had been on the table since Monday, two from the Hermes kids in the same hand, a parcel from the canoe shed wrapped in oilcloth and string with FORGE written on it in Clarisse's block capitals, and Silena's square, which went in flat and did not show, and then nothing else.

"Forty letters and two parcels," Percy said, having counted them the way he counted anything, out loud and slightly too late. "Forty and two."

"Forty and two and one sack," Thalia said, and let the rim go, and the sack settled on the boards with a sound like a cat lying down, and for a second neither of them touched it, which was the part of the evening that neither of them ever mentioned later: the second where the whole camp's writing was in one container and anybody could have picked it up.

Thalia picked it up, hoisted it onto her shoulder, and then, with the light from the open door going across her face, handed it back to Percy.

"You carried it last week," she said.

"You carried it last week."

"And the week before that, and I've had my turn at it, and tonight it's yours, and before you say anything: yes, I'm staying on the hill, and no, I'm not walking down with you, because somebody has to be on the porch when the last lamp goes up and it may as well be somebody who likes standing in the cold for no reason." She put her hands in her pockets. "Go on. It's going to be dark in twenty minutes and I'd like the mail to make it to the hook before the truck."

Percy took the sack. Annabeth came up the porch steps past him, put her hand flat on the canvas for exactly as long as it took to say something to it without saying anything, and then took the other corner of it, and the two of them stood in the porch light with a bag of forty letters between them while Thalia Grace leaned against the doorframe with her arms folded and looked at them with the expression she reserved for things she had decided not to comment on.

---

Percy Jackson carried the mail down the hill at ten past six, and the hill let them go the way hills do, slowly and with a lot of commentary from the path.

Annabeth walked on his right with the corner of the sack in her hand and her hood down, and neither of them had suggested that she let go of it, because holding the sack was not really a job anybody had been assigned and it gave her a reason to stay at the exact distance from him that she had chosen when they came off the porch step, which was close enough that their arms moved together. The first lamp at the boathouse turn was lit, and the second at the brake, and the third, which had been dark an hour ago and was now somebody's item crossed off a list, burned in its bracket at the curve where the path bent away from the water.

Down below, the pavilion was at the part of dinner where the whole camp talked at once. The sound came up the slope in a single warm mass with no words in it. Beyond that the lake lay flat and black with a band of light along its far edge that was not the moon, because the moon was up behind the hill and half full: it was the hall, still going, steam going up off the water inside it in a column that showed as a pale moving smudge against the pines, and somebody had put a lantern on the outside step, which meant Thursday's swimmers had come and that whoever had come was in no hurry.

"They'll all be up the path by nine," Annabeth said.

"They'll be up the path by nine with wet hair and no coats."

"That's a hall-night problem. It's going to be a good problem." She looked at the light on the water a moment longer. "We should go one Tuesday. Not this Tuesday. One where we've done nothing all day and can afford to be useless for an hour."

"We've never done nothing all day."

"Then one where we've done nothing all day and it was on purpose." She gave the sack a small tug, which pulled her half an inch nearer to his shoulder, and said it into the middle distance in the tone of a person filing something. "I've put it in the book. It's got a heading."

At the gate they stopped, because the road began at the gate and the gate was where the camp's portion of the evening ended and the rest of it belonged to nobody. Percy set the sack on the flat stone beside the post, and Annabeth let go of her corner, and the cold came into the space between them as if it had been waiting to be invited, and he put his hands in his coat pockets to keep them, and found her square.

It had been in there since the parlor. It was still warm.

He took it out under the gate lamp, where the light was enough, and unfolded it along the crease she had made, and she stood with her hands behind her back looking at the road and not at him, which was the most transparent thing she did, and which he had never once told her about, because telling her would end it.

*You wrote with your arm the whole afternoon and never once looked over except to slide this under my cover, and I let you, and I counted it, which is my version of not saying anything. The sentence is finished. The page is finished. Walk down with me and walk back slower, and if the mail is heavy I'll take the side that's heavier. A.*

Percy read it standing up. Then he read it sitting on the flat stone with his elbow on his knee, which was the way he had learned to read anything that had her in it, and the third time he read it he read it out loud, the last two lines, in a voice that came out rougher than he had planned.

"Walk back slower," Annabeth said.

"That's what it says."

"I know what it says. I wrote it." She turned her head then and looked at him under the lamp with her chin up and her hands still behind her back, and the cold had put colour into the whole of her face and none of it anywhere useful, and she said, "You can put the paper away, Percy. It's going to be there later. It's going to be there in your coat pocket for a week if you leave it, and I'll know it's there, and I'll like knowing it's there."

"Then I'm leaving it."

"I know you're leaving it."

He stood up, and the road was dark in front of them, and the camp was lit behind them, and neither of those facts had anything to do with the next four minutes, which he took anyway, the way you take a thing you have been given every Thursday since the summer and still can't quite believe has not been withdrawn.

He put his arms around her and she came into it without any of the small negotiations that two people do when they are still working out whether a thing is allowed, and the whole of her fitted against the cold jacket and the jacket was not the point of it, and he could feel her breath and she could feel his, going up white together between them and then not going up at all, because he had lowered his head and she had lifted hers and they met at the middle with their mouths closed and their eyes open for exactly as long as it takes to find out whether the other one is going to laugh, and neither of them did.

The kiss was slow in the way that a fire is slow when it has stopped having to prove anything. It went on past the point where a kiss is an event and into the part where it is just two people breathing with their foreheads touching and their mouths not quite apart, and then it started again, because there was no reason for it not to, and Annabeth made a small sound against his mouth that was almost a laugh and entirely not one, and her hands came from behind her back and went up inside his collar, and the cold on the backs of her fingers made him breathe in once, sharply, and she used the breath.

They broke for air with their noses against each other and stood there letting the cold come back onto their cheeks, and he felt her heartbeat against his sternum through two layers of wool and one of cotton and it was not slow and it was not pretending to be.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi."

"That's the first one."

"That was the first one," Percy agreed.

"There's a second one on account of the week." She put her hand on his chest, flat, right over the place she had just been measuring, and looked up at him with an expression that was entirely a dare and entirely something else, and said, "You've got about ten minutes before the light's properly gone off this road and I'd like to spend them."

So he kissed her again, and this one had more of her in it and less of the cold, and it lasted long enough that he lost track of which lamp was behind them and gained track of a number of things he had no business knowing, like the exact way her breathing changed when he stopped for a second and then started again rather than finishing, and the way her hand went from his chest to the back of his neck and stayed there with her thumb against his hairline, holding him there the way you hold a thing you have already decided not to let go of, and the fact that she was smiling in the middle of it, actually smiling, with her mouth against his, in a way that he could feel and could not see and recognised anyway.

When they finally went back from each other neither of them went far. Their foreheads stayed together. Their breath went up in the same small cloud and they both waited in it like it was shelter.

"I'm going to be late for the mail," Percy said.

"You're not going to be late for the mail. The mail doesn't have an opinion. The mail is a sack on a stone."

"The sack on the stone has been very patient."

"It has." Annabeth stepped back the half step it took to make the road exist again, and picked up her corner of the sack, and the cold arrived in the gap and immediately began doing its work on her face and his, and she said, "Down and back. Slower on the back. That's the arrangement."

"That's a good arrangement."

"It's the arrangement I wrote down, so it's binding." She looked north along the road, where it ran straight and empty toward the trees. "Come on. I want to see you hang it."

---

The county road ran north out of the gate in a straight line with no opinion in it, and at twenty past six it was dark enough that the two of them could see all of it and none of what was beyond, which is the particular condition of a country road on a clear night in October: gravel going grey underfoot, the fence posts losing their posts and keeping their gaps, and over everything a sky with more stars in it than a person could use.

They carried the sack between them, each holding a corner, which meant their shoulders touched at every step and neither of them adjusted for it. Percy had wanted to sling the whole thing over his own shoulder and had wanted it for exactly as long as it took to notice that Annabeth's hand fit into the corner fold of a canvas bag in a way that also, incidentally, kept her hand next to his. So they walked at the pace of a load that did not want to be hurried, and the cold came up off the ploughed field on their left in long slow breaths that smelled of turned earth and frost that had not yet decided to be frost, and somewhere behind them the camp went on with its dinner without them.

"Forty and two," Annabeth said, after a while.

"Forty and two."

"You counted twice."

"I counted twice because the first count was thirty-nine and I don't trust a count that starts by being disappointing." He shifted his corner. "Forty letters. Forty people decided this week that they'd rather write something down than wait."

"Forty and two. The parcels are people too. One of them's Clarisse's, going to a hardware man she's never met, and she wrote his name in capitals big enough to be read off a truck."

"She'd read a name off a truck if it saved her a second."

"That's why she writes capitals." Annabeth was quiet for four or five steps, and then she said, in the voice she used for things she had decided to hand over: "Mine's third from the top, on the left. Mother's. Four pages and a blotter mark on the second one that I left in because she'll know what it is."

"What is it?"

"Where I stopped to think. She used to do the same thing and leave the blot, and she told me once it was the only honest part of a letter." Annabeth looked at the road ahead. "Thalia's is in there somewhere too. Nineteen lines in capitals. She folded it twice and didn't tell me why, and I didn't ask, because I've been folding things twice since June and nobody's ever asked me either."

Percy did not say anything to that, and the nothing he did not say was comfortable, and after a moment she leaned her shoulder properly into his arm as they walked, which was the thanks, and the sack went on swinging gently between them with every other step.

The post came out of the dark before they were ready for it, the way it always did: not a building, not a gate, just a single upright in the middle of nowhere with a hook bolted to it and a board on it that had been painted by somebody in the 1950s who had taken the job seriously. The paint had gone silver and the letters had stayed. The hook was iron and had been rubbed bright on its underside by forty years of sacks being lifted off it at six in the morning.

Percy took the sack in both hands and hung it. It turned a quarter round on the hook and he steadied it with two fingers and let it go, and it hung there the way a thing hangs when it is exactly the right weight for what is holding it, slightly forward, square to the road, patient.

"That's it, then," he said.

"That's the plan, you mean." Annabeth stood beside him with her hands in her sleeves and looked at the sack with her head on one side. "The plan is that at six in the morning a man in a truck comes along this road from the north, lifts that down without getting out of the cab if he's got the arm for it, and has it in town before the kettle's on. And then it's not ours any more. It's post."

"It's post," Percy agreed. "Everything in it stops being ours at six."

They stood and considered that for a moment, the way you consider a fact you have agreed to in advance. In the sack, four pages were lying on top of a page in block capitals. Under them a square folded twice with a ledger argument in it. Under that, in oilcloth and string, a parcel for the forge, and under that forty other people's Thursdays, and none of it was theirs to open, and all of it was theirs to carry, and the road ran north and south in the dark with nothing on it either way.

"We should go back," Percy said eventually, "and we should go back the way you wrote it."

"Which way did I write it?"

"Slower."

"Then we're bound." Annabeth took his hand out of his pocket the way she took most things from him, without ceremony and with a firm grip, and laced her fingers through his, and put their joined hands back inside his coat where the cold had already been, and the four of them, the two hands and the two coat pockets, went north to south at a speed that would have annoyed anybody with a schedule.

They did not talk much on the way back, and what talking there was came in pieces: her thumb moving over his knuckle once, twice, in the slow circles she did when she was thinking and had decided to think with a part of her body that was not her brain; his shoulder taking the whole of her weight for a stretch of thirty yards where the road dipped and she let it, and then straightening up again without either of them naming it; the gate coming back into view with one lamp beyond it and the hill going up behind the lamp into the dark.

At the gate she stopped him, and for a second he thought it was for the same reason as before, and it was, and it wasn't: she put her hand flat on his chest, looked up at him under the gate lamp with her hood fallen back and her hair going in every direction that a wind had left it, and said, "One more. Short. Then up the hill, because Clarisse will count us in from the porch and I'd rather not be counted."

It was short. It was also, by any honest measurement, not short enough to be a single event, and when they broke apart Annabeth laughed once, quietly, into his collar, at herself or at the week or at the fact that a fourteen-year-old girl with four pages of correspondence in a sack a quarter mile down the road could not keep a schedule she had written herself, and Percy held her while she did it, and the canvas sack hung on its post behind them and did not rush them either.

The hill began at the gate, and the west path began at the bottom of the hill, and by the time they reached the first lamp it had been lit for an hour and the glass was warm.

---

The third lamp was burning now, which meant somebody had done lamp duty before dinner rather than after it, and the nine of them went up the west path end to end the way they had been put there in September: one at the boathouse turn, two at the brake, the odd one at the curve, amber through glass with a small hard centre to each of them and a circle of ground under it where the frost was going to come late because light, in October, was a kind of weather.

Percy and Annabeth went up it with his arm across her shoulders and her hand under his coat at the belt, and they went slowly, and the slowness had stopped being a rule they had made and become the thing their bodies were doing on their own, the way two people walk when they have decided without discussing it that arriving is not the point. Her hood had fallen back again and he could feel the cold going into her hair and wanted, in a completely practical way, to fix it, and did not, because the last three times he had fixed her hood she had looked up at him with an expression that had cost them both four minutes.

"That's the ninth," she said, at the top of the rise, where the lamp stood at the end of the row with the cedar brake behind it.

"That's the ninth."

"And the tenth's on the porch, and the porch is ours tonight." She said it without any particular emphasis and went on walking, and Percy, whose arm was around a girl who had just described a cabin light as being theirs, took a second to be a person standing on a hill in October with his whole life arranged around a corner bunk and a stove, and then took the second and let it go, because the porch was thirty yards off and the night was not going anywhere.

The tenth lamp was lit and hanging on its hook by the door, and under the lamp, cut into the wood of the frame with the point of a nail and inked over by two different hands, one small and upright and one that had done its best, were the two lines they had put there in September. WRITTEN TO EVERY THURSDAY. COUNTED. Percy read them the way he read everything now, which was to check that they were still true, and they were, because forty letters had gone down the hill an hour ago and come back to nobody and the lines had not needed changing.

Annabeth sat down on the top step with her back against the post and took her field book out from under her arm, and Percy sat beside her, and the two of them did the thing that they had been doing in one form or another since half past one in the afternoon: they took each other's handwriting out and read it in the light.

His square was in the back cover where it had been since the parlor. Hers was in his coat pocket where it had been since a girl slid it in while he was reaching for a water jar. They sat a foot apart with a lamp above them and the whole dark hill below them and read each other's notes without comment for the length of two slow breaths each, and the silence was not empty, it was the sound of two people who had spent an afternoon writing at one table finding out that the afternoon had been folded up and given back.

"Walk back slower," Percy said, for the second time that night.

"It's a binding arrangement. I wrote it down." She closed the book on his square, flat, and put her hand on the cover. "Same time next Thursday?"

"Same time next Thursday, and I'll count the letters out loud again if you want."

"I want. You count like a man who's been cheated and it's the most reassuring thing about you." Annabeth stood up, and the cold came off the wood where she had been sitting, and she bent down and kissed him once, briefly, with her hand on top of his head in the flat and slightly ridiculous way she had, and then she went up the last stretch of the path toward Cabin One with the field book under her arm and her hood pulled up this time, and did not look back, because looking back was his job and they had divided the labour months ago.

Percy went in. Clarisse was at the long table with the winter list and a cup of tea that had gone cold in the shape of a ring, and Silena was at the stove with water on for nobody in particular, and the stove was ticking down into its coals, and the room took him the way it always did, all at once and without comment, and he shut the door on the cold with his back.

He opened the door again before the latch settled because a gust had made the porch lamp flare. The glass hood was shut, but the wick had begun burning unevenly and was smoking against the inside of the chimney.

"You're letting the heat out," Clarisse said.

"The lamp's doing something stupid."

"Then make it stop."

Percy pulled his coat over his shoulders and stepped out far enough to lower the wick. He checked that the chimney was seated in its fitting and that the flame burned cleanly before coming back.

Down the west path he could see the post at the road. The canvas sack was already there with the forty letters and two parcels they'd counted in the parlor that afternoon. The hook looked small from this distance. Percy imagined the runner finding it at six, lifting its weight, and carrying the week's messages farther from camp than he could see.

Silena came to the doorway with a cloth in her hands. "Everything all right?"

"Just the lamp."

She looked up at it. "That chimney needs cleaning after the next delivery. Put it on the slate before you forget."

Percy went in and wrote LAMP GLASS beneath Clarisse's note about the west-path steps. She read it, made a small tick beside the entry, and passed him the dry cloth so he could put it away with the others.

He had nothing left to do for the letters. The people who had written them would wait, and the people who carried them would need a clear path and a working light when morning came. Percy shut the door properly and helped Clarisse move the dry kindling away from the stove.
