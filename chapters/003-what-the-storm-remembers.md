## Chapter 3: What the Storm Remembers

Annabeth woke on the floor of Cabin One with her blanket wound around her legs and her throat aching from a scream she could not remember making aloud. The floor was cold beneath her cheek. For several terrible seconds she could feel the older darkness still pressing against her, a darkness that had not been the absence of light but the presence of a whole world that wanted her and Percy dead. She reached for him before she opened her eyes, expecting his hand to be there among the stones of Tartarus, and closed her fingers around a fold of blanket instead. Her mind supplied every detail with the ruthless precision she generally admired in herself: the crumbling ground, the way an echo had travelled without telling them how far the walls stood, the terror of losing his hand when the passage narrowed. Somewhere above the real room a shutter knocked in the wind, and a watch horn sounded from the camp boundary. Neither noise belonged to the nightmare. She forced herself to identify them one at a time, because if she could name enough ordinary things, perhaps the body that believed it was still falling would listen.

Cabin One took shape slowly around her. A strip of thin morning light lay beneath the door, crossing the dark floorboards and ending against a table leg. The lamp had burned low during the night, and the air smelled faintly of old oil, clean wool, and the cedar shavings that Thalia sometimes scattered in the storage chest. Overhead, the rafters were decorated with little carvings of clouds and branching lightning, the sort of ornament made by people who thought a god's symbols belonged in every room his children occupied. Annabeth had not carved them, although there were stories that she had helped to repair the smaller ones when she was younger. Beyond the window lay a camp both familiar and unrecognizable, with cabins arranged along paths she could navigate with her eyes closed and additions she had no memory of watching rise from their foundations. The entire place was an argument she was unable to win because the evidence on either side of it refused to vanish.

She remained on the floor until she could breathe without counting. In her previous life, waking from a dream of Tartarus had usually meant finding Percy beside her, equally exhausted or pretending not to be. They had learned how to live around the memories without persuading themselves they were gone. Here he slept across the green in a different cabin, wearing a twelve-year-old face, and they had agreed to protect the truth of their return until they understood what had happened. She could go to him after breakfast. She could say she had been frightened, and he would understand without requiring her to describe the part that frightened her most. What she could not do was make him appear beside her whenever the darkness became too convincing. The younger body had a younger voice and shorter reach, but its terror did not diminish the memories it carried.

Thalia's bunk across the room was already made. The blanket lay flat enough to show the corners of the mattress, and the pair of boots beneath it had been cleaned and turned toward the door. On the shelf above, a battered book stood between a small whetstone and a bundle of spare bowstrings. Thalia arranged necessary things where she could find them in a hurry, a habit learned long before anybody at camp had begun to call her a capable leader. Annabeth knew that much from watching her in this new history, and from the scraps of altered childhood the people around her expected her to recall. She had not lived the nights during which Thalia had protected her as a little girl, had not walked through all the years of hunger and uncertain safety that had shaped the older sister who now watched her so carefully. She loved Thalia. She also felt an almost humiliating grief for those missing years, because the person Thalia loved as her little sister had an entire childhood Annabeth could only borrow from other people's accounts.

She stood, folded the tangled blanket twice, and laid it over the foot of her own bunk rather than tucking it away. The camp beads around her neck clicked against one another when she bent to pull on her boots. She stopped and held them in her palm: small, bright records of summers this body had evidently lived, each one a witness to a shared history she could not retrieve. Part of her wanted to take the necklace off. Doing so would not only invite questions; it would wound Thalia, who had been beside her when many of those beads were earned. Annabeth could imagine her sister trying not to show the hurt, then explaining the significance of every bead with the patience she reserved for a frightened child. The thought was almost enough to make Annabeth angry, and she was ashamed of that anger because Thalia had done nothing to deserve it.

By the time she reached the dining pavilion, the camp had filled the paths with noise and movement. An Apollo camper was carrying a cracked instrument case toward the arts shed, complaining to his friend about rain that might not arrive until afternoon. Two younger children from the Hermes cabin were negotiating over a missing mitten as though it were the subject of a treaty. Smoke from the breakfast fires climbed into a pale sky above the pines, and the scent of bread mingled with damp earth and horse leather. The pavilion had long tables worn smooth by elbows, scratches, and generations of meals. Its roof held the sound of conversation but let the morning wind travel between the posts, so that warmth from the food reached her before the heat of the sun did. People greeted Annabeth as she passed. Some used an old nickname. She answered where she could and left the rest as smiles, uncomfortably aware that a person could look perfectly at home while feeling like a visitor to her own life.

Thalia was at the Zeus table talking to two older campers about the watch schedule. She had pushed her hair back from her face and was holding her cup by the rim, a habit Annabeth had begun to recognize as a sign that she was paying attention to more than one thing at once. When Annabeth approached, Thalia's gaze moved first to the tray in her hands and then to the dark hollows beneath her eyes. She did not turn away from the conversation immediately, which gave Annabeth a moment to sit down and reach for the bread before being asked what had happened.

“Before you tell me you slept fine, can I ask you something?” Thalia said once the other campers had moved down the bench. She lowered her voice enough that nobody beyond the table would have to listen. “I heard you get up in the night. I was going to check on you, but by the time I came over, your lamp was out again. Was it another nightmare?”

Annabeth turned the piece of bread in her hands and considered lying. Thalia was already looking for signs of it, not because she distrusted Annabeth in every matter but because there had apparently been years when the younger girl would insist she was fine until she could scarcely stand. A lie would invite more attention than an incomplete truth. “It was a bad one. I woke up on the floor, but nothing happened to me. I just needed a little time before I came down.”

“That's been happening a lot since you were sick,” Thalia said. The concern in her face was complicated by irritation, though Annabeth knew the irritation wasn't directed entirely at her. “I wish you'd talk to Chiron about it. He's dealt with more than a few campers who couldn't sleep after something frightening, and he might actually know how to help.”

“You make it sound as though I haven't thought of that.” Annabeth took a breath and made herself look at her sister. “I know you're worried, Thalia. I am too, sometimes. But I can't explain everything just because you ask while we're having breakfast.”

Thalia's fingers tightened around her cup. For a moment Annabeth could see the argument gathering, the instinct to insist that sisters did not keep important things from one another. Then Thalia glanced at the other campers and took a slower breath. “I'm not trying to make you tell me anything in front of everyone. I'm worried because you're exhausted, and I don't know what to do when you won't tell me what wakes you up. If there's somebody you'd rather talk to, tell me who, and I'll stop offering names.”

The admission put Annabeth in an impossible position. She had wanted Thalia to recognize a boundary without having to defend it, yet the words reminded her that the person on the other side of that boundary was frightened too. She could have told Thalia about Tartarus if explaining the nightmare were the only problem. It would not be. She would have to tell her about the other war, the life that had never occurred here, and the fact that the sister Thalia had raised now remembered years in which their relationship was entirely different. Annabeth was not ready to make Thalia carry that knowledge merely so she could sleep easier. “I might talk to Chiron,” she said. “I haven't decided. Can we leave it there for now?”

“We can leave it there for breakfast,” Thalia replied, unable to resist the qualification. She pushed a plate of eggs closer to Annabeth without taking food from her own tray. “And if you want company later, you can come find me. I won't make you tell me the dream first.”

Annabeth nodded. It wasn't a resolution, but the plate between them and Thalia's hand resting beside it gave the disagreement a manageable shape. She had almost finished the first mouthful when a commotion rose near the pavilion entrance. A satyr stumbled beneath the roof, breathless and spattered with mud, his hooves scraping against the stones. He caught the nearest post to stay upright and tried to speak before he had air enough to make the words clear. Somebody offered him water; he waved it away and pointed toward Half-Blood Hill.

“There's a monster at the boundary,” he managed. “A drakon. Some of the younger campers were on the lower path, and it came through before we could turn them back.”

The pavilion changed around the sentence. Benches scraped the floor, cups were abandoned, and people who had been arguing over breakfast began reaching for the weapons they were permitted to carry. Thalia stood so quickly that her cup nearly fell, but she caught it and set it down before turning to the nearest table of younger campers. She began giving instructions, not as a performance of authority but with the practical urgency of someone who knew that a crowd running in several directions was worse than a crowd held in place. Annabeth's first instinct was to calculate the monster's probable route and ask whether the children had shelter. Her second was to reach for the blade she no longer wore as an adult would. She recognized the kind of danger that waited beyond the pavilion, and the recognition carried no comfort. She had fought drakons before in another life. This body had neither the strength nor the reach of the fighter she remembered. The problem was no longer how to pretend she knew less than she did. It was how to use what she knew without asking a twelve-year-old body to pay a price it could not survive.

---

The drakon lay across the lower path beneath Half-Blood Hill like a piece of the hillside come alive and determined to destroy everything around it. It was longer than the distance between two of the boundary stones, its scales dark red beneath a film of mud, and when it raised its head the gold of its eyes seemed to gather all the thin morning light into two terrible points. A young oak had been snapped at its base. Beyond the broken branches three campers stood trapped against a steep bank, pressed together in the uncomfortable belief that if they remained still enough the creature might choose a different prey. Its jaws opened, and the hiss that came from its throat was not the sound of an ordinary snake but a long rasp of heated breath that stirred the leaves around its head. Annabeth knew the species from the older life she carried. She knew the rapid healing of its scale and muscle, knew that a wound which might stop an ordinary monster would merely anger this one, and knew there was a narrow place beneath the armored throat where a decisive strike could reach what mattered. Knowing all of that did not put a weapon in her hand or make the danger any smaller.

Thalia reached the path ahead of the other defenders, her spear held across her body so that nobody following her would mistake the direction of the point. She saw the stranded campers first. Annabeth recognized the calculation passing across her sister's face: distance to the children, loose earth beneath the monster's body, the broken tree that obstructed the nearest approach, and the uncertainty of whether the drakon would move toward the camp or turn back uphill. Thalia called for the younger campers to stay behind the large boundary rock and ordered the others to clear a route toward the pavilion. Her voice carried, strong enough to be heard above the hiss and the running feet. When she moved forward, electricity began to stir along the spearhead, lighting the metal with quick blue-white flashes that made the hairs on Annabeth's arms lift.

Annabeth wanted to tell her to stay out of reach of the tail. She had seen monsters use the length of their bodies as weapons, lulling an opponent into watching the jaws while the muscles farther back gathered for a strike. But the warning caught in her throat when she realized Thalia already knew enough to keep to the drakon's flank. She had not fought alongside this Thalia through the wars Annabeth remembered. She did not know how much experience the older sister had gained in this history, nor what price it might have cost her. Forcing her way forward to demonstrate knowledge would be reckless. Remaining with the other younger campers felt intolerable. The two choices pressed against her until the boundary rock became a place where she could barely keep her feet still.

Thalia feinted toward the beast's head, then shifted aside when its jaws snapped shut on empty air. The spear glanced along the edge of a scale and drew a dark line through the mud beneath it; a moment later the skin already seemed to be drawing together. The drakon recoiled, not with pain so much as angry surprise, and struck again from a different angle. Thalia planted one foot in the firmer ground beside the broken oak and turned the shaft to intercept the blow. Annabeth watched the exchange in furious detail. Her sister's movements were skilled, disciplined, and frighteningly fast, yet even discipline could not make the monster predictable. Its body continued to move long after its head had turned, and the churned soil beneath its belly made every step treacherous.

The tail came around in a sweep so sudden that Annabeth had time to see what was happening but not to make anybody hear her warning. Thalia caught the movement at the last instant and tried to turn with it, but the heavy scales struck her across the middle and lifted her from the ground. She landed near the broken tree, rolled hard, and lost the spear for a moment. The monster reared toward her. Annabeth felt an old and terrible certainty settle over her, the certainty of having seen a person she loved fall and knowing how little time remained to help. She could no longer remember why staying behind the rock had seemed the correct choice.

A practice sword lay in the grass where someone had dropped it while running. Annabeth seized the hilt, and the difference between memory and muscle nearly dragged it from her hand. The weapon was heavier than she expected, with a balance that belonged to training rather than battle, and she needed both hands to lift it without exposing her wrist. She had held better blades against worse monsters in Tartarus. That did not matter. She had twelve-year-old arms and a body that could not supply the force her mind demanded. She ran anyway, using the tree's broken trunk to screen part of her approach, and swung toward the drakon's muzzle as it turned to strike Thalia.

The blade hit scales with a jarring shock that travelled up both her arms. She had aimed to interrupt the creature's movement, not defeat it, and the strike was just strong enough to change the angle of its head. Its mouth closed on air instead of her sister, but the moment of success vanished as the drakon turned its gold eyes toward Annabeth. The blade was slipping in her hands. She saw the sharp ridge along its jaw and the contraction of the great muscles beneath the throat, and understood that she had traded Thalia's immediate danger for her own. The realization was cold and precise. She had relied on the instincts of a body five years older than this one, and she had brought herself within a reach she could no longer escape.

“Annabeth, get down!” Thalia's voice came from behind the broken tree, hoarse with pain and terror. It carried the command of an older sister who had spent years pulling a child out of danger, but there was something else in it now, a plea Annabeth could hear even over the monster. The air tightened around them as if a storm had descended from the sky into the narrow space between the boundary stones. Annabeth threw herself toward the earth, dragging the practice sword beneath her to keep its edge away from the campers behind the rock.

Lightning struck the drakon's back in a blinding flare. The charge ran along its wet scales, and the creature reared with a shriek that drowned out the voices on the hillside. Annabeth looked up through the afterimage and saw Thalia on her feet, one arm braced against the broken trunk while the other raised the spear. Light crawled around the metal tip and along her fingers, turning loose strands of her hair into a dark crown against the morning. The power was real, but so was the effort required to direct it. Thalia's jaw was clenched and her breath came hard. The blast had bought them a moment, not made the monster harmless.

She used that moment. With the drakon's head lifted and the vulnerable place beneath its jaw exposed, Thalia sent the spear forward. The weapon struck through the narrow gap in the armor at the creature's throat, and the monster convulsed, wrenching itself away from the broken oak. Its tail struck the ground and showered the path with soil and leaves. Then the enormous body began to lose its shape, scales dissolving into the familiar dark dust of a monster sent back to the place from which it might someday return. Annabeth stayed crouched until the last coils had fallen. The silence was not complete; people were still crying, calling for one another, dragging the younger campers away from the bank. But the violent pressure that had occupied the path was gone.

Thalia crossed the distance between them before Annabeth could get to her feet. Her sister caught her by the shoulders and looked her over with such desperate attention that for a moment there was no anger in her face, only fear. Then the anger came, not manufactured for an audience but gathered from every dreadful possibility the last minute had opened. “What were you thinking? You ran straight toward it, Annabeth. I told everybody to stay behind the rock, and I looked up and you were practically inside its mouth. I thought I was going to watch you die.”

“I saw it coming for you,” Annabeth said. The words sounded painfully inadequate. Her hands still shook from the impact, and she was afraid that if she let go of the training sword she would not be able to pick it up again. “You were down, and I thought I had time to push its head away. I wasn't trying to fight it on my own.”

“You didn't have time. You couldn't have known whether I'd get up, and you certainly couldn't have known it wouldn't turn on you.” Thalia looked toward the spot where the sword had struck, then back at her sister's pale face. She swallowed, and when she spoke again she sounded less like a commander trying to restore order and more like the girl who had once stood between Annabeth and dangers nobody else could see. “I know why you did it. I know you weren't trying to make things worse. But please don't do that to me again without giving me a chance to protect you.”

Annabeth wanted to object that Thalia could not protect her from everything, that love was not permission to decide where another person could stand. Both things were true. Neither would help the sister holding her so tightly now. She let the sword fall carefully to the ground and said, “I'm sorry I frightened you. I thought I could do something useful, and I didn't understand how little I could reach until I was there. I don't know what I would have done if you hadn't got up.”

Thalia drew her into an embrace that pressed against Annabeth's sore arms. Annabeth rested her face against her sister's shoulder and felt the pulse racing beneath the fabric of her shirt. In one life Thalia had been a fierce friend and something like a chosen sister; in this one she had spent years raising Annabeth in circumstances that had made affection and vigilance difficult to separate. Annabeth remembered none of that childhood, yet she knew this woman loved her. She could allow the embrace to be real without pretending she had recovered the years that gave it its particular shape. When Thalia loosened her hold, Annabeth stayed close for another breath before they turned together toward the campers who still needed help.



---

It was the ordinary work after the drakon's destruction that finally made Annabeth understand how close they had come to losing somebody. During the fight she had been able to measure distances and openings, to name the danger, and to give her fear a useful task. Once the monster had dissolved there was nothing left to strike, so the fear settled into her hands instead, making her fingers tremble whenever she tried to loosen them. Chiron came up the lower path in his centaur form with a medical satchel slung beside his quiver and began giving instructions to the campers gathering around the broken oak. He sent two older children to check the boundary markers, had a third count everyone who had been on the path, and knelt beside a boy whose ankle had twisted when he scrambled up the bank. His calm did not make the scene less serious. It made the seriousness possible to manage. Annabeth had seen armies after battles and knew how much of their survival depended on people who could count the injured, find clean water, and remember where the supplies had been put.

Thalia stood near the broken tree, insisting she was capable of walking, while one of the healers examined the bruising along her side. A few campers had begun to congratulate her, speaking over one another about the lightning and the final throw. She answered only when they asked something practical, and twice Annabeth saw her look beyond the crowd toward the place where her sister had been standing. Annabeth knew Thalia would come after her as soon as Chiron finished examining the injury, and she could not yet bear another conversation in which her own recklessness became proof that Thalia had failed to protect her. She told the nearest counselor that she was going back toward the cabins and waited for him to nod before walking away. The promise she made was small, merely that she would not leave the central grounds, but it was still a promise and she intended to keep it.

She went to the canoe lake because she knew the route well enough that she did not have to think while walking. The sun had risen clear of the trees and lay across the water in long broken strips, turning the shallows near the dock green while the deeper middle remained dark. The reeds along the bank had begun to dry at their tips. A pair of dragonflies hunted just above the surface, darting through the air with such precise confidence that Annabeth found herself watching them out of an instinctive envy. There was a sort of engineering in their movement, wings answering each other almost before their bodies changed direction. No designer she knew could have built a machine that small with so much ability to recover from a gust. For several minutes she let herself attend to that fact instead of the scrape across her knuckles or the memory of gold eyes turning toward her.

The dock boards were warm beneath her hands, though the water that struck the piles had the lingering cold of springs rising from deeper ground. She sat near the end without putting her feet in, and examined the marks left by the practice sword. The blisters on her palms were not terrible. She had acquired worse ones in earlier training sessions. What bothered her was their position. The sword had twisted at the moment of impact, and the skin had torn where she had failed to control the weight. The body that remembered how to intercept a drakon's strike was not the body that had delivered it. She had made a calculation with the strength of a seventeen-year-old fighter and tried to carry it out with the reach of a twelve-year-old girl. It was the sort of error she would have torn apart on a practice field if another camper had made it. Remembering that did not make her regret trying to save Thalia, but it stopped her from calling what had happened a success simply because both of them were alive.

She heard Percy before she saw him. One of the loose boards near the shore creaked beneath his foot, and he slowed rather than pretend he had managed to approach silently. Annabeth appreciated the decision. She was tired of surprises, even small ones. He came along the dock with his jacket folded over one arm and a waterskin in his hand, glanced at the scraped skin across her knuckles, and sat down close enough for their shoulders to touch without making a show of the injury. The water moved toward his side of the dock almost imperceptibly. He noticed it, frowned, and let it settle again.

“I went by the infirmary first,” he told her. “Thalia's there. Chiron says they want to examine her ribs and keep her from going back to training before someone can make sure nothing serious happened. She asked where you were, and I told her I'd look around the lake.”

Annabeth turned toward him, relieved and uneasy at once. “Is she angry with you for leaving before she could send somebody else?”

“Not yet. She seemed to think I ought to have enough sense to find you without a search party. I didn't argue, mostly because she was standing beside a healer who was already annoyed with her.” Percy offered the waterskin, and when she took it, his eyes moved again to her hands. “I heard what happened from three people who weren't close enough to see all of it, so I'm not going to act as though I know. Are you hurt anywhere they didn't check?”

“Just the hands,” Annabeth said. She drank before giving it back. “And a very deserved blow to my opinion of my own judgment. I saw the tail hit her, Percy. I knew what the drakon would do next, and for one second I was sure I could get there with a sword before it reached her. In the old life I probably could have. Today I almost dropped the weapon before I landed the strike.”

Percy pulled at a loose thread on the cuff of his jacket, his expression growing more serious as she spoke. “I know what you mean about expecting your body to be where it used to be. Half the time I turn too quickly in training because I remember having another six inches of reach. The other day I stepped toward a higher rung on the climbing wall without thinking, and Clarisse asked whether I was trying to throw myself off it.” He looked across the lake, then back at her. “But you were frightened for Thalia. That's not the part you should be ashamed of. Maybe we need to work out which of our old instincts this version of us can actually use.”

Annabeth rubbed her thumb along the edge of her waterskin, considering how quickly he had found the distinction she herself had been circling. “We should. I can write drills for distance and grip strength, and you can stop treating every practice sword like Riptide. I don't want another lesson that requires somebody to throw lightning over my head.” She tried to smile, but the image came back too easily. “What I'm more worried about is what Thalia thinks she saw. She keeps telling me stories about learning to fight when we were younger. I remember none of them, and then I go running at a drakon as though I've been doing this for years. It isn't fair to her.”

Percy was quiet while a little wave broke against the dock. He did not immediately tell her that Thalia would understand or that everything would work out. Those answers had never satisfied Annabeth, and after everything they remembered she would have doubted him if he offered them. At last he said, “You know how many people told me about the things I did as a little kid in Atlantis? Rhode and Benthesikyme can finish each other's stories about me. Amphitrite knows exactly which food I refused to eat when I was small. I believe them, but when they tell me about it, all I have is the picture they make in my head. It feels like hearing about another boy who happens to have my name.”

“Do you think that's what Thalia sees when she looks at me?” Annabeth asked. “Somebody who looks right and answers the questions, but doesn't belong to the memories she's trying to share?”

“I don't know. She might be wondering why you're suddenly so careful around her. Or maybe she just sees her little sister, who nearly got killed this morning, and hasn't had time to think about anything else.” He shifted his hand across the boards until their fingers met. “I don't think we can decide what she feels for her. You can tell her which parts of the morning frightened you, though. That much is yours to say.”

The answer frustrated her precisely because it was sensible. Annabeth wanted to sit with a plan detailed enough to account for every reaction her sister might have, and there was no plan that could restore a history she had not experienced. She leaned against Percy's shoulder, letting their joined hands rest between them, and watched a canoe move slowly along the far shore. A pair of campers were using it to gather reeds blown across the water. One reached too far, and the boat tilted until the other steadied it with a paddle. Annabeth found herself following the familiar sequence of motion, correction, and trust. It seemed almost unfair that ordinary people could depend on one another without explaining the foundations of their lives first.

“There's something else,” she said when the canoe had passed. “This morning you asked me whether I was settling into Cabin One. I told you it was fine, and I wasn't lying. Thalia brings me tea when I can't sleep, and she knows which books I want before I ask. She's spent years learning me. But I keep thinking of everything I don't remember doing with her, and it makes me feel as though I'm stealing the affection meant for the girl who grew up here.”

Percy turned toward her, his face troubled in a way she recognized from the days after Tartarus, when he had wanted so badly to protect her from something neither of them could put into a single sentence. “Do you remember what you said to me when I thought I wasn't the same person after we got out? You told me that surviving something doesn't replace every year that came before it. I know this is stranger than that. Maybe you're not the girl Thalia remembers in every detail. You're still the one who got scared when she fell, and the one who came running. I wouldn't call that stealing anything.”

Annabeth gave him a look. “It's annoying when you remember my better advice.”

“I keep a collection. It takes up most of my brain, which explains a lot of my mistakes.”

She laughed, unwillingly at first, and then because he was grinning at her with precisely the crooked expression she had loved before either of them had been forced into this new beginning. She was glad for the familiarity. It did not solve Thalia, and she did not need it to. She rested her forehead against his shoulder until the laughter passed, listening to the sound of the freshwater lake beneath their feet, so different from the deeper pressure that followed Percy whenever he spoke of Atlantis.

“Do you miss Sally?” she asked after a while. The question came from the sight of his hands as much as anything he had said, from remembering how often he used to describe his mother's kitchen when the world became too difficult to bear. “You can tell me if you don't want to talk about it.”

“I miss her,” Percy said. He did not attempt to make the admission gentler. “This morning Silena gave me another lecture about wet socks, and all I could think about was Sally putting dry ones on the radiator before school. Amphitrite looks after me in ways I haven't earned the memories for, and she doesn't deserve to have me standing there comparing her to somebody she can't know the way I do.” He bent his head, studying their hands. “I love that she wants me home. It doesn't stop me missing the home I remember.”

Annabeth squeezed his fingers. She had feared that loving Thalia and missing Athena were contradictory, or at least evidence that she was failing to appreciate what this life had given her. Hearing Percy speak without trying to rank his mothers made the question less cruel. The people who loved them in this history were not mistakes because an earlier history had existed. Nor were their old losses imaginary because they had been offered something else in the present. They would have to live with both truths. She could already imagine Thalia objecting to the phrase, insisting that truths were supposed to fit neatly together, but Annabeth was learning how little of a human life ever did.

“I'm going to speak to Thalia after the healers finish with her,” Annabeth said. “Not about the old life. About today, and about what I can and can't do when I'm frightened. She deserves an explanation for why I ignored her warning, even if she won't like the explanation.”

“I can walk you to the infirmary if you want,” Percy offered. “Or I can stay here and pretend I came to inspect the dock.”

“You're a terrible liar, Seaweed Brain. If we go, we should go because I asked you to come with me.”

“Then ask.” He leaned closer, still careful of her sore hands. “I won't turn it into a rescue mission.”

Annabeth smiled at him and stood, drawing him up beside her. “Come as far as the path. I want to talk to her alone once I get inside.” Percy accepted the limit without arguing, and they walked back toward camp with their shoulders brushing whenever the track narrowed. From the infirmary they could already hear somebody protesting that an injured rib did not prevent a person from giving perfectly good orders. Annabeth knew the voice, though she could not remember all the years that had made it part of her earliest days. She went forward to meet the sister who possessed those years, determined at least to give her the truth about this one.

---

Thalia hated being told to sit still, and the infirmary seemed designed for the purpose: benches too high, windows that barely opened, and a healer whose voice suggested she'd practiced disappointment for years.

"Deep breath," the healer said.

Thalia inhaled; something beneath her ribs pulled sharply. "Again."

"That was deep."

"About half a breath. Try once more."

Thalia gave her a look but obeyed. The healer listened, pressed two fingers below her ribs, and nodded. "Bruised. I don't feel a break. No sparring for forty-eight hours. If the pain worsens, come back."

"I'm on border rotation."

"Not for the next two days."

"That's not how rotation works."

"Then camp has the perfect opportunity to discover how it works without you."

Silena, who had volunteered to bring supplies from the stores, made a small choking sound from the doorway. She tried not to laugh; Thalia could tell from the unusual concentration on the ceiling.

"Don't," Thalia warned.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to."

"I was about to say the bandages go in the left cupboard."

The healer took the bandages from Silena.

"Thank you. Thalia, the instructions stand."

Thalia put her shirt back on slowly enough that neither woman could accuse her of arguing. The bruise was already turning dark along her left side. She could keep people from seeing it if she adjusted her jacket. The problem would be pulling the jacket on without wincing.

Outside, the camp had returned to its ordinary business with indecent speed. The armory was open. A group of Apollo kids were practicing scales beside the pavilion. Somewhere beyond the trees a pegasus made a noise like a rusty hinge.

The drakon had dissolved to dust on the hill. The only obvious remains were a dark patch of scorched grass and a length of broken fencing.

Thalia would have preferred evidence that required less imagination.

Chiron waited at the Big House with a clipboard. "I need your incident account."

"I killed it."

"I know. I need the sequence of events; the satyr's account isn't enough without yours."

Thalia lowered herself into the porch chair without thanking him for putting it there, then described the alarm, the campers behind the hill, the first strike, the tail that caught her in the ribs, and Annabeth running forward with a practice sword. Chiron took notes without interrupting.

"I hadn't cleared the children from the footpath," she said. "That's on me."

"How long had you been at the hill?"

"Under a minute."

"Did you sound an evacuation horn?"

"No time."

"Did you send anyone to do it?"

"Yes. One of the Hermes boys. Red hair. He had a horn."

Chiron found something on his page.

"He did. That matches his account."

"Then why do you keep asking?"

"Because none of us sees an entire fight."

She looked toward the hill.

"I saw enough."

"Enough to stop the drakon. Not enough to know why it came as far as the camp's inner path."

Thalia's hand tightened on the chair arm.

"The wards were supposed to hold."

"They may have held for some time and then failed at one point. They may have functioned exactly as designed against an unusually persistent monster. We have not finished examining them."

"You say that every time."

"Because every time someone wants an answer before we have one."

Thalia looked at the fresh mark Chiron had drawn on the map. It sat near the eastern approach, not far from the lookout point where she'd tested Percy the previous afternoon. She had told him there were no monsters within a mile.

She had believed it.

"Does this mean I misread the border?" she asked.

"I don't know. What did you sense yesterday?"

"Nothing. Normal static. The sort you get before rain."

"Did it rain?"

"A little. Not much."

Chiron wrote that down.

She disliked him for writing it down. She disliked him more for making the question sound reasonable.

"Don't put Annabeth on border duty," she said.

"I had no intention of doing so."

"Not until she's had training. Not until—"

"Thalia."

She stopped.

"Your sister did not cause the drakon to appear. She was in danger because the creature reached the camp. You are allowed to be frightened by that without making it her responsibility."

Thalia looked away.

It was easy for Chiron to say. He had never spent a winter checking motel locks with a little girl sleeping behind him, never counted change at a bus station and tried to decide whether food or transport would get them farther from danger. He had watched them grow up at camp, though. He had been there for the birthdays when their father sent storms instead of presents. He knew more than she liked to admit.

"She fought with a practice sword," Thalia said.

"Yes."

"She saw me go down and she came straight at it. Didn't even wait to see if I'd get up."

"Would you have waited?"

"That's different."

"How?"

"I'm sixteen."

Chiron's mouth moved slightly. He didn't quite smile.

"That is a difference. I was asking about the choice."

Thalia had no satisfactory answer.

When Annabeth had been little, she used to hide behind Thalia during thunderstorms and then insist she was protecting Thalia from them. Thalia remembered a motel outside Richmond where the window shook so hard they'd stuffed a towel against the latch. Annabeth had fallen asleep clutching the edge of Thalia's shirt.

She was twelve now. She had run toward a drakon. She had stared at Thalia afterward as though afraid of something neither of them could name.

Thalia missed the version of her sister whose fear made sense to her. The thought was unfair and ugly, and she couldn't argue it away by reminding herself that Annabeth was still right there.

"I yelled at her," she said.

"She was in danger."

"I yelled after it was over."

"She knows you were frightened."

"That's not the same as apologizing."

"No."

Silena emerged from the house carrying an empty supplies basket.

"Are you done being questioned?" she asked Thalia.

"Depends. Are you about to start?"

"I was going to ask whether you've eaten."

"That counts as questioning."

"Then guilty."

Chiron stood. The interview was over. He told Thalia to rest and said he would arrange a closer inspection of the eastern ward line before the next evening patrol. Nothing in his tone promised a revelation.

Thalia waited until he had gone inside.

"Have you seen Annabeth?" she asked.

Silena nodded toward the lake. "With Percy. She looked as though she needed a minute."

Thalia almost got up.

Silena set the basket on the porch and sat in the other chair.

"Give her one."

"She's been missing dinner and sleeping badly and now she nearly gets eaten."

"She also went to Chiron about her nightmares, didn't she?"

"She said she'd think about it."

"Then perhaps ask her again. Not as a commander. As her sister."

Thalia rubbed at the scuff on her boot with her thumb. Silena had been at camp long enough to know when Thalia wanted advice least, which was usually when she needed it most.

"Everyone thinks they know how to handle her now," Thalia said.

"Not everyone. I hardly know her. But I know what it's like when people decide being worried about you gives them the right to decide everything."

Silena's voice didn't change. She was holding the basket handle against her knees and looking out toward the fields. Thalia remembered, belatedly, who Silena's father was and how rarely she saw him.

"You mean Poseidon?"

"I mean people." Silena glanced at her. "Sometimes Poseidon."

Thalia let out a breath. "I didn't ask him to send me a sister."

"No. But she didn't ask to have you responsible for everything either."

For once Thalia didn't argue.

Silena stood.

"I'm going back to the stores. If you want dinner, I'll save you a plate. If you want to be miserable on the porch until dark, I will still save you a plate."

"That's a terrible incentive."

"You're welcome."

After Silena left, Thalia sat with the incident form balanced on one knee. She could take it inside and rewrite the whole thing, correcting every mistake she'd made before the drakon reached them. The page wouldn't change what happened.

She signed it instead.

On the final line, Chiron had left space for an observation. Thalia thought about the feeling at the eastern border yesterday, about the normal static she hadn't questioned, and wrote: *Check whether the warning came late, or whether I read it wrong.*

She went looking for her sister before the dinner horn, moving slowly because of her ribs and because she was trying, for once, not to arrive ready for a fight.

Annabeth was no longer at the lake.

That was all right. Thalia could ask her where she'd been when they got back to the cabin. It would be a question, not an accusation.

She hoped she could remember the difference.

---

That night, Annabeth couldn't sleep.

She lay in her bunk, staring at the ceiling, listening to Thalia's steady breathing from across the room. The nightmare was still with her — fragments of Tartarus, the feeling of Percy's hand slipping from hers, the smell of sulfur and despair.

She got up and took her shoes from beneath the bunk. After the fight and the healer's orders, she needed a few minutes where Thalia wouldn't have to monitor her breathing or decide whether to follow.

The cabin was dark and quiet. She dressed quickly and slipped out into the night. The camp was still, the cabins dark, the only light coming from the dining pavilion's dying bonfire and the moon overhead.

She walked without thinking, her feet carrying her to the lake, to the dock, to the place where she and Percy had talked the night before.

He was already there.

Percy was sitting on the dock with his feet in the water, cold lapping around his ankles as he stared up at the stars. He didn't turn when she approached, but he scooted over to make room, and Annabeth sat beside him.

"Couldn't sleep either?" she asked.

"Never can. Not here."

They sat in silence for a while. The night was cool, lake-water and mint on the air, wood-smoke faint from the dying bonfire, and the stars were bright, and the water lapped gently at the pilings.

"Annabeth," Percy said finally. "I think we need to start investigating. Who changed the timeline. Why. What they want."

"I know."

"I think we should start with the biggest changes. The ones that don't make sense. Like why are there so many more big three kids in this timeline? Why is the camp built differently? Who decided to put a Hall of the Gods in the center of everything?"

"Those are divine decisions. Not mortal ones."

"Maybe they are connected," Percy said. "Or maybe the Hall was approved for reasons nobody bothered to explain to new campers. We should see the records before deciding who did what."

Annabeth considered that. "You think it's a god?"

"I think it's someone who understands gods. And monsters. And the way fate works."

"That's a short list."

"Too short."

They sat with that for a heartbeat. Then Annabeth said, "We should start tomorrow. We'll need to be careful — we can't raise suspicions. But we can observe. Compare what we remember with what exists here. Look for patterns."

"Agreed."

"And we can't assume whoever changed our lives knows we've noticed," Annabeth said. "It's a possibility, not a fact. If we start treating our fears as evidence, even the right clues won't be any use."

Percy's jaw tightened. "That's what I'm afraid of."

"Me too."

The wind picked up, cold off the lake, pine needles hissing like static, rustling the trees around the lake. Annabeth shivered, and Percy pulled her closer. They stayed like that for a long time — watching the stars, feeling the cool night air, trying to remember what it felt like to be ordinary.

At the far side of the lake, a watch horn sounded and another answered it. Annabeth folded their notes with the questions on the inside so they wouldn't get damp on the walk back. They still knew almost nothing about the rewrite, but they'd agreed what to ask for first. She could live with the uncertainty for one night.

---

The next morning, Annabeth went to Chiron's office with an ordinary question she hoped he could answer: how much storm training did a daughter of Zeus usually receive at twelve?

Chiron listened while she described the strange pressure she'd felt just before the drakon appeared. He set his quill down when she admitted the feeling had frightened her.

"You did the right thing by reporting it," he said.

"I didn't do anything with it. Thalia did the fighting."

"Your sister's control took years. You're beginning from a different place. We can arrange supervised exercises."

Annabeth had practiced the question for half an hour. The sensible answer should have relieved her. Instead she found herself studying the papers on his desk.

"You changed the training schedule when the Hall was built," she said. "Didn't you?"

Chiron followed her gaze. "The gods authorized additional facilities. There were new requirements for children of the Big Three."

"Who asked for them?"

"A council decision. Your father was among those who approved it."

"That's not what I asked."

"No," he said, watching her. "It isn't."

Percy appeared at the door. Annabeth had asked him to meet her afterward, not interrupt, but Chiron beckoned him in.

"I gather you have questions about the new buildings as well," the centaur said.

Percy stopped beside Annabeth. "They weren't here before?"

"Before you arrived? Of course they were. But the Hall is relatively new, and the archives surrounding its construction aren't complete."

Annabeth kept her hands on her knees. "Records missing from Chiron's own camp? That's odd."

"It is. I have been attempting to reconstruct the approval process for some time."

"Because someone took them?" Percy asked.

"Because I don't know where they went," Chiron corrected. "I won't accuse anyone without evidence. Three years ago, the council became much more interested in this camp. The building work followed. That is what I can tell you."

Annabeth wanted to ask whether he knew anything about her memories. She did not. Chiron was an experienced teacher, not a person who could read the history she kept in her head.

"What do you want us to do?" she asked.

"Learn to use the abilities you have. Leave the investigation of stolen records to me. And if your powers frighten you, tell someone before they hurt you or a camper standing nearby."

Percy made a small noise. "You know we're going to ask more questions."

"I assumed as much," Chiron said. "I also assumed you might try asking them in daylight."

Annabeth took that as a warning and an invitation. She thanked him for his time. Outside, Percy waited until the office door had closed before asking whether she had learned anything.

She had. The construction was real and documented. The records were incomplete. Neither fact proved anyone had rewritten a childhood, and Chiron hadn't been told that they remembered different ones.

---

They left Chiron's office in silence, processing what they'd learned. The corridor was quiet, the camp's daily noise muffled by distance and stone.

"Sanctioned," Annabeth said finally. "The gods approved the buildings."

"Yeah."

"That doesn't tell us who changed the history. The council may have been responding to something they don't understand either."

"Or some of them know more than they're saying. We can't tell yet."

"But what's the plan? What are they preparing for?"

Percy didn't know. The questions were stacking up faster than the answers.

"We need to keep looking," he said. "Keep comparing what we remember to what exists. Keep asking questions."

"And keep not telling anyone the full truth," Annabeth added. "Because we can't afford to be seen as threats."

"Exactly."

They walked in silence for a while, then Percy said, "Can I ask you something?"

"Anything."

"How are you really doing? With the sister thing. With Thalia."

Annabeth was quiet for a long time. "I'm managing," she said. "I try to respond to things she says the way I should. I try to act like I remember what she tells me about our childhood. But Percy, it's exhausting. It's like acting in a play where you don't know the lines, and everyone else does."

"Do you want to tell her the truth?"

"Some days more than anything in the world. But if I tell her the truth — the whole truth — she'll think I'm insane. Or worse, she'll think her sister is gone and there's just some stranger wearing her face."

"It's not that bad—"

"It's that bad. Because some days I'm not sure she's wrong." Annabeth's voice cracked. "Some days I don't know who I'm anymore. I remember being Athena's daughter, but my hands answer to Zeus now, and both facts feel real, and I can't — I can't tell which one is me."

Percy stopped walking. Turned to face her. Took her hands.

"You're Annabeth," he said. "You're the girl I fell in love with. You're smart and brave and you care about people too much. That's not a timeline thing. That's a *you* thing."

"Percy—"

"And you're also the girl Thalia raised. You're her sister. You've twelve years of history that you can't remember but that shaped who this version of you became." He held her gaze. "Both of those things are true. Both of those things are you. You don't have to choose."

Annabeth looked at him for a long moment, and something in her expression shifted. Not a smile. Something deeper. Something that looked like relief at hearing a familiar reassurance.

"Thank you," she whispered.

"Always."

She leaned up and kissed his cheek — soft and brief. Percy's heart did complicated things in his chest.

"Let's go," she said, stepping back. "We have work to do."

"Work to do."

And they walked on, together, into the mystery of a timeline that hadn't been built for them.

---

Annabeth spent the afternoon learning how little a promise and a good idea had in common.

The practice ground Chiron chose was not the arena. It was a strip of bare earth behind the stables, away from the weapons racks and the creek, with a low stone wall on one side and an open patch of sky overhead. He had brought a weather vane, a small wooden stand and a box of paper squares.

Thalia arrived carrying a length of copper wire.

Chiron looked at it.

"No," he said.

"I was only going to use it as a marker."

"Your sister is not learning to throw lightning through a wire."

"That wasn't the plan."

"The fact you have to explain it makes me less comfortable."

Thalia tucked the wire into her back pocket. Annabeth pretended not to smile.

Their first lesson had been meant for the morning of the drakon attack. Thalia had offered it after the damaged shutter, after Annabeth admitted the sparks in her hands. Then a monster came across the hill, Annabeth almost got herself killed, and the rest of the day had disappeared into an infirmary, reports and a lecture from three different counselors.

They were trying again.

"Before we begin," Chiron said, "I want to know what you're trying to accomplish."

"Control," Annabeth answered.

"Of what?"

"The electricity. The air, I suppose. The pressure changes."

"That's three things. Choose one."

She looked at the weather vane. Its little arrow twitched in a weak breeze.

"Move it."

Thalia set the paper box down. "It's windy already. How will you know you did it?"

"I could record its angle before and after."

"And how would you know the wind didn't change?"

Annabeth hated how pleased she was to hear a good objection.

"Put it inside something transparent," she said. "A lantern with the glass removed from one side. That would keep most of the wind out but give the air somewhere to go."

Chiron nodded, although he hadn't brought a lantern.

Thalia went to the stable door and returned with an empty glass-sided case used for keeping grooming brushes dry. It was cracked along one edge. She placed the vane inside, leaving a gap under the lid. Annabeth examined the setup and moved the stand until she was satisfied it wasn't touching the glass.

"Now can I try?"

"You can," Chiron said. "After you take off the metal bracelets."

Annabeth looked at her wrists. She had forgotten the little bronze clasp Thalia had fixed for her last winter, or rather had forgotten that she'd been wearing it since waking up in the changed life. She removed it and held it out.

Thalia took it.

"You always forget that one."

Annabeth swallowed. "Apparently."

Chiron sat down on a low bench outside the marked practice area. He had his bow nearby, but nothing in his manner suggested he expected to need it.

"Start with breathing. Not deep breathing for its own sake. Notice how your chest moves. When you feel an impulse to reach for the power, tell us where the feeling begins."

Annabeth planted her feet. She'd been given battle orders by gods who expected her to know what to do before they said it. Being told to report what happened inside her own body was unexpectedly harder.

"Shoulders," she said at last.

"Good."

"Hands, too."

"Do you feel heat?"

"A little."

"Then do nothing with it yet."

Thalia leaned against the stone wall, arms folded. Her face was still bruised where the drakon had struck her. She had dismissed it that morning as a minor hit. Annabeth had caught her wincing when she bent to tie her boots.

Annabeth looked back at the vane.

She tried to make the arrow move.

Nothing happened.

She tried again, remembering the way the air had changed around Thalia's spear. The sensation was familiar enough to frighten her. There was an opening in her mind she hadn't known existed when she was Athena's daughter, something that responded to a thought before the thought became a command.

The glass fogged at one corner.

"Stop," Chiron said.

Annabeth let go of whatever she had reached for.

"That wasn't wind," Thalia said.

"No. Condensation," Annabeth answered. "I changed the temperature."

"You may have changed the temperature," Chiron corrected. "Or your hands were warmer near the glass. We shouldn't jump to conclusions."

She stepped back. Her hands had been six inches away, not touching anything. Chiron was right that the observation alone didn't establish the cause. She resented him for being right.

"Again?" she asked.

"After a rest."

"I'm not tired."

"You told me your hands were getting warm. That is information. A pause lets us find out what it means."

Thalia removed the case and set it on the bench. The little arrow settled crookedly on its axle. Annabeth looked away before she could glare at it.

"I could move the thing," Thalia said.

"Yes, you could," Annabeth snapped.

Thalia straightened.

"That's not what I meant."

"Then what did you mean?"

"If I show you how I pull air around my hand, it might help. That's all."

"I know you can do it. Everyone knows you can do it."

"And nobody knows you can yet. Is that what you're angry about?"

Annabeth wished Thalia had said something easier to dismiss.

Chiron took an interest in his notes. The distance between them and the practice area was small, but he was leaving the decision to the girls.

"I'm angry because you make it look easy," Annabeth admitted. "I know you've been practicing for years, and I know I can't learn all of it in one afternoon. But I hate standing here waiting for my hands to do something when I used to know exactly how to make myself useful."

Thalia's answer came out sharper than she intended. "Then be angry about learning. Don't act like I'm showing off every time I try to help you. I want you to get better at this, Annabeth. I don't need to win a competition against my own sister."

"I know." Annabeth looked away from the glass case. "I wasn't being fair to you."

Annabeth's chest tightened. She could have ended the conversation by apologizing and asking Thalia to demonstrate. That was probably the sensible option. But all morning she'd been forced to accept that her memories of training did not give her the control she wanted, and that frightened her more than she wanted to admit in front of anyone.

"I know what to do when something attacks me," she said. "Or I used to. Now I can't even move a piece of paper."

Thalia's anger faded into concentration.

"You're comparing two different skills."

"I know."

"You don't act like you know."

Chiron cleared his throat.

"Annabeth, I would like you to observe while Thalia demonstrates, but your sister will keep the exercise to a mild movement of air. No electrical discharge."

Thalia held up her empty hands, making a show of compliance. Then she stood beside the little case and closed her eyes.

The arrow moved perhaps a quarter turn.

It wasn't dramatic. Annabeth expected a crack of thunder or a gust that lifted leaves. Instead Thalia made a small gesture with two fingers and the vane responded, slowly enough that Annabeth could see it beginning to move.

Thalia stopped.

"That's it," she said.

"Do it again."

"Not until you tell me what you saw."

Annabeth watched her.

"Your left hand was open. You weren't looking at it. You breathed out before it started moving."

"Good."

"And you weren't drawing the air at the vane. You were moving it past the vane, so the arrow followed."

Thalia's expression changed. She looked at Chiron.

"That's right."

"She notices," Chiron said mildly.

"I've noticed."

"No," Annabeth said. "You usually tell me what to copy before you let me look."

Thalia rolled her eyes, but there was no malice in it.

"Fine. Your turn. Tell me what you're going to do."

"Move the air past it, not straight at it."

"And what are you going to do when you feel your hands heating up?"

"Stop."

"Even if you think you can finish?"

Annabeth didn't answer quickly enough.

"Annabeth."

"Stop," she said.

They reset the stand and stepped away. Annabeth breathed out. Instead of imagining a blast of wind, she tried to picture the space just beside the vane, a narrow corridor through which air could pass.

For several seconds nothing happened. Her fingers began to prickle. She started to tense her shoulders, noticed herself doing it, and relaxed them again.

The arrow shifted a fraction.

She thought she might have imagined it.

"Did you see—"

"Keep looking," Thalia said.

The arrow shifted again.

Annabeth felt an instant of triumph so sharp that she reached for more. The air in the box swirled hard, knocking the vane sideways. Its paper fin tore loose and stuck against the glass.

Annabeth pulled her hands back.

The prickle vanished.

Chiron stood, not alarmed but attentive.

Chiron waited until Annabeth's breathing steadied before asking her to describe the moments before the paper tore.

"It finally moved, and I got excited," she said. "I tried to make the air go faster. I could feel the pressure building but kept pushing because I didn't want it to stop. Then it all happened at once. I couldn't tell the difference between a small adjustment and sending the whole thing sideways."

"That gives us something useful to practice," Chiron said. "Recognizing when the pressure starts changing, rather than only noticing when you've already passed your limit."

Thalia lifted the lid and rescued the torn paper square. She held it up by one corner, considering the damage.

"That's better than last night's shutter," she said.

Annabeth blinked. "Better?"

"Yesterday you didn't know you were doing anything. Today you made something move on purpose and stopped when it went wrong." Thalia looked at the paper. "I mean, the thing's dead. But the thing was a piece of paper."

Annabeth wanted to laugh. She did, once, then had to sit down because her hands were shaking.

Thalia brought over her water bottle and sat beside her on the wall. She didn't touch Annabeth's hands or ask whether the shaking meant anything terrible.

"You should've seen me when I started," she said after a while. "I blew the lid off a kettle. There was soup everywhere."

"Was anyone hurt?"

"Only my pride. And Luke's patience."

"Did you ever fix the kettle?"

"Eventually. We ate soup out of a pot for a month."

Annabeth looked at the torn square of paper. She could keep it and tell herself she had failed, or she could remember that the vane had moved twice before it broke.

"I'm going to write down what I did," she said.

Thalia handed back the bronze bracelet she'd taken from her wrist.

"That's my sister."

The words were casual. They caught Annabeth somewhere unprotected.

She tucked the bracelet into her pocket instead of putting it back on. She wanted to remember to take it off before the next lesson without having to be reminded.

When Chiron called an end to practice, Annabeth helped Thalia pack the glass case and paper box. She tried to lift the stand alone, discovered that its base was heavier than it looked, and held one end while Thalia took the other.

They carried it together.

Neither of them made anything of that.

---

That evening, Percy found Annabeth's evening routine interesting — she disappeared every night at sunset and returned after an hour, smelling faintly of lake water and mint.

Annabeth caught him watching and shrugged without explanation. "Private time," she said. "Everyone needs it."

He let it go. He had his own private hours — the nights when the ceiling pressed down, the hours when the faces of people he'd failed in his original life flickered past his closed eyes. Those hours weren't something he wanted to share, and he understood why she wanted space for hers.

Two nights later, he found her in the strawberry field while looking for a quiet place to think. He nearly turned around, but she saw him first.

She didn't go to the lake. She went to the strawberry field — the far corner, past the amphitheater, where the rows grew wilder and the light was softer. She knelt between two rows and started picking berries, working with the focused efficiency of someone who'd been doing this alone for years.

Percy hung back, giving her space, but after a moment she spoke without turning.

"I know you're there."

"Sorry. Didn't know you were here."

"You're not eavesdropping. You're lurking. There's a difference." She turned, and her face was pale in the fading light. "Does Thalia know?"

"Knew what?"

"I was awake. Both times." She rose and walked over to him. "I was pretending to sleep so you wouldn't know."

Percy said nothing. He had no idea what else to do.

"My secret is that I hate sleeping," she continued. "In Tartarus, sleeping meant letting go of your hand. It meant trusting the ground and the air when neither was safe. Now I wake up and check where I am, and then I want to check where you are. I know we're at camp. Knowing doesn't always help."

She looked at him, and her expression was raw. "So when you tell me you're awake because you can't sleep, I understand. Because I've been there too. And because right now, you're the person who remembers being there with me, who knows why I can be so afraid of losing you that you can't let go of it, even for a few hours."

Percy felt something crack open in his chest. This girl — this impossible, brilliant, broken girl — understood. She understood him in a way that no one else could, because she was carrying the same fear.

"Yeah," he said. "That's exactly it."

They stood there in the strawberry field, two kids who hadn't slept properly in months, and the silence between them wasn't empty. It was full. Full of understanding, and grief, and something that might have been hope.

"Come on," she said after a moment. "Help me pick. If you're going to follow me, you might as well be useful."

They picked strawberries in the fading light, and didn't talk, and it was the most comfortable silence Percy had felt since arriving.

---

The next morning, Annabeth brought something to breakfast that made Percy's eyebrows rise.

Eggs, cooked in a shell that was definitely not a chicken egg. It was blue-green and slightly luminescent, and it sat on her plate like a small alien artifact.

"What is that?" he asked.

"Breakfast. Silena gave me one from the box her dad sent." She tore into it with her fork. "It's a sea serpent egg. Apparently they're a delicacy."

"You eat sea serpent eggs for breakfast?"

"Silena says she's had them before. We can share this one." She offered him a piece. "Here. Try it."

Percy bit into it. It tasted surprisingly good — salty and rich, with a hint of something like lemon.

"Okay, that's actually delicious."

"Most people say that." Annabeth smiled. "I mean, they usually say that after throwing up. So it's a compliment."

Their conversation was interrupted by a loud crash from the Zeus cabin — someone had apparently dropped something, or maybe broken something, because a second later Thalia's voice rang out, sharp with anger.

"That's it! That's the last straw!"

Annabeth sighed. "She's mad at someone again. This should be fun."

"Why fun?"

"Because you get to watch." Annabeth stood, abandoning her egg. "Come on. I want to see who she's fighting with."

They followed the stream of campers toward the Zeus cabin, and when they got there, they found Thalia standing in the middle of the common room, pointing at a boy Percy didn't recognize — a nervous-looking kid with curly hair and the perpetual expression of someone who'd made a terrible mistake.

"— don't care if it was an accident! You broke it! You broke the portrait!"

"It was already cracked!" the boy protested. "I swear, it was already—"

"You don't know that! You don't know anything! Maybe you broke it worse and you're just trying to cover—"

"Annabeth." A counselor — one Percy didn't recognize — was trying to defuse the situation. "Can you talk to your sister? Please?"

Annabeth's jaw tightened, but she nodded. She stepped forward, putting herself between Thalia and the boy, and said in a voice that brooked no argument, "Thalia. Walk with me."

"I'm not the one—"

"I know you're not. But you're making everyone uncomfortable. So walk with me."

They left, and the crowd dispersed, and Percy watched from the doorway as Annabeth led her sister away. He could see Thalia's shoulders, rigid with anger. He could see Annabeth's hand finding her sister's, squeezing gently.

He couldn't hear what they were saying, but he could see Thalia's shoulders slowly relax.

That was something. Whatever else Annabeth struggled with, she could still be the sister Thalia needed.

Maybe they all could.

---

Annabeth didn't let go of Thalia's hand until they reached the empty archery shed.

It was one of the few places at camp where nobody went during the middle of the day. The targets were stacked against the back wall, straw showing through old canvas. The door had swollen in its frame and needed an extra push to close.

Thalia stood with her back to it.

"He's not hurt," Annabeth said.

"I know."

"You shouted at him in front of half the camp."

"He broke our things."

"He broke a portrait that was already cracked. Accidentally, from what he said."

"You're taking his side."

"I'm telling you what I saw."

Thalia's jaw tightened. The bruise under her shirt was still bothering her; Annabeth had seen the way she'd held herself during breakfast. That didn't excuse the shouting. It did explain why her sister looked one remark away from coming apart.

"Did you see him put it back?" Annabeth asked.

"What?"

"The portrait. Did he try to put it back when it fell?"

"He picked up the pieces."

"Then he wasn't trying to hide what happened."

"You're very sure about a boy you don't know."

Annabeth nearly answered that evidence mattered more than whether she knew somebody. She caught herself. Thalia had spent a great deal of her life being told that she was unreasonable when she was trying to protect something. A lecture about logic would sound suspiciously like another dismissal.

"Why does the portrait matter so much?" she asked instead.

Thalia laughed without humor.

"It's the portrait of our father. In our cabin. I don't know, Annabeth. Maybe because that's where portraits go."

"But you hate that portrait."

Thalia stared at her.

Annabeth had seen it above the small shelf in Cabin One, framed in dark wood, one corner chipped. A severe-looking man in formal robes sat against a marble sky. He had gray hair, a square jaw and eyes the painter had made too bright. The painting looked more like the idea of Zeus than any actual person.

The first time Annabeth saw it, Thalia had told her their father used to appear with that face. The next time he'd come to camp, he hadn't looked anything like the painting.

"You know I hate it?" Thalia asked.

"You said so."

"Last month?"

"Earlier."

Thalia's expression closed. Annabeth had become skilled at recognizing the exact instant a question shifted from an ordinary conversation into a test of missing memory.

"I don't remember when," Annabeth said.

"Fine."

"It's not fine."

"Then what do you want me to say?"

Annabeth looked at the rows of arrow shafts on the wall. She could count the ones missing their fletching without turning her head. She couldn't count the times Thalia had looked after her in the childhood she didn't remember. Every conversation seemed to require her to choose between pretending to know and making Thalia hurt.

"I want you to tell me why you keep it," she said.

Thalia folded her arms and sat on an overturned crate. The wood protested under her weight.

"Because you wanted it," she said.

"I did?"

"When we first got here. You were little. You kept asking what Dad looked like, and nobody could give you the same answer twice. Chiron found that portrait in storage and said it was as good a likeness as any."

Annabeth imagined a child begging for a picture of her father. In the life she remembered, Athena had been a goddess who arrived rarely and somehow expected her children to understand the absence. It was a familiar hurt wearing different names.

"Did it help?" she asked.

"At first." Thalia rubbed her palms against her jeans. "You used to tell him about your day. The painting. When you got a new bead or learned a new Greek word, you'd stand under it and talk."

Annabeth's stomach twisted.

"That sounds embarrassing."

"You were six."

"Still."

"It was embarrassing," Thalia admitted. "You'd tell him when I was mean to you, too. I used to threaten to turn the thing around."

"Did you?"

"Once. You cried for an hour. Luke made me turn it back."

That memory was Thalia's alone. Annabeth could only listen, like someone being told what an old photograph showed.

"And he never came?" Annabeth asked.

Thalia looked up sharply.

"Sometimes. Never when you asked. He'd come in a different face and tell us we should have known it was him, then leave before we could ask where he'd been." She kicked at a splinter on the floor. "You stopped talking to the painting a couple of years ago."

"So you kept it for me."

"I kept it because it's ours." Thalia pushed back her hair. "We got to decide what went on our wall. Not him. Not the council. Us. That stupid portrait belonged to the cabin when we barely had anything else that did."

Annabeth looked at her sister and finally understood why the boy dropping the frame had made her so furious. It was not because their father had been honored. It was because someone had accidentally damaged an object that proved the girls had been here together.

The realization didn't make the boy guilty.

"He's still owed an apology," she said.

Thalia gave her an incredulous look.

"You're going to start that again?"

"Yes."

"He was careless."

"Then tell him to be careful. Don't accuse him of lying when you don't know."

Thalia stood too quickly, hissed as her bruised side caught, then pretended it hadn't happened. Annabeth felt her own temper rise.

"You can't turn every mistake into an attack," Annabeth said.

"And you can't act like being right makes it all right to tell me what to do."

"I'm not telling you what to do. I'm telling you you scared him."

"I've scared worse things than a camper who knocks over furniture."

"He's not a drakon."

Thalia went still.

Annabeth wished she hadn't said it like that. The memory of the drakon on the hill was fresh for both of them, and Thalia had been carrying the bruise from it while everybody else congratulated her. But the words were true. Annabeth wasn't going to take them back simply because her sister didn't like them.

"No," Thalia said at last. "He's not."

They stood in the silence that followed. The boards creaked in the wind.

"I didn't want you to get hurt," Annabeth said.

"At the hill?"

"At the hill. Today. Any of it."

Thalia rubbed a hand over her face.

"I keep looking around and thinking something else is going to change."

"Something already has."

"Yeah." Thalia dropped her hand. "And I don't know whether I can stand finding out what it is."

Annabeth didn't know what to say. She could admit that she remembered a different life. She could tell Thalia that the portrait belonged to a girl who had really existed, but whose years together with her sister remained inaccessible to her. That was not a secret she could reveal carelessly, least of all when Thalia was angry and tired.

"You're allowed to be worried," she said. "But when you yell at people because you're worried, they're still people."

"I know."

"I mean it."

"I know, Annabeth."

Thalia walked to the shed door and put her hand on the latch. For a moment Annabeth thought she was leaving without another word.

Instead she asked, "You think I should go find him?"

"Yes."

"With you?"

Annabeth was tempted to say yes. She'd been fixing conversations all day and could probably manage another. But Thalia needed to make this one without being led through it.

"You know what to say."

"Apparently not."

"Start with 'I shouldn't have shouted.' Don't follow it with a speech about how the portrait is important."

"That's the part I'm going to struggle with."

Annabeth smiled despite herself.

"Then make it short."

Thalia eased the door open. Daylight fell across the stacked targets. She hesitated, then looked back.

"Will you help me repair the frame afterward?"

Annabeth thought about the ruined corner, the loose joint and the peeling paint. Repairs were something she could do, not just something she could pretend to remember.

"Yes. But if we need new wood, we ask the forge for it. We don't steal it off the archery shed."

"That happened once."

"Did it?"

Thalia grimaced.

"That was a trap."

"It was a question."

"Sometimes they're the same thing with you."

She went to find the boy.

Annabeth stayed long enough to straighten the targets against the wall. As she lifted one, a sliver of old wood lodged in her finger. She set the target down and pulled the splinter free. The sharp little pain was almost welcome. A problem with a size and a cause and an obvious solution.

By the time she got outside, Thalia had caught up with the boy near the pavilion. He looked wary. Thalia's shoulders were stiff.

Annabeth couldn't hear the words from where she stood, but she saw Thalia stop talking and let the boy answer. He pointed toward the cabin, explaining something with his hands. Thalia listened. Then she nodded once.

It wasn't a perfect apology. Annabeth was too far away to know whether it was a good one. But the boy's expression had changed by the time they parted.

She walked down the slope to the cabin. The damaged portrait lay against the wall, the corner still separated from the frame. Annabeth set a folded cloth beneath the painted surface and began sorting the loose nails.

She would repair the frame with Thalia later.

She would not pretend that doing so gave her back the afternoon when six-year-old Annabeth had spoken to a picture and sixteen-year-old Thalia remembered her voice. But she could make sure the portrait stayed on the wall, if that was what both of them wanted.

The next time Thalia came into the room, she would have a place to begin.

---

Later that day, Percy asked Clarisse about the portrait.

"The one in the Zeus cabin? That old painting of Zeus?" Clarisse snorted. "Yeah. Some kid knocked it over when we were redecorating. It was already damaged, but Thalia acts like nothing matters more to her than that cabin's history."

"Did she really think the kid broke it on purpose?"

"I don't know. Probably not. But Thalia sees threats everywhere. That's how she survives." Clarisse shrugged. "She's spent her whole life watching for danger. You can't blame her for being paranoid."

Percy thought about the weight Thalia carried — her mother's addiction, Jason's disappearance, the responsibility of raising her sister. He thought about the way Annabeth had put her hand on Thalia's shoulder, the way Thalia's anger had dissolved under that touch.

"She's not paranoid," he said. "She's protective."

"Same thing, coming from Thalia."

Percy shook his head. "No. It's not."

Clarisse studied him for one held breath, then shrugged again. "Whatever you say, Jackson."

But there was something in her expression — a flicker of recognition, maybe — that suggested she understood more than she was saying.

And Percy was grateful for that. Because understanding, in the end, was what they all needed most.

---

A few afternoons later, Annabeth attempted a controlled exercise near the lake, with Percy standing well clear of the path.

She held a dry leaf in her palm. Chiron had advised starting with movement, not lightning, and she had spent the better part of an hour trying to push a current of air across the skin of her wrist.

"Nothing," she said.

"You're moving your hand," Percy offered.

"Brilliant observation."

"Trying to be useful."

The leaf lifted a fraction. Annabeth went rigid. The air dropped away again, and the leaf settled where it had been.

She tried to repeat it. This time a gust struck from the wrong direction, blowing grit into Percy's eyes and scattering the leaf into the grass.

"Sorry!"

"I'm fine." Percy rubbed his face. "I think your leaf got away."

"That's not funny."

"It wasn't supposed to be."

She crouched to pick it up. Her hands shook. She had commanded soldiers and calculated impossible odds in a different life; controlling one inch of air was apparently a new skill with its own humiliations.

"I thought having Zeus for a father would make it easier," she admitted.

"Thalia's been training at this for years."

"Thalia already knows I've started supervised lessons with her. I'm worried she'll think I don't trust her if she finds out how much I still practice on my own."

"Then tell her," Percy said. "You don't have to explain everything to let her know what you're working on."

Annabeth looked at him sharply. "I'll decide when and how to ask her, Percy. That's still my decision."

"Fair." He raised both hands. "For what it's worth, I'm terrible at the leaf part."

She almost smiled. "You're terrible at plenty of things."

"There she is."

They spent another twenty minutes moving the leaf a few inches at a time. By the end she could direct it toward a stone twice in a row. The third attempt sent it into the water.

Percy retrieved it with a finger's movement and placed it beside her shoe. Annabeth gave him a dirty look, then put the leaf in her notebook. A small, damp piece of evidence that she'd improved.

---

That night Percy tried to make sense of what he'd learned about the camp's new buildings. Chiron had said the council authorized them, but the paperwork was incomplete. That wasn't a conspiracy by itself. It was a question with an answer somebody might know.

He pulled a clean page from the back of his notebook. At the top he wrote *Hall of the Gods*. Below it he added the year it had been built, the missing authorizations, and Zoe's name. He hadn't spoken to her yet.

Across the room Clarisse was trying to get comfortable on a mattress that squeaked every time she moved.

"If you keep writing, I'll make you sleep in the library," she muttered.

"That'd be convenient. Then I could study all night."

"Exactly why you need to stop."

Percy closed the notebook. Clarisse was right about his sleep, although he had no intention of telling her so. He set the page where he'd find it in the morning and switched off the lamp.

In Cabin One Annabeth would be trying to sleep too. They had agreed to investigate together, but he could not do it for her. He'd ask whether she wanted to visit the Hall after breakfast.

He lay back. The walls of Cabin Three carried their steady underwater rhythm, and eventually he let it replace the list of questions in his head.
