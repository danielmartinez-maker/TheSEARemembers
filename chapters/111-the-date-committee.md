## Chapter 111: The Date Committee

Clarisse declared herself chair of the date committee while Percy was trying to rescue the last piece of toast from a plate of scrambled eggs.

She didn't ask whether there was a committee. She put her elbows on the Cabin Three table, pushed his cup away from the spreading egg yolk, and said, "You're taking Annabeth somewhere."

Percy looked at the cup. "Good morning to you too."

"Somewhere that isn't the lake."

"I like the lake."

"Everybody knows you like the lake. That's why it doesn't count."

Silena arrived carrying two bowls of strawberries and the expression she wore when Clarisse had begun a fight before she had finished breakfast. She sat across from them, set one bowl in the middle, and tasted a berry before speaking.

"Where is he taking her?"

"That's the first item," Clarisse said. "He needs options."

Percy gave Silena a look of betrayal. "You're both on this committee?"

"I'm observing," Silena said. "Clarisse appointed me secretary."

"I appointed you the person who stops him from choosing the lake."

"I'm not sure you know how secretaries work."

Clarisse reached across Percy for a strawberry. He moved the bowl just out of reach.

"I've been dating Annabeth for a long time," he said.

"We're aware," Clarisse answered. "We live with you."

"And we've seen you take her places," Silena said. "We just haven't seen you ask her what she'd actually like to do lately."

That landed more cleanly than Percy wanted it to. He had walked with Annabeth after the campfire. They'd worked on the shrine drainage channel together and stolen time at the library when other people wanted one or both of them. Those hours mattered. He knew they did.

He could also remember the last time he had planned something only because he thought it would make her happy, without checking. He had ended up holding a basket of bread outside a meeting she was already running. She'd come out amused and a little annoyed and told him, quite reasonably, that she loved the bread but wanted to finish what she was doing.

Clarisse watched him think and mistook the silence for surrender.

"Good. The orchard at sunset."

"No," Percy said.

"Fine. The climbing wall."

"You want me to take her to a place where the rocks are trying to kill us?"

"You're both good at it."

"That's not the problem."

Silena pushed the strawberry bowl back to the middle. "Stop giving him assignments. He's supposed to ask her."

"Then why do we need a committee?"

"We don't," Percy said.

"That is the least cooperative attitude I've heard all morning."

Percy raised his eyebrows. "You spent breakfast yelling at Connor Stoll about a spoon."

"He stole my spoon."

"It was the communal spoon."

"It was in my hand."

He laughed despite himself. Clarisse stole three strawberries while his guard was down.

Across the pavilion, Annabeth was sitting with Thalia and a pair of younger campers. A roll of drawing paper stood against her chair. She was listening to the campers describe a leak near the Zeus cabin roof, and she had already begun drawing something in the margin of her breakfast napkin. When she concentrated, she held her pencil closer to the point than anybody Percy knew. He could tell she was frustrated by the problem from the little crease between her eyebrows. He could also tell she wanted to solve it.

In the life they remembered, he had loved that about her. He still did. It made him reluctant to interrupt.

"Now," Clarisse said.

"What?"

"Go ask."

"She's busy."

"You are using her being busy as a reason not to ask whether she's free," Silena pointed out.

"That's different."

"Is it?"

He looked at Annabeth again. She glanced up, caught him watching, and made a small, questioning gesture with the pencil. Percy lifted his own spoon in answer. She rolled her eyes, smiling, and went back to the drawing.

"I can talk to her later," he said.

Silena took another strawberry. "Then tell her later. It doesn't need a speech."

Clarisse pushed her chair back. "All right. New committee agenda. We make sure he doesn't bring the same wilted bunch of flowers he once pulled out of the strawberry field."

"Those were wildflowers."

"They had roots," Clarisse said.

"They were alive when I found them."

"Exactly. You had no idea what to do with them."

That was unfair, mostly because it was true. Percy had stood at the Zeus cabin porch with a fistful of dirt and flowers, and Annabeth had calmly supplied a jar. They were still together. He considered that evidence in his favor.

Silena, who remembered the incident with painful accuracy, patted his wrist. "Ask her first. Then decide whether it needs anything fancy."

Percy stood with his plate. "This committee is dissolved."

Clarisse saluted with the communal spoon, which she'd somehow recovered. "Motion denied."

He took his dishes away before she could demand a vote.

---

By midmorning, Annabeth had heard about the committee from three different people, none of whom were actually on it.

Katie Gardner wanted to know if she needed flowers from the gardens. Connor Stoll offered to arrange music for a small fee. Grover asked whether the whole thing was a surprise and looked relieved when Annabeth said she hoped it wasn't.

She carried her roll of drawings up the Zeus cabin steps, set it on the porch, and found Thalia kneeling beneath the gutter with a screwdriver between her teeth.

"Your boyfriend's planning something," Thalia said around the tool.

"Apparently."

"Clarisse has a list."

"That's more concerning."

Thalia removed the screwdriver. "She says you're overdue for a date."

Annabeth planted her hands on her hips. "Overdue according to whom?"

"According to Clarisse. That should tell you everything."

The gutter sagged between two brackets. Water had stained the paint where it spilled over the edge. Someone had tried fixing it with a bent nail, and the nail had given up. Annabeth crouched by the lower step to check the angle.

"We need another bracket," she said.

"We have another bracket."

"Then why is that one holding on with string?"

"Because somebody borrowed the ladder."

"Who?"

"Your boyfriend's sister."

"Which one?"

"Both, depending on who you ask."

Annabeth took the screwdriver from Thalia, reached under the gutter, and found that the timber holding the first bracket was damp enough to crumble beneath her nail. The problem was worse than the visible sag. Refastening the bracket would only make it fail again.

"Don't climb on that section," she said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were looking at the roof."

"I look at things. It's a hobby."

"Thalia."

"Annabeth."

They stared at each other until Thalia leaned back on her heels.

"All right. No roof," she said. "But we promised the little ones their bunks wouldn't get wet again."

"They won't. We can use a temporary downspout until we replace the board."

Thalia looked dubious. "You can make one?"

"With a bucket, a length of pipe and somebody who can cut the end at the right angle."

"Sounds like a forge problem."

"It is a forge problem. Which means you should go get Beckendorf before you improvise another nail."

Thalia's mouth opened.

Annabeth held up a hand. "I'm serious."

"I know. You've been serious since you were about four."

There was affection in it and an old confidence that Thalia knew what Annabeth had been like when she was four. Annabeth didn't share that memory. She had learned to stop correcting the stories merely because they made her feel absent from her own childhood, but sometimes the absence still caught.

"Then you ought to know I don't like seeing jobs done twice," she said.

Thalia studied her face, then rose and dusted off her knees.

"Fine. I'll get Charlie. You going to spend the entire afternoon drawing pipe fittings?"

"I might."

"You have other plans, apparently."

Annabeth tightened the cord around the rolled drawings. "I haven't agreed to any plans."

"You know he's going to ask."

"I know there's a rumor about asking."

Thalia's expression changed just enough to be irritating. "You're excited."

"I haven't said that."

"You don't have to."

"That's not an answer."

"Neither is that."

Annabeth picked up the drawings. "I can decide whether I'm free without everybody running a meeting about it."

Thalia's teasing faded. "I didn't say you couldn't."

"You said I have other plans."

"I said apparently."

"Then leave it there."

Thalia glanced at the exposed rotten wood. It was easier to argue about a gutter than about Annabeth deciding how to spend an afternoon, and they both knew it. She went to the door and collected her spear from the hook.

"I'll find Charlie," she said. "Do you want me to carry the plans to the Big House?"

"I can carry them."

"I was offering."

Annabeth looked at her. "I know. Thank you."

It wasn't a perfect exchange. Thalia still looked as though she wanted to ask three more questions. Annabeth still felt watched. But when her sister headed down the steps, she didn't demand a schedule, and Annabeth didn't follow to reassure her.

She went inside, spread her drawings across the table, and made a note in the margin: replace rotten board before the next rain.

Then, because she had been thinking about it before the committee rumor started, she turned to a clean page and drew the amphitheater.

From the top row, the stage looked almost flat. From the lower seats, the columns seemed taller than they were. Somebody had chosen those proportions carefully. She wanted to know how.

And she knew who wouldn't mind sitting beside her while she found out.

---

Percy found the ladder leaning against the forge, exactly where Clarisse insisted it wasn't.

Charlie Beckendorf was bent over a square of copper sheet, wearing thick gloves and an expression of patient disappointment. He had penciled a cutting line across the metal. Beside him, a younger Hephaestus camper was making an impressive mess of some nails.

"Don't touch that," Charlie said without looking up.

Percy stopped with one hand halfway to the ladder. "How'd you know?"

"Because it's what everybody does when they come into the forge and see something they want."

"I was going to ask."

"Then ask."

"Can I borrow the ladder?"

"No."

"That was fast."

"It's got a loose rung. We're fixing it." Charlie raised the copper sheet to the window and checked the line against the daylight. "What's broken?"

"The Zeus cabin gutter. Apparently the wood behind the bracket is rotten."

"Annabeth find it?"

"Yes."

"She send you?"

"No. I heard about it from Thalia."

Charlie lowered the sheet. "Then she probably has a plan."

"She does. That doesn't mean I can't help."

"It means you should find out what help looks like first." He handed Percy a short length of pipe. "Hold this against the mark. Straight, not like you're trying to win a tug-of-war."

Percy held it. Charlie measured and made another mark. The younger camper tried to hammer a nail into the workbench, missed, and took a step backward before anybody had to tell him.

"So," Charlie said, "you're here about the gutter or something else?"

Percy looked at him. "What did Clarisse say?"

"Nothing. Silena came through earlier and asked whether I could make something that might survive being carried to the amphitheater."

"Oh, gods."

"That's what I said."

The forge wasn't hot enough to make Percy sweat yet, but he felt warm anyway. "I'm trying to ask Annabeth out. My sisters have decided this requires a governing body."

Charlie set the pipe down. He didn't laugh. Percy appreciated that for three full seconds.

Then Charlie said, "Silena once tried to make me a schedule for our repair shifts so I wouldn't forget lunch. She color-coded it."

"That sounds useful."

"I'd been eating lunch on my own for years."

"Did you tell her?"

"I did. I also used the schedule. Both things can be true."

Percy considered that while Charlie bent to inspect the ladder's loose rung.

"Maybe I want it to be more than the usual walk," he said.

"Then make it more than the usual walk."

"That's terrible advice."

"You're welcome."

Percy watched the copper sheet catch the light. "I don't know what Annabeth wants. I know a lot about what she used to want. I know what she likes now. But sometimes I think because we've already done so much together, I ought to know every answer before she says it."

Charlie tapped the ladder. "That sounds exhausting."

"It is."

"Ask her."

"Everybody keeps saying that."

"Maybe everybody's right about one thing."

Percy let the argument go. The younger camper had abandoned his nails and was trying to fit two square pieces of wood through the same round opening. Charlie stepped away to rescue the project.

A moment later, Silena appeared at the forge entrance with a basket over her arm. Her hair was tied back with a blue cloth; there was paint on her left wrist.

"There you are," she said. "Chiron needs the ladder after the gutter."

"Charlie said it's broken."

"Charlie said it would be ready after lunch." She looked at Charlie, who held up two fingers without lifting his head. "Two hours. Which means we have time to decide whether you're actually going to talk to Annabeth."

Percy dragged a hand through his hair. "I am. I was looking for the ladder."

"Yes. That's what made me come here. Your girlfriend wants to spend time with you and you're doing errands for every building in camp."

"Buildings are easier."

Silena's smile lost its teasing edge. "Maybe. But you don't have to earn an afternoon with her by fixing the whole camp."

He was about to protest when he saw the strawberries in her basket. "Those are for the committee?"

"These are for the kitchen. I've got a shift, remember? I didn't stop having things to do because Clarisse invented a committee."

"Fair point."

Silena took one berry out and held it up. "I can save some for you if you both decide you'd like a picnic. I won't choose where."

"Thank you."

"And tell Clarisse I don't have time to make the banner."

"She wanted a banner?"

"She wanted to know whether I could sew 'Congratulations on Your Date' onto a sheet."

Percy closed his eyes. "Please tell me you said no."

"I said she was welcome to learn embroidery."

She left him with a quick touch to his shoulder. He could hear her laughing at something Charlie said as she crossed the yard. The laugh wasn't about Percy, at least not entirely. Silena had a kitchen shift and a painting project and people she cared about who weren't him. She wasn't standing around waiting to be useful to her little brother.

It was a better example than any lecture.

Percy put the pipe where Charlie indicated, carried the repaired ladder to the marked rack rather than taking it, and headed toward the Big House.

He nearly made it before Clarisse stepped from behind a stack of timber.

"Progress report," she said.

"No."

"That's not a report."

"I haven't asked yet."

"Why not?"

"Because I've spent the entire morning doing everything except asking."

"Good diagnosis." Clarisse held out a folded square of paper. "I had ideas."

Percy looked suspiciously at it. "What's on that?"

"Options. The amphitheater. The orchard. The western trail. A sunset sail if Chiron approves it."

"Did you write 'sunset sail'?"

"Silena did. I wrote 'boat.'"

He took the paper. It was covered with corrections in two different hands. Clarisse had crossed out the word romantic so hard the paper had torn. Silena had written Ask Annabeth at the bottom and circled it twice.

Percy folded the list again, more carefully.

"Thanks," he said.

Clarisse blinked. "That sounded sincere."

"It was. But you have to stop."

"Stop what?"

"Deciding she's going to like something before she tells us. You can help me with the strawberries later. If she wants them."

Clarisse folded her arms. Her first instinct, visibly, was to argue. She was good at being in charge of things. She was less practiced at accepting a job with no rank attached.

"Fine," she said at last. "I'll be available for consultation."

"I'll alert the council."

"You think you're funny."

"Sometimes I get lucky."

He walked away before she could change her mind.

---

Annabeth found Chiron in the amphitheater, standing in the center of the stage and squinting up at the seats.

The open-air theater was quiet between rehearsals. A leaf had caught in the crack between two stone steps, and a row of empty cushions waited beneath a bench for someone to put them away. Near the rear wall, a piece of painted scenery leaned against a support column. The stage looked larger without actors and props filling it.

"You wanted to see me?" Annabeth asked.

"I heard you were interested in the sightlines."

"From different seats. The columns look straight from below but slightly narrower from the back row. I wanted to measure whether that's intentional."

"It was intentional once," Chiron said. "Some of the stonework has been repaired since then."

"Not always well."

"That is an accurate but impolite observation."

Annabeth almost smiled. Chiron stepped aside so she could see a hairline crack running down the base of the far column.

"Can you tell me whether this is cosmetic?"

She knelt, set down her drawings and examined the joint. The mortar around the crack was newer than the blocks on either side, and a fine line of pale dust had collected at the base. She didn't touch it yet.

"Has the gap changed?"

"I believe so. Only slightly."

"Then it's not only cosmetic. We need to measure it over time, and nobody should use the stage edge for heavy scenery until we know whether it's moving."

Chiron looked at her with an expression she was still learning to interpret. He respected her judgment, but he also knew she had a habit of assigning herself work until nothing was left for anybody else.

"I don't expect you to solve it this afternoon," he said.

"I wasn't going to."

He lifted an eyebrow.

"I was going to sketch the columns," she admitted. "And possibly come back with a plumb line tomorrow."

"That would be sensible. For today, an initial measurement and a note for the repair crew will do."

Annabeth wrote down the crack's width relative to the edge of her pencil, then stopped herself and drew a proper scale. She had no intention of turning a rough comparison into an official measurement. Chiron offered her a narrow rule from a nearby toolbox. She recorded the result and marked the exact place with a small chalk line that rain could wash away.

A breeze lifted the edge of her paper. She caught it with one hand.

"Do you know who built the first version?" she asked.

"Many people, over many years. Why?"

"It would be easier to understand the changes if we knew what they were trying to preserve."

"That is true of more than buildings," Chiron said.

Annabeth gave him a sideways look. "You're not going to turn my drawings into a lesson about destiny, are you?"

"Not if you don't turn a day off into a structural inspection."

"Point taken."

She gathered the pages. Chiron took the rule back and pointed toward the middle rows.

"Try the fourth bench. There's a useful view of the arch from there in the late afternoon."

"Thanks."

"And Annabeth?"

She stopped.

"You may put this problem down until tomorrow. Other people can carry part of it."

She considered telling him she knew that. It was the sort of answer she gave when the person saying it was Thalia.

Instead she looked at the chalk mark she'd made. Someone would need to keep campers away from the unstable edge. Someone else would need to check the crack after rain. There was a full list of work to do. She could not finish it all by herself.

"I'll leave the measurements with you," she said.

Chiron inclined his head. "Good."

When she came down the theater path, Percy was waiting at the bottom with nothing in his hands. No basket. No flowers. Not even the folded committee list.

He saw her drawings and offered to take the heavier roll, but he didn't reach for it.

"Hey," he said. "Are you free after dinner?"

"What for?"

"A date. If you want one."

Annabeth smiled before she could help it. "I do."

"Do you want to pick where?"

"Yes. The amphitheater."

Percy looked past her toward the very building she'd just left. "Really?"

"Really. I want to draw the stage when the light moves across the arch. It's different from where I was standing just now."

"You'll want me to sit still?"

"I wouldn't ask the impossible."

"Good. Because that would go terribly."

She adjusted the drawing roll against her hip. "Bring a blanket. And something to eat. I'll bring the pencils."

"Strawberries?"

"Yes."

"Okay. I can manage strawberries."

"One more thing." Annabeth glanced at the steps behind her. "I don't want anybody else deciding what this is supposed to look like."

Percy didn't pretend not to understand. "I've already told Clarisse."

"Did she listen?"

"Eventually."

"We'll see."

"We will."

For a moment they stood smiling at each other in the path, two people who had shared more years than anybody here knew and were still perfectly capable of being pleased by a simple invitation.

Then Annabeth lifted the roll of paper. "You can carry this to the library if you're coming that way."

Percy held out his arms. "I thought you'd never ask."

She handed it to him. It was heavier than he expected. He made an undignified noise, and she laughed all the way to the turn in the path.

---

Thalia came back with Charlie, two pieces of pipe, a new board and a complaint she had been saving since breakfast.

"She said no ladders," Thalia told him.

Charlie inspected the section of porch beneath the gutter. "Sounds like she was right."

"That's not the complaint."

"Then I'm glad we got that part settled."

They worked on the ground first. Charlie showed her how the replacement board would sit against sound wood on either side of the damaged stretch, then had her hold a brace while he checked it for twist. When Thalia tried to lift the old gutter before he was ready, he told her to put it down.

"I can hold it," she said.

"I know. I need to take the other end loose, and if you lift it now we'll dump a bucket of dirty water over the steps."

Thalia lowered it. A thin stream of water ran out of the seam anyway, dark and unpleasant. She stepped aside.

"Point made."

They had been working for half an hour when Silena passed on her way from the kitchens with a bundle of cloth tied under one arm. She paused at the bottom of the steps.

"How's the rescue?"

"Ask us when the roof stops trying to rot," Thalia said.

Silena looked at the new board and the brace. "Looks better."

"Did you come to borrow something?"

"Actually, I came to see whether you'd noticed the missing paintbrushes. I left them beside the porch yesterday."

Thalia looked around. The porch had been emptied to make space for the repair. She found the brushes in a clay jar under the stair, wrapped in a rag to keep the bristles from drying out.

"Here."

"Thanks." Silena inspected the ends. "I thought I'd lost them."

"You're painting today?"

"If Charlie finishes the frame. But he's apparently repairing every roof in the valley."

Charlie called from the ladder, "I can hear you."

"Good. I need the small hinges, not the big ones."

"On the shelf by the door. Labelled."

Silena looked pleased. "See? You can manage two things in the same afternoon."

Thalia tried not to smile. There was something quietly funny about Silena issuing instructions to a forge worker three times her size and then going on as though she hadn't. Charlie had not even argued. He'd simply told her where the hinges were.

"The date committee still meeting?" Thalia asked.

"It has no official members," Silena said. "Percy dissolved it."

"He did?"

"He looked very serious about it."

"And Clarisse?"

"Still believes she has authority over refreshments. I told her to bring the strawberries and leave the relationship alone."

Thalia glanced at the Zeus cabin doorway. Annabeth's drawings were gone. A few chalk marks remained beside the gutter, measuring the damage; Annabeth had been thorough even while being angry.

"I wasn't part of the committee," Thalia said.

"No one accused you."

"Your face did."

Silena tucked the paintbrushes into her bundle. "I was going to ask whether you wanted to come with us when we carry the basket."

"To the amphitheater?"

"Only to the path. They're doing their own thing after that."

"Clarisse's idea?"

"Mine. If we don't have somewhere to be, Clarisse will sit in the bushes and try to provide commentary."

That sounded painfully likely. Thalia could picture her standing behind a tree and giving Percy advice on how to sit down, as if dating were an arena exercise. Annabeth would know they were there within seconds.

"Maybe I should come," she said.

Silena's eyebrows lifted. "To keep Clarisse from interfering?"

"Exactly."

"Are you sure that's the only reason?"

Thalia turned back toward the gutter. It would have been simple to call the question insulting and end the conversation. She had spent years being the person who stood between Annabeth and things that could hurt her. She knew she could turn any decision into a safety concern if she tried hard enough.

"She's going to be alone with Percy after dark," she said.

"At the amphitheater," Silena answered, "with half of camp within shouting distance."

"I know where it is."

"You trust Percy."

"Of course I do."

"You trust Annabeth."

Thalia felt her shoulders tighten. "Yes."

"Then I don't understand what the problem is."

"The problem is that I don't like getting told I should stop worrying because she's growing up."

Silena didn't respond at once. She set the bundle on the rail, careful to keep the damp paintbrushes away from the wood.

"Nobody said stop worrying," she said. "Annabeth said stop deciding."

"That's a convenient distinction when you're not the one who had to make decisions for her."

"I think you're right about that."

Thalia looked at her, thrown off balance by the agreement.

Silena went on. "I don't know what it was like for you. I wasn't there. I know what it's like when people assume they'll be fine if I do all the work for them. It can feel worse when they stop needing you, even if you wanted them to get there."

Thalia studied the jar where the brushes had been. The clay was still stained blue along the rim. She wanted Silena to be wrong, but there was too much that fit.

"When Annabeth was small," she said, "there were days I didn't know what would happen if I let go of her hand. I used to count the steps between places where I knew we could get help. She doesn't remember all of it. I do."

Silena nodded. "That doesn't disappear because she wants to choose a date."

"No."

"But she hasn't gone anywhere. She asked you to get Charlie. She wanted you here working on her plan. You got to do something useful without having to decide the whole afternoon for her."

Thalia watched Charlie fasten the temporary spout in place. The first bracket took his weight. The second sat tight against the sound section of board. The next rain would test it; after that they would know whether the repair needed changing.

"I hate how reasonable you sound," Thalia said.

"I make a point of not doing it very often around Clarisse."

They laughed, and the laughter relieved something without deciding the argument.

Charlie came down the ladder. "If it rains tonight, someone should check the bucket. We can reroute the spout permanently once Chiron approves the materials."

"I'll do it," Thalia said.

"And Annabeth can approve the drawings tomorrow," he added. "Tell her I used the brace she sketched, not the nail she crossed out."

"She'll ask whether it was cut square."

"It was."

"She'll check."

"I know."

Silena reclaimed the brushes and started down the path. Thalia followed after collecting the tools, because somebody needed to return them. Halfway down the slope, she stopped.

"Silena?"

"Yes?"

"Tell Clarisse the basket stops at the bottom of the steps."

Silena smiled. "I'll tell her."

"And if Annabeth asks whether I've been spying, don't lie for me."

"Then don't spy."

Thalia sighed. "You're enjoying this."

"A little."

They walked on. Thalia was still worried. Annabeth was still her little sister, and the world hadn't grown safer merely because the younger girl wanted to make her own choices. But Thalia had a gutter to monitor, a repair to finish, and enough other people to care about that the entire afternoon didn't need to be built around watching Annabeth.

It was not a comfortable adjustment. She made it anyway.

---

Percy reached the kitchens to find Clarisse treating a basket of strawberries like supplies for a military operation.

She had divided them by size. The smallest berries were in one bowl, the largest in another, and a cloth-lined basket stood between them.

"Why are you sorting them?" he asked.

"Because half are bruised."

"They're strawberries. They're supposed to be soft."

"These have been sitting in a crate since dawn. The bad ones go into the kitchen jam. The good ones go with you."

He looked closer. The system made sense. Clarisse had done the work rather than tell Silena to do it, and the kitchen staff were getting the usable leftovers. A little bowl of sugar sat near her elbow.

"You're not putting sugar on them?" Percy asked.

"No. I remembered she's picky about that."

"She's not picky. She likes to taste things."

"Then she can taste these," Clarisse said. "Don't you have a blanket to find?"

"I have one."

"A clean one?"

"Yes."

"Does it have sand in it?"

"Does everything I own have to be inspected?"

Clarisse looked at him.

He looked back.

"Fine," he said. "I'll check."

She pushed a berry toward him. "Here. Sample."

He ate it. It was sour enough to make his jaw ache.

Clarisse watched his face and smiled in triumph. "Exactly. That one goes to the jam."

"You're enjoying your new power too much."

"I am very good at logistics."

"You are good at a lot of things, Clarisse. That doesn't mean you're in charge of my date."

Her smile disappeared. Percy hadn't meant to make the exchange tense, but he'd been holding that line all morning and he didn't want to lose it because she was doing something kind.

"I know," she said after a moment. "You made that clear."

He set the sour berry stem aside. "I'm grateful for the strawberries."

"You're welcome."

"And I don't want you standing in the bushes."

"I wasn't planning on it."

Percy waited.

She sighed. "I thought about it."

"Please don't."

"I won't."

Silena came in while they were looking at each other. She stopped at the threshold and judged the silence with one glance.

"Did the committee try to reconvene?"

"Nobody's reconvening," Clarisse said.

"Good. I've got a painting waiting for its hinges and no time to organize anybody else's evening." Silena lifted the cloth-lined basket. "These are ready. Percy, you can carry them. Clarisse, you can help me put away the bowls."

Clarisse opened her mouth.

Silena pointed at the empty bowls.

Clarisse closed it again and took one.

Percy picked up the basket. He had not expected having sisters to involve so much negotiation over fruit. He had also never expected to find himself hoping their voices would still be there when he got back.

"I'll see you after," he said.

"Have fun," Silena replied.

Clarisse rubbed her hands dry on a towel. "And if you step on her drawings, move out of the way before you apologize. Don't drip juice onto the page."

"Useful," Percy said.

"Some advice is useful."

"Even from a dissolved committee."

"Especially then."

He left before the argument could restart. Outside, the wind had shifted enough to stir the leaves on the road toward the amphitheater. He checked the blanket in his bag. It was clean except for a thread of blue wool at one corner, which he didn't see a reason to pull out.

The berries were in good condition. Annabeth had chosen the place. He was supposed to meet her after dinner, and there was no need to improvise a complicated surprise in the hours before that.

Percy took his time walking back to Cabin Three. For once, preparation felt more like making space than setting a trap for a perfect moment.

---

Annabeth almost missed dinner because a roof diagram refused to behave.

The temporary spout at Cabin One would hold for a night. Charlie had done good work, and Thalia had checked it twice. But Annabeth had changed one measurement on the drawing, then realized the new angle put the end of the pipe exactly where somebody carrying a full laundry basket would walk into it.

She rubbed out the mark, drew it again, and moved the bracket.

"You're going to wear a hole in the page," Thalia said from the porch.

"I'm moving the outlet."

"Charlie said he can come back tomorrow."

"I know."

"Then this can be tomorrow's problem."

Annabeth looked up. Thalia was leaning against the doorframe with her arms folded, but she wasn't blocking the exit or watching for Percy. There was a smear of dark wood dust on her wrist and the screwdriver she'd used earlier was sticking out of her belt.

"You sound like Chiron," Annabeth said.

"I'd rather not. He tells long stories."

"You do too."

"Mine are interesting."

Annabeth placed the pencil across the page. "Can I leave this here without somebody deciding it's finished?"

"Of course."

"Because I want to think about it tomorrow."

Thalia's face softened for an instant. "I'll leave it exactly where you put it. Unless it starts raining through the roof again, in which case I might move it out of the puddle."

"That seems reasonable."

They went down to the dining pavilion together. Thalia talked about Charlie's repair and Silena's insistence that they use the proper screws, then let Annabeth describe a sightline experiment she'd been planning. Annabeth expected a joke about how architecture was a strange choice for a date. Thalia didn't make one. She only asked whether the old amphitheater had any dangerous sections.

"The edge of the stage," Annabeth said. "Chiron knows. He's going to cordon it off."

"Good."

"You're not coming to check?"

"Why? Do you need me to?"

"No."

"Then I'm not coming to check."

Annabeth tried not to make the answer more important than it was. She was glad anyway.

At the pavilion, Clarisse was engaged in a whispered argument with Percy about whether carrying a basket in his lap looked ridiculous. Silena sat beside them with an orange smear of paint on the cuff of her shirt, eating as if the fight had nothing to do with her.

Percy saw Annabeth and immediately moved the basket under the table.

Annabeth narrowed her eyes.

"It's not a surprise," he said.

"Then why are you hiding it?"

"Because Clarisse thinks everybody needs to inspect my picnic."

Clarisse leaned forward. "You can tell the strawberries are good just by looking at them. I did the sorting."

"I believe you," Annabeth said. "Percy, you're allowed to put the basket on the bench."

He did, looking relieved. Then he looked at Annabeth's empty hands.

"Where are your drawings?"

"At the cabin. I'm picking up a smaller sketchbook after dinner."

"Can I carry that?"

"You can carry the strawberries."

"I think I've got that part covered."

Clarisse started to say something about his grip. Silena put a piece of bread in front of her, and the observation never made it out.

Annabeth sat with Thalia and told Percy where she wanted to meet him. The plan took less than a minute to arrange. There was no announcement, no toast, no vote from a self-appointed committee. Percy went back to his dinner, Clarisse stopped arguing long enough to steal a bite from his plate, and the conversation shifted to the evening's archery scores.

Annabeth liked that most of all. Her date could matter without requiring everybody else to stop living.

---

The amphitheater was empty when Annabeth arrived.

Evening light ran across the upper row and left the stage in a cool, slanting shadow. The benches held the day's warmth. Somewhere behind the back wall, a bird gave three irritated calls before settling down for the night.

She chose the fourth bench, as Chiron had suggested, and placed her sketchbook on her knees.

From here, the columns seemed evenly spaced. They weren't. She could tell by comparing the gaps against the arch behind them. If she moved three seats to the left, the narrowness became more visible. Three seats right, it almost disappeared. That was the interesting part. The design hadn't been built to look identical from every position. It had been built to give an audience an impression of order despite the awkward slope of the ground.

She was halfway through the third sketch when Percy arrived carrying the basket and a rolled blanket under one arm.

"You're early," he said.

"So are you."

"I didn't know when you wanted the light."

"That's considerate."

"I had a whole argument with Clarisse about the difference between being early and being suspiciously early."

"Who won?"

"I escaped with the strawberries, which I count as a victory."

He stood at the end of the row until Annabeth shifted her pencils and nodded to the space beside her. He spread the blanket over the stone, then sat down and examined the stage with exaggerated seriousness.

"All right," he said. "Show me what we're looking at."

Annabeth gave him the sketchbook.

He rotated it the wrong way first. She caught his wrist before he could turn it all the way around.

"That end is the top."

"I knew that."

"You were studying it upside down."

"I'm exploring different perspectives."

She laughed and tapped one of the drawn columns. "See this? The stone is wider at the bottom. Not much. Just enough that from this seat it looks straight."

Percy leaned forward, comparing the sketch to the real column.

"Huh."

"That's your expert verdict?"

"Give me a minute. I'm working with limited expertise."

He moved one seat along the row and looked again. Then he moved back.

"You're right. From there it leans. From here it doesn't."

"It doesn't actually lean. The thickness changes."

"So if I stood right against it, I'd see the trick."

"Exactly."

"Could you design it so somebody hiding behind it looked smaller?"

Annabeth stared at him.

"What?"

"You really do think of every building as a place to hide from monsters."

"Not every building. Sometimes I think of them as places monsters can hide from me."

She pointed her pencil at him. "That's a terrible way to appreciate architecture."

"It's an extremely practical way."

"All right, then. You tell me what you see."

Percy looked around again, this time without the joking exaggeration. Annabeth watched his eyes go from the stage opening to the row of seats and then to the aisle stairs. He was measuring distances, not with a pencil but with the old alertness she'd recognized in him from the moment they'd found each other again.

"Two exits on this side," he said. "One on the left. The back wall would make it hard to see someone coming down the service path, but there's no cover for the last few yards. If you needed to move a crowd fast, the middle aisle would jam."

"The middle aisle is the widest."

"Which is why everybody would use it."

Annabeth looked down at her drawing. She had marked the stair widths, but not the way people's instincts would concentrate them in one place.

"You're right," she said.

Percy turned toward her. "Are you sure? I was making it up as I went."

"No, you're right. The geometry gives you the available routes. It doesn't tell you which routes people will actually choose."

She added a few arrows to the plan.

"That feels suspiciously like homework," Percy said.

"It's not homework if I'm interested."

"That's what people who enjoy homework say."

She nudged his knee with hers. "Then stop being useful."

"Gladly."

He opened the basket. Inside were strawberries, a little packet of bread, two small cups and a folded cloth that Percy said Silena had insisted on adding.

Annabeth picked up a strawberry and examined it.

"If you're checking Clarisse's work," he said, "she'd like you to know these passed a very thorough inspection."

"How many did you taste?"

"The ones she rejected."

"How many was that?"

"Enough to understand why she rejected them."

Annabeth bit into the fruit. It was good, sweet toward the middle and sharp near the skin. She offered Percy another. He accepted and settled back against the bench.

For a while they ate without speaking. She drew two more lines, put down her pencil, then picked it up again because the shadow had moved far enough to change the shape of the arch. Percy didn't complain. He watched a late group of campers cross the far end of the path, then turned back to the stage.

"The top row would be a good place to listen," he said.

"For music?"

"For secrets."

"Percy."

"I'm not planning to spy on anyone. I'm saying you could hear everything from up there if the acoustics are as good as they look."

"How would you know how acoustics look?"

"I wouldn't. That's why I said if."

Annabeth stood, took her sketchbook and moved three rows higher. Percy waited until she turned toward him.

"Say something," she called.

"Something."

"Try being useful."

"You told me to stop."

She rolled her eyes. "Read me a sentence from your camp notes."

Percy pulled the folded committee list from his pocket.

Annabeth hadn't expected him to keep it. She watched as he unfolded the paper and squinted at the writing.

"'The orchard,' he read. 'The climbing wall. Boat. Do not bring wet flowers. Ask Annabeth.'"

His voice traveled cleanly up the stone, louder and clearer than it had any right to. Annabeth grinned. The acoustics had not disappointed her. She moved to the far end of the row and asked him to try again.

"You're asking me to read my sisters' embarrassing date plan across an empty theater."

"Yes."

"You are enjoying this too much."

"Yes."

He read the list again. The acoustics carried it to her. By the time he reached Silena's circled instruction, Annabeth was laughing hard enough to lose her place in her notes.

"Ask Annabeth," she repeated when she came back down.

"Apparently that's the part everybody agreed on."

"They were right about that part."

"Don't tell Clarisse. She'll make it official."

Annabeth sat beside him and took the paper. She didn't read the rest. She folded it along its existing creases and handed it back.

"You can keep that if you want," she said.

"For evidence?"

"For something to laugh about later."

He slipped it into his pocket.

She picked up her pencil, tested its point and found that it had worn flat. Percy offered her one from the small bundle she'd asked him to carry. It wasn't sharpened perfectly, but it was serviceable.

"You did bring the pencils," she said.

"You asked me to."

He said it with no particular pride. That was the thing she liked. He had listened and done an ordinary job, and he didn't expect the job to turn him into someone else. She leaned against his shoulder for a moment before going back to the drawing.

---

They moved down to the lower rows as the sun sank.

From the front, the stage looked smaller but the back wall looked taller. Annabeth had to tilt the page to show Percy what she meant. He held one corner steady while she drew, and when a breeze came through the open arch, he moved the basket so it couldn't tip.

She was focused on the relationship between the arch and the rear columns when she noticed the chalk mark Chiron had left near the cracked base.

A younger camper was approaching the side of the stage, following a lost wooden practice knife that had skidded under the first row. He was looking at the ground, not at the warning rope.

Annabeth stood so quickly her pencil rolled off her knee.

"Wait," she called.

The camper stopped.

"Not that way. That stage edge is closed."

"I dropped my knife."

"I can see it. Come around the aisle. You don't need to step up there."

He hesitated, then did as she asked. Percy retrieved the wooden knife from beneath the bench and brought it over. The camper took it with an embarrassed thank-you and ran back down the path.

Annabeth watched him go. The warning rope Chiron had ordered was tied to the railing, but it had sagged low enough that a child could miss it in the dim light.

"We should fix that," she said.

Percy followed her gaze. "We could tell the night patrol."

"Or I could move the rope now."

"Can you?"

"Yes. I just need to tie it higher."

She looked at the sketchbook on the blanket. The light she had come to draw was going away. She could climb the steps and spend the next twenty minutes adjusting the rope, then discover two more problems to solve because the theater was full of them.

Percy didn't tell her to leave it. He didn't tell her to fix it, either.

"Do you want me to hold the end?" he asked.

Annabeth considered. "I want to make it visible. Not redesign the whole barrier."

"Okay."

They walked to the railing together. Percy held the rope while Annabeth lifted it and retied the knot above the lowest line of sight. She used a strip of pale cloth from the picnic basket to mark the center. Then they tested it from the path. The cloth moved against the darker stone and caught the remaining light.

"Good enough until Chiron replaces it," Annabeth said.

"Do you want me to tell him?"

"Yes. Tomorrow. And I'll note the crack measurement."

They returned to their blanket. A few more minutes had passed. The light across the arch was thinner now, but still there.

Annabeth sat down and drew it quickly. No careful revision, no second sheet. She recorded what she had wanted to see while it still existed.

Percy sat beside her with the basket between his feet. He didn't try to compliment her drawing while she was making it. He knew she hated that. He waited until she placed the pencil across the page and leaned back.

"Did you get it?"

"Most of it."

"Good."

She looked at the sketch. The arch wasn't perfectly proportioned, and one shadow should have been longer. It was, however, unmistakably the theater at that exact moment, the one she'd been trying to understand since breakfast.

"I think I did," she said.

Percy offered her the last strawberry.

She took it, split it with the small knife from the packet, and handed him the larger half.

He examined it. "You always do that."

"What?"

"Give me the bigger half."

"You always notice."

"Because you always do it."

"Then maybe I'm predictable."

"No," Percy said. "I think you just like pretending you don't know how much I eat."

Annabeth smiled. "I do know how much you eat. That's why I brought bread."

He laughed. She liked hearing him laugh in the theater, the sound returning faintly from the back wall. The building was doing exactly what it had been made to do: carrying a voice to someone who wanted to hear it.

---

Annabeth put the sketchbook aside when it grew too dark to see the lines.

Percy had stopped eating. He was leaning forward with his hands between his knees, looking toward the opening above the stage where the last stretch of daylight still showed through the trees. She followed his gaze and saw nothing but dark leaves moving in the wind.

"Did you want to do something else?" she asked.

He glanced around. "I thought this was what you wanted."

"It is. I'm asking what you wanted."

He opened his mouth, shut it, then looked at the basket. "I wanted to spend time with you without spending the whole time wondering whether I was getting it wrong."

"You haven't done anything wrong."

"That's not exactly the same thing."

Annabeth considered that. It would have been easy to tell him how much she liked the theater, how interested she was in the columns, how grateful she was for the strawberries and the pencils. All of it was true. It would also have answered a question he hadn't asked.

"What would you have chosen?" she said.

"Honestly?"

"No, Percy, I'd like a carefully prepared lie."

He grinned. "A sail. Not far. Chiron probably wouldn't have approved it this late, but I wanted to see the coastline after the lights came on. Maybe bring a lantern. There are places you can hear the water even when you can't see it."

"That sounds nice."

"Clarisse said boat, and I decided I couldn't choose it after she said it."

"That seems unfair to the boat."

"Very unfair."

Annabeth turned on the bench to face him. "Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because I asked you to choose, and you did."

"And now I'm asking what you would have chosen."

He looked at her, then looked down at the blanket as if it might provide instructions. "I don't want to make you feel like I didn't enjoy this."

"I wouldn't have brought you here if I didn't want you here." She tapped the sketchbook. "I could have drawn this alone."

"I know."

"But if you want to do something with me, tell me. Even when it isn't what I'd pick first."

"Okay."

"That doesn't mean I'll always agree."

"Trust me, Wise Girl. I know."

She felt the familiar pleasure of that old nickname, the one that carried more history than anyone in this camp could hear. She reached for his hand.

"Next time, show me the coastline," she said.

"Would you like that?"

"I think I would. I can draw a coastline too, you know."

"Then the date committee will have to revise its minutes."

"There shouldn't be minutes. There shouldn't be a committee."

"Tell Clarisse."

"I intend to."

He laughed, but she didn't release his hand. The conversation had left her feeling oddly shy, not because she doubted what he felt, but because she'd heard something he hadn't said before. She was good at planning around other people's practical needs. So was he, when he wasn't being impulsive. They were both learning that a plan could be generous and still leave somebody out if the planner never asked.

"Are you hungry?" Percy asked.

"Not anymore."

"Good. Because I ate most of the bread."

"I noticed."

"You notice everything."

"Not everything."

He followed her glance toward the opening in the trees. Beyond camp, the Sound would be darkening. She knew he could feel that water even from here. Lately he had gone quiet when someone mentioned Atlantis, not always sadly, but as if he were doing a difficult calculation in his head.

She could have asked about it now. He would probably have answered. He had promised to be more honest when he was afraid, and she knew he meant to try.

Instead she asked a smaller, present question. "Do you want to stay until the stars come out?"

"Yes."

"Then let's."

She leaned her head against his shoulder. Percy reached for her hand where it rested on the blanket and threaded his fingers through hers. They watched the theater change around them, the pale columns becoming darker than the sky, the open stage turning into a shape made of shadow. It was still the same building. Annabeth liked that it could look so different without being anything else.

There were sounds from the camp beyond the low wall: bowls being cleared at the pavilion, a late laugh from the path, the creak of leather armor as the night patrol changed shifts. The amphitheater itself remained quiet.

Then a metal cup struck stone somewhere behind the third row.

Annabeth sat upright.

Percy turned his head. "Was that a cup?"

There was a pause, followed by an urgent whisper.

"I told you not to bring it."

"You said we'd be out all evening."

"I said we could help carry the basket home."

"Then why are we hiding?"

"Because you ducked first."

Annabeth closed her eyes. "Oh, you've got to be kidding."

From behind the nearest column, Clarisse called, "We're not spying!"

Silena followed a moment later. "We are absolutely not hiding behind a column!"

Percy made a noise that was half laugh and half groan. Annabeth rose.

"Then come out," she said.

The sisters emerged into the aisle. Clarisse had a small tin in one hand and an expression that suggested the entire incident was someone else's fault. Silena held her paintbrush bundle and looked mortified.

"We were going back to the forge," Silena said.

"The forge is the other way," Annabeth pointed out.

"We took the scenic route."

"Through a theater you have no reason to be in?"

Clarisse gestured with the tin. "He forgot the extra cups."

"He already has two cups."

"Then he forgot the spare cups."

Percy stood beside Annabeth. "Clarisse."

"What?"

"You said you wouldn't."

Clarisse's jaw set. "I didn't sit in the bushes."

"No," Percy said. "You hid behind a column."

Silena looked at the floor. "We should go."

"We should," Clarisse agreed, but she didn't move. She was looking at Percy, and Annabeth could tell she felt stung by the seriousness of his tone. Clarisse was accustomed to being the person who caught mistakes before anyone else could be hurt. It was harder for her to hear that her attention was sometimes a mistake of its own.

Annabeth didn't want to punish someone for caring. She did want her evening back.

"I don't need an audience," she said. "Neither does Percy."

"We weren't watching the whole time," Clarisse said.

Annabeth stared at her.

"Not the whole time," Clarisse repeated, less confidently.

"That's not helping," Silena murmured.

"We had an arrangement," Percy said. "You helped with the food. I said thank you. Then you agreed to leave us alone."

Clarisse looked from him to the empty basket. Her shoulders dropped a fraction.

"All right," she said. "I said I wouldn't come. I came anyway."

"Why?"

"Because you're my brother and I wanted to know if you were having a good time."

Percy shifted his weight. Annabeth knew he was tempted to reassure her, to make the awkwardness disappear because it was embarrassing for everybody. She squeezed his hand once, and he stayed quiet long enough for Clarisse to continue.

"That was stupid," Clarisse said, almost under her breath. "You can tell me tomorrow if you had a good time. You can also not tell me."

Silena looked relieved. "We really are going now."

"Yes," Annabeth said. "You are."

Clarisse lifted the tin. "Do you want the cups?"

"No," Percy and Annabeth said together.

"Fine."

She started up the aisle. Silena lingered just long enough to catch Annabeth's eye.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I knew the difference between dropping something off and coming to check. I came to check."

"Thanks for saying it."

"We'll see you in the morning."

"Goodnight, Silena."

Silena followed her sister out. Their footsteps faded, followed by another whispered argument. This one concerned whose idea the scenic route had been.

Annabeth sat down again. Percy picked up the fallen pencil and placed it beside her sketchbook.

"They're terrible at this," she said.

"They care."

"I know," she answered. "That's what makes it difficult."

He lowered himself onto the blanket. "I was afraid you'd want to leave."

"For a minute I did."

"And now?"

"Now I want to see whether there's anything left of the evening."

He pointed up. The first star had appeared above the center of the stage, bright in the narrow band of open sky.

"One thing," he said.

Annabeth looked at it. She'd been so busy measuring the structure earlier that she hadn't considered what the structure might frame once the light was gone. The builders couldn't have known which particular star would appear there tonight, but they had made a space where someone could notice it.

"All right," she said. "That's worth staying for."

Percy relaxed beside her. After a while, she rested against him again.

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"You just did."

"Very funny."

"I learned from the worst."

He reached for the blanket and pulled its loose edge over her knees. "Did this count as a date? Even with the building inspection and my sisters throwing cups at the floor?"

"Yes."

"Good."

"But next time I pick the food and you pick the place."

"Deal."

"And we tell Clarisse about it afterward."

"That might require a security detail."

Annabeth laughed. "Don't give her ideas."

The stars multiplied while they sat there. Percy kissed her once, gentle and familiar, and didn't treat the kiss as proof that the evening had succeeded. She liked that. The night had included useful work, a disagreement, some very good strawberries and one terrible intrusion. None of those things needed to vanish for her to have enjoyed it.

Eventually they collected the cups and folded the blanket. Percy offered to carry the sketchbook. Annabeth handed it over after closing it carefully, making sure the charcoal wouldn't smear against the opposite page.

At the end of the aisle, she took another look at the stage. The chalk mark showed faintly beneath the warning cloth. She would tell Chiron about the rope tomorrow and ask him to replace it properly. Tonight there was nothing else she needed to do there.

She took Percy's hand and led him out.

---

By the time Percy reached Cabin Three, Clarisse had arranged the rejected strawberries into a bowl on the table and was pretending to read a training schedule.

Silena was at the other worktable, fitting a painted frame around a small square of canvas. Her hinges lay in a neat row. Charlie had cut them exactly to size.

Percy put the basket down.

"Was the theater still standing when you left?" Clarisse asked without looking up.

"Yes."

"Good."

He set the extra cups beside the sink. Silena glanced over the top of her frame.

"How was it?"

He considered giving them a complete report. Clarisse would have wanted to know what Annabeth had eaten, whether he had remembered the blanket, whether the seats had been comfortable. Silena would ask fewer questions, but she'd notice the answers he wasn't ready to give. He didn't want to turn a private evening into a committee's performance review.

"It was good," he said. "Annabeth finished a sketch she'd been wanting to do. We're planning another outing."

Clarisse put down the schedule. "Where?"

"We haven't decided."

"Good," Silena said, before Clarisse could ask anything else.

Percy turned to Clarisse. "Thank you for sorting the berries."

"You're welcome."

"And thanks for apologizing."

She made a face. "Don't make it a ceremony."

"I won't."

Silena fitted the first hinge into its recess. It stuck. She pulled it out, checked the screw length, then tried a different one. Percy watched for a moment.

"Need help?" he asked.

"No. I need the right screw. Charlie gave me three sizes, and I thought I could tell which one it was without testing them."

"Want me to hold the frame?"

"Yes, that would help."

He held it while she worked. Clarisse got up to wash the bowls from the kitchen and, after a while, offered to sand the rough back edge of the frame. Silena handed her the sandpaper. Nobody spoke about the date for several minutes.

It was exactly the sort of evening Percy liked coming home to.

When the frame was finished, Silena stood it against the window. She had painted the cottage near the strawberry fields, the one with the narrow porch and the crooked little steps. Percy could see the way the light fell across the doorway. A person in the painting had left a watering can beside the steps.

"That's Katie's place," he said.

"She asked me to paint it for the garden room. It's a thank-you for the herbs she brought when you were sick."

"You didn't tell me."

"It wasn't about you."

Silena sounded amused, but there was a point beneath it. Percy grinned.

"Fair."

"I have an entire life that happens while you're walking around with Annabeth, you know."

"I've heard rumors."

"Most of them are true."

Clarisse snorted from the sink. Percy helped carry the frame to a safe spot where it could dry without getting knocked over by somebody looking for a weapon. He turned in early, tired from spending so much of the day anticipating an evening that had turned out to be ordinary in the best possible way.

For a few minutes after the lamps went out, he could hear his sisters discussing the next morning's errands. Clarisse wanted to use the dry weather for drills. Silena needed to deliver the painting. Neither was making a list for him.

He smiled into his pillow.

---

Annabeth went straight to her table when she reached Cabin One.

Thalia was sitting on the edge of her bunk with a book open and her boots off. The roof diagram lay exactly where Annabeth had left it, except for a clean scrap of paper placed beneath the corner that had been trying to curl.

"You're back," Thalia said.

"I am."

"How was the amphitheater?"

"Good."

Thalia waited. Annabeth began setting out the sketches.

"I got the light I wanted," she said. "Percy brought strawberries. The back row has better acoustics than I expected. There's a cracked column that needs a proper barrier."

"That last bit sounds less like a date."

"It wasn't the whole date."

"I wasn't implying anything."

Annabeth looked over at her sister. Thalia had made no attempt to leave the cabin and check on her. That should have been unremarkable. It wasn't quite yet.

"Clarisse and Silena came," Annabeth said.

Thalia's book slid down to her knees. "They what?"

"To the theater. With cups."

"Of course they did."

"They apologized. Mostly."

"Did you tell them not to do it again?"

"Yes."

"Good."

Thalia picked up her book, then put it down again. "For what it's worth, I told Silena I wasn't coming. I meant it."

"I know."

"You don't actually know. You could have thought I was behind a tree."

"I looked for you," Annabeth admitted.

Thalia stared at her. "You did?"

"At the beginning. Just for a second."

"Wow. That makes me feel both very predictable and a little insulted."

"It wasn't fair."

"No," Thalia said. "But I see why you looked."

Annabeth came to sit beside her. For a moment they both watched the moonlight move across the gutter's new spout outside the window. The bucket beneath it was empty.

"Thanks for fixing that," Annabeth said.

"Charlie did the hard part."

"You got him. You kept the work moving."

"That I can do."

"And tomorrow I want to redo the outlet angle before you fasten it permanently."

"I guessed you would."

Annabeth leaned her shoulder against Thalia's. "If you want to see the sketches from tonight, I'll show you."

"Really?"

"Really. They're not secrets."

"Your dates can be private without everything in your life being private," Thalia said.

Annabeth looked at her sharply. "You came up with that yourself?"

"Don't sound so surprised. I have thoughts."

"Sometimes very loud ones."

"Those are my best thoughts."

Annabeth laughed. She brought the sketchbook over and turned the pages slowly, showing Thalia the column proportions and the arch in the fading light. Thalia asked a question about how the steps would drain in heavy rain. Annabeth drew the path water took around the side of the stage, then caught herself before she opened a new page for a complete diagram.

"Tomorrow," she said.

Thalia closed the sketchbook and handed it back. "Tomorrow."

She did not ask for details about Percy kissing her or what they had said while they were alone. Annabeth didn't offer those details. Their conversation moved instead to the roof repair, a missing set of nails and the question of who had to return the borrowed bucket.

She went to sleep with the sketchbook on the table, the first drawing still faintly dusted with charcoal. A few things had been put right today. A few others hadn't, and would need another conversation or a bit of work when morning came.

She had time for both.

In the morning, the theater still stood, the rope was still tied above the stage edge, and the painted cloth marker hadn't blown away.

Annabeth took her measurements to Chiron before breakfast. He looked over the drawings, asked where she'd stood to measure the crack, and wrote a work order for the camp repair crew. She wanted to volunteer to supervise the whole job. Instead she offered to check the final repair against the original dimensions once the crew finished.

Chiron accepted. It was enough work to matter without belonging entirely to her.

On the way back, she found Percy carrying a stack of clean practice shields toward the arena. Clarisse walked ahead of him, counting them aloud and complaining about the way some campers treated their gear. Silena was on her way to deliver the painting.

Annabeth waited until Percy put down the stack.

"Morning," she said.

"Morning. How's the stage?"

"Being repaired by people who know what they're doing."

"That must have been hard to arrange."

"It was not my favorite part of the day."

He smiled and reached for her hand. She gave it to him.

"About the coast," she said.

"What about it?"

"I'd still like to see it after dark. When we can get permission and the weather's right."

"All right."

"And I might bring my sketchbook."

"I'd be worried if you didn't."

She looked past him toward the arena, where Clarisse was already directing the shield carriers into the correct line.

"You're busy."

"A little."

"Then I'll see you after drills."

Percy glanced toward the distant glimmer of the Sound. It was a brief look, but Annabeth noticed it. He turned back and squeezed her hand.

"After drills," he said.

She let him go. She had her own errands and another sheet of drawings waiting in Cabin One. He had shields to carry, sisters to annoy, and thoughts he would tell her when he was ready.

They had chosen an evening together. It didn't have to swallow everything around it to have been theirs.

Annabeth waved to Silena, nodded once to Clarisse, and headed up the path with her rolled plans under one arm.
