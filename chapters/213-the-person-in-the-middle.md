## Chapter 213: The Person in the Middle

Percy made the first boat from the corner of a list Chiron had already copied into the supply ledger.

Annabeth watched him crease the paper.

“That’s a path inventory,” she said.

“It had an empty back.”

“It also had a title on the front.”

“The title is still on the front.”

“You tore through the last two letters.”

“Then the title has a view of the water.”

The rain ticked along the Big House windows. Beyond them, the camp paths shone dark and flat, and the lower stretch toward the lake had become a long brown ribbon that no one was using until the ground firmed. Chiron had taken the route list inside before the ink blurred. Percy had come to return it and stayed because the kitchen was warm, Annabeth was already there, and she had a stack of scrap paper spread across the end of the table.

The paper came from a box of old lesson sheets, the kind with a single line of handwriting across the top and an empty back. Annabeth had been testing folds. Four boats sat in a row beside her elbow. None had touched water yet.

Percy set his list-boat beside them. It looked like a hat that had been forced to become a ship.

Annabeth picked it up by the center seam. “This is going to take on water before it reaches the first bend.”

“It isn’t going to a bend.”

“It has a bow.”

“It has a point.”

“A point that is facing the wrong direction.”

“Maybe it wants to see where it’s been.”

She put the paper boat down again, but the corner of her mouth had lifted. Percy took that as permission to sit. He pulled his chair closer, leaving the other side of her work clear. The pile of sheets was hers; he could ask before taking another.

“Are you making an entire fleet?” he asked.

“I’m testing folds. The first one twists as soon as the seam gets wet. The second is too narrow. The third—”

“Is there a fourth?”

“The fourth is why there are four.”

He picked up the closest boat and turned it over. “This one looks fine.”

“You haven’t tested it.”

“I’m evaluating it by sight.”

“Your sight is not a test.”

“Maybe I’m good at boats.”

“You are good at some boats.”

He looked up. “That sounded like a compliment with a locked gate on it.”

“It was a compliment with a boundary. The kind you say you like.”

He did. He could remember being on a ship with Annabeth, both of them in bodies that had been older, taller, tired in ways that had taken years to learn. He also knew this was a different life and this table belonged to this afternoon. He turned the paper boat once more and handed it back.

“What are you going to test it in?” he asked.

“The washbasin. It’s clean, shallow, and we can see if the fold leaks. No lake, no current, no pretending the basin is a map.”

“That last part was for me?”

“That last part was for both of us.”

Percy leaned his forearms on the table. “We could set up a little fan.”

“The room has no fan.”

“We could ask the kitchen for the bellows.”

“We could make a draft with a board. A steady push is easier to repeat.”

“You already have a test.”

“I have the beginning of one.” She rotated a boat and used a pencil to mark a tiny line near the bottom. “We can see whether it floats with an empty cargo square and with one coin.”

“Is this a date?”

“Do you want it to be?”

“I thought it was.”

“Then yes.” She tapped the second boat. “It’s a date with a basin.”

“Good. I’ve had worse.”

She nudged the coin toward him. He slipped it into his palm instead of testing it, and she gave him a look.

“Don’t steal the cargo,” she said.

“I’m protecting it.”

“From the boat?”

“From the water.”

She took the coin back. “I can protect my own test.”

“I know.”

“I mean it.”

“So do I.”

She slid the coin into the paper boat. It held the weight for three seconds before one side sagged. Percy laughed once, not loud enough to draw anyone from the hall.

“First voyage,” he said. “It has a leak.”

“First basin test,” Annabeth corrected. “It has a low side. I can fix the fold.”

“Can I help?”

“You can hold the paper flat while I mark the crease.”

He held the opposite edge. Her fingers pressed the seam into place. The work was so small that neither of them had to talk over it. When she finished, her hand brushed his. Percy turned his palm up, and she rested two fingers there for a moment before pulling away to check the fold.

They had been a couple before this life made them younger and moved their families around them. Some afternoons still made the history between them feel like an extra chair at the table: present, real, and not always useful to point at. Percy didn’t need to ask whether she wanted to sit beside him. She had chosen the chair herself.

The boats waited in a row, their pointed bows all turned toward the basin.

“You said we could take a short break,” Annabeth said.

“I did.”

“This is the break.”

“I know.”

“You’re still looking at the door.”

“I’m looking at the room.”

“The door is in the room.”

“You’re very good at this.”

“Architecture?”

“Finding the exact sentence I’m trying not to say.”

She set the coin beside the boats. “What sentence?”

He glanced at the wet tracks on the window. “I was thinking about how Leandros left yesterday. He had those current notes and that map argument, and he looked like he wanted somebody to tell him what it meant.”

“Did he ask you?”

“Not exactly.”

“Then don’t decide what he wants before he says it.”

“I was going to ask.”

“Good.”

The sound of boots came along the hall. Percy looked up as Leandros stepped in from the porch, shaking water from his sleeves. A thin notebook was tucked under his arm, protected by the front of his jacket.

“Is Chiron here?” Leandros asked.

“He went to the stables,” Percy said. “He took the inventory sheet.”

“I know. I have the duplicate.”

Leandros glanced at the table and the paper boats. His gaze stopped on Percy’s list-boat.

“That one won’t hold a coin.”

“It already failed,” Annabeth said. “We’ve marked it.”

“I could fold one that holds weight.”

“You can if you want,” Percy said. “The basin’s free.”

Leandros set his notebook down but kept one hand on it. “A basin isn’t a river.”

“No,” Annabeth said. “That’s why we’re using a basin.”

He looked from her to Percy. “You always make a test this small?”

“When I want the answer to stay small,” Annabeth said.

Percy saw Leandros consider that, then look down at the notebook. He pulled out a single page where two current arrows had been drawn with times beside them. The notes were separate from the equipment route, just as Annabeth had asked.

“I was going to check whether the shift between the two readings matched the wind,” Leandros said. “But there’s no water outside that’s useful right now.”

“There’s water in the basin,” Percy said.

“Not the kind I read.”

“Then don’t read it. Fold a boat.”

Leandros’s eyebrows lifted, as if Percy had offered him an assignment rather than an invitation. He looked at Annabeth.

“The test is Percy’s,” she said. “You can join if you want. The basin stays here.”

“I know what a basin is.”

“I didn’t say you didn’t.”

He made a sound in his throat that was almost a laugh and almost not. “Fine. I’ll make something that doesn’t sink under a coin.”

Annabeth pushed a clean sheet toward him. “Use this one.”

“I know where the scraps are.”

“That one is scrap.”

“I can see that.”

Percy caught Annabeth’s eye. She was watching the paper, not the boy, but he knew the difference between her ignoring a remark and filing it away for later. He set his hands flat on the table.

Leandros turned the sheet and began folding. His thumbs were quick, the paper corners lined with the care he used on rope. He pinched a narrow keel into the base, then widened the sides. Annabeth’s own boats had clean, even shapes. Leandros’s was lower, longer, and more angular.

“That’s a lot of fold for a basin,” Percy said.

“It’ll make the boat stable.”

“You’ve already decided?”

“I can see the balance.”

“You can see the paper.”

“And the balance.”

Annabeth rested her pencil on the table. “Let it float before you name it.”

“It’s a boat.”

“It’s a folded sheet.”

“It’s shaped like a boat.”

“Still not a result.”

Leandros ran a finger along the hull, smoothing the seam. “You’re all very careful with words.”

“We have reasons,” Annabeth said.

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

He glanced at Percy. “Your sister does that. Answers like the question is a test.”

“She’s good at tests,” Percy said.

“I asked her.”

“And she answered.”

Annabeth set the pencil down. “If you want to work with us, then work with us. You don’t have to like my answer.”

“I didn’t say I did.”

“Good.”

A second set of footsteps paused just beyond the threshold. Ianthe stood in the doorway with her weather notebook tucked to her chest and rain darkening the shoulders of her jacket. She looked at the table, then at Leandros’s folded sheet.

“Are you testing the boats against a draft?” she asked.

“We might,” Percy said.

“Then you should mark the direction first.”

Leandros tapped the paper. “It’s a basin.”

“Wind can still change which side moves.”

“You don’t have to direct the test.”

“I didn’t. I said what the test needs.”

“You said I should mark it.”

“Because you started a test.”

“I started a boat.”

“Those are not the same thing.”

The words landed harder than they should have. Percy could see it in the way Leandros’s fingers stopped on the folded seam. Ianthe crossed the room and set her notebook down beside the clean sheets.

“Which way is the draft?” she asked.

“The window’s shut,” Leandros said.

“That doesn’t mean the room is still.”

“No one said it did.”

“You were about to put a boat in water.”

“Percy and Annabeth were.”

Annabeth tilted her head. “I said I wanted to test the fold. I didn’t say I wanted you to set the conditions.”

Ianthe’s eyes moved to Annabeth, then Percy, checking whether she had stepped into a disagreement that was not hers. She didn’t take her notebook back.

“I can help with the draft,” she said. “If you want it.”

Leandros flattened his palm over the hull. “You don’t have to manage it.”

“I’m not managing it. I’m asking.”

“You came in and told us where to start.”

“I said mark the direction.”

“That is telling us what to do.”

“Then say no.”

“I’m saying no.”

“Fine.”

She took the pencil. “I’ll record my own observation from here.”

Leandros glanced at Percy. “This is what happens when someone brings a notebook.”

Percy looked at Ianthe. She was writing a small arrow on the margin of her own sheet, not the boat paper, not Annabeth’s test. Her jaw had tightened, but she kept her line straight.

“That’s what happens when we’re all in the same room,” Percy said. “People bring their own opinions.”

Leandros looked back at him. “You sound like Chiron.”

“He tells me I do that sometimes.”

“Do what?”

“Talk like the answer gets easier if I say it calmly.”

“That must be convenient.”

“It isn’t.”

Annabeth picked up the boat with the sagging side. “The first fold leaked. The second one will be tested without the coin. Then we can add it.”

“We?” Leandros asked.

“Whoever is still here.”

Ianthe finished her arrow and turned the sheet toward them. “The draft comes from the hall, not the window.”

Leandros lifted his head. “You didn’t check that.”

“I felt it.”

“You can feel air. That doesn’t tell you where it comes from.”

“It tells me where it reaches the table.”

“Those aren’t the same thing.”

“No. They’re not.”

For a moment they looked at each other as if the distinction had been a challenge and not a point on which they agreed.

Percy wished the room had a place for him to stand that wasn’t directly between them.

---

Annabeth turned the clean basin sideways so its long edge was parallel with the table.

“Each boat gets one test,” she said. “Same amount of water. Same place. We’ll mark the starting line with the pencil. First, empty. Then the coin.”

“Who is we?” Leandros asked.

“Anyone who wants to test a boat.”

“That’s what I thought.”

“What did you think I meant?”

“You say ‘we’ when you’ve already decided where everyone stands.”

Annabeth’s face changed by a fraction. Percy saw her recognize the complaint. She didn’t hurry to smooth it over.

“I decided where the basin goes,” she said. “It’s my test. I didn’t decide where you stand.”

“That’s not what it sounded like.”

“Then tell me where you want to stand.”

Leandros pointed to the far end of the table. “There.”

Ianthe shifted the weather notebook away from that spot. “I’m using this corner.”

He looked at her. “You can use the other one.”

“I got here first.”

“You put a notebook on it.”

“That's a way of saying I’m using it.”

“Then say that instead of moving things around.”

She drew the notebook closer to herself. “I’m using this corner.”

“Fine.”

“Good.”

Percy placed the basin in the middle of the table. He’d expected the game to be simple. Fold, float, sink. Maybe they would laugh when the list-boat opened like a wet mouth. Instead, every object seemed to arrive carrying a small decision about who could touch it.

Annabeth measured a finger’s width from the basin’s edge and marked the starting line on a blank strip. “We can hold a flat board over the water and move it once. No blowing on the boats. That makes the push different for every person.”

“I can make the air consistent,” Ianthe said.

“How?” Annabeth asked.

“Use the paper screen from the kitchen and push it the same distance.”

“Who moves it?”

“I can.”

“That means you’re choosing the force.”

“I can repeat it.”

“That still doesn’t tell us whether it’s repeatable.”

Ianthe’s mouth tightened. “I can measure the movement.”

“With what?”

“My hand span.”

“That’s not a measurement unless you mark the start and end.”

“I was going to.”

“Then say that.”

Ianthe looked down at her notebook. “I was going to.”

Leandros tapped his new boat. “The test can be simpler. Put it in. See what it does.”

“That will show what it does under one unrecorded push,” Annabeth said. “It won’t show why.”

“I’m not trying to write a report.”

“Then you don’t need a report. You asked to test its balance.”

“I said it would hold a coin.”

“You can find that out with no draft.”

Leandros lifted the boat by its sides. “Then we test the weight first.”

“Okay.”

The quick agreement seemed to unsettle him more than another correction would have. He held the paper boat over the basin, looking at the short seam he had pressed into the hull.

“Are you putting it in?” Percy asked.

“I said we’d test the weight.”

“Do you want me to—”

“I can put a paper boat in water.”

Percy leaned back. “Okay.”

Leandros lowered the boat into the basin. The bottom touched, lifted, and settled. A bead of water spread along the seam. The hull held. He placed one small coin near the center. The paper sides dipped but did not fold.

“See?” he said.

“It floats,” Annabeth said.

“It holds weight.”

“For this long.”

“Four seconds so far.”

“You want me to count?”

“No.”

Ianthe wrote something on her page.

“What are you recording?” Leandros asked.

“The test.”

“There wasn’t any draft.”

“There was still water.”

“Not a current.”

“Then I can write no current applied.”

“That makes it sound like the water was just sitting there.”

“It was.”

“It wasn’t a test of the boat.”

“It was your test. You put it in.”

“Why are you writing down something you didn’t do?”

“Because it happened.”

He picked up the boat. A darker wet line had formed down the seam. One corner was soft.

Annabeth held out a hand. “Could I look?”

Leandros hesitated, then set the boat in her palm.

She turned it once, careful not to press the wet fold. “The seam held longer than the first one. It’s starting to lift here.”

“It held the coin.”

“Until it didn’t.”

“It held it.”

“And the paper is still wet.”

They both stared at the tiny boat, its bow now curved downward.

Percy wanted to defend the test. He wanted to defend Leandros. He wanted to ask Ianthe whether she had meant to sound like she was writing a failure down. He noticed the list of interventions gathering in his mind like chores.

Annabeth put the boat on a dry cloth. “It can dry before we decide what to change.”

“Or it can be thrown out,” Leandros said.

“It’s your boat,” she said. “You decide.”

He rubbed a damp thumb against his fingers. “I don’t need you to tell me that.”

“I didn’t.”

“Sometimes you say a choice like you’re giving someone permission.”

“Sometimes I’m making sure they know the choice is theirs.”

“Which makes it sound like you can take it away.”

“That’s not what I’m doing.”

“No?” Leandros looked toward Percy. “Do you always do this?”

“Do what?”

“Wait until someone says the wrong thing, then tell them the choice is theirs.”

Annabeth’s jaw set. “I’m not your sister.”

“I know.”

“Then don’t ask Percy to answer for me.”

“I asked him if you always do it.”

“He doesn’t speak for me.”

“Annabeth—” Percy started.

She turned to him. “I can answer.”

He closed his mouth. It felt awkward in the room, though he knew he had done what she asked. He looked at Ianthe, who had stopped writing. She held the pencil above the page without touching it.

“I don’t want to be in the middle of every conversation,” Percy said.

“No one said you were,” Leandros replied.

“You just looked at me when you asked about Annabeth.”

“I asked you a question.”

“And I’m answering it.”

Annabeth moved the wet boat farther from the basin. “We said we were testing boats.”

“That was before it got personal,” Leandros said.

“It got personal when you asked him to assess me.”

“I didn’t ask him to assess you.”

“You asked him whether I always make choices sound like permission. That’s a judgment.”

“It’s true.”

“Do you want to keep testing?” Percy said. He heard his own voice tilt into the calm tone he used when he hoped someone would stop. He hated how often it sounded like he was assigning them a task.

Leandros looked at him. “You want me to say yes?”

“I want to know.”

“Then ask what you want to know.”

The answer hit cleanly because it was fair. Percy had asked a softer question to avoid saying he was worried. He looked at the basin. “I want to know if you’re done with the test.”

“Not yet.”

“Okay.”

Ianthe folded her page in half. “I can move my notebook.”

“You can keep it there,” Annabeth said.

“I’m finished with the first observation.”

“You don’t have to leave.”

“I didn’t say I was leaving.”

Leandros’s boat rested on the cloth between them. The wet paper had softened, but it hadn’t collapsed. It was the only thing in the room that had completed the test.

Ianthe took the pencil and wrote a short line at the bottom of her page. “I won’t add anything about the boat.”

“Why?” Leandros asked.

“You don’t want me to.”

“I didn’t tell you that.”

“You asked what I was recording. I answered. You kept challenging it.”

“I challenged the test.”

“You challenged my notes.”

“I can do both.”

“Then I can stop writing them.”

“That’s your choice.”

“Yes.”

Percy glanced at Annabeth. She had moved to the window with her arms crossed, but she hadn’t left the table. Her gaze wasn’t on him. He recognized the feeling in the room: the argument was no longer about a paper hull, and nobody had yet decided whether to say so.

“Could we give it a minute?” he asked.

Leandros frowned. “Why?”

“Because you both look like you’re waiting for the other person to prove something.”

“That’s not about the boat,” Ianthe said.

“No. I know.”

“Do you?”

He didn’t have a quick answer. He could hear the hall behind them, the small house sounds of people passing, a chair scraping, rainwater dripping from a coat. He took a step away from the table.

“Annabeth,” he said quietly. “Can I talk to you?”

She looked over at him. “Yes.”

They moved toward the hallway, leaving the table in view. Percy did not close the door. He didn’t want the two of them to think he was taking something away with them.

“Do you think we should step in?” he asked.

“Which part?”

“The part where they keep turning every disagreement into a test.”

“Leandros and Ianthe?”

“Yes.”

“Do they want us to?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then ask.”

“I don’t want to make it worse.”

“Neither do I.”

He waited for her to say more. She looked back through the open doorway at the two siblings. Ianthe had picked up one of the dry sheets. Leandros was checking the damaged boat’s seam.

“Maybe we can tell them to stop before it becomes a fight,” Percy said.

“Did it become a fight?”

“They’re hurting each other.”

“They’re disagreeing. That can hurt. It can also be theirs.”

“You think we should just let them.”

“No.” Annabeth turned to him. “I think there’s a difference between leaving them alone and deciding what their argument means. If we decide the important part, they have to react to our version of it. Then we’re in the middle.”

“I’m already in the middle.”

“You feel in the middle.”

He looked down at his hands. “Leandros is my brother. I know what it’s like when people keep telling you what you’re supposed to remember or what your family means.”

“And I know what it’s like when people say they’re helping while they decide the answer for you.”

“Are you talking about them or me?”

“Both.”

He winced. “I didn’t decide anything.”

“You nearly told Ianthe what she meant by writing. You nearly told Leandros what he wanted from the test. You looked at me like I should agree with you before you asked.”

“I did?”

“You did.”

He felt his face warm. “I thought you were going to tell me not to get involved.”

“I was going to tell you not to appoint yourself.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“No.”

The answer was simple enough that he had nowhere to push it. Percy had wanted her to say something worse, maybe because then he could argue instead of admitting he had already started making himself useful.

“I hate that I hear one sharp sentence and think it’s my job to fix the room,” he said.

“You don’t have to fix the room.”

“But if no one does, they might—”

“Then we can respond to what happens.” She touched his wrist, just above the blue cord he wore. “I’m not asking you to pretend it doesn’t matter. I’m asking you to let them tell us what they want before we decide what help looks like.”

He looked at her. “And if they don’t ask?”

“Then we can ask once.”

“Once.”

“Not send questions through each other. Not ask Clarisse how Leandros feels. Not ask Leandros to explain Ianthe. Not turn every answer into an assignment.”

“Do you want me to ask him?”

“I want you to ask him what he wants from you. That’s not the same as asking him to explain the whole thing.”

“And you?”

“I can ask Ianthe whether she wants company when she’s ready to leave the table. I’m not going to ask about her father or her room.”

“You don’t have to do that.”

“I know.”

“Do you want to?”

“I want to be kind to her. I don’t want to decide that being kind means I owe her every conversation.”

He nodded. That made sense. He liked that it made sense. It still left him with a feeling he couldn’t sort into a task.

“Are we okay?” he asked.

Annabeth glanced at the room. “We’re having a disagreement.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“I know.” She slid her hand down his wrist and took his fingers. “We’re okay. I’m not going to stop telling you when you’re doing the thing.”

“I’m not going to stop being the person who does it.”

“I know.”

He squeezed her hand once. “I can try to ask before I decide.”

“That would help.”

“Even when the answer is no?”

“Especially then.”

“I can manage no.”

“You can manage it after you complain.”

“I can complain quietly.”

“You can try.”

He smiled, and her thumb traced one of his knuckles. She leaned in and kissed him, quick and familiar. He didn’t mistake it for an end to the conversation. It was just Annabeth choosing to be close while they still had something to work out.

When they separated, she rested her forehead against his for a breath.

“We don’t have to make them talk,” she said.

“No.”

“And they don’t have to agree with us.”

“No.”

“If someone asks for help, we help with the thing they asked for.”

“Not the whole family.”

“Right.”

He looked back through the doorway. Ianthe had placed a new sheet on the table, and Leandros had moved the basin a few inches away from the notebook. Neither was speaking. They had not left.

“Once,” he said.

“Once,” Annabeth agreed.

---

Percy stepped back into the room without announcing that he was back.

Leandros had made another boat, this one wider and less tidy. Ianthe had set a pencil beside the page that recorded her draft observation. Annabeth was holding the basin steady while water dripped from the first hull onto the cloth.

“Do you want the test to continue?” Percy asked.

Leandros glanced up. “Yes.”

Ianthe looked at her notebook. “I do.”

“Do you want to do it together?”

“That depends,” Ianthe said.

“On what?”

“Whether he’s going to tell me where I’m allowed to stand.”

“Then I won’t.”

“You already did.”

“I was telling you where the air came from.”

“You said I shouldn’t stand there.”

“I said the draft came from the hall.”

“You said I had to mark it.”

“I said we should.”

“You said we.”

Leandros’s new boat bent slightly in his fingers. “If you two want to argue about who said ‘we,’ I can test mine.”

“Do that,” Ianthe said. “No one stopped you.”

“Could you stop giving orders for one minute?”

“I’m not giving orders.”

“You told me to stop talking.”

“I didn’t.”

“You said ‘do that.’”

“I meant the boat.”

“Then say the boat.”

“Fine. Test the boat.”

He placed it in the basin. This time he didn’t use the coin. The longer hull moved slowly toward the far side, then spun when the water circled against the bowl. It bumped the rim and stopped.

“Good,” Percy said.

Leandros picked it up before the sides softened. “It turned.”

“It stayed afloat,” Annabeth said.

“It turned because the basin is round.”

“Or because you set it down crooked,” Ianthe said.

He looked at her. “I didn’t.”

“You placed it at an angle.”

“I placed it straight.”

“You placed it along the line.”

“The line is straight.”

“The basin edge isn’t.”

“You said the basin was your idea.”

“It was.”

“Then it’s your fault.”

“That doesn’t make sense.”

“You said the shape mattered.”

“I said the draft mattered.”

“You keep changing what the test is.”

“I keep clarifying it.”

“Those are different things.”

“They are.”

Percy wanted to tell them both that he could see the boat was still fine. Instead, he watched Leandros pinch the stern and Ianthe reach for the pencil. She stopped herself before she touched it.

“Can I mark where it started?” she asked.

“You can mark your own sheet.”

“I’m asking whether I can mark the strip beside the basin.”

“Why?”

“So we know if it turns in the same place again.”

“It won’t.”

“How do you know?”

“It already went around.”

“That’s not the same as knowing.”

“You don’t have to prove everything.”

“I do if you say I’m wrong.”

“I didn’t say you were wrong.”

“You said the basin was my fault.”

“It was your basin.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

Leandros rubbed the wet edge of his boat. “You don’t have to put your name beside it.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You wrote the direction.”

“That was mine.”

“Then write the direction and leave my boat out of it.”

“Fine.”

Ianthe put a short dash on the strip, at the place where the bow had bumped the rim. She wrote nothing else.

Leandros watched her. “You wrote that down anyway.”

“I marked where it hit. That’s what you asked me not to do?”

“I didn’t ask you anything.”

“That’s what you keep saying.”

He opened his mouth, then stopped. Percy had seen him hold a line that way before, as if his first answer would be to push until the other person gave ground. This time he held the boat instead.

“The turn doesn’t tell us anything about the lake,” he said.

“No,” Ianthe replied. “It tells us what happened in the basin.”

“It was the curve.”

“Maybe.”

“The water moved around the side.”

“Maybe.”

“Don’t write that as if it proves the current did it.”

“I won’t.”

“Good.”

Her shoulders stayed high, but she lowered the pencil to the table. The small exchange had cost them both more effort than the test.

Annabeth set the basin on the dry cloth. “We have a second boat to try.”

“Is it yours?” Ianthe asked.

“It’s mine.”

“Can I see how you folded the sides?”

“Sure.”

Ianthe leaned over, then stopped an inch short of reaching. “Can I pick it up?”

“Yes.”

She took the boat by its rim. Annabeth showed her the seam. “The first fold is here. If you push it flat, the paper doubles at the bottom.”

Ianthe traced the line with one finger. “That makes it stronger.”

“It does.”

“Then why didn’t you use the same fold on the other side?”

“I did. I’m testing whether the width makes it twist.”

“Oh.”

She handed it back. “I thought you’d forgotten.”

“I hadn’t.”

“I see that.”

Annabeth didn’t answer. She returned the boat to the cloth and looked at Percy. Her expression asked whether he was ready to continue, not whether he would take the whole room in hand. He nodded.

They moved the basin closer to the center. The water had settled. Percy held the paper screen, not the new boat. Ianthe stood at the hall end and set the pencil down at the place where she wanted the push to begin.

“Can I move the screen once?” she asked.

Annabeth looked at Percy, then back at Ianthe. “You can. One push. We’ll see how far it moves.”

“Do we want a count?” Leandros asked.

“Count the breaths,” Percy said. “Same for every boat.”

“How many?”

“Three?”

“Three doesn’t mean the same thing for each person.”

“Then make the push,” Annabeth said. “We’re not comparing who has stronger lungs.”

Ianthe moved the screen in a steady arc. The water rippled. Annabeth placed her boat at the start line. It slid half the basin’s length, wobbled once, and settled against the far side without taking on water.

“Again,” Leandros said.

“I thought one push,” Percy said.

“It held.”

“Then we learned that.”

“One push isn’t enough to know.”

“That’s a fair point,” Annabeth said. “But we agreed on one.”

Leandros’s eyes moved from her to the boat. “You’re stopping because you decided to.”

“I’m stopping because the test has an agreed limit.”

“I didn’t agree to that.”

“You were here when we said it.”

“I didn’t say yes.”

“Did you say no?”

“No.”

“Then we should have asked.”

Annabeth picked up her boat. “We should have.”

Leandros looked at her. “You can admit it that easily?”

“It wasn’t a difficult mistake.”

“It was the rule.”

“Then we change the rule.”

Ianthe lifted her pencil. “Do you want a second push?”

“I do,” Leandros said.

“Same strength?”

“I don’t know how you measure that.”

“I can repeat what I did.”

“Then make the same movement.”

“I can try.”

“Will you write down that it was the same?”

“I’ll write that it was intended to be.”

Leandros studied her. “That’s more honest.”

“I thought you wanted a test.”

“I want the test to say what happened.”

“And I want that too.”

Percy heard something in that sentence that wasn’t a challenge. It didn’t sound like agreement either. It was simply a place they both stood for a second.

Ianthe moved the screen again. The next boat went farther, turned, and touched the opposite rim. One corner darkened. Annabeth inspected the fold.

“The side held,” she said. “The bow is too wide. It turned when it met the ripple.”

“Or when it hit the edge,” Leandros said.

“It didn’t hit the edge.”

“Not yet.”

Ianthe pointed at the water. “It turned before it reached the edge.”

“Then the push wasn’t the same.”

“It looked close.”

“You didn’t measure it.”

“I said it looked close.”

“So it’s an observation, not a measurement.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

She watched him. “You don’t have to sound pleased every time you correct me.”

“I’m not pleased.”

“You sound pleased.”

“I’m trying to be precise.”

“You’re trying to win.”

“Is that what you’re doing?”

“Maybe.”

“Then we’re both trying to win.”

“I’m not trying to win a boat test.”

“What are you trying to win?”

Ianthe put the screen down. “I want you to stop treating every thing I notice like I’m claiming you’re wrong.”

“Sometimes you are.”

“Sometimes you are too.”

“I didn’t say I wasn’t.”

The rain hit harder for a moment. The basin trembled on the cloth, and the two paper hulls rocked against each other. Percy pressed his palm to the table. Annabeth steadied the bowl. Neither boat sank.

Leandros glanced at the narrow first boat, now wrinkled along one side. “Your father would call that a successful test.”

“Which one?” Ianthe asked.

“Zeus.”

“You don’t know what Zeus would call it.”

“You get to tell him what you think. He listens to your notes.”

“I told you he sometimes asked about them.”

“You said he remembered the marks.”

“Yes.”

“So he listened.”

“Sometimes.”

“More than he listened to me.”

“How would I know?”

“Because he was there for you.”

“He was there for my notes.”

“Same difference.”

“No.” Her voice sharpened. “It isn’t.”

---

“You said he was there for me,” Ianthe said. “I said he sometimes asked about the weather notes. I liked that he remembered. That’s not the same as being there whenever I needed him.”

Leandros shifted his weight. “You told him about your notes.”

“Sometimes.”

“And he asked.”

“Sometimes.”

“I thought that meant he saw you.”

“He saw part of me.”

“Isn’t that something?”

“It is. It’s not everything.”

He looked at the boat in his hand. Water had gathered in the hollow. He tipped it into the basin, and a thin stream ran over his thumb.

“I got a sign,” he said. “That was the first thing I got from him.”

“A trident?”

“Yes.”

“You knew then?”

“I knew what it meant.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No.”

Percy felt the familiar pull of that exchange, the temptation to tell them all the parts he knew about a father who had taken him in, a home he remembered only through other people’s stories, and a childhood he could neither claim nor reject. He had more to say than either of them had asked to hear. He kept it to himself.

Leandros glanced at him anyway. “You know what it’s like when people tell you who your father is before you know him.”

“I know what it’s like to hear about a home I can’t remember,” Percy said. “That’s mine. I don’t know what it’s like to grow up at your marina.”

“Do you know what it’s like to have him visit?”

“I know what it’s like to have more of him than you did.”

The answer surprised Leandros. Percy hadn’t meant it as a confession, but it was true. Poseidon had brought Percy into Atlantis after Sally died. Amphitrite and Triton had raised him. He knew those people had loved him, even if he didn’t carry the memories of being small with them. He knew Poseidon had given him a family and a home. He did not know how to make that feel fair to Leandros.

Leandros looked down. “And you don’t remember it.”

“Not the way they do.”

“Sometimes it sounds like a convenient thing to say.”

“It isn’t.”

“I know.” Leandros's voice was low, but he didn't take back the words from their first argument. “I know what you said. It still sounds strange.”

“It is strange.”

Ianthe put her pencil down. “You both keep talking like having a different kind of absence makes the other one smaller.”

“I didn’t say yours was small,” Leandros said.

“You said he was there for me.”

“Because you told me he was.”

“Sometimes. About the weather notes.”

“I heard that part.”

“Then stop making it mean more than I said.”

He rubbed the bridge of his nose. Percy knew the gesture. Leandros was trying to keep his temper from winning the whole conversation.

“Fine,” he said. “You had some attention. You wanted more than that.”

Ianthe tilted her head. “That sounds closer.”

“I’m not saying it was good.”

“You sounded like you were saying it was enough.”

“I thought it might be easier to know a father had noticed you, even if it was only a notebook.”

“Maybe it was easier in one way.”

“Which way?”

“I knew he could remember what I wrote.”

“And the hard part?”

“He could remember the marks and still not know what I meant when I asked him a question.”

Leandros turned the boat in his hands. “What did you ask?”

Ianthe's eyes narrowed slightly. “That isn't yours.”

“Okay.”

“I'm not trying to hide it from you. I don't want to explain every conversation I had with him just to prove I am allowed to be upset.”

“I didn't say you weren't.”

“You asked.”

“I asked what you asked him.”

“That's the same thing right now.”

He stared at her a beat, then looked at the basin. “I didn't think of it that way.”

“Maybe think before you ask.”

“I'll try.”

The answer came too quickly to be a polished apology. He seemed to hear it himself; his shoulders lifted, then settled. Ianthe waited. She didn't reward the attempt with a smile.

Percy found himself wanting to tell them both that they had done enough for one afternoon. The urge sat between his teeth. He wondered if Annabeth felt it too.

“What do you want from him?” Ianthe asked.

Leandros looked at her. “I don’t know.”

“That’s an answer.”

“It’s not a helpful one.”

“You don't owe me a helpful one.”

He glanced at her weather notebook. “I thought if you knew more about your father, you’d know what to do when he was gone.”

“I don’t.”

“But you have something.”

“I have a few things. They don’t make a plan.”

“You have the notes.”

“I have notes about wind. I don’t have a schedule for Zeus.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

He looked at the damp hull. “I think I keep looking at what you have and deciding it answers what I want.”

Ianthe took that in without nodding. “And?”

“And it doesn’t.”

“No.”

“It doesn’t mean I have to stop wanting it.”

“No.”

The room grew quiet again. Not the silence before the next argument, exactly. The rain had softened. In the hall someone laughed and then shushed another person. The basin water was still.

Percy had heard Ianthe tell Thalia that Zeus sometimes asked about her weather observations at home. Now he heard her tell Leandros. It belonged to them, spoken here, and not to Percy to carry elsewhere. She had not told him about her private note. He wouldn't ask.

Leandros looked at Percy. “Do you want to say something?”

“Do you want me to?”

“I asked if you wanted to say something.”

“That’s not the same.”

“Now you sound like Annabeth.”

“Maybe I’m learning.”

Ianthe picked up the pencil. “I want to know whether the boat floats with the coin.”

“We already did,” Leandros said.

“We did for four seconds.”

“It held.”

“It started to soften.”

“Maybe the seam can be pressed flat.”

“Can I press it?”

“It’s my boat.”

“I asked.”

He looked at her fingers, then at the seam. “You can show me where you’d press. I’ll do it.”

“Why?”

“Because it’s my fold.”

She seemed to consider whether the answer was only ownership. “Okay.”

Ianthe pointed to the near side. “Not here. The fold is lifting on the other edge.”

Leandros turned the hull around. “This side?”

“Yes.”

He placed it on the dry cloth and pressed the corner. The paper caught beneath his thumb.

“Not too hard,” she said.

“I know.”

“You’re about to tear it.”

“I’m not.”

“The paper’s wet.”

He stopped, lifted his hand, and examined the seam. The corner had split a little. “I did.”

“Can it still float?”

“I don’t know.”

“Do you want to try?”

He looked at the boat. “Not yet.”

“Okay.”

He did not ask her to fix it. She didn’t reach for it again.

---

Annabeth cleared a dry space on the table and set three clean sheets there.

“We can make a third boat,” she said. “No names on it unless the person who folds it wants a name.”

“I wasn’t writing names,” Ianthe said.

“I didn’t say you were.”

“I know. I’m telling you.”

“Okay.”

Leandros touched the split seam. “The paper is too thin.”

“Then we can use the back of the thicker sheet,” Percy said.

“That one has Chiron’s notes on it.”

“The blank side is blank.”

“It is not.”

They all looked. A faint pencil mark showed through from the other side, a list of hooks copied from Chiron’s page.

“Could be a legend,” Percy said.

“No,” Annabeth said. “It’s a list.”

“Some boats have a list.”

“Those are terrible boats.”

“Maybe the list is what keeps it afloat.”

Leandros snorted. The laugh surprised Percy because it came before the comment had been dismissed. Annabeth turned the sheet over and set a blank page in front of him.

“Here,” she said.

He accepted it. “I didn’t ask for the thick one.”

“You said the paper was thin.”

“I said it was too thin for this fold.”

“Then use it or don’t.”

He looked at her. “You don’t have to sound annoyed.”

“I’m not.”

“You sound it.”

“I’m thinking about the next fold.”

“Could you think without making it sound like an order?”

“Could you disagree without turning it into a test?”

“I can.”

“Good.”

Neither softened. Annabeth slid the blank paper closer to him anyway.

Ianthe took one of the narrow boats Percy had folded earlier. “Would you show me how you made this seam?”

“Sure,” he said. “It’s not really a seam. It’s just the top folded over twice.”

“Can I copy it?”

“Yeah.”

She pulled a clean scrap from the stack, then paused. “Is this one yours?”

“Not anymore.”

“I’m asking.”

“Use it.”

She folded slowly, pressing the edge with her thumbnail. Percy watched the new boat take shape. It had a high bow and a flat bottom. The paper creased a little crooked at one end.

“I can fix that,” she said.

“You can,” Leandros replied.

She looked up, wary. “I know.”

“I didn’t say you couldn’t.”

“You said it like you expected me to.”

“I said you can.”

“Fine.”

He held up his own blank page. “I want to try the wide fold again. Not the deep keel.”

“Then I can show you the side crease,” Annabeth said.

“Can I use it?”

“It’s a fold. You can.”

“I mean can I see how you do it?”

“Yes.”

The question hung there for a beat. Percy realized how easily the word can could mean permission, possibility, or challenge. He also realized that his sister had heard him use the same word differently in the hall. He stopped himself from turning that thought into advice.

Annabeth folded the sides of a fresh boat. Leandros watched her hands.

“Your corner is uneven,” he said.

“I know.”

“It’ll make it lean.”

“Maybe.”

“Do you want me to straighten it?”

“No. I want to see what it does.”

He studied her face. “Okay.”

She set the imperfect boat beside the other three.

Percy went to the kitchen and came back with four mugs of warm spiced cider and a bowl of sliced apples. The cook had left the cider on the stove for people who came in wet. Percy hadn’t asked whether everyone wanted some before carrying it, so he set the tray down where they could reach it instead of putting a mug into each hand.

“Cider?” he asked.

“Yes,” Annabeth said.

Leandros nodded.

Ianthe looked at the cups. “Does it have pepper?”

“Cinnamon. No pepper.”

“Then yes.”

Percy poured the cups. Leandros took his and held it between both hands. Ianthe waited until the mugs were set before reaching for hers. The steam rose in a thin line and vanished above the table.

“Is this part of the experiment?” Leandros asked.

“It’s part of the weather,” Percy said.

“Warmth isn’t a variable.”

“Maybe it is if we all get less annoying after a cup.”

“I’m not annoying,” Ianthe said.

Leandros said, “You are.”

She turned to him. “So are you.”

“I know.”

The answer stopped her. He took a sip. It was too hot, and he put it back down.

Percy took one of the apples and sliced it smaller with a kitchen knife. Annabeth moved the bowl closer to Ianthe without placing it directly in front of her.

“Thanks,” Ianthe said.

She picked one slice. Leandros reached for another, then paused, his hand hovering.

“Take one,” Percy said.

“It’s not mine.”

“It’s the bowl.”

“Silena’s rosemary bread was hers.”

“This is an apple.”

Leandros glanced at Annabeth. “I can take one?”

“I brought the bowl,” Percy said. “You don’t have to ask me.”

“I asked because you brought it.”

“Then yes.”

He took an apple slice. Percy realized he’d answered the question more than once. Leandros chewed, staring at his boat. His eyes moved to the cider and back.

Leandros rolled the mug between his palms. “My mother drove me to the bus station. She waited until Callum said the trail was clear. She wanted me to stay home.”

“Did you want to?”

“I wanted to leave and not leave. It’s possible.”

Ianthe considered that. “Both can be true.”

“Yes.”

He heard something in her answer, but he didn’t ask. Percy felt a prickle of alarm at the easy way they were turning toward one another, as if the next sentence could demand something neither had offered. Then Ianthe pulled her notebook from beneath her sleeve and opened it to the page with her own wind marks.

“I’m not saying my mother is the same,” she said. “I didn’t say anything about her.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I don’t know your mother.”

“Right.”

“You don’t know mine, either,” he said.

“No.”

“That’s the part I want to keep separate.”

“Then keep it separate.”

“I’m trying.”

“You keep telling me that.”

“Because I’m trying.”

“Trying is not the same as doing it.”

“No. It isn’t.”

She shut the notebook. “I don’t need you to understand it today.”

He looked into his mug. “I don’t know if I need to understand it. I want to know what it’s like when someone says he listened to your notes.”

“Sometimes it felt good.”

Leandros looked at her.

“Sometimes it was the only thing he remembered,” she continued. “I liked that he knew them. I wanted him to ask about more. At the last visit, he praised me for listening and for careful notes, then called them difficult by comparison. I liked that he noticed me, for half a second. Then it felt as if I had been handed a prize for being compared with my own sisters.”

“I wasn’t there.”

“No.”

“I didn’t know he did that.”

“Now you do.”

“Does he always do that?”

“That isn’t a question I can answer for him.”

“I meant with you.”

“I don’t know what he’ll do next time. I don’t know when there’ll be a next time.”

Percy let the cider warm his fingers. The details were new to him, but not a secret he had been asked to carry. Ianthe had said them to Leandros, and he was at the table. He could remember them as her report, not as his own experience.

Leandros nodded once. “I’m sorry I said he was there for you.”

“You don’t have to apologize for noticing something.”

“I’m apologizing for saying it as if it settled the question.”

She took another apple slice. “Okay.”

The word wasn’t forgiveness. Percy could tell from the way she kept her eyes on the notebook. It wasn’t rejection either.

“You haven’t said what you want,” Ianthe said.

“I don’t know.”

“Try.”

“Why?”

“So I can stop guessing.”

He turned the paper boat in his hands. “I want him to visit when it’s about me. I want him to ask something that isn’t about Percy or the water or whether I’m keeping the camp safe. I don’t know how to ask without sounding like I’m begging.”

“That’s more than ‘I don’t know.’”

“It’s not a plan.”

“It’s not supposed to be a plan.”

“I thought everyone needed a plan.”

“Not every feeling is a work list.”

He raised an eyebrow. “You say that, but you brought three lists.”

“One is a record. One is a draft. One is an old work list I haven’t thrown away.”

“Four, if you count the notebook.”

“I’m not counting the notebook.”

“You always count the notebook.”

“I do not.”

“You have a separate page for each section.”

“That’s called organization.”

“That’s what I said.”

“You are making fun of me.”

“I am.”

“Why?”

“Because you do it to everyone else.”

“I don’t.”

“You do.”

“I can stop.”

“Can you?”

She paused. “I can try.”

Leandros nodded. “That’s more than ‘I don’t.’”

She gave him a flat look. “Don’t use my words against me.”

“I’m not. I’m repeating them.”

“That’s worse.”

Percy smiled into his mug. Annabeth looked at him.

“Don’t encourage him.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You were.”

“I was thinking about something else.”

“Are you?”

“Maybe.”

“Then tell me what.”

---

“Nothing important,” Percy said. “I’m thinking I should get a second bowl of apples before you two finish them.”

“There are four slices left,” Annabeth said.

“That’s enough for a second bowl.”

“You can ask the kitchen.”

“You’re sitting closer to the door.”

“I’m also sitting with a boat that hasn’t been tested.”

“You don’t have to supervise it.”

“I’m not supervising it.”

“You’re looking at it like it owes you something.”

“It owes me a clean result.”

“Boats don’t owe you results.”

“Then why are we testing them?”

“To learn what they do.”

“Which is a result.”

Annabeth tipped her cider toward him. “You win this round.”

“I wasn’t trying to win.”

“You were.”

“I was asking a question.”

“You were trying to make me say it the way you did.”

“Maybe.”

“Good.”

She put the mug down. The discussion between them had not vanished, but it had stopped pushing. Percy liked that they could leave the door open without stuffing the disagreement inside it. Annabeth still had her own work to finish; he still wanted to check on Leandros. Neither had to become the other’s job.

“I’m going to ask him what he wants from me,” Percy said.

“Okay.”

“If he wants me to listen, I can do that.”

“Okay.”

“If he wants me to ask Clarisse something, I’ll tell him I can’t carry it.”

“You can also ask him whether he wants to speak to her himself.”

“I thought we agreed on that.”

“We did.”

He looked at her. “Are you going to ask Ianthe if she wants company?”

“I’ll ask if she wants to walk with me when we’re done. I won’t ask her to tell me what Zeus said.”

“Even though you know?”

“Because I know some of it doesn’t mean I own all of it.”

He nodded. He knew that answer was for both of them.

“Do you want to come with me?” he asked.

“No.”

He looked at her.

“I want to finish the boat test, then decide whether to go back to my room or take a walk,” she said. “You asked.”

“I did.”

“You can be disappointed.”

“I’m not.”

“You looked disappointed.”

“I’m thinking about the second bowl.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s true.”

She considered him. “You can get the bowl. I’ll finish the test.”

“Do you want me to wait?”

“No. I want to finish it.”

He stood. “Okay.”

He had made it halfway to the kitchen when Ianthe called his name.

“Percy?”

He turned.

“Could you ask whether the cook has another cup?”

“Sure.”

“For Leandros?”

“For me,” Leandros said. “I’m still drinking this one.”

“I want it before it gets cold,” Ianthe said.

“That’s not what you asked.”

“No. It isn’t.”

Percy waited.

“Could you ask if there’s another cup?” she said.

“I can.”

“Thanks.”

He went into the kitchen. The cook handed him one clean cup and a small bowl of apple slices, then asked whether the basin was out of the way. Percy said it was on the Big House table, not the kitchen counter. He carried the cup and bowl back and set them between the other plates.

Ianthe thanked him and poured the last of the cider into the new cup. She didn’t assign the bowl to anyone. Leandros took another slice when she nudged it nearer the center.

The third boat was folded from the thicker sheet. Ianthe made the high sides. Leandros widened the base and reinforced the bow. The two features didn’t match perfectly, so the boat leaned to one side on the table.

“Do you want to fix it?” he asked.

“Yes.”

He turned it toward her. “Which side?”

“The left.”

“That’s the side with the double fold.”

“I know.”

“It might make it too heavy.”

“Maybe.”

“Do you want to keep the fold?”

“I want to see what it does.”

“Fine.”

She pressed the raised edge flat with her thumb, then withdrew her hand. Leandros picked up the boat, inspecting it for a moment. He didn’t say the seam was crooked. She didn’t say the sides were uneven.

“Can I write down the basin conditions?” she asked.

“What conditions?”

“Same water as before. No outside draft that we could see. One screen push, intended to match the last one.”

“Did you measure the push?”

“No.”

“Then write that.”

“I will.”

“Don’t write that it matched.”

“I said intended to.”

“Good.”

“Can I include that the boat leaned left before the test?”

“It did.”

“Okay.”

She wrote the condition. “I’m not writing either of our names.”

“That’s fine.”

“If you want your name on it, you can add it.”

“I don’t.”

She left the page open in the middle of the table. She had not hidden the record or made it official. It sat beside the paper boats, waiting for the next choice.

Leandros lowered the new hull into the basin. This boat sat higher than his first and lower than Ianthe’s. It shifted when the water moved, corrected itself, then drifted to the far side. The screen pushed a narrow wave against the left wall. The boat turned halfway, but it did not touch the edge.

“Is it leaking?” Annabeth asked.

Leandros lifted it and checked the seam. “Not yet.”

“I see one dark spot,” Ianthe said.

“So do I.”

“Do you want to mark it?”

“Not while it’s wet.”

“Okay.”

He set the hull on the cloth. The boat was damp along one corner and intact on the other. Percy looked at it and thought it would last a few more tests but not the afternoon.

“Better,” Leandros said.

“Better than what?” Ianthe asked.

“The one I folded alone.”

“That’s a comparison.”

“It is.”

“You can make it.”

“So can you.”

“I didn’t say I couldn’t.”

“Are you trying to win?”

“Maybe.”

He smiled faintly. “So am I.”

The brief warmth between them did not last long enough to become a promise. Ianthe turned to her notebook. Leandros took his own paper and set it beside the boat, not under it.

“Can I see the notes you made yesterday?” Ianthe asked.

“The current ones?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“I want to know whether the directions changed.”

“Not to test the basin.”

“No. Just to see.”

He opened the notebook to the page with the two arrows. He kept his thumb between the sheet and the rest of the book, blocking the next page.

“Here. These two observations.”

Ianthe leaned closer, staying on her side of the table. She looked at the times and the arrows.

“The wind went east, but the lower current went west.”

“Then it changed.”

“It didn’t change at the same time.”

“No.”

“Did you ask Beckendorf when you can check it again?”

“I asked. He said we wait until the bank firms and the dry line clears.”

“That's a good condition.”

“It’s a condition.”

“Not a test?”

“Not yet.”

She gave the notebook back. “I’m not going to copy it.”

“I didn’t think you were.”

“Good.”

He closed the notebook but left it on the table. Their words had not repaired the anger between them. It had given the anger a more exact edge.

---

Ianthe closed her notebook and began gathering her things. Annabeth moved the paper boat away from the cider.

“Do you want to walk to the porch with me?” she asked.

“No.”

“Okay.”

Ianthe slid the notebook under her arm. “I need to check whether the kitchen has a place to dry these pages.”

“Want me to ask Chiron?” Annabeth said.

“I can ask him.”

“Okay.”

There was no argument over the offer. Ianthe had said no, and Annabeth let it stand.

Leandros kept his hands on the edge of the table. “Are you going to tell Thalia I said what I said?”

Ianthe’s expression went still. “What did you say?”

“That you had some attention from Zeus.”

“I didn’t ask you to keep that secret.”

“So you don’t mind?”

“I mind you making it a measure. I don’t mind Thalia knowing we talked.”

“I wasn’t asking for permission to tell her.”

“Then why ask?”

“I don’t know.”

She shifted the notebook against her side. “I’m not going to report our conversation. If Thalia asks, I’ll tell her I spoke with you. I won’t give her every word.”

“That’s fair.”

“I know.”

She looked toward Annabeth. “I’m leaving.”

“Thanks for telling me.”

Ianthe left by the hall, taking her weather notes with her. Her page with the draft arrow stayed on the table, beside the boats. She returned for it, then tucked it into the notebook. The water-current page remained in Leandros’s closed book.

Percy sat again. The small paper fleet had taken over the center of the table. He could see why it had become easier to talk around them than to face one another directly.

“Do you want to talk?” he asked Leandros. “Or do you want to stop?”

Leandros glanced at the damp boat. “I want you to stop trying to ask the right thing.”

“That’s fair.”

“You can ask what I want.”

“What do you want from me?”

“I don’t know.” He picked up the boat and put it down again. “Not about Ianthe. Not about Clarisse. I’m not asking you to call Poseidon and explain me.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were thinking about it.”

Percy had been. He didn’t argue. “I was.”

“I don’t need you to do that.”

“Okay.”

“If he comes here, I want to talk to him myself.”

“Okay.”

“I don’t know what I’m going to say.”

“You don’t have to know now.”

“I might ask him to come back.”

“I can’t promise he will.”

“I didn’t ask you to.”

“I know.”

Leandros looked at him. “You did the same thing again.”

“What?”

“You answered a question I didn’t ask.”

Percy nodded. “I did.”

Leandros returned his attention to the boat. “I want to say I don’t want him to talk to me like I’m a route he has to check before supper.”

“That makes sense.”

“Don’t say it like I’m right.”

“I can say it makes sense without saying you’re right about everything.”

“Can you?”

“I’m trying.”

“That isn’t the same.”

“No,” Percy said. “It isn’t.”

Leandros pressed the split corner down with one finger, then let it rise again. “I’m still mad at you about Atlantis.”

“I know.”

“I might stay mad.”

“I know that too.”

“You don’t have to stand there and wait for me to like you.”

“I’m not waiting for a report.”

“Good.”

Percy could have said that he wanted them to be brothers. He did want it. He didn’t know whether saying so would make the wish another thing Leandros had to answer.

Annabeth appeared in the doorway with her jacket on. The last boat was drying on the table behind her. “Are you ready to go?”

“In a minute,” Percy said.

“Okay.”

She went back to the table and began stacking the unused sheets. She didn’t touch Leandros’s notebook or the wet boat. Percy watched her match corners and make a tidy pile.

“Do you want another test?” he asked him.

“Not today.”

“Okay.”

Leandros picked up the boat and set it on the dry cloth. “It can dry.”

“It might be too warped.”

“Then it might not.”

“Are you going to keep it?”

“For now.”

He slid the boat to the far end of the table, where the air from the hall wouldn’t reach it. Ianthe’s draft arrow was gone; the page had left with her. The notes they had shared remained separate, as did their stories.

Percy stood. Annabeth met him at the door. She offered her hand, and he took it. They walked out together beneath the eaves, where rainwater struck the ground in a steady line.

“Are we still doing our date?” he asked.

“We’ve been on it the whole time.”

“I thought the basin was the date.”

“The basin was the activity.”

“And the date?”

“Us doing something together.”

“That’s a good distinction.”

“It’s a useful one.”

He leaned closer to her shoulder as they stepped around a puddle. “Do you want the rest of the cider?”

“No. I want to walk.”

“Then we’ll walk.”

Behind them, the Big House door closed on the damp paper fleet. One boat had leaked. One had held. The third had moved farther than either of them expected and still needed to dry.
