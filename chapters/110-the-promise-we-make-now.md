## Chapter 110: The Promise We Make Now

The sketch stayed beside Annabeth’s breakfast plate after she took Percy’s hand.

Percy did not ask to see the drawing again. He had already looked when he found it in the inner pocket beside the photograph, and he knew what it showed: two figures at the lake, their shoulders touching, the shoreline drawn in a few confident strokes and a few corrected ones. It was not the picture from the other life. It had no banner, no unknown camera, no promise hidden in its background.

At least, that was what he thought. Annabeth had drawn it; she could decide what it meant.

The breakfast tables were filling around them. Spoons clinked against bowls. Someone at the Hermes table was trying to convince a younger camper that the last piece of toast had been left there as a trap. Percy kept his hand beneath the table, not hiding it so much as leaving the gesture between them. Annabeth’s fingers rested over his. Neither of them had said anything about the photograph since the night before.

Across the pavilion, Clarisse stood beside the serving table with a coil of rope over one shoulder. She had apparently found it while Percy was still wondering whether to ask Annabeth what she wanted to do after breakfast.

“Shrine,” she called. “If we’re going to keep pretending that channel will fix itself, we might as well at least look at it.”

“We aren’t pretending that,” Annabeth said. She drew her hand back and set the sketch carefully on top of the folded napkin. “We’re waiting for the proper check.”

“Fine. We can wait closer to it.”

“That’s not the same as checking it.”

“It’s closer than here.”

Percy watched Annabeth’s face for the small change that meant she had decided whether Clarisse was being difficult or useful. She gave Clarisse a level look.

“We can bring the open questions and make sure nobody shifts anything,” Annabeth said. “That’s all.”

Clarisse shrugged. “I didn’t say I was going to dig up the foundation.”

“No one said you did.”

“I know. I’m saying I didn’t.”

Silena arrived behind her, carrying two empty bowls. “Is this a meeting, or is Clarisse arguing with the concept of a meeting?”

“It’s a site visit,” Percy said.

“Is it?” Annabeth asked. “We’re not doing maintenance.”

“Then it’s a not-maintenance visit.”

Silena’s mouth curved. “Good. I can attend that.”

Percy glanced down at the card on the table. Annabeth followed his eyes and placed her palm over it—not possessively, just enough to stop the napkin from slipping as someone brushed past. She slid the sketch into the space beside her plate, keeping it away from the serving dishes.

“I’ll come back for it,” she said.

“Okay.”

It was not a question, and Percy did not make it one. He stood and gathered their cups. Annabeth folded the napkin around the sketch without bending the card and tucked it into the front of her own notebook. The photograph remained in the inner pocket of Percy’s bag, which he had left back in Cabin Three. He did not have it with him. No one else at the table knew what it showed.

---

They walked to the shrine after breakfast. The day had the clean, ordinary brightness of a camp morning. A few clouds sat over the tree line, but none looked heavy enough to turn the walk into a weather decision. Percy carried the coil of cord. Annabeth had her notebook and the folded drainage drawing from the outer pocket of his bag, which she had asked him to bring down. Clarisse had the tool box handle, and Silena carried a smaller case of hand tools. Thalia came up from the path with a box of spare tiles held against her hip. Grover followed with a pitcher of lemonade and a basket of cups. Ella arrived last, notebook open, pencil moving before she had joined the group.

They were not all there for the same reason. Clarisse wanted to know whether the work could be made less annoying. Silena had agreed to return a hammer and had stayed when she heard there would be a group. Thalia had brought spare tiles because they had been stacked beside the repair materials after the last site visit. Grover had brought drinks because he had been told there would be people outside. Ella had come because any gathering of more than three people seemed to her like a possible record.

Annabeth held up one hand before the tool box reached the flat stone.

“Set it outside the marked area, please.”

Clarisse stopped. “I know.”

“I know you know. I’m making the boundary visible so we all use the same one.”

Clarisse set the box down on the path. Thalia placed the tiles beside it. Nobody moved the stones around the drainage channel. The marks on Annabeth’s drawing still showed where the measurements had started and ended; the separate leaf near the upper stone remained separate from the leaves at the grate. They had a question about the water beneath the edge stones, not an answer. Percy had seen the difference in the drawing after Annabeth revised it. A line on a page did not mean the water had followed it.

He stretched the cord between two old stakes, well back from the site, only to show where they had agreed not to step. It did not cross the channel or touch the stones. He tied it loosely enough that anyone could lift it if they needed to pass, then checked the path from both directions.

“Does that work?” he asked.

Annabeth looked from the cord to the approach. “It marks the area. It doesn’t keep anyone out by itself.”

“Right.” Percy stepped back. “It’s a reminder, not a fence.”

Thalia shifted the box of tiles away from the cord. “And these stay here until someone says where they go?”

“They stay staged,” Annabeth said. “They’re not a decision about the repair.”

“Got it.” Thalia set them down without opening the box.

The worksite was quieter than Percy expected. The channel made no sound he could distinguish from the breeze through the nearby trees. There was nothing to watch but the pale cut of stone, the darker shadow below the shrine’s base, and a couple of leaves caught near the grate. He was used to the water answering when he asked. Here, he had promised himself not to make the site answer a question the maintenance crew had not tested. His power could move water; that did not make his guess reliable.

Annabeth checked the drawing against the visible marks from the path. She did not take out the measuring cord. She did not lean over the channel or step inside the line. She looked at the endpoints she had marked earlier, then closed the notebook.

“Nothing new?” Percy asked.

“Nothing we can establish from here.”

“Are we supposed to wait for Chiron to arrange the check?”

“Yes. I gave him the questions. The crew has to inspect the foundation and test the flow.” She tapped the folded page. “We shouldn’t make a second version of the same guess because we’re standing here.”

Clarisse tilted her head toward the channel. “So we’re here to look at a thing and not touch it.”

“That is sometimes what looking means,” Annabeth said.

Clarisse made a face as if the sentence had personally inconvenienced her. “I can do that.”

“I know.”

Percy glanced at the tools. They had arrived ready for work, but the work they could do was not the repair. They could keep the approach clear, return anything they had borrowed, and make sure the crew would have room to inspect later. That was not nothing, though it lacked the satisfying sound of a hammer hitting a nail.

Ella’s pencil moved across the page. She had written the names of everyone there, then a line beneath them. Percy couldn’t read the words from where he stood.

“Are you making a list of the things we need to do?” he asked.

“I’m making a record of who came and what was said.” Ella held the notebook at chest height. “If we decide anything, I don’t want to lose it.”

“We haven’t decided anything,” Clarisse said.

“Then I’ll write that.”

Annabeth looked toward the notebook. “Can you leave space before you write down what people say?”

Ella’s pencil stopped. “Why?”

“Because we haven’t agreed this is a meeting or a record. I want to hear what everyone thinks first.”

Ella lowered the notebook a fraction. “I thought writing it down would help.”

“It might,” Annabeth said. “I’m not asking you to put it away. I’m asking you to wait until we decide what belongs on the page.”

The request left Ella looking between Annabeth and the blank space beneath the names. She did not close the notebook. She turned the pencil in her fingers, then set its point against the margin without writing.

Percy knew that feeling: wanting to make the next part easier by doing something now, before anyone could disagree about what the task was. He also knew Annabeth was not trying to take the notebook from Ella. She had asked for a pause, not a promise.

“What if we decide there’s no record?” Silena asked.

“Then I won’t write one,” Ella said. “I can still take notes about the shrine, if Annabeth wants.”

“I already have my notes,” Annabeth replied. “This is different.”

Clarisse leaned on the tool box. “Maybe we’re not here to make a rulebook.”

“We’re not,” Percy said.

He looked at the circle they had made without meaning to. The cord marked the work boundary behind them. The tools sat together on the path. Grover had put the lemonade pitcher on the flat stone, and Ella stood between the cups and her notebook. No one had called a meeting, but everyone had stopped moving.

Annabeth watched the group, waiting. “We could make a small agreement about how we treat each other while we work. Not a camp rule. Not a promise to fix everything. Something we can choose.”

Clarisse lifted an eyebrow. “A promise?”

“Maybe.” Annabeth sat on the low ledge beside the path. “Only if people want to.”

“That’s still a big word,” Percy said.

“We don’t have to make it big.”

He thought about how easily a promise could become a way to shut down a question. People said forever when they were scared. They said always when they wanted the other person to stop asking what they needed today. The things Annabeth had said the night before were not a contract. The sketch at breakfast had not been a prediction. He wanted the new agreement to stay the same size as the people who chose it.

“Then maybe we start with what we actually want to do here,” he said. “And if anyone doesn’t want to promise something, they can say that.”

Clarisse gave him a narrow look. “You’re making this sound like a drill.”

“I’m trying to make it sound like something we can stop.”

“Good,” Silena said. “Then I might be willing to stay.”

Grover set down the pitcher. “I’m willing.”

Thalia leaned the tile box against the path-side of the tool case. “I’ll listen first.”

Ella closed the notebook over her pencil but kept one finger between its pages. “I’ll wait to write.”

Clarisse did not answer immediately. She rolled her shoulder, then looked at Annabeth. “I’ll hear the proposal. I’m not agreeing to anything until I know what it is.”

“That’s fair,” Annabeth said.

No one moved the cord. No one called the gods or asked the shrine to witness them. The sky stayed ordinary. The tiles remained in their closed box. Percy could hear the campers crossing the far path on their way to the arena, and the faint scrape of someone dragging a bench along packed dirt. The day did not pause for them.

Annabeth looked around the group. “I was thinking we could start with three things: ask before we assume; let someone say no without making them defend it; and tell the truth when we’re afraid, without demanding that everyone share everything.”

“Those aren’t the same thing,” Clarisse said.

“No. They’re three different things.”

“They sound like rules.”

“Then we can change them.”

“Before or after we promise?”

“Before. Or we can decide not to promise them at all.”

Clarisse considered that. “All right. Say the first one again.”

Annabeth repeated it, more slowly. “Ask before we assume.”

“What if I already know what someone wants?”

“Then asking shouldn’t take long.”

“What if they’re busy?”

“Then wait, or ask when they’re free.”

“What if it’s urgent?”

“Then say why it’s urgent. It doesn’t mean you get to make every decision for them.”

Clarisse tipped her chin. “Better.”

“Do you want to keep it?” Annabeth asked.

“I want to hear the rest.”

“Okay.”

The word did not sound like a vote. It sounded like the conversation could continue.


Silena sat on the ledge beside Annabeth and set the returned hammer between her boots. “The second one needs a boundary. People should be allowed to say no, but the person asking should also be allowed to say why they need something.”

“Those can both be true,” Annabeth said.

“They can. I’ve seen people treat no as an insult. I’ve also seen people take a yes before the other person understood the question.” Silena nudged the hammer handle with her toe. “If we’re saying this out loud, I want it to include asking clearly.”

Percy looked at the hammer. Its handle had a pale scrape near the head, but nothing was broken. Silena had not brought it over to ask permission to give it back; that part was easy. The difficult questions were the ones where people wanted an answer before the other person had a chance to decide.

“Maybe the first promise covers that,” he said. “Ask what someone wants instead of guessing. Then they get to answer.”

“Unless the way you ask makes it hard to say no,” Silena replied.

Clarisse folded her arms. “That’s not a promise. That’s noticing when somebody is being a pain.”

“It can be both,” Silena said.

“I’m not promising to never be a pain.”

“Nobody asked you to.”

The quickness of Silena’s answer made Clarisse pause. She glanced down at her arms, then let them fall to her sides.

“Fine,” Clarisse said. “I’ll promise not to turn a no into a challenge.”

Silena looked at her. “Is that what you want to promise?”

“I said it, didn’t I?”

“You did. I’m asking if you mean it.”

Clarisse’s mouth tightened. “I mean I’ll try to hear no the first time. If I forget, you can tell me. I’m not promising I’ll be happy about it.”

“That’s more honest,” Annabeth said.

“It’s less tidy,” Ella said, opening the notebook again.

The pencil hovered over the page. Percy saw Ella look at the words she had already written, then at the group. She pressed the pencil down lightly but did not move it.

“Maybe tidy isn’t the point,” he said.

Ella tilted the notebook so the page faced herself. “If I don’t write what people say, I might get the wording wrong.”

“Could you write a version and ask us before keeping it?” Thalia said. “Or not write it down at all.”

Ella’s fingers tightened around the cover. “I can do either.”

“Which do you want?” Annabeth asked.

Ella glanced over the page. A thin line separated the names from the notes below. “I want to make sure I remember what we decide. But I don’t want to turn anyone’s words into a record they didn’t ask for.”

“Then we can decide what belongs in your notebook,” Percy said. “You can keep your own promise in there. The rest can stay with the people who said it.”

Ella nodded, once. She turned to a clean corner and wrote: **Ask before keeping someone else’s words.** Then she drew a square around it. “That is mine. If I write anything about the group, I’ll show it to everyone first.”

Annabeth smiled a little. “That works.”

“Do you want the notebook closed while people speak?”

Ella considered. “Yes.” She shut it and slid the pencil beneath the cover. “I can remember one conversation without taking minutes.”

“Good,” Clarisse said. “Now we can stop acting like this is a hearing.”

“We haven’t started,” Annabeth pointed out.

“We’ve been talking for ten minutes.”

“Then maybe we have started.”

Clarisse rolled her eyes, but she did not leave. She stepped toward the cord, then stopped before crossing it. “What do you want from me, exactly? Not for the promise. For the site.”

Annabeth looked at the toolbox. “Nothing inside the marked area. If you want to help, keep the path open and return the hammer. The inspection belongs to the crew.”

“Could we move the spare tiles closer?”

“No. They’re already staged. I don’t know whether any of them are the right size.”

“Then they stay there.”

Clarisse turned toward the box. “Thalia, does it need to be under cover?”

“It’s dry,” Thalia said. “I can check the label without opening it.”

She stepped around the cord and read the chalk mark on the outside. It was old enough that one letter had rubbed away. She ran her thumb beside the mark, not over it. “These are the spare ones from the east storehouse. I can put a note on the lid, but I won’t label them as approved.”

“Can you make the note now?” Annabeth asked.

Thalia looked at the label, then at her sister. “Do you want me to?”

“Only if you think it would help the crew tell what they are.”

“It would.” Thalia reached for a chalk stub in her pocket. “I’ll write where they came from. Not what they’re for.”

Annabeth moved to the other side of the path to give her room. “Thank you.”

Thalia wrote the source in small block letters. She did not add any instruction about where the tiles belonged. When she finished, she turned the box so the writing faced the approach and pressed the lid down again.

“You could have done that without asking,” Percy said.

“I could have.” Thalia dusted the chalk from her fingers. “I promised I’d protect my sister without treating her like she belonged to me. That doesn’t mean I need a permit to use chalk.”

Annabeth's smile reached her eyes this time. “No. It means you check when the choice belongs to me.”

Thalia nodded. “That’s the part I’m trying to learn.”

There was no answer Percy could add that would improve it. He looked at the cord, then at the clear path. The tiles were labeled without being assigned. The tool box had not moved. The channel remained untested. Each small decision made the worksite easier to understand, but none of them turned the morning into a repair.

Grover poured lemonade into the cups. He filled one, then stopped when Ella raised a hand.

“Not yet,” she said. “I’m going to move the notebook away from the pitcher.”

He waited. She set the closed notebook on the opposite end of the ledge, then nodded. Grover finished pouring. The lemonade caught a sliver of light in the glass. He took the pitcher back to the flat stone and made sure it was far from the papers.

“Thanks,” Percy said.

Grover handed him a cup. “I was just waiting.”

“Sometimes that’s the useful thing.”

“I know.” Grover settled on the low wall. “I’m still trying for my Searcher’s license. I don’t want to give it up just because I might fail another time.”

He said it lightly, but his hand stayed around his cup even after he had stopped drinking. Percy saw the way Grover’s eyes went to the path, where two younger campers were carrying a basket between them. They were arguing over who had the heavier end. One of them stopped, set the basket down, and asked the other to switch sides. The dispute ended there.

“What would help?” Percy asked.

Grover shrugged. “I don’t know yet. I don’t need a whole plan made for me.”

“Okay.”

“But maybe I could practice describing a route without having to be the person leading it. Just once. See if I can explain what I notice.”

“Do you want someone to listen?”

Grover looked at him. “Maybe. Not right now.”

“Then not right now.”

The answer was plain. Grover’s shoulders lowered a little, and he took a drink. Percy did not ask when he wanted to practice or who should help. The promise did not create an appointment. It only made it easier to leave a door open without walking through it for someone else.

Annabeth took a cup and stood beside Percy. She looked at Grover, then at the tool case. “Would you like the quiet side of the ledge while you drink?”

Grover smiled. “I would, actually.”

They moved the cups away from the notebook and left the space beside him clear. Grover sat without the group gathering around him. The two campers with the basket passed along the path, still arguing about which end had been heavier.

Clarisse looked at the cord. “Are we going to circle up or stand around until lunch?”

“If people still want to,” Annabeth said.

Silena stood and wiped the dust from her palms. “I do.”

Thalia joined her. Grover finished his cup and came back to the ledge. Ella kept the notebook closed. Clarisse stepped away from the toolbox. Percy followed Annabeth toward the open ground beyond the cord, where nobody would have to stand inside the work boundary.

They did not make a perfect ring. The path bent, and the low wall took up one side. Clarisse stayed near its edge. Thalia stood beside Annabeth but left a little space between them. Silena stood across from Percy, with Grover and Ella on either side of her. The tools remained behind them. No one took the hammer or the tiles into the circle.

---

Annabeth waited until everyone had settled. “We can say what we want to promise. If we don’t want to say one, we can pass. We can change the words before we say them, and we can revise them later. We’re not promising for anyone who isn’t here.”

“Or the people who are here but don’t want to,” Percy added.

“Right.” Annabeth glanced around the loose circle. “Does that still work?”

Clarisse gave a short nod. “It works.”

Grover lifted his cup. “I’m ready.”

Silena looked at Ella. “You don’t have to write any of this down.”

“I know.” Ella kept both hands on the closed cover. “I’m going to leave it closed unless the group asks me to open it.”

“And if we ask later?”

“I’ll ask each person what they want kept. I won’t assume their answer.”

“Good,” Silena said. “That can be your promise.”

Ella took a breath and nodded. “I promise to keep writing things down when they help, and to put the notebook away when someone needs me to listen. I’ll ask before keeping other people’s words.”

The promise sounded different from the first line she had put in the notebook. It had room for the part she cared about and the part that might make another person uncomfortable. She did not open the cover to record it. She let the pencil stay tucked between the pages.

Clarisse took her turn next, although she had said she wanted to hear the rest first. “I promise to let people help before I’m bleeding on the floor.”

Silena tilted her head. “Would you want to add that you don’t have to be bleeding first?”

“I know what I mean.”

“I know you do. I’m asking whether the wording says it.”

Clarisse let out a breath through her nose. “Fine. I promise to let people help before something gets bad enough that I can’t handle it alone.”

“That’s more flexible,” Thalia said.

“It’s less funny,” Clarisse replied.

“You can keep the joke too.”

“Then I’ll keep both.” Clarisse looked around the circle. “I promise to ask for help before I’m bleeding on the floor. And before then, if someone sees I’m making a mess of something, I’ll try not to turn their offer into a fight.”

Silena did not smile at the joke this time. She stepped toward Clarisse and held out her hand, palm up. “Would you like help returning these tools later?”

Clarisse looked at the toolbox. It was not heavy, and there was no danger in carrying it. She could have said no. Percy watched her jaw move once before she answered.

“Yes. You can take the small case. I’ll take the box.”

Silena nodded. “All right.”

It was not a crisis and it did not need to become one. Clarisse had asked for a hand with one task, and Silena had accepted the size of the offer. The promise had already become something more useful than an impressive sentence: a way to ask without forcing either of them into a story about what the answer meant.

“Thalia?” Annabeth asked.

Thalia looked at her sister for a moment. “I promise to protect you without treating you like you belong to me.”

Annabeth’s face softened, but she did not reach for Thalia or ask her to explain. “Thank you.”

“I also promise to ask for help before I’m exhausted.” Thalia glanced at the tiles. “I’m still allowed to carry my own box.”

“Of course,” Annabeth said.

“I’m not trying to make one promise cover every minute.” Thalia folded her arms, then unfolded them. “If I’m already tired, I’ll say so. If I’m not, I don’t need anyone to decide for me.”

“That sounds fair,” Percy said.

Thalia’s mouth twitched. “You don’t get to grade it.”

“I was agreeing.”

“I know. I’m still not asking you to grade it.”

Annabeth gave a quick laugh, and Thalia looked pleased with herself. She did not put an arm around her sister or turn the moment into a family speech. She stayed where she was and let Annabeth decide what came next.

Silena folded her hands behind her back. “I promise to say what I want, not only what everyone else needs.”

Clarisse studied her. “Do you know what you want today?”

“I do.” Silena shifted her weight. “I want to take the hammer back and leave the rest of the worksite alone. Then I want to sit somewhere sunny and not be the person who remembers every practical thing.”

“You already remembered the hammer,” Percy said.

“That was the last thing I agreed to remember.”

“Do you want help with the case?” Clarisse asked.

“Yes. And after that I want people to leave me alone for a little while.”

Clarisse nodded. “We can do that.”

Silena gave the smallest smile. “Thank you.”

The request was simple. She was not asking them to make her day easier in every way, or to guess how long “a little while” meant. She wanted to carry a smaller case, return the hammer, and take some time to herself. Percy could understand that without turning it into a promise he had to supervise.

Ella had already made her promise. Grover glanced at the others, but he did not seem ready to speak. Annabeth looked toward him and waited rather than filling the pause.

After a few seconds, Grover said, “I promise to keep trying for my Searcher’s license, even if I fail again.”

“Do you want to add anything?” Percy asked.

“Maybe that I can ask for help when I know what kind.” Grover raised the cup. “But I don’t want to sign up for practice today.”

“You don’t have to.”

“Good.” Grover drank. “Then that’s the promise.”

Percy went after him. The words came easily enough until he imagined saying them where everyone could hear. He could make a joke, keep it short, make it sound like something no one could use to look too closely. He did not want the circle to become a report on his relationship or his memories. He could make a promise about what he would try to do now without explaining where the words had come from.

He could choose something true without giving them that history.

“I promise to tell people I care about when I’m scared, instead of acting like nothing bothers me.”

Clarisse raised an eyebrow. “That one might be hard to check.”

“I don’t think you need to check it.”

“Good answer.”

“I’m not trying to make everyone responsible for fixing it. Just tell the truth before I start pretending I’m not worried.”

Annabeth watched him, her expression quiet. She did not reach for his hand. She did not ask what he was scared of. The room they had made for a promise left room for its limit too.

“What about your promise?” she asked.

Percy glanced at her. “I’ll stop making terrible seaweed jokes when you’re trying to be serious.”

“That’s impossible,” Annabeth said.

“Then I’ll try.”

She smiled. “That’s better.”

It got a laugh from Clarisse and Grover. Nobody asked him to prove that it would be difficult. Nobody asked Annabeth why she had smiled. The joke could stay a joke, and the part beneath it could stay Percy’s to explain when and if he chose.

Annabeth took her turn. “I promise to ask what people want instead of deciding for them. And I promise to say clearly when I want company and when I need space.”

Thalia looked at her sister. “Those go together.”

“They do for me.” Annabeth glanced around the ring. “But I don’t want that to mean everyone has to explain every private thing before they can take space. Asking isn’t a way to make somebody justify the answer.”

“Agreed,” Silena said.

“I’m not saying we should keep secrets that matter,” Clarisse said. “I’m saying there are things people can keep to themselves until they’re ready.”

“Or forever,” Percy said. “If it’s theirs.”

Clarisse looked at him. “That sounds like a rule.”

“It’s my opinion.”

“Then I can disagree with it.”

“You can.”

Annabeth let them have the silence that followed. It did not need a vote. She had not promised to tell the circle everything about herself. She had not promised to hand over the photograph or explain the part of her life only Percy could understand. She had promised to ask what other people wanted and to speak plainly about whether she wanted company. Those were choices she could make without turning privacy into a betrayal.

Thalia’s shoulders eased. She kept her eyes on the space between the group, not on Annabeth’s face. “I promise to ask before I decide what you need,” she said. “Especially when I’m worried.”

Annabeth nodded. “Thank you.”

The promise did not erase the way Thalia had learned to watch over her sister. It gave her a new question to ask before she acted. Percy could see the difference in the way she held herself: still alert, still Thalia, but not braced to take responsibility for every answer.

Ella touched the notebook cover. “I’m keeping mine as written in my head. I won’t write anyone else’s promise unless they ask me to.”

“Then we don’t need a copy,” Annabeth said.

Ella looked at the closed book. “I can leave a blank page for the rules of the process. Not names or promises. Just: ask, choose, change.”

“Do you want to?”

“Yes.”

“Then it’s yours to write.”

Ella opened the notebook to a fresh page. She wrote three short words, then turned the notebook around so everyone could see them. Beneath them she added: **No one has to speak. Words can change. Nothing here is a camp rule.** She stopped there and put the pencil down.

“Is that the copy?” Clarisse asked.

“It’s a reminder for me,” Ella said. “If we decide it isn’t useful, I can cross it out.”

“Keep it,” Annabeth said. “It isn’t a record of what any of us promised.”

Ella closed the notebook again. “Then I’ll keep it that way.”

---


The conversation lasted longer than the promises themselves. People changed a few words, asked questions, and made sure nobody had agreed to anything by standing in the same loose circle. When they finished, Ella put her pencil back between the pages and slipped the notebook into her bag.

“Do we have to do anything with it now?” Percy asked.

“We have to put the tools away,” Clarisse said. “That’s one thing.”

“And make sure the path stays open,” Annabeth added. “The maintenance crew will need room if they come by.”

“Then we do those things,” Silena said. She picked up the small hand-tool case. “Clarisse, this is the one you wanted me to take?”

“Yes.”

Silena waited while Clarisse lifted the larger box. The latch held. Clarisse set it down again and tested the catch with her thumb.

“It’s jammed,” she said.

“Do you want me to try?” Silena asked.

Clarisse looked at the case, then at Silena’s hand. “Hold it steady while I press the side. Don’t pull the latch.”

Silena shifted her grip. Clarisse pressed once, then adjusted her thumb and pressed again. The latch clicked open. Nothing had been stuck inside except a rag folded around the hammer head and a short measuring cord left over from earlier work. She checked the contents, closed the box, and set the hammer in the top tray.

“Thanks,” Clarisse said.

“You’re welcome.”

She did not add that it had been easy or that she could have done it alone. Clarisse did not offer to carry the case after all. They had worked out the help she wanted, and that was enough. Silena took the small case and started along the path toward the storehouse. She stopped after a few steps and turned back.

“I’m going to sit on the sunny side of the dining pavilion after I return this,” she said. “I’d like a while by myself.”

“Do you want us to come find you after?” Thalia asked.

“No. I’ll come back if I want company.”

“Okay.”

Silena left at an ordinary pace. Clarisse watched her go, then lifted the larger box again. She was not hurt. She was not struggling. She had asked for the part she wanted, and her sister had given it to her.

Percy started to reach for the opposite handle. Clarisse’s eyes flicked toward him.

“Do you want another hand?” he asked.

“Not for this.”

“Okay.”

She carried it two steps and set it with the spare tiles. The spot was still clear of the cord. Percy coiled the rope and returned it to the tool case without crossing the boundary. Annabeth checked that the drawings were tucked away before she moved the notebook to the dry ledge.

“Should we leave the cord here?” Percy asked.

“It doesn’t need to stay. The crew can mark the area if they need to. We can tell Chiron the path is clear.”

“Then I’ll take it back.”

“Would you?”

The question was small, but Percy heard the difference between handing him a task and asking whether he wanted it. “Sure.”

He coiled the cord carefully, taking care not to drag it across the dirt. He had tied it loosely before; now the loops had begun to kink. He worked them free one by one, turning the rope through his hands until it lay flat in the coil. No knot had damaged it. The path opened back up as he lifted the line from between the stakes.

Thalia stood at the edge of the staged materials, reading the label she had written on the tile box. “It could use a second set of hands to shift it back from the path.”

Percy looked at the box. “Want me to take the other side?”

“Yes. The far handle, please.”

They lifted together. It was awkward rather than heavy, and the lid rattled once. Percy shortened his grip. Thalia did the same. They set it down a little farther from the path, still within the staging area and outside the work boundary. No tiles were removed, and the box stayed closed.

“Thanks,” Thalia said.

“Anytime.”

“Not anytime. When I ask.”

Percy smiled. “When you ask.”

She looked at the line she had written on the lid. “That could turn into a sign.”

“Don’t put ‘when I ask’ on a box of tiles.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Good. Someone will think it’s an instruction for the shrine.”

Thalia laughed. “You’ve been reading too many plans.”

“Annabeth’s plans are clear.”

“Not the plans. The labels.” She tapped the chalk note. “This one says where the tiles came from. It doesn’t say what they’re for. That’s clear enough.”

She had written the fact, not a guess. Percy liked that. It made the box useful to the next person without pretending it had an answer for the repair.

Annabeth stood by the ledge, watching the path. The folded drainage drawing was tucked into her notebook, separate from the two new shrine viewpoint cards. The university-facade study, with the lake drawing on its reverse, remained among her other cards. She had not laid the pages out for the group to pass around; Percy had seen the drainage drawing and shrine study during their earlier conversations. She checked that nothing had been left on the flat stone, then looked over the tool case, the cord, the tiles, and the clear channel approach.

“Anything else?” he asked.

“No.” She closed the notebook. “That’s enough for today.”

Clarisse crossed her arms. “You’re good at saying that.”

“I’m trying to be.”

“The old Annabeth would have checked one more measurement.”

Annabeth looked up. “You don’t know what the old Annabeth would have done.”

“I meant the one who was here yesterday.” Clarisse gestured toward the drawing. “You were back at the shrine before you had any reason to come back.”

“Oh.” Annabeth’s expression shifted. “I wanted to understand what I was seeing. I still do. But there’s a difference between looking at a question and answering it.”

Clarisse nodded. “I can tell.”

The words were quiet enough that Percy almost missed them. Clarisse did not add a joke or ask Annabeth to prove the distinction. She had seen the new cards and the old drainage drawing. She knew the maintenance crew had not checked the foundation. That was all she needed to know.

“Where are you going?” Grover asked.

“To put the tools away,” Clarisse said. “Silena’s taking the small case.”

“I’m carrying the cord,” Percy said.

“I can carry that,” Clarisse replied.

Percy looked at the coil. “Do you want it?”

“No. I’m telling you I can.”

“Right. I’ll carry it.”

Clarisse’s mouth bent in a quick smile. “See? Asking is working already.”

“Don’t make me do it all day.”

“I won’t.”

“Thanks.”

She started toward the tool shed and he walked beside her with the cord. The other campers drifted along behind them. Annabeth stayed by the ledge long enough to tuck her papers away. Thalia checked the lid once more. Grover collected the empty cups. Ella walked last, notebook still closed.

“Could I ask you something?” Percy said when Clarisse was out of earshot.

“You just did.”

“I’m trying to get better at that.”

“Then ask the actual thing.”

“Do you think we’ve made enough promises for one day?”

Clarisse glanced at the coil in his hands. “We haven’t made any promises to me yet.”

“You made one in the circle.”

“I said I’d try not to turn help into a fight. I didn’t say I’d be happy about it.”

“That sounds like a promise.”

“It sounds like a thing I’m going to try. I can change the wording if it stops fitting.”

“What would you change it to?”

“I don’t know. That’s why I said I can change it later.” She stepped around a shallow rut. “You’re acting like we need to get the wording right today.”

“I guess I want it to mean something.”

“It can mean something without being perfect.”

They reached the shed. Clarisse stopped by the door and held it open with her shoulder. Percy carried the coil inside and put it on the labeled peg. He did not see a reason to ask what she wanted him to do with the tool case. She had already picked it up.

“I’m not saying it doesn’t matter,” Clarisse said. “I’m saying you can’t make a rule so good that nobody ever has a bad day again.”

“I know.”

“You don’t have to look so disappointed about it.”

“I’m not.”

“Your face is.”

“My face is fine.”

Clarisse snorted. “Sure.”

He leaned the shed door open while she set the box on a low shelf. The hammer returned to its place with a soft clank. The measuring cord stayed on its peg. When she closed the door, she checked the latch without pulling at it.

“Are you coming to lunch?” Percy asked.

“Eventually.”

“Silena wanted some time alone.”

“I heard her.”

“You won’t go looking?”

Clarisse frowned. “I said I heard her.”

“Right. Sorry.”

She examined him for a moment. “You’re not supposed to turn every promise into a test.”

“I didn’t mean to.”

“I know. Just—if I say I’ll do something, give me a chance to do it before you check.”

Percy nodded. “I can do that.”

“Good.” She started back toward the pavilion. “Now let’s get food before the toast disappears again.”

The toast was not going to last until lunch. Percy did not point that out. He followed her toward the path, trying to remember that trust could include leaving a promise alone for a while.


The pavilion filled quickly at lunch. Percy sat at the Cabin Three table with Clarisse, while Silena chose a seat at the far end near the open side. She had found a spot in the sun, exactly as she said she wanted. Clarisse looked at her once, then returned to the food. She did not wave Silena over or ask when she would be done.

Annabeth sat with Thalia. She had put the folded sketch from breakfast back in her notebook, and her own card stack stayed tucked away. The photograph was still in Percy’s cabin bag. The shrine promise had not made either object public, and no one at the pavilion knew what the photograph contained.

“You’re quiet,” Clarisse said.

“I’m eating.” Percy lifted his fork. “It’s hard to do both.”

“Not for most people.”

Silena glanced up from her plate. “He can manage two things at once. He just chooses the wrong two.”

“Thanks for the support.”

“You’re welcome.”

She returned to her meal. Percy had expected her to join the banter, but she had asked for a while without having to make the group comfortable. He let the quiet stand. Clarisse did too, though she gave him a look when he almost said something else.

“What?” he whispered.

“You’re about to ask if she’s okay.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You were.”

He put his fork down. “I can ask later.”

“She said she’ll come back if she wants company.”

“I heard her.”

“Then let her.”

It was easy advice when Clarisse said it. Percy reached for the bread basket instead of looking across the table again. The crust was still warm from the oven. He tore off a piece and passed it to Clarisse. She took it without making a joke about who had earned it.

Annabeth and Thalia were talking on the other side of the pavilion. Their conversation rose and fell with the other campers’ voices, too low for Percy to follow. He did not try. Annabeth caught his eye once and lifted her cup. He lifted his in answer. That was enough across the distance.

After lunch, Percy had arena duty. Clarisse went ahead to check the racks. Thalia carried the newly labeled tile box to the place it had been staged before, keeping it well clear of the path. She asked one of the younger campers to hold the door for her, then thanked him when he did. The boy jogged off toward the pavilion, pleased to have been useful.

“You didn’t need my help,” Percy said when she passed him.

“No. I needed someone to hold the door.”

“That’s still help.”

“Exactly.”

She continued toward the storage building. Annabeth was a few paces behind, with her notebook under one arm. She was not carrying the architecture cards out in the open. Percy could see the edge of the notebook, but no drawing. The distinction had become ordinary: a thing could matter without being displayed.

The arena was busy. Campers were rotating through practice pairs, and Clarisse had put the padded shields in a row along the fence. She checked the straps herself, then called for Percy to hold one of them while she tested the buckle.

“Want me to hold it?” he asked, more out of habit than doubt.

“I just asked you to.”

“Right.”

He braced the shield against his thigh. Clarisse pulled the strap tight, then loosened it by one notch. She tugged the edge to make sure it held. It did.

“Good,” she said. “That one’s ready.”

“Any other jobs you want me to do?”

“No. Go run the warm-up.”

Percy took a step toward the group, then turned back. “If I see something you should know about, do you want me to say it?”

“Say it.”

“And if you don’t need help?”

“I’ll tell you.”

He nodded. Clarisse had not promised she would accept every offer. She had promised to try not to turn it into a fight. The difference mattered. Percy went to the center of the arena and called for the younger campers to pair up by height and reach, not by who they liked best. Two of them groaned at the assignment. One looked toward Clarisse, ready to argue.

“Try it once,” Percy said. “If it’s a bad match, we’ll switch.”

The camper scowled, but stepped into place. The first drill was slow. The pair traded padded strikes with enough space between them to hear the count. One person moved too early, then stopped and reset. No one was hurt. No one had to pretend the first try was perfect.

Clarisse watched from the shield rack. She did not take over when Percy’s count faltered. She waited until he corrected it. When one of the campers raised the shield at the wrong angle, Percy asked him to hold still while he checked the strap. The boy let him. Percy tightened it, then asked if it felt comfortable. The camper said yes and resumed the drill.

The work had no shrine stones or uncertain measurements. It was a practice round with a clear start and stop, but Percy found himself watching for the same small thing: whether people could say when something did not work and still continue. The promise they had made at the site did not make the arena safer on its own. It gave them words to use when they noticed a problem.

When the drill ended, Clarisse picked up the shields one by one. Percy stepped over to help, then stopped within reach.

“Want me to take half?”

“Yes. The blue ones.”

He collected the three she indicated. They stacked them on the rack, checking the straps as they went. One buckle had twisted under its loop. Clarisse pointed it out. Percy fixed it without comment.

“That’s all,” she said.

“Okay.”

She waited a moment, perhaps expecting him to say something else. Percy did not. Clarisse put the final shield away and shut the rack door.

“See? Nobody died.”

“Maybe the day’s young.”

“Don’t tempt it.”

They left the arena together. Clarisse went toward the forge path, and Percy headed for the dining pavilion to return a borrowed cup. He kept thinking about her not asking for every kind of help, and her not refusing the one she wanted. It seemed small. He could imagine how quickly he might forget it if he treated the promise as a lesson he had already passed.

The sun had shifted by the time he reached the pavilion. He saw Silena at the sunny table, her plate clean and a small pouch of beads open beside her. She was turning a piece of blue glass between her fingers. There was room at the opposite end of the table, but Percy did not sit down.

“Is that a new design?” he asked from a few steps away.

“It’s a possibility.”

“Do you want me to look?”

“Not yet.”

“Okay.”

She looked up. “You’re learning.”

“I’m trying.”

“Good. I don’t want to explain it before I know whether I like it.”

“That makes sense.”

“Do you need something?”

“No. I was returning a cup.” He held it up. “I can go.”

“You can sit if you want. I’m not asking you to.”

The offer was different from an invitation she had to manage. Percy set the cup on the return tray. “I’ll see you later.”

Silena nodded and turned back to the bead. He left her at the table with the sunlight and the choice of whether the blue glass belonged in a necklace, a bracelet, or nowhere. There was no new gift or promise in it. She was allowed to try a design and change her mind.

When Percy crossed the path, he found Grover sitting on the low wall with his pipes across his lap. The case was open at his feet. He did not look as if he were about to play. He was drawing a rough map on a scrap of paper with a stick.

“Working on a route?” Percy asked.

“Maybe.” Grover smoothed the page against his knee. “I thought about what I said this morning.”

“About the license?”

“And about practicing. Not the whole test. Just explaining what I notice.” Grover glanced at the map. “Would you listen to me talk through the route from here to the amphitheater? You don’t have to decide if it’s right. I just want to see whether I can say it clearly.”

“Do you want me to ask questions?”

“After I finish.”

“Okay.” Percy sat on the other end of the wall. “Go ahead.”

Grover set his finger on the scrap. “We go east from the pavilion until the path forks near the pine. The amphitheater is down the left branch. If someone takes the right branch, they end up near the garden sheds.”

“Where’s the pine?”

“Which one?”

“The one at the fork.”

Grover looked at the paper, then at the path. “That’s a fair question.” He added a second marker. “There’s a split trunk. The taller pine is farther up, and the fork is beside the split one.”

“Would that be enough if you’d never seen the place?”

“Probably not. I could use the painted sign. It’s on the post just before the split.”

“Is the sign visible from the pavilion?”

“No. But I can say it comes after the low fence.” Grover marked the fence. “Then the left path slopes down. The right one stays flat.”

He spoke more steadily after that. The instructions did not need to be beautiful. They needed to help someone find the next point. When he finished, he ran his thumb along the edge of the paper.

“Now you can ask.”

“Would the route still work if someone came from the cabins instead?”

“I’d have to start at a different place.”

“Would you rather draw a second start, or say the starting point first?”

“Say it. Then they know which route I’m describing.”

He wrote **from pavilion** at the top of the paper. It was a small correction, but he made it himself. Percy did not tell him he would pass the licensing exam or that this was exactly what the testers wanted. He did not know either thing.

“Thanks for listening,” Grover said.

“Thanks for asking.”

“Do you think it was useful?”

“Yeah. I could follow it after you added the split pine and the sign.”

Grover rolled the paper once and tucked it into his pipe case. “That’s useful to know.”

He did not ask Percy to schedule another practice. Percy did not offer one on the spot. Grover had chosen what kind of help he wanted; for today, one route had been enough.


---

The afternoon stretched into a quieter part of the day. Percy finished his arena duty and went back toward the pavilion to return a pair of practice gloves. The paths were less crowded. A few campers crossed the grass carrying armfuls of laundry, and someone was humming near the forge. The ordinary noises made the shrine feel farther away than it had that morning.

He found Annabeth at the edge of the practice field, sitting on a low bench with her notebook closed beside her. She was not drawing. The cover lay under one hand, and she was watching two campers try to stack a set of wooden targets without letting them topple.

“You’ve been out here a while,” Percy said.

“I was waiting for Thalia.”

“Did she say she was coming?”

“No. I thought she might.” Annabeth glanced at him. “I could have asked.”

“You could still ask.”

“I could. I decided I didn’t need to.” She leaned back against the bench. “I’m trying not to turn every thought into a task.”

“Good plan.”

“Is it?”

“I don’t know. It sounds like a plan you can change.”

Her smile was brief but real. “That’s the standard now?”

“It’s a good standard.”

She tapped the notebook with one finger. “I brought the shrine cards back with me. I’ve been thinking about what I’m allowed to do with a drawing once someone else can see it.”

“What do you mean?”

“If a person sees a line and assumes it’s an instruction, that’s partly because I didn’t label what the line means. But I can’t label everything so no one ever misunderstands it. I can be clear about what I saw, what I inferred, and what’s still unknown. After that, I have to let the person ask.”

“Or ask them what they think it means.”

“Yes.” She looked down at the notebook. “I want to get better at that. I keep acting as if being precise will let me control what happens next.”

“You’re allowed to want the drawing to be precise.”

“I do. I’m not sure I need it to control the other person.”

Percy watched the two campers with the wooden targets. One of them had put a wide piece on the bottom. The other had tried to balance a narrow one over it. The stack leaned. They both pulled their hands away before it fell.

“Maybe you can make it easier to understand without deciding what someone does,” he said.

“Maybe.”

The campers began again, this time swapping the positions of the pieces. The stack stayed upright for three levels, then wobbled. They steadied it with their palms and began laughing. One of them called the arrangement a tower. The other said it was a wall. Neither one insisted on a name.

Annabeth rested her hand on the notebook. “The group promise could be like that. A way to ask how someone sees the thing, not a way to decide for them.”

“Do you think they’ll remember the wording?”

“Not perfectly.”

“What if they change it?”

“Then they change it.”

The answer was easy, but Percy could tell she was watching him to see whether he believed it. He did. He also knew how quickly he could turn “we’ll keep choosing” into “you promised you would.” He had done that in the other life, sometimes without meaning to. He could remember the sound of the old vow and the way it had made the future seem like a path they could both see.

“I like that it can change,” he said. “I don’t want to be the person who keeps the original version and uses it to win.”

“Neither do I.”

“Even if the original version was good?”

“Especially then. A good promise can still be wrong for a person later.”

He knew what she meant. The group’s circle had not asked them to leave their private lives behind. It had not required either of them to name the photograph or the promise they remembered making before this life. Annabeth had not explained what she was carrying to Thalia. Percy had not told Clarisse why the line about fear came naturally to him. Their friends had not asked for those things.

Annabeth looked toward the path that led down to the lake. “Do you want to walk after the fire tonight?”

“Do you want to?”

“Yes.”

“Then I do too.”

“Good. If either of us changes our mind, we can say so.”

“That sounds less like a date and more like a weather report.”

“It’s still useful.”

“I didn’t say it wasn’t.”

She shifted her notebook to her other side. “I want to ask you something, and I don’t want you to answer because you think it’s the answer I want.”

“All right.”

“Do you want the group to meet again? Not today. Some other time.”

Percy considered it. He liked the idea that the circle could become something people came back to, but he did not want it to turn into an obligation or a monthly inspection of whether they had all succeeded. “Maybe. If they want to. I wouldn’t want a date set now.”

“Neither would I.” Annabeth pressed the notebook’s cover flat. “We can say we might ask again next month, if it still seems useful. That’s an intention, not a schedule.”

“And if we don’t?”

“Then we don’t. Nothing breaks.”

“Would you want Ella to write that down?”

“No. It can wait until someone actually asks.”

“Then we don’t have to decide now.”

She nodded. The exchange did not add an item to Ella’s page or put a date on anyone’s calendar. It stayed between them as an idea they might offer later, if the group still wanted it. No one had to make a promise about meeting again in order for today’s circle to count.

The campers at the practice field had reached five levels. The top piece was shaped like a crooked star. It tilted to one side, and both of them held their breath as it settled. Then the stack stood. One camper let out a cheer. The other raised both hands as if she had won a tournament.

Annabeth watched them for a moment. “That’s what I mean about a plan changing.”

“I thought it was a tower.”

“It became a tower.”

“Maybe they’ll call it a wall tomorrow.”

“Then we’ll ask.”

A breeze lifted the edge of her notebook. She held it down with her palm. Percy wanted to ask whether she had drawn anything about him in the shrine study, but the question would have made the sketch about him. He let it go.

“Do you want me to walk you back to Cabin One?” he asked.

“I’m going to find Thalia.”

“Want me to come?”

“No. I want to talk to her alone.”

“All right.”

He did not need to know what the talk would be about. Annabeth rose and put the notebook under her arm. She paused at the path’s fork.

“Will you be at the fire?”

“I think so.”

“I’ll see you there if I am.”

“That works.”

She went up the path toward the cabins. Percy waited until she had passed the corner, then turned back to the pavilion. He had asked what she wanted. She had answered. The next choice belonged to her.

The rest of the afternoon passed in small jobs. Percy helped carry water to the arena, returned a stack of practice cloths to the laundry basket, and repaired a broken strap on one of the padded shields with a spare buckle from the supply drawer. Clarisse checked the fit when he finished. It held. She told him to leave the other straps for tomorrow’s gear check, then went to meet a pair of younger campers who needed an extra practice partner.

He did not ask whether Silena had come back from her quiet time. He saw her later near the cabins, walking with Thalia and carrying the small bead pouch. She was talking with her hands, the pouch held safe in her closed fist. Thalia laughed at something she said. Silena looked relaxed, but Percy couldn’t know what she had decided about the design. He did not ask.

Grover passed him on the path with his pipes in their case. The folded route sketch was tucked into his pocket. Percy nearly asked whether he wanted to practice another one. He stopped himself before the question came out. Grover had already said he might ask for help later. Percy could wait for him to decide.

“See you at the fire?” Grover asked.

“If you’re going.”

“I think I am.”

“Then I’ll see you there.”

Grover nodded and continued up the hill. The map stayed in his pocket. Percy did not know whether he would use it again, and there was no reason he needed to know yet.

---

When the campfire was lit, the sky had gone from pale gold to a deeper blue. Percy sat with his back against a log, knees drawn up, while the first campers gathered around the ring. The fire made a low crackle as a branch shifted. Grover took out his pipes and tested a few notes, not yet starting a song. The sound carried across the clearing and returned from the trees in a softened echo.

Ella had brought her notebook, but she kept it closed beside her. She did not pass it around. The three words she had written at the shrine were hers to keep as a reminder; they did not become a list everyone had to sign.

Clarisse sat beside Silena. Silena had returned to the group, but she chose the edge of the log where she could leave without stepping over anyone. Clarisse put a cup down near her and did not ask whether she wanted company. Silena picked it up and drank. That was her answer for now.

Thalia sat on Annabeth’s other side. Percy took the open place beside Annabeth and touched his shoulder to hers. She leaned against him, then settled back on her own. He did not need to hold her there. Their bodies could be close without one keeping the other from moving.

Grover began a song. It was one Percy knew, a slow tune with a repeated phrase the campers could hum after hearing it once. A few voices joined in. Others kept quiet and listened. The fire popped. Sparks rose and disappeared before they reached the branches.

When the song ended, Ella glanced at her notebook. “Would anyone want to talk about doing the circle again sometime?”

Clarisse answered first. “Not tonight.”

“Me neither,” Silena said. “I’d rather just be here.”

Grover looked down at his pipes. “Maybe another time. I don’t know when.”

“That’s enough of an answer,” Ella said. She kept the notebook closed.

Thalia nudged her boot against the dirt. “We could ask again next month. If we still want to. No date now.”

“Maybe,” Annabeth said. “I’d want it to stay optional. Not because we’re supposed to have a meeting every month.”

“Agreed,” Percy said. “If we want another one, we can ask. If we don’t, we don’t.”

Clarisse leaned back against the log. “That’s the first schedule I’ve ever liked.”

“It’s not a schedule,” Annabeth said.

“Exactly.”

Ella tapped the cover of her notebook once. “I can remember to ask. I won’t put it on a calendar.”

“Good,” Silena said. “I don’t want a reminder telling me I’m late to a promise.”

“No one’s late,” Grover said.

“Then we agree.” Silena set the cup down. “I might want to come. I might not. I can decide when there’s something to decide.”

The idea stayed where they had left it: possible, not booked. No one set a date or asked Ella to record a future meeting. The circle had been made once, in the daylight, without a ceremony. If they did it again, it would be because they chose to return.

A camper from the far side of the fire stood up and brushed ash from his trousers. He looked around as if he were waiting for someone to tell him whether he could go. Grover shifted to make a gap in the circle.

“You heading out?” Grover asked.

“Yeah. I promised my brother I’d help him with the canoe rack.”

“All right. See you tomorrow.”

The camper nodded and walked toward the path. Nobody called him back or asked why he was leaving. Grover watched until he had passed the edge of the clearing, then turned toward the fire again.

“I don’t think we should make people stay just because they’re alone,” he said. “If someone wants to sit here, we make room. If they want to go, we let them.”

“That’s different from leaving someone alone,” Thalia said.

“It is.” Grover rested his pipes across his knees. “Maybe I want to promise that no one gets left out when they want to be here. And that we don’t decide they want to be here if they say they don’t.”

Silena lifted her cup. “That’s a better promise.”

“Then I’ll try to keep it.”

The fire burned low enough that one of the campers fetched another log. He asked where it should go, and Clarisse pointed to the side where the flames needed fuel. He set it down carefully and returned to his place. The circle changed shape to make room for the new log and the person carrying it. No one stood up to redraw it.

Percy looked around at the people gathered there. Their promises had different sizes. Some were about how to ask; some were about how to make space; one was about continuing to try for a license. Nobody had promised to be perfect. Nobody had promised that their friends would never be frightened, lonely, or difficult. The words were not a spell, and the shrine had not answered them. The fire kept burning because someone had fed it another log.

That seemed right to him. A promise could ask a person to choose something again. It could not make the choice for them.

“Do you think the circle counts if Ella doesn’t write it down?” he asked.

Ella heard him. “It counts if we remember choosing it. I can write my own note. I don’t need to keep everyone else’s words.”

“What if we forget the words?”

“Then we can ask each other again.”

Clarisse snorted. “That’s more meetings.”

“Only if we want one.”

“Fine.”

Grover began another song. This time the tune was quicker and someone clapped along too fast. Silena corrected the rhythm by tapping her cup with one finger. Thalia joined in, badly on purpose. Annabeth laughed and shook her head. Percy leaned closer.

“You promised to stop making seaweed jokes,” she whispered.

“I did.”

“Are you going to keep it?”

“I’m going to try.”

“Good.”

“I can’t promise what I’ll do if the seaweed starts it.”

She bumped his shoulder. “Then I reserve the right to remind you.”

“You can remind me. You can also change the rule.”

“I might.”

The exchange was quiet enough that it belonged to them, but it did not require a secret explanation. Percy took her hand when she offered it. She held his fingers for a while, then let go when she reached for her cup. The promise had not made the contact permanent. It made the moment something they could both choose.

After the song, Clarisse and Silena went to return their cups. Thalia stayed by the fire with Ella, who had opened the notebook to her own page and was adding a small line beneath the three words: **ask again only if wanted**. She showed it to Thalia, who nodded. The rest of the page remained blank.

Percy waited beside Annabeth. He did not ask whether she wanted a walk before she had finished her drink. When she set the cup down and looked at him, he met her eyes.

“Want to go?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Lake?”

“If you want.”

“I do.”

They said goodnight to the others and walked away from the fire. Behind them, Grover’s pipes started another tune. The camp’s laughter rose and fell between the trees. The path opened toward the lake, dark where the branches crossed it and silver where the stars showed through.

They walked side by side, not holding hands at first. Percy could feel the old promise in the back of his mind: the words he and Annabeth had once used to tell each other they would always come back. He remembered it as a real promise from a real life. He did not need to pretend he had forgotten it, and he did not need to make it a rule for the present.

Annabeth brushed her knuckles against his. “You’re thinking.”

“I am.”

“Do you want to tell me?”

“Yes. I remember the old promise. The one about coming back to each other.” He paused. “I don’t want to use it as proof that we owe each other a future.”

“Neither do I.”

“I still want to choose you.”

“Today?”

“Today.”

She took his hand. “I choose you today too.”

They reached the dock. The campfire was a small glow behind them, and the stars were scattered over the lake’s dark surface. The water held their reflections unevenly, breaking the light whenever a small ripple reached the posts.

“Do you think we’ll remember this later?” Annabeth asked.

“I will.”

“Even if everything changes again?”

“Especially then.”

She held his hand more firmly. “Then let’s make it worth remembering.”

Percy kissed her once, softly. Annabeth leaned into him, and he put an arm around her shoulders. They stayed close for another few seconds, listening to the camp behind them and the water below.

They were not keeping a vow that someone else had written for them. They were choosing a moment they could keep, and choosing each other inside it.

For tonight, the world was simple enough: her hand in his, the sound of camp behind them, and the promise they had made because they wanted to.

That was enough to start with.
