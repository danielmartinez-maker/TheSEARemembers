## Chapter 53: River

The Arch let them go all at once.

One minute they were standing in its shadow with their ears still ringing from gods and monsters and falling water, and the next they were just four tired kids walking downhill toward the river with the sun bleeding out low over St. Louis.

Percy felt it in his knees first. Adrenaline had carried him up all six hundred and thirty feet and back down again. Now that it was gone, his legs remembered every stair in the Gateway Arch, every jolt of the tram, every second he'd spent trying not to die that day. His backpack dug into his shoulders. His shirt stuck to his back. His shoes squelched.

Behind him, Annabeth was limping slightly on her left ankle. She was trying not to show it. Thalia had a split lip and a bruise blooming dark along her jaw where a telkhine's elbow had caught her. Grover kept stopping to pull a pebble out of his shoe, except he wasn't wearing shoes, exactly, and the pebbles were stuck in the soft fake feet he'd pulled over his hooves, and every time he stopped he sighed like the world was ending.

None of them talked much on the walk to the riverfront.

They didn't need to.

The Mississippi was right there, huge and brown and moving fast after the rain up north. It didn't look like the ocean. It didn't smell like the ocean. It smelled like mud and diesel and wet concrete and fried food from the riverboats. But it was water, and it was moving, and it was alive, and Percy felt his whole body lean toward it the way a plant leans toward light.

He stopped at the low concrete wall that separated the walkway from the slope down to the water. Put his hands on it. Breathed.

"You okay, Kelp Head?" Annabeth asked, coming up beside him.

"Yeah," he said. "Just... need a minute."

She studied his face. She didn't push. That was new, this past year — she used to push. Now she waited.

"We'll be by the steps," she said. "Don't fall in."

"I won't," he said, and tried to smile. "Unless the river asks nicely."

She snorted, but her eyes stayed worried for half a second longer before she turned and followed Thalia and Grover toward a patch of grass that was mostly mud, where Grover had already collapsed dramatically onto his backpack.

Percy stayed by the wall.

The river kept moving.

He hadn't planned to talk to it. He was too tired for god stuff. Too tired for spirit stuff. But the water tugged at something behind his ribs, that old familiar tug he'd felt since he was twelve and had healed himself in the creek at Camp Half-Blood without understanding how. Water wanted him to notice it.

So he slipped down the riprap slope when no mortals were looking, crouched close enough that spray kissed his sneakers, and put his fingertips in.

*Hey,* he thought, not out loud. *Thanks for not letting us drown today.*

For a moment there was nothing but cold and current.

Then the river answered.

It wasn't like talking to a god. Gods were loud. Even nice gods filled up your whole head. This was older and slower, like waking up from a long nap. The Mississippi didn't speak in words at first. It spoke in feeling — silt and summer storms and a hundred thousand tributaries all humming at once, catfish and paddleboats and drowned trees, Civil War iron and prehistoric sharks' teeth buried deep in the bluffs.

*Salt-born,* it said at last. The voice was deep, male, amused, with a drawl that wasn't Southern so much as sedimentary. *Little salt-born. Far from your father's house.*

Percy's breath caught. "You know who I am?"

*I know whose smell is on you.* The water curled around his fingers, warmer than it should have been. *Poseidon. Earthshaker. He came through here once, long before this was called Mississippi. When the ice melted. He walked the flood and told us where to run. Rivers remember.*

Percy sat down hard on a wet rock. "He was here?"

*Many waters know him. We are all one water, in the end. Rain to creek to river to sea.* A pause, a slow eddy. *You carry more than his blood, young one. You carry stories.*

A chill went up Percy's spine that had nothing to do with the water. "What do you mean?"

The river spirit coalesced a little — not a full form, never a full form, but a face in the ripple, eyes like sun on muddy water, a beard of foam and sticks. Ancient. Tired. Kind in the way very old things are kind because they've seen too much to be cruel about it.

*Atlantis,* the river said. *You dream of it, don't you? White towers. Bells under the sea.*

Percy's mouth went dry. He hadn't told Annabeth about all the dreams. Not the ones where he stood on a balcony of orichalcum and coral while faceless guards moved below, where tide-tablets shone with signs he could not read awake, where a woman he could not place laughed and called him *my little minnow*. They felt familiar while he slept. That did not make them memories, and daylight never gave him the names he wanted.

"How do you know that?" he whispered.

*Because the sea remembers, and so do its sons.* The face shifted, became younger, sadder. *I was young when Atlantis fell. A small meltwater stream then, babbling to the ice. But even I felt it. The sea screamed. Poseidon screamed. He tried to hold his city above the waves and the Fates cut his strings.*

Percy pressed his hand flat to the water. His palm tingled.

"I keep seeing it," he admitted. His voice sounded thin against the wide river. "Not like a movie. Like... like I was there. In the dream there's the smell of a harbor. Hot bronze and salt and lemons. Palace doors closing. Water everywhere. I wake up feeling like I was drowning."

The river was quiet. Traffic hummed on the bridge behind them. A barge horn moaned far downstream.

*There were children of the sea in Atlantis when it fell,* the river said gently. *Not you, salt-born. Not your sisters. Old grief travels a long way through water. Dreams can carry its echoes, but an echo is not a childhood you have forgotten. Do not mistake the story for your own years.*

"Is that why — " Percy stopped. He didn't know how to ask. Is that why the city in the dreams feels different from the home they tell me about? Is that why sometimes when I'm underwater I hear bells? Is that why Dad looks at me like I'm a ghost?

*Yes,* said the Mississippi, simply.

Percy laughed, short and wet. "Great. I'm homesick for a sunken city. As if I didn't have enough problems."

*It is not a curse, salt-born. It is a story you may ask about.* The water tightened around his fingers, almost like a hand holding his. *Your father speaks of the home he has now. Ask him about the older city too. You need not have lived its loss to listen. Do you understand?*

Percy thought of his dad in his battered fishing shirt on the beach at Montauk, smiling sad around the edges. Thought of Tyson babbling about the undersea forges and palace corridors he knew so well. Thought of the way the ocean sometimes felt less like a place and more like a person holding its breath.

"Yeah," he said quietly. "I think I do."

*Good.* The face began to dissolve back into ripple. *Rest now. Tell your wise girl to rest her ankle. Tell the daughter of Zeus the sky will hold. Tell the goat-boy his tin is waiting.*

Percy blinked. "My what? Tin?"

But the presence was already fading, sinking back into silt and current and barge-wake. The water around his hand was just cold river water again. On the far bank, the sun touched the water tower and turned the whole brown river gold for three heartbeats.

Percy pulled his hand out. His fingers were wrinkled, but the bone-deep ache in his shoulders had eased. The cut on his knuckle from the Arch had closed to a pink line.

"Thanks," he said to the river.

The river, being a river, said nothing. But a small wave patted the rock beside him, leaving a smooth white shell.

There are no white shells in the Mississippi.

Percy picked it up, turned it over in his palm, and put it in his pocket for Annabeth.

---

Thalia found the steps first.

They were concrete, wide and damp, leading down toward the water but stopping short before the mud took over. Good enough to sit on. She dropped her bag, dropped herself, stretched her long legs out and tipped her head back with a groan.

"Ow," she announced to the sky. "I am ow-shaped."

"Same," Grover said, flopping beside her. He pulled off his fake feet with a sigh of pure relief and wiggled his hooves. "My hooves have blisters on their blisters. I need a meadow. I need clover. I need a juice bar."

"You need a shower," Thalia said.

"You need a shower," Grover shot back without heat.

Annabeth lowered herself more carefully onto the step above them. Her ankle throbbed. She'd twisted it on the Arch stairs when a pigeon — a normal, mortal pigeon, which was somehow more insulting — had flown straight at her face and she'd dodged into a railing. Percy had caught her elbow. She'd said she was fine. She was not fine, but she was functional, which in their line of work was basically the same thing.

She pulled her knees up, wrapped her arms around them, and watched Percy down by the water. His shoulders were hunched. His hand was in the river. The set of his jaw told her he was listening.

"He does that a lot," Thalia said quietly, following her gaze.

Annabeth glanced at her. "Does what?"

"Goes somewhere else where we can't follow."

There was no jealousy in Thalia's voice. Just observation. Thalia had spent years watching for danger on the road while raising her little sister, and she noticed things.

"He's talking to the river," Annabeth said.

"Think it'll answer?"

"It always answers him." Annabeth picked at a loose thread on her jeans. "Water likes him."

Thalia was quiet for a moment. Traffic hissed by on Memorial Drive behind them. A kid on a skateboard rattled past. Normal life kept happening six feet away, oblivious.

"Can I ask you something?" Thalia said. "Sister to sister? Not lieutenant to strategist or whatever."

Annabeth looked up, surprised and a little wary. Thalia didn't do vulnerable often. When she did, it mattered.

"Yeah. Of course."

Thalia fiddled with the silver bracelet on her wrist, the one with the owl charm Annabeth had given her two winters ago. She never took it off.

"Are you scared?" Thalia asked. "Right now. On this quest."

Annabeth blinked. "Yes. Are you kidding? I'm terrified most of the time."

Thalia huffed a laugh. "Good. Me too. I thought maybe it was just me getting soft."

"You're not soft. You're the least soft person I know."

"Tell my split lip that." Thalia touched her jaw and winced. "It's just... after the Arch. When that thing came through the floor and Percy yelled for us to run and I just — I froze. For like half a second. I was thinking about Jason."

Annabeth's chest tightened. "You haven't heard from him?"

"No. And I keep telling myself he's fine, he's Roman, he's got a whole legion, but..." Thalia's voice dropped. She stared at the river instead of Annabeth. "I dreamed last night he was falling. Off a cliff. Into sky. And I couldn't fly fast enough to catch him. I woke up and my hands were shaking."

Annabeth reached over without thinking and put her hand over Thalia's. Thalia turned hers palm-up and squeezed hard.

"I'm scared all the time too," Annabeth said softly. "I'm scared I'm going to miss something. Some clue. Some trap. Like if I were smarter, faster, better, nobody would get hurt. Today on the stairs, when that telkhine grabbed Grover, I knew what it was going to do half a second before it did it, and I still wasn't fast enough. Percy had to — " She swallowed. "I'm scared one day I'm going to be half a second too slow and it's going to be Percy."

Thalia nodded slowly. "Yeah. That's the one for me too. Not me dying. Them dying. You guys." She glanced at Grover, who was pretending to be asleep but had one ear — one human ear — tilted toward them. "I kept people at a distance because I thought if I didn't love anyone else, I couldn't lose anyone else. Stupid, right?"

"A little stupid," Annabeth said fondly. "Effective stupid, but stupid."

"Then you kept drawing people in anyway. Percy. Grover. And Reyna tries to pretend she doesn't count but she does." Thalia laughed wetly and wiped her nose on her sleeve. Gross. Very Thalia. "Fear's part of it, I guess. Loving people."

Annabeth leaned her head against Thalia's shoulder. Thalia leaned back. They sat like that for a minute, two daughters of Zeus and Beryl, sisters since birth, finding their way through the same fear.

"Athena told me in my old life that fear is a tool," Annabeth murmured. "Use it or it uses you."

"My dad told me to zap things until the fear stopped," Thalia said. "Her advice is better."

"Don't let her hear you say that. She'll put it on a plaque."

They both laughed. Percy was walking back up the slope now, hands in his pockets, hair wind-mussed, looking lighter than he had an hour ago. He caught Annabeth's eye and smiled — small, real.

Thalia saw the look pass between them and squeezed Annabeth's hand once more before letting go.

"For what it's worth," Thalia said, low enough only Annabeth could hear, "he's scared too. Not of dying. Of you dying. He'd burn down Atlantis all over again if it kept you safe."

Annabeth's throat closed. "I know."

"Good. Then you're both idiots." Thalia stood, wincing as her knees cracked. "Come on. Grover's drooling on his backpack. We need to find a motel before he starts eating trash."

"I heard that," Grover mumbled without opening his eyes. "I would never eat trash in Missouri. Too much concrete. I prefer New York trash. Artisanal."

Grover found the tin can because he was looking for food.

It wasn't his fault. Quest logic said: riverbank plus city park equals mortals dropping half-eaten pretzels. He'd gotten up to scan the grass while Annabeth re-tied her shoe and Thalia argued with her spear (it had gotten stuck in a crack in the steps), and his nose caught something metallic and old under the sweet rot of leaves.

"Hold on," he said, nose twitching. "Hold on, hold on—"

"Grover, if that's another 'magical taco'—" Thalia started.

"No, no, this is different. This is—" He knelt in the mud, uncaring, and scrabbled under a half-buried log. His fingers closed on cold metal. "Ha!"

He held it up triumphantly. A tin can, coffee-sized, dented, rusted around the rim, with a plastic lid jammed on crooked. Someone had painted a blue trident on the side in flaking paint, and underneath, in Sharpie, now half-washed away: *FOR THE FISH BOY — PASS IT ON.*

Percy stared. "What is that?"

"It's a drop," Annabeth breathed. She was suddenly fully alert, strategist mode snapping on despite the limp. "Underground demigod mail. Satyrs and dryads leave supplies for questers. Grover, open it. Carefully."

Grover pried the lid off with reverent fingers. Inside, packed tight in a Ziploc bag to keep out the damp, was a bundle of folded notebook paper, a little water-warped, and on top, three individually wrapped peanut butter crackers and a note that said *EAT ME FIRST — LOVE, S.*

"Oh, gods," Grover whispered. "Camp mail."

They gathered around him on the steps like kids around a campfire. Even Thalia went still.

Grover unfolded the first letter with shaking hands.

*Percy (and Annabeth and Thalia and Grover — Ella says I have to include everyone or it's rude),*

*If you're reading this, Connor Stoll's dead-drop network actually works and I owe him twenty bucks. Don't tell him I said that.*

*Camp's fine. Don't worry. Well, fine-ish. The strawberry fields got flooded last week because the naiads got in a fight with the water pump. Again. Our visiting brother (Tyson — hi, Tyson, if Ella reads this out loud) cried and then fixed the pump by hitting it. It works now. Don't ask.*

*We got your last Iris-message. Barely. Rainbow was all staticky. We heard ARCH and MONSTER and then you cut out. Typical. Next time try a better rainbow. Or don't almost die on a national monument. Your choice.*

*Clarisse.*

Annabeth laughed so hard she snorted. Percy grinned so wide his split cheek hurt.

"She cares," Grover said solemnly. "She just shows it with threats."

He unfolded the second letter. This one was on pink stationery with little Pegasus stickers in the corners. The handwriting was loopy and careful.

*Hi!!*

*It's Silena!! Clarisse said to keep it short because "paper is for tactics, not feelings," but I told her feelings ARE tactics!!*

*We miss you so much. The Aphrodite cabin braided friendship bracelets for all of you. Annabeth, yours has gray and gold beads. Thalia, yours has black and silver and a tiny lightning bolt. Grover, yours is green and DESTROYED because the strawberry vines ate it, sorry, I'll make another!! Percy, yours is blue (obviously) and I sealed it with waterproof glue so the river can't take it!! They're in the can under the letters!!*

*We are all praying for you. Really praying. Not just "oh, hope they're okay" praying. Like, altar offerings and everything. Drew complained, but even she left lip gloss for Aphrodite for good luck, so you KNOW it's serious.*

*Come home safe. Tell Annabeth her blueprints for the new climbing wall are on her bunk and NOBODY touched them (okay, Malcolm moved them two inches, but he put them back).*

*Love love love, Silena (and the whole cabin!!)*

*P.S. Ella helped me spell "altar." She's the best!!*

Thalia was already digging in the can. Sure enough, under the letters, four braided bracelets in a tiny cloth pouch. She pulled hers out and tied it on immediately, fingers clumsy.

"She remembered I like silver," she muttered, staring at it. "Damn it."

Annabeth tied hers on with a tight throat. Percy held his wrist out to her without a word, and she tied his on, double-knotted.

Grover, eyes shiny, unfolded the third letter. This one was different. The paper was covered edge to edge in neat, tiny typewriter-like handwriting, with no margins. Words crowded up the sides. At the top, in red crayon, someone — probably Clarisse — had written: *SHE WOULDN'T STOP WRITING. JUST READ THE HIGHLIGHTED PART.*

Highlighted in yellow marker was a single stanza in the middle:

*Ella says:*

*When the river bends and the arch falls behind,*
*Let the salt boy listen and the wise girl bind.*
*Three peanuts, four hearts, one tin in the mud —*
*The goat finds the way where the wild things bud.*
*Rest. The water is kind tonight.*

They were silent.

"Ella," Annabeth whispered. "She's... she's remembering things again. Prophecy fragments."

"Three peanuts," Grover said, holding up the crackers. "Four hearts. That's us."

"Rest," Thalia read over his shoulder. "The water is kind tonight."

Percy looked back at the river. The sun was gone now, leaving a violet wash. The water moved, steady, unhurried. He thought of the shell in his pocket. *Tell the goat-boy his tin is waiting.*

"She knew," he said softly.

"She always knows a little," Annabeth said. She took the letters and folded them with infinite care, sliding them into her bag's waterproof pocket. "We need to keep these."

Grover was already splitting the peanut butter crackers with the solemnity of a priest. Four equal pieces — well, three crackers into four people meant some math, and Grover did satyr math, which involved a lot of sniffing and breaking and giving Percy the biggest piece because "you talked to the river, you need salt."

They ate sitting on the steps, shoulder to shoulder, legs touching. The crackers were stale. They were perfect.

"To Connor Stoll's dead-drop network," Thalia said, raising her last crumb like a toast.

"To Silena's waterproof glue," Percy said.

"To Ella," Annabeth said quietly.

"To snacks," Grover said, "appearing exactly when needed."

They all clinked crumbs.

---

The motel was a miracle.

It was the kind of place Annabeth usually vetoed on principle — flickering neon star, ice machine humming like a dying cyclops, pool covered with a tarp — but the woman at the desk took one look at four limping teenagers with backpacks and black eyes and said, "Honey, you want the ground-floor room by the vending machines? Quietest. And there's a first-aid kit under the sink you can borrow," and charged them half price because "you look like you've been through it."

Room 7 smelled like bleach and old carpet and heaven.

Thalia kicked the door shut behind them, threw her bag on the nearest bed, and face-planted onto the mattress with her boots still on.

"Bed," she groaned into the comforter. "Bed is my god now. I worship bed."

"Boots off the bed," Annabeth said automatically, but she sat down heavily on the other bed and didn't enforce it.

Percy locked the door, checked the window, checked the bathroom — quest habits — then came back and dropped cross-legged on the floor between the beds because there wasn't a third bed and he wasn't going to argue about it. Grover immediately collapsed next to him, using Percy's leg as a pillow.

"First aid," Annabeth said. "Come here. All of you. Before you get comfortable."

She fished the kit out from under the sink and laid it open on the bedspread like a surgeon. Gauze. Antiseptic wipes. Butterfly bandages. Ibuprofen. A little tube of arnica gel Silena had shoved in her bag back at camp that she'd forgotten until now.

"Thalia. Lip."

Thalia sat up, swung her legs over the edge, and submitted with exaggerated martyrdom while Annabeth cleaned the split in her lower lip and checked her jaw.

"Does it hurt to open?" Annabeth asked, fingers gentle under Thalia's chin.

"Only when I talk, smile, eat, breathe—"

"So yes." Annabeth smeared a dot of antibiotic ointment on with a Q-tip. "No kissing strangers until it heals."

"I make no promises."

"Grover. Feet."

Grover held up his hooves mournfully. They were raw where the fake feet had rubbed. Annabeth wrapped them in cool damp washcloths first, then in gauze, tutting the whole time while Grover sighed happily.

"You have nurse hands," he said. "Like Juniper when I step on a thorn."

"Hold still, goat boy."

"Percy. Hands. And face."

Percy scooted forward. His knuckles were scraped, his left cheek had a shallow scratch from flying concrete dust at the Arch, and his shoulders were sunburned pink under his T-shirt.

Annabeth cleaned his knuckles one by one. Her touch was efficient, practiced — three years of camp infirmary duty — but her thumb lingered a second longer on his palm.

"Does this hurt?" she asked.

"No," he said. It did a little. He didn't care.

She put butterfly bandages on the worst scrape, then dabbed at his cheek. He winced.

"Sorry," she whispered.

"'S okay."

Thalia watched them from the other bed with her chin in her hand and a look Grover recognized as *big sister observing romance in the wild and finding it disgusting but endearing.* She caught Grover's eye and mouthed *finally.* Grover mouthed back *I know.*

Annabeth felt their stares. "What?"

"Nothing," Thalia and Grover said in unison, too fast.

Annabeth narrowed her eyes, but she was too tired to interrogate. She handed Percy two ibuprofen and a paper cup of water from the bathroom tap.

"Drink. Then eat something real. What do we have left?"

Inventory, motel-floor picnic style: half a bag of cheese puffs (Percy), a gas-station beef jerky stick of questionable origin (Thalia), three granola bars (Annabeth, emergency stash), a slightly squashed box of Nerds (Grover), and two warm Cokes from the vending machine because the cold ones were sold out.

They spread it all out on the scratchy comforter between the two beds and sat cross-legged around it like it was a feast.

"This," Thalia said, tearing the jerky with her teeth, "is the best meal I've ever had."

"It's literally the worst meal," Annabeth said, but she was eating cheese puffs straight from the bag Percy held open for her, orange dust on her fingers.

"Best worst meal," Grover corrected. He poured Nerds into his palm and offered them around. "Taste the rainbow. Except it's all sugar. No rainbow."

Percy cracked open a warm Coke, took a sip, made a face, and passed it to Annabeth. She drank after him without thinking about it. Thalia raised an eyebrow at Grover. Grover wiggled his eyebrows back.

Nobody said *we almost died today.* Nobody needed to. It sat under the jokes like the river under the city — moving, present, but not drowning them. Not tonight.

After they'd eaten, Grover curled up on the floor with his wrapped hooves on a pillow and his head on Percy's backpack and fell asleep mid-sentence about clover. ("Tell Juniper the Missouri grass is—") Thalia took the shower first, came out in sweats with her hair dripping, claimed the bed by the window, and was snoring softly within minutes, one hand curled around her new bracelet.

That left Percy and Annabeth on the other bed, backs against the headboard, legs stretched out, sharing the last granola bar.

"You should sleep," Annabeth said, but she didn't move.

"You should," Percy said. He broke the granola bar unevenly and gave her the bigger half. Classic Percy.

She ate it slowly. The motel air conditioner rattled. Ice clinked in the machine outside. A TV murmured through the wall.

"Percy?" she said quietly, so as not to wake the others. "What did the river say?"

He was quiet for a moment, turning the white shell over in his fingers. Then he held it out to her.

"It gave me this. For you."

Annabeth took it. It was smooth, pearly inside. She held it to her ear out of habit, smiled at herself for doing it.

"I hear traffic," she said.

He laughed. Then his smile faded into something softer, more serious.

"It remembered Atlantis," he said. "The river. It said... it said the dreams could be old stories, not my childhood. It said I should ask Dad instead of guessing."

Annabeth set the shell down carefully on the nightstand and took his hand. His knuckles were bandaged. Hers were orange with cheese dust. They fit anyway.

"I think that's true," she said. "I think gods get lonely in their memories. Asking him might help more than trying to turn a dream into a fact. You can listen without having lived it."

Percy looked at her. Really looked. Her hair was coming out of its ponytail in damp frizzled strands. She had a smudge of antibiotic ointment on her chin from patching Thalia. Her gray eyes were tired and bright and entirely his favorite thing in any world, sunken city or not.

"You always know what to say," he murmured.

"I don't," she said. "I just love you, so I try."

The words landed simple and unadorned in the humming motel room. No monsters. No prophecy. Just Annabeth, saying it plain.

Percy's ears went red. "I love you too. A lot. Like, stupidly a lot. Like—"

"I know, Seaweed Brain."

He grinned, relieved and goofy. Then his eyes went to her hair.

"Can I... do you want me to braid it? For sleeping? So it doesn't tangle?"

Annabeth stared at him. "You know how to braid?"

"...Theoretically?" Percy said. "I've seen Silena do it. How hard can it be? Three pieces, right?"

Thalia, apparently not fully asleep, mumbled without opening her eyes: "Do not let him touch your hair. He once tried to braid Tyson's hair and Tyson cried."

"I did not— Tyson did not cry, he was — he was moved," Percy protested, but he was already shifting behind Annabeth, gently gathering her curls over her shoulder. "Come on. Trust me."

Annabeth laughed — real, startled, bright laughter that made Grover snort in his sleep and Thalia smile into her pillow. "Okay. Okay. But if I wake up bald, I'm killing you."

"I accept these terms."

Percy divided her hair into three sections with intense concentration, tongue stuck out slightly. He crossed left over middle. Then right over middle. Then left — wait, which was left? He frowned. Annabeth could feel him counting under his breath.

"This is harder than sword fighting," he muttered.

"It's literally just pattern repetition," she said, shaking with suppressed laughter, which made his work harder.

"Stop moving, you're ruining my braid."

"Your braid is ruining my braid."

He tied it off with the spare hair tie from his wrist — he carried one for her now, always — and sat back proudly. "Done. Masterpiece."

Annabeth felt the back of her head. The braid started high, veered sharply left halfway down, had a mysterious bump in the middle where he'd accidentally included his own thumb, and stuck out at a jaunty angle. It was lumpy. It was uneven. It was perfect.

She grabbed the little mirror from her bag, looked, and burst out laughing so hard she had to clamp a hand over her mouth to stay quiet.

"It's awful," she gasped.

"It's artisanal," Percy said defensively, but he was laughing too, forehead pressed to her shoulder.

"It's terrible. I love it. Don't touch it. Leave it forever."

"Forever?"

"Forever."

She turned around, still laughing, eyes wet at the corners, and kissed him. Soft, cheese-puff-flavored, tired, happy. His hand came up to cup her cheek, careful of the braid — his braid — and her hands fisted in his T-shirt.

Across the room, Thalia pulled her pillow over her head. "Get a room, you have a room, oh wait, you're in it, never mind, be quiet."

They broke apart, giggling like twelve-year-olds.

Annabeth leaned her head against Percy's chest, his terrible braid squished between them. His heartbeat was steady under her ear. The river hummed far away through the walls, through the pipes, through his blood.

"Tell me about Atlantis sometime?" she whispered. "The dreams. All of it. When you're ready."

"Yeah," he said, arms tightening around her. "I will. Tomorrow. After we rest. The water is kind tonight."

She fell asleep like that, to the sound of his breathing and Grover's soft snoring and Thalia's muttered sleep-curse at Zeus, with a lumpy braid in her hair and a white river shell on the nightstand and letters from home folded safe in her bag.

Outside Room 7, the Mississippi kept moving south in the dark, carrying silt and stories to the sea.

The sea remembered.

And for one warm, cramped, snack-crumb-covered night in Missouri, so did its son — without drowning in it.
