## Chapter 100: The Thousandth Campfire

The morning of the thousandth campfire dawned clear and gold over Camp Half-Blood, and Percy Jackson woke to someone hammering on the door of Cabin Three.

He sat up with his heart already running. For one breath the sound put him in a memory from the life he remembered: darkness, an alarm, the sense that the day had gone wrong before it began. The blue-green light through the shell windows brought Cabin Three back around him. Then he heard the voice.

"Up! Up! Thousandth campfire day! Everyone up!"

It was Connor Stoll, banging a wooden spoon against a pot as he ran down the cabin row, Travis right behind him with a conch shell he was blowing badly.

Percy groaned and flopped back onto his pillow. The fountain in the corner of the Poseidon cabin trickled. Morning light filtered blue-green through the shell windows. From the guest bunk across the room, Tyson, staying through the celebration on forge leave, snored on, blissfully immune to Hermes cabin wake-up calls.

The door cracked open and Clarisse La Rue stuck her head in, already in full armor except for her helmet, her camp necklace swinging.

"Tell Stoll if he hits my door with that spoon again I'm going to feed him the spoon," she said.

Percy grinned sleepily. "Morning to you too, sis."

Clarisse snorted and came in far enough to toss something onto his bed. A folded orange T-shirt. Freshly printed. Percy unfolded it.

Across the front in bold blue Greek letters it said 1000TH CAMPFIRE, and underneath in smaller print, Camp Half-Blood — Since Forever, Probably Before You Were Born. On the back was a list of cabin symbols circling a big bonfire.

"Silena made them," Clarisse said. "Aphrodite cabin stayed up half the night printing. She saved you a medium because she says you always steal larges and then swim in them."

"She knows me," Percy said, pulling the shirt over his head. It smelled like fresh ink and strawberries.

Clarisse leaned against the doorframe, arms crossed. The corner of her mouth kept pulling up; she pressed it flat each time she caught him looking. Percy let her try.

"You know what this is about, right?" she said. "Chiron's calling it the thousandth campfire. Ella counted. She went through the camp records and the harpy memory chants and figured out tonight is number one thousand since the camp was refounded in its current spot."

Percy whistled. "A thousand?"

"A thousand," Clarisse said. "And the old satyrs say the fire remembers all of them. So tonight we remember back."

There was a softness in her voice under the gruffness. Percy heard it. He sat up straighter.

"Big party?" he asked.

"The biggest," Clarisse said. "Dinner feast. No training after lunch. Everybody decorates. Apollo music. Stories. Grover's got a speech he's been rewriting for a week and won't show anyone. And Ella and Rachel made some massive scroll thing they're keeping secret in the Big House attic. Silena's been crying about it already and she hasn't even seen it finished."

Percy laughed. "That sounds like Silena."

"It sounds like all of us," Clarisse said. She pushed off the doorframe. "Get up. Breakfast. Annabeth's probably already been up for two hours making lists."

She was right. When Percy got to the dining pavilion ten minutes later, hair still damp from a quick rinse in the fountain, Annabeth Grace was already there at the Zeus table with a wax tablet and three different colored styli, talking fast with Thalia while she ate toast with one hand.

Annabeth looked up and waved him over with her stylus. Her smile made him forget the question he had been about to ask Clarisse. He lifted two fingers in greeting, then went to make his offering.

He scraped a bit of pancake into the brazier at the Poseidon table, then carried his plate to the edge of the Zeus table and took the end of the bench Annabeth had saved for him.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi," he said, bumping his shoulder against hers. "Heard you were making lists."

"I am making lists," Annabeth said solemnly. "Thousandth campfire lists. Very important. Chiron asked me to help coordinate the story circle. A lot of people already asked for a turn. Some haven't, and they don't need one."

Thalia Grace, sprawled across the bench on Annabeth's other side with her boots up on the table until Chiron gave her a look and she swung them down, grinned at Percy.

"Seaweed Brain," she said by way of greeting. "You ready to get all nostalgic tonight? Cry in front of everybody?"

"I'm hydrated and emotionally prepared," Percy said.

Annabeth laughed and stole a strawberry off his plate. "Good, because Silena's already made three playlists. One for dinner, one for stories, one for dancing."

Percy paused. "Dancing?"

Annabeth's cheeks went faintly pink, and her gray eyes flicked to his and held. "Yeah," she said softly. "Dancing."

He squeezed her hand under the table. Their first dance had been clumsy and public, and she had still said yes when he asked again afterward.

"Good," he said quietly. "I was hoping."

Annabeth rolled the wax tablet around so he could see the schedule. “Chiron asked me to help with the story circle. He did not ask me to make people tell stories.”

“Important distinction,” Percy said.

“Very.” She had marked the amphitheater path in blue and drawn a second line around the low steps. “This leaves a wide aisle. We need room for people carrying food, and for anyone who doesn't want to climb the center steps. I asked Chiron to keep the side entrance open.”

Thalia sat across from them, one boot hooked over the bench rung. She used her left hand to hold down the corner of the schedule; the right palm was still wrapped. Percy noticed the clean bandage, then looked at the marks Annabeth had made instead of asking her to test it.

“Good route,” Thalia said. “If Connor puts a lantern in the aisle, I’m making him carry every one back himself.”

“He would call that a game,” Percy said.

“Then I’ll make it a boring game.”

Annabeth added a note. “The fire lane stays clear. No training after lunch, but we still need the path open after dark. The story circle needs a place for anyone to pass without crossing in front of the fire.”

“Can you make a path that doesn’t look like another map?” Thalia asked.

“No.”

“Fine. At least the map will be legible.”

Percy leaned over the tablet. The list had boxes for food, water, blankets, the fire crew, lanterns and the scroll. Annabeth had added a line for “people who want to help but haven't chosen a job.” He tapped it.

“That one’s you,” she said.

“I have a job.”

“You have three jobs. You keep agreeing to things before asking what they involve.”

“I can carry logs, keep the aisle clear and make sure Grover eats before the speech.”

“Those are three jobs.”

“Then I’m efficient.”

Thalia’s mouth twitched. “He’s right about the food part. Grover gets nervous and forgets to eat.”

“Noted,” Annabeth said, writing it down.

A pair of younger campers hurried past with a crate of painted paper stars. One of them slowed when she saw the schedule. “Do we have to tell a story tonight?”

“No,” Annabeth said. “You can listen. You can leave when you want. If you do tell one, you choose what part.”

The girl nodded, then kept moving with her friend. Percy watched Annabeth wait until they were out of earshot before she let out a breath.

“Good,” he said.

“It's not a rule if we only remember it when the room is quiet,” she said.

Thalia leaned back. “You don't have to manage the whole camp by yourself.”

“I know.”

“You say that like it's a fact you read in a book.”

“I'm practicing saying it like I mean it.”

Percy set his hand over hers beneath the edge of the table. “We can take the next thing on the list.”

Annabeth looked at their hands, then at him. “The scroll is covered. Go see if Ella needs help. Don't promise her anything until you hear the question.”

“That's unfairly specific.”

“It's also necessary.”

He stood and held out a hand. She took it, and Thalia scooped the tablet up with her left hand. The bandaged right stayed close to her side as she rose.

“I'm coming too,” she said. “I can point. Nobody needs me carrying anything.”

Clarisse, passing behind them with a coil of rope over one shoulder, heard her. “Good. I already have enough people who think heavy things are an invitation to prove something.”

“Was that aimed at me?” Percy asked.

“It was aimed at everybody. You get the first warning because you're standing here.”

---

By noon the whole camp smelled like paint and pine and sugar.

There was no training after the morning war games. Instead Chiron dismissed everyone with a wave of his hand and a rare, wide smile.

"Go," he said from his wheelchair at the head of the pavilion. "Prepare. Tonight we do not train for war. Tonight we remember why we fight to have a home at all."

The cheer that went up shook the grapes off the arbor.

Percy spent the afternoon with campers from the Poseidon and Ares cabins hauling logs to the amphitheater. A thousand campfires deserved a big one, and Beckendorf's old plans for a pyramid stack were pulled out of the Hephaestus files and followed with reverent care. Clarisse directed like a general, pointing with a spear.

"Higher! No, not that log, that one is crooked, are you blind? Tyson, that one there, yes, perfect, good job little brother."

Tyson beamed every time Clarisse called him little brother. He was taller than the stack already, but he still ducked his head shyly and placed each log as gently as if it were made of eggshells.

The stack leaned a little toward the wind. Percy saw it before anyone said anything, a slow shift at the top where one branch of the pyramid sat too high. Clarisse saw it too.

“Hold,” she called.

The workers stopped. Tyson kept both hands on the log he was placing until Clarisse had checked the brace at the base. Nobody stood beneath the high side. Chiron had marked a broad arc around the stack, and two Hephaestus campers waited outside it with long poles.

“We can take the top row down,” Percy said.

“We can,” Clarisse answered. “Or we can pretend it's fine and have it fall when someone's carrying a plate past it.”

One of the Hephaestus campers tapped the old drawing. “Beckendorf's version went higher. It had another row at the peak.”

“The drawing doesn't have people walking past it,” Clarisse said. She pointed the spear at the chalk line around the stack. “This is where the aisle goes. We need room for two people to pass without touching the logs.”

The camper studied the line, then looked at the pile. “If we lower it, the top won't catch as fast.”

“Then we feed it from the side after the crew clears the lane,” Percy said. “We don't need it to burn all at once.”

Tyson nodded. “A slower fire is still a fire.”

Clarisse lowered the spear. “There. We can keep the shape and change the height.”

She pointed to the poles. The Hephaestus campers eased the upper branches out one at a time. Tyson watched the joints while Percy and one of the Ares campers carried the pieces to the side pile. The stack settled lower and wider.

“Better,” Tyson said. He walked around the boundary, not under it, and pressed his shoulder against the base from the outside. It didn't move.

Clarisse checked the gap between the stack and the aisle. “Keep that open. When it gets dark, people won't see a small rope across the path.”

“I'll put two lanterns on the outside edge,” Annabeth said from the marked route. She had her tablet under one arm and used her free hand to point. “Not on the ground. On the stakes.”

“Nothing flammable on the stakes,” Chiron called from the path.

“Metal lanterns,” Annabeth replied. “The blue ones from the Hephaestus cabin.”

“Then yes.”

Percy looked at the clear route between the amphitheater and the fire crew. For a thousandth-campfire celebration, the safe version was less dramatic than the first drawing had been: a lower pile, a wider gap and enough room for someone to walk past without asking anyone to move. He liked it better.

Tyson wiped his hands on his trousers. “I could bring the new hammer from the forge. It might help with the brace.”

Clarisse shook her head. “Your tools can stay where you left them. We have the kit here, and you're on leave. You're allowed to stand around and eat cookies.”

Tyson thought about that. “I don't want to stand around.”

“Then work with your hands. You don't need to bring your whole forge out here.”

He looked at the stack, then at his palms. “I can do that.”

Percy set his hand on Tyson's shoulder. “And you can stop when you want.”

“I know.” Tyson gave him a crooked smile. “I want to finish this row.”

Clarisse did not praise him or take the job away. She handed him the next branch and stayed close enough to catch the other end if it turned. They set the row together. When it held, Tyson stepped back and looked at the whole stack, checking the shape for himself.

“Now it looks like a fire that won't fall on the people,” he said.

“That's the standard,” Clarisse told him.

Percy wiped sweat off his forehead and grinned at his sister. "You're in bossy mode."

"I'm always in bossy mode," Clarisse said. "This is just bossy mode with a purpose."

Down by the Big House, the Aphrodite cabin had set up a shirt station, a flower-crown table and a face-paint board. Silena Beauregard moved between them with a rag tucked into her back pocket, her dark hair tied up in a blue bandana. Gold paint had already smudged the sleeve of her thousandth-campfire shirt.

She had arranged the tables so there was room to pass behind the people waiting. The face paint was optional; so were the crowns. A small card, written in Silena's neat hand, said that the group picture would happen after the feast and that anyone could step out of it.

Triton had brought a camera. He stood beside the table with the strap around his wrist, but it stayed lowered until Silena asked if he would take a photo of the finished shirts. He checked whether the designs were ready, then waited while she gathered the ones whose wearers had said yes.

When Percy came over, she waved the camera away and hugged him instead, careful of the paint.

“Percy! Isn't it perfect? The weather, the fire, everything. I told Drew if it rains I'm holding her personally responsible.”

He laughed and hugged her back. “You've done amazing, Si. The shirts are great.”

“I know they are. The left sleeve is crooked on mine, but I decided that's how you can prove it's mine.”

“Very official.”

“It's a limited edition.”

She showed him the flower crowns. Several had been made with grape leaves and paper stars; a few were only a loop of blue ribbon, because some campers didn't want flowers in their hair. Silena didn't try to talk them into anything. She tucked the extras under the table and left the brush cup where it could be reached without asking.

A younger girl came up, looked at the paint board, then changed her mind. Silena handed her a blank paper star instead. The girl's shoulders loosened. She took it and sat with a friend to color.

“I just keep thinking,” Silena said when they had a moment alone. “A thousand fires. How many people sat at the edge of one and didn't know whether anyone would make room?”

Percy glanced toward the amphitheater. “We can save them a place.”

“That's the part I want them to remember.” She rubbed a streak of gold off her wrist. “My first night was rough. I was scared, and I didn't know anyone yet. You made room. You brought an extra blanket and told me the fire would keep the bad dreams away.”

“You shared your animal crackers,” Percy said.

“And you ate the ones that had fallen on the floor.”

“I was hungry.”

“You were nine kinds of gross.” She smiled, but her eyes had gone shiny. “If I choose to tell a story tonight, it'll be the blanket one. Not the rest.”

“Your story. You choose.”

“Exactly.”

Clarisse came up behind them and nudged a paint tray away from the edge of the table. “You have six shirts left and no water.”

“That's because everyone wants one.”

“That's because you keep promising everyone one.”

“I promised the ones who asked.”

“And then the ones who didn't want paint.”

“They asked for paper stars.”

Clarisse looked at the girl coloring. “Fine. I'll get water. Don't start the next station until I come back.”

Silena gave her a mock salute. “Yes, boss.”

“I'm not the boss.”

“You're the one giving orders.”

“That's different.”

Silena laughed, and Clarisse stayed beside her instead of walking off. Percy went to carry the water before either of them could make the argument into a contest.

---

By late afternoon the amphitheater had transformed.

Garlands of grapevine and olive branch hung from the stone steps. Lanterns made by the Hephaestus cabin, blue fire in bronze cages that would not burn anyone, lined the aisles. The Apollo cabin had set up a low platform with guitars and violins and drums and Grover's special stool left empty in the middle.

And at the top of the steps, under a white canopy to protect it from sun, lay something long and rolled, covered with a sheet. Two figures guarded it.

One was Rachel Elizabeth Dare, the Oracle, paint on her nose and a pencil behind her ear. The other was Ella, the harpy, her red feathers preened neat, her talons clutching a leather satchel full of scroll tags, muttering to herself.

"Ella is excited. Ella has counted and recounted. One thousand campfires in the record. There are gaps. Ella will keep what people choose to tell."

Percy climbed up with Annabeth to say hi. Annabeth hugged Rachel, then crouched to be eye-level with Ella.

"Is it ready?" Annabeth asked gently.

Ella nodded furiously, her eyes bright. “Ella and Rachel made a timeline. A timeline scroll. From the first campfire in the count to tonight. Some entries come from the old books. Some are from people who were there. Some are stories people told Ella.”

Rachel held up a bundle of paper tags. “And some spaces are blank because we couldn't verify them. That's not a mistake.”

“Ella wanted to fill them,” Ella said. “Rachel said blank can be honest.”

“It can,” Annabeth said. “A missing note doesn't mean nobody remembers.”

Ella considered this, then tucked the tags more securely into her satchel. “Ella can keep the notes. The scroll can stay blank.”

“Every time Percy fell in the lake is still on there,” Ella said, brightening. “And Annabeth laughed and Thalia said—”

“‘That's my baby sister's boyfriend, be nice,’” Thalia supplied, appearing behind them with two lemonades. She handed one to Annabeth and one to Percy. “I did say that. Once. Under duress.”

"You say it weekly," Annabeth said.

"I say it threateningly," Thalia corrected. She ruffled Ella's feathers lightly. "You did good, Ella. I saw the early draft. It's going to make everyone cry."

“Ella hopes for good crying. Not sad crying. Remember crying,” Ella said. “Ella wrote down the slow dance too. Percy stepped on Annabeth's toes twelve times. He said sorry fifteen times. Grover played pipes. Thalia and Clarisse watched from the steps.”

Percy nearly choked on the lemonade. Annabeth glanced at him, then at Ella. “The dance can go on the scroll. The count doesn't have to.”

Ella tipped her head. “But the count is funny.”

“It is,” Percy said. “It's also ours.”

Rachel crouched beside them, one hand resting on the edge of the covered scroll. “We can keep the public version simple: you danced, Grover played, and your friends were there. The exact count can stay in Ella's notes.”

Ella looked from Annabeth to Percy. Then she took a little tag from her satchel and wrote on it with a blunt pencil. “Story kept. Count private. Ella still remembers.”

“That's good,” Annabeth said.

Ella tucked the tag into the satchel instead of tying it to the scroll. Percy saw her do it and felt the knot in his chest ease. Nothing about the dance had been taken away. They had simply chosen what the camp would carry forward.

Thalia glanced at the tag. “You can still tell me the number later.”

“Not if Percy wants to keep it,” Annabeth said.

“Then I'll make Percy tell me.”

“Good luck,” Percy said.

---

The conch called the camp to supper at sunset. Warm bread and roasted apples scented the pavilion, and the tables groaned under platters from the kitchens, Demeter's fields and the nymphs' honey and berries. Mr. D wore a Hawaiian shirt patterned with flames. He waved a hand and made the goblets refill with whatever each camper loved most, then stood for a moment before the offerings.

"A thousand campfires," he said, sounding almost sincere under the bored drawl. "I have been here for a great many of them. Most of you are insufferable. Some of you are tolerable. All of you are, annoyingly, family. Try not to burn the place down tonight. I would have to fill out paperwork."

"To Mr. D!" Travis shouted, and everyone cheered, and Mr. D pretended to be annoyed but sat a little straighter.

Percy sat with Annabeth between the Poseidon and Zeus tables, which everyone had pushed together for the night. Thalia sat across from them with Clarisse and Silena on either side, and Grover squeezed in next to Percy, his goat legs folded awkwardly under the bench, his reed pipes tucked in his pocket and his new button that said STAFF SATYR - ASK ME ABOUT NATURE gleaming on his shirt.

Amphitrite had come for a brief surface visit, with Chiron's arrangements and Triton beside her. She sat near the Poseidon table, close enough to see Percy, Tyson, Clarisse and Silena without being pulled into every conversation at once. Triton carried his camera, but after Silena's request he asked before taking individual pictures and put it away whenever someone declined. Beryl was not at the campfire. Thalia had made no visit arrangement, and the letter she had chosen to keep remained her own business.

Percy saw Amphitrite watching the preparations while the tables filled. She had brought no royal escort or ceremony; she listened when Tyson explained where he had helped with the firewood and asked Clarisse whether the meal would be warm enough. Clarisse told her the kitchens had it handled. Amphitrite nodded as if that answer was complete.

When Percy reached her table, she touched the sleeve of his orange shirt. “Silena made these?”

“She did.”

“Then I should thank her before I leave.”

“She'll tell you the paint is still wet.”

“Then I will wait until it's dry.” Amphitrite looked toward Tyson, who was carrying a tray with both hands. “And you?”

“I helped with the stack.”

“Did you eat?”

“Not yet.”

She gave him a look that reminded him of the palace kitchen without making it feel like a checkup. “Then help yourself before you start another job.”

He reached for a piece of bread. “That's the plan.”

It was a short exchange, with no grand speech or question he wasn't ready to answer. She let him go back to his friends when he wanted to, and he was glad she had come.

Tyson came to the table a few minutes later with a tray balanced in both hands. He set it down, checked that no cup had tipped, then looked at Amphitrite.

“Mother, I did not go to the forge today.”

“I know. You're on leave.”

“The hammer and chisels are there. The hinge is not finished.”

“You don't have to finish it tonight.”

Tyson looked toward the amphitheater. “I helped make the fire stack safe. That is a different thing.”

“It is.” Amphitrite glanced at the marked aisle. “And people will be able to pass it without stepping around a fallen log.”

“Yes. Clarisse checked.”

He didn't say he would return to the forge tomorrow, and she didn't ask him to. Percy noticed the easy silence after the answer. Tyson reached for a piece of bread and stayed at the table until Clarisse called him back to help move the popcorn bowls.

After the offerings burned, Chiron rose. The pavilion fell silent.

“Ella has counted one thousand campfires in the records of this camp since it was refounded here,” he said. “The oldest entries are not complete. Some of what we know comes from people who were there; some comes from stories handed down. The number is an honest count of the record, not a claim that anyone can list every fire that ever burned on this hill.”

The quiet changed. Percy could hear the scrape of a plate being set down.

“I lit the first fire in the count she could verify with me,” Chiron continued. “I remember the children around it. I remember the wind. I do not remember every name, and I will not pretend I do.”

He looked around the pavilion, meeting eyes table by table.

“Tonight is for the stories that are here to be told. We will go to the amphitheater. We will sing. We will unroll the timeline our Oracle and our historian have made. And our oldest friend will speak.”

He nodded to Grover. Grover went very still, his ears twitching nervously. Percy squeezed his shoulder hard.

"You will be great, G-man," Percy whispered.

Grover swallowed. "I rewrote it eleven times. Juniper says the eleventh is good but the ninth made her cry more."

"Go with eleven," Annabeth whispered. "Crying is good but breathing is also good."

Everyone laughed, and the procession to the amphitheater began. The lanterns bobbed ahead, but the aisle stayed open. The campers had been asked to carry blankets and food along the marked path; no one was sent down to the dock or the closed north trail. Percy walked beside Grover for part of the way, then slowed when a younger camper stopped to retie a shoe. The group moved around them without complaint.

Grover looked over his shoulder toward the lights. “I practiced the first line so many times I forgot what it was.”

“Do you remember it now?”

“No.”

“Then start with something else.”

“That's your advice?”

“It's my best advice.”

Grover gave a small, nervous laugh. “Juniper said the same thing.”

“You should listen to Juniper.”

“I do. That's why I have eleven drafts.”

They reached the lower steps. Percy offered his arm when Grover's boot caught on an uneven stone, but Grover waved him off and found his footing by himself.

“I'm fine,” he said. “Just making an entrance.”

“Very graceful.”

“Thank you.” Grover straightened his jacket and took a breath. “Don't let me forget to eat afterward.”

“I put it on Annabeth's list.”

“That's worse. Now she'll make a schedule.”

“I think she already did.”

“Then I'm doomed.”

“Probably,” Percy said, and they found their places before the fire was lit.

---

The Hephaestus kids waited until the fire crew had checked the water barrels and cleared the closest path. Chiron nodded. The first torch touched the kindling.

Flame took its time. It found the dry twigs, then a split log, then moved upward in a steady line. The children at the edge gave a cheer when the first larger piece caught. Someone on the top step started clapping out of rhythm; the rest joined in until the crew signaled that the fire had settled. No one had to shout over an emergency, and the open lane remained open.

As the logs caught, the light climbed the amphitheater wall and reached the people seated farther back. Percy could see faces come into view one by one. The smoke rose almost straight at first, then bent toward the Sound. A campfire was still a campfire, no matter how carefully they built its frame: heat close to the knees, ash on the stone, a little dry taste at the back of his throat.

Percy sat with the people he thought of as family halfway up the steps. Silena had spread a blue blanket over two rows of stone, its embroidery faded except for the newer flowers she had added. Annabeth took the place beside him; Thalia sat on her other side, her bandaged hand kept close beneath the sleeve of her leather jacket. Clarisse and Silena settled below them with Tyson, Grover and Juniper. They passed popcorn along the row, and Tyson held the bowl as if it were a fragile tool.

Percy had been to plenty of campfires. He couldn't have said how many without making up a number. The people around this one were the same ones he looked for when a day went wrong, but they didn't all have the same memories of how the family had formed. Some of those memories were theirs alone. Some had been told often enough to become camp stories. That didn't make one kind more real than another.

Chiron raised his hands. “Who wants to start?”

Annabeth had asked the storytellers to name where a story came from when they knew: something they had seen, something a friend had told them, or something in the old camp records. She had not made a rule that every story needed a footnote. She and Ella had simply left room for a correction if someone wanted to offer one. Percy had watched a few campers choose to sit quietly instead of taking a turn. No one called on them.

As Chiron waited for someone to begin, Percy noticed Silena's fingers resting on the edge of her cup. He leaned toward her. “Do you still want to tell the blanket story?”

“Maybe another night.” She glanced toward the younger campers, who were quietly deciding whether to volunteer. “I told you the part I wanted you to know. The rest is still mine.”

“Of course.”

“And I want to hear what they bring.” Silena gave his sleeve a quick tug, then settled beside Clarisse. She kept her place in the circle and turned toward the first voice that rose.

A hand shot up. A little Hermes camper stood and shouted, “The time Percy fought the Minotaur with no pants!”

Several people laughed. Percy smiled, then lifted a hand. “That's not how I arrived here. I came up from the Sound with Amphitrite and Triton. Clarisse pointed me toward Cabin Three.”

“But did you fight it?” the camper asked.

“No one at this camp witnessed that,” Percy said. “If you write it down, mark where you heard it.”

Clarisse nodded. “I was there when he came from the Sound. I pointed him toward Cabin Three. I didn't fight a Minotaur with him.”

The boy looked at his note. “Were his pants ripped?”

“I don't know who started that part,” Percy said. “It's changed every time it's been told.”

“The Hermes cabin has you riding it like a surfboard,” Travis called.

“That isn't in the camp's record either,” Annabeth said. She tapped the corner of her tablet. “If you keep it, label it as a rumor.”

The boy crossed out a line. “So what should I say?”

“Say who told you,” Annabeth said. “Then let Percy correct the parts he wants to.”

“The story gets mixed up,” the boy said.

“That part is true,” Clarisse replied.

“And what happened when you came from the sea?” he asked Percy.

“Clarisse told me she was my sister and pointed me toward Cabin Three. She said Silena was busy at the pavilion, so I asked too many questions about my mother.” Percy glanced at Clarisse. “That's probably a story for another night.”

“It is,” Clarisse said, without giving him a hard time.

The boy sat down with a grin. Percy let his shoulder rest against Annabeth's. The old memory stayed where it belonged, between the two people who carried it. The camp's record could hold what Clarisse had seen when Percy arrived. He was glad the boy had asked before deciding which version to keep.

The stories rolled like waves after that.

A Demeter camper stood next. “I wasn't there when Thalia and Annabeth first reached the hill. This is the story the older kids told me.” She repeated the details she had been given: Thalia asking for her sister, Annabeth being checked by the healer, then running across the grass and knocking them both down with the force of the hug.

Annabeth listened without correcting the story. She had no picture of that day to add. When Thalia's shoulder touched hers, she let it stay there.

“Some of it came from Chiron,” the camper said. “He said they were both covered in dirt.”

“That's true,” Thalia said. “I remember the dirt.”

The girl smiled, relieved to have a witness confirm one piece, then returned to her seat.

An Apollo boy described Grover playing pipes on a ridge during a fire. He said he had heard Grover's account at a counselor meeting and knew the version he had learned might be short. Grover nodded once from his place near the front. He didn't correct the boy's telling, but when it ended he lifted one finger.

“I didn't play for six hours,” Grover called. “It felt like six hours. My mouth hurt for a day.”

The campers laughed. The boy bowed from his seat.

A Hephaestus camper told the story of Clarisse holding a line against a drakon while wounded campers were brought back. He didn't give a length of time; he said he had been near enough to hear the roar and had never forgotten it.

Clarisse stared at the popcorn in her lap.

“You told me it was a few seconds,” Silena whispered.

“That's how long it felt.”

“Does that mean I can say three minutes?”

“No.”

Silena smiled and took her hand. “Fine. I'll say you held the line.”

Clarisse squeezed back once, then returned her attention to the fire.

Katie Gardner stood, but she did not walk to the center. “I wasn't in Cabin Three when Annabeth was sick,” she said. “I only know she had to rest. I can say the people here made room for her to do that. I don't know the rest, and I don't need to.”

Annabeth's shoulders eased. Katie did not name a fever, describe a night, or ask Annabeth to fill in the missing part. She let the sentence be enough and sat down.

The fire shifted. A coal broke from one log and fell into the basin beneath it. One of the attendants moved the tongs closer, then waited until the flame settled before returning them to the stand.

Amphitrite watched the story circle quietly. She didn't ask Percy what the illness had been or why it mattered. When their eyes met, she only lifted her cup. He lifted his own in answer.

Thalia leaned her head briefly against Annabeth's. Percy touched his mouth to Annabeth's hair. She turned her face toward him.

“You stayed,” she whispered.

“When you asked,” Percy said. “And when you wanted me to sleep, I did.”

“I know.” Annabeth put her hand over his on the blanket. “That part is mine to tell.”

He nodded. The answer gave the rest of the room somewhere else to look.

Between stories, the Apollo cabin played.

They played the old campfire songs, the silly ones about eating goblins and the ghost in the canoe, and everyone sang too loud and off-key on purpose. They played the brave ones, the hymns to the gods that made the fire burn bluer. Austin Lake, now tall and confident, played guitar. Kayla Knowles played violin. And in the middle, Grover played pipes.

The pipes carried a tune Percy knew, though he could not remember who had first taught it to Grover. The melody rose and fell without a hurry. Annabeth rested her hand in his. Somewhere farther down the steps, a toddler hummed the last four notes a beat late, then started over when her father caught up.

After the songs, Chiron nodded to Grover.

It was time for the speech.

---

Grover Underwood stood up on legs that shook, and walked down to the front of the fire alone.

He was not a kid anymore, not really. He was Lord of the Wild now, bearer of Pan's spirit, with lines at the corners of his eyes and a circlet of grapevine in his curly hair that Juniper had woven for him that afternoon. But as he stood before a thousand faces and one great fire, clutching his pipes in one hand and a crumpled piece of paper in the other, Percy could still see the nervous young satyr who had once eaten tin cans and pretended to be brave on a bus to the Underworld.

Grover looked at the paper. He looked at the fire. He folded the paper and put it away.

A murmur rippled. Juniper, sitting with her hands clasped so tight her knuckles were white, smiled through tears and mouthed, Good. Eleven was the no-paper one.

Grover took a breath.

"Hi," he said.

Everyone laughed gently.

“I'm Grover.” He glanced at the paper, then back to the fire. “Which is probably obvious. I'm trying to remember what I practiced, and I can't, so this is the version you get.”

The shoulders in the front rows loosened. Percy saw Juniper cover her mouth to hide a smile.

“Pan taught me that titles are useful if you have to fit them on a badge. They don't tell you whether someone will show up when you call.” Grover rubbed his thumb along one of the pipes. “I remember my first campfire. I stayed behind Chiron's chair until somebody gave me a marshmallow. I burned it black. Chiron ate it anyway, which was generous, because I don't think there was another one.”

Chiron looked down at his hands. Grover grinned at him before the moment could turn too solemn.

“I've been to a lot of these. I couldn't give you a number. I remember some because of the song, and some because it rained, and some because the next morning we had to clean ash out of the benches. A few ended with a name on a bead and a place left open in the circle. I don't need to tell you which ones. You remember your own.”

Chiron smiled, eyes wet.

“I've played at funerals here,” Grover said. “When I do, I don't try to make the song sound happy just because we're sitting by a fire. The people who are gone were part of this place. Their friends get to remember them in whatever way they can.”

Silence fell, deep and respectful. The fire popped.

“But mostly I think about how a fire gives us somewhere to stand together. You can come late, or leave early. You can sit close to someone or keep a place between you. You can talk, listen, or eat too many cookies while the counselors pretend not to notice.” A ripple of laughter moved through the rows. “There's room for all of that.”

He took a breath. “A new kid can arrive and not know anyone. Someone else can be leaving for school. A satyr can fail his searcher's license twice and still be asked to help with the fire. You don't have to be the best at something before people make room for you.”

He looked straight at Percy, at Annabeth, at Thalia, at Clarisse, at Silena.

“I got lucky. I found people who stayed around long enough for me to become their friend. Some of you are very difficult to be friends with.”

“That's fair,” Thalia called.

“I'm not naming names,” Grover said. The laughter came easier now. “The campfire didn't make us family in one night. It let us keep coming back until we could call each other that.”

He looked down at the folded speech in his hand. “Ella counted the fires. That's a lot of entries. It isn't everybody's memory, and it isn't every story. But it's a place to start if you want to tell one. If you don't, you don't owe the fire anything.”

He raised his pipes.

"To the next hundred," he said. "To the next thousand. To showing up."

The cheer that went up shook sparks into the sky. Juniper was on her feet, clapping and crying. Percy was on his feet without realizing it, shouting Grover's name, Annabeth beside him whistling with two fingers the way Thalia taught her. Thalia had her fingers in her mouth whistling loud enough to startle the harpies in the rafters.

Grover bowed, ears scarlet, and hurried back to his seat where Juniper threw her arms around him and Percy pounded him on the back so hard he nearly fell over.

"You folded the paper!" Percy said. "You didn't even read it!"

"It was the eleventh draft," Grover said breathily. "Juniper said speak from the heart. So I did."

"You were perfect," Annabeth said, hugging him fiercely. "Absolutely perfect."

---

After the speech came the scroll.

Rachel and Ella carried the scroll to the front together. Ella flew low to keep the edge from dragging; Rachel walked backward with the opposite end, calling for two campers to move a lantern stand. People leaned to see it unfurl, but they waited until the volunteers had anchored both corners before stepping closer.

The paper crossed most of the amphitheater. It wasn't a straight timeline so much as a braided one: dates where the records had dates, seasons where the records had seasons, and blank spaces where no one could say. Rachel had used color to separate public camp events from personal stories. Ella had marked some entries with a tiny eye for a witness and a small open book for something preserved in a record. A few carried a note that the story had been passed along.

The title ran across the top: **The Thousandth Campfire: What We Remember**.

Percy leaned forward. Annabeth sat on the edge of the blanket, and Thalia stood to see over the row ahead. Clarisse and Silena shifted around Tyson's shoulders.

The first panel showed Chiron beside a small fire. Ella had written beneath it: “First confirmed entry in the current count. Chiron remembers the night; the names of the other campers were not preserved.”

The next line showed the Big House, then a capture-the-flag field, then a place left blank where an early record had gone missing. A shroud appeared as a simple fold of white cloth. No names had been added without permission.

Then the later entries came closer together. Thalia on the hill. Percy walking up from the Sound after his journey from Atlantis. Clarisse pointing him toward Cabin Three. Grover's first search. Clarisse winning her first chariot race. Silena singing after a healing that worked. Each picture had a short label, and where a storyteller had given the detail, the note said who had told it. There was no Minotaur panel. Percy was glad to see that; the Minotaur belonged to the other life he remembered, not to the camp's own record of his arrival.

Some parts were funny: a trident dripping lake water, a canoe turned sideways in a tree, a row of camp shirts that all seemed to be the wrong size. Other panels stayed spare. An owl with gray eyes. A pine on the hill. Five figures sitting around a fire with no names beneath them.

Ella had resisted filling every empty place. That restraint made the scroll feel more honest than if she had guessed.

A young Hermes camper pointed at a space in the middle. “There was a campfire when we made the terrible stew last winter. It should be here.”

Rachel looked toward Ella. “Do we have it in the book?”

“Not yet,” Ella said. “Do you want your story on the scroll?”

“Only if it says the stew was terrible.”

“That is confirmed,” Connor called from the back.

“It was not,” Travis answered.

The boy laughed. “Write both.”

Ella untied a paper tag and held it out to him. “You can write what you remember. You choose your name.”

The boy wrote his initials and the words “too much rosemary.” He tied the tag to the section for the current summer. The scroll did not become complete. It became a little more his.

Sketches in the margins. A trident dripping with lake water. An owl with gray eyes. A boundary pine with two sisters beneath it, smiling. A spear crossed with a flower. A set of panpipes with musical notes turning into leaves.

The quotes on that stretch were from public camp moments: "Seaweed Brain." "Wise Girl." "Thally." "Kelp Head." "For Pan." "For family."

Annabeth traced the line with her finger when they were allowed to come close during the intermission. She stopped at a small panel Rachel had painted with exquisite care.

Two figures in a dirt circle, with a few notes of music painted above them. The caption said, “First slow dance. Grover played. Friends watched from the steps.” The count of steps and apologies was not on the public scroll.

Annabeth made a small sound. Percy looked over her shoulder.

“They kept the part I wanted,” she whispered.

“And you chose what they could keep.” He took her hand. “Do you want to— I mean— would you—”

Annabeth looked at him, her gray eyes catching the firelight. She waited, smiling a little while he tried to find the rest of his sentence.

"Are you asking me to dance, Seaweed Brain?" she asked.

Percy held out his hand. It shook a little.

“Annabeth Grace,” he said, his voice catching on the middle of her name. “Do you want to dance with me?”

It came out too fast. A kid from the Nemesis cabin two rows up turned to look.

Annabeth blinked. Then she smiled, wide and bright, that crinkled the corners of her eyes.

"Yeah," she said. "Yeah, I do."

She stood and slipped her hand into his. Her fingers were warm, with calluses from pencils, climbing and dagger grips. Her palm fit against his.

Thalia saw them and made a loud kissing noise. “Thalia!” Annabeth hissed, cheeks pink. A laugh ran through the rows; the people who knew the old dance story had been waiting for this one.

"She will stab you if you step on her feet, Jackson!" Thalia called cheerfully.

"I will not!" Annabeth said over her shoulder.

"She will! I've seen her practice knives!"

Percy laughed, shaky but real, and led Annabeth down to the dirt circle. Austin, understanding without being asked, strummed a chord. It rang out clear and bright. Kayla joined on violin. And Grover, from his seat, lifted his pipes and joined in, eyes closed.

The slow song began with the same few notes Grover had played at their first dance. Austin and Kayla changed the arrangement as they went, giving the tune room to grow.

Percy and Annabeth stepped into the circle together.

---

Their steps held for the first turn, until Percy followed the violin instead of the pipes and drifted half a beat ahead. Annabeth corrected him with a pressure at his shoulder. He let her set the pace; she had the song, and he had learned enough to notice when he was making the floor harder for both of them.

Percy put one hand on Annabeth's waist without having to be told. Annabeth rested her hand on his shoulder without hesitation. Their other hands joined, fingers laced.

They swayed through the next phrase, letting the pause stretch before they moved again. Dust scuffed around their sneakers in the firelight.

"You still look at my feet," Annabeth whispered, teasing.

"I'm checking for toes," Percy whispered back. "I've learned. I'm a toe-conscious dancer now."

Annabeth laughed, that same breathy laugh, and leaned her forehead gently against his.

Percy turned a little too far. Their joined hands tugged; he stopped before his shoe found hers.

“You're thinking about your feet again,” she said.

“I'm thinking about both of our feet.”

“That's more generous.”

She gave him a small turn under their joined hands. He nearly went the wrong way, caught the rhythm at the last second, and came back facing her. Annabeth's smile widened.

“You caught it.”

“I was always going to catch it.”

“Sure.” She tapped her fingers once against his shoulder, keeping time for the next measure. He followed her count until the music changed and then trusted the pipes to bring them back together.

Their foreheads touched. Percy could hear Grover's pipes, and somewhere behind him a little kid whispered the words to the song. Annabeth's fingers tightened once around his.

He remembered dancing with her the first time, the awkwardness and the fear of getting it wrong. He remembered the life he had lost, too—too much past for someone his age to carry, and no one else in the amphitheater could carry it with him. Annabeth could. They didn't need to name it here.

"I'm glad it's us," he whispered, the same words he had said at twelve, because they were still true. "I'm glad we get to do this. Without rushing. Just music and fire and —"

"And stepping on my feet?" Annabeth supplied.

"I haven't stepped on them yet!" Percy protested. "I'm doing great. This is growth."

"You stepped on my foot twice getting down the steps," Annabeth said solemnly. "I counted. Ella will want the number."

Percy groaned. "Tell Ella it was artistic interpretation."

"Already did," Annabeth said. She pulled back just enough to look at him, eyes bright, cheeks flushed. "We're doing perfect."

They danced through the whole song. The camp watched, though Percy could see a few people making an effort to look somewhere else. Little kids whispered. Older campers leaned on each other. Triton waited until Annabeth nodded toward him before he raised the camera; he took one photograph and lowered it again. Amphitrite watched from the table, her eyes bright, and did not interrupt them.

When the song ended, neither of them moved right away.

Applause broke out. Annabeth squeezed his hand.

"Next time?" Percy asked quietly, echoing his twelve-year-old self.

Annabeth squeezed back. "Every time."

They climbed the steps hand in hand, dazed and happy, and found their seats saved again.

A blanket spread across three steps. Two cups of water. A plate of cookies.

Clarisse saw them looking and scowled. "What? Silena saved those. Not me. I just — I was sitting here. Don't make it weird."

It was word for word what she had said at twelve, and Thalia burst out laughing so hard she nearly fell off the step.

"She saved you seats, she means," Thalia said, enunciating clearly through laughter. "She fought off three Hermes kids for this spot. It was violent. It was beautiful."

"I did not fight anybody," Clarisse snapped, ears pink as everyone laughed harder. "I told them to move and they moved because I am intimidating."

Annabeth looked at Clarisse, eyes soft. "Thanks, Clarisse."

Clarisse looked away. "Whatever. Sit down before I change my mind and take the blanket back."

Percy and Annabeth sat. The stone was warm. The blanket was soft. The water was cold. The cookies were chocolate chip, still a little warm.

Percy handed Annabeth a cup first. She bumped her shoulder against his in thanks.

Triton came over after he had put the camera away. “Would you want a print of the dance?”

Annabeth looked at Percy. He had liked knowing the moment was theirs before the camera caught it. “Maybe later,” he said.

“If we ask,” Annabeth added.

Triton nodded. “If you ask.” He didn't press for an answer or reach for the camera again. Percy watched him go back to Amphitrite's table, then took a cookie from the plate.

Thalia leaned over Annabeth's head to smirk at Percy. "So. Jackson. How were the toes? Did my sister survive?"

"My toes survived," Annabeth said.

"Barely," Percy said. "I'm going to practice. So next time I don't —"

"Next time?" Thalia interrupted, eyebrows shooting up, playing her part to perfection. She looked at Annabeth. "Next time?"

Annabeth went scarlet. "Thalia!"

"What? I'm just repeating what he said. Next time." Thalia clutched her chest dramatically. "My baby sister is growing up. Having next times."

Everyone around them howled. Even Clarisse laughed, a short surprised bark she tried to turn into a cough. Silena leaned against her, giggling.

Percy put his arm around Annabeth's shoulders. She leaned into him, her temple near his.

The blanket had been patched at one corner. Thalia's hair had a streak of gray from the winter storms on her scouting trips, and Clarisse's chin scar caught the firelight whenever she turned to laugh. Silena's healer bead rested at her throat. Grover had his circlet, and Juniper kept her hand over his. Nothing in the row was frozen at the age of their first dance. The people had changed; they had kept choosing each other anyway.

---

Later, when the fire had burned low and the little kids had fallen asleep on blankets and the songs had gone soft and slow, Thalia stood and stretched.

She looked at Clarisse. She looked at Silena.

"Walk?" she said simply.

Clarisse frowned. "Where?"

"Just up. Top of the steps. Air."

Silena stood immediately, understanding something Percy did not yet understand. She held out a hand to Clarisse.

"Come on," Silena said softly.

Clarisse grumbled but stood, and the three of them climbed to the top of the amphitheater together, past the rolled edge of the timeline scroll, past the lanterns, to where the hill overlooked the Sound and the stars were thick overhead.

Percy watched them go, curious. Annabeth watched too, her head still on his shoulder.

"What are they doing?" Percy whispered.

"Being big sisters," Annabeth whispered back.

At the top of the steps, Thalia stopped and faced the other two. The wind tugged at her hair. She kept her wrapped right hand close to her side and held out her left instead.

“Thanks,” she said.

Clarisse frowned. “For what?”

“For not making my sister explain herself when she was sick. For coming when she wanted you, and leaving when she wanted quiet.” Thalia looked at the fire below them. “I was six the first time I sat up with her through a fever. I thought if she needed something, I had to be the one who did it. I didn't know how to ask anyone else.”

Silena's eyes filled. “You did ask.”

“Yeah. Eventually.” Thalia rubbed her thumb against her own knuckles. “You both showed up. I know that sounds obvious. It wasn't obvious to me.”

Clarisse glanced away. “You don't have to thank us for being there.”

“I'm going to anyway.”

“Don't make a habit of it.”

Silena gave a quiet laugh. She stepped closer, but waited until Thalia moved first. Thalia lifted her left arm around Silena's shoulders. Clarisse stayed where she was for a beat, then came in on the other side. Thalia kept her right hand out of the hug.

Down below, Percy could see the three of them, but he couldn't hear the next words. Annabeth watched too, her fingers warm in his.

“They're hugging,” she whispered. “Thalia doesn't do that in public.”

“Maybe she does tonight.”

Annabeth's eyes were wet. “She doesn't owe me a story about the first time she took care of me.”

“No,” Percy said. “And you don't owe one to anyone else.”

She leaned into him, then sat up again. “I can tell people what happened today. I can tell them I was sick. I don't want them quoting the things I said half asleep.”

“I know.”

“And if they ask, I'll tell them myself.”

He nodded. “I'll let you answer.”

Annabeth turned back toward the hill. “Good.”

The three figures above them stayed close for a minute longer. Percy could see Clarisse say something that made Silena laugh. Then they came down the steps, Thalia walking between them and keeping her bandaged hand tucked close.

“They were there for you,” Percy said. “They didn't need to make you explain anything.”

Annabeth nodded. “That's why I love them.”

She looked up at Thalia when they reached the blanket. Thalia's face had gone back to its usual set, but she touched Annabeth's shoulder with her left hand before sitting down. Clarisse dropped into her own place and passed Silena the cup she had been holding. Nothing else had to be said in front of the camp.

“I have the best sisters,” Annabeth said. “And the best boyfriend.”

Percy kissed the top of her head. “We have the best sisters. Plural. I'm sharing mine with you and you're sharing yours with me. That's the deal.”

“Best deal,” Annabeth said.

The fire crackled. Grover played one last soft tune, barely louder than breathing. Ella, perched in the rafters with Rachel, recited quietly to herself, "One thousand campfires. Sisters hugged. Percy did not step on toes this time. Growth. Ella will record growth."

Everyone laughed when they heard, even Clarisse from the top of the hill, who shouted, "I heard that, harpy!"

---

The party wound down the way campfires always wound down, slowly and reluctantly, with people drifting off in twos and threes, carrying sleeping little siblings, folding blankets, whispering.

Chiron banked the fire but did not put it out. The thousand-and-first fire would be lit from its coals in the morning, continuity unbroken.

Percy and Annabeth stayed.

They climbed past the sleeping forms and the stacked plates, past Grover dozing with his head in Juniper's lap and Tyson asleep with Ella tucked against his shoulder, her wings folded close. Thalia, Clarisse and Silena sat shoulder to shoulder on the top step, talking quietly. Amphitrite and Triton had gone toward the Big House guest room; Percy had seen them wave goodnight before he and Annabeth left the steps.

Percy and Annabeth went to their spot on Half-Blood Hill, just below the pine tree, where the grass was soft and the view opened to the Sound and the whole bowl of stars.

They lay back side by side in the grass, hands linked between them, sneakers touching.

The stars burned cold and bright overhead. The sea whispered. The banked campfire glowed faintly below, smoke rising straight up like a prayer.

Annabeth pointed. "There's Zeus. There's Heracles. There's the Huntress."

Percy pointed. “There's the small star near the entrance. Ella said it stands for the people who hold the door open for newcomers. No names.”

Annabeth was quiet for a moment. Then she turned her head to look at him.

"A thousand campfires," she said.

"A thousand," Percy said.

“Do you know how many of these we've been to?”

“No.” He looked back toward the fire. “We can ask Ella, but I don't think she can count the ones we remember separately.”

Annabeth watched the smoke lift into the dark. “I like that the scroll has the first dance.”

“Even with my terrible footwork?”

“Especially with your terrible footwork.” She nudged his shoe with hers. “But I don't want every good thing to become a record just because Ella can remember it.”

“We can keep some things between us.”

“Some things can be for the people who were there. Some can stay ours.”

Percy thought of the dance that had happened years ago in the life he remembered. That memory was still his, but it did not turn tonight's music into a repeat. “This one happened here,” he said.

“It did.”

“So even if Ella writes down the song, she won't have all of it.”

“No.” Annabeth's fingers settled between his. “No one does.”

“Grover said to the next hundred,” Annabeth said.

Percy looked at her, then up at the stars, then back at her. A bit of grass had caught in one curl. A smear of marshmallow clung to her cheek.

He brushed it off gently with his thumb.

"To the next hundred," he said.

Annabeth smiled, small and private, just for him.

"To the next hundred," she whispered. "To the next thousand. To showing up."

They stayed until the fire crew had finished the last check and the amphitheater had gone quiet. Annabeth's hand remained in his. From the hill, Percy could see the blue lanterns marking the open path back to camp. The stars were bright above them, and the banked coals still held a low line of red.
