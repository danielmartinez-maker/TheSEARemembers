## Chapter 197: The Room We Keep

On Thursday morning, Silena put her painting things on the table before she put anything on the stove.

The order was the point.

She set down the flat tin with the dented corner, the glass jar wrapped in a strip of old linen, two brushes washed clean the night before, and the little square palette that fit in the heel of her hand. Then she took the portfolio from beneath her bunk and rested it against the wall where the damp air from the sink could not reach it. She did not open it yet. First she checked the window. The winter blanket still covered most of the glass, but the seam above the latch had loosened. Cold came through in a thin, patient line.

A chair scraped behind her.

“You’re setting up before breakfast,” Clarisse said.

“I’m setting up before everything.”

Clarisse went to the stove and lifted the lid off the pot. She glanced at the supplies. “Is that the sketch?”

“The sketch is inside the portfolio.”

“You brought the portfolio out.”

“I did.”

“Those are different statements.”

“They’re both true.”

Clarisse put the lid back down. “You have a plan?”

“I have a morning.”

“That isn’t a plan.”

“It’s enough of one to start.”

The cabin’s other sounds gathered around them: Percy’s sleepy footstep on the stair, the low hiss from the stove, a pair of younger campers whispering as they looked for a missing glove. The heat had not fully filled the room. Their breath still showed near the door, and the window blanket gave a small inward shiver whenever the wind found it.

Silena pulled the chair closest to the lamp. “I need until second bell.”

“Then take until second bell.”

“I can do the apples after.”

“I was going to do the apples.”

“You hate slicing them.”

“I hate when you say I hate slicing them.”

“You cut them too thick.”

“I cut them the size I want.”

“That’s what I mean.”

Clarisse looked at the portfolio again, then at the table. “All right. Second bell. I’ll make breakfast.”

“Thank you.”

“Don’t make a speech out of it.”

“I was going to say thank you.”

“You said it.”

Silena smiled and reached for the bowl of water. She heard Clarisse move to the stove, heard her knife strike the board with brisk, even chops. Clarisse was angry at Leandros. She had been angry at him before she had met him, in a way, and then he had supplied more reasons. That did not make every morning in the cabin about him. Silena was trying to keep one part of the day from being claimed by the new problem before she had even opened her paints.

Percy came down with his hair flattened on one side. “Is it breakfast?”

“It will be,” Clarisse said.

“Is it my breakfast?”

“No.”

He paused, considering whether there was a difference between “not yours” and “not being made for you.” “Can I help?”

“After you wake up enough to hold a knife.”

“I’m awake.”

“You walked into the table.”

“I was checking it.”

Silena turned. “Good morning.”

“Good morning.” Percy looked at the arrangement she had made. He stopped before he stepped closer. “Are you working on the painting?”

“I’m going to.”

“The copy?”

“Maybe.”

He did not hide that he wanted the answer to be yes. He did hide the rest of it better than he would have a few months ago.

“I promised you one,” she said. “I still want to make it. I want to decide what it looks like before it belongs in anybody’s letter.”

“That makes sense.”

“And if I don’t finish it this morning, I don’t finish it this morning.”

“Right.”

She watched him take that in. He did not tell her it was a good idea, or that Amphitrite would like it, or that he could make the envelope. He had learned a little about the difference between an offer and a direction. He still had to remember it in the moment.

“If you need a second set of hands,” he said, “ask me.”

“I will.”

He glanced at the old map paper rolled beside the portfolio. “Annabeth came by looking for a straightedge. The stool’s still curing. She said nobody should sit on it until tomorrow.”

“I’ll keep that in mind.”

“Do you want me to get you a clean glass?”

“The jar is clean.”

“Then I won’t.”

“You can still drink water.”

He grinned. “I’ll do that.”

A knock sounded on the doorframe. Leandros stood outside, holding a folded cloth over one arm. He had learned to knock after Clarisse made him repeat the cabin rule three times and refused to treat the fourth as a joke. His gaze moved from Silena to the table.

“Is this the dining table?”

“It’s the worktable until second bell,” Clarisse said.

“I need somewhere to lay out the canvas cover for the paddle rack.”

“Then you need the other table.”

“The other table’s crowded.”

“Move the things that are crowded on it.”

Leandros looked at the portfolio, the brushes, the glass jar. “I could put these away for you.”

“No,” Silena said. “I’m using them.”

“I only meant to clear a space.”

“I know. Don’t move them.”

He looked as if he expected the explanation to continue. Silena didn’t give him one.

Clarisse turned from the stove. “You can have the table after second bell. Not before.”

“I’m not asking you to schedule my whole morning.”

“You’re asking to use a table that already has a schedule.”

“It’s a table.”

“It’s the table where people work.”

Leandros’s jaw shifted. “Fine.”

He did not leave. His attention stayed on the portfolio, and the look was not contempt this time. It was curiosity trying to disguise itself as assessment.

“Is it the picture of the three of you?” he asked Silena.

“The copy I promised to make.”

“For the queen?”

“For Percy.”

“But he’s sending it to her.”

“That’s the plan.”

“So it is for the queen.”

“It is for Percy to decide whether he wants to send it.”

Leandros gave a small shrug. “That’s close enough.”

“It isn’t.”

Clarisse’s knife stopped. Percy reached for his mug, then set it back down without saying anything. Leandros folded the cloth tighter around his arm.

“I didn’t mean the picture,” he said. “I meant who it’s for.”

“I know what you meant.”

“Then why—”

“Because I decide what I mean when I make something.”

He opened his mouth. For a moment Silena thought he would ask what the difference was. Then his eyes shifted to the worktable, to Clarisse, back to Silena.

“Second bell,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I can wait.”

“That would help.”

He left the doorway. The cloth moved out of sight after him.

Clarisse went back to chopping. “He thinks deciding the purpose is the same as deciding the owner.”

“Sometimes those are the same.”

“Not for this.”

“No.”

Silena took the lid off the water jar and dipped the first brush. The one she had used for the outline was worn soft at the tip. She ran it between her fingers, then reached for the portfolio. The old group sketch lay inside a sheet of brown paper, its corners protected by folded cloth. Pencil had caught the curve of each wave without making them into portraits. Clarisse’s broke against the stones; Silena’s ran wide and calm; Percy’s curled toward a small starfish.

She had promised a color copy on a day when they had gone to tide pools together. The promise had been easy then, made in the afterglow of a good day, when the three of them had still been new to saying *siblings* without checking the word for weak spots. Since then, there had been cabins, errands, mail, questions that belonged to other people, and a mother’s letter she could not yet decide how to send. The sketch had waited. It had not become a debt just because it had waited a long time.

She set it beside the clean paper. The drawing had softened at one edge where the paper had been handled. She weighted it down with a smooth shell, then took a second sheet from the portfolio. A copy was not a tracing. She could use the first picture for proportion and still make each mark with her own hand.

The room was not quiet. Percy got his water. Clarisse finished the apples. The campers by the bunks found the glove under a blanket and argued over whether it belonged to Noah or one of the twins. Someone outside dragged a crate across the frost-stiff ground. The cabin continued being a cabin around her work.

That, too, was the point. She did not need a locked room or a silent morning. She needed to be allowed to choose the shape of her attention and to have the choice last longer than one sentence.

She sharpened a pencil. The first wave took form in a line that looked too much like a mountain. Silena erased it, then turned the sheet a little. She held the original beside the blank page and studied the space where Percy had drawn toward the starfish. If she copied the line exactly, the water would look like it had been caught reaching. If she changed the angle, it would look as though the wave were going somewhere.

She drew it again.

Clarisse put a plate on the table’s free corner. “Eat while it’s warm.”

“I’ll eat in a minute.”

“You’ll eat now.”

Silena looked up.

Clarisse held out the plate. “I’m not asking you to leave the work. I’m asking you not to make the first thing you do with your morning forget you have a body.”

Silena took the plate. “That’s fair.”

“I know.”

The apples were warm, cut thick enough to hold their shape, with honey at the edges. Clarisse had set a second piece of bread beside them. She did not ask Silena what she was painting. She did not ask whether she was making it for Amphitrite, or Percy, or the cabin. She let the paper remain open between the brush jar and the shell.

Silena ate. The first bite was hot enough to make her slow down. The second was sweet. She put the fork down before she was finished and looked at the faint brown shadow she had laid beneath the first curve.

The copy had not become a picture yet. It was still paper with a beginning on it.

For the next hour, that was all it had to be.
---

Second bell came with a handbell from the pavilion, not a bell in the cabin. Silena heard it through the window blanket and looked at the first wash of blue on the page. The color had settled unevenly. At the bottom of the wave, a pale seam appeared where she had left a strip of dry paper between two strokes.

She liked it.

She wrote the day’s date in the corner, then crossed it out before the ink had dried. The day of the week was enough for her private notes; the year had never mattered to the picture. She put the sheet on the board and carried it outside with the palette and jar held in a basket. The paper was still damp, so she did not tuck it back in the portfolio.

Clarisse had divided breakfast and repair work without giving anyone a speech. She had a list by the door, two rows marked complete and three that still needed names beside them. The torn awning and the Cabin One rail had not moved. The fence was braced. Annabeth’s stool still needed its glue to cure. The north-step frost check could wait until the ground stayed cold enough to make the result useful.

Silena added nothing to the list. The painting was not a camp task.

At the pavilion, she found a corner table with enough light to see the pigment. She sat where she could watch the paper without putting it in the direct sun, then placed the jar to the left of her elbow. A loaf, a small crock of honey, and a plate of cut oranges had been set out for anyone who had missed breakfast. She took one orange wedge and ate it before she opened the portfolio.

The old sketch seemed smaller outside the cabin. The three waves were only pencil lines, no thicker than the stem of a leaf. She set it beside the copy and used the pencil to mark where each line turned. There were details she had not put down on the first page, though she remembered making them: the small interruption in Clarisse’s curl; the broad quiet space around her own wave; the reach in Percy’s line toward the starfish.

The starfish was not part of the water. It was an idea the water had noticed.

“Is that the table you need?” Chiron asked.

Silena looked up. He had stopped at the edge of the pavilion, a rolled sleeve caught beneath one hand. “For now.”

“Then it’s yours until lunch. I’ll keep the other end clear.”

“Thank you.”

He set a tin cup of clean water on the far end of the table. She glanced at it.

“Not for paint,” he said. “For drinking.”

“I have the jar.”

“Then you have two kinds of water.”

“I suppose I do.”

“Amphitrite is visiting after lunch,” he said. “I thought it might be easier if the meal stayed here rather than moving everyone to the Big House. She asked to see Percy, Clarisse, and Silena. I asked whether she would like to meet Leandros as well, if he wanted that. I haven’t asked him for you.”

Silena set down the brush she had been holding. “Does he know she’s coming?”

“Clarisse told him before breakfast. He said he would decide later.”

“And the meal?”

“Open to anyone who wants to come. No one has to host.”

“No one has to explain anything for him?”

“Not unless they choose to.”

She studied Chiron’s face. He had made the invitation; now he was trying not to make it a responsibility. “I can come to lunch.”

“You can. You can also keep painting.”

“I want to be there.”

“Then I’ll set a chair.”

He had not asked whether she wanted to prepare something, smooth over an awkward welcome, choose the food, or decide who sat beside whom. His restraint did not make the invitation smaller. It gave the choice back to her.

“Thank you,” she said.

Chiron nodded and moved on.

Silena looked at the copy. The lower part of Percy’s wave was still only an outline. She did not need it finished by lunch. The paper could dry on the board, and she could bring it to the meal if she wanted. The plan changed as soon as it stopped being a plan she could choose.

She touched the brush to the palette and made a new blue by adding the tiniest amount of brown. The color was duller than the lake on a bright day. It was the shade underneath the surface when the light went thin.

A boy from the Hermes cabin stopped at the table. He was carrying a small bowl with two bent spoons inside.

“Is the jar for everybody?” he asked.

“The clean water is at the other end,” Silena said. “The paint water is here.”

“Which one is clean?”

“The tin cup.”

He reached for the cup, then noticed the sheet. “Are you making a picture of the cabins?”

“No.”

“Could you?”

“Maybe, if I wanted to.”

He considered that, then gave the spoons a small shake. “I’m looking for the kitchen. Chiron said the spare spoons were here.”

“Those are the spare spoons,” Silena said. “The kitchen is behind the pavilion.”

“Thanks.”

He started toward the wrong door.

“Other side,” she called.

“Oh.”

Silena turned the board a little to keep the wet edge from the breeze. She had not intended to spend the morning being asked directions. She did not mind helping one person find a kitchen. She minded when one kind of helpfulness was treated as a permanent job that canceled the one she had chosen.

Leandros came to the table carrying a bundle of canvas and thread. He had taken his canvas-cover patches to the lower bench, but the wind there kept pulling the loose ends from his lap. He looked first at the available table, then at her work.

“I can put this farther down,” he said.

“Farther down where?”

He indicated the open end. “There.”

“That’s where the cup is.”

“I can move the cup.”

“Ask the person who owns the cup.”

“Chiron left it.”

“Then ask Chiron.”

Leandros looked toward the pavilion doors. “He’s gone.”

“Then use the bench.”

“The wind catches the cloth there.”

Silena considered him. The bundle had a torn edge that needed a careful patch, the sort of work he could not do while the canvas kept folding back on itself. “You can sit on the other side of the table if you keep the thread away from the paint.”

He set down the canvas. “I can do that.”

“And don’t move the board.”

“I’m not going to move the board.”

“Good.”

He pulled the chair back, but stopped before he sat. “You’re painting the three waves?”

“Yes.”

“Same as the old sketch?”

“Mostly.”

“Why not add another?”

Silena looked at him. “What would the fourth wave be?”

He glanced down at the lines. “Me, I suppose.”

“Then it wouldn’t be the same picture.”

“It would be the one we have now.”

“It isn’t a family roll call.”

“Maybe not. But if you’re going to show Mother—”

“I don’t know whether I’m showing it to her.”

“You said it was for a letter.”

“I said I promised to make a copy for Percy. He decides whether it goes in a letter. I decide whether I’m ready to hand it over.”

“You’ve already decided to make it.”

“Yes.”

“Then why not make it about all of us?”

“Because I was making it before you arrived.”

The words landed harder than she meant them to. Leandros lowered himself into the chair. His fingers worked the canvas edge into a straighter line.

“Right,” he said.

Silena did not soften the answer by turning it into a different one. “You can be in a new picture. You can ask me sometime. I might say yes. I might say no.”

“Could I ask now?”

“You just did.”

“I meant properly.”

“Then wait until I’m finished with this one.”

He looked at the image again, not quite looking at her. “Fine.”

The needle slipped in his fingers. He pushed it through the canvas too close to the frayed edge and pulled the thread until a small section puckered.

“Your stitch is going to gather the patch,” Silena said.

“I know how to sew canvas.”

“Then you know the stitch has to cross the tear.”

“I know.”

He began to unpick it. The work was small, but the seam would not hold if he left it as it was. Silena turned back to her paper. She was not going to take his needle and show him how. He had not asked for a lesson; he had also not done a good job. Both things could be true.

They worked in silence for a while. His thread moved through canvas. Her brush moved across paper. The wind lifted the corner of a napkin. Someone at the far table laughed; someone else asked for the honey.

Leandros finished one side of the patch and held the patched canvas up to inspect it. “It’s better.”

“Is it?”

He looked at the seam. “It holds.”

“That’s what matters.”

He set the canvas down. “You’re making the same color again.”

“I’m correcting the first one.”

“Why?”

“The first one dried lighter.”

“Couldn’t you make it darker the first time?”

“I could have. I didn’t know how much it would change.”

“You could test it.”

“I could have.”

He nodded, as if the exchange settled the issue. Silena dipped the brush again. She had made mistakes with color long before Leandros arrived, and she would make more when he was gone. She did not need him to admire the fact that she could fix one.

A shadow fell over the table. Percy had returned with a coil of twine under one arm. He looked at the wet painting, then at the canvas-cover patches.

“Are you both taking the table?”

“Until lunch,” Silena said.

“Then I can take the twine to the shed.”

“You can take it wherever you need it.”

“I meant, are you using the far side?”

“I am.”

“Then I’ll go.”

He did not ask whether she wanted him to move Leandros or help her clear the table. He gave her a small nod and went out the other door.

Leandros watched him leave. “Does he always ask before he does anything?”

“No.”

“Does he have to?”

“No.”

“Then why did he do that?”

“Because he asked whether I needed the table.”

Leandros picked up his thread again. “I’m not trying to crowd you.”

“Then don’t.”

She heard the small click of the needle passing through canvas. After a moment he said, “I’m not.”

Silena let the conversation end. She did not need to tell him he was trying. She needed the boundary to remain ordinary enough that neither of them had to make a speech about it.

By the time the lunch bell rang, the blue on the page had dried into two different shades. The first wash would stay. The correction made the second wave turn farther out than the pencil line. Silena could erase the outline and redraw it, but she liked the movement better now.

She placed a square of clean paper over the painting, weighted its corners, and washed the brush in the jar. Then she rinsed her hands in the tin cup and let the cold water wake her up.

Leandros tied off the canvas-cover patch. He kept his fingers clear of the painting.

“Your seam’s straight,” Silena said.

“It should be.”

“It is.”

He tucked the needle into a cork. “I could make a fourth wave in the blank part.”

“You could.”

“You don’t want to?”

“Not on this one.”

He waited. She took the answer at face value and moved the painting to a dry place.

---

At lunch, Chiron kept the table open and the meal plain.

There was a pot of lentils, a basket of dark bread, a dish of roasted carrots, and one bowl of apples cooked soft with cinnamon. The food was warm enough to make the pavilion smell like a house. The benches had been wiped down; nobody had arranged place cards or hung a banner. The table did not look like a ceremony.

Silena noticed because she had expected to be asked to help make it look like something.

She carried the basket of bread to the table, then set it down at the end farthest from the paint. Nobody assigned her a seat. Nobody asked her to serve. Clarisse took a bowl from the stack and sat beside Percy. Annabeth arrived with the revised map rolled under her arm, checked the stool from a distance as if she could tell from across camp whether the glue had cured, then sat with Percy on her other side.

Leandros came last. He held the chair opposite the main doors for a moment, then took it. He had combed his hair and changed into a clean shirt. The sharpness of the collar made him look younger, not older. He rested both hands on the table and kept them there, as if he had decided the safest thing was to touch nothing.

“You can still leave if you don't want to be here,” Chiron said.

“I know.”

“Do you want to?”

Leandros glanced toward the entrance. “No.”

“All right.”

That was all Chiron said. He left the space beside him empty.

Silena sat two places down from Clarisse, with the painting turned face down on a dry board beside her. She had brought it because she wanted to see whether she could live with the color she had chosen. She had not decided to show it to anyone. That was not the same as deciding not to.

A low murmur moved through the pavilion. The outside light changed. There was no thunder, no ripple across the cups, no announcement from the air. Amphitrite came through the main entrance with a wool shawl wrapped around her shoulders and salt-bright beads at one wrist. She looked like someone who had crossed a cold hill on foot and had not made the hill’s difficulty anybody else’s problem.

Percy stood. So did Clarisse. Silena rose after them, not because anyone had asked her to, but because she wanted to greet the woman who had once welcomed her into a home beneath the sea without calling the invitation an obligation.

“Percy,” Amphitrite said, and touched his cheek with the backs of her fingers. “You’ve been eating?”

“Mostly.”

“Clarisse?”

“Ask him how much of the apple dish he ate.”

“I’m not keeping count,” Percy said.

“I am,” Clarisse replied.

Amphitrite smiled at them. She did not move to hug Clarisse. She had learned that Clarisse might choose a greeting and might not, and that deciding for her did not make a warmer moment. Clarisse leaned in and put her arms around her for one brief beat. Amphitrite returned it, then let her go.

Silena stepped forward. “Hello.”

“Silena.” Amphitrite's expression opened. “I'm glad to see you.”

“I'm glad you're here.”

She meant it. The words surprised her with how easy they were.

Amphitrite glanced toward the empty chair beside Chiron. “And you must be Leandros.”

He stood but did not cross the room. “Queen Amphitrite.”

“You can call me Amphitrite here.”

“Would you prefer that?”

“I would.”

He nodded. “Then Amphitrite.”

She did not correct his posture or ask him to sit. She drew out the chair beside Chiron and settled into it, setting her shawl across the back. The greeting had not changed his place at the table or made him a son of hers. He remained the son of Poseidon, a boy with his own mother and his own past. Silena saw him measure the distance between the words and the chair.

“Thank you for coming,” Chiron said.

“I was curious about the cabin that grew by one while I was away.” Amphitrite looked to Clarisse and Silena, then to Percy. “And I wanted to see how the winter work was going.”

“Slow,” Clarisse said.

“Honest, at least.”

“Mostly.”

Percy laughed. “That’s a good report for us.”

Amphitrite took the cup Chiron offered. “I also wanted to make sure I was not arriving as if I had been invited to inspect anybody.”

“Then don’t,” Clarisse said.

“I wasn’t planning to.”

“Good.”

Silena pulled bread from the basket. She did not feel as if she had to make a place for Amphitrite in every pause. Clarisse could set her own limits. Percy could tell his mother what he wanted to tell her. Leandros could decide whether he wanted a conversation. Their different relationships did not need one person to organize them.

“Did my father know you were visiting?” Leandros asked.

“Chiron invited me. I don’t know whether Poseidon knows.”

“He didn’t tell you I was here.”

“No. I heard from Chiron.”

“And he didn’t tell you about me before that?”

“No.”

Leandros looked down at the bread. “So he said nothing.”

“Not to me.”

He turned the crust over in his hands. “You were away.”

“I was.”

“Where?”

“Under the sea.”

“Which part?”

“Home.”

His mouth tightened at the simplicity of the answer. Silena knew the feeling of wanting a detail and getting a boundary instead. She could tell by the way he watched Amphitrite that he wanted the space made into an account of his father, of family, of why nobody had arranged this table before. He might ask. He might not.

Amphitrite turned to the serving dishes. “The carrots smell good.”

“Silena did those,” Percy said.

“I did not,” Silena replied. “Chiron did.”

“Chiron?” Amphitrite said.

“I cut them. He did the spices.”

“That is a reliable division of work.”

Chiron gave a small bow with the ladle. “I accept the compliment.”

“Somebody has to,” Clarisse said.

The first few minutes of lunch went easily. Clarisse told Chiron the fence brace had held through the night. Annabeth mentioned the map could wait until the shore ground dried. Percy asked whether the bread had come from the Big House kitchen; Clarisse told him it had come from the basket he had walked past. The talk stayed ordinary. It was not empty. It gave everyone something to do with their hands.

Leandros ate two pieces of bread and did not ask who had baked it. He passed the apples when Silena reached for the bowl. He even thanked Annabeth when she moved the map roll away from his plate.

Then his attention caught on the board beside Silena. The painting had shifted in the draft and a corner of the paper showed. The blue line on Percy’s wave was visible from where he sat.

“Is that the one?” he asked.

Silena put her fork down. “The copy, yes.”

He leaned back, looking past her to Amphitrite. “Silena is making it for you.”

“I am not,” Silena said.

The correction came quickly. A few heads turned. Leandros looked surprised by the force of the interruption.

“You said it was going in a letter.”

“I said I was making a copy for Percy. He can decide whether he sends it.”

“And if he does, it goes to Amphitrite.”

“If he decides to send it.”

“So it’s for her.”

“It’s for me to make. It’s for Percy to choose what to do with. It is not a gift I promised you on my behalf.”

The table went quiet. It was not a large silence. It was large enough.

Amphitrite's gaze had moved from Leandros to Silena. She did not ask to see the painting. A faint blue mark showed beside Silena’s thumbnail. “Is the paper still wet?” Amphitrite asked. “The edge of the color looks soft.”

“It is. I let the first wash dry, then put a second over it. I’m still deciding whether I want the line that way.”

“Does the paper usually change the color as it dries?”

“Always. This one buckles if I use too much water, so I have to leave room for it to move.”

Amphitrite nodded, studying Silena’s hand rather than trying to turn the board over. “I like that you can see where the water was.”

Silena felt a small smile begin. “That’s what I’m hoping for.”

“Do you give the colors names?” Amphitrite asked.

“Sometimes on the palette. Not on the page.”

“Why not?”

“Because the name never looks the way the water did.”

“How do you find a color again?”

“I keep a sample in the portfolio. This one is close, but it isn’t the same. The paper changes it, and the light does too.”

Amphitrite looked at the strip. “Then the name is less useful than the mark.”

“Most of the time.”

“Is the rest of the picture settled?” Amphitrite asked.

“No.”

“I thought you had chosen the lines.”

“I chose where they start. The rest can change.”

Amphitrite looked again at the small scrap. “I might have filled in the star.”

“I may leave it in pencil.”

“Then leave it.”

Silena glanced at her. “You’re allowed to think it should be colored.”

“I do think that.” Amphitrite took a sip of water. “I also know it is yours.”

There was no heavy pause after it. Silena went back to the table with the scrap. She liked that Amphitrite had not tried to turn the thought into a promise.

The brush had made a thin crescent of blue along the paper’s lower edge. Silena tested a clean stroke beside it. The color spread until it touched the border. She dabbed it with the cloth, then waited. The paper held some water and gave the rest away.

“Does it always behave like that?” Amphitrite asked from her chair.

“Only when I don't want it to.”

Amphitrite laughed. “Then it has a sense of timing.”

Silena washed the brush twice, once in the jar and once in the tin cup. “The first water keeps the pigment. The second tells me whether the brush is actually clean.”

“That seems like a useful distinction.”

“It is if I want the next color to stay itself.”

Leandros’s attention went from the blue mark on Silena’s thumb to the covered picture. “Silena is making it for you,” he said.

“There isn’t,” Silena said. “Not yet.”

“All right.”

Silena felt the first pull of embarrassment in her neck. She had chosen to come to lunch. She had chosen to bring the board. She had not chosen to have the choice turned into a public announcement.

Leandros folded his napkin once, then flattened it. “I thought if you were making a picture of the family, then—”

“It isn’t a picture of all the family.”

“Those are the three of us.”

“It was drawn before you arrived.”

“That's what I meant.”

“No,” Silena said. “That’s what you wanted it to mean.”

He looked at her. “I wasn’t trying to take it away from you.”

“I know.”

“Then—”

“You made it into something I hadn’t said it was.”

His gaze dropped. Amphitrite had not moved toward the painting. Percy had kept his hands on his own plate. Clarisse looked at Leandros with a stillness that was not the same as a rescue or a threat. She was letting Silena answer for herself.

“I thought you would want her to see it,” Leandros said.

“Why?”

“Because she’s here.”

“That’s not a reason to show her everything I make.”

Amphitrite placed her spoon in the bowl. “Silena decides who sees her work.”

“Of course,” he said.

His voice had the thin edge it took on when a boundary surprised him. He did not shout. He did not apologize. The word *of course* came out like he believed it ought to end the subject.

Silena looked down at the edge of her plate. “I’m going to take it back to the cabin.”

“You don’t have to,” Percy said.

“I know. I want to.”

“Do you want me to come?”

“Not yet.”

“All right.”

He let her push her chair back. Nobody grabbed the board for her. She picked it up herself, keeping both hands beneath the dry corners. The fresh wash was protected under the paper. The painting had not been damaged. She still had the right to leave.

Behind her, Amphitrite said, “We’ll keep eating.”

“Thank you.”

The words came out with more feeling than she meant. She carried the board through the pavilion doors and heard the talk resume slowly behind her. Nobody followed.

Outside, the air had turned colder. Silena set the board on the low wall beside the path and checked the paper. The blue had dried. The curl of Percy’s wave was there, and the dark shade at its base still looked right.

Her throat tightened anyway.

She did not regret taking the work to lunch. She did not regret sitting with Amphitrite. She regretted that the moment of greeting had become a moment of explanation. She had wanted to talk about paint, not defend the right to keep a picture unfinished.

She put her palm flat against the wall. The stone held the cold from the night before. After a moment, she lifted her hand and carried the board toward Cabin Three.
---

The cabin door stood open when Silena came back. She stepped around the loose threshold board and carried the painting to the clear part of the table. The room had warmed while she was gone. A line of light sat across the wall above the bunks, and the window blanket lifted once when the wind changed.

Percy and Annabeth were at the far end of the table with the map between them. Annabeth held the edge down with her palm. Percy had a pencil but had not marked anything.

“Is the stool still drying?” Silena asked.

“Yes,” Annabeth said. “The top is level, but Chiron wants it left alone until tomorrow.”

“Good.”

Annabeth glanced at the board. “You took the painting back.”

“I did.”

“Do you want to tell us about it?”

“Not yet.”

“All right.”

There was no disappointment in her voice. Annabeth rolled the map up, careful not to crease the pencil marks, and set it near her own things.

Percy stood. “I can make space.”

“There’s space.”

“Right.”

He sat again. He put the pencil down and touched Annabeth’s hand where it rested on the map. She turned her palm beneath his. For a second, they were quiet together. Percy’s thumb moved once across her knuckles, then Annabeth bumped his shoulder with hers and went back to the route line. It was small enough to belong to them.

Clarisse came in carrying a folded blanket. “You left lunch.”

“I left the table.”

“Same amount of food.”

“I’m not hungry anymore.”

“Do you want me to tell Chiron not to save you any?”

“No. I might be later.”

“Then I won’t tell him anything.”

Clarisse set the blanket by her bunk. She did not ask why Silena had returned. Her eyes touched the painting and then moved away. Silena appreciated the discipline of that. Concern could feel like a second audience when she had not asked for one.

“Can someone hold the window blanket?” Clarisse asked. “The upper pin is slipping.”

“I can,” Percy said.

Annabeth reached for her coat. “I’ll get the stool clamps from the Big House before they disappear.”

“They’re not going anywhere,” Percy said.

“They have a talent for going places when nobody is looking.”

She took the coat and slipped her arm through Percy’s as they passed. He looked down at her hand where it rested over his sleeve, then at her face.

“I’ll meet you at the corner,” he said.

“Take the map with you.”

“I can do that.”

They stepped out together. Annabeth leaned in to say something near his shoulder. Percy bent his head to hear it. Neither of them looked back at Silena to see whether she had noticed.

The cabin had its own repairs to make. Clarisse dragged the chair beneath the loose window seam. Silena moved her paint things to the safe end of the table, then stood on the chair and pressed the blanket up against the frame while Clarisse tightened the cord. The fabric pulled at her fingers. She held it until Clarisse tied the knot.

“Good,” Clarisse said.

Silena stepped down. “It should hold for now.”

“It’ll hold until I can get the proper pin.”

“Can I have the table back?”

Clarisse looked at her. “It’s yours.”

“Thank you.”

Clarisse’s mouth tightened, like she was deciding whether to say more. Then she nodded and walked to the door. “I’ll be outside. If you need anything, tell me.”

Silena turned back to the work. The room quieted a little after Clarisse left. She took the cover off the paper and found the paint still soft at one edge. The cool air from the open door had slowed the drying. She lifted the board toward the light. The blue was not what she remembered, but it was not wrong.

She did not work for several minutes. She let the silence after the pavilion settle into something she could use. The picture had been her choice that morning. It was still her choice. Leandros had not made the promise smaller by naming it incorrectly. Amphitrite had not taken it from her by looking at the color.

When she touched the brush to the paper, she chose a lighter blue.

The next line ran too far. She lifted it with a clean brush, blotted the edge with the corner of a cloth, and waited to see what remained. She had learned that sometimes a mistake stopped being a mistake if she gave it time. Sometimes it was only a mark she had to live with. Neither answer required her to pretend she had meant it.

Leandros knocked at the open doorframe.

Silena kept her eyes on the paper. “What?”

“Can I come in?”

“You can stand there.”

He stayed in the doorway.

“I have to finish the canvas-cover patch,” he said. “The table is better than the bench, but I can take it back outside.”

“You can use the low table. Keep the canvas on your side.”

“Okay.”

He set the canvas and the thread down where she had indicated. Then he stood with his hands at his sides. He was not looking at the art now.

“I told Amphitrite the picture was for her,” he said.

Silena rinsed the brush. “You did.”

“You told me you were making it for Percy. I knew that.”

“Yes.”

“I thought the person who got it at the end was what mattered. I didn’t think about you deciding whether it went at all.”

“It matters to me.”

“I get that now.”

“Do you?”

He considered it. “Not all the way.”

Silena set the brush in the jar. “Then don’t make it a certainty.”

“I shouldn’t have spoken for you.”

“No.”

“I’m sorry I did.”

She looked at him. He was embarrassed and had not covered it with anger. That did not make the behavior disappear. She let the apology sit without helping him feel better about it.

“I wanted her to like it,” he said. “And I thought if she saw us in the picture, that would make the table less strange.”

“It’s not your picture to use for that.”

“I know.”

“You don’t have to prove that the three of us are a family with something I made.”

“I wasn’t thinking about it as proof.”

“What were you thinking?”

“That it could make her see me with you.”

“Those are not the same thing.”

He looked toward the window, where Clarisse’s knot held the blanket in place. “No.”

Silena was tired of the conversation, but she was not willing to let him think it had been about an invisible rule she had forgotten to mention. “If I show it to Amphitrite, it will be because I want to. If I send it, it will be because Percy wants to and I say yes. If I keep it, it stays mine. You don’t get to turn the picture into a place you can stand.”

Leandros’s gaze returned to her. “I understand what you said.”

“That isn’t the same as liking it.”

“I don’t have to like it.”

“Good.”

“I still think it would be good if there were a picture of all four of us.”

“You can want that.”

“Could you make one later?”

“I don’t know.”

“All right.”

He did not ask her to promise. He moved to the low table and started unpicking the crooked stitch. His first attempt had gathered the cloth. He worked the needle under the tight loop, loosened it, and began again more carefully. The canvas spread flat under his hand.

Silena turned back to her page. After a while she said, “The needle is too close to the edge.”

He shifted it inward without looking up. “Here?”

“A little more.”

He moved it another fraction.

“That’s better.”

He did not say he had known. She did not ask whether the apology was enough. Both of them kept working.

The shadow at the window had moved down the wall by the time Percy and Annabeth returned. Annabeth brought a small tin of nails, not the clamps. She apologized to Chiron for taking the wrong thing, and Percy carried them to the Big House while she went back to her map. Neither of them made a story out of the mistake.

“Do you want the light?” Annabeth asked Silena.

“Not yet.”

“Then I’ll work on this side.”

She set the map on the far end of the table and began revising one of the shore marks. Percy came back with the clamps and put them beside the stool. The cabin made room for three separate tasks. Nobody asked Silena to stop hers.

Leandros finished the seam. He held the canvas edge taut and checked that the patch did not pucker. The new stitch was not beautiful. It was strong enough to use.

“Can I put this away?” he asked.

“Where?”

“In the canvas bin.”

“Then yes.”

He rolled it loosely, keeping the repaired edge outside. “I’ll return the table after I clean the thread.”

“Thank you.”

He collected each scrap from the floor. One piece had stuck to the sole of his shoe. He peeled it off and dropped it in the waste tin.

At the door, he paused. “I’m going to go back to the pavilion.”

“Okay.”

“To help Chiron carry the rest of lunch things.”

“Why are you telling me?”

“I don’t know.” He frowned at himself. “You asked me not to make you responsible for the table. I thought maybe I should say where I was going.”

“That’s your choice.”

He nodded. “I’ll be back before supper.”

Silena watched him go. Clarisse was outside, speaking with one of the younger campers about the winter pin. Leandros moved past her without offering advice. Clarisse did not call him back or ask whether he had apologized.

Silena returned to the color.---

The afternoon light shifted through the pavilion windows while Silena worked on the second wave.

She had brought the painting back because the table was open, not because she had changed her mind about showing it. She set the board on the dry end and turned it until the light struck the blue wash sideways. From that angle, she could see where the paper had lifted and where the pigment had settled. The wave did not need another color. It needed room at the edge.

She made three small swatches on a scrap. One ran too far. One was pale but even. The third kept a dark line where the brush stopped. She put the scrap beside the painting and waited for it to dry.

Amphitrite was still at the pavilion, speaking with Chiron near the serving table. She had taken her shawl off and folded it over the back of her chair. The visit had no timetable Silena knew about. It was not a ceremony that had to be completed by sunset.

Leandros was gone. The canvas cover had been laid out by the far doors with its corner mended, and the needle, thread, and loose ends were in a small tin beside it. Someone had cleaned the table after lunch. The two chairs at Silena’s end remained where they had been.

She picked up the swatch with the pale, even color and walked over to Amphitrite.

“Could I ask you something about the water?”

“Of course.”

Silena held up the paper. “This is a test. I’m trying to decide how much of the pigment should show along the edge.”

Amphitrite looked at the swatch, not for long. “The pale one?”

“Yes.”

“It keeps the white of the paper. The dark one makes a sharper line.”

“Which do you like?”

“Those are two different questions.”

“They are.”

“Then I like the pale one as a piece of color. I like the dark one as an edge.” She considered the marks again. “But that doesn’t tell you which one belongs in your painting.”

Silena smiled. “No.”

“Would you like to show me the painting?”

Silena looked toward the table. The portfolio lay closed beside the board. She could have carried the painting over. She could also leave it where it was. “Not the whole thing. You can look at the test.”

“That’s enough.”

Amphitrite sat beside her, leaving space between their shoulders. She did not reach for the paper. Silena turned it so they could both see.

“The dark line reminds me of the current under a calm surface,” Amphitrite said. “You can miss it if you only look at the shine.”

“I think I need the lighter edge. It’s a picture of a wave trying to curl. The original is only pencil. If I make the water too dark, the starfish disappears.”

“You’re thinking about the space around it.”

“I have to. The picture gets crowded if I fill every part.”

Amphitrite nodded. “That sounds like more work than I expected.”

“It is.”

“I’m glad you told me.”

Silena tilted the paper again. The three swatches were almost dry. “The middle one is closer.”

“Then that is what you should use.”

“Maybe.”

“You don’t have to decide because I said it.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

Amphitrite laughed, quiet and brief. It was the first time Silena had heard her laugh that day without trying to keep the rest of the table comfortable.

“Do you paint often?” Amphitrite asked.

“When I have time.”

“Do you find it?”

“Sometimes.”

“That’s different from having it.”

“Yes.”

Amphitrite did not turn this into a lesson about making time or honoring a gift. She only looked at the strip of paper and asked, “Would you like a place to work if I come again?”

Silena considered how the question felt. It was not a promise she had to make. “I like this table. I don’t want it saved for me all the time.”

“Then I won’t ask anyone to save it.”

“I can ask for it when I need it.”

“Good.”

The answer left the next visit open without making the table theirs. Silena carried the swatch back to the board. She used the pale color, then put the brush down. The edge of the second wave held. The starfish had enough blank paper around it.

When she glanced up again, Amphitrite had returned to Chiron’s conversation. She had not tried to follow Silena’s painting with her eyes.

Silena tucked the unused swatches inside the portfolio. The finished parts were still hers, and the unfinished parts were hers too. She wrote a small note on the paper’s backing: *blue dries lighter when the room is cold*. Then she crossed out *cold* and wrote *slowly*. The room had been cold earlier. The paint had dried slowly. She could remember the difference without making it a rule.

Clarisse came in carrying a small plate from the kitchen. “They had more bread.”

“Thank you.”

“Chiron said you hadn’t eaten since the morning.”

“He wasn’t supposed to keep count.”

“He didn’t. He asked whether the bread had been left out.”

“That's not the same as asking whether I ate.”

“It was close enough that I answered.”

Silena took a piece. The bread had a thick, crisp crust, and warmth still came from its center. She did not need a meal, but the smell changed her mind. She ate standing beside the table, brushing crumbs from her fingers before returning to the painting.

“Do you want me to talk to him?” Clarisse asked.

“No.”

Clarisse nodded. “All right.”

“I already talked to him.”

“I figured.”

“You didn’t need to stop the lunch.”

“I know.”

“You looked like you wanted to.”

“I did.”

“But you didn’t.”

“Because you were answering.”

Silena swallowed the last bite. “Thank you.”

Clarisse took the plate back to the serving table. “I’m not going to stand here and make you thank me for that.”

“I know.”

“I’m going to check the awning bracket. It’s still on the list.”

“Tell me if you need another hand.”

“I will.”

Clarisse left the pavilion with the empty plate. She had not made the apology her own job. She had let Silena keep the line where she had put it.

The day moved on. Campers came and went, carrying a basket of laundry, a small box of nails, the wrong pair of gloves. Chiron showed Annabeth where the map’s shore mark had not yet been checked. Annabeth took the rolled paper and set it on the far bench, where it would stay dry. She and Percy spoke with their heads bent over the same mark. Percy pointed once. Annabeth shifted the paper and asked him something. He shook his head, then smiled when she tapped the end of his nose with the charcoal. He kissed the side of her hand and returned the pencil.

Silena saw it without needing it to mean anything about her own afternoon. The two of them had made a space for the disagreement and then moved on to the map. The warmth between them had not required anybody else to smooth it out.

Leandros came back shortly before dinner with the repaired canvas cover folded in a square. A new tear had opened near one seam, but the patched corner lay flat.

“Chiron wants it by the paddle rack,” he said.

“Then it goes by the paddle rack,” Clarisse called from the doorway.

“I know where that is.”

“Good.”

He started past the table, then paused. “Silena, I can put it away if you want me to.”

“I don’t need help with the painting.”

“Okay.”

He carried the cover outside. It was not an apology, or a gift, or a request to be let into her work. It was something that needed putting away, and he did it.

When he came back, he stopped at the door. “Are you still working?”

“Yes.”

“Do you want the table clear for supper?”

“I can move the paints in a minute.”

“I can set the other table.”

“Ask Clarisse where the bowls are.”

He glanced toward the stove. “I can find them.”

“Then find them.”

He did not complain. He found the bowls and placed them at the other table, which was smaller and needed a cloth. He spread it crooked on the first try, noticed, and fixed it without calling for Silena to do it. Clarisse came in behind him with a basket of spoons. She moved one bowl to the left, then stopped before moving the rest.

“Does this work?” she asked.

“It works,” Silena said.

It was enough to make the room ready for supper.

The meal was simple: thick soup left from lunch, warm bread, a dish of carrots, and the last of the baked apples. Silena sat between Percy and Clarisse. Annabeth came over after she finished at the map table, bringing no map with her. Leandros took the chair nearest the door, not at the head. Nobody told him where to sit.

The first stretch of dinner passed on practical talk. Clarisse told Percy the awning bracket had bent farther than she thought, and they would need a new one. Annabeth said the north-step frost test still had to wait. Percy remembered a loose board on the porch and offered to check it before bed. Silena listened, ate, and kept her brush case closed beside her chair.

Then Percy asked, “Did you get any of the blue right?”

“Some of it.”

“Do you want to show me?”

She looked at his face. He wanted to see the copy because it belonged to a promise between them, not because he thought it belonged to him.

“Not tonight.”

“Okay.”

“I might tomorrow.”

“I can wait.”

Leandros tore a piece of bread. “You could still make one for all of us.”

Silena placed her spoon down. “I could.”

“Would you?”

“I don’t know.”

He looked as if he wanted to argue, but he turned the bread over in his hand and examined the crust instead. “All right.”

Clarisse passed him the apples. “These are the ones Silena likes.”

“I can hear you,” Silena said.

“I know. That’s why I said it.”

Leandros looked at the bowl. “I can take the last one.”

“You can take any one,” Silena said. “I didn’t make them.”

“Clarisse did.”

“Then ask Clarisse.”

Clarisse held the bowl out. “Take one before Percy eats the whole dish.”

“I haven’t eaten the whole dish.”

“You’re thinking about it.”

“I am not.”

“Take an apple.”

Leandros selected one and passed the bowl to Annabeth. “Thank you.”

Clarisse nodded. There was no smile, but the silence around the bowl had eased.

After dinner, Percy carried the bowls to the wash basin. Annabeth gathered the spoons and followed him. Their shoulders touched when they reached for the same dish. Percy said something Silena could not hear; Annabeth shook her head and then laughed. They were still together inside the small disagreement, still choosing the next task side by side.

Leandros took the bread cloth to the wash line and returned with the chairs. He put them back where he had found them. Silena moved her painting things onto the worktable again.

“You’re working after dark?” Clarisse asked.

“I’m finishing the edge.”

“Use the lamp.”

“I will.”

Clarisse checked the window blanket one last time. “The seam’s holding.”

Silena turned the palette under the light. “So is this.”

The copy still needed the curl of the last wave and the small starfish. She did not rush them because the day had gone strangely. The paper stayed where she had set it, the portfolio stayed closed until she needed it, and no one treated the work as a promise that could be claimed just because the family could see it.

From the far end of the room, Leandros cleared the table where the bowls had been. He wiped away the ring of water, then set the cloth to dry. He did not comment on the painting.

Silena dipped the brush in the pale blue. The color moved to the paper, then began to soften at the edge.

She had chosen the space it would take.


Silena dried the brush and laid it across the folded cloth. The copied waves were only halfway there: Clarisse’s break had a dark underside, her own swell still needed its long curve, and Percy’s wave held a pale reach toward the starfish. The original sketch sat beside it, exactly as she had kept it. The paper edges were soft from being handled, but none of the lines had faded.

She had drawn the three of them as waves because she knew how to draw waves. She had also wanted to see whether the shapes could look like people without putting faces on them. Clarisse had called hers a breaker. Percy had grinned at the starfish in the corner and said it had better be a real one. Silena had not changed the little star after he said it. That was part of the picture too: what they had said when the day was theirs.

The image did not need to include every person who might share a cabin with them later. It did not need to prove the three siblings had once gone out together or that they belonged to one another now. The day had happened. The drawing held it without asking permission from a new day.

She could make another picture if she wanted. She could draw a fourth wave on a separate sheet, or paint the cabin table after dinner, or decide she did not want any picture of all of them. None of those choices would alter the old pencil sketch. None of them would settle the question of what Poseidon had done or failed to do, or how much each of his children had been given. A piece of paper could not do that work, and she did not want it to try.

The cabin stayed busy around her. Clarisse came back from checking the awning bracket and shook the cold from her fingers. Percy and Annabeth sat near the map, each holding one end of the roll while they argued about whether the north-path line should bend before or after the stand of birches. Percy said the difference was half a step. Annabeth said that was enough to matter if someone arrived in the dark. He handed her the charcoal and let her draw the line.

Leandros lifted the canvas cover from the chair where he had set it to dry. He checked the patch once and carried it outside. It was a small task completed, no more proof of anything than the shelter it would give the paddles.

“Will you need the table tomorrow?” he asked Silena from the doorway.

“I don’t know yet.”

“Okay.”

He left without another question. He was still there, part of the cabin’s ordinary noise and movement. He had not turned into a brother Silena trusted because he said sorry. He had not disappeared because she had told him no. Both facts could stay where they were.

She returned to the page and changed the angle of the long blue curve. It ran a little higher than the pencil line, leaving a strip of white between the water and the starfish. The blank space kept the wave from closing over it.

Silena waited for the paint to settle. Then she wrote no title on the paper.

For tonight, the copy could remain what she had chosen to make: one unfinished picture of a day with Percy and Clarisse, held in a room where all of them could be present without owning one another’s work.
