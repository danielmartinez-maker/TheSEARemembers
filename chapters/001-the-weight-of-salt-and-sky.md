## Chapter 1: The Weight of Salt and Sky

Percy Jackson woke up underwater.

Not drowning. Not dreaming. *Underwater.* The pressure rested against his chest without frightening him; his body seemed to know the room before he did. Faint blue-green light filtered through coral windows he could not remember seeing before.

He opened his eyes. The ceiling was vaulted stone carved with scenes of naval battles, the mother-of-pearl inlay catching light that shouldn't exist this deep. His hand lifted toward an engraved trireme before he stopped it. The gesture felt practiced. He had no memory of practicing it, or of any morning beneath this ceiling.

For a breath — one single, disoriented moment — he thought nothing was wrong.

Then the memories arrived.

Not the memories of this childhood. The *other* one. The one that was his, that had to be his, because he remembered every second of it with the kind of clarity that didn't belong to dreams or hallucinations.

Sally's apartment. The smell of blue chocolate chip cookies. Yancy Academy. The Mythomagic board game. The Minotaur's breath on his neck, hot and rancid, its claws tangled in his hair. Chiron's kind eyes behind half-moons. Annabeth.

*Annabeth.*

Percy sat up so fast his head spun. His hands — he looked down at his hands — were smaller. Twelve-year-old hands. Smooth, uncalloused, the fingers slightly webbed in a way that spoke of years spent in the water.

"This isn't—" he started, and his voice cracked on the second syllable. A child's voice. A stranger's voice.

Salt air sharp in his lungs, he was in his chambers in the palace of Atlantis, and he was twelve years old, and he remembered being seventeen. He remembered Tartarus — the ash, the screams, the way Annabeth's hand had felt in his, like the only solid thing in a collapsing world. He remembered the Giant War, the pages of the prophecy, the taste of ambrosia on his tongue. He remembered New Rome, the university pamphlets, the apartment they'd circled in a real estate listing, the three recommendation letters that stood between him and a normal life.

He remembered going to sleep in that life. In their bed. With Annabeth's hair tickling his nose and her breath steady against his collarbone and the ordinary, perfect silence of a world where nothing needed saving for once.

And now he was here.

Percy threw off the covers — the sheets were woven from something like silk but heavier, sea-silk, and they pooled on the floor like water — and his feet hit the cold, salt-slick stone. He caught his reflection in the polished bronze mirror across the room.

A boy stared back. Dark hair, longer than he remembered, curling at the nape of his neck. Green eyes — those were the same, at least. Leaner than his original twelve-year-old frame, more accustomed to swimming than running. He was wearing a sleep-shirt that fell past his knees, and there was a faint luminescence to his skin, the way all Atlanteans had, as though the ocean lived just beneath the surface.

He looked like himself. He looked like a stranger.

"Annabeth," he said out loud, and the name felt like a rope thrown into darkness. Was she here? Was she somewhere else, equally displaced? Was she —

The door opened.

"Percy, you're going to be late for—" Amphitrite paused in the doorway, her brow furrowing. She was wearing robes of deep sea-green, her dark hair crowned with coral and pearl, and she looked at him with affection so familiar to her that his failure to recognize it hurt. "What's wrong, *agapi mou*? Did you've the nightmare again?"

The nightmare. Percy didn't know what nightmare she meant, and the not-knowing was a cold stone in his stomach.

"I—" he started, and stopped. How did you tell your mother that you remembered a different mother? That the woman who raised you in this timeline was real and loved and present, but that somewhere else — in some other version of his life — a mortal woman named Sally Jackson had made him blue cookies, lied to monsters to protect him, and raised him in Manhattan? He remembered her alive, waiting for him to come home. This woman knew a childhood he could not find in his head.

Salt air sharp in his lungs, he couldn't. He simply couldn't.

"I'm fine," he said. "Just — weird dream."

Amphitrite crossed the room and sat on the edge of his bed, reaching up to press her palm against his forehead. Her hands were cool and smooth, and the gesture was so achingly maternal that Percy's throat closed.

"You're not warm," she said, more to herself than to him. "Are you nervous about the surface trip? You've been talking about it for weeks."

The surface trip. Amphitrite spoke as if they had planned it together. Percy listened for something he could use: Camp Half-Blood, Chiron's training, other demigods his own age. Apparently he had been asking to go for years. He knew the destination from his original life, but none of these underwater preparations.

In his original timeline, he'd already been to camp. He'd already done everything this version of him was still anticipating.

But this Amphitrite didn't know that. This Poseidon didn't know that. To them, he was a sheltered twelve-year-old who'd spent his whole life under the sea.

"Okay," Percy said slowly. "Yeah. I'm nervous."

Amphitrite smiled, and the smile transformed her face from divine to maternal in a way that made Percy's chest hurt. "You'll be wonderful. Your father has spoken to Chiron personally. And Grover will be there to meet you — he's very excited to finally meet you."

Grover. The name sent a jolt through Percy's system. In his original timeline, Grover had been his best friend for years. His protector. His brother in everything but blood. This Grover — the one who existed in this altered life — had never met him. Wouldn't know him. Would see a strange, sheltered prince from the sea instead of the partner-in-mayhem Percy remembered.

But at least Grover existed. At least that was one thing the change hadn't taken away.

"I should get dressed," Percy said, shifting his weight against the cold.

Amphitrite kissed his forehead. "Breakfast in twenty minutes. Your father wants to see you before you go."

She left. Percy sat on the edge of the bed for another thirty seconds, breathing carefully, and then went to find clothes.

---

The palace of Atlantis was as breathtaking as Percy remembered from his original life. He'd walked these halls for years — past columns of living coral, through gardens of anemone that swayed in currents created by minor sea gods, under ceilings of crystal that fractured light into prismatic patterns. He knew which corridors had loose flagstones, which merfolk guards would wave him through without question, which wing held the training arenas where Triton had taught him to fight.

It was all familiar. It was all alien.

Poseidon's private study was in the eastern tower, carved from a single massive chunk of obsidian that had been dragged up from a volcanic vent. The door was open — Poseidon rarely closed it, because the ocean itself would announce any visitor — and Percy found his father standing at the great window that looked out into the blue-black depths.

Poseidon was in his usual form for this timeline: a broad-shouldered man with sea-green eyes and hair the color of foam, dressed in robes that might have been white once but seemed perpetually damp. He turned when Percy entered, and his smile was the same warm, slightly perplexed expression he always wore when looking at his youngest son.

"Come here, boy."

Percy crossed the room. Poseidon engulfed him in a hug that smelled like salt and deep water, cold iron and kelp, warm from sun on the surface, and Percy — who had not been hugged by Poseidon in the original timeline in years, not since the wars, not since he'd learned to stand on his own — had to bite the inside of his cheek to keep his face steady.

"I've been thinking," Poseidon said, releasing him but keeping a hand on his shoulder. "About this camp business."

"Dad, we've talked about it—"

"I know. I know." Poseidon rubbed the back of his neck. "It's just — Amphitrite worries. I worry. You've never been to the surface. Never been around mortals your own age. If anything happened to you—"

"Nothing's going to happen to me," Percy said, and the words came out more confidently than he felt, because in his original timeline, he had faced down gods and Titans and the literal underworld, and the idea of Camp Half-Blood being *dangerous* was almost funny.

Almost.

Poseidon studied him. "You've changed," he said quietly. "These past few weeks. You seem older. More serious. I can't put my finger on it."

Percy's heart hammered. "I'm just... excited about the trip. Nervous. You know."

"Hmm." Poseidon didn't look convinced. "Well. The arrangements are made. Chiron expects you in a week. Grover will meet you at the port. And Percy—" He gripped his son's shoulder tighter. "You are a prince of Atlantis. Never forget that. The surface world will try to make you smaller. Don't let it."

"I won't," Percy said, and meant it with every fiber of his seventeen-year-old soul.

Poseidon studied him for another moment, and Percy had the uncomfortable feeling that his father was looking for something — some sign that his son was still a child, still the boy who used to chase minotaurs through the palace corridors and ask when he could visit the surface. He didn't find it. Percy was not that boy anymore. He hadn't been for years.

"Your mother and I will miss you," Poseidon said finally. "We'll talk every day, yes?"

"Every day."

"And if you need anything — anything at all — you'll tell us."

"I will."

"Good boy." Poseidon kissed his forehead, and Percy felt the warmth of divine affection, and the guilt of not deserving it settled into his bones.
Poseidon walked him to the door of the study, and then stopped. Percy had never seen his father hesitate before — not about anything. It was such a strange expression on that broad, weathered face that it took Percy a moment to understand what he was seeing.

"Percy." His father's voice was careful, controlled. "I need to tell you something. Amphitrite doesn't think I should."

Percy waited.

"Before you were born," Poseidon said slowly, "I made a promise. To the old gods. The ones who lived here before the Titans fell. And they came to me with a request. They wanted to know where they'd hidden their sacred places — the places where they'd performed rituals that bound the elements to their will."

Percy's brow furrowed. "Why?"

"I don't know the full reason. Something about preserving power. Something about keeping the old magic alive until the new gods could replace it." Poseidon's jaw tightened. "I told them I'd tell no one. Not even you. And I haven't."

"What are you saying?"

"I'm saying that there are things in the world, Percy — things beneath the sea, things that predate the Olympians — that even your mother and I don't fully understand. And I need you to be careful on the surface. Not because the surface is dangerous."

"Then why?"

Poseidon met his eyes. "Because I've felt something lately. A disturbance. Something wrong in the deep places where the old gods used to dwell. And I can't ignore it."

"You haven't told Amphitrite?"

"I haven't told anyone." He gripped Percy's shoulder. "That's a secret I've carried for a thousand years. But I'm telling you now because you're going to the surface, and I need you to listen to the things the sea tells you. Even when they sound like warnings about things that shouldn't exist."

Percy nodded slowly. He didn't understand what his father was telling him. But he understood the press of it — the fear beneath the words, the way Poseidon's hand on his shoulder trembled slightly.

"I'll listen," Percy said, shifting his weight against the cold.

"Good boy." Poseidon pulled him into a hug one more time, and this time Percy let himself enjoy it — let himself feel the warmth and the strength and the love that his father had spent twelve years making sure he knew about.

The walk to his chambers was quiet. Amphitrite waited outside his door, concern written in every line of her face.

"Is he all right?"

"He's fine," Poseidon said. "He'll be fine."

And then they were gone, leaving Percy alone in the corridor with the weight of his father's secret pressing down on him and the knowledge that he was about to face something he couldn't prepare for.

---

The week passed in a blur of goodbyes and preparations and the particular anxiety of a boy who knew too much pretending to know too little.

Percy said farewell to Triton, who ruffled his hair and told him not to let any satyrs give him trouble. "You know how they get," Triton said, grinning. "Six of them surrounding a kid and calling it a lesson. Don't fall for it."

"I've faced worse," Percy said, shifting his weight against the cold.

Triton's grin faded. "Have you?" he asked, and his voice had changed — lower, more serious. "Percy, you're still young. Whatever you think you've faced, whatever you think you know—"

"I know what I know," Percy said, cutting him off. It came out harder than he intended. Triton looked at him for a long moment, then nodded and walked away.

Salt air sharp in his lungs, Percy spent extra time with Amphitrite, memorizing her face in case — in case of what? He didn't know. In case this was temporary. In case something went wrong. In case the timeline changed again. She taught him to play a game called *Tessera*, a strategy game played with coral pieces on a board of shifting currents, and by the third day he was beating her. She pretended to be upset. He knew she was proud.

He packed bags he wouldn't need, practiced being twelve years old, and tried not to think about Annabeth with a desperation that bordered on panic.

Where was she? Was she at camp? Was she even on land? Had she woken up in this altered world the same way he had, disoriented and displaced and wondering where he was? Or was she somewhere else entirely, in some other corner of this rewritten history, equally alone?

He couldn't know. He couldn't reach her. He could only go to camp and hope.

---

The night before his departure, Percy couldn't sleep. He sat on the balcony of his chambers, watching the bioluminescence drift past — tiny creatures of light that moved in patterns only they understood. Triton found him there.

"Can't sleep either?"

"Too excited."

"Liar." Triton sat down beside him, dangling his legs over the edge. The water was close enough to touch, warm from the volcanic vents, sulfur-soft, humming faintly against the skin. "You're scared."

"I'm not scared. I'm—"

"You're scared," Triton repeated. "And you don't want your parents to know, because they'd never let you go. Am I wrong?"

Percy didn't answer, which was answer enough.

Triton was quiet for a breath. Then he said, "I went to the surface once. When I was young — younger than you. Father took me to see the sky."

"What was it like?"

"Terrible. Loud. Smelly. Too bright." He paused. "But there were these creatures — birds, they called them — and one of them landed on my shoulder. Just landed there, like it belonged. And it sang."

"Sang?"

"Like the ocean, but different. Air instead of water. It was the most beautiful thing I'd ever heard."

Percy smiled despite himself. "Sounds like you need a friend up there."

"Maybe." Triton stood and stretched. "Bring back a girl for me. Or a boy. Or a really good sandwich. Something interesting."

"I'll see what I can find."

As his brother walked away, Percy turned back to the water. The bioluminescence drifted past, and he thought about what Triton had said. About how the surface was terrible and beautiful at the same time. About how sometimes the thing that scared you most was the thing you needed.

---

That evening, Percy walked the palace corridors alone. The bioluminescence was brighter tonight — some kind of festival, or seasonal change, or just the ocean's capricious mood. The light drifted past him in ribbons of blue and green, painting the walls in shifting color.

He could imagine missing this. That was the strange, painful truth of it: the palace's quiet had begun to settle around him, and people here treated him as someone who belonged. In his original life, camp had been home, and he'd fought and bled and nearly died for it. But this version of him — this version of Percy who had grown up here, who had never known the surface world or mortal life or the grinding, beautiful chaos of being a demigod — had a claim on this place too.

A pair of smaller children — maybe six, maybe seven — swam past him, chasing each other through the corridors. They were the children of minor sea spirits, and they seemed to expect him to join a game whose rules he did not know. Now they looked at him with the particular shyness of kids who had heard he was leaving and didn't know how to feel about it.

"Are you really going?" one of them asked.

"Yeah. Tomorrow."

"Will you come back?"

Percy smiled. "I promise."

They raced off, and he watched them go, and something in his chest ached in a way that had nothing to do with missing Sally and everything to do with the strangeness of loving a place that he'd discovered he'd lose.

He went to his chambers and lay on his bed, staring at the ceiling. The vaulted stone with its naval battles. The mother-of-pearl that caught impossible light. The triremes his hand kept reaching toward, though he could not recall the child who had learned their outlines.

With the water cool against his face, he thought about Annabeth. He pictured finding her at camp and seeing recognition in her eyes. If she remembered too, they could compare what had happened.

If. He had not found her yet.

The hope was enough to keep him moving through the preparations, though it proved nothing about who had brought him back or what they wanted.

But who? And why?

Percy lay down and listened to the ocean through the long night. Sleep came in scraps. By morning, he had stopped shaking.

---

Amphitrite found him later, in the garden of anemones where the current was warmest. She was reading — she always was reading, some scroll or text in a language Percy didn't recognize — but she closed it when she saw him and set it aside.

"Trouble sleeping?" she asked.

"No. Just thinking."

She patted the coral bench beside her, and he sat. The water was slightly warmer here, heated by vents far below, and Percy could feel it against his skin like a gentle hand.

"You were thinking about Sally," she said.

He looked at her sharply. She said it without accusation or curiosity — just fact, the way she'd say the water was warm or the current was slow.

"How did you—"

"You kept looking at her picture in your father's study." Amphitrite's eyes — deep blue, older than they looked — met his. "Sally was your mother, Percy. You can ask about her. Loving her does not hurt me."

Percy's chest tightened. "I remember her raising me. An apartment in Manhattan. School. Montauk in the summer. Years of it. I know how strange that sounds."

Amphitrite stayed quiet for a moment. "That isn't the childhood I know. But I believe you remember it."

"But in this timeline, Poseidon took me here. And you raised me."

"Not I alone. Poseidon was there — more than you might think. He plays with you, Percy. He teaches you. He's your father in every way that matters." She paused. "But you're right. It wasn't always me."

Percy looked at his hands. "What happened to Sally? In this timeline?"

"She was mortal. She and Poseidon had a brief affair — a season, maybe less. She was a remarkable woman, Percy. Brave. Stubborn. The kind of person who punched a god when he behaved badly and didn't apologize for it." Amphitrite smiled, and the expression was complicated — fond and sad and something else. "She knew what she was getting into. She knew Poseidon couldn't stay, that the Olympian world was dangerous, that any child of theirs would be a target. And she chose him anyway."

"What happened?"

"She lived by the sea for a while. Your father keeps the pictures she took there. She raised you for your first two years, and then she became ill and died." Amphitrite watched his face. "You were so young. We have talked about it before, but you can ask again whenever you need."

Percy's throat was tight. "She was alive when I went to sleep. In the life I remember."

Amphitrite's hand found his — cool, gentle. "I don't know how those memories came to you. I can only tell you what happened here. She died when you were two. Your father brought you home to us."

He wanted to argue. Sally had protected him every day without making it sound heroic. The promise of getting home to her had helped keep him moving through wars and through Tartarus. He had never had to carry her death like this.

But Amphitrite was telling him a loss everyone else had lived with for ten years. For him it had happened in the space between waking and this conversation.

"So she just... died," Percy said quietly.

"She lived," Amphitrite corrected gently. "For two years, she lived, and she loved you, and then she rested. And Poseidon brought you here, and I raised you, and we became your family. Both families are real, Percy. Both are true. You don't have to choose."

He wanted to argue with that. He wanted to say that you couldn't have two mothers, that grief didn't work that way, that the ache in his chest for Sally was real and didn't care what timeline he was in.

But Amphitrite was looking at him with such understanding, such patience, and he was twelve years old and tired and so desperately lonely in a world that looked almost right but wasn't.

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"Anything."

"Did you know I would wake up remembering something different?"

Amphitrite was quiet for a heartbeat. "No. I knew you as the child I raised. I did not know about these memories until you told me."

"Then how—"

"I don't know." She reached up and brushed a strand of wet hair from his forehead. "I don't know how any of this works. But I know you're my son. And I know that whatever brought you here, whatever you're supposed to do, you won't be doing it alone."

Percy leaned into her touch. It wasn't Sally. It wasn't the same. But it was love, real and present, and after a moment he let himself accept it.

"Thank you," he said.

"Thank me when you come home." She smiled, and it was warm and real. "Now go. Breakfast is waiting, and your father will worry."

She shooed him off with a gentle current, and Percy swam back toward the palace feeling, for the first time since he woke up, something that almost resembled hope.

Not much. Not enough.

But something.

---

The morning of his departure, Poseidon and Amphitrite accompanied him to the surface in a chariot pulled by hippocamps, the sea horses' manes streaming like foam. The water lightened around them, shifting from black to deep blue to cerulean to the blazing turquoise of the shallows, and then they broke through into sunlight so bright Percy winced.

The surface. He'd forgotten how *loud* it was. The sun on his face, the wind in his hair, the salt spray — everything sharper, more immediate than anything in the depths.

The chariot carved through the waves, and the water grew lighter as they rose. Percy could see things down there that he'd never seen before — structures at depths that should have been crushingly dark, schools of fish that moved like liquid crystal, a whale so large it looked like an island unto itself.

"The surface animals are different," Poseidon said, following his son's gaze. "Smaller, mostly. Faster. But none of them have teeth."

"None of them are terrifying either."

"No. But the surface has its own terrors." Poseidon's expression darkened. "Monsters, mostly. The kind that can't survive underwater. And humans — humans who don't believe in gods and therefore can't see the divine. You'll need to be careful, Percy. Careful and clever."

"I know how to fight."

"Not the way you need to up there." Poseidon leaned forward, gripping the chariot's rail. "Underwater, you're strong. Powerful. The sea answers to you. On the surface, you're weakened. No ocean to call on. No currents to ride. Just you and whatever power you can muster."

Percy thought about his original life. About how hard he'd fought on the surface — how every battle had taken everything he had, because he never had the advantage of the water. It had made him sharper, faster, stronger in ways that had nothing to do with divine power.

"I'll be okay," he said.

"I know." Poseidon sat back. "You've always been more adaptable than you realize. That's what makes you dangerous, Percy — not your power. Your ability to grow into whatever the moment requires."

Amphitrite turned to him. "Are you coming with us? To the surface?"

Poseidon hesitated. "I've to be at court. The Olympians are... restless. Something is happening on Olympus that I need to address."

"You'll stay away," Amphitrite said. It wasn't a question.

"For now." He looked at Percy. "But I'll be watching. The sea always watches, Percy. Wherever you're on the surface, I'll know if you're in danger."

Percy nodded. He didn't tell his father that he could feel it too — this thread of connection that ran from his chest to the ocean, humming with awareness. He didn't tell him that he could sense the water in the clouds, in the rain, in the blood of everyone around him.

He kept those things to himself. They were weapons, and he wasn't ready to show his hand.

The chariot continued to rise. The light grew brighter. And Percy Jackson, son of Poseidon, prince of Atlantis, boy of the sea, took a breath of salt air and prepared to meet the world above.

"Close your eyes briefly," Amphitrite said. "Let your eyes adjust."

Percy closed them. The light shifted from blinding to merely bright, and when he opened them again, he could see.

The sky. Blue and endless and streaked with clouds that moved too fast. The sun — a disc of white fire that he had only ever seen as a pale reflection on the ocean's surface. The wind — not water, not air, something between, pressing against his skin with a force he could feel in his teeth.

"It's beautiful," he said, and meant it.

They emerged off the coast of Long Island Sound. In the distance, Percy could see the shape of an island — not his original Camp Half-Blood, but a different configuration of hills and trees and buildings. The camp existed here too, but it was *different*, altered the same way everything else had been altered.

His stomach clenched.

"You'll call every day," Amphitrite said, her voice tight.

"I will."

"And if anything — *anything* — feels wrong—"

"I'll come straight back. I promise."

She pulled him into a fierce embrace, and Percy held on a moment longer than his twelve-year-old self would have. When she released him, her eyes were suspiciously bright, and Poseidon had to look away.

"Go on, then," the sea god said gruffly. "Before your mother changes her mind."

Percy stepped onto the dock. The wood was sun-warmed and slightly rough under his bare feet, heat giving way to cold sea-spray on the wind — Atlanteans didn't wear shoes in the palace — and the sensation was so foreign that he almost laughed. He turned back once. His parents were still there, watching, their figures framed against the sparkling sea. He raised his hand. Poseidon raised his back.

Then Percy walked up the beach toward the tree line, and the forest swallowed him.

---

The satyr was waiting at the base of Half-Blood Hill.

Percy saw him before Grover saw him — a slight figure in a tattered hoodie, goat legs hidden beneath the fabric, curly hair sticking out in every direction. He was pacing nervously, muttering to himself, and the sight of him hit Percy like a physical blow.

Grover. His Grover. His best friend.

Except this Grover was younger than the one Percy remembered, too. And he was looking for a stranger.

"Uh — excuse me?" Grover said as Percy approached. "Are you — are you Percy?"

Percy's throat was too tight. He nodded.

"Oh! Oh, good. I was worried I'd — I mean, you're right on time, that's — hi." Grover stuck out his hand, then thought better of it and withdrew it, fidgeting. "I'm Grover Underwood. Your dad asked me to escort you to camp. Are you — is this your first time on land?"

"Yeah," Percy said, and the lie tasted strange. "First time."

Grover's expression softened with immediate sympathy. "It's a lot, right? Don't worry, I felt the same way my first time. Well, not the same way, exactly, I mean I grew up in New York, but — anyway. Come on. It's not far."

They walked together up the hill. Percy had made this journey before — in his original timeline, Grover had been the first friend he'd made, the one who'd protected him from monsters, who'd believed in him when no one else did. The dynamic was backward now: Grover was the guide, the protector, the one who knew the way.

It was disorienting. Percy kept wanting to fall into step beside his best friend and make jokes about Mythomagic and complain about his ADHD, but this Grover didn't know him. This Grover was being careful and polite and slightly awkward, the way you were with someone you'd only met.

"So," Grover said, glancing sideways at him. "Your dad said you grew up in Atlantis? That must be... different."

"It is."

"Do you miss it? The ocean?"

Percy thought about the blue-black depths, the coral halls, the drag of water against his skin. He thought about Poseidon's hand on his shoulder and Amphitrite's hug and the way the palace looked in the morning light.

He thought about Sally's apartment. The smell of blue cookies. The small kitchen table where they'd eaten breakfast together, only the two of them, before the world went to hell.

"Yeah," he said quietly. "I miss it."

They walked through the camp slowly, and Percy catalogued everything he saw. The cabins were arranged in a rough semicircle around the central clearing — familiar, but the order was wrong. In his original timeline, the order followed the Twelve Olympians in a specific sequence, with the three big three cabins at the center. Here, the arrangement looked almost random, as though someone had shuffled the deck and dealt a new hand.

He counted again. Poseidon. Zeus. Athena. Ares. Apollo. Artemis. Demeter. Hephaestus. Aphrodite. Hera. Dionysus. Hades. Twelve cabins, but the positions were different. The position of the Poseidon cabin was farther from the center than it should have been, almost at the edge, and the Zeus cabin was prominently placed near the front.

Someone had made a statement with the layout. Percy didn't know what statement, but he was sure it meant something.

"You've gone quiet," Grover said, shifting his weight against the cold. "Everything okay?"

"Yeah. Just taking it all in."

"Cool. I mean, it's less crazy than I expected, right? Big cabins, training grounds, the whole thing. When I was here for my first time, I threw up. Twice."

"You threw up?"

"Right after my mom died. My real mom, I mean. I'd been living with my cousin and my stepdad and things were — well. Chiron took me in, and I was so overwhelmed that I just... threw up. Everywhere. It was gross."

Percy smiled despite himself. That sounded exactly like something his Grover would have done.

"I'm glad you're here," Grover said quietly. "Honestly. The camp needs more kids who aren't — you know. Insular."

"Insular?"

"Some of the big three kids are kind of..." Grover searched for the word. "Entitled? Like, they've grown up knowing who their parents are, which is a big deal at camp. Most of us had to figure it out. We had to earn our place here. But the big three kids just... showed up. And they get treated differently."

"I'm not like that."

"I didn't say you were. I said some of them." Grover kicked a pebble down the path. "You'll be fine. You're not like the others. You can tell."

Percy wasn't sure what "the others" meant, but he filed it away. Another piece of the puzzle. Another difference between this timeline and his own.

They walked on, and the camp grew larger around them, and Percy tried to memorize every detail while pretending he wasn't terrified.

The path down from the Athena cabin was winding, shaded by old oaks that had been there longer than the camp itself. Percy walked beside Grover, matching his pace, trying to look like someone who belonged.

"Can I ask you something?" Grover said suddenly.

"Sure."

"The way you looked at her — at Annabeth — it was like you already knew her. But you said you just got here." Grover's expression was puzzled, not suspicious. Not yet. "Is there something I'm missing?"

Percy's heart rate spiked. "What do you mean?"

"Like, when you saw her, you didn't just react. You — I don't know how to describe it. You looked like you'd been waiting for her. Or like you'd found something you'd lost."

Percy was careful. "Sometimes you meet someone and there's just a... recognition. Like, something clicks. That's all."

"Yeah, maybe." Grover didn't sound convinced, but he let it go. "It's just weird. You're kind of weird, Percy. I'm not saying that's bad. You're just — different. From anyone I've met."

"Most of my childhood was underwater. I'll take different."

"Yeah, fair." Grover kicked a pebble. "Hey, what do you think of camp so far? Like, the cabins, the training stuff — it different from what you're used to?"

Percy thought about the original camp. The simpler layout. The absence of the Hall of the Gods and the Tempest Hall. The different power structure.

"It's incredible," he said honestly. "It's more than I expected."

"Good. Because you're gonna need it. The big three kids — there's a lot of pressure on you. The gods are watching, the monsters are watching, everyone's waiting to see if we're worth the trouble." Grover shrugged. "It gets easier once you stop caring what they think."

"Is that advice or a threat?"

"Both." He grinned. "Come on. Let's get you settled in. You've got a whole life to learn how to live."

Percy followed him down the path, and the camp opened up around them, and he thought about Grover's words. About the pressure. About the waiting. About the gods watching and the monsters watching and the whole impossible weight of being a child of the Big Three in a timeline that had already been rewritten once.

He had a whole life to work out.

And apparently, that life had already started.

He was terrified. He was terrified and he was alone and he was twelve years old in a body that didn't quite feel like his, and somewhere in this world, Annabeth was waiting for him.

And that was enough. That had to be enough.

They crested the hill, and Camp Half-Blood spread out below them.

Percy had seen the camp before. He'd seen it in its original configuration — the cabins arranged by godly parent, the dining pavilion, the climbing wall that doubled as a lava fight, the strawberry fields that stretched toward the border. This version was similar but not identical. The cabins were arranged differently — he counted, quickly, and realized the lineup had changed. And there was a structure he didn't recognize, a sort of open-air temple with columns of white marble, positioned near the center of camp.

"What's that?" he asked, pointing.

Grover followed his gaze. "Oh! That's the Hall of the Gods. They built it a few years ago — it's for divine visitations. Lord Zeus comes sometimes. It's — well, it's mostly for the big three kids. There's not many of them, you know. You're the first new Poseidon camper in a while. Your sisters are already here."

The big three. Percy's stomach flipped. If the Hall of the Gods was for the big three, and this timeline had rearranged the cabins —

"Grover," he said carefully. "What cabins are there? Like, for the major gods."

Grover ticked them off on his fingers. "Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Demeter, Ares, Athena, Apollo, Artemis, Hephaestus, Aphrodite, Dionysus, and — oh, and Hades. Thalia's cabin is the Zeus one. She's been here the longest."

Thalia. Percy's heart rate spiked. In his original timeline, Thalia had become a pine tree at age twelve, then been resurrected at fifteen and joined the Hunters of Artemis. She'd never stayed at camp. But in this timeline —

"Thalia's a camper?" he asked, trying to keep his voice casual.

"Yeah! She's amazing. Best fighter at camp, probably. She's got a younger sister in the same cabin — Annabeth. Cute kid. Bit of a handful, from what I hear."

Annabeth.

Percy stopped walking. The name was a detonation in his chest, a pulse of heat that started in his core and radiated outward until his fingertips tingled.

*Annabeth.*

She was here. She was at camp. She was alive and twelve years old and a Zeus kid, and Percy — who had been trying so hard to hold himself together, to be twelve years old, to be the sheltered prince from Atlantis — felt his composure crack down the middle.

"Whoa," Grover said, turning. "You okay? You look kind of pale."

"I'm fine," Percy said, shifting his weight against the cold. "I just — can I meet her? Annabeth?"

"I mean, sure, I guess? She's probably using the Athena cabin's library table again. She lives with Thalia in Cabin One. I can introduce you after you check in with Chiron—"

"No," Percy said, and his voice came out sharper than he intended. "Now. I want to meet her now."

Grover blinked. "Okay. Sure. She's probably in the cabin — come on."

They walked down the hill together, and Percy's heart hammered against his ribs like something caged. Every step brought him closer to her — to Annabeth, to the girl he'd loved for years, to the person who might be the only other person in this altered world who remembered the life they'd shared.

The Athena cabin was a low, gray building with owl carvings above the door. Grover knocked — and then thought better of it, because the door opened before his knuckles connected.

A girl stood in the doorway.

Percy's breath stopped.

She was small. Twelve years old, just like him, with storm-gray eyes and blonde hair pulled back in a practical ponytail. She was wearing camp beads and an orange t-shirt, and she was glaring at Grover with an expression of profound irritation.

"What," she said flatly.

"Hi, Annabeth," Grover said nervously. "This is Percy. He's new. He's a Poseidon kid. I thought maybe you could show him around—"

"I'm busy," Annabeth said, and pulled the door shut.

"Annabeth."

Percy's voice. Raw and desperate and not twelve years old at all.

The door stopped. Annabeth's head turned. Her gray eyes met his.

And something happened — a flicker, a spark, something that passed between them like an electric current. Her expression shifted from irritation to confusion to something achingly close to recognition. Percy wanted to trust it, but he didn't yet know whether she remembered their life together.

The way she was looking at him — like she was searching for something in his face, like she'd been waiting for someone who looked exactly like him — made his breath catch.

"Percy," she repeated, testing the name. "That's... that's a sea name."

"Yeah," he said. "Yeah, it is."

They stared at each other. Grover looked between them, clearly baffled. The air around them felt charged, pressurized, like the moment before a storm.

"Can I talk to you?" Percy asked. "Alone?"

Annabeth hesitated. Then, slowly, she nodded.

She stepped out of the cabin and closed the door behind her. Grover looked like he wanted to protest, but Percy's expression must have been enough to silence him, because the satyr simply backed away, muttering something about finding Chiron.

The two of them stood in front of the Athena cabin — the daughter of wisdom who wasn't, the son of Poseidon who remembered a different girl entirely — and Percy felt the whole world narrow to the space between them.

"You know me," Annabeth said, shifting her weight against the cold. It wasn't a question.

"Yes," Percy said, shifting his weight against the cold. "I know you."

"How?"

"Because I've known you for years. Because we survived Tartarus together. Because we were going to go to New Rome University together. Because I went to sleep next to you three nights ago and you were seventeen years old and the daughter of Athena and I was trying to get three recommendation letters from the gods so I could go to college."

Annabeth's face went white. The color drained from her cheeks like water from a cracked glass, and for one held breath Percy thought she might faint.

"You—" she whispered. "You remember too."

"Remember what?"

"Everything. The wars. The prophecies. The — oh my gods." Her hand flew to her mouth. "You're real. You're actually real. I thought I was — I thought maybe I'd lost my mind."

"You haven't," Percy said, shifting his weight against the cold. "I promise. I remember all of it. I remember you."

Annabeth's eyes filled with tears. She blinked them back fiercely, the way she always did — the way she had in Tartarus, in the war, in every moment when vulnerability was a luxury she couldn't afford.

"I woke up in Cabin One," she said rapidly. "In this body. With this name. And everyone keeps telling me I'm a daughter of Zeus, and I've a sister I don't remember, and a father who — I don't remember any of it, Percy. I don't remember being her. But I remember being *me*. And the me I remember doesn't have a sister or a divine father or — or any of this."

"I know," Percy said, shifting his weight against the cold. "I know. I woke up in Atlantis. I've parents here — Poseidon and Amphitrite — and I remember Sally. I remember growing up with Sally. I remember being you — " He caught himself. "I remember being *us*. And now we're here and everything's different."

Annabeth wiped her eyes with the back of her hand. "How did this happen?"

"I don't know."

"Who did this?"

"I don't know."

"Are there others? Do you think anyone else—"

"I don't know." Percy ran a hand through his hair. "I don't know anything except that you're here and I'm here and we remember."

Annabeth nodded shakily. Then, with a deliberateness that told him she was holding herself together by force of will, she straightened her spine and squared her shoulders. The gesture was so quintessentially Annabeth — the daughter of wisdom who had commanded armies and faced down gods and never, ever backed down — that Percy's chest ached.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. We need to be careful. If anyone finds out—"

"No one's going to find out."

"Percy, my sister is *very* observant. And she's already noticed that I've been acting strange."

"Thalia?"

"Yes. She's — look, it's complicated. In this timeline, she raised me. She's not my friend; she's my *sister*, and she has all these memories of our childhood together that I don't have. She keeps mentioning things I'm supposed to remember and I can't — I can't pretend anymore. It's exhausting. And now you show up and you know me — you *actually* know me — and I—"

She broke off. Percy wanted to reach for her, to pull her into his arms, but they were standing outside the Athena cabin and anyone could see and they couldn't afford to explain this yet.

"We'll work through it," he said, shifting his weight against the cold. "We always do."

Annabeth let out a shaky laugh. "We always do. Gods, I missed you."

"I missed you too."

They stood there for half a breath, looking at each other, and the weight of everything they'd lost and everything they still had pressed down on them like the ocean itself.

Then a voice rang out across the camp.

"Annabeth!"

Percy turned. A girl was striding toward them from the direction of the dining pavilion — tall for sixteen, with wild black hair and electric blue eyes and an expression of fierce protectiveness that Percy recognized instantly.

Thalia.

In his remembered life, Thalia had been turned into a tree at twelve, returned at fifteen, and joined the Hunters. She'd been a friend, an ally, a sister-in-arms. She'd never been Annabeth's guardian. She'd never raised her.

But in this timeline, Thalia had stayed. She'd grown up at camp. She'd become Annabeth's sister in truth.

And she was looking at Percy like he was a threat.

"Annabeth," Thalia repeated, reaching them. Her eyes swept over Percy with unmistakable suspicion. "Who's this?"

"This is Percy," Annabeth said, and her voice was carefully neutral. "He's new. Poseidon's son."

Thalia's eyes narrowed. "I know who he is. I asked who he is to *you*."

Percy felt Annabeth stiffening beside him. Thalia's tone was protective, bordering on hostile, and the dynamic was so different from anything in his original timeline that it took him a moment to adjust.

"We just met," Percy said, holding out his hand. "I'm looking forward to getting to know everyone."

Thalia ignored his hand. She was still looking at Annabeth, searching her sister's face with an intensity that spoke of years of practiced observation — the kind of attention that came from someone who'd spent a childhood learning to read moods and predict reactions and keep a smaller, more vulnerable person safe.

"Fine," Thalia said, the word carrying a weight of skepticism. "But dinner's in an hour. Don't be late." She turned and walked away, but not before shooting Percy one last look over her shoulder — a clear message: *I'm watching you.*

Percy watched her go. Beside him, Annabeth let out a breath.

"She's going to be a problem," Annabeth murmured.

"I know," Percy said, shifting his weight against the cold.

"She thinks you're — I don't know what she thinks. But she doesn't trust you."

"She doesn't trust anyone," Annabeth said, shifting his weight against the cold. "That's kind of her thing."

Percy looked at her — studied at her. The daughter of Zeus. The sister of Thalia Grace. The girl who should have been Athena's daughter, who should have run away from home at seven and met Percy for the first time at twelve and fallen in love with him over years of shared danger and ordinary moments.

That girl didn't exist in this timeline. This Annabeth had grown up differently, loved differently, been shaped by different forces. But she was still Annabeth. Still brilliant, still stubborn, still the person Percy had chosen to spend his life with.

"We'll piece it together," he said again, because it was the only thing he knew for certain.

They stood there for another long moment, the afternoon light catching the planes of Annabeth's face, and Percy catalogued the differences he could see. Her hair was lighter than he remembered — not the dark blonde he'd known at seventeen, but almost gold. Her eyes were the same gray, but the shape of them was different, the bones underneath slightly altered. She was shorter, too — she would grow, he knew, but in this moment she was small, and young, and afraid.

He was terrified that someone would come around the corner and see them standing there together, two kids having an intense conversation outside the Athena cabin. He was terrified that Thalia would appear and start asking questions. He was terrified that he'd slip, that he'd say something that couldn't be explained, that the whole careful facade would come crashing down.

But he was more terrified of not having this moment. Of not having her.

"I've been going crazy," Annabeth admitted. "For weeks. I thought — I thought maybe I was losing my mind. Like, all these memories that nobody else had. A whole life that just... vanished. And I was the only one who remembered it."

"What about Thalia? Your sister?"

"She's—" Annabeth's expression flickered. "She's wonderful. And she loves me. And I don't — I can't—"

"She what?"

"I can't love her the way she needs me to." The words came out in a rush, like she'd been holding them back for too long. "Percy, she's been my sister for twelve years. She raised me. She taught me to fight. She sleeps in the same cabin as me and knows when I'm having a nightmare before I wake up. And I don't remember any of it. I remember her as someone I met later, someone I befriended, but this Thalia — this version — she's..."

"She's your sister," Percy finished quietly.

"She's a stranger who knows me better than I know myself." Annabeth's voice cracked. "How am I supposed to — how does any of this work?"

Percy reached out and took her hand. He did it without thinking, the way he'd done a thousand times in another life, and the contact sent a jolt through them both — not electricity, but something simpler and more profound. Recognition.

"You'll work it through," he said, breath fogging faintly in the pine-sharp air. "And in the meantime, I'm going to be here. Whatever you need. Whatever she needs. I'll help you talk to her if you want help, or I'll keep her away if you need space. Whatever you need."

Annabeth looked at their joined hands. Her expression was unreadable. Then, slowly, she turned her hand over so their fingers interlaced. It was a small gesture, but it meant everything.

"I'm glad you're here," she said again.

"So am I."

They stood there, hands clasped, two children who weren't children holding onto the only solid thing in an uncertain world.

Annabeth looked at him, and for one bare heartbeat, the mask slipped. She was twelve years old and displaced and grieving a life she couldn't prove had existed, and she was so tired of pretending.

"Yeah," she said softly. "We will."

They walked together toward the dining pavilion, side by side, and for the first time since Percy had woken up in Atlantis, the world felt almost bearable.

Dinner at Camp Half-Blood was chaotic in a way Percy remembered fondly. Demigods from every cabin crowded the long tables, laughing and arguing and forking down food that smelled like home in a way that the Atlantean palace never quite had. The dining pavilion was open-air, the ceiling carved with constellations that shifted slowly overhead, and the bonfire in the center of the pavilion cast everything in warm, smoke-sweet light that crackled against the cold night, flickering light.

Percy sat at the Poseidon table with two places set beside his own. The girls assigned to them were still carrying their offerings to the fire; he had not been introduced yet. He tried not to stare at Annabeth. She was three tables away, sitting between Thalia and another girl Percy didn't recognize, and every time Thalia leaned in to say something to her, Percy's jaw tightened.

He knew Thalia wasn't a threat. In his timeline, Thalia was brave and loyal and one of the best people he knew. But in this timeline, she was Annabeth's sister, and she had history with Annabeth that Percy didn't, and the territorial way she watched him made it clear that she considered Annabeth hers to protect.

Percy understood the impulse. He just didn't know how to navigate it.

"You're the new kid, right?"

Percy looked up. A girl had materialized beside him — maybe fourteen, with close-cropped dark hair and the build of someone who spent a lot of time lifting things. She was smiling broadly.

"Percy Jackson, yeah."

"Clarisse. Poseidon cabin." She said it like a warning. "You're Poseidon's kid, huh? That's cool. Not many of us around anymore."

"Us?"

"Big three kids. There's me, there's the Zeus sisters, and now you. We're kind of special." She grinned, and it wasn't entirely friendly. "Try not to drown in the canoe lake, newbie."

She sauntered away. Percy exhaled slowly.

Big three kids. Zeus sisters. That meant Annabeth and Thalia. And himself. The cabins had been rearranged to accommodate them — the Hall of the Gods had been built for them. In this timeline, the children of the big three weren't rare anomalies; they were fixtures.

And the changes went deeper than cabin assignments. Percy could feel it — in the way people looked at him, in the way the camp was laid out, in the way the power structures had shifted. Someone had rewritten the rules of his world, and he didn't know who or why or what they wanted.

After dinner, Percy found Annabeth by the canoe lake. She was sitting on the dock with her feet in the water, cold lapping sharp around their ankles while sun lingered warm on their shoulders, staring out at the dark surface, and the moonlight turned her hair silver.

Percy sat down beside her. Their shoulders almost touched.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey."

They sat in silence for a while. It was the first quiet moment they'd had since arriving at camp, and Percy felt the exhaustion of the day settling into his bones.

"I can't stop thinking about it," Annabeth said finally. "About the life we remember. About — about my mom. In this timeline, she's alive. Beryl Grace. She's living in New York and she sends letters sometimes and Thalia reads them to me because I — because this me — doesn't know how to feel about her. And I don't know either. I remember Athena. I remember being Athena's daughter and running away from home and meeting you and — and Thalia keeps telling me about a mother who drank too much and couldn't take care of her children. I don't have those memories. I don't know how to answer her letters."

"The life we remember was real," Percy said, shifting his weight against the cold. "And the history everyone here knows is real to them. Someone changed what came before us."

"But they don't remember it changing."

"No. Just us."

Annabeth pulled her knees to her chest. "It's lonely when everyone expects a story you can't tell."

Percy thought about Sally. About the blue cookies and the small apartment and the way she'd smiled when she was scared. About the fact that in this timeline, she'd been dead for ten years and no one remembered her the way he did.

"I know," he said quietly. "I remember Sally. And here, she's been gone since I was two. No one tells stories about her. No one knows she was brave. And I — I've to just carry that. All by myself."

Annabeth looked at him. In the moonlight, her eyes were luminous. "You don't have to carry it by yourself anymore."

Percy's throat tightened. "Neither do you."

They sat there together, the son of Poseidon and the daughter of Zeus, two displaced souls in a world that wasn't quite theirs, and for the first time since they'd arrived in this altered timeline, the loneliness felt survivable.

"We should go back," Annabeth said eventually. "Thalia will worry."

"Yeah. She already hates me."

"She doesn't hate you. She's just protective."

"She looked at me like I was a monster."

"You *are* a kind of monster. You're a Poseidon kid." Annabeth's mouth twitched. "We're all monsters here. It's kind of the point."

Percy smiled. It was the first real smile he'd managed since waking up in Atlantis, and it felt like cracking open a door.

They walked back to the cabins together. At the fork in the path — left toward the Poseidon cabin, right toward the Zeus cabin — they stopped.

"Tomorrow," Annabeth said, shifting her weight against the cold. "We'll piece together what to do. Who did this. Why."

"Tomorrow," Percy agreed.

She turned to go, then stopped. "Percy?"

"Yeah?"

"I'm glad you're here. I'm glad I'm not the only one."

"Me too."

She smiled — small and tired and real — and then disappeared into the Zeus cabin. Percy stood there for a single heartbeat, watching the door close behind her, and then turned toward his own cabin.

That night, Percy lay in his bunk in the Poseidon cabin and listened to the unfamiliar sounds of the camp through the open window. Distant laughter. The clang of practice weapons. Someone singing badly. The rustle of wind through pine trees.

In his original timeline, he'd had his own cabin — the same one, actually, but it had been empty when he arrived. Just him, the salt smell, and the loneliness of being the only Poseidon camper until Tyson came.

Not anymore. The cabin had four bunks now: his, two clearly used beds, and a spare for visitors. Before dinner, the girl with close-cropped dark hair and a warrior's posture had introduced herself as Clarisse and informed him that wet towels belonged on hooks. Across from her, a quilt lay folded beside a notebook with blue ink on its cover. His other sister's bed. He would meet her properly in the morning.

Poseidon's daughter. His half-sister, technically, though they'd never met in this timeline.

Percy thought about the web of family he hadn't asked for. Amphitrite and Poseidon. Triton, who'd teased him mercilessly but loved him anyway. And now Clarisse, who shared his father but nothing else.

It was going to take time to learn who they all were. But for the first time since he woke up in Atlantis, Percy didn't feel entirely alone.

He closed his eyes and let the sounds of the camp wash over him, and gradually his breathing slowed, and exhaustion pulled him under.

Tomorrow, he'd start figuring out how to save the world.

Tonight, he slept.

Somewhere in the distance, thunder rumbled, low through the soles of their feet, ozone-sharp on the tongue — not a warning, but a reminder. The storm was coming, in one way or another. And when it did, Percy Jackson would be ready.

He had to be. He and Annabeth were the only ones who remembered.

Tomorrow. They had tomorrow. And the day after that. And all the days it would take to piece together what had happened to their world and who was responsible.

They'd faced worse odds before.

But this time, they were twelve years old, and they were alone, and they didn't know who they were fighting.

Percy looked up at the stars. They were the same stars he'd seen from the deck of the *Princess Andromeda*, from the hills of Camp Jupiter, from the window of the apartment he'd shared with Annabeth. Constant. Unchanged. The only constants in a world that had been rewritten around him.

"We'll figure it out," he whispered to the night. "We always do."

And then he went inside, and the darkness swallowed him, and the stars watched over Camp Half-Blood like they always had — silent and ancient and offering no answers, only witness.
