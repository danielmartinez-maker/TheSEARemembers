## Chapter 98: Fathers' Day (Gods)

The trouble started on a Friday morning with two letters arriving at the Big House at exactly the same time.

Percy Jackson knew because he was there, half-asleep on the porch steps with Annabeth Grace leaning against his shoulder, both of them sun-warmed and sticky from morning sword drills. Chiron had trotted out with a package under one arm and two shimmering envelopes in his hand, his expression already doing that complicated centaur thing where his eyebrows tried to leave his face.

One envelope smelled like sea salt and low tide and barbecue smoke. The other crackled faintly when Chiron moved it, like distant static.

Annabeth sat up straight. Percy sat up straighter.

Chiron held them out. "From Olympus," he said carefully. "For both of you."

Percy took the sea-smelling one. Annabeth took the crackling one. They tore them open at the same time, and then they stared at each other, and then they read aloud at the same time.

"'My son —'" Percy started.

"'My daughter —'" Annabeth started.

They stopped. The porch went quiet except for the distant clink of swords from the arena and Argus humming somewhere by the strawberry fields.

"'I will visit this weekend for the Fathers' Day barbecue,'" Percy read, his voice getting slower with every word. "'Proud to stand beside all five of my brave children at camp. Bring your appetite. — Dad.'"

Annabeth looked down at hers. "'My daughter — I will visit this weekend for the Fathers' Day barbecue. I would like to speak with you and your sister. I have remembered the important dates. — Zeus.'"

There was a long pause.

Thalia Grace chose that exact moment to vault over the porch railing, land in a crouch, static sparking off her black hair, and say, "Tell me I'm hallucinating and Dad did not just send a mass text via divine mail."

Annabeth wordlessly handed her the letter. Thalia read it twice. Her electric-blue eyes widened.

"He remembered the important dates," Thalia said flatly. "That is either character growth or the end of the world."

Percy was still staring at his own letter. All five of my brave children at camp. He felt his stomach do something complicated and warm. Percy and his sisters would be there with Tyson on forge leave and Triton visiting from Atlantis. To put all five of them in one letter for one weekend felt different. Intentional. Like Dad was trying to say something without having to actually say it, which was very Poseidon.

"Wait," Annabeth said. "Both of them? The same weekend?"

Chiron cleared his throat in the way that meant the gods had not consulted him at all. "Yes. I have received confirmation from Olympus. Poseidon will arrive Saturday morning. Zeus will arrive Saturday morning. They have agreed — and I use that term loosely — to a joint barbecue."

Percy groaned and let his head fall back against the porch post. "That's not a barbecue. That's a natural disaster with hamburgers."

Thalia flopped down beside Annabeth, shoulder to shoulder, the two daughters of Zeus sitting in eerie identical slouches. "Joint barbecue," she repeated. "Last time those two were at a joint anything, the Atlantic developed a new trench."

"It'll be fine," Annabeth said, in the voice she used when she was already making a three-column plan in her head. "We just need a seating chart. And a weather contingency. And possibly an evacuation route."

Percy looked at her fondly. "Babe. Your dad is the weather contingency."

Annabeth shoved his shoulder, but she was smiling. It was nervous, though. Underneath the planning, he could see it. Zeus had never been the show-up-with-a-card kind of dad. Thalia's stories made it clear that Zeus's place in their childhood had often been a blank space with lightning around the edges. Annabeth couldn't remember that childhood herself. But there were two Zeus girls at Camp Half-Blood now, and Zeus had started trying in these stiff, awkward, thousand-year-old-king-of-the-gods ways that made Annabeth want to scream and cry at the same time.

Percy laced his fingers through hers. "Hey. He remembered the dates. That's something."

"Yeah," she said quietly. "I just hope he remembers us."

---

By Saturday morning, Camp Half-Blood had transformed into a high-alert potluck war zone.

The Ares cabin had built three extra grills out of old shields. The Demeter cabin had produced enough corn, peppers, and watermelon to feed a small army of gods, which was good because they were about to. The Apollo cabin had hung a hand-painted banner across the dining pavilion that read HAPPY FATHERS DAY!!! with the S backwards until Will Solace fixed it under death threat from Clarisse.

Chiron had assigned jobs with military precision. Percy, Clarisse, Silena, Annabeth, Thalia, Triton, and Tyson were on Grill Duty, which everyone understood to mean God-Wrangling Duty.

Triton arrived first, emerging from the lake at dawn like he owned the concept of mornings, which as Poseidon's eldest and messenger, he kind of did. He wore mortal board shorts and a linen shirt unbuttoned over his scaled shoulders, his sea-green hair tied back, his trident collapsed into a tasteful gold bracelet so as not to intimidate the mortals. He took one look at Percy sitting on a cooler eating cereal out of the box and said, "Little brother. You look like you slept in a fishing net."

"I did sleep," Percy said. "On the beach after I left Cabin One because she and Thalia stayed up making a Dad Conversation Flowchart."

Triton raised an eyebrow. "A flowchart?"

"With color coding," Percy said solemnly. "Thalia's in black. Annabeth's in gray. Red means abort and pretend the grill is on fire."

"Prudent," Triton said. He sat beside Percy and stole a handful of cereal. "Father is nervous. He spent all morning in Atlantis rearranging his trident collection. He polished the big one. He never polishes the big one."

Percy stared. "Dad gets nervous?"

"About all five of you? Constantly," Triton said simply. "He wants today to go well. Especially with Zeus present."

As if summoned by the name, the sky gave a low, pretentious rumble. Everyone at camp looked up.

Thalia and Annabeth came down the hill from Cabin One — Zeus's cabin — walking side by side. They had clearly coordinated without coordinating: both in jeans and dark tank tops, both with their hair pulled back, Thalia's spiked bracelet glinting, Annabeth's Yankees cap tucked in her back pocket. They looked like sisters even before you knew. Blue eyes beside gray. The same stubborn set to both their chins.

Tyson lumbered behind them carrying an entire folding table under one arm like it weighed nothing, beaming. "I MADE POTATO SALAD!" he announced proudly to the entire camp. "With extra pickles! And I did not smash the potatoes too much this time!"

"You did great, big guy," Percy called, jumping up. He hugged Tyson hard around the middle, and Tyson lifted him half off the ground.

Triton stood and, to Percy's surprise, clapped Tyson warmly on the shoulder. "Brother. I saved you the good seat near Father. You can tell him about the forges."

Tyson's single eye went wide and shiny. "Really? Dad wants to hear about forges?"

"He asks about you every council," Triton said. And Percy knew it was true because Triton's voice did that older-brother thing where it went soft around the edges.

That's when the air changed.

First came the sea smell — salt and rain and kelp and sunscreen. A wave rose up off Long Island Sound without breaking, curling impossibly high, and Poseidon stepped out of it onto the beach as if stepping off a porch. He wore khaki shorts, flip-flops, and a Hawaiian shirt covered in tiny tridents, his beard neatly trimmed, his sun-bleached hair tied back, a pair of mortal sunglasses perched on his head. He carried a six-pack of nectar sodas and a paper bag that said ATLANTIS FARMERS MARKET.

Half a second later, the sky cracked open with a polite thunderclap — polite for Zeus — and a bolt of lightning struck the top of Half-Blood Hill with surgical precision, leaving behind a tall man with a face neither daughter had seen before, in a crisp white polo and pressed khaki pants, his gray-streaked beard trimmed square, his blue eyes sharp behind aviator sunglasses. He held a store-bought pie in a plastic container and looked deeply uncomfortable holding it.

The entire camp froze.

Poseidon spread his arms wide and bellowed, "PERCY! CLARISSE! SILENA! TYSON! TRITON! MY CHILDREN!"

Zeus nodded stiffly at his daughters from twenty feet away and said, "Thalia. Annabeth. I brought... pie."

Annabeth later swore she could hear Clarisse whisper, "Oh, this is going to be amazing."

---

The joint barbecue pit had been set up in the center of the green: two massive grills side by side, a long picnic table, coolers, and a hand-lettered sign from the Hephaestus cabin that read GODS GRILL HERE (MORTALS DO NOT TOUCH VALVES).

Poseidon claimed the left grill immediately. Zeus claimed the right grill immediately. They stood shoulder to shoulder without looking at each other, tongs in hand, like two rival dads at a Little League cookout which, Percy supposed, was exactly what they were.

"Brother," Poseidon said expansively, clapping Zeus on the shoulder hard enough to make the sky flicker. "Good to see you out of the throne room! Sun suits you! You should get out more!"

"Poseidon," Zeus said, with the air of a man who had rehearsed this. "I trust the sea is... wet."

"Wetter than ever! You should come swim! Clears the sinuses!"

"I prefer the sky."

"Yes, yes, dry and full of judgment," Poseidon said cheerfully, flipping a burger with divine precision. "Just like old times!"

Chiron gave Percy a look that clearly said intervene before your father starts a war over charcoal.

Percy and Triton lunged forward. "Dad! Let us help!" Percy said, grabbing the spatula. "You sit—"

"Nonsense! I am grilling for my five brave children!" Poseidon declared. He pointed the tongs at each of them in turn. "Triton! My heir, my herald, who held the Atlantic passes alone against Oceanus's vanguard last winter! Tyson! My strong, kind boy, whose hands built the shields that saved Olympus! And Percy! My hero, my tide-turner, who keeps standing between his family and danger and still shows up for Saturday pancakes!"

Percy's ears went red. "Dad—"

"No, let me finish, I rehearsed this with Amphitrite," Poseidon said, and his voice actually wobbled. "People think I am proud because you are powerful. I am proud because you are good. Triton could have let pride make him cold, and instead he teaches young merfolk to read tides. Tyson could have let cruelty make him cruel, and instead he makes potato salad with extra pickles." He gestured grandly at the bowl Tyson was clutching. "And Percy—" He paused, eyes bright and impossibly old and impossibly young. "Percy chose to be mortal and kind in a world that kept giving him reasons not to be. What father would not brag about that?"

Tyson was crying openly. "DAD!"

Triton, who had faced down sea monsters without blinking, was blinking very fast. "Father..."

Percy felt his throat close up. "Dad, come here," he muttered, and hugged his dad around the ribs, Hawaiian shirt and all. Poseidon waved Clarisse and Silena over from the bun table. "And my daughters! Clarisse, who keeps her siblings standing when the tide gets rough. Silena, who teaches them how to heal as well as fight. I am proud of you too." Clarisse folded her arms, red-eared; Silena went straight into the hug. Poseidon gathered all five close, and for a moment the barbecue smoke smelled like home.

From the corner of his eye, Percy saw Annabeth watching with her hands clasped tight in front of her, smiling so hard it had to hurt. Thalia had her arms crossed, jaw tight.

Zeus had gone very quiet beside them, still holding his pie, watching Poseidon with something unreadable on his face. Not anger. Something closer to envy, or shame.

Annabeth stepped forward. "Hi, Dad," she said. Her voice was steady. Planner voice. "Thanks for coming. And for the pie."

Zeus looked down at the pie as if surprised to find it still in his hands. "It is apple. The... cashier said apple is reliable. Are you... do you like apple?"

"I love apple," Annabeth said. And Percy knew she was thinking of the apple orchard behind her childhood house in Virginia in the old life, the one she used to map with string and stakes. It was not the childhood Zeus knew, but he had guessed right.

"I am glad," Zeus said gravely. He turned to Thalia. "Thalia. Your... spear work. In the last war games. Chiron sent a report. You broke La Rue's shield wall with a throw from forty yards. In the rain."

Thalia blinked. "You read the war games report?"

"I read all the reports," Zeus said, a little defensively. Then, softer: "Yours twice. Your mother... she pretended not to be impressed when I sent her the report, then asked for another copy to show people. I thought you should know someone noticed."

Thalia's tough expression cracked. "Forty-two yards," she muttered. "But who's counting."

"I counted," Zeus said.

There was a beat. Annabeth glanced at Percy, eyes shining. Percy gave her a tiny thumbs-up behind his dad's back.

Zeus cleared his throat and turned back to Annabeth. "And your map," he said. "The defense map of the camp's western approach. Chiron forwarded it. With the... the overlapping fields of fire and the evacuation lanes through the woods."

Annabeth's breath caught. That map had taken her three weeks. She had redrawn it eleven times. "You saw that?"

"I had Athena review it," Zeus admitted. "She said — and I quote, though it pained her — 'The girl thinks like a general. Tell her to reinforce the creek bed.' I told her you already had, in appendix C."

Annabeth stared. "You read appendix C?"

"I read all of it," Zeus said. "Twice."

Tyson, still tearful from Poseidon's speech, walked over and offered Zeus a deviled egg. "Do you want one? I made them with paprika!"

Zeus stared at the cyclops, then at Poseidon's proud grin, then took the egg. "Thank you... Tyson. Your father speaks of your forge work often."

Tyson glowed. "HE DOES?"

"Every solstice," Poseidon said, winking. "Don't let him fool you, he brags about the potato salad too."

The tension cracked. People laughed. Zeus even almost smiled, which for Zeus was basically a belly laugh.

"All right!" Percy announced loudly, sensing an opening. "Burgers! Dad, you're on patties. Sir — uh, Zeus, sir — you want to do hot dogs? Lightning dogs?"

"Lightning dogs," Zeus repeated slowly.

"It'll be great," Thalia said quickly. "You just... hold them and... no. No zapping the food, Dad. Health code."

"I am the king of the gods," Zeus said. "I do not answer to the health code."

"You do at Camp Half-Blood," Annabeth said, and handed him the tongs. Their fingers brushed, and for a second Zeus just looked at her hand in his — small, scarred, capable — and his expression softened into something very mortal and very lost.

"Very well," he said. "Show me."

---

The barbecue, against all prophecies, settled into a rhythm.

Poseidon told fishing stories that got taller with every burger flip. By the third flip the fish had been the size of a minivan. By the fifth it had been the size of a minivan with teeth and a grudge. Tyson listened with rapt attention and asked if the fish was nice, and Poseidon said solemnly that it was not nice at all, which was why he had let it go, because Tyson had taught him that being big did not mean being mean.

Zeus attempted small talk with a group of terrified Apollo kids about archery ("Your grouping at fifty yards is... adequate. Keep your elbow higher.") and they looked like they might faint from joy. Will Solace actually did sit down very suddenly on the grass and had to be handed a juice box by Nico, who told Zeus with complete deadpan sincerity that his aim was also adequate, which made Zeus stare and then, to everyone's shock, laugh — a short, startled thundercrack of a laugh.

The real comedy began when both gods insisted on actually cooking.

Poseidon treated charcoal like a personal insult. He crouched in front of the left grill, Hawaiian shirt riding up, sunglasses sliding down his nose, and poked at the briquettes with the tongs while muttering, "In my day we cooked over volcanic vents. This is... these are tiny black rocks. Where is the fire?"

"Dad, you have to wait for them to heat up," Percy said, kneeling beside him with Triton on his other side. Tyson hovered behind them holding a tray of raw patties like an offering.

"I am the god of the sea," Poseidon said. "I do not wait. I command." He waved a hand. A tiny spout of seawater arced onto the coals. They hissed and went out.

The Hephaestus kids collectively gasped. Beckendorf's younger brother Jake snatched the lighter fluid away. "Sir! Sir, with respect, please stop helping the grill!"

Triton smoothly intervened, the way only an eldest sibling who had managed divine embarrassment for millennia could. "Father. Allow me to demonstrate mortal technique. You observe and provide moral support." He relit the coals with a practiced flick of a long match, fanning them with a paper plate while explaining, "You see? Patience. Like waiting for tides."

Poseidon watched, chin in his hands, utterly absorbed. "You learned patience," he said proudly, loud enough for the whole green to hear. "My boy learned patience! When he was young he once tried to hurry a whirlpool and flooded two villages! Now look! Patience!"

Triton closed his eyes briefly. "Thank you, Father, for sharing that."

Percy choked. Tyson whispered, "Were the villages okay?" Triton said, "They got new boats," which did not really answer the question.

On the right grill, Zeus was having the opposite problem. He had no trouble with fire. He had too much fire.

"Watch," Zeus told Annabeth and Thalia with grave king-of-the-gods confidence. He held up a hot dog on the tongs. A tiny spark jumped from his fingertip. The hot dog instantly turned black and smoking. He frowned at it. "Hmm. Too much?"

"Dad," Thalia said. "You vaporized it."

"It is... well-done," Zeus said.

"It is a charcoal stick," Annabeth said gently. She stepped in close and adjusted his grip on the tongs, the way she had once taught rookie campers to hold a sword. "Like this. Lower. Slower. You don't have to smite it. Just... warm it."

Zeus looked down at her hands guiding his. He went very still. "Annabeth," he said quietly, so only she and Thalia could hear. "You included a map of an orchard with your camp plans. With escape routes. You said it came from the place you remember in Virginia, the childhood I cannot share. Chiron sent it to Olympus with the other pages."

Annabeth froze. "You kept that?"

"I kept it," Zeus said. He looked embarrassed. "Athena laughed at me. She said I was sentimental. I told her kings are allowed archives." He turned the hot dog carefully. "You had marked the apple tree as a safe zone. With a star. I remembered that when I bought the pie."

Thalia stared. "Dad. That is the most embarrassing and sweetest thing you have ever said."

"I contain multitudes," Zeus said stiffly, but his ears were red. He held up the hot dog. "Is this... is this correct now? Slightly golden?"

Annabeth inspected it like a general inspecting troops. "Perfect. Slightly golden. Excellent work."

Zeus glowed. Actually glowed, faint static lifting the hair on his arms. "Excellent," he repeated. "Your sister said forty-two yards. I said forty. I will update the record."

Percy, watching from the other grill while pretending to arrange buns, caught Annabeth's eye and mouthed, He kept the map. Annabeth mouthed back, I know, and her eyes were suspiciously bright.

Then came the Great Sauce Incident, which campers would talk about for years.

Poseidon produced a bottle of homemade marinade from his farmers market bag — seaweed, lemon, garlic, and something blue that glowed faintly. "Family recipe!" he announced. "Amphitrite's grandmother's! Good on everything!"

Zeus produced a small glass jar of golden, crackling sauce. "Ambrosia glaze," he countered. "From my private stores. Good on everything."

They looked at each other. The air crackled. The sea breeze picked up.

"Joint barbecue," Poseidon said slowly, a competitive gleam in his eye that Percy recognized with horror from every capture-the-flag game ever. "Taste test?"

"Blind taste test," Zeus agreed, eyes narrowing. "My daughters will judge fairly. Your children will judge fairly."

Chiron buried his face in his hands. Clarisse started chanting, "TASTE TEST! TASTE TEST!" until the whole camp joined in.

What followed was twenty minutes of pure chaos. Tyson volunteered as first taster and ate four burgers back to back and declared both sauces "YUMMY AND SPICY AND TINGLY" and then hiccuped lightning and seafoam at the same time, which sent the Demeter kids scrambling. Triton tasted both with the gravitas of a wine critic and said, "Father's has depth and citrus. Zeus's has... voltage. I declare a tie, for the sake of continued peace." Thalia ate both and said, "Poseidon's is better on burgers, Dad's is better on chicken, please don't kill me." Annabeth, ever the diplomat, made a chart on a napkin rating flavor, texture, after-effects, and likelihood of causing divine war, and declared a tie with detailed footnotes.

Poseidon roared with laughter and clapped Zeus on the back. "A tie! Diplomacy! She gets that from you, brother — always negotiating!"

Zeus, holding his sauce jar like a trophy anyway, said, "She gets thoroughness from Athena's review, but I will accept partial credit." He glanced at Annabeth's napkin chart and folded it carefully into his polo pocket. "I am keeping this. For the archives."

Annabeth watched him pocket a mustard-stained napkin like it was a treaty. Her throat felt tight.

Percy found himself on drink duty with Annabeth, sneaking glances at their dads while they restocked the coolers.

"He's trying," Annabeth murmured, watching Zeus awkwardly accept a paper plate from Katie Gardner. "Like, really trying. He remembered the spear throw. He read my map."

"He brought pie," Percy said.

"He brought pie," Annabeth agreed, like that explained everything, which it did.

"He's nervous," Percy said. "Triton said Dad was nervous too. I think they're both just... dads. Today."

Annabeth leaned her head against his arm for a second. "Your dad's speech was good."

"He made Tyson cry," Percy said proudly. "That's how you know it worked."

Across the green, as if on cue, Triton and Thalia had ended up side by side at the condiment table, both reaching for the mustard at the same time.

"After you, sky princess," Triton said with an elegant half-bow.

"After you, fish prince," Thalia said, with an equally mocking curtsy.

They stood there squirting mustard onto their burgers in silence for a moment, united by the universal embarrassment of having divine fathers who loved them loudly in public.

"So," Thalia said. "Your dad polished his big trident for today."

"Your dad brought a pie and held it like a shield for ten minutes," Triton countered.

"Fair." Thalia took a huge bite, chewed, swallowed. "Is yours always like that? The big public proud-dad thing?"

"Always," Triton said. "When I was eighty — which is, admittedly, quite young for us — I lost a race against a school of dolphins and cried for a week. He told the entire court I had 'shown great emotional range and impressive lung capacity.'"

Thalia snorted so hard mustard came out of her nose. "That's nothing. When I was twelve, Dad sent me a thunderhead for my birthday with a card that said 'BE BRAVE.' I lived on the streets. Where was I supposed to park a thunderhead?"

Triton laughed, a bright, surprised sound. "When I was two hundred, he tried to give me 'the talk' using seahorses as visual aids. Amphitrite had to intervene."

"Oh, I win," Thalia said immediately. "When I turned fifteen, Zeus sent a bald eagle to follow me for a month to 'watch over me.' I was running from monsters. A giant eagle is not subtle. I named him Kevin. He ate my fries."

"I concede," Triton said, clutching his chest. "Kevin defeats seahorses."

They grinned at each other, sudden allies.

"They mean well," Triton said, quieter, watching Poseidon show Tyson how to flip a burger without smashing it, one big hand guiding Tyson's. "They are ancient and powerful and they have no idea how to be small enough to fit in our lives. So they bring tridents and pies and hope it translates."

Thalia followed his gaze to where Zeus was listening intently while Annabeth explained something, nodding along, asking a question that made Annabeth light up. "Yeah," she said huskily. "Yeah, they do."

She bumped his shoulder with hers. "Your little brothers are good kids, by the way. Percy's an idiot, but he's our idiot."

"Ours?" Triton teased.

"Camp's," Thalia corrected quickly, but she was smiling. "You did good with them. The older brother thing. I see you checking Percy's plate to make sure he eats vegetables."

"I see you braiding Annabeth's hair before every battle," Triton shot back. "Overprotective sisters club?"

"Overprotective everything club," Thalia said. "Gods help anyone who messes with my sister. Dad included."

Triton raised his soda. "To overprotective siblings and overbearing fathers."

Thalia clinked it. "I'll drink to that."

They stood there a moment longer, watching their fathers with the same critical, fond expression. It was Triton who broke first.

"Can I ask you something, as one eldest to another?" he said. "Does it ever... does the worry stop? Percy sails into a storm and my hands itch for my trident. Tyson picks up a hammer and I count his fingers afterward. I am three thousand years old and I still check that Percy ate vegetables."

Thalia laughed, but it was soft. "No. It never stops. Annabeth sneezes and I am ready to declare war on pollen. When she was little — before either of us knew you — she would stay up all night building models of Olympus out of toothpicks and I would sit up with her because I was scared if I slept something would take her. Old habit." She shrugged, her spiked bracelet glinting. "Overprotective is just love with armor on."

Triton nodded slowly. "Love with armor on. I like that. Father says I hover. He says I learned it from him, which is rich coming from the god who once stopped a hurricane with his bare hands because I had a cold."

"He stopped a hurricane for a cold?" Thalia said. "Okay, that beats my dad sending Kevin. Barely."

"He did," Triton said, smiling into his soda. "And Zeus? He sent you a thunderhead and an eagle. Different element. Same instinct. They hover in the only way they know how — big, loud, weather-sized."

Thalia was quiet for a beat, turning her burger in her hands. "You think they'll ever figure out how to do it small? Like... just show up and not make it a whole thing?"

"I think today is them trying," Triton said. He gestured with his chin toward Poseidon, who was now wearing a paper crown Tyson had made him out of napkins and wearing it like the crown of Atlantis. Toward Zeus, who was letting a tiny Demeter kid show him how to properly put cheese on a burger without it sliding off. "Pie. Napkin charts. Polished tridents. This is their version of small."

Thalia watched Zeus nod seriously at the cheese advice. Her voice went rough. "It's not terrible. For old weather gods."

"No," Triton agreed. "It's not terrible at all." He hesitated, then added, "For what it is worth, your sister brags about you constantly. Annabeth told Percy — who told me, because Percy tells me everything when he is tired — that you taught her how to throw a spear by saying, quote, stop thinking like an architect and throw like you're mad at the sky."

Thalia barked a laugh, eyes wet. "She was overthinking the arc vectors."

"And Percy brags about you too," Triton said. "He said you stood between Annabeth and a manticore once and told it to pick on someone your own size. He tells that story to every new camper."

Thalia blinked fast and looked away toward the Sound. "Damn sea family. Going to make me cry at a barbecue."

"We specialize in that," Triton said cheerfully, and offered her a napkin. She took it and shoved him, and he pretended to stagger into the condiment table, and she laughed for real this time, bright as lightning.

Across the green, Poseidon caught Triton's eye and gave him a small, proud nod — not for holding a pass or winning a battle, but for making a lonely sky girl laugh at a party. Triton nodded back, suddenly understanding his father a little better.

---

By late afternoon, the sun hung low over the Sound, turning the water to hammered gold. The grills were cooling. The watermelon was gone. Someone had started a lazy game of volleyball with a beach ball enchanted to scream every time it was spiked, and Tyson was winning by virtue of being twelve feet tall and delighted.

Percy and Annabeth had escaped — briefly — to the dock, shoes off, feet in the water, sharing a slice of Zeus's apple pie on a paper plate. It was, objectively, a little soggy from being held too long. Annabeth ate it like it was ambrosia.

"He remembered appendix C," she said for the third time, shaking her head. "Athena said I think like a general."

"You do think like a general," Percy said. "A hot general with a pie."

"Percy!"

"What? It's true. You're strategizing right now about how to get the last bite without me noticing."

She laughed and fed him the last bite instead, and he caught her fingers with his lips and made her blush, and for a moment it was just them, dock and water and pie.

Which was, of course, when the shadows fell over them.

Poseidon and Zeus stood at the end of the dock, side by side, both trying to look casual and failing spectacularly. Poseidon had his hands in his pockets. Zeus had his arms crossed, then uncrossed them when Poseidon elbowed him.

"Kids," Poseidon said cheerfully. "Mind if we join you?"

Percy nearly fell in the lake. "Dad!"

Annabeth sat up so fast she almost launched the paper plate. "Dad!"

The two gods sat — Poseidon with a groan, dangling his feet in the water, fish gathering instantly; Zeus stiffly, pants rolled up two precise cuffs, feet hovering just above the surface.

There was a long, awkward silence. The kind that only fathers can create.

Finally Poseidon said, "So. You two are... together."

Percy and Annabeth looked at each other. Percy felt his face go hot. Annabeth's ears went pink, which was how you knew she was really flustered, because Annabeth Grace did not blush, she strategized.

"Yes, sir," Percy said, looking at Zeus because Zeus was the scary one. "Yes, sirs. We are. For a while now. Through the change, and all this time here."

"I know," Poseidon said happily. "Triton keeps me updated with pictures. Very cute. The canoe lake sunset one? Adorable."

"DAD."

Zeus was quiet for a long moment, studying them. The weight of his gaze was physical, like pressure before a storm. Annabeth straightened under it, chin up, Thalia-like defiance flickering. Percy reached over and took her hand, lacing their fingers, and held it up where both dads could see it. No hiding.

Zeus looked at their joined hands. Then at Annabeth's face. Then at Percy.

"You," Zeus said to Percy. "Son of Poseidon. You have... you have looked after my daughter. Through the dangers you have faced here, and through the memories you carry together, as I understand it."

Percy swallowed. "Yes, sir. She looked after me more."

"I know," Zeus said. "I read the reports." He paused. "All of them. Twice."

Annabeth's grip tightened.

Zeus sighed, and for a moment he looked less like the King of the Gods and more like a tired dad in a polo shirt with sunburn on his nose. "I will be honest, as fathers must. When I first heard... I grumbled. Old habits. Sea and sky do not mix. Storms and tides. You understand."

Poseidon snorted. "You grumbled for three hours and threw a lightning bolt into the sea. I felt it."

"I grumbled," Zeus allowed. "But." He looked at Annabeth, and his voice softened. "My daughter chooses well. She maps the terrain before she walks it. She read appendix C before she lived it. If she has chosen you —" he nodded at Percy "— then I will respect it. I will... attempt to understand it."

Percy blinked. "Sir?"

Poseidon beamed and threw an arm around Zeus's shoulders. "He approves! In Zeus language, that was a wedding toast!"

"I did not say approve," Zeus snapped, shrugging him off, but there was no real heat. "I said respect. There is a difference. A large, sky-sized difference."

Annabeth was staring at her father with bright eyes. "Thanks, Dad," she whispered.

Zeus looked away, ears red. "Eat your pie," he muttered. "And... Percy Jackson. If you hurt her, I will smite you in ways that will be studied by future civilizations."

"Understood, sir," Percy said earnestly. "Triton and Thalia already have a list. And Tyson cries if I'm late for dinner."

"Good," Zeus said. "Good. Then we understand each other."

Poseidon leaned over and stage-whispered to Percy and Annabeth, "I approve completely, by the way. Fully. Loudly. I told Amphitrite you two would end up married with a lighthouse and three kids and a dog named Biscuit."

"DAD," Percy groaned, while Annabeth buried her face in her hands.

"What? It's a good name for a dog!"

Zeus shook his head, but Percy saw the corner of his mouth twitch. "A lighthouse is impractical. High maintenance. Erosion."

"See? He's already planning the wedding real estate," Poseidon said, delighted. "He loves you kids."

"I tolerate you all," Zeus corrected. But he stayed on the dock, feet almost touching the water, watching the sunset with them. When Annabeth leaned against Percy's shoulder, Zeus didn't frown. He just looked out at the horizon, where sea met sky without fighting for once.

After a while, Clarisse, Silena, Thalia, Triton, and Tyson wandered down the dock with leftover burgers and sodas, and the nine of them sat in a row, feet in the water — gods and demigods and one cyclops — eating and teasing and watching the light fade.

Poseidon told the story of how he and Zeus once tried to barbecue on Olympus and set a cloud on fire. Zeus corrected the details with increasing indignation until everyone was laughing, even Zeus. Triton stole Thalia's fries and she threatened to electrocute him and he said "Kevin would be disappointed in you" and she laughed so hard she almost fell in. Tyson fed pieces of bun to the fish and named each one. Annabeth rested her head on Percy's shoulder and Percy rested his head on hers, and neither father said a word against it.

Later, when the stars came out and the campfire was lit up the hill, Poseidon stood to leave, hugging each of his children in turn — long for Triton, lifting for Tyson, fierce and proud for Percy, careful with Clarisse, who let him, and warm for Silena.

"Take care of each other," he told them. "That's all I ask. And call your stepmother. She worries."

Zeus stood more awkwardly. He shook Thalia's hand, then thought better and hugged her, brief and hard. She hugged back just as hard, hiding her face for a second. Then he turned to Annabeth.

Annabeth stood, hands twisting. "Thanks for the pie, Dad. And for... reading everything."

Zeus nodded. He reached out, hesitated, then tucked a loose strand of hair behind her ear, the way mortal dads do. "Your map was excellent, Annabeth. Your spear throw was... Thalia, yours. Both of you. Excellent. I am... I am proud to be present for the Fathers' Day barbecue."

It was stilted. It was awkward. It was perfect.

"Next year," Thalia said gruffly, "bring cherry. And don't hold it like a shield."

"Noted," Zeus said solemnly. "Cherry. Held... casually."

He stepped back into lightning and was gone. Poseidon stepped back into wave and was gone. The dock felt suddenly empty and full at the same time.

Percy slung an arm around Annabeth's waist. Thalia slung an arm around her sister's shoulders from the other side. Triton, Tyson, Clarisse, and Silena closed the circle.

"Well," Percy said. "We survived joint barbecue."

"Without a new trench in the Atlantic," Annabeth said.

"Without anyone getting smote," Thalia added.

"Without smashing the potatoes too much," Tyson said happily.

Triton smiled at them all, his younger brothers and sisters and their two Grace friends. "Fathers' Day," he said. "Gods. What a concept."

And together, the seven of them walked up the hill toward the fire, where the rest of their strange, sprawling, divine family was waiting.
