## Chapter 98: Fathers' Day (Gods)

The trouble began on Friday morning, when two letters arrived at the Big House together.

Percy Jackson knew because he was there, half-asleep on the porch steps with Annabeth Grace leaning against his shoulder. They had finished morning sword drills and were still warm from them. Chiron came out with a parcel under one arm and two shimmering envelopes in his hand. His eyebrows had drawn together in the way that usually meant he had been given a problem without being asked first.

One envelope smelled like sea salt and low tide and barbecue smoke. The other crackled faintly when Chiron moved it, like distant static.

Annabeth sat up straight. Percy sat up straighter.

Chiron held them out. "From Olympus," he said carefully. "For both of you."

Percy took the sea-smelling one. Annabeth took the crackling one. They tore them open at the same time, and then they stared at each other, and then they read aloud at the same time.

"'My son —'" Percy started.

"'My daughter —'" Annabeth started.

They stopped. The porch went quiet except for the distant clink of swords from the arena and Argus humming somewhere by the strawberry fields.

"'I will visit this weekend for the Fathers' Day barbecue,'" Percy read, his voice getting slower with every word. "'Proud to stand beside all five of my brave children at camp. Bring your appetite. — Dad.'"

Annabeth looked down at hers. "'My daughter — I will be at the barbecue on Saturday. I would like to speak with you and your sister, if you both want that. — Zeus.'"

There was a long pause.

Thalia Grace came up the steps, keeping her wrapped right hand close to her side. She read the letters over Annabeth's shoulder. "Tell me I'm reading this wrong. Dad and Poseidon planned the same barbecue?"

Annabeth wordlessly handed her the letter. Thalia read it once, then looked back at the line asking whether they both wanted a conversation.

"If we both want it," Thalia said. She tapped Zeus's letter with her left index finger. "At least he remembered to ask. I want to know what he means by speak."

Percy was still staring at his own letter. All five of my brave children at camp. The words made him warm and wary at the same time. Clarisse and Silena would be there; Tyson had been approved for a weekend away from the forge, and Triton was traveling up from Atlantis. Poseidon had named all five of them. Percy wanted that to mean something. He knew it could also be a sentence written before anyone had to sit down together.

"Wait," Annabeth said. "Both of them? The same weekend?"

Chiron cleared his throat in the way that meant the gods had not consulted him at all. "Yes. I have received confirmation from Olympus. Poseidon will arrive Saturday morning. Zeus will arrive Saturday morning. They have agreed — and I use that term loosely — to a joint barbecue."

Percy let his head rest against the porch post. "That's not a barbecue. That's a weather problem with hamburgers."

Thalia sat beside Annabeth, shoulder to shoulder. "Joint barbecue," she repeated. "We'll keep them away from the waterline and the weather controls."

Annabeth glanced toward the Big House door. "We need a plan for the meal and a place to talk if anyone wants privacy. We don't need an evacuation route unless Chiron says the weather calls for one."

Percy looked at her fondly. "Babe. Your dad is the weather contingency."

Annabeth shoved his shoulder, but the smile faded quickly. Percy had heard Thalia's accounts of a childhood where Zeus's visits were brief and unpredictable. Annabeth did not remember that childhood herself. She remembered the visits she had attended, and the way a new face could make a familiar voice feel like a trick. This letter did not settle any of that. It meant only that he intended to arrive Saturday and had left her room to decline a conversation.

Percy laced his fingers through hers. "You don't have to decide now what the letter means."

"I want to see whether he asks about the life I'm living now," she said. "And whether he listens when I answer."

Thalia held the letter by its corners. "Do you want me there when you talk?"

"At the beginning, maybe. If I ask you to stay, stay. If I ask for a few minutes alone, don't turn that into a rescue."

Thalia nodded once. It was the sort of answer Annabeth had learned she could trust from her: not a promise to guess right, but a promise to wait for the actual words.

Percy folded his letter along its existing crease. He wanted to offer to manage both fathers, keep each conversation from turning sharp, and make sure nobody left disappointed. The urge arrived complete, a plan with himself in the middle of every path. He knew how that usually ended: everyone else got a say after he had already decided what they needed.

"What would make Saturday feel okay for you?" Annabeth asked him.

"I don't know yet," he said. "I want to see Dad. I don't want to be the person who makes everyone else get along."

"Then don't assign yourself that job," Thalia said. "If they start a fight, we can each decide what we want to do."

She did not say that nothing would happen. Percy appreciated that more than a promise that everything would be fine.

Chiron had waited beside the door while they spoke. Now he tipped his head toward the rec room. "Would it help to review the practical arrangements before the camp starts volunteering you for tasks?"

Annabeth picked up her field notebook. "Yes. But the map is still a public survey on trial. It isn't a defense plan, and I'm not giving either god a private copy."

"That is clear," Chiron said. "The map will remain where it is. We can use the correction card to check the route to the pavilion, and we will ask before changing any marked path."

The rec room smelled of paper, cedar polish and the lemon oil someone had used on the table. Annabeth's large survey stood on its wheeled easel where the campers had left it. The correction card hung beside it. One corner carried Ella's note about the missing Cabin Six window. The north trail was still marked closed; the dock's third board still flexed; neither warning had been resolved overnight.

Percy had helped test one route with Chiron the day before. That did not make the map a guarantee for every visitor or every chair. He watched Annabeth trace the route with one finger, then stop at the long path around the rut.

"For Chiron's chair, this was the route we tested yesterday," she said. "It worked once, in dry weather. We should keep the turn space clear, but we can't write that the whole camp is accessible because one route worked for one chair."

"Good," Chiron said. "We will use the open side of the green, and I will bring my chair through the route I tested. If the ground changes, I will take the paved path and tell you what failed."

Annabeth wrote that on the correction card rather than altering the map. "The trial is for catching what we missed. It isn't a promise that every mark is finished."

Percy looked again at the public page. It showed where people could find the kitchens, cabins and pavilion. It did not show the unlocated old-life path Annabeth had kept in her notebook. No line on this sheet explained the private dedication, either. The camp could see the map and use it without inheriting the reasons Percy and Annabeth had chosen one small phrase.

"We'll leave the clear lane beside the pavilion table," Chiron said. "And set the grills on the lawn, away from the dry grass. The Ares cabin can help with the equipment; the Hephaestus counselors will supervise the fuel. No one has to invent a route around an obstacle we can simply move."

Annabeth checked the dimensions and marked a tentative table position on a separate planning sheet. She wrote MEAL SETUP across the top so it could not be mistaken for a map revision.

"And if Zeus asks for the survey?" Percy asked.

"He can look at the same public copy everyone else can see," Annabeth said. "If he wants a copy after the trial, he can ask Chiron. I won't explain private notes that aren't on it."

Chiron looked at her, then at Percy. "That is a reasonable boundary. No one has submitted a request for the map."

They left the rec room with one planning sheet, a list of unresolved hazards, and no new assignment for Annabeth. It felt less impressive than a color-coded defense plan. It also sounded more like the camp they actually had.

Clarisse and Silena were waiting on the porch. Clarisse had the barbecue roster rolled into a tight tube; Silena carried a basket of clean cloths and a tin of chalk for the menu board. Their eyes went first to the letters, then to the planning sheet.

"Before anyone gives me a job because Poseidon is coming," Clarisse said, "I already have training in the morning. I'm not changing it to work the meal."

"You're not on meal duty," Percy said.

"Good. Keep it that way."

Silena set the basket on the porch rail. "I volunteered to help with food because I wanted to. That doesn't make me responsible for keeping the conversation sweet. If the gods get sharp with each other, I'm not going to sing them back into a good mood."

"Nobody asked you to," Clarisse said.

"People don't always ask before they decide that's my job." Silena's tone stayed light, but she looked directly at Percy. "You were about to make a schedule for everyone's feelings, weren't you?"

Percy put the planning sheet down. "Maybe."

"Then write down the grill shifts and let the rest of us choose our own conversations."

Annabeth glanced at the list of camp tasks. "We can put the meal on a schedule without putting the family on one."

Clarisse considered that. "I can help move the tables after my training. I'll decide then whether I want to talk to him."

"I'll bring the menu board," Silena said. "And I'll ask before I take over anyone's plate or start handing out food."

Percy had not realized how often he treated help as a role he could distribute. He wrote down the work they had actually chosen. Clarisse would check the table spacing after training. Silena would set up the menu board and cloths. Percy would ask before changing either plan. Annabeth would bring no private notebook pages to the public display.

Thalia drew the roster closer and crossed out her name beside "lift tables." She used her left hand; the wrapped right stayed tucked against her side. "I can put the small signs up. I'm not cleared to brace or carry the heavy table."

"Right," Percy said. "We can ask someone else."

"We can ask the crew whose job it is," she corrected. "You don't have to make it personal."

He added the task to the roster and left the name blank until Chiron could assign it. The empty space bothered him more than it should have. He let it stay empty.

Friday went on around them. Percy did his assigned work, then crossed out two things he had volunteered for without asking. Annabeth returned to the map's correction card and kept the private notebook closed. Thalia went to rest her hand. Clarisse went to training, and Silena took the menu board to the pavilion. Nobody spent the day rehearsing lines for their fathers. By dinner, the plan had fewer boxes than the one Percy had imagined and a better chance of belonging to the people who would use it.

---

By Saturday morning, Camp Half-Blood was busy with the ordinary work of putting on a meal for more people than the pavilion usually held.

The grills stood on the lawn, far from the pavilion's dry edge and the narrow paths between cabins. The Demeter table held corn, peppers and watermelon. An Apollo banner stretched between two posts; one letter had been painted backward, and Will was still trying to fix it without taking the banner down.

Chiron had posted the jobs on the pavilion board. Percy would check food temperatures with an Apollo counselor; Silena had the menu board and cloths; Clarisse would check table spacing after her training; Annabeth was free to leave for any conversation she chose; and Thalia had the small signs. A Hephaestus counselor supervised the fuel. Triton and Tyson had offered to help where they saw a need, but their names remained beside the tasks they chose, not as a pair assigned to manage their father.

After crossing from Atlantis through the sea, Triton came up from the Sound at dawn, his shirt still dark from the water. He had tied his sea-green hair back and folded his trident into a gold bracelet. He took one look at Percy sitting on a cooler with a bowl of cereal and said, "Little brother. You look like you slept in a fishing net."

"I slept," Percy said. "On the beach. I left Cabin One before Annabeth and Thalia finished talking."

Triton raised an eyebrow. "About what?"

"Mostly what they want from the visit," Percy said. "And what they don't want me deciding for them."

"That sounds sensible," Triton said. He sat beside Percy and stole a spoonful of cereal. "Father checked the meal list twice before I left. He asked whether Tyson would have enough space for the food he brought."

Percy looked at him. "He asked about Tyson?"

"He did," Triton said. "You too. He asked whether you'd want to help at the grill or be left to eat in peace. I told him you should get to choose when you arrive."

The sky rumbled once. Several campers looked up; Percy kept his eyes on Triton.

Thalia and Annabeth came down the hill from Cabin One walking side by side. Both had tied their hair back against the heat; Thalia's spiked bracelet caught the light, and Annabeth's Yankees cap stuck out of her back pocket. Their eyes were different colors. Their expressions, at that moment, were not.

Tyson followed with a covered bowl held in both hands. "I MADE POTATO SALAD!" he announced. "With extra pickles! And I did not smash the potatoes too much this time!"

"You did great, big guy," Percy called, jumping up. He hugged Tyson hard around the middle, and Tyson lifted him half off the ground.

Triton stood and greeted Tyson with a clasp of the forearm. "Brother. I left you room by the end of the table. You can sit wherever you like, though."

Tyson's single eye went wide. "Thank you. I want to tell him about the small bronze hinges. They stopped bending when I changed the angle."

"Then tell him that," Triton said. "He can ask you himself."

That's when the air changed.

First came the sea smell — salt and rain and kelp and sunscreen. A wave rose up off Long Island Sound without breaking, curling impossibly high, and Poseidon stepped out of it onto the beach as if stepping off a porch. He wore khaki shorts, flip-flops, and a Hawaiian shirt covered in tiny tridents, his beard neatly trimmed, his sun-bleached hair tied back, a pair of mortal sunglasses perched on his head. He carried a six-pack of nectar sodas and a paper bag of food.

Half a second later, the sky cracked open with a polite thunderclap — polite for Zeus — and a bolt of lightning struck the top of Half-Blood Hill with surgical precision, leaving behind a tall man with a face neither daughter had seen before, in a crisp white polo and pressed khaki pants, his gray-streaked beard trimmed square, his blue eyes sharp behind aviator sunglasses. He held a store-bought pie in a plastic container and looked deeply uncomfortable holding it.

Conversation paused across the green. The wave slid back into the Sound without reaching the path, and the thunder's echo traveled east over the cabins.

Poseidon set the bag and sodas on a dry patch of sand. "Percy. Clarisse. Silena. Tyson. Triton." He looked at each of them rather than calling them together as one crowd. "I'm glad you came."

Tyson hurried forward with his covered bowl. "I made potato salad. Extra pickles. I did not mash it too much."

"I can see that," Poseidon said, lifting the lid only after Tyson nodded. He took a spoonful, chewed, and looked properly serious while he decided. "The pickles are good. You kept the potatoes in pieces."

Tyson's shoulders rose. "I changed the angle of the spoon. It works better."

"Show me later," Poseidon said. "If you want to."

Triton greeted their father with the practiced clasp of two sea-born adults. Poseidon turned to Percy, arms partway open. Percy crossed the sand and hugged him. The shirt smelled of saltwater and clean cotton. He let the embrace last, then stepped away before it could become a public announcement about what it meant.

Poseidon looked toward Clarisse. She did not move from beside the table. He lowered his hands.

"Hi, Dad," she said. "You can talk to me without making a speech first."

"I can try," he answered. He did not reach for her again.

Silena approached at her own pace. "I brought the menu board. You can read it after Chiron checks the table route."

"I'd like that," Poseidon said. "And if you don't want to stand here with me, that's fine too."

"I'm deciding," she said, and went to show Tyson where to set his bowl.

Zeus had not moved from the hill path. His white polo and pressed khakis made him look like someone Percy might have seen waiting in a bank lobby, except for the blue spark that ran across one aviator lens and disappeared. Percy had seen his face change on other visits; the man standing here was a different one again. The resemblance to Thalia or Annabeth was not in his features. It was in the careful way both daughters watched him.

Zeus set the pie on the bench nearest him. "Thalia. Annabeth." His gaze shifted between them. "Thank you for letting me come. I can leave this with Chiron if you would rather not speak today."

Thalia folded her arms, then shifted the injured hand beneath her left elbow. "You can stay for the meal. I haven't decided about a conversation."

"Understood." Zeus nodded. He did not ask her to explain.

Annabeth stepped closer to the bench, leaving space between them. "I'll take the pie to the kitchen when Chiron says where it can go."

"Thank you." Zeus looked as though he had prepared more words. He set them aside. "It is apple. I don't know whether that's what you like."

"I do like apple," Annabeth said. "But you didn't need to guess. You could have asked."

"I should have." Zeus gave her the box. "I'll remember to ask next time."

Annabeth took it. Percy watched her face, but she gave him no signal to step in. He let her carry the pie to the kitchen with Chiron, and stayed on the green with Thalia. The Saturday had begun. None of the greetings had decided what the rest of it would be.

---

The grill stations stood on two separate heatproof mats. A chalk line kept curious younger campers away from the coals, and the food tables left the pavilion lane open. Chiron read each station's posted instructions before he let anyone light it. Poseidon set down his bag and reached for the tongs; Zeus reached for the other pair at the same moment.

Poseidon let go first. "You take the first batch."

"I don't need a turn," Zeus said.

"Then you can wait while the campers cook."

Zeus looked at the grill, then at the counselor beside it. "That seems reasonable."

Percy had been ready to wedge himself between them. Instead, he picked up the bowl Tyson had brought and carried it to the food table. Neither god had asked him to referee. Neither had started anything worse than a competition over tongs.

Clarisse finished the last of her morning training before she came over. She checked the table edge against the line Annabeth had marked on the setup sheet and asked a counselor to move it a handspan. It cleared the route Chiron had tested the previous day, though the far turn remained untested. She pointed this out before anyone could call the arrangement finished.

Poseidon watched her make the correction. "You run the open practice?"

"I help with it," Clarisse said. "The map says to ask before joining. We changed that because a fixed time didn't fit the way people actually use the arena."

He glanced at the public survey, visible through the Big House window. "I saw the words. I didn't know what they meant."

"Then ask the person who chose them." Clarisse returned the measuring tape to its hook. "I have training again tomorrow. You can watch if you want, but I'm not turning it into a performance."

"I can do that," Poseidon said.

Silena came over with the menu board. The list was written in three colors, with a mark beside dishes that contained honey or nuts. She had left space at the bottom for substitutions. Poseidon leaned close enough to read without touching the board.

"You made this?"

"I made the lettering. The kitchen checked the ingredient list. If you want a plate, ask me what's in it and I'll tell you. Don't assume I know what everyone can eat just because I wrote the menu."

"All right." Poseidon stepped back from the board. "What did you want me to notice?"

Silena studied him for a moment. "That I did this because I like making a meal work. Not because I volunteered to manage everyone at it."

Poseidon lowered the tongs. "I understand."

"You might. We'll see." She turned to bring Tyson a cloth for the bowl he had set down. Percy caught her eye; she shook her head once, asking him not to turn the exchange into a victory.

Poseidon looked between both daughters, then at the prepared words he had written on a small card beside the sodas. Percy could read only the first line: I AM PROUD OF ALL OF YOU. His father had made space in the day for a speech. Clarisse and Silena had made clear they did not need one from him to know their names.

"I was going to say something," Poseidon admitted. "I thought it would be easier if I had it ready."

"For you, maybe," Clarisse said.

He folded the card and put it in his pocket. "Probably."

The grill counselor called for someone to bring the first tray. Tyson looked toward the serving table, but Poseidon did not order him to carry it. Tyson chose to go, and Triton walked with him. Percy stayed at the drinks. Clarisse returned to the tables. Silena went to check that the menu was visible from both ends of the line.

Thalia stood with Annabeth beside the banner. Her wrapped right palm remained tucked against her ribs. When Zeus looked her way, she did not step forward to reassure him. She waited to see what he would say.

"How is your hand?" Zeus asked.

"Still healing. Will hasn't cleared it for bracing or sparring."

"Then I won't ask you to carry anything." He looked toward the sign in her left hand. "Would you like me to hold that while you tie it?"

"No. I can tie it with my left. You can keep the path clear."

He moved aside. Thalia tied the sign without help. The bow came loose on the first try, so she tightened it and tied it again. Zeus watched, hands at his sides. Percy could not tell what either of them made of the silence, and he stopped trying to name it.

Annabeth returned from the kitchen after leaving the pie with Chiron. Zeus waited by the green, a few steps from the serving tables. He had not followed her.

"Hi, Dad," she said. "Thank you for coming."

"Thank you for letting me stay." His eyes went to the map through the open rec-room door. "Is that the survey you were working on?"

Annabeth looked at the easel and then at Chiron. He nodded. The map was out for the camp to read; she could choose to talk about it or not.

"Yes. It's the first edition. It's on trial in the rec room for a week, with a separate correction card."

Zeus studied the map from the threshold. "I thought it might be a defense plan."

"It isn't. It's a public wayfinding survey. It shows the paths and places people have agreed to mark. The north trail is closed, and the dock board is still unsafe. Those are warnings. The map doesn't show private notes or tactics."

"I see." Zeus looked back at her. "I was wrong to call it something else."

"You hadn't called it anything yet," Annabeth said. "You asked."

"Then I will ask more carefully." His hand rested on the doorframe but did not cross the threshold. "Would you show me the symbols?"

"I can show you the public ones."

She led him inside. Thalia followed, not in front of Annabeth or behind Zeus, but beside her sister. Chiron stayed close enough to hear. The page held only what the trial allowed: cabins, shared paths, the pavilion, the open arena, and marks residents had approved. Annabeth explained the correction card and why she had kept it separate. She pointed out the Cabin Six window count, which Ella had caught, and the trail closure that remained until someone checked the brace. She did not open her field notebook.

Zeus leaned toward the dock marking. "This board is still loose?"

"It flexes. We haven't repaired it. Until someone checks it, the map tells people not to step there."

"And the alternate route?"

"The one Chiron tested worked for him once, in dry weather. We haven't certified it for everybody."

Zeus looked again at the correction card. "You put the limit beside the result."

"If I leave the limit out, someone else might rely on more than we checked."

He held her gaze, as if he might turn the sentence into praise. Instead, he asked, "What do you want people to do with the map?"

Annabeth's pencil stopped above the correction card. "Use it to get around. Tell us what is wrong or hard to read. Don't treat it as finished just because it looks finished."

"I can do that." He paused. "May I take a copy after the trial?"

"If Chiron approves the final copy. Everyone gets the same one."

"That is fair." He did not ask to photograph the private notebook or take the map from its easel.

Thalia looked at him. "You can ask Annabeth about her work. You don't need me to translate it, and you don't need her to prove she remembers being your daughter."

Thalia stood close beside Annabeth, but Annabeth answered for herself. "I don't remember the childhood you remember, Thalia. I believe you when you tell me about it. I want to talk about what I know now, too."

Zeus's jaw moved once before he spoke. "I don't know how to make up the years I missed."

"You don't have to solve that today," Annabeth said. "You can stay for the meal. You can ask about the things I'm doing. If I say I don't want to answer, let it end there."

"I can try."

"Trying isn't a plan," Thalia said. Her voice was flat. "But staying is something you can do today."

Zeus turned toward the door. "Then I will stay."

Back on the green, Poseidon had the serving tongs in one hand and a paper plate in the other. He seemed about to make an announcement. Silena caught his eye and shook her head. He stopped, then put the plate down and asked the nearest counselor where the first batch was going.

The grill stations had started producing food. Percy carried the first covered tray to the serving table and checked with the Apollo counselor before setting it down. Tyson brought his potato salad to the cold table. Zeus asked before lifting the pie from the kitchen counter, and Annabeth told him where it could sit. They had found a way to share the work without turning the afternoon into a competition over who could give the biggest gift.

---

The first trays went out in uneven batches. The grill counselor checked each one before it reached the serving table, and Percy repeated the temperatures while he wrote them on the slate. The work was simple enough to leave room for conversation, which turned out to be harder than Percy expected. He had to stop himself from looking for a new job whenever one of the fathers went quiet.

Poseidon stayed by the grill after the counselor asked him to step back from the fuel. He did not argue. He watched the counselor demonstrate how to turn the patties without pressing them flat, then asked if the first batch could be saved for the campers who had been waiting. It was a small question, but Percy noticed he asked the person running the station instead of announcing what should happen.

Zeus took a plate from the stack and moved to the side table. He had the pie in the kitchen, out of the sun. He looked at the banner once, then at the corrected letter. He did not use lightning to repair it. Will tied a second cord through the loose corner and asked Zeus to hold the post steady while he worked.

Zeus did. When Will finished, he said, "Thank you," without looking up.

"You're welcome," Zeus said.

The words were ordinary. The camp around them stayed ordinary too: a hammer ringing from the forge, a pair of younger campers arguing over the last red cup, the kitchen door opening and closing. Percy took the next tray and carried it to the table. He had not expected to feel relieved by a conversation that lasted four words.

Silena checked the menu labels with the kitchen counselor. One dish had no ingredient card. She did not guess; she set it aside until the cook could confirm what was in it. Poseidon saw the pause and started to reach for the dish, then stopped.

"Would you like me to bring it back to the kitchen?" he asked.

"No, thank you. I can carry it." She lifted the tray with both hands and took it through the clear lane.

Poseidon stayed where he was. He had been given a chance to help and had waited for an answer. Percy wondered whether he had learned that somewhere else or whether this was the first time he had tried to do it in front of them. He could not tell from watching one afternoon.

Clarisse came back after checking the table spacing. A younger camper had moved one chair into the lane to get a better view of the grills. Clarisse asked them to shift it and explained why the route had to stay open. The camper moved it. When Poseidon started toward the space, Clarisse held out one hand.

"Not through there. Take the end of the table. We haven't checked that turn with Chiron's chair yet. He can use the ramp route we tested, but this is still a work in progress."

Poseidon took the longer way. "You caught it."

"It was in the way."

"I mean, you noticed before somebody got stuck."

"People notice things. That's not a special job I have." She picked up the chair and moved it against the fence. "I don't need a medal for telling someone to move a chair."

"I didn't say you did."

"Then let me be clear." Clarisse set the chair down. "Don't turn today's visit into a list of what I do for everyone. I moved a chair because it blocked the route. I came to see you as Clarisse, not to stand in for my siblings."

Poseidon lowered his eyes to the tongs in his hand. "I can do that."

"You can start by asking what I want to show you."

He took a moment before answering. "What do you want me to see?"

She pointed past the pavilion toward the arena. "The open-practice note on Annabeth's map. I helped decide the wording. No fixed hour, no promise that someone will always be there. The person running practice chooses who joins. It's how I want to lead it."

Poseidon followed her gesture to the public survey visible in the rec room. "I saw the words. I didn't know what they meant."

"Then ask the person who chose them." Clarisse returned the measuring tape to its hook. "I have training again tomorrow. You can watch if you want, but I'm not turning it into a performance."

"I can do that," Poseidon said.

"If I'm still there."

"If you're still there," he agreed.

The exchange did not end with a hug. Clarisse returned to the table and took a drink from her cup. Poseidon stayed by the grill, where the counselor was explaining why pressing the patties squeezed out their juice. He listened and tried again with the next one.

Percy had heard Clarisse's story before: when she was eight, Poseidon had watched her drills for ten minutes, criticized her shield arm and left. She had told Percy and the others in the kitchen while they were trying to bake cookies for Sally. She had not told Poseidon that she remembered the visit. Percy kept that knowledge to himself. Clarisse had chosen when to bring it up.

Later, when the grill had cooled enough for the second batch, she walked to the shaded edge of the green. Poseidon had put the tongs down. He came only after she looked back and tilted her head toward the path.

"You remember the visit when I was eight?" Clarisse asked.

"I remember coming to the camp. I remember you were practicing with a shield." He folded his hands. "I don't remember what I said. That doesn't make it less true if you remember it."

"You said my shield arm was weak. Then you left. I cried in the kitchen after you were gone."

Poseidon drew in a breath, then let it out. "I'm sorry I left after ten minutes. I don't have a reason that would make it better."

Clarisse's mouth tightened. "I didn't tell you so you'd apologize and get to leave again."

"I won't leave because you said it."

"You don't know how long you'll stay."

"No," he said. "I know I am here now."

She looked toward the pavilion. Silena was writing a fresh ingredient label while Tyson held the board steady. Thalia stood beside Annabeth. Zeus was a few steps from them, not speaking.

"I wanted you to see me train," Clarisse said. "Not hear a report about my shield arm. I want you to know what I do here because I chose it."

"Can you show me?"

"Not today. I have finished training and I don't want to do it again for an audience." She stopped herself before he could answer. "Maybe tomorrow. Ask me in the morning."

"I will ask."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll listen."

"Good." Clarisse rubbed her thumb over the rim of her cup. "I'm not ready to say this fixes anything."

"I know."

She did not reach for him, and he did not try to take her hand. They walked back at the same pace, a few steps apart. When they reached the pavilion, Clarisse returned to the table and Poseidon asked the counselor where he could help next.

Silena's conversation with him happened later, after the first group of campers had eaten. She stood at the menu board, rewriting one dish that had run out and marking the substitute. Poseidon waited until she turned from the chalk.

"I heard you say you made the lettering," he said. "Would you tell me what part of the meal you wanted to plan?"

"The board and the order the food comes out. The kitchen already had the recipes. I wanted to put the choices where people could see them before they joined the line."

"You made it easier to choose."

"That was the idea." Silena drew a small arrow to the substitute. "I like making things clear without making people feel as if they need permission to eat. It took a few tries."

Poseidon looked at the colored marks. "What did you change?"

"The first version had too much on one side. People were bunching up to read it. I moved the labels above each dish and asked the kitchen to put the ingredient cards where they belonged. The menu is easier to use now."

He leaned away so she could see the full board. "It is."

Silena held the chalk between two fingers. "I made the menu because I like making choices clear. I don't want you to see me only as the healer or the person who smooths things over. I can choose to help; I won't be assigned to keep you and Clarisse comfortable."

"I won't call you that."

"You can ask me about what I'm making or writing. You can ask what I want to do next. Just don't ask me to make Clarisse feel better about you. That's between the two of you."

Poseidon nodded. "I won't."

She looked at him for another second, then passed him the clean cloth. "Could you wipe the edge of the board? The chalk keeps catching there."

"Of course."

He wiped the frame while she finished the ingredient label. When he was done, Silena took the cloth and checked the corner herself. She thanked him. He didn't treat the thanks as forgiveness.

The family meal began when the first food reached the long tables. No one called for a toast. Poseidon stood with a plate in his hand, glanced at the five children, then sat again. A few minutes later he spoke to them without raising his voice.

"I wrote a speech," he said. "I thought I should say something about each of you. I don't think the card has much to do with what you want from me today. Would you mind if I kept it short?"

Tyson looked up. "You can say one thing."

Triton sat with his arms folded, listening. "One thing is enough."

Silena glanced at Clarisse. Clarisse gave a small shrug that meant she would decide for herself. Percy kept his plate on the table. He did not look at his sisters to see how they would answer.

Poseidon set the card on the bench. "I'm proud of all five of you. I am glad to be sitting here while you tell me what matters to you, instead of guessing at it later."

He stopped. There was no list of titles or distant accomplishments. He let the sentence end.

Tyson smiled and asked whether his father wanted another spoonful of potato salad. Poseidon said yes. Clarisse began eating. Silena passed the chalk to Annabeth so she could mark the time the first tray had come out. Triton asked Poseidon what he wanted to know about the hinges Tyson had mentioned. Their meal found its own pace.

"Could you explain them to me?" Poseidon asked Tyson. "Only if you want to."

Tyson brightened and turned his empty plate around. He traced two short lines on the paper with a fingertip, then placed one finger across them to show where the hinge would sit. "The first angle was too flat. The door pulled on it when it moved. I made the second side steeper. The metal does not bend now."

"And you found that by testing it?"

"Yes. I made one that bent. I changed one part and tried it again. Then I changed another part, but that made it worse, so I went back."

Poseidon leaned in to see the drawing. He didn't touch it. "How many times did you test it?"

Tyson counted silently, moving his lips. "Seven. The last one stayed straight."

"That sounds like work."

"It is good work," Tyson said. His voice grew louder. "I like when the door moves without catching. You can hear it when it's right."

Triton shifted closer to look at the two lines. "Does the hinge have to be exactly that size?"

"For that door, yes. The next door is different. I have to measure it first."

Poseidon nodded. "Then I won't pretend I know what the next one needs. You can tell me when you have measured it."

Tyson smiled and erased the lines with his napkin. The paper went into the kitchen waste basket with the other used plates. There would be no drawing for anyone to keep, only what Tyson had made and the words he had chosen to use for it. Poseidon asked another question about how the hinge sounded when it closed. Tyson answered with a small description of the click, then asked if his father wanted to hear it next time he was near the forge. Poseidon said he would, and Tyson returned to his food.

Zeus took his plate to the end of the table, where Thalia and Annabeth were sitting. He did not choose a seat between them. Thalia moved her cup away from the wrapped hand and left a space on the bench. Annabeth kept her chair turned toward the table, not toward him.

"You don't have to sit with us," Thalia said.

"I know." Zeus paused. "May I?"

Annabeth looked at Thalia, then turned back to her father. "You can sit there."

He sat at the end. For a while he listened to the campers around him. A younger camper asked whether the pie was for the whole table. Zeus said it was, and asked Chiron to cut it when the meal was over so the filling would not spill.

Thalia told Annabeth what Will had said about keeping her hand wrapped. Annabeth asked whether she wanted the small signs moved closer. Thalia declined and used her left hand to adjust the one at her elbow. Zeus watched the exchange without trying to turn it into a lesson about either daughter.

When the first plates were cleared, he asked Annabeth what the map had taught her that the drawing alone could not. She told him the missing window had been easy to overlook until someone counted from the other side, and that a route could work for one person in one set of conditions without being safe for everyone. He asked what she planned to check next. She said the north trail remained closed until the brace inspection happened.

"Would you like help with the inspection?" he asked.

"Chiron has people assigned to it. You can ask him if he wants an extra pair of hands. I don't need you to take over the map."

"I will ask Chiron."

"All right."

He did not say the map made her a general. He did not ask where she had learned to think about paths. Annabeth kept her field notebook in her bag, and Zeus did not ask to see it.

For a few minutes Percy ate beside Annabeth while the rest of the table talked. She told him the first tray had reached the serving table without the lane being blocked. He told her the Apollo counselor had made him repeat each temperature before he could write it down. She smiled at that.

"You let him check your work," she said.

"I did. I might even have learned something. Don't tell Clarisse."

"She'll notice." Annabeth rested her shoulder against his for a moment, then sat upright when someone asked her to pass the water jug. She answered her father's questions about the survey and had chosen which parts to show him. Percy was proud of her, but he left the thought unspoken. She had heard enough people tell her what she must feel for one day.

The afternoon grew hotter. The line stretched out past the tables, and the last bowls needed to be moved away from the sun. A breeze picked up from the Sound and pushed smoke across one end of the serving area. Chiron paused the line and sent the counselors to move the serving table behind the pavilion, where the wind would not blow across the food. Clarisse checked the new spacing. Annabeth marked the changed setup on the separate planning sheet, not on the trial map. The food waited while the campers moved the table.

Poseidon stepped forward to help, then checked with the counselor. He carried the empty water cooler after permission. Zeus took the light end of the folded table only when the Hephaestus counselor asked him to. Thalia stayed where she was, keeping her hand out of the lifting. No one had to tell her she was allowed to rest.

When the line opened again, Tyson brought his bowl to the cooler shade and set it down himself. He had saved a small portion for later. Poseidon asked if he could try it after the meal; Tyson said yes, but only if he used the green spoon because the red one was for tasting. Poseidon obeyed.

The task list was not a family agreement. It was a sheet of paper with food temperatures, table spacing, and a few names beside jobs they had chosen. Percy folded it once and put it in Chiron's tray. The camp had fed the people who came. The rest of the questions were still with the people who had asked them.

By late afternoon, the sun hung low over the Sound. The grill counselor had turned off the heat, and the last trays were on the serving table under the pavilion roof. The breeze had shifted twice. The table had been moved when smoke crossed the food, and Annabeth had written the change on the meal sheet. The map stayed in its one-week trial in the rec room, with the dock board still marked unsafe.

Percy found Annabeth at the far end of the lawn, where the grass sloped toward the beach. They sat above the path, away from the dock. She had a paper plate with a small piece of apple pie on it. Percy took the seat beside her and set his cup between them.

"You held it together," he said.

"I helped keep the route open. Chiron held the meal together. The counselors did the work." She scraped a bit of crust from the plate. "You checked the food temperatures instead of solving everyone's conversation. That's progress."

"I thought about it a lot."

"I know." She looked over at him. "I want to tell them something before they leave. If you want to."

He knew what she meant. "About us?"

"Yes. I don't want it to come out as a joke or an announcement from someone else."

"We can tell them together. We don't have to ask them to decide anything."

Annabeth nodded. She set the plate down on the grass and reached for his hand. He took it. She pressed her thumb over the old scar near his knuckle, a familiar touch that belonged to the life they were living now as much as to the memories only they shared. He did not mention the other life. He did not have to.

Poseidon and Zeus came along the path a few minutes later. They stopped several steps away.

"Could we join you?" Poseidon asked.

Annabeth looked at Percy. He nodded, then she turned to the fathers. "You can. We want to tell you something, though, and we want you to hear it without making it into a vote."

Poseidon sat on the grass instead of taking the bench beside them. Zeus remained standing until Annabeth patted the open space on the other side of Percy. Then he sat, keeping a careful distance from the edge of the slope.

"We're together," Percy said. "We've been together for a while. We wanted to tell you ourselves."

Poseidon looked at their joined hands and then at Annabeth. "Thank you for telling me. Do you want me to call you a couple?"

"Yes," Annabeth said. "That's what we are."

"Then that's what I'll call you."

Zeus took a breath through his nose. "I had been going to ask whether I should be concerned. That would have been the wrong question."

"It would," Thalia said from the path. She had come over with Triton, both carrying empty cups. Her right hand was still wrapped. "Annabeth knows what she wants. You don't need me to check it for you."

"I wasn't asking you to," Zeus said.

"You have before." Thalia set the cups on the grass. "I'm glad you asked permission to sit. That doesn't make today's visit a repair."

Zeus looked down at the folded edge of his sleeve. "I understand."

"Do you?" Thalia asked.

He did not answer at once. "Not entirely. I know I have a history with you that I can't replace by showing up today. I have arrived with a different face and expected you to treat each meeting as a beginning."

Thalia's expression tightened. The words were close to what she had told him before, but Percy could not tell whether hearing them now made anything easier. She did not take his hand.

"That's true," she said. "And I don't want to explain it again every visit."

"You shouldn't have to."

"Then don't ask me to make this comfortable for you."

Zeus nodded. He let the silence stand without filling it with a promise.

Poseidon turned to Percy. "And you? Is there anything you want from me about this?"

"No blessing," Percy said. "No warnings about how much trouble she's in. Just treat her like you respect her choices."

"I can do that."

"And don't make her responsible for how I feel about it," Poseidon added, glancing at Zeus. "She's the one in the relationship."

"I heard him," Zeus said. He looked at Percy. "I will not make you prove that you deserve her. She decides who she chooses."

Annabeth kept her fingers in Percy's. "We aren't asking for a blessing. I wanted you to know because you're our fathers, and I don't want to hide something ordinary from you."

The word ordinary made the conversation smaller. Percy could feel his shoulders drop. Neither father made a speech. Poseidon said he was glad they trusted him with the news. Zeus said he would try not to turn it into a hearing. Annabeth thanked him for that, then told him it would matter more if he remembered it next time.

"I can try to remember," Zeus said.

"Good," she answered. "That's enough for today."

Thalia looked at Annabeth, waiting. Annabeth leaned into her shoulder. Thalia rested her left arm around her sister, careful not to press the bandaged hand between them. The contact lasted only a moment before Thalia shifted to spare her palm. Nobody commented on it.

The four of them returned to the pavilion together. Poseidon stopped beside the menu board where Silena was taking it down. "Thank you for showing me what you worked on," he said. "I won't ask you to manage the rest of the family."

Silena rolled the board into its cloth sleeve. "All right. I'll tell you if I want to talk more."

"I would like that."

"Then you can wait until I tell you."

"I can wait."

She tucked the chalk into the basket. Poseidon didn't try to make her promise a second conversation.

Clarisse was checking that the cooler lids were shut. Poseidon approached only after she turned toward him.

"I'll ask in the morning whether you want me at training," he said. "If you don't want to show me, I won't turn it into an argument."

"Ask in the morning," she said. "That's all I'm agreeing to."

"That's all I'm asking."

Clarisse snapped the cooler lid shut. "Then we're clear."

The next few minutes belonged to the practical end of the day. The empty platters went back to the kitchen. Tyson took his bowl to the wash station and stayed to scrape it himself. Triton carried the clean cups to Chiron's porch after checking where they belonged. Thalia read the small signs once more and left them in place. Annabeth put the separate meal sheet in Chiron's tray, not in the map's correction sleeve.

Thalia found Zeus near the pavilion steps while Chiron was checking the folded table. She asked him to wait. He did. Percy was close enough to hear them over the kitchen door and the scrape of benches, though he kept his eyes on the cups he was carrying.

"I want to say one thing before you go," Thalia said. Her right hand stayed against her side. "Every time you visit, you come with another face. Then you ask the same questions, like the last visit didn't happen."

Zeus looked at her. "You told me that before."

"I did. You asked whether Annabeth was doing well in her lessons. Before that you asked why she hadn't written. You can change your face as much as you want. You're still the same person asking us to start over."

The line was close to what she had told him in the Big House. Percy remembered the quiet that had followed it. He set the cups down in the basket and kept listening.

"I remember you saying I was asking you to earn my attention. I heard it and still came back expecting you to start over," Zeus said.

"You don't have to explain it. You have to stop doing it." Thalia's voice was low enough that the campers at the table kept talking. "I don't want another conversation where you ask Annabeth to explain her history or ask me to explain hers. If you want to know what she's doing now, ask her. If she doesn't want to answer, stop."

"I did ask her today. She showed me the survey."

"And she chose what to show you. That's the point."

Zeus's hands closed once and opened again. "I don't always know where to begin with you."

"You don't need a beginning. You have years of conversations. You can start from the last one."

"I keep changing my face," Zeus said. "I can't ask you to ignore that."

"No. But you can tell me it's you before you expect me to know what to say. And you can ask if I want to talk before you start asking about my sister."

"I can do that."

"You can try. I'll decide whether it matters when you do it."

"I know it's you because you say my name," Thalia continued. "That isn't the same as knowing you. Every new face asks me to look at a stranger and trust my father at once. I can't do both just because you arrived."

Zeus kept his hands at his sides. "You don't have to make it easier for me."

"Good. Because I'm not going to."

He glanced toward the hill, where the paved route bent out of view. "That is fair."

She shifted her weight. "I don't know what I want from you. I want you to stop making me reassure you that I still want a father when you show up. I do. That's part of why this is hard."

Zeus's gaze dropped to the wrap on her hand. "I am sorry."

"I heard you."

For a moment neither spoke. Percy could hear the pavilion boards cooling in the evening air and someone inside asking where the last clean plate had gone. Thalia had not asked Zeus to stay. Zeus had not asked her to forgive him.

"Do you want me to say goodbye before I leave?" he asked.

"Ask Annabeth yourself. I'll decide whether I want to be here."

"All right."

Thalia went back to the small signs. Zeus waited where he was. He did not follow her or ask Percy what she had meant.

Zeus waited by the porch while Chiron told him the ramp route remained a single successful test in dry weather. He asked if he could use the path as marked. Chiron said yes, while the ground stayed firm. Zeus thanked him and took the longer paved way down to the hill. He did not ask Annabeth to walk him there. At the bottom he turned back once, raised a hand, and waited until she chose to return the gesture.

Thalia did not wave. When he left, she kept looking toward the hill for several seconds. Then she asked Annabeth if she wanted to go to the cabin. Annabeth said not yet. Thalia stayed beside her while Chiron finished closing the pavilion.

Poseidon said goodbye to each of his children separately. Triton clasped his forearm. Tyson asked if he could have the green tasting spoon for next time; Poseidon said he would bring it back clean, and Tyson put the spoon in the bowl himself. Percy accepted a hug. Silena offered her hand, and Poseidon shook it. Clarisse stood with her arms folded. He asked before coming closer; she said no hug today. He accepted the answer and told her he would ask in the morning, as they had agreed.

"You don't have to stay overnight just because I said maybe," Clarisse told him.

"I know. I'll ask Chiron about the guest arrangements, and I won't assume your answer." Poseidon looked once toward the arena, then back to her. "Thank you for telling me what you remembered."

"I told you because I wanted to. Don't make it into a debt."

"I won't."

He stepped backward toward the Sound. The air smelled of wet stone and salt. The wave that returned him was smaller than the one that had carried him onto the beach. Percy watched until his father disappeared below the curve of the water.

When the camp started clearing the lawn, the seven of them worked without arranging themselves into a circle. Silena gathered the cloths. Clarisse carried two light chairs. Tyson took the empty bowl. Triton picked up the folded table sheet. Thalia collected the signs with her left hand. Annabeth carried the corrected meal plan to Chiron, and Percy took the basket of cups.

At the top of the hill, the campfire was beginning. There were other campers there, people who had worked the meal and people who had come only to eat. The seven of them joined the edge of the crowd. No one said that the barbecue had gone perfectly. No one had to.

"Do you think Dad will ask tomorrow?" Percy said to Clarisse.

"He said he would." She looked toward the arena. "I'll decide when he does."

Silena slid the menu board against the porch wall. "I don't know whether I'll want to talk to him again."

"You don't have to know tonight," Annabeth said.

Silena gave her a brief smile. "I know. That's why I like saying it out loud."

Thalia adjusted the wrap on her right palm with her left fingers. Annabeth asked if it was still comfortable. Thalia said yes, and that Will would check it in the morning. No one suggested the meal had healed it or made it less tender.

Percy glanced at the porch, where the plan for Saturday had been folded into Chiron's tray. The paper said who had checked the food, where the tables had moved, and which questions remained open. It did not list a promise that the family would be different now. The meal had been one day, with two arrivals, several honest answers and time left between them.

Annabeth slipped her hand into his as they walked toward the fire. She was not asking him to carry the day for her. He was not asking her to say it had been enough. They could be glad about a few things and still wait to see what came next.

The campfires burned steadily in their stone rings. Beyond the cabins, the Sound shifted in the dark, and the sky remained clear. The seven of them found places on the benches with the other campers. They listened to the stories already underway, added a few details about the meal, and let Saturday end without deciding what the next visit would mean.
