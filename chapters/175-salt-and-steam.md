## Chapter 175: Salt and Steam

The door of Tempest Hall was iron, and it was always warm, and Percy had lived in the cabin for five months without ever once opening it on purpose.

That was the strange part. He had grown up in water. He had been raised in a palace where the halls were full of it and the ceilings were full of it and the lamps were fish, and he had spent the whole summer at a camp built around a lake, and yet the one room in the world built to hold an ocean indoors had sat behind a warm iron door six feet from his bunk like a thing that belonged to somebody else.

He had asked Clarisse about it once, in July. She had said it was the old pool, from before, and that the cabin used it in the winter sometimes, and that it was cold in the middle and full of leaves from a skylight somebody was always meaning to fix, and that had been the whole of her interest in it, which coming from Clarisse was almost a warning.

She had fixed it in the rain on Monday. That was the thing nobody had seen her do. Somewhere between the winter list and the towel line, Clarisse La Rue had gone down to the old saltwater hall with a mop and a bucket and the flat expression of a woman cleaning a room she intended to use, and had got the leaves out of the water, and had found the heater, and had found out what was wrong with the heater, which was nothing, which was that it had never been switched on.

So on Tuesday at ten to seven, the door stood open for the first time all year, and steam came out of it into the cold passage, and Cabin Three stood in front of it like people at the edge of a party they had not been invited to and had, in fact, been ordered to attend.

"Rules," Clarisse said, and held up the slate.

The slate had five lines on it in her blocky capitals, and they had plainly been written out twice, because there was a small ruling line between the fourth and fifth that suggested she had reconsidered something and won.

ONE: IN AND OUT THE SAME DOOR.
TWO: NOBODY SWIMS ALONE. SOMEONE ON THE EDGE, AWAKE.
THREE: THE DEEP END IS NOT A DARE.
FOUR: NO GLASS ANYWHERE NEAR THE TILE.
FIVE: AND WHOEVER WANTS TO COME, COMES.

"That's four rules and a policy," Percy said.

"It's five rules. Read the fourth one."

"No glass anywhere near the tile is a rule about cups."

"It's a rule because the last time there was glass near the tile, the tile lost, and the tile is eight hundred years old." She turned the slate around and hung it on its nail by the door, where the whole world could read it, and stood back from it with her arms crossed and her jacket still on. "Everybody in. Shoes in the racks. If you've got a suit, you've got a suit, and if you haven't, Silena has four extras in a bag."

"Why does Silena have four extras in a bag?"

"Because Silena has four extras in a bag," Silena said, from behind him, in the mild voice of a woman who had been packing spare suits in her beach tote since she was eleven. "Don't make it a whole thing."

The hall took them in one at a time, and it took each of them differently.

It was older than the cabin and it did not look like the cabin at all. Black tile floor, black tile walls, a barrel ceiling of unmatched Roman brick patched in three places with brighter brick where somebody had rebuilt it, and a skylight in the long curve of the roof with the rain lying on the glass and the last of the daylight coming through in a soft gray sheet. The water was the same green-black as the lake on a dull day, and it moved in small slow hills of its own where the hot water came in at the far end, and the whole room breathed steam, and it echoed before anybody made a sound, the way a room does when it has not heard voices in a while.

Percy stopped three steps in.

The smell hit him before the water did. Salt. Not sea salt, not the Atlantic, not the lake, which smelled of pine roots and cold mud and reeds. This was the other thing: warm mineral salt, and with it a tug of familiarity he associated with the deep palace. The association offered no room or event, only an ache of wanting he could not quite place.

He stood there in a camp hall in October, and the smell of warm saltwater brought up a wanting under his ribs, so fast and hard that he had to look at the far wall for a second and read the patched brick like a sentence.

The smell was not a memory. That was the distinction he had been learning to keep this year. The smell was a smell; what came with it was a wanting without a scene attached. The palace was part of his life even when the details would not come, and the warm saltwater in this camp hall belonged to Tuesday. Both could be true without the smell supplying a memory he did not have.

"You've gone somewhere," Annabeth said, at his elbow.

"I've gone three steps in."

"You've gone somewhere and you've come back. That's fine. That's what the room is for." She said it without any weight at all, the way she said ballast: not a comfort, just an accurate report about a thing that was true. She had her hair up off her neck in a knot with a pencil through it, which was a solution so Annabeth that he could not look directly at it, and she was already reading the depth markings on the wall, and already disapproving of them. "The foot marks are nine inches apart. Nine inches. You can't take a sounding with a ruler that thinks in shoes."

"I'll get you a rope."

"You will not. You'll swim, and I'll measure nothing, and for one evening that's going to be fine, and I've decided it in advance so nobody has to argue with me."

Then everybody got in and the room stopped being a room.

That was the other thing about water: it did not care how careful people were being. Olive Nakamura had on a pair of goggles built for an adult and they slipped down her face the moment she put her head under, and Marcus went in with the practiced economy of a fifteen-year-old who had decided in advance that he was going to behave, and Thalia went off the block at the deep end in one flat dive that made a hole in the surface you could have read through and came up twenty feet down the hall with the steam rolling off her shoulders and the enormous satisfaction of a person who had been off her feet for four days with a bad heel.

"The heel's fine," she announced, to the ceiling.

"The heel's bandaged."

"The bandage is a hat for my heel, and it has nothing to say about my dive."

Clarisse went in without a sound and came up without a sound and swam one long fast lap of the hall, and then a second, and then stopped at the shallow end and stood there with the water at her chest and looked around at the whole room like a quartermaster checking a delivery.

Thalia surfaced at the rail, pushed her hair back with both hands, licked the back of her own wrist, and made a face.

"It's salt," she said.

"It's a saltwater hall."

"I know it's a saltwater hall. I'm telling you it's *salt* salt. I climbed out of the canoe lake on Saturday and licked my wrist and it tasted like a puddle." She did it again, experimentally, like a person checking a gauge. "This tastes like the sea. Which I've never actually been in. I've read about it. It's got a whole different thing going on."

"It's the same stuff," Percy said. "Just more of it. And it holds you up better."

"Holds you up better?"

"Try it." He went over to the deep end rail and put his hands flat on the tile and looked down the black length of the water. "Float on your back down there for a minute and try to sink. You can't. You get tired of floating before the water gets tired of you."

Thalia looked at him for a second with her eyebrows up, and then, because Thalia Grace did not decline a claim about water from a son of Poseidon, she went down and floated on her back in the deep end of a black-tiled hall with her arms out and her ears under and her black hair spread out around her shoulders like ink, and stayed there, staring at the patched brick in the ceiling, for a minute and a half.

Olive Nakamura got there one minute in. She climbed down the ladder with her goggles on wrong and stood on the bottom rung and did the thing she had been doing all summer, which was watching a skill and then deciding, audibly, that she could have it.

"Why's she floating like that?"

"It's salt water," Percy said. "It's easier than the lake."

"Then why — " Olive stopped, reconsidered, and asked it properly, which was the whole reason Annabeth liked her, and the whole reason Percy did too. "Then why isn't there salt in our lake?"

"Because the lake's fed by rain and melt and a spring up under the ridge, and salt in water comes from the sea or from rock, and our rock hasn't got it. Some lakes have salt. Ours is sweet. They're both real water, they just come from different places and they hold you differently."

"Hold you differently," Olive repeated, in the slow voice of somebody filing a fact.

"Yeah. The lake's got mud and cold on the bottom and it makes you work for it and you earn it. This hall's a party. It wants you to be here." He pushed off the rail and went down deep, ten feet down, where the water went from green to black and the light came off the tile in slow moving coins, and stayed there with his eyes open because he could, and then came up and shook his head like a dog and found the whole rail looking at him.

"Show-off," Clarisse said.

"It's my whole personality."

"You've said that before."

"It keeps being true."

Silena had four extra suits in a bag, exactly as advertised, and by ten past seven every one of them was in use, on Olive and two of the Apollo kids who had heard the door and come down out of pure nosiness, and a small girl from the Demeter cabin who had been told there was warm water and had run the whole way.

"That's seven," Clarisse said.

"That's seven," Silena agreed. "Eight's coming — Marcus went back for Katie."

"Nine, then. I'm going to keep count all winter and you're all going to find it very annoying."

"It's not annoying," Percy said. "It's the whole reason the room works."

Clarisse looked at him the way she looked at a spear she had decided not to check. "Jackson, don't get sincere at the pool."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

The small ones arrived in a heap at about ten past seven.

Katie Gardner brought six of them down the hill in one line, all in coats over whatever they had, and the smallest was a Demeter girl named Rosie who was seven and had been told there was warm water and had run the last fifty feet. They stopped in the doorway in a clump, because a black-tiled hall full of steam at night is a large thing when you are seven, and stood there breathing it in with their eyes wide, and nobody in the whole room said a word about it out loud, because everybody in the whole room was quietly doing the arithmetic on how long it had been since they had felt warm in October.

"Suits," Silena said, from the dressing room, in the flat busy voice of a quartermaster. "Adults' rack on the right, kids' on the left, and the bag of smalls is the blue one. No, you can't wear your own shorts in it, they go transparent, ask me how I know."

"How do you — "

"Don't ask me how I know."

So the four spare suits came out of the bag and went onto three of Katie's kids and a Hermes boy who had come down out of pure nosiness and was now, very obviously, staying. Neve from the forge got one with a silk repair in the left shoulder, done in small even stitches, which nobody said anything about. Silena handed out goggles from a jar and took back two pairs that were cracked and put them in her pocket to fix, which was a thing she did without appearing to look at anybody while she did it, which was the closest thing Cabin Three had to a public service.

Then they all got in, in the slow scared way people get into a strange pool, and the room changed character completely.

That was the part Percy had not expected. He had grown up in water and had never once in his life been afraid of it, and he had assumed, without ever examining the assumption, that everybody came to it the way he did. What he saw instead, in a black-tiled hall on a Tuesday, was six kids sitting on the edge with their feet in up to the ankles and their hands clenched on the tile, breathing hard, laughing at nothing, working themselves up to it one inch at a time; and a small Demeter girl who could not swim at all and had therefore decided, with the airtight logic of seven, that she was going to learn tonight.

"You put your face in and you count three," Percy told her. "That's the whole thing. Face in, count three, head up, look at something you can see. It's not a big deal and nobody's watching."

"Everybody's watching."

"They're not. Look around." He tipped his chin at the room, at Marcus pretending to be occupied with the ladder, at Thalia upside down in the deep end with her ears under, at Silena teaching Neve to kick water at the wall. "Nobody in this hall has ever once been good at anything they liked on the first try. I live with these people."

Rosie put her face in and counted three with her whole body tense, and came up coughing and furious and already leaning back down again. She did it eleven times in a row, because being seven and furious is a renewable resource, and on the twelfth she came up with her hair flat to her face and said, in a small fierce voice, "Can we do four?", and Percy had to look away for a second and busy himself with a towel, which was the standard procedure for that exact emotion and had been since June.

Clarisse watched the whole thing from her bench and wrote nothing down, and when Percy came past her to get to the shallow ladder she said, without moving her head, "You're good with the little ones. That's new since summer. You used to talk to them like they were guests."

"I used to think they were."

"Yeah," Clarisse said, and that was the entire conversation, and both of them understood that it had been a large one.

What followed was, as far as Percy could tell, the best evening anybody at this camp had had since the middle of September, and none of them had planned a single piece of it.

They raced, badly, with all the seriousness of a family for whom racing was not a game. Percy lost to Clarisse twice over two lengths and to Thalia once under the rail, and won once against Annabeth, who had declined to race and then had quietly counted the whole thing from the edge and beaten him by two seconds without ever being in the water. Marcus organized a campaign to move one of the big floats from the deep end to the shallow end, which took six people and eleven minutes and was abandoned the moment it arrived. Olive spent twenty minutes learning to float on her back with her ears under, which Silena taught her the way she had taught it to everyone else, holding two fingers under the back of the neck and saying nothing at all until it happened.

And underneath all of it the room did the thing rooms do when water is in them: it turned every small noise into a big one, and every big noise into a fact. A shout became architecture. A laugh came back at you from the far end wearing your own voice. The steam hung in the long curve of the ceiling in a slow flat plane, and when Olive finally got her ears under and lay back and opened her eyes at it, she said, in the enormous solemn voice of a ten-year-old who has just discovered the ceiling, "It's like the whole hall's inside a cloud," and everybody who heard it agreed with her out loud, which is the highest compliment available and is rarely paid to a ceiling.

At half past eight, Clarisse got out and stood on the edge and counted, out loud, deliberately, in her flat voice: one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine. Then she said, "Nobody's gone anywhere," and pulled her jacket on over her suit, because she was not going to be seen in a suit without a jacket even in a building full of steam, and went to sit on the low bench by the door with the winter list and the slate and the whole warm room in front of her, keeping watch, which was the one job she had assigned herself and the one job she would be doing twice a week until March.

---

By nine the small ones had gone and the hall belonged to the five of them, and an empty hall full of warm salt water at nine o'clock at night is one of the very few rooms in the world that improves by being emptied.

Annabeth sat on the edge with her feet in the water and her field book open on her knee and the steam going up past her face in slow sheets, drawing.

Nobody had asked her to draw the hall. That was the point. They had all come down here to swim, and swim was a thing that ended, and she had discovered at some point around the third lap that she had been in a room for an hour that nobody had ever put on paper and that she was, in fact, the cartographer of this camp, which meant the room was, strictly speaking, an oversight.

So she drew it. The tile plan first, four walls unrolled and laid flat around her, the doorway, the racks, the benches, the patched brick in the ceiling marked with a small cross and the words REBUILT, UNVERIFIED. Then the water: the line where the warm inlet came in at the far end, the curl of the steam over it, the deep end's black rectangle with the ladder bolted to the wall.

And then, because she had never once in her life been able to leave a wrong number alone, she drew the depth marks.

They were painted up the tile wall every nine inches in the old camp's hand — somebody long dead had knelt in this room with a pot of white paint and done it — and they were wrong. Not slightly wrong. Across a three-fathom wall they were off by nearly a foot by the deep end, because whoever had painted them had measured from the shallow edge outward with a mistake that grew as it went, the way small errors always do: quietly at first, then all at once.

Annabeth sat in the steam and looked at the wrong marks and felt the particular happiness that only ever arrived when the world turned out to be wrong in a way she could fix with a pencil.

"You're drawing," Percy said, arriving in the water beside her elbow with his arms on the edge and his hair flat to his head.

"I'm correcting."

"You're drawing."

"I'm correcting," Annabeth said, "which is a superior form of drawing, and if you say one more thing I'll put you down for a survey crew on Saturday and you'll be sorry."

"I'm already on the Saturday survey. Chiron put me on it. It's official. I have a duty and the duty is to be enjoyable."

"Then enjoy the tile."

Percy read the page, upside down, with his chin on his own forearm, which was a thing he could do and did, and she let him, because his reading her work had stopped being an intrusion somewhere in June and had become a kind of company. "Third one from the shallow end's a full nine inches off," he said. "Then it doubles."

"It compounds. There's a difference."

"There's always a difference." He was quiet a second, looking at the wall of white numerals through the steam. "You're going to repaint all of them, aren't you."

"In the winter. With a line and a plumb, and I'll do it in one night so nobody has to swim a wrong number in March and lose an argument with Clarisse about it."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said to me."

"It's a depth correction."

"That's why it's romantic," Percy said, entirely seriously, and she had to close the field book for a second, because there was a certain kind of Percy Jackson remark that did not have an answer, and the only correct response was to put both hands in the warm water and splash him.

Which she did. And then there was a war, which Clarisse watched from her bench with the winter list and did not participate in, and which Thalia joined from across the hall with the flat overhead throw of somebody who had been raised with a sister and no other amusements, and which eventually ended because Silena got out and stood on the edge with both hands on her hips and said, "If anyone drowns in eighteen inches of water I am going to be so embarrassed," which is the only known way to stop a water fight in Cabin Three.

After that they did the statue game.

It was Clarisse's invention, which meant it had rules and no name, and it went like this: shallow end, bottom of the water, sitting, whoever stayed down longest without moving wins. No pushing off. No surfacing. Thalia, who had competitive opinions about everything, went first and stayed under for fifty-one seconds with her arms folded and her hair floating around her head like smoke, and came up furious about a number that nobody had asked her for. Marcus, who had come back for his goggles, went under for thirty and came up laughing. Clarisse did forty-four, without hurrying, and got out and said "Fifty-one's nothing, she was on a ridge for four days," which was her way of admitting defeat.

Percy went under last and stayed at the bottom of the shallow end with his eyes open, watching the light come through the skylight and flatten into a soft gold coin on the tiles above him, and it was easy, and that was the whole trouble with it: the warm saltwater and the light stirred a wish to stay somewhere he could not picture. It was only a bodily tug, not a memory he could follow; he had to choose, down there, deliberately, to come up.

He chose. He came up with his ears roaring and the steam on his face and took a breath that was all November-cold by comparison, and there was Annabeth on the edge with her field book closed, watching him, and the look on her face was the look of somebody who had counted the whole thing and had already been worried and had decided in advance not to say so.

"Sixty," she said.

"Was it?"

"Sixty-two, if I'm honest, and I'd rather be accurate."

"Put it in the book."

"It's in the book," Annabeth said, and it was: he saw it later, in the margin next to the tile plan, in her small upright hand, no context, no explanation: P. under: 62. good lungs. bad habit. Nobody else at camp would ever be able to read it and know what it meant, and he was going to think about those two lines for a week.

The others began going up to the cabin in the wet dark with their towels and their suits and the sound of nine people's worth of arguing going ahead of them. Silena stayed behind to collect the towels; Annabeth did not go with the others, and neither did Percy, and neither of them said anything about staying, which was how it had been decided.

The hall at the end of the night was the best it ever got. The heater ticked as it cooled. Steam stood in the ceiling's curve in one long flat sheet and did not move. The skylight had gone black with rain on it, and the black tile held the lamplight in the shallow end in a slow green shine, and there was nobody in the whole room except the two of them, and the water made the only sound there was, which was a small patient lapping against the ladder.

Annabeth got in off the edge without a splash, which was a thing she had learned to do out of spite in June and now did better than anybody in the cabin, and stood in the chest-deep warm water with her hair up and both arms folded on the surface and her shoulders pink with the heat, and looked at him across two feet of clean green water.

"Clarisse is going to charge us," Percy said. "She's keeping count all winter. She said so."

"Clarisse can charge us. She's already deducted the whole thing on Sunday and she knows it."

Then she crossed the two feet, and the water did the small sound it made when two people stood in it together, and she put her arms around his neck the way you put your arms around a thing you have decided to lean on, and kissed him with the steam going up around both their faces and the salt on her mouth and the warm water at their waists and absolutely no one anywhere in the world watching.

It was slow. It was the kind of slow that has no clock in it. He had both hands on her back and her hair was up off her neck and she had one hand flat against his chest, and the hall gave their one small sound back to them from all four walls at once, which made it larger than it was, which made her laugh against his mouth and then stop laughing, and then neither of them said anything for a while, and the heater ticked, and the steam stood still in the ceiling's curve like a held page.

"We should go up," she said eventually, into his shoulder.

"We should go up," Percy agreed, and did not move, and neither did she, and the room let them stay there in the warm salt water talking about nothing at all until the lamp got low and her fingers went wrinkled and the last of the Tuesday went down the drain with the overflow, without comment, the way the lake took everything.

---

Silena stayed behind to do the towels, because Silena always stayed behind to do the towels, and because there were eleven of them now instead of the four that had gone down, which meant eleven people had swum in a room that had been locked for a year, which meant somebody had done something right the day before and nobody had said a word about it.

She went along the edge with the flat basket and collected them, wet and warm, and hung them on the racks in the little dressing room, and stood in the doorway a minute in her suit with her own towel around her shoulders and listened to the hall behind her.

It had gone quiet the way a room goes quiet after a party, which is not really quiet: it is the memory of noise, holding still. The heater ticked. Somewhere in the wall a pipe gave one small clank and settled. The water lay in its long black shape under the lifted lid of lamplight, still as a floor.

Then the outside door opened, and the cold came in, and Charlie Beckendorf came in with a tool roll under one arm and a canvas coat over his work clothes, and the whole room changed temperature in a way that had nothing to do with the doors.

"You're open," he said, stopping two steps in, looking at the water, at the eleven used towels, at her, at the slate with the five rules hanging on its nail. He read all five rules. He read the fifth one twice. "Who wrote the slate?"

"Clarisse."

"The slate is a Clarisse slate," he agreed. "The pump's on her winter list. I came to look at it tonight because tomorrow I've got the greenhouse and Thursday I've got the lamps and if I waited till Friday the pump would have gone through the top of it."

"It's working."

"It's working tonight. It's been stopped up since April and somebody switched it on today and it ran and it'll hold for about a week and then it'll die in a very inconvenient way, which is what stopped things do." He had already crouched at the inlet box and had the roll open beside his knee, and Silena, standing in the doorway of the dressing room with a towel around her shoulders and her hair wet down her back, understood with total clarity that she was about to have to walk into this room in a swimsuit and stand there and be a person.

There was no version of leaving that was not worse. She knew that the way you know the shape of a chair in the dark. She put the basket down and went and got the second lantern off its hook and came and knelt near the inlet box with it, and held it up, and said, in the steadiest voice she had, "Where do you want it."

Beckendorf glanced up once, briefly, the way a person checks the position of a lamp. "Low and left. The box shadow's the problem, not the dark."

So they did the pump together, which took about twenty minutes, and which was made of the following things: his hands, which were enormous and careful, and did not hurry, and moved a fitting by an eighth of a turn and then re-checked it, and then moved it back; the lantern in her hand, which got warm and then hot and then had to be set on the tile and relit twice because the wick was old; the smell of wet stone and machine oil, which cut straight through the salt and the steam and was, she thought, the entire smell of the hill above the camp; and the water moving very slightly under the ladder, and the two of them not talking, mostly, except in single lines about the work.

"Hold it there."

"There."

"Higher."

"Higher."

"You're stronger than you look, holding that over your head."

"I've been lifting bread for four years. It's a whole regimen."

Then he got the strainer out of the inlet and showed it to her, and it was a flat disc of rust-coloured lint and hair and ancient leaf-mould, and he said, "That's April," and she said, "That's disgusting," and he said, "That's plumbing," and she laughed, out loud, in the steam, before she could stop it, and the hall threw the laugh back at her from four walls at once, and Beckendorf's face did something that she had not seen it do before, which was to be caught.

He covered it by working. He got the new strainer seated and the fitting closed and sat back on his heels and wiped his hands on the rag, and then he did the thing that took the whole evening apart.

"I brought the glass," he said.

"What glass?"

"The lantern glass." He dug in the side pocket of the tool roll and came out with two flat panels wrapped in a cloth, and unwrapped them on the tile between them like a person setting a meal down. "Amber and plain. I said nine lamps and I meant nine lamps, but I wanted to see the amber against the tile before I cut nine of them, because the tile's black and the tile's green depending on the light, and I don't buy nine of a colour I've only seen in a catalogue."

Silena sat down on the tile beside the panels, and looked at them, and did not say anything for a moment, because what she was thinking about was not glass.

What she was thinking about was Monday: a wet evening and a hill and a question about windows, and a girl who had said *the amber reads as a house* and then walked home in the rain and told herself it had been a small thing. And here it was, on a Tuesday, on the tile, in a cloth, carried up the hill by a boy who had to be up at six for the greenhouse.

"That one," she said. "The left one. Against the black tile it goes to honey. The other one goes gray and makes the path look like a hospital corridor."

Beckendorf picked up the amber panel and held it up in the lamplight and looked through it at the wall, and the whole room changed colour, and he was quiet for a second.

"Honey," he said. "That's the word for it. I've been calling it *the third one from the top* in the shop for a week, and nobody knows which one I mean."

"Now you've got a word."

"Now I've got a word." He wrapped the panels back up, both of them, which meant the plain one went back to the shop as a sample, which meant the amber was going to happen nine times on the paths of this camp, and he did it all without once looking at her directly, and that was the way she preferred it and also, unfortunately, not the way she preferred it at all.

Then, standing up with the tool roll under his arm, he said the other thing. "You keep suits in the bag."

Silena's hands went still on the lantern handle. "What?"

"The bag in the dressing room. Four suits, adult sizes, two small, all rolled, and a jar of goggles. Neve had one on tonight that's got a repair in the shoulder done with silk thread, which is a sewing-thread repair, which means somebody in this cabin mends swimwear with a needle like it's a shirt." He shrugged into his coat. "There's kids on this hill who've got no suits and no niceness about it. You keep four in a bag and you don't say anything. I noticed. That's all."

There was a long moment in the steam in which Silena Beauregard, who could talk her way through a whole pavilion, could not think of one single sentence, and stood there with a lantern in her hand and her hair drying on her shoulders and the black tile behind her going green-gold in the light, and looked at a boy who had noticed the thread in a shoulder of a suit nobody had been asked to look at.

"They're not mine to talk about," she said, at last, and heard how it came out, which was flat and honest and not a joke, which was not how she usually said things.

"Didn't ask you to," Beckendorf said. "I asked where to hang the amber."

"Staggered. Left-odd, right-off, and the odd one at the curve." She got her voice back on the last three words and was very glad of it. "I told you Monday."

"I wrote it down Monday," he said, and went out of the door into the cold, and pulled it to behind him, and the room was the room again, warm and empty and full of somebody's absence, and Silena stood there a good while longer than she needed to with a lantern in her hand.

The heater ticked. The water held still. She looked down at the tile where the amber panel had been, and there was nothing there, no mark, no crumb, nothing at all, and she stood in the middle of a black-tiled hall on the third of October and understood the whole shape of it at once, the way you understand the shape of a chair in the dark.

Then she blew out the lantern and hung it up.

She did not go up to bed.

She went into the dressing room instead and did the thing she always did at the end of a day like that, which was to take inventory of the kindness people had not noticed. Eleven towels on the racks, warm and going cold. The blue bag in the corner with the four spare suits rolled the way she rolled them, smallest at the top. The jar of goggles, minus two cracked pairs, in her pocket, to be fixed on Thursday with the needle and the silk thread and the strip of rubber from the repair kit under her bunk. Three kids from the Demeter cabin, one Hermes boy, one forge girl, all of them in warm water for the first time since August, none of them asked for anything, none of them thanked her, which was not the same as not being grateful and was not the same as not needing it.

The suit with the silk repair was on the left end of the rack, and she took it down and looked at the shoulder in the lamplight for a while. Four stitches and a knot. Left shoulder, where a strap takes weight, where a suit gives out first. She had done that repair in August on a suit belonging to a girl who had outgrown it, and the girl had no idea, and the suit had gone in the bag, and tonight it had been on Neve from the forge.

There was going to be a fifth spare before the frost came. She decided it there, in the steam-smelling dark, with the towel basket on her hip, the way she decided everything: not out loud, not at a meeting, just filed, and then done, and then never spoken of.

Then she gathered the eleven towels, and went up to bed.

Clarisse La Rue was still on the bench by the door at ten, with her jacket on over her suit and the winter list flat on her knee, doing the arithmetic of a good evening, which is harder than the arithmetic of a bad one.

Nine at the count. Eleven towels. Four kids who did not live in this cabin and had no business knowing the hall existed and were going to be back on Thursday, because that is what happens when you leave a door open and put a rule about it on a slate where people can read it.

She wrote *9* at the bottom of the page, and then, because the number was a lie without the rest of it, she wrote under it: *and 4 borrowed. and 1 who can't swim who floated. and O'Fee's opinion pending.*

The door opened at a quarter past ten and the cold came in with hooves in it, and Clarisse did not stand up, because standing up for Chiron was a habit that made the man uncomfortable and Clarisse La Rue did not do things to make people comfortable but she did not do pointless things either.

"Warm," Chiron said, from the doorway, looking in at the steam and the black tile and the water. "It was warm when I was young, too, and then somebody lost the key, and then it was a story we told the twelve-year-olds."

"I found the key in June. It was in the tackle room, in the tin with the boat plugs."

"It has been in that tin since 1953." Chiron wheeled himself in off the threshold, and the wheels left two thin tracks in the condensation on the tile, and he looked around the hall the way Annabeth looked at a building, which was not the way Clarisse had expected him to look at anything. "Who cleaned the leaf-fall out of the deep end?"

"I did."

"With what?"

"A rake and a net and a bucket, and then I shocked it, and then I ran it hot for six hours and skimmed it again." Clarisse kept her eyes on the list. "It's not an approved procedure. The manual's gone. I did it the way I'd do a cistern."

"The way you would do a cistern is correct," Chiron said. "The manual said to drain it, which is why the manual is gone." He stopped in front of the slate and read the five rules, and Clarisse watched him read them and did not move, and when he got to the fifth one he read it, and then read it again, and then said nothing about it, which was the entire reason she respected him.

"Conditions," Chiron said.

"There always are," said Clarisse.

"There always are, and you have pre-empted four of them, which saves us both a quarter of an hour." He produced, from the folds of his coat, the clipboard, which was the same clipboard he had brought down to the north beach, and it had crossed the whole camp's list of permissions, and on the top page was a line in his square hand: TEMPEST HALL — CONDITIONAL. "One: a grown-up knows who is in the water on any given evening. Which means the slate comes up to the porch with the count on it, or it does not open. Two: the deep end is supervised or empty. If nobody senior is in the room, the deep end is a wall."

"Three," Clarisse said, "the harpy."

"The harpy," Chiron agreed, "has been informed, by me, this evening, and has responded by asking whether the hall has a skylight and whether the skylight opens, which I have declined to answer because I do not know, and because I suspect she was asking for reasons I would rather not learn."

"It opens. There's a crank on the wall."

"Then I will not be telling her that." Chiron turned the clipboard over. "You have also, apparently, written the words *and whoever* on a camp document."

"It's my winter list."

"It is a camp document on a clip which I hold at this moment, and it says *and whoever*, which means the hall is not Cabin Three's hall, which is a larger decision than a bench and a list." He let that sit, and the heater ticked, and the water moved along the ladder. "I am not going to unmake it. I am going to ask you once, so that I can write the answer down. Are you opening this because it is good for the camp, or because you cannot stand the eleven weeks until the lake comes back?"

Clarisse looked at her own hands on the list. There were two answers to that question and she had known both of them since Sunday, and she had spent Monday pretending the first one was the only one.

"Both," she said. "The camp part is real. Katie's got six kids under eleven who've never been in warm water in their lives, and the greenhouse kids are soaked through six days a week and go to bed cold, and a door's not a big ask. But I'd have done it anyway. I'm not putting this cabin through November in the dark with no water. I watched what that does to a house once. I'm not doing it here."

Chiron wrote something down, which she could not see, in a hand she could not read, and then he said, "Good. That is the answer I wanted and it is also the answer that means I will have to make up a rotation of grown-ups, which I dislike." He looked up. "Whose idea was the fifth rule?"

"Mine."

"You wrote it last, and you wrote it in a different pen."

"It was a different day," Clarisse said, "and I lost the first one," which was a lie so small and so deliberate that Chiron would have had to insult them both to notice it, and he chose, in the way he chose things, not to.

He went out at twenty past ten, and the cold came in and went out again, and Clarisse sat on the bench a while longer with the hall to herself and the list on her knee.

Percy came down at half past ten with a towel over his shoulder and his sandals on and no coat, because he was fourteen, and stood in the doorway and looked in at the water and at her.

"You're not supposed to be in here alone," he said.

"I'm not in it. I'm on a bench on the dry side, which is a thing the rules allow, which you'd know if you could read."

"I can read. I read the fifth one out loud yesterday and Silena said don't." He came in and sat down on the bench beside her anyway, without being asked, which was a thing he had started doing in September and which she allowed because stopping it would have required a conversation and because it was, in the end, the thing she had spent the summer teaching him.

They sat and looked at the water for a while. It was very good water. It had been a very good evening. Clarisse had said the word *good* out loud exactly zero times, and had written it nowhere, and was aware that this was, in the language of her family, the highest possible praise and that it was also a thing she could not say on pain of being charged for it by everyone who lived with her.

"The hall worked," Percy said.

"The hall worked."

"You know the rule about saying it out loud."

"There is no rule about saying it out loud."

"There is a rule about saying it out loud, and if I say it a third time you'll make me do laps of the shallow end in a coat," Percy said, and she looked at him sideways, and he was grinning the way he grinned when he was not going to be moved, and she let her mouth do the thing it did when she decided not to kill somebody. "You cleaned a year of leaves out of a pool on a Monday and didn't tell anybody and then made a rule about who's allowed in. That's not logistics. That's a family move. Silena's the only other person in this cabin who does that."

"Don't be soft at me on a Tuesday."

"I'm soft at you at least twice a week. You've never once noticed." He stood up and took his towel and went to the door, and then stopped with his hand on the frame and said, without turning around, "Katie asked at the pavilion. About Thursday. She asked me because she didn't want to ask you."

"What did you tell her."

"I told her seven, and bring your own towel, and if she brought eight she should bring eight towels, because the one who counts will know."

"Good," Clarisse said.

"She also asked if it was really for whoever."

"And what did you say."

"I said it's written on the slate," Percy said, "and the slate's the law," and went up to bed, and Clarisse sat on the bench in the steam by herself for another ten minutes, counting nothing at all, which for her was close to a holiday.

Thalia Grace sat on the porch of Cabin One at eleven at night with her heel up on the rail and her survey field book open on her knee and did not go to bed, because she had spent four days on a ridge and had come back with a ridge-shaped hole in her sleeping.

The porch faced downhill, which meant it faced the lake, which at that hour was nothing at all: a black nothing between the black trees with the camp's lamps salted along the paths above it. Four amber lamps so far. Beckendorf had started with the two by the pavilion and the one at the curve, because those were the three places people actually fell over, and Thalia, who had strong opinions about path lighting and had never told anybody, had noticed on the way up that he had staggered them, two and one and two, exactly the way somebody would stagger them who had been told to.

The door opened, and her sister came out with two mugs and a quilt over her arm, in socks, with her hair in the plaited mess of somebody who had given up on it for the night.

"You're still up," Annabeth said.

"I'm being a professional."

"You're avoiding bed. It's different. Professionals sleep." She handed Thalia a mug and put the quilt down on the boards and sat on it with her back against the wall and her feet on the rail beside her sister's heel, and for a while the two of them just drank cocoa on a porch in October, which was, as far as either of them was concerned, the whole point of having a porch.

"I read your copy again," Thalia said. "The slab entry. You wrote *shifted approx. 1 foot since June* and then you put a question mark in the margin."

"I put a question mark because I estimated it with my boots."

"Your boots are a good instrument. I wanted to tell you that. On the ridge I measured half that whole line in boot-lengths because the tape was wet and I trust my feet more than I trust a wet tape, and then I came home and a centaur read it and did not laugh at me, and I have decided that means it was real work."

Annabeth looked at her over the mug. "It was real work. I've been telling you."

"Yes, well. You're my sister. Your opinion is compromised and admissible only in writing." Thalia turned a page. "Saturday."

"Saturday."

"You, me, Marcus with the rod, Olive with the sketchbook, and Percy with the enthusiasm."

"Chiron assigned him."

"Chiron assigned him the way a person assigns themselves a nicer afternoon." Thalia drank her cocoa and grinned into it. "I don't mind. He carries things and he's quiet at the right times and he doesn't fuss when I tell him a bearing. I've had worse survey crews."

"You've had no survey crews."

"Correct, and the worst one was still him on the day he asked me whether north was the one with the moss."

They were still laughing about that when the Hermes kid came up the path.

He was one of the younger ones, eleven or twelve, in a coat two sizes too big, and he was running the last night's deliveries the way the Hermes cabin always did, which was late and cheerful and with a strong sense that the clock was a suggestion. He had a canvas satchel and a lamp, and he stopped at the bottom of the steps and read the two of them off like a clipboard. "Grace, Thalia? Grace, Annabeth?"

"That's us."

"Nothing for you. Two for the cabin, went in the box." He hitched the satchel. "But the news is that we've got a returner, so they said tell the tables. Calder's expected back Friday. Rhys Calder. Apollo. He's been gone a year at a school in Maine the whole time, and he's coming back for the winter."

Thalia took a drink of her cocoa and said, in a voice of absolutely no interest whatsoever, "Who cares."

"He shoots better than anybody," the kid said, with the enormous enthusiasm of a person delivering gossip to the uninterested. "Two summers running he took the whole archery board and left it up a month so everybody had to look at it. My sister hates him."

"Then your sister is correct." Thalia drank. "Who cares."

"He's tall, too."

"Who cares."

"Okay," the kid said, entirely undeterred, and ran off down the path with his lamp swinging, and Thalia Grace sat on the porch of Cabin One and drank three swallows of cocoa and looked out at the black nothing where the lake was.

Annabeth did not say anything for a full minute, which was generous, and then said, in the exact tone she used for reading out a measurement, "You said it three times."

"I said what three times."

"*Who cares.* Three times. In under four seconds." Annabeth drank her cocoa. "I've never said anything three times in my life. If I say a thing once, it's decided. You said it three times, which means the number isn't in the room yet and you're still arguing with the arithmetic."

"You're insufferable."

"I'm accurate. There's a difference and you're the one who taught it to me."

Thalia put her mug down on the rail and pulled her feet in and let her head go back against the wall, and looked up at the porch roof, and then past it, where the sky was clear for the first time in two days and the stars were doing their early-October business over the pines.

"I've spent my whole life being the one who arrives," she said, in a flatter register than the porch had been using. "At every house, at every school, in every room. I'm the one that shows up and stands in the door and does the count and figures out where the exits are and what the grown-ups want to hear. He's supposed to walk in here Friday and he's going to be somebody who *gets back*, which is a thing I've never once been, and everybody's going to look at him like he did something."

"He went to school."

"He went to school and he came back," Thalia said. "That's the whole trick. Half these kids can't. Half of them get home for a summer and never come back to the hill and we get a letter and then the letter stops, and we put them in the pile and say *they went home*. Somebody who goes away and turns around and comes here on purpose — " she tipped her head back down and looked at her sister — "that's not a rumor, it's a statement. That's a person making a claim about where they belong. I don't know what to do with that and I'm not going to practice on Friday."

Annabeth thought about it, the way she thought about a contour, wanting the true line.

"Then don't," she said.

"That's it? Don't?"

"You've got four days," Annabeth said. "You'll know what to do on Friday when you see him, because that's how you actually work, and the part where you rehearse being unbothered is the part that costs you sleep. Meanwhile, it's Saturday that matters, and I want you rested for it, because I am going to be standing on a ridge with a rod and a book in October and I need the person up there with me to be the one who sees things, not the one who's been awake for three nights writing a speech about a boy."

Thalia was quiet a while.

"Two days," she said at last. "I'll be asleep tonight."

"See that you are." Annabeth stood and gathered the cups, then stopped in the doorway with her hand on the door and said, without looking back, "Also, mail day's Thursday. If he's back for the winter, then he wrote to somebody before he came, and it comes up the hill in a bag with everybody else's, and you'll be standing right there when the Hermes kid reads his name out. Plan for that instead — if he's back on Friday, the letter will already be a day old by then."

"You're a menace."

"I'm a cartographer. I plan for terrain nobody can see yet." The door shut, and the lamp in the window went out, and Thalia Grace sat on the porch in the cold a while longer with her heel up on the rail, looking north where the ridge was, which she could not see at all and knew exactly, and did not once say anything out loud.

Percy woke at three in the morning because the cabin was warm, which the cabin had never once been in the middle of the night since June.

He lay in his bunk a while with his eyes open, listening to it. There was the soft tick of the heater through the tile in the passage, going on and off in slow long intervals. There was the sound of water moving very slightly, which was a sound this cabin had never made at three in the morning, and which pulled up a feeling he associated with the deep palace: stone holding warmth, water nearby, the impulse to listen. It gave him no age, room, or person on the other side of a wall.

He got up, because there was no version of him that lay there listening.

The passage was warm. The iron door was warm. He stood in front of it with one hand flat on the iron. The gesture felt familiar in the muscles of his palm, but it gave him no scene to set it in.

He did not open it. He didn't need to. Behind it the hall was holding the whole evening in the dark: nine kids, four borrowed, one who couldn't swim who floated, a girl with a rake in October, a boy with two rivets in a pan and nine lanterns yet to hang, an elevator of wrong numbers climbing a tile wall that somebody was going to fix in the winter with a plumb line.

The smell was coming under the door in a thin clean thread of warm salt.

Percy stayed with his palm on the iron door a moment longer. The heated saltwater of the hall moved against its tiled basin on the other side. It wasn't the freshwater canoe lake, and it wasn't the halls of Atlantis, but there were ways in which the sound joined both places in his mind.

He checked the latch before he left. It had not seated all the way, and a thin line of warm air was escaping into the passage. He eased it shut until he heard the click. The air no longer moved past his hand.

At the foot of the stairs he stopped again, irritated with himself. He had come out because a noise had reminded him of a place he could not fully remember in this life. He had nearly forgotten the only job that needed doing, which was closing a door before the hall lost its heat.

Silena would have had an opinion about that. Clarisse would have put the latch on her inspection list. He could imagine the difference between their reactions clearly enough to smile.

He went back to his bunk without waking either sister. The blanket had slipped off the mattress while he was gone; he picked it up and shook the corner free of the bedframe before pulling it over his feet.

In the morning somebody would want the hall checked before the next swim, and he would tell Clarisse the latch needed attention. For now he could let the water stay where it belonged. He was tired, the door was secure, and no one had called him to a problem he had to solve in the dark.
