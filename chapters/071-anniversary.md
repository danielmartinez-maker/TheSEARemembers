## Chapter 71: Anniversary

Percy Jackson woke up on the day of his belated arrival-anniversary celebration with seawater in his shoes.

That wasn't a metaphor. He sat up in his bunk in Cabin Three, blinked at the thin gold light coming through the seashell window, and felt a distinct squelch when he swung his feet down. His sneakers were full of water. Again.

"Ha, ha," he said loudly to the quiet cabin. "Very funny. Tyson, if that's you—"

It wasn't Tyson. Tyson was snoring in the guest hammock beside the window, a mountain under a blue blanket, one huge foot sticking out. There was a little puddle trail leading from the doorway to Percy's bunk, and a single piece of seaweed draped over his alarm conch like a party streamer.

Percy stared at it. Then he smiled in spite of himself. Fifteen months ago, in January, he had come up from the harbor with Grover, salt in his hair, a bag full of palace clothes, and no idea how to explain the life he remembered. Annabeth had found him later by the lake: his own girlfriend, twelve again, looking at him with recognition and disbelief tangled together. They had remembered each other when nothing else fit. Even then she had found time to remind him of their original first meeting.

"You drool when you sleep," Annabeth Grace had said, quoting her younger self from that other life.

"Some things never change," Percy had croaked.

Now, more than a year later, he had his own cabin and his own bunk and seawater in his shoes. Growth.

He dumped the water out the window, pulled on a dry pair of socks, and checked the little driftwood calendar Annabeth had made him last winter. She'd carved it herself, neat Athenian letters burned into the wood. Today's square had a tiny trident in blue ink, circled three times.

*Year one,* she'd written underneath in her small, precise handwriting. *Celebrated at last. You stayed.* January had gone by amid schoolwork and winter drills, with her gift still unfinished. Today was the day they'd finally made room for the party.

He touched the carving with his thumb. His chest felt tight in a good way, like right before a wave breaks.

Outside, the camp was already awake. It was April, that uneven spring stretch where the mornings were cool and a sunny afternoon could turn honey-thick, and everybody tried to finish schoolwork fast enough to get outside. The grass on Half-Blood Hill was wet with dew. The strawberry fields smelled sweet and sharp. Down at the canoe lake, mist was burning off the water.

Percy took the long way to breakfast, because on a day like this he wanted to remember everything. Past the cabins — all quiet, shutters closed, except the Hermes cabin where someone was already shouting about a missing shoe. Past the forge, where the smoke was just starting to curl. Past the climbing wall, which was dripping lava for morning practice and hissing where the lava hit the dew.

More than a year. He'd arrived here from Atlantis with Grover showing him the path and Amphitrite's goodbye still in his ears. The Minotaur's horn belonged to his remembered original arrival, not this one. He'd been twelve again and furious and terrified, sure the gods had made a clerical error. Two lives in one head? With *him*?

Now he was thirteen. He had a cabin that smelled like salt and cedar. He had a best friend who was a satyr and a brother who was a Cyclops and a pegasus who liked sugar cubes. He had calluses from sword practice and a scar on his shoulder from summer spear drills. The Underworld heat had blistered his palms during the quest, but the memory that stayed was Annabeth's hand finding his in the dark and the way they had held on.

He had Annabeth.

He found her waiting for him on the Big House porch steps, the way she had so many mornings through the winter and spring. She had her notebook on her knees, her pencil behind her ear, her camp necklace with four beads catching the light. Her hair was pulled back in a messy ponytail, curls escaping everywhere, and there was a smudge of ink on her cheek.

She looked up when she heard him, and smiled — that quick, bright, real smile she only gave him — and Percy's heart did the stupid, swoopy thing it had been doing for months.

"Hey, Seaweed Brain," she said.

"Hey, Wise Girl." He sat down next to her, close enough that their shoulders touched. "Did you put seawater in my shoes?"

Annabeth blinked, all innocence. "Would I do that?"

"Yes."

"Well, I didn't. This time." She bumped his shoulder. "Happy camp-iversary."

He grinned. "You remembered."

"Of course I remembered, Percy. It's—" She stopped, looked down at her notebook, and flipped it shut a little too fast. "It's a big day."

Percy leaned in. "What was that? Were you drawing? Let me see."

"No!" Annabeth hugged the notebook to her chest. Her ears went pink, which Percy had learned meant she was either embarrassed or plotting something, and often both. "It's nothing. Just — camp stuff."

"Annabeth Grace, are you hiding something from me?"

"Me? Hide something? Never." She stood up abruptly, hauling him up with her. "Come on. You're going to be late for breakfast. And you need to — uh — you need to go to sword practice early today. Chiron wanted you at the arena at nine. Special lesson."

Percy frowned. "Chiron never does special lessons before breakfast. He says fighting on an empty stomach leads to poor decisions and vomiting."

"Well, today he does. Go." She gave him a little push toward the dining pavilion. "I'll catch up. I have to — help with Athena cabin inspection."

"You already did inspection yesterday."

"Surprise inspection. Double inspection. Bye!" She kissed him very quickly on the cheek — so fast he almost missed it — and darted off toward the Athena cabin, notebook clutched tight, ponytail bouncing.

Percy stood there touching his cheek and feeling deeply suspicious.

By lunch, he was certain something was going on.

First, Grover intercepted him on the way out of the arena, wearing a party hat hidden under a large straw hat and smelling strongly of frosting.

"Hey, man!" Grover said too loudly, throwing an arm around Percy's shoulders and steering him firmly away from the Big House. "Hey, buddy, pal, best friend of more than a year, finally getting a party today, wow, crazy, time flies! How about we go check the woods? I heard there's a really interesting moss. Really — really boring moss. Takes hours to look at. Let's go look at it for hours."

"Grover," Percy said, "are you wearing a party hat under that hat?"

"What? No. This is — this is a moss-looking hat. For moss."

"Grover."

"Okay, yes, it's a party hat, but it's not what you think! It's — it's for a goat thing! Goat — graduation! I graduated from moss!" Grover's eyes darted sideways. "Please come look at moss with me for the next six hours?"

Percy stared at him. "No."

"Percy—"

"No. Whatever it is, no. I'm going to find Annabeth."

He found Thalia instead, which was almost as good. She was up on Half-Blood Hill, sitting under the boundary pine with her bow across her knees, fletching arrows. Her own camp chores could wait a little longer; her black hair was spiked with silver, her silver hairband glinting. When she saw Percy, she grinned wide.

"There's the anniversary boy," she said. "Your first year survived, and a few bonus months. New record."

"Hey!" Percy flopped down in the grass beside her. "I've been trying very hard not to die. It's a lifestyle choice."

"And yet you still drool." Thalia nudged him with her boot. "Annabeth told me about the infirmary. First words she said to you in the old life were about drool. Romance for the ages."

Percy laughed. "We tell that old story differently every time. Last time she said my first words to her were 'are you a goddess?'"

"Were they?"

"No! I said 'where am I and why does my everything hurt?' Which is basically the same thing." He leaned back on his elbows, looking out over the valley. Camp spread below them, bright and busy in the noon sun. "You think she remembers? Like, really remembers? The day I got here this time?"

Thalia was quiet for a moment, turning an arrow over in her hands. "Yeah," she said, softer. "I think she does."

She checked the fletching against her thumb and set the arrow across her knees. Percy waited for her to make another joke about his sleeping habits, but she didn't.

"Did she tell you about the first night?" Thalia asked.

Percy looked toward Cabin One. "Which first night?"

"Here. After you got off the boat." Thalia corrected herself. "After Grover brought you up from the harbor. She was sitting in our cabin trying to draw a floor plan and she had written the same measurement four times. Every number was different."

"That sounds terrible for a floor plan."

"It was. She'd put the door inside the wall." Thalia smiled, then let the smile go. "I thought she was upset because she'd met somebody new. One of her friends from the city, maybe. I couldn't understand why one boy getting to camp had shaken her up so badly. She wouldn't tell me most of it."

Percy studied the string on her bow. It was recently waxed, a small uneven patch shining where she'd passed the wax over twice.

"She was trying not to tell you something that wasn't hers alone to explain," he said.

"I know that now." Thalia turned the arrow over. "I didn't then. I was her sister. I thought that entitled me to be the person she told everything."

"It doesn't."

"No." She gave him a flat look. "You don't get to sound so satisfied about that. I learned it the hard way."

Percy raised both hands. "I wasn't gloating."

"Your eyebrows were."

He touched his eyebrows. "They have an independent political agenda."

That earned half a laugh. Thalia looked down the slope where the younger campers had begun a game with wooden swords. One girl was trying to convince a boy twice her size to switch weapons. The boy kept shaking his head.

"She did tell me you were important," Thalia continued. "Not why. Not all the reasons. Just that she'd known you before you got here, and that she needed me not to turn it into a fight."

"You weren't exactly thrilled with me."

"I was furious. She was twelve and acting like she knew you better than she knew me. And I was the one who'd been standing beside her at school and camp and every stupid meeting with our father. I knew where she kept the spare pencils. I knew she only ate the crust of the bread when she was anxious. I knew she'd wake up when the wind changed, before the thunder started. Then you walked in and she looked at you like you'd brought the sun back."

Percy felt the old, uncomfortable pinch behind his ribs. Thalia had lived a sisterhood with Annabeth that Annabeth couldn't remember from inside. It wasn't a lie, and it wasn't an inconvenience the universe could simply apologize for.

"She does know you," he said. "Better than she did then."

"Yeah." Thalia scraped a thumb against the leather wrapping of her bow. "She asks me stuff now. Not just the big dramatic stuff. Dumb stuff. Whether a shirt looks stupid. If a headache's going to turn into a storm. Whether Jason would like a drawing of our cabin more than a drawing of New Rome."

"What did you tell her?"

"That Jason would probably like both. Then I asked him in a letter, because I'm developing a personality outside of guessing."

Percy grinned. "Dangerous."

"Extremely. Next I'll be using complete sentences."

A gust lifted the grass in a green ripple. Down by the archery targets a counselor called for a cease-fire, and the campers lowered their bows.

Thalia slid an arrow into her quiver. "So when everybody started putting the party together, I thought she might hate the idea."

"Why?"

"Because it's hers too, isn't it? The day you arrived here. She'd spent all those weeks trying to make sense of you being back and her life being different. I thought an entire camp shouting about it might feel like someone taking that day away from her."

Percy stopped picking at the grass. Until then, he'd thought of the anniversary as something Annabeth had invented for him, a gift as uncomplicated as the driftwood calendar. He hadn't considered that she'd had to share its most private meaning with people who understood only parts of the story.

"Did you ask her?" he said.

"Obviously."

"What'd she say?"

"She said the day belongs to both of you, but she wanted the party to be about the year that followed. About the things everybody here can actually remember. She gave the Stolls three versions of the arrival and told them only one was permitted on the official banner. They made a fourth anyway."

"That sounds more like her."

"Yeah. And she told me not to tell you any of this." Thalia grimaced. "I've already failed at being a co-conspirator."

"You haven't told me what you got me."

"I didn't get you anything."

Percy narrowed his eyes. "Really?"

"Really. I'm going to give you a lecture about posture and call it a gift."

"You're a terrible person."

"You love me."

"I tolerate you for your access to juice boxes."

She punched his arm gently, but her next words came without the joke he expected.

"Percy, when she says she remembers, don't make her prove it. She already knows which life the story belongs to. Sometimes she just wants to be happy about this one."

He looked at the camp, at the walkways and green roofs and the bit of bright water between the cabins. He had spent so long guarding the things he and Annabeth remembered that he sometimes treated remembering as a test. If she could recall the exact sound of a subway, the color of Sally's kitchen walls, the last snowfall before the war, then the first life would still be real. When a detail blurred, he panicked.

"I don't ask her to prove it," he said.

Thalia lifted an eyebrow.

"Not on purpose."

"That's better."

He took a stone from the grass and rolled it over his knuckles. "I asked her last week whether she remembered what color the blanket was in the Athena cabin. The one she used when we stayed up studying. She said green. I thought it was blue."

"And then?"

"I kept asking. Like if we argued long enough one of us would remember correctly."

"How'd that go?"

"She got annoyed. Said the blanket had been ugly no matter what color it was and maybe there had been two."

Thalia's mouth twitched. "Wise girl."

"Yeah." Percy stared at the stone. "I apologized. Eventually."

"Well, look at you, growing as a person."

"Don't spread it around. It'll ruin my reputation."

Thalia stood and offered him a hand. He let her pull him up. His legs had gone numb from sitting in one position, and he staggered a little.

"Come on," she said. "I'll walk you down. You look like someone who's about to interrupt a surprise because he can't stand not knowing."

"Would I do that?"

"Yes."

"Fair."

They took the narrow path along the strawberry fields instead of the main track. Thalia was too experienced at surprises to march him straight to the Big House. She talked about a snapped bowstring and an upcoming inspection of the west trail, small ordinary things that made it easier to pretend this wasn't an extraordinary day.

At the point where the path forked, they found Beckendorf standing beside a handcart with a wheel off. His work shirt was dark with sweat despite the cool morning, and a pile of boxes rested on the cart's wooden bed.

"Morning," he said. "Either of you free for a minute?"

Thalia looked at the boxes and then at Percy, making a decision he wasn't supposed to notice.

"Percy has an appointment," she said.

"I do?"

"You do."

Beckendorf shrugged. "Then no problem. I'll get the wheel back on."

Percy was already kneeling beside the cart. The axle pin had slipped halfway out and the retaining clip was bent open. He could see the problem even without Beckendorf pointing it out.

"It'll take two minutes," he said.

"The boxes can wait," Beckendorf replied.

"So can whatever Chiron has invented to make me miserable."

Thalia made a noise suspiciously like a swallowed laugh. She stood back while Percy held the cart steady and Beckendorf pressed the wheel into position. The first clip broke when they tried to close it. Beckendorf fetched a spare from his apron pocket.

"Don't use the first one just because it's almost good enough," he told Percy.

Percy thought of the little memorial board Annabeth had been measuring the previous afternoon, the one that couldn't go up until a brace was repaired. "You talking about this cart or the memorial fence?"

"Both, if you're listening."

"How's the brace?"

"On tomorrow's repair list. A notice isn't a repair." Beckendorf passed him the cotter pin. "Hold that still."

Percy kept the axle straight while Beckendorf seated the pin. The wheel turned freely when they tested it.

"Thanks," Beckendorf said. "You get a cookie when I deliver these."

"Deliver them where?"

"Somewhere with cookies." Beckendorf pulled the cart onto the path and pushed it away before Percy could ask anything else.

Thalia was shaking her head.

"What?"

"You are the only person I know who can be distracted from his own party by a cotter pin."

"Counterpoint. The cart really needed a cotter pin."

"And now it has one. Miraculous."

They reached the dining pavilion, where Percy could hear Grover arguing with someone about frosting. He looked back toward the Big House. The windows were closed. One had a blue streamer caught in its latch.

Thalia noticed where he was looking.

"You can wait five more minutes," she said.

"I'm not impatient."

"Percy."

"Okay, I'm a little impatient."

She tucked her bow under one arm, steadied his shoulder with the other hand, and turned him toward the pavilion.

"You get one surprise a year," she said. "Let the people who care about you do it properly."

He let her steer him inside. She was wrong about one thing, though. This year he'd already been surprised by too many people to count. None of them had demanded he know exactly how to deserve it.

---

Annabeth remembered everything about that day, which was the problem, because everybody else kept mixing it up with the original arrival she and Percy had described. This camp had seen a boy walk up from the harbor. Somehow, in more than a year of retelling, that boy had acquired the Minotaur again.

"No, listen," Travis Stoll said, arms waving, standing on a chair in the Big House rec room with a half-hung banner in one hand. "The way I heard it, Percy showed up riding the Minotaur like a surfboard. Like, he was standing on its back, holding the horn he'd ripped off, screaming 'COWABUNGA.'"

"That is not what happened," Annabeth said, hands on her hips, trying not to laugh. She had blue paint on her fingers and a streak of it in her hair. Around her, the rec room was chaos — streamers, balloons, a table covered in presents, Silena directing the Aphrodite kids like a general. "He did not surf the Minotaur in either life."

"He did surf the Minotaur a little," Connor Stoll said helpfully from the floor, where he was blowing up balloons. "Grover told me what Percy told him."

"Grover exaggerates when he's nervous."

"So do you," Silena called sweetly from the ladder, where she was hanging paper lanterns shaped like jellyfish. She winked at Annabeth. "You said he was cute when he was unconscious."

"I did not— I said he looked *dead*, which was concerning—"

"You said, and I quote," Clarisse rumbled from the corner, where she was trying to tie a balloon and making it look like a threat, "'he's got nice eyes for a drowned rat.'" She tied off the balloon with a snap. "I was there. By the lake. Looking for my brother. I heard you."

The whole room erupted. Annabeth covered her face with her hands. Her ears were burning.

"Okay! Okay, time check!" She clapped, paint and all. "Percy will be done with Grover's moss distraction in twenty minutes. Is the cake here?"

"Tyson's bringing it up from the kitchens!" Silena said. "He said he wanted to add something special. I told him no lava, extra frosting, and he looked very disappointed, so expect lava."

"And the gifts?" Annabeth asked.

Clarisse jerked her chin at a lumpy package wrapped in brown forge paper on the gift table. "Ours is there. Don't shake it. It's got sharp bits."

"And Thalia—?"

"Thalia's got her speech," Travis said. "She's been practicing on the hill. She made Chris cry. Chris doesn't cry."

Annabeth took a breath and looked around the room. The banner read HAPPY CAMP-IVVERSARY PERCY! in wobbly blue and green letters — Connor had insisted on the pun and now everyone loved it. There were streamers in sea-green and storm-gray. Somebody — probably Tyson — had built a little wave sculpture out of chicken wire and tissue paper. On the wall, the Aphrodite kids had hung a poster board titled PERCY: YEAR ONE (AND COUNTING, WITH PICTURES). It had a sketch of Percy as a soggy twelve-year-old coming up the harbor path, a sketch of Percy falling off the climbing wall, a sketch of Percy asleep in his mashed potatoes at the dining pavilion, and, in the center, a sketch Silena had done from their descriptions: Percy and Annabeth on the quest bridge, hands linked, back to back with swords out.

Underneath, Silena had written in looping script: *He stayed.*

Annabeth's throat went tight. She touched the edge of the poster gently.

"Hey." Silena came down the ladder and slipped an arm around her shoulders. "You okay, Wise Girl?"

"Yeah." Annabeth blinked fast. "Just — when he first arrived he didn't want to be here. Remember? He tried to run away twice in the first week."

"And you dragged him back both times," Silena said, smiling.

"Somebody had to. He kept forgetting these arms didn't have his old reach. He held the sword like it weighed twice as much." Annabeth laughed, a little wet. "He asked me if the gods had finally got email."

"He called Ares 'the war guy with the farming vocabulary,'" Clarisse said, coming over with a balloon for each hand. She said it gruffly, but her expression had gone soft in a way Clarisse would deny under oath. "Dumbest new kid I ever met. Couldn't even insult me right. Called me 'Claire.'"

"To be fair," Annabeth said, "you punched him into the lake five minutes later, so he learned fast."

Clarisse snorted. "Best decision I ever made. Kid needed baptizing."

They all laughed, and Annabeth felt that warm, fizzy feeling she got sometimes at camp — like sparkling cider in her chest. More than a year. A whole first year of Percy learning his younger reach again, of Percy learning who lived in each cabin now, of Percy feeding Blackjack sugar cubes and teaching Tyson to high-five and falling asleep on Annabeth's shoulder during war council meetings and waking up with drool on her sleeve and saying, every single time, "I was resting my eyes."

A year of him staying.

Annabeth had almost abandoned the card project on the second Tuesday in February.

She remembered it because Malcolm had been wearing his green sweater inside out, and because a cold rain had started halfway through their meeting in the workshop. The table was covered with tiny squares of paper, inks, a magnifying lens borrowed from the library and two cards that had come by post from a collector in New Jersey. They were creased at the corners, the little painted monsters softened by years of handling.

Malcolm slid one across the table. "That's the right edition, I think."

Annabeth checked the printing mark. "It's missing a digit."

"Because the corner's worn off."

"Or because it's a later reprint." She turned the card under the lamp and examined the edge again. "He told us it was an original."

"He told us he thought it was an original. The difference is six dollars and a lot of disappointment if you're planning to accuse a stranger of cheating."

Annabeth put the card down. Malcolm had not asked what the gift was supposed to fix. He knew it was for Percy, that it involved a game Percy liked, and that Annabeth had persuaded him to help because he knew more about restoration inks than anyone else available. He had never asked why she cared whether the card matched a deck Percy couldn't produce.

She loved him a little for that.

"It's the wrong shade of green," she said.

"Then it's the wrong shade of green. Does Percy care?"

"I don't know."

Malcolm stopped sorting the sleeves. "You usually know what Percy cares about."

"That's the problem."

He looked at her over the magnifying lens. Rain ticked against the workshop window.

Annabeth folded her hands beneath the table. For three months she'd been sending polite letters to collectors and not-so-polite follow-ups to people who promised a response and then forgot. She had paid for one package with chores, traded a week of library duty for another, and walked to the camp post office twice in a storm because somebody had reported a delay. Each day brought another nearly-right thing. A spell card with a name spelled differently. A hero whose statistics had changed between editions. A water-damaged deck box with a smell so strong it made her think of wet schoolbooks.

And at the back of her mind was an apartment that wasn't there.

She had only seen pieces of Sally's home, in the life she remembered: the kitchen where Percy had offered her burnt pancakes, a little shelf of blue plates, a battered table with a wobbling leg. Percy had described where the cards had lived in a drawer and how Sally had occasionally tried to learn the rules. He remembered ordinary afternoons with such sharp affection that Annabeth wanted to return one intact.

But the card on the table had the wrong green.

"He'll recognize that it isn't the same," she said.

"Well, it isn't the same."

"I know."

"Do you?"

She was annoyed at him for asking it so simply. "I didn't come here to be psychoanalyzed."

"Good. I'm terrible at it. I barely passed the unit on the brain." Malcolm pushed the card back to the middle of the table. "I'm trying to build you a present. I need to know whether we're making a replica that will disappoint you or a new deck that Percy can actually play with."

Annabeth looked at the mismatched cards. The problem was that the answer hurt.

"I wanted it to look like his old one."

"You can keep some of that. But if we put your friends on cards, it's already going to be different." He tapped the unfinished sheet beside him, where Silena had painted Tyson grinning with his helmet tilted over one eye. "You asked for this, right?"

"Yes."

"Then tell me which part you're trying to preserve."

Annabeth found it difficult to speak for a moment. She took off the cap she'd worn into the workshop and set it beside the ink bottles.

"When everything changed, Percy was twelve and he knew things that hadn't happened," she said carefully. That much was not a new disclosure; Malcolm knew she and Percy had difficult memories of a life they discussed with one another, though he had never been invited into their full account. "People here knew him as someone else. They expected him to remember all sorts of things. I kept thinking that if I could find one thing from before, something stupid and ordinary, he wouldn't have to explain himself every time he looked at it."

Malcolm's expression softened. "Does he have to explain himself to you?"

"No."

"Then maybe you don't need to make him a perfect museum piece. Maybe you need to make him something he can spill lemonade on."

She stared at him.

"Not my first choice of conservation method," Malcolm admitted. "But I know Percy."

Annabeth laughed reluctantly.

She took a clean sleeve and slipped the mismatched card into it. The green was still wrong. The name was still a little blurred. It didn't stop the card from being itself.

"All right," she said. "A deck he can use. We keep the real cards that we find. We say which ones are restored and which ones we invented. No pretending the new ones were always there."

"Now you're giving me a design brief."

"You should feel honored."

"I do not. You keep moving the deadline."

She handed him a pencil. "That's what professionals call iteration."

He threw an empty sleeve at her.

They stayed until the rain stopped, sorting and writing notes. Annabeth drew up a clean inventory so nobody would have to guess which card belonged to which edition. When Silena arrived after dinner with the finished portraits, she asked before opening the folder, and Annabeth was careful to ask which paintings could be displayed at the party. Silena had agreed to make them as a gift, not to hand over every sketch she'd tried along the way. Annabeth had been reminded of that distinction very recently by Clarisse's black notebook.

Silena laid Tyson's card in the center of the table. Malcolm held it up to the light.

"He really looks like he's going to break the frame," he said.

"He wanted his attack number to have seven zeros," Silena told them.

"He asked me for twelve," Annabeth said.

"Compromise," Malcolm said, writing ten more little zeros on a scrap.

Annabeth looked at the bright face on the card and thought of Percy at the dining pavilion when Tyson arrived, pretending not to watch as his brother tried to decide where to sit. That day, Percy had moved over before anyone needed to ask. She could remember that with certainty. The color of a blanket from a different life could be wrong. This wasn't.

She stopped calling the deck a replacement after that.

Now, in the crowded rec room, she had the completed cards hidden beneath her notebook, their edges squared and sleeved. There was a list inside identifying the hand-painted additions and who had worked on them, so Percy could ask questions and receive real answers. If he noticed that one monster's claws were painted too many or that a familiar character had a different number, she would not correct his memory on the basis of a collector's guess.

The gift was an invitation to play.

"Okay," Annabeth said, wiping her eyes and picking up her notebook. Tucked inside the back cover was a flat, carefully wrapped package, no bigger than a paperback. She'd spent three months on it, trading favors all over camp, writing letters to libraries and collectors, begging the Athena kids for restoration help. "Places, everyone. Silena, lights? Connor, door? Thalia—"

As if summoned, the door banged open and Thalia stuck her head in, hair wild from running down the hill. "He ditched Grover! He's heading for the dining pavilion! We have like five minutes!"

"Battle stations!" Travis shouted.

The room exploded into motion. Annabeth dove behind the makeshift curtain with the cake table, heart hammering. She clutched her gift to her chest. Through a gap in the curtain she could see the door, and she heard it — Percy's voice, confused, and Grover's say-nothing bleating, and then—

"Surprise!"

---

Percy had never been surprised less successfully and loved it more.

He pushed open the door of the Big House rec room, expecting — he didn't know what, maybe Chiron with extra sword drills, maybe Mr. D with another complaint about his existence — and instead got hit with a wall of shouting and confetti and off-key party horns.

"SURPRISE! HAPPY CAMP-IVVERSARY!"

He stood frozen in the doorway, mouth open, while half of Camp Half-Blood screamed at him. The room was strung with blue and green streamers. Balloons bobbed everywhere. There was a banner with his name on it, spelled right on the second try — someone had crossed out PERSY and written PERCY above it in glitter. Tyson stood behind a massive sheet cake shaped like a wave, wearing a party hat balanced between his eyebrows, beaming so hard Percy thought his face would split.

"Surprise!" Tyson boomed, loudest of all. "ONE YEAR OF PERCY!"

Percy laughed — he couldn't help it — big and startled and helpless. "You guys— what— Grover said there was moss—"

"There was moss," Grover said solemnly from behind him, nudging him inside. "Very distracting moss. You're welcome."

Annabeth emerged from behind the curtain, paint in her hair, cheeks pink, smiling like sunrise. She had something flat wrapped in cloth clutched behind her back. Percy looked at her, at the room, at all these people who'd stopped their day to fill his shoes with seawater and hang jellyfish lanterns, and his throat closed up.

"You guys did this? For me?" he said, quietly, because loudly wasn't working.

"Duh, Seaweed Brain," Annabeth said, and her voice wobbled just a little. "One year. You have to celebrate surviving us."

The room cheered. Somebody shoved a party hat on his head — it said BIRTHDAY BOY with BIRTHDAY crossed out and CAMP stenciled in. Somebody else — Chris, laughing — draped a lei made of seashells and blue candy around his neck. The Stolls pelted him with confetti. Clarisse, to Percy's utter shock, clapped him on the shoulder hard enough to rattle his teeth and said, "Don't get mushy, Jackson. Cake first, feelings later."

"There's cake?" Percy said.

"There's cake," Tyson confirmed proudly, gesturing like a game-show host. "I made it! Vanilla with lemon! With blue frosting! And waves! And no lava! Silena said no lava!"

Everybody applauded. Tyson bowed.

What followed was the kind of happy chaos Percy had dreamed about before he'd ever known the word demigod. They ate cake off paper plates — blue frosting everywhere, Percy got it on his nose in the first minute and Annabeth wiped it off with her thumb while everyone made kissing noises. They played the retelling game, where each cabin told the story of Percy's arrival, and each version got more ridiculous.

Hermes cabin went first. Travis stood on the table, Minotaur horn prop made from a traffic cone held aloft.

"And so," he declaimed, "Percy Jackson, son of Poseidon, Lord of the Seas, rode up Half-Blood Hill on a hellhound, doing kickflips—"

"I arrived with Grover while trying not to throw up," Percy corrected, laughing. "There were no kickflips."

"Lies," Connor said. "Kickflips happened."

The Ares cabin was next. Clarisse stood for them and Cabin Three, arms crossed, deadpan, on longstanding joint-drill authority.

"I punched him into the lake," she said. "That was the whole story. Best day of my life. The end."

The room howled. Percy clutched his stomach. "It's true! I was there! I lost a shoe! The naiads laughed at me!"

"You still lose shoes," Annabeth said fondly. She was sitting beside him on the floor, legs tucked up, plate balanced on her knees, shoulder pressed to his. She hadn't stopped smiling. "First week, he lost three shoes. Three. I started labeling them."

"Hey, that lake is hungry," Percy protested. "It eats shoes. It's science."

Athena cabin's version was a three-act play with footnotes, performed by Malcolm, which concluded that Percy was "statistically unlikely but narratively inevitable." Apollo cabin sang theirs. Tyson's version was just him hugging Percy around the middle, lifting him off the ground, and shouting, "AND THEN MY BROTHER CAME TO CAMP!" which got the biggest cheer of all.

Through it all, Percy kept looking around — at Grover harmonizing badly with the Apollo kids, at Chiron smiling from the doorway in his wheelchair, at Mr. D pretending not to care while eating a second slice of cake, at Thalia laughing so hard she had to sit on the floor, at Silena leaning her head on Clarisse's shoulder while Clarisse pretended not to notice.

He leaned into Annabeth and whispered, "Did you plan all this?"

"Mostly Silena," she whispered back. "But I helped. Are you mad?"

He looked at her. Paint in her hair. Frosting on her chin. Gray eyes bright as storm glass.

"I'm the opposite of mad," he said. "I don't think there's a word for it."

"There is," she said softly. "It's called happy, Seaweed Brain."

Before he could answer, Thalia stood up, tapping her goblet with a spoon. The room quieted — Thalia had that effect, even without trying. She looked around, suddenly nervous, turning her goblet in her hands.

"Okay," she said. "Okay, so I'm not good at speeches. Jason's the speech guy. I'm the punch-things guy. But Annabeth said I had to, so—" She glanced at Annabeth, who nodded encouragingly. "So. Percy."

Percy sat up straighter. Tyson gave him an encouraging squeeze that nearly cracked his ribs.

"When he arrived," Thalia said, "I was here, giving Chiron a headache and trying to keep Annabeth from dismantling the lake pavilion. When Percy arrived, everybody started telling me about this new Poseidon kid. 'He's brave,' they said. 'He's loyal.' Grover said, 'He's really good at getting into trouble I have to get him out of.'" Laughter. Grover bleated protest. "Annabeth wrote me a note — she actually used paper, guys, instead of shouting across our cabin — and she said, 'There's this boy, and he's an idiot, and he might be the best person I know.'"

Annabeth made a small mortified noise. Percy stared at her. "You wrote that?"

"I was twelve! Shut up!"

Thalia grinned. "So I met him. And yeah, he was an idiot. He challenged Ares' cabin to a capture-the-flag rematch with no plan. He tried to feed a pegasus pizza. He fell asleep during my tragic backstory." More laughter. Percy covered his face. "But here's the thing. I spent years being angry — at the gods, at my mom, at Luke, at everybody. I thought growing up meant getting harder." Her voice steadied. Her blue eyes were bright and fierce. "Then I watched Percy. This kid who lost his mom, who had a dad pulled between camp and the sea court, who got thrown into a war he didn't start — and he just kept choosing people. Every time. He chose Grover in the woods. He chose Annabeth on the quest. He chose Tyson when everybody else stared. He chose camp, over and over, even when camp was hard."

The room had gone very quiet. Percy felt Annabeth's hand find his under the table and grip tight.

"Growth isn't getting harder," Thalia said. "It's staying soft when the world gives you every reason not to. Percy stayed soft. He stayed kind. And he made the rest of us softer too." She lifted her goblet. "To Percy — one year down. Thanks for making this place feel like home again. Even for a storm girl."

"To Percy!" everyone shouted, goblets and paper cups raised. Percy felt his eyes sting. He tried to blink it away and failed completely.

"Speech!" Travis shouted. "Anniversary boy has to give a speech!"

"No—" Percy started, but they were all chanting his name, and Tyson was chanting loudest, and Annabeth was looking at him with that look that meant *you can do this*, so he stood up, wobbly, frosting on his shirt, party hat crooked.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. I'm also bad at speeches. I'm bad at a lot of things. Ask Annabeth, she has a list." Laughter. Annabeth elbowed him. "Last January I came up from the harbor and Annabeth reminded me I drooled, and I thought, great, I'm going to die here and my girlfriend has brought a checklist to supervise my collapse." He smiled down at her. "I was half right. She is scary. She does have a checklist. But I didn't die. Because every time I tried to be alone — and I tried, a lot, that first month — somebody showed up. Grover showed up with enchiladas. Tyson showed up with — actually he just showed up and wouldn't leave, which worked. Clarisse showed up to punch me, which — weirdly helped."

Clarisse raised her goblet. "You're welcome."

"Thalia showed up and taught me how to throw a punch without breaking my thumb. Silena showed up and taught me which fork to use and pretended not to notice when I used the wrong one anyway. Chiron showed up and taught me — everything." He looked at the doorway, where Chiron smiled, eyes shining. "And Annabeth—" His voice caught. "Annabeth showed up every day. Even when I was a jerk. Even when I didn't get it. She stayed and explained, and argued, and dragged me to lessons, and believed I could be — more. She still does."

Annabeth was crying now, openly, smiling through it. Percy sat down hard beside her and took her hand with both of his.

"So thanks," he said simply to the room. "For not letting me run. For letting me stay. Best year of my life."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Tyson burst into tears, scooped Percy and Annabeth both into a hug, and the room erupted into applause and cheering and somebody started crying because Tyson was crying and Grover was playing a sad song on his reed pipes until Travis stole them.

"Gifts! Gifts!" Connor shouted, pounding the table. "Presents for the anniversary boy!"

---

The gift pile was gloriously weird, the way only demigod gifts could be.

From the Hermes cabin: a pair of shoes guaranteed to stay on in water ("We tested them," Travis said solemnly. "In the lake. Connor almost drowned. Worth it.") From Apollo: a playlist of "songs to brood to" and a coupon for one free healing ("No questions asked, even if it's stupid," Will said). From Grover: a tiny potted strawberry plant named Fred Jr. and a poem about moss that made Percy cry laughing. From Tyson: a bronze automaton seahorse that whirred and puffed steam and followed Percy around nipping his shoelaces. From Chiron: a battered copy of *The Odyssey* with notes in the margins in Chiron's neat hand — *You remind me of him here. Stubborn. Good-hearted. Try not to anger Poseidon.*

From Thalia: a silver arrowhead from the camp forge on a cord. "For luck," she said gruffly, tying it around his wrist herself. "And so you remember your girlfriend's big sister is watching your back. Don't make it weird."

"It is weird," Percy said, hugging her anyway. "Thank you."

But the two gifts that made the room go quiet were the last two.

Clarisse and Silena stood up together, which was itself a surprise — his sisters didn't usually agree on every part of a design. Clarisse carried the package, flat and heavy, wrapped in brown forge paper. Silena walked beside her, hands clasped, smiling that soft, knowing smile.

"Okay," Clarisse said, shoving the package at Percy. "We made this. Mostly me. Silena helped with the pretty parts because I don't do pretty. Don't cry about it."

Percy unwrapped it carefully. Inside was a trident — hand-carved from dark driftwood, polished until it gleamed, about as long as his forearm. The tines were tipped with Celestial bronze, the handle wrapped in leather, and along the shaft Clarisse had burned, in rough but careful letters: *PERCY — YEAR ONE — DON'T DIE IN YEAR TWO.*

Set into the base was a frame made of seashells — pink and white and pearled blue, glued in a careful spiral — holding a charcoal sketch Silena had drawn: Cabin Three at sunrise, light on the water, Percy's silhouette sitting on the roof with his feet dangling.

The room made a collective *aww*. Percy's mouth opened and nothing came out.

"Clarisse— Silena—" He turned the trident over in his hands. It was perfectly balanced. It was beautiful. "You made this?"

"I carved it," Clarisse muttered, ears red. "In the forge. Took three weeks. Silena did the shell thing and the drawing. She said you needed something that wasn't for fighting. Something for —" She gestured vaguely. "Home stuff."

Silena sat down beside Annabeth, leaning in. "Every hero needs something that reminds him where he belongs," she said softly. "That's home, Percy. You."

Percy stood up and hugged them both — Clarisse stiffly at first, then all at once, hard, and Silena laughing into his shoulder. The room applauded. Somebody wolf-whistled. Clarisse threatened to kill them all, but she was smiling.

"My turn," Annabeth said quietly.

She'd been waiting, Percy realized — patient, knees pulled up, that flat package still clutched in her hands. Now she held it out, suddenly shy. The whole room seemed to lean in.

"I— this took a while," she said. "Don't — just open it."

Percy unwrapped the cloth. Inside was a deck of cards. Not just any deck — Mythomagic. The game he'd loved as a kid, the game he'd told her about one rainy night at a borrowed Athena workbench when they were supposed to be planning battle strategy and instead he'd confessed that he'd missed being a normal kid who collected stupid cards. His original deck had vanished with Sally's apartment and the rest of the life only he and Annabeth remembered. Nothing in this altered childhood could replace that ordinary piece of home.

This deck was complete. Every card, sleeved and sorted. But more than that — Annabeth and the Athena cabin had rebuilt it. They'd reprinted the missing cards by hand, painted new art, written new stats. There were new gods — Poseidon in all his moody glory, Tyson as a limited-edition hero card (*TYSON — BIG HUG — ATTACK 1000000*), Grover with his pipes, Thalia with lightning, Clarisse with a spear, Silena with charm. And at the very back, in a hard plastic sleeve, a custom holographic card: *PERCY JACKSON — SON OF THE SEA — LOYALTY: INFINITE.*

And beside him, another: *ANNABETH GRACE — DAUGHTER OF THE SKY — ALWAYS AT HIS SIDE.*

Percy stared. His hands shook.

"Annabeth," he breathed. "How—?"

"I wrote to every collector on the East Coast," she said in a rush, pink to her ears. "Malcolm helped restore the water-damaged ones. Silena painted the art — she's better than me at faces. Tyson carried the boxes. Grover — Grover morally supported. I know it's silly, it's just a game, but you said it was the last normal thing you had before — before everything, and I thought — you should have something normal back. Something that's yours."

Percy looked up at her, eyes burning. The room was completely silent. Even the Stolls weren't joking.

"It's not silly," he said hoarsely. "It's — Annabeth, this is —" He couldn't finish. He just pulled her into him, cards clutched to his chest, and held on while everyone aww'd so loud the windows rattled.

"My turn! My turn!" Percy said suddenly, scrambling up, wiping his eyes, digging in his pocket. "I have something too. I — okay, it's not as good, it's not — here."

He pulled out a long tube, tied with blue string. Annabeth untied it with fumbling fingers and unrolled it on the floor between them. Everyone crowded around.

It was a star map. Hand-drawn on dark blue paper in silver and white ink, constellations connected with fine lines, labeled in Percy's messy handwriting and Annabeth's neat one where she'd helped him study. But it wasn't just the Greek sky — down one side, in shifting blue-green ink that moved when you tilted it, was another sky entirely. Strange stars. Spiral currents. A city of domes and light at the center labeled *ATLANTIS*.

"Whoa," Travis breathed. "Is that—?"

"Atlantean sky," Percy said, a little breathless. He knelt beside her, pointing. "Tyson told me about it. Under the sea, they navigate by different stars — light through water, bioluminescent currents, stuff like that. I asked him and my dad — I Iris-messaged my dad, which was super awkward, 'hey Dad, can you tell me about stars' — and I charted both. Greek sky on top, Atlantean sky below. So—" He glanced at her, nervous and hopeful. "So you'd always know where you are. No matter which world you're in. Above or below. You'd never be lost."

Annabeth stared at the map. Her fingers traced the silver lines — Cassiopeia, Athena's owl hidden in the corner as a joke, the little ship labeled *SEAWEED BRAIN + WISE GIRL* sailing between the two skies.

"Percy," she whispered.

"I know it's not — it's not perfect, the ink smudged here, and Tyson sneezed on this part—"

She launched herself at him, map crinkling between them, arms around his neck, laughing and crying at once. "It's perfect, you idiot! It's the best thing anyone's ever given me!"

The room exploded. Cheers, whistles, Grover sobbing into his hat, Tyson clapping so hard the table shook, Silena wiping her eyes on Clarisse's sleeve while Clarisse pretended to be annoyed and held the map up for everyone to see.

"Okay!" Travis shouted over the noise. "Now kiss— I mean — cake! More cake! Everyone eat more cake!"

They ate more cake. They told more stories — how Percy fell in the lake, how Percy befriended a hellhound, how Percy talked Annabeth into midnight swims and Annabeth talked Percy into library research and somehow both survived. Chiron told the story of Percy's first sword lesson in his younger body, when he'd overbalanced on a lunge and declared himself ready anyway. Mr. D told the story of Percy calling him "Mr. Dude" to his face, which got a bigger laugh than anything.

As the afternoon gold slanted through the windows, the party wound down into that soft, sleepy, happy tiredness. Campers drifted off in twos and threes — to chores, to practice, to naps in the grass. Tyson fell asleep under the gift table with the automaton seahorse curled on his chest. Grover fell asleep with his head on a balloon. Thalia, Clarisse, and Silena ended up on the floor together, leaning against each other, shell lei shared three ways, laughing quietly about something only they heard.

Percy tried to pack the cards away neatly. He made it through three sleeves before Nico appeared at his elbow.

"Are those usable?"

Percy looked up. "What?"

"The cards. Did Annabeth make a game or a thing that's too nice to touch?"

"Both, apparently."

"Can I see the stats?"

Percy handed him the Tyson card. Nico turned it over, examined the tiny lettering, then let out an incredulous sound.

"Attack a million? That's not balanced."

"Tyson requested it."

"Tyson doesn't understand balancing."

"Tyson understands hitting things."

"That's why it's unbalanced."

Percy pulled a chair over and cleared a place on the table. There were sticky rings from cups and crumbs in the seam, so he borrowed a cloth from the kitchen and wiped the surface until it was clean enough to use. Annabeth looked over from where she was talking with Silena.

"What are you doing?"

"Testing your gift."

She stared at the open sleeves. "Already?"

"Didn't you make it so I could play?"

A surprised smile crossed her face, fast enough that he might have missed it if he hadn't been watching.

"Yes," she said. "Just don't bend the custom cards. Malcolm will personally haunt you."

Percy drew a hand of six. Nico chose his own from the restored originals, rejecting two whose corners had been reinforced with strips of archival paper. He didn't explain why. He had always been particular about the feel of cards, Percy remembered from a life in which the boy had seemed impossibly young and then suddenly older than anyone should have been.

That old Nico was not standing at this table.

This Nico had a smear of blue frosting near his collar and was glaring at a game rule as if it had insulted his father.

"Your version gives defensive units three actions," Nico said.

"Mine?"

"Whoever wrote these rules."

"Annabeth," Percy said.

"Of course."

He called Annabeth over. She read the contested rule, then glanced at the pile of cards Nico had chosen.

"You're trying to use the same defense three times," she said.

"Because it's an excellent defense."

"It has a cost."

"Which you made absurdly high."

"Which keeps a defensive card from winning the game by itself."

Nico folded his arms. "I'd like to appeal."

"Appeal denied."

"You didn't even hear my case."

"I saw your hand."

Percy laughed. It was the first time that afternoon the cards had behaved like a game instead of an artifact, and he felt something settle. Annabeth took the chair beside him, close enough that their knees bumped. She helped Nico read the printed version of the rule, then admitted that one of the new handwritten cards needed clearer wording.

"We'll mark it," she said.

"Your own card is overpowered," Nico told her.

"So is Percy's."

"That doesn't make it fair."

"Correct. It's a commemorative edition."

Nico seemed satisfied by that distinction. He slapped down a card showing a skeletal warrior and announced an attack against Percy with obvious delight.

Percy countered with Grover, whose special ability allowed him to slow an opponent's next turn by playing music. Nico stared at the card.

"Grover doesn't do that."

"Have you heard him practice before breakfast?"

"That's a good point."

They played three turns before Tyson woke under the gift table and lumbered over, still half asleep. His mechanical seahorse followed him, clicking softly against the floor. Tyson saw the card bearing his name and demanded to play it. Nico, who had been calculating probabilities with considerable care, let Tyson take a turn just to see what would happen.

Tyson placed his card sideways.

"That isn't how it works," Nico protested.

"Tyson protects Percy."

"Yes, but you have to spend points."

"Tyson has many points." He gathered the tiny counters in one enormous hand.

Nico looked at Percy for help. Percy looked at Annabeth. She pressed her lips together to keep from laughing.

"Okay," Percy said. "House rule. Tyson can protect me once if he gives the counters back."

Tyson returned them with a solemn nod. He placed his card over Percy's and sat beside him, satisfied.

Nico took the next turn. He had been planning it for some time, and with one move he cleared both cards from the table.

"Ha," he said.

Tyson looked down at the empty spaces.

"Percy died?"

"In the game," Annabeth said quickly.

"Only in the game," Percy agreed. "I can come back next round."

Tyson considered this and then grinned. "Good game. Tyson still loves Percy."

"Not sure if the rules allow that after defeat," Nico said.

"They do now," Percy replied.

It wasn't a grand revelation. There were no tears, no speech, no attempt to explain the cards as evidence of an earlier life. Percy simply gathered them into a neat stack and found that he wasn't afraid of what might happen to them anymore. There were new marks on some of the sleeves where Nico's thumbs had pressed. One corner of the rules sheet had a spot of frosting. Annabeth had written *clarify cost* beside the disputed defense.

The deck was already acquiring a history in this camp.

That mattered more than the precise shade of green on a card Percy could barely remember holding.

When Nico went to help Grover gather the empty plates, Percy caught Annabeth studying the handwritten inventory tucked in the deck box.

"Something wrong?" he asked.

"I listed one of the restored cards as original. Malcolm corrected me this morning. I meant to change the note."

Percy looked at the little line she'd circled. He could have argued that it wasn't a big deal. He knew better than to do that when a correction mattered to her.

"Can we fix it now?"

"I have a pencil."

"Of course you do."

She changed the description in three careful words and showed him. He nodded. She closed the box and laid it in his hands.

"You okay with it being different?" she asked.

"Wise Girl, you made Tyson a god-tier limited edition. Different is kind of the point."

"That's not what I mean."

He knew. He took a breath before answering.

"I remember a drawer in Sally's apartment," he said quietly. "I remember spending whole afternoons making up battles I always won. But I don't know if the cards were in a blue box or a red one. And when I try to picture the printed art, I keep getting the picture you made instead."

Annabeth's eyes searched his face. "Does that scare you?"

"A little."

"I didn't want to replace it."

"You didn't." He touched the new box. "You gave me something to remember for next year."

A shout from across the room interrupted them. Travis was trying to carry six leftover cake slices at once. Clarisse told him to use a tray. He ignored her, and the top slice slid down his arm onto the floor.

Percy watched it happen and decided that memory didn't always need elaborate preservation. Some things would survive because nobody present was ever going to let Travis forget them.

He took Annabeth's hand. "Come outside with me?"

She gathered his star map, checking that the blue-green ink had dried. "Where?"

"Somewhere without a game referee."

"Nico's actually very good at it."

"He's terrifying."

"That too."

Percy and Annabeth slipped out with the map and the cards, hands linked, bare feet silent on the Big House steps.

---

The hill was quiet. The sun was low, spilling gold over the valley, turning the Sound beyond to hammered copper. The breeze smelled of strawberries and salt.

They sat on Half-Blood Hill, under the shade of the boundary pine, the boundary wards glowing gold above them. Percy spread the star map on the grass between them, weighting the corners with stones. Annabeth laid out her favorite Mythomagic cards in a row — Percy, Annabeth, Tyson, Grover, Thalia — like a little family.

"Year one, finally celebrated," Annabeth said softly, leaning her head on his shoulder. "Do you remember what you said to me that first day? After the old drool joke?"

"I said 'are you going to kill me?'" Percy guessed. "Because you had a knife."

"I did have a knife." She laughed. "You said, 'If you're going to kill me, can you wait until after lunch? I'm starving.'" She turned her face into his shoulder. "I thought, there's my hero. He's still making jokes when his world falls apart."

"And now?"

"Now I think—" She lifted her head, gray eyes meeting his green, serious and shining. "Now I think you're stuck with us. With me. Forever, probably. Sorry."

Percy smiled, slow and sure, and tucked a curl behind her ear. His hand lingered on her cheek, thumb brushing frosting he hadn't wiped off earlier.

"Not sorry," he said. "Not even a little."

He kissed her then — soft and sweet and tasting of blue frosting and sunshine, with the camp humming below them and the stars just starting to prick through the blue above. Her hand came up to cup his jaw. His fingers tangled in her ponytail. The map rustled under their knees. Somewhere down the hill, somebody — Travis, probably — whooped, and somebody else shushed him, and Annabeth laughed against Percy's mouth.

When they pulled apart, foreheads resting together, Percy whispered, "Happy camp-iversary, Wise Girl."

"Happy camp-iversary, Seaweed Brain."

They stayed there until the stars came out — both sets, Greek and Atlantean, real and remembered — and Annabeth pointed out constellations while Percy pointed out currents, and the map lay between them like a promise: above or below, lost or found, they would always know the way home.

Because home wasn't a cabin or a hill or even a camp.

Home was each other. And they had stayed.

They would keep staying, year after year, for as long as the gods allowed — and maybe a little longer than that, just to be stubborn.

Year one, celebrated at last. Already partway into the next, with everything else still ahead.
