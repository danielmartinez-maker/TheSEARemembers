## Chapter 45: Grover's Crush

Percy knew something was wrong with Grover before breakfast was even over.

It was not a monster-sense kind of wrong. It was not a the sky is turning green and Kronos is dreaming again kind of wrong. It was the kind of wrong where his best friend had eaten half a tin can, forgotten to chew, and was staring across the dining pavilion like his brain had floated off with the morning mist.

"Grover," Percy said. "Dude. You're drooling on the tin."

Grover blinked. "Huh? What? No I'm not."

"You are," Annabeth said from across the table. She did not look up from her toast, but Percy could tell she was watching. She always watched everything. "It's on your chin. Left side."

Grover wiped hurriedly at his chin and smeared jam and juice together. His ears twitched, pink at the tips.

"I wasn't drooling," he said quickly. "I was thinking. Thinking about — uh — clover. Yeah. Clover taxonomy."

Percy followed his gaze before Grover could stop him.

Across the pavilion, near the edge where the dryads sometimes came to trade strawberries for gossip, stood a girl with bark-brown hair tied back with green vine ribbon and eyes the color of new leaves. She was laughing at something one of the Demeter kids said, and when she laughed, little white flowers opened in her hair like they had been waiting for permission.

Percy recognized her. Juniper. She looked after the young berry bushes on the north side of the strawberry fields. She was a dryad, which meant she was technically much older than twelve in tree years, but in person-years she acted about thirteen. She was kind and nervous around new people and she always hummed while she worked.

Juniper glanced over. She saw Grover looking. She smiled, quick and shy, and tucked a flower behind her ear. Then she ducked her head back to her plate.

Grover made a sound like a squeezed bagpipe.

"Oh no," Annabeth said.

"What?" Grover said, too loud. "What oh no? There's no oh no. Everything's fine. Everything's normal. Pass the apples."

"You hate apples," Percy said.

"I love apples today," Grover said desperately. "Apples are great. Apples are —" He looked down at the apple in his hand like he had never seen one before. "Round."

Annabeth set down her toast. She folded her arms and gave Grover the look she usually reserved for Percy when he said something especially dense about Greek myths.

"You have a crush on Juniper," she said.

Grover choked on air. "I do not! I — I barely — she's just — she's nice to trees! Everyone likes people who are nice to trees! That's a normal platonic tree-appreciation feeling!"

"Your ears turn red when she looks at you," Percy said.

"My ears always turn red! I'm a satyr! Circulation!"

"Your ears turned red just now because I said her name," Annabeth pointed out.

Grover put his head down on the table with a soft thud. The tin can lid he had been chewing fell out of his mouth and rolled across the wood.

"Okay," he mumbled into his arms. "Okay fine. Maybe. A little. Don't tell anyone."

Percy grinned so wide his cheeks hurt. "Grover's got a crush."

"Shhh!" Grover sat up, panicked, hooves scraping under the bench. "Gods, Percy, keep your voice down! She'll hear you! Dryads have excellent hearing! They can hear a beetle sneeze from forty feet away!"

"She's like sixty feet away," Annabeth said.

"She can still hear beetle-level gossip!" Grover hissed. He pulled his cap lower over his horns, even though he was indoors and everyone in camp already knew he was a satyr. "Please. You guys can't tell anyone. Especially not — you know — the older campers. They'll make it weird."

Percy and Annabeth exchanged a look. That look meant, we are absolutely going to help, and Grover is going to regret asking us not to tell anyone, but in a good way.

"We won't tell," Percy promised solemnly. "We'll help."

"Yeah," Annabeth said. "That's what friends are for."

Grover peered at them suspiciously. "What kind of help?"

Percy thought about it. What did you do when your friend liked someone and could barely say her name? He knew Annabeth well enough to make her smile, but that had taken years in their remembered life, and Grover and Juniper were still finding their way. He glanced at Annabeth to see if she knew what to do.

Annabeth was already thinking. He could tell because she had her planning face on, the little crease between her eyebrows.

"First," she said, "we need information. How long have you liked her? Have you talked to her? What does she like?"

Grover twisted his fingers together. "Um. A while? Since last spring? She helped me untangle my pipes from a blackberry bramble and she didn't laugh when I cried a little because the thorns were in my fur. She just put this cool leaf paste on it and hummed. And we talk sometimes. About grass. Really good grass behind the Big House. And music. She likes music. She said my piping makes the strawberries grow straighter."

"That's good," Annabeth said firmly, like she was taking notes in her head. "That's material. Percy, write that down."

"I don't have paper," Percy said.

"Then remember it. Strawberries. Humming. Leaf paste. No laughing at crying."

"Got it," Percy said.

Grover buried his face again. "This is hopeless. She's so cool and I'm — I'm me. I eat cans. I fall over when I'm nervous. Last week I got scared by my own shadow and ran into the lava wall. The lava wall! Who does that?"

"You do," Percy said loyally. "And Juniper still smiled at you just now. So maybe she likes you back."

Grover lifted his head just enough to peek out. "You think?"

"I think we need a plan," Annabeth said. "Something simple. Something sincere. No grand quests. No monsters."

Percy nodded. "Something Grover-style."

"What's Grover-style?" Grover asked miserably.

Percy thought for a second, then bumped his shoulder against Grover's. "Kind. And a little goofy. In a good way."

For the first time that morning, Grover smiled a tiny smile.

Across the pavilion, Juniper got up with her plate. As she passed their table on the way to the dish bins, her eyes flicked to Grover. She hesitated, then left a small strawberry on the edge of his tray when she thought no one was looking. A perfect one, heart-shaped, with the green top still on.

"For your pipes," she said softly. "For later. For the plants."

Then she hurried off, cheeks greenish-pink.

Grover stared at the strawberry like it was made of Olympian gold. He did not eat it. He just held it very carefully in both hands.

"Oh gods," he whispered. "I'm doomed."

Annabeth kicked Percy under the table. Hard.

"Help him," she mouthed.

Percy nodded. Operation Help Grover was officially on.

---

By mid-morning, Operation Help Grover had expanded beyond Percy's control.

It started innocently enough. Percy and Annabeth took Grover to the creek behind the cabins to talk strategy. Percy brought extra tin cans. Annabeth brought a wax tablet.

"Okay," Annabeth said, sitting cross-legged on the grass. "We need to decide on the approach. I think a note is best. Something short. Something honest. Then maybe a song, since you're a musician."

Grover bleated nervously and paced in a little circle. "I can't write a note! What if I spell something wrong? What if she thinks it's stupid?"

"Trees do not talk about love notes," Annabeth said.

"They absolutely do," Grover said. "Maples are the worst gossips."

Percy tossed a pebble into the creek. "Then we'll help you write it. What's one thing you want her to know?"

Grover stopped pacing. He looked at the water. "That I like how she hums when she thinks no one's listening? That sounds creepy, right? Like I've been listening when she's not listening for me listening?"

"It's not creepy if you say it nice," Percy said. "Like — I like your humming because it makes work feel less like work."

Annabeth wrote that down. "That's actually good, Percy."

"Really?" Percy brightened.

"Don't let it go to your head."

They were just getting into a rhythm — Percy suggesting lines, Annabeth trimming them down to something less rambling, Grover vetoing anything with the word thee or thou — when Clarisse found them.

Which was bad, because Clarisse never just walked by. Clarisse arrived like a storm front.

She stomped down the creek bank in full armor, spear over her shoulder, Silena trailing behind her trying to keep her sandals out of the mud. Thalia was with them too, hands in her jacket pockets, a silver circlet glinting in her black hair. The three of them stopped and stared at the little huddle by the water.

"What are you losers doing?" Clarisse demanded.

"Nothing," Grover said quickly. Too quickly. "Absolutely nothing romantic or note-related or crush-related at all."

Silena gasped and clasped her hands. "Oh! Is someone writing a love note? Who is it? Is it Percy? Is it for Annabeth?"

"No!" Percy and Annabeth said at the same time, both turning red at the prospect of having their private notes read aloud.

"It's for Grover," Annabeth admitted after a beat, because lying to Silena never worked. Silena could smell secrets like perfume. "But you can't tease him. He's nervous."

"I'm not nervous," Grover said, voice cracking on nervous. "I'm perfectly calm. See? Calm." His leg was shaking so fast it blurred.

Clarisse's face split into a grin that Percy had learned meant trouble for someone, usually him. "Grover's got a crush? Finally. About time, goat boy."

"Don't call him that," Percy said automatically. It was what friends did. Grover had stuck up for him often enough; Percy could return the favor.

Clarisse ignored him and dropped down heavily onto the grass, armor clanking. "Okay. You need advice. Lucky for you, I'm an expert."

"You've never had a crush in your life," Thalia said dryly, sitting on a rock.

"I have too had crushes," Clarisse said. "I had a crush on Ares's chariot. Once. It was fast and red and didn't talk back."

"That's not the same thing," Silena said, settling gracefully beside her sister and smoothing her skirt. She smiled at Grover with real warmth. "Don't worry, Grover. We'll help you. You need romance."

"I don't know if I need romance," Grover said faintly. "I think I just need to not throw up."

Thalia snorted. "Same thing, at our age."

And just like that, Operation Help Grover became a committee meeting.

Percy tried to keep it on track. He really did. But Clarisse, Silena, and Thalia each had very strong opinions, and none of them agreed.

"Listen," Clarisse said, jabbing her spear butt into the dirt for emphasis. "Girls like confidence. You gotta walk right up to her, look her in the eye, and show her you're tough. Challenge her to something. Arm wrestling. A race. A fight with practice swords. Chicks dig a guy who can take a hit."

Juniper was a dryad who apologized to flowers when she picked them. Percy doubted she wanted to arm wrestle.

"I don't think —" Grover started.

"No, she's right about confidence, but wrong about the method," Silena cut in. She tucked a strand of hair behind her ear. "You need a grand gesture. Rose petals. A poem. Show up under her tree at sunset with a bouquet."

"I throw up when I try to recite," Grover said.

"You'll practice," Silena said encouragingly. "And rose petals fix everything."

"That's terrible advice," Thalia said. "If you try too hard, you'll freak her out. Play it cool. Act like you don't care that much."

"That sounds like lying," Annabeth said, frowning. She looked to Thalia, half admiring, half annoyed, the way little sisters always looked at big sisters who were cooler than them. "And manipulative."

"It's strategy," Thalia said with a shrug. "Works on mortals. Works on monsters. Probably works on dryads."

Grover looked back and forth between them like a deer caught between three wolves. "So I should — fight her, shower her in petals while reciting, but also pretend I don't care?"

"Yes," Clarisse, Silena, and Thalia said together.

"No," Percy and Annabeth said together.

Percy put a hand on Grover's shoulder. "Dude. Ignore them. You don't need to be tough or smooth or poetry guy. Just be you. Juniper already likes you-you. She gave you a strawberry."

"It was a friendship strawberry," Grover muttered, but he was holding that strawberry still, wrapped in a leaf in his pocket now, like treasure.

"It was not a friendship strawberry," Silena said wisely. "There is no such thing as a casual heart-shaped strawberry."

Clarisse snorted. "Ugh. Feelings. Fine. If you're not going to wrestle her, at least don't write something lame like roses are red, violets are blue. That's pathetic."

"What's wrong with roses are red?" Percy asked.

"Everything," Clarisse and Annabeth said together, then glanced at each other in surprise.

Thalia stood and stretched. "Look, kid. My advice stands. Don't overthink it. The more you overthink, the more you'll trip over your own hooves. Trust me. I once spent three days trying to figure out how to tell Luke I thought his sword drills were cool without sounding dumb. In the end I just said it while we were cleaning blades and he said thanks and it was fine."

Annabeth looked up sharply at that. "You were nervous talking to Luke?"

"Everyone gets nervous, Beth," Thalia said, ruffling her sister's hair. Annabeth batted her hand away but she was smiling. "Even daughters of Zeus."

Silena leaned in and patted Grover's knee. "Sweetie, Clarisse is wrong about wrestling, and Thalia is wrong about playing cool, but they're right that you should be brave. Juniper is shy too. Someone has to go first. It might as well be you."

Grover swallowed. "What if she says no? What if she just wants to be friends?"

"Then you still have a friend," Percy said simply. "And that's not so bad."

Grover looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, slow.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. Let's write the note. But no wrestling. And no pretending I don't care. I do care. A lot."

Annabeth flipped to a fresh section of wax tablet, trying not to look too pleased. "Good. That's the right answer."

Clarisse rolled her eyes but she stayed. Silena stayed. Thalia stayed. All of them crowded around while Grover tried to put his heart into words.

It took all afternoon.

---

The note went through eleven drafts.

Percy counted. Annabeth kept smoothing the wax flat with the back of the stylus, erasing and restarting, muttering about word choice like the fate of Olympus depended on whether Grover used like versus really like.

They tried fancy versions first — flowery, tough, and cool — but none of them sounded like Grover, so they scrapped them all.

"Forget the fancy stuff," Percy said quietly. "Just tell her what you told us. About the blackberry bramble. About the humming."

Grover blinked. "Just that?"

"Just that," Percy said. "And maybe that you saved her a strawberry too. But you ate half your tray, so maybe pick a new one."

Grover laughed, surprised, a little bleat escaping. "Okay. Okay, yeah."

Annabeth handed over the stylus. "Your turn. We'll help with spelling, but the words have to be yours."

Grover took the tablet with shaking hands. He stuck his tongue out in concentration. He wrote slowly, pressing hard. The others leaned in to watch, and Annabeth shooed them back.

"Give him air!" she hissed. "He can't breathe with all your big-sister energy on him!"

"I'm his best-friend energy," Percy said. "I can stay."

"You can stay," Grover agreed quickly. "You and Annabeth can stay."

So Clarisse, Silena, and Thalia retreated to the creek to skip stones — badly, in Clarisse's case, who threw a stone so hard it stuck in the opposite bank — while Percy and Annabeth crouched on either side of Grover.

Letter by letter, the note took shape.

Dear Juniper,

Thank you for helping me with the thorns in spring. You were nice when I cried and you didn't make fun of me. I still remember the song you hummed. It was the one that goes la la laaa la la? I hum it when I clean my pipes.

You said my music helps the strawberries grow straight. That was the nicest thing anyone said about my music all year. Mr. D said my music made his wine turn to vinegar, so your compliment wins.

I like talking to you about grass. You know a lot about grass. I thought I knew a lot about grass because I'm a satyr, but you know more. That's cool.

If you want, I could play a new song for you. I made it up. It's not perfect yet. Annabeth says the middle part needs work and Percy says the ending sounds like a donkey, but I think you'd like the beginning. We could just sit by your bushes and I could play and you could tell me if the berries like it.

Your friend (hopefully more than friend? Is that okay to say? If not just friend is good too!),

Grover

P.S. I saved you a strawberry but then I got nervous and ate it. I will pick you a new one. A really good one.

Percy read it over Grover's shoulder and felt his throat get tight in a good way. It was so Grover. Honest and a little lopsided and kind all the way through.

Annabeth was quiet for a second. Then she said, gruffly, "The middle part does need work, but the note is perfect. Don't change a word."

"Really?" Grover whispered.

"Really," she said. "It's sincere. That's better than smooth."

"Can I add one thing?" Percy asked. "At the P.S.?"

Grover handed him the stylus. Percy carefully added: P.P.S. He chewed 3 tin cans while writing this. That's how nervous he was. That's true bravery. — Percy (witness)

Annabeth added underneath in neat letters: Confirmed. — A.

Grover stared at the additions, then laughed until tears leaked out. "You guys are the worst."

"We're the best," Percy corrected. "And we're going to help you fix the donkey ending on your song."

Thalia read it and softened. "That is actually good, kid." Silena hugged Grover. "I am proud of you for being brave." Clarisse grunted. "It is not terrible. No headbutting."

Grover clutched the tablet to his chest like armor. "Now I just have to give it to her."

Five pairs of eyes stared at him.

"Oh gods," Grover said. "I have to give it to her."


---

An hour before dinner, Percy discovered that being part of a committee came with a problem. The committee thought the note belonged to the committee.

Silena had borrowed Annabeth's final copy to check the fold. Thalia had made a suggestion about the opening. Clarisse had announced that any romantic letter beginning with *Dear Juniper* ought to end with something better than *Sincerely*. Ella wanted a drawing of a strawberry in the corner. None of them had noticed that Grover had stopped speaking.

Percy noticed because he had been sitting beside Grover long enough to feel the nervous tapping of his hoof through the bench.

They were under the pavilion roof, the evening light making copper strips across the tables. Annabeth had gone to get another sheet of papyrus from the Big House. Silena was explaining the difference between a letter and a card. It would have been useful advice for somebody else.

"Can I see it?" Grover asked.

Silena gave him the papyrus. "Of course. I only changed the folds. Look, it opens like this, and the message is still the first thing she'll see."

Grover unfolded it. His lips moved over the words. Percy could tell exactly when he got to the last line, because his ears stopped twitching.

"That's not what I wrote."

"Well, we cleaned it up," Silena said. "You wanted help."

Grover traced the last sentence with a finger. "I said I liked listening when she hums while she works. This says she makes the whole forest sing."

"That's prettier," Silena said, then looked at him properly. "Isn't it?"

"No." His voice was so quiet Percy nearly missed it. "It's not even true. The forest sings without her. That's one of the things I like about her. She listens before she starts."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

Percy took a breath. He could have rescued the conversation with a joke, something about Silena's poetry threatening the local wildlife. He almost did. Then he thought about what he'd told Grover that morning, that he didn't have to become somebody else for Juniper to like him. Apparently that advice also applied to the people helping him.

"Give him his first draft back," Percy said.

Clarisse frowned. "You can't give somebody a first draft. It's a first draft."

"He's giving somebody a letter," Percy said. "Not turning in an essay."

Thalia's eyebrows went up. "You're taking this seriously."

"It's his."

That ended the argument, or at least the loud part. Silena reached into her satchel and found the sheet they had started with. It was creased, and the corner had a muddy thumbprint where Grover had caught it when Annabeth dropped the wax tablet. His letters leaned uphill. One word had been crossed out four times.

Grover held the two versions side by side.

"Mine's embarrassing," he said.

"Mine was worse," Percy told him.

"When did you write a love letter?" Clarisse asked immediately.

"Never. That's how bad it was."

She stared at him, deciding whether to be annoyed. Percy didn't wait. He slid the cleaner papyrus toward Grover.

"You can make a new copy in your handwriting. Keep the part you actually meant. Annabeth will help if you can't spell something, but you get to pick the words."

Grover looked at Silena first. Her expression had changed. She was staring at her own neat folds with the embarrassed concentration she usually reserved for a seam she'd stitched backward.

"I got carried away," she said. "Sorry, Grover."

"I liked the fold," Grover admitted. "It's the singing forest part I don't want."

Silena nodded. "Then don't use it. I'll show you how to fold your version."

Thalia leaned back and pushed one boot against the bench. "For the record, I didn't touch the middle."

"You wanted him to sound mysterious," Clarisse said.

"I wanted him to stop apologizing in every sentence."

"That's because he's Grover," Percy said. "His favorite words are sorry and please and where did my lunch go."

Grover snorted. It wasn't much, but it was the first noise he'd made in several minutes that hadn't sounded like a hoof caught in a door.

Annabeth returned carrying two fresh sheets and a stylus, took one look at their faces, and set everything down.

"What did I miss?"

"Grover's decided to use his own words," Percy told her.

"Good," she said, with a quick nod that made it clear she thought this should have been obvious from the beginning. "What do you want checked?"

"Spelling," Grover said. "Just the spelling."

They spent the next twenty minutes at a quieter table. Percy kept the others occupied by asking Clarisse how she would respond to a declaration of love delivered during spear practice. Her answer involved making sure the admirer wasn't holding the spear. Silena taught Ella how to fold a square of scrap papyrus into a little pouch. Thalia went to collect the tools she had abandoned at the arena.

Annabeth sat beside Grover rather than across from him. She pointed out two misspelled words, asked whether he meant the old bramble or the new one, and handed his stylus back without making the choice for him.

Percy watched Grover write the last line himself.

He paused a long time over the space beneath it. Then he added a postscript.

*You don't have to write back fast. The letter can wait.*

It was not something any of the rest of them would have suggested. Percy thought it was the best line on the page.

Grover folded it the way Silena showed him, badly at first. Silena didn't reach over to fix it. She waited until he asked where the corner went, then demonstrated on a piece of scrap.

When he finished, the note was slightly crooked and completely his.

"Now," Grover said, swallowing hard, "I just have to give it to her."

"Yep," Percy said.

The others were tactful enough not to cheer.

---

Giving the note to Juniper turned out to be harder than writing it.

Grover carried the folded note, written out again in his own uneven hand and folded with Silena's help, in his pocket all through sword practice, dinner prep, and capture-the-flag strategy meeting. He kept patting his pocket to make sure it was still there. By dinner, the papyrus was soft and a little damp from nervous hoof-sweat. Annabeth made him copy it over again on a fresh sheet.

"You can do it after dinner," Percy coached, walking with Grover to the pavilion. "Just walk up, say hey Juniper, I wrote this for you, hand it over, and walk away if you need to. You don't have to wait while she reads it."

"What if I freeze?" Grover whispered. "What if I bleat? I bleat when I'm nervous!"

"Then bleat," Percy said. "Bleating is cute. Girls like cute."

"Clarisse said girls like tough!"

"Clarisse likes tough," Percy said. "Juniper likes music and grass. Be grass-music Grover."

Grover nodded rapidly, hyperventilating a little. "Grass-music Grover. Right. I can be him. He's me."

But at dinner, Juniper sat with a cluster of dryads at the far edge of the pavilion, laughing and braiding each other's hair with clover. Every time Grover started to stand, another dryad looked over, and he sat back down so fast he rattled his plate.

"She's with friends," he hissed to Percy. "I can't do it in front of friends! What if they laugh?"

"They won't laugh," Annabeth said from across the table. She had maneuvered to sit where she could see both Grover and Juniper without being obvious. "But okay. New plan. Wait until after dinner, when she goes back to the fields. Catch her alone on the path."

Grover nodded, relieved. "Yes. Alone. Good. Less audience for my humiliation."

After dinner, the three of them lurked by the path to the strawberry fields, trying to look casual and failing completely. Percy leaned against a fence post whistling. Annabeth pretended to study the clouds for Zeus omens. Grover hid behind a bush that was much too small for him, his horns poking out over the top.

"She's coming," Annabeth whispered. "Act natural."

"I don't remember how to be natural!" Grover whispered back. "What does natural look like?"

"Like this," Percy said, standing up straight and immediately tripping over his own feet.

Juniper rounded the bend with a basket of empty jars, humming that same la-la song. When she saw them, she stopped. Her eyes went wide, then happy.

"Hi!" she said. "Were you waiting for someone?"

Grover stood up behind the bush. Leaves stuck to his fur. A twig stuck out of his hair. He opened his mouth. Nothing came out.

Percy elbowed him. Annabeth mouthed, the note!

"The — uh — I — you — grass!" Grover blurted. He thrust the papyrus forward so fast he nearly poked Juniper in the eye with it. "For you! Sorry! Bye!"

He turned and bolted. Hooves pounding, cap flying off, bleating in terror, he vanished into the strawberry rows.

Juniper stood there holding the note, blinking.

Percy wanted to sink into the ground. Annabeth covered her face with both hands.

"Um," Juniper said slowly, looking at the crumpled papyrus, then at the dust cloud where Grover had disappeared. "Is he okay?"

"He's great," Percy said quickly, retrieving Grover's cap. "He's just — he's really nervous because he likes you. Like, likes you likes you. He wrote you that note. With help, but mostly him. He means every word. Especially the strawberry part."

Juniper's cheeks flushed green. She looked down at the note, then back up. "He likes me?"

"So much he ate three tin cans," Annabeth said, recovering. She stepped forward, sincere. "He's my friend, Juniper. He's the kindest person I know. He cries at sad songs and he shares his enchiladas even when he's starving and he remembers everyone's favorite grass. If you don't feel the same, that's okay, just — please be gentle with him?"

Juniper held the note to her chest. She smiled, small and bright.

"I've liked him since last spring," she admitted softly. "Since the blackberry bramble. He cried and then he laughed at himself for crying and then he played his pipes to make me laugh too, even with thorns still in his fur. No one's ever done that for me." She unfolded the note carefully. "Can you tell him I'll read it tonight under my tree? And — tell him thank you for the almost-strawberry."

Percy grinned. "We'll tell him."

Juniper hurried off down the path, reading as she walked, humming a little higher than before. Twice she stopped to press the papyrus flat and re-read a line, smiling.

Percy and Annabeth found Grover curled in a hollow between two strawberry mounds, knees to chest, cap pulled over his face.

"She hates it," he moaned before they could speak. "I poked her with it. I said grass and ran. I'm going to move to Canada. Do satyrs live in Canada? I'll find out."

"She doesn't hate it," Annabeth said, sitting beside him. "She's reading it right now. She said she's liked you since last spring."

Grover went very still. "What?"

"She said to tell you she'll read it under her tree tonight," Percy said, sitting on his other side and handing back his cap. "And thanks for the almost-strawberry."

Grover lowered the cap. His eyes were huge and wet. "Really? She really said that?"

"Really," Annabeth said. "You did it. You were brave."

Grover stared at them. Then he started laughing, breathless and disbelieving, and then he was crying a little, and then Percy was laughing too.

"I said grass," Grover giggled. "I just said grass!"

"It worked," Percy said. "Grass is your love language."

From the path behind them, a new voice piped up. "Did it work? Did she say yes?"

They turned. Ella the harpy stood there, clutching a scroll and flapping her wings with excitement. Ella was younger than them, small and feathered and brilliant in a scattered way, always collecting books and shiny words. She had followed them from dinner, curious.

"Um — sort of?" Percy said. "She's reading the note tonight."

Ella clapped her wings. "Oh, good! Words are like flowers for the ears! That is even better!"

Grover smiled shyly at her. "Thanks, Ella."

Ella beamed and dug in her bag. She pulled out a slightly squashed daisy chain. "For luck! For tomorrow! When you play her the song! You must play her the song! Music is the best wooing! That's chapter seven!"

"We haven't fixed the donkey ending yet," Percy admitted.

"Then we'd better fix it tonight," Annabeth said decisively, standing and brushing dirt off her knees. "Come on. Pipes. Creek. Practice. No audience except us and Ella."

Ella cheered and flapped in a circle. "Audience of friends! Best kind!"

Grover stood slowly, still dazed, still holding the leaf that had wrapped Juniper's morning strawberry. He looked toward the fields where Juniper had gone, then at his friends.

"Okay," he said. "Let's practice."

---

They practiced until the fireflies came out.

Down by the creek, away from cabins and curious sisters, Grover sat on a flat rock with his reed pipes, Percy and Annabeth on the grass in front of him, Ella perched in a low branch overhead swinging her feet.

The song was simple. That was its strength. It started with a low, warm trill like wind through grass, then lifted into the la-la melody Juniper hummed, then wove in something new — something Grover had made, hopeful and a little wobbly, like a young deer learning to walk.

The middle part wandered. The ending did, in fact, sound like a donkey. Grover knew it. He winced every time he hit that sour note.

"Stop," Annabeth said. "Cut the extra notes. Go straight back to the Juniper theme. Simple."

Grover tried it. Better. Still donkey-ish at the very end.

Percy, who knew nothing about music but knew a lot about Grover, said, "You're rushing the ending because you're nervous about finishing. Just breathe. Pretend she's already smiling."

Grover breathed. He played the ending slower. The donkey faded into something soft and round and sweet.

Ella clapped so hard she nearly fell out of the tree. "Yes! Yes! That! That was chapter seven! That was wooing!"

Grover blushed to the tips of his horns. "You think she'll like it?"

"I think the strawberries will stand up straight," Annabeth said, which was her way of saying yes.

"Hey," he said suddenly. "What if we all come tomorrow? When you play for her? So you're not alone?"

Grover's pipes squeaked. "All? Like — everyone?"

"Not everyone everyone," Annabeth said quickly. "Just us. Moral support. At a distance. Hidden."

"Hidden moral support," Percy agreed. "Best kind."

"And flowers!" Ella said. "For giving! Boys should give flowers! Chapter three!"

Grover thought about it, turning his pipes over in his hands. Then he nodded.

"Okay," he said softly. "But if I throw up, you're all pretending it didn't happen."

"Deal," Percy and Annabeth and Ella said together.

Percy closed his eyes and listened, and hoped Juniper was under her tree reading her note and humming along.

---

Juniper sent her answer at breakfast.

Percy knew because Grover did not show up for breakfast, which never happened. When his seat stayed empty, Percy stole an extra pancake and went looking with Annabeth.

They found him behind the satyr stable, sitting in the straw, holding a fresh sheet of papyrus with both hands like it might fly away. His eyes were red. His smile was enormous.

"She wrote back," he whispered before they could ask. "She left it on my pipes this morning. Smells like strawberries."

Annabeth sat down hard in the straw. "Well? Read it!"

Grover cleared his throat and read, voice shaking:

Dear Grover,

Thank you for the note. I read it three times under my tree. My tree liked it too. She dropped a blossom on the part about the almost-strawberry, which means she approves. Trees are good judges.

I remember spring too. You played even though your hands hurt. I hum your song when I'm lonely. I didn't know you hummed my song too. That makes me happy.

Yes, please play the new song for me. Tomorrow at sunset, by the north bushes? We can see if the berries like it. I will save you a seat on the soft moss. No thorns, I promise.

Your friend (and maybe more than friend, if you want to see how the song goes),

Juniper

P.S. You don't need to bring flowers. Just bring your pipes. And yourself. Yourself is my favorite.

Grover lowered the letter. A single tear rolled down his furry cheek into his beard.

"She likes myself," he whispered. "My favorite self."

Percy felt his own eyes sting. He bumped Grover's shoulder hard. "Told you. Grass-music Grover wins."

Annabeth was smiling so wide it looked like it hurt. She punched Grover lightly on the arm, the way Thalia punched her when she was proud. "See? Sincere beats smooth every time."

"Can I hug you guys?" Grover asked thickly. "Is that okay?"

They hugged him, straw and all. Ella fluttered in and joined the pile.

Word spread fast, because camp was camp. By lunch, everyone knew Grover was going to play for Juniper at sunset by the north bushes. Clarisse claimed credit for the confidence. Silena claimed credit for the papyrus. Thalia claimed credit for the cool. Annabeth let them, because she knew who had actually done the work, and so did Percy, and so did Grover.

"They're going to come watch, aren't they?" Grover said anxiously that afternoon, pacing behind the cabin while Percy helped him pick out a non-chewed shirt and Annabeth helped him comb burrs out of his fur. "Everyone's going to watch me be nervous in public."

"They'll be respectful," Annabeth promised. "I'll make them. I'll set boundaries. Cones. String. Threats."

"I'll stand in front," Percy said. "So you have someone to look at if you get scared. Just look at me. I'll give you thumbs up."

"What if I need more than thumbs up?" Grover asked.

"Then Annabeth will hold up cue cards," Percy said. "Breathe. Slow down. Don't run."

"I can make cue cards!" Ella offered, already scribbling on bark with charcoal. She held up her first card: BREATHE! with seven exclamation points and a drawing of a flower.

Grover laughed, nerves easing just a fraction. "Okay. Okay. Sunset. Pipes. Song. Juniper. I can do this."

"You can," Percy said.

At sunset, half of camp ended up by the north bushes, despite Annabeth's cones and string and threats.

But they were good about it. Clarisse kept the younger kids quiet. Silena handed out wildflowers. Thalia nodded encouragement from the fence.

Juniper was already there, sitting on soft moss under her favorite young maple, wearing the vine ribbon and a crown of the white flowers that bloomed when she laughed. She had saved the seat beside her, patting the moss smooth over and over.

When Grover walked into the clearing with his pipes, carrying a single perfect strawberry in his pocket for after, the whole crowd held its breath.

Percy saw Grover's knees shake. He saw him look at Juniper, then panic, then look for Percy.

Percy gave him a huge thumbs up. Annabeth held up Ella's BREATHE! card. Ella flapped silently and mouthed, you can do it!

Grover breathed. He sat on the moss — not too close, leaving respectful space, just like Annabeth had coached — and lifted his pipes.

"Hi," he said to Juniper, soft enough that only the front row heard. "This is for you. It's called 'Humming in the Berries.' Sorry about the middle. We fixed it."

Juniper smiled. "I'm sure it's wonderful."

Grover played.

The beginning was warm wind through grass. The Juniper theme floated out, sweet and familiar, and Juniper gasped softly and pressed her hands to her mouth. The new middle part rose, hopeful and brave, no wandering, no showing off. The ending came slow and round, landing gently like a firefly on a leaf.

No donkey. Not even a hint of donkey.

When the last note faded, no one spoke for a second. The strawberry leaves rustled. Somewhere, a berry plopped ripe to the ground.

Then Juniper started crying — happy crying, leaf-paste-smelling hanky to her eyes — and she took Grover's hand. Just held it. Fingers intertwined, satyr and dryad, both trembling.

"I loved it," she whispered. "The berries loved it too. Look."

Around them, the strawberry plants along the north row stood straighter, leaves lifted toward the music like they were still listening.

The crowd erupted.

Clarisse whooped. Silena threw wildflowers. Thalia whistled. Ella fell out of her tree and was caught. Annabeth cheered and grabbed Percys arm.

Percy cheered loudest of all. "That's my best friend! That's Grover! Donkey ending defeated!"

Grover turned bright red, but he did not run. He stayed on the moss, holding Juniper's hand, and with his other hand he offered her the perfect strawberry from his pocket.

"I picked you a new one," he said. "For real this time."

Juniper took it, bit half, and gave him the other half. They ate it together, laughing through tears.

"Hand-holding! Strawberry-sharing! All correct! Sweet! Sweet! Sweet!" Ella cheered.

Later, when the crowd finally drifted off to dinner, Percy and Annabeth stayed a minute longer.

Grover and Juniper sat on the moss, not talking much, just humming together. His low pipe-hum, her leaf-hum, fitting together like they had always known how.

Percy watched them and felt something settle in his chest. No monsters. No prophecies. Just a friend being brave and being loved for it.

Annabeth leaned against the fence beside him, arms crossed, smiling that soft smile she rarely showed.

"We did good," she said quietly.

"We did," Percy agreed. "Team Grover."

"Team Grover," Annabeth repeated. She glanced at him sideways. "You know, for someone who can't spell 'sincerely' without help, you give good advice."

"Hey!" Percy protested. "I can spell sincerely. S-I-N — uh — S-E-R —"

Annabeth laughed and shoved him lightly. "Come on, Seaweed Brain. Let's get dinner. Give them some space."

They left Grover and Juniper under the maple as the stars came out, holding hands, sharing quiet humming.

Behind them, Ella perched on the fence, writing furiously in her notebook: Grover + Juniper = brave + kind = music + strawberries = happy ending. Must remember for book. Must tell everyone love is sweet.

Percy offered Ella his arm like a gentleman, the way Silena had taught him as a joke. Ella took it solemnly, flapping once for balance.

The three of them walked back to the pavilion together, while from the north bushes, faint pipe music drifted after them — no longer nervous, no longer wobbly, just happy.

Grover's song, finally finished, played for the girl who had always liked his beginning.

And the whole camp, for once, agreed on something: some kinds of bravery deserved cheering.
