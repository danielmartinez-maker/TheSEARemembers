## Chapter 6: The Sister

Thalia had been awake for most of the night, which was why she knew exactly how much noise a person could make while trying to leave a cabin unnoticed.

Annabeth wasn't particularly good at it. She stopped to find her other shoe beneath the bed, nearly knocked a book off the shelf, and spent an absurd amount of time easing the latch up so it wouldn't click. The latch clicked anyway.

Thalia kept her eyes shut. She heard her sister freeze in the doorway, wait, and finally slip out.

Only then did she sit up.

The repaired shutter rattled softly in the wind. Two weeks ago she would have gone after Annabeth without thinking, and she wasn't entirely sure why she stayed put now. Perhaps it was the conversation they'd had after the first forbidden trip to the Big House. Annabeth had promised to tell her where she was going. She hadn't promised to stop going.

On the desk, a folded note stood against the lamp.

*With Percy at the pavilion. We'll be back before morning lessons. I couldn't sleep.*

It wasn't much. Thalia read it twice.

Annabeth's handwriting had always been small and compact, letters pressed so closely together that their mother once accused her of hiding entire conversations between the lines. The handwriting on the note was familiar. It was the same hand that filled the margins of their old camp books with diagrams and arguments. The note did not explain the sudden gaps in her sister's memory. It did at least mean Thalia wasn't being left to guess where to look.

She put the paper back and went to the window.

The camp's eastern path was closed again. Lanterns hung at the edge of the training field and canvas covered the damaged side of the Hall of the Gods. A smaller drakon had reached the colonnade yesterday, forcing its way through a place that should have been protected by several overlapping wards. Chiron insisted that no one yet knew whether the loose fixture Nico found was a cause or a consequence of the breach. Thalia agreed with him in principle. In practice she was sick of watching the adults stand around damage and tell everyone not to draw conclusions.

She could have offered to help inspect the Hall. She had already offered. Chiron had put her on morning drills with the younger campers instead.

The insult wasn't the assignment itself. Training children mattered. Thalia knew what happened to children who couldn't defend themselves. The insult was being treated as though she couldn't learn anything useful from a repair order because her father's name appeared on it.

She turned away from the window and began putting away the practice equipment she'd left by the door. Her spear went on its usual hooks. Her gloves, still stiff from yesterday's rain, went over the back of a chair. A chipped mug sat beside Annabeth's bed, empty except for a spoon and a line of honey at the bottom.

There had always been a mug there.

When Annabeth was six, she'd insisted that honey made the tea stronger, although she had meant sweeter. Thalia had corrected her every time, until the day their mother came home furious and Annabeth crawled into Thalia's bed without asking for anything at all. After that, Thalia made the tea however her sister wanted it. The argument hadn't seemed worth having.

Now Annabeth sometimes looked at the mug as though it belonged to another child.

The change had begun after the fever. Chiron called it a difficult recovery. A few campers had privately called it a curse until Thalia threatened to make them explain exactly what they meant. Annabeth had woken up knowing how to read texts she'd struggled with the week before but unable to finish stories they had told each other since childhood. She had begun spending more time with Percy, whose own arrival came so close to the illness that even Thalia knew the coincidence didn't prove anything. She also knew she would have distrusted it less if Percy had been anybody other than Poseidon's young prince, a boy who seemed to have a complicated opinion about every god he hadn't met.

She had spent sixteen years noticing things people hoped she wouldn't notice. Beryl's slurred words when she answered the telephone. The moment a pleasant dinner was about to become something neither child could stop. A landlord's impatience at the door. An adult who asked too many questions about whether two girls were traveling alone.

The habit didn't disappear because the camp had a border and a centaur to keep watch.

In the morning, Annabeth returned with damp shoes and a slice of bread folded inside a napkin. She stopped when she saw Thalia already dressed.

"I left you a note," she said.

"I read it. Thanks."

Annabeth looked taken aback. "You're not going to ask where I went after the pavilion?"

"Did you go somewhere else?"

"No. We sat at the east end of the benches because there was a light on in the Big House and Percy thought somebody might want to know if we were keeping watch."

Thalia picked up the empty mug. "That's possibly the worst reason I've ever heard for sitting outside at night."

"It wasn't our reason. We just couldn't sleep."

That, Thalia believed. Her sister had dark circles under her eyes and a pencil mark on her jaw that she had apparently missed while washing her face.

"You've got charcoal on your cheek," Thalia said. "Or graphite. I can't tell with you anymore."

Annabeth rubbed the wrong side.

"Other cheek. There."

Annabeth managed to make the smudge worse. Thalia wet the corner of a cloth and held it out rather than reaching for her face herself. For a moment Annabeth stared at the cloth. Then she took it and cleaned the mark.

It was a small thing. Thalia had expected to feel proud of herself for allowing the space. Instead she felt tired.

"Chiron wants the older cabin leaders at the Hall before lunch," she said. "They're checking the ward brackets. I'm going to see whether he's changed his mind about letting me look at the orders."

Annabeth stopped folding the napkin.

"Can I come?"

"Why?"

"Because I'm good with diagrams, and because I was there when Nico found the fixture. I can help compare what he drew with the repair record."

Thalia wanted to say no. Annabeth had already spent too much time investigating dangerous things and too little time sleeping. But the offer was ordinary enough that it deserved an ordinary answer.

"Ask Chiron. If he says it's safe, you can help. Just don't turn this into another excuse to crawl around the attic."

Annabeth's mouth twitched. "I was planning to use a door."

"That would be a refreshing change."

The conversation might have ended there. Then Annabeth hesitated, studying the spoon Thalia had put beside the washing basin.

"Did I use to drink honey tea every night?" she asked.

Thalia held still.

"You used to ask for it when you couldn't sleep. You'd tell me you weren't scared, you only wanted to make sure I wasn't scared either."

Annabeth smiled, but it wasn't a smile of recognition.

"That's kind of sweet."

"It was a nuisance. I had to wash the mug every morning."

"Then why did you do it?"

"Because you were six."

The answer came out sharper than Thalia intended. Annabeth lowered her eyes to the napkin. Thalia thought about apologizing and couldn't find a way to do it without making the whole conversation about how much she missed a child who was still standing in front of her.

She took the mug to the basin instead.

"I'll see you at the Hall," she said.

Annabeth waited another second, as though she might ask something else. Then she picked up her bag and left for breakfast.

Thalia washed the mug slowly. She was beginning to understand that a person could do everything she'd always done to keep somebody safe and still fail to make them feel known.
---
Chiron had converted the western side of the Hall into a workshop. Canvas screens kept younger campers away from the damaged colonnade, and a line of tables held broken bronze fittings, measured drawings and stone fragments in numbered trays. A pair of Hephaestus campers were working on a bracket in a vise.

Thalia arrived before Annabeth. She wanted Chiron to see her as someone who had come to inspect the repairs, not as an older sister assigned to keep a twelve-year-old away from sharp things.

Nico sat at the first table with his ledger open. He had copied a purchase order into one column and a maintenance entry into another, using colored threads to connect names he believed referred to the same people.

"You're making this look like a murder investigation," Thalia said.

"It would be a lot easier if the paperwork agreed with itself." Nico held out the ledger without ceremony. "The replacement bracket was ordered by Zeus's steward before anybody recorded damage to the original. Either the work was done later than the report says, or the report isn't describing this bracket."

"That signature isn't my father's," Thalia said.

"I know. It belongs to the steward who handles his official orders."

"Then why does everybody keep calling it Zeus's signature?"

"Because they don't want to explain the difference." Nico paused. "You do know how he signs his personal letters, don't you?"

Thalia thought of an expensive sheet of paper sent to Cabin One at the start of summer. Zeus had written three paragraphs on responsibility, expectations and the value of representing their family well. He'd asked whether she and Annabeth were training properly. He hadn't asked what either of them wanted for their birthday.

"When he writes to us, he tends to make it obvious that it's him," she said. "He puts the titles at the top, a personal seal at the bottom, and usually several sentences about what we ought to do next. But the steward could imitate that if he wanted."

Nico made a note. "So we can't use the formatting alone."

"Don't sound so pleased about another thing you don't know."

"I'm pleased when I can rule out an easy mistake."

She almost told him that some people preferred answers. Instead she pulled a chair beside his and asked him to go through the dates.

The drawing showed an old bracket with four holes and a replacement with six. The newer piece could be fixed over damaged stone without drilling out the entire original setting. In the margins somebody had written that the surrounding marble had shifted after a cold winter.

"That's ordinary maintenance," Thalia said.

"Probably. Except the order is dated earlier than the inspection."

"Then find the worker."

"I'm trying."

They had worked through half the entries when Chiron emerged from behind the canvas. He had plaster dust on one sleeve and a wooden molding tucked beneath his arm.

"Thalia. I see you've joined the records team."

"I asked yesterday. You sent me to drills."

"Because the younger campers needed someone who had dealt with a drakon before. They were frightened, and you could teach them what to do if the alarm sounded again."

She hadn't considered that explanation. She didn't particularly like that it was sensible.

"I want to help here, too," she said.

Chiron looked at the bruise still visible above her collar. "You can compare the fittings with their maintenance records. No heavy lifting, and no crossing the active ward line until the installers have cleared it."

"I'm not going to break anything."

"Your assurance would carry more weight if your last inspection hadn't involved trying to lift a cracked paving slab by yourself."

"That was one time."

"It was yesterday."

Nico bent his head over the ledger, hiding a smile. Thalia took the drawings and moved to the next bench.

Annabeth arrived shortly afterward with a straightedge, a notebook and several sheets of clean paper. She hadn't brought Percy. Thalia noticed and immediately resented the fact that she'd noticed.

"Chiron said I could compare the old plans," Annabeth explained. "Where should I start?"

"With the holes," Thalia said, pointing to the bracket. "They don't line up on the two drawings, and somebody signed for the replacement before the original was supposed to be damaged."

Annabeth leaned over the table. For several minutes she said nothing, measuring distances and drawing a smaller diagram beside the first. She turned the paper around twice, then asked Nico for the dimensions of the stone block.

"If the newer bracket covered the original holes, it might hide the crack beneath," she said. "Is that what the replacement was designed to do?"

A Hephaestus camper looked over. "Partly. We needed a larger base plate after the marble started shifting. The six-hole version spreads the load farther from the edge."

"Then it isn't automatically suspicious," Annabeth said.

"No," Nico agreed.

Thalia watched the two of them discuss measurements. Annabeth was younger and smaller than the version of herself Thalia remembered from last summer, a girl who'd once gotten angry because the camp architects wouldn't let her redesign an entire porch. The strange illness seemed to have given her more precision and less patience. It was familiar enough to hurt.

"Can we see where the bracket came from?" Annabeth asked.

Chiron took them to the safety line. The removed bracket had left a pale outline on a northern column, with tiny fragments of broken stone collected beneath the lowest hole. Annabeth crouched to sketch the cracks.

Thalia reached automatically for her collar when she leaned near the loose rubble. Annabeth flinched and looked up.

"I'm not crossing the line."

"I saw the edge moving."

"That was the worker's boot. I'm not going to pull the column down by looking at it."

Thalia lowered her hand. The instinct to grab Annabeth before she fell had survived every argument they'd had and had brought her no closer to knowing what was wrong.

"Sorry," she said, meaning it without enjoying the admission. "Finish the drawing."

Annabeth did. She had to sharpen her pencil twice, and on the second attempt Thalia held the paper steady so the breeze couldn't lift it.

They spent the rest of the morning comparing bracket after bracket. One showed ordinary wear. Another had scratches consistent with a tool slipping during installation. A third had a crack that might have been widened by the drakon. None of it gave Thalia the name of someone who wanted the Hall damaged.

When the midday horn sounded, she was tired of making notes and less certain than when she'd arrived. Nico appeared content with that outcome.

"We know which parts were replaced and which work orders need checking," he said, arranging the pages in a neat stack. "That's more than we knew yesterday."

"It doesn't tell us whether somebody made an opening for a monster."

"It tells us where to look if they did. We can ask the installers why the drawings changed instead of accusing whoever paid the bill."

Thalia looked at the steward's seal again. Her father had enough reasons to be absent without her inventing a new one, and she wasn't going to let his name protect him from questions either.

"Then I want to be there when you ask," she said.

Chiron agreed to arrange a supervised interview. It was the first thing that morning Thalia felt she could take home and use.

Outside, Annabeth put her notebook away and offered Thalia the last apple from her bag. Thalia took it, aware that she had spent several hours with her sister without asking why she trusted Percy more than her own family.

It wasn't a solution. It was, at least, something they had done together.
---
Thalia found Percy in Tempest Hall after lunch, finishing a training session with an instructor who looked glad to be rid of him.

He was swimming laps along the outer wall of the practice pool. The water in the middle was moving against him, pushed through narrow channels by machinery the Hephaestus cabin maintained. Every few lengths Percy stopped to catch his breath, and when the instructor called an end to the session, he gripped the side of the pool for a moment before climbing out.

Thalia had seen the boy fight. Whatever he remembered about the sea from Atlantis, his new body didn't always follow his orders. That was the first reassuring thing she'd noticed about him.

He glanced up, saw her waiting, and accepted a towel from the instructor.

"Am I in trouble for moving the practice shields? Because I've already promised Annabeth I'd put them back where you wanted them."

"That isn't why I'm here."

"Then I'm going to assume it's worse."

She didn't appreciate the attempt at humor. She appreciated even less that it was close to the sort of thing Luke used to say when she was about to lose her temper.

"Walk with me," she said.

He followed her out through the side doors. The training yard was crowded with campers and work crews, so she led him toward the woodpile where the pines muffled the noise. She could see the Hall's white columns beyond the trees.

Percy stopped beneath a low branch and pulled on a dry camp shirt.

"What do you want to know?"

Thalia had spent the morning collecting facts. She had planned to ask questions the way Nico asked them, with dates and carefully chosen words. Standing beside Percy, she discovered that none of her prepared questions was the one she wanted answered.

"Why does Annabeth tell you things she won't tell me?"

He looked at her for several seconds. He was twelve, the same age as her sister, and there was nothing about his small wet hands or the way he had to push his hair out of his eyes that made him look dangerous. That didn't mean he couldn't hurt her.

"I can't answer that for her," he said. "I know she's having a hard time remembering some things. I'm not trying to make that worse."

"Then tell me what you're talking about when you spend hours together. She comes back from the library looking like she's been awake for days, and half the time she can't tell me whether she's eaten. I've spent three weeks asking whether she's sick again. She looks at me as though she can't understand why I'd be worried."

Percy shifted the folded towel beneath his arm.

"We talk about the Hall, mostly. About records and the things Chiron wants us to stop speculating about. Sometimes we talk about other things. She doesn't have to tell me every thought she has, and I don't tell her every thought I have. I don't think that's unusual."

"It is when you barely knew each other a month ago."

He didn't argue. That was irritating in itself.

"Look," he said, "I understand why the timing bothers you. I'm not expecting you to be happy about it. But I can't explain something Annabeth has asked me not to explain. It wouldn't be fair to her, and it probably wouldn't answer the questions you actually have."

Thalia looked at the towel. He kept twisting one end around his hand until she wanted to take it away.

"You know what questions I have?"

"I think you're worried that whatever happened during her illness hasn't stopped happening. I think every time she forgets something, you feel like you're losing another part of her."

Thalia took a step toward him. "Don't tell me how I feel about my sister."

"Then tell me what I got wrong."

The answer stopped her. It was a reasonable request, and she hated having to consider it.

"What you got wrong is thinking this is some new problem you can explain because she's talked to you a few times. I was there when she was little enough to hide behind my legs. I was there when our mother disappeared for days and she pretended she wasn't hungry because she didn't want me to worry. I was there during the fever. I know when she's trying to make herself smaller so somebody else won't be frightened."

Percy's expression had changed. He wasn't smiling anymore.

"You're right," he said. "I don't know those things. I wouldn't pretend I did."

"Then why does she go to you?"

He ran a hand through his damp hair, searching for an answer.

"Maybe because I don't know those things. Maybe it's easier to talk to somebody who doesn't expect her to remember every story about being six." He paused. "That doesn't mean she'd rather have me as family."

Thalia felt the words as a blow, not because they were cruel but because they were almost the thought she'd spent weeks refusing to finish.

"You're making a lot of guesses for somebody who says he doesn't know."

"That's fair. I'm sorry." Percy looked toward the Hall. "What I can tell you is that Annabeth hasn't stopped wanting you around. I heard her asking Chiron whether she could work with you today. She didn't ask him to keep you away."

"I was there. I know she wanted to help with the drawings."

"Then believe the part you saw. You don't have to trust me for that."

The argument had nowhere easy to go. Thalia had been hoping to catch Percy in a contradiction. He kept giving her answers that left Annabeth in charge of her own choices.

"I don't trust you yet," she said.

"All right. I'm not asking you to."

"I don't like that she sneaks around with you."

"Neither do I. The sneaking part, I mean. We made a bad decision with the attic, and Chiron made that pretty clear. I don't want Annabeth to get hurt because I decide I know better than everybody else."

Thalia looked sharply at him. "You took things from the attic together?"

He stared back. She could see the instant he realized he'd said more than he'd meant to.

"Chiron knows," he said. "We returned them. He gave us archive work for taking them without permission."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because I wasn't sure what Annabeth had told you. And because I wasn't particularly proud of it."

Thalia took a slow breath. There were apparently two separate reasons to be furious now, and she was in danger of confusing them.

"Fine. I'll ask Chiron about that later. But if you keep pulling her into things where she might get hurt, I will make sure you answer for it."

"If I hurt her, she can tell me. You can tell me too. I'm not going to pretend being her boyfriend means I never get anything wrong."

Thalia looked at him, startled less by the word than by his willingness to use it. Their closeness had been obvious for weeks; she had nonetheless managed not to call it a relationship because giving it a name made it harder to dismiss as a temporary infatuation.

"Boyfriend," she repeated.

Percy looked embarrassed, though not apologetic.

"Yeah. She is my girlfriend."

"You're both twelve."

"I know how old I am."

"So does she, apparently. You've each become very good at reminding me."

Thalia folded her arms. "I'm going to speak to Annabeth about that. Not because I think she needs permission to like someone, but because I have a right to know whether she's safe when she goes somewhere with you."

"I'd rather you spoke to her than had another argument with me about what she ought to want."

There it was again: a boundary she couldn't force him to cross without making herself look unreasonable.

She left before she said something worse. At the edge of the path, she turned back.

"Percy."

He looked up.

"If you ever ask Annabeth to choose between telling the truth to you and telling the truth to me, I want you to understand which choice I'll expect her to make."

He considered that with an expression that was suddenly very old for his face.

"I won't ask her to choose between us," he said. "But neither of us can make her tell somebody what she's not ready to say."

Thalia walked away with more questions than she'd brought. She hadn't caught him in a lie. She had learned that he was much more important to Annabeth than she'd wanted to admit, and that he could be stubborn without raising his voice.

It wasn't enough to make her trust him. It was enough to make her wish she'd asked a better first question.
---
By the time Thalia got back to Cabin One, Annabeth was sorting a pile of old papers at the desk.

The lamp gave the room a warm patch of light that couldn't reach the dark corners. Beside it sat the honey mug Thalia had washed that morning, filled again. Annabeth had put a second cup next to it.

"That one's yours," she said without looking up. "Silena gave me some mint after dinner. I thought you'd want something warm when you got back."

Thalia set her jacket on the chair. The gesture was familiar and not familiar at once. She could remember Annabeth bringing her tea after bad patrols, although until recently she'd usually accompanied it with a dozen questions about what had happened. Now she seemed to be trying to give Thalia the cup without letting it become a conversation.

"Thanks," she said.

Annabeth kept sorting. The papers were public copies of the Hall records, all stamped with Chiron's permission. Thalia recognized the drawing they'd made that morning.

"I spoke to Percy," she said.

Annabeth's hand stopped halfway to a folder.

"About the Hall?"

"About you. And him."

Annabeth sighed, not loudly, but enough that Thalia could hear how tired she was.

"Thalia, I've told you where I go and when I'll be back. If you want to know something about me, could you try asking me before you corner Percy at training?"

"I tried asking you. You kept saying everything was fine when it obviously wasn't. Do you have any idea how that sounds to somebody who's spent years knowing when you're frightened?"

"I know it frightens you," Annabeth said. "I don't always know how to answer without making things worse."

"Then don't give me an answer just to get rid of me."

Annabeth put down the folder. She sat back, visibly deciding not to hide behind the paperwork.

"All right. Ask me what you want to know."

Thalia hadn't expected the offer. Every question she'd prepared sounded awful now that she had permission to ask it.

"Do you actually want to spend time with him?" she asked. "Not because he understands some mysterious thing better than I do, or because he asked you to help with the Hall. Do you like being with Percy?"

Annabeth looked at her in astonishment.

"Of course I do. I like him a lot. He's funny, and he listens to me argue through a problem even when I'm being impossible. Sometimes he notices when I'm exhausted before I notice it myself. You can dislike that I spend so much time with him, but it isn't something he's making me do."

"He's your boyfriend," Thalia said.

Annabeth flushed a little. Thalia was pleased to discover that her sister could still look embarrassed about an ordinary human thing.

"Yes," Annabeth said. "Is that the problem?"

"It's one of the things you didn't tell me. I had to hear it from him."

"I thought it was obvious. Half the camp's been teasing us about it."

"Obvious isn't the same as having you tell me. I'd like to be somebody you talk to when something makes you happy."

Annabeth looked away. The last sentence had come out more vulnerable than Thalia intended, and she was tempted to cover it with a joke. She didn't.

"I am happy with him," Annabeth said quietly. "It doesn't mean I don't love you. I know you've been worried since I was sick, and I know I keep making it harder. But Percy isn't the reason I've been different. I can't explain the whole reason yet. I'm trying to understand it myself."

"You're allowed to change," Thalia said. "What I can't stand is being the last person to realize you're changing."

Annabeth reached for the tea, found it had gone cold and put it down.

"Some of it happened too fast for me, too."

Thalia sat opposite her. For once she made no attempt to look at the notebook.

"Will you tell me one thing that's been difficult? Something I could actually help with?"

Annabeth considered. "When you tell stories about us as kids, I sometimes can't picture them. It's like hearing about someone who looks like me and has my name, but I don't remember being there. I don't know whether that means the fever damaged something, or whether I'm just having a difficult time finding the memories. Every time you expect me to laugh at a story I can't remember, I feel as though I've disappointed you."

Thalia stared at the cup in her hands.

She had noticed the gaps. She'd even started keeping a little mental list, trying to find a pattern she could take to Chiron. Hearing Annabeth admit the problem out loud made her relieved and miserable in equal measure.

"You could have told me that," she said.

"I was scared you'd think I didn't care about our childhood."

"Annabeth, I was there. I know you cared."

"But I wasn't, in the way you mean. I don't have the memories that prove it to me." Annabeth rubbed the back of her hand, where a faint electrical burn had healed. "When you talk about our mother or the places we lived, it feels as if I'm pretending to belong in my own family."

Thalia set down her mug. She wanted to correct the phrase, tell Annabeth she couldn't possibly be pretending. But that would answer a question Annabeth hadn't asked, and it wouldn't give back a single lost afternoon.

"Then I'll stop testing you on things you don't remember," she said. "I keep telling the stories because they matter to me. I didn't realize it felt like an exam."

"Don't stop telling them. Just don't make me finish the punchline."

Thalia smiled despite herself.

"That's going to be difficult. You were always good at the punchlines."

"Then you can enjoy them twice."

It was almost an ordinary conversation. Thalia felt herself relaxing enough to drink the tea.

Then Annabeth moved the folder aside and exposed a page covered in notes about the Hall. Beside it she'd drawn two versions of the camp layout. One was the current arrangement. The other placed the cabins differently, in a pattern Thalia had never seen.

"What's that?" she asked.

"An old plan I'm comparing with the current one."

"An old plan from where?"

Annabeth hesitated.

It was such a small hesitation, but Thalia had learned to recognize it. The conversation had reached the edge of whatever Annabeth wasn't willing to tell her.

"Please," Thalia said. "We just had ten minutes of actually talking. Don't send me away now."

"I'm not sending you away. I'm asking you to trust that I can't explain the source of that drawing tonight."

Thalia glanced at the page. Percy had been asking about the changed cabin placements from the day he arrived. She didn't want to suspect that her sister was lying. She did suspect there was more here than a lost architectural sketch.

"Do you trust him with the source?"

"Yes."

"Then why not me?"

Annabeth's mouth tightened.

"Because it isn't only my secret. I can't decide what Percy tells people about his family or about what he remembers. And even if I could, I'm not sure you'd believe us. We're trying to find facts other people can check before we tell a story that might hurt them."

Thalia didn't know what could be so unlikely that Annabeth thought it would be kinder to leave her guessing. She thought of Beryl insisting that everything was fine when the electricity had been cut off for the third time. She thought of the way adults used the word *trust* when they wanted two children to stop asking questions.

"I hate hearing that word used as a reason not to answer me," she said.

"I know. But I'm not asking you to ignore what you see. I just can't give you a complete explanation yet."

Thalia pushed back from the table.

"If you're keeping something from me because you're frightened, I want to help. If you're keeping it because Percy asked you to, that's different."

"He's not making me do this," Annabeth said, her voice sharpening. "Please stop turning every choice I make into something he must have decided for me."

Thalia stood. She didn't trust herself to stay seated and argue without becoming unfair.

"I'll talk to you in the morning," she said.

Annabeth looked exhausted. "I'd like that. We can talk without the papers if you want."

Thalia took her jacket. She didn't leave because she wanted to punish Annabeth, or because she thought the conversation was finished. She left because she could hear herself getting closer to saying something that could not be taken back.

Outside, the wind had turned cold. The Hall was visible above the trees with lanterns burning beside its damaged wall. Thalia walked toward the canoe shed and listened to the ropes knock against the poles.

She didn't know what was wrong with her sister. She knew more about how the uncertainty felt to Annabeth than she had an hour earlier, and that made it harder to turn her fear into anger at Percy.

It did not make it impossible.
---
Thalia came back to the cabin after the last watch bell.

Annabeth was asleep, or pretending to be. Her notebook had been closed and placed beneath the lamp, and the two mugs were washed and set upside down on a cloth. Thalia hung her jacket, checked the latch, and sat on her own bunk without turning on the light.

She remembered a motel outside Richmond. Beryl had left them for a weekend with enough money for one proper meal and directions to a grocery store that wasn't there anymore. Thalia had counted the coins five times, bought bread and peanut butter, and told Annabeth that eating the same thing for three days was an exciting experiment.

Annabeth had believed her. Or had been kind enough to pretend.

Thalia could still hear her asking whether the bread would last until their mother came back. She had wanted to say yes with such conviction that she almost convinced herself.

Now she couldn't even tell her sister that the drawings on the desk were safe to keep, because she didn't know where they'd come from. She could protect Annabeth against monsters. She had no idea how to protect somebody against an absence inside their own memory.

She lay down but didn't sleep. At some point she heard Annabeth shift in the other bed.

"Thalia?"

"Yeah."

"Did you come back because you were worried about me?"

"Mostly because I live here."

Annabeth made the soft noise she made when she knew a joke was bad but appreciated it anyway.

"I meant the conversation."

Thalia stared into the dark. She could choose a comforting lie. Annabeth might even believe it, and they could both pretend the evening hadn't hurt.

"I came back because I don't want every argument to end with one of us walking out," she said. "I still want to know what's going on. I'm still angry that you won't tell me. But I don't want you to think that means I don't want you here."

"I don't," Annabeth whispered. "I just don't know how to fix the part that's hurting you."

"Then stop trying to fix it tonight. Go to sleep."

For a while she heard nothing. Then the mattress creaked and Annabeth settled.

Thalia turned onto her side. The repaired window catch held in the wind, and the room stayed dark. She had no revelation, no promise that her sister would wake with her memories restored, and no reason to believe tomorrow would be easy.

It was still their cabin. For now she could bear the difference.
---
Thalia found Annabeth in the Athena cabin before breakfast, sitting at a desk that someone had lent her for the morning. Four books lay open in front of her. One was a treatise on old Greek foundations; another had diagrams of Roman building techniques. The other two seemed to be ordinary camp maintenance logs.

An apple sat untouched beside her notebook, turning brown along the cut edge.

"You know they'll kick you out of here if you leave food on the drawings," Thalia said.

Annabeth looked up. Her hair was coming out of its braid, and she'd left a gray fingerprint across the side of her neck.

"I wasn't eating over them. I put it there to remind myself to have breakfast."

"That's a system with obvious flaws. How long have you been here?"

"Since the Athena counselor opened the library. I've been trying to work out how old the northern Hall foundation is."

Thalia sat on the edge of the adjoining table. She took the apple, examined the cut side and bit into the unbruised half.

"Since you've already decided not to eat it, I assume you won't mind if I do."

"Go ahead. I was starting to think it had become part of the experiment."

The easy answer encouraged Thalia more than she wanted to admit.

She watched Annabeth compare two drawings. One showed the current foundation stones. The other was a much older plan, with fewer columns and a different path leading toward the cabins.

"Is this why you've spent so much time in the library?" she asked.

"Part of it. The Hall is supposed to be three years old, but some of the older plans refer to structures that weren't there when the work started. I don't know whether they reused an existing site or copied a plan that had already been drafted."

"Chiron told us the building history was incomplete."

"He did. I'm trying to figure out which part is actually missing and which part was never written down."

Thalia nodded toward the older map. "You can ask Zoe whether Hecate's cabin has the old construction notes. Chiron said she'd be at the Hall tomorrow."

"I will." Annabeth paused. "I know you think I'm being secretive again. This isn't a secret. You can look at the measurements. I'm just not ready to explain every reason I'm asking these questions."

Thalia felt a little ashamed. She had been about to ask whether Percy had given her the second plan, although she knew the answer would not help.

"I did want to ask you something else," she said. "About our conversation last night."

Annabeth shut the nearest book carefully. "All right."

"When I talked about the tea, you seemed to think I was disappointed you didn't remember it. I wasn't trying to make you feel bad. I just thought it might help you remember something familiar."

"I know. Sometimes it does. Sometimes it makes me feel as though everybody has a better memory of my life than I do."

"Do you want me to stop telling stories?"

"No." Annabeth looked frustrated that the question kept returning. "I want to hear them. I just wish you wouldn't wait for me to tell you the ending. When I can't, you get that look on your face and then both of us pretend it wasn't a test."

Thalia remembered all the little quizzes she'd invented since Annabeth's illness. She'd asked what color their first blanket at camp had been, what song Luke used to sing when it rained, which diner they'd visited on the way east. They had sounded like harmless questions. She'd been searching for evidence that her sister was still the child she knew.

"I can stop doing that," she said. "No more surprise tests."

"You don't have to stop asking me things, Thalia. You just need to tell me when something matters to you. If you say you miss a memory, I can understand that. When you pretend it's a joke and then look disappointed, I don't know what I'm supposed to do."

Thalia took the final bite of the apple and turned the core in her hand. The simplest answer had been sitting in front of her for weeks. She didn't enjoy admitting it.

"I miss our old routine," she said. "You'd finish my stories before I'd told them properly, and half the time you'd correct details I didn't even remember getting wrong. Now I can't tell whether the problem is your memory or whether you don't want to talk about any of it."

"It's the memory. Mostly. Sometimes I don't want to talk because I know I'll get the facts wrong."

Thalia nodded. She couldn't make those memories return. She could at least stop making their absence another thing Annabeth had to manage.

"Have you spoken to Chiron about it?" she asked.

"About the memory gaps, yes. He says we should keep a record of what comes back and what doesn't. I don't want to turn every morning into an examination."

"Then we won't. But if there's something practical I can do, I'd like to know."

Annabeth thought for a while.

"Would you help me label these drawings? Some of the old names for the paths don't match what people call them now. You've been at camp longer than I can remember in this life, and you'd know which ones changed."

Thalia looked at the spread of papers. For once Annabeth wasn't asking for patience in the abstract. She was asking for a task with a clear beginning and end.

"Give me the pencil. The eastern path was called the old orchard road before Chiron moved the supply sheds. And that mark isn't a shrine. It's the pump house they demolished when we got the new filters."

Annabeth moved the notebook toward her. She asked for dates, and Thalia gave the ones she knew. When she wasn't certain, they left a blank. That proved harder than Thalia expected; she hated leaving anything unfinished where her sister might need an answer.

After half an hour they had corrected three labels and identified two that needed somebody older to confirm. Annabeth was about to go on when the breakfast horn sounded for the second time.

Thalia closed the notebook.

"We should eat. Your beautiful experiment has been trying to turn an apple into dust for an hour."

Annabeth looked toward the doorway. "I wanted to finish the map."

"It'll still be here after breakfast. And you have training. You've missed enough sessions that Matt asked whether I'd started hiding you under the floorboards."

"Yesterday's session was optional."

"It wasn't optional for your footwork."

Annabeth rolled her eyes, but she packed her bag and followed Thalia out. Neither sister pretended the previous night had been resolved. It was easier to walk together when they had finished a small piece of work that belonged to both of them.
---
Training began with a horn that nobody was expecting.

Thalia had told the younger campers they were practicing an emergency movement drill. She had not told them when. That was the whole point: if they only knew how to run to a safe position when a counselor gave a five-minute warning, the next drakon would find them standing in the middle of the path arguing about which direction to go.

She blew one long note from the hill overlooking the arena.

A dozen campers looked up. Several reached for weapons; two ran in opposite directions. The youngest, a girl with a shield nearly as large as she was, stayed where she stood and waited for somebody to tell her what the horn meant.

Thalia counted three seconds before Clarisse's voice cut across the field.

"Movement, not contact. Don't start fighting shadows. Everybody with the practice group, follow the marked route to the lower pavilion."

The younger campers began moving. Thalia watched from the hill as they formed something that almost resembled a line. Bea, the Hermes camper Clarisse had been teaching, took one look at the narrow path and moved to the front. She did not notice that she was leaving the smallest camper behind.

Thalia came down the slope, cutting across the line before they reached the woods.

"Hold. Everybody back to the marker."

A collective groan rose from the group.

"That was the first attempt," she told them. "A useful one, because at least now we know which mistakes we're going to make if something actually comes through the wards. Bea, you were quick. What happened to the people behind you?"

Bea's face reddened beneath her borrowed helmet. "I thought we were supposed to reach the pavilion as fast as we could."

"You're supposed to reach it as a group. If the people at the back get caught because the fast ones sprint ahead, we haven't evacuated anyone safely. That's just a race."

Clarisse stood beside the practice rack, arms folded. She had the patient look of somebody waiting for Thalia to finish giving a lecture so she could get back to weapons.

"Go again?" she asked.

"After everybody knows the route."

Annabeth was waiting near the path with a small slate and the map they'd worked on that morning. She had volunteered to help mark places where campers would be forced into single file. Thalia had almost refused, then remembered their conversation in the Athena cabin and asked Chiron whether mapping counted as an acceptable noncombat duty.

It did.

Annabeth had been delighted enough that Thalia was embarrassed she'd nearly taken it away from her.

Now Annabeth pointed to the narrow stretch beneath the overhanging willow.

"They can't all fit through there at once. If someone falls, the people behind will pile up. We should take the wider route around the old pump house."

"That takes longer," Clarisse said.

"Thirty seconds if they're running properly. More than that if they're dragging somebody out of the ditch."

Thalia looked at the two paths. The narrow one was shorter, but a loaded cart would barely fit between the trees. She had walked it a hundred times and never thought of it as a place where a group could become trapped.

"She's right," she said. "We'll use the wide route."

Clarisse inclined her head. It wasn't agreement about everything, but it was agreement about the path.

They went through the route on foot, pointing out where the group should stop if anybody needed help. Thalia asked Bea to walk beside the smallest camper and make sure she could see the signal marker at each turn. Bea looked as though she was about to protest that she could run much faster than this. Then she adjusted her shield and slowed to match the younger girl's stride.

At the pavilion they stopped for water. Several campers were breathing hard even though they had barely covered the length of the arena. Thalia made a note to suggest shorter, more frequent drills rather than pretending every child could run as long as she could at sixteen.

Clarisse handed her a cup.

"You could've warned them before the horn," she said.

"Then it wouldn't have tested their reactions."

"Sure. But half of them are still convinced a drakon is hiding in the woods."

Thalia looked at the nearest camper. He was gripping a practice spear with both hands, eyes fixed on the trees.

She hadn't intended to frighten him. She'd been thinking about the previous attack, the way the warning had come late, and how close Annabeth had been to the creature's mouth. Some part of her believed that if she could train everybody hard enough, none of it would happen again.

"I told them it was a drill," she said.

"After they panicked."

Thalia took the criticism badly. Mostly because Clarisse wasn't wrong.

She walked over to the boy with the spear and crouched until she could see his face.

"That was a practice signal," she said. "There isn't a monster approaching. If there had been, we would have sent you farther from the boundary and the counselors would be armed. You did the right thing by waiting for instructions, but next time I want you to check which horn you heard before you start preparing to fight. Can you tell me the difference between movement and contact?"

The boy looked uncertain. Then he repeated the signals, stumbling on one of them but getting the rest right.

"Good. We're going to do another round in a few minutes, and you'll know exactly why the horn is sounding this time."

He nodded and loosened his grip.

When she got back to the water barrel, Clarisse was examining the drill map with Annabeth. The two of them had begun discussing what would happen if the wide path was blocked by a fallen tree.

"It would force everyone toward the lower bridge," Clarisse said. "Which is worse if the creek is high."

"Then we need a second option that doesn't use either path," Annabeth replied. "Maybe through the orchard. The fence comes down if we remove two pins."

Thalia leaned over the map. "Would there be enough room to move the younger campers without trampling the plants?"

"Probably not without damaging the beds," Annabeth admitted. "But if there's a monster, the strawberries can survive being stepped on. Campers might not."

Silena would have had something to say about the strawberries, but Thalia suspected she'd agree with the priority.

They walked the orchard route together before approving it. The fence did come apart, though one of the pins was bent and required Clarisse to pull it out with a pair of pliers. Annabeth marked the obstruction and added a note that it should be replaced before anybody relied on it during an evacuation.

The little job gave Thalia time to notice how Annabeth and Clarisse worked together. Clarisse asked whether the plan would hold under real conditions. Annabeth showed her the measurements instead of pretending the question was a personal attack. When Annabeth suggested a route that would put the youngest children near the creek, Clarisse explained why she didn't trust it. They disagreed, changed the drawing and moved on.

Thalia had spent so long worrying about the secrets Annabeth was keeping that she'd almost forgotten what her sister looked like when she was doing something well.

"How do you want to run the next attempt?" she asked Annabeth.

Annabeth glanced up, surprised.

"Start with the group where they are and have Bea lead them through the wider path. Then block it with a marker and make them take the orchard route. That way we'll find out whether they understand the backup plan instead of just memorizing the first one."

"Good. You can explain the route before we start. I'll handle the horn."

Annabeth took the slate and addressed the group. She was precise, perhaps too precise for the ten-year-olds who mostly wanted to know when lunch would happen. Thalia watched her realize she was losing them and simplify her instructions without being prompted.

"You see the red ribbon? That's the place you stop if the path is blocked. You don't decide the next route by yourselves. The person leading the group checks the backup on the map, and everybody stays together. We're going to practice it once slowly, then once at a run."

Bea raised her hand.

"Can I lead again? I know I went too fast, but I think I can do it properly this time."

Annabeth looked toward Thalia. Thalia almost said that Bea should wait until she was older. Instead she asked Clarisse whether the girl was ready.

"She's ready to try," Clarisse said. "I'll walk behind them and make sure nobody gets left."

The exercise worked better the second time. Bea checked the campers behind her, and when they reached the blocked path, she took a few seconds to read the map rather than assuming she knew the route. A younger boy tripped on a root near the orchard fence, but Bea stopped, waited for Clarisse to help him and didn't restart the line until he was walking steadily.

Thalia was proud enough to feel a little ridiculous. She hadn't taught them anything about fighting during the drill. She had taught them not to leave one another behind.

When the group finally reached the pavilion, Clarisse gave Bea a brief nod and told her she'd led them well. The girl stood a little straighter, as though the helmet had finally begun to fit.

Thalia glanced at Annabeth.

"You were right about the path. I would've sent them under the willow if you'd let me."

"It wasn't a bad route for two people," Annabeth said. "It was bad for fourteen. We had to test it to know the difference."

"You say that as though you think I enjoy changing plans."

"I think you enjoy plans working. You're just less enthusiastic about the part where somebody tells you they won't."

Thalia laughed. She hadn't expected to, and it felt good.

For a moment she wanted to tell Annabeth that working together had reminded her of being younger, before the fever, before the books began filling with drawings of a camp she didn't recognize. She decided not to. It wasn't fair to turn every good moment into evidence in a case Annabeth hadn't agreed to argue.

Instead she asked whether her sister wanted to help with the next lesson.

"I have an archive appointment after lunch," Annabeth said. "Chiron wants me to finish labeling the public records we borrowed. I can come back afterward."

Thalia nodded. "I'll see you there if we're not finished."

Annabeth looked at her with something close to relief. She gathered the slate and went to return it to the supply bench.

Clarisse stood beside Thalia, watching her go.

"She's good at that," Clarisse said.

"She always liked maps."

"I meant explaining them to the little ones. Half the cabin listens better when she talks than when I do."

Thalia didn't answer right away. She could have told Clarisse what she'd noticed about Annabeth's memory since the illness. She could have asked whether Percy had mentioned anything strange. She was tired of turning every conversation about her sister into an interrogation.

"Then maybe we should ask her to help more often," she said.

Clarisse gave her a brief, measuring look, then returned to the arena with the practice shields.

Thalia remained by the pavilion until the rest of the campers had collected their equipment. The map was still on the table, held flat by a stone. It showed two routes with arrows and one blocked path crossed out in red. It did not show whether Annabeth would remember being here a month from now or whether Thalia would be allowed to understand the drawings in the other notebook.

It showed enough for the next drill.

Thalia waited near the training ground until Percy's bout was over. She hadn't meant to seek him out again, but the question about the Hall stayed with her. So did Nico's report about Zeus's steward signing the orders for new wards.

"Jackson," she called.

Percy put down his practice sword. "Are we having yesterday's conversation again?"

"A different one. About the Hall."

He glanced toward the other campers. She led him to the water barrel at the edge of the field, far enough away to speak without drawing an audience.

"Nico says my father's people signed the equipment order," she said.

"That's what the paperwork shows."

"And he thinks Zeus made the rest happen."

"I don't know what Nico thinks. He showed us a name on a receipt. That isn't proof your father sent the drakon, and it sure isn't proof he did anything else."

Thalia was taken aback. She had rehearsed an accusation, not an answer with qualifications.

"You don't think he did it?"

"I think he might know more about the Hall than Chiron does. I think that's worth asking. I don't know what he knew about the prophecy, and I don't know who wrote that note."

"You think my father would lie about it."

Percy paused. "I think gods lie. Mine does too. That doesn't tell us which god did what."

It was the first thing he'd said that sounded like somebody who understood that loving a parent and distrusting him could happen in the same afternoon.

"Don't use Annabeth to get at him," Thalia said.

"I'm not going through her to get to Zeus."

"People always do," Thalia replied before she could stop herself.

Percy waited.

Thalia could hear children shouting on the far side of the training ground. She had spoken to too many adults who said they only wanted a word with their father and somehow found themselves questioning the children instead.

"Annabeth is doing this because she wants answers," Percy said. "And she gets to decide how involved she is. If you want to know why, ask her. She isn't a messenger for either of us."

"I did ask her."

"Then I hope you listened."

Thalia looked at him sharply. The boy had no idea how difficult that was for her. Or maybe he did, and that was why he had stopped trying to persuade her.

"I'll ask about the Hall myself," she said.

"Good." Percy picked up his practice sword. "Chiron can show you the repair order."

She walked away still angry, but no longer sure the anger belonged to Percy.

Percy found Annabeth sitting on the low roof of Cabin One just before evening curfew. She was close enough to the window that she could climb back inside without setting foot on the steps. Her shoes were beside her and her notebook lay open across her knees.

"You realize the whole point of a roof is that you're supposed to stay underneath it," he said, testing the ladder she'd left against the wall.

Annabeth looked over the edge. "I'm inspecting the shutter Thalia and I repaired. We didn't have a chance to test it properly before the weather changed."

"You're sitting on the opposite side of the roof."

"That's where the best light is. Come up or go away, but don't stand there telling me where I'm allowed to sit. Thalia already has that covered."

Percy climbed carefully. The shingles were rough beneath his hands, and the roof sloped more steeply than it looked from the path. He sat a little distance from her, mindful that if he knocked her notebook off the edge she'd make him retrieve every page before she spoke to him again.

"How was the drill?" he asked.

"Better than I expected. Thalia made me help plan the evacuation routes, which was actually useful. We found a place where a group of fourteen campers would've gotten stuck if somebody fell. Clarisse helped us test the backup through the orchard."

"Did Thalia like your suggestions?"

"She didn't throw the slate at me, so I'd call that progress."

He smiled. Annabeth closed the notebook and rested it beneath one palm.

"We talked about the memory gaps, too. Properly, for once. I told her how strange it feels when she tells me about a childhood I can't remember. She was trying so hard not to look hurt that I nearly stopped talking."

"Did you?"

"No. She needed to hear it, and I was tired of pretending I'd remember the next story if I tried hard enough."

Percy looked out across the cabins. He could see the light in Cabin Three, where Silena was probably rearranging the duty rota yet again. His sisters had known him for only a few weeks; they didn't ask why he could describe monsters he'd supposedly never met, but he sometimes caught them noticing how much he understood and how much of his own palace childhood he couldn't recall.

"I keep doing the same thing with Amphitrite," he admitted. "When she writes about something we did when I was little, I answer around it. I tell her what I've been doing at camp and hope she doesn't ask whether I remember the day she's talking about."

"Have you thought about telling her your memories are strange, without explaining why?"

"I have. But she's already worried about me being here. I don't want her to think the distance has made me forget her."

Annabeth nodded. She knew the difference between concealing a fact to protect somebody and concealing it because admitting the truth might change the relationship. They had been doing a little of both.

"Thalia asked me whether I actually wanted to be with you," she said.

Percy looked at her. "And?"

"I told her the truth. That I like being with you because you make me laugh, and because I can say a thing without first deciding whether I remember it from the correct childhood. I think she was surprised that I had such an ordinary answer."

"That's almost insulting. I was hoping you said I had magnificent hair."

"Your hair was not mentioned."

"Unfair. That should've been a central part of my defense."

Annabeth smiled, then put her hand over his. Percy turned his palm and held hers properly, careful of the notebook balanced on her knees.

"I don't want you to feel like you have to choose between me and Thalia," he said. "I've seen what she does when she thinks you're in trouble, and I know she was there for years I can't give you back."

"She's angry about being excluded. Some of that anger is reasonable. Some of it isn't." Annabeth squeezed his fingers. "I'm not going to apologize for loving you, Percy. And I'm not going to pretend I have the same memories she does just because she needs me to. I can care about both of you without agreeing to everything either of you wants."

He thought about that for a minute.

"Have I been asking you to do things you don't want?"

"Sometimes you decide we'll go after a mystery before I finish telling you why it's a bad idea. But I usually tell you when I think you're wrong, and you usually listen eventually."

"Eventually sounds generous."

"It is. I'm being nice to you."

He laughed, then looked down at the ladder. "We should get off this roof before Thalia finds us and adds a new rule to the cabin."

Annabeth kept his hand for another moment.

"She's already told me she doesn't like it when I disappear without saying where I'm going. So I left her a note this time. It says I'm checking the shutters and will be back before curfew."

"Did you mention me?"

"Yes. In perfectly legible handwriting."

"That's considerate. I'd hate to be an undocumented presence."

She put the notebook under her arm and stood. Percy went down the ladder first and steadied it while Annabeth climbed after him. At the bottom, the window on the side of Cabin One opened. Thalia looked out, took in the ladder, and frowned.

"Next time you inspect the roof, use a safety line," she said. "I don't have the patience to explain to Chiron why two supposedly intelligent campers fell off our cabin."

Annabeth looked at Percy. He could tell she was trying not to smile.

"We'll use one," she promised.

Thalia closed the window without another word.

Percy carried the ladder back to its hook. Annabeth went inside after giving his hand one last squeeze. The three of them still disagreed about plenty of things, but at least tonight nobody had needed to win the argument before they could go to bed.
