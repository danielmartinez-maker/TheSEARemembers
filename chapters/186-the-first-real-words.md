## Chapter 186: The First Real Words

Nobody in Cabin Three was in a hurry on Sunday morning, and Percy knew it before he opened his eyes, because the stove had been lit by somebody who had lit it at their own speed and then sat back down again, which was a sound the cabin only ever made on a day that had nowhere to be.

He lay a moment and let the room tell him the rest. The light coming past the shutter was the flat grey white of a late October morning over water, which meant it was past eight and the sun was behind the ridge. Two of the far bunks were empty and two were not, and from the not-empty ones came the particular breathing of people who had decided, collectively and without a vote, that the day could wait. Somewhere in the wall a beetle was doing something industrious and entirely unimportant. The tenth lantern, on its hook outside the window, had gone out hours ago and left a smell of cold brass and burnt honey coming in around the frame.

"Percy." Clarisse's voice came across the room with no greeting in it at all. "If you're awake you're eggs. It's Sunday and Sunday's you."

"I'm being the eggs."

"You're being the mattress. There's a difference and one of them is breakfast."

He got up, because that was a sentence that did not improve by being answered, and went across the cold boards in his socks to the stove, and found that whoever had lit it had done a good job, which meant Silena, which meant the eggs were going to have to be bad on purpose just to keep the family's hand in.

Six apples out of yesterday's harvest sat in the enamel bowl on the table, all of them bruised in the places where somebody had decided a bruise was a seasoning. Behind them stood the jug from the press shed, wrapped in a cloth the way Silena wrapped things that were still thinking about what they wanted to be, and the whole room smelled of cider and wet wool and the honest sweet rot that a bag of windfalls leaves behind a door. Down at the bottom of the slope the lake lay closed under a sky scrubbed flat by yesterday's wind, and the hill above it was doing nothing: no axe, no cart, no rope ladder on the climbing wall. The wood crew had Sundays. So, apparently, did everybody else.

Clarisse had the winter list and her tea, and was reading it the way she read everything, which was with her thumb flat on the table and her jaw set, as though the page might try something. She had not written on it since Friday. Percy knew this because he had watched her pick it up, read it from the top, put it down, pick it up again, and then leave it face up, which in this cabin was the closest thing to a day off that that particular woman had ever declared.

"The pine's still there," he said, at the stove.

"The pine knows where it is."

"You've read that page four times since I've been standing here."

"Five," Clarisse said. "And reading's free. Put the butter out."

From the corner came the sound of a needle going through wool with more decision than the wool deserved, and Silena's voice after it, mild and entirely without looking up: "He's stalling. He always stalls before eggs. It's the whisk. He's afraid of the whisk."

"I'm not afraid of the whisk."

"You held it wrong for a week in July and said it was a grip thing."

"It was a grip thing. My grip's changed."

"Your grip hasn't changed," Silena said, and drew the thread through and bit it off, "and the eggs are going to tell us that in about four minutes."

She had taken the corner of the table with her darning and her basket and the apron she had worn since Friday, and she worked the way she cooked, which was with a rhythm that did not need supervision and a pair of eyes that missed nothing anyway. Every so often her hand went to the pocket of the apron and touched it, flat, the way a person checks a door they have already locked, and then went back to the wool, and nobody in the room remarked on it because nobody in the room had seen it except Percy, and Percy had decided in July that his middle sister's pockets were none of his business and had stuck to the decision in October.

"So what's on today," he said.

"Whatever's left of Saturday," Silena said. "Cider in two places, apples in three, and the press shed wants a wash. The cider's had its day, so it can be tasted at lunch and I'll hear about it at lunch, and I've already decided how I feel about the verdict."

"You've decided it's good."

"I've decided it's mine."

Clarisse made the short noise that meant she had enjoyed that and was not going to do it again. "Frost tonight," she said, to the page. "Or tomorrow. It's been coming a week and it's been rude about it."

"Who said frost."

"Annabeth, at dinner, with the glass." Clarisse turned the list over, face down, which was the Friday-night rule applied to a Sunday and which meant the argument was over. "Also there's a front. Also nobody's to leave the pump dripping, because last time the pump froze and Marcus made it everybody's problem."

"Last time Marcus was fifteen."

"Marcus is still fifteen. Time doesn't fix that." She pushed her chair back and came to the stove and looked into the pan with the expression of a woman inspecting a defensive position, and took the whisk out of his hand, and did the eggs properly in about eleven seconds, and put the whisk back. "There. That's the grip. That's the whole thing."

Percy looked at the pan, and at his sister, and said, "You've been waiting to do that since July."

"I've been waiting since July," Clarisse agreed, "and if you tell anybody I'll deny it and it will be Silena's word against mine, and Silena's word against mine is a thing this family survives twice a week."

"Three times," Silena said, from the corner. "Tuesdays are mine."

The eggs were, in fact, very good, and Percy said so, and was told that saying so was not the same as having made them, and the four kids who lived in the far bunks came in one at a time in their coats with their hair flat, and the table filled up the way a table fills on a Sunday, slowly and with a great many returns for the bread.

Annabeth came down at half past eight for the second time that morning, having gone up the hill once already for her field book and come back down for the toast, which she explained by holding up the toast and saying, "This is a survey instrument. It's keeping my hands warm so they can hold the pencil," and then sat down and ate it like a person who had simply wanted toast.

"The glass at the Big House has been falling since Friday teatime," she said, to the room, while Clarisse went and got the list again and turned it face up. "Chiron says hard frost before tomorrow morning, and he said tonight, and then he said he'd rather not guess between them, which is his way of saying he's already guessed and won't say which."

"So tonight," Clarisse said, writing.

"So tonight," Annabeth agreed. "Bring the tools in off the porch. Not the greenhouse — that stays where it is, the bank's the bank's problem and it wants a warm day." She looked around the table with the flat blue-grey eyes that did not miss a spoon, and added, "And somebody's been at the cider jug."

"Somebody's been at the cider jug," Percy said, "and somebody's going to be at the cider jug again at lunch, and I recommend the left-hand mug, which has less cloth in it."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer this family's got before nine on a Sunday," Annabeth said, and took the left-hand mug herself, and drank, and made the face she made when a thing was worse than she wanted and better than she expected, and put it down, and looked at him over it with her whole attention for about a second and a half, which at eight forty in the morning in a full cabin was the entire conversation and also plenty.

Percy had spent a lot of his first months on this hill checking things: the moorings, the weather, the faces, whether the ground would hold. That was what a person did when they had been handed a family they did not remember and a life they had not chosen. What he had not expected, and what had quietly stopped happening somewhere around the end of September, was the checking. The cabin went on being the cabin. The stove lit. The list got read. Clarisse counted things and Silena fed people and Thalia took the stairs two at a time from a hill that did not have a rail, and in the middle of it there was a girl with an opinion about mugs who had walked down a hill twice for toast, and the whole business had become the thing he had not known he was being given, which was a Sunday.

He put the kettle on, because the kettle was on the stove and the stove was lit, and because that was how a morning like this one ended.

---

Annabeth Grace went back up the hill at ten to nine and did not take the path that went past the range, which was not on her way, and which she took anyway, at a distance, in the flat morning light, because she had been raised by a woman who had learned early that some things get said faster when nobody is watching them get said.

The range was brown and empty and the targets at twenty paces were pale with weather, and there was one set of prints coming up the west end of the field and one set going back, and the prints going back had a small drag to the left one, which was the drag her sister had been putting into the ground since a Thursday in September, and which she had been removing, one quarter-inch at a time, from a bow hand instead.

Cabin One was quiet and cold and smelled faintly of the cocoa that had been made at an hour no cabin should have cocoa, and the granite stone with its words sat on the sill of the weather window doing its work. Annabeth put the field book on the table, opened it to the new sheet, uncapped the ink, and started the margin line for Sunday while the room was still empty: 09:02. WIND N. GLASS FALLING. FROST BY MORNING LIKELY.

She was writing the second half of the sentence when the door came in ahead of a piece of the outdoors, and her sister arrived the way a weather system arrives, which was all at once and with opinions.

Thalia Grace had her bow over her shoulder and her hat pushed back and her hands in the state hands get into at eight o'clock on an October morning when they have spent an hour holding something cold. Her cheeks were up. Her temper, by the evidence of the door, was excellent, and her entire manner was the specific cheerfulness of a woman who intends to be cheerful until anybody asks her a question.

"Cocoa," she said.

"There's no cocoa."

"There's cocoa in the making. I'm making it. I'm eighteen and it's my house and there's no law." She put the kettle on and then stood in front of the stove with her back to it and her arms out, warming, and looked at the table and the ink and the sheet, and said, "You've been up an hour."

"I've been up an hour."

"You've been up an hour and you've already been down."

"I've been down twice," Annabeth said. "Toast."

"You walked down twice for toast."

"I walked down once for the book and once for the toast, which is two trips with one purpose, and you're counting my trips because you don't want to talk about the field."

There was a small pause of exactly the length that a person needs to decide how many layers to put on a sentence, and then Thalia said, in the voice of a woman reading an inventory: "The field was brown. The wind was off the left. The targets are going soft. Calder was four minutes late and had a hole in his left glove, and I shot a forty-seven, and that's the whole morning and I've said it all in one breath so nobody can interrupt with a question in the middle."

"You noticed his glove had a hole in it."

"I noticed everybody's gloves. I'm a protector. It's a job." The kettle went on and Thalia turned around and came to the table and took the piece of bread that had been sitting under Annabeth's notes, which was the actual reason she had come up the hill, and they both knew it, and neither of it was mentioned.

Annabeth wrote WIND N in the margin again, in a smaller hand, because the pen needed something to do while her mouth was being careful.

Her sister had been shooting on Sunday mornings for three Sundays now. Two weeks, Annabeth thought. She has corrected it twice and both times she corrected it the same way, which means she has been counting too. There had been a Sunday in October when Thalia had come back from the range with her ears red and her elbow changed, and had put her bow in the corner and said nothing about it for four hours, and had then, at about nine at night, said the word *Sunday* out loud in the middle of a sentence about rope and seen what she had done and gone red up the neck. And every Sunday since had been a Sunday with an eight o'clock in it, and Annabeth had watched the eight o'clock arrive three times now and had not once said the word back.

She also knew about the boot. She had known about the boot since a Thursday in September, which was the sort of knowledge a sister acquires by being quiet in a doorway, and she had spent the whole of Saturday's orchard run watching the good boot take the slope first and had not said a single word about it in either direction. That was the arrangement. The arrangement was that Thalia did the staging and Annabeth did the not-seeing, and both of them kept it up out of the specific love that consists of leaving a person their dignity at exactly the size they have chosen.

"You've got ink on your ear," Thalia said.

"I've had ink on my ear since August."

"Yeah, but today I'm saying it, so today it's a thing." Thalia chewed, swallowed, and then did the thing Annabeth had been watching the whole morning for without letting her face prepare for it, which was that her eyes went past the window, down the slope, along the flat brown line of the range, and stayed there about two seconds longer than the view had earned. Not at the targets. At the west end, where the path came up out of the pines.

Annabeth kept her pencil moving.

"He says things," Thalia said, to the window, in a completely different voice, flat and factual and aimed nowhere, "and then he doesn't take them back. That's the whole problem. A man says a thing about arrow spine in September and you think he's talking about arrows, and then it's five weeks later and you're standing in your own cabin at nine in the morning and you find you've still got the sentence and he's still got the glove, and that's — no. It's nothing. It's a field with two sets of prints in it."

"It's a field with two sets of prints in it," Annabeth agreed.

"Don't do that."

"Do what."

"Agree in that voice." Thalia put the crust down and came around the table and put both hands on Annabeth's shoulders from behind and looked down at the sheet with her chin almost on her sister's hair, and her hands were still cold, and she left them there. "What's the margin say?"

"Frost by morning likely."

"Likely."

"He said he'd rather not guess between them."

"That's a man who's guessed," Thalia said, "and doesn't want to be quoted in the spring." She squeezed once, hard, the way she had done since Annabeth was small enough to be lifted, and let go, and went to the kettle, and over the rising of it said, in the voice that had been reconstructed from parts and was entirely normal, "Right. Cocoa. Two cups. And if you write the word *Sunday* anywhere on that page, genius, I'll tell Chiron about the seam you drew at the cairn and how you can't do an axe."

"I did the seam in one pass."

"I know. That's the best thing about it. A person who could do that in one pass and still can't do an axe is worth the whole trip up the hill." Thalia set the second cup down beside the field book without being asked, and stood over it a second with her hands around her own, and said, without looking up, "He's coming Friday with the good arrows. I'm not telling you that. I'm telling the cocoa."

"Received," Annabeth said, and wrote nothing about it anywhere, and drank the cocoa that had been made too thick, which was how her sister liked it and would never ask for, and the morning went on over the two of them in the small warm room while the glass kept falling somewhere down at the Big House and the frost went on not having happened yet.

---

Percy had brought nothing to do on Sunday afternoon, and had carried it down the hill with him folded under one arm in the shape of a promise he had made at lunch in front of both his sisters, which was that he was going to sit somewhere and be no use to anybody until dark.

Clarisse had gone to the press shed about the wash. Silena had the corner. Thalia had the range, because Thalia had the range. The camp had gone on being the particular quiet thing it became on a Sunday, which was not asleep and not idle so much as it was a hill with its hands in its pockets, and by the time he and Annabeth came down the west path at one o'clock there was nobody on it but a wood-crew kid carrying an armload of nothing in particular and a dog that had opinions about the frost.

The nine lanterns along the switchbacks stood cold in their brackets, amber glass gone dull the way good glass goes when it has nothing to do, with a bead of wax under each hook and a black thumbprint of soot on every bracket where somebody's hand had been in the dark. Up on the Cedar Brake the tenth had been taken down and relit and left for the evening, and the smell up there was burnt honey and cold brass, and below it the whole path ran down the hill in the flat afternoon light with the grass on either side of it gone silver-brown and stiff, and the lake at the bottom of it all lying still and grey and enormous.

Annabeth had the fair sheet, the ink, the pencil, and the small leather roll, all of it in the oilcloth under her arm, and she had a spot, which she had chosen at lunch in the tone of a person announcing a survey permit.

"The boathouse planks," she said. "South corner. They get the light until three and the wind goes over the roof. It's the best working real estate on this hill and I'm claiming it before the ducks do."

"You've been thinking about this since breakfast."

"I've been thinking about it since Saturday at two in the afternoon, standing on a slab, and I didn't have the ink." She went ahead of him down the last switchback without looking back, because she knew exactly where he was without looking, and because knowing where he was without looking was one of the load-bearing facts of her life that she had stopped examining somewhere around the middle of September.

The boathouse was shut for the season and did not look it so much as it looked patient. Its doors stood open on the cold with the rope coiled inside and the hooks full of the summer's ghosts: a paddle with a mended blade, the blue bag with the swimsuits in it, a coil of somebody's fishing line going grey in the corner. Past it, on the rack past the boathouse, six canoes lay upside down and roped the way they had lain since the end of September, green hulls up, gunwale rings dull, each one wearing its tie-down like a belt it had been told to keep on.

And there, at the end of the rack where the planks ran out toward the water, sat the skiff, and on the skiff's bow sat the heron, carved out of whatever the previous owner had had to hand, painted once and weathered since, standing with its head up and its beak pointed at the far shore at an angle of permanent and slightly comic disapproval.

Percy looked at it the way he looked at it every time, which was to say he did not look away fast.

"You're doing the heron thing," Annabeth said, behind him, over the oilcloth.

"I'm appreciating a carving."

"You're doing the heron thing where you look at it and decide not to say the thing about it, and you've done it four times since September, and it's on my list of things I'm not asking you about."

"It's a good list. It's short. It's mostly herons."

"It's one heron and one man who won't talk about it." She put the oilcloth down on the planks at the south corner, where the light was, as promised, lying flat and weak and warm along the boards, and where the wind went over the roof of the boathouse and came out the other side with nothing in it, and unrolled the sheet and put the pencil behind her ear and stood back to look at the site like a person taking possession of a county.

They had learned, over months, the shape of an afternoon like this one, and neither of them had ever written it down. She worked. He did not. She needed both hands for the pen and needed him within about four feet of her for reasons she had never stated and he had never asked about, and he had learned in the spring that the correct number of comments to make during the first hour was somewhere under three, and that the correct amount of help was exactly none until offered, and that offering before being asked was a specific crime with a specific punishment, which was that she stopped talking out loud about what she was doing, and half of him had come down here for the talking.

So he sat with his back against the boathouse wall with the blanket across both their knees and let her work, and she worked the way she did everything, which was at a rate she had decided on beforehand and could not be hurried off: the sheet pinned flat under the heel of her left hand, the pen nibbed, the flow line inked in one unhurried pull from the slab down through the shelves, the stations ticked in the margin with their numbers in her small block hand, the ridge line gone over twice because a line that has been surveyed gets a heavier line than a line that has been guessed.

The camp went past them in the distance while she did it. A door. The far ring of the forge hammer, once, the way a smith hits a thing on a Sunday to see whether it is still there. Three kids crossing the meadow with a rope and no purpose. The lake, which did nothing at all except sit there being the exact colour of a nickel and occasionally moving a sheet of thin ice at the reeds.

"You can talk," she said, at about twenty past one, without looking up.

"I know."

"You're being quiet on purpose. You're doing the face."

"What face."

"The face that says *I'm being supportive*, which is the same face you do when you're being smug, and the only way I can tell the two apart is that the smug one comes with a slower blink."

Percy put his hands behind his head and looked at the roof boards and said, "You've got the flow line going into the wet corner exactly on the number and you haven't once said the number out loud, which means you're saving it, which means it's good, which means in about ten minutes you're going to turn around and tell me the whole thing with the pen in the air and ink going on your wrist, and I've decided to just sit here and be the audience."

Annabeth Grace stopped drawing. She put the pen down on the cloth, quite carefully, and turned around on the planks and looked at him with an expression he knew the entire family tree of, and which started out as outrage and passed through amusement on its way somewhere better.

"I don't do the pen in the air."

"You do the pen in the air."

"I do the pen in the air twice a week at most." She picked up the corner of the blanket and moved down the planks toward him under it, one increment, the way a person advances on a position they have already won, and sat beside him with her shoulder against his and the cold coming off the water at them both. "Give me the blanket corner. Both hands. It's got my wrist in it."

He gave her the corner, and the blanket went across both their knees again, and her cold wrist went into the fold of it, and her shoulder stayed where it was, and for a while neither of them said anything at all, and the nothing was the kind that had been earned, the kind you get after five weeks of mail and mountains and family dinners and a girl who had walked down a hill twice for toast.

Her hand found his under the blanket without either of them making a production of it. That was the thing about hands, he had decided back in June, and had never once managed to explain to anybody: they were the only part of a person that could hold an entire conversation without ever once risking a sentence. She did the thing where her thumb went across his knuckles, once, back, twice, the same width each time, which was her version of a person settling into a chair. He turned his hand over and let her palm be flat to his, and did the thing where he ran one finger down the middle of it, slowly, the way you read a line on a chart, and she watched it go and did not say cartography, which he had been entirely expecting, and which meant she was saving that one too.

"That's a very serious life line," he said.

"That's a sea line. It's a different line. It goes off the edge of the hand instead of around the thumb, and in a couple of traditions it means the person's been in more water than was strictly planned for."

"You made that up."

"I made that up in its specifics," Annabeth said, "and not in its spirit." She laced her fingers into his and squeezed once, in the particular rhythm they had ended up with, one long and two short, which had started as a joke in the canoe shed in July and had since become, without either of them naming it, the way they said *here* and *still* and *not now but don't go anywhere*, all in about a second and a half. "Hold that. I've got four stations left."

She went back to the pen. He held the line. The light on the planks moved about a foot to the left, and the wind went over the roof, and somewhere out on the water a sheet of ice let go of the reeds with a small sound like a page turning, and Annabeth got to the fourth station, and said, "The temperature at three feet into the bowl was — " and did not finish the sentence.

She did not finish it because she had turned her head to look at him while she was saying it, and had found him looking back, and had arrived at the same conclusion from the other direction, which was that the fourth station could wait and the afternoon could not.

"Oh," she said.

"That was going to be the whole number."

"I know what it was going to be." She put the pen down without looking at where she was putting it, which was a thing she did about four times a year and which was, in the economy of Annabeth Grace, the equivalent of a banner being torn off a wall. "Say something else."

So he did, but not with words.

It started the way their kisses had started for about a month now, which was slowly, and with a question in it that neither of them bothered to ask out loud any more, and it tasted of the cold air and the cider she had been drinking out of a tin cup on the walk down, and it went on long enough that the flat afternoon light moved another inch along the planks without either of them tracking it. Her hand came up to his jaw and stayed there, cold at the fingers and warm at the palm, and his went to the small of her back, flat, steady, and the blanket slipped off both their knees onto the boards and neither of them reached for it.

They broke for breath with their foreheads together, and she said, in a voice she had clearly meant to be even and had not entirely managed, "You had ink on your ear after all."

"It's your ink. It's contagious."

"That's not how ink works."

"Then it's your fault anyway," Percy said, and she laughed into the kiss that came after it, and the laugh went into the kiss and came back out the other side as something quieter, and this was the part of an afternoon like this that he had stopped being able to think about in advance, which was that a laugh could turn into a long slow kiss with her fingers in his hair and his thumb finding the corner of her jaw and the whole grey lake going on doing nothing whatsoever behind their closed eyes.

The second half of it was slower than the first, because they had nowhere to be and both of them knew it, and because somewhere in the last month they had worked out the trick of it, which was that a kiss was not a sentence with a full stop at the end but a way of holding a room still. Her mouth against his, and a pause where they just breathed, and then the same thing again at a slightly different angle, with the cold tip of her nose finding his, and his hand moving once up her spine and back, unhurried, and her fingers curling into the front of his jacket in a way that said more than the four words in the back cover of the field book said, and which the field book's owner had, he suspected, done on purpose.

When they came apart for the second time the light had gone the colour of the inside of a jar of honey and the wind had turned, and Annabeth did not open her eyes for a second, and then she did, and looked at him with the specific and entirely unguarded expression that she kept for about nine people and no gods.

"There's a number in this sheet," she said, "that I have been saving since Saturday."

"Is it a good number."

"It's the best number I've had all year and I've had a lot of them." She uncurled her fingers from his jacket, found the pen on the boards by feel, put it in his hand, and took his hand in both of hers and held it there for a moment, his cold fingers around a pen that had been in her warm ones, which was, he thought, as close as either of them ever came to saying a thing straight out and then handing you the tools to write it down. "Write it in the margin for me? While I say it? My hands have gone to water and I'd rather they didn't do it on the fair sheet."

"What is it."

"Thirty-one feet," Annabeth said. "In three hundred yards. All of it downhill. Go on. It goes under the temperature."

He wrote it in the margin in his own uphill hand, which was larger than hers and leaned to the right and had no business being anywhere near a cartographer's sheet, and she watched him do it, and did not correct the numbers, and did not comment on the letter f, and then she took the sheet and the ink and the pen and rolled the whole thing up in the oilcloth and put it in the bag and buckled the bag, and neither of them said anything about the fact that the buckling took a long time and was done standing up with both their hands on the same buckle.

The walk back up the west path took them twenty minutes because it took them twenty minutes, because the light was going and the cold was coming up off the water behind them in a long slow push, and because they were holding hands the whole way, which in October, on a path with nine cold lanterns in brackets above them and a whole camp at the top of it, was a thing they did the way other people walked: without deciding to, and without letting go at the corners.

They parted at the junction above the cedar brake, because that was where the two cabins' ways split and because Annabeth Grace had a fair sheet to think about and Percy Jackson had a kettle and neither of them was the kind of person who needed the goodbye spelled out in front of a lantern bracket.

"Four-thirty," she said.

"Four-thirty. Fireside. I'll have the corner."

"You'll have the corner if Clarisse doesn't take the corner, and Clarisse always takes the corner."

"Then I'll have whatever's left of the corner and be smug about the brackets."

"There are no brackets on a corner."

"There are brackets on everything once I've decided to be supportive about it," Percy said, and Annabeth made the sound that was half a laugh and half the exhale of a girl who had spent an afternoon being kissed on a boathouse plank and had come out of it with a number in a margin, and went up the hill backwards for three steps, looking at him, the way her sister did it, which was a thing she would have denied in writing and which she had clearly learned from somebody.

Cabin Three at half past four on a Sunday was the warmest room on the hill, and it was warm the way a room gets warm when it has been kept at it all day rather than suddenly given permission: low, and even, and already settled into itself.

The stove had been fed at three and had reached the part of the evening where it no longer needed anybody's opinion. The lamp over the table was lit and turned down. Four kids were on the floor at the far end with a deck of cards and a bowl of the small hard apples that nobody had wanted in September and which had, through some turn of October economics, become the most popular food in the building. And in the corner, as predicted, with her back to the wall where she could see both doors, sat Clarisse La Rue, with the winter list, a cup of tea, and the expression of a woman holding a position.

Annabeth came in out of the cold with the bag over her shoulder and the smell of lake reeds on her coat, and looked at the corner, and said, "You took the corner."

"The corner was free."

"The corner was free at four."

"It stopped being free at four-ten," Clarisse said. "Sit somewhere. There's cider on the table and Silena wants a verdict and I've told her mine twice."

Annabeth took off her coat, hung it, took the mug, drank, and gave the verdict the only kind of rating she had ever given anything, which was a number and a direction. "Seven now. Eight by Wednesday. It wants the sugar to come out of the skins and it won't do it in a cold room."

"It's in the cold room."

"It's in the coldest room and that's the problem. Put it in the corner by the stove for two days and don't let Thalia in there, she drinks it out of the jug and calls it quality control."

"Quality control is what she calls it," Clarisse agreed, and wrote nothing down, which was how Annabeth knew the verdict had been heard.

Percy came in from the woodpile with two split logs and his cheeks red at the edges and the specific self-satisfaction of a man who had been useful outdoors, and put the logs in the box, and then, without any change of expression and without looking at anybody, sat down on the floor at the near end of the blanket that was already spread by the stove, and did not take the blanket's other corner for almost a minute, and then did.

Under the blanket, where the table hid the whole transaction from a room of nine people, her cold hand found his, and his fingers did the one-long-two-short, and she answered with two short and one long, which was not a word in any language and which both of them had understood since July.

"The corner's taken," she said, aloud, to the room.

"I told you," Percy said.

"You told me and I walked into it anyway, which is your whole plan in a sentence." She put her shoulder against his and opened the field book on her knee, and did not read it, and was entirely content.

Silena had the other end of the table, with the basket and the darning and the apron on, and she worked through the whole of the cider verdict with her eyes down and her hands going, and then, at about ten to five, she put the needle into the wool to hold it, stood up, and said, in the voice of somebody announcing a chore, "I'm going down to the beach."

"About what," Clarisse said.

"About the quilt. It's mine and it's going to be the coldest night of the year so far and I want to look at the eyelets before somebody tells me about them in the morning." She took the lantern off the hook by the door — the tenth, the porch one, which lived inside on Sundays and nobody argued about it any more — and filled it from the can on the shelf, and trimmed it, and did the whole business with the flat efficiency of a woman who had been trimming lamps since before most of the camp had opinions about lighting. "It'll hold an hour. There's bread and there's the jug and I'll take a cloth."

"You're taking a lantern down a hill in the dark to look at sixteen bits of brass," Clarisse said.

"Sixteen bits of brass and a quilt with a fish in it," Silena said, "that four camps worth of people made in a night, and which is going to have ice on it by morning, and which I would like to look at before I go to bed, and which I am going to look at whether or not this table has an opinion."

"The table's got an opinion and the opinion is bring the cloth."

"Then we agree." Silena went to the door with the lantern in one hand and the cloth in the other, and stopped in it, and said, without turning round, "There's two of you on that blanket and one of you's got cold hands, so one of you's been outside longer than you're admitting." And then she was gone, and the door shut on the cold, and the lamp on the hook by the door left a small warm rectangle on the boards where her arm had been.

Clarisse looked at the door for a moment.

"She'll be forty minutes," she said.

"She'll be an hour," Annabeth said.

"She'll be an hour and ten and she'll come up the west path in the dark and tell me the left corner's still off an inch, which I already know, and which she knows I know." Clarisse turned the list over in her hands and did not write on it, and looked down at the page the way she looked at the page at four in the morning, which was as an adversary. "Three lines left on this thing. Greenhouse, awning, pine. Three jobs that want a warm day, and there's a front coming, and I've had the same three lines up since the second week of October and I'm not crossing one of them off to make a Sunday look better."

"Nobody's asking you to."

"Good. Because the pine knows where it is." She put the list face down on the salt and stood up and went to the stove and took the poker to it with the specific precision of a woman who was not, at that moment, entirely thinking about the fire, and the room went a shade brighter, and at the far end of it somebody won a hand of cards and four kids said different things at once.

That was the shape of the evening, and Annabeth let it go on around her for a while without putting herself in the middle of it, because she had learned this year that the correct way to love a room was sometimes to sit in the corner of it with your shoulder against a boy and your hands warming up and your field book open at a page you were not reading.

Thalia came in at six with her coat full of cold and her hat pushed back and announced that she had brought the weather with her, which she had, and then she took the second mug of cider and drank it standing up and told the room, at speed and in a flat voice, about a wind gust on the range at half past five that had taken three arrows off the line and given her back two of them, and how she had left the third one out there because it was dark and because arrows were, in the end, only arrows and she was, in the end, eighteen and could buy more.

"Buy more," Clarisse said. "With what."

"With the money I've got, which is not your business and which is also not very much." Thalia dropped into the chair beside the stove and put her boots toward it and looked at the two of them on the blanket with the field book and the blanket and the two mugs, and her face did the thing where it was fond and exasperated at the same time, which was the exact expression she had worn since Annabeth was six. "It's a good room," she said, to nobody. "It's the best room. Don't tell Poseidon."

"He's not listening," Percy said.

"He's never listening, that's the whole problem with the man." Thalia stretched, and did the shoulder, small, quick, thinking the angle was hidden by the chair, and Annabeth looked at the field book and let it go past, and the two of them went on pretending, for the third time in twenty-four hours, that they had not noticed each other noticing anything at all.

Outside, the light went from grey to the colour of iron, and down at the bottom of the west path, small and far and unmistakable, the first lantern of the evening caught and stood up out of the dark like a coal being breathed on, and then a second one further down, and then nothing for a while, because the person doing the lighting had stopped to see to the first one properly, which was a thing only one person on this hill ever did.

Annabeth watched the second one settle and thought about a girl walking down a hill in the dark with a lantern of her own, toward sixteen bits of brass and a quilt with a fish sewn into it, and thought about the four nights in September that had made both of them, and did not say anything about it, because some things in this family were best left to arrive on their own.

"Bed by ten," Clarisse said, to the room that was not going to be in bed by ten. "There's a frost coming and I want the pump dripping and the tools off the porch before anybody gets horizontal."

"The pump's dripping," Percy said.

"The pump's dripping because I made it drip at four. The rest of you are catching up." Clarisse put the poker back on its hook and stood with her hands on her hips and looked at the stove, and the cards, and the two of them on the blanket, and the empty hook by the door where the tenth lantern had been, and said, in the voice she used for the bottom line of an inventory, "Right. Somebody tell me when the door opens."

Silena Beauregard went down the west path at ten past six with the lantern in her right hand and the cloth under her arm, and the first thing she saw, halfway down the first switchback, was a boy at a bracket with a lighting rod, stopping to see to the first lamp properly: holding the flame to the wick until it caught and steadied, and then waiting, the way you wait for a thing you have made, before he moved on to the next one.

She stopped on the path above him and let him finish, because he had not seen her yet and because watching Charlie Beckendorf light a lantern was a thing a person did not interrupt. He did it the way he did everything at a bench: no flourish, both hands steady, and one extra second at the end that nobody had ever asked him for. Then he shook the rod out, hung it back on the bracket, and shouldered the oil can, and turned down the path toward the beach, and Silena counted to a hundred in her head and then went down after him, at her own speed, in her own light.

The north beach fire ring sat where it had always sat, in a scoop of sand above the reeds where the wind went round the outside instead of through the middle, and the two iron poles stood up out of it with the quilt between them, and the quilt had been out here for eight nights now and had started to belong to the place. It had gone the colour things go when they sleep outdoors: the squares still showed their own colours in the middle and along the seams, but the whole surface had taken on a grey cast, like a photograph left in a window, and the sixteen brass eyelets along its edges had gone dark at the holes and bright everywhere else, so that in the lantern light they ran around the quilt like a line of small coins being counted.

Sand came up over the boots. The reeds hissed at the low edge of the water, which was thinning at the margins toward ice and letting the sound of it travel further than it had any right to in October. Silena set the lantern on the flat rock by the ring, put the cloth over it half to shield the flame and half because a covered light was a habit of a girl who had grown up in a house where lamps got knocked over, and then she went along the quilt's edge with her fingers on the eyelets, one at a time, feeling each one for the thing you could not see in the dark, which was whether the brass had started to pull through the cloth.

She had got to the eleventh when she heard the boots coming down the sand behind her, unhurried, and did not need to turn around, and turned around anyway, and took exactly as long as it took to be a person who had heard a step and was entirely calm about it.

Beckendorf had the oil can over one shoulder and something else in his left hand: a coil of waxed cord, and a small tin, and the flat look of a man who had walked down a beach at night with a reason and intended to be right about the reason.

"Evening," he said.

"Evening."

"You took the eleventh."

"I've been taking them since I got here." She put her hand flat on the eyelet and left it there, which was braver than it sounded. "They're pulling at the cloth on this side. The wind comes round the outside of the ring and this is the side it finds."

"I know." He put the can down in the sand and unshook the coil and the tin, and opened the tin, and held it out to her with four small brass pins in the bottom of it, catching the light. "I came down to do the sixteen before the frost."

"The sixteen are fine."

"The sixteen were fine on Friday. It's Sunday and there's a front." He looked at her hand on the eyelet, and then at her, once, and then at the quilt, which was the order of operations she had come to know about him, and which meant he had something to say and was putting it in the queue. "I can do them from this end. You'd have to hold the cloth."

"I can hold the cloth."

"Then hold the cloth."

So she held the cloth, and he re-laced, and the work took the shape work always took with the two of them, which was that it needed four hands and neither of them had ever once had to be asked twice for any of them. He ran the waxed cord through the eleventh eyelet and round the iron pole and back through, and she kept the tension with two fingers flat on the cloth while he cut the cord with the knife on his belt and turned the end over the brass pin and set it, and their hands met on the tin of pins for about a second and a half, and then did not meet again for a while, and both of them kept their eyes on the eyelet for a second longer than an eyelet required.

"How many have you done," she said.

"Eleven. Fourteen's got a knot in it that somebody tied in a hurry."

"That was me. It was the night it went up. It was dark and Clarisse was counting and I tied it in a hurry."

"It's a good knot in a hurry," Beckendorf said. "It's still holding. I'm going to leave it and put a second one under it, because a knot that's held that long has earned the right to keep its own name." He moved down the quilt's edge with the coil, and she went with the cloth, and they did the twelfth and the thirteenth and got to the fourteenth, and the wind came round the outside of the ring and went through the sixteen brass holes with a small sound like a wire being plucked, one after another, all the way down the quilt, and neither of them said anything about it because both of them had stopped to listen to it.

"You sewed the fish," he said, at the fourteenth.

"It's my square. It's a bad fish and it's my square."

"It's not a bad fish. It's a fish that's been in a storm." He set the pin and tapped it flat with the side of the pliers and did not look up, and said, in the same level voice, the way a person mentions a measurement: "I looked at it for a while that night. The night it got made. Everybody else was sewing squares and you were sewing the fish in under the light of a lamp somebody had put on the table, and I stood in the doorway with my coat dripping and I thought — " He stopped. He put the pliers in the tin. "It's a good fish."

"You stopped in the middle."

"I stopped in the middle and I'm going to leave it there, because if I finish that sentence I'm going to say it wrong, and I've been saying things wrong in workshops my whole life and I'd like to have one." He straightened up and looked at her with his ears doing what his ears did, and said, "Fourteenth's done. The rest are on the far side and the far side's fine."

"The far side's fine," Silena agreed, and her voice came out entirely even, which she noted, and filed, and intended to examine later at length, in private, with her face in a pillow.

They carried the lantern round to the windward side anyway, because the windward side was where the cloth needed a second look, and while it did not need a second look they gave it one, and then the cloth was folded at the corner and the tin was packed and the cord was coiled and there was no reason left on the beach for two people to be standing in the dark, and neither of them moved.

"Sit down a minute," Beckendorf said. "Your hands are going."

"They're not going."

"They're going and you're holding the tin to warm them. Sit down, Beauregard."

She sat down. He sat down about three feet away, with his back against the far pole and his shoulders up around his ears against the cold, and the lantern between them on the flat rock throwing their two shadows up the inside of the quilt, where the fish went on being a bad fish and a good fish at the same time, and for a while the only sounds were the reeds and the water thinning toward ice and the small ticking of hot glass cooling.

"It came back all right," Silena said, to the quilt, without any preamble at all, because the sentence had been in her coat pocket since Friday night and had walked down a beach with her and was not going to wait any longer. "The mending. It came back all right and it came back with the grey thread still in it and I've got the needle in it now and it's going to be done on Tuesday and I — " She stopped, and restarted, and went at it from the other end, which was the thing she did when a sentence had come out facing the wrong way. "Thank you. For bringing it up. You didn't have to carry it up a hill in the dark."

"I was coming up the hill anyway."

"You were coming up the hill anyway," she said, "and you put a piece of paper in the top of the basket before you came up the hill anyway, and I want you to know that I read it at the door, standing up, before I'd even taken my coat off."

The wind went round the outside of the ring. The lantern ticked. Somewhere a long way out on the lake, a sheet of ice let go of the reeds with a sound like a page being turned, and went on turning pages all the way out to the middle.

"I wrote it at the bench," Beckendorf said, eventually, and his voice had gone about half a size smaller, which was the only sign a person got with him that a thing was being said carefully. "It took three goes. The first two are in the bin and they should stay there. I'm not — " He put his hands flat on his knees and looked at them, which was a thing he did when he was deciding whether to be accurate or comfortable, and he always, Silena had learned, chose accurate. "I'm better at writing a thing down than saying it out loud. That's all it is. It's not a — it's a shop fact. It's how the shop works."

"A shop fact," she said.

"You know what I mean."

"I know exactly what you mean," Silena said, "and I want you to know that the shop's my favourite shop, and that I have been saying that sentence in my head since July and have not once managed to say it out loud in the shop."

He did not say anything to that. He did the thing where he looked at her for a second with his whole attention and no performance anywhere in it, and then went back to looking at his hands, and his ears had gone past what ears were supposed to do, and Silena sat against an iron pole in the cold with her hands in her lap and thought: *this is it, this is the whole thing, this is what it actually is, and it is entirely made out of the same material as a lamp being lit properly and nobody being asked.*

They talked after that. Not about the note. About the quilt, and the night it had been made, and who had sewn what, and Beckendorf's theory that a quilt was only a building drawn flat, and Silena's theory that a building was only a quilt with planning permission, which he laughed at — once, quietly, with his shoulders — and which she decided, then and there, to keep. They talked about the fish. They talked about Clarisse, in the careful and entirely fond way that people who were both slightly afraid of her talked about her. They talked about the bakery, in half of its sentences: he said the window was on the wrong side and windows moved, and then did not say the rest of it, and she said she had never once thought about who would move it, and then did not say the rest of it either, and the rest of it stayed in the middle of the flat rock between them, warm, undisturbed, entirely understood by both parties, and neither of them touched it.

And then, somewhere in the middle of a sentence she had started about the frost and the cloth, they both stopped.

Not one of them stopping and the other trailing off. They stopped together, at the same instant, the way two people walking in step both hear a sound, and the silence that opened up in front of them was not an empty silence and it was not an awkward one. It was a silence with a shape to it, and it lasted — she did not count it and she would count it later, twice — it lasted long enough for the lantern to tick four times, and for the wind to go round the ring twice, and for the whole beach to rearrange itself around two people not talking, and neither of them started it back up again, and neither of them wanted to.

Beckendorf reached over and put the pin tin in her lap, which was not a thing that needed doing and which they both understood as a way of saying something without saying it, and said, "Keep them. There's four spares and the cloth's yours."

"They're your pins."

"They're four bits of brass and you're the one who'll be on this beach at two in the morning when a knot lets go."

"I will be," Silena said, "because it's my quilt." She picked up the tin and held it for a second in both hands, and then put it in her apron pocket, next to a square of paper that had been folded and unfolded so many times it had gone soft as cloth, and patted the pocket once, flat, the way you check a door you have already locked, and did it with her eyes entirely open and looking straight at him, which was a thing she had never done in her life before and which she did not take back.

They got up then, and shook the sand out of the cloth, and banked the ring's cold coals out of habit, and the lamps down the switchbacks went on burning below them in their line, amber one after another through the dark, and the two of them started up the sand with the lantern and the oil can and the tin in a girl's apron pocket, and the beach went behind them being a beach, holding a quilt.

He asked the first question where the sand gave up and the path began.

"The sixteen," he said. "Are they enough for the winter, or should it have more before the wind starts properly?"

And Silena, who had expected questions about the cloth and the corner and the left inch, who had prepared for all of them on the way down an hour earlier, in the dark, with a lantern, standing in her own doorway, felt the top of her head go slightly warm and said, in a voice she had not ordered: "It's got sixteen. Sixteen is a good number. It's four times four and it's two eights and it's — it's plenty. It's enough." And then, because he was walking beside her with the oil can and not saying anything, she added, from somewhere under the whole sentence: "It's enough until the spring. And then if it wanted more, I'd want to be here when they went in."

"That's the answer," Beckendorf said.

"I said it in about nine parts."

"You said it in the right part at the end." He walked four steps in the dark and then asked the second question, in the same level voice, at the same height, as though he were reading off a list he had made somewhere and had carried down a beach with him: "Did the mending come back all right?"

And that was the question he already knew the answer to, which she knew, and which he knew she knew, and he had asked it anyway, because the asking was the actual question, and the mending was only the cloth it had been folded up in.

"It came back all right," Silena said. "The mending came back all right. And the note came back all right. And I've checked the note five times since Friday, which is a number I have told to exactly one person and which you are not allowed to be the second."

"Noted."

"And the answer's yes," she said, and heard her own voice do it, and could not stop it, and did not want to: "Whatever the note asked, the answer's yes, and I've known it since the eyelets, and if you'd written it on the workshop wall in charcoal I'd have said yes to the wall."

The path turned up through the pines. Above them the whole hill stood under a sky with more stars in it than anybody had names for, and the cold had gone from the ordinary kind to the serious kind, and somewhere over on the western ridge the wind was doing something to the trees that would be on this beach by morning.

And then the third question, and he asked it looking straight ahead, with his chin down and his shoulders up, the way a man asks a thing he has been carrying a long way and would rather put down than keep:

"So do you come down for the stove," he said, "or — "

"That's not a question."

"It's half of one. It's the half I've got." He put the oil can in his other hand. "You've got a stove. You've got the best stove on this hill and a family round it and a corner table with a needle in the wool. You don't come down to a forge for the stove. So why do you — " He stopped, and left his own sentence standing in the path between them, unfinished, the way she had left one on the flat rock, and stood there and let her have it.

And she answered it badly. She answered it in the wrong order, the way a person does when the true part has been standing at the back of the line the whole evening and the false parts have all got friends.

"I come down because the shop's warm," she said. "It's the warmest room on the hill in the afternoon and the fire's lit at six and the light's good at the bench and there's always something worth holding, and a girl can sew in a warm room — that's — no." She stopped in the middle of the path in the dark and put her hand flat on her own apron pocket and stood there with it, and started again from the true end, quietly, with her eyes on the pine trees and her heart going like a hammer on a soft iron. "No. That's all of it backwards. I've got a stove. I'd sew in a cold room. I've come down every week since July and I have never once been cold in that shop and it's got nothing to do with the fire, Charlie, and I knew that by the second time, and I've known it in my hands ever since, and I've never once said it out loud to a person before and I'm not — " She let out a breath that went up into the pines and stayed there. "That's the right end. You can have it."

Beckendorf stood in the path for a moment with the oil can in his hand and the whole dark hill above him and a girl who had just put a thing down that she had been carrying since July, and then he said, in the voice he used for a job well done at a bench, which was one word and entirely insufficient and absolutely everything:

"Good."

They came up the hill after that. Not quickly. The lamps along the switchbacks had begun to gutter in the rising wind, and they went out one after another as the two of them climbed: the one at the boathouse turn, with a small pop and a bead of smoke; the two at the cedar brake, together, like a decision; the odd one at the curve, which went last and reluctantly, so that for about eight seconds it lit the two figures on the path below it, a boy with an oil can over his shoulder and a girl with her hand on her apron pocket, both of them walking exactly as slowly as walking takes, and then it went out too, and the hill was dark, and they came up the rest of it in the dark, not touching, in step, saying nothing at all, which was, Silena Beauregard decided, walking up a black hill in October with her heart still going like a hammer, the best conversation she had ever had in her life.

He stopped at the top, past the last bracket, where the path went two ways.

"Good night, Beauregard," he said.

"Good night," she said, and then, without planning to, added: "Charlie."

There was about a second and a half in which she could see him deciding not to say anything else, and doing it on purpose, and being right about it, and then he put the oil can on his shoulder and went down the path and did not look back, which was the correct amount of looking back, and which she knew was the correct amount because he had known it first.

She went in through the Cabin Three door with the cold on her face and the lantern in her hand and the tin in her apron pocket and a square of paper next to it, and Clarisse, at the table with the list, looked up and looked at her for about two seconds, and said nothing, and went back to the page, and Silena put the lantern on its hook by the door and stood there a moment with her back to the room, and knew — knew, the way you know a number you have just written down — that she was smiling, and that she had been smiling since somewhere around the fourteenth eyelet, and that she was not going to be able to stop for the rest of the night, and that there was not one single thing in the world about that which she intended to fix.

Percy Jackson went out onto the Cabin Three porch at twenty to ten in his coat and his boots with no purpose in him at all, which was a kind of luxury he had not known existed until he had been given a life that allowed it.

The night had turned. All afternoon it had been building something out on the water and he had not been looking, and now it had finished: the wind had stopped, the way a wind stops when it has done the whole of its work, and the air that came up the hill out of the reeds was not cold the way Sunday had been cold, which was damp and argumentative. It was cold the way a knife is sharp. It was the cold of a night that had decided on a number and was not going to argue about it, and it lay over the hill with everything in it already half gone to frost by intention.

The nine lanterns down the switchbacks were out. All nine. He stood at the rail and looked at the dark path for a while and worked out the shape of it, which was that somebody had lit them at dusk and the wind had come up off the water around nine and taken them one at a time going down, and that nobody had been out to relight them because it had been ten minutes' work for nothing and tomorrow was Monday. The brackets stood empty along the hill with the wax beads on them and the soot thumbprints, and the hill without them was a hill he had walked a hundred times in the dark, and which he could still find every step of, and which he would not have to.

Inside, the stove ticked. Through the window he could see the table with the lamp turned down and the winter list lying face down on the salt, and his sister at the end of it with her chin in her hand and her eyes on the door, doing the last thing she did every night, which was to know where everybody was before she slept.

The lake had stopped being a thing with edges. Out at the reeds the thin ice had spread in the dark, sheet on sheet, and the sound it made was a small dry clicking, like a page being turned a long way off, and above it the sky had done the thing it did on the nights that mattered, which was to come out whole and unbroken and enormously busy with stars, all the way down to the black line of the ridge, where the pines stood up into it like something being written on.

Annabeth's glass had said hard frost before morning, and Chiron had said he would rather not pick a night between this one and the next, and the pump had been dripping since four, and the tools were off the porch, and somewhere up on a shelf in the Big House a barometer was doing quiet arithmetic that would be proved right by about six in the morning, when the whole hill came out white and the world went back to being a place with edges.

The screen door opened behind him and Clarisse came out in her coat over her night things with her arms crossed and stood at the rail beside him and did not say anything for a while, which was how she said most things.

"Frost by morning," she said.

"By morning."

"The pump's dripping. The tools are in. The greenhouse stays where it is and the awning stays torn and the pine stays down, and I've got three lines on a page that want a warm day and there's no warm day coming." She breathed out, and the breath went up into the starlight and stayed there a second, and then she said, in the voice she used for the bottom of an inventory: "Good day, though."

"The best one this month."

"It's been a good month." She let go of the rail and went to the door and stopped in it, and said, without turning round, "There's cider in the jug and it'll be eight by Wednesday if anybody at this table has an opinion about fruit, and if you're out here for another five minutes you're the one who relights the top lamp in the morning, because I'm not doing it in the frost." And then the door shut, and the latch went over, and the tenth lantern's hook by it stood empty because the lantern was in for the night, which was, Percy thought, exactly the right way round.

Percy stayed on the porch until his fingers began to hurt from the cold. Down near the beach a quilt hung between two poles, waiting to dry properly after the evening's gathering. The wind had moved it so that one edge was catching on the iron.

He looked toward the door. Clarisse had already warned him about the top lantern. Going down to fix a piece of cloth might earn him the morning's work, too. But the quilt would tear if it kept catching, and there was no chance he could forget it by daylight.

He took the short way down the path, freed the fabric, and tied the loose end farther from the pole. It took less than five minutes. He didn't go near the water, which had already gone dark and cold, and he checked that the knot would release easily when someone came for the quilt in the morning.

Coming back he met a Hermes camper on the steps, carrying an empty basket and rubbing his hands together.

"Anything need taking up?" the boy asked.

"No. Just make sure that basket doesn't roll down the hill."

"That's not my plan."

"Good plan."

Percy waited until he'd passed, then returned to the porch. Clarisse had left the inner lamp burning at the table. The door was closed but not bolted, because she'd heard him go out and expected him back.

"You're late," she said when he entered.

"Five minutes."

"Five minutes and a wet cuff. What happened?"

"The quilt was catching on the iron."

She checked his sleeve, then the clock. "You can look at it in the morning."

"Already fixed."

She seemed to consider an argument. Then she handed him the last mug of cider and went back to her list. Percy took it gratefully, sat near the stove, and let his hands warm around the cup. He had nothing to prove by staying outside longer.
