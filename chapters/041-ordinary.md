## Chapter 41: Ordinary

Percy woke up because somebody was counting outside his window.

"Forty-seven. Forty-eight. Forty-nine."

He knew that voice. It was too early for it to belong to anyone reasonable, which meant it could only belong to Clarisse La Rue.

He rolled over in his bunk and stared at the ceiling of Cabin Three. The wood above him was dark with morning, the seashells set into the walls catching the first thin gray light and throwing it back in dull glints. The air tasted like salt. It always tasted like salt in here, even with the windows shut. Somewhere under the floorboards, water moved in the pipes that his dad had insisted on, or maybe that was just the sound of the sea beyond the dunes. After weeks at camp, Percy had stopped trying to tell the difference.

"Fifty. Fifty-one."

Percy sat up. His blanket slid off his shoulders. Across from him, the guest hammock was folded up neat in the corner. Tyson stayed over sometimes when he visited, but he did not live here full-time. The cabin belonged to three people only. Clarisse's bunk, against the near wall, was empty and already made, corners tight enough to bounce a drachma off. Silena, fourteen and the oldest, was still asleep in her bunk along the far wall, dark hair spilling over her pillow, one arm thrown over her eyes against the light.

Percy was twelve, the youngest by a full year. Clarisse was thirteen. Silena was fourteen. That was the whole of Cabin Three.

He padded barefoot to the window and pushed the shutter open.

Clarisse was out on the grass in front of the cabin in a gray camp shirt and shorts, hands planted in the damp grass, doing push-ups. Her hair was pulled back in a knot. Sweat darkened the back of her neck even though the sun wasn't up yet. She had always liked to train before breakfast, steady and stubborn, the way the tide kept coming whether anyone watched or not. Percy had never understood it until this exact moment, when he saw the look on her face — not angry, not showing off, just steady.

"Morning," he croaked.

She didn't stop. "Fifty-two."

"What are you doing out there? You live here. You have a door."

"Fifty-three. Your — my — our grass is flat." She dropped to her knees, then stood and wiped her hands on her shorts. "Arena ground has a dip. Twists my wrist."

"You have a whole arena."

"Our grass is flatter." Clarisse La Rue, daughter of Poseidon, came up to the window and leaned her forearms on the sill, breathing hard. She smelled like dew and salt. "You're awake early, little brother."

"You're loud early."

Clarisse grinned. It was a real grin, not the one she used before sparring. "Get dressed, Seaweed Brain. If you're up, you can spot me."

"I don't know how to spot push-ups."

"You stand there and look impressed."

Percy laughed, and the sound surprised him. He hadn't had a nightmare. That was the first thing he noticed, now that he was fully awake. No dreams about thrones or tides or voices under the water telling him to wake up. Just sleep, black and quiet as a stone dropped in deep water.

He pulled a shirt over his head and climbed out the window instead of using the door so he wouldn't wake Silena. The grass was cold and wet under his feet. The sky over the Sound was turning from gray to pearl to a pale, buttery gold at the edges. Mist hung low over the strawberry fields.

Clarisse dropped back down into plank. "Count for me."

"One," Percy said.

"Don't start at one. I did fifty-three."

"One," Percy repeated, solemnly.

She snorted and kept going. Percy counted. The camp woke up around them in small sounds — a door banging at the Hermes cabin, somebody laughing, the distant clank of pots from the dining pavilion, a conch horn from the harpies doing a slow morning circle high above the woods. The air smelled like wet earth and salt and coming breakfast.

When Clarisse hit thirty — eighty-three, by her count — she flopped face-first into the grass and stayed there.

"You're good at those," Percy said.

"I'm good at everything." Her voice was muffled. She rolled over and stared up at the sky. "Huh. No clouds."

"Good day?"

"Ordinary day," she said. "Best kind."

She lay there while Percy sat beside her, and they watched the sun come up over the water, turning the mist pink. Neither of them said anything important. From inside the cabin, Silena mumbled in her sleep and rolled over.

For a while, there was nowhere else to be.

---

Before breakfast, Percy found a note pinned to the door of Cabin Three.

It was from a younger camper named Micah, one of the Hermes children whose trunk had flooded during the rain. He'd written in careful block letters, asking whether Percy would still be able to show him how to use the shallow canoe dock. Percy had offered a lesson three days earlier. He'd completely forgotten.

The lesson was scheduled for noon.

Annabeth expected him at the library at twelve. Clarisse wanted him in the arena at ten. Silena had asked him to help at the forge later in the afternoon, and Grover had made a plan for the canoe before they'd even finished breakfast yesterday. The day he'd thought was empty had somehow acquired an appointment in every part of it.

"What's the matter?" Clarisse asked, coming in from her push-ups.

Percy showed her the note.

"You gave him a time?"

"Yes."

"And you didn't write it down?"

"No."

She looked at him with the expression she reserved for people who had made simple problems much harder than they needed to be.

"Then you need to talk to him."

"I know. I thought I could do everything today."

"You can do a lot of things. You can't be in two places at the same time."

"That's not entirely true for all demigods."

"It is for you."

Silena emerged from the washroom with a towel around her hair and took the note.

"If the boy's nervous about the dock, don't leave him waiting there while you decide," she said. "Can you move something else?"

Percy considered. The forge session was scheduled because Silena needed help with a clasp, but she'd told him the time could be adjusted. Annabeth was doing independent reading at the library and wouldn't be injured by his arriving late. Grover wanted a relaxed afternoon, not a fixed appointment. The canoe lesson was the only arrangement someone younger had built his day around.

"I'll keep the noon lesson," Percy said. "Then I need to tell Annabeth and Grover about the change."

"And ask the water-safety instructor whether you're allowed to teach him," Clarisse added. "You're not a certified counselor."

Percy looked at her. She was right. He had offered to show Micah how to use the dock without checking whether he was authorized to instruct him. He knew how to swim and handle water, but that didn't automatically make him responsible for a child who might panic in a boat.

He went to find the instructor after breakfast. The woman running the beginner water program knew Micah and explained that he had been cleared for a supervised familiarization session, but not an unsupervised paddle.

"You can help him from the dock while I watch," she said. "No magic to stabilize the canoe unless we agree ahead of time that there's a safety need."

"That works."

"Then get him here at noon and bring the proper equipment. You're both responsible for checking the paddles and flotation gear."

Percy nodded and went to find Micah. The boy was eating at the Hermes table beside two older campers and looked up anxiously when Percy arrived.

"I got your note," Percy said. "Noon still works. The instructor will be with us."

Micah looked relieved. "I thought you'd forgotten."

"I did," Percy admitted. "I'm sorry. I should have written it down."

Micah considered. "Are you still going to show me how to get into the canoe without tipping it?"

"Yes. But the instructor is going to make us check everything first."

"That's okay."

Percy left the table feeling slightly embarrassed and much better than he had when he found the note. He could not fix his forgetfulness by pretending he remembered everything. He could make it less costly to other people by recording the commitments he made.

At the Zeus table, Annabeth was marking a page in her notebook.

"Can we change our library time?" Percy asked. "I offered to help Micah at the canoe dock and forgot I had done it."

She looked up. "What time?"

"Noon. I can come after one if your research is still going."

"That's fine. I'll be reading by myself. You can find me whenever you finish."

"You're not annoyed?"

"Only if you try to make me rearrange my research because you didn't write something down. You asked, and I have no reason to insist you be there at a particular minute."

He smiled. "Thank you."

She offered him a blank page in the back of her notebook.

"Want to start a schedule?"

He took it, then remembered that borrowing her notebook would mean she couldn't use it for her own work.

"Could I have a piece of paper instead?"

"Yes."

He wrote down the canoe lesson, the adjusted library visit, Grover's afternoon paddle and Silena's forge task. He checked the times with each person rather than assuming the old plan would fit.

By the end of breakfast, the paper looked embarrassingly full. Percy had thought he wanted an ordinary day because ordinary days required no decisions. He was learning that the opposite was true. The decisions were smaller, but they still mattered to the people who had asked him to show up.

He folded the note and put it in his pocket. Clarisse saw him do it.

"You'll lose that."

"Probably."

"Then put it somewhere you check."

He went back into the cabin and pinned a copy beside the door. When he came out, Clarisse looked satisfied.

"Now you'll know when you're supposed to be annoying someone."

"I think you enjoy giving me schedules."

"I enjoy not having to chase you across camp."

He grinned and followed her toward the arena.

---

The dining pavilion was loud. Sunlight poured through the open sides and lit up the dust and the steam rising off trays. The air was thick with bacon and syrup and toasted bread and the sweet rot of the strawberry juice they served on Saturdays. Harpies wove between tables with platters balanced on their arms, shrieking at anyone who tried to sneak extra.

Percy carried his plate — scrambled eggs, three pancakes, an apple he intended to trade — and slid onto the bench at the Poseidon table. Silena was already there, hair tied back, braiding a bit of blue thread into the end for no reason except that she liked how it looked. Clarisse sat across, elbows out, guarding her bacon like a dragon.

"You're late," Clarisse said to Percy without looking up.

"I was watching you do push-ups."

"Romantic," Silena said flatly, and then she reached over and fixed Percy's collar where it was folded under. She did it without thinking, the way older sisters did. "You have bed-head. Tragic. Unfixable. Sit still."

Percy ducked, laughing. "I'm twelve, I'm supposed to look like this."

"You look like a drowned gull," Clarisse said, and stole half his toast. Not because there was a rule. Just because she was thirteen and he was twelve and that was what older sisters did. She tore it in half and gave him back the smaller piece. "Little brother tax. You counted my push-ups like a gentleman."

"That is not a thing."

"It is now," Silena said, and stole his other piece of bacon. "Baby of the cabin pays in bacon. Them's the rules I just made."

Percy stole it back and ate it fast, while both of them pretended to be outraged. Being the youngest meant getting teased from both sides at once, and Percy had learned to wear it like armor. They teased because they loved him. He knew it the way he knew the tide.

"Did you sleep?" Silena asked him, quieter. The older kids always asked that. The weeks since his arrival had been loud — training, storms, and the eclipse nobody wanted to explain twice.

Percy nodded. "All night. No dreams."

Silena's shoulders dropped a fraction. Clarisse bumped his knee under the table with hers, hard enough to mean something.

Tyson arrived then with a tray stacked impossibly high — twelve pancakes, eggs, sausages, a whole pitcher of juice. He did not live in Cabin Three, but he visited for breakfast whenever he could, and the Poseidon kids always saved him a spot. He sat down beside Percy and beamed. "GOOD MORNING, BROTHER AND SISTERS."

"Morning, Tyson," the three of them chorused. Tyson remembered everyone's birthday. He braided friendship bracelets out of old rigging line and gave them away.

"What are you doing today?" Tyson asked Percy, mouth already full.

"Training. Library. Lake. Forge. Dinner. Stars," Percy said, counting on his fingers. "Busy man."

"That's not busy," Clarisse said scornfully. "That's just camp."

"Exactly," Percy said.

At the next table over, the Hermes kids were flicking grapes at each other. At the Zeus table, Thalia Grace and her sister Annabeth Grace sat shoulder to shoulder, daughters of Zeus both. Annabeth was reading while she ate, a book propped against the juice pitcher. Thalia was trying to steal her bacon without her noticing. Annabeth turned a page and, without looking up, stabbed Thalia's fork with her own.

Percy watched it all and felt something warm settle behind his ribs. Nobody was whispering. Nobody was packing a bag under the table. Mr. D was at the head table doing the crossword and complaining loudly about the nectar coffee. Chiron was trotting between tables, pausing to ask the Demeter kids about the new melon patch.

Percy scraped his plate clean. Silena pushed her crusts at him and he ate those too.

"Offerings," Silena reminded them. They all stood, scraped a portion into the fire. Percy whispered his so only the flames heard: *Thank you for sleeping all night.*

The fire flared green-gold, warm against his face.

On the way out, Clarisse slung an arm around his shoulders and knuckled his head. Silena took his tray so he wouldn't have to carry it. The sun was fully up now, bright and clean. The grass was drying. The day smelled like the first warm spell after winter.

---

Thalia liked mornings when the arena dust hadn't been kicked up yet. At least, that was how it looked to Percy when he showed up at ten, on time for once, hair still damp from the showers, Riptide in his pocket.

"You're late," Thalia said, because somebody had to say it.

"I'm exactly on time."

"You're late in spirit."

The arena was mostly empty. In the far corner a couple of Ares kids were running shield drills on their own, shields thudding.

Off to one side, Clarisse was working through spear forms by herself, slow and precise, daughter of Poseidon training the way the sea trained — patient, then sudden. The sun slanted in through the open roof beams and lit the dust in gold bars. The smell was leather and oil and dry sand.

Thalia tossed Percy a practice spear. "No sword today."

"What? Why?"

"Because you rely on it. Catch."

He caught it, clumsy. Thalia Grace, daughter of Zeus, sister of Annabeth, fought like weather. Fast, loud, impossible to predict. Percy fought like water, which was worse, because you forgot water could knock you flat until you were already down.

They circled. Thalia tapped her own spear against her shield, setting a rhythm.

"Rule," she said. "No powers. No earthquake foot. No calling the hose."

"You always make rules."

"Rules make you better."

They sparred. Percy was terrible with a spear. That was the truth. He held it too far back, telegraphed his thrusts, left his left side open every third step. Thalia knocked him into the sand four times in the first ten minutes and was merciless about it.

"Footwork," she barked. "Again."

"Are you having fun?" Percy asked from the sand, squinting up at her.

"So much fun."

He got up, spat sand, reset. Thalia watched his face change the way it always did when he stopped performing and started paying attention. His jaw set. His eyes went to her shoulders instead of her spear tip. He started breathing through his nose.

The fifth exchange lasted longer. Percy blocked, slipped, turned the shaft and swept. Thalia jumped it, laughed, caught his spear with hers and twisted. They locked, forehead to forehead almost, both breathing hard.

"Better," she admitted.

"I'm still going to lose."

"Yeah. But you'll lose slower."

They went again. And again. Sweat ran down Percy's back. Thalia's bracelet — silver, with tiny lightning bolts — clinked against her shield. Percy got a touch on her ribs and whooped like he'd won the Olympics. From across the arena, Clarisse yelled, "DON'T CELEBRATE, FINISH!" and Percy immediately got knocked down again.

Thalia offered him a hand up. He took it. Her palm was gritty and warm.

They sat in the sand after, sharing a waterskin, listening to the steady thud-thud-thud from the far corner where the Ares kids were still drilling.

"You sleep okay?" Thalia asked. She asked it casual, eyes forward.

"Yeah. You?"

"Dreamed about my mom's old apartment. The one with the broken buzzer. Nothing bad. Just — the hallway smelled like Pine-Sol." She laughed a little. "Weird what sticks."

Percy nodded. He understood about ordinary dreams. For kids like them, an ordinary dream was a souvenir from a country you'd never visited but always hoped existed.

"Annabeth's waiting for you in the library," Thalia said. "My sister said if you're late she'll reorganize your cabin by color."

"She wouldn't."

"She would, and you'd thank her."

Percy groaned and stood, brushing sand off. Thalia stayed a moment, tilting her face up to the sun coming through the beams. Her muscles ached in a good way. No quest packing. No war council. Just practice, and a friend getting slowly less terrible with a spear.

"Same time tomorrow?" Percy asked.

"Obviously," Thalia said. "Somebody's got to keep you humble."

He saluted with the spear and nearly took out a dummy. Thalia laughed all the way to the showers.

---

When Percy left the arena, Thalia stayed behind to put away the practice swords.

She'd spent the morning making jokes about his footwork, and he had taken them in the spirit she'd intended. It was easy to be generous when she was winning a friendly training match against someone she liked. It was harder when she saw one of the younger campers watching from the edge of the arena, visibly wishing he could join.

The boy was from Hermes cabin. Thalia recognized him by face but not by name. He was holding a wooden sword and doing the same four movements over and over, never quite reaching the correct position.

"Do you want somebody to check that?" she asked.

He froze. "Sorry. I was trying not to get in the way."

"You're not in the way."

He looked at the sword. "I know the names of the positions. I just keep doing them wrong."

"Then show me."

His first movement was too fast. He raised the sword before shifting his weight, which left his back foot behind him and made the blade wobble. Thalia almost said, *Stop being afraid of it.* The words rose automatically, sharp and impatient.

She remembered Zeus shouting at her during a visit, asking why she wasn't stronger after months of training. He had changed his appearance and his standards from one meeting to the next, leaving her trying to guess what would earn his approval. She hated how quickly her own voice could approach that tone when somebody did something badly in front of her.

She swallowed the comment.

"What's your name?" she asked.

"Julian."

"Julian, would you like me to show you where your feet should go?"

He nodded.

She put down her own sword and demonstrated the movement slowly. Then she asked him to copy only the footwork without holding a weapon. His first attempt was awkward, but she could see the moment he began understanding where the weight needed to move.

"Better," she said.

He looked up, hopeful. "Really?"

"Yes. You moved your back foot before you lifted the blade. That's the part that was missing."

They practiced the footwork five times. Thalia didn't speed up the exercise until he could complete the motion without stumbling. When he picked up the wooden sword again, the blade moved more smoothly.

"Do it once more," she said.

He did. Then he grinned as though he'd won a tournament.

"Can I try it with somebody attacking?" he asked.

"Not without your instructor. This is enough for now."

He looked disappointed but accepted it. She gave him the name of the technique and told him which training group was practicing it later in the week.

As he went to return the sword, Clarisse appeared from the weapons rack.

"You were patient," she observed.

"Don't sound so shocked."

"I've seen you teach before."

"Then you know I'm excellent at it."

"You're excellent at showing people what you can do. Teaching them how to do something else takes more work."

Thalia looked at her. Clarisse wasn't trying to insult her. She was making a distinction Thalia had been reluctant to accept.

"Did I get it right this time?" she asked.

"Mostly. You looked as though you were going to shout, then didn't."

"I thought of a better instruction."

"Good."

They carried the last practice swords inside. Clarisse mentioned that Julian's instructor had been trying to help him find his balance for weeks. He was clever and careful, but every time he made a mistake in front of older students he rushed the next attempt and made it worse.

Thalia thought of the way Zeus's criticism had made her reach for more lightning instead of learning to stop. She didn't want Julian to associate her with the same fear.

"Can I help with his lesson next week?" she asked.

Clarisse glanced at her. "You can ask. Don't promise him before the instructor agrees."

"I know."

They finished shelving the swords. Thalia checked the rack, then left for lunch with the satisfying ache of a morning spent training. She hadn't had to make a child feel small to demonstrate that she was good at something. She wondered why that had taken her so long to learn.

At the pavilion, Annabeth was drawing the edge of a roof in her notebook. Thalia sat beside her and stole a piece of bread.

"How was training?" Annabeth asked.

"Fine. I helped a younger camper."

"Did you make him cry?"

"No."

"Congratulations."

"You're unbearable."

Annabeth smiled and handed her the rest of the bread. Thalia ate it while listening to her describe a problem with the roof angle. This was another lesson in patience: letting a person explain something she understood better than Thalia did and asking a question instead of trying to win the conversation.

She looked forward to doing it again, even if she would never tell Annabeth that. When lunch ended, she found Julian beside the entrance to the arena, already practicing the steps on the flat stones where he could see his own feet. He stopped when he noticed her, expecting another correction. Thalia lifted a hand.

"Not too long. Your instructor's giving you a full lesson tomorrow."

"I know. I just wanted to remember."

"Then write down the part you keep forgetting. It worked for Annabeth when she learned the storm-control exercise."

Julian nodded. He took a stub of pencil from his pocket and made a small note on a folded card. Thalia watched him put it away, then continued toward her own patrol assignment. She couldn't promise every beginner would improve at her pace. She could make her instructions clear enough that a boy like Julian knew what to practice after she left.

---

Micah was already waiting at the shallow dock when Percy arrived.

He had put on his flotation vest backward. The instructor was standing a few steps away, watching him try to fasten a strap that didn't reach the buckle.

"Hold on," Percy said. "Let me help you turn it around."

Micah looked down, embarrassed.

"The pictures on the tag were confusing," he admitted.

"They are. I put mine on wrong the first time I used one on the surface."

That was only partly true. Percy had used all kinds of flotation gear during his time in Atlantis, but he didn't remember those lessons from childhood. His first clear memory of a surface vest belonged to a school trip with Sally in the life he remembered. The equipment had been orange and smelled like plastic, and the instructor had spent ten minutes showing everyone how to secure it properly.

Percy asked the water-safety instructor to check Micah's straps once the vest was turned around. She adjusted the fit and made him demonstrate that he could move his arms without the vest riding up.

"Good," she said. "Now tell me what you'll do if the canoe tips."

Micah looked at Percy. Percy didn't answer for him.

"Stay with the boat and call for help," Micah said.

"And keep the vest fastened. We'll be close enough to assist you."

They went through the rest of the safety instructions before touching the canoe. Micah had wanted the lesson because his cabinmates made paddling look easy, but he had never been comfortable with deep water. The flooded trunk hadn't helped. He knew the rain inside the cabin wasn't the same as a lake, yet the sound of water moving where it shouldn't still made him nervous.

"I don't want to fall in," he told Percy.

"Then we'll practice at the dock first. You don't have to paddle out today."

The instructor approved. She secured the canoe alongside the lowest platform, with its broadest section beside a stable step. Percy held the bow line while Micah practiced placing one foot at a time, keeping his weight low and using his hand on the gunwale for balance.

The first attempt was too quick. The canoe shifted, and Micah jumped back onto the dock.

"I'm terrible."

"You moved before you'd settled your weight," Percy said. "Try it more slowly."

Micah made another attempt. He stopped halfway and looked down at the dark water below the dock.

"What if it moves?"

"It will move. That's what boats do. The goal isn't to make it stop moving. It's to know how to sit so the movement doesn't throw you over."

The boy considered the advice. He was used to Percy's demonstrations with water, the way a wave could become a shield or a staircase. He'd hoped Percy might simply make the lake hold the canoe still.

"Can you keep it from rocking?" he asked.

"I could, but then you wouldn't learn how it feels when nobody is controlling the water. The instructor can stop us if we need to."

Micah looked toward her. She nodded.

They tried again. This time Micah entered the canoe and sat where the instructor indicated. He gripped the sides so hard his fingers whitened.

"Relax one hand at a time," the instructor said. "Keep the paddle across your knees for now."

He did. The canoe rocked gently against its lines. Nothing dramatic happened.

Percy smiled. "There you go."

Micah stayed seated for a few minutes, learning what normal movement felt like. He wanted to try the paddle next, but the instructor decided they had enough time only for basic handling at the dock.

"That's still progress," Percy told him.

"I know. I wanted to go farther."

"Then we'll ask about another lesson when your counselor says you're ready."

Micah looked at the open lake. A group of older campers was returning from a longer route, their paddles moving together. He could imagine doing that someday without feeling sick with worry. He wasn't there yet, and Percy hadn't pretended he was.

They helped return the canoe to its rack. Micah checked that his vest was clean and hung it where the instructor indicated. He thanked Percy, then asked whether he would teach the next session.

"I can help if I'm available and the instructor agrees," Percy said. "I won't promise a time until I check my schedule."

Micah laughed. "You forgot this one."

"I did. I'm trying not to do it again."

He went to lunch with the other beginners. Percy stayed to help the instructor record the completed familiarization in the class log.

"That was good," she said.

"I didn't teach him very much."

"You helped him stay with a task that frightened him. And you didn't use your power to make the exercise easier than the conditions he'd face on his own."

Percy looked at the canoe racks. He had once thought being a child of Poseidon meant every water problem should be easier for him than for anyone else. Today the most useful thing he'd done was hold a rope and let another boy learn at his own pace.

He finished the log, returned the pencil and hurried toward the library. Annabeth would be waiting with her own work, and he had several hours of ordinary plans left to keep.

---

The camp library at midday was quiet, with light coming straight down through the high windows and making squares on the floor.

Percy slipped in shortly after one, out of breath, sand still in his hair. The place smelled of old paper and cedar and the lemon oil the Athena kids used on the shelves. In the early afternoon it was empty except for dust and Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus and Thalia's younger sister, with her boots up on a table and three books open around her.

She was already deep in architecture again — not war machines and siege engines, but temples. How they held weight. How they let light in. How people thousands of years dead had figured out how to make stone feel like sky.

"You're late," Annabeth said without looking up.

"Thalia says hi."

"Did she beat you?"

"She educated me."

Annabeth smiled and marked her page with a strip of blue cloth. Percy dropped into the chair across from her and immediately put his feet up too, mirroring her. He peered at her book upside-down.

"Temples again?"

"Temples again." She turned the book so he could see — a cutaway drawing of the Parthenon, all columns and light. "Look at this. They built it so it looks perfectly straight, but nothing is actually straight. The columns lean in. The floor bulges. It's all illusions to make it perfect for human eyes."

Percy studied it. He was a better reader than he pretended to be when teachers were watching. "So they cheated to make it beautiful."

"They understood people to make it beautiful," Annabeth corrected. "There's a difference."

He accepted that with a nod and pulled the second book toward him — myths, illustrated, the pages soft with handling. He flipped to the story of Poseidon and Athena contesting Athens. Annabeth watched him look at the picture of the olive tree and the salt spring.

"Weird," he said, "to read about your own parents like they're characters."

"Weird to read about yourself," Annabeth said. She'd recently found a scroll that mentioned a daughter of Zeus with gray eyes and a stubborn chin. She'd copied the line into her notebook and underlined it three times.

They read in silence. That was the thing Percy loved most about being with Annabeth, though he'd never say it in those words — she could be quiet with him. Other people filled silence. Annabeth let it sit. The library ticked around them — the old clock, the pages, the faint buzz of bees outside the window. A breeze lifted the corner of her notes.

After a while Percy said, very quietly, "Read me the temple part?"

Annabeth read aloud. Her voice was steady in the sunlit room. She read about measurements and marble and how the builders had carved tiny curves you couldn't see unless you knew to look. Percy listened with his chin in his hand, eyes half-closed, not asleep, just resting.

When she finished, he said, "We should build something."

"We are building something."

"I mean with wood. Like a bench. Or a boat."

Annabeth laughed. "You want to build a boat."

"I want to build a boat with you. A small one. For the lake. So Grover can say he sailed."

"We'd need plans."

"I knew you'd say plans. I love when you say plans."

Annabeth felt her ears go warm. She covered it by sliding her notebook across the table. "Fine. We'll draw it. Keel here. But if it sinks, it's your fault."

"If it floats, it's yours."

They spent an hour sketching a terrible, lopsided boat that would absolutely sink, shoulder to shoulder, arguing about oarlocks. The sun moved across the floor. The squares of light climbed the table legs and then Percy's sleeve.

When the horn blew for free hour, neither of them moved right away.

"This is nice," Percy said simply.

Annabeth, who collected words the way other kids collected shells, tucked that one away exactly as he'd said it. *This is nice.* Not grand. Not eternal. Just true.

"Yeah," she said. "It is."

Grover met Percy at the canoe rack at three, with a bag of oranges and a blanket and absolutely no plan to paddle anywhere strenuous. Grover Underwood — satyr, Lord of the Wild in training wheels, Percy's best friend since the first day of sixth grade — had learned that sometimes protecting your friends meant standing between them and monsters, and sometimes it meant making sure they ate an orange and sat down.

"Behold," Grover said, gesturing grandly at the canoe he'd picked. "The *Sea Cucumber.*"

"You named it?" Percy asked.

"I name all the boats. That one is *Barnacle Bob.* That paddleboard is *Kevin.*"

Percy laughed and helped him drag the *Sea Cucumber* down to the water. The lake glittered. Canoe Lake, they'd always called it, though Annabeth insisted its proper name was something in Ancient Greek that nobody could pronounce. Dragonflies stitched the air. At the far end, the naiads were sunning on the dock, tails in the water, gossiping loud enough to carry.

They pushed off. Grover sat in front, Percy in back steering without thinking about it — water was water, and Percy had never tipped a canoe in his life. The hull cut a clean V through the reflections.

They paddled to the middle and shipped the paddles. The canoe drifted. Grover peeled an orange and handed Percy half. Juice ran down their chins. It was perfect.

"So," Grover said. "How's the ordinary day?"

"So far so good. Clarisse did push-ups. Silena fixed my collar and stole my bacon. Thalia killed me. Annabeth is going to build a boat that sinks."

"Classic." Grover leaned back against the gunwale and let his hooves — disguised as sneakers for the afternoon, but he slipped them off when no one was looking — dangle over the side. His reed pipes rested in his shirt pocket. "I had a council meeting this morning."

"Yeah? How's the wild?"

"Good. Pines are happy. The strawberry fields have a gopher problem, which the Demeter kids swear is a spiritual crisis." Grover grinned. "I told them I'd negotiate with the gophers. I did. They want more clover. Reasonable demands."

Percy laughed, and Grover felt that familiar loosening in his chest. He'd seen Percy have panic attacks during his first weeks in this altered camp. To see him here, sun on his face, orange juice on his chin, steering a canoe called *Sea Cucumber* without a care — that was the whole quest, as far as Grover was concerned.

A fish jumped nearby — silver flash, plop. Percy watched it with interest, not hunger, not alarm. Just interest.

"Do you ever think about staying?" Grover asked. "Like — not just for summer. Year-round."

Percy was quiet a moment, trailing his fingers in the water. Tiny ripples spread out and caught the light. "Yeah. Sometimes. My mom would miss me, though. And school — well, school is school."

"Amphitrite could visit. She sent those cookies. The blue ones."

"She'd bring them for everyone and Tyson would cry."

"I'm serious," Grover said, though he was smiling. "You've got two older sisters looking out for you. You've got — us. It's okay to want it."

Percy looked at him then, really looked. "I do want it. Today especially. Is that okay? To just want today?"

Grover felt his throat get tight. He covered it by playing a quick trill on his pipes — three bright notes that scattered across the water and made the naiads clap. "That's the most satyr thing you've ever said. Today is sacred, man. Carpe diem and all that. Eat the orange. Float the boat."

They floated. Grover played lazily — not magic, just music — while clouds built themselves into castles and then unbuilt themselves. Percy lay back with his hands behind his head and watched. A turtle surfaced beside the canoe, eyed them with ancient disdain, and sank.

When the horn blew for forge hours, Grover pretended to be devastated. "Back to shore, sailor."

Percy paddled them in, smooth and easy. They flipped the canoe, racked it. Grover kept the orange peels to compost.

On the path up from the lake, their shoulders bumped. Neither moved away.

Silena had been waiting for a chance to work on the circlet all week.

She'd designed it herself, which was still a novelty among the people who knew her primarily for organizing the cabin and making everybody look presentable at celebrations. The circlet was for a small performance in the amphitheater, not a royal commission. It needed to hold the fabric headpiece securely without pinching the person who wore it, and she had decided to make the clasp as beautiful as the rest.

The first mock-up failed on a wooden mannequin. The metal loop was narrow enough to hold under normal movement, but it caught the fabric whenever she tried to remove the headpiece.

"Then make the loop bigger," a Hephaestus apprentice suggested.

Silena looked at the design. "If I do that, it might slip when the performer moves."

"Then add a second fastening."

"That creates another place where it could snag."

The apprentice shrugged. He had offered a reasonable idea and wasn't particularly interested in spending the whole afternoon discussing a hair ornament. There were armor fittings waiting to be inspected, and he had a workshop assignment due before dinner.

Silena realized she'd been expecting him to keep helping because he had helped with the first attempt.

"You don't have to stay," she said.

He looked up, relieved. "Can I go finish the hinge work?"

"Yes. Thank you for looking at it."

After he left, Silena sat with the circlet in her hands and looked at the unsuccessful fastening. It was tempting to ask Percy to solve the problem as soon as he arrived. He was good with fiddly tasks when he slowed down, and she loved working with him. She also wanted to know she could design something without making her brother responsible for finishing it.

She went to the scrap drawer and found three pieces of thin copper wire. They were not strong enough for the final clasp, but she could use them to test the motion without wasting the better metal. She bent one into a wider curve, another into a hook and the third into a simple sliding catch.

The first two failed for the reasons she expected. The sliding catch opened easily but would not stay closed when she shook the mannequin.

She wrote down the results. It seemed absurd to keep notes about a little circlet. Annabeth would have approved, and that was an irritating reason to keep doing it.

Beckendorf returned from checking a weapon fitting and saw the pieces laid out on the table.

"Having trouble?"

"Yes. The shape works. The fastening doesn't."

He examined the three prototypes.

"Which matters most, removal or security?"

"Both."

"Then you may need a separate release mechanism. Something that stays closed under ordinary movement but opens when you press in a particular direction."

Silena thought about the suggestion. She had been treating the clasp as one part that had to do every job. A small spring or lever could separate the holding force from the release motion.

"Could I test a release tab?" she asked.

"Yes, but use the practice material first. You'll need to make sure it doesn't cut the cloth."

He showed her where the small tools were kept and left her to try it. She was grateful. It was different from having someone take the project out of her hands.

She made a small cardboard model, then a more precise one in soft metal. The release tab was too easy to press at first. She shifted it to the inside, where it would be protected during performance but could still be reached deliberately. The third attempt held when the mannequin moved and released without catching the fabric.

Silena smiled. She'd spent most of the afternoon on a device nobody in camp would notice if it worked properly. That was exactly why it mattered to her. The person wearing it would be able to move and perform without thinking about the clasp.

When Percy arrived from the canoe lake, she had a task ready for him.

"I need you to test this," she said. "Not to fix it. To tell me whether you can open it while keeping the headpiece in place."

He examined the model. "Do you want me to wear it?"

"No. Put it on the mannequin and try the release."

He did. The first attempt took too long because he pressed the wrong side of the tab. Silena adjusted the small marker, then watched him try again. This time it opened cleanly.

"Good," she said.

"That's all you needed?"

"For now."

He looked at the finished prototype. "I thought you wanted help making it."

"I did. Then I solved most of it. You're helping me check whether the solution works for somebody who didn't invent it."

Percy smiled. "That's a very Annabeth answer."

"She's occasionally right about things."

He laughed. Silena went to get the final metal. She had enjoyed making the circlet because it let her be the person who designed something useful and beautiful, not only the sister who remembered everyone's appointments. The project was small. It belonged to her.

They finished the clasp together, with Percy holding the piece steady while she made the last adjustment. When the instructor checked it, he approved it for the rehearsal with a note that the performer should test the release before going onstage.

Silena put that instruction in the cloth bag with the circlet. She would be there for the rehearsal, but the headpiece should be usable even if she wasn't standing beside the stage.

The forge at golden hour was roaring and warm, with fires matching the light coming through the doors.

Silena was waiting for Percy at the entrance to the forges with her hair tied up, soot already on her cheek like a beauty mark, gloves tucked into her belt. Silena, fourteen, daughter of Poseidon and the oldest sister in Cabin Three, which people always misunderstood — they thought the sea cabin meant only waves and storms, but Silena knew it meant making things people loved. Armor that fit right. A clasp that didn't pinch. A gift that said *I see you.*

"You're my volunteer," she announced when Percy arrived, pink from the lake, smelling of oranges.

"Do I get to hit things with a hammer?" he asked hopefully.

"You get to polish." She handed him a rag and a breastplate that gleamed dully. "And listen."

The Hephaestus kids moved around the anvils in their heavy aprons, shouting over the bellows. Sparks rose in fountains. Someone was quenching something that hissed like an angry cat. The air tasted like coal and hot metal and the orange soda Beckendorf kept in the ice chest.

Percy sat on a stool beside Silena's workbench and polished. He was good at repetitive tasks when he wasn't being watched too closely — steady, patient, the way water smooths stone.

Silena worked on a circlet — silver, with small shells set around it. Her own work, shaped from sea-glass and shell, brought to the forge to have the clasp set right. For herself, Percy realized after a minute. She'd been talking about shells for weeks.

"You're good at that," Silena said, nodding at his polishing.

"Thalia says I'm good at losing slowly."

"Thalia's a romantic." Silena's needle flashed. "How's your day?"

"Ordinary," Percy said, and he said it like Clarisse had — like it was praise.

"Good. You need ordinary. You're collecting it." She bit off a thread. "You know what I collect?"

"What?"

"Moments when people look like themselves." She held up the circlet to the light, checking the setting. "Like this morning — Clarisse laughed at breakfast because Chris blew a bubble in his milk. Real laugh, not war laugh. I saw it. I keep it here." She tapped her temple.

Percy was quiet, polishing. The breastplate began to shine, reflecting the forge fire in a warm smear.

"Can I tell you something?" Silena asked. She always asked permission before she went deep. It was her way.

"Always."

"I'm glad you're here. Not — not because of prophecies or big stuff. Because Clarisse sleeps better when you're in the cabin. Because Tyson stands up straighter when you laugh. Because Annabeth reads louder when you're listening." She set the circlet down. "You're good for the camp's face. You make us look like ourselves."

Percy stared at her. His ears were red. "I don't know what to say to that."

"Say you'll help me with the clasp on this and walk me to dinner."

He did both. The circlet closed with a soft click. The breastplate shone like a mirror. Outside the forge doors, the sun was dropping, turning the whole camp gold. The bellows sighed. Someone laughed.

Silena slipped the circlet into a cloth bag and slung her arm through Percy's as they walked out into the evening. The air was cooling. Crickets had started. She was a head taller, and Percy leaned into it the way little brothers did when they knew they were safe.

"Ordinary looks good on you, Percy Jackson," she said.

Dinner was chaos, and chaos was holy.

The dining pavilion at sundown was Percy's favorite place in the world, though he'd never rank his places because Annabeth would make him defend his rankings with citations. Long tables filled with kids in orange shirts, plates heaped high, the central fire crackling. The sky above the open roof went pink to violet to deep blue while they ate. The first stars pricked through.

Percy sat with his cabin — Silena on one side, Clarisse on the other, shoulder to shoulder — with Tyson visiting across from them with a mountain of mashed potatoes. But dinner was never just your table. Chris Rodriguez leaned over from the Hermes table to steal a roll. The Stoll brothers staged a three-second straw war. Katie Gardner from Demeter came by to tell Grover the gopher negotiations were going well and to ask him to bless the clover. Thalia, at the Zeus table, caught Percy's eye and mimed a spear thrust to the ribs. He mimed dying. Annabeth, beside her sister, rolled her eyes so hard Percy felt it from two tables away.

Mr. D stood to make announcements. "Yes, yes. Capture the flag Friday. No, you may not use the lake monster. Yes, that rule is because of Percy Jackson. No further questions."

Cheers. Percy bowed. Silena ruffled his hair. Clarisse stole a roll off his plate and ate it in front of him while maintaining eye contact.

Chiron raised his goblet. The old centaur looked tired in the way teachers look tired when they're happy-tired. "To today," he said simply. "To work well done, to friends well kept, and to peace, which we do not take for granted."

"To today!" the pavilion echoed. Two hundred voices. Glasses clinked. The fire flared.

Percy drank — grape juice, dark and sweet — and felt it warm all the way down. He looked around at the faces lit by firelight: Clarisse with her arm around Chris, laughing with her mouth full; Silena showing the shell circlet to a Demeter girl, who actually looked impressed; Beckendorf giving Tyson a fist bump; Grover playing a soft tune on his pipes between bites; Connor Stoll wearing a napkin as a hat; Annabeth, gray eyes bright, actually eating instead of reading, Thalia stealing her dessert and Annabeth letting her.

After dinner they processed to the amphitheater for the campfire. It was too nice a night to skip, even with no new campers to welcome. The Apollo kids led songs. Percy sang off-key and loud. Tyson, visiting for the sing-along before heading back to his bunk in the forges, sang on-key and louder. Silena leaned against Clarisse halfway through the second song, and Clarisse covered her with a blanket when the wind picked up. The fire crackled and sent sparks up into the dark like tiny constellations rising to join the real ones.

Percy didn't think about quests. He didn't think about prophecies or titans or the sea stirring in his blood. He thought about how the log bench was warm under him, how the smoke smelled like pine, how Annabeth, three rows up, turned and found him in the crowd and smiled — just a quick, ordinary smile that meant *I'm here, you're here.*

The fire burned low. Chiron dismissed them with a blessing in Ancient Greek that Percy mostly understood: *Go in peace. Sleep well. Tomorrow will come, and we will meet it.*

Cabins filed out by torchlight, laughing, shushing, tripping over roots. The night was cool and smelled like pine and woodsmoke.

Ordinary. Best kind.

After dinner, Percy found Micah outside the Hermes cabin with a scrap of paper in his hand.

The boy had been waiting beside the steps, watching the older campers come back from the pavilion. When he saw Percy, he stood up so abruptly that he almost dropped the paper.

"Can I show you something?"

Percy nodded. Micah held out a drawing of a canoe with three arrows marking the way a person should step into it. He'd drawn a small figure in a flotation vest beside the lowest part of the dock. The vest was bright orange, colored so thoroughly that the paper had begun to tear.

"I made it after the lesson," Micah said.

Percy studied the drawing. The canoe was lopsided and the arrows didn't quite match the angle of the dock. But the order was right: hold the side, keep the weight low, sit before reaching for the paddle.

"You remembered."

"Most of it. I couldn't draw the way it rocks."

"Nobody can draw every movement. You could write a note about it."

Micah considered. "I don't want to write too much. The pictures are easier."

"Then draw the boat in two positions. It tilts a little, then comes back."

The boy took the pencil and added a second line beneath the canoe. It made the drawing less tidy and more accurate.

"Like this?"

"Yes."

Micah looked pleased. He had spent months at camp thinking he'd never be comfortable on water. Today he'd sat in a canoe without anyone making the lake do something unnatural to keep him safe. The lesson hadn't made him brave enough to paddle across the lake. It had made him willing to try again.

"Can I have another lesson?" he asked.

"Ask the instructor. If she schedules it and I'm free, I'll come help."

"Okay."

Percy gave back the drawing. He was tempted to tell Micah that he'd be excellent at canoeing one day. He couldn't know that. The boy might never enjoy long paddles, and there was no reason he had to. The goal was to make water safe enough that he could choose what he wanted to do near it.

"Good work today," Percy said.

Micah smiled. "You too."

The compliment surprised Percy enough to make him laugh.

He returned to Cabin Three and found Silena placing the circlet's cloth bag on her shelf. She had promised to deliver it to the performer tomorrow, and she was checking the rehearsal time against the workshop note.

"How was your day?" she asked.

"Busy."

"That sounds like everybody's day."

"I thought ordinary meant we wouldn't have things to worry about."

She considered. "Ordinary is mostly things people have to do. It just means we can do them without somebody announcing a quest first."

Percy looked at the circlet. She had solved a problem in the forge because she'd spent time thinking and testing, not because she had access to the sea. Clarisse had given him an honest correction about his schedule. Annabeth had worked on buildings he wasn't required to join. Grover had paddled with him because they enjoyed one another's company.

He wanted to remember that none of it had required a prophecy.

Silena handed him a cloth to wipe down the shelf.

"Would you mind doing this while I put the tools away?"

"Sure."

He cleared the space, cleaned away the dust and moved a stack of old cards to the right place. He had already spent the day keeping promises. He could finish it with one more small task for his sister.

Clarisse came in with a training strap in her hand. She inspected the neat shelf and raised an eyebrow.

"Who did that?"

"Me."

"Good. Tomorrow you can do the other one."

"That's how you reward people?"

"That's how I know you can be trusted."

She went to her bunk. Silena laughed and gave Percy back the cloth.

He set the circlet bag neatly in its place, then took out the schedule from his pocket. One line remained unchecked: *ask Micah how the lesson felt*. He marked it done and left the paper beside his bed so he could use it again if another day filled up faster than he expected.

He was tired in a different way than after a fight. He liked it.

Percy waited until the Poseidon cabin was quiet.

It didn't take long — Clarisse out before her head hit the pillow, Silena mumbling about shell settings in her sleep. The guest hammock in the corner stayed folded. Tyson had headed back to the forges where he bunked when he visited. Percy lay in his bunk and listened to his sisters settle, feeling like a lighthouse keeper making sure all the lights were out except his.

When the breathing went slow and even, he slipped out the window — the door creaked — with a blanket over his arm.

Annabeth was already on the hill.

Half-Blood Hill, above the cabins, where the Golden Fleece glowed faintly in the branches of the pine and the stars came out in full, unapologetic glory. Annabeth sat with her back against the rock, knees up, blanket around her shoulders, looking up. Her hair was loose, catching starlight. She'd saved him a corner of the blanket.

"Hey," Percy said.

"Hey." She lifted the blanket. He sat and tucked it around them both. Their shoulders touched.

Below them, camp slept — dark cabins, the dying campfire's last ember, the lake a black mirror with the moon laid across it like a path. Crickets sang. An owl called once from the woods. The breeze was cool and clean.

They didn't talk for a while. They'd talked all day, in pieces — at breakfast over bacon, at the arena through Thalia, in the library over temples and terrible boats, at dinner across two tables with just their eyes. Now there was nothing left that needed saying right away.

Annabeth pointed. "Cassiopeia. See? The W. Thalia says —" She stopped, considering the bright zigzag. Their father was Zeus, king of the sky, distant and difficult; their mother was Beryl, still writing letters that Thalia read slowly before she answered. Annabeth knew the ache of wanting to be seen. "Thalia told me once that vanity is just wanting to be seen. I think about that when I look at Cassiopeia."

Percy followed her finger to the bright W. "She's easy to find."

"She wanted to be." Annabeth leaned her head against his shoulder. It was a small movement, but it rearranged the whole night. "What did you see today?"

Percy thought. He thought about Clarisse counting in the cold grass, Silena fixing his collar, Thalia's laugh when he nearly took out the dummy, the squares of light in the library, the turtle's disdain, the forge fire reflected in polished bronze, two hundred voices saying *to today.*

"Everything," he said. "I saw everything."

Annabeth smiled against his sleeve. "Good answer."

They named constellations until they ran out of ones they knew, then made up the rest — the Orange Peel, the Canoe, the Push-Up, the Terrible Boat That Sank Immediately. Percy invented a constellation called the Bacon Thief and claimed it looked exactly like Tyson. Annabeth laughed so hard she snorted, then clapped both hands over her mouth.

The moon climbed. The Fleece glowed gold-green behind them. The grass was cool under their legs. The blanket was warm.

After a long quiet, Annabeth said, very softly, "Do you ever worry you'll forget this?"

"What?"

"This. Ordinary days. When — whatever comes next comes."

Percy considered. He was twelve. He'd seen enough to know that peace was not permanent, that the gods were restless and the world tilted toward trouble. He knew there would be quests. There would be storms. There would be mornings when Clarisse wasn't doing push-ups on his lawn because she was somewhere else, bleeding or leading or both.

He also knew this: the sun rising pink over the Sound. His sisters teasing him over bacon. His best friend's pipes over still water. A girl reading aloud about temples while light moved across the table.

"I don't think so," he said. "I think this is the stuff I am. Like — the water remembers the shore even when it's out at sea. You know?"

Annabeth was quiet. Then: "That's actually profound, Seaweed Brain. Don't let it go to your head."

"Too late."

She laced her fingers through his, and they sat there while the stars wheeled slow overhead, ancient and unhurried. Camp Half-Blood breathed below them — two hundred sleeping heartbeats, monsters kept at the borders, gods watching or not watching, it didn't matter.

Percy thought about tomorrow — training, library, lake, forge, dinner, stars, or something completely different. He thought about his mom's blue cookies and his sisters' braids and Grover's ridiculous boat names. He thought about Annabeth beside him, solid and warm, planning temples in her head while she held his hand.

He was happy here.

Whatever came next — quests, wars, growing up, growing apart and back together, all the bright and terrible things the Fates kept in their pockets — he was happy here, now, on this hill, on this perfectly ordinary day that had asked nothing of him except to live it.

The wind moved through the pine. The Fleece shimmered. A shooting star cut across the sky, brief and white-gold, and Annabeth squeezed his hand.

Percy closed his eyes and kept it — all of it — exactly where it belonged.
