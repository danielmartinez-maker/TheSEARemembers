## Chapter 133: Annabeth's Birthday

Annabeth woke before the conch and knew, before glimpsing the calendar penciled with Thalia's stars, that she was fourteen.

The number settled in her mind like a newly placed stone. It brought no sensation of age. Dawn laid a thin gold line along the cabin wall, dust motes drifting through it; the floor creaked beyond her bunk; Thalia had left water within reach, cool clay sweating faintly in the morning warmth. Fourteen arrived without trumpet or storm. Inside it, she remained herself.

She lay still and let the number rest.

In one life, she had belonged to Athena. Pride of being claimed lived alongside the ache for a distant mother's attention to warm. Those memories stood beside the days she had lived since returning: Thalia's hand at her collar, Jason's solemn face on a visit, her own laugh thrown against storm windows.

Neither the years she remembered nor these newer days needed permission to be true. Neither could dictate what she should do next.

Across her chair lay the skirt and sweater chosen the night before, wool soft with folding, lamplight still remembered in their creases. Beside them waited her sketchbook, its elastic loose from use. She opened the cover, then closed it without drawing. A birthday might invite planning, but she refused to design herself into a feeling before it arrived.

"You're awake," Thalia said from the far side of the room.

Annabeth looked over. Perched on her own bed, Thalia tied her boots, dark hair loose from sleep. Behind her, the bedpost bore a row of old scratches. Thalia said Annabeth had insisted at seven that the measurements were wrong.

"I was thinking."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It is, but my stamina is excellent."

Thalia pulled a knot tight. "Happy birthday, Annie."

The name landed gently. Gratitude warmed Annabeth's chest. "Thank you."

"How does fourteen feel?"

"Like thirteen, except the calendar shows a different number."

"Birthdays generally function that way."

"Then I comprehend the system."

Thalia smiled and stood. Experience had taught her not to hug Annabeth without warning, giving a moment's glance and an open arm instead of surprise. Love and permission could share space.

"I asked Chiron whether we could hold a small party after dinner," Thalia said. "He said the pavilion is free, provided we replace the benches. I promised we'd keep it small."

"How small?"

"Small enough that nobody must deliver a speech. Large enough that Grover brings cake."

"Suspiciously precise measurement."

"With you, vagueness is unwise."

Thalia lifted a folded shirt from the chair. "You needn't hold the party if you'd rather not. We can tell everyone the plan changed."

Annabeth weighed the offer, glad the choice was genuine. "I want it. Only I don't want all of camp watching me open presents one by one as though I were a ceremonial offering."

"I can station Clarisse to threaten anyone who tries."

"She would enjoy that excessively."

"Distinguishing enjoyment from duty might prove difficult."

Annabeth sat up, blanket pooling around her waist. "I want dinner with people I like, and cake. No speech about how swiftly time passes."

"Done."

"And nobody must pretend I've become a different person because I'm fourteen."

Thalia's expression softened. Crossing the room, she settled on the edge of Annabeth's bunk, mattress dipping. "No schedule obligates you to become anybody."

Annabeth studied the scratches on the bedpost, where Thalia had measured her height through the years. "Do you ever think about the other version of me?"

"The Athena one?"

"The one who grew up without you as a sister. The one who knew you as a friend after you returned."

Thalia kept her gaze steady on her sister's face. "I think about the fact that you remember her. I won't pretend to know what she felt from within."

"I don't always know what she felt from within, either."

"Then no neat explanation is required." Thalia nudged her shoulder. "Today you get to be fourteen as whoever you are. Should another memory surface, it may sit with us. It doesn't get to commandeer the party."

"That sounds like a rule you invented because you know I like rules."

"I know you like rules you helped create."

Leaning against Thalia, Annabeth remembered another Thalia — older, distant, a friend who had survived a life Annabeth had lost. This Thalia was here: eighteen, warm, near enough that Annabeth felt the scratch of her sleeve and the steady rise of her breathing. The memories need not interlock perfectly.

"Happy birthday," Thalia said again, quieter.

"Thank you," Annabeth said, meaning all of it.

---

Thalia tried to make breakfast feel ordinary by asking Annabeth to help fold the spare blankets before they went to the pavilion.

Annabeth knew the tactic. When her sister was anxious about an event she couldn't control, she started arranging things that could be folded, polished or counted. The blankets had been folded the night before. Thalia still shook each one out and squared the corners as though they had developed imperfections while everyone slept.

"Are you expecting him?" Annabeth asked.

Thalia looked up. "Who?"

"Our father."

The question landed between them like a dropped tool. They had never developed a way to ask it without one of them wanting to make the answer easier for the other.

"He didn't promise to come," Thalia said.

"That isn't what I asked."

"No. I don't know whether he'll come."

Annabeth laid her blanket on the chair. It was tempting to pretend the answer was enough. The older sister had once done most of the worrying for both of them, and Annabeth had learned to let the silence protect her. Now she was fourteen. She wanted the choice of saying what she hoped for, even if it embarrassed her.

"I would like him to remember," she said. "I don't need a ceremony. I don't need another lesson disguised as a present."

Thalia's mouth moved. She looked toward the window, where pale sky lay quiet above the cabins.

"He knows the date."

"Then that's a beginning."

"Knowing isn't always doing."

"I know."

Thalia sat on the edge of the bed. Her boots were still wet from the early watch, and Annabeth noticed she'd left the outer buckle undone. The woman who could stand in the training field and challenge three older campers at once sometimes forgot to take care of small things when she was trying to protect her sister from disappointment.

"Did he come for your fourteenth?" Annabeth asked.

"He sent a storm cloud that turned the barn silver," Thalia said dryly. "It lasted six hours. The horses hated it."

"That sounds like him."

"He meant it as something magnificent."

"Did you like it?"

"Not much. I liked when the stable hands helped clean up afterward."

Annabeth laughed before she could stop herself. The story was ridiculous and sad, and there was no reason they couldn't find both things in it.

"I can write him a note," Annabeth said.

"On your birthday?"

"Why not? I want to ask whether the instrument he sent last time was meant for drawing or for training. He's a god, not an excuse for unanswered technical questions."

"He may not appreciate that."

"He doesn't have to appreciate every question for me to ask it."

Thalia looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded once, not quite a blessing and not quite surrender.

"Fair."

Annabeth opened her desk drawer and found the folded note she'd been working on for Jason. It wasn't a birthday invitation. He had his own life at Camp Jupiter, and she didn't want him to think she expected him to cross the country whenever she wanted company. She'd written about the new garden plan, and about a set of old service corridors that might have been built before the cabins they knew.

"Could we send him a message after breakfast?" she asked Thalia.

"If the relay is open, yes."

"I want him to know I'm fourteen."

"He knows your birthday."

"I know. I want to tell him anyway."

That got a real smile from Thalia. They had so few chances to speak to their younger brother without turning the contact into a negotiation among adults and camps. Annabeth wanted to hear his voice. She wanted to tell him that she was older, although she understood perfectly well that an extra year didn't grant her authority over his decisions.

They finished folding the blankets. Thalia tucked the ends of Annabeth's birthday ribbon under her collar, asked whether the knot was too tight, and loosened it when Annabeth said yes. That tiny correction felt more useful than another lecture about growing up.

On the way to the pavilion they passed the climbing wall. A first-year Apollo camper called happy birthday from above a ledge. Annabeth shouted thanks and then laughed at herself for sounding embarrassed. By the time they reached the path below the cabins, she had decided she could enjoy the attention without treating it as proof that she had changed.

Thalia walked beside her rather than half a step in front. Annabeth noticed. She didn't comment. They were still sisters, and the difference between being protected and being accompanied could be as small as the space between their shoulders.

By the time Annabeth reached the pavilion, camp had already heard about the birthday. Silena, sixteen, who remembered everyone's birthday, seemed the likely source. Clarisse, fifteen, remembered too, though she would deny it if questioned.

They waited at the Zeus table. Between the plates Silena had arranged wildflowers, their stems damp and fragrant, petals still beaded from the meadow; Clarisse guarded a covered dish with one hand, its warmth seeping through the cloth.

"Is that cake?" Annabeth asked.

"It's breakfast," Clarisse said.

"That evades my question."

"It's a sweet roll." Silena lifted the lid, releasing cinnamon and baked apple. "Apples and cinnamon. Clarisse helped."

"I carried the tray," Clarisse said.

"She stopped the kitchen from adding too much honey."

"Already sweet enough," Clarisse said.

Annabeth slipped into the seat between them, wood smooth beneath her palms. "Thank you. Breakfast wasn't necessary."

"We know," Silena said. "We wanted to."

Clarisse slid the dish nearer. "Eat before Percy arrives. He'll claim he wants one piece, then take the largest."

"I would never," Percy said from behind Annabeth.

He carried two cups of tea and a plate of toast, butter melting into the crumb. Damp hair curled from the lake, lake-air still on him, and a graphite smudge crossed one thumb, dark against sun-browned skin. Annabeth noticed and filed the question away.

"You absolutely would," Clarisse said.

"I have changed."

"Since yesterday?" Clarisse asked.

"Possibly."

Setting one cup before Annabeth, steam curling with bergamot and honey, he said, "Happy birthday."

"Thank you."

"I brought tea. The sweet roll appeared to require supervision."

"It has survived thus far," Annabeth said.

"Hence my vigilance."

Percy sat beside her, knees touching beneath the table, warmth steady through denim. Their relationship had grown through small, chosen acts — tea carried without asking, a seat saved, a question held back until welcome. Old memories didn't dictate how this moment felt; she wanted him here now, for the warmth of his shoulder and the tea gone slightly too strong.

Silena placed a narrow wrapped parcel on the table. "From Clarisse and me. Open now or later, as you prefer."

"Now," Clarisse said. "We didn't haul it here for you to admire wrapping."

"Both admiration options remain available," Silena told her.

Pale blue wrapping, tied with dark red cord. Inside lay a scallop shell polished smooth, its ridges worn to silk under the fingers, fitted with a fine bronze hinge that clicked softly when moved. Opening it, Annabeth found a tiny compass nestled in the inner curve, needle balanced on a pin no wider than a thorn. Engraved marks ringed it, some crisp, others softened by a patient hand.

She tilted it toward the window, morning light pooling across the sill. The needle trembled, then found north, steadying as her hand stilled.

"It's beautiful," she said.

"It functions," Clarisse replied. "Silena checked it against three compasses, then forced me to verify."

"The needle was bent," Silena said.

"I corrected it."

Annabeth traced the tiny engraved marks with her fingertip. "You built this together?"

"The shell came from Atlantis," Silena said. "We gathered it from the reef. Since you map every place you visit, I thought it might serve you."

"Clarisse built the mechanism," Silena added. "She insisted a compass should work even when its bearer is distracted."

Clarisse shot her a warning look. "It should."

"Kind description of me," Annabeth said.

"Accurate." Clarisse tapped the shell. "The hinge locks, and the casing will survive a drop."

"I won't drop it."

"Good. Then it will last."

Both sisters' labor showed in the gift: Silena's careful eye, Clarisse's sturdy craft. It carried a piece of the sea without pretending Annabeth had lived there. She had visited, drawn the gardens, received welcome. The shell preserved that truth without claiming more.

Closing the shell, Annabeth cradled it in her palm, its surface warm already. "Thank you. I love it."

Silena's smile brightened. "Good."

Clarisse studied her own plate. "You can carry it on your next expedition."

"Plan or warning?"

"Both."

Percy touched the cord. "Superior to the compass I built at eight."

"What failed in yours?" Annabeth asked.

"It pointed at my hand."

"That isn't a compass."

"It was a small hand."

"That doesn't redeem it."

They ate, debating whether a compass ought to point toward the nearest place a person desired, crumbs gathering on the blue wrapping and tea cooling beside the shell. Beside her tea Annabeth kept the shell open, watching the needle hold direction while she rotated it, fascinated by its quiet certainty.

---

The gift from Zeus arrived at noon.

Annabeth was in the pavilion with Thalia, helping Chiron rearrange benches for the party, wood scraping stone with each shift. A shadow crossed the grass, though summer blue stretched cloudless above camp. The air tightened with the sensation of a storm debating whether it had been invited, ozone faint on the breeze.

Thalia looked up. "Did you order weather?"

"No."

"Good. I wasn't prepared to argue with the sky before lunch."

A small white cloud descended over the pavilion's open side, cool mist brushing their cheeks. Beneath it, suspended by silver ribbon, hung a parcel. As it drifted down, the parcel altered shape: first a square box, then a tall cylinder, then a narrow case with a pointed top. It touched the table, and the cloud dissolved in a soft puff of rain that left no dampness.

"Subtle, that," Thalia said.

"For him?" Annabeth asked. "Perhaps."

No name marked the parcel, only a small lightning bolt stamped in silver. Leaning closer, Annabeth watched the stamp shift into an eagle, then a trident, then a pair of wings, before settling back into a bolt.

"At least he refrained from sending an actual thunderbolt," Thalia said.

"I believe this represents restraint."

"Disturbing that you can recognize his restraint."

Annabeth touched the ribbon. It loosened itself and slipped away. The case opened, though nothing within stayed still. A silver object rose, shining with the pale brightness of cloud-lit metal. It folded into a crown, then a tiny shield, then a book with blank pages. A plume formed at its tip; the plume lengthened into a compass needle; the whole object drew itself into a long ruler marked in faint gold.

"Intended behavior?" Thalia asked.

"Presumably."

The ruler flickered into a sword, blade no longer than a dinner knife. Then a miniature column, a feather, a small silver owl wearing severe disapproval, and at last a drafting compass. Its legs opened and closed like a nervous insect.

Annabeth extended her hand, and the object drifted toward her palm, trailing faint cold as mountain air. Cool, light metal. A quiet voice rose from the case. Above it bloomed an Iris image: Zeus's face shifting from an old stern man to a younger one, then to a third face Annabeth had never seen. He never wore the same face twice.

"Annabeth. You are fourteen today." A pause, as though Zeus had rehearsed several openings. "I wished to send something useful. I understand you hold an interest in design. I was also advised that a daughter of Zeus should not receive merely a work tool for her birthday."

"Who advised you?" Thalia murmured.

The shifting face hesitated, then answered, "Your sister." It changed again, as though Zeus had remembered Thalia listening. "The gift assumes several forms. Some succeed better than others. Should it settle in a form you dislike, it can be altered."

In Annabeth's hand the compass became a small open-winged bird, then folded back into neat drawing dividers. The little silver feet rested against her skin.

"Happy birthday," Zeus said. "I hope the day proves good."

A small rush of air ended the message. The image vanished; the case snapped shut.

Thalia retrieved the card tucked under the lining. The script shifted from broad and formal to narrow and slanted. Aloud she read, "I was informed that presents should not oblige a daughter to defeat a monster to open them. This struck me as a sensible improvement."

Annabeth blinked. "Did he write that himself?"

"Possibly his handwriting." Thalia turned the card. "Or one of his other handwritings."

The object became a ruler etched with fine measurements. Some transformations served show; others might prove useful. Careful workmanship marked the silver, even though the gift felt assembled from several guesses about what a fourteen-year-old girl should want.

"He tried," Annabeth said.

"He did," Thalia agreed. "The crown was excessive."

"You believe he expected me to wear it?"

"I suspect he considered it a safe opening move. Gods guess poorly when they don't ask."

"People share that flaw."

"True." Thalia offered the note. "Keep it?"

Annabeth watched the tool fold into a drafting square and hold shape for three full seconds. "Yes. I want to discover its capabilities. And I want to tell him the sword is unnecessary."

"Include that in your thank-you note."

"I'll say it in person, should he ask."

"Your right as a daughter."

Daughter opened both a door and a distance. In this life she was Zeus's daughter; in the other, Athena's. She could miss Athena as her mother and remain curious about Zeus as her father. An awkward gift could be accepted without declaring its giver flawless.

Returning the object to its case, she watched it shift once more, assume a small silver bird, then settle into the outline of a compass.

"Perhaps it has a favorite form," Thalia said.

"Then we share something."

---

After Zeus's gift arrived, Thalia asked Annabeth to come with her to the empty practice room behind Cabin One.

"I don't want to hide it," Annabeth said, clutching the case.

"I'm not asking you to."

"Then why are we in here?"

"Because everybody in the pavilion was watching your face, and I wanted to know what you actually thought."

Annabeth set the box on the bench. The gift inside shifted between shapes when she touched it, silver flowing into a little bird, then a compass, then a folded piece of metal that suggested it might be a key. It was beautifully made. It was also from their father, who could send something tender in the morning and make the same daughter afraid to speak by evening.

"I like it," Annabeth said.

"You're allowed."

"And I don't like that he didn't come."

"You're allowed that too."

Annabeth watched the metal return to a compass. "Does it have a purpose, or does it just change because it can?"

Thalia sat beside her. "We could ask the forge cabin."

"Would that be rude?"

"Testing a gift before you rely on it? No."

They went to the workshop together. Beckendorf examined the case without prying into the mechanism and asked where it had come from. Annabeth told him. He didn't make a grand comment about Zeus. He checked whether the object had a sharp edge in any of its forms, whether it became hot, and whether it changed when she wasn't touching it.

"It's responding to you, mostly," he said. "But I can't tell you what the enchantment intends. I wouldn't use it as a compass on a journey until we verify it points the right way."

Annabeth looked almost offended. "It seems accurate."

"It seems accurate here, where we already know where north is."

He put a plain magnetic compass beside the gift and turned both carefully. The silver object followed in two positions, then hesitated near the forge's iron tools. It turned in a different direction from the ordinary needle.

"So it doesn't always point north," Annabeth said.

"Not in this room. It may be pointing toward something else. If you can ask the person who made it, do. In the meantime, treat it as a beautiful object with an unknown function."

That was not the answer Annabeth wanted, but she wrote it down. She had spent enough time with unreliable equipment to know that wanting a thing to work did not make it safe.

Thalia watched her close the case. "Does knowing that spoil it?"

"No. It makes me feel as though I can decide what it means to me without pretending I know what it does."

They returned to Cabin One so Annabeth could put the gift on a shelf before the party. As she did, she noticed a smaller parcel near her pillow, wrapped in ordinary brown paper. Thalia had left it there before breakfast.

"You weren't going to tell me about this?" Annabeth asked.

"You'd have found it."

Inside was a little leather pouch with separate pockets for pencils, compass and a rolled map. The stitching was uneven along one edge. Thalia had clearly made it herself, rather than asking Silena to do the work.

"That's why you kept disappearing after dinner," Annabeth said.

"I have many mysterious obligations."

"You stabbed your finger sewing it."

"Several times. It was a vicious opponent."

Annabeth tried the pockets. The silver gift's case fitted in one of them if she chose to carry it. Her ordinary compass fitted in another. Neither had to replace the other.

"I love it," she said.

Thalia looked away, embarrassed in a way no sparring defeat had ever managed.

"Good. It might last an entire year if you don't fill it with rocks."

Annabeth put her arms around her older sister. Thalia hugged her back, hard enough to be awkward, then let her go before the moment became a performance.

"Fourteen," Thalia said.

"Yes."

"I keep forgetting you aren't seven."

"I have noticed."

"I'm trying to do better."

"You are."

Thalia took her hand as they went toward the pavilion. They didn't pretend the gift from Zeus had been unkind or that his absence didn't hurt. Both were true in different ways. Annabeth carried a pouch Thalia had sewn and a silver mystery she didn't yet understand, and she knew which one would be useful when she needed to find her way home.


---

Without any plan Annabeth could diagram, the party assembled. Campers hauled lanterns and plates into the pavilion, glass chiming and sandals slapping stone. Grover bore a lopsided cake while two satyrs strung lights across the rafters, pine resin and hot sugar threading the air. Nico received charge of the candles.

"Fourteen," he announced upon Annabeth's arrival.

"Good," she said. "Thirteen worried me."

"I checked. Four extras wait in case one breaks."

"Practical."

"I thought so."

Grover set down the cake: blue icing, uneven gold stars, a tiny sugar owl beside a much larger fish, frosting ridged where eager fingers had smoothed it. The fish was probably a dolphin.

"A group effort," Grover said. "Ella made the stars. I made the fish. Nico managed candles."

"The fish is good," Annabeth said.

"Thank you. It needed a friend."

The gathering included Percy and his sisters, Thalia, Chiron, Grover, Nico, Ella, and campers from the Athena, Zeus, and Poseidon cabins. No announcement summoned them. People came because they wished to, carrying chairs and making room.

Easy noise filled the pavilion, plates clattering and laughter overlapping under the strung lights. Silena arranged plates; Clarisse barred the entrance against anyone bearing a spear; Thalia righted wandering chairs, wood scraping with each rescue. Percy brought Annabeth the first plate.

"You needn't serve me," she told him.

"I know. I wanted to."

"Different thing."

"Entirely." He passed the plate. "I also wanted to ensure the largest piece survived."

"From you?"

"From the crowd."

"Admirable devotion to my cake."

"I try."

An Apollo camper played guitar while Ella sang about a chicken who found a golden apple and forgot its hiding place. Silena joined the chorus; Nico improvised a second verse about the chicken's suspicious friends. Annabeth laughed until she had to set down her cup, tea sloshing.

When cake time came, Chiron lifted a hand and the pavilion settled. Flames leaned toward the open doors in the breeze. All fourteen candles looked like points on a map of somewhere she had yet to visit.

"No speeches," Thalia reminded everyone.

"I only meant to say —" Grover began.

"No speeches," Clarisse repeated.

"I meant to say I made the fish."

"That isn't a speech."

"Then it's permitted."

Annabeth surveyed the table: Thalia's hand resting beside hers, scarred knuckles relaxed, Clarisse easing when Silena bumped her shoulder, Nico's careful count under his breath, Grover's anxious hope she liked the fish, Percy smiling from across with crumbs on his sleeve.

Fourteen, she thought. Not a perfect number. Simply the next one.

She closed her eyes. Her wishes — for timelines to stop casting shadows, for her family to stay safe, for the gods to grow easier to understand — exceeded one breath. Instead she wished this feeling could be real without needing to last forever, and that she could remember the evening without converting it into a symbol.

Then she blew out the candles.

Applause burst through the room. Ella clapped her wings; Grover danced stiffly; Clarisse pretended indifference. Percy cheered loudest.

"Happy birthday," he said again.

"I heard you the first time."

"I wanted certainty."

At the table, rather than before all of camp, they opened gifts, paper rustling softly and thank-yous overlapping. Annabeth thanked people for an embroidered handkerchief, its stitches tiny and even, blue candies that clicked like sea-glass, architectural postcards from an Athena camper showing vaults she had never seen. The shell compass rested beside her plate; Zeus's tool glimmered faintly in its case.

Once the crowd turned toward cake, Thalia touched Annabeth's arm. "May I give you yours now?"

"Yes."

Thalia produced a long package wrapped in brown paper and tied with blue cord, edges crisp with care. Inside lay a hardbound sketchbook with a dark blue cover, brass corners catching lamplight, binding sturdy enough for a bag of tools.

"You made this?"

"Beckendorf helped with the binding. I handled cover and corners." Thalia tapped the spine. "For sketches, notes, maps. Whatever you choose."

Inside the front cover waited a pocket for a photograph. Beneath the ribbon marker, Thalia had written: *For all the places you have been, all the places you remember, and the ones you haven't drawn yet.* Twice Annabeth read it.

"I left the pages unlabeled," Thalia said. "You may decide which memories belong where. Or use it for floor plans. Your book."

Annabeth ran a thumb across the brass corner, cool and solid. "You made room for the photograph."

"You choose what fills the pocket," Thalia said. "I know other memories live in you from before. I don't know them all, and I won't feign otherwise. I want space here for them, and for everything after today."

Across Annabeth's knees the sketchbook rested. It didn't decree which life mattered most. Space for a photograph, a drawing, an old memory, a new one — that was what Annabeth had wanted without knowing how to request it.

She leaned into Thalia, and this time her sister's arm encircled her, jacket smelling of cedar, campfire, and the faint oil Thalia used on her bowstring. Annabeth allowed herself to be held, cedar and campfire on Thalia's jacket.

"Thank you," she said against her sister's shoulder.

"You're welcome."

"It's perfect."

"It's a sketchbook."

"It's an excellent sketchbook."

"Acceptable." Thalia squeezed once. "Happy birthday, Annie."

The old photograph hung on the cabin wall. She could place a copy in the new book, draw the laundromat floor as an ocean, sketch the Athena cabin or the one they occupied now. The pages demanded no choice.

---

Before the last guests left, Annabeth discovered that somebody had moved her birthday drawing.

It was a small plan of the west garden, the one she'd made after noticing the gap between two terraces. She'd set it beside her gifts, and now it was propped at an angle near the candles where a curious camper could see it. The pencil line she'd drawn to indicate the closed section looked, at a distance, like an open trail.

"Who put this here?" she asked.

Thalia looked over. "I moved it so nobody would spill juice on it. Sorry."

"You didn't know what it showed."

"No."

"It's not a finished public map." Annabeth took it down and tucked it into her folder. Her tone had gone sharp. She could hear it herself.

Thalia looked at the guests and then at her. "I was trying to keep it safe."

"I know. But I would rather you ask before moving my work."

"Fair." Thalia folded her arms, visibly stung. "I'd also rather you tell me what needs to stay private than expect me to read your mind."

Annabeth wanted to defend herself. On her birthday, especially, she wanted one day without feeling she had to explain every decision. But Thalia wasn't wrong.

"I should have labeled it," she said. "I'll do that."

"And I'll ask." Her sister's smile was small. "Can I help put away the rest of the drawings?"

"Yes. The finished ones."

They sorted the pages together, placing the public sketches in one pile and the working maps in another. There were still candles to blow out and presents to open. Annabeth was glad the disagreement had not been swallowed for the sake of a perfect party; it had been dealt with, and could stay dealt with.

Percy waited until the cake had mostly vanished and the crowd had dissolved into smaller clusters before offering his gift. Beneath his chair he had kept a flat envelope all evening. Twice Annabeth had seen him check that it remained.

"This is yours," he said. "I made it. Well, I drew it. I didn't manufacture the paper."

"Useful distinction."

"I thought so." He rubbed the graphite on his thumb, worsening the smear. "Open here or later. Either suits me."

"Here."

He passed the envelope. Inside lay a large sheet of cream paper, folded once, its weight substantial between her fingers. Carefully Annabeth opened it, paper whispering, graphite faintly smudging the air with its mineral smell.

An architectural sketch: a house on a low bluff, sea on one side, pines on the other, gulls implied in two quick strokes above the eaves. A wide porch faced the water; a glass room contained a worktable, maps, a drafting lamp, its shade tilted to throw light across paper.

The floor plan filled the lower half: a large kitchen, a library opening onto the porch, careful stairs, enough bedrooms for everyone to keep space. One held a balcony; another, a window seat facing the water.

Neither palace nor camp cabin, it looked like a modest home built to hold people who came and went. Beside the glass room, a note read *Annabeth's study?*

She looked at Percy. "When did you draw this?"

"A little at a time, mostly since Atlantis." He touched the paper's edge. "I kept remembering your sketch of the gallery, the garden, how you called camp yours. I wanted a place that felt like both without replacing either."

Between water and trees the house sat, both held in its view, shingles silvered as driftwood and windows catching the imagined sea. She liked the sturdy porch, its posts thick enough for a swing, the light-filled study, the generous kitchen, the question mark beside her name in Percy's quick hand.

"You included room for maps and a worktable."

"Of course."

"And sensible stairs."

"I consulted your notes."

"I said stairs shouldn't surprise people."

"Finest architectural advice I've received."

Her eyes traced the porch. A home they might build when older lay distant; neither knew what roads they would travel. The sketch demanded no promise. A someday that didn't cancel the homes they already loved.

"You needn't make this a plan," Percy said. "I'm thirteen, you're fourteen, and a thousand decisions wait. I wanted to draw it. Alter anything."

"I would alter some things."

"Expected."

"The west windows need more shade. And the porch roof runs too narrow for anyone hoping to sit outside through heavy rain."

"I can draft a wider roof."

"Include it in the next draft."

"A next draft will exist?"

"If you'd like one."

Relief and pleasure eased his smile. "I would."

Sliding the page into the sketchbook pocket Thalia had made, Annabeth said, "I like that you didn't draw it as the only home I could want."

"You possess more than one."

"So do you."

"I know."

Palm up, he offered his hand. Annabeth took it. She could remember loving him in another life, but this Percy wasn't asking her to replay the old story. He had drawn a future as a question and left room for her answer.

"Thank you," she said.

"You're welcome."

"It's the kind of thing I would have wanted in either life."

"The house?"

"A plan that leaves room to change."

"Then I got it right."

"You got one important part right."

"I'll accept that."

They talked through the sketch: its size, the storage closet tucked wisely under the eaves, the porch Percy had angled away from the wind, pencil notes crowding the margins. He had left blank space for another door, or a path not yet existent, paper bare and expectant there.

No promise. Attention given shape.

As the party wound down, lanterns guttering low and shadows lengthening across cleared tables, Chiron thanked campers for replacing benches. Grover and Ella carried the remaining cake to the Big House kitchen, its blue icing tilting with each step. Clarisse escorted Nico back to the cabins, claiming she acted because he carried too many candles. Silena walked with them, humming Ella's chorus under her breath.

Thalia began gathering lanterns, glass warm in her hands. Annabeth closed the sketchbook and tucked it under her arm. Beside her Percy waited without filling the quiet.

"I think I'd like a minute at the lake," she said.

"Want company?"

"Yes."

Together they walked down the path. Cool grass brushed beneath their shoes, clover damp and sweet-smelling, air still warm from the day. Between trees moved fireflies, faint green lights tracing short uncertain routes, winking out and rekindling. At the lake, water held the stars in broken pieces, ripples silvering and shattering with each breeze. Annabeth sat on the dock and set the book beside her, wood creaking and cool dew seeping through her skirt. Percy settled close enough that their shoulders touched, denim and cotton, steadying.

"Good birthday?" he asked.

"Good day."

"Acceptable."

"A birthday needn't tell me who I am."

"No."

"Still, having people notice who I am helped."

Percy watched the water. "I notice you."

"I know."

"I mean, I try. I don't always succeed."

"You ask."

"I can continue."

She leaned her head against his shoulder, his shirt sun-warmed and smelling faintly of lake and soap. Behind them, Camp Half-Blood murmured with familiar sounds: a door closing, laughter spilling from the pavilion, a chair scraping the wrong way across stone. Thalia's voice rose briefly, warning someone against leaving a lantern in the grass. Clarisse answered that she had it. Silena told them all to stop arguing and go to bed.

Camp wasn't the sole place Annabeth belonged. She had been Athena's daughter and was Zeus's now; she had known Thalia as friend and sister. None of those truths erased the others or made Annabeth a stranger to herself.

Percy shifted slightly, then turned his face toward her. "May I ask something?"

"You did already."

"Warm-up."

She studied him. His expression had gone careful in a way she recognized: not precisely nervous, but making room for no, lamplight catching the green in his eyes and the lake-wind tangling his hair.

"Could I give you a birthday kiss?" he asked.

Warmth rose to her face. Against the dock the lake lipped a small wave. "Yes."

"Yes?"

"Yes, Percy. You may kiss me."

Slowly he leaned in, giving her every chance to pause. Halfway she met him. Brief, soft, familiar; a quiet thing they chose together, nothing more complicated, salt and sugar lingering faintly between them. Parting, his hand stayed beside hers on the dock, open and waiting. She threaded her fingers through his.

"Happy birthday," he said.

"Thank you."

"I wanted to ask."

"I know." She smiled. "Glad you did."

Hands joined, sketchbook between them, its cover cool with evening dew, they sat. Its blank pages could hold the lake, the old photograph, the shape of a home not yet built. She would draw what she remembered, what she had been told, what she chose now.

Fourteen hadn't made her someone new. It had granted another day to be herself, lamplight steady on the dock and her sister's party still warm in memory.

In the water the stars trembled, then steadied as the breeze passed, silver reforming into scattered constellations across the dark water.
