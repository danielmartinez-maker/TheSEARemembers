## Chapter 156: What the Hands Know

The boathouse smelled like morning before anyone had a chance to be useful in it.

Percy got there first, which was not a plan so much as a failure to sleep past the part where the light came through the high Cabin Three window and laid itself across his face and stayed there. He had dressed in the half-dark without waking anyone, which at Cabin Three meant without waking Clarisse, because Silena slept like a person who had decided to be kind to the morning and Clarisse slept like a person who expected it to try something. The faded orange camp shirt off his hook, the one with the trident washed to almost white across the shoulder and the left sleeve hem coming undone in a way he kept meaning to fix. Swim shorts that had been his in April and were mostly string by September. Sneakers still damp from yesterday, laces knotted until the bow sat sideways. In his pockets: a hair tie, a pebble, a pencil stub.

He carried the bread. Silena had wrapped half a plain brown loaf in a dish towel and set it on his bunk with a note that said EAT BEFORE YOU LIFT ANYTHING HEAVY, and he had eaten a heel off the end on the walk down.

The path down was already warm underfoot. Early September held summer in its fists by day and let go finger by finger at night. The canoe lake lay flat past the reeds, green and glassy, the far shore doubled in it. Freshwater smell, mud and sun-warmed stone. No wind yet.

Percy pushed the boathouse door open with his shoulder and let the smell come out to meet him: varnish and wet wood and rope and the particular dust that lived in rafters no matter how often you swept.

The canoe sat on sawhorses in the middle of the floor where they had left it two days ago, after the decisions had been made and written down and put where they belonged.

That was the shape of the week now. Clarisse had seen the panel. Chiron had logged it in his office in his neat hand and put it in the flat drawer where the old surveys lived, where the siblings could get to it if they needed to. Thalia had left at three the previous afternoon for the boundary line with a sealed note from Annabeth in her pack and four days of trail mix in a sock, which was the Thalia version of packing. Nobody was re-litigating any of it. The panel was where it was. The cove was where it was. Today was for the thing with a beginning and an end.

The canoe was a sixteen-foot canvas green hull they called the pickle, on account of its color and its smell in July, and Tyson loved it with the whole of his enormous heart.

That was in the letter. Silena had read it aloud on the north beach: two weeks, and he wanted to know if the canoe was fixed, and he wanted to know about the lake. Percy had said out loud, because he needed to, that Tyson visited from the undersea forges but did not live in Cabin Three — forge, mother, nine hundred brothers-in-arms, no bunk — and Silena had said nobody thinks he is, and Clarisse had thrown a pebble at his ankle, and that had been that.

Except the canoe was not fixed. The starboard gunwale had split along three feet where somebody had beached it too hard on stone. Two ribs showed through where the canvas had worn thin. The paint had gone chalky and pale, and the thwarts were gray with weather, and the stern seat hung by one bolt and a prayer. It sat on the sawhorses like a patient animal, waiting to see if anyone meant what they said.

Percy set the bread down on the workbench, moved the tin of screws aside with his elbow, and put both hands flat on the hull.

The canvas was cool under his palms, rough with sun, with the faint give of old filler underneath. He walked his hands along the split, feeling the lift of the wood where it had sprung, the sharp edge of the crack, the soft punky spot at the end where water had gotten in and stayed. His fingers knew this kind of damage the way Annabeth's knew a bad contour line. Boats told you what they needed if you stood still long enough to let them.

"You're early," Clarisse said from the doorway, "and you're touching the boat like it's going to tell you a secret."

She came in with her braid still damp from the pool and her boots unlaced, carrying a canvas tool roll under one arm and a dented tin of sandpaper under the other. Her camp shirt had gone yellow at the collar and the sleeves were cut short with the edges rolling, cargo pants with the knees blown out and patched with a square of sailcloth Silena had sewn on crooked on purpose. A pencil sat behind her ear. Her clipboard hung from two fingers of her left hand, which meant she had already been up long enough to have opinions.

"It's a canoe," Percy said. "It's allowed one secret."

"It's allowed to be sanded." She dropped the tool roll on the bench with a clank and looked at the hull the way she looked at everything she intended to fix, which was like an opponent she respected. "Where's your girlfriend?"

"Big House. Copying the tally page for Chiron before the map kids get here." He did not say panel, because they had agreed not to leave words lying around the boathouse where any passing Stoll could pick them up and make them into a rumor. "She said to start without her and not to cut anything until she gets here."

"Wise woman." Clarisse unrolled the tools with a snap, lining up chisels by size out of a habit she would deny having. "Where's Silena?"

"Coming. She went to get the paint."

"She went to get the paint an hour ago. The paint store is a closet."

"She went to get the right paint. There's a difference and you know it."

Clarisse grunted, which from her before nine in the morning was practically singing, and bent to sight along the gunwale with one eye closed. "Thalia's hammer's still here," she said, jerking her chin at the workbench where Thalia's hammer lay beside the bread, handle worn smooth, head taped where it had loosened in August. "She left it. Said it was too heavy for four days of trees."

"She said it was too honest for four days of trees," Percy said. "I was there."

"You were standing behind Annabeth making faces."

"I was standing behind Annabeth providing moral support."

"You were making faces." Clarisse straightened and flicked sawdust off the hull with two fingers. "Day two. She's out on the line somewhere past the old quarry, probably yelling at a stone marker. Empty seat at breakfast. Argus asked where she was and Connor told him she'd joined the circus."

Percy laughed before he could stop himself, and the boathouse threw the sound back off the rafters. It felt good to say her name like that, plain and missing and not worried. They had agreed not to invent a crisis to bring her back. Four days was four days. The line needed walking. Annabeth had told her to go. That was the whole story and it was allowed to be boring.

"Map kids at nine?" he asked.

"Nine." Clarisse checked the sun through the open doors, the way she checked everything, by looking at it like it owed her money. "Olive and Marcus. Annabeth said Olive asked three times if she could help and Marcus asked once and meant it more. No Stolls. I made that a condition."

"You made a lot of conditions."

"I make conditions the way you make faces." She tossed him a block wrapped in sandpaper, underhand, fast. He caught it. "Start on the port side. With the grain. Not against. If I see swirls, I'm taking the block away and giving you rope to coil."

"That's fair," Percy said, and climbed onto the low stool, and set the block to the hull, and began.

The first pass caught and squeaked. The second found the rhythm. Dust came up pale green and settled on his forearms, in the creases of his knuckles, along the frayed edge of his sleeve. The smell rose, dry and chalky, old paint giving way to the gray beneath. Out on the lake a fish jumped with a flat smack and was gone. Somewhere up the hill somebody was hammering at the forge in short bursts, and the dining pavilion was clanking through breakfast cleanup, and the whole camp smelled faintly of bacon and pine.

He worked the length of the thwart shadow, breathing through his mouth so he would not sneeze, and thought about his brother's hands.

Tyson's hands were the size of dinner hams. They could bend a horseshoe and could also fold a paper boat so small it fit in a shell. Last July he had sat on the north beach with this exact canoe pulled up beside him and run one huge finger along the split gunwale and frowned the way he frowned at the forge when a weld was lying to him, and said, in his deep surf voice, BROTHER, THIS BOAT IS TIRED, and Percy had said that they'd fix it, and Tyson had nodded with his whole upper body, the way he did, and said, WHEN I COME BACK WE GO OUT IN IT. YOU STEER. I BRING PEANUT BUTTER. And Percy had carried that sentence around for six weeks like a stone in his pocket, smooth from handling.

The doors darkened. Silena came in sideways with a paint can in each hand and a paper bag hooked over one wrist, hair in a haphazard bun with a paint stirrer stuck through it like a pin.

"Don't start," she said, before either of them could speak. "I know I'm late. The closet was locked. Then it was the wrong green. Then it was the right green but somebody had written ORANGE? on the lid in marker, and I had to open it to make sure it wasn't orange, and it wasn't, and now I know, and you both owe me bread."

She set the cans down with a clang and shook her hair back into place. Her camp shirt was tied at the waist over her swimsuit, sleeves pushed to the elbow, a smear of lemon oil on one cuff, pockets full of the things she always carried. Bare feet in worn sandals gone dark with water.

"Olive's on her way," she said, tearing the bread bag open with her teeth. "She ran ahead. Marcus is carrying the rope bucket because Olive said the rope bucket is heavy and Marcus said nothing and picked it up, which is Marcus for yes."

Clarisse took bread without thanks, which from her was thanks. Percy took bread and got sawdust in it and ate it anyway.

"Thalia's seat empty?" Silena asked, quieter, looking at the hammer.

"Empty," Percy said. "Argus noticed."

"Good for Argus." She picked up the hammer, weighed it in her hand, set it back down exactly where it had been, handle parallel to the bench edge. "Four days of trees. She'll come back with bark in her boots and opinions about granite."

"She already has opinions about granite," Clarisse said.

"She'll have more." Silena smiled, and it was the warm one, the one that made the boathouse feel smaller in a good way. "Percy. Move over. You're hogging the good dust."

He moved over. She pulled up the other stool, took the second block, and set to work beside him shoulder to shoulder, and the two blocks moved in opposite directions, squeak and hush, squeak and hush, and the dust came up and settled on both of them, and the morning got properly underway.

---

Silena knew the exact moment a boat stopped being a job and started being a person.

It happened about twenty minutes into sanding, when her shoulders had gone warm and her forearms were powdered pale green to the elbow and the hull under her block had gone from chalky to smooth in a long pale band that showed the grain of the canvas beneath. She ran her palm over it, feeling for swirls, for ridges, for the tiny raised blisters where old filler had let go, and the boat gave back the particular smoothness of a thing that had decided to let you help it. The dust sat in the lines of her hands. Her bun had slipped sideways. The stirrer she had stuck through it was gone, probably on the floor, probably going to be found by somebody's foot.

She liked this part. She had always liked the quiet part, the oiling and the sanding and the measuring twice, the work that did not need to be brave to matter. Clarisse was the brave one. Percy was the water one. Thalia was the sky one, gone now for four days to walk a line of old stones. Annabeth was the map one, coming down the hill with a notebook full of other people's attention. Silena was the hands one. She had decided that at eleven, lying back in the pool with a life preserver under her head, and she had never found a reason to resign.

"Olive!" Percy called, lifting his chin at the doorway without stopping his block. "You made it before the dust settled."

Olive Nakamura came into the boathouse like a question that had decided to be an exclamation. Ten years old, small for ten, with a red notebook clutched to her chest in both arms and a pencil stuck behind her ear and another in her pocket and a third, Silena suspected, somewhere she had forgotten. Her camp shirt was two sizes too big, sleeves rolled to the elbow and already dusty, hem hanging to her knees over shorts gone white at the seams, sneakers with mismatched laces, one blue, one white. Her hair was pulled back in two uneven braids her mother had done that morning, one thicker than the other, with a paint smear on her left cheek she had not yet noticed.

Behind her came Marcus, fifteen and tall and quiet, with the rope bucket in one hand and a canvas bag of brushes in the other. His shirt was faded to the color of dishwater, sleeves cut off clean with a knife, showing rope-burn scars on both forearms he never explained. Cargo shorts with every pocket full, boots worn soft at the ankle, hair cropped short enough to show the pale line where goggles had sat all summer at the forge. He set the bucket down without a word and nodded once at Clarisse, who nodded back, which for those two was a whole conversation.

"Okay," Olive said, breathless, eyes huge on the canoe. "Okay. Annabeth said we get to help fix the Tyson canoe. She said Tyson is your brother who visits from the forges and does not live in Cabin Three but loves this boat. She said we have to listen to Clarisse about tools and Silena about paint and Percy about water and Marcus about — Marcus, what are you about?"

"Rope," Marcus said.

"Marcus is about rope," Olive announced, delighted. "I'm about noticing. Annabeth said noticing is a job. I brought my notebook. I already drew the boathouse. Do you want to see? I drew the way it smells."

Silena set her block down and held out her hand. "I want to see."

Olive opened the red notebook. The page showed the boathouse in brown and green pencil, the canoe on sawhorses, three figures with dust on their arms, and around it all, in wavy lines, the smell: varnish and rope and lake.

"That's the doors breathing out," Silena said. "Can I show you something about sanding?"

Olive nodded so hard both braids bounced. Silena patted the stool beside her. Olive climbed up, knees tucked under her, notebook set carefully on the bench away from dust, and Silena put the spare block in her hands and closed her own hands over the girl's to show her the angle.

"Flat," she said. "Not tipped. Let the paper do it. With the grain, toward the stern. Like you're petting a cat that only likes one direction."

"I know that cat," Olive said solemnly, and pushed. The block squeaked. Dust bloomed. Her face lit up like someone had turned a lamp on inside her. "I'm doing it! Marcus, I'm doing the boat!"

Marcus, who had already knelt by the rope bucket and begun sorting coils with the economy of a boy who had spent a summer at the forge, glanced up and gave her the smallest smile in the world. "Good," he said. "Even."

"Even," Olive repeated, and pushed again, tongue between her teeth.

Percy leaned around Silena to grin at the girl. "You're hired," he said. "Pay is bread."

"Pay is bread," Olive agreed, "and Annabeth said pay is also learning where the ribs are. Where are the ribs?"

Silena lifted the girl's hand and laid it flat on the hull where the canvas had worn thin and the wooden rib showed through in a pale curve. "Feel that? That's the skeleton. The canvas is the skin. We're going to patch the skin and paint it so the water can't get in."

Olive felt it with her whole palm, brow furrowed, mouth open a little. "It's warm," she said, surprised. "The boat is warm."

"It's been in the sun," Silena said. "Boats hold heat. Feel the inside. That's cooler."

Olive leaned over the gunwale and pressed her hand to the inner hull. "Cold! Why?"

"Shade," Marcus said, without looking up. "And water memory."

"Boats remember water?"

"Everything remembers water," Marcus said, ears pink.

Silena caught Percy's eye over the girl's head. He was doing the face, the one where he looked at the water like it had cheated at a game, only now he was looking at a fifteen-year-old boy like the boy had said something true on purpose by accident. She kicked his ankle lightly under the sawhorse.

"Marcus is right," she said. "Wood remembers every lake it's been in. That's why we rinse with fresh. This lake is freshwater, sweet water, and it treats canvas kind if you let it dry right." She tapped the thin spot. "This patch goes here. Percy, show her the patch cloth while I mix filler?"

Percy set his block down and pulled the folded canvas patch from the bench, shaking it out one-handed. The cloth was pale and stiff, smelling of sizing. "This goes over the thin spot like a bandage. We glue it, smooth it, paint it. The best patch is the one you forget."

"Like Annabeth's map corrections," Olive said. "She said the best correction is the one nobody notices because the map was always right."

"Exactly like that," Percy said, and Silena watched him file the sentence away the way he filed all of Annabeth's sentences, like letters he intended to reread.

Clarisse, who had been sighting along the split gunwale with a steel rule, cleared her throat in the way that meant instruction was coming. "Olive. Come here. I'm going to show you the crack and then you're going to go back to sanding, because sanding is what ten-year-olds are for."

"I'm ten and a half," Olive said, sliding off the stool with great dignity.

"You're sanding," Clarisse said, "and you're going to look at this crack first so you know what the sanding is for."

Olive came and stood where Clarisse pointed, hands behind her back the way Annabeth had taught her map kids to stand around other people's work. Clarisse laid the rule along the split and showed her the lift, the punky end, the bright wood inside where Percy had already cleaned it with a knife.

"See the dark?" Clarisse said, blunt as a hammer. "That's rot. Water staying where it shouldn't. We cut that out. See the bright? That's what we keep. You don't argue with rot."

"I won't," Olive breathed, eyes wide. "Can I hold the rule?"

"No. You're sanding." But Clarisse let the girl sight along the rule with one eye closed, the way she had done herself, and Olive stood on tiptoe with her tongue out and sighted, and Marcus looked up from his rope and watched with an expression Silena could not read and did not try to, because fifteen-year-old boys at the forge had their own weather.

The doors darkened again. Annabeth came in with her notebook under her arm and a folded paper in her pocket and sawdust already on her shoes from just walking in.

"Sorry," she said. "Chiron wanted the copy. Olive, did you start without me?"

"We started without cutting," Olive said importantly. "Percy said no cutting until you get here. Clarisse said you were wise. Silena showed me ribs. Marcus is about rope."

Annabeth's mouth did the thing it did when she was trying not to smile and failing in a precise way. She wore the gray camp shirt with real pockets she had been living in all week, sleeves shoved past the elbows, hem untucked on one side, over shorts with a permanent white line of dust at the hem and white scuffed sneakers double-knotted. Her ponytail was coming loose. Her pencil was behind her ear. The folded paper in her pocket was the tally copy, going to Chiron's flat drawer to live beside the panel where the siblings could get to it. Settled. Not for today.

"Good report," Annabeth said. "Marcus, show me the rope."

Marcus held up a coil without standing, turning it so she could see the splice. "Bow line's frayed. Stern line's good. Painter needs whipping. I can do it."

"You can do it," Annabeth agreed, crouching to look, knees cracking faintly. "Whip the painter first. Olive, keep sanding. You're doing the most important job."

"She tells everyone that," Clarisse muttered, just loud enough to hear, "and they all believe her, which is why her map has eleven students."

"It's true every time," Annabeth said, without looking up. "That's why it works."

Silena laughed, and picked up her block, and the boathouse settled into its rhythm: squeak and hush, the soft slap of rope on wood, Clarisse's steel rule ticking against the gunwale, Olive narrating her own sanding under her breath — "even, even, even, like petting the cat" — and outside the doors the lake lying flat and green in the morning sun, freshwater lapping at the ramp, waiting for whatever they made to come and try it.

---

Clarisse hated cutting out rot because rot argued.

She had the chisel in her right hand and the split gunwale clamped between two blocks of scrap, and the wood under the edge gave in the exact spongy way she had known it would, dark fibers crumbling off in damp flakes that stuck to her wrists. Good wood rang when you tapped it. Rot thudded. She tapped, listened, moved the chisel a quarter inch, tapped again. The boathouse had gone quieter around her without her noticing, the way it did when she was working and people had the sense to let her.

"Hold it," she said, without looking up.

Percy's hands came down on the gunwale, steadying it. His knuckles were dusted green-white. A thin line of sweat ran down his temple and he wiped it on his shoulder, missing most of it. He held without fidgeting, which he had learned to do the summer he turned fourteen, and Clarisse filed that under things she would never tell him she had noticed.

"Tap," Olive whispered, from her stool. She had stopped sanding to watch, block frozen mid-stroke. "She's listening to the wood."

"I'm listening for the lie," Clarisse said. "Rot lies. It looks like wood until you lean on it." She set the chisel angle with her thumb, glanced once at Marcus to make sure he was not about to offer help — he wasn't; he had learned in June that Clarisse with a chisel was a closed temple — and drove the cut.

The chisel went in clean. A curl of dark wood came up, then another, smelling of mildew and old lake. Underneath showed bright, pale, honest grain. Clarisse grunted, satisfied despite herself, and pared back another inch, then another, until the whole punky end lay on the bench in damp crumbs and the gunwale showed a clean notch three inches long, bright as a pulled tooth.

"There," she said. "That's the worst of it. Percy, let go. Olive, come look, then go wash your hands, because rot gets in your nails."

Olive scrambled down and peered into the notch with both hands behind her back. "It looks like a missing tooth."

"It is a missing tooth. We're making a new tooth." Clarisse picked up the dutchman she had cut that morning from a scrap of ash, fitted it into the notch, tapped it once with the mallet. It sat proud by a hair. She marked the high spot with her pencil, lifted it out, pared the mark with two strokes. "Marcus. Whipping done?"

"Painter's done," Marcus said from his corner. He held up the rope end, where the whipping sat in neat white turns, waxed twine pulled tight, ends trimmed and melted. "Bow line next. Needs back-splice."

"Show Olive the whipping when she's washed," Clarisse said. "She's allowed to touch that one."

"Okay," Marcus said, ears pink again, and Olive beamed like she had been given a medal.

Annabeth came to lean on the sawhorse beside Clarisse, notebook closed, pencil down, which meant she was off duty from measuring and on duty as a sister. "Nice cut," she said.

"It's a cut," Clarisse said. "Don't make it weird."

"I'm not making it weird. I'm noticing it." Annabeth bumped her shoulder lightly against Clarisse's arm, the way she did now when Thalia wasn't there to do it, a small borrowed gesture that Clarisse pretended not to keep track of. "Thalia would say you show off with sharp tools."

"Thalia shows off with a spear," Clarisse said. "I work."

"Thalia's walking a line of old stones and thinking about granite," Annabeth said. "She left me a whole paragraph about granite in her pack list. Granite doesn't move, markers do, people do, granite stays. It was very Thalia."

"Day two," Clarisse said. "She's past the quarry by now. Probably yelling at a cairn."

"Probably winning," Annabeth said, and they both smiled, briefly, the Grace-sister-shaped hole in the morning acknowledged and left open, the way you leave a door open for someone who is coming back.

Silena, mixing filler at the bench, said, "Tell them the rule, Clarisse."

Clarisse eyed the notch. "You don't argue with rot. You take it out. Happy?"

"Very happy," Olive said. "Annabeth, can lessons go on the map?"

"Lessons go in the margin," Annabeth said. "That's what margins are for."

Marcus brought the whipped painter over for Olive to touch, and Olive ran one finger reverently along the turns. "It's so even," she whispered. "How do you make it even?"

"Count," Marcus said. "Twelve turns. Pull each one same. Don't hurry the last two."

"Twelve," Olive repeated. "I can count twelve."

"You can count thirty-nine," Percy said lightly, and then glanced at Annabeth, and Annabeth glanced at the pocket with the folded tally copy, and the moment passed through the boathouse like a cloud shadow, there and gone. The panel sat in Chiron's flat drawer. The copy sat in Annabeth's pocket. Nobody was digging today. The words settled back into sawdust.

Clarisse fitted the dutchman again. It sat flush. She nodded once, reached for the glue pot Silena had warming on the bench, and began to brush glue into the notch with short hard strokes. The glue smelled hot and animal, sharp enough to make Olive wrinkle her nose.

"Stinks," Olive announced.

"Stinks means sticks," Clarisse said. "Hold this." She handed Olive the mallet, which was nearly as big as the girl's forearm. Olive held it with both hands, solemn as an acolyte. "When I say, you tap. Not bang. Tap. Like you're knocking on a door you respect."

"Like the Big House door," Olive whispered.

"Like any door," Clarisse said. She set the tooth, aligned the grain with her thumb, checked the line by eye. "Now."

Olive tapped. The tooth went home with a soft thunk. Glue squeezed out in a thin bead. Clarisse wiped it with her finger, quick, before it skinned, and Olive tapped once more for good measure, unasked, and Clarisse let her, because the second tap was straight.

"Good," Clarisse said. "You're hired for teeth."

Olive glowed. Marcus, back at his bucket, said, "Good taps," without looking up, and Olive glowed harder.

Percy, still holding the patch cloth in place while the glue tacked, watched his sister wipe glue off her thumb on her cargo pants, adding to the constellation of stains already there — paint, tar, bread, lake mud — and thought about saying something and decided against it, and then said it anyway, because he was fourteen and stupid in the specific way of brothers.

"You're good at this," he said.

Clarisse did not look up. "I'm good at everything."

"You're good at teaching it," he said. "You didn't used to be."

"I used to throw things at people who held wrong."

"You threw a clamp at me in June."

"It was a small clamp." She sighted along the repair, ran her thumb over the joint, found it true. "And you held wrong."

Silena, stirring filler, said mildly, "He holds right now."

"He holds adequate," Clarisse said. "Don't push it."

But she left her hand on the gunwale a moment longer than she needed to, flat on the new tooth, feeling the joint, and Percy saw it, and did not say anything, because some things in Cabin Three were said by not saying them, and this was one.

The morning wore on toward ten. The filler went on white and smooth. The patch cloth went down over the thin spot, pressed and smoothed. Marcus finished the bow line splice. Olive sanded until her arms were white to the shoulder, whispering — "even, even, tooth, even" — and Annabeth handed Clarisse the right wrench without being asked, twice, which meant thanks.

It was good. It was the kind of good that made Clarisse's chest feel tight in a way she had no intention of examining, so she examined the rope instead.

Until Percy said, from inside the hull where he was smoothing patch edges with his thumb, "He asked about the lake."

Clarisse's hands stopped on the coil. "What?"

"Tyson. In the letter." Percy did not look up. His thumb moved in small circles, pressing the cloth into filler. "Silena read the first part. There was a second part. He wants to know about the lake. He's been asking for a month. He says the forges hear things. Currents wrong. Metal cold. He wants to know if our lake is —" He paused, searching for the word in the letter. "If it's behaving."

The boathouse went quiet in the way it went when someone set down something heavy. Olive stopped sanding. Marcus's hands stilled on the rope. Silena's stirring slowed. Annabeth closed her notebook with a soft snap.

Clarisse set the coil down very carefully, the way she set down anything she wanted to throw.

"He visits," she said. Her voice came out flatter than she meant. "He has a forge. He has a mother. He has nine hundred brothers with one eye. He does not live here. He sleeps in the corner bunk for three weeks in July and makes the bed every morning and leaves a rock like an egg, and then he goes home. That's the deal. That's always been the deal."

"I know," Percy said. He sat back on his heels inside the hull, filler on his hands to the wrist, dust in his hair. "I said that. On the beach. I'm saying it again. He's not moving into Cabin Three. Triton doesn't either. They visit. They have a sea. We have a lake. Triton said so over tea he didn't like." He looked at her directly, which he did not always do, because looking at Clarisse directly was like looking at weather. "But he's coming in two weeks. And he's asking about the lake. And I don't know what to tell him."

Clarisse picked up the mallet Olive had set down. She turned it over, feeling the weight. She thought about the ledger she had kept all spring and retired in August — reassessing her policy, she had written across the top — because a ledger could not hold people. It sat in her footlocker under her spare shirt. She still knew every line in it.

"Tell him the truth," she said.

"Which is?"

"That the lake is freshwater and the canoe is getting fixed and his brother is an idiot who sands against the grain when he thinks nobody's looking." She pointed the mallet at him. "And that we're here. That's the truth. The rest —" She jerked her chin hillward, toward the Big House, toward the flat drawer, toward everything they had agreed not to leave lying around. "The rest goes in the drawer until Chiron says otherwise. Tyson gets the lake he can swim in. Not the — the other thing. Not until we know what it is."

Percy nodded slowly. "Okay."

"Okay?" She narrowed her eyes. "Just okay? No argument? No face?"

"I'm doing a face," he admitted. "I'm doing it on the inside."

"Keep it inside." But her voice had lost the edge, and she set the mallet down and came around the sawhorse and stood over him, looking down into the hull where he knelt among patch cloth and filler pots. "Jackson."

"Yeah?"

"You think I'm scared you're going to leave."

He went very still. Olive looked at Silena. Silena looked at the filler. Marcus looked at the rope with great concentration. Annabeth stayed leaning on the sawhorse, pencil down, watching with the flat attention she gave to things that mattered.

"I didn't say that," Percy said.

"You didn't have to. You've got a brother under the sea and a girlfriend with a map and a lake that talks to you, and every time one of them tugs you look like you're measuring the rope." Clarisse's voice was blunt as ever, grudging as ever, but underneath it was something she would have cut out like rot if she could. "So listen. You're fourteen. You're my brother. You're annoying and you hold adequate and you eat bread with sawdust in it. If you go somewhere, you come back. That's the rule now. Not the ledger. Just that. You come back and you tell me where you went. That's all I need."

Percy stared up at her with filler drying on his hands and dust in his eyebrows. "That's — that's a pretty good rule."

"It's the only one I've got." She picked up her steel rule and smacked it lightly against his shin, not hard, the Clarisse version of a hug. "Now get out of the hull. You're making the patch nervous."

He got out, grinning despite himself, and Olive, who had been holding her breath, let it out in a rush and said, "Is that a margin lesson?" and Annabeth said, "That's the whole map," and Silena laughed with flour-filler on her nose, and Marcus tied off his whipping with a small hard knot and cut the end, and the boathouse breathed again.

Clarisse went back to her coil, hands moving in the old rhythm, and did not look at anyone for a while, because she had said the true thing and the float was over and nobody was allowed to ask.

Annabeth could tell the temperature of paint by the way it moved off a stick.

She stood at the workbench with the green can open and the stirrer in her right hand, watching the paint fold over itself in slow ribbons, and decided it was too thick for the heat. Morning had gone to late morning while they worked, the sun standing higher through the doors, the plank of light on the floor gone from gold to white. Outside the lake threw back the sky in hard flashes. The air in the boathouse had gone warm and close, smelling of glue and dust and the sharp mineral tang of fresh paint. Her sleeves were damp at the cuffs where she had wiped sweat without noticing. Her ponytail had come fully loose and hung down her back in a way Thalia would have called a hazard and fixed with one yank.

"Thin it," Silena said, without looking up from where she was feathering filler along the patch edge with a putty knife. "Ten percent. The heat's going to skin it otherwise."

"Twelve," Annabeth said. "The canvas will drink it."

"Twelve," Silena agreed, "and you owe me a margin note."

Annabeth measured the thinner in the cap the way she measured everything, by eye and then by check, poured, stirred, watched the ribbons go loose and glossy. The green was right — not the pickle green of before, faded and chalky, but the deep lake green Tyson had pointed at in July, running his huge finger along the hull and saying GREEN LIKE HOME, GREEN LIKE OUTSIDE THE FORGE WINDOWS WHERE THE WATER IS DEEP. She had written it down on the spot in her blue notebook: TYSON GREEN — DEEP WINDOW WATER. Now the can said it back to her.

Olive climbed onto the bench beside her, knees drawn up, red notebook open to a fresh page. "Can I watch the paint?"

"You can do more than watch," Annabeth said. "You can be the second pair of eyes. Every painter needs one. Your job is to stand on the other side and tell me where I miss, where it runs, where it's too thin. Cartographers make good paint-checkers because we notice."

Olive straightened with the gravity of a ten-year-old given a title. "I notice everything. Marcus says I notice too much."

"Marcus is wrong," Marcus said from his rope corner, without looking up. "You notice enough."

Olive beamed and took up her post on the far side of the canoe, hands behind her back, eyes huge. "Ready."

"Rule one," Annabeth said, pouring paint into the tray with a slow glug. "Thin coats. Two thin coats beats one thick coat the way two measurements beat one guess. Rule two, we go with the length, bow to stern, no scrubbing. Rule three, if you see a run, you say so right away, no being polite about it. Polite ruins paint."

"Impolite saves paint," Olive repeated, committing it to memory. "Got it."

Percy, sanding the stern seat on the floor with his legs stretched out in front of him, said, "She's going to put that on the map."

"Margins," Annabeth and Olive said together, and Silena laughed, and even Clarisse made the noise that meant she was laughing on the inside.

Annabeth loaded the roller, rolled it out on the tray ribs until it was even, and laid the first stroke along the port side. The paint went down glossy and dark, covering the pale sanded band in one clean pass, the roller whispering over canvas. The smell bloomed, sharp and sweet. She worked bow to stern in overlapping lengths, breathing through her mouth, eyes moving ahead of the roller to catch holidays and bubbles.

"There," Olive said, pointing. "Bubble. Left. No, your left. There."

"Good," Annabeth said, and tipped it out with the corner of the brush. "Again."

They fell into it. Roll, check, tip, roll. The green spread along the hull like water over sand. She had tied her hair back with a strip of rag Silena handed her without asking. Paint speckled her forearms. A drop landed on her sneaker, and she left it.

"Annabeth," Olive said, after a while, quieter, in the voice she used for real questions. "Is Thalia coming back for the paint?"

Annabeth's roller paused mid-stroke. "She's on the boundary line until Friday. Four days of trees and stones. She left her hammer because it was too heavy. She left me a list that says GRANITE DOESN'T MOVE in capital letters."

"Do you miss her?"

It was the kind of question only a ten-year-old could ask straight, and only a fourteen-year-old with an eighteen-year-old sister gone for four days could answer straight.

"Yes," Annabeth said. "She checks me thirty-one times a day and I told her to stop and she did, and now she checks me zero times a day and I keep looking up to be checked. It's stupid."

"It's not stupid," Olive said. "When my mom goes to the forge to argue with the foreman, I keep listening for her shoes."

"Exactly like that," Annabeth said. "Thalia's shoes are very loud. She walks like she's arguing with the ground."

Olive giggled. Marcus, coiling finished line, said, "She walks like she wins," and Annabeth pointed her brush at him.

"That's better. Write that in the margin, Olive. Thalia walks like she wins."

Olive wrote it in her red notebook in careful round letters: TALIA WALKS LIKE SHE WINS, and then, catching the spelling, added the H with a caret and a look of great satisfaction.

Silena, working filler on the starboard side, said, "Tell them the measuring rule."

"Measure until the wood agrees," Annabeth said. "The wood doesn't care how many times you think you measured. Clarisse taught me that."

"Measure until the wood agrees," Olive repeated, writing. "Marcus, write that down."

Marcus pulled a folded square of paper from his pocket and wrote it down, and Olive nodded like a foreman.

Percy, from the floor, said, "Chase, you're a lot."

"I am a productive amount," Annabeth said automatically, and the boathouse laughed, because they all knew that line now, the way you know a family's lines, and Olive wrote PRODUCTIVE AMOUNT in her book and underlined it twice.

The first coat went on. Annabeth stepped back, roller dripping, and surveyed with her head tipped, the pose she hit when the world was being recalcitrant. A few holidays near the stem. One run starting under the gunwale. She tipped the run, touched up the holidays, handed the roller to Silena for the starboard side.

"Your turn," she said. "Even hands."

Silena took it, tested the weight, laid a stroke as clean as Annabeth's. They worked from opposite ends toward the middle, not speaking, the way sisters work when they have done it before. Paint smell, roller whisper, Olive calling "good, good, tiny bubble, good" like a small lighthouse.

Clarisse, fitting the repaired gunwale tooth with a clamp, watched them with the steel rule in her teeth and said around it, "Don't drip on my tooth."

"We would never drip on your tooth," Silena said.

"I'd drip on your tooth," Percy said from the floor, and Clarisse threw a rag at him, and he caught it, and wiped his hands, and went back to sanding the stern seat with exaggerated care, tongue between his teeth.

Annabeth crouched to check the waterline. The green lay wet and deep, reflecting the doors. It was good.

"How long?" Olive said.

"Till this afternoon for touch," Annabeth said. "Tomorrow for water. Tyson gets it in two weeks, which is plenty. Paint needs to cure. Like bread needs to cool. You don't cut it early."

"Everything is like bread with you people," Marcus observed.

"Everything is like bread," Silena said. "That's Cabin Three theology."

Annabeth stood, wiped her hands on a rag that was already green, and looked at the hull with the particular satisfaction of a person who had put a true thing on a surface and watched it hold. Then she looked at Olive's notebook, open to the page where the girl had drawn the canoe in green pencil, with wavy smell lines and the words TOOTH and EVEN and TALIA WALKS LIKE SHE WINS in the margin.

"Can I copy that?" Annabeth asked. "For the Big House wall. The map needs a canoe in the margin. Every good map has something alive in the corner."

Olive stared. "My drawing? On your map?"

"Your drawing. Credited. O. Nakamura, age ten and a half, boathouse survey."

Olive's mouth opened. No sound came out. Then she closed it, opened her notebook flat, and pushed it toward Annabeth with both hands, the way you hand over something precious. "Yes," she whispered. "Yes, please."

Annabeth copied the canoe in quick clean lines on the back of a paint lid with a pencil stub, initialed it O.N. in the corner, and Olive watched with her chin in her hands and her feet swinging, and Marcus watched from his rope with the smallest smile, and the paint dried a shade darker, and the morning tipped toward noon.

Percy had paint in his eyebrows and did not know it until Annabeth told him.

They had carried the canoe out into the sun to tack, six hands under the hull, Marcus calling the lift, Olive walking backward shouting "clear" until Clarisse told her to just walk. The grass by the ramp was warm. The lake lay out beyond it, afternoon riffle coming up, freshwater smell sharpening with sun. They set the pickle down on pads where the sun could get at it, green hull gleaming, and stepped back.

"Lunch," Silena announced, producing the rest of the bread and a paper bag of apples from the boathouse bench like a magician. "Shade. Water. No one sits on the boat."

They sat in the shade under the eaves with their backs against the warm boards, passing bread and apples. Olive sat between Marcus and Annabeth, braids powdered, a second paint smear joined the first on her chin. Marcus turned his apple to find the unbruised side. Clarisse drank from the jug and handed it to Percy. Silena closed her eyes for exactly one minute.

Percy sat with his knees up, dust in his cuffs, filler under his nails, paint on his forearms. He ate his apple down to the core and then ate part of the core.

"You've got paint," Annabeth said beside him, "in your eyebrows."

"That's a look," he said. "That's the artisan look."

"That's the idiot look." But she licked her thumb and wiped at his brow before he could duck. Her fingers were cool. Her gray shirt had a green thumbprint on the pocket flap she had not noticed. A strand of hair stuck to her neck with sweat.

"Leave it," he said. "The thumbprint. It suits the shirt."

"It's unprofessional."

"It's cartographic. It says you've been places."

She snorted, which was the Annabeth version of laughing when she was tired, and leaned her shoulder against his, just for a second, just the weight of it, and then straightened because Olive was watching with great interest and taking notes.

It was after lunch, in the loose hour when paint tacks and nobody is allowed to touch it, that Percy found the feather.

He had gone back to the stern seat, which lay on the bench waiting for its bolt, to sand the last rough spot under the lip where hands would grip. He ran his thumb along the underside, feeling for splinters, and his fingertip met something soft wedged in the joint where the seat met its support — not wood, not filler. He worked it free.

A single gray feather, long as his hand, downy at the base, vane a little ragged. Heron gray. The exact gray of the bird that had stood on the sunken skiff and watched them.

He turned it over once. The boathouse was quiet. Nobody was looking. No heron had been in the boathouse. No heron could have gotten under a bolted seat.

He slipped it into his pocket beside the pebble and the pencil stub, without a word, without breaking rhythm, and picked up his sandpaper again. Not for today. The panel sat in Chiron's drawer. The copy sat in Annabeth's pocket. The lake kept what it kept. Today was for hands.

But his thumb rested a moment on the pocket where the feather lay, through cloth, feeling the soft give of vane against pebble, and he thought, not yet, the way the lake had said it, and went back to work.

At three the heat broke a little. Clouds came up in a thin sheet. The breeze steadied out of the west, ruffling the lake into green chop. The paint had gone from wet-gloss to satin tack. Silena tested it under the gunwale, nodded. "Second coat at four," she said. "Thin. Then we cover it for the night."

"North beach," Annabeth said quietly, beside Percy, while the others were arguing about thinner. "Ten minutes. The paint doesn't need us for ten minutes."

He looked at her. Notebook closed, pencil down, hands paint-flecked and empty. Her eyes were tired the way they got when she had been precise all morning.

"Yeah," he said.

They slipped away without announcement — down the reed path past the canoe launch, around the point where the willows started, to the curve of sand and warm stone they called the north beach because it faced north and because it was theirs.

The Poseidon cabin's private spot. Everybody knew it and nobody went there without asking, which was the kind of rule that only worked because everyone wanted it to.

Afternoon light lay low across the water, turning the chop to beaten silver-green. The stones were warm. The sand was tracked with gull feet and one set of small bare feet from that morning, already softening. Freshwater lapped in small clear rushes, hissing on sand, withdrawing. The air smelled of sun-warmed rock and the faint sweet rot of late summer.

Annabeth sat on the flat stone with her knees drawn up, shoes off, toes in sand. Percy sat beside her, shoulders touching, shoes off too. They did not speak for a while.

Up the hill the forge hammered in short bursts, stopped, hammered again.

"You did good today," Annabeth said at last, looking out at the water. "With Clarisse. The — the rule. Coming back and telling her."

Percy picked up a flat pebble, turned it over, set it down. "She said it. I just listened."

"You listen well. That's rare." She pulled grass up by the roots, one blade at a time. "She's scared you'll leave. Not to the sea. Just — leave. Grow past. The way Thalia's scared I'll stop needing her. Same fear, different shoes."

"Are you going to stop needing her?" he asked.

"No. I'm going to need her differently. Ask first. Walk next to me. That's free forever." Annabeth's voice went precise, then softened. "Clarisse doesn't have a rock. She has a clipboard. She holds by fixing. Teeth. Ropes. Splints. You."

"Me?"

"You most." She looked at him then, directly, gray eyes clear. "You're her little brother who came late and learned fast and might go where she can't follow. Of course she's scared. I'd be scared."

He was quiet. The water hissed in, withdrew. His hand found hers on the warm stone, paint-flecked fingers lacing without hurry.

"I choose it," he said. "Every day. The cabin. The sisters. You. I told you that in the cove. I meant it on land too."

"I know." She turned her hand over, palm to palm, thumb along his knuckles where filler sat white in the creases. "Say the other part."

"What other part?"

"The part where you're allowed to be happy about Tyson out loud without qualifying everything. Silena told me what she told you. On the beach. About the bread."

Percy laughed, surprised. "She tells everyone everything."

"She tells people what they need in the order they can hear it." Annabeth leaned her head against his shoulder, just for a moment. "So be happy out loud. He's coming in two weeks. He visits, he doesn't live here, we all know the deal. But he's coming, and he loves that stupid green boat, and we're fixing it for him. That's allowed to be the whole story for ten minutes."

"It is a stupid green boat," Percy agreed, and she laughed into his shoulder, and he put his arm around her, carefully, the way you put your arm around someone on a warm stone with the whole lake watching and no one watching at all.

They sat like that while the light moved. His chin rested against her hair. Her hand stayed in his. It was not a kiss. It was the thing before and after kisses, the holding that made kisses mean something.

After a while she sat up, though she left her hand where it was, and said, "Kiss me before we go back. The paint needs us, but not yet."

So he did. It was a quiet kiss, sun-warm and paint-smelling, brief and certain. Her hand came up to his cheek, and his thumb rested at her temple the way it had in the cove, and the lake hissed on sand, and then they broke apart and stayed with foreheads together, and she smiled with her eyes closed.

"Okay," she said. "Now the paint needs us."

"Obviously," he said, and she laughed, and they stood, brushed sand off, picked up shoes, and went back up the reed path hand in hand until the boathouse came in sight, when they let go by mutual unspoken agreement, because Clarisse would throw something.

At the last reed-clump before the open grass, Annabeth stopped him with two fingers on his cuff and slipped a small folded square of notebook paper into his pocket — quick, practiced, like a cartographer filing a correction.

"What's this?" he asked.

"Read it later," she said. "Not at dinner. Later. When the paint's done and nobody needs your hands."

"Is it a map?"

"It's a note." Her ears went pink. "It's just a note. Don't make a face."

He didn't make a face. He put his hand over the pocket, feeling the square of paper through cloth, and nodded once, solemn as an oath, and she nodded back, and they walked the last twenty yards apart the way siblings' rules required and lovers' patience allowed.

Behind them the north beach kept their footprints beside Silena's morning ones, and the lake took the shallows and smoothed them, patient, the way it kept everything.

Silena felt evening come the way she felt everything worth feeling, in her hands first.

The air off the lake went cool around four-thirty, just as the second coat went on. The light slanted low through the doors, turning the dust gold. The chop flattened as the breeze dropped, going from silver-green to glass-green. Up the hill the dinner bell was being argued over. The smell of evening fish came down with the first of the cool, and the frogs started in the marsh, one voice and then fifty.

She pulled the borrowed hoodie over her head without stopping her roller — Percy's old navy one, sleeves too long, smelling of lake and bread — because her camp shirt had been fine at noon and was not fine now. Early September evenings did that.

"Second coat," she said, to no one and everyone. "Thin as water. Olive, eyes."

"Eyeing," Olive said from her post, hands behind her back, paint on her chin, notebook closed at last. "Marcus, are you eyeing?"

"Eyeing," Marcus said from the stern, taping the waterline. His rope bucket sat empty and coiled, every line whipped and hung. "Port bow. Thin spot."

"Got it," Silena said, and tipped it out, and Annabeth, working from the other end, nodded without looking up, the way sisters nod when the work is going right.

They painted the second coat in the gold light, Olive calling "good, good, run, got it, good" softer now. Percy fitted the stern seat bolt by feel. Clarisse spliced the last oar leather, braid over one shoulder.

Silena watched them all while she worked, and felt the fullness of a day that had a beginning and an end.

This was the purpose. Not the panel in the drawer. Not the seam or the rings or the thirty-nine marks. Those sat in Chiron's office where the siblings could get to them, settled. Today was for the thing you could hold: canvas and ash and rope and paint, made smooth by hands that loved the person who would sit in it.

She thought about Tyson. His huge hands folding a paper boat to fit in a shell. Peanut butter on his thumb. BROTHER IS PASTA. The corner bunk made every morning, the round white rock like an egg. Visiting, not living — they all knew the deal. He had a forge and a mother and nine hundred brothers-in-arms under the sea. He did not have a Cabin Three bunk. But he had a green canoe in the sun, and six people making it true.

"Silena," Olive said, tugging her sleeve with paint-sticky fingers. "Can I do a stroke? One? Annabeth said I could when I'm eleven, but I'm ten and a half, and the boat is almost done, and I sanded even."

Silena looked at Annabeth. Annabeth looked at the hull, at the light, at the girl, and did the calculation with her head tipped. "One stroke," she said. "Stern quarter, port side, where Silena shows you. Thin. Bow to stern. No scrubbing."

Olive's face lit like a lamp. Silena knelt, put the brush in the girl's hands, closed her own hands over them to show the angle and the pressure — light, lighter, let the paint do it — and guided the single stroke along the hull. The green went down glossy, perfect, catching the low sun.

"There," Silena whispered. "That's yours. Forever. When Tyson sits in it, he'll sit beside your stroke and never know, but we'll know."

Olive stared at the stroke with her mouth open. "Forever?"

"Paint lasts," Marcus said quietly. "If you do it right. Years."

"Years," Olive breathed, and stepped back, hands clasped behind her, and did not touch anything for a full minute out of reverence, until Clarisse handed her an apple and she ate it with paint on her chin and happiness all over her.

At six the light went gold-green through the willows, too early for lamps, too late for sun. The second coat lay wet-dark and even. Silena tested tack under the gunwale. "Cover it," she said. "Tarp, loose. It sleeps in the boathouse tonight. Tomorrow we bolt the seat. Day after, water."

They tented the tarp over the hull, weighted the corners with paint cans. Percy set Thalia's hammer on the bench beside it, handle parallel to the edge. Clarisse hung her clipboard on a nail. Annabeth slipped the paint-lid canoe into her notebook. Marcus pocketed the last scrap of twine. Olive hugged her red notebook, eyes huge on the covered boat.

"It looks like a sleeping animal," she whispered.

"It is," Silena said. "We're letting it dream about water. Freshwater. Sweet water. The kind that treats canvas kind if you let it dry right."

They stood around it in the cooling boathouse, six people with paint in their hair, tired in the good way. Outside the lake lay flat and doubled, the first star coming on over the pavilion, the loon calling once and then quiet.

Percy put his arm around Clarisse without asking, and Clarisse let him. Annabeth leaned against Silena's side, head on her shoulder for three seconds, the Grace-sister lean she usually saved for Thalia, borrowed because Thalia was out past the quarry yelling at a cairn and winning. Silena squeezed her once, hard.

"Okay," Clarisse said, straightening, businesslike, because someone had to be. "Tools cleaned. Brushes in thinner. Roller covers in the bag. Olive, you carry nothing sharp. Marcus, you carry everything sharp. Percy, you carry bread, because you'll eat it otherwise. Move."

They moved. Brushes clanked in thinner. Tools rolled shut. Olive walked backward to the doors, watching the boat until the doors closed, then ran ahead up the path shouting "we fixed the tooth, we painted green, I did a stroke," and Marcus followed with the sharp things, and Annabeth with her notebook, and Clarisse with her clipboard, and Percy waited for Silena at the doors.

"You coming?" he asked.

"In a minute," she said. "Go on. Save me a seat where the fish isn't."

He went, paint in his eyebrows, feather in his pocket beside the pebble. She watched him go up the path in the dusk and thought about little brothers who are scared to be happy out loud and big sisters who are scared to stop holding and how both fears fit in one boathouse with room left over for apples.

Then she turned back, alone with the covered canoe.

She laid her palm on the tarp over the new tooth, feeling the shape of it through canvas. The hull was cool now, giving up the day's heat. It smelled of paint and glue and sun, and underneath, faintly, sweet freshwater.

"Two weeks," she said quietly. "Green like outside the forge windows. Your brother steers. You bring peanut butter. We'll be on the beach."

The tarp did not answer. Boats never did, until water. The lake lapped at the ramp, the frogs kept count, and Silena set Thalia's hammer back down exactly parallel and went up the path to dinner, leaving the canoe to dream about water.
