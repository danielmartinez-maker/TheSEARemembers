## Chapter 162: The Day With Nowhere To Be

Percy woke before the conch because the pool was already awake and thinking loudly.

It did that on warm September mornings, the indoor channel in Tempest Hall moving a little faster than it needed to, pushing its cool mineral smell up through the floor grates. Salt-rain and wet stone. He lay in his bunk a minute with his eyes open, listening to it, and to Clarisse breathing hard in the bunk across the room the way she did after early laps, and to Silena's needle already ticking faintly against a thimble by the window. Wednesday. The whole day set aside. The thought arrived with its own weather.

He sat up. He wore the faded orange camp shirt he had slept in, sleeves cut short back in June, and soft gray sweatpants gone thin at the knees. His feet found the cool tile. On the shelf above his bunk, laid out with a care that was not his own, were the day's clothes, folded square: navy swim trunks, a dry camp shirt, a clean towel, and a pale blue button-up Silena had pressed without asking, for later, when the sun went down and the wind came off the pines.

"You laid out the nice shirt," Percy said.

Silena did not look up from her mending. She was sixteen, cross-legged on her bunk in her lake-gray sleep shirt and cutoff sweats, hair in a loose nighttime braid gone fuzzy at the edges, needle flashing. "I laid out options," she said. "The nice shirt is an option. You don't have to wear it. But you'll want it at eight o'clock when you're cold and pretending you're not."

"I don't pretend I'm not cold."

"You pretend about everything thermal," Clarisse said from the pool deck doorway, blunt as a door shutting. She was fifteen, braid still wet from laps, in her cutoff practice shirt with the jagged sleeves and black swim shorts, towel around her neck, spear propped against the jamb. "Put the nice shirt in the bag. Take it out when she gets quiet and starts rubbing her arms. That's your cue, Jackson. Girls get cold first because they're smarter about noticing."

Percy pulled yesterday's jeans off the hook and stepped into them, swapping shirts with his back turned out of long cabin habit. "Good morning to you too."

"Morning." Clarisse toweled her face hard and threw the towel at the peg without looking. It landed. "Eat. Row. Swim. Eat again. Be back by full dark. That's the schedule. Silena made food for six and packed it for two, so you'll be carrying a satchel that weighs more than Annabeth."

"Seven pounds," Silena said, tying off a stitch with her teeth. "I weighed it. Two peaches, bread, cheese, apples, cold chicken, lemon water, honey squares. Plus the blanket. The blanket's extra."

Percy laced his damp sneakers. The north laundry line was empty — Tyson's forge shirt gone, the visitor towel washed and folded on its hook, the corner bunk made up tight with the round white rock still centered on the pillow where Tyson had left it Tuesday morning before the ferry. The cabin smelled faintly of forge-smoke under the lemon oil, even a day later, the way a room keeps the shape of somebody after they've gone. Percy checked his pockets out of habit: lake pebble, hair tie, the folded vellum love note from Annabeth with the cove sketch, a honey wrapper gone soft at the edges. Tools checked.

"Corner bunk's still made," he said.

"Corner bunk stays made," Clarisse said. "That's the rule now. Ping spring and all." Her voice did the flat thing it did for true things, then covered it with business. "Listen. Date rules. One, from me."

"Here we go."

"One," Clarisse repeated, holding up a finger, "you bring her back dry enough to walk. I don't care if you swim all day. Wring out before the path. Silena's blanket can't carry two wet teenagers and a picnic." She pointed at him with the whole hand. "That's logistics. Two is —" She stopped, looked at the pool, looked at Silena, looked back. "Two is you be decent. She's fourteen. You're fourteen. You know what I mean. I don't have to spell it."

"I know," Percy said quietly.

"I know you know. That's why I only say it once." She came over and straightened his collar with two flat pats, the way she had straightened Tyson's collar Sunday night, hard enough to be a shove and soft enough to be the opposite. "And three — have a good day. You earned a slow one."

Silena set her mending down and crossed to the shelf above the spear rack, where a folded square of paper leaned against the lemon-oil tin. "Thalia left this before survey," she said. "She said to give it to you at breakfast so you'd think she was here being annoying in person."

Percy unfolded it. Black block letters in Thalia's impatient hand: JACKSON — SURVEY TILL FRIDAY. DON'T SINK. DON'T BE AN IDIOT. BRING HER BACK BY STARS. IF YOU KISS HER STUPID, KISS HER SMART AFTER. — T. Underneath, smaller, in pencil: P.S. Ten minutes per occurrence WAIVED for today only. Don't tell Clarisse.

Percy grinned. "She waived it."

"I heard that," Clarisse said. "Waiver denied. Ten minutes per occurrence, retroactive, double for dates." But she was fighting a smile and losing. "Tell Grace I said her handwriting's terrible and her survey lines are worse."

"I'll tell her Friday."

Silena pressed the packed satchel into his hands — canvas, heavy, smelling of bread and lemon — and the folded quilt over that, and a tin cup hooked on the strap. Her eyes were bright and warm and perceptive, the look that had kept this cabin's arithmetic for five years. "One more thing," she said. "From me. Not a rule. A note." She tucked a loose thread back into his cuff. "You don't have to make it big. You had the notes Monday, the first walk Tuesday. Today's just — inhabit it. Eat slow. Hold her hand when there's nothing to say. That's the whole trick."

Percy bumped his shoulder against hers. "When did you get wise?"

"When you started folding like a raccoon and I had to love you anyway." She pushed him toward the door. "Go. She's waiting at the boat ramp. I told her eight. It's seven-forty and you'll be early, which she'll pretend annoys her."

He went out into the September morning with the satchel on his shoulder and the quilt under one arm, sneakers slapping the path, pines cool and resinous overhead. At the bend he looked back. Clarisse stood in the cabin doorway with her arms folded, spear over one shoulder, watching him go with the particular scowl she wore for tenderness. Silena leaned against the jamb beside her, needle already moving again. They waved him off together — Clarisse with two fingers, Silena with the whole hand — and Percy waved back with the quilt, feeling absurdly, completely looked after, the way you feel when people who tease you also pack your lunch.

---

Annabeth was at the boat ramp twenty minutes early, because she was Annabeth, and she had brought a map she had promised herself she would not bring.

She stood at the top of the ramp in the thin gold light with her bag over one shoulder and her hands in her pockets, watching the two green skiffs knock gently against the pilings. She wore sturdy olive shorts with real pockets, a dark blue one-piece swimsuit underneath for swimming, and her faded orange camp shirt loose over it, sleeves rolled past the elbows, hair rebraided tight for water. Beside her on the warm boards sat a second bag, smaller, with her pencils and her field notebook and the half-finished north-beach inset she had told herself was just ballast. The lie sat there in canvas form, obvious to everyone including her.

The lake lay flat and bright, freshwater blue with the morning shine on it, smelling of wet stone and sun-warmed pine. Up the hill the breakfast bell was still deciding whether to ring. A loon called once from the cove end and did not call again.

Percy came down the path with the sun behind him, quilt under one arm, satchel smelling of bread, hair still damp at the temples, grinning like the day had personally invited him. Annabeth felt the grin land the way it always landed — catch and keep — and stood up straighter, and tucked the map bag a fraction behind her leg.

"You're early," she said.

"You're earlier." He set the satchel down and took her hand without breaking stride, fingers lacing cold and warm, the way they had learned on the dock. "Hi."

"Hi." She held on a beat longer than strictly necessary, which was, she had decided Monday night while writing it down, allowed on Wednesdays. "What's in the satchel? It smells like Silena won an argument with a bakery."

"Seven pounds. I have the inventory." He recited, counting on his free hand: "Bread, cheese, cold chicken, two peaches, apples, lemon water, honey squares. Plus blanket. Blanket's extra." He nodded at her second bag. "What's in the ballast?"

Annabeth's ears went pink. "Nothing. Contours. I wasn't going to bring it."

"But you did."

"I did," she admitted, precise even in embarrassment. "I told myself I wouldn't map today. Then I put the notebook in anyway. It's like bringing a sword to a picnic. You feel stupid holding it and stupid leaving it."

Percy picked up the map bag and slung it over his other shoulder alongside the satchel, settling both with the ease of a boy who had carried laundry wrong for a brother all weekend and learned something about weight. "Bring it," he said simply. "If you want to draw, draw. If you don't, it's ballast. No rules today."

"No rules," Annabeth repeated, testing it. "Thalia waived the ten minutes."

"Clarisse unwaived it. Double for dates."

"Clarisse would." Annabeth took his hand again, and they walked down the ramp together into the smell of warm boards and clean green water. The good skiff waited with its cord tied the way she always tied it, because he always forgot. She checked the oars, the bailer, the painter line, out of habit — tools checked — and he loaded the satchel and the quilt and both bags with care, and they pushed off into the flat bright morning with the whole lake ahead of them and nowhere to be.

The row out was easy water. Percy rowed with the wrists the way Thalia said he did, and Annabeth sat in the bow with her knees drawn up and her cuffs showing bare ankle, watching the shoreline go by in sectors out of sheer love: canoe launch, swimming buoys, boundary rock with its white X still new enough to smell like paint if you were close. Past the rock at an easy four miles an hour, the loons calling, the lake taking the sound of the oars and keeping it.

"Where to?" Percy asked. "Cove or north beach?"

Annabeth considered the question the way she considered a contour: carefully, wanting the true line. The north beach was their rock, Cabin Three's rock, the half-moon of sand and warm flat stone where the float had happened and Tyson had said held twice. The willow cove was the green shade, the sunken skiff and the heron, the place where everything hummed low under the surface. Both were theirs. The day was whole. The choice felt deliciously, terrifyingly free.

"Cove first," she said. "Morning shade. Then beach for the hot hours. Then —" She gestured out at the lake, at the whole bright thing. "Then whatever the water decides."

"Whatever the water decides," Percy agreed, and pulled for the green.

They came up on the willow crowns by mid-morning, and the cove took them the way it always took boats: slow, in long broken sheets of green-gold through the leaves, air cooler under the crowns and smelling of mud and willow bark. The shallows at the mouth gave up their heat as they entered — warm at the top, cool underneath — and the skiff slid into the shade like a hand going into a pocket.

The heron was there, gray and patient on the bow of the sunken skiff, one leg folded. It turned its head and looked at them, smoothed one feather, looked away. Annabeth felt something in her chest loosen at the sight, the way you feel when a fixed point on your map is still where you left it.

"Morning," Percy told the bird quietly. "We're just visiting. No surveying. Possibly surveying. We'll see."

The heron settled deeper into itself, witness to the whole ordinary precious thing, and said nothing, because that was its tradition too.

Percy shipped the oars and let the skiff drift the last yards on its own momentum, the way you let a good morning finish its own sentence. The hull whispered through the green shade past the pebble wreck with its flat top, past the point where the reeds closed in, toward the flat rock that sat in the middle of the cove like a held breath. Annabeth trailed her fingers in the water over the side, feeling the seam she knew was there — the soft change where the cold shadow water met the warm open water and did not mix — and then pulled her hand in and wiped it on her shorts, and looked at him, and smiled the small close smile she saved for plans coming true.

"What?" he said.

"Nothing. Today's data." She stepped out onto the flat rock with the easy care she used on anything that floated or held still for floating, and held the painter while he beached the skiff on the pebble edge. "Water warm on top. Cold underneath. Light in coins. Boy rowing with his wrists."

"Hey."

"It's observational. It's allowed." She sat down on the sun-warmed top of the rock and pulled her sneakers off, lining them up heel to heel, and rolled her olive shorts an extra turn at the cuff out of habit. Her bare feet found the stone, warm and gritty, and she sighed out loud without meaning to, the sound of somebody setting down a weight.

Percy tied off, shouldered nothing — for once, deliberately, he left the satchel and the quilt in the boat — and came up onto the rock beside her in his jeans and damp sneakers, smelling of sun and lake and honey wrapper. He sat shoulder to shoulder with her, knees touching, and they looked at the cove together without talking, because there was nothing to say the water was not already saying: drip of willow leaves, tiny knock of wet rot against wet wood, frogs keeping a lazy morning count, and under all of it the low steady pulse Percy felt in the stone against his palms rather than heard.

After a while Annabeth leaned her head against his shoulder. Her braid spilled over his arm, sun-warmed and smelling faintly of lemon from Silena's soap. His shirt was warm from the row. The sun came through the willow crowns in slow coins and lay on their knees, their hands, the rock between them.

"This is the part where I'd usually be writing it down," she murmured.

"You can. I brought the ballast."

"I know. I don't want to yet." She turned his hand over and traced the lifeline with one fingertip, precise as drafting. "I want to just — sit. Is that a thing we can do? Sit on a rock for an hour and call it a date?"

Percy laced his fingers through hers, cold and warm together. "I think that's the definition."

So they sat. The heron preened one wing with great deliberation and resettled. A willow leaf came down and landed on Percy's hair and he left it there out of the old stubbornness, and Annabeth reached up and took it out and pressed it into his palm instead, and he closed his hand around it, and she laughed — the small close laugh that lived in the collarbone, the one he could feel against his side like a second heartbeat.

He kissed her then, unhurried, with the whole morning ahead of them and no bell to beat. It was the willow kind of kiss, the kind that took its time: slow, patient, warm at the top and cool underneath like the cove water itself. He tasted like the lake and like the bread from the satchel he had already stolen a corner of on the path. She had one hand in his hair, and he had his thumb at her temple, and the willow leaves came down around them like small applause. When they broke apart they did not break apart. They stayed with their foreheads together, breathing the same cooled air off the water, and she put her hand flat over the place where his heartbeat lived under his shirt and felt it going the way it went — fast, then steadying — and thought, with the clarity she saved for architecture and for him, that she could live inside this hour for a year and not get tired of the address.

"Hi," he said against her forehead.

"Hi," she said back, and kissed him again, lingering, because there was time, because the day was built for lingering, because lingering was allowed when it was tenderness and attention and nothing else. His arms went around her the way they always did, one around her back and one finding her hand, and her arms went around his shoulders, and they sat like that in the green light with the heron standing over them, and the long time was not wasted, which is the rarest thing about waiting.

When they finally stood, stiff and warm and blinking, the sun had moved a full handspan through the leaves. Annabeth pulled her camp shirt off over her head, folded it twice with brisk precision, and set it on the rock beside her sneakers — revealing the dark blue one-piece underneath, functional and plain, straps secure — and Percy peeled off his own camp shirt and laid it beside hers, down to his navy swim trunks, folding with the raccoon technique Clarisse despised. The clothing change was practical, unremarked, the work of two swimmers getting ready for water.

"Race you to the sunken skiff?" he said.

"You'll win. You're Cabin Three."

"I'll let you win. I'm a gentleman."

"You'll try to let me win and fail because I'm faster off a rock." She was already up, already grinning, already gone — off the flat rock in a clean shallow dive that barely broke the green, surfacing halfway to the wreck with her braid streaming. Percy went in after her with a whoop that scattered the frogs, and the cove took them both, cold and bright and honest, freshwater green closing over their heads with its low roar.

They swam the slow length of the cove and back, side by side, no hurry in it. Percy showed her the seam from underneath — the visible shiver where the two waters met — and she dove it twice to feel the temperature change on her face, coming up blowing water and laughing. He towed her once around the sunken skiff by one hand while she floated on her back, and she towed him back by the ankle while he pretended to drown with great theatricality for an audience of one heron. They climbed out onto the rotted gunwale together, dripping, and sat with their feet in the water, shoulders touching, watching the light move.

"This is better than mapping," Annabeth said quietly.

"This is mapping," Percy said. "You're just doing it with your whole body."

She looked at him sidelong, water beading on her shoulders, gray eyes clear. "That's annoyingly wise for a boy with a leaf in his hair."

"There's another leaf?"

"There are three." She picked them out one by one, flicking each into the water. "Okay. Beach. I'm getting hungry and Silena's seven pounds won't carry itself."

They rowed out of the cove together, Annabeth in the bow with her feet braced and her hair dripping down her back, Percy at the oars with water beading on his shoulders. The passage from shade to sun was like pushing through a curtain: green-gold, then white-gold, the willow smell giving way to pine resin and hot stone. Annabeth trailed one hand over the side and watched the wake V out behind them, neat and brief, the lake closing over it the way it closed over everything. A heron feather, gray and fine, drifted past the hull; she leaned and caught it before it went under, shook it dry, and tucked it into the map bag's outer pocket without a word. Ballast, and also not ballast. Some things you kept because your hands decided before you did.

Percy watched her do it and smiled, and rowed on, and did not ask. That was the morning's whole education: ask about some things, let other things be kept in silence. The lake flashed around them, freshwater blue with the sun standing straight up over it, honest and cold underneath its morning shine. His arms burned pleasantly with the row. Her shoulders burned pleasantly with the swim. Both of them were hungry in the clean, total way that only water makes.

---

By noon the sun stood high and white over the pines, and the north beach lay in full gold.

Percy beached the skiff above the waterline on the half-moon of sand, flag of the painter line furled around the bow cleat, and Annabeth carried the quilt and the satchel up to the warm flat stone that was Cabin Three's rock — the same stone where the float had happened, where Tyson had said the water holds me, where the trident scar still showed pale on the sandbar if you knew where to look. The shallows ran glassy over white sand, warm at the edges, the boundary rock sitting mid-lake with its white X bright in the noon light. Pine resin and sun-warmed stone and clean green freshwater. Somewhere up the hill the pavilion clattered distantly, somebody's radio playing a ball game, cicadas sawing in the trees.

Annabeth spread the quilt with two snaps, the way Silena had taught her — corners out, smoothing with flat palms — and Percy set the satchel down in the middle like an anchor and flopped backward onto the quilt with a groan. "Seven pounds. Clarisse was right. Silena packs like we're invading."

"Silena packs like she loves people." Annabeth sat cross-legged beside him, hair unbraided now and drying in loose waves over her shoulders, camp shirt back on loose over her swimsuit, sleeves flopping past her wrists. She unpacked with methodical pleasure: bread wrapped in cloth, cheese in wax paper, cold chicken in the tin, apples polished red, two peaches with their blush intact, lemon water in the sealed jar, honey squares in a twist of paper. "Inventory," she announced, lining it up. "This is beautiful. This is — she even cut the cheese in triangles. Triangles taste better. It's proven."

"Triangles are proven," Percy agreed solemnly, sitting up, toweling his hair with the corner of the quilt. He had pulled his dry camp shirt back on over his trunks for the meal, damp hems and all, out of the old modesty of shared blankets. "Guests first."

"There are no guests. It's just us."

"Sisters first, then. Annabeth first." He poured the lemon water into the tin cup and handed it to her, the way Clarisse had taught him, and she drank — lemon and cold and sweet all at once — and handed it back, and he drank, and they shared the bread, passing it back and forth, shoulder to shoulder on the quilt with their feet in the warm sand.

They ate slowly, the way Silena had told him to without telling him: no hurry, no drills, no patrol. The chicken was peppery and cold. The cheese triangles were, in fact, better. The apples were crisp and September-sweet. Percy told her about the morning send-off — Clarisse's date rules, Thalia's note, Silena's whole-hand wave — doing the voices with fair accuracy, and Annabeth laughed until she had to set the cup down, and then she told him about the loft yesterday, Thalia stealing the grape instead of the peach, keep the place, and Percy went quiet in the way he went when something true went past without armor.

"Keep the place," he repeated. "I like that. That's better than ten minutes per occurrence."

"Thalia's wise because she's eighteen. It's in the job description." Annabeth split the first peach with her pocket knife, neat halves, and handed him the bigger one. Juice ran down their wrists. They ate with their heads bent together, knees touching, hands finding each other on the quilt without looking, fingers lacing sticky and warm.

After the food they lay back on the quilt together with the tin cup between them for ballast, looking up at the pines moving against the white sky. The sun lay on their faces, warm and heavy. The lake moved at the edge of hearing. Percy's hand found hers and held on, sustained attention, nothing else needed: thumb moving once across her knuckles, then still, then once again, the way you keep time without counting.

Annabeth turned her head. He was watching the sky with his eyes half closed, sun on his skin, lake drying in his hair, fourteen and sun-browned and entirely, unguardedly hers. She leaned up on one elbow and kissed him — slow, sun-warmed, honey and peach between them — and he kissed her back with his free hand coming up to cup her jaw the way he always did, thumb warm against her cheek, careful as Tyson with a quilt. The kiss deepened by degrees, unhurried, dizzy in the way that comes from tenderness and breath and the sheer strangeness of being allowed to stay: no bell, no watch, no one above the pines who cared what two fourteen-year-olds did in the sun. Just warmth, heartbeat, breath, lake air, the weight of his hand at her jaw and hers in his hair, the world narrowed to attention.

They broke apart, foreheads touching, breathing the same warmed air, hands still laced on the quilt.

"Hi," he said.

"Hi," she said, pink-eared, smiling into it. "That was —"

"Peach-flavored."

"Shut up." She kissed his forehead, quick and sure, and lay back down with her head in the nook of his shoulder, his arm around her, her hand flat over his heart, both of them under nothing but sun in the shape of one thing. His heartbeat went under her palm, fast, then steadying. Her breathing slowed to match his without either of them deciding it. The pines moved. The lake kept its silence around the beach with the discretion of a good witness.

Annabeth listened to his heart the way she listened to waterlines: for the true line underneath the noise. It hammered at first, the way hers did, the sweet unsteadiness of being kissed in daylight with nowhere to hide it. Then it settled, beat by beat, into the slower rhythm of sun and full stomach and held hands. She matched her breathing to it deliberately, the way she matched pencil strokes to a straightedge, and felt his arm tighten a fraction when she did — noticed, kept. His thumb moved once across her shoulder, then stilled. Her fingers curled once against his chest, then rested. Sustained attention had a texture, she decided. It felt like sun on skin overlaid with shade from the pines moving: warm, then cooler, then warm again, never still, never leaving.

A cicada sawed itself hoarse in the branches above them. Somebody's radio up the hill faded in and out with the wind, a ball game going to static and back. The quilt warmed underneath them until the warmth came up through their backs like the rock was holding them too. Percy turned his head and breathed against her hair without saying anything, and she felt the breath move through the loose waves, warm and even, and closed her eyes tighter, and thought that if cartography was the art of recording where things were so somebody not there yet could find them, then this was the same art practiced without paper: she was recording him, heartbeat and breath and sun-weight, for the winter version of herself who would need to know exactly where they had been.

"This," Annabeth said into his shoulder, "is the place. I'm keeping it."

"Filed?"

"Permanent collection." She closed her eyes. The sun moved a fraction. The quilt warmed underneath them. For a long time neither of them said anything at all, and the long time was not wasted.

---

The trouble began at three, when Annabeth unbuckled her satchel.

It wasn't an unreasonable thing to do. The sun had moved far enough west for the rocks to cast shadows across the north beach, which made the shoreline easier to measure than it had been at noon. Her field book was inside the satchel. So were two pencils and the short length of knotted cord she used when a proper measuring tape would have taken longer to fetch than the work itself.

Percy saw the book and sat up on one elbow. Sand had left a red pattern on his forearm.

"I thought we weren't working today."

"We aren't." She opened to the page with the unfinished north-beach inset. "I'm going to draw for a few minutes."

"That's working."

"To you, maybe."

He looked out across the lake. A breeze had put little dark wrinkles on the water. "You said we could have one whole day without anybody needing anything."

"Nobody needs this." She held the page up by its corners. "I want it."

That should have been a simple distinction. Annabeth could see it plainly. She liked drawing things accurately; Percy liked lying on the sand after swimming; the beach was large enough for both interests. But Percy pushed himself upright and began gathering the empty peach wrapper and the tin cup as if somebody had announced that their day was over.

"Where are you going?" she asked.

"Nowhere. Getting our stuff together."

"I didn't ask you to do that."

"You don't have to."

Annabeth shut the book on her pencil. The pencil slid out, struck the rock, and rolled toward the sand. She caught it with one hand before it disappeared between the stones, which was satisfying, though the satisfaction did not improve her temper.

"Percy, if you want to go back, say so. Don't make me guess because I picked up a notebook."

He stopped folding the quilt. One corner was still caught under her foot.

"I don't want to go back."

"Then what do you want?"

"I wanted this." He gestured at the beach, the blanket, the tiny heap of lunch things. "Without having to turn it into evidence that we did it properly."

She almost laughed. He had managed to make the most Annabeth-shaped accusation imaginable. He was also looking at her with that stubborn expression he wore when something mattered too much to joke about.

"That's not why I'm drawing," she said.

"I know."

"Then please stop telling me what I'm doing."

He let go of the quilt. "All right."

Silence followed. It was less comfortable than the silence they had shared at noon. Annabeth smoothed the bent corner of her page. Percy took the cup down to the water to rinse it, then checked himself and came back with it still dry because they were packing food scraps to carry uphill. He set it beside the satchel without looking at her.

She could have put the book away to end the argument. For a moment she considered it. But she disliked the thought of putting it away because he was angry, and disliked even more the possibility that he might think the concession proved she hadn't really wanted to draw. She also knew she had told him this morning she could leave the map at home. Instead she had brought it in the bottom of her bag and hoped the promise would somehow survive the contradiction.

"I did say I wasn't bringing the map," she admitted.

Percy glanced at the satchel. "You said you weren't bringing a schedule."

"I said both."

"You did."

"And I brought it anyway." She pressed her thumb over the pencil's blunt point. "I should have told you when I changed my mind."

"I should've said I was disappointed instead of acting like you'd scheduled me out of my own afternoon."

"Yes," she said. "You should have."

He looked at her properly then, surprised that she wasn't going to rescue him from the sentence. It made her feel mean for about two seconds. Then he gave a small, reluctant smile.

"Fair."

Annabeth took the knotted cord out of the bag. She had been using it to compare distances between boulders when the beach was empty. At home, if she'd called this work, Thalia would have wanted to know whether she had eaten first and why a day off required equipment. Thalia was not entirely wrong. She was simply incapable of being right quietly.

"You don't have to help," Annabeth told Percy. "I can do it myself. I wanted you here, not assigned to me."

He rolled the cup between his palms. "I thought we were going to be together the whole afternoon."

"We are together."

"You're looking at a shoreline."

"I can look at two things in one afternoon."

"That's a lot of faith in your eyesight."

She refused to reward him with a laugh. He was smiling more openly now, though. That counted for something.

"Go swim, Percy. I'll be twenty minutes."

He sat there a beat too long, then rose and wandered toward the shallows without taking his shirt off. At the water's edge he crouched to pick up a stick that had drifted in and began tracing shapes through the wet sand. Annabeth watched him for a moment. His shoulders were tight. Neither of them was having the afternoon they'd planned.

She got to work.

The beach had changed since she'd last surveyed it. She knew that much before she'd put down a single point. The water was several finger-widths farther up the sand than it had been on their arrival, despite the absence of any meaningful tide on a freshwater lake. Wind was pushing a narrow chop into the cove. If she traced the wet edge as a permanent boundary, her map would be wrong by tomorrow.

She measured twice, using the shadow of the same flat rock as a reference, then wrote WINDWARD WATERLINE, TEMPORARY in the margin. Under that she added the time, because she'd forgotten to do so on the last page. She moved uphill to find a second reference point and nearly stepped on the peach pit she'd left beside the quilt. The pit was small, absurdly neat, and as far from official cartography as a thing could be.

It was also exactly where they'd eaten lunch.

Annabeth drew a small circle around its position.

Percy was still at the water when she finished her first line. The stick had become a miniature breakwater. He had made a barrier of wet sand and was watching the next run of lake water cut its own channel through the middle.

"Your engineering is upsetting the environment," she called.

"That's why I built a spillway."

"You built a dam with a hole in it."

"Controlled failure. Very advanced."

She went down to see. The water had carved a shallow trough beside the little wall, carrying most of the sand away from one end while leaving the center intact. His experiment would be gone in ten minutes. He seemed perfectly content with that.

"Look," he said, pointing with the stick. "The waves don't come in straight. They bend around that rock first."

She followed his line of sight. The shadowed current made the water deepen abruptly on one side. From where she'd been sitting she couldn't have seen it. She made a quick mark on her page, then stopped herself.

"What?" Percy asked.

"I was about to tell you to stand still so I could measure the angle."

"Was I about to be assigned a job?"

"Probably."

He looked pleased to have caught her at it. She shut the book.

"I'd like to measure the angle," she said. "You don't have to help me."

Percy tilted his head, considering. Then he put the stick down carefully above the waterline.

"I'll hold the cord if it saves you from falling in. Not because you look like you need a supervisor."

"Good. I don't."

"I've noticed."

He held one end against the rock while she stretched the cord along the bank. The line was short by two knots. Annabeth had to move her own end and start again. Percy waited without commentary until she asked him to shift his hand, then did so.

On the second try the wind caught the loose sheet in her notebook. He caught it against his knee.

"You're doing a terrible job of not helping," she said.

"Maybe I like you."

"That's going in the margin."

"No, it isn't."

She wrote it there anyway, too small for anyone else to read, then returned the book to the bag. She had enough measurements to make the inset honest. More could wait. It wasn't a completed map, but for once she wasn't irritated by an unfinished page.

They walked back to the quilt. Percy brushed sand off one corner and sat down, leaving room for her. Annabeth stayed on her feet long enough to collect the peach wrapper and the cup. When she sat, he offered her the last of the cold tea.

"I wasn't actually bored," he said.

"I know that now."

"No, I mean I wasn't worried about the drawing. Not really." He turned the cup in his hands. "Tyson left yesterday. The cabin feels different. This morning I thought we could spend a day doing nothing, and if I got it exactly right I wouldn't have to think about him being gone."

Annabeth put her book aside. She'd known the farewell hurt. She hadn't realized he'd made their empty afternoon responsible for relieving it.

"That's a lot to ask of a beach."

"Apparently I've been making unreasonable demands of the geography."

"You have." She touched his wrist. "I miss him too."

"He always knew when somebody was leaving. He'd start packing three days early so he could spend the last day not packing. I thought he was being strange." Percy looked toward the place where his little dam had already vanished. "Turns out I was doing the same thing. Just with fewer shirts."

Annabeth considered the map. Her own plans were often attempts to make a difficult day behave. She could admit that without giving up her right to enjoy making them.

"You can tell me you want my attention," she said. "Even when I'm doing something else. I'd rather you said that than told me my work doesn't count as having a day off."

"I can try."

"And I can tell you before I bring work on a date."

"Was this a date?"

"It had lunch, swimming, and a boy who argued with my stationery. What else would you call it?"

"A very expensive stationery intervention."

She laughed, finally. He leaned back against the quilt, and after a little while she settled beside him. They didn't need to solve the question of what a day off was. Tomorrow she'd want a map again. He might want an afternoon with no plans again. Both could be true.

She opened the book once more, this time to the blank corner beneath her measurements. Percy made a protesting noise, but he was smiling. She drew two very small figures on a blanket beside a circle marked PEACH PIT, then added a crooked line in the lake for his collapsed sand wall.

"That looks nothing like my dam."

"It no longer exists. This is the best evidence available."

"Put 'controlled failure' under it."

She did. Beside it she drew a second line, the actual shoreline with the temporary wind mark clearly distinguished. It pleased her to leave the difference visible. When she closed the book, she placed it beneath the folded edge of the quilt so the breeze couldn't take it.

Percy lay down with one arm under his head. She stayed sitting a little longer, watching the shadow of the flat rock reach the first knot's distance from the water. At last he opened one eye.

"Are you coming down here or taking attendance?"

"I'm deciding whether you qualify as ballast."

"I've had excellent reviews."

She stretched out beside him, and he put an arm around her shoulders. For several minutes they listened to the water, which refused to keep a straight boundary for anybody. Annabeth thought about telling him that she loved the new measurement and the imperfect day and him in three entirely different ways. Instead she took his hand and gave it a squeeze. He squeezed back.

The small argument hadn't disappeared. It had left them with a better way to ask for what they wanted, and a book with a corrected line in it. That seemed enough for an afternoon.

Percy's breathing slowed. The sun slid a little lower. By the time Annabeth lifted her head to check on him, he was asleep, his fingers still loosely folded around hers. She kept the quilt over his shoulder and let the unfinished afternoon continue.

Percy woke to the sound of somebody being careful, and for one disoriented second he thought it was Tyson with the quilt.

It was Annabeth, sitting up on the quilt with the field book closed beside her, pulling the pale blue button-up Silena had pressed over her camp shirt against the late-afternoon wind. The sun had slid low behind the far shore, gold going rose at the edges, wind thin off the pines with the first October edge under it. The beach lay in long shadow. The shallows went rose-gold at the edges, warm on the feet from the day and cold underneath, holding its summer deep down and giving it back slow. Somewhere in the willow shade the heron shifted on the bow of the sunken skiff and settled, far and gray and still.

"What time is it?" he said, voice scraping with sleep.

"Six." She tucked the blanket over his shoulders — one quilt, the only way to share — and bumped him gently. "You were out for an hour. You drooled on the survey."

"I did not."

"You did. I labeled it. LAKE, WITH DROOL." Her gray eyes were clear and fond, hair loose and windblown, sleeves flopping past her wrists. She looked, he thought, with the flat easy attention he gave her when he thought she wasn't logging it, like the best thing that had happened to him today: precise and sun-pink and entirely unhurried, a girl who had drawn him into her map and then lain down beside him inside it.

He sat up, rubbed his face, pulled his dry camp shirt straighter over his trunks. The air had cooled fast once the sun dropped, the way mid-September evenings did, gold going blue at the edges. He dug in the satchel for the nice shirt and shook it out — pale blue, Silena-pressed, still holding its creases — and pulled it on over the camp shirt, buttoning halfway. Annabeth watched the operation with her chin on her knees.

"Silena's cue," she said. "Eight o'clock when you're cold and pretending you're not. You're two hours early. You're learning."

"I'm multitalented." He poured the last of the lemon water into the tin cup, handed it to her first, drank after. They shared the final honey square, passing it back and forth, bites alternating, sugar and lemon between them. Nonsexual and total, the way fourteen-year-olds who had chosen each other did everything: shoulder to shoulder, knees touching, hands finding each other on the quilt without looking.

"We should swim once more," Annabeth said. "Before the light goes. Warmest at five, after that it's just bragging — Clarisse's rule."

"Clarisse has a rule for water temperature?"

"Clarisse has a rule for everything. It's why we like her." She stood, shed the button-up and the camp shirt together in one brisk motion, folding both twice and setting them on the quilt — back to the dark blue one-piece, functional and plain — and Percy toed off his sneakers and left the nice shirt folded with care on top of the satchel. They went in off the flat rock together in a ragged line of two, no testing, the way Cabin Three went in: knees, waist, chest, the freshwater coming up colder and cleaner than the afternoon had left it, green and honest.

Percy lay back and the lake took him, and it was different every time and the same every time: the cold layer three feet down, the warm skin on top from the day's sun, the low green roar when his ears went under. Annabeth floated beside him with her hair spreading in the rose light, hands spread, eyes open to the sky. For a minute neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to say the water was not already saying. Then, because traditions were things you chose and not laws, Percy spoke — not a true thing, not explaining anything, just the ordinary thing that came after:

"The water holds me," he said quietly, borrowing it on purpose, honoring it. "Even though the day's over. It doesn't drop me."

Annabeth reached along the surface and found his hand under the water and held it, cold fingers and warm, graphite and lake. "Held twice," she said. "By lake and by me."

"By lake and by you," he agreed, and they floated a while in the rose-gold shallows, two heads in a row, and the water held them the way it held the skiff and the heron's reflection and the boundary rock, the same and also not the same. A fish jumped once out past the sandbar with a flat silver smack and was gone.

They thrashed around after that, because two teenagers in a lake at golden hour could not stay solemn long, until the shallows were foam and the second peach had to be rescued from the rock. They crawled out at last, dripping, and sat with feet in the water and the quilt over shoulders, toweling off with the quick competence of swimmers who had done it a hundred times. Annabeth pulled the camp shirt and the button-up back on over her swimsuit, sleeves to the elbow now, hem to mid-thigh over her olive shorts; Percy pulled the camp shirt and then the pale blue button-up over his trunks, leaving it open at the throat. Dry enough to walk, per Clarisse's logistics. Wring out before the path. He wrung out. She wrung out. The blanket could carry them.

They packed the satchel in comfortable silence — tin cup rinsed, apple cores saved for compost, quilt shaken and refolded square so it would not hurt anyone — and Percy beached the skiff higher, painter line secured, oars shipped. Annabeth checked the third cleat of nothing, out of new habit, with her foot: steady, silent, the dock far away but Tyson's work holding everywhere by extension.

Then they walked the switchback up to the dock overlook with the quilt over both their shoulders and the satchel between them, pine needles soft underfoot, lake flashing dark-silver through the trees to the left, and sat at the end of the dock with their feet in the evening-dark water under its last rose shine, the way they had sat so many nights before and never once like this: with the whole day underneath them like a foundation.

The pavilion lanterns ticked on one by one up the hill. A loon called once. The wind brought pine and dishwater and somebody's distant singing — Silena, probably, bribing a harpy for honey with a hymn.

Percy pulled the quilt tighter around them both and Annabeth came in against his side like it was the position a body assumes at fourteen and keeps for life: her head in the nook of his shoulder, his arm around her, her hand flat over his heart, both of them under one blanket in the shape of one thing. The boards were still warm from the day under the quilt. The water moved cool around their feet. His heartbeat went under her palm, steady and slow. Her breathing found his without deciding it. Warmth, heartbeat, breath, lake air, sun's last heat in the wood, hand-holding savored past the point of sense: the romance of sustained attention, hour after hour, chosen.

"Name a constellation," Annabeth said in the dark, in the voice she reserved for closing arguments.

Percy waved vaguely at the sky, where the first stars were coming out one by one over the far shore. "That one. The Toe."

"The Toe." She laughed — the small close laugh, the one that lived in the collarbone, the one he could feel against his side like a second heartbeat. "You're looking at a tree."

"Trees can be constellations if you believe in them."

"That's not how constellations work."

"That's exactly how constellations work. Somebody looked at dots and believed." He pointed with his free hand, tracing. "See? There's the ankle. There's the — the toe part. The distinguished toe. The heroic toe."

"The heroic toe," Annabeth repeated solemnly. "Fixed point. Put it on the survey."

"Already noted." He kissed her hair once — it tasted like lake and sun and the end of a long day — and she turned her face up, and he kissed her, and it was the night-water kind of kiss they owned: slow, warm at the top, cool underneath, with the moon-road just starting to lay itself bright on the lake beside them and the whole day folded into it. Her hand came up and rested at the back of his neck, and his held its place at her jaw like it had been built there, and the lake kept its silence around the dock with the discretion of a good witness. Lingering, tender, dizzy through emotion rather than anything else: the dizziness of being fully noticed for a whole day and noticing back.

They came back out of it by degrees, foreheads down, breathing the same cooled air off the surface.

"Hi," he said.

"Hi," she said, and kissed his jaw, quick and sure, and settled back into the nook. Above them the stars kept coming, the way stars do — without permission, without hurry — and the sky did what sky does over a lake, which is double itself.

Percy counted them the way he counted sounding lines when he was nervous: one, then five, then more than he could hold. The moon-road lay on the water the whole way out to the far town lights, silver on dark, repairing the night one plank of silver at a time. The boards were warm under the quilt. The water was cold around their feet. Annabeth was warm along his whole side, her hand over his heart, her breathing slow against his shoulder. He thought about the morning's arithmetic — seven pounds of food, two peaches, one quilt, a whole day with nowhere to be — and how it had all been spent, every ounce of it, and how unspent he still felt, like a tide that had gone out and come back fuller.

He turned his hand over under hers and laced their fingers, cold and warm together, and held on the way Silena had told him to that morning: when there's nothing to say. There was nothing to say. The lake moved. The pines hissed. A night bird crossed the whole water in one line. Somewhere behind them the Hermes cabin started something with itself and lost. Annabeth's thumb moved once across his knuckles, then rested. His heartbeat went under her palm, steady as a kept promise. The dizziness was still there, faint and sweet, the kiss-dizziness that came from tenderness and breath and being fully noticed — not from anything else, never from anything else, just from the strangeness of a whole day of attention coming to rest in one held hand.

"Do you ever think," she said, quieter now, "that somebody up there had us in the plans?"

"All the time," Percy said. "Today especially."

"Today especially," she agreed, and you could hear the smile in it without seeing it.

They lay there a long time like that — sat, really, feet in the water, quilt over shoulders, but it felt like lying down inside the day — and the stars came down to the water, and the water held them, and there was no moment at which either of them could have said when her breathing and his had started going at the same speed, or when her thumb had stopped moving on his chest and simply rested there, over the heart, keeping its place.

After a while Annabeth sat up straight, because she had one more thing, and she was going to say it, and there was no reason in the world to be coy with it. She pulled something from her overshirt pocket — a folded square of paper, creased soft, carried all day in with her pencils.

"Here," she said. "I made this this morning before breakfast. Don't laugh. It's not to scale."

He unfolded it in the lantern light from the hill. A sketch: the flat rock from above, two figures on a quilt, and out past the boundary rock a small heron glyph with its head turned. Underneath, in her square hand: WEDNESDAY — the day with nowhere to be. Water warm. Current kind.

Percy looked at it a long time. "Annabeth. This is —"

"It's a love note," she said, matter-of-fact, ears pink even in the dark. "You keep yours in your pocket. I keep mine on paper. Pockets and paper. Same thing." She took his hand again. "Monday we wrote. Tuesday you walked me back. Today I wanted you to have the whole day written down. So it doesn't wash out."

He folded the paper with care and slid it into the pocket of the nice shirt, beside the pebble and the hair tie and the vellum morning, patting it flat. "Pocket things," he said softly. "Staying things."

"Staying things," she agreed. She kissed him again, brief and warm, lake on their lips and honey between them, no hurry in it. When she drew back her eyes were bright. "Tools checked?"

"Tools checked." He patted the pocket. "Boyfriend checked."

"Boyfriend held twice," she said, remembering, holding his hand a little tighter under the quilt. "By water and by me."

They sat until the lanterns blurred and the loon called again, and then the tiredness came the way it comes after sun and water — all at once, honest, total. Annabeth's head grew heavy in the nook of his shoulder. Percy's arm grew heavy around her. The quilt slipped and he pulled it back up without waking fully. They slept side by side on the dock for twenty minutes, platonically, deeply, foreheads touching, hands still laced on the boards, breathing the same cooled air — two fourteen-year-olds who had spent a whole day choosing tenderness and had earned the nap inside it.

Annabeth dreamed of contours. Not the anxious kind, where the lines would not close — the good kind, where the pencil moved by itself and every sounding came up true. Water warm, she wrote in the dream, current kind, and the words stayed where she put them. Percy dreamed of the pool in Tempest Hall moving faster than it needed to, pushing its cool mineral smell up through the floor grates, and of Tyson's voice saying the water holds me from very far away, like a sounding called up from deep water. They breathed at the same speed all the way through it, her thumb resting over his heart, his hand warm at her jaw even in sleep, the quilt holding them both like a kept place. When the wind came off the pines with its thin October edge, neither of them woke; he pulled the quilt higher around her shoulders in his sleep, and she turned her face deeper into the nook in hers, and the lake kept its silence around the dock with the discretion of a good witness.

Percy woke first, to the sound of boots on the ramp boards trying to be quiet and failing the way only one person failed.

Clarisse stood at the dock's root with a lantern in one hand and her spear over her shoulder, braid rebraided for night, cutoff shirt dry at last. She looked at the two of them — quilt, laced hands, satchel packed, skiff secured — and her face did the scowl that meant tenderness.

"Full dark," she said, not unkindly. "That's the schedule. You made it."

Annabeth sat up, pink-eared, smoothing her button-up. "We fell asleep."

"I can see that. Ten minutes per occurrence, retroactive, waived for naps." Clarisse set the lantern down and came out onto the dock, boots soft. She looked them over with the quick inventory of an older sister: dry enough, warm enough, fed, whole. "Silena sent tea. It's up at the top. She said to tell you the cheese triangles were a hit and she's adding them to the rotation." Her voice went into the flatter register, the true one. "And — good day?"

Percy stood, folded the quilt, offered Annabeth a hand up. "Good day."

"Best one," Annabeth said quietly, shouldering her map bag, taking Percy's hand again. "Water warm. Current kind. Fixed point."

Clarisse snorted, turned fast, walking back up the dock with her shoulders set — but halfway she stopped and looked back, her face unguarded in the lantern light. "Hey. Chase. Thanks for —" She gestured with the spear, out at the lake, at the quilt, at Percy. "For bringing him back like this. Loose. Lit. Guard down. That's — that's good work."

Annabeth held her gaze a second. "He brought me back the same way."

"Yeah," Clarisse said thickly. "I know. That's why I said it." She lifted the lantern. "Come on. Path's dark. I'll walk you up. Silena's waiting and Thalia gets back Friday and wants the whole report, so you'd better have details about the Toe."

They walked up the hill hand in hand through the blue dusk, sneakers slapping, borrowed shirts flapping, pockets full of tools and pebbles and love notes — Clarisse ahead setting the pace with the lantern, counting heads at every switchback the way she always did, one, two, three, all present, all kept. At the bend they stopped, as Percy had that morning, and looked down to the willow cove where the heron stood gray and still on the bow of the half-sunk skiff in the dark, patient, holding the boat's place, witness to the whole ordinary precious thing.

"She's still there," Percy said.

"Good," Annabeth said, leaning her head against his shoulder as they walked on. "I like knowing something's waiting."

The path leveled past the pines and the cabin light came through the trees, warm and gold: Tempest Hall with its high windows lit, pool breathing small and steady inside, Silena in the doorway with two mugs of tea going to steam in the cool air. She was sixteen, hair down and damp from the evening wash, lake-gray sweater over her sleep shirt, mending tote abandoned at last for the day. She waved with the whole hand when she saw them, and Percy felt the day close the way water closes over a dive — cleanly, completely, without losing anything.

"Seven pounds," Silena called. "How much came back?"

"Apple cores and the tin," Annabeth reported, holding up the satchel. "And the quilt. The quilt survived."

"Quilts survive. That's their job." Silena handed them the tea — honeyed, lemoned, exactly right — and looked them over with the quick warm inventory of a sister who had packed for every outcome: sun-pink, lake-tired, whole. "Sit a minute? Thalia's survey lamp is still out and Chiron's office is dark. No panel tonight. Just tea."

So they sat on the cabin steps with the tea going to steam around their faces, Clarisse on the rail with her spear across her knees, Silena on the top step with her shoulder against the doorframe, Percy and Annabeth on the middle step with the quilt over their knees and their hands laced under it. Nobody said much. The tea warmed their hands. The pines hissed. The lake flashed dark-silver far below through the trees, holding the doubled stars. Annabeth leaned her head against Percy's shoulder one more time that day, and he leaned his against hers, and Clarisse pretended not to see, and Silena saw everything and smiled into her mug.

After a while Annabeth stood, brushed sand off the button-up, shouldered her map bag. "Curfew," she said, pink-eared but sure. "Athena cabin. Vellum to file."

"I'll walk you," Percy said.

"I know." She took his hand again, and they went up the path together in the lantern light from the doorway, two figures for scale, with a tiny peach pit between them in spirit if not in fact. The pines went by on both sides, needles cool under their sneakers, lake flashing once more through the branches and then gone. Her fingers were warm from the tea. His were lake-cold underneath the warmth, the way they always were, the way she liked them. Neither of them hurried. The day had taught them how.

Silena had heard her that morning on the bench of memory — two days after today, then the ferry — no. This was Wednesday, and Tyson was gone, and the arithmetic had closed. What remained was the keeping: the visitor towel washed and folded, the rock centered on the pillow, the forge-smoke faint under the lemon oil. Silena looked at her brother with his nice shirt gone soft at the collar and his pocket full of staying things, and at the girl beside him with graphite still on one cuff and a heron feather in her map bag, and felt the thing she had felt at lunch Sunday shift without resolving: not fixed, not answered, just touched. Touched was enough. Touched was what presence was for.

Behind them the lake kept everything — the rings, the seam, the rings' slow pulse, the day's whole account laid out like water over a dive: morning shade, noon sun, three o'clock mended, night stars doubled. And ahead the cabin light showed warm through the trees — Tempest Hall, pool breathing small and steady, three bunks made up and one kept, lemon oil and lake in the air, a sister with tea and a sister with a spear and a sister on survey keeping the boundary so everybody inside it could float.

Tomorrow would come. Thursday, sibling-fluff, whatever the water decided. But today — bread and peaches and cold chicken, one true line drawn and one clumsy word forgiven, kisses slow as weather, the Toe heroic overhead, a love note in a nice-shirt pocket — today had been inhabited hour by hour. Today they had chosen each other all day long, on purpose, with witnesses.

That was the purpose. That was what stayed.
