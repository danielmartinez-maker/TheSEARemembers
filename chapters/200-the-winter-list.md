## Chapter 200: The Winter List

Percy found Annabeth in the Big House kitchen, trying to persuade a kettle to heat faster by looking at it.

“It’s not a stopwatch,” he said.

“I know.”

“Then why are you watching?”

“Because it’s making a noise like it wants to be.”

The kettle ticked on the stove. A pot of oatmeal sat beside it, its surface just beginning to cloud at the edges. Annabeth had rolled up her sleeves. There was a smear of graphite near her thumb, probably from the bed drawing she had carried over from Cabin One. She had not brought the drawing into the kitchen. Percy had noticed that much.

He set a small basket of apples on the table. “I brought these.”

“Did you take them from the kitchen?”

“They were in a bowl labeled for the kitchen.”

“Good.”

“I have learned to read.”

“I’m relieved.”

He pulled out a chair. The basket bumped against the sugar tin. One apple rolled toward the edge, and Annabeth caught it without looking away from the kettle.

“How was the guest room?” he asked.

“I don’t know. I wasn’t there.”

“I meant Ianthe.”

“I don’t know either. I haven’t asked her.”

“Right.”

Annabeth tilted her head. “You can ask if you want to.”

“I can?”

“You’re allowed to speak to people.”

“Sometimes I need confirmation.”

“Then there it is.”

She took the kettle off the stove and poured hot water over the oats. Steam lifted between them. Percy reached for a bowl, but Annabeth held out the spoon.

“Could you stir while I get the plates?”

“Sure.”

He took the spoon. The oatmeal had thickened at the bottom. He worked it loose while Annabeth opened a cupboard. The ordinary kitchen sounds—ceramic, wood, water—made the morning feel as if it had already decided what kind of day it would be. Percy wished he could believe that.

Annabeth set four plates on the counter.

“Four?” he asked.

“If Ianthe wants breakfast.”

“She might.”

“I said she could come.”

“You said you could invite her.”

“I did invite her.”

“When?”

“Yesterday, while we were in the cabin.”

“You said she could come to breakfast tomorrow.”

“Tomorrow is now.”

He moved the spoon through the oats. “That sounds like an invitation.”

“It was one.”

“Good.”

She leaned her hip against the counter and watched him. “You’re looking for a way to make sure I did it correctly.”

“I’m looking for a way to say I’m glad you did it.”

“That’s different.”

“I know.”

“You can say that.”

“I’m glad you did it.”

Her expression softened, though not by much. “Thank you.”

A door clicked somewhere in the hall. Footsteps crossed the entry, paused, then came closer. Annabeth put one more bowl on the counter.

Ianthe appeared in the kitchen doorway with her hair braided over one shoulder. She wore a borrowed gray sweater with sleeves pushed up to her elbows. There was a faint crease on one cheek. Percy wondered if she had slept badly, then reminded himself he didn’t know her well enough to turn a crease into a story.

“Morning,” Annabeth said.

“Good morning.” Ianthe looked from the pot to the four plates. “You meant breakfast?”

“I did.”

“I thought you said you could ask me.”

“I asked. You came.”

Ianthe studied her, trying to place whether that was a joke. Annabeth did not help her. After a second, Ianthe nodded.

“Thank you.”

“There’s oatmeal,” Percy said. “And apples.”

“Are those from the dining pavilion?”

“The kitchen basket.”

“Then I assume they’re for eating.”

“That’s what the label implied.”

A tiny smile pulled at one side of her mouth. She stepped inside but stayed near the door, as if the room had a line she was waiting to see crossed. Annabeth offered her a bowl.

“You can take as much as you want. The honey’s there.”

“Is this the only breakfast?”

“Today?” Percy asked.

“Here.”

“Today, yes. There are other things in the pavilion.”

“I meant—”

“I know what you meant,” Annabeth said. “This is what I have.”

Ianthe took the bowl. “That’s fine.”

The words were smooth. The pause before them wasn’t. She sat at the end of the table and set the bowl directly in front of herself, not close to anyone else. Percy put the spoon down and brought the pot over.

“Do you want the big spoon?” he asked.

“I can manage.”

“I wasn’t worried.”

“You asked if I could manage.”

“I asked which spoon.”

“Percy,” Annabeth said.

He stopped. “Sorry. The small one’s there.”

Ianthe chose it. She added honey and looked at the oatmeal as if she had expected something more complicated. Percy sat beside Annabeth, leaving the chair across from him open. Annabeth took a piece of apple and cut it with the side of her spoon.

The room’s back door opened. Clarisse came in with a folded sheet tucked under one arm. She had a smear of dirt across the knuckle of her right hand and a thin line of sawdust on her sleeve. She glanced at the table, then at Ianthe.

“You’re early.”

“I was awake.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“I noticed.”

Clarisse set the sheet on the counter but didn’t sit. She wasn’t wearing a coat. The camp had turned colder overnight, enough that Percy’s breath had shown when he crossed the hill. Clarisse looked like she had come straight from the cabins.

“You want oatmeal?” Annabeth asked.

“I want the list first.”

“You can have both,” Percy said.

“Not if you let the pot burn.”

“I’m sitting right here.”

“That’s what I’m afraid of.”

Annabeth pushed another bowl toward her. Clarisse accepted it with a brief nod and carried it to the counter, where she filled it herself. She glanced at the page, then back at the table.

“Chiron’s making a winter-work list,” she said. “We’re meant to check what’s still open before people start fixing things just because they can see them.”

“I like lists,” Ianthe said.

“Then you can read one.”

Ianthe’s spoon paused. “I said I liked them.”

“I heard you.”

Clarisse took a bite. Percy watched Ianthe, waiting to see if she would answer. She turned her attention back to her bowl.

The hall door opened again. Someone came in carrying a damp jacket and said something to Clarisse about the green house. Clarisse answered that she’d check after breakfast. She took the paper from the counter and unfolded it.

The list was a single sheet, written in Chiron’s careful block hand with additions in Beckendorf’s thicker pencil. Percy could make out the first line from where he sat: fence brace—inspect, do not load. Below it were the torn awning and its bent bracket, the loose rail at Cabin One, the north-step frost check, and a note about the greenhouse soil bank. Annabeth’s stool was on the page too, with a small mark beside it: glue still curing.

“Is this the same one from last week?” Percy asked.

“Same work,” Clarisse said. “Different day.”

“The fence has been holding.”

“Holding is not fixed.”

“I didn’t say it was.”

“You were about to.”

He wasn’t sure if she was right. Maybe he had been about to say it. The brace had stayed in place through the rain; he’d seen it while walking back from the pavilion. The fence leaned, but not as much as it had before.

Clarisse turned the paper so the others could see. “Chiron wants the jobs separated into three columns: what’s safe to use, what needs a repair, and what we’re waiting on. No one moves anything until the person who knows the task says what’s needed.”

“I can do that,” Ianthe said.

“Do what?” Clarisse asked.

“Organize the page.”

“You can organize the page.”

“I can also plan the order. If the awning is exposed to the wind, we should deal with it first. The rail might be less urgent.”

“Maybe.”

“I can look at both and decide.”

Clarisse stopped chewing. “You can look.”

The difference landed on the table as plainly as the paper. Ianthe lifted her chin.

“I didn’t say I’d do it alone.”

“You said you’d decide.”

“I can decide what’s urgent.”

“Based on what?”

“On the condition of the work.”

“You haven’t seen it.”

“I can see the awning from here.”

“You can see the canvas. You can’t see the bracket.”

Ianthe glanced at the list. “Then I’ll inspect the bracket.”

“That’s not how this works,” Percy said.

Clarisse looked at him. “Which part?”

He couldn’t tell if she was asking or warning. “I mean, it’s okay to help with the plan. We have people who know which repairs are safe.”

“Like you?” Ianthe asked.

“Not like me. Like Beckendorf. He checked the bracket.”

“Then why did he leave it?”

“Because the wind was up, and the replacement part needs—”

“I know what a bracket does.”

“I didn’t say you didn’t.”

“Then don’t explain it.”

Percy set his spoon down. He had started doing that again: answering for people, filling space before anyone asked. He had thought it would make the morning easier. It had only added another voice to the table.

Clarisse folded the list along its old crease. “We’ll go over it with Chiron after breakfast. Nobody is assigned anything yet.”

“I could still make a clean copy,” Ianthe said.

“That would be useful,” Annabeth said. “If you keep the three columns and leave the notes from Beckendorf.”

“I can do that.”

“Then ask Chiron for the sheet.”

Ianthe nodded once, accepting the task as if it had been hers all along. She scraped the last of the honey from the bowl and set the spoon beside it. Percy noticed how neatly she aligned them. He noticed, too, that she had not asked Annabeth whether the drawing stayed in Cabin One or whether she could see it later.

The front door opened. A boy with a dark duffel over his shoulder walked in behind Chiron. Leandros’s hair was still wet at the ends, though the rain had eased. He stopped when he saw Clarisse and the list, then looked at the table.

“Are we eating?” he asked.

“Some of us are,” Clarisse said.

He looked at the pot. “What is it?”

“Oatmeal,” Percy said.

“Is there anything else?”

“Apples.”

“That’s not breakfast.”

“It is if you eat enough of it.”

Leandros’s eyes moved over the room and settled on Ianthe. “Who’s she?”

“Ianthe,” Chiron said. “She arrived Friday. Ianthe, this is Leandros.”

They looked at each other, both taking in the same details: the borrowed sweater, the damp hair, the bag, the space between them. Ianthe sat straighter.

“Zeus’s daughter?” Leandros asked.

“Yes.”

“Poseidon’s son,” he said, as though he had to confirm it for her.

“I know.”

“I wasn’t sure if you’d been told.”

“I have.”

Clarisse put her bowl in the sink. “You’re both here. That’s the information you need.”

Leandros took the empty chair beside Clarisse. “You’re the other new one.”

“I’m the one who arrived later.”

“By a day.”

“More than a day.”

He frowned, then reached for a bowl. “It’s close enough.”

“It isn’t if you’re trying to decide who came first.”

“Who said I was?”

“You did.”

Percy watched Annabeth’s eyes move between them. She stayed quiet, not trying to make the introduction feel friendlier than it was. He was grateful for that. He also wanted to say something—welcome, or we’re all still figuring this out—but the words felt like a second serving of oatmeal no one had asked for.

Leandros filled his bowl. “Do you have a place to sleep?”

“I have the guest room.”

“Then you’re not in the cabin yet.”

“I know where I sleep.”

He took a spoonful and swallowed without looking at her. “I didn’t say you didn’t.”

Ianthe turned her spoon once in the bowl. “You seem to say things and then decide afterward what you meant.”

“So do you.”

“Maybe.”

Clarisse picked up the list again. “If you’re both finished, Chiron has work.”

“After breakfast,” Percy said.

Leandros tipped his bowl toward the pot. “I am having breakfast.”

“No,” Clarisse said. “You are having a bowl of oats while you read a list you haven’t been given.”

He followed her gaze to the page. “That’s the winter list?”

“It’s a list.”

“What does it say?”

“It says you haven’t been assigned anything.”

His expression shifted. Not anger exactly. More like the moment before he decided anger would be useful.

“I’m older than Percy,” he said. “I’ve done this kind of work before.”

“Then you can tell us what work you’ve done when Chiron asks,” Clarisse replied. “Age doesn’t assign you a crew.”

Leandros’s eyes turned to Percy. “Do you want to explain that?”

Percy heard the invitation and the challenge in the same sentence. He looked at Clarisse, then at the list. “I think she already explained it.”

Clarisse’s brows lifted. Not approval, but something close to acknowledgment.

Leandros leaned back. “Fine.”

Ianthe scraped a little oatmeal from the side of her bowl. “If we’re making a list, somebody should still decide what happens first.”

“Chiron will,” Clarisse said.

“Then what is our role?”

“Ask.”

The kettle clicked as it cooled. No one reached for it. Percy felt the morning settle into a silence that did not need fixing, though every part of him wanted to try.

---

After breakfast, Chiron pinned the list to the Big House board beneath the schedule for lessons and meals. The paper curled at its corners from the damp air. He used a smooth river stone to hold the lower edge flat while Beckendorf read the marks aloud.

“Fence brace, inspect without loading it. Awning bracket, wait for a calm hour and the right fasteners. Cabin One rail, don’t lean on it until it’s checked. North step, no frost check until we get the first hard frost. Greenhouse bank, done for now. Cabin One stool, cure time.”

“Could we write ‘not ready’ beside the stool?” Annabeth asked.

“We can write what it is,” Chiron said. “The glue is still curing. That is more helpful than a verdict.”

She took the pencil and added the words. Percy watched her underline still. The stool had been a simple repair that now required patience. He understood why she wanted to test it. He had spent the week reminding himself not to ask whether it had held yet.

Chiron handed her the paper. “Thank you.”

Ianthe stepped forward with a fresh sheet. “I made a copy.”

“That’s helpful,” Chiron said. “We’ll compare them before we replace the original.”

“Why compare? I copied everything.”

“Because copies can introduce errors.”

“I checked twice.”

“Then it should be quick.”

She gave him the page. Her letters were straight and even, with the three columns Clarisse had mentioned. Beside each task she had made a box for a name and a second box for a time.

“It’s clear,” Chiron said. “The name column should say ‘working with’ rather than ‘owner.’ A task belongs to the camp. We record who is doing it so the next person knows whom to ask.”

Ianthe’s mouth tightened. “That’s a lot of words for one box.”

“It’s two more.”

Beckendorf took the page and looked at the bottom. “You copied the note about waiting on fasteners. Good.”

“I left an extra row for priority.”

“Not needed,” Chiron said.

“But we need to decide order.”

“We need to learn what can be done safely. The order may change when we inspect.”

“Then what’s the point of a list?”

“To see what we know and what we don’t.”

Leandros leaned over the page. “The fence should come first. If it’s weak, the whole line can fall.”

“That’s why the note says not to load it,” Beckendorf said. “We’ll inspect the brace, then check the post and the ground. We won’t pull on the fence to prove it’s loose.”

“I can tell if wood is under strain.”

“Maybe,” Beckendorf said. “You can tell me what you notice. You won’t pull on it.”

Leandros turned toward Clarisse. “Who’s going to inspect it?”

“Beckendorf,” she said. “I can help.”

“I can help too.”

“You can ask him.”

“Why do I have to ask you?”

“You don’t. Ask him.”

Leandros’s eyes narrowed. Beckendorf waited, as if he had all morning.

“I can help inspect the fence,” Leandros said.

“We need someone to carry the boards and the tool box,” Beckendorf replied. “You can start there. If I need your eyes, I’ll ask.”

Leandros looked at the box near the wall. It was scuffed and carried a dull iron smell. He had offered his knowledge and gotten assigned carrying.

Percy knew the feeling of wanting a job that proved something. He also knew what it was to take the work nearest him just to get included. It came naturally to him to tell Leandros that the boards mattered. The sentence rose, ready-made.

He let it pass.

Ianthe tapped the extra column. “If no one has priority, whoever gets there first will decide.”

“No,” Chiron said. “The person responsible for each inspection will explain what can happen next.”

“Responsible,” she repeated. “Then the name column is still an owner column.”

“Responsible for the inspection. Not in charge of everyone else.”

“Wouldn’t someone have to be?”

“Sometimes. On a task, someone with the right experience gives instructions for safety. That does not make them in charge of the people doing it.”

Ianthe looked at the new copy. “Then it needs a column for the person giving instructions.”

“It needs the existing notes. We don’t need to make a rank out of it.”

The word rank made Leandros look up. He set the tool box on the bench and wiped his palm against his trousers.

“Who makes sure they listen?” he asked.

“People listen when they know what’s being asked and why,” Beckendorf said. “If they don’t, you stop and figure out what’s wrong.”

“That’s optimistic.”

“It’s more reliable than shouting.”

Clarisse took the smooth stone off the board and moved the page higher so a corner would stop curling. “You can read the old list while the others prepare.”

“I already read it.”

“Then you’ll be quick.”

Leandros didn’t answer. He seemed to be testing which part of the refusal he could push against without looking as if he had lost. Percy recognized that calculation from arguments in the arena. He also recognized what he wanted to do: give the boy an easy win. Let him inspect something he knew. Let him feel useful before he tried to lead everyone.

That wasn’t the same as giving him authority. Percy knew the difference. He wasn’t sure he was acting like it.

Annabeth came to stand beside him. “Do you have a job?”

“Apparently I can read.”

“I mean one of the inspections.”

“Beckendorf has the fence.”

“Then ask where you’re useful.”

“He gave Leandros the boards.”

“That is a job.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Percy looked at her. She had drawn a line in the mud on her shoe with the toe of the other boot, rubbing it back and forth. She seemed calm, but the line got darker.

“I think he’s going to take the carrying as an insult,” Percy said.

“Maybe he will.”

“He’s sixteen.”

“So are a lot of people.”

“He’s also new.”

“Yes.”

“Doesn’t that count for anything?”

“It counts as a reason to explain how things work. It doesn’t count as a reason to give him the part he wants.”

“I’m not saying we should.”

“You’re about to make sure he doesn’t feel bad about it.”

Percy looked past her. Leandros had picked up the tool box, perhaps because nobody else had claimed it. “I want him to stay.”

“You’ve known him six days.”

“I want him to have somewhere to stay.”

“That’s not the same as wanting him to stay in Cabin Three forever.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

He felt heat rise in his face. “You can ask that without making it sound like I’m trying to take over.”

“I’m not trying to make it sound like anything. I’m saying what I see.”

“I didn’t ask you to read my mind.”

“You asked me to look at the list with you.”

“I asked if you wanted to join us.”

“Then I decided to.”

The answer was small and sharp, but Annabeth did not walk away. He had meant the invitation as a way to include her, to let her choose; she had chosen. Now he was treating that choice as an agreement with his plan.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

She studied him. “For which part?”

“For deciding you were here to help me explain him.”

“I’m not.”

“I know.”

“You keep saying that after I correct you.”

“Because I’m trying to show I heard it.”

“Then show me by stopping.”

He looked at the pencil in her hand. “Okay.”

She glanced at the list. “I want to check the rail with Beckendorf. It’s outside the cabin, and I want to know whether it’s safe before anyone leans on it.”

“I can come.”

“You can ask him what he needs.”

“I’m not going to tell you not to do it.”

“I didn’t say you were.”

“You said you wanted to check the rail.”

“I do.”

“Then I’ll come too, if he says there’s room.”

“Fine.”

They stood shoulder to shoulder in front of the list. It felt less comfortable than touching her hand beneath a table and more honest than telling each other they agreed. Percy wanted to reach for her. He didn’t. Annabeth was looking at the paper, thinking.

Chiron came over with two wooden tags, each marked with a task name. “We’ll work in small groups. Beckendorf will lead the fence check. Clarisse can inspect the Cabin One rail with him. Annabeth, you can observe and take notes if you still want to.”

“I do.”

“Percy, could you carry the board and tool box to the fence with Leandros?”

“Sure.”

Chiron turned to Ianthe. “Would you help me sort the new copy against the original? After that, we can take the list to the greenhouse and compare it with the actual soil bank.”

“I thought I was planning the work.”

“You made a clear copy. Now we need to make sure it matches.”

“And then?”

“Then we’ll know which things are ready.”

Ianthe glanced at Annabeth. “You asked me to breakfast. Are you working too?”

“I’m checking the rail.”

“Because it’s your cabin?”

“Because I want to know if it’s safe.”

“Would you tell Chiron if it was fine?”

“I would tell Chiron what I saw.”

Ianthe looked between Annabeth and the list. “I can help with that.”

“Chiron asked you to check the copy.”

“Afterward.”

“Afterward, I’ll already be done.”

“You don’t have to make every choice by yourself.”

“I’m not.”

“Annabeth,” Thalia said from the doorway.

Percy hadn’t heard her come in. She stood with one hand on the frame, her splint-braced heel set lightly on the floor. Her expression was neutral, but she was watching both girls.

Ianthe gave a brief smile. “I was offering to help.”

“You can,” Annabeth said. “I didn’t ask for help with the rail.”

Thalia took in the list, then looked at Annabeth. She waited. Percy could see the old impulse in her—to step between, interpret, keep the conversation from turning. She had managed to stop before speaking for Annabeth. When Annabeth looked back, Thalia only raised her eyebrows.

“I’m fine,” Annabeth said.

“Good,” Thalia replied. “The tea’s cold.”

“I can get more.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I want to.”

“Okay.”

It was an ordinary exchange, almost nothing. But Percy saw the careful room around it. Thalia could stand in it without choosing the sentence for her sister.

Chiron lifted one of the wooden tags. “We should begin before the clouds move in.”

“Are they going to?” Ianthe asked.

“They may.”

“I can tell from the pressure.”

“What does it tell you?”

“That the wind will rise before noon.”

Chiron looked toward the open door. A ribbon tied to the porch post stirred and fell still. “Then we should avoid lifting canvas until we check the bracket and the gusts.”

“I can check the gusts.”

“You can tell us what you notice.”

“That’s what I said.”

“Good.”

Ianthe folded her clean copy and held it in one hand. “I’ll come to the greenhouse first.”

Chiron nodded, accepting it without turning it into a command. Leandros lifted the tool box again. His mouth stayed set.

Percy stepped toward him. “Do you want me to take the boards?”

“No.”

“Okay.”

“I can carry them.”

“Okay.”

Leandros looked as if he had expected another question. Percy didn’t have one. He reached for the wooden tag instead and followed Beckendorf toward the path.

At the door, Clarisse fell in beside Annabeth. “You want me to hold the level or the end of the tape?”

“Whatever Beckendorf needs.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the answer I have.”

Clarisse smiled a little. “Good.”

Behind them, Ianthe said something about the three-column layout. Leandros replied that the list needed a proper order. Neither voice was loud. Both were certain.

The camp had not yet picked up a hammer. Still, Percy could feel the old arguments taking shape around the tools: who knew, who decided, who was allowed to say no. He kept his hands empty until Beckendorf gave him something to carry.

---

The fence line looked sturdier from the path than it did up close.

The brace leaned against the post at an angle, its lower end buried in a patch of soft earth. Someone had wedged a flat stone under it. The fence itself still slanted toward the field. Rain had darkened the boards but left the ground muddy only in the lowest spots.

Beckendorf set down the tool box and crouched beside the brace. “We look first. Nobody pushes on it.”

Leandros carried the stack of boards a few paces beyond him. He put them down with care, keeping the edges even. “The post is turning.”

“We don’t know that yet,” Beckendorf said.

“You can see the line.”

“I can see the line.”

“You can see it’s shifted.”

“I can see where the paint’s worn.”

Leandros stepped closer. “The ground under it is wet.”

“It rained all night,” Percy said.

“That doesn’t mean all of it’s wet.”

Percy glanced at the fence. Water had collected in the narrow dip beside the field, but the mud farther up was only dark on top. Leandros pointed with his chin, not his hand.

“There. It’s running toward the post.”

Beckendorf leaned out and looked. “That’s runoff from the path.”

“It’s collecting on this side because the ground drops.”

“Could be.”

“It is.”

Beckendorf took a thin wooden strip from the tool box and laid it along the ground. He didn’t touch the fence. The slope was almost invisible until the strip showed the gap beneath one end.

“Good eye,” he said. “The water’s pooling where the ground dips.”

Leandros’s shoulders eased a fraction. “So the brace should go higher.”

“Not today. We’d need to check where the post is set and whether the ground’s packed around it. The brace could be doing exactly what we need until we can dig the channel.”

“Then make the channel.”

“We don’t know where the water should drain.”

“Downhill.”

“Into the footpath?”

Leandros looked toward the path. It was worn between the cabins and the field. “No.”

“Then we wait until we can check the lower ground. You can mark where the water gathers.”

Leandros glanced at Percy, as if the right answer should be obvious to both of them. “I can mark it.”

“Do you have chalk?” Beckendorf asked.

Percy found the piece in the box and handed it over. Leandros took it, then stopped before making a mark. He looked at Beckendorf.

“Here?”

“On the stone, not the post.”

Leandros crouched, circled the shallow pool on the flat rock, and wrote a small arrow toward the path. He did not look pleased that the useful observation had turned into a note rather than a repair.

“Thanks,” Percy said.

Leandros dusted the chalk from his fingers. “You don’t have to thank me.”

“I know.”

“Then why did you?”

Percy didn’t have a good answer. “Because it helped.”

Leandros shrugged and turned away. Beckendorf drew a line on the original list: water pools at lower side after rain; check drainage route when soil is firmer. He wrote his initials beside the note, not Leandros’s.

“Shouldn’t he get credit for noticing?” Percy asked.

Beckendorf glanced at him. “I wrote what we observed.”

“I didn’t mean—”

“I heard him. It’s on the page.”

Percy looked at the list. The words were there. It mattered more than which name followed them, though Leandros might not feel that way.

The group continued along the fence. Beckendorf checked the brace’s foot without touching the post, then the boards beside it. Percy carried the extra lengths to the dry side of the tool box. Leandros remained close enough to watch and far enough not to be in the way. He looked restless.

At the far end of the run, they found two boards that had been pushed out of line, but neither was broken. Beckendorf marked them for a later check and told Percy not to stack anything against them.

“Are we finished?” Leandros asked.

“With this inspection.”

“What about fixing it?”

“We’re waiting on the ground.”

“So the list says nothing gets done.”

“The list says what we know.”

“What if it gets worse before we check again?”

“Then Chiron will decide what to do.”

“Why not put someone on it?”

“Because no one can fix the drainage safely until we know where it goes.”

Leandros pressed his lips together. “You could at least brace it again.”

“It’s already braced.”

“It’s not enough.”

“We don’t know that.”

“Do you always need a second person to tell you what you can see?”

Beckendorf sat back on his heels. He didn’t look angry, but Percy saw his patience get more precise. “I need enough information before I tell someone to dig near a fence post. That’s part of the work.”

Leandros looked away. The wind stirred the grass and lifted the edge of the list. Percy pinned it beneath his palm.

The first inspection took less time than Percy expected. It also left more unanswered questions. He found that more annoying than he wanted to admit.

On their way back, they crossed the open space in front of Cabin One. Annabeth was crouched beside the porch rail with a folded measuring tape. Clarisse held the other end. Thalia stood on the step with her heel resting lightly on the board, away from the rail. She had a mug in one hand and was watching the work.

Ianthe was there too, a clean copy of the winter list in her hand. She had come from the greenhouse with Chiron. Her pencil marks filled one margin.

“Is the rail broken?” Leandros asked.

“Loose,” Annabeth said. “We’re checking how much.”

“Do you have a level?”

“Beckendorf has one,” Clarisse said.

“I can get it.”

“We have it.”

Leandros leaned over the tape. “It’s the outer post. The one at the bottom.”

“We know,” Clarisse said.

“Then you should brace it from here.”

“Why?”

“Because the angle’s wrong.”

Clarisse let go of the tape. “You’ve looked at it for two seconds.”

“Long enough to see where it moves.”

“It doesn’t move. We haven’t touched it.”

“You said it was loose.”

“Not the same as broken.”

“Then why are you measuring it?”

“To find out what needs doing.”

He stood between the porch and the work, blocking Annabeth’s view of the rail. She leaned sideways to see around him.

“Can you move?” she asked.

Leandros stepped left, but not far enough.

“More.”

He moved another step. “You could ask me before you start measuring.”

“I did ask you to move.”

“I mean before you decide the repair.”

“We haven’t decided.”

“Then let me look.”

“Clarisse is leading this check,” Beckendorf said, arriving behind them.

Leandros turned. “Why?”

“Because she knows this cabin’s rail, and I’m checking the fasteners.”

“She’s not a carpenter.”

“Neither are you,” Clarisse said.

“I’ve repaired boats.”

“This isn’t a boat.”

“I know what a loose brace looks like.”

“Then you can tell Beckendorf what you see. He’ll decide whether it applies.”

Leandros lifted his chin. “I’m older than Percy. I’m the oldest son of Poseidon here. Why am I hauling boards while she decides what happens to the cabin?”

Clarisse did not glance at Percy. “Because you’re not in charge.”

“I didn’t say I was.”

“You just said being the oldest son should decide.”

“I said I should be involved.”

“Then be involved. You can be involved from over there.”

“Clarisse,” Percy said.

She looked at him. “What?”

He had meant to say that Leandros was new, that he was trying, that there was a way to let him help without turning this into a challenge. He could feel the shape of that sentence forming again. He stopped before it came out.

Clarisse waited.

“Nothing,” Percy said. “Go on.”

Her eyes stayed on his for a moment, then she turned back to Leandros. “There’s no prize for being the oldest. There isn’t even a job that comes with it.”

“I didn’t ask for a prize.”

“You asked for the decision.”

“Someone has to decide.”

“Chiron decides what gets assigned. Beckendorf checks the repair. Annabeth measures. I’m here because it’s our cabin. You can have a look if Annabeth wants you to.”

Annabeth stood. She had the tape looped in one hand. “I don’t.”

Leandros looked at her. “You don’t want me to look?”

“I don’t want another person standing over me while I’m measuring.”

“You said you wanted help.”

“I said I wanted the rail checked. Clarisse and Beckendorf are doing that.”

“Those are different things.”

“Yes.”

He opened his mouth, then shut it. The silence felt bigger than the porch.

“Can I at least hold the level?” Percy asked.

Annabeth considered him. “Beckendorf has it.”

“Then I can hold the tape.”

“Clarisse is holding it.”

“Right.”

Annabeth’s mouth twitched. “You can carry the pencil.”

“I can do that.”

She handed him the pencil. Percy took it. The graphite felt warm from her hand.

Ianthe had stayed a few steps away. “The order of the list says the rail is next after the awning.”

“The list is not an order,” Chiron said as he came up the path.

“Then why are the tasks in that order?”

“Because that is how they were written down.”

“Someone chose the order.”

“Someone wrote a column from the earlier notes. It does not mean the jobs should happen one at a time.”

Ianthe studied the page. “I marked where the work could happen in parallel.”

“That may be useful,” Chiron said. “Show me what you saw.”

She brought the copy toward him. Instead of pointing at the boxes for priority, she indicated two rows and wrote “if safe” beside each. “The fence inspection and this rail inspection don’t need the same people.”

“That’s true,” Beckendorf said. “But the awning still needs a calm hour. It isn’t ready to schedule.”

“I marked that.”

Chiron turned the page to read. “You have.”

The approval seemed to lift her. She looked at Annabeth. “I can help with the rail once I’m done.”

Annabeth kept holding the tape. “We’ll see what it needs first.”

Ianthe’s face tightened, then smoothed. She looked down at her copy. Percy wondered if the tightness came from the no or from the fact that she had expected the yes to follow automatically.

Clarisse stepped toward the rail again. “Annabeth, take the measurement from the post to the bracket.”

“Do you want inside or outside?”

“Outside. I’ll watch the base.”

Annabeth knelt. Percy moved back to give her room and rolled the pencil between his fingers. Thalia sipped her tea. She didn’t offer advice. The heel rested on the step, but Percy did not ask how it felt. Chiron had given them a plan; he knew what day it was supposed to be revisited. Asking now would be one more way of making her body the center of a task she hadn’t chosen.

Beckendorf checked the metal fasteners without leaning on the rail. He had Annabeth call out the measurement twice. The lower bracket had pulled half an inch away from the post. The upper one was secure. No one tested it by putting weight on the rail.

“The bottom bracket needs a longer screw,” Beckendorf said. “And the post needs a second look from underneath.”

“Can it be fixed today?” Annabeth asked.

“If the shed has the right screws. I’ll check. I don’t want to put a short one back in.”

Clarisse added the new measurement to the list. “Mark it out of use until then.”

“It’s already not being used,” Thalia said.

“I know,” Clarisse replied. “I’m making sure the next person knows.”

“I can write it,” Ianthe said.

Clarisse looked at the page in her hands. “You can add the measurement after Beckendorf confirms it.”

Ianthe lifted her pencil. “He just confirmed it.”

“The screw length, not the measurement.”

“I can do both.”

“Ask Annabeth where she wants the note.”

Ianthe’s hand paused above the paper. “You can tell me.”

Annabeth kept her eyes on the rail. “Put it under the Cabin One rail. Add the measurement and ‘do not use until repaired.’ Leave the owner box blank.”

“Why blank?”

“Because no one’s assigned the repair yet.”

Ianthe wrote it down. Her letters were still exact. She left the box empty.

“Thank you,” Annabeth said.

“You’re welcome.”

Clarisse read the note over her shoulder. “Good.”

It was the first time that morning Ianthe was praised for something she did without adding a second step to it. She looked pleased and annoyed at once.

The list stayed in Clarisse’s hand. The rail stayed unused. The task had become more specific, but no one had repaired it or promised when they would.

Beckendorf found the longer screws in the shed, but the post needed a brace while he checked the underside. He asked Clarisse to hold the board steady. Annabeth stayed to record the measurements. Percy carried the empty tool box back.

Leandros took the boards without being asked. He stacked them against the Big House porch, clear of the door. He did not look toward the Cabin One rail again.

Percy walked beside him for half the path. “You were right about the water.”

“I know.”

“Beckendorf wrote it down.”

“He wrote what he saw.”

“He also wrote the note.”

“He didn’t put my name beside it.”

“He didn’t put anyone’s name beside an observation.”

Leandros gave him a sideways look. “You’re explaining the list again.”

“Sorry.”

“It’s not that I can’t read.”

“I know.”

“Stop saying that.”

Percy pressed his lips together. “Okay.”

They walked a few steps in silence. Campers were crossing the green with blankets and baskets. The cloud cover had lifted but the air still smelled damp. A pair of younger campers held a door while an older one carried a tray through. Nobody was in a hurry. The camp had found a pace that morning without anyone assigning it.

“Do you like being told what to do?” Leandros asked.

“Depends what it is.”

“You were fine carrying boards.”

“They needed to be carried.”

“You could have inspected the fence.”

“I don’t know enough about the posts.”

“You could learn.”

“Sure.”

“But you didn’t ask.”

“Beckendorf was checking it.”

“He didn’t ask you to.”

“No.”

“Then you just do what they tell you.”

Percy shifted the tool box to his other hand. “Sometimes.”

“Why?”

“Because sometimes the work needs doing.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the one I have.”

Leandros watched him for a moment. “You’re their brother. They still give you the carrying.”

“They also ask me to do other things.”

“Like what?”

“Whatever needs doing.”

“That’s not a position.”

“It’s not supposed to be.”

Leandros slowed, then stopped near the stone path. Percy stopped too. He could feel the urge to make the answer easier, to reassure him that the camp would see his value eventually. It sounded like a promise he had no right to make.

“I’m older than you,” Leandros said.

“By a couple years.”

“Then you should be able to tell me how this works.”

“I can tell you what I’ve seen. I can’t give you a job title.”

“Clarisse gets to decide. Chiron gets to decide. Beckendorf gets to decide. What do you get to do?”

“Choose whether I’ll help.”

Leandros looked back toward the fence. “That’s all?”

“It’s more than nothing.”

“Is it?”

Percy thought of his first week at camp, and then the years after that. He could name people who had given him a place at the table without asking him to prove he deserved a chair. He could also name times he had been made to sit where someone told him, for good reasons he did not understand. The answers did not fit in one sentence.

“Most days,” he said.

Leandros lifted the tool box handle from Percy’s hand without asking. It was heavy enough to make the gesture real. “I can carry it.”

“I know.”

Leandros gave him a look.

“Right. I’ll stop.”

He kept the box. Percy let him.

At the Big House, Chiron and Ianthe stood beside the winter list. She had spread the original and the copy over the table, weighted by the river stone and a tin of pencils. A third sheet was covered in short notes: wind direction, ground condition, who had inspected what. Her letters stayed even, but she had written the notes close together.

“Does the north step have to wait?” Ianthe asked.

“Until there’s a hard frost,” Chiron said.

“Couldn’t we check the underside now and then return to the top?”

“We checked the underside last week.”

“Then we have the information.”

“We have information from before the rain.”

“So we should schedule a second check?”

“We should wait until the condition the list names actually happens.”

Ianthe put her pencil down. “I understand the phrase.”

“Then the task is clear.”

“Why does it feel as if every answer ends the discussion?”

“Because this one is simple.”

She pushed the copy toward him. “I could still mark the inspection date as pending.”

“You can.”

She wrote “first hard frost” in the margin. Her hand hesitated over the priority box, then moved to the weather note.

“What do you see?” Chiron asked.

“The wind has been moving east since early morning. It will turn south before noon.”

“What would that mean for the awning?”

“If it turns south, the canvas will pull against the bracket.”

“Could it be safe to work on?”

“Not if the wind rises.”

“Can we tell whether it will rise?”

Ianthe looked toward the ribbon tied to the porch post. “The pressure dropped before breakfast. I think it will.”

Chiron did not write that down. “Then we’ll check again before anyone touches the canvas.”

“You don’t believe me?”

“I believe that you have an observation. I want to compare it with what the wind does around the hill.”

“Thalia said that too.”

“Did she?”

“She corrected my chart yesterday.”

“Then perhaps you can learn from her observation.”

“I have.”

“Show me how.”

Ianthe turned the copy around. She had added two arrows to the weather box: one for the wind before it met the ridge, one for the direction it took afterward. A note beneath it read *local path varies near the cabins*.

“That’s good,” Chiron said. “Keep the original chart separate from the work list. The two papers answer different questions.”

“I know.”

“You can still mark that the wind might affect the awning.”

Ianthe added a small note with a question mark. “If it doesn’t turn, then we can work?”

“Then Beckendorf can inspect the bracket and decide.”

“Of course he can.”

The words sounded like they had been placed on the table rather than offered. Chiron did not pick them up.

---

Silena entered carrying a covered pot in both hands. Percy caught the door before it swung shut behind her. Warm air escaped from under the lid, carrying the smell of beans, onions, and something sharp with rosemary.

“Lunch?” he asked.

“If you’re hungry.”

“Always.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“Then yes.”

She set the pot on the counter and removed the lid. “I made enough for the people who are checking the list. There’s bread by the stove. Don’t use the stool.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Percy, last week you sat on a chair with one missing leg because you thought the chair was the stool.”

“That happened once.”

“It happened in front of me.”

“I made a mistake.”

“That’s usually when it happens.”

Annabeth came in with Clarisse and Beckendorf. The new screws had been found, and Beckendorf planned to brace the rail after lunch. The list did not say the rail was fixed; it said the measurements were taken and the materials found. Annabeth wrote that distinction down herself.

Chiron called the group to eat. They carried bowls to the long table. Ianthe chose a seat with a view of the list. Leandros sat opposite her, the tool box under his chair. Percy took the space beside Annabeth. Clarisse sat near the end, far enough from Leandros that she would not have to keep an eye on his hands.

Silena ladled soup. The steam clouded the window over the sink. She put a basket of bread in the middle and a dish of softened apples beside it. The apples had been warmed with cinnamon and a little honey. Percy took one and felt its heat through the spoon.

“Is this the same kind you made last week?” Leandros asked.

“No,” Silena said. “That was baked.”

“What’s this?”

“Apples.”

“With cinnamon,” Percy said.

“I know what cinnamon is.”

“I’m sure you do.”

“Then why are you telling me?”

Percy looked at his bowl. He could feel Clarisse watching him, but he didn’t look up.

Silena passed Leandros the bread. “There’s enough.”

“Thank you.”

Ianthe took a small piece. “It smells good.”

“It is,” Clarisse said.

“I was talking to Silena.”

“I know.”

Silena sat across from her. “Thank you.”

The table settled into spoon sounds and the scrape of bread against bowls. For a few minutes, everyone ate. Percy felt the warmth spread through his hands where the bowl rested. The room grew louder by small amounts: a question about the weather, a request for more bread, Beckendorf describing the size of the screws he’d found. The day narrowed from a list of unfinished things to a table, a pot, and steam on the glass.

Ianthe lifted her eyes to the two new siblings. “So, we’re both the newest.”

“Not exactly,” Leandros said. “You arrived after me.”

“By a day.”

“More than a day.”

“Does that matter?”

“It did when you said it.”

“I said I arrived later. I didn’t say it made me less new.”

“Then what did you mean?”

“That I’m not going to let you decide where I belong because you slept here first.”

He tilted his head. “I wasn’t going to.”

“You were deciding who I was before you finished asking my name.”

“I asked your name.”

“After you asked if I was Zeus’s daughter.”

“That was how I knew why you were here.”

“Maybe I don’t want that to be the first thing people know.”

Leandros paused. “Then what do you want them to know?”

The question sounded genuine. The answer did not arrive quickly.

“I don’t know,” she said. “I only got here Friday.”

“Then you don’t know where you fit.”

“Neither do you.”

“I know where I sleep.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

Leandros looked at Percy, then Clarisse. His face had gone still. “You’re all deciding.”

“Deciding what?” Annabeth asked.

“Whether we fit.”

“That’s not a vote,” Clarisse said.

“Then who gets to decide?”

“Everybody decides what they’re willing to share.”

“That sounds like a way of saying no without saying it.”

“It might be,” Clarisse replied.

“Clarisse,” Silena said. Not warning, exactly. More like a hand laid flat on the table.

Clarisse glanced at her. “What?”

“He asked a question.”

“And I answered.”

“You answered the question you wanted him to have asked.”

Clarisse’s jaw set. She had not meant to speak for Silena. She had meant to cut off the argument before it reached the family’s old wounds. The distinction did not make the answer better.

“Fine,” Clarisse said, and pushed the bread basket toward Leandros. “Ask what you meant.”

Leandros didn’t take the bread. “I want to know who can tell me I’m allowed to stay.”

“No one has said you can’t,” Percy said.

“But nobody has said I can.”

“You have a bed in Cabin Three,” Clarisse said. “That’s where you’ve been sleeping. Chiron will talk to you if it changes.”

“I don’t need a bed assigned. I need to know if I’m expected to leave.”

“I don’t know,” Clarisse said.

He frowned. “You don’t know?”

“I don’t. I’ve known you a week.”

“Six days,” Percy said.

Clarisse looked at him.

He felt the correction in his mouth as soon as he made it. “Sorry.”

Leandros looked down at his soup. “If I’m supposed to be part of this family, it would help to know.”

The table went quiet again. No one rushed to fill it. Percy felt the old reflex rise: tell him he was part of the family, because the word would make the room easier. He did not know what that would obligate. He did not know whether Clarisse or Silena wanted to say it. He did not know what Leandros wanted the words to do.

“Being part of the family doesn’t tell us who holds the hammer,” Annabeth said.

“Those are different things,” Ianthe added.

Leandros glanced at her. “You’re the one who thinks someone needs to decide.”

“I do. I don’t think the person with the title has to be the person who decides.”

“Then why does it matter?”

“Because I want to be the one who makes the list.”

Chiron set his spoon down. “You can help make it. You cannot assign other people’s work without asking.”

“I know.”

The words sounded practiced now, and they did not change what she wanted.

Leandros looked around the table. “So I have to ask whether I can help. She has to ask whether she can decide. Everyone asks.”

“Usually,” Clarisse said.

“What if no one says yes?”

“Then you do something else.”

“That’s a very comfortable answer for people already here.”

“It’s not comfortable for everyone,” Annabeth said. “But it’s still the answer.”

Ianthe ran one finger around the rim of her bowl. “Maybe if we did something together, it would be easier.”

“Maybe,” Silena said. “But that doesn’t mean the table has to decide today.”

The door to the kitchen opened. One of the younger campers came in for the bread basket. They shifted a chair to reach it and asked Clarisse if the path by the fence would stay closed. Beckendorf said yes until the brace was checked again. The conversation moved back toward the work list, though it did not leave the question behind.

Leandros took a piece of bread. He did not ask for the tool box to be moved. Ianthe added an apple to her bowl and ate it slowly. The cinnamon had gone into the fruit. Its warmth lasted longer than the steam on the window.

---

After lunch, Beckendorf and Clarisse returned to the Cabin One rail. The sky had brightened, but the air had sharpened. The ribbon at the porch post pointed south, then dipped, then pointed south again.

“Ianthe said the wind might rise before noon,” Percy said.

“It’s almost noon,” she replied from the steps. “It has turned.”

“Will it rise?”

“I think so.”

Beckendorf looked at the awning across the path. “We’ll wait before we take the canvas down.”

“I didn’t say to take it down.”

“No one did.”

She glanced at the list and added a note beside the awning: *wind turning south; check again before work*. The question mark remained in the margin.

Clarisse held the rail’s post while Beckendorf worked underneath. Annabeth recorded the screw length. Percy asked before he joined them.

“Can I hold the board?”

“Not until Beckendorf says where.”

Beckendorf pointed to the top of the post. “There.”

Percy held it there, careful not to push. Leandros picked up the spare boards and moved them out of the walkway.

“Those go by the shed,” Clarisse said.

He turned toward her. “I know where the shed is.”

“I didn’t say you didn’t.”

He carried them there anyway. He came back without the tool box this time. Beckendorf used the longer screw he’d found and tightened the bottom bracket. He checked the post with the level, then used the heel of his hand to test whether the bracket shifted. It held.

“Is it safe?” Annabeth asked.

“It’s secure for ordinary use,” Beckendorf said. “But the rail should still be checked after the next heavy rain. The ground near the post is soft.”

Clarisse wrote the note in the list. “So, repaired, recheck after rain.”

“Right.”

“Can I mark it done?” Ianthe asked.

“You can write ‘repaired’ and add the recheck,” Annabeth said.

Ianthe did. She left the owner box blank.

Thalia watched from the porch with her mug. When Clarisse asked if she wanted to come down, she shook her head.

“I’m fine here.”

Clarisse nodded and did not make a second offer.

Leandros was waiting by the shed. Percy crossed to him with the spare board.

“Could you help carry this back?”

Leandros glanced at the board, then at the path. “Where?”

“To the fence.”

“I thought we were done there.”

“We are. Beckendorf wants the boards clear of the walkway.”

“Then they should go in the shed.”

“That’s where the extras are. These are the ones we took out.”

“Why not leave them by the fence?”

“Because someone could trip.”

“They could see them.”

“A person carrying a basket might not.”

Leandros looked at the board, weighing how much this counted as work. “Fine.”

He took one end. They carried it together. Halfway across the green, Leandros asked, “Did the rail pass?”

“Beckendorf said it was secure for ordinary use.”

“That’s not the same as repaired.”

“It is the same as repaired, with a note to check it after heavy rain.”

“So they fixed it.”

“Yes.”

“Who?”

“Beckendorf. Clarisse held the post. Annabeth measured.”

“And you held a board.”

“I did.”

Leandros looked at him. “You don’t mind being last on the list?”

“I wasn’t on the list.”

“Exactly.”

Percy shifted his end of the board so it wouldn’t bump against Leandros’s leg. “The list isn’t a ranking.”

“Everything is a ranking if people keep deciding who gets to do what.”

“Sometimes it’s a list of who knows how to do what.”

“You’re defending them.”

“I’m describing what happened.”

“Same thing to you.”

Maybe it was. Percy had been glad when the rail was fixed. He had wanted to be part of it. Now he was walking with Leandros toward the shed with a board between them, explaining a system he had not made and did not always like.

“Maybe,” he said.

Leandros’s grip tightened around the board. “I’m not trying to be your rival.”

“I didn’t think you were.”

“You said nothing.”

“You asked if I would have done more.”

“I asked if you think this is how it works.”

“I don’t know. It’s how today worked.”

“Do you want a brother?”

Percy almost said yes. It was true, but it was also too large to lay between them like a tool they were both expected to carry. Leandros had arrived with his own life, his own mother, his own history. Percy could want him without knowing what he had been through.

“I want to know you,” he said.

“That’s not a yes.”

“It’s what I have.”

Leandros stared straight ahead. “You all say that.”

“Who’s all?”

“Clarisse. Annabeth. You. We’ll see. We’ll talk later. We don’t know.”

“We don’t.”

“Then why invite us?”

Percy had no answer ready. He was glad this time. “Chiron invited you to camp. I didn’t.”

“Would you have?”

“I don’t know you yet.”

Leandros turned his head, surprised. Percy kept his eyes on the path.

“I’m not saying I don’t want you here,” Percy added. “I’m saying I can’t decide what we are after six days.”

“Six days.”

“Right.”

They put the board in the shed. Leandros left it with the other spare lumber and checked that the door could shut. It did. On their way back, he did not ask Percy to carry anything else.

Near the Big House, Ianthe stood with the list. She had added the rail repair and the new fence note in clean lines. The awning row still had no name.

“The wind turned,” she said.

“You were right,” Percy said.

“I didn’t say the awning was safe.”

“You wrote the note.”

“I wrote the wind direction.”

“Still useful.”

She looked at him as if she were deciding whether he meant it. Then she folded the page once and carried it inside.

“Are you going to tell Chiron what happened at the rail?” Leandros asked her.

“He was there.”

“Still.”

“I’ll tell him the rail is repaired and the awning needs another check.”

“Not who did it?”

“Why?”

“So people know.”

“They know.”

Leandros made a small sound of disbelief. Ianthe ignored it and went inside.

The next gust came before the bell for afternoon lessons. It rattled the porch shutters and tugged at the loose corner of the awning. No one went near the bracket. Beckendorf checked the list, saw Ianthe’s note, and sent a camper to tell Chiron the wind had picked up. The canvas stayed tied down.

Percy found Annabeth by the board. She had added the rail repair and its next check in the margin of the list copy. The shoreline map remained dotted. The stool was still marked as curing.

“Do you want company?” he asked.

“I’m writing.”

“That’s an answer.”

“Do you want to carry the list to the Big House?”

“I can.”

“I don’t need you to.”

“I know.”

She looked up at him.

“Sorry,” he said. “I mean, I can carry it if you want. If not, I’ll go.”

She considered him. “You can stay. I want to finish this line first.”

He stayed by the window and watched the clouds gather over the ridge. She wrote *rail repaired; recheck after heavy rain*. The letters were smaller than the rest because the margin was narrow. When she finished, she held the page up to make sure the ink had dried.

“Does that fit?” he asked.

“It fits.”

“Does the repair?”

“Beckendorf said it does.”

“Good.”

She set the pencil down. “You almost stepped in for Clarisse earlier.”

“I did.”

“You stopped.”

“I started.”

“That counts too.”

“I don’t want to tell you who you’re allowed to help.”

“I know.”

He waited.

Annabeth reached for his hand. “I also don’t want every disagreement to become a question about whether you can be a brother.”

“I don’t either.”

“Then let it be about the disagreement.”

“I can try.”

“Good.”

They held hands by the table, her thumb moving over the graphite smudge on his knuckle. It was a small quiet thing, and it did not answer what Leandros wanted. Percy was grateful she did not ask him to make it answer.

A knock sounded at the open door. Chiron stood there with the updated list.

“The rail is repaired. The fence remains braced, with the drainage route to be reviewed once the soil is firmer. The awning will wait for calmer weather. The north step waits for the first hard frost, and the greenhouse bank stays as it is.”

“Stool?” Annabeth asked.

“Still curing.”

She nodded.

“Can I update the master copy?” Ianthe asked from the hall.

“Of course,” Chiron said. “Use the same wording.”

Ianthe came in, took the page from him, and copied the notes. She did not write a name in the owner box. At the awning she added the time of the wind observation. Percy noticed she had used the question mark again.

Leandros stood just beyond her shoulder. “You could put my name beside the fence note.”

Ianthe looked at the page. “You noticed the water.”

“I did.”

“Then tell Chiron that.”

“He heard me.”

“Then he knows.”

“He didn’t write it down.”

She held out the list. “You can add what you want him to know.”

Leandros did not take it. “You’re telling me to ask.”

“I’m telling you the pen is here.”

He looked at her, then at the pencil. After a moment, he picked it up and wrote in the lower margin: *water pooled below brace after rain; marked on stone*. He did not sign his name.

“Is that what you wanted?” Ianthe asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Neither do I.”

She took the pencil back and put it down.

Chiron pinned the updated list to the board. The completed items were small: a rail repaired, one dry inspection recorded. The fence still leaned. The awning still waited for calmer weather. The next frost had not arrived.

Leandros stayed by the board for a minute, reading the notes. Then he picked up the tool box and carried it to the shed without being asked. He set it on the shelf, handles out.

No one thanked him. No one told him to stop.

When he came back, the list had not changed. Percy wondered if that was what the day had given him: a place to see the work, not a right to direct it. He wondered whether it was enough. He knew better than to decide that for him.

Outside, the wind turned the porch ribbon south again. The camp’s winter work had begun with a page of open boxes and a few careful marks. The list could say what had happened. It could not say yet who belonged beside it.
