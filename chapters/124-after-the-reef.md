## Chapter 124: After the Reef

The morning after the garden visit, Percy woke to the sound of dry land.

In Atlantis, even quiet had a current. It slipped along the palace corridors, stirred the fronds around the nursery and carried distant voices through water. Here, the pines worried at the air outside the window. The canoe lake gave a small lap against the dock. Somewhere near the strawberry fields, a hammer rang three times, stopped, and rang again as if someone had forgotten the rhythm.

He had gone to bed late, after putting the route notes on his shelf and trying to explain to Clarisse why he had written down the name of every person who'd helped them. Now his shoes were still damp from the saltwater shore. Annabeth's copy of the guest record lay beneath a stone where the cabin draft wouldn't scatter it.

Percy sat up and heard Silena moving around the worktable. She was soaking the kelp-fiber sample in a shallow basin, turning it carefully with two fingers.

"Does it still work?" he asked.

"I don't know yet. It was stiff last night. I want to see whether it softens without breaking."

Clarisse came in carrying a small bag from the Big House and a folded note.

"Chiron says a pair of hippocampi left this at the coast after breakfast," she said. "There's a hamper from Amphitrite. They didn't wake us for the delivery."

Percy took the note first. It said the palace's first inspection of the faulty passage charm had found no obvious fracture, and the attendants were continuing to test it under different current conditions. The record didn't claim the cause had been settled. Amphitrite had added a sentence in her own hand thanking them for telling the household about the problem.

"That's good," Annabeth said from the doorway.

Percy hadn't heard her approach. Her hair was still damp from washing, and she had a pencil tucked behind one ear.

"Good that it failed?" he asked.

"Good that they didn't make up an explanation before they knew."

Clarisse set the hamper on the table. Inside were sea-glass beads, a silver-veined stone from Triton and a little packet of preserved fruit marked for the kitchen to inspect before serving.

"No touching the food until it's cleared," Silena said.

"Did you hear that, Percy?" Clarisse added.

"I have restraint."

"Historically unproven."

He read Amphitrite's note again. She'd promised the inspection report, not a perfect answer. That was a kind of care he trusted. It felt different from being told there was nothing to worry about.

They carried the hamper to the Big House and handed the food packet to the kitchen staff. Chiron inspected the parcel inventory and asked whether the objects inside were meant to be shared or kept by particular people. Percy said the beads were a gift to Cabin Three; the stone had his name on a small tag. Clarisse had the coral token from the visit. Silena had the sample of woven kelp and a little pink shell she planned to keep at her sewing table.

"Then label them," Chiron said. "A visitor's gift can become a camp mystery in less than a week."

Annabeth fetched pencil and paper. The inventory took only a few minutes. Afterward she asked Chiron whether the revised public map needed to identify the saltwater departure point they'd used. He said the main shoreline access could be marked, but the guarded channels below the surface should remain in the confidential route file.

Percy watched her divide the two kinds of information without protest. She was learning that leaving something off a public map was sometimes more responsible than filling every blank.

They walked back through camp with the empty hamper. Younger campers were gathering at the arena, and the dining pavilion smelled of bread. Life had continued while Percy spent an afternoon admiring the coral garden, then it had continued through the night as he slept. The ordinariness made him feel less divided between his two homes.

At the fork near Cabin Three, Silena and Clarisse took the path down toward the lake. Percy knew they had been talking about their visit and wanted a little time without him asking whether they were happy about it.

"Come along if you want," Silena said.

"You're not required," Clarisse added.

He considered the offer, then shook his head. "I need to write a letter."

"You've got plenty of time," Silena told him.

"I know. I want to do it while I remember the details."

Clarisse raised her coral token. "Then don't write in my name."

"I won't."

They went on toward the lake, Silena carrying her shell carefully, and Percy returned to the cabin with the empty hamper. The sea had given them something to talk about, but not a single story they were all obliged to tell the same way. He could thank Amphitrite without making his sisters' feelings part of the bargain.


---

In Cabin Three, everything was where Percy had left it: the blanket rumpled on his bunk, practice clothes over a chair, the conch shell on its shelf. He opened the window. Pines, a path, and the lake’s blue reflection filled the view. The camp had kept on being itself while he was away. That might have been the reason it mattered so much.

Inside, the damp shirt gave way to dry cloth. After washing the salt from his hands, the sting fading from his cracked knuckles, he unpacked the gifts. Amphitrite had sent sea-glass beads for the cabin shelf; Triton had given him a silver-veined stone from a reef that, he claimed, had survived three wars and a foolish renovation by a god of carpentry. Percy wasn’t sure the story was true. The stone was nice either way.

Both gifts went onto the shelf beside the conch, glass catching the late light.

Then he took out a sheet of paper.

Messages through shells and pools had carried his words before. This time he wanted them to sit somewhere he could see. He took a sheet from Annabeth’s stack, sharpened a pencil until cedar filled the air, and wrote, “Dear Amphitrite,” then stopped. The trouble was not finding something to say; it was choosing which of the many thank-yous to write first.

“Dear Amphitrite,

Thank you for welcoming us to Atlantis. Returning with my sisters was different from visiting alone. I was glad to show them the garden and the palace from afar, and even gladder they could make their own memories there instead of borrowing mine.

Silena still has the shell she found. She says it is a little piece of the reef that she can carry in her hand. Clarisse said it was a shell, but she made sure Silena didn’t leave it behind.

I hope you know I was happy to be there. I missed you, and Father, and Triton. I missed the sound of the palace. I missed the way the garden moves when the current changes. Coming back to camp doesn’t make that less true.

I wanted to tell you because I don’t want you to think that loving this place means I’m choosing it instead of Atlantis. And I don’t want to think that going home to you means I’m choosing Atlantis instead of camp. I’m still learning how those things can fit together.

Thank you for making room for all of us. Please tell Father that Clarisse thinks the sea-horses need better reins, and tell Triton that I still remember the card game he claims I only won because the current moved the table.

I love you. I’ll write again soon.

Percy.”

Twice read, the letter earned a tiny sketch of the sea-horse that had carried them. It came out more like a determined goat with a fin. He added a mane of foam curls and decided it was close enough.

There was a knock at the open door.

“Come in,” he said.

Thalia leaned against the frame. A loose strand of her hair had caught in the collar of her shirt. The bronze tray was nowhere in sight; he guessed it had been put somewhere safe from people who might try to use it as a shield.

“I was looking for you,” she said. “Annabeth said you’d probably be writing.”

“She knows me too well.”

“She knows you have a lot to say when you don’t want to talk.” Thalia came inside and glanced at the letter. “You don’t have to show me.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Good. I wasn’t going to read it.”

Wood scraped as she pulled the chair from the other side of the table and sat backward on it, forearms resting along the top. Her eyes were bright in the low sun, the blue-gray of storm clouds that had decided to stay beyond the horizon.

“Can I ask you something?”

“Sure.”

“What was it like, seeing your sisters underwater?”

Down at the letter, then out the window toward pine and path, Percy looked. It was a simple question, but it opened a place in him that did not have simple edges.

“At first, I kept waiting for something to go wrong,” he said. “I thought they’d be cold, or scared, or that they’d hate the way everyone looked at them. I knew they could breathe there, but I still wanted to make sure they were all right every second.”

“That sounds like you.”

“It was annoying.”

“Also sounds like you.”

He smiled. “Then we got past the reef arch, and Clarisse wanted to know where the paths went. Silena wanted to know if the garden flowers had names. And I stopped thinking of them as visitors. They were my sisters, asking questions and getting distracted by barnacles and light on water.”

“Did the reef feel different with them there?”

“Yeah.”

He searched for the right words. “Everyone tells me the palace was my whole world when I was little. Since arriving here, I’ve been learning I can belong in both places. I worried Atlantis might feel like the real home and make everything else smaller.”

Thalia listened without interrupting.

“It didn’t,” Percy said. “The world got bigger. I followed the garden path; Clarisse watched the ridges; Silena made friends with a reef spirit before I introduced myself. It was my old place and their new place at once.”

“And you liked that?”

“I liked being able to turn around and find my sisters there. Especially Clarisse.”

Thalia gave a quiet laugh. “She believes you more than she lets on.”

He could see how carefully Thalia had asked. She was making room in her own map of family for different roads to meet without becoming the same road.

“Annabeth looks like herself in her sketch of it, too,” Thalia said. “Marking the coral arches and asking about the garden beds. Not becoming somebody else.”

“No,” Percy said. “She didn’t.”

Thalia nodded once, as if that settled something. Then she stood and tapped the letter with one finger. “Don’t forget to send it before the camp harpies decide the paper is a nesting material.”

“I’ll send it tonight.”

“Good.” She paused at the door. “And, Percy?”

“Yeah?”

“It makes sense that both places are home.”

He felt his shoulders drop. “Thanks.”

“I didn’t say it to be nice. I said it because it makes sense.”

“That’s even better.”

“I know.” She walked out before he could answer, but her footsteps went slowly down the porch, unhurried and sure.

Percy set the folded letter beside the sea-glass beads. After dinner, the water would know how to find Amphitrite.

---

Chiron's first question about the visit was not whether the garden had been beautiful.

"Did anyone get hurt?"

Percy had spent the walk up from the shore describing coral terraces, iridescent fish and the way Silena had taken a line of woven kelp and turned it into a working sleeve. He was almost offended that Chiron wanted to talk about injuries.

"Nobody seriously," he said.

"That wasn't the question."

Clarisse held out her forearm. The skin was bruised where she'd braced the nursery shelf, with a dark mark along the outside of her wrist. She had insisted the ache was nothing all the way back to camp. Now she looked annoyed at having to display it.

"The shelf moved," she said. "I held it while they fixed the line."

Chiron inspected the bruise without touching the sorest part. "Can you turn your wrist?"

She did.

"Make a fist."

She did that too, a little more slowly.

"You may have strained it. Have Will examine the range of movement and tell you whether it needs rest. And no spear drill tonight."

"It wasn't in my plan."

"Then we agree."

Silena produced her small envelope of woven kelp and the sketch Annabeth had made of the nursery. She laid them on the table beside the guest record, which had survived the return passage in a waterproof tube.

"This part wasn't ours to fix," she explained. "The gardener knew the structure. We helped with what she asked."

Chiron looked at the drawing. "Good. You're learning to distinguish assistance from ownership."

Percy wondered why every adult in his life seemed able to say things like that while looking directly at him.

Thalia leaned against the doorway. "The route home had a problem too."

Annabeth explained the charm that had warmed and lost part of its protective pattern. She described where they stopped, who replaced it and how the guard marked the faulty shell so no other visitor would use it. Chiron asked if the escort had remained with the whole group. She said yes. He wanted the precise hour, and she gave him the closest estimate from the measurements she'd been making.

"Did you feel unwell?" he asked Silena.

"No. I felt the charm get warmer, and I said so."

"That was the correct response."

"I know," she said, but Percy could tell she was pleased to hear it.

Chiron wrote a note in the camp ledger. The page had entries for storms, illnesses, missing equipment, broken fences and all the other things that made camp a place where people actually lived rather than a picture of a happy summer.

"May I add something?" Annabeth asked.

"Please."

She drew a line across the return route, pointing to the cold shelf where the charm had changed. "It's possible the local current caused the shift. The guard wasn't sure. We shouldn't write that as the cause unless the palace inspection confirms it."

Chiron altered his note. "Suspected current interaction, cause undetermined."

"Thank you."

Clarisse rolled her wrist once and winced. Percy noticed. She noticed him noticing.

"Don't start."

"I'm not starting."

"You made a face."

"I have a face. It happens."

Silena handed her a folded cloth. "Will's expecting us at the infirmary after dinner."

Clarisse frowned. "You told him already?"

"I asked whether he had time. You still decide whether to go, but Chiron recommended it."

"I heard."

"Good."

They carried the remaining papers to the Big House. Chiron gave Percy a small task: make an inventory of what belonged to camp and what had come from Atlantis. The borrowed practice spear had been returned. The garden sketch was Annabeth's. Silena's kelp sample was a freely given scrap, not living material removed from the nursery. The coral token belonged to Clarisse by Amphitrite's gift. The shell charm belonged to Annabeth, although the failed one had gone back with the escort for inspection.

Percy wrote it all down, adding the names of the people who had made the arrangements. He could feel how much work was hidden in the trip: ward checks before departure, a guarded route, a log to make sure everybody came home, and people whose entire day was interrupted when a visitor wanted to see a beautiful place.

He folded the page and carried it to Chiron.

"That's thorough," the centaur said.

"Annabeth helped."

"Then I shall thank both of you."

Percy waited. Chiron set down his pen.

"Was there something else?"

"Do you think I should write Amphitrite?"

"Why?"

"To thank her. But also to tell her about the charm and the bruise and the fact that Clarisse didn't love having her relationship written down wrong."

"Would you be writing on your sisters' behalf?"

Percy thought about the morning's conversations. "I guess I was going to."

"Then ask them whether they want you to."

It was irritating how often the answer to a complicated question was simply *ask*. Percy thanked him and went back to Cabin Three, where Silena was trying to decide how to dry the kelp without making it brittle. Clarisse had gone to see Will and left her coral token on the table.

"I want to send a letter," Percy said. "Not in your names unless you want me to."

Silena looked up. "What would you say?"

"That I was glad she invited us. That the garden matters to me. And that the trip home had something we need the inspection record for."

"That's fair."

"Do you want me to tell her you enjoyed the visit?"

"I'll write that myself if I want to."

"All right."

Clarisse returned with a loose wrap around her wrist and instructions to avoid heavy sparring for two days. Percy waited until she'd read the infirmary slip before asking about the letter. She wasn't angry at the question. She did want the description of the nursery repair to be accurate, and she insisted that her bruise was not a complaint against the gardeners.

"Write that I volunteered," she said. "And that I liked the token."

"Do you want her to know you kept it?"

"I told her I would."

Percy added the sentence. The letter became better once he stopped trying to make everyone sound grateful in exactly the same way.

Annabeth came by to compare the route record. She pointed to a place he'd described as *safe* and changed it to *safe under escort with working charms*.

"You're making it sound less impressive," he complained.

"I'm making it true."

He didn't change it back.

When he finally sealed the note, it held neither a perfect family portrait nor a list of grievances. It was a record of a real afternoon, including the ways they had been welcomed and the ways they had needed help. He thought Amphitrite might value that more than a letter insisting every moment had gone beautifully.


---

After dinner, Annabeth took over an end table in the pavilion, spreading her sketchbook and pencils in a patch of lantern light. Around them, plates clinked in the wash basins, Hermes campers accused a stump of moving, and Apollo campers tuned their instruments on the steps.

Percy approached the table with two mugs of tea. He set one beside Annabeth’s elbow.

“What is it?” she asked, eyes still on the page.

“Mint. Chiron said it might help after traveling.”

“Did he say that, or did you tell the kitchen you wanted mint?”

“Both.”

“Excellent.” She lifted the mug and took a sip, then gave him a small nod. “You can stay.”

“I was hoping.”

He sat across from her. The page held the reef garden as a memory: pale stone, branching coral, ribbon-kelp, and the palace reduced to glints and lines. The walkway was wider than Percy remembered, the arch a little too tall, and a broad clearing had been left open for the glassfish.

“You drew the path wider,” he said.

“I’m trying to fit the feeling in the space.”

“And leave room for the fish?”

“Exactly.” She added a few quick curves. The fish circled the clearing, one hovering near where Clarisse had stood.

“You’re putting her in the picture?”

“I’m putting the moment in the picture.”

“That’s not quite the same thing.”

“No.” Annabeth’s pencil paused. “But I think she should be there.”

Annabeth usually drew to understand how a building stood. The reef asked something different of her: its shapes were alive, and the current could alter them. Her lines stayed careful without trying to control the garden’s motion.

“I thought I’d sketch the palace first,” she said. “But I keep seeing the garden. The blue flowers, Silena holding that shell up to the light, Clarisse pretending not to be delighted when the spirit called her ‘the brave one with iron shoulders.’”

“Clarisse was definitely not delighted.”

“She asked if the spirit had said anything else.”

“Maybe she was checking whether it was going to insult her.”

“Maybe.” Annabeth shaded the path. “It was strange seeing you there. I knew you were home and we were guests, but the garden made room for us. Somehow those things didn’t contradict each other.”

“That’s what I was thinking.”

She turned the page slightly so he could see it better. “Do you like it?”

“Yeah. It looks like the garden felt.”

“Not exactly like it looked?”

“No. But if it looked exactly like it looked, it wouldn’t be your drawing.”

She studied him, considering whether he was teasing. “That’s almost a good answer.”

Mint steam curling off the surface, he sipped his tea. It had cooled enough to drink without burning his tongue. “You could send it to Amphitrite.”

“Maybe. I want her to see what I noticed, not just what she already knows is there.”

“She’d like that.”

Annabeth nodded, then carefully drew a line that curved around the open space. “I want to remember it properly.”

“You will.”

“I know. I want to remember it in a way that belongs to me.”

Percy understood. “Then draw the parts you want.”

“That’s what I’m doing.” She glanced up at him. “You should write the parts you want.”

He looked toward the path that led to the lake. The letter waited in the cabin. “I did.”

“Good.”

She touched the back of his hand. He turned his palm up, and she slipped her fingers into his while sketching with the other hand. It was not easy, but Annabeth had always been able to make a plan with imperfect tools.

---

Clarisse and Silena returned from the lake as the sky turned violet. Silena tucked the pink shell into her pocket; Clarisse’s hair was still damp.

“Dinner was terrible,” Clarisse said.

“She ate two bowls,” Silena told Percy. “For a fair sample.”

They came to the table. Silena pulled out a chair for Clarisse, who sat with a creak and a sigh. She leaned one elbow on the table. “What are you drawing?”

“The garden,” Annabeth said, shifting the sketchbook so they could see.

Silena bent over it. Her face changed as she followed the lines. The smile that came was slow and private. “There’s the place where the flowers opened.”

“I thought you might recognize it.”

“And this is the path where the fish followed Clarisse.”

Clarisse looked at the page. “Those fish did not follow me.”

“They followed your armor.”

“That sounds like following me.”

“I’m glad you agree,” Annabeth said.

Silena reached for the edge of the page, careful not to touch the drawing. “Could you leave a little space here?”

“For what?”

“I don’t know yet. Maybe another cluster of flowers. Maybe the place where I found the shell. I want to add something, if that’s all right.”

“Of course.”

Clarisse looked from the sketch to the shell in Silena’s pocket. “You’re going to draw the shell?”

“Not the shell. The spot where I picked it up.”

“Why?”

“Because I want to remember what it felt like when I found it.”

Clarisse looked away, but not before he caught the thoughtful crease between her brows. The moment at the lake returned to him: Clarisse walking beside Silena, neither speaking much, the shell traveling from one hand to the other without being treated like treasure or joke. He guessed they had found their own quiet way to talk about the visit.

Silena told them about an anemone bigger than a canoe. Percy insisted it had been bigger still; she said it had looked smaller from her side. Clarisse described a guard whose ribbonfish beard looked like a nest of eels. Annabeth promised to label him “serious beard” if she drew him.

Then Silena described the garden’s music, the small sounds of leaves and shells when the current moved through them. Clarisse added the feel of the smooth paths beneath her boots. Their memories did not line up: Silena recalled colors, Clarisse the guards, Annabeth the shifting path. Percy remembered where each of them had stood when they noticed something different.

The garden had held all of them without requiring them to see it the same way.

When the last of the sun disappeared, the Apollo campers began to play an old camp song with a new, lilting turn. Someone sang along and missed every third note. The harpies made a disapproving sound from the pavilion roof but did not interrupt.

“I thought the reef song was going to follow us,” Silena said. “I heard it in my head all afternoon.”

“Did you?” Clarisse asked.

“A little.”

“I heard the palace doors.”

“The doors?” Percy asked.

“The way they opened. Like a wave pulling back from shore.” She stared at the table, tracing the grain with one finger. “It was quiet in there. Not empty. Just quiet.”

“It can be,” Percy said.

“I know.” Clarisse’s hand closed over her wrist. “I liked being there.”

Silena’s expression softened, but she did not jump in to answer for her. She waited.

Clarisse glanced at the lanterns and mismatched benches, then looked at Percy.

“I felt welcomed,” she said. The words seemed to surprise her more than anyone else. “Not tolerated. Not displayed. Welcomed. Your mother made sure people knew who we were. She didn’t make a big show of it. She just—”

Clarisse stopped. Percy waited.

“She asked if I wanted to see the outer reef,” Clarisse finished. “Like it was my choice.”

“She wanted you to have a good visit,” Percy said.

“I know.” Her jaw tightened, then eased. “That’s the problem.”

Silena folded her hands around her tea mug. “Why is that a problem?”

“Because I liked it.” Clarisse looked down at her hands. There were small red marks across her knuckles from the arena that afternoon. “I liked the garden. I liked the way the water looked around the columns. I liked being treated like I could be there without owing anyone anything.”

“You don’t owe anyone anything,” Percy said.

Clarisse’s gaze came back up. “I know what you mean. But it’s easy to say when it’s your house.”

He could have said that it was her house too. He almost did. But the words would have made it sound as if he could grant her the place, as if he had a key she needed from him. He was her brother, not the prince of anything she had to earn.

“It’s my father and Amphitrite’s home,” he said. “Amphitrite invited you because you’re my sisters, and because they wanted you there. You don’t have to call it your house. You don’t have to want to go back. You don’t have to decide tonight what it means.”

Clarisse studied him. “And if I want to?”

“Then you can want to.”

There was no crown to offer her, no palace door he could open by himself. He could only be her brother, telling her a good thing did not have to be refused because it was frightening.

“Wanting it doesn’t make you disloyal to where we came from,” Silena said. “Or to camp.”

“I know,” Clarisse replied. Her voice was quieter now. “I’m trying to believe it.”

Percy tapped the back of her knuckles with one finger, careful not to make her feel comforted in front of an audience.

“You can be scared and still want it,” he said. “You can want to go back and still be yourself here. You don’t have to want it the right way.”

Clarisse looked at his hand, then gave him a brief, hard nudge with her elbow. “You’ve been practicing speeches underwater.”

“I haven’t.”

“That was smooth as glass.”

“I can make it worse if you want.”

“Please don’t.”

Silena smiled at them, eyes shining. “I think she means thank you.”

“I know what she means.”

“You don’t,” Clarisse said. “But you’re allowed to guess.”

The tension eased. The Apollo campers began a brighter song, and one of the Stolls spun an empty plate nearly into a chair.

Clarisse stood. “I’m going to stop him before he injures the chair.”

“The chair can defend itself,” Annabeth said.

“It’s a chair. It has no tactical experience.”

“Neither does Connor.”

“That’s why I’m going.”

Silena watched her go, her smile turning thoughtful.

“She’s been afraid to hope for a long time,” she said, low enough that the music nearly covered her voice.

Percy nodded. “I know.”

“You can’t make her feel safe.”

“I know that too.”

“But you can keep making room.”

Clarisse had reached Connor and was demonstrating the correct way to carry a plate. “I can do that,” Percy said.

Silena’s fingers brushed the shell through her pocket. “I’m glad we went.”

“Me too.”

“Even if the stew is terrible?”

“Especially then. We’ll have something to complain about when we’re back.”

---

After the music ended, the camp thinned into little groups and then into scattered lanterns. Chiron had gone to the Big House to finish a letter of his own. Thalia walked Annabeth back toward Cabin One, both of them talking about whether the sketch needed a scale mark. Silena left with Clarisse, the shell still tucked safely in her pocket.

Percy carried his letter to the lake.

Stars trembled on the lake. Percy sat at the end of the empty dock, folded the page into a packet, and placed it inside his message-shell. It warmed in his hands as a thread of seawater curled around it, shining faint blue.

“For Amphitrite,” he whispered.

The current took the message with a soft pull. It turned just below the surface like a leaf, then slipped toward the Sound.

He heard footsteps on the path behind him. Annabeth stopped a few feet away. She held the sketchbook to her chest and had her jacket draped over one arm.

“I thought you’d be here,” she said.

“I sent the letter.”

“Good.”

She sat beside him. The night was warm, and the cabin lights broke into gold ripples across the lake.

“I finished the garden,” she said. “Mostly.”

“Can I see?”

She opened the sketchbook. The reef garden had grown across the page while Percy was talking to Clarisse. The flowers were blue and silver; the coral branches reached around the path; the palace windows shone in the distance. Annabeth had added a small, curved figure beside the clearing, drawn with a thick shoulder line and a slight tilt of the head.

“Clarisse?” Percy asked.

“I didn’t write her name. I thought she might want to decide whether she wants to be in it.”

“That’s kind.”

“It’s accurate.” Annabeth rested the sketchbook between them. “I added the sound too, a little. Not with notes. Only lines around the shells, the way the water hummed through them.”

Percy traced the page with his eyes, careful not to touch it. “It feels like being there.”

“And like not being there?”

“Yeah.”

“I’m glad.”

Crickets and a night bird filled the quiet. Percy heard the camp breathing: a door closing, footsteps in grass, water at the dock posts.

“I was thinking about what you said before we left,” he said. “About being my chosen person.”

Annabeth turned the pencil in her fingers. “I said that because I wanted to be sure.”

“I know.”

“I don’t want to be invited as a sister because Thalia is invited. Or as a camp friend because everyone else is going. I wanted to know you wanted me there for me.”

“I did. I do.” He looked down at their hands resting between them. “I like that I have a family in Atlantis. I love Amphitrite and my father and Triton. I’m glad I got to show you the garden. But going there with you didn’t make you part of my past. It made you part of being there now.”

Annabeth’s face softened. “That’s what I wanted.”

“And coming back here didn’t make Atlantis disappear.”

“No.”

“Sometimes I worry I’m supposed to pick one place. Or that picking a place is the same as picking the people in it.”

“You don’t have to make home smaller to make room for me,” Annabeth said. “And I don’t have to make camp smaller to love Atlantis with you.”

Percy let the words settle. Home had once seemed like a single door, one family or another. Now it was a path bending around a reef, crossing a hill, reaching the lake, and continuing on. Nobody along it demanded he choose where to stop.

“You’re my chosen home,” he said. “Not instead of the others. With them. I want to keep coming back to you, wherever we are.”

Annabeth watched him steadily. “You’re mine, too. Not because I need you to replace anything. Only because I choose you, every day.”

The words were quiet, without ceremony. That made them feel more like theirs.

Percy leaned closer, giving her time to move away if she wanted. She did not. Their kiss was gentle and brief, a soft meeting of lips that carried no promise bigger than the one they had just said aloud. When they separated, Annabeth rested her forehead against his for a moment.

“Good,” she whispered. “Because I don’t want a throne. I want a place on the dock.”

“I can arrange that.”

“And the cabin floor when it rains.”

“That part comes with the cabin.”

“And the right to make corrections to your maps.”

“That’s a lot of power.”

“I can handle it.”

“I know.”

She smiled, and he felt the rest of the night loosen around them.

From somewhere up the hill came the thump of a door, then a sharp hiss as a lantern sputtered back to life. A moment later, Thalia called goodnight. Clarisse answered with something that sounded like “go to sleep.” Silena laughed in the background.

The lake took their voices and softened them into ripples.

Percy slid his hand into Annabeth’s. The stars above them were not the stars seen through the palace’s crystal ceiling, though he could imagine the same constellations looking down into the sea. The night held both pictures at once: the reef bright with its own secret light, and the camp dark beneath the pines.

Annabeth closed her sketchbook. “Tomorrow I’m going to draw the lake.”

“The lake doesn’t have a reef.”

“It has you.”

“That sounds like an unfair advantage.”

“It is. I’m the architect.”

He leaned his shoulder against hers. The water touched the posts and withdrew, touched them again, keeping its own unhurried time.

For tonight, home was a dock with splintered boards, a girl beside him, and the path behind them leading up to cabins full of family. Far below, another home held a letter that would soon reach the hands of a woman who had made room for him twice: once as a child, once as the boy he had become.

The sea remembered.

The sky kept its stars.

And Percy, between them, had room to belong to both.
