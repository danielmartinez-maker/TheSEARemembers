## Chapter 36: Five Months

*Five months had passed since Percy and Annabeth woke in the changed history. The first bolt quest still belonged to a future that might never arrive on schedule.*

---

Percy woke to the sound of the lake.

That was new. In his first days back in Cabin Three, he had woken to salt in the air, his sisters breathing across the room, and the feeling that the sea itself was holding its breath around him. The familiar family sounds belonged to a childhood he could not remember. Then the lake found him. Not all at once. First it was gulls before breakfast. Then it was the slap of small waves against the canoe dock. Then, after enough mornings of listening, he started waking a half-second before the conch horn, eyes already open, listening to water move against the shore like it was checking on him.

Today the conch didn't blow right away.

He lay there and stared at the ceiling. The cabin smelled like cedar and brine and the lemon soap he used to scrub his sword-calluses. His blanket was kicked down around his knees. A pair of socks — one blue, one gray — hung over the foot of the guest bunk Tyson had used on his forge visit, now empty again. Sunlight came in blue-green through the shell curtains Annabeth had helped him hang recently, after she'd declared his windows "a defensive liability and also depressing."

Five months of mornings at this camp. They had arrived before the first bolt quest in the life they remembered, but that didn't mean they knew how much time remained before any similar event here. Percy traced the blanket seam with one finger. He and Annabeth had returned in twelve-year-old bodies, and their families had already changed before either of them opened their eyes here. Thalia had been sixteen when they arrived; Percy could no longer rely on the old ages as a calendar.

In the original life, the summer solstice had meant a deadline. A stolen bolt. A war about to start. Here, that solstice was still ahead. They could prepare for what they remembered without assuming every clue would arrive on its old date.

Today, no accusation had reached camp.

He sat up and rubbed his face. His hair stuck up in seventeen directions. His feet hit the cold floor and he hissed, then laughed at himself, then pulled on jeans and a Camp Half-Blood shirt gone soft from a hundred washes and his blue hoodie with the frayed cuff.

The calendar Annabeth made was still tacked to the wall by his desk. She'd made one for both of them after their return, neat grids in black ink, red circles around patrol rotations and lessons and new-moon tides. Beside the coming summer she'd written in pencil, very lightly, like she didn't want to commit to ink: *old deadline?*

Percy tapped it with two fingers as he passed.

"Morning," he told the fountain in the corner. The water jumped a little higher, pleased to be noticed. He'd named the little hippocampus carved at its rim Skip. Annabeth said naming camp plumbing was unsanitary and sentimental. She'd also brought Skip a tiny laurel crown one afternoon when Percy was sick with a fever and couldn't get out of bed, so her opinions on the matter were officially void.

He stuck his head out the door.

The morning was gold and green and impossibly normal. Mist sat low over the strawberry fields. The Big House stood white against the hill, smoke curling from its chimney. Somebody — probably the Apollo kids — was already running the track, their laughter carrying clear across the grass. The lake glittered beyond the cabins, flat and bright as a held coin.

Percy breathed in. Salt. Strawberries. Bacon from the pavilion.

"No war before breakfast," he told himself. It was a narrower promise, but one he stood a chance of keeping.

Skip splashed behind him like applause.

He grabbed his towel and his battered copy of *The Sea Wolf* that Tyson had sent with a note — *FOR BRAVERY AND BOREDOM* — and headed for the showers, already hearing Cabin Six's door bang open across the way.

---

Annabeth Grace knew the exact date without looking at the calendar.

She knew it the way she knew the number of steps from Cabin One to the dining pavilion (one hundred forty-two, one hundred thirty-nine if she cut behind the forge), the way she had learned which floorboard in Cabin One, her present Zeus cabin, creaked loudest at 2 a.m., the way she knew Percy's tells when he was trying not to worry her (left thumb tapping his thigh, jokes getting faster).

She still marked their return on the calendar. It was an anchor for what she and Percy could remember themselves, beside all the older family dates other people had to explain. Each new square meant a day they had actually lived here.

Annabeth sat up in bed and reached automatically for the notebook on her crate. The page for today was already half-filled from last night: tide tables, lesson schedule, a sketch of a new footwork drill she'd thought up watching Clarisse, a list titled *THINGS THAT HAVE NOT HAPPENED* with three items crossed out and rewritten so many times the paper was thin.

She stared at that list a moment longer than she meant to.

Then she got dressed with military precision — camp shirt, jeans, sneakers double-knotted, hair pulled back in the high ponytail Thalia called her "commander mode" — and did her morning checks. Bunk made square. Dagger sheathed. Shield polished. Owl figurine from her dad facing the door. The little blue sea-glass turtle Percy had found for her on a shoreline walk sitting beside it, facing the same way, standing guard. She touched both for luck before she left. She would deny it if asked.

Outside, the air was cool enough to make her breath puff. Jake from the Hephaestus cabin jogged past with a toolbox, waved. The Demeter kids were already out in the fields, sleeves rolled up, singing something off-key about corn. Normal. So normal it almost itched under her skin.

Percy was waiting on the steps of Cabin Three with two towels over his shoulder and his hair still damp. He grinned when he saw her.

"You're up early," he said.

"You're counting days too," she said. It wasn't a question.

He shrugged, but his thumb tapped once against his leg. "Maybe."

They walked to breakfast together, shoulders bumping every few steps. They had walked this way in their old life too. Here, with shorter strides and too many bags of camp laundry, it had become navigation again.

The dining pavilion was loud in the best way. Sun striped through the open sides. The long tables were filling — Ares kids arm-wrestling over the orange juice, Aphrodite kids braiding each other's hair and critiquing everyone's sandals, Hephaestus kids with soot on their noses, Apollo kids still glowing from their morning run, Dionysus kids half-asleep into their pancakes. Mr. D sat at the head table behind a newspaper and a cup of Diet Coke, pretending he didn't know anyone's name. Chiron stood by the brazier, smiling that centaur smile that made new kids stop crying on their first night.

Annabeth scraped her bacon and eggs into the fire — *for Zeus*, she thought, and the prayer felt less like a performance than it had on their first mornings back — and took her plate to the Zeus table. Percy peeled off toward Poseidon, saving places for his sisters after their early drills, but he sat sideways so his knees almost touched hers across the aisle. Thalia dropped onto the bench beside Annabeth with a clatter, hair a black cloud, jacket smelling of pine sap and night air.

"Tell me that's not burnt," Thalia said, stealing half of Annabeth's bacon.

"It is," Annabeth said. "You ate it anyway."

"I'm a team player." Thalia stole Percy's toast too, leaning across Annabeth to do it. Percy made an outraged noise. "What? You live here now, Jackson. You know the tax."

Grover slid in across from them, tray piled dangerously high, Ella the harpy fluttering down beside him with her own small plate arranged in perfect color order. Ella had come to camp after their return, all red feathers and darting eyes and memorized book passages spilling out when she got nervous. Now she sat at the table like she belonged there, wings folded neat, talons clicking softly on the bench.

"Good morning," Ella recited carefully, like she'd practiced. "'Good morning' is a greeting used in — in ordinary days. Today is an ordinary day. The weather is fair. The porridge is — porridge is adequate."

"It's better than adequate," Grover said loyally, mouth full. "It's great porridge, Ella."

Clarisse arrived with Silena, both of them windswept from early drills. Clarisse dropped her helmet on the table hard enough to rattle cups. Silena set down a basket of warm rolls she'd talked the kitchen nymphs into and started distributing them with the efficiency of a general.

"Still quiet," Silena said softly, handing Annabeth a roll. Their eyes met. Silena knew what they were watching for; Percy and Annabeth had told her.

"Still quiet," Annabeth echoed. She tore the roll open. Steam rose. "And the sky hasn't fallen."

"Don't jinx it," Clarisse grunted, but there was no heat in it. She knocked her knuckles twice on the wooden table and then, after a beat, Percy did too, and then Grover, and then Ella copied them all very seriously, rap-rap-rap with one claw. Everybody laughed.

Annabeth laughed too, and felt something tight in her chest loosen one notch. She catalogued it the way she catalogued everything: *Breakfast, ordinary. Bacon stolen by sister. Boyfriend's knee against mine across the aisle. Porridge adequate.*

She wrote it down later. She wanted to remember exactly how safe it felt.

---

The trouble began over a pencil.

Annabeth had borrowed Thalia's black carpenter's pencil to mark the date on a scrap of card. The pencil was blunt, which made it better for the thick lines she wanted, but it belonged to Thalia and had lived in the same pocket of her jacket for almost a year. Thalia found the card when she came back to Cabin One for her shield.

FIVE MONTHS, Annabeth had written.

Beneath it were five little squares. Four had been shaded. She was drawing the fifth when Thalia asked, "What's that supposed to be?"

Annabeth kept coloring. "A marker."

"For what?"

"For how long we've been here."

Thalia knew which *here* Annabeth meant. It wasn't Cabin One or Camp Half-Blood. It was this history, the one her sister had woken up in with memories nobody around her could share. Five months since Annabeth and Percy had come back to bodies that were twelve years old, in lives that had already happened without their remembering them.

"You're making a countdown?"

"It's not a countdown."

"Then what happens when you color the last square?"

"Nothing. That's the point."

Thalia put down her shield. She had an hour before patrol, and she had not intended to spend any of it arguing about time. But Annabeth was using the same careful voice she used before a fight, and Thalia knew from experience that walking out now would make things worse.

"Can I look?"

Annabeth slid the card across the desk. The five squares didn't have labels, only the date and a small sketch of the lake. Thalia thought of the letters to Jason waiting in the approved Roman correspondence channel. Every day that passed made her more afraid of what he would assume about her silence. She had not been counting in squares, but she had been counting all the same.

"Why today?" she asked.

"Because it's the date."

"I mean, why make something?"

Annabeth set down the pencil. "I woke up and remembered it. Percy remembered it too. Nobody else has a reason to know. I thought I could mark it without having to make a speech."

Thalia turned the card over. The back was blank.

"Do you want me to do something?"

"No. I didn't ask you to."

Thalia looked at her. "You don't sound happy."

"I'm not unhappy."

"Annabeth."

"I'm irritated because you're acting as though the fact that I remembered a date means I'm preparing for a disaster."

Thalia was quiet. She had spent the previous months studying the two children's accounts of the old war and bracing for the first accusation from Olympus. When Annabeth carried a list, she looked for a plan. When Percy asked Chiron about an old prophecy, she looked for signs the trouble was coming. She didn't always distinguish ordinary worry from evidence.

"That's fair," she said.

Annabeth stared at her. "It is?"

"Don't look so shocked. I've been wrong before."

"Once. In Ohio."

"Twice, if we're counting the time I bought you a hat with a hole for your ponytail."

"That was a bad hat."

"You still wore it."

"I was seven. I had no standards."

Thalia laughed and sat on the edge of the desk. There were photographs in the trunk beneath Annabeth's bed, most of them from the childhood only Thalia could remember. She had shown Annabeth some of them. Every time she did, she watched her sister search for a feeling that might prove the old images belonged to her. It was an unfair thing to expect, and Thalia knew she'd done it anyway.

"Can I ask a different question?" she said.

"Yes."

"Is this a date you want to remember or a date you're frightened of forgetting?"

Annabeth considered for longer than Thalia expected.

"Both, I think. When we first came back, I was certain the important thing was figuring out what had changed. I thought if I didn't write down every difference, we'd lose the old life entirely. Now I can go a whole morning without comparing anything, and it scares me that I can do that."

Thalia held the card. "You haven't lost it."

"You don't know that."

"No. I don't. But I watch you and Percy talk. You remember things that happened there. And you're still learning how to live here."

Annabeth rubbed the ink off her fingers. "I didn't know I could love a childhood I don't remember."

Thalia looked toward the trunk. She remembered Annabeth as a little girl leaving chalk cities on motel pavement, asking questions while Thalia tried to pack enough food for them to keep moving. She remembered a child who'd been frightened and funny and capable of turning a cardboard box into a palace. The person standing in front of her was still that girl, but with a second set of memories. Sometimes Thalia wanted to tell Annabeth that nothing had been lost because it was easier than admitting how much had changed.

"I miss the little kid you were here," Thalia said. "Not because you're worse now. Because I knew her for twelve years, and I don't know whether you'll ever remember the same things I do."

Annabeth looked up. They had talked about that before, but usually after an argument. It was different to hear Thalia say it while neither of them was angry.

"I can listen," Annabeth said. "When you want to tell me stories."

"I know."

"And you don't have to make them prove something."

Thalia nodded. "I'll try."

The last square on the card was still empty. Annabeth reached for the pencil but stopped before she took it back.

"Do you want to shade it?"

"Why?"

"Because you've been here for these five months too. Even if you remember a different beginning."

Thalia thought about that. She hadn't been pulled through the change with the same memories. For her, the five months had begun with a younger sister asking questions no child should have had to know how to ask. The years before that were hers, and the days since belonged to all of them.

She shaded the last square. Her hand was less precise than Annabeth's. The graphite went over the edge in one corner.

"You ruined the line," Annabeth said.

"You're welcome."

Annabeth took the card back and placed it between the pages of her notebook, not in the section where she kept the prophecy timelines but beside a sketch of the cabin's front steps. Thalia watched her choose the place.

"Are we telling Percy?" Thalia asked.

"He already knows the date. I can show him the card if he wants to see it."

"Good."

Thalia picked up her shield. Annabeth returned the carpenter's pencil.

"You can keep it," Thalia said. "Mine isn't the only one in camp."

"You complained because I borrowed it."

"I complained because I didn't know what you were making."

"Then ask next time."

Thalia held up a hand. "That is an unreasonable expectation to put on an older sister."

Annabeth smiled and nudged her toward the door. They walked to morning lessons together, leaving the card safely in the notebook. Neither had decided whether five months deserved a celebration. It had been marked. That was enough for now.

---

Morning lessons were swordplay, and swordplay was Clarisse.

"Again," Clarisse said.

Percy wiped sweat out of his eyes and reset his stance. The arena sand was hot under his sneakers. The dummies stood in a row, straw poking from their necks, painted faces chipped from a hundred blades. Behind the fence, the morning class of younger kids sat cross-legged, whispering. Annabeth was there with a whetstone and a critical expression, ostensibly sharpening her dagger, actually taking notes on everyone's footwork.

Percy lunged. Cut. Parried the counter-swing from Clarisse's blunted spear. His arms burned. His shield felt like it weighed a hundred pounds. When he first returned, his twelve-year-old reach had betrayed the combat he remembered. Now his body was catching up to those older lessons — the turn of the wrist, the shift of weight, the breath out on the strike.

"Better," Clarisse said. It was the highest praise she gave before lunch. "Your left side's still open. Annabeth, show him."

Annabeth stood, brushing sand off her jeans. She picked up a practice sword and faced Percy, and the younger kids went *ooooh* because everybody knew about them, the Poseidon kid and the Zeus kid who trained like they shared a brain.

They sparred slow at first, then faster. Percy could hear Clarisse calling corrections — "feet, Jackson, feet" — but mostly he heard the rhythm: step-step-breath, Annabeth's blade kissing his shield, his answering cut, her grin widening every time he kept up.

"You're thinking too loud," she murmured, circling.

"You're monologuing," he shot back, and she laughed, and he used the opening — she'd taught him that trick in their old life, laugh and strike — and tapped her ribs with the flat of his blade.

"Point," called Grover from the fence, where he was supposed to be mending armor and was instead refereeing. Ella flapped excitedly beside him.

"'A hit, a very palpable hit,'" Ella quoted. "*Hamlet*, Act Five. The — the fencing scene ends in — in tragedy, but this is practice. Practice is not tragedy. Practice is — safe."

"Practice is safe," Grover agreed, handing her a strip of leather to braid. "Good memory, Ella."

Annabeth lowered her sword, breathing hard, eyes bright. "Not bad, Seaweed Brain."

"Not bad, Sparky."

Clarisse blew her whistle. "Break. Hydrate. Jackson, run the footwork ladder twice before you drink."

Percy groaned. Annabeth tossed him her water bottle anyway while Clarisse wasn't looking, and their fingers brushed, and it was such a small ordinary thing that Percy felt his throat go tight for no reason he could name.

He ran the ladder. Twice. Annabeth counted for him, swinging her feet from the fence, calling out, "Faster, slower, no, that was actually good, don't let it go to your head."

When he finished, she hopped down and walked the cool-down lap with him around the arena, shoulder to shoulder, out of earshot of the others.

"You feel it too, right?" she said quietly. "Today?"

Percy didn't pretend not to understand. "Yeah. Everybody's waiting for something. Even Mr. D keeps looking at the sky like he's checking for — I don't know. Mail."

Annabeth kicked a pebble. It skittered across the sand. "In the life we remember, the theft was already setting up the quest long before anyone told us. The accusation came later. We know the old sequence, but we don't know which parts are happening here." Her voice stayed light, but her hand found the hilt of her dagger. "It's weird to know that sequence and just... go to lessons."

"Is weird bad?" Percy asked.

She glanced at him. "I don't know yet. I keep checking my notebook like the date will change if I look away. Like I missed the instructions."

Percy thought about the calendar on his wall. *Old deadline?* "Maybe there are no instructions this time," he said. "Maybe we just get — Tuesday."

Annabeth huffed, but she bumped his shoulder. "Tuesday. You'd like that, wouldn't you? Just Tuesday forever. Swim lessons and toast tax."

"I'd like Tuesday with you guys," Percy said simply. "If we get another quiet Tuesday, I want to use it."

She was quiet for a step. Then: "Walk with me to the lake after lunch? Before archery? I want to — I just want to sit somewhere that isn't for training."

"Always," Percy said.

Behind them, Clarisse's whistle blew. The younger kids scattered shrieking with laughter. The sun climbed higher, hot and gold and entirely unremarkable, and Percy thought, *Okay. Okay, I'll take it.*

---

The lake at midday was Percy's church and Annabeth's puzzle.

She sat on the dock with her sneakers off and her feet in the water, watching him swim. He moved like something the water had invented for fun — no splash, no wasted motion, just glide and turn and surface, hair slicked back, grin white against sun-browned skin. Every so often he'd dive and not come up for long enough to make her nervous, and then he'd erupt beside the dock and splash her ankles and laugh when she yelped.

"You are so mature," she said, flicking water back at him.

"Twelve and thriving," Percy declared, floating on his back. "Come in."

"My notebook—"

"Will survive thirty seconds without you." He held up his hands. "I'll even keep your pencil dry. Sacred oath."

Annabeth looked at the lake. She was a good swimmer from the life she remembered — daughter of the sky here, but still stubborn in the water. Thalia had tested her after their return and declared her buoyancy acceptable — but the deep water still made her stomach flutter. Not fear, exactly. Respect. The sky was hers; the sea was his. She liked having a place that belonged to him, where she was the guest.

She set her notebook carefully inside her shoe, rolled her jeans to her knees, and slid in off the ladder. The water closed cool around her calves. Percy whooped and immediately got water up his nose.

They floated side by side under the dock's shadow, holding the pilings, kicking lazily. Dragonflies stitched the air above them. A canoe full of Apollo kids drifted past, singing badly. Someone on the far shore was playing a reed pipe, thin and sweet.

"Do you remember what you said after we got here?" Annabeth asked suddenly. "When I was trying to match every new detail to the old camp and we were standing at the lake after the first dinner and you said—"

"'At least we're lost together,'" Percy supplied. He smiled at the memory. "You said, 'We're not lost, we're on a scenic route to destiny,' and We both said we'd look at the facts before blaming anybody."

Annabeth laughed, water rippling around her shoulders. "I was so sure. I had it all planned. We'd get to camp, we'd train, and then — then the bolt would go missing or somebody would accuse somebody and we'd get a prophecy and go. I had a packing list. I had a *packing list*, Percy."

"I remember. Three kinds of rope."

"Four. The fourth was theoretical." She tipped her head back against the piling. Sun dappled through the boards. "And then the days kept being different. No accusation yet. No oracle calling our names. Just... breakfast. Lessons. Laundry."

Percy was quiet a moment, water lapping at his chin. "Does it bother you?"

She considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. Annabeth Grace did not lie to Percy Jackson; they had learned the cost of hiding worries from each other in their old life, and had renewed the promise over a stolen bag of marshmallows here. "Yes," she admitted. "And no. Yes, because I studied for a test and now I don't know which questions are still on it. And no, because —" She gestured at the lake, the dock, the camp spread green and gold up the hill. "Because I like Tuesdays. I like knowing Clarisse will steal my toast and Silena will have extra rolls and Grover will be late because he stopped to talk to a tree. I didn't know I could like that. I thought I needed the quest to matter."

"You matter without a quest," Percy said. He said it like it was obvious, like it was arithmetic. "You matter on Tuesdays."

Annabeth felt her eyes sting. She blamed the sun off the water. "Sappy, Jackson."

"Hydrated and sappy." He splashed her gently. "Hey. Whatever comes — late, early, never, whatever — we handle it, right? Like we handled the harpy in the laundry room and the hellhound drill and Thalia's cake incident."

"The cake incident was your fault."

"Allegedly." Percy grinned. Then, softer: "I'm glad we got today, I think. We got to just — be kids. Be us. If the future wants us, it knows where we live."

Annabeth looked at him — twelve years old, lake water beading on his shoulders, utterly earnest — and felt the tight notebook-page in her chest smooth out. She reached over and squeezed his hand where it gripped the piling. Water and sun and splinters.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Tuesday, then."

They stayed until their fingers pruned and the conch blew for afternoon lessons, and Annabeth wrote in her notebook with damp hands, ink feathering: *Lake, midday. Water cool. P talked me down from prophecy spiral. Tuesday is enough.*

---

Thalia Grace liked the afternoon best, when the light went sideways and the pine's shadow stretched long across the hill and the whole camp smelled like sun-warmed grass and pine.

She spent it on patrol, the way she always did. Spear across her back, shield loose on her arm, boots dusty. It was supposed to be border duty — walk the line, check the wards, report anything weird — but during this run of quiet days, patrol had become something else. A walking conversation with camp itself.

She started at the pine. She always started at the pine.

"Hey," she told it quietly, pressing her palm to the bark. The boundary pine stood older than all of them, needles whispering, camp wards humming through its roots. Thalia had a theory that the pine remembered every kid who'd ever touched it. She touched it every day so it wouldn't forget her. "Quiet again. Good. Keep it that way."

The wind moved through the branches. Somewhere below, a conch blew faintly. A laugh. A whistle.

Thalia walked the perimeter slowly, the way Chiron taught. She checked the wards out of habit, fingertips tingling where the magic ran. All solid. All humming their low, steady song. No monsters. No strange scents on the wind. Just strawberries from the fields, smoke from the forge, lake water and pine.

She found Grover in the meadow halfway around, sitting cross-legged in a patch of clover with his reed pipes in his lap and Ella perched on a rock beside him, both of them surrounded by the most ridiculous audience: three rabbits, a squirrel, and a very attentive chipmunk.

"Don't stop on my account," Thalia said, dropping onto the grass.

Grover startled. "Thalia! I was — this is nature communion. Advanced. Chiron said—"

"Chiron said practice your pipes and stop bribing the naiads with crackers," Thalia said, but she was grinning. She nodded at Ella. "Hey, bookworm."

Ella bobbed her head, feathers ruffling in the breeze. "'Hello' is — is also a greeting. Thalia patrols the borders. The borders are secure. 'Secure' means safe. Safe is —" She paused, searching, then beamed. "Safe is good."

"Safe is great," Thalia said. She lay back in the grass and stared at the sky. Blue on blue on blue. No storm clouds. No ominous birds. Just a red-tailed hawk circling lazy over the woods, minding its own business.

She remembered the worried days after their return so clearly it hurt — Annabeth's knee bouncing through explanations, Percy reaching for her hand, Grover talking until somebody finally smiled, Thalia gripping her spear too tight because putting it down meant thinking about what they had told her.

Only Percy and Annabeth remembered the old war. Thalia knew the shape they had described to their small circle: the lightning thief, the accusation, the quest. Thalia had braced for it the way you brace for a punch — shoulders up, jaw set. She'd trained the two twelve-year-olds like their lives depended on it, because she believed their lives *would* depend on it, and soon.

And between all that preparation... Tuesdays. Ordinary ones. Pancakes and patrols and laundry duty and Percy shrieking when the water was cold and Annabeth color-coding the training schedules and Clarisse pretending she didn't care and Silena remembering everyone's birthdays and Grover learning Ella's favorite crackers and Ella learning everyone's names.

Thalia hadn't unclenched all at once. It was slower than that. A loosened finger here. A breath there. A recent morning when she'd woken up and realized she'd slept through the night without her spear in her hand for the first time since she was seven, and she'd cried a little, quietly, so nobody would hear.

"You're thinking loud," Grover said, nudging her ankle with his hoof. "Goat senses."

Thalia snorted. "Just counting."

"Days?"

"Yeah." She plucked a stem of grass and chewed it. "You ever feel guilty about being happy when you were supposed to be — I don't know. Ready?"

Grover was quiet, which meant he was thinking, which meant it mattered. He set his pipes down carefully. "My dad used to say readiness isn't the same as waiting for bad things," he said at last. "He said the best protectors are the ones who know what ordinary feels like. So they know what they're protecting." He glanced at Ella, who was now braiding clover into the squirrel's fur with intense concentration. "I think — I think these ordinary days *are* readiness. Just not the kind in the stories."

Thalia stared at him. "When did you get wise, goat boy?"

"Last week," Grover said solemnly. "It was a Tuesday."

Thalia laughed so hard the rabbits fled. Ella looked up, startled, then laughed too — a bright, rusty harpy giggle that made both of them laugh harder. The hawk cried once overhead. The clover smelled sweet. The wards hummed.

"Okay," Thalia said, sitting up, wiping her eyes. "Okay. Tuesday readiness. I can live with that."

She finished her patrol with a spring in her step, and when she passed the pine again on her way down, she patted it twice.

"Keep holding, old girl," she murmured. "We've got the rest."

---

The forge was too hot for late afternoon, but Clarisse liked it that way.

She stood at the grindstone with her sleeves rolled up, working the edge of her spearhead while Nyssa from Hephaestus checked the temper and talked about carbon content like it was gossip. Sparks drifted. The bellows wheezed. Outside the open doors, the camp went about its golden ordinary business — kids heading to archery, someone shouting about a lost sandal, the smell of dinner already starting from the pavilion kitchens.

Silena sat on an overturned bucket nearby, mending a strap on Clarisse's shield with neat stitches, humming under her breath. She'd been there for twenty minutes without being asked. That was Silena. She showed up with needle and thread and snacks and stayed until you remembered you liked company.

"You've been quiet," Silena said at last, not looking up from her stitching. "Quiet-quiet, not mad-quiet. There's a difference. Mad-quiet is louder."

Clarisse snorted and kept grinding. "Just thinking."

"Dangerous."

"Shut up." But Clarisse was smiling. Before these long camp days together, she wouldn't have smiled. She'd been ready to tell Percy and Annabeth exactly where they could put their prophecies — her youngest brother with his worried eyes, his girlfriend with her big plans. Ready to be the hardest, the least frightened, the one who didn't need anybody. Then Percy had thrown up on her shoes during capture the flag orientation (accident, mostly) and Annabeth had handed her a rag without flinching and said, "Welcome to camp, we've already lowered property values," and Clarisse had been doomed.

"Still quiet," Clarisse said abruptly.

Silena's needle paused. "Yeah."

"Percy and Annabeth told us about their old solstice. The theft. The war talk. We're not there yet, but I keep waiting for the first sign. I had my bag packed. I was gonna volunteer to go. I was gonna—" Clarisse stopped, jaw working. The grindstone sparked. "I spent three years being the kid who was ready to fight and now there's nothing to fight and I don't know what to do with my hands."

Silena tied off her stitch with a neat knot and bit the thread. She held up the shield strap, inspected it, nodded. "You could do with your hands what you've been doing. Teaching boneheads not to hold swords backwards. Stealing bacon. Braiding Ella's head feathers last week when she asked — don't deny it, I saw the pink ribbon."

"It was tactical," Clarisse muttered, ears red. "Camouflage."

"It was adorable." Silena stood and crossed to her, shield in hand. She didn't touch Clarisse — Silena knew better than to touch Clarisse uninvited — she just held the shield out so Clarisse could see the repair. Clean. Strong. "You're still a fighter, Clarisse. You're just also — the person Grover trusts to carry Ella across the creek when the current's fast. The person Annabeth asks to check her shield wall. The person Percy imitates when he thinks no one's looking." She smiled, soft and certain. "War doesn't get to decide what you're for."

Clarisse stared at the shield strap. Her throat felt thick. She hated that. She hated feelings before dinner; they ruined her appetite. "Since when are you the wise one?"

"Since always. You just weren't listening." Silena bumped her shoulder. "Come on. If we hurry we can steal the good bench at dinner before the Apollo kids harmonize all over it."

Clarisse set down her spearhead, lifted her shield, tested the strap. Solid. She clapped Silena on the shoulder once, hard, which in Clarisse language meant *thank you, I love you, don't make it weird.*

Silena, fluent, grinned and made it weird anyway by handing her a cookie from her pocket. "For the road."

"Dinner's in twenty minutes."

"Pre-dinner cookie. It's called strategy."

They walked out of the forge together into the gold light, bickering about cookies, and the camp bell rang, and the shadows stretched long and peaceful across the grass, and neither of them looked at the sky for enemies even once.

When Silena returned to Cabin Three, she found Percy sitting on the floor in front of the storage chest, holding a photograph upside down.

That was unusual enough. Percy normally treated the storage chest as the place where Silena kept things he was supposed to ask before borrowing. She had labels for the fishing gear, the spare uniforms and the jars of salt they brought from Atlantis. The photographs were at the very bottom, wrapped in cloth to keep out the damp.

"Did you find something?" she asked.

Percy turned the picture around. "I was putting back the sail mending kit. The cloth under it came loose."

She examined the photograph. A much younger Percy sat on a stone bench with a wooden sword across his knees. His hair was shorter. There was a missing front tooth and an expression of exaggerated seriousness he must have copied from Triton. Silena stood behind him in a dress she'd disliked from the moment Amphitrite chose it, trying to hold his shoulders still for the picture. Clarisse was visible at the edge of the frame, about to leap in.

Silena had forgotten the photograph existed. She remembered the afternoon, though. Percy had insisted he could stand guard outside the palace nursery. He was five or six, and Clarisse had convinced him that keeping the sea gulls away from the windows counted as an official duty. The royal photographer had come for a family portrait and found all three of them arguing about whether the gulls were enemy scouts.

"Do you remember this?" Percy asked.

"Yes."

"What happened?"

Silena settled beside him on the floor. "You wanted to wear your practice helmet. It was too large, so you took it off for the picture. Clarisse said that meant you were abandoning your post."

"I can imagine that."

"You tried to get Triton to sign an order declaring the nursery a military station. He told you to ask Amphitrite first."

Percy laughed. The laugh was real. Silena had been afraid he'd look at the photograph as if it were another test, but he was looking at Clarisse's half-visible hand.

"Why is she holding a fish?"

"She wanted to prove she'd captured an actual enemy scout."

"What happened to the fish?"

"She put it back in the water after the photograph. She insisted it had been released under a treaty."

Percy smiled and turned the picture over again. There was a date on the back and a note in Amphitrite's hand: *My three brave nuisances*. Silena had hated the word *nuisances* when she was little because it sounded as though the queen had grown tired of them. Now she could hear the affection in it.

"Do you want to keep looking?" she asked.

Percy considered. "One more."

She unwrapped the cloth. The photographs were arranged by year, though some had fallen out of order when the chest traveled from Atlantis to camp. There were summer festivals under green water, birthdays with cake that floated in enchanted glass bubbles, training days and a picture of Triton looking appalled because Percy had managed to decorate his armor with colored shells.

Silena picked up the last one. "He still complains about this."

"Really?"

"Every time you mention the festival. He said he'd been trying to look serious for the council and you made him look like a children's parade."

"Was he angry?"

"Only until Amphitrite laughed. Then he pretended he had planned it."

Percy studied Triton's face. His older brother looked younger than he did during the recent visit to camp, but the line between his eyebrows was the same. Percy had begun learning what that expression meant in the five months he'd spent here. It usually meant Triton was worried and wanted everybody to believe he was merely disappointed.

"I wish I remembered doing that," he said.

Silena didn't answer immediately. She had told Percy stories from childhood before and watched him make jokes to keep the sadness from showing. Today he'd asked. That mattered, but it didn't give her a way to make the missing years return.

"I liked it," she said. "You were little and thought his armor needed cheering up. I'm glad I remember, even if you don't."

Percy looked at her. "You don't mind that you always have to tell me?"

"I mind that we lost the easy part. I used to say two words and you'd know the rest. Sometimes I still do, and then I see your face and remember that I have to start at the beginning."

He lowered the photograph.

"I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize. I can feel sad about something without making it your fault."

The cabin door banged. Clarisse entered with a basket of clean towels and stopped when she saw them on the floor.

"Oh, gods. Did you find the fish picture?"

Percy held it up. "Apparently you negotiated a treaty."

Clarisse dropped the towels and came over at once. "That was a legitimate exchange. The fish had stolen a ribbon from my training spear."

"How?"

"It jumped."

"Fish don't steal ribbons by jumping."

"This one did."

Silena laughed so hard she had to put down the album. Clarisse snatched the picture, inspected it and sat beside Percy to explain exactly how the fish had been captured. Her version involved a daring chase, three guards and a rescue operation. Silena remembered two children arguing beside a shallow pond.

"You're making it sound heroic," she said.

"It was heroic."

"Percy fell into the pond."

"He was providing a diversion."

Percy laughed. The three of them sat among the photographs until the sun slanted through the cabin window and turned the glass panes blue. Clarisse described a festival race Silena didn't remember, Silena corrected the date of a palace concert and Percy asked questions when he wanted to know more. When the stories became too many, he said so. His sisters stopped.

They put the photographs back in order. Percy chose the picture of Triton wearing shells to keep on the cabin shelf, where everyone could see it. He left the childhood sword portrait in the album.

"I like that one," Clarisse said, disappointed.

"So do I. But I don't know the little kid in it yet."

Silena understood. He could be curious about his childhood without being ready to display every image of a person he hadn't learned to remember. She put the picture in its protective sleeve.

"What do you want to put beside Triton's picture?" she asked.

Percy looked around the cabin. There was a recent photograph from the forge, Silena's braid crooked and Clarisse glaring at the camera with a smear of grease on her cheek. He took that one and stood it beside the festival portrait.

"This," he said.

Clarisse examined the pair. "I look terrible in both."

"You look exactly like you," Silena said.

"That's what I mean."

They spent the next ten minutes arguing over who would dust the shelf before the photographs went back. Percy lost because he had been the one to open the chest. He didn't complain much, and when Silena handed him the cloth, he used it carefully.

Later she caught him looking at the two photographs again. She thought about offering another story. Instead she went to wash the mugs they'd left out from breakfast. He knew where to find her if he wanted one.

Grover's favorite place at sunset was the strawberry fields, and Ella was learning why.

They sat at the edge of the rows with a basket between them, supposed to be picking for dinner dessert, mostly eating. The sun hung low over the lake, turning the water to hammered copper. The air smelled like warm berries and cut grass. Bees droned drunk and happy. Up on the hill, the pine stood black against the light.

"Try this one," Grover said, holding out a berry. "This one's perfect. See the shape? No white at the top. All red. That's how you know."

Ella took it delicately in her talons and examined it from every angle, head tilting. "'Red as — as blood,'" she said thoughtfully. "No. That's — that's the wrong book. 'Red as — as summer.' Summer is — summer is warm. Summer is strawberries. Summer is —" She popped the berry in her beak and her eyes went wide. "Summer is sweet!"

Grover laughed, ears wiggling. "Summer is sweet. You got it."

Ella carefully placed another berry in the basket, then another in her own small pile, arranged by size. She'd gotten so much steadier since arriving. The first week, she'd startled at every dropped tray, recited whole pages when anyone raised their voice, slept under the table in the satyrs' room at the Big House instead of a bunk. Now she had a bunk — with a nest of blankets she'd arranged herself — and a job (library sorting, which she loved with a passion that frightened the Apollo kids) and friends who saved her the color-sorted crackers.

"Grover?" she said suddenly, not looking up from her sorting. "The — the calendar. Annabeth keeps counting. Annabeth says — says she remembers a quest. When will it start? Does the — the calendar know?"

Grover's smile faded a little. He plucked a blade of grass and twisted it. He'd been wondering the same thing, in the way satyrs wonder — not with lists like Annabeth or jokes like Percy, but with his hooves in the dirt, feeling for tremors. The earth had been quiet. Pan-quiet, but also just... quiet. No monster surges. No oracle dreams. Chiron's smile lines had deepened. Mr. D had almost — *almost* — learned a new kid's name.

"I don't know," he admitted. "Maybe the old dates don't fit the changed story. Maybe we need another clue." He looked at Ella. "Does it scare you? That it might still come?"

Ella considered, feathers puffing in the cooling air. "'It is — it is a far, far better thing that I do, than I have ever done,'" she whispered. "*A Tale of Two Cities*. Sydney Carton. He was — he was afraid, but he — he went anyway. Because — because of love." She met Grover's eyes. "I am — I am sometimes afraid. Of loud. Of — of leaving. Of the sky when it is too big. But — but I have — I have crackers. And library. And — and you. And Thalia says I fly very fast now. Very fast is — is useful."

Grover felt his chest go warm. "Very fast is very useful."

"So if — if the story comes," Ella said carefully, like setting down something fragile, "we will — we will do the better thing. Together. Because of — because of love. And strawberries." She added the last part firmly, like it belonged in the book.

Grover blinked hard. Stupid pollen. "Yeah," he managed. "Yeah, Ella. Strawberries and love. That's the whole plan."

Ella nodded, satisfied, and held out a berry. "Perfect one. For you. For — for readiness."

Grover ate it. It tasted like sun and dirt and these days of ordinary miracles.

The conch blew for dinner, long and low. They stood, Grover shouldering the basket, Ella fluttering up to ride the thermals for a moment — just a moment, wings spread copper-gold against the sunset — before landing lightly on the fence post beside him.

"Race you?" Grover offered.

Ella tilted her head. "'Run, run, as fast as you can,'" she quoted, crouching. "*The Gingerbread Man*. He — he loses. I will — I will win, but — but kindly."

They raced down the hill together, laughing, berries bouncing, sunset at their backs.

Chiron was repairing a broken chair when Annabeth came to the Big House.

The chair belonged to a classroom on the lower hill. One of its legs had split along the grain after a camper stood on the seat to see over the heads of taller children. Chiron could have handed it to the workshop and had it repaired before tomorrow. Instead he'd borrowed wood glue and a clamp and was doing the work himself because he'd promised the instructor he would return it.

"Do you need help?" Annabeth asked from the doorway.

"I have the pieces held in place. What I need is time."

She looked at the clamp. "How long?"

"Long enough to be inconvenient."

She smiled, then became serious. "I wanted to ask about the dates. Percy and I were talking about the old quest."

Chiron set down the rag in his hand. He had known this conversation would come. The children had been at camp five months, and the days they feared from their remembered lives were approaching without quite matching the events they expected.

"Do you want to know whether anything has changed?" he asked.

"I want to know whether you've heard anything from Olympus."

"Nothing that establishes the particular accusation you described. No request for a bolt investigation. No summons for either of you."

Annabeth absorbed the answer. Chiron had told her similar things before, and he understood why she asked again. Every new day without the old disaster could be good news or evidence that a different one was coming.

"Can you show me the messages you've received?" she asked.

"Some of them. The private ones belong to their senders. I can show you the notices that affect camp's preparedness."

He took a folder from the shelf and laid it beside the chair. The pages contained the familiar business of running a place that sheltered demigods: a request to strengthen a boundary marker, a question about a late-arriving camper, a report of a monster sighting beyond the main road. There were things Chiron wanted to monitor. None matched the sequence Annabeth remembered.

She read carefully, placing each page back in order. He didn't interrupt. When she finished, she rested her fingertips on the folder as though trying to feel some hidden answer through the paper.

"It would be so much easier if the change meant we were safe," she said.

"Would you believe me if I told you it did?"

"No."

"Then you already understand why I won't say it."

She looked up, frustrated. "I'm not asking for false reassurance. I'm trying to decide what we should do with what we know."

"Then separate what you remember from what you can verify today. The remembered future may tell you what you're capable of enduring. It can't tell us which specific events will happen in this history."

Annabeth considered that. Her first plan had been to follow the old quest almost exactly, preparing the same supplies and watching for the same warning signs. She had imagined that her memory would make it possible to save people she'd lost. Five months of changed small details had taught her how little the sequence could be trusted.

"Does that make the memory useless?" she asked.

"No. You and Percy know things nobody else has experienced. We take your account seriously. But if we act as though every detail must repeat, we may neglect the people and problems actually in front of us."

He nodded toward the broken chair. Annabeth looked at it, then back at him.

"You're not comparing a war to furniture."

"No. I'm saying the chair still needs repairing even if we expect a battle. Tomorrow a student will need somewhere to sit. I promised a safe chair and intend to keep that promise."

She studied the clamp. "You used too much glue."

Chiron laughed. "Perhaps. Would you like to inspect the joint?"

She did. She tested the alignment without moving the clamp, then pointed out a small excess bead of glue that would need to be removed before the finish dried. They worked on it together with a cloth and a narrow tool. She found herself feeling useful in a way that didn't require knowing anything about the future.

When the joint was clean, she asked whether Percy could come with her to examine the old prophecy copies later that week.

"Of course," Chiron said. "We'll agree on a time and make sure the documents are available. I'd like you to bring questions you can answer from the records rather than questions that expect them to explain the entire change."

"That's a very Chiron thing to say."

"I've had many years of practice."

She thanked him and started toward the door, then stopped.

"Did you ever wish you could know the next five months exactly?"

Chiron looked at the chair. He had taught enough young heroes to know how tempting it was to imagine certainty could protect them.

"Frequently. Particularly when it would have allowed me to prevent somebody's suffering."

"And if you could?"

"I'd still need to ask what they wanted, what I actually knew, and what consequences my actions would have. Knowing a thing may happen isn't the same as knowing everything required to stop it."

Annabeth nodded slowly. The answer wasn't a map. It was a way of recognizing when a map had stopped being useful.

She went out into the early evening. The dining pavilion was beginning to fill, and she could hear Clarisse arguing about whether the shortcake was going to be served before or after the offerings. Percy was waiting at the foot of the steps, holding a clean towel he'd been sent to deliver.

"Did you get an answer?" he asked.

"Several. None that solve everything."

"Sounds like Chiron."

She told him about the messages, the chair and the folder of notices. He listened without asking whether the world was finally safe. At the end, she mentioned that they could inspect the old prophecies together.

"After dock duty," he said.

"Why after?"

"Because Clarisse assigned me dock duty first."

Annabeth laughed. She wanted to object on principle, but the docks also needed looking after, and he'd already agreed to be there.

"Then after dock duty," she said.

They walked toward dinner. She had been hoping for certainty and found a chair waiting for its glue to dry. She thought she could live with that for one evening.

Dinner at the end of that ordinary Tuesday tasted like everything good.

Percy noticed it first because Percy noticed food the way Annabeth noticed architecture — structurally, spiritually. The tables groaned: roast chicken with lemon, buttered corn from the Demeter fields, fresh bread Silena had supervised, strawberry shortcake with berries Grover and Ella had picked, nectar and ambrosia in careful measures for the scrapes of the day. The pavilion glowed with torchlight as the sun went down, turning everybody gold. The brazier fire crackled. The wine-dark sky — Percy had read that somewhere and finally understood it — deepened overhead, first stars pricking through.

He sat with his sisters and the people he thought of as chosen family. Saying it still felt big enough to catch in his throat. Annabeth on his left, Thalia across from him stealing his corn, Grover on his right with Ella perched on the bench-back behind him braiding his hair with clover, Clarisse and Silena at the end arguing about whether shortcake needed a second biscuit layer (it did, Silena was right, Clarisse would die before admitting it).

Chiron stopped by their cluster on his rounds, resting a hand briefly on Annabeth's shoulder, on Percy's. "Look at you," he said quietly, smiling. "You have grown."

"Vertically?" Percy asked. "Annabeth says I haven't."

"You have grown in every direction that matters," Chiron said, eyes twinkling, and moved on.

Mr. D, passing with his Diet Coke, paused — actually paused — and squinted at Ella. "Harpy. Library girl. You reshelved the — the thing. The poetry."

"'The collected poems,'" Ella said promptly, talons twisting nervously. "Alphabetical by — by author, then by — by title. The — the Dickinson was misfiled under — under despair. I fixed it."

Mr. D grunted. "Good. Don't let the Apollo brats touch it." He walked away. Grover mouthed *he knew your name* at Ella, and Ella glowed so bright Percy thought she might lift off.

After the offerings — Percy scraping in his chicken with a silent *thanks, Dad, for Tuesdays* — after the eating and the seconds and the thirds (Thalia), after the singing (Apollo kids, mercifully brief), Annabeth stood up.

She didn't plan to. Percy could tell by the way she glanced at him, startled at herself. But she stood, cup in hand, and the table quieted, because Annabeth standing meant words, and Annabeth's words mattered.

"So," she said. Her ears were pink. "Today."

A murmur. Clarisse knocked twice on the table for luck. Ella copied her.

"I had a speech," Annabeth admitted. "In my notebook. It was — it was very organized. Three points. Historical context." Laughter. Percy grinned up at her, proud fit to burst. "But — it's just this. We got here and every morning I braced for the sky to fall. Today, it didn't. And I kept thinking that meant we'd failed some test. Like we'd missed our cue."

She looked around the table — Thalia, fierce and soft; Clarisse, arms crossed but listening; Silena, smiling; Grover, nodding; Ella, swinging her feet; Percy, hers.

"But today was just — breakfast and training and the lake and patrol and the forge and strawberries. And it was good. It was really good. So maybe — maybe the point wasn't the deadline. Maybe the point was this. Learning how to be — us. Before whatever comes."

Thalia stood too, cup raised. "To Tuesdays," she said.

"To Tuesdays," Clarisse echoed, standing, and Silena, and Grover, and Ella fluttering up with her tiny cup of nectar held very carefully in both talons.

Percy stood last, shoulder to shoulder with Annabeth. Their cups clinked. The torchlight caught her gray eyes — storm-colored, his favorite weather — and he thought about their return and the changing calendar clues, lake water and bacon tax and every ordinary day they had lived here.

"To family," Percy said quietly. "Whatever comes, we face it as family."

"Family," they repeated. Ella said it twice, like she was memorizing the shape: "Family. Family is — family is people who — who save you crackers. And — and count days with you. And — and stay."

They drank. The brazier popped. The stars came out thicker. Somewhere the harpies — the wild ones, not Ella — called goodnight over the woods. The pine whispered. The lake lapped. The wards hummed low and steady.

No accusation from Olympus reached them that night. No oracle cried out. No war began.

There was only camp, and dinner, and each other — seven kids at the end of a table, laughing, full and safe and together, with all the time in the world stretched gold before them.

Percy wanted to believe they could keep building this life without having to sacrifice the one they remembered. He didn't know how. But he knew whom to ask for help.

Annabeth had left a list on the corner of the table: the ward inspection, the old prophecy copies, and a note to ask Chiron about the coming solstice. Percy folded it and put it in his pocket.

Clarisse noticed. "You working tomorrow?"

"After breakfast."

"Good. You're on dock duty first."

He looked at Annabeth, hoping for support. She took the last roll instead.

The plates needed clearing, and Silena had already put a stack into his hands. He carried them toward the kitchens. The list could wait until the dishes were done.

At the kitchen door he found Annabeth struggling with a tray that was wider than her shoulders. She had agreed to help clean up, then taken the largest one because nobody had specifically told her not to.

"Want an extra pair of hands?" he asked.

"Yes, actually." She shifted it toward him. "The handles are in the wrong places."

"That sounds like an architectural complaint."

"It is."

They carried the tray between them. A harpy pointed them toward the wash station and told them not to leave any plates balanced on the edge. Percy followed the instruction. Annabeth was looking at the corner where the shelves joined the wall, already thinking about how to make the space easier to use.

"Don't start drawing," he said. "We have to finish the dishes first."

"I wasn't."

"You were calculating."

"That's different."

He handed her a towel. They worked until the last plate was dry. The date was still in her notebook, the future still uncertain, and the kitchen floor still needed sweeping. He took the broom.
