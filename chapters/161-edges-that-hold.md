## Chapter 161: Edges That Hold

Percy Jackson woke before the conch because the cabin was already awake in the way it got when nobody wanted to admit they were awake.

The light told him first. Tempest Hall held morning in its high window in a thin September plank, dust turning slow inside it, cooler at the edges than the gold of a week ago. The air smelled of wet stone and lemon oil and the mineral damp that lived in the tile, and underneath it the warm metallic breath of forge-smoke, because Tyson had slept four nights in the corner bunk and the whole west end of the cabin had started to smell like somebody had been making things. Percy lay still with his eyes half open and listened to the particular quiet of five people pretending to sleep.

To his left, the pool moved water through the channels under the floor with its low salt-rain hush. Somebody — Clarisse, by the rhythm — was turning over too carefully on the bunk above the spear rack, springs giving one soft complaint and then stopping, as if she had decided halfway through the turn that moving was a kind of confession. Across the room Silena's breathing had gone into the false-even pattern she used when she was awake and doing arithmetic in her head. And in the corner bunk, the visitor's bunk, Tyson breathed slow and deep and entirely honest, the way he did everything, with one arm over his eyes against the window light and the round white rock from the forges centered exactly on his folded forge shirt at the foot of the mattress.

Today was Tuesday. Ferry morning.

Percy stared at the ceiling beam where somebody — Silena, two summers ago, bored during a storm — had burned a tiny trident into the wood with a heated pin. He had looked at that mark a hundred mornings. This morning it seemed to be waiting for him to do something. He sat up quietly and swung his legs over the side. He wore the faded orange camp shirt he had slept in and soft gray sweatpants gone thin at the knees, feet finding the tile cool enough to wake him the rest of the way. On the shelf above his bunk his day clothes were already laid out from the night before, folded with uncharacteristic care because Silena had folded them while lecturing him about leaving creases to do their own work: clean camp shirt, jeans with the cuffs still damp from yesterday's lake, spare socks, the lake pebble and yesterday's cookie wrapper already transferred to the jeans pocket by lamplight so the morning inventory would come out right.

He crossed to the corner bunk and stood there a moment looking at his brother.

Tyson in sleep looked younger than his size. Six and a half feet of broad shoulder and scarred knuckle, one large brown eye closed, mouth a little open, hair flattened on one side where the pillow had held it. The good towel — the thick blue visitor towel Silena kept washed separately and never let anybody else touch — hung straight on its hook above him. His boots stood exactly even underneath the bunk, toes out. His pack sat packed and strapped by the door, lumpy with folded clothes and smelling faintly of oil and apples, because Silena had been feeding it for two days whenever Tyson wasn't looking.

Percy felt the morning arrange itself around the sight the way water arranged itself around a stone, and felt his throat do the thing it did when something true went past without armor. He had been holding it together since Sunday night, since Chiron had said bounded on the sand and everybody had nodded like it didn't hurt. He intended to keep holding it together through the dock and the wave-off and the walk back up. He was Cabin Three. Holding was what his arms were for, short or otherwise.

Tyson's eye opened.

"Morning," Percy said softly. "You awake?"

"I was pretending," Tyson said, with complete honesty, sitting up in one movement that made the left spring go ping. "I heard Clarisse pretend. Then Silena pretend. Then you pretend. So I pretended too. So nobody would be first." He rubbed his face with both hands, huge palms rasping. "Is it time to not pretend?"

"Yeah," Percy said. "It's time."

Tyson nodded and swung his massive legs over the side and sat there in his forge shirt and borrowed sleep shorts, looking at his packed bag by the door. He did not look at the white rock yet. Percy knew he was saving it.

"I slept well," Tyson reported, as if filing a morning report. "The spring went ping twice. Once at night. Once now. I said goodbye to it already, in case I forget later when there are people." He folded his hands in his lap. "Is that all right? To say goodbye early to a spring?"

"It's more than all right," Percy said. "It's polite. Springs like warning."

"Good." Tyson stood, and the cabin seemed to resettle around his standing, floorboards taking the weight with a familiar sigh. He went to the hook and took down the good towel and folded it — once, twice, corners exactly together — and laid it on the corner of the stripped mattress. Then he made the bunk. Sheets pulled tight enough to bounce a drachma off, pillow centered, corners tucked with forge precision. The whole ritual slow and thorough, the way he did everything that mattered: like seam work, like a weld you didn't hurry.

Clarisse gave up pretending across the room with an audible exhale and sat up, hair in a night braid gone fuzzy at the temples, cutoff shirt twisted from sleep. "If you're going to clean, do my bunk next," she said, gruff with morning, but she was already swinging down and her voice lacked its usual velocity. "Actually don't touch my bunk. You'll make the rest of us look bad."

"I already looked at your bunk," Tyson said seriously. "It is good. The blanket is straight. You kicked it straight in your sleep. You are talented."

Clarisse stared at him a second, then snorted, then rubbed her face hard with one hand so nobody would see what her face was doing. "Flattery from the giant. Fine. Breakfast in thirty. Wear the good shirt. Silena laid it out."

"I will wear the good shirt," Tyson promised. "And I will carry the bags. That is my chore. Last-day chore."

"You don't need a chore, you're a guest," Percy said automatically, and then, catching Silena's pillow thrown with sniper accuracy at his head, caught it one-handed and corrected himself. "I mean — yes. Last-day chore approved. By management."

Silena sat up in her bunk, hair in a loose dark river, mending tote already on her knees like she had slept with it for company. She wore her patched camp shirt and soft flannel pants printed with tiny lemons, feet bare against the cool tile. At sixteen she ran mornings the way Chiron ran camp, and this morning her warm perceptive gaze moved from Tyson's made bunk to Percy's caught pillow to Clarisse's deliberately busy hands, doing the inventory she always did: who needed seconds, who was quiet, who was performing fullness to hide emptiness. "Management approves," she said. "Management also says teeth, faces, laces. Ferry waits for nobody. Chiron sent word last night — harpy post, very official, very early. Boat at eight bells from the south dock. Which gives us one hour to be human beings."

"One hour," Tyson repeated. He picked up his forge shirt from the foot of the bunk — clean, mended at the shoulder where Silena had taken three stitches Sunday night without being asked — and held it up. "Then the ferry. Then the forges." He said it the way you would set a heavy pot down on a table: carefully, because it was heavy, without apology for its weight. "I do not want to go. Is it still all right to say?"

Percy crossed the three steps between the bunks and bumped his shoulder hard against Tyson's arm, the PCS language, shoulder to shoulder. "Still all right," he said quietly. "Every day. Including today."

"Especially today," Silena said, already up and moving, bare feet slapping tile, pulling her hair back with the tie Percy always seemed to lose and she always seemed to find. "Say it as many times as you need. We've got an hour. Spend it honest."

Clarisse stood at the pool end with her towel over one shoulder, blunt and barefoot in her cutoff shirt and sleep shorts, braid fuzzy, eyes bright and furious at being bright. "Say it once more for the cheap seats," she said. "Then brush your teeth. Your breath smells like a forge."

Tyson smiled — the small complete smile of a boy who had paid his ticket and found it fair — and went to brush his teeth.

Percy stood in the cool tile smell of his own cabin with his brother's made bunk empty behind him except for the white rock waiting on the folded shirt, and felt grief come up under the ordinary like the cold layer three feet down in the lake in September: present, honest, not yet touching the warm skin on top. He breathed in, breathed out, checked his pockets out of habit — pebble, wrapper, hair tie Silena had returned — and went to get dressed for the morning that would take his brother.

Clarisse La Rue had a theory about last mornings, and the theory was this: you kept your hands busy or your mouth would do something stupid.

She stood at the washbasin in Tempest Hall with cold water to the elbows and scrubbed her face twice, hard, the way she did everything before eight in the morning. Around her the cabin moved through its ferry-morning choreography with the over-precise efficiency of people who had agreed without discussing it to be competent instead of sad. Silena braiding her hair with quick fingers while simultaneously inventorying the pack by the door. Percy lacing his damp sneakers with his tongue caught between his teeth. Tyson dressing in the good shirt — his own forge-gray, mended shoulder out, sleeves rolled to show forearms corded from hammer work — with the slow dignity of a boy putting on armor.

Clarisse watched all of it in the mirror with her face dripping, and hated middles and ends with equal fervor, and reached for her towel.

She wore her uniform without thinking about it, the way soldiers wore things that had stopped being clothes and started being decisions: clean camp shirt with the sleeves cut jagged two summers ago, open over her functional dark one-piece swimsuit because the morning would end at the water and practical girls dressed for the whole day at once, sturdy khaki shorts with the knife pocket empty for ferry day, braid tight and high for wind. She had laid Tyson's day out the same way in her head at dawn while pretending to sleep: good shirt, clean jeans cuffed for the dock, sandals because his boots pinched on boats and she remembered that from July without ever having written it down. Functional, unshowy, warm enough for September wind off the strait. That was the whole rule in Cabin Three, though she would rather have bitten her whistle than called it a rule: dress for work, dress for water, dress for weather. Everything else was noise.

"Boots or sandals?" she asked Tyson, turning around, towel over her shoulder.

Tyson held up one of each. "Boots are strong. Sandals are cool. The ferry is windy. The forges are hot." He looked at her with total trust. "Which is right?"

"Sandals on the boat, boots in the pack." Clarisse crossed and took the boots and set them by the pack, laces tucked. "Your feet swell on water. July you complained for an hour. Don't pretend you didn't."

"I did complain," Tyson admitted. "My feet were loud. I remember." He set the sandals by the door, exactly even, toes out. "Thank you for remembering my feet."

"Somebody has to. Jackson only remembers his own pockets." But she bumped Tyson's elbow with hers as she passed, grudging-tender, the way she did affection before breakfast: sideways, deniable, real. "Sit. Let me check the braid — no, your hair. Sit."

Tyson sat on the corner bunk — the made bunk, careful not to wrinkle it — and Clarisse stood behind him with the comb and worked the forge tangles out with blunt patience, section by section, the way Silena had taught her years ago when she kept ripping her own hair out by impatience. The cabin went quieter. Percy folded the spare quilt into a square so it would not hurt anyone. Silena tied off the pack straps and retied them.

"You don't have to do that," Tyson said quietly, head bowed under her hands. "I can go tangled. The wind will tangle it anyway."

"I know I don't have to." Clarisse worked a stubborn knot at the nape with her fingers, gentle in the exact measure she used for spear maintenance and nothing else. "I want the wind to have to work for it." She finished, set the comb down. "There. Now you look like somebody's brother instead of somebody's shipwreck."

Tyson turned and looked up at her, one great eye bright. "I am somebody's brother," he said. "I am yours."

Clarisse's hands stilled on his shoulders. Fifteen years old, blunt as a shield rim, terrified of middles since she was twelve and old enough to do the subtraction on every visitor Cabin Three ever kept, and her half-brother had just said the thing straight, without armor, the way he said everything. She squeezed his shoulders once, hard, the way she gripped a spear before a throw.

"Yeah," she said, level. "You are. Don't forget it when the forges get loud."

"I keep the important things inside," Tyson said, tapping his chest. "There is a lot of room inside. You are inside. Percy is inside. Silena is inside. The spring that goes ping is inside. The Demeter girl who is good at over is inside." He counted on his fingers to be sure. "There is still room. I checked."

Percy made a small sound over by the quilt, half laugh, half something else, and came over and sat on the bunk edge and leaned his head briefly against Tyson's shoulder. Fourteen, observant and wry even with his eyes wet, trying hard to be easy about a brother leaving in an hour. "You got a whole cabin in there," he said. "Fire code violation."

"I am large," Tyson agreed. "I can hold large things."

Silena came over with the pack and set it down and knelt to check the straps a third time, buying herself a minute with her face hidden. When she stood, her eyes were bright and she let them be bright. "Up," she said. "All of us. Porch inspection. If anybody's got a loose lace or a tragic collar, I'm fixing it now and never again."

They lined up on the porch of Tempest Hall in the thin September sun — four siblings and one visitor who was also a sibling, which was the same thing after four nights of ping springs and laundry lines — and Silena went down the row straightening collars and tugging hems and wiping a smudge of charcoal off Tyson's jaw with her thumb. The lake flashed through the pines to the left, freshwater blue going rose at the edges where morning touched it, cicadas starting late, pine resin and cool stone on the air. Somewhere up the hill the conch sounded for breakfast, thin and far.

Clarisse slung the pack over one shoulder before Tyson could lift it — stealing the chore for exactly six steps, because she could — and then handed it back. "Carry it," she said. "Last-day chore. Don't drop it. It's got your sister's baking in it."

"It is heavy with love," Tyson said, settling it with pleasure. "I can feel the love through the canvas. It is warm."

"That's the honey bread," Silena said. "It just came out of the oven. Don't let him fool you with metaphors. It's bread."

"It's both," Tyson said firmly, and shouldered the pack, and led the way up the path toward the pavilion with his sandals slapping and his good shirt straight and his hair combed, and Clarisse watched him go with her whistle cord sliding through her fingers and thought, two hours, one hour, bells, boat, empty bunk, and then made herself stop counting by counting his steps instead, which was the same thing but slightly more useful.

Silena Beauregard had learned, over five Septembers of feeding people, that farewell breakfasts had their own weather.

They sat at the end of the Poseidon table in the pavilion with the morning sun coming sideways through the awnings and the smell of tomato and basil already starting underneath the breakfast smell of honey and hot grain. Silena wore her day clothes — clean camp shirt with the sleeves pushed to the elbow, denim shorts over her swimsuit, hair braided back with the tie Percy kept losing, flour already on one forearm though she hadn't been near the ovens that morning, because flour lived on her the way forge-smoke lived on Tyson. Percy sat beside Tyson in his clean camp shirt and jeans with the cuffs unrolled, hair still damp at the temples from the washbasin, sneakers laced double. Clarisse across from them, braid tight, whistle on its cord, boots dusty from the porch. Annabeth slid onto the bench beside Percy with her tray, fourteen and precise even at breakfast, camp shirt with graphite on the left cuff over sturdy olive shorts, hair rebraided tight, smelling of paper and pencils and the peach she had eaten yesterday and told Thalia about. Five of them. Six places set, because Silena had set six without thinking and left one open; Thalia had been back from the boundary survey since Friday, even if she was not at breakfast yet.

Tyson had three sandwiches and carrots arranged by size and a boiled egg he kept turning over like it might hatch. He ate with deep concentration, the way he did everything involving provisions: thoroughly, gratefully, as if each bite were a separate delivery to confirm.

"Eat the egg," Silena told him. "It's from the good hens. I asked."

Tyson ate the egg. "Good hens," he agreed. "Tell them thank you."

"I will. They like notes." Silena pushed the honey bread across — two loaves wrapped in cloth, still warm through the weave — and the waxed packet of cheese and the oilcloth of apples, biggest to smallest, and the tin of cookies with the lid tied down with string. "Road food," she announced, in the voice of a person bringing a matter before a committee that has already decided. "For the ferry and the forges. Bread for today, cheese for tonight, apples for whenever, cookies for Beckendorf's brother because he saves you the good hammer and good hammers deserve cookies. There's tea in the small jar but drink it before the strait or it'll slosh. I put your name on everything so nobody steals it. I wrote it big."

Tyson stared at the pile growing beside his tray. "This is a lot of food," he said. "Is it too much? Will the ferry sink?"

"The ferry will not sink," Annabeth said, dry and fond, precise-planning even in affection. "I checked the displacement. I did the math in the loft yesterday. You plus food plus pack is well within tolerance. I wrote it on the tag." She slid a small oilcloth tag across the table — ferry times, forge contact, letter schedule, all in her neat hand. "Tied to the pack. Waterproof ink. If you get confused, read the tag. If the tag gets confused, ask the captain. His name is on the back."

Tyson picked up the tag with huge care and read it twice. "You made me a map for leaving," he said.

"It's not a map. It's a —" Annabeth stopped, gray eyes bright, and corrected herself with the honesty she saved for people she loved. "Yes. It's a map for leaving. Keep it dry."

"I will keep it inside," Tyson promised, tapping his chest, then his pack. "Both. Two copies. Like a good engineer."

Percy sat shoulder to shoulder with Tyson, eating fast and talking faster, bumping the great arm beside him every few minutes like checking a wall was still there. He had been holding it together since the bunk, and Silena could see the holding in the set of his jaw, in the way he kept refilling Tyson's cup before his own, in the wry observant jokes landing half a beat too quick. Fourteen years old and already the boy who carried laundry wrong on purpose to make children laugh, who held a giant's ankle like it was his job, who had spent yesterday afternoon in it all the way with no counting and was now counting every minute out loud to make it feel chosen instead of taken.

"Slow down," she told him quietly, ruffling his hair as she passed the pitcher — water with lemon, because lemons were her whole personality and she put them in everything in September. "The boat won't leave early because you inhale."

"Boat might leave early if Tyson's on it," Percy said. "Ballast. Speed."

"I am good ballast," Tyson agreed, pleased. "I sit still. I do not slosh."

Clarisse reached across the table without a word and set a small bundle down in front of Tyson — canvas wrapped, tied with cord, her knotwork blunt and perfect. Inside: a whetstone, a spare whistle on a short cord, and a single bronze bolt, large, oil-dark, the twin of the one in his pocket for the dock.

Tyson stared. "A whistle?"

"For the forge," Clarisse said, blunt, eyes on her tray. "It's loud. If the machines get loud and you need — if you need people to hear you, you blow it. Three times. Somebody'll come." She jerked her chin at the bolt. "Bolt's for luck. Not for use. Don't put it in anything. Just keep it. So you've got one for the dock and one for — for elsewhere."

Tyson held the whistle in his palm like an egg. "Will you hear it?" he asked. "If I blow it at the forges?"

Clarisse looked up. The pavilion clattered on around them, ordinary and precious, awnings moving in the wind, lake flashing beyond the pines. "No," she said honestly. "Too far. But somebody there will. Beckendorf's brother. The good hammer guy. And you'll know I gave it to you, which is almost the same as me hearing it." Her voice went into the flatter register, the true one. "Blow it when you need to. Don't be shy. Shy gets people hurt. Ten minutes per occurrence, and I'm waiving it for whistles, don't tell Thalia."

"I will blow it loudly," Tyson promised. "And I will think of you. Then it will be almost the same."

He tied the whistle around his neck with great care, over the good shirt, and Silena felt her throat close and covered it by pushing the cookie tin an inch straighter. Percy bumped Tyson's shoulder again, harder, the boy checking the wall. Annabeth reached under the table and found Percy's free hand and held it, cold fingers and warm, graphite and lake, and Percy held on like she was the dock and he was the boat.

It was Percy who brought out the last gift, when the trays were mostly empty and the sun had moved one awning-stripe west: a small flat parcel wrapped in oilcloth, tied with Silena's hair tie — the one he kept losing, surrendered at last for a better cause. Inside: a sketch on cardstock, edges taped against damp. Annabeth's drawing, copied by Percy in his own lopsided hand — the canoe lake, the north beach, the flat rock, the boundary rock with its white X, five heads in a row in the shallows off the rock, labeled in Percy's block letters: HELD TWICE. Underneath, in smaller letters, the line he had been carrying since yesterday's float: THE BIGGEST WATER YOU CAN SEE THE OTHER SIDE OF TOO.

Tyson stared at it for a long time.

"I drew the heads too big," Percy said, wry and miserable and proud all at once. "Annabeth's original has better heads. Mine are potatoes. But the rocks are right. I measured."

"They are potatoes," Tyson agreed, with enormous love. "They are excellent potatoes. I can tell which potato is me. I am the biggest potato." He touched each head with one fingertip, naming them softly. "Percy. Clarisse. Silena. Annabeth. Me." He looked up, eye bright with lake. "Is this the map for staying?"

Percy nodded, throat too tight for words. Annabeth answered for him, precise and thick: "That's the map for staying. Keep it dry too."

"I will keep it inside," Tyson said. "With the whistle. With the tag. There is still room. I checked." He folded the sketch with immense care and slid it into his breast pocket, over his heart, patted it flat. "Now I have leaving and staying. Both. I am rich."

"Rich in maps," Percy managed. "Rich in days. Rich in potatoes."

"Rich," Tyson agreed, satisfied, and picked up his pack, and stood, and the table stood with him, because standing was what you did when the standing time came and competence was kinder than delay.

Silena gathered the road food into the pack's top pocket with quick hands, tucking the cloth tight so nothing would slosh. Her eyes were wet and she let them be wet. Apples help, Percy had told Tyson in July, and Tyson had remembered inside, and now Tyson reached into his own pocket and handed her an apple — cool and red and perfect — without a word, and she took it with both hands and laughed through the wet.

"Eat it on the walk back," Tyson told her. "So you will not be sad on an empty stomach. Percy told me. It is science."

"It is science," Silena agreed, holding the apple like a vow. "Thank you, brother."

They walked down to the south dock together through the September morning, pine needles soft underfoot, lake flashing through the trees, the whole small procession: Tyson huge in the middle with the pack heavy with love, Percy at his right shoulder, Clarisse at his left, Silena and Annabeth behind with the spare satchel and the tea jar. Camp went on around them — arena shouts, forge smoke thin on the wind, somebody's distant singing — ordinary and precious, a Tuesday holding its shape.

At the top of the ramp stood Chiron in his wheelchair with a clipboard, white hair catching the sun, arranging the ferry with the ease of long practice: captain's name, crossing time, forge pickup confirmed. No mystery in it, no panel, no revelation — just the background kindness of an adult doing logistics so children could do grief. He checked Tyson's tag, initialed it, shook Tyson's hand with both of his, and said, "Bounded was the promise, Tyson. You kept your end beautifully. The forges will be glad to see you."

"I kept the bunk neat," Tyson reported. "I folded the good towel. I said goodbye to the spring. It went ping."

"Then you left it better than you found it," Chiron said. "That's the whole job." He nodded to the rest of them, kind and brief. "Take your time on the dock. Boat won't leave without my wave."

They took their time on the dock.

Percy felt the boards warm through his sneakers as they walked out to the end, because you didn't say goodbye halfway down a dock. The ferry waited — broad-beamed, blue-trimmed — water slapping the hull in a clean September rhythm. Percy thought, eight bells, that's the edge, and then made himself feel the boards with his feet the way Tyson listened with his.

Tyson stopped at the third cleat from the end and crouched and felt it with his palm. Then he looked up, surprised and pleased. "Tight," he announced. "Somebody fixed it. With a bolt. It does not wobble. There will be no paperwork."

"I wonder who," Percy said, and Tyson laughed — the deep forge laugh that started in his chest and came out like warm air off bronze — and stood and set his pack down and looked at all of them in turn, one great eye moving from face to face, memorizing.

It was Clarisse who went first, because Clarisse always went first at hard things the way she went first into cold water: without testing, so nobody would see her hesitate.

She stepped forward, blunt and barefoot — sandals abandoned halfway down the dock for better grip, toes curled against the warm boards — and stood in front of her brother with her hands in her shorts pockets and her whistle cord moving in the wind. Fifteen, terrified of leaving since she was twelve, counting since dawn, done counting now because the number had arrived.

"All right," she said. "Rules for the forges. One: drink water. You're big, you forget. Two: don't lift anything hotter than your head without gloves. I know you can. Don't. Three: write. Even if it's just three lines. Even if it's just GOOD HAMMER. GOOD FIRE. MISS YOU. That's enough. Four —" Her voice went flat, true. "Four: if you get lonely, blow the whistle. Don't decide you're fine when you're not. You're allowed to be loud about it."

Tyson listened with total concentration, hands folded, whistle on its cord bright against the good shirt. "Drink water," he repeated. "Gloves for hot. Write three lines. Blow whistle when lonely." He nodded, filing each one inside where there was a lot of room. "I will obey. You are a good captain."

"I am a good sister," Clarisse corrected, and then, grudging-tender, the thing she had been carrying since the volleyball court: "You were good with the kids. The Demeter girl's still talking about over. You left her better than you found her. That's — that's the whole job. Don't forget you can do that."

"I will not forget," Tyson promised. "I keep it inside." He opened his arms — huge, forge-scarred, gentle as a closed gate — and Clarisse stepped into them without hesitation, and he lifted her half an inch off the boards the way he had lifted the Demeter girl, the way you would lift something precious to confirm its weight, and set her back down. "Goodbye, Clarisse," he said into her braid. "Thank you for the whistle. Thank you for remembering my feet."

Clarisse held on a second longer than she meant to, face hidden against the good shirt, shoulders doing one thing she would deny forever. Then she stepped back, eyes bright and furious at being bright, and shoved his shoulder. "Go on. You're making the dock wobble. You're too big for goodbyes."

Tyson turned to Silena.

Silena stood with the apple still in both hands, jacket zipped against the wind, hair coming down in dark strands, sixteen and warm and perceptive and completely undone by laundry metaphysics for the third day running. She had fed this boy for three mornings, mended his shoulder, taught him how to hang a sheet so it would not twist, set six places for five people because you kept the place anyway. She had done the subtraction at dawn lying in her bunk listening to him breathe, and the subtraction had come out the same as always: hollow after, held now.

"My turn," she said, and her voice came out thick, and she let it be thick. "Tyson. You eat the bread first, it's honey, it goes stale fastest. Cheese wrapped, keep it shaded. Apples whenever. Cookies for the hammer man — don't let anybody else steal them, I wrote your name big, show them the writing if they argue." She stopped, laughed wetly at herself. "That's the feeding part. Now the sister part." She stepped forward and put her arms around his waist — she barely reached his chest — and leaned her forehead against the whistle cord. "You said edges don't stop the drying. You were right. So go get dry somewhere else for a while. Be warm. Be useful. Let them hear you coming. And come back when the lines are free. We'll hang you right back up."

Tyson held her with immense care, one hand cupping the back of her head the way you would hold a bird with a hurt wing. "I will come back," he said. "When Chiron says. When the forges say. I will write three lines. GOOD HAMMER. GOOD FIRE. MISS YOU." He paused, then added, with deep sincerity, "I will miss you most at breakfast. Because you put the extra apple. I notice. I file it."

Silena laughed and sobbed at once and went up on her toes to kiss his cheek. "Notice forever," she said. "File everything. Eat the apple on the boat so you won't be sad on an empty stomach — no, that's your line. My line is —" She wiped her eyes with the back of her floury wrist. "My line is I love you. That's my line. Take it with you."

"I will keep it inside," Tyson said. "With the whistle. With the maps. There is still room. I checked." He handed her a second apple from his pocket — he had been carrying two all morning, provisions against grief — and she took it, and now she had two, one from the table and one from the pocket, and she held both like vows.

Annabeth went next, quick and precise, because Annabeth did hard things the way she drew contours: carefully, wanting the true line.

She stood in front of Tyson in her camp shirt with graphite on the cuff and her olive shorts and her sneakers, hair whipping, gray eyes clear and bright, fourteen years old and official camp cartographer and already the girl who had drawn a visitor's bunk into Cabin Three's inset because any honest map had to admit who was sleeping where while they slept there. "Tyson," she said. "Keep the tag dry. Read it if you get confused. Write back if the forge lets you. The address is on the back in waterproof ink. I tested it. I dunked a copy in the fountain."

"I will write," Tyson promised. "Three lines. Plus a drawing. I will draw the ferry. It will be lopsided. Percy will label it."

"I'll label anything," Percy said from behind her, thickly, and Annabeth reached back without looking and found his hand and held it.

Tyson bent — he had to bend nearly double — and Annabeth stood on her toes and hugged his neck, brief and hard, the Grace-sister hug adapted for giants: all collarbone and pencil smell and pine. "Thank you for calibrating my compass," she said into his shoulder. "In July. You held it steady while I turned the ring. I've used it every day since. That's — that's on every map. You're on every map."

Tyson went very still. Then he straightened, eye bright with lake. "I am on every map," he repeated, wonderingly. "Even when I am at the forges. Even when I am gone."

"Even then," Annabeth said. "Keep the place. That's the job. Somebody wise told me."

Percy knew, the moment they reached the north beach, that the afternoon had decided to be kind.

It was early afternoon going gold — September light thinner than August but generous where it landed, laying itself across the half-moon of sand and warm flat stone in one long plank, pines running right down into the water on both sides. "That seems like a strange thing to be disappointed about," Annabeth said.

"It's the point of taking the quilt to a beach."

Percy handed it over. Silena shook it once, and a small spray of grit scattered onto the path. She looked satisfied. Then she held up the corner where Tyson's repair thread still showed, a thick blue line against the older fabric.

"He finished that before he left," she said. "I saw him working after you went to bed."

Percy touched the uneven stitches. He had spent the day thinking about the ferry leaving, and had missed the evidence of Tyson staying until the last possible minute. The towel was suddenly more difficult to give up than it ought to have been.

"Don't wash that corner too hard," he said.

Silena raised her eyebrows. "I wasn't going to put it through a forge."

Annabeth's hand found his. She didn't explain him to his sister or claim to understand what Tyson had meant. She just waited while Percy took one more look at the thread.

Inside the cabin Clarisse had left a scrap of paper on the table: WEDNESDAY: CHECK CLEATS BEFORE ANYONE TAKES OUT A BOAT. Under it somebody had drawn an enormous foot. Tyson's handwriting filled half the page.

"That is a very large foot," Annabeth said.

"It was a very large repair," Percy answered.

He set the note beside the folded towel, where it would still be there at breakfast. Wednesday had a job on it now, and Percy had a reason to get up and do it.
