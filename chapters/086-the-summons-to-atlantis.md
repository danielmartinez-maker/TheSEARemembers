## Chapter 86: The Summons to Atlantis

The summons came on a Thursday afternoon, during sword drills.

Percy Jackson was paired with Clarisse, which always drew a crowd. She was taller than him by half a head, her dark hair cut short for the warmer days of June. He was thirteen, leaner, faster. Their blades rang and scraped, and sand kicked up around their sneakers.

Silena sat on the fence with Annabeth, calling the points. Her long dark hair was braided back. She had a notebook open on her knees and a half-eaten strawberry in one hand.

"Point to Percy," Silena said. "Clarisse, your guard dropped."

"It did not drop," Clarisse said, circling. "Your eyes dropped."

"My eyes are perfect," Silena said. "Annabeth, back me up."

Annabeth Grace, thirteen, blond ponytail tucked through the back of a camp cap, lifted both hands. "I am neutral. I am Zeus. I do not intervene in sea disputes."

"You intervene constantly," Percy said, grinning, and lunged.

Clarisse parried, shoved him back with her shoulder, and was about to riposte when a conch sounded from the beach. Not the camp conch for assembly. Deeper. Older. The sound rolled up the hill and settled in Percy's teeth like cold water.

All three Poseidon kids stopped at once.

Silena sat up straight. Clarisse lowered her sword. Percy felt the tug behind his navel, familiar as a tide.

"Was that—" Annabeth started.

A hippocampus burst out of the surf down at the lake, scales flashing gold-green, a leather message tube tied to his harness.

Chiron trotted down from the Big House, tail swishing, and waved the three of them forward.

They ran, Annabeth right with them, swords still in hand. The hippocampus — Percy recognized him, Rainbow's cousin, a pushy young male named Foam — nuzzled Percy's shoulder hard enough to knock him sideways, then presented the tube.

Percy opened it. Inside was a sheet of mother-of-pearl, thin as paper, etched with glowing blue script that moved slightly if stared at too long.

He read it aloud, because Clarisse and Silena were leaning over his shoulders.

"My children — Clarisse, Silena, Perseus. The currents are kind this weekend and the palace is quiet. Come home to Atlantis. Friday to Sunday. Bring your guest, Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus, with my compliments and thanks to my brother. Triton will meet you at the strait. Mind your sister. Mind your brother. — Father."

Silence.

Foam snorted and nudged Silena's notebook, as if to say, well?

"Did he just invite all three of us," Clarisse said slowly, "at the same time. To Atlantis. For the whole weekend."

"And Annabeth," Silena whispered. She grabbed Percy's wrist. "Percy. He signed Father."

Percy stared at the pearl. He had been to Atlantis before, many times, but almost always alone. He had stories for his sisters, pieces, but he had never walked those halls with them beside him.

Annabeth was very still. "With my compliments and thanks to my brother," she repeated. "Is that — is that normal? For Poseidon to invite a Zeus kid?"

Chiron smiled, something soft and old in his eyes. "It is unusual. It is also deliberate. Your father and your uncle have been speaking more this year. And you, Annabeth, have kept his son alive more than once. I think this is gratitude."

"Gratitude with palace protocol," Percy said faintly. He looked at Annabeth. Her cheeks were pink.

"So?" Clarisse said. She punched his shoulder, but gently, for her. "Are you going to breathe, little brother, or do we have to carry you?"

"I am breathing," Percy said.

"You are not," Silena said, laughing, and hugged him sideways, notebook and strawberry and all. "We are going to Atlantis. Together. All of us."

---

Friday morning smelled of salt and low clouds.

Mist hung over Long Island Sound, and the water at the camp beach was flat silver. Chiron stood with his hands folded, Thalia Grace beside him with her arms crossed, her black hair streaked with electric blue, her electric-blue eyes fixed on her little sister.

Thalia was older now, a camp scout, tall and sharp. She had come down specially to see Annabeth off, which Annabeth pretended to find annoying and secretly treasured.

"Rules," Thalia said, holding up fingers. "One, if Poseidon tries to feed you anything that is still moving, make Percy eat it first."

"Noted," Annabeth said solemnly.

"Two, if Triton tries to scare you, do not flinch."

"Three," Thalia said, and her voice softened, "send an Iris-message every night. Dad may be king of the sky, but I am queen of worrying about you."

Annabeth hugged her sister hard. They were Zeus sisters, same storm-gray eyes, same stubborn chin. Thalia's jacket smelled of pine and rain.

"I will," Annabeth murmured. "Promise."

Percy gave them a moment, then Thalia turned on him and pointed.

"Jackson."

"Yes?" Percy said.

"You keep her safe underwater."

"Always," Percy said. It was not a boast. It was a vow, simple as stone.

Thalia studied him, then nodded once and clapped him on the shoulder hard enough to sting. "Good. And keep your sisters safe too, kelp-head."

"Hey," Clarisse said from where she stood with her duffel. "We keep him safe."

Silena wore a blue windbreaker over her camp shirt and had packed three extra bags, one of which was entirely snacks. "We brought ambrosia, nectar, towels, and a first-aid kit," she announced. "Annabeth made a checklist."

"Annabeth always makes a checklist," Percy said fondly.

Annabeth shouldered her own pack, excitement and nerves warring on her face. "I have never been to Atlantis. The scrolls contradict each other on depth and dome structure."

Percy took her hand, just for a second, to steady her. Her fingers were cold. His were warm. Around them the mist thinned as Foam and two other hippocampi surfaced, bridles jingling with shells.

"There is air in the palace," he said. "Dad keeps domes for guests. And you will be able to breathe anyway. I will make sure of it."

Annabeth looked at him, and her shoulders dropped half an inch. "Okay," she said. "Okay, partner."

Clarisse made a gagging noise. Silena elbowed her.

"Mount up," Clarisse ordered, swinging onto the lead hippocampus. She landed sideways and had to be righted by Foam.

Silena mounted more gracefully, giggling. Percy helped Annabeth up behind him, showed her where to hold, and felt her arms lock carefully around his waist, her chin near his shoulder.

"Ready?" he asked his sisters.

Clarisse raised her fist. Silena raised both hands with a whoop.

Foam dove.

The Sound closed over them without cold, without pressure, without darkness. Percy's power reached out on instinct, wrapping Annabeth in a pocket of breathable current, smoothing the water from her face, keeping her hair from tangling too badly. He felt her gasp against his back, then laugh, bubbles streaming silver past his ears.

Down they went, out past the shelf, south and deep, the water turning from green to blue to violet-dark. Silena waved at everything. Clarisse whooped into her breathing bubble.

Annabeth's grip tightened when the trench fell away beneath them and Atlantis rose.

Percy had tried to describe it to her once, at night by the canoe lake, and had failed. Words flattened it. Now he felt her go still behind him and knew she was seeing it truly for the first time.

Atlantis spread across a ridge of white rock, domed and spired, all pearl and coral and living shell. Bridges arched between towers. Gardens bloomed in impossible colors. Light came from everywhere. At the center stood the palace, vast and calm, its gates open.

"Oh," Annabeth breathed, her voice carried clearly through the water charm. "Oh, Percy. It is — it is engineered like — the pressure distribution — the light —"

He laughed, relief cracking open in his chest. Of course she would see engineering first. Of course she would love it.

"It is home," he said quietly. "Part of it. You will see."

At the strait gate, a tall figure waited on a chariot pulled by twin sharks.

Triton, son of Poseidon and Amphitrite, heir of Atlantis, twenty-something in immortal years and eternally annoyed about younger siblings, stood with his trident crossed and his golden eyes narrowed.

He wore armor of shell and bronze, his green hair braided with pearls, his tail — currently legs, for formal greeting — planted wide. He looked every inch the prince.

Then he opened his mouth.

"Oh, excellent," Triton called as they rode up. "They multiplied. One barnacle I could tolerate. Now there are three. And a sky child. Did Father order in bulk?"

Clarisse bristled immediately. "Who are you calling barnacle?"

"The loud one, obviously," Triton said. "Clarisse, I see you haven't softened since you tried to knock me across the camp arena. Father says you shout at waves. The waves complained."

"I do not shout at waves," Clarisse said. "I command them."

"Same thing, but louder," Triton said. He turned to Silena, who was trying not to laugh. "And Silena, still pretending to be the sweet one who never causes trouble. Father showed me the picture of the palace you drew for him. He keeps it in the council room. The generals pretend not to see the sun with a smiley face."

Silena turned scarlet with delight. "He kept it? Really?"

"He framed it," Triton said, deadpan. "In coral."

Percy snorted. Annabeth was watching with wide eyes, her hands still gripping Percy's shirt.

Triton turned to him last. "Perseus." His expression flickered, just for a heartbeat, into something warmer. "You brought the whole school, I see."

"I brought my sisters," Percy said firmly, sliding off Foam and helping Annabeth down. He stood between his sisters without thinking, shoulders square. "And my best friend. Dad invited her."

"So I heard," Triton said. He circled them slowly, sharks circling with him. "Three half-grown demigods and a storm princess. Father's weekend of peace is ruined."

"We will be good," Silena said quickly.

"No, you will not," Triton said. "You are Poseidon's. None of you are good at being good. You are good at being trouble in clear water." He stopped in front of Annabeth and bowed, formal and precise, which startled her. "Lady Annabeth Grace. Atlantis welcomes you.

Annabeth, to her credit, bowed back, a little wobbly in the current. "Thank you, Prince Triton. I will try not to drown."

Triton grinned, sharp and sudden and almost like Percy's own grin. "I still like her. She has manners. Keep her, little brother."

"She is not mine to keep," Percy said, ears burning. "She is her own. She is — she chose to come."

Annabeth's hand found his again, quick and warm, then released. Triton saw it and his grin widened, but he said nothing more, only whistled to the hippocampi.

"Come," he said. "Mother is waiting. And Father is pretending not to wait, which is worse."

---

The first disagreement happened before they entered the palace.

A young attendant met the visitors near the lower dock carrying an itinerary on a silver board. The schedule divided the family into categories: *Prince Percy*, *the sea-born daughters*, and *the honored sky guest*. Each category had its own reception room and different arrival instructions.

Clarisse read the board once.

"No."

The attendant looked up, startled. "My lady?"

"I'm not going to a separate room while my brother gets a parade."

"It is protocol."

"Then protocol can take a swim."

Percy took the board before the argument grew louder. The young attendant looked close to tears. He had probably spent the whole morning trying to get the schedule right. Percy knew the feeling from the Roman exchange, where one wrong contact number had nearly kept him from boarding a plane.

"Who approved this?" Percy asked.

"The steward's office, my prince."

"Did anybody ask my sisters?"

The attendant looked blank. "They are guests."

"Yes. Guests who can answer questions."

Annabeth stood beside Clarisse with her notebook tucked safely inside a waterproof sleeve. Silena had been examining the delicate coral railings, looking delighted to be somewhere she'd only visited briefly before. Now she looked from the board to the attendant and raised a hand.

"I don't mind having time to get dressed," she said. "But I don't want the first hour of our visit to be spent alone in a room where I don't know anyone."

Clarisse nodded. "Exactly."

Percy looked at Annabeth. "What about you?"

"I want to see the dome construction. But I didn't ask to be put in a separate reception party either."

The attendant waited while Percy considered. He was tempted to solve the whole thing by claiming princely authority and telling everybody the schedule had changed. That would be satisfying and probably make more problems for the staff who needed to arrange the rooms.

"Could you ask the steward whether we can meet together first?" Percy said. "Then people can choose what activities they want after that."

The attendant bowed. "I can inquire."

"Good. We can wait here."

Triton, who had been watching from a few steps away, crossed his arms. "You know that if you keep asking the staff to change plans, Mother will start expecting you to do the seating arrangements."

Percy grinned. "Sounds like her."

"It does," Triton admitted.

The steward arrived a few minutes later, took one look at Clarisse's expression and decided the family could have a joint reception in the smaller garden hall. He asked whether Silena still wanted to join the clothing preparations, and she said yes. Annabeth asked for access to the library later; he agreed to arrange it. Clarisse said she wanted to see the training facilities before anybody put her into silk.

The steward wrote down the requests.

Percy watched the names being entered without any reference to which parent had raised which child. Poseidon was their shared father. That was enough to make them family, but it didn't mean they were interchangeable or that every guest had to enjoy the same thing.

"Mother will like this better," Triton said quietly as they started walking.

"Why?"

"She spent an hour telling the steward that people aren't decorative objects. He did not understand."

Percy laughed. "Then I get to make her look wise."

"She manages that herself."

The garden hall was open to a slow current and smelled of fresh kelp. There were seats for all of them and enough space for Tyson, who had come from the forge to welcome the visitors and was trying to restrain himself from hugging everybody before they had taken off their travel gear.

"Brother Percy!" he shouted.

Percy hugged him.

"Clarisse! Silena! Annabeth!"

Tyson embraced his sisters with permission and offered Annabeth a huge hand to shake. She chose a hug too, laughing when he carefully bent down to make it possible.

"I told the staff you might come," Tyson said. "They prepared the good bread."

"Smart man," Clarisse said.

Silena asked whether the palace kitchens could show her how they made the bread in clam shells. Tyson said he knew the baker and could introduce them, provided the kitchen was not in the middle of the lunch rush.

Percy looked at his siblings and felt something settle in his chest. They weren't starting from nothing. They had shared dinners, letters, the April beach visit, the spring dance, family promises and arguments about laundry. Atlantis was adding a setting to those relationships. It wasn't inventing them.

When the steward returned with the updated itinerary, Percy read it aloud. Everybody approved the parts that concerned them. The attendant who had carried the first board looked relieved, and Clarisse gave him a short nod to show she wasn't angry at him personally.

Then they went inside together.

The palace gates remembered Percy.

That was the only way he could describe it to himself as they swam, then walked, up the great ramp. The coral recognized the shape of his current. Guards smiled when they saw him and then stared at the two girls behind him.

Whispers followed them down the pearl corridor.

"The son — and — are those — ?"

"Three? Poseidon has three now above?"

"And a sky daughter—"

Percy felt Clarisse tense beside him and Silena shrink half a step closer. He slowed, just enough that their shoulders brushed his.

"Ignore them," he murmured. "They stared at me too, the first ten times."

"Ten?" Silena whispered.

"At least," Percy said.

Annabeth walked on his other side, her eyes everywhere. She kept stopping to stare at arches grown rather than built, at windows of transparent shell.

"Percy," she whispered, tugging his sleeve. "That column — it is grown around a thermal vent for heating, is it not? And the dome — is that a single piece?"

"Yes," he whispered back, proud swelling in his throat. "Dad grew the main dome in one tide. Mom says he was insufferable for a month after."

She laughed, quiet and bright, and wrote something in a tiny waterproof notebook she had brought.

Then the corridor opened into the Sun Court, and Amphitrite was there, and Poseidon beside her, and Percy's heart slammed against his ribs.

He had seen his father recently, but never like this, at home, tall and calm, his dark hair streaked with gray-green. He wore a simple chiton of deep blue, no crown, his trident left leaning against a pillar.

Amphitrite stood beside him, regal and kind, her black hair coiled with pearls, her gown shifting from green to silver with the light. Her eyes found Percy first, then moved past him, hungry and tender at once, to the girls.

For a moment no one moved.

Then Poseidon stepped forward and opened his arms.

"Kids," he said, his voice rough.

Clarisse broke first. She crossed the court at a run — Clarisse, who never ran to anyone — and hit her father's chest. He caught her, laughed, lifted her slightly off her feet though she was hardly little anymore. Silena followed, crying openly, and he gathered her in with his other arm. Then he looked over their heads at Percy and held out a hand.

Percy went. He was thirteen and tried to act steady, but his father's hand on the back of his neck was warm and huge and smelled of salt and storms, and for a second he could almost picture the child everyone remembered, small and dripping and showing his dad a shell he had found. The feeling came without the memory.

"Father," he mumbled into his father's shoulder. "We came. All of us."

"You did," Poseidon said. He held all three for a long moment, while the court pretended to be very busy elsewhere. "My three. Together under my roof at last."

Amphitrite waited, hands clasped, tears bright in her eyes but unshed because queens did not cry in court. When Poseidon released them, she stepped forward.

"Clarisse. Silena." Her voice trembled. "May I?"

Silena nodded, speechless. Clarisse, fierce Clarisse, nodded too, her jaw tight.

Amphitrite embraced them each in turn, carefully, as if they were precious and might break, which made Clarisse scowl and cling harder to cover it.

"Welcome home," the queen said. "Your brother has told us so much about you that I feel I have watched you grow. Silena, he says you sing to horses. Clarisse, he says you once held a bridge against six dracaenae with a broken spear."

"He exaggerates," Clarisse muttered, ears red.

"He does not," Silena said, wiping her eyes. "Thank you — thank you for having us — Stepmother? My lady?"

"Amphitrite," the queen corrected gently. "Or Mother, if you wish it in time. No rush. We have tides."

Poseidon turned then to Annabeth, who stood a little apart, clutching her pack, trying to be invisible and respectful. Thunder in the sea palace. A sky child far from sky.

Poseidon bowed to her, deeply, a god bowing to a thirteen-year-old girl, and the whole court went silent.

"Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus," he said. "Be welcome in Atlantis. Your father is my brother and my rival and my oldest friend. You are his pride, as my son tells me whenever storms gather. I thank you for standing beside my boy when the sea could not reach him. My house is yours this weekend. Ask for anything."

Annabeth's face went white, then red. She bowed low, her voice steady despite everything.

"Thank you, Lord Poseidon. Thank you for inviting me. I — I will honor your house."

Poseidon smiled, and the water in the fountains leapt slightly, joyful. "Good. Then come. Triton, stop looming. Percy, show your sisters the nursery wing first. I believe they have waited long enough."

---

Percy had rehearsed this in his head a dozen times on the ride down and still felt nervous.

The nursery wing was at the back of the palace, quieter, lower-ceilinged, windows looking out over a garden of anemones. No one used it now. It was kept clean by palace magic, dustless, waiting.

He pushed open the double doors of shell and coral.

"Okay," he said to Clarisse and Silena, his voice echoing slightly. "So. This was mine. When I was little, in this history. Amphitrite told me Dad brought me here after Sally died when I was two. She raised me here. This was my room for years. I know the stories better than I know the room."

Annabeth hung back near the door with Triton, giving the siblings space, but watching with soft eyes.

The room was round, walls painted with a vast mural that wrapped all the way around. A blue whale, life-size, swimming through deep water, her calf tucked close to her side. Around them, dolphins, turtles, a tiny hippocampus foal with oversized eyes. In the corner, near the floor, small handprints in gold paint — Percy's, from when he was five and had decided the whale needed barnacles.

Silena gasped and went straight to the wall, tracing the whale's eye with one finger.

"Percy," she breathed. "Did you — did someone paint this for you?"

"Dad did," Percy said, embarrassed and proud at once. "Most of it. He is not artistic, really. Mom — Amphitrite helped with the faces. Triton painted the turtle because Dad's turtles looked like rocks."

"They did look like rocks," Triton said from the doorway, arms crossed but his voice mild. "I was seven hundred years old and still better at turtles."

Clarisse walked slowly around the room, touching everything. The small bed shaped like a clam shell. The toy chest full of carved whales. The low table where Percy had learned his letters.

On the table lay a drawing, preserved under glass: a crayon picture of three stick figures holding hands, labeled in wobbly letters CLARISSE, SILENA, PERCY. Amphitrite had told Percy he had drawn it when he was six, after Poseidon first told him he had sisters above.

Clarisse stared at it for a long time.

"You knew about us," she said quietly. "Even then."

Percy nodded, throat tight. "That's what they told me. Dad said I had two sisters on land, brave and bright, and that the sea was keeping me ready for them. Apparently I talked to that picture when storms kept me awake. I told you about my day. I wish I could remember it."

Silena made a small sound and sat down hard on the little bed, which creaked. Clarisse sat beside her, stiff at first, then leaning into her sister's side. Percy sat on the floor in front of them, knees up, like he had when he was small.

"Amphitrite said I used to pretend you would come down and we would all sleep here," he admitted. "Silena would take the bed because she is oldest. Clarisse would take the toy chest lid because she said beds were soft. I would sleep on the rug with the whale watching."

"We would have," Silena whispered, tears sliding down her cheeks. "If we had known how."

Clarisse cleared her throat hard, eyes bright. She reached out and ruffled Percy's hair, rough and tender. "Dibs on the toy chest lid," she said. "For tonight. For real."

Percy laughed, watery. "It will not hold you. You are huge."

"I am strong," Clarisse corrected. "There is a difference, kelp."

Annabeth slipped in quietly and sat on the rug beside him, shoulder to shoulder, saying nothing. Triton leaned in the doorway, watching the three of them with an expression Percy had never seen on his brother's face — something unguarded and almost envious.

"The whale mural," Annabeth said softly, to fill the quiet. "It is beautiful. Your father — he loves you very much. It is all over this room."

Percy looked at her, grateful beyond words. She understood. She always understood without needing explanation.

Silena wiped her face and stood, pulling Clarisse up and Percy up with her. She wrapped both arms around them, squeezing hard.

"Okay," she declared, watery but fierce. "New rule. No more growing up apart. We are together now. Atlantis weekend is ours."

"And we are not crying in the nursery," Clarisse said, elbowing her eyes. "Triton is watching."

"I am touched," Triton said dryly. "Truly. I may weep."

"You may leave," Clarisse shot back.

"I may not. Father assigned me as escort, unfortunately. Come, barnacles. Mother wants to dress you for dinner. That should be entertaining for everyone except you."

---

Lunch was served on a terrace overlooking the gardens, at a long table of driftwood.

There were grilled fish, kelp salads, bread baked in clam shells, and a platter of something that wiggled until Triton poked it with his trident and told it to stop.

Poseidon sat at the head, Amphitrite at his side, the three kids on one side, Annabeth placed in honor on Poseidon's right, which made her sit very straight and eat very carefully.

Triton, across from them, made it his mission to torment all three siblings equally.

"So, Clarisse," he said, spearing a piece of fish. "Little brother says you once tried to fight a tidal wave."

"I did fight it," Clarisse said. "And I won."

"You survived," Percy corrected. "There is a difference."

"You were not there," Clarisse said. "You were eight."

"I heard about it," Percy said. "Dad told me. He was proud and terrified."

Poseidon nodded, smiling into his cup. "I was both. She stood on the beach at Montauk and shouted at my wave to come back and take her little boat instead of the ferry. The wave, confused, did a loop."

Clarisse turned red to her ears. "Father!"

"It was brave," Poseidon said. "Foolish. Brave."

Triton turned to Silena. "And you, sweet one. Percy says you calm horses by singing off-key."

"I do not sing off-key," Silena said primly, then glanced at Percy. "Do I?"

"You sing like a siren with a cold," Percy said lovingly. "The horses love it. They fall asleep."

Silena threw a bread roll at him, which he caught and ate. Annabeth laughed into her napkin.

"And Perseus," Triton said, leaning forward. "Shall I tell them about the shark? Headfirst. Stuck. Mother had to pull you out by your feet?"

"You promised, when you told me that story, never to tell anyone else," Percy said, horrified, while his sisters howled.

"I promised Mother never to tell mortals," Triton corrected. "These are family. And Lady Annabeth is practically family, the way this one looks at her."

Percy choked on water. Annabeth choked on bread. Poseidon thumped them both on the back with a hand that nearly knocked them off their stools, laughing deep and bright.

"Easy, son," Poseidon said, eyes dancing. "Your brother teases because he is outnumbered for the first time in a thousand years. Let him enjoy it."

Amphitrite smiled behind her hand. "Triton, be kind. You hid his toy trident so he would have to stay and look for it."

"I did no such thing," Triton said stiffly.

"Amphitrite told me you hid it in the shark tank," Percy said. "Apparently I was seven."

"You retrieved it," Triton said. "Barely."

"That is how you two keep telling it," Percy agreed. He looked at his sisters. "She says he pretended not to watch, but he followed me the whole way with a rescue net."

Triton threw his napkin at him. The whole table laughed, and even the guards by the pillars smiled.

Annabeth caught Percy's eye across the table and mouthed, You were adorable.

Percy mouthed back, You have no proof.

She held up her little notebook, where she had sketched the whale mural from memory, quick and accurate. Under it she had written: Percy's room — loved.

His chest ached in the good way.

After lunch, Amphitrite rose and clapped her hands.

"Now," she said. "Dinner is formal. Court dress. Silena, Clarisse, come with me. We will find you something proper. Percy, your father wants to walk with you. Annabeth, dear, you are welcome to join us or rest. There is a library if you prefer quiet."

Silena lit up. Clarisse looked like she had been sentenced.

"Formal?" Clarisse said. "I have armor. Armor is formal."

"Armor is not formal in Atlantis," Amphitrite said sweetly. "Come, dear. It will be painless. Mostly."

---

The women's chambers smelled of pearl oil and flowers.

Annabeth had chosen to come, curious, and sat on a cushioned bench with her notebook, watching as Amphitrite's attendants — three nereids with swift hands — fussed over the Poseidon sisters.

Silena was in heaven. She stood on a dais in a gown of sea-green silk, her dark hair loose down her back, while Amphitrite herself stood behind her with a comb of coral.

"Your hair is like your father's tide," Amphitrite murmured, weaving in strands of pearls. "Thick, dark, with light underneath. My son has the same, but he never lets me touch it. He says braids are for girls and princes."

"He let me braid it once," Silena said, glowing under the queen's hands. "When he was nine and had a fever and did not notice. Clarisse has pictures."

"Delete those pictures," came Clarisse's muffled voice from behind a screen.

Percy was not there to defend himself, but Annabeth laughed and made a note: Ask Silena for braid photo bribe material.

Amphitrite's hands were gentle, rhythmic. She braided Silena's hair into an intricate crown, looping and tucking, her rings flashing.

"There," she said softly. "A princess of the sea should feel crowned even without a crown. Do you like it?"

Silena looked in the mirror of polished shell and burst into tears.

"It is beautiful," she whispered. "No one ever — my mom — she has not been here for things like this in so long. I did not expect — thank you."

Amphitrite's eyes shone. She kissed the top of Silena's head, careful not to disturb the braid.

"You are welcome, daughter of my husband," she said. "You are lovely. And kind. The sea needs kind princesses."

Annabeth blinked hard and pretended to be very interested in ceiling architecture.

On the other side of the room, comedy unfolded.

Clarisse emerged from behind the screen in a dress of deep crimson overlaid with bronze scales, cut for movement but unmistakably courtly. She walked as if the floor were lava.

"I cannot breathe," she announced. "I cannot sit. I cannot fight. This is a trap."

"It is a dress, dear," Amphitrite said calmly. "Breathe out. Shoulders back. Chin up, but not like you are about to headbutt someone."

"I always look like that," Clarisse said.

"You do," Annabeth agreed. "It is your charm."

Amphitrite circled her, adjusting. "Court manners in Atlantis are simple. Bow to anyone older than three hundred years unless they bow first. Do not challenge anyone to a duel at dinner unless dessert has been served."

"What if they insult my brother?" Clarisse asked.

"Then you smile," Amphitrite said, demonstrating a smile so sharp it could cut glass, "and you say, 'How interesting,' and you tell me later, and I handle it."

Clarisse attempted the smile. It looked like a snarl.

"Less teeth," Silena giggled.

Clarisse tried again. Less teeth, more menace.

"Better," Amphitrite said. "Frightening, but better. Now walk. Glide. Pretend the floor is water and you are a shark."

Clarisse glided. It looked like a shark. A shark about to attack.

Annabeth clapped. "Perfect. Terrifying. Very you."

Clarisse finally laughed, helpless, and twirled once, awkward and joyful, the skirt flaring. "Fine. Fine! I look good. Silena, do I look good?"

"You look like war in a dress," Silena said. "Beautiful war."

Amphitrite turned to Annabeth then, and her expression softened further. "And you, dear storm girl. Come. Let me fix your hair as well. Guests of honor should shine."

Annabeth hesitated, then sat on the stool Silena vacated. The queen's hands in her blond hair were careful, respectful.

"Your sister Thalia has hair like lightning," Amphitrite said. "Yours is like sun through shallow water. Percy talks about you the way sailors talk about stars. Constantly. Annoyingly. Adoringly."

Annabeth went scarlet. "He — does?"

"He does," Silena and Clarisse chorused.

"He says you are the smartest person he knows," Amphitrite continued, weaving a simple blue ribbon through a neat braid. "He says you make plans the way the sea makes currents — you do not force, you guide. He is proud of you. We are grateful to you, for keeping him afloat in both worlds."

Annabeth stared at her hands. "He keeps me afloat too," she said quietly. "When I get — when my mind spins too fast, he slows me down. He is my — he is my best friend."

Amphitrite tied off the braid and rested her hands briefly on Annabeth's shoulders. "Then guard each other well. Young love, young friendship — the best currents start small." She smiled in the mirror. "There. Beautiful."

Annabeth looked at herself — cheeks pink, gray eyes bright, hair neatly braided with sea silk — and smiled back, shy and pleased.

Clarisse threw an arm around her shoulders, careful of both their hair. "Welcome to the embarrassing family hair club, sky girl."

"I am honored," Annabeth said, leaning into her.

---

While the girls were with Amphitrite, Poseidon walked with his son along the private terrace.

No guards. No Triton. Only father and son, and the deep blue beyond the dome.

Percy was quiet, his hands in his pockets, his formal tunic already feeling stiff. Poseidon walked with his hands clasped behind his back, matching his son's pace.

"You did well," Poseidon said at last. "Bringing them. Sharing your home. That took courage."

Percy shrugged, embarrassed. "They are my sisters. This is theirs too."

"It is," Poseidon agreed. "But you lived here first, alone. It would be natural to guard it. Instead you opened the doors wide. I am proud of you."

Percy kicked at a loose pearl on the path. "Clarisse was scared. She hid it, but she was. She thought Atlantis would not want her. Because — because her mom is not — because she is not —"

"Because she is not Amphitrite's?" Poseidon finished gently.

Percy nodded.

Poseidon stopped and turned to face his son fully.

"Listen to me, Perseus. Clarisse is mine. Silena is mine. You are mine. The sea does not divide. Any who say otherwise in my halls will answer to me."

Percy's throat closed. "Tell them that. Not me. I know."

"I will," Poseidon said. He placed a large hand on Percy's head, briefly, the way he had when Percy was small. "You have grown, my boy. Thirteen, and already a bridge between worlds. Between your sisters. Between sea and sky."

At the mention of sky, Percy blushed.

Poseidon laughed. "Ah. Yes. The sky girl. Annabeth." His smile turned knowing. "She has good manners. Good eyes. She looks at my palace like she wants to understand it, not conquer it. I approve."

"Father," Percy groaned.

"What? A father may approve. Amphitrite wrote to me that you two are sweet together. Happy. Young. True." Poseidon's voice softened. "Hold her hand when the corridors get dark. That is all I ask. Be kind. Be patient. The rest will come with tides."

Percy stared out at the trench to hide his burning face. "I want to show her my cove. Is that okay? The secret one. With the shells."

Poseidon's eyebrows rose. "Your secret cove? You have shown no one that, not even Triton."

"I know," Percy said. "But she — she would get it. She keeps things. She treasures small things. I found something for her there."

Poseidon studied him for a long moment, then nodded slowly, a smile spreading.

"Then take her. At sunset. The light is best. And Percy —"

"Yeah?"

"Bring your sisters back for dinner alive. Your stepmother will have my trident if Clarisse duels a duke."

Percy laughed, and the ache in his chest eased into warmth. "Yes, sir."

They walked back together, father and son, shoulders brushing, while the sea glowed around them.

---

Dinner was grand and chaotic.

The great hall filled with music. Lords and ladies took their places. At the high table, Poseidon presided with Amphitrite beside him.

Silena glowed, chatting happily with a nereid duchess about horses. Clarisse sat very straight, executing her court smile at intervals.

Annabeth sat beside Percy, her notebook finally put away. She tried new foods, asked three lords about dome construction until they begged for mercy, and defended Clarisse when a young merman snickered at her fork grip.

"She holds it like a spear because she could kill you with either," Annabeth said sweetly, and the merman paled and apologized.

Clarisse stared at her, then grinned wide and true. "Sky girl has teeth."

"Always did," Percy said proudly.

Between courses, Poseidon stood and raised his cup.

"To my children," he said, his voice carrying without effort. "Clarisse, who is valor. Silena, who is mercy. Perseus, who is loyalty. Different mothers, same sea. Different shores, same home. And to our guest, Annabeth Grace, who proves that sky and sea can stand together."

The hall stood and drank. Clarisse gripped the edge of the table to keep herself steady. Silena cried openly and did not bother to hide it. Percy felt Annabeth's hand slip into his under the table, squeeze once, then release, proper and quick and everything.

Triton stood last, raising his own cup with a smirk.

"To my new barnacles," he said. "May you grow fast and stick tight."

Even Clarisse laughed at that, wiping her eyes angrily.

After dessert — which was ice cream made from sweetened cloud-water traded from Zeus's stores, a diplomatic dessert — Percy tugged Annabeth's sleeve.

"Come," he whispered. "Sunset. I want to show you something. Just us."

Annabeth glanced at Silena, who waved her off with a knowing smile, and at Clarisse, who mouthed, Be back by curfew, and at Amphitrite, who nodded permission with twinkling eyes.

Percy led her out through a side passage he knew by heart, down narrowing corridors, past the nursery wing, to a small door of plain coral half-hidden behind a curtain of kelp.

"They told me this was my escape," he explained as he pushed it open. "When councils got boring. When Triton was annoying. Now I come here when I need quiet. When I miss Sally."

Beyond was a tunnel, short and natural, opening onto a cove.

Annabeth stepped out and stopped, breath catching.

The cove was small, enclosed on three sides by white rock, open on the fourth to the deep water beyond, but shielded by a curtain of light — the dome's edge, thin here, letting in the last of the surface sunset filtered through miles of water. The light came down in shafts of gold-green and rose, striking the sand and the shallow pools.

The sand was not sand but crushed shell, pink and white, soft as powder. Tide pools dotted the rock, each a tiny world of starfish and anemones. And everywhere, shells — perfect, unbroken, sorted by size in a way that spoke of a lonely child with time and love: spirals, fans, conches, cowries, lined along natural shelves.

In the center, a flat rock served as a table. On it lay Percy's childhood treasures: a smooth piece of driftwood shaped like a horse, a sea-glass marble, a broken trident tip he had found and never told Triton about.

"This is yours," Annabeth whispered. "Your secret place."

Percy nodded, suddenly shy. He was thirteen, a veteran of monsters, and his hands shook.

"No one else knows," he said. "Not even Dad, I think. Or he pretends not to. I used to come here and talk to the water. I told it about camp before I ever went. About you, after I met you. I said, 'Water, this is Annabeth. She is brilliant. You will like her.'"

Annabeth turned to him, eyes bright with tears she would never shed in court. "Percy—"

He went to the rock table and picked up a small box he had left there that morning, woven from kelp and sealed with wax. He had prepared it before lunch, hands clumsy.

"I found this last summer," he said, opening it. Inside, on soft sea cotton, lay a shell. Small, perfect, a spiral of palest pink fading to white, with a natural hole at the tip and a luster like dawn. "It is a sunrise shell. They only wash up here after storms. I kept it because — because it reminded me of you. Smart and — and bright and — it survives storms."

He held it out. "I made it into a necklace. So you can keep it. If you want."

Annabeth stared at the shell, then at him. She was very still, the sunset light turning her hair to gold.

"You made this for me," she said.

"Yeah," Percy said, ears burning. "Silena helped with the cord. It is adjustable, for — for swimming or — whatever. You do not have to wear it if—"

Annabeth reached out and closed his fingers around the shell for a moment, holding his hand closed, her own hands over his.

"Percy Jackson," she said seriously, gray eyes locked on green, "shut up and put it on me."

He laughed, shaky with relief, and she turned, lifting her braid. He fastened the cord — blue sea silk, knotted with care — around her neck, his fingers brushing her skin lightly. The shell rested just below her throat, glowing against her skin.

She turned back and touched it reverently.

"It is the most beautiful thing anyone has ever given me," she whispered. "I will wear it always. Even flying. Especially flying."

They sat together on the pink sand, shoulders touching, knees drawn up, watching the light shafts move.

Annabeth leaned her head against his shoulder, trusting, the way she had through their remembered quests and these new days. He leaned his head gently against hers, careful of her braid.

"Thank you for bringing me to your other home," she said quietly. "I was scared. Everyone thinks Zeus kids are proud, but I was scared to be the sky girl in the sea. But this — your nursery, your sisters, your cove — you shared it all. I feel — I feel like I understand you better. The parts that are quiet."

Percy swallowed. "You are my best friend," he said. "My partner. Wherever I am home — camp, the apartment I remember, here — you make it more home."

She slipped her hand into his, sandy and warm, and they stayed like that while fish flickered beyond the light curtain and the water breathed in and out.

"I love this place," Annabeth said. "I love — I love how loved you are here. Your dad's mural. Your brother teasing. Your — your stepmother braiding hair. You were a happy little boy here, even when you missed us above. I can feel it."

"It looks like I was happy," Percy admitted. "And lonely, from the stories they tell. I must have missed having friends who knew both worlds. Now I have three someones. Four, with Thalia shouting from above." He squeezed her hand. "Especially you."

Annabeth bumped her forehead lightly against his temple, a small, sweet touch that made his heart thump. "Especially me," she agreed, teasing to cover the tenderness. "I am exceptional."

"You are," he said simply.

They laughed, and the cove echoed it back, kind.

---

They returned for curfew to find Silena and Clarisse waiting in the nursery, in pajamas borrowed from the palace — soft tunics embroidered with waves — sitting on the little clam bed and the rug, clearly waiting up.

"There you are," Silena said, patting the rug. "Tell us everything. Did you show her the cove?"

Percy stopped in the doorway, Annabeth beside him, shell necklace gleaming. "How did you know about the cove?"

"Triton told us," Clarisse said. "He said, and I quote, 'The brat has a secret sobbing cove where he talks to shells.'"

"I do not sob to shells," Percy protested, sitting with them. Annabeth sat with him, naturally, showing Silena the necklace.

Silena admired it properly, gasping, while Clarisse nodded approval.

"Good shell," Clarisse said. "Good cord work. Good boy, little brother."

"Thank you," Percy said solemnly. "Good dress. Good smile. Terrifying, but good."

Silena braided Annabeth's loose strands back into place while Annabeth told them about the cove's light and pools, her voice growing sleepy. Clarisse demonstrated her court smile until they all collapsed laughing. Percy showed them the gold handprints and let Silena add her hand beside his, palm to wall, laughing when the magic left a faint silver print.

Triton found them there an hour later, supposed to enforce bedtime, and stopped in the doorway.

The three Poseidon kids were asleep in a pile on the rug — Silena in the middle, Clarisse sprawled with one arm over her brother, Percy with his head on Silena's knee — and Annabeth dozed against the clam bed, Percy's blanket tucked around her, sunrise shell glinting at her throat.

Triton stood for a long moment, golden eyes unreadable. Then he fetched blankets from the chest and covered each of them carefully, even Annabeth, with surprising gentleness. He dimmed the pearls to night-blue.

Poseidon and Amphitrite came later to check, drawn by Triton's silence, and found their heir sitting against the wall, watching the four sleepers with a small, unguarded smile.

Poseidon rested a hand on his eldest son's shoulder. Amphitrite pressed her fingers to her lips, tears bright again.

"Three barnacles and a star," Triton murmured. "They stuck."

"They did," Poseidon whispered. "At last."

He bent and kissed each child's forehead — Clarisse, Silena, Percy — and bowed to Annabeth in her sleep, grateful.

In the morning they would return to camp, to drills and prophecies and mortal school worries. Thalia would demand her messages. Chiron would ask about protocol. Campers would crowd for stories.

But for now, under the whale mural, with the sea humming through the walls, the three children of Poseidon slept side by side in the nursery for the first time, Percy's girlfriend and his sisters' friend safe beside them, home together at last.

The sea remembered this night, and kept it.
