## Chapter 9: The Oracle

The morning after their second visit to the attic, Percy tried to repair a practice shield while Clarisse watched him from the doorway of the armory.

"You've got the strap backward," she said.

"I know."

"Then why are you sewing it on?"

"Because I thought it might appreciate the change."

Clarisse crossed the room and put down the stack of bucklers she'd been carrying. Percy had volunteered for an hour of equipment repair after the storm. It was supposed to give him a task he could finish with his hands, which had seemed appealing when the alternative was another argument with Annabeth about the Oracle. Instead he'd spent fifteen minutes threading a needle through the wrong side of a leather backing.

"Let me see," Clarisse said.

Percy handed over the shield. His sister unpicked the last three stitches and turned the leather right side out. She did not tell him he was being an idiot. The restraint was more alarming than an insult would have been.

"Did you sleep?" she asked.

"Some."

"How much?"

"Enough to be sitting upright."

"Percy."

He watched her test the torn buckle. "I didn't want you to have to worry about what we're doing."

"That's a terrible excuse for making me worry about what you're doing."

She handed the shield back and showed him where to put the next stitch. He followed the needle through the leather, pulled the thread tight, and waited. Clarisse didn't move away.

"I can tell when you're lying about how tired you are," she said. "I can't tell whether you're in trouble until you tell me. Those are different things."

Percy thought about the note in his jacket, still sealed and addressed to Chiron. He and Annabeth had carried it through the previous night's trip and brought it back unopened. It hadn't made entering the attic sensible. It had mostly made him aware that they were prepared to write down their reasons, but not prepared to tell anyone else they had gone.

"We found a copy of a prophecy," he said. "An older one. We're trying to get someone who knows the Oracle to tell us whether it means anything."

"And you can't wait for Chiron?"

"We've tried."

"Try harder."

He could almost hear her thinking of an argument she didn't quite know how to make. Clarisse was good at taking responsibility for a room full of campers who were about to do something stupid. She was less practiced at admitting she cared about the stupid one.

"Annabeth isn't the only person who'll go looking for you if something happens," she added.

Percy looked up. His sister was studying the torn strap as though she hadn't said anything unusual.

"I know."

"Then remember it before you decide what you can risk."

Clarisse reclaimed the shield and demonstrated the next stitch. Her hand was steady and her instructions exact. Percy thought about how thoroughly he remembered losing people in a world she had never known. That fear was part of what drove him toward answers, and she had no way of guessing it. She could still ask him to be less careless with the people who were here.

By the time they finished the repair, he had promised to take his copied notes to Chiron that morning. Clarisse insisted on checking the buckle before letting him go.

The handwritten note from Chiron arrived at breakfast. He had seen their request to question the Oracle and would not authorize an interview while both the attic's protections and the Hall's ward network were under inspection. He invited them to submit specific questions to the archivist and warned against entering the building outside posted hours.

Annabeth read it twice at the end of the table, then folded it very carefully.

"He knows about the older copy," she said. "We gave him the date. He could at least tell us whether it was filed with the rest of the patrol reports."

"Maybe he hasn't checked yet."

"Then he could say that."

Percy pushed his bowl out of the way. The pavilion was loud enough to cover their conversation, but he kept his voice low.

"We can ask him directly after inspection. Before we decide anything about tonight."

"I've already tried directly."

"You sent a letter. It's not quite the same as looking him in the eye while he explains why he won't let you see something."

"You're becoming very committed to other people's procedures."

"I got assigned to repair an entire rack because I ignored one. It's made an impression."

Annabeth glanced at the strap he'd sewn. "Did you finish your shield?"

"Clarisse provided expert consultation."

"That means she fixed it."

"It means we are a functioning team."

For a moment Annabeth smiled. Percy was relieved to see it. Then her expression changed when she saw Thalia carrying a stack of practice mats past the pavilion.

"We should tell her about the refusal."

"We should," Percy said. "Not just when we need someone to help us get around it."

After breakfast they found Thalia at the climbing wall, where she'd taken over the youngest campers' warm-up because the regular instructor was helping with Hall repairs. She listened to Annabeth describe the archivist's response while tying a safety line around a boy's waist.

"Chiron said no," Thalia repeated. "Then he said he'd consider access after the inspections."

"That's what he wrote."

"Which part of that is confusing?"

Annabeth folded her arms. "The part where nobody will tell us when those inspections end, and a disputed transcript may be moved into protected storage before I can compare it with the other copies."

"Protected storage is not a furnace, Beth. They aren't burning it."

"I know what protected storage means."

Thalia checked the knot and waved the boy toward the instructor. Only when he was safely on the wall did she turn back.

"If you want my help asking for a date, fine. If you want me to say it's reasonable to climb through another window, forget it."

Percy opened his mouth. Thalia pointed at him.

"And you. Last time you told me you were going to be more careful."

"I am being careful."

"That's not the same as being honest."

The remark landed harder than Percy expected. He didn't know what Thalia had figured out about the previous night and did not want to force her to say it in front of the younger campers. Annabeth stepped between them, not to protect him from the argument but to prevent it from becoming the afternoon's entertainment.

"We're asking for another meeting," she said. "We haven't agreed to do anything else."

"Then do the meeting," Thalia replied. "I'll ask about the archive transfers myself. You can decide what you want to tell me afterward."

She returned to her students. The exchange had not produced an ally for a midnight visit, but it had produced something less convenient: a person who expected them to keep their word.

They went to the Big House in the middle of the afternoon, carrying their public notebook and a copy of the earlier four lines. Percy had left the pages about their remembered past in Cabin Three, wedged beneath the false bottom of a drawer Clarisse never used. He didn't like leaving them there, but the alternative was taking an account of Sally's living childhood into a building where every god and counselor might decide it was their business.

Chiron met them on the porch. He had been overseeing a delivery of replacement timber and looked as though the mere sight of another form made him tired.

"Mr. Jackson. Miss Grace. The archivist has your questions."

"We know," Annabeth said. "I was hoping you could explain why the Oracle has to remain inaccessible even when we're offering to go in with supervision."

"Because the protections around the chair are active and changing," Chiron said. "We do not yet know whether they are connected to the unstable ward in the Hall. A supervised visit is not safe merely because a supervisor is present."

"What if we stayed at the stairs?"

"The stairs are inside the same warded area."

"Could you take a rubbing of the bronze strip? Or have Zoe look at it while someone else stays with the Oracle?"

Chiron's gaze sharpened. "What bronze strip?"

Annabeth hesitated. Percy felt her searching for a truthful answer that wouldn't require confessing exactly when she'd last seen the chair. Chiron noticed.

"We copied an older floor survey," Percy said, then wished he hadn't made the explanation sound so complete. "It showed a bronze fitting near the chair. We wanted to know if that fitting is still there."

"An older floor survey," Chiron repeated.

"Yes. It's in the public records."

Chiron extended one hand. Annabeth gave him the catalog number. He read it, his expression difficult to interpret, then handed it back.

"Thank you. I will check whether it corresponds to the present installation. But I cannot promise to share everything I find while there is a potential threat to campers."

Annabeth looked past him toward the attic windows. "You keep telling us to trust the process. If we don't know what part of it we're waiting for, how are we meant to tell whether anything is happening?"

"You may ask me for a status update after the eclipse preparations," Chiron said. "I will not invent a deadline in order to reassure you."

That was probably the fairest answer she would get. Percy knew it. He also knew from the way she gripped her notebook that she was angry enough to keep arguing.

"Could we help with the preparations instead?" he asked. "There are maps we already made for the younger campers. Annabeth knows which drainage paths flooded during the storm."

Chiron turned toward him. "That would be useful. The maintenance crew needs a separate count of barriers and safe crossings. I will make sure the steward includes your notes."

He looked at Annabeth again, gentler now. "Being willing to wait is not the same as surrendering your curiosity. I have no intention of letting these questions disappear. But if I let a twelve-year-old enter a dangerous ward because she is right to be worried, I have failed her as surely as if I refused to hear her at all."

Annabeth stared at the porch boards. "I understand."

Percy could tell she understood the words. He could not tell whether she accepted the decision.

They left with an assigned safety map, a promised status report and no permission to see the Oracle. The work was important. Annabeth knew that better than anyone. It was also exactly the kind of work that might take all afternoon and leave them with no more knowledge than they'd had at breakfast.

At the edge of the green she stopped and turned toward Percy.

"You still want to wait?"

"I want to know what we're going to do if we don't."

"There's an old lock on the attic door. It was changed when they repaired the upstairs landing, and the side window is still loose. I know how to open both."

"That sounds less like a question than a prepared answer."

"I've been thinking about it since yesterday."

He sat on the fence rail. A couple of Hermes campers hurried past with buckets of nails, arguing about whether anyone would notice if they took three extra for a prank. Percy watched them go, envying the fact that their worst problem was likely to be punishment from a counselor.

"We don't touch the chair," he said finally. "We don't try to undo any marks. If the air changes or the ward reacts, we leave."

"And we bring the transcript copies, not the private notebook."

"And before we go, we write down where we'll be. If we don't come back, Chiron finds us. He can be angry about it when he's done rescuing us."

Annabeth looked unhappy with that part but nodded.

Percy had hoped he would be able to refuse her. He had also heard the fear in Chiron's voice when the centaur mentioned unstable protections, and he remembered what the black smoke from the Hall had done to her bandaged fingers. The risk of entering the attic was real. Their decision did not become responsible just because they had drawn a map of it.

"We shouldn't be doing this," he said.

"I know."

"Then why are we?"

"Because I want to ask her a question before somebody else decides which questions she can hear."

He couldn't argue with that without pretending he wanted something different. He did want answers. He wanted to understand why the girl beside him had a childhood with Thalia that she could not remember and why his own mother's death had become a settled event to everybody except him. It frightened him how quickly those wants could justify almost anything.

They walked separately back to their cabins to wait for curfew. Percy finished his assigned rack repair and handed the instructor his tool count. Annabeth checked the public route map one last time and left a signed copy with Bea. Neither used the afternoon's work as proof that their midnight plan was reasonable.

By dusk, they had agreed on a route, a limit, and the possibility that they might find nothing at all.

Locks were suggestions; guards were a more serious problem. Percy and Annabeth had spent years learning the difference, although neither of them liked how much of that practice still fitted into twelve-year-old hands. They went at midnight anyway, agreeing once more to leave the chair untouched and to abandon the attempt if the ward behaved unpredictably.

---

In Cabin One, Annabeth spread a blanket across the floor and tried to lay out two versions of the Hall's floor plan without letting their corners overlap.

Thalia sat on the opposite side of the blanket, removing knots from an old coil of climbing cord. She had brought it home from the wall because half the newer ropes had been borrowed for repairs and she wasn't willing to trust the next morning's lesson to somebody else's hurried inspection.

"Why the blanket?" she asked.

"The floor has a loose nail. It catches the edge of the paper."

"That one? I told the cabin steward about it."

"He said he was going to fix it after the Hall inspections."

Thalia snorted. "Of course he did. The whole camp is going to spend the next month living with every problem nobody noticed before the storm."

Annabeth flattened a corner of the older plan with a book. She had been trying to calculate where the new drainage channel connected to the old foundation. It was a question the repair crew could answer if she managed to give them a legible drawing. She didn't need her memories of an entirely different camp to do it.

"Can you hand me the ruler?"

Thalia found it beneath a sweater and passed it over. Annabeth tried to grip it with her injured fingers, winced and shifted to her other hand. Her sister noticed and reached for the notebook.

"I can hold the paper."

"I've got it."

"Then I can hold the ruler."

Annabeth looked up. It was easy to argue because Thalia had offered the help, and harder because she hadn't tried to take anything away. She let the ruler go. Thalia held it along the scale while Annabeth marked the measurements.

They worked for several minutes without talking. Thalia made a series of knots in the cord, tugging each tight and loosening it again. The work was familiar enough that her hands barely seemed to need her attention.

"You always hated fractions," she said eventually.

"I still hate badly written ones."

"You used to make me count the steps between the kitchen and the front door. Then you'd tell me I'd got the distance wrong because I was taller."

Annabeth tried to picture it. She could imagine a kitchen, a narrow hallway and a child making measurements, but the space did not attach itself to any memory she actually possessed. Thalia's account was plausible; that was part of what made it painful.

"What did you do?" Annabeth asked.

"I made you walk it again in my boots."

Annabeth laughed, surprised. "That can't have helped."

"It didn't. You fell over twice. Then you refused to take the boots off because you said the numbers would only be consistent if you wore them until supper."

"That does sound like me."

"It was." Thalia looped the cord around her thumb. "You were seven. We argued all afternoon. I had to explain to Chiron why your ankles were bruised, and you told him I'd interfered with your experiment."

Annabeth smiled because the story was funny. It was also her sister's, not hers. Thalia told it with the ease of someone who could step into that afternoon whenever she wanted. Annabeth could only borrow the picture.

"Was I right about the distance?"

"Not even close."

"Then I had a flawed method."

"You were seven."

"People can have flawed methods at seven."

Thalia shook her head, amused. Then she looked at Annabeth more carefully.

"You don't remember any of that, do you?"

The humor stayed in the room for a moment before it thinned.

"No," Annabeth said. "I wish I did."

Thalia turned the cord over. She had known the answer before asking. Annabeth could see that.

"I keep bringing up things you don't remember," Thalia said. "I tell myself if I find the right story you'll laugh the way you used to, and it'll feel normal."

"I laughed just now."

"You laughed because it was funny. That's not the same thing."

Annabeth put her pencil down. She had a dozen possible responses, most of them technically true and none useful. She could tell Thalia about Luke finding her in the woods with a knife and a stolen jacket, and how that Thalia had given her a place to belong. Her sister might recognize some part of it, or she might be frightened by a story that didn't match any year of the life she'd lived.

"I can't give you your memories back from my side," Annabeth said at last. "I know you were there. I know those things happened to you. I just can't pretend they happened to me the way you remember."

"Are you asking me to stop talking about them?"

"No. I want you to keep telling me. Sometimes I can picture it afterward. Sometimes I can't. But it matters that you remember."

Thalia stared at the cord. She was silent long enough that Annabeth considered adding something. Then her sister said, "If you want me to stop for a day, you can tell me. I'd rather know than find out afterward I've been making you miserable."

"Okay."

"And if you want to ask about something I know, you can ask without doing that thing where you act as though you're interviewing a witness."

Annabeth looked at the notebooks beside her. "That might take practice."

"It will."

They went back to the map. Thalia pointed to a missing measurement at the northern entrance and admitted she had taken a shortcut through the closed path the previous morning to check the drainage line. Annabeth stared at her.

"You went through a restricted area?"

"Outside the barrier, not inside the Hall."

"That's not what the sign says."

Thalia stopped knotting. For a moment they both looked at one another, and Annabeth saw how much easier it was for her sister to justify a rule broken in the name of safety than a rule broken in the name of knowledge.

"You could have asked maintenance for the measurement," Annabeth said.

"I did. They couldn't tell me until the afternoon, and the route was being used that morning."

"So you decided to check."

"From outside the barrier. With Bea standing nearby. And I told Chiron afterward."

Annabeth considered that carefully. She had been preparing to enter the Big House after midnight without telling anyone. Thalia had made her own risky judgment but had shared it with someone who could help. It wasn't the same thing, which annoyed Annabeth more than if it had been.

"Thanks for telling me," she said.

Thalia resumed the knot. "I'd like you to do the same."

Annabeth glanced at the secret notebook beneath the blanket. She had planned where to go and what to copy. There was still time to tell her sister that she was thinking about returning to the Oracle. The honest words formed in her mind and stayed there.

Instead she asked whether Thalia could keep holding the ruler.

Thalia did. They finished the map, labeled the measurements they couldn't confirm, and took it together to the noticeboard before curfew. Annabeth was glad they'd managed to work without fighting. She was ashamed of how much of the evening she'd spent deciding what not to say. Both feelings went with her when she returned to Cabin One, folded the private notes and waited for the house to go quiet.

---

Camp lay dark, cabins shuttered, the dining pavilion cold. The surf broke faintly beyond the trees. Annabeth carried her notebook beneath her jacket, the known versions of the prophecy copied on facing pages. She had removed the pages comparing their remembered lives and left those under a loose board in Cabin One. Percy kept checking the windows of the Big House. A discovery made under a shaded lamp could look less convincing in daylight, especially when explaining why they had been in a forbidden attic.

Before they reached the Big House, they had to cross the stretch of open ground between the cabins and the green. Someone had replaced the lantern on the path after the storm, and its light fell across a new sign announcing that the attic and the Hall were closed to all campers pending review. Beneath the notice Chiron had added the date and his signature. It would have been easier to ignore the rule if it were an old, forgotten thing nobody expected them to read.

Annabeth studied the sign as though its exact wording might offer a technical exception.

"It says access is restricted," she whispered. "It doesn't say the entire house is restricted."

"You're not going to find a loophole that makes climbing through a window less like climbing through a window."

"I wasn't proposing one. I was reading."

"That is your most dangerous hobby."

She did not smile. Percy stopped teasing and moved to the shadow of the nearest oak. From there they could see the corner of the porch and the path that ran down toward the infirmary. The camp had changed its patrol routes after the storm. That, too, was a sensible measure that made the decision to sneak around them feel worse.

The first pair passed six minutes after the conch. Two senior campers walked the path with covered lanterns and a clipboard. One paused to inspect the hinges on the storehouse gate, then wrote something down. Percy waited until their footsteps faded.

"Who are they?" he asked.

"Hermes and Apollo, I think. It doesn't matter."

"It matters if we have to explain ourselves."

"If we have to explain ourselves, we're not going to pretend we were out for a walk."

He touched the sealed note inside his jacket. She had insisted on keeping it with them rather than leaving it on an empty bunk. Percy still thought a note was more useful if someone could find it before a search became necessary, but leaving it in public might expose their secret for no reason. They had argued about that at dinner, quiet enough that Silena had mistaken it for a dispute over a homework assignment. In the end he'd compromised by writing the route and date on the outside of the envelope. It was an imperfect plan. Everything about tonight was.

Another patrol passed behind the cabins, closer than either expected. Percy moved back without thinking, letting the darkness between the trees cover them. Annabeth followed, but her heel caught a root. He caught her sleeve before she went down.

"Thanks," she murmured.

"Your hand?"

"Fine. Stop checking every time I move."

He let go. The response was sharp enough to sting, but he understood why she'd said it. Every time someone saw the bandage on her fingers they asked whether she could still hold a pencil, lift a cup, draw a bow, climb stairs. She had been the person who figured out how to keep them alive in Tartarus. Her new body needed rest and her powers needed training. Neither fact made her patience inexhaustible.

When the next patrol had moved beyond the kitchens, they crossed the green at a low run. Percy's breath came faster than he wanted. It frustrated him that his body still had to catch up to intentions formed when he was seventeen. He had to stop beneath the side window to listen for movement inside.

"You're breathing like you ran from the border," Annabeth whispered.

"I blame the architecture. Too much distance between places."

"You ran too fast."

"I also blame the architecture for that."

She handed him the shaded lamp. Her face was pale in its small circle of light, and for a moment he wanted to call the entire thing off. Instead he asked whether she could still climb.

"I can," she said. "And before you ask, if my hand starts hurting I'll tell you. I'm not interested in falling out of a second-story window to win an argument."

He nodded. That was the best answer he was going to get.

A cart stood beneath the eaves, loaded with old library boxes destined for the temporary archive. The day crew had stacked the boxes by category and tied a list to the top one. Annabeth glanced at the list, then surprised Percy by stepping away from the wall to look.

"Is that our catalog series?" she asked.

"Which one?"

"The patrol reports. The crate labels begin with the same letters."

He joined her. Each box had been sealed with a strip of cloth under twine and stamped with a transfer number. The fifth held materials from the years they had been investigating. It was marked *Inspection pending*. Not an invitation. Not a confession. Just a box waiting to be checked by people who had more authority than they did.

"Do you want to open it?" Percy asked.

"No. If the seals are broken they'll know somebody interfered, and we'll lose the only record we can trace properly."

"Glad to hear we're developing standards."

"Don't be smug. We are still trespassing."

She copied the crate number in the public notebook, not the private one. Percy held the lamp while she wrote. It was strange how easily a few scratched letters could feel like progress. He knew the boxes might contain nothing more than ordinary patrol receipts. Annabeth apparently knew that too.

Then a light appeared behind the window.

They froze. A figure moved through the downstairs hallway, carrying a lantern low at one side. It stopped near the storage room. Percy thought he recognized the shape of Chiron's shoulders, then realized he was drawing conclusions from a shadow thrown across a curtain. The figure bent over something. A drawer opened. After a moment the lantern moved away toward the front of the house.

Annabeth caught Percy's sleeve.

"Was that him?"

"I don't know."

"If it was, he's awake."

"Possibly. That makes this an even worse idea than it already was."

She looked at the glass above them. "We can leave."

He expected her to argue after the words. She didn't. Percy considered the old copy they'd found, the dates that didn't match, the silence around the Oracle, and the fact that they were no closer to knowing whether anyone had placed that bronze strip deliberately. He could almost hear Chiron saying that curiosity did not make a dangerous ward safe.

"If we go in, we use the route we planned," he said. "We don't look in other rooms. We don't take anything. And if you see that black smoke again, I don't care whether it says your name. We leave."

Annabeth gave him a look. "It hasn't said my name."

"Consider this planning for contingencies."

She examined the dark upper window. "All right."

There was no heroic feeling in the agreement. Percy steadied the lamp and tested the cart wheel to make sure it would not roll if they used it as a step. He disliked how quickly ordinary objects became part of an operation he'd have condemned if one of the younger campers proposed it. Before he boosted Annabeth up, he looked once more toward the lantern that had disappeared inside the house.

He hoped Chiron was already asleep. He also hoped Chiron would be able to help them if anything went wrong. Those wishes belonged together whether Percy liked it or not.

The side window still stood ajar. He braced the frame while she climbed through, then followed, scraping his palm on old paint.

Below, the ground floor smelled of straw, leather, and Mr. D's spilled soda. They picked their way around the boards that complained and eased up the narrow stair.

The attic door was locked.

"Give me room," Annabeth whispered.

She worked a hairpin into the lock. Percy watched her shoulders while he listened for footsteps. He remembered this concentration, the small adjustment of her wrist when a solution began to give. The power in her hands had changed. The girl using them had not become a stranger.

A soft click. She caught the latch before it snapped back.

Attic air rolled out: damp wool, candle wax, copper. The mist gathered thickest around the old chair. The mummified Oracle sat where Percy remembered her, with her mouth open and her hands resting on her knees.

He thought of Rachel, the living Oracle they had known years later. Whatever had happened to this history, he had no reason to expect her here now. He remembered how uncomfortable it had been when adults treated her as an answer machine rather than a person, especially when the answer frightened them. He was in danger of doing the same thing to the silent woman in the chair.

Annabeth studied the bronze strip from a distance. The mark was not engraved in the surrounding wood. It had been fixed on top of it, with nails of a different color than the old fittings. Percy had seen enough repair work over the last week to recognize an installation someone had changed. That did not tell him who had changed it or when.

"We ask whether she can speak," he whispered. "Then whether she recognizes the record. If she doesn't respond, we leave. No pushing until she gives us the answer we want."

Annabeth looked at him. "I wasn't going to interrogate her."

"I know. I just want us both to remember."

She took a breath and closed the notebook to the copied prophecy. Percy held the lamp lower so the light no longer fell directly across the Oracle's face. He was conscious that they had entered a restricted room on the assumption that the person within might need help. He didn't want their own fear to become another kind of restraint.

Annabeth stopped several feet from the chair.

"Spirit of Delphi," she said. "We found two different records. We need to know whether either is true."

For a moment only the shingles ticked.

Then green vapor slipped between the Oracle's teeth.

---

The green mist spread across the floor until it reached a strip of bronze nailed beneath the chair. Then it stopped, as though the metal had become a wall. Percy had noticed the strip on earlier visits without understanding why it looked newer than the wood around it.

Now a pattern burned along its surface: three hooked strokes meeting at a circle.

Annabeth opened her notebook.

"The Hall floor," she whispered. "Same mark, under the northern column."

The voice arrived without a breath behind it.

"Speech is bound."

Percy held still. "By whom?"

The vapor pressed toward the bronze and recoiled. A sharp smell of heated pennies filled the attic.

"A hand hidden from the witness."

Not an answer. Not a name.

Annabeth turned the notebook so Percy could see the dated margin they had copied. The four earlier lines stood beside the longer transcript.

"We shouldn't touch it," he said.

"I'm not going to." Her thumb rested over the page. "Can you tell us what this record means?"

She read:

*The oath shall break, the king shall fall,*
*The son of Poseidon shall face the call.*
*One throne shaken, one sister bound,*
*In darkness and fear, the truth is found.*

The vapor rose abruptly. Percy caught her elbow and pulled her back from the chair.

The bronze strip rattled against its nails. From beneath it came a thin black thread of smoke, distinct from the green mist. It reached toward the notebook and broke apart before touching it.

Annabeth shut the book.

"That wasn't you," Percy said to the Oracle.

"The witness is bound. The record is contested."

He looked at the chair, then at the mark on the bronze. "Contested by whoever put that there?"

The voice thinned.

"An answer offered too easily is another door."

The mist withdrew into the old body's open mouth. The attic became an attic again: boxes, dust, a chair neither of them wanted to approach.

Percy listened to his own pulse until he could distinguish a different sound.

Footsteps below.

---

They slid behind a stack of sheet-draped furniture. Annabeth pressed the notebook against her chest, keeping the loose page from rustling.

The door opened.

Chiron stood in the lamp glow with Mr. D beside him. Percy could see their legs beneath the hanging sheet, one pair of shoes and four carefully placed hooves.

"Again," Chiron said.

Mr. D sighed. "I heard it."

"The binding has grown."

"And your campers have grown curious. An unfortunate combination."

Annabeth's hand tightened around Percy's. He did not move.

Chiron approached the chair. The lamp light shifted across the bronze strip.

"Three years of revised records," he said. "Now this. If I lift it without knowing what it connects to—"

"Then we discover why its owner wants you to lift it."

"There will be an eclipse."

"Yes, horse. I can read a calendar."

"The markings match the Hall."

Silence. Percy watched a drop of lamp oil tremble on the rim of the lantern.

Mr. D spoke more quietly. "Empty the building before the shadow reaches it. Keep the children away. We can learn what is underneath once they are safe."

"And the Oracle?"

"Stays untouched until we have help that knows the difference between a knot and a trap."

They left. Chiron locked the door behind them.

Annabeth waited until the steps had faded before letting her breath out.

"We tell him about the mark," she whispered.

Percy nodded. They could show Chiron the copied inscriptions without explaining how they remembered a different childhood. It was a distinction he suspected Chiron would notice, but it remained theirs to make.

---

They went out through the window, down the ivy, and into the trees above the lake. Annabeth landed with both feet on wet grass and immediately checked her copied page.

"They know the mark," Percy said.

"They know more than we do. Not everything."

"Chiron said three years."

She nodded. "The records disagree over three years. That doesn't tell us when our childhoods changed, or whether the same person made all the changes."

Percy had been trying to put one culprit behind every wrong thing they found. It had felt tidier that way. He disliked how quickly a tidy explanation could become a person to blame.

"Who benefits?" he asked.

Annabeth looked up.

"Thalia's alive. Silena's alive. You have a sister. I have sisters. Somebody wanted those things."

"We want those things."

"That doesn't mean we did it."

"Exactly." She tucked the notebook back beneath her jacket. "A better outcome for someone isn't evidence that they caused it."

He scuffed his shoe against a root. "I know."

"Do you?"

He could hear the worry beneath the challenge. He had been watching Thalia as if her protectiveness concealed an answer. She watched him as if he concealed a threat. Between them stood Annabeth, already tired of translating.

"I'll be careful," he said.

"With her, too."

"With her, too."

The lake showed through the trees. They followed the path down until there was enough space to sit without having to look over their shoulders at the Big House.

---

The dock was cold through Percy's jeans. Annabeth sat beside him and leaned against his shoulder.

"What if finding the truth costs us this?" she asked.

He knew which parts she meant. Thalia's bunk beside hers. Clarisse's blunt concern. Silena saving him a place at breakfast. Amphitrite asking when he would come home.

"We don't know that it does."

"I know. I keep arguing with possibilities."

"You're very thorough."

She nudged him with her elbow, and for a moment the joke belonged wholly to them.

He looked down at their hands. Hers was smaller than the one he remembered holding in Tartarus. He knew the little scar beside her thumb anyway.

"I don't want us to become a spell somebody uses," he said. "Old memories, new powers, whatever we're supposed to be. We can say no to a bad idea."

"Even when the bad idea is ours."

"Especially then."

She turned her face toward him. "You still remember all of it?"

"Us?"

She nodded.

"Yes."

"Me too." Her thumb moved once over his knuckles. "I can lose track of which kitchen Thalia means. I can look at a picture and feel nothing. But I don't lose you."

He held her closer without answering immediately. She had said it plainly, and he wanted to let it stay plain.

Eventually she sat up.

"Chiron in the morning," she said.

"With the actual pages."

"And no touching suspicious bronze."

"A rule you might have mentioned before tonight."

"You didn't touch it."

"I was being impressively restrained."

She laughed softly. They walked back toward their separate cabins, stopping where the paths divided. He kissed her forehead, and she made him promise he would sleep before trying to investigate anything else.

---

---

Thalia woke when the Cabin One door settled against its frame.

It wasn't the sound that frightened her. Annabeth had been taught to close a door quietly years ago, at least according to the memories that belonged to Thalia. The problem was that she had learned to distinguish every noise her sister made when trying not to wake her. A shoe placed carefully on the floor. A breath held near the bedside. The scrape of a notebook pushed beneath the mattress.

Thalia lay on her side facing the wall and listened.

Annabeth came to the bed by the window. She stood there longer than necessary. Eventually the mattress gave a small sigh and the sheets rustled. No lamp was lit. The cabin held its ordinary smell of cedar, dust, and rain-soaked training clothes left too long by the door.

Thalia wanted to ask where she'd been. She wanted to point out that she had offered help and been given a promise to speak before another visit. She knew she'd get a careful answer, probably one that didn't qualify as a lie. She could start a fight in the middle of the night and make them both miserable, or she could wait until morning and give her sister the opportunity to come to her.

She didn't know which decision would hurt less.

Annabeth shifted again. The small click of a pencil case being closed told Thalia she was writing something before trying to sleep. That, at least, was familiar. The new part was how quickly the knowledge made her angry.

When Annabeth had been younger, she'd come into Thalia's bed after a nightmare, no matter how determined she had sounded that afternoon about being too old. Thalia had learned to move over without making a fuss. Now her sister was twelve, older in some mysterious ways, and so careful about what she could say that she couldn't even admit she'd been afraid. She seemed to have decided Thalia could carry her worry more easily if she didn't know the details.

Thalia kept her eyes shut until the room quieted.

At dawn she found a folded piece of paper on the floor beside Annabeth's shoes. It was a copy of one of the emergency maps they'd posted near the Hall, with corrections marked in small, familiar handwriting. On the back Annabeth had written a list of questions for the archivist. Thalia picked it up to move it out of the path of her boots and noticed that her sister had crossed out the word *tonight*.

There was no reason to assume what that meant. She assumed anyway.

She left the map on Annabeth's desk and went to the training field before breakfast. Bea was there counting coils of rope. Clarisse was testing the grip on a replacement shield. Both looked up when Thalia arrived, and both knew her well enough not to ask why she'd brought her sword to a rope inspection.

"We need the assembly route checked again," Thalia said.

Bea showed her the list. "I did it at first light. The upper-bank path is still muddy. We're keeping the younger campers on the pavilion route until maintenance signs it off."

"Good."

Clarisse turned the shield over. "You going to inspect every path in camp?"

"Only the ones that have people on them."

"Then you've got your work cut out for you."

Thalia pulled a rope taut between two posts and tested the knot. "I'm fine."

"I didn't ask," Clarisse said.

Thalia tightened the line more than necessary. Clarisse let her do it, then stepped close enough to look at the frayed fibers.

"That's going to snap if somebody leans into it," she said. "Undo it and start again."

"I'm aware."

"Then do it properly."

Thalia stared at her. She had been afraid of monsters before. She had been afraid of Zeus. She knew what it was to count supplies because running short could mean a younger child's life. But there was something humiliating about a frayed rope being the one thing she could solve while everything she wanted to ask Annabeth remained out of reach.

She loosened the knot.

"Annabeth was out last night," she said, quietly enough that Bea wouldn't hear from the far post.

Clarisse kept her eyes on the rope. "Was Percy with her?"

"That's my guess."

"I'll ask him."

"Don't threaten him."

"Wasn't planning to."

Thalia let go of the rope. "I offered to go with her to Chiron. I thought if she let me help with the records, she wouldn't need to do something dangerous. Apparently that wasn't the point."

"Maybe she needed the thing Chiron wouldn't give her," Clarisse said. "That doesn't make it right. It also doesn't mean she doesn't need you."

Thalia hated that she had to work to believe this. She knew Clarisse wasn't offering an excuse. She was naming two facts that could exist at once.

"Can you leave Percy to me?" Clarisse asked. "You've got your own sister to talk to. If we all stand around them like a tribunal, neither is going to say anything useful."

Thalia nodded. She was grateful for the help and resentful that Clarisse seemed more certain what to do. Both feelings disappeared when the rope snapped against her glove and brought her attention back to the work.

By breakfast, the new path markers were ready for installation and Thalia had decided she would ask Annabeth about the night without beginning with an accusation. She wanted the truth, not a confession obtained because Annabeth was too tired to argue.

Annabeth came down the steps with dark circles beneath her eyes and an untouched piece of toast in one hand. She stopped when she saw her sister by the signboard.

"You're up early," she said.

"So are you."

"I couldn't sleep."

"I noticed."

The words landed badly. Annabeth's face closed, almost imperceptibly.

"Did you need something?" she asked.

Thalia glanced at the other campers filing past and pointed toward an empty bench beside the equipment shed.

"Five minutes. No audience."

Annabeth sat, still holding the toast. Thalia didn't sit right away. The impulse to pace was strong, and she resisted it partly because she had spent years telling Annabeth not to treat every difficult conversation like a race she had to win.

"Did you go to the Big House?" she asked.

Annabeth looked at her toast. "Yes."

"With Percy?"

"Yes."

"Did you get what you were looking for?"

"We heard something. I want to talk to Chiron before I tell anyone else what we think it means."

Thalia could have demanded the details. She could have pointed out that this was exactly the half-answer she'd feared. Instead she asked, "Were you hurt?"

"No. We didn't touch the chair or any wards. My hands are fine."

"That's not what I asked." Thalia sat beside her. "I don't just mean the burns."

Annabeth took a breath. "I was frightened. The Oracle answered us, and I don't know how much of what she said to trust."

The admission made Thalia feel suddenly older, not because she was better equipped to hear it but because it was the first time in days her sister had given her something she hadn't already solved in private.

"You don't have to tell me all of it here," Thalia said. "But when you decide to go somewhere dangerous, I need you to be honest that you're going. You can't keep asking me to treat you like an adult and then hide the parts of your decisions you think I'll dislike."

Annabeth's jaw tightened. "You would have stopped us."

"I would have tried."

"Exactly."

Thalia waited. She wanted her sister to hear the words that came next instead of reacting to the first argument.

"And then we would have had to deal with each other," she said. "Maybe I would have been unreasonable. Maybe you would have been. But we wouldn't be standing here pretending it was an accident that I know you were gone."

Annabeth tore the toast in half and stared at the pieces. "I'm sorry I didn't tell you."

"Thank you."

"I'm not saying I wouldn't go again."

"I know you aren't."

The bench was cold beneath them. For a while they watched the younger campers pass the new signs and argue over who was late to sword practice. Annabeth ate one piece of toast. Thalia resisted the urge to comment, then failed.

"You're going to need more than that."

Annabeth gave her a sidelong look. "I had eggs."

"When?"

"Yesterday."

"That doesn't count."

"It was a large serving."

Thalia shook her head and stood. "Come to breakfast. You can explain what happened to Chiron on a full stomach."

She offered her hand. Annabeth looked at it, then took it, careful of the fingers that had recently been burned. Thalia knew the gesture did not resolve everything they'd said. She also knew she would have to earn the chance to ask again, and Annabeth would have to decide whether she was ready to answer.

They went toward the dining pavilion together.

---

Percy was already at the pavilion when Silena handed him the inventory slip he'd forgotten to sign. She did not know what had happened in the attic. He could tell she knew he'd hardly slept.

"You wrote your name in the quantity column," she said, showing him the paper.

"I may have been estimating my value."

"Then you would have written a smaller number."

He corrected the form while she counted sealed packets of bandages for the infirmary. Her practical concentration was one of the things he liked most about living in Cabin Three. It also meant he couldn't get away with standing uselessly beside a table and looking worried.

"There's a rumor Chiron is moving everything from the attic," Silena said.

"He's transferring some archive crates. Annabeth copied the labels."

"Then ask him where they'll be stored. Half the trouble with old camp records is that nobody knows which key opens which cupboard."

Percy set down the pencil. "You make it sound like paperwork can solve everything."

"It solves the things that paperwork caused. If you want a god to solve the rest, I wouldn't trust him to bring the right file."

He laughed, then saw Clarisse coming up the steps. She had dirt on her boots and a look that meant she was going to ask a question he'd rather not answer.

"Did you go with Annabeth last night?" Clarisse asked without sitting.

Percy glanced at Silena, who continued counting packets. She had decided to allow the conversation to happen without pretending she wasn't listening.

"Yes."

"Was it worth it?"

"Probably not in the way you're asking."

"Try answering me in the way I'm asking, then."

Percy told her they'd seen the Oracle react to a bronze strip, that they'd heard Chiron and Mr. D discussing a possible binding, and that both he and Annabeth had come back safely. He did not repeat the words of the prophecy where other campers could overhear. Clarisse listened without making a face, which he found considerably more frightening than if she'd shouted.

"You were there again when the camp was on restricted access," she said.

"Yes."

"You're going to tell Chiron."

"We're taking him our copies after breakfast."

Clarisse leaned on the table. "Good. If he gives you chores, I am not doing your share."

"Fair."

"And if he forbids you to help with the repair site, find something else useful to do. Sitting around inventing theories won't make the Hall safer."

Silena counted the last packet and put the inventory into her bag. "I'll need someone to deliver these after breakfast. The infirmary's short on a size we use for burns, and they asked for another box."

"I can," Percy said.

"Sign here first."

He signed. It didn't make him innocent of anything. It gave him a small obligation that would still exist after he had faced Chiron, and he was beginning to understand how much those ordinary obligations kept a camp together when nobody had an answer to the biggest problem.


Percy and Annabeth reached Chiron's office after breakfast carrying their copied notes in separate folders. Percy had the envelope he'd written before the break-in. He had considered leaving it in Cabin Three because the date on the outside would make it impossible to pretend the night had gone differently. He brought it anyway.

Chiron was seated at his desk, sorting repair orders by the color of the stamps. A stack of older records had been set aside beneath a clean cloth. He looked at their folders before he looked at their faces.

"I assume this is not merely a request for additional archive access."

"No, sir," Percy said.

Annabeth placed her notes on the desk. "We went to the attic last night. We tried to speak to the Oracle."

Chiron set down his pen. "You did what?"

"We didn't touch the chair," Percy said. "Or the bronze strip."

"That is not the beginning of an answer."

Percy showed him the sealed envelope. "We planned where we'd go and what we'd touch, and we wrote it down before we left. I thought you should have the account."

Chiron read the outside, then the note within. His expression did not soften.

"You understood there was a danger to the entire camp," he said. "You had been told the area was restricted. You prepared an account of how you intended to break that restriction and then did it anyway. Do you believe that makes your decision safer?"

"No," Percy answered. He had already tried to justify the plan to himself in five different ways. None seemed likely to survive a minute in front of Chiron.

"Then why bring the note?"

"So we can't claim it happened by accident."

Chiron put it beside the other papers. "At least that is honest."

Annabeth drew the mark they'd seen under the chair, reproducing the three hooked strokes and their position on the bronze. She explained that the mist stopped at the strip and that a black thread of smoke had risen toward the copied prophecy. Percy described the smell and the way the bronze rattled, then repeated only the words the Oracle herself had spoken. Chiron asked each of them where they'd stood and how close they'd come to the chair. He made them mark their positions on a drawing of the attic.

He did not interrupt when they told him what they'd overheard from the landing. That was somehow worse.

"We know you and Mr. D are investigating the mark," Annabeth said. "We didn't mean to listen to a private conversation. We were hiding because we thought we'd get caught."

"You were hiding because you knew you had already done something you had been forbidden to do," Chiron replied. "Do not make that part sound accidental."

"You're right," she said, after a moment.

He studied the copied symbol. "The Oracle has not produced a new prophecy in three weeks. That much is not secret. It is also true that we suspect an interference with the protections around her chair. You may not repeat the details of what you heard to other campers until we have established whether the information would endanger anyone."

"Can we tell Zoe?" Percy asked.

"Yes. I will ask her to examine the relevant maintenance records. She is familiar with the older warding methods and has already been working with the Hall inspection team."

Annabeth looked relieved, though not entirely satisfied. "Do you know whether the older transcript is authentic?"

"I know the handwriting resembles a set of notes taken during an earlier patrol meeting," Chiron said. "I cannot tell you whether that copy represents the original spoken prophecy. The official record was made through several hands. Some were careful. Others were not."

"Did you write the one we found?"

"Possibly. I will check the archival index and compare the actual pages. A memory of writing a note is not a proper substitute for looking at the document."

Percy watched Annabeth restrain a question. Chiron caught it anyway.

"No, Miss Grace. You cannot accompany me into the attic. Nor can you assist in opening a ward until Zoe and I determine where its connections lead."

"What about the eclipse?" she asked. "You told us it might affect the Hall's protections. If the strip is connected to that floor, wouldn't you rather know before the shadow reaches camp?"

"That is exactly why we need an inspection team, not an unauthorized test."

He stood and moved toward the window. From there the Hall's northern colonnade was visible beyond the trees, partly hidden by scaffolding. He looked older than Percy remembered from his first life. Perhaps that was only the effect of a week spent dealing with frightened children and a building no one had fully understood before they started using it.

"There will be consequences for entering the attic," Chiron said. "You will both lose unsupervised library access for a week. Any archive research will be conducted with an assigned counselor. You will continue your safety-mapping work, and you will file a separate, factual report of what you observed last night. That report is not punishment. It is evidence, and I need you to treat it as such."

Percy swallowed an objection. The library restriction would hurt Annabeth much more than it would hurt him, and the unfairness of that made him want to protest. Then he remembered they had agreed not to touch the ward but had still entered a place Chiron believed could be dangerous. Consequences that acknowledged their choice rather than their intentions were reasonable.

"Understood," he said.

Annabeth asked, "Can Nico still bring us public records if he's working with the archivist?"

"Provided the archivist approves their release, yes. You may read them at a supervised table. I am not forbidding you to think."

She lowered her gaze. "Thank you."

Chiron sighed. The sound held less anger than fatigue.

"I have known children who mistook courage for permission," he said. "A surprising number of them were very intelligent. Most wanted to protect someone. I am not asking you to stop caring about this. I am asking you to let the people responsible for camp know what danger they are dealing with before you run toward it."

Percy remembered the Hall door and the white light spreading across the floor. He remembered how much he had wanted to help and how quickly Chiron had been forced to compensate for a decision Percy had made without enough information.

"I'll write the report," he said.

"So will I," Annabeth said.

They left Chiron with the notes and two new obligations. Percy had expected the centaur to be angry, and he had been. He had also been precise enough about what needed doing that Percy could see the difference between being punished and being excluded. They weren't the same thing, although neither felt pleasant.

Outside, Annabeth leaned against the porch rail.

"A week," she said.

"At least he didn't ban us from asking questions."

"One week of supervising somebody every time I want to look at a book."

"I can probably ask Clarisse to supervise."

"That would be worse."

Percy considered it. "She'd make you organize the shelf labels."

"She'd correct them."

"Then argue with the librarian about which labels were better."

Annabeth made a reluctant sound that might have been a laugh. She collected herself quickly.

"He asked about the strip. He hadn't seen the mark exactly as we drew it, or he would have asked different questions. Zoe might know where the other copy comes from."

"We can find her."

"Before you do, promise me something."

He waited.

"If you hear somebody say the Oracle is bound, don't turn that into a story where Thalia's father must have done it. We don't know whether the binding has anything to do with the changed history."

"I know."

"I need you to act as if you know."

That was harder. Percy looked at the Hall and imagined the accounts his own father would be asked to give when the Olympians became involved. Poseidon had changed the shape of Percy's life simply by being more present in it, yet Percy knew the god could not understand what he'd taken from him in the process. He did not want that contradiction to become an accusation against every person who cared for him here.

"I will," he said.

Annabeth took the copy of their notes from his hand, folded the page to hide the private margin, and pointed toward the library. "Then go ask Zoe what she knows. I'll finish the report and bring the documents Chiron requested."

He watched her head back into the Big House. Her step was quick, and she held her injured hand close to her jacket to keep it from catching on the notebook. Percy knew she hadn't forgiven the week-long restriction. She was going to comply with it anyway. For the moment, that was as much discipline as either of them could honestly claim.


Morning found Zoe Nightshade in the library, surrounded by books and a mug gone cold. Percy had slept for three hours, and Chiron had already copied their observations and asked for Zoe's maintenance ledger. He had questioned them closely about the transcript, and Percy still wished he'd had a better answer for why they'd entered the attic after Chiron had forbidden it. Percy still wished they had a better answer prepared.

She looked up when Percy entered.

"The attic?" she asked.

He stopped. "How did you—"

"Chiron asked for everything this library has on seals used near prophetic sites. You look as if you have already met one."

That was an observation. He reminded himself to leave it as one.

"Annabeth's bringing our notes to him," he said. "I wanted to ask you about the mark."

He drew it on a scrap of paper. Zoe moved her mug away before leaning over the drawing.

"Have you seen it?" he asked.

"A damaged version. In a maintenance ledger."

"Did you put it there?"

Her eyes lifted to his. "No."

He felt heat climb his face. "Sorry."

"Keep the question. Lose the certainty." She opened the book nearest her and pushed it across the table. "Sit. I can show you what I know. It is less than either of us would like."
