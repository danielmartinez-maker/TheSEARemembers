## Chapter 180: Everything Out of the Trunks

The cold came in on Monday the way it had been coming in for a week — incrementally, with manners, like a houseguest who keeps asking whether the window should stay open — and by the time Percy Jackson was properly awake it had already moved in. The tin cup on his trunk wore a skin of ice. The pane over the door had fogged on the inside. And the two far bunks held the silence of four people who were awake and none of whom intended to be the first pair of feet on a cold plank floor.

He was, as always, the first pair of feet on a cold plank floor, an honor nobody had asked him to accept.

"Boots," said Clarisse from under her blanket, eyes shut.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to. You do a thing with your jaw before you do a thing with your mouth." The blanket folded itself back by one arm and Clarisse sat up, her hair having won whatever argument had been conducted with it overnight, and she looked at the window, and then at the ice in the cup, and then she said, with the flat satisfaction of a foreman reading a gauge, "Good. That's actual. Yesterday it was only slush."

"Yesterday was Sunday."

"Sundays are slush," Clarisse said, and put her feet on the floor, and did not make a single sound about it, which Percy had privately decided was the most impressive thing about her and which he had told her exactly once and had been told in exchange that if he said it a second time she would put his mattress in the lake.

The stove in Cabin Three was a cast-iron thing with a bad left door, and it held coals the way Clarisse held grudges: banked, patient, and ready to be brought up in under a minute. He had the door cracked and the kindling in before the others were sitting up. Silena came down from the top bunk at the sound of it, barefoot on the cold boards with her cardigan on backwards — on purpose, so the pockets hung front, where a needle could be kept safe — and she looked at the cup, and looked at Percy, and made the face.

"There was ice," Percy said.

"There was ice," she agreed, in a tone that located the responsibility precisely without naming anybody, and went to get the kettle.

The first grey was coming up over the cedar brake. The stove caught and began to push a thin wall of warmth down the length of the floor, and five people in three bunks and two cots took turns at the washstand.

It was Clarisse who made it a day.

She did it the way she did everything, which was with a piece of paper and no discussion. At ten past seven, with the kettle going and Silena's oats in the pot and Percy half-laced in damp boots that had never once dried properly since the storm, Clarisse came out of the back room with her winter list on its clipboard, turned it over to the blank half of the page, and wrote four words at the top in her block hand:

COLD KIT — TODAY.

"Today," she said, to the cabin at large.

"It's Monday," Percy said.

"Monday's the day the dryers are empty. Everybody washed on Sunday and nobody hung anything, so the machines are free and the hut's warm." She wrote a fifth word underneath, which was ALL, and underlined it. "I've read the forecast off the Big House porch for six days. The man on the radio said the word *freeze* twice. So today everything comes out of the trunks, gets washed or mended or traded, and by tonight nobody in this cabin is wearing a summer thing and pretending."

"That's a lot of policy for seven in the morning," Percy said.

"That's a lot of cold for a boy whose socks are still on the hook," Clarisse said, without looking up, and won so completely that Percy closed his mouth and went to get his trunk.

His trunk sat at the foot of his bunk, olive-painted pine with rope handles and a lid re-hinged twice, and he dragged it onto the floorboards by the near end and let the far end bump. The smell of it when he got the lid up was the smell of a season put away: cedar, old canvas, the faint sweet dust of things laundered in August and not thought about since. On top, as always, the blue bag with four rolled swimsuits and a fifth planned, which he moved aside the way you move aside a sleeping animal. Under that the summer things, including a camp shirt with Silena's blue stitches across the shoulder where the seam had given out in the storm. And at the very bottom, in the flat of the lid where nothing could crush it, the last letter Tyson had written: folded in thirds, a thumbprint of forge soot at the corner, four lines of a Cyclops's careful slanted hand about a bolt, a hook for a tote handle, and the prow of a canoe he had promised to come back and fit.

Percy read it again, which he did about once a week, and put it back in the same place, and went on digging.

Winter came out of the trunk in lumps: a heavy grey sweater he could still feel on his shoulders from a life he did not remember, a wool hat folded flat from being kept folded, a second pair of trousers. And at the very bottom, wedged where the hinge met the wall, the piece of him he had been looking for without admitting it — his one good warm layer, a hooded sweatshirt in faded navy, washed on Thursday, hung on the hook, and then lost somewhere in the general weather of Friday and Saturday.

He had not lost it. It was on the floor by Silena's trunk, where it had been living since Saturday, because Silena had been cold on Saturday and had not asked, and he had not noticed it leaving, and this was how the Poseidon cabin worked and had always worked, and there was no court for it.

He picked it up. It was dry, and it was warm from being near the stove, and he pulled it on over his green shirt and felt his spine unclench.

He got four steps toward the door before Clarisse said, "That's mine."

Percy looked down at himself. The sweatshirt was navy. Clarisse's sweatshirts were navy. His own lost layer had, he now recalled, been olive.

"It's not yours," he said, with no conviction.

"It's navy."

"Navy's a color, not a deed."

"Percy." Clarisse held out her hand, palm up, the way a person holds out a hand for a receipt. "I have four sweatshirts. One is in the wash. One has a burn hole in the elbow from a Tuesday I don't want to discuss. One is a size too small because it went into the hut with a hot load in September. That one is the only navy one I own that fits, and you are standing in my cabin wearing my future." She tilted her hand. "Give. Or trade. I'll take a trade."

"What trade."

"The blue shirt. The one over your trunk. You never wear it and it's got good cuffs."

Arguing with Clarisse before breakfast cost more than it paid, so Percy took off the navy sweatshirt, handed it over, and put on his own blue overshirt, which was thinner and which, he discovered, had been living on the porch since Thursday. Clarisse tugged the navy down once and had it buttoned under her jacket before he had finished lacing the second boot.

"That wasn't a trade," he said. "That was a toll."

"That was a toll," Clarisse agreed, pleased. "Tolls and trades are the same thing with different paperwork. You'll learn."

Silena, at the stove, stirring the oats with the long spoon, said, without turning around, "He's the youngest. He learns it last and keeps it longest." Then she looked over her shoulder at the blue shirt and the navy hood and the trunk with its whole summer on the floor, and said, "Right. If the two of you are emptying trunks, I'm emptying mine, and one of you is carrying the load to the hut, because the machines are free until eleven and after that the Athena cabin has them booked and they wash like they're paid by the item."

"I'll carry it," Percy said.

"You'll carry it and you'll bring back the warm ones," Silena said, and already had her own trunk out by the handles. The smell of it was different from his — lavender and something faintly like the pavilion bakery — and out of it came a folded oatmeal cardigan with a button missing at the shoulder and a green scarf that had simply always been in the trunk. She put the cardigan on over her night things, worked the loose button's hole shut with a strand of her own hair in three quick movements Percy had watched since he was small enough to need it demonstrated, and wrapped the scarf once and tucked the ends in.

"You look like a painting," Percy said.

"I look like an October," Silena said. "Come on. Basket's by the door. Everything that's thin, everything that's damp, everything that anybody has been pretending is fine."

The laundry hut stood behind the cabins — stone, plank roof, a door that stuck — and at eight in the morning it was the warmest room on the hill. Two of the machines were Hephaestus-modified and rattled in a way that suggested opinions, and the folding tables ran warm from dryers that had been going since six. Somebody had been in before them: socks on the dowels by the window, a basket of somebody else's shirts under the table, and on a nail by the door a card in Clarisse's block hand reading, in the camp's now-familiar grammar: DRYERS FREE UNTIL 11. HUT IS WARM. SIT DOWN IF YOU'RE COLD.

Percy loaded both machines — thin things in one, heavy things in the other — fed the coins in from the tin on the sill, and sat on the warm bench under the window with three kids from the west cabins who had had the same idea. For twenty minutes he did nothing except exist in a warm stone room while a machine turned, which he was of the opinion the whole camp should be made to do twice a week.

Silena came at nine with the second basket and the two of them sorted on the warm tables, and she did the thing where she could tell, by feel, in the dark, with her eyes closed, whose socks were whose — Percy's because the toe was worn in the same place it always wore, Clarisse's because she hemmed them short on purpose, her own because of a darn at the heel — and she had all five pairs of Cabin Three sorted into five piles before he had found two of his own.

"How do you do that," he said, for the hundredth time.

"You've all got the same feet," Silena said. "You've just worn them differently."

At five past ten he went down with the basket of warm socks, and the cold in the yard hit him like a door, and he walked the fifty yards holding the basket against his chest with both arms, because the heat coming off the wool was the best thing he had felt since Friday. He gave them out in the doorway of Cabin Three: a pair to the far bunk without being asked; a pair to Clarisse, who put them straight on over the ones she was wearing and handed him back the cold pair without a word, which was Clarisse's entire vocabulary for *thank you*; and a pair to Silena, who held them to her face for a second first, and then put them on, and then went back to her cardigan and her needle.

By half past ten the whole camp had caught it. From the Cabin Three porch you could see it happening: trunks out on the porches and the commons, lids up, coats over chair backs, a queue three deep at the hut door, the Hermes cabin running a swap table on their steps where you could trade a summer thing for a winter thing with no questions asked about who had owned what. The pavilion already smelled of woodsmoke and soup at half past ten in the morning, which meant somebody had given up on lunch being a separate event.

Percy stood at the rail in his blue overshirt and watched the hill do it, and thought, without putting it into more words than that, that this was what the last week had been for: not the ridge, not the spring, not the five names on the wall — those were Saturday, and Saturday was finished — but the ordinary business of a place finding out it was going to be cold and deciding to be ready for it together.

Clarisse came out with the clipboard and stood beside him, and for a while neither of them said anything.

"Greenhouse bank's still on the list," she said eventually, reading the page without needing to. "Torn awning. The pine that came down by the forge. Three things that need a warm day and four hands."

"You're not going to get a warm day."

"I know," Clarisse said. "That's why they're still on the list." She made the small mark that meant *looked at, not solved*, and then, because the yard below was full of people in other people's coats and nobody was watching either of them, said, quieter and sideways, "Nice socks."

"They're your socks."

"I know whose socks they are. I counted them out of the basket myself." She went down the steps with the clipboard under her arm, and at the bottom she turned and said, without any change of tone at all, "Blue suits you. Don't get used to it."

Then the bell went for the pavilion, and Cabin Three went up the hill in a file of five people, all of them in layers that had not belonged to them an hour earlier, and the cold came off the north ridge and found the gap at Percy's collar exactly where the navy hood should have been. Behind him Silena said, "Ask her for it back at lunch."

"Ask who for what back?"

"Annabeth has been wearing your olive sweatshirt since Sunday, little brother, when I carried it up the hill because she was cold. You have been far too polite about it for three days."

Percy, who had indeed been far too polite about it for three days, said nothing at all — which was Clarisse's vocabulary for *thank you*, and which Silena took for exactly what it was, and laughed at him the rest of the way to the door.

Annabeth Grace went up to look at the wall at half past eleven, which was the hour she had told herself she would go, and she went alone, and she told herself the alone was efficiency and did not examine the sentence any further than that.

Her kit had assembled itself overnight, the way a good kit does: the field coat re-waxed at the cuffs, the grey sweater with the mended elbow, her own boots laced on the correct side. Her sister's wool hat, which had come into her possession on Sunday and had not been discussed since. And over all of it, at the last minute, because the porch had been cold and the thing had been on the hook by the door, a faded olive hooded sweatshirt two sizes too big in the shoulders, warm the way a thing is warm when somebody who runs hot has worn it.

She had worn it since Sunday without asking who it belonged to. She had known within about four seconds of putting it on, and had thought about it exactly once, late, in the dark, and decided that knowing and asking were two different errands and that she was only going out on one of them.

The wall was on the west side of the pavilion, in the passage between the square and the cooking fires — the busiest hundred yards of camp between ten and the bell. It was plain grey board that had carried notices for sixty years, and on it, in a space swept and leveled on Saturday afternoon, the survey hung under four brass brackets: three sheets of brown paper in a row, a title sheet above them in Annabeth's smallest hand, and at the bottom of the title sheet, five names.

She stopped in front of it the way she stopped in front of everything, which was at a distance, with her hands in the sweatshirt pockets, checking the edges first.

The brackets were square and the sheets were level to within a quarter degree — hung with a plumb line from her own belt loop in the dark on Saturday, while Clarisse held the lamp and made two comments about the plumb line's parentage. She looked first at the dotted line, which came out at the wet corner of the north path exactly where she had said it came out, and which she had inked twice so it would survive being argued with.

Behind her the camp went past in its new October gear: kids in other kids' coats, a Hephaestus boy dragging a trunk lid across the frost as a sledge, two of the little ones from the west cabin sharing one scarf by walking shoulder to shoulder and refusing to let go of either end. Nobody stopped at the wall.

*Half the camp is going to walk past that wall on Monday and not look at any of it,* she had said, on Saturday, in the dark, on somebody else's porch steps, and she had been right, and she had not minded being right as much as she would have minded it a month ago.

Three minutes into her inspection Marcus came by with the leveling rod over his shoulder and a length of ironmongery nobody had asked him about, slowed, looked, and said without breaking stride, "Your dotted line's a hair low."

"It's not."

"It's a hair low at this end." He put the rod flat against the plank and sighted along it. "That much. You drew it at the angle it leaves the ground instead of the angle you'd draw it on a proper contour sheet. If Olive sees it she'll say so, and she'll be right in a way that's annoying."

Annabeth looked at the line for eleven seconds. Then she took a pencil out of her pocket, ruled a correction in the margin with the words LOWER AT OUTFALL — 1:100 under it, and said, "She'll be right in a way that's annoying."

"She will," Marcus said, with enormous satisfaction, and went off toward the forge. The camp went on walking past the wall, and Annabeth stood with her pencil in her hand and thought that a survey was a thing you could fix forever, and that this was the best property it had.

"Nice hat."

Her sister came up the passage without a sound — eighteen years of practice and a heel that made her land on the flat of her foot on purpose. Thalia Grace was in her survey coat with the collar up, her bow case over one shoulder, two mugs of something hot in her hands, and she held one out without stopping. It was cider from the pavilion kettle, hot enough to hurt for the first half second, and Annabeth wrapped both hands around it and looked at her over the rim.

"Nice cider," she said. "Did you bring two on purpose or were you planning to drink both?"

"I brought two on purpose because I'm not an animal." Thalia leaned her shoulder against the plank and looked at the sheets for a while. "It's been standing here since breakfast," she said eventually. "Not you. The wall. Nobody's looked at it and it's handled it with a lot of dignity."

"It's handled it the way I expected it to."

"Which is the part where you're quietly right and the rest of us are eating porridge." Thalia turned her mug a half circle in her hands. "The brackets are good."

"The brackets are the best part."

"The brackets are the best part," Thalia agreed, "and you did them in brass, and you levelled them, and there is not one person in this camp who will ever say the word *bracket* to you, and you did it anyway, which is the single most Grace thing anybody on this hill has ever done." She drank. "Don't tell anyone I said that. I have a reputation for only caring about things that shoot."

"You care about the wall."

"I care about the crew," Thalia said, flat and quick and without looking at her, and then she looked at the title sheet, and read the five names off it, slowly, the way you read a list you intend to remember, and did not say anything else about it.

The sun came round the corner of the roof and put a warm stripe across the bottom sheet, and Annabeth thought, not for the first time, that her sister had a genius for arriving at the exact minute a thing needed sharing and an equally developed genius for pretending she had arrived by accident.

"You're wearing my hat," Thalia said.

"You left it on the table."

"I left it on the table in a way that meant *I'm coming back for it in ten minutes*. That's my fold. I've had it since I was sixteen." She took the hat off Annabeth's head, put it on her own, and in the same motion took Annabeth's gloves out of her coat pocket and put them on her own hands. "Trade."

"That's not a trade. That's two confiscations in one direction."

"It's a trade and you'll thank me in an hour, because I've had these gloves by the stove and you've had that hat outside." Thalia wiggled the fingers of the left glove, which did not fit her at all, and went on wearing them anyway. "How's the elbow today?"

"Mine?"

"Your boyfriend's future. The one I'm going to beat him with." Thalia rolled her shoulder, and the roll came out smoother than it had on Friday, and neither of them commented, because they had both been on that brown field on Sunday morning in a cold wind, and the not-saying about it had become a shape they both respected.

"I'll ask," Annabeth said. "He says his shoulders are fine and his nose is a separate condition."

"He's got a cold."

"He's got a nose."

"Annabeth." Thalia turned her whole body to face her, and the two mugs went to one hand, and her face did the thing it did when it was about to be sincere and intended to be quick about it. "The hoodie's his."

"The hoodie is his."

"It's been his since before he was fourteen. I watched him buy it in a shop in Montauk in a life I don't talk about, and it has been on every hook in this camp, and now it's on you, with the shoulders folded under so it looks deliberate." She picked the cider back up. "I'm not telling you to give it back. I'm telling you that you've been caught, by me, on a Monday, and that you should have folded the shoulders the other way."

Annabeth felt her ears go hot inside the hat that was no longer on her head, and she was not going to give her sister the satisfaction of touching them. "It's warm."

"It's his. Those are different words."

"He runs hot."

"He does," Thalia said. "That's how you know it's his." And then, because she was eighteen and could not let a moment rest near its own weight, she said, "Also you've got a line of sap on the left cuff that's been there since the beach, and he hasn't mentioned it, which means he's known three days and has decided it's a feature," and Annabeth looked down and found the amber sap exactly where her sister had said, in the fold where the sleeve turned when she put her hands in her pockets, which she had done every time she wore the thing — six times in two days — and she stopped looking at it.

They finished the cider standing up, and Thalia collected both mugs off the passage sill on her way out, which counted as a minor miracle and which Annabeth noted and let pass.

"You going to stand here all day?" Thalia said, shouldering the bow case.

"I'm going to stand here until somebody reads the flow line out loud, and then I'm going to go and stand somewhere else."

"That could take a week."

"Then it takes a week." Annabeth pulled the too-big sleeves of the olive hood down over her hands, which was not deliberate and which she then left where it had landed. "Go and shoot straight."

"I shoot straight," Thalia said, from the corner of the wall, "and now I shoot straight with an elbow that cost me four years, and if you tell anybody here that I enjoy that, I'll tell them about the sap." She went off up the passage with the mugs under one arm and her bow over her shoulder, and at the top of the steps she turned and walked backwards three steps, in the cold, in the wrong boots, without looking down. "It suits you, actually. The olive. He'll be insufferable about it."

Then she was gone around the corner, and Annabeth stood in front of the wall with her hands in his sleeves and read her own title sheet for the sixth time, and on the seventh time did not read it at all, looking instead at the sap on the cuff and the thumb-hole worn into the pocket by somebody else's hand, and thinking that a person can lend you a thing for three days and never once ask for it back, and that this too is a kind of survey: standing at the low corner and watching where the water goes.

Thalia Grace did not enjoy the elbow.

She wanted that written down somewhere, in a place where a future person might look, because the story that was going to get told — and she could already hear it being told badly by Clarisse at Friday dinner, in pieces, with gestures — was going to be a story about a girl who had discovered a small improvement and had been delighted by it, and the truth was smaller and less attractive than that. The truth was that the elbow had been wrong for four years and she had not known, and knowing had not been a gift. Knowing was a bill. Knowing said: *here is four years of arrows you'll never get back, and here is the shape of your own shoulder, and you were told about it by a man who then sat down on the grass as though he had all the time in the world.*

So she did not enjoy it. She used it. That was a different thing and she was going to keep the two carefully apart.

The training flat lay behind the armory, a rectangle of packed earth that the Hephaestus kids had put a proper drainage fall under in August and that was, at two in the afternoon on Monday, exactly as cold as a rectangle of packed earth has a right to be in the second week of October. Sixteen campers were on it in three ranks, in the new weather, and the whole flat had the look of a group of people who had discovered at once that winter training was not summer training wearing a coat.

Clarisse had the drill.

She had it by right and by clipboard, and she ran it the way she ran everything, in short blocks with hard edges: four minutes on the line, then the line swapped back, then four minutes off with the hands moving. That was the rule and it was new, and she had put it on the armory door in her block hand that morning, next to the older rules, and it read: COLD HANDS DO NOT HOLD THINGS. MOVE, SWAP, MOVE. IT ISN'T RUDE TO ROTATE, IT IS STUPID TO DROP A SWORD.

Thalia stood at the near end of the flat with her bow case at her feet and watched the first rotation and said nothing for two full minutes, which was as long as she ever lasted.

"Your third's got the same problem the pole had in September," she said.

Clarisse did not turn around. "Which pole."

"The one at the seep that you put in on a lean because the ground was wet and you didn't want to argue with it."

"The ground was wrong," Clarisse said, "and I noted it, and I don't want to discuss it." But she looked at the third camper in the second rank — a boy of about eleven who was holding a sword the way a person holds a wet towel — and after a second she said, flatly and without any change of volume at all, "Second rank. Heel line. You're all standing like you're on a boat. The boat sank. Find the ground." And the line found the ground, and stayed found, because that was what happened when Clarisse La Rue told a room where the ground was.

They worked for an hour and a half in a cold that came up off the earth through the soles of boots and lived in the metal. The hilts were the worst of it. Every thirty minutes somebody's hand would go and a sword would go with it, and Thalia had a rule too, hers old and unposted and never once broken, which was that she pulled that person off the line before they had finished being embarrassed, and put a warm weapon in their hands, and said one sentence about grip, and sent them to the back, and never mentioned it again. She did it six times in ninety minutes. Two of the six were twelve-year-olds and one was a fifteen-year-old from the Apollo cabin who had come out to the flat in a shirt with no sleeves on it on purpose, on a dare, from himself, and who was given Clarisse's actual jacket thrown at his head from eight feet away without a word and who put it on without a word and did not take it off until four.

Thalia's own boots were the wrong boots and she had known they were the wrong boots when she put them on. The left one had a hard sole, which was good for standing, and the right one had a soft sole, which was good for the thing her heel needed, and she had gone out of the Cabin One door at five to two wearing one of each in the dark, on purpose, and had caught herself in the daylight halfway down the path and had gone back, and had come out wearing the two hard ones, because two hard boots look like a decision and one of each looks like a secret.

Nobody said anything about it. Nobody had ever said anything about it. She had run this hill in bad boots in front of two hundred people for three years and the only person who had ever looked at her feet for longer than a second was the man who had said *sit down* on a brown field on a Friday in October, and he had looked at her feet in order to tell her the truth about her shoulders, and she was never going to be able to sort out how she felt about that, so she kept it in a drawer with everything else.

At ten to four, with the light going amber over the cedar brake and the last rotation finishing, the flat emptied the way cold flats empty: fast, in clumps, everybody talking at once about nothing, gloves being beaten against thighs. Thalia picked up her bow case. The archery field was beyond the armory, empty, its butts standing in the low sun like a row of patient animals, and she looked at it for about four seconds, and then she looked at the ground instead, and did it deliberately, the way you close a drawer.

"Nobody's there," said a voice beside her.

Percy had come off the flat with his hands in his armpits and his hair doing what his hair did when he had been wearing a hood, and he was grinning in the particular way he grinned when he had noticed a thing and was deciding whether to be kind about it.

"There's nothing to be there," Thalia said. "I was looking at the ground."

"You were looking at the butts."

"I was looking at the ground *in front of* the butts. It's my ground. I'm allowed." She started up the path toward the Big House, and he fell in beside her, because he was fourteen and constitutionally incapable of walking past a person who had just been caught.

"Friday's the next one," he said.

"Friday's a family dinner."

"Friday's a family dinner at six and archery at three, and Chiron put it on the board, and you've already looked at the board twice today, because I watched you look at the board twice today while I was eating soup."

"You watch me look at boards?"

"I watch everybody look at everything," Percy said, with the serene honesty of a boy who had decided long ago that this was a personality rather than a problem. "It's a hobby. I'm going to be great at it when I'm old. And I just wanted to say that the elbow's better and that you're allowed to have noticed that without it meaning anything, because Annabeth told me—"

"Annabeth told you what."

"Annabeth told me nothing," Percy said, with a speed that was almost impressive. "Annabeth has told me nothing about anything, ever, about any subject, and I have asked her to keep it that way out of self-preservation. I'm saying it as a friend. The elbow's better. You're allowed."

Thalia walked up the path beside him with the bow case on her shoulder and the borrowed gloves on her hands and thought about all the ways she could answer that, and discarded six of them, and finally said, "If you tell anybody on this hill that I *enjoyed* something, I'll tell them you cried at the county fair."

"I was nine."

"You were nine and you cried and I was there and I have kept it for you for five years like a jar of jam, and that is the most generous thing I have ever done for anyone." She put her hand on his head as she passed him, which she could do because she was four years older and eighteen, and left it there for exactly as long as it took to be affectionate and not a second more. "Soup, then. And if you sit at my left I'll let you keep the seat, and don't read anything into that either."

The Big House kitchen at four in the afternoon was the warmest room in the camp that did not have a forge in it, and the soup was Clarisse's, which meant it was thick, and the bread was Silena's, which meant there was more of it than a person could reasonably eat and a note wrapped in paper on top of the basket telling you to eat it anyway. Thalia took a mug in each hand again, the way she had that morning, out of a habit she had started that morning and was already not examining, and stood in the steam of the kitchen with her back to the stove while the camp went quiet and cold outside and the lamps started to come on down the west path, one at a time, the way they had every evening since a boy from the Hephaestus cabin had decided that a person walking up a hill ought to see a light and think somebody was home.

Three were lit. It was four in the afternoon and three were lit, which meant somebody was already cold, and Thalia looked at the window, and at the line of amber going up the darkening hill, and said nothing at all, because it was somebody else's job to say things and hers to hold both mugs.

Silena Beauregard mended on the porch because the lamp on the porch was the tenth one and because a person doing careful work in October wants a lamp that is already lit and a door to go through when the fingers go.

The mending was in a canvas tote at her feet, and it had been in that tote since a Friday, and she had begun it on Sunday, and it was, she had decided on Sunday and re-decided twice since, the most difficult mending she had ever been given, and none of the difficulty was in the stitches.

It was Charlie Beckendorf's, all of it, and it was the kind of mending that tells you a whole life in nine pieces. A work shirt with the shoulder seam pulled out on the left side where a bar goes over, and the tear clean, which meant it had been pulled once, hard, and not worried at afterwards. Two socks, darned at the ball of the foot, done by somebody else, done badly, done in a white thread he did not own. A canvas sleeve with a burn along the forearm, the edge melted rather than scorched, which was the mark of hot metal that had been flicked and not fallen. And the glove, the storm glove, the one whose thumb she had already put back in on a Wednesday in a room full of eleven children and two lamps, and whose palm she had patched across the wear line with a square of the same grey flannel as the quilt squares, because the flannel was the right weight and because, at the time, choosing it had felt like nothing.

She had the shirt across her knees at eight o'clock. The porch rail held the lamp. Inside the cabin the stove had gone to a low tick and Clarisse was at the table with the winter list and a mug, and the two in the far bunks had gone up early because of the drill, and the cabin had the sound it made when five people were in it and four of them were being quiet.

The needle she was using was a long one, borrowed out of the workshop tin, and she was using it because the shirt was heavy and the seam was a felled seam and a fine needle would have fought it. Her thread was grey. Her stitches were small, slanted, and even, and she ran them a finger's width apart the whole way down the shoulder, and she did not once have to think about the stitches, which meant she had a great deal of capacity left over for everything else, and she spent all of it.

She spent it on the cuff. The left cuff of this shirt had been turned and re-turned by a person who worked with his arms out in front of him all day, and the fold was set into the cloth the way a fold is set into a paper that has been in a pocket for a year, and she opened it out to stitch the lining and found the inside of the cuff worn thin and slightly shiny, the way good cotton goes when it has absorbed a few hundred days of somebody's own hands. She looked at it for longer than the cuff needed. Then she turned it back, folded it exactly along the old line, and stitched it down as it had been, because a cuff that has been turned that many times is telling you where it wants to live, and it was not her place, on a porch, in the cold, to argue with it.

From inside, Clarisse's voice: "Whose is it."

"Beckendorf's."

"Mm." A page turned. Then, without any change of tone at all: "You're doing the cuffs."

"I'm doing the cuffs."

"Right," Clarisse said, and did not say another word about it, which was, from Clarisse, an entire conversation's worth of leaving somebody alone, and Silena put the second stitch into the second cuff and was glad, in the way a person is glad in a room where nothing has to be explained.

Percy came out at twenty past eight in his blue overshirt with his collar up and his hands in his pockets and sat down on the porch step without asking, which he had stopped asking permission for somewhere around the age of eleven, and he looked at the shirt, and at the thread, and at the tote with its nine pieces of somebody else's life in it, and he said, "That's a lot of shirt."

"It's a work shirt."

"It's a shirt that's been worked." He put his chin on the rail, upside down, looking at her from an angle. "Sis."

"No."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to ask who it's for and then you were going to ask why it's taken three weeks and then you were going to offer to walk it down to him yourself, and I have four stitches left on this seam and I would like to have them." She did not look up. "Go and put more wood on."

"It's got plenty of wood."

"It's got exactly enough wood for a person who isn't sitting on my step." Then she relented, because he was fourteen and it was cold and he had come out to sit near her and not to talk, which was the whole reason he had come out, and she said, "If you want to be useful, there's half a loaf in the basket and two mugs in the rack, and there's a girl on this hill who has been wearing your olive hoodie for two days and has not been asked for it back once."

Percy went and got the loaf, and took it up the hill, and Silena heard his boots go off along the path and the door of the cabin open and shut, and she smiled at the shirt for about four seconds and then put the smile away and finished the seam.

At nine she went down to the water to stand up and get the cold out of her knees, because that was the sensible thing to do, and because the forge was still lit, which she had known since seven without admitting she knew it, and which she could see from the third step down at the top of the beach: the long low window, the orange of it, the shadow of a large person moving about inside it doing the end-of-day business that a forge has at the end of a day, banking, oiling, hanging. He was in there alone. He was often in there alone at nine. He had been in there alone at nine on Wednesday, and on the Friday before, and she knew this the way she knew the shape of the camp in the dark, not because she had gone looking but because she had once looked up from a needle and found that the information was already in her head, and had then sat very still about it.

She stood on the steps in the cold with her hands in her cardigan pockets and did not go down the path.

It was not, she had decided on Sunday, that she had nothing to say. She had a great deal to say. She had a running list of it in her head, some of it so specific that it embarrassed her — the weight of the thread he used for canvas repairs, which was the correct weight, which she had checked against three other spools in the workshop because she had needed an excuse to be holding the spools; the way he had said *I'd build it* about a drawing of a bakery that a girl had made in her own hand and then folded and put in a box, and had said it flatly, the way he said everything, as though he were reading a measurement off a thing. She had plenty to say. She had, in fact, a small and terrible surplus of things to say, and the problem was that every single one of them, when she turned it over and held it up to the lamp, had the same defect.

It would not fit in a tote.

Words that come back with the mending are words that a person has to carry for a year. She had watched that happen to other people, to girls in this camp and boys in this camp, and she had been the person they told about it afterwards, in the meal line, holding two bowls, being extremely kind and extremely useless. She was not going to do it. If there was going to be anything, she was going to put it into an object where it could be folded and handed over and not have to be answered on the spot, and until she had found the object she was going to say nothing whatsoever and keep both hands on the needle.

She went back up at ten past nine. The forge window had gone orange-black, banked, and a shadow crossed it once with a lantern, and then the shadow went the other way and did not come back, and Silena went in, and took the tenth lamp off its hook, and stood in the doorway with it while she decided whether she wanted the cold on her back or in front of her, and then she went in and shut the door on all of it.

At the table she took the shirt out of the tote again, and the glove, and the two darned socks, and the canvas sleeve, and she laid them out in a row on the boards, wear-side up, the way you lay out tools at the end of a job so that you can see in one look what is finished and what is owed.

There was a scrap of the good grey flannel left over from the quilt squares, about the size of a man's palm, and she looked at it for a while, and then she put it back in the mending basket, and said, out loud, to a cabin in which three people were asleep and one was pretending, "Not yet."

Clarisse, from the dark at the far end, said, "No. Not yet," and turned a page, and the stove ticked, and the lamp on the hook by the door went on burning its tenth light for the bottom of the hill.

Percy carried a loaf up the hill at half past eight and felt, for the fourth time that day, that he had been handed a life he had not earned and was going to spend carefully anyway.

The hill was properly dark by then. The stars had come out in the enormous unbothered way they came out over this valley, and the cold had stopped being weather and started being a fact, and the west path was doing what the west path did after supper, which was to fill up with people going nowhere in particular in a great deal of clothing. He passed two of the Hermes kids in a pair of boots between them, which he chose not to audit. He passed Marcus and a girl from the Athena cabin looking at a piece of paper by lantern light and arguing about scale in low voices, the way other people sang. He passed the third lamp, freshly lit, the oil still smoking in the wick, and the smell of it — hot metal and lamp oil — went after him up the path and stayed in his coat.

Annabeth was on the Cabin One porch with the field book on her knees and her boots up on the rail in the specific way that meant she had been there a while and intended to be there longer, and she had on the olive hoodie, and the hood was up, and the hood was up not for the cold because the hood of that hoodie had never once stayed up in its own history and was up now, which meant she had put it up on purpose, which meant she had known he was coming.

"I brought a loaf," he said.

"You brought a loaf because Silena sent you."

"Silena sent me because you haven't eaten."

"Silena doesn't know whether I've eaten."

"Silena has known whether everyone in this camp has eaten since before you could reach the table," Percy said, "and she has a system, and the system is: she notices, and then she becomes the weather." He sat down on the step below her, which was the arrangement, and put the loaf between them, and the two mugs on the boards, and for a while they did the thing that was, by some distance, the most useful thing either of them did all week, which was to eat bread in the cold and not talk about it.

Inside the cabin, through the window, Thalia went past twice with a mug and a bowstring and once with a look that lasted a quarter of a second and meant, in the Grace family's whole grammar, *I see you and I am choosing to be generous about it*.

"The elbow's better," Percy said.

"I know."

"He told me the elbow's better."

"She told you the elbow's better. I was there. I handed her the cider." Annabeth tore the loaf in half and gave him the bigger piece without any appearance of having made a decision. "Also I've been wearing your hoodie for two days and you have not once asked for it back, and I want you to know that I noticed that on Sunday at eleven in the morning and that I have been thinking about it for two days in a row, in detail, at night, which is not a thing I do about clothing."

Percy looked at the rail, and at the boots on the rail, and at the line of amber sap on the left cuff, and then at her, and said, "I know about the sap."

"I know you know about the sap. Thalia told me you know about the sap. She told me you've known for three days and have decided it's a feature."

"It is a feature."

"It's sap."

"It's *your* sap now. That's how the law works. Possession of sap." He drank. "You can have it. I'm not asking for the hoodie back."

"I know you're not. That's the part I've been thinking about at night."

The wind came down off the north ridge and went along the porch and took the steam off both mugs at the same time, and Annabeth put her boots down off the rail, and said, without looking at him, "Do you want to sit somewhere that isn't the place we sat last night?"

And because he understood exactly what she was asking, which was not about geography at all, Percy said, "Boathouse porch. It faces away from the wind. I'll bring the blanket."

He got the blanket off the fire-ring stack on the way down — the heavy wool one, brown, with the smell of woodsmoke permanently in it — and they put it over both their shoulders on the bench at the end of the boathouse porch, which was a bench that had been built for two people and which had never once been used by more, and which faced the water, and the water was flat and black and moving very slowly, and the far shore was a line of dark pines against a dark sky with a rim of stars on top of it.

They sat with the loaf between them and the blanket over both of them and their shoulders touching from the shoulder to the elbow, and for a minute neither of them said anything, and the minute was not empty, and neither of them filled it, which was, Percy thought, the single most valuable thing that had happened to him in the whole fourteen years of his life, including the years he could not remember.

Then Annabeth said, "Trade," and put a folded square of paper into his hand.

It was folded the way she folded things — twice, along the same creases, flat — and he had, in the back cover of a field book and in the breast pocket of a mended shirt and in the inside of his trunk lid where nothing could crush it, eleven of these now, and he knew the weight of each of them the way Silena knew whose socks were whose.

His said: *YOU HAVE KNOWN ABOUT THE SAP SINCE SATURDAY. YOU SAID NOTHING. I AM NOT WASHING IT. — A*

And under that, smaller, in the hand she used only when she was being honest on paper and not being honest out loud: *Nobody has ever let me keep a thing of theirs this long without asking what I thought of it. I don't know what to do with it except keep it. Consider this a survey note. It has my initials.*

Percy read it twice, and then a third time with his thumb on the initials, and then he folded it along her creases and put it in his shirt pocket and put his hand flat over it, which was the whole of his answer and which she read as easily as she read everything.

"Yours," he said, and put his into her hand.

Hers said: *I WENT BACK AND CHECKED THE DOTTED LINE AGAIN AT FOUR. IT'S LOW AT THE OUTFALL. I'VE RULED THE CORRECTION IN THE MARGIN. MARCUS CAUGHT IT AND I LET HIM. THIS IS ME TELLING YOU SOMETHING YOU'D HAVE CAUGHT BY FRIDAY. — P*

And under that, smaller: *I have been cold since Thursday because you have my sweatshirt and I have not once been cold at you about it. That's also a survey note. My initials are the whole thing.*

Annabeth laughed at that — the real one, the one that came from below the ribs and went out across the water and came back changed — and then she read the small print twice, and then she put the note inside the field book and into the back cover where eleven of his already lived, and she slid the back cover shut with her thumb, and she said, "It has your initials."

"It's got the whole thing."

"It's got two paragraphs and a lot of feeling in a handwriting that leans uphill." She turned to him under the blanket. "Percy."

"Annabeth."

"You could have asked for it back on Saturday."

"I could have."

"You didn't ask because you wanted me to keep it."

"I wanted you to keep it," he said, "and I wanted to find out whether you'd tell me you'd got it, and you didn't tell me, and so I got to spend two days knowing that you had my sweatshirt and were not going to mention it, which was better than asking, because asking ends a thing and this is still going."

She looked at him for a while with her shoulder against his under the wool, and the cold was in the air between them and the warmth was in the blanket and the bench was hard and neither of them was going to say a word about the bench.

"Come here," she said, and he did.

The kiss took its time about starting, the way it always did with them now, which was: her hand first, on the front of his coat, the way she had done on somebody else's porch steps two nights ago, and then the tilt of her head, and then the whole slow business of the cold coming off both their faces and meeting in the middle and giving up. It went on across a stretch of quiet that neither of them counted. His hand found the side of her hood and stayed there, thumb in the seam at the edge of the wool, not moving, holding the hood in place against the wind that came off the water in slow sheets, and her fingers went from his coat to his collar and stayed there too, and for a while the entire content of the evening was the small warm space between their two faces and the fact that neither of them had to do anything at all with it.

They broke for air with their foreheads together, and Percy said, into the wool at her temple, "Your ears are doing the thing."

"My ears are fine."

"Your ears are the color of Clarisse's good pen." He kissed the side of her face, once, lightly, at the edge of the hairline where the cold had got in. "They've been like that since the porch. Since before the porch. Since the wall, probably."

"Since about eleven forty this morning," Annabeth said, with her eyes shut, in the voice of a person filing an admission. "Thalia took my hat and I didn't get it back and I stopped caring about that about four seconds later, and I've been sitting on that information all day like a hen."

Percy laughed, which moved both of them, and she put her hand flat on his chest to take the laugh, and left it there, and the hand was cold through the blue shirt and then not cold at all.

Then they did the thing that took the rest of the evening and that Percy was, afterwards, going to remember in pieces rather than in order: her head came down onto his shoulder and stayed, and the blanket came up over both of them to the chin, and his arm went along the back of the bench and then, after a while, around her, and she took his left hand out of the sleeve of his shirt and put it on his own knee and then took it over and put it in hers and looked at it there, in the lamp spill from the boathouse window, the way a person looks at a page she is going to draw from memory.

"You've got a new callus," she said.

"I've had that callus."

"You got it in September. It moved. Calluses move. I've been watching it move." She turned his hand over and put her thumb in the centre of the palm and pressed, once, gently, in the exact place where the rope had sat all Saturday, and Percy's entire spine did something that he would deny to anyone alive. "There. That's the rope line. That's Saturday. That's going to be there in a week."

"It's a hand."

"It's your hand and it's got Saturday in it and I've been looking at your hands for two years." She said it the way she said everything on paper — flat, total, without any decoration at all — and then she laced her fingers into his and closed them and put the joined pair down on his knee again, and said, in a completely different voice, "Trace something."

So he did, with his free hand, on the back of her glove, which she had taken off at some point without either of them noticing: a shape, in the cold air, over the knuckles. She watched it go. Then she turned her hand over and let him do it again on the palm, slower, and she said, "That's the ridge."

"It's the ridge."

"That's the whole ridge line, in four strokes, in the dark, with your hand going numb." She closed her fingers on the last of it. "Don't stop being able to do that."

"I'll put it on the list under *noted*," Percy said, and she made the sound that was a laugh with no air in it, and then she was quiet for long enough that he thought she had gone, and then she hadn't gone at all, because her breathing had done the slow thing and her weight had come into his shoulder in the particular way that meant she was asleep in about ninety seconds and intended to be.

He did not move. That was the entire skill of it, and he had got good at it: he sat, and the wind came off the water, and the far shore stayed a line, and the boathouse window made a square of amber on the boards at their feet, and somewhere out past the point the heron stood on the bow of a half-sunk skiff in the dark and held a boat's place and asked nothing of anybody, and Percy stayed awake for all of it, which he was of the opinion was his job, and did not once think about the cold, which was the only thing about him that had changed in two years and which he had spent two years not mentioning to anyone.

At ten minutes past nine the boathouse door opened and Clarisse came out with a second blanket over her arm and the winter list under it and the expression of a woman inspecting a load she had not personally tied.

She looked at the two of them on the bench. She looked at the first blanket. She looked at Percy's face, which was doing nothing at all, because he had grown up with two older sisters and had learned the only defense there was, which was to be extremely boring to look at.

"There's soup," she said.

"We had soup."

"There's more soup, and the stove's going, and the door's got a bolt on it." She put the second blanket over Annabeth's feet, which were the only part of either of them that the wind had got at, and tucked the ends in with two efficient pulls. "Two o'clock. I'm bolting the door at two. If you're on the porch at two, you're on the porch, and I'm not saying another word about it, and there's a rug." Then she went back inside and shut the door on both of them, and the bolt went across, and through the window he could see her put the list down and pick up her mug and not look at the window once, which took more discipline than anything any of them had done on the north ridge on Saturday.

Annabeth Grace woke on a boathouse bench at ten minutes past nine with her shoulder gone numb and her own handwriting cold in her pocket, and did not move for a full minute, because the minute was the best part.

Percy was awake. He had been awake the entire time, which she knew without asking, because his arm had not once gone heavy and because the blanket had been pulled up over her shoulder twice in the last hour without either of them discussing it. He was looking at the water with his chin on the top of her head, and his breath went out in slow shapes over the boathouse rail and did not come back.

"I fell asleep."

"You fell asleep in four minutes and you counted down to it. You said *ninety seconds* and then you said *sixty* and then you were gone."

"I say numbers in my sleep now?"

"You say numbers when you're being held, which is a new development, and I have decided to treat it as an honour." He squeezed the hand that was still holding his. "Also there's frost on the rail. There wasn't frost on the rail an hour ago."

She looked. There was. The whole rail had gone white along its top edge while she slept, thin and even, the way the first frost of an evening lays itself down on anything horizontal and patient, and beyond the rail the black water had that faint new roughness on it that means the cold has started working on the surface from the edges in.

So they folded the blanket — her end, his end, the way you fold anything that has to live outside — and hung it over the fire-ring stack on the way, and walked up.

They did it the way they did everything now, which was with a great deal of practice and no discussion at all: his hand, then her hand, then both, on the flat stretch behind the canoes where the path was wide enough for two people to walk abreast without negotiating. She had the field book under one arm. He had the two mugs, because a person does not leave mugs on a boathouse porch in October, and because the mugs were going to the Cabin Three porch anyway and he was going that way and she was going up, and they had four minutes.

The camp had gone quiet in the particular way it went quiet on a Monday: not asleep, but done. The forge dark and banked. The pavilion swept, the smell of it — soap and bread crusts and cold stone — lying along the path. The Hermes swap table still out on their steps under a tarp, a shape in the dark with a whole winter under it. Somebody's shutter, somewhere, ticking once every few seconds in the wind off the ridge, in no hurry to be fixed.

"You ruled a correction in the margin," Percy said, at the corner.

"I ruled a correction in the margin."

"Marcus caught it."

"Marcus caught it and I let him catch it and then I wrote his initials next to it, which he will find on Wednesday and be unbearable about for a week."

"You wrote his initials."

"I write everybody's initials on my sheets when they're right. There are five sets of initials on the title sheet and one of them is his, and one is a ten-year-old's, and one belongs to my sister, and—" She stopped. They had got to the bottom of the west path, which was as far as four minutes went, and she stopped there, on purpose, because the arrangement was that four minutes was four minutes and it did not get extended in public, and because the first lamp was ten yards up the hill and the tenth was on the porch behind them and neither of them was going to be the one to say so.

He put the mugs down on the stone at the foot of the path. Then he did the thing he had been doing since they were twelve and which had never once, in two years, stopped working: he waited, with his hands at his sides, in the cold, for her to close the distance.

She closed the distance. It was a short one. It was a good one — her hands in the front of his blue shirt, his forehead down to hers, and then the slow kind, the kind that starts nearly still and then takes its time about the middle, and the frost going on forming on the rails around them and nobody on the hill to see any of it, and his thumbs finding the edge of the hood and staying there the way they had stayed all evening, holding the wool in place against the wind, as though the hood were a small structural problem and he had decided to solve it personally.

"Go up," he said, into the last of it.

"Go up," she agreed, and did not go.

"Annabeth."

"I'm going. I'm going in a second." She put her forehead against his collarbone for exactly as long as it took to say that, and then she picked up the field book, and took the first mug off the stone, and gave him the other one, because a person who is carrying one mug walks slower and she wanted to walk slower, and she started up the path, and at the second lamp she turned around once, which was the budget, and spent the whole of it, and he was still standing at the bottom with a mug in each hand being a lighthouse, and then the path went left through the cedars and he was gone and the tenth lamp was there instead, low and behind her, at the bottom of the hill.

The west path climbed in switchbacks between the cedars, and the lamps were on the outside of every turn, nine of them, amber in grey iron brackets, and she counted them the way you count anything you intend to keep: not out loud, and not quickly.

Third was fresh — the oil still smoking in the wick, the smell of it going after her up the slope. Fifth had a moth in it, going around and around the glass in a slow, unhurried, entirely pointless orbit that she recognised, with a tired sort of fondness, as exactly her own behaviour at the wall that morning. Seventh had gone out at some point in the evening and come back; the glass was sooted at the top and the flame was low and stubborn and had no intention of going out again, and she looked at it for a second the way you look at a person who has had a bad evening and made a decision about it.

The cold had settled properly by then. It had stopped coming and gone. It was in the ground now, in the way the frost took the path in a fine even skin and gave back the print of every boot that had gone up it since supper, and in the way her own breath had stopped being an event and become a fixture, and in the far corner of the valley where the lake lay with its new rough edge and its dark, and up on the shoulder of the north ridge where a plate of granite the size of a cabin door had been holding water underground since June and would go on holding it, and where five people had stood on Saturday and where four of them had been, one way or another, on this Monday: one at a flat with a bow, one at a table with a list, one on a bench with a needle, and one at the bottom of a hill with two mugs.

At the top she let herself in. The Cabin One room was dark and warm and smelled of the stove and of her sister's hair oil and of the two mugs of cider that had, in fact, been collected from the passage sill, which meant Thalia had walked back for them, which meant Thalia had made a point of it, which meant she would be teased about it exactly once and never again.

"Late," Thalia said, from her bunk, into the dark, in the voice of a person who was nearly asleep and was saying it for the record.

"Late," Annabeth said, and put her boots by the bed on the correct side, laced, and slid the field book under the edge of her pillow where it lived, and stood for four seconds with her hand on the cover, feeling the shape of the new one in the back cover — flat, folded twice, along the same creases, not loose — and then she got into the cold sheets in the cold room in the cold camp at the bottom of a cold hill and lay on her side with her face toward the wall and her hand out of the blanket, and thought, in the order she had been refusing to think it all day: the trunks, and the socks out of the basket hot enough to hold with both hands; the wall, and the low line at the outfall, and her sister's two mugs and her sister's two confiscations; the flat, and the boy in the borrowed jacket, and a bow case picked up at four; nine stitches down a felled seam, and a grey square left in a basket on purpose; a loaf torn in half without a decision; a line of amber sap, being left, deliberately, un-washed, on a cuff she was going to keep wearing until somebody made her say why.

Annabeth lay awake for a minute after Thalia's breathing slowed. The field book was under her pillow, with the newly folded note tucked into the back cover. She had promised herself she'd stop taking notes in bed, but a loose corner was pressing into her cheek and she couldn't bear leaving it like that.

She took the book out, slid the note into the flat pocket beneath the cover, and set it on the little table. It was a sensible place for it. She could still reach it in the morning.

From the other side of the room Thalia said, without opening her eyes, "You're moving your book again."

"It was under the pillow."

"Yes. I heard you put it there."

"I don't want to bend the new page."

"Then stop using it as a mattress."

Annabeth smiled in the dark. She moved the book half an inch farther from her water cup, which had no lid and was exactly where a sleepy hand would knock it over.

"Thalia?"

"What?"

"Thank you for walking back for the blankets."

There was a longer pause. "You were going to go back yourself."

"I was. You did it anyway."

"Go to sleep, Annie."

Annabeth did. The repaired seam would still need checking after the next storm, and the flat on the wall would require another measurement when the frost settled in properly. Her sister would probably have a new list at breakfast. None of those things needed doing in a dark room in which they were both finally warm enough to rest.
