## Chapter 107: The Words We Choose

Percy had said *I love you* to Annabeth before: in their old life, and here after the eclipse disturbance struck the Hall and Thalia helped shield her. Both remembered those words. Today he wanted to say them in an ordinary moment too.

The thought followed him all morning.

The first test came in the arena. Clarisse had set up a basic shield drill for him and two younger campers. One stepped in with a padded staff while Percy held a practice sword and tried to remember the angle Clarisse had shown them the day before.

“Ready?” she asked.

He nodded.

The first strike came toward his left shoulder. Percy moved too late. The padded staff tapped his arm.

“Again,” Clarisse said.

The second strike came from the other side. Percy saw it, knew what to do, and still raised his sword an inch too far. The staff touched his ribs.

“You okay?” the younger camper asked.

“Yeah. Keep going.”

Clarisse stepped between them. “You can take a break if you need one.”

“I don't.”

“Then pay attention.”

He nodded, annoyed with himself. Clarisse backed away and the drill resumed. The third strike was the easy one; it came straight down, a line he had blocked hundreds of times. He missed it anyway, catching the staff against the flat of his blade only after it had reached his chest.

Clarisse called the drill off. “What's going on?”

“Nothing.”

“That's not what your sword says.”

The younger camper was already stepping back, trying not to look as if he had heard.

“It was a mistake,” Percy said.

“Three mistakes.”

“I know.”

“Does something hurt?”

“No.”

“Then take a minute and get your head back in the arena.”

Percy set the practice sword on the rack. He didn't want to tell Clarisse that he had spent half the night thinking about an ordinary sentence. It sounded silly beside weapons and drills. He also didn't want to lie to her, not after she'd carried him through worse mornings.

“I'm thinking about Annabeth.”

“That's not unusual.”

“I know.”

Clarisse waited. She didn't ask what had happened between them or whether he was in trouble. Percy appreciated that more than he could say.

“I want to tell her something,” he said. “I keep trying to get the words right.”

“For what?”

“Because they matter.”

“Then don't practice them on the younger campers.”

“I wasn't.”

“You were practicing everything except the drill.”

He looked over. Clarisse's face was blunt, but not mocking. She tapped the rack with two fingers. “You can think about it after you're done. Right now, you have a job. Either do it or tell me you need to sit out.”

There it was: a choice that didn't require him to prove he was fine. Percy picked the sword back up.

“One more round.”

Clarisse nodded to the younger camper, who took his mark. Percy watched his feet. The next strike came at his shoulder. He blocked it properly. The one after came toward his ribs. Percy turned the blade and caught it. When the third came, he moved early and knocked the staff sideways.

The camper lowered his weapon. “You got those.”

“I got those.”

“Good. Now do it again slower,” Clarisse said. “He needs the practice too.”

They repeated the drill at half speed. Percy called the direction before the staff moved. The younger camper learned to change the angle without winding up so far. Clarisse corrected both of them, then made them switch sides. Percy helped the boy adjust his grip so the padded end didn't twist in his palm.

At the end, the camper managed three clean strikes and Percy blocked them without guessing. Clarisse looked at the two of them. “Better.”

“Can we do it again tomorrow?” the boy asked.

“If your counselor says the schedule works,” Clarisse answered. “Ask before you assume the arena's free.”

He nodded and left to find his counselor.

The second camper stepped forward, the padded staff tucked too close against their chest. Clarisse pointed to the mark the first boy had used. “Same drill. Percy, you take the staff this time. Don't go faster just because you know the pattern.”

Percy traded the practice sword for the padded weapon. The camper tried a strike toward his shoulder but pulled back halfway through, uncertain whether to finish. Percy lowered his hands. “You can complete it. I'm ready.”

The next attempt came with more force than the first. Percy caught the staff at an awkward angle, and the padded end slid along the flat of his blade-hand before he could settle the block. It didn't hurt, but it showed him why Clarisse had warned against crowding the weapon.

“Give yourself room to move,” he said. “If you hold it that close, you can't change direction.”

The camper widened their stance. Clarisse checked the feet, then nodded for another attempt. This time the staff stopped where Percy expected it. He turned, let the padded end pass, and tapped the other weapon aside.

“Again,” Clarisse said.

The camper feinted toward Percy's ribs and swung high. Percy reacted late, but he caught the strike with the blade turned correctly. Clarisse let them repeat it slowly, then had them switch back. Nobody was keeping score. The point was to see the line and move without stepping into the other person's reach.

At the end of the second round, the camper could finish a strike without pulling away and Percy was no longer guessing from the first motion. Clarisse took the staff and returned it to the rack.

“That's enough for now,” she said. “You both know what you need to practice. Leave the weapons where they belong and tell your counselors what we worked on.”

The first camper returned before they left. “What if I step backward and he follows?”

“Then you have to watch where you're going,” Clarisse said. “Don't let the weapon decide your feet for you.”

Percy picked up his sword again. “Try moving to the side instead of straight back. That gives you another angle, but don't cross your feet.”

The camper took a position across from him. Percy held the practice sword low and waited. The padded staff came toward his shoulder. He stepped aside, let the strike pass, and kept his balance. When the camper tried the same movement, their feet crossed and they stumbled into the next step.

“Reset,” Clarisse said. “Slow down. First move your lead foot, then bring the other one with it.”

The camper tried again. This time the step was short and clean. Percy turned his blade to meet the staff after it passed, and they both stopped before either could crowd the other.

“There,” Clarisse said. “Do it the same way three times before you make it faster.”

The camper repeated the move. On the third pass, Percy almost answered the strike too early. He caught himself and waited until the staff committed. It was a small correction, but Clarisse saw it.

“Better,” she said.

The camper grinned and went to find the counselor. The second camper put the gear away. Percy returned his practice sword to the rack and checked that the staff sat in its slot instead of leaning against it. Clarisse noticed and gave a small nod. He had come into the arena thinking about one sentence; he left having helped two campers get through the drill without turning it into a contest.

Percy set the sword away. His arms felt normal. His thoughts had not disappeared, but they had stopped crowding the whole room.

“You're not in trouble,” Clarisse said.

“I didn't think I was.”

“You looked like you did.”

“Maybe I was mad about missing.”

“You can be mad. Just don't pretend the drill was easy after you missed it.”

He looked at the rack. “Can I try again at the next practice?”

“Yes.”

“Thanks.”

“Don't thank me. Show up.”

He had gone to the pavilion afterward and forgotten to carry his plate to the fire. It sat where he'd left it until he saw the stain of syrup on the table and remembered. The mistake wasn't important by itself, but it was another sign that his attention kept turning toward a conversation that hadn't happened yet.

By the time Annabeth began explaining her shrine drainage improvement, he'd been distracted long enough for her to stop talking.

"You're somewhere else," she said.

Percy blinked. "Sorry. I was listening."

"What did I just say?"

"Something about rainwater and the north wall."

"That was five minutes ago." She closed her sketchbook. "What's going on?"

He could have lied. He could have said he was tired. But he'd promised they would talk instead of guessing, and this was the sort of thing that promise was for.

"I remember telling you I loved you before," he said. "After the eclipse. We both remember that. I want to say it now in an ordinary moment, but I don't want it to sound like I'm asking for anything."

Annabeth's expression turned thoughtful. "Are you asking me to answer before you've even said it?"

"No. I'm trying to explain why I keep losing my place."

"Then say what you mean when you're ready. I don't need a rehearsal."

"That's probably why I'm still rehearsing."

"Then stop rehearsing." She tipped her head toward the sketch. "You were about to tell me the drainage plan was impressive."

"Something about your drainage system being impressive."

"It is impressive."

"I know."

She laughed, and the moment loosened.

Instead he got up too quickly and caught his hip on the low stone wall.

“Careful,” Annabeth said, one hand already lifting toward him.

“I'm fine.”

“You hit the wall.”

“The wall moved.”

“It has been there longer than you.”

“Maybe it was jealous.”

Her smile came back. Percy was relieved enough to laugh. Then he noticed his shoulders had been held up around his ears since breakfast and lowered them by force.

“Ready to look at the channel?” she asked.

“Yes. I can pay attention to something with measurements.”

“You can try.” She tucked the sketchbook under her arm. “Just don't let the level distract you from the path.”

They left the shrine by the lower path. The first few paces were quiet. Percy had hoped the words would settle as soon as he'd spoken them, as if he'd been carrying a bucket and could set it down. Instead they seemed to have taken up less space in his head but more in his body. He was aware of his hands, his face, the air between his teeth when he tried to breathe normally. Annabeth walked beside him without touching him. She didn't seem to be avoiding him. She had a sketchbook to keep level and a path to watch.

“Should I ask you what happened in the training yard?” she asked.

“I missed three blocks.”

“Four.”

“You were counting?”

“Clarisse was counting. I was trying to pretend I wasn't.”

“I thought I was getting better.”

“You are. You can be getting better and have a distracted morning at the same time.”

“That sounds suspiciously reasonable.”

“I keep a few reasonable thoughts for emergencies.”

He glanced at her. “Was this an emergency?”

“You left your breakfast plate on the bench.”

“I usually remember the fire.”

“You do.”

He could hear the camp now: a hammer tapping at the forge, a cabin door shutting, the faint calls of campers moving between lessons. The morning was ordinary enough to make his first thought seem dramatic. That didn't make it less real. It only meant he could have it without the world stopping to make room.

At the pavilion corner, a low worktable stood where the maintenance crew kept stakes and measuring tools. The tools had been sorted into shallow trays, each with a chalk label. Annabeth stopped to check the one marked LEVELS. She took a short wooden level and a folding ruler, then looked at Percy.

“I asked Chiron if we could use these. He said yes, but we need to put them back.”

“I can handle that.”

“You can handle carrying them. I'll handle writing down the measurements.”

“I didn't say I wanted to write anything.”

“You were looking at the blank side of your hand.”

Percy looked down. There was no mark there. “I was checking for pencil.”

“That's a kind of looking.”

She handed him the level. He took it carefully, thumb resting over the small brass bubble window. It was lighter than it looked. He had expected a carpenter's tool to be heavier, maybe because it seemed like the sort of thing that knew when a person was wrong.

“You can tell if something's level?” he asked.

“That's what it's for.”

“I thought it was for making you feel bad about walls.”

“That too.”

She had the pencil and paper ready. They crossed the last stretch of grass toward the shrine garden. The bells Annabeth had planted along the south edge had spread, their white flowers nodding under the leaves. The thyme beds were still small. Someone had set a watering jug beside the low wall, its handle turned inward so it wouldn't catch on the path.

At the far side of the garden, the drainage channel disappeared behind the shrine's lower stones. The ground there was a little darker than the path. No water was running now. Percy crouched and looked along the shallow cut. The channel was narrow enough that he could have stepped across it, but that would have been the whole reason to come: he needed to know where the edges were before putting his weight anywhere.

“Do you want me to stay on the path?” he asked.

“Yes, until I check the stones.”

He stood back. He had expected to be useful by doing something immediately. Annabeth was already kneeling with one hand near the channel, not touching the water line, her eyes moving from the outlet to the lip. It occurred to him that asking first had not made him less useful. He still had the level, and she had not asked him to put it down.

“The sketch said it pooled at the lower bend,” he said.

“It did last week. I marked the highest line I could find.” She pointed to a brown trace on the stone. “But the channel is dry now, so I'm checking whether the slope matches what I drew.”

“And if it doesn't?”

“Then I change the drawing.”

“That simple?”

“No. But that's the next step.”

She slid the folding ruler along the near edge, stopping when it met a flat stone. Percy watched her check a measurement, write it down, then check it again from the other direction. The first number differed slightly. She frowned at the marks, not at herself.

“How much is slight?” he asked.

“Enough that I don't want to guess.”

“That's not a number.”

“It's a reason to measure again.”

Annabeth unfolded the ruler and set its hinge back at the first point. “Hold this end against the stone. Keep your hand out of the channel.”

Percy crouched on the path and braced the ruler where she showed him. He kept his weight over his heels. The ground beside the cut looked firm, but it was not part of the route they were measuring, and the edge stones had not been checked.

Annabeth aligned the other end with a shallow seam. “This is the mark I used yesterday.”

“And the other time?”

“I started at the corner.”

“That's a different place.”

“A little different.” She compared the two points, then looked at the sketch. “That's probably why the readings don't match.”

“Do you want to use the corner or the seam?”

“I don't know which one gives the useful line yet.” She tapped the page with her pencil. “If I choose now just because it makes the numbers agree, then I'm choosing the answer first.”

Percy looked at the ruler. “So we write down where you started both times.”

“Yes. Then I can ask the crew which surface they use as their reference.”

She added two small marks to the sketch and labeled them with brief notes. The lines looked nearly identical to Percy. Annabeth leaned over the page until the paper shadowed her face.

“I can tell the old mark is higher here,” she said, pointing to the stone. “I can't tell whether that means the water rose there, or the stone slopes, or the mark belongs to more than one rain.”

“Does the dark soil help?”

“Only as a sign the ground stayed damp. It doesn't tell me which direction the water took.”

Annabeth shifted to the other side of the outlet so she could see the grate without stepping into the channel. A narrow twig and several leaves were caught along its edge. She pointed them out on the sketch but didn't reach in to pull them free.

“Could those be blocking it?” Percy asked.

“Maybe. They could also have collected there after the water stopped. I can't tell from what we can see.”

“If we move them, we lose the chance to ask the crew what they make of it.”

“Right. We should leave the grate as it is until someone who knows the system can inspect it.”

Percy looked for another angle. The stones covered the place where the channel disappeared, and the gap was too narrow to see beneath them. He did not push the leaves aside or try to move the water. Annabeth added “debris at grate—cause unknown” beside the earlier mark. It gave the maintenance crew one more thing to examine without turning it into a diagnosis.

The sketch began to curl in the breeze. Percy caught its corner before it folded over the measurements, and Annabeth shifted it onto the low wall, weighted by the level. She looked from the drawing to the grate, then back again, checking whether she had marked the obstruction at the right edge.

“I can see the twig from here,” Percy said. “But I can't see whether it reaches under the bars.”

“Neither can I.” Annabeth crouched lower, staying on the path. The view changed, but the dark gap beneath the stone remained hidden. “I don't want to put my hand in there. We don't know how loose the edge is, and I don't know what's under it.”

“Could the crew lift the grate?”

“Maybe. They may have a way to check without lifting it.”

“So that's another question.”

She drew a small line on the sketch to mark the grate's visible edge, then stopped before the pencil touched the part that disappeared beneath stone. “I'll ask what they can inspect from the path and what they need access to. If they have to move anything, they can tell us first.”

Percy followed the line of the channel toward the outlet. The mud around it was darker than the path, but there was no standing water. A dry leaf had stuck to one stone farther up; it might have fallen there after the pool had drained. He pointed it out without reaching for it.

“That one isn't at the grate,” Annabeth said. “I'll note it separately. We shouldn't make one thing look like a collection.”

She added a second mark, then read both entries aloud: leaves and twig at the grate; one leaf on the upper stone. Percy listened for a diagnosis hidden in the wording. There wasn't one. She had recorded what they could see, and left the cause for someone who could inspect the whole channel.

“That's a lot of effort for a leaf,” he said.

“It's less effort than fixing the wrong thing because I guessed.”

“Fair.”

She rolled the sketch closed, then immediately opened it again to check the labels. “I don't want to lose the difference between these notes and the measurements.”

“I'll hold the edge.”

He did. She checked each mark in turn, and he kept the page from folding without covering the writing. When she was satisfied, she folded it along the old crease and tucked it under her arm.

He glanced at the channel, dry and narrow between the stones. It would have been easy to let the stain become a map in his mind. A line went from one place to another; water had to have followed it. But Annabeth was right: a dark patch showed dampness, not the path that had produced it.

“So the sketch needs two kinds of notes,” he said. “What you saw and what you still need to ask.”

“That's what I'm trying to separate.” She wrote a short note in the sketch margin and left the question mark beside it. The first measurement stayed on the page beside the second; she didn't hide either one or decide which was right yet.

He held the level out. “Want this?”

“Yes. Set it on the stone, not over the channel.”

He followed her directions. The brass bubble slid toward one end and stopped. Annabeth looked at it from the side, then asked him to shift the tool a finger-width. The bubble moved back toward center.

“The stone's tilted,” he said.

“Or the surface is uneven.”

“Aren't those the same thing?”

“Not if I'm trying to decide what to change.”

He nodded. He could have used water to tell her something about the channel. The urge came easily, as it always did around a problem with water. But she hadn't asked him to move anything. She wanted an observation from the tool and the ground.

“Can I ask something?”

“You can ask.”

“Would it help if I made a little water go through the channel?”

She considered the outlet and shook her head. “Not yet. We don't know how much water would be a useful test, and I don't want to make our own runoff problem.”

“I can keep it small.”

“I know. But I'd rather find out what the maintenance crew uses for a flow test. If we do the wrong test, we might make the drawing look more certain than it is.”

It was a good answer. Percy was a little disappointed anyway. He wanted to offer the part of himself that could move water and have it count as help. He could feel that desire sitting inside the rest of the morning, not quite about the drainage at all.

“Okay,” he said.

Annabeth looked up at him. “Thanks.”

“I didn't do anything.”

“You asked before doing anything. That was useful.”

He looked at the level in his hand. “I'll try not to let it go to my head.”

“I doubt the tool will let you.”

They made a small list of questions for Chiron and the maintenance crew: whether the outlet had been measured after the last rain, whether the gravel by the path was meant to be replaced or only leveled, whether the notch in the stone lip was wide enough for a heavier flow, and how to check that water wasn't passing underneath the edge stones. Annabeth wrote each one in the margin of her sketch. Percy repeated them aloud so she could catch a missing word.

“You don't have to memorize them,” she said.

“I'm not. I'm making sure I can tell them apart.”

“That's fair.”

He read the list back, pointing with one finger. “Last rain measurement. Gravel. Notch. Under the stones.”

“Good.”

“And you think we can get answers today?”

“Some. The person who checks the drains may be busy.”

“Then we wait.”

“Or we leave a clear request and continue with the parts we can verify.”

Percy glanced toward the shrine. The garden was quiet. There was no sign that the uneven measurement had hurt anything. The bell flowers leaned toward the sunlight, and the earth around the thyme was dark from an earlier watering. Nothing about the place demanded a heroic fix.

“We can start with the part we know,” he said.

Annabeth turned a page and made a small box around the question list. “Exactly.”

“You're going to use that word a lot.”

“Only when it's exact.”

They gathered the ruler and level. Percy checked that neither had touched the soft dirt. Annabeth folded her revised sketch so the measurements stayed inside. At the path, she paused and looked back at the channel.

“I thought I'd have the whole thing figured out by now,” she said.

“You did figure something out.”

“I found another question.”

“That's kind of what happens when you look closer.”

“It's annoying.”

“That also sounds like what happens when you look closer.”

She tucked the paper under her arm. “I want the garden finished. I don't want to put a bench somewhere that floods or plant something that dies because I guessed about the water.”

“You won't.”

“You can't know that.”

He stopped himself before reassurance could become a promise. “You're right. I don't know yet. But I can help you find out.”

Annabeth let the words sit. “That's better.”

They walked back to the worktable to return the tools. Percy set the level in its tray with the brass window turned up. Annabeth checked the label and put the ruler beside it. He had expected the walk to the shrine to feel like a small errand. Instead they had come back carrying a list of things they still needed to learn. It was a different kind of progress, and he could tell Annabeth was trying to let herself count it.

---

They took the questions to the Big House before lunch. Chiron was at the front desk with a stack of camp forms and a half-finished cup of tea. The door stood open to the porch; a breeze moved the edge of a map pinned beneath a brass weight.

Annabeth waited until he looked up. “We checked the shrine outlet. I changed the measurements on the lower stones, but we haven't tested the flow. We need to know whether the gravel is meant to be leveled or replaced and whether the maintenance crew has a standard test.”

“Good,” Chiron said. “What did you observe?”

She opened the sketch and showed him the brown mark on the stone, the channel and the places where the level suggested uneven surfaces. She separated what she had seen from what she thought might be happening beneath the path. Percy stood beside her with the tools in his hands, careful not to block the page.

“The stone slopes,” she finished. “Or the top is uneven. We need a second measurement before deciding which.”

“And the lower edge?”

“I can't tell if the gravel is moving or if it was laid that way.”

Chiron looked over the margin notes. “What do you need to know first?”

“Whether the edge stones and foundation are stable,” Annabeth said. “Then how the crew tests the outlet. The gravel can wait until we know what the water is doing.”

“And the debris?”

“There are leaves and a twig at the grate. I don't know whether they blocked anything or got caught after the water stopped. I left them there.”

“Good. If the crew needs them moved, they can decide how to inspect the grate.”

Percy watched Chiron turn the page so the measurements faced him. “What about the level reading?”

Annabeth pointed to the two marks. “The endpoints weren't identical. I need the crew to show me where they take the reference from. The surface is uneven; I can't tell whether the channel itself has the same slope.”

Chiron read the notes once more. “Then that's what I'll tell them: check the foundation before moving anything, explain the outlet test, and show you how to measure from a consistent point.”

“And if they want to clear the grate first?” Annabeth asked.

“They'll tell us why. You can ask to observe, but the crew will decide what they need to do.”

Annabeth nodded. She did not argue for a promise of access or a particular method. She held the sketch steady while Chiron finished reading it, then folded it along the crease without covering the new notes.

Chiron nodded. “I'll pass that request to the maintenance crew and ask them to show you their measurement method.”

Annabeth's chin lifted slightly. “Can I be there when they inspect?”

“If the crew agrees and your lessons permit it.”

“I'll ask.”

“And until we know more, no stones get shifted.”

“Understood.”

Percy set the level on the desk. Chiron looked at him. “You were planning to use your abilities?”

“I offered. Annabeth said not yet.”

“That was sensible. You can help with measurements and observation without altering the water we're trying to study.”

“I know.”

Chiron looked at the folded sketch. “It can be hard to wait when you see something you might be able to fix.”

“We'll wait for the crew's schedule,” Chiron said. “In the meantime, the path is open. If the edge changes or water pools there again, tell me. Don't try to correct it yourself.”

“We won't,” Annabeth said.

Percy nodded. He was glad she answered too. The project belonged to her, and knowing where the limit was didn't make him less part of the walk.

They returned the tools to the maintenance table, then crossed to the dining pavilion. Percy placed his plate in the right spot at the Poseidon table, took the serving spoon, and remembered to carry the leftovers to the fire. It felt ridiculous to count these things as successes, so he didn't. He only noticed they were done.

Silena sat across from him, laying a folded cloth beside her lunch. A small vial of sea-glass beads rested on top. She kept it near her plate rather than putting it away.

“Are those the beads?” Percy asked.

Silena smiled. “Yes. I haven't decided what to do with them.”

“You could make a necklace.”

“I could. Or I could use them for a clasp. Or keep them until I know.” She moved the vial a little farther from the edge. “I like that he didn't tell me what I should make.”

Percy understood the detail. He remembered Poseidon's gifts arriving in forms that felt like instructions, and also the ways his father had tried to make room for a no. It was easier to say that to himself than to decide what any of it meant for Clarisse.

“Did he ask you to show him the finished piece?”

“No. He said it was for my work.”

“That's good.”

“It is.” Silena picked up her spoon. “And I haven't decided about the rehearsal, either.”

“You told him you'd put it on the board if you schedule one.”

“Right. I haven't scheduled one.”

“I know.”

She studied him for a second. “You're trying to remember what belongs to other people.”

“Trying.”

“That's enough for today.”

Clarisse came in carrying two training shields and a roll of leather wrap. She set them beside her bench and sat down with the carefulness of someone who had done drills before breakfast. “Did you remember your plate?”

“Yes.”

“Did you remember the fire?”

“Also yes.”

“Good. You're improving.”

“I had one distracted morning.”

“You had four missed blocks and an abandoned plate.”

“I didn't know they had formed a committee.”

“We didn't. You left a plate where people sit. It's a one-person report.” She tore a length of wrap and measured it against a shield handle. “What's the sentence?”

Percy coughed. Silena's eyebrows rose.

“What sentence?” she asked.

“The one he wants to tell Annabeth,” Clarisse said. “He doesn't need to make it a performance. Say what you mean and let her answer in her own time.”

“I know.”

Silena nudged a piece of bread toward Clarisse. “You can have advice and lunch at the same time.”

“I'm eating.”

“You haven't touched your food.”

“I'm fixing the shield.”

“The shield can wait.”

Clarisse glared at her sister, then set the wrap down. She tore the bread in half and ate one piece before picking up the shield again.

“Which one is giving you trouble?” Percy asked.

“The handle's loose.” Clarisse turned the shield so he could see the leather. “The old wrap stretched. I'm replacing it before someone has to catch a hit with their fingers.”

“Want me to hold the end?”

“Hold tension. Don't pull.”

Percy took the loose end between two fingers. Clarisse laid the strip across the handle, pressed it flat with her thumb, and began winding. On the first loop he pulled too hard; the leather narrowed and twisted.

“I said hold it.”

“I thought I was.”

“You were trying to help.” She unwound the loop. “Just keep it from slipping while I do the turn.”

He eased his grip. Clarisse drew the wrap over itself, keeping the edge aligned with the previous pass. The handle looked ordinary again once the leather settled, but it no longer shifted when she tested it with her palm.

“There,” she said. “That's better.”

Silena picked up the bead vial and turned it so the light showed through the glass. For a few minutes she and Clarisse discussed what sort of string could pass through the holes without slipping. Silena had opinions about the hole size; Clarisse had opinions about whether a clasp should catch on a sleeve. They didn't choose a design. Neither asked Percy to arbitrate.

He sat with them and listened while Clarisse tested the shield wrap and Silena turned the bead vial in the light.

Clarisse found Percy later, hauling timber toward the shrine.

"You want the other end?" she asked.

"If you don't mind."

She took the plank. "Where are we taking it?"

"The staging area. Chiron said no stones move until the maintenance crew checks the foundation."

"Then let's get it there without hitting anything."

They lifted together. The plank was long enough that Percy had to turn his shoulders to clear the path. Clarisse took the leading end and kept it low while Percy watched the rear. They moved at a pace that let them stop quickly if someone came around the bend.

Percy could see Clarisse's hands but not her feet. The plank blocked most of the ground between them, and the path changed direction ahead. He realized he was matching her pace without knowing whether she could see him doing it.

“Call it before you move,” he said.

“I can see the corner.”

“I can't see you turning.”

Clarisse looked over her shoulder. “Fine. I'll say front moving. You say when the back is clear.”

“And if I say stop?”

“We stop.”

“Even if you're already past the turn?”

“Especially then.”

They tried it once in the wider part of the path. Clarisse said “front moving” and took two steps. Percy followed only after the end cleared a tree trunk beside the path. He called “back clear” when the plank had room. The words sounded excessive for carrying a piece of wood, but they kept both of them from guessing.

“Again,” Clarisse said.
This time he called out first. They shifted the plank a few inches away from the grass and continued toward the shrine.

Annabeth had been waiting near the shrine when Percy returned. She was sitting on the low wall, sketchbook open on her knees, pretending to work while watching the path.

She stood when she saw them. “Hold on before you turn.”

Clarisse stopped. Percy took another half-step before he realized she had stopped, and the back of the plank drifted toward the garden bed. He corrected it before it touched the low wall.

“Sorry,” he said.

“You're fine,” Annabeth said. She walked ahead to the narrow turn where the lower path passed the drainage channel. “This is the part I was worried about.”

“It's wider than it looks,” Clarisse said.

“The path is. The turn isn't. If the front end goes around too fast, the back end swings across the channel.”

Clarisse glanced at the plank. “Then we don't turn fast.”

“We also don't want to scrape the stones or step into the channel to make room.” Annabeth pointed to a spot before the turn. “Set it down here for a second. We'll clear the path and decide how to pivot.”

Percy and Clarisse lowered the plank together. They kept it on the dry edge, away from the dark line that marked where water had collected after the last rain. Percy brushed his palms against his shorts. The groove from the plank was red but shallow. It didn't hurt.

Annabeth crouched beside the turn, checking the clearance from the outside edge of the path to the nearest stone. She did not touch the stone. She traced the route in the air, then stood and followed the line toward the staging area.

“If we keep the front end pointed down the path and move the back end in a smaller arc, we can stay clear of the channel,” she said. “But we need a person watching each end.”

“I'll take the back,” Percy said.

“I can guide,” Annabeth replied. “Clarisse can take the front. We can stop between movements.”

Clarisse crossed her arms. “You don't need to carry it if you're guiding.”

“I know. I can see the path better from here.”

Percy looked from Annabeth to the plank. He wanted to offer to take both ends, as if strength could make the turn less narrow. Instead he said, “Tell us when to move.”

Annabeth nodded. “Clarisse, lift your end just enough to clear the path. Percy, don't follow until she has room. Then bring the back end toward you, not over the channel.”

“Got it.”

They raised the plank. Clarisse moved first. Percy waited, keeping his feet on the dry path, then shifted the rear end in the direction Annabeth indicated. It cleared the corner by less than a handspan.

“Stop,” Annabeth said.

Both of them stopped.

The plank was level, not because the ground was level but because they were holding it. Percy could feel the weight pull at his arms. Clarisse didn't rush him. Annabeth checked the space ahead and then looked back at his end.

“You have room to bring it forward two steps,” she said. “Then we pause again.”

They did. The front end cleared the narrowest point. Percy could now see the line of the path ahead, but the plank was still at an angle. He had to back up a step to keep the rear from scraping the stone lip.

“A little left,” Annabeth said.

“My left or yours?”

“Yours.”

“That's a useful distinction.”

“I thought so.”

Clarisse kept the front end steady while Percy adjusted. The edge of the plank passed the stone without touching it. Annabeth checked the channel, then the garden bed. Nothing had shifted. Nothing had been disturbed.

They had nearly made the turn when a younger camper appeared on the path with a basket of folded towels. The camper stopped well before the plank, eyes moving between Clarisse and the narrow opening.

“Do you need to get through?” Annabeth asked.

“I'm taking these back to the cabins.”

“We can wait. There's room if we set the plank down.”

Clarisse looked at the patch of path behind them. “Not there. The rear would block the crossing.”

Percy shifted his grip. “We can finish the turn and move to the staging place. It's just past the wall.”

Annabeth checked the route. “If we keep it still while the camper passes, the basket has enough clearance. Nobody moves the plank until they're through.”

Clarisse nodded. “Your call.”

The younger camper waited while Annabeth stepped to the garden side and Percy drew the rear end closer to his hip. Clarisse held the front. The basket passed between the wall and the plank, the towels folded neatly above its rim. Percy kept his hands fixed and his feet planted. The camper thanked them and continued down the path.

“Now,” Annabeth said. “One step at a time.”

They completed the turn. The marked staging patch was beside the path, far enough from the channel that a carried load wouldn't need to cross the stones. It was clear of loose equipment, with room for the crew to stand around the plank once it was set down. Clarisse lowered the front end. Percy followed, but Annabeth raised a hand before they let go.

“The far end still crosses the approach,” she said. The plank had cleared the bend, but its angle left one corner over the path people would use to reach the shrine.

Clarisse kept hold of her end. “We can move it closer to the garden.”

“Only if it stays off the bed.” Annabeth walked the edge and checked the space between the plank and the thyme. “We have room to turn it parallel. The staging patch is wide enough if we shift it in small steps.”

They lifted again. Clarisse called “front moving,” then rotated her end toward the path. Percy waited for the turn to clear before bringing his end around. Annabeth watched the garden edge and the drainage stones, asking them to pause whenever either end came close. They did not drag the plank across the ground.

The second placement left the lower path clear and kept the plank inside the marked staging patch. Clarisse lowered her end; Percy followed. They set it down parallel to the path. It no longer blocked the approach to the shrine, though the plank itself occupied the patch while it waited for maintenance.

Annabeth walked the route once more, checking that the edge stones were still visible and that the plank did not cover the place where the maintenance crew would need to stand. She opened her sketchbook and added a short note beside the lower-channel drawing: keep the approach clear during inspection; plank occupies marked staging patch pending maintenance. She did not mark a repair or add a measurement she hadn't taken.

“Can someone still come through here while the crew is looking at the outlet?” Percy asked.

Annabeth stepped to one side and walked the path as if she were carrying the sketch and tools back from the worktable. The plank's nearest end left her room to pass without turning sideways. She went to the outlet, turned around without stepping onto the garden bed, and came back.

“The approach is clear,” she said. “If they need to set anything down, they'll have room on the other side.”

Clarisse looked at the space between the staging patch and the path. “And people carrying pads can go the same direction without cutting across the channel.”

“As long as they stop at the wide part if someone is already coming through,” Annabeth said.

Percy tried the path with empty hands, then turned back toward the staging area. He could see why Annabeth had asked for the plank to lie parallel. If it were left across the patch, a person approaching the outlet would have to step around it and closer to the stones. If it lay the way they had placed it, the lane stayed open.

“We should leave it like this,” he said.

“We have,” Annabeth replied. “The crew can decide whether it belongs anywhere else.”

“That's where it should stay for now,” she said.

“Until the crew tells us what needs moving,” Percy said.

“Exactly.”

Clarisse flexed her fingers after letting go. “Next time, we carry it before lunch. Less foot traffic.”

“We don't know when there'll be a next time,” Annabeth said. “Or whether they need this plank at all.”

“Then we'll make a plan if they do.” Clarisse brushed her palms together. “I have to put the shields away.”

“Thanks for helping,” Annabeth said.

“It was a plank.” Clarisse glanced at Percy. “Don't make it sound like I rescued you.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“Good.” She headed back up the path.

---

Percy set down the plank and wiped his hands on his shorts. "Can I tell you something?"

"You can."

He stood in front of her. He had thought about it all afternoon, rehearsing different versions. The words he settled on were simpler.

"I love you," he said. "Not because I remember saying it before. Not because I expect you to say anything back. I love the person you are with me now. I love how you make room for people. I love that you notice when someone needs help, even when you're trying not to show that you noticed. I love that you argue with the weather when it ruins your plans."

Annabeth's mouth twitched. "The weather is often unreasonable."

"I know. I love that too." He took a breath. "You don't have to answer. I just wanted you to know what I mean when I say it."

Annabeth set her sketchbook aside and stood. She was close enough that he could see the faint pencil smudge on her cheek.

"I love you too," she said. "Not because I remember. I do remember, but that's not why. I love the boy who asked what I wanted instead of assuming. I love the one who lets me be angry and doesn't try to smooth it over. I love how you listen when I talk about things you don't understand yet. And I love that you think a blue shell can be a good present."

"It was a good present."

"It was."

She kissed him. Percy put his hands lightly at her shoulders, giving her plenty of room to pull away. She stayed. The kiss was warm and unhurried, a little clumsy when his nose bumped hers, and full of everything they'd just said.

When they separated, Annabeth's smile trembled at the edges. She kissed him once more, then rested her forehead against his. Percy closed his eyes and let the quiet of the camp settle around them.

They left the shrine with the approach lane clear and the plank occupying the marked staging patch. Annabeth carried her sketchbook under her arm. The questions about the outlet remained in the margin, unanswered until the maintenance crew could inspect it. Percy walked beside her toward dinner, not looking for another way to explain what he had said.

They told their friends at dinner.

Not with a speech. Annabeth sat beside Percy and leaned into him while Silena passed around a bowl of roasted vegetables.

Before everyone had settled, a stack of plates started sliding toward the end of the table. One of the younger campers had set them down crookedly, and the top three tilted when somebody bumped the bench. Percy caught the stack with his palm. Annabeth steadied the bottom plate before it could slip.

“Thanks,” the camper said, reaching for them.

“Here,” Annabeth said. “Let's set them flat first.”

Percy shifted the stack to the middle of the table. Annabeth took two plates and passed one to the camper, then handed the rest along to Silena. No one made a production of it. The plates went around; the camper found a place at the bench; the conversation picked up where it had paused.

When the food arrived, Annabeth asked Percy to pass the bread. He did. She offered him the browned edge of a roasted vegetable, and he took it.

Clarisse noticed. So did Thalia. Silena had already guessed.

"Did you two have another very earnest conversation about the thing?" Clarisse asked.

Percy choked on his water. "What thing?"

"The word that starts with L and isn't 'lake.'"

Annabeth looked at her. "How did you know?"

"You both look like you just finished a very long conversation and got the answer you wanted. Also, Percy has been smiling at his plate for ten minutes."

Silena smiled. "I'm happy for you."

Thalia watched Annabeth carefully. "Are you happy?"

"Yes," Annabeth said. "I am."

Thalia nodded. "Then I'm happy too."

Clarisse scooped another spoonful of vegetables onto her plate. “Can we return to the part where Percy was smiling at his food? I need to know whether it was because of the conversation or because he finally got a decent piece of bread.”

“The bread was good,” Percy said.

“There it is,” Silena said. “An answer that tells us nothing.”

“I can be happy about two things at once.”

“That's very advanced of you,” Clarisse said.

“That's a reasonable prediction,” Thalia said.

Clarisse pointed her fork at her. “You don't get to join in.”

“Why not?”

“Because you're supposed to be on my side.”

“I am on your side. I'm also right.”

Silena passed Percy the bowl. He took a smaller portion and handed it to Annabeth before she had to reach across the table. Their hands brushed. He didn't turn the gesture into a signal. It was just the way the bowl moved between them, one person noticing the other was waiting.

The conversation drifted to the training schedule. Clarisse wanted the younger campers to get another shield drill before the dawn practice. Silena thought the instructions needed to be clearer, because the last group had spent more time arguing over who was meant to carry the pads than actually moving. Thalia suggested a rotation so the same campers wouldn't have to fetch equipment every time.

“That's a good idea,” Clarisse admitted.

“You can say it without looking pained.”

“I could. I choose not to.”

Annabeth asked whether the rotation could also keep the equipment away from the path to the shrine. The plank and tools had been set in the staging area, but the route was narrow when people carried things in both directions. Clarisse said she would keep the lane clear for the morning. Percy listened as they decided where the pads could go without blocking the passage.

“If the next group comes up while we're still carrying the shields back, they should wait on the arena side,” Clarisse said. “One pair moves through at a time.”

Silena frowned at the table. “If the pads are stacked across the path, nobody can get around them without brushing the flower bed.”

“Then they don't go across the path,” Annabeth said. “Keep the remaining space around the staged plank clear. The crew needs to be able to stand beside the outlet.”

Clarisse nodded. “I'll tell the campers before we start. They can bring the pads in smaller groups and return them along the same side.”

“And if someone needs to pass?” Percy asked.

“The carriers stop somewhere wide enough to set things down,” Annabeth said. “Not at the turn.”

Thalia tapped the table once, as if marking the route. “That gives everyone one clear path instead of two groups trying to squeeze past each other.”

Clarisse considered it, then nodded again. “All right. We can do that.”

“The person at the front should call before moving,” Percy said. “The person behind confirms the back end is clear.”

Clarisse nodded toward him. “Like the plank.”

“It worked.”

“Then I'll use it. If someone needs to stop, they say stop. Nobody tries to finish the turn first.”

Annabeth tapped the edge of her sketchbook. “And no one leaves loose equipment around the plank. The crew needs room to stand and inspect, even if they're only looking.”

“I'll tell them that too,” Clarisse said. “We can practice moving as a group without pretending the path belongs to us.”

Silena handed the serving bowl to Thalia. “Good. I don't want to be the person carrying pads and trying to yell over everyone.”

“You can make me the caller,” Clarisse said.

“That sounds like a threat.”

“It's a job.”

“I'll consider it.”

Clarisse lifted her cup. "To the word that starts with L."

"To love," Silena corrected.

"I was being subtle."

"No, you weren't."

They clinked cups. Percy felt Annabeth's hand find his beneath the table. He squeezed it once.

After dinner, Percy walked Annabeth to the Zeus cabin. They took the path past the shrine staging area. The plank still lay parallel to the path, clear of the turn and the outlet. Annabeth glanced at the sketch tucked under her arm, then at the open approach, making sure the placement had held.

Two campers came toward them carrying practice pads. Percy and Annabeth stopped at the wider part of the path, leaving the bend free. One of the campers was the boy from the arena. He recognized Percy and shifted his end of the pad so it wouldn't swing into the garden.

“We can wait,” he said.

“You don't need to,” Annabeth told him. “We'll hold here until you're through.”

The campers moved one at a time. The first carried the pad close to their body; the second waited until the narrow turn was clear before following. Neither had to turn sideways or step into the bed. Percy watched the spacing more than the gear. The rule was simple, but it worked: stop where the path widened, let the person already in the turn finish, then continue.

“That went faster than trying to go together,” the boy said.

“It usually does,” Annabeth replied.

He nodded and continued toward the arena. The second camper followed, still carrying the pads. Percy and Annabeth started up the path again. The plank remained in the marked staging patch, and there was room to approach the shrine without crossing the stones.

At the Zeus cabin door they said good night. Annabeth went inside, and Percy turned back toward the Poseidon cabin, where Clarisse and Silena had a card game waiting.

---

He went in smiling.

Clarisse had opened the deck and Silena was sorting the cards into a neat stack.

“You were supposed to deal,” Clarisse said.

“You started without me.”

“You were walking Annabeth home.”

“That's what I said.”

Silena pulled out a chair with her foot. “Sit. We saved you a hand.”

Percy joined them at the table. The cards were soft at the corners, and one had a crease that made it sit crooked against the others. Silena explained the rummy rules in a few sentences: draw, make sets or runs, discard; when one player went out, they counted the cards left in their hands.

“That's all?” Percy asked.

“That's all,” Silena said.

Clarisse dealt seven cards to each of them. Percy sorted his hand once, then stopped trying to read meaning into every card. Silena collected a run. Clarisse held onto a pair of fives. Percy kept a nine he thought might help him and discarded a king.

Clarisse picked it up. “Thanks.”

“You're welcome.”

“You didn't mean to help me.”

“I didn't say I did.”

Clarisse tucked the king into her hand, then took a card from the stock and discarded one of her own. The motion made the game clearer to Percy: the discard pile was a choice, not a penalty. He could reach for a card someone else had let go, but then he had to decide what he was willing to lose in exchange.

Silena watched him study his cards. “You don't have to make a set right away. You can hold pieces until they connect.”

“How long?”

“Until someone goes out, or until your hand makes sense.”

Clarisse snorted. “That second condition could take you years.”

Percy arranged the cards along the table edge, keeping them low enough that nobody else could see. A pair almost looked useful until he noticed the numbers were different. He separated them again. The nine stayed near the corner of his hand, a possible fit for something he hadn't drawn yet. He had no way to know if it would arrive. Waiting for it would be a choice too.

“You look like you're trying to get the cards to confess,” Silena said.

“They know what they did.”

“They were dealt,” Clarisse said. “Blame the dealer.”

“You dealt them.”

“Exactly. I know what they did.”

Silena drew, looked once, and discarded. Clarisse took her turn without comment, the crease in the nine of hearts catching on the table when she laid it down. Percy drew from the stock rather than the pile. The new card did not help the nine, but it joined another card he had nearly discarded. He kept both, then let a different one go. The choice was small and immediate. It did not need a speech or a guarantee; he could see what the next turn brought.

He found himself following the order now. Draw. Decide. Discard. Clarisse watched the pile as if the next useful card might appear if she waited long enough; Silena's run took shape one card at a time. When Percy asked if he could lay down a run before he was ready to go out, Silena explained that either player could set out a complete meld, then build on one already on the table. Clarisse said she preferred rules that let people finish a thought. Silena replied that she preferred rules that let people change their minds. Neither looked at Percy to settle it; their house rule allowed both.

“So a run can be finished even if the rest of my hand isn't?” he asked.

“Yes,” Silena said. “But if you put it down, everyone can see it.”

Percy looked at his cards. “I'll keep mine for now.”

“Your choice,” she said.

Another turn went around. Clarisse picked up a card and immediately discarded one, her jaw tightening as if the deck had challenged her. Silena waited, considering the visible melds and the two cards still tucked in her hand. Percy stopped trying to predict who would win and watched what each person chose to keep. His own hand remained incomplete. That did not mean he had to force it into a shape it did not have.

The hand moved quickly after that. Silena laid down her run and went out. Clarisse had one card worth three points; Percy still had two cards, totaling four. Silena won the hand, and Clarisse finished one point ahead of Percy.

Percy set his two remaining cards faceup while Clarisse did the same with hers. He checked the values once, then counted again, moving each card to the side after he named it. Four. Clarisse's single card was three. The difference was small enough to make her stare at it as if one point had been misplaced under the table.

“You were ahead of me,” Percy said.

“I was.”

“By one.”

“A margin is still a margin.”

Silena gathered her laid-down run with the cards from the discard pile. “No extra hand to settle it. I said one hand.”

Clarisse pushed the three-point card toward the center. “We settled it.”

“You lost to me by one and beat him by one,” Silena said. “That's a very tidy result.”

“Don't make it sound planned.”

“Wouldn't dream of it.”

Percy turned the nine of hearts so the crease lay flat against the table. “This card's been bent longer than I've been playing.”

“Then it has a history,” Silena said.

“It has a fault line,” Clarisse replied. “Replace it before somebody uses it to cheat.”

“I didn't cheat,” Silena said, already collecting the cards.

“Didn't say you did.”

“You implied it.”

“I implied the card could.”

Percy slid the cups together so they wouldn't tip while the sisters litigated the difference. The disagreement stayed light; Silena sorted the deck by suit while Clarisse looked over the score one last time. Percy had been waiting all day for an answer he couldn't control. Here, the result had arrived without ceremony: one hand, a count each of them could check, and no need to play again just because the margin felt unfinished.

Clarisse checked the count, then nodded. “Fine. You won.”

“Thank you.”

“Don't sound so pleased.”

“I won.”

Percy gathered the cups while the sisters debated whether the crease in the nine of hearts made it a marked card. Silena said it did not. Clarisse said they should replace it anyway. Neither of them accused the other of cheating, and the card went back in the deck.

Silena put the cards away and took the bead vial to the drawer beside her jewelry tools. Percy glanced over.

“It's safe there,” she said before he could ask.

“Okay.”

She closed the drawer and returned to the table. Clarisse put the leather wrap away, then checked that the deck was back on its shelf.

“Dawn,” she reminded him.

“I'll be there.”

He went to his bunk and set his shoes where he could find them. His practice clothes were folded at the foot of it. Across the room, Clarisse put out the lamp. The cabin settled into quiet, with the cards put away and the plank still waiting at the shrine staging area. The day's questions about the outlet would wait for the maintenance crew. Percy let them wait too.
