## Chapter 17: Lake Day


The canoe lake smelled of mud, cut grass, and sunscreen. Percy stepped onto the dock, surprised by how little it reminded him of salt water.

That was Percy's first thought when his bare feet hit the warm planks of the dock, and he immediately felt guilty for thinking it, because everyone else was cheering like the canoe lake was the best thing that had ever happened.

It wasn't that it smelled *bad*. It smelled like — sun-warmed pine and sunscreen and cut grass and that sharp green algae smell around the edges where the water went shallow. It just didn't smell like home. Home smelled like cold salt and deep current and kelp forests and the iron tang that hung around the forges in Atlantis. Home smelled blue and far down.

This lake smelled bright and shallow and *loud*.

"Okay, stop making that face," Clarisse said, elbowing him hard in the ribs. She was already in her swimsuit, hair pulled back in a knot, a towel slung over her shoulder like a cape. "You look like someone told you the lake personally offended you."

"It didn't offend me," Percy said. "I'm observing it. With respect."

"You're sulking," Silena said, appearing on his other side. She had three bottles of sunscreen tucked under one arm and was already bossing people with the other hand. "Percy Jackson sulking on a free day should be illegal. Smile."

Percy smiled. It felt crooked.

It wasn't that he didn't like the lake. He did — in theory. Free day. No training. No lessons. Mr. D had announced it at breakfast with all the enthusiasm of a man reading a parking ticket — "Fine, yes, lake day, don't drown, if you drown I have to do paperwork" — and the whole dining pavilion had erupted. Nico had actually pumped his fist. Ella had immediately started making lists of picnic supplies on a napkin. Grover had looked up from his aluminum-can collection with grass stains already on his knees.

And Annabeth — Annabeth across the pavilion at the Zeus table, catching his eye for exactly half a second and quirking her mouth in that small private smile that meant *we get a whole day, Seaweed Brain* — that had made his chest go warm and tight.

They were trying to be discreet about it now. A few weeks into camp, everybody knew they were together, as the flag game had made embarrassingly clear. Thalia noticed everything, Zoe commented on everything, and Clarisse preferred to pretend anything romantic didn't exist unless it hit her with a spear. At breakfast Percy had passed Annabeth the milk pitcher without even looking at her and she'd said "thanks" in her flat, practical voice, and under the table her pinky had hooked around his for two seconds.

Two seconds was enough to live on for a morning.

But now, standing on the dock with twenty-odd campers yelling and cannonballing and smearing zinc on their noses, Percy felt that familiar Atlantean itch under his skin. The water was calling him in a language this lake didn't speak.

In Atlantis, you didn't *swim* on the surface. You moved *through*. During his recent lessons, Triton had told him to let the current take his weight, think about where he wanted to be, and listen for the water's answer. Legs were — well, his altered body knew tricks his mind couldn't place. Form-change lessons from age six, Amphitrite said, though he remembered none of them. He had been learning the deep-water movements again, following what his body could do without inventing memories of childhood races.

Up here, with these stubby human legs and this bright flat water that ended twelve feet down in mud? He was going to look ridiculous.

"Race!"

Zoe Nightshade's voice cut across the dock. She was already in the water, hair slicked back, treading easily. She'd tied a strip of blue fabric around her upper arm to keep her hair out of her face. "Jackson! You claim to be Poseidon's son. Prove it. Buoy and back."

A cheer went up. Clarisse whooped. Someone started chanting his name.

Percy looked at the buoy bobbing about a hundred yards out. For him, that was — what, four tail-flicks? He could have sneezed and ended up there.

Annabeth was sitting at the end of the dock with Thalia, legs dangling over the edge, already watching. She raised one eyebrow. *Be careful*, that eyebrow said.

Right. Hold back. Big rule for today. Chiron had pulled him aside that morning while Percy was pulling on his swim trunks. "Percy, a word. I know what Atlantean children can do in water. Today, I need you to be —" Chiron had searched for the word, "— approximately as talented as a reasonably athletic twelve-year-old from Ohio."

"I can do Ohio," Percy had said solemnly.

"Twelve-year-old from Ohio who took *one* summer of swim lessons," Chiron clarified.

"Got it. Mediocre Ohio."

"Please lose at least once today, Percy. Graciously."

So Percy stepped up to the edge of the dock, toed the splintery wood, and tried to remember everything he'd ever seen about how surface kids started races. They crouched? They dove? In Atlantis you just tipped forward and let go.

"Ready?" Silena called, holding her hand up. "On three! One — two —"

"Wait!" Ella fluttered up, half-tripping over her own beach bag, which was roughly the size of a small boat. "Wait, I want to watch! Hold on — okay! Go!"

"THREE!"

Percy dove.

And immediately knew he'd messed up, because his body wanted to do the Atlantean arrow — arms pinned, spine straight, no splash — and he had to force himself at the last second to do a messy human belly-flop that stung his whole front and sent water up his nose.

Laughter erupted on the dock. Clarisse cackling loudest. "NICE FORM, SQUID!"

Zoe was already three strokes ahead, doing a clean freestyle that ate up the distance. Percy scrambled after her, forcing himself to kick from the knees like he'd seen in swim class, forcing himself to *breathe* every three strokes even though his lungs could have gone another ten minutes easy.

It was weirdly hard. Like trying to write with his wrong hand on purpose.

He let Zoe stay half a body ahead all the way out. At the buoy she slapped it and turned, droplets flying, grinning fierce. Percy slapped it a second later, made his turn wide and sloppy on purpose, and then on the way back he let his control slip just a little — just enough to feel the water answer him, just enough to glide.

He remembered at the last second and slowed, letting Zoe touch the dock ladder first by a full two seconds.

The dock exploded.

"ZOE! ZOE! ZOE!"

Zoe hauled herself up, panting, and stared at him as he clung to the ladder. "You let me win."

"What?" Percy sputtered, lake water dripping off his nose. "No way. You're fast. That turn was — you're really fast."

"You hesitated at the buoy," Zoe said, eyes narrow. Not angry, just sharp. Huntress sharp. "You slowed down."

"I got water up my nose," Percy said, which was true, and tapped the side of his nose for effect. "Ohio swim lessons. Only one summer."

Zoe stared at him for another beat, then laughed and held out a hand to haul him up. "Fine. Rematch after lunch. No excuses."

"You're on," Percy said, and when his feet hit the dock he glanced toward Annabeth.

She was laughing into her hand, shoulders shaking. Thalia was grinning. And Annabeth mouthed, very clearly, *Ohio.*

Percy grinned back before he could stop himself, and quickly turned it into a cough.

Grover Underwood had decided, by 10:47 that morning, that the canoe lake was the best place in the entire world to think about his future.

He was sitting in the shade of a big pine with his hooves tucked under him — he'd told the new kids his sneakers were "orthopedic" and nobody questioned it after the first week — with a half-finished reed pipe in his lap and his notebook open to a page that said at the top in careful block letters:

*WHY GROVER SHOULD BE A SEARCHER (reasons by Grover)*

Underneath he'd written:

1. Good with animals (goats are animals too??? ask Chiron)
2. Good listener
3. Can play music (getting better!!)
4. Cares a lot!!! (triple underline)
5. ???

He tapped his pencil against number five.

The thing was, everyone at Camp Half-Blood had a thing they were supposed to want. Clarisse wanted to be a warrior — obviously, she already was. Silena wanted to run the infirmary and the arts cabin and basically everything involving making people feel better. Zoe wanted to hunt with — well, Zoe got quiet when people asked, but it was something about stars and sisters and the wild. Nico wanted to learn every myth ever written and then correct people about them. Ella wanted to memorize the whole library and then add snacks to it.

And Annabeth — Annabeth wanted to build things that lasted. You could see it in the way she looked at buildings, like she was seeing the bones underneath. Thalia wanted — Grover wasn't sure, actually. Thalia wanted to protect Annabeth so badly it came off her like heat, and maybe that *was* the want.

Grover wanted to find Pan.

He hadn't told anyone except Percy, late one night in the Poseidon cabin when Percy was homesick and Grover was homesick and they were both pretending not to be. "The god of the wild," Grover had whispered. "He disappeared, like, two thousand years ago. Everyone says he's dead. But satyrs don't believe that. Searchers go out and look. With pipes. They play and listen for an answer."

Percy had been quiet for a long time. Then: "Do you think you'd hear him?"

"I think I have to try," Grover had said.

Now, sitting by the lake with dragonflies landing on his horns — he'd glamoured them down to nubs, but insects always knew — Grover watched Percy lose a second race on purpose (to a kid from the Hermes cabin who swam like a windmill, arms everywhere) and felt that tug in his chest again. That *try* feeling.

"Hey."

Nico flopped down next to him, damp and sandy, holding a plastic cup full of lake water with a minnow in it. "Whatcha writing?"

Grover slammed the notebook shut so fast he nearly took off his own finger. "Nothing! Pipe schematics! Boring satyr stuff!"

Nico eyed him. Nico was small and saw everything. "You're writing with exclamation points. That's not schematics. That's feelings."

"It's —" Grover sighed. Nico was hard to lie to, mostly because Nico would just stare until you caved. "Okay. I'm thinking about being a Searcher. Don't laugh."

"Why would I laugh?" Nico asked, genuinely puzzled. He held the cup up to the light. The minnow flickered. "That's the coolest thing I've ever heard. You go out into the world with your pipes and find a lost god? That's like — that's main-character stuff, Grover."

Grover's ears went hot under his curls. "Yeah, but — Searchers have to be brave. And good at music. And I'm —" He gestured at himself. The round face. The nervous hands. The reed pipe with one hole drilled crooked. "I'm the guy who cried during capture the flag because a bunny got scared."

"You fed the bunny your granola bar and carried it to the woods," Nico said. "I saw. That's exactly who should find the god of the wild, dummy."

Grover stared at him.

Nico shrugged, cheeks pink, and tipped the minnow gently back into the lake. "Just saying. If Pan's out there, he wouldn't answer some jerk with a perfect song. He'd answer someone who actually likes things. You know?"

The minnow darted off. Grover watched the ripples spread and felt something settle low and warm behind his ribs.

He opened the notebook and, under number five, wrote: *5. Likes things.*

Then, after a second, he added: *(Nico says this counts)*

"Hey, Grover!" Ella called from the picnic blankets, waving frantically. "Grover, come help! The naiads are robbing me again!"

Grover capped his pen. "Duty calls," he said, and Nico grinned and chased after him, and for a little while the future could wait until after lunch.

Ella's picnic was, by any objective standard, perfect.

She had planned it for four days. She had a list. She loved lists. The list said:

- sandwiches (turkey + cheese, PB&J, cucumber for Silena who likes fancy things)
- fruit (grapes, apple slices with lemon so they don't go brown, strawberries if the kitchen has them — THEY DID!!!)
- cheese cubes + crackers
- lemonade (two jugs, one regular, one pink)
- cookies (oatmeal raisin + chocolate chip, in separate bags so they don't fight)
- emergency backup cookies (in case of cookie emergency)
- blanket (biggest one, check for ants first)
- book (for reading aloud if people get sleepy, currently: *D'Aulaires' Book of Greek Myths*, very relevant)

She'd hauled it all down in three trips with Nico's help, spread the blanket in the perfect spot — half sun, half shade, close enough to the water to hear it but far enough that sand wouldn't get in the cheese — and stood back with her hands on her hips.

"Behold," she announced to no one in particular. "Picnic."

It lasted eleven minutes.

The first theft was subtle. Ella was cutting apple slices and handing them around — Clarisse took three, Percy took a handful of grapes, Thalia stole a cookie and denied it with crumbs on her face — and Ella turned to pour lemonade and turned back to find the entire plate of cheese cubes gone. Just gone. Empty plate with one cracker balanced on the rim like a joke.

"Okay," Ella said slowly. "Who took the cheese?"

Nobody answered. Everyone looked innocent. Nico's mouth was full of sandwich, so he was innocent by default because you couldn't steal cheese with a full mouth — that was physics.

Then Percy pointed at the lake. "Uh. Ella?"

Three heads broke the surface near the reeds. Girls with green-tinted skin and long dark hair strung with pondweed, grinning with mouths full of cheese cubes. One of them waved a cracker at Ella like a tiny flag and sank back under with a *ploop*.

"Naiads!" Silena said delightedly. "Oh, they're so cute!"

"They're so RUDE," Ella said, clutching her cookie bag to her chest. "That was good cheese! That was *camp-store* cheese!"

It got worse. Every time Ella turned her back — to get napkins, to shoo a wasp off the lemonade, to show Grover the exact passage about Demeter's picnic in her book — something else vanished. A whole cluster of grapes. Two sandwiches. One entire jug of pink lemonade, lifted clean off the blanket by four naiad hands that rose out of the shallows like a coordinated heist crew while everyone watched and laughed instead of helping.

"Stop laughing and defend the food!" Ella yelled, brandishing a baguette like a club. She'd packed a baguette for *atmosphere* and now it was a weapon. "Nico! You're on cookie guard! Percy! You speak water! Tell them to stop!"

Percy held up his hands, laughing so hard he nearly fell off the blanket. "Ella, I can't — they're naiads, they're freshwater, it's like — it's a jurisdictional thing! I'm saltwater! I have no authority here!"

"You're the son of Poseidon!" Ella said. "Pull rank!"

"I tried!" Percy said. "One of them just offered me a grape! *Your* grape!"

The final straw was the oatmeal raisin cookies. Ella loved oatmeal raisin and nobody else ever wanted them — chocolate chip got all the glory — so she'd packed a whole private bag. She set it down for *one second* to rescue the lemonade cups from tipping over, and when she looked back the bag was sliding across the sand toward the water, dragged by a reed wrapped around it like a rope, with two naiad eyes just above the surface watching it come.

"OH NO YOU DON'T!" Ella shrieked, and charged.

She splashed into the shallows in her sundress, talons slipping in mud, and grabbed the cookie bag with both hands. The reed went taut. The naiad surfaced — young, maybe Ella's age in god-years, with lily pads in her hair — and they had a full tug-of-war, both giggling.

"These are MINE!" Ella said, laughing despite herself.

"Sharing is nice!" the naiad sang, tugging.

"Sharing is *asked for!*" Ella tugged back, and the bag split open and cookies went everywhere — into the water, onto the sand, one landing directly on Ella's head.

The whole beach howled. Clarisse fell off the blanket. Grover dropped his pipe. Even Annabeth — cool, composed Annabeth, sitting up on the dry sand with her sister — was laughing so hard she had to wipe her eyes.

The naiad, victorious, held up a soggy cookie like a trophy. "Trade?" she offered, and held out a smooth, perfect spiral shell, pink inside as sunrise.

Ella stared at it. Then she took the shell, pressed a (mostly dry) cookie into the naiad's hand, and shook on it solemnly. "Truce. But no more cheese."

"No promises!" the naiads chorused, and vanished in a flurry of ripples.

Ella waded back to the blanket, soaked to the knees, sand between her talons, cookie in her hair, clutching the shell like treasure.

"Best picnic ever," Nico said solemnly, handing her a sandwich that had somehow survived. It had sand on the crust. She ate it anyway.

"Second-best," Ella corrected, bumping his shoulder. "Best will be next time, when I bring a lid."

She opened her book to read aloud while everyone chewed, and when she got to the part about Demeter inventing bread she did all the voices, and Percy heckled, and the naiads resurfaced just to listen, chins on the sand at the water's edge, mouths full of stolen grapes.

Clarisse La Rue had been dreading this day for a week.

Not because she didn't like lake days — she did, in the abstract, the way you like birthday parties for other people — but because she'd watched Percy at the beach yesterday during free swim and realized with a sinking feeling that her little brother had no idea how to *play*.

He could swim, obviously. He could swim like a torpedo with a grudge. What he couldn't do was the stupid surface stuff everyone else grew up doing — the games that didn't matter and therefore mattered more than anything. Chicken fights. Marco Polo. Cannonball contests. The kind of thing where you yelled and splashed and got water up your nose and called it winning.

In Atlantis, play had been different. Racing through the kelp maze. Eel-tag in the deep trenches where the light went blue-black. Wrestling on the training mats while their father watched with his arms crossed and that almost-smile. Clarisse remembered Percy at eight, fearless, launching himself off the palace wall to tackle her, both of them tumbling while Silena yelled at them to be careful.

Up here, on the surface, with these spindly-legged camp kids? He hung back. He smiled and clapped and let other people start things. It drove her nuts.

So after lunch, while everyone was drowsy on the picnic blankets and Ella was reading and Grover was dozing with his hat over his face, Clarisse stood up, planted her hands on her hips, and pointed at Percy.

"You. Water. Now."

Percy blinked. "Hi. Yes. What did I do?"

"You're learning cannonballs," Clarisse announced. "It's embarrassing that my brother can't do a cannonball. Silena! Back me up."

Silena, who was braiding Thalia's hair and had sunscreen on her nose, nodded gravely. "It's true, Percy. Yesterday yours looked like you fell down stairs. Lovingly said."

"I did it on purpose!" Percy protested. "Ohio! One summer of lessons!"

"Ohio my butt," Clarisse said, dragging him up by the wrist. "Come on. Annabeth, Thalia, you judge."

Annabeth looked up from where she was lying on her stomach, chin in her hands, and Thalia — Thalia who never volunteered for anything splashy — sat up with sudden interest. "Oh, I'm judging," Thalia said. "I was born to judge."

They gathered at the end of the dock. Clarisse lined up the contestants: Percy, Nico (who claimed he'd never done one), and a Hermes kid named Connor who insisted his cannonball had once soaked a fully dressed counselor from twenty feet away.

"Rules," Clarisse said, in her best drill-instructor voice. "Tuck your knees. Hug your shins. Make the biggest splash humanly possible. No Atlantean cheating —" she poked Percy hard in the chest, "— I will know."

"How will you know?" Percy asked.

"Because if there's no splash and you just vanish like a seal, I'm throwing you back in."

Nico went first. He did a respectable tuck, a decent splash, and came up yelling. Connor went second and nearly emptied the lake — the splash hit the dock and soaked Zoe's towel. The judges held up scores on wet napkins: Annabeth gave him a 9, Thalia gave him a 10 with "SHOWOFF" written under it.

Then Percy.

He stood at the edge of the dock, skinny twelve-year-old knees knocking, and looked down at the water like it had personally challenged him. Clarisse saw him take a breath — that big Atlantean breath that went all the way down — and shook her head warningly.

Percy let half of it out. Bounced once on his toes. Jumped.

He tucked — too late, legs everywhere — and hit the water sideways with a crack that echoed off the trees and sent a wall of water straight up and straight onto the dock, drenching Clarisse, the judges' napkins, and Ella's book.

Silence. Then —

"TEN!" Thalia screamed, holding up a soggy napkin. "TEN! THAT WAS HORRIBLE AND PERFECT!"

"That was a belly-flop with extra steps!" Annabeth gasped, laughing, water dripping off her chin. She tried to write a score but the pen went through the napkin.

Percy surfaced, hair plastered over one eye, clutching his red side. "Ow! How do you do that on purpose? That hurts!"

"You do it *wrong* on purpose!" Clarisse yelled, laughing so hard her stomach hurt. She jumped in after him without even plugging her nose and came up sputtering. "That's the whole game! You suffer for comedy!"

Next was Marco Polo, and Percy was terrible at that too — he kept peeking, badly, and answering "polo" to his own "marco" — and then chicken fights, where Clarisse put Silena on her shoulders and Percy put Grover on his (Grover shrieking the whole time, "I'M TOO FRAGILE FOR THIS! MY PIPES!") and the girls won in eleven seconds because Grover threw himself backward to avoid falling and took Percy down with him.

"You let us win," Silena accused, treading water with her chin barely above the surface, hair in wet ropes.

"I did not," Percy said from underneath Grover, who was draped over his head like a fainting goat. "I was betrayed by my steed."

"I'M A PACIFIST STEED!" Grover yelled.

Clarisse floated on her back and watched her brother laugh with lake water in his ears and sand under his nails, finally looking like a kid on a lake day instead of a prince holding his breath, and felt something in her chest unknot.

She'd worried, when he came up — their little brother arriving from Atlantis to join their loud bright camp — that Percy would never get to just be twelve. He had told his sisters he remembered too much. Seventeen years of war and loss and drowning and *her* — Annabeth, gone, and then found again but different — and he carried it behind his eyes even when he smiled.

But right now, getting splashed by Nico and heckled by Thalia and corrected on his chicken-fight stance by a ten-year-old from the Demeter cabin? He was twelve. Loudly, messily twelve.

Clarisse rolled over and dunked him. He came up sputtering and dunked her back, and she let him, and Silena declared it a tie before anyone drowned.

Annabeth had been awake since dawn with a knot between her shoulder blades that wouldn't loosen.

It wasn't a bad knot. Not danger, not prophecy, not the cold finger-trail she'd learned to recognize in the life she remembered when something was coming. Just — noise. Too many people, too many expectations, too much *remembering*. She remembered being seventeen and planning sieges and holding a knife to survive, and she remembered being seven and running away and Thalia's hand around hers so tight it hurt. And now she was twelve in a swimsuit with zinc on her nose, sitting next to a sister who was alive here and had lost years as a pine tree in the history Annabeth remembered, and she didn't know what to do with her hands.

Thalia seemed to know. Thalia always seemed to know lately, which was new and strange and wonderful.

"Come on," Thalia said after lunch, when the races were done and the picnic was ruins and everyone was scattered. She stood and brushed sand off her legs and held out a hand. "You and me. No boys, no races, no performance. We're building something."

Annabeth took the hand and let herself be pulled up. "Building what?"

"You'll see."

Thalia led her past the blankets to the stretch of damp sand near the reeds where the naiads had left a scatter of smooth stones — payment, Ella insisted, for the cheese. Thalia sat down cross-legged, patted the sand next to her, and when Annabeth sat, Thalia dumped an armful of pebbles, shells, and driftwood between them.

"Olympus," Thalia said.

Annabeth blinked. "What?"

"We're building Olympus. In sand. You do the planning, I do the —" Thalia gestured vaguely, "— smashing things into place. Like old times."

Annabeth stared at her. Then, slowly, she reached out and smoothed a patch of sand flat.

It had been so long — in the life she remembered and the weeks since she arrived here — since she'd built anything just to build it. In her Athena life, everything she'd made had a purpose: a model for a quest, a plan for a battle, a blueprint to prove she was smart enough to be listened to. In this life, as Zeus's daughter, people expected lightning, drama, temper. Thalia got the "there goes another storm" jokes, Annabeth got the sideways looks: *are you going to be like your sister?*

Nobody asked her to just — make a little palace out of sand.

"Okay," Annabeth said quietly. She drew with her finger: a central hill, a ring wall, six big structures. "The palace is here. Big courtyard. The throne room faces east so the sunrise hits the doors."

"Why east?" Thalia asked, already piling pebbles for the wall.

"Because —" Annabeth paused. She'd been about to say something about tactical advantage and sight lines. Instead she said, "Because it's pretty. Sunrise through gold doors. Can you imagine?"

Thalia grinned. "Yeah. I can."

They worked in comfortable quiet while the lake noise washed around them — Clarisse yelling "TUCK YOUR KNEES," Grover yelping, Ella reading about Arachne in three different voices. Annabeth shaped towers out of wet sand, Thalia found the perfect flat stone for the throne room floor, and together they lined the courtyard with shell mosaics.

"You used to do this," Thalia said suddenly, pressing a piece of driftwood into place as a column. "When you were little. Before — before we ran. Mom would be screaming inside and you'd be out on the curb with chalk, drawing cities. I'd sit with you and you'd tell me who lived where."

Annabeth's hands stilled. She could picture it from Thalia's description: a small Annabeth with scraped knees, telling her furious older sister about an invisible city. But the Grace childhood remained closed to her, however familiar chalk and building felt. "Did you like it?" Annabeth asked. "Or did you think cities were traps?"

"I said a lot of dumb stuff when I was angry," Thalia said. She didn't look up, focused on balancing a pebble. "I liked listening, though. You always had a place for me. Biggest house. Best view. Even when I was being a brat."

"Then you get the tower now," Annabeth said, throat tight. "With the lightning rod. So you could yell at the sky up close."

Thalia laughed, short and wet. "Yeah. I did."

They sat shoulder to shoulder, knees touching, and built in silence for a while. Annabeth added tiny staircases with a stick. Thalia guarded the walls from an invading column of ants with great seriousness.

"You know," Thalia said after a while, voice low so only Annabeth heard, "when you told me about the other life — the Athena one — I kept thinking about how much your Thalia missed. Tree. Then Huntress. You growing up while she was somewhere else." She swallowed. "I can't remember that life. I can hate the thought of you being left alone in it."

Annabeth leaned her head against Thalia's shoulder. It was bony and sun-warm and solid. Alive. "You're here now."

"I'm here now," Thalia agreed. She bumped her temple against Annabeth's hair. "So build me a good tower, little sister. I want a balcony."

Annabeth built her a balcony out of two shells and a flat stone, and Thalia declared it structurally perfect, and when a wave from a cannonball — Percy's, enormous and sloppy — lapped up and took out half the outer wall, they both gasped and then laughed until sand stuck to their wet faces.

"Rebuild?" Thalia asked.

"Stronger this time," Annabeth said, already packing sand. "With a moat."

And for the first time in weeks — in years, in lifetimes — the knot between her shoulder blades eased, and she was just a girl building a sand city with her sister while the sun moved slow across the water.

It was Zoe who made it possible, without knowing.

"Everyone out!" she yelled around four o'clock, standing on the dock with a whistle she'd stolen from the lifeguard's chair. "Canoe rotation! Poseidon cabin, you get the leaky green one! Zeus cabin, you get the one with the spiderweb! GO!"

There was grumbling and cheering and a stampede for paddles. Percy hung back, pretending to look for his towel. Annabeth hung back, pretending to fix Thalia's braid. Thalia, who missed nothing, tied off the braid, gave Annabeth a look that said *five minutes, go*, and loudly announced, "I call front of the canoe! If Percy tips us, I'm killing him!"

"Love you too!" Percy called.

In the confusion, with everyone hauling canoes and arguing about life jackets, Percy slipped into the water on the shady side of the dock. Annabeth slipped in thirty seconds later from the other side. They met in the middle, under the planks, where the water was cool and green-dark and the sun came through in thin stripes.

"Hi," Annabeth whispered, treading water, hair floating around her face.

"Hi," Percy whispered back.

Above them: footsteps, thuds, Clarisse yelling about paddles, Ella asking if anyone had seen her book (Nico had it, he was reading ahead). Below: quiet, except for the lap of water on the pilings and a curious minnow that darted between them.

Percy reached out under the water and found her hand. Her fingers were wrinkled from swimming all day. He loved that he knew that — that he got to know the pruny-finger version of her, not just the war-hero version.

"Ohio," Annabeth said solemnly, and then dissolved into silent giggles, hand over her mouth.

"Hey. I was very convincing," Percy protested, pulling her a little closer by the hand. The piling was rough against his shoulder. "Did you see Connor's face when he beat me? He'll tell that story for years. 'I beat Percy Jackson.' Let him have it."

"I know," Annabeth said. She was looking at him the way she did when nobody else was watching — soft and unguarded, seventeen in twelve-year-old eyes. "You're good at that. Letting people have things."

"You let Thalia win at the shell-collecting this morning," Percy pointed out. "Don't think I didn't see. You saw the big one first."

Annabeth shrugged, ripples spreading from her shoulders. "She wanted it for our Olympus. It had a hole all the way through. Perfect for a window."

"How's Olympus?"

"Standing. Mostly." Annabeth smiled. "We built you a temple. Underwater entrance, obviously. Thalia insisted. She said you'd complain otherwise."

"Tell her I love it. Tell her —" Percy hesitated. The water moved between them, cool on his sunburned neck. Under the dock it smelled like wet wood and shade and her sunscreen. "Tell her thanks for giving us five minutes. She's good at that."

"She's good at a lot of things," Annabeth said quietly. "I forgot, for a while. In the other life she was — gone so much. I got used to missing her. I don't want to get used to that again."

Percy squeezed her hand. He knew about missing people who were right in front of you. He'd spent the days before his surface trip missing this exact girl, afraid she wouldn't remember him, and then weeks at camp wishing the three tables between them would disappear. She remembered him. That still felt like the one fixed point in a shifting world.

"We don't have to miss anyone today," he said. "Today's just lake day. No quests. No prophecies. Just — you got water on my sandwich at lunch."

"You put a minnow in my shoe," Annabeth countered.

"Nico did that. I just didn't stop him."

"Liar." But she was smiling, and she drifted closer until their knees bumped under the water and the dock creaked above them as someone — Clarisse, probably — did a running jump that shook the whole structure and rained sand down on their heads.

They both froze, then laughed silently, brushing sand off each other.

"Five minutes is almost up," Annabeth whispered. She didn't move away, though. She reached up and pushed his wet hair off his forehead, fingers lingering. In the striped light her eyes were gray as storm water. "Percy?"

"Yeah?"

"Thanks for losing today. I know it was hard. I saw you at the buoy — you wanted to *go*."

Percy looked down at their joined hands under the surface, her pale fingers laced with his. The water around them hummed faintly, happy to hold them. "It's okay. I get to be fast at home. Here —" He shrugged. "Here I'd rather be the guy who does terrible cannonballs and gets dunked by his sister. That's — that's a good guy to be."

Annabeth's expression did something complicated and tender. "You're both guys," she said. "But today I like this one best. The Ohio one."

"Ohio Percy is my favorite too," Percy said. "He gets to hold your hand under a dock."

Above them Zoe blew the whistle — two sharp blasts. "CANOES IN THE WATER! IF YOU'RE NOT IN A CANOE IN SIXTY SECONDS YOU'RE SWIMMING BEHIND IT!"

"We should go," Annabeth said, but she leaned forward first and bumped her forehead against his, water beading between them. It wasn't a kiss — not quite, not where anyone could see — but it was a promise in their private language: *I'm here, you're here, we're us.*

"See you in the leaky green one," Percy whispered.

"Save me a seat that isn't underwater," Annabeth whispered back, and ducked under, surfacing ten feet away with a splash, already yelling, "Coming! Hold the canoe!"

Percy waited ten seconds — *be discreet, be discreet* — then surfaced on the other side, shaking water out of his ears, just in time to see Annabeth climbing into a canoe with Thalia, both of them sandy and sun-pink and laughing, and Clarisse waving him over with a paddle.

"Move it, Squid!" Clarisse yelled. "You're paddling! Ohio boys paddle!"

Percy laughed and waded out to the canoe and climbed in, rocking it wildly while Silena shrieked and Grover clutched the sides.

As they pushed off, paddle dripping, the sun low and gold over the pines, Percy looked across the water at the other canoe — Annabeth in the back, Thalia in front, Zoe heckling them both — and Annabeth caught his eye and smiled, sand still stuck to her cheek, shell bracelet from their Olympus city tied around her wrist.

 lake day. No villains. No quest. Just sunburn and stolen cheese and a sand palace with a balcony for his sister-in-all-but-blood and a girl who held his hand in the dark under the dock.

Percy dug his paddle in and splashed Clarisse on purpose. She splashed him back. The canoe tipped. Everyone yelled.

Best day ever.
