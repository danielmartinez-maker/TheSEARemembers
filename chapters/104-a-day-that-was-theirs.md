## Chapter 104: A Day That Was Theirs

Annabeth called it a date.

She did it at breakfast, between passing Percy the honey and telling Thalia the Zeus cabin's rain gutter was still leaking.

"We should go somewhere today," she said.

Percy looked up. "Where?"

"Somewhere that isn't camp. Not a quest. Not a patrol. Just a day out."

Thalia lowered her spoon. "You mean like a date?"

Annabeth's cheeks colored. "Yes. A date. If Percy wants."

Percy felt suddenly aware of everyone at the table. Clarisse was watching him with the expression she usually reserved for an opponent who'd just volunteered to spar. Silena looked delighted. Thalia was trying very hard to appear casual.

"I want," he said.

Annabeth smiled. "Good. Chiron said we can go to Montauk for the afternoon if we stay together and keep our armor hidden. We can take the camp van."

"The van?" Clarisse asked. "The one that makes that noise when you turn left?"

"That one."

"Sounds romantic."

"It's a vehicle," Annabeth said. "It doesn't need to be romantic."

Silena sighed. "That is not how dates work."

"I thought dates were about the people, not the vehicle."

"Both can be true," Percy said.

Annabeth pointed at him. "Exactly."

Thalia leaned across the table. "Take the emergency drachmas. And tell Chiron what time you'll be back. And don't go anywhere near the water if the weather turns."

"We're going to the coast," Percy said. "The water is the point."

"Then don't go anywhere near the *dangerous* water if the weather turns."

"Got it."

Clarisse slid a small bronze knife across the table. "Take this too."

Annabeth looked at her. "We don't need a weapon for a date."

"You don't need one until you do. Keep it in your bag."

Percy tucked it away. "Thanks."

"Bring it back sharp."

The entire table watched them go over their preparations: emergency drachmas, water, snacks, a map, a jacket in case it cooled off. The normal things demigods packed before leaving camp, except that this time the plan was to eat fries and look at the ocean.

"You two are making this a bigger deal than it is," Annabeth said.

Silena leaned close. "It's your first Montauk afternoon date here. Of course it's a big deal."

"We've been together for years."

"In the memories you share," Silena said gently. "Here, it's your first date to this beach. Let it be special."

Annabeth went quiet. Percy reached under the table and found her hand.

"It can be both," he said. "We can remember what we had and still make something new."

She squeezed his fingers. "Okay. A date, then."

---

The camp van smelled like pine cleaner, old fries, and the rubber mat that covered the floor. Argus drove, leaving Percy and Annabeth in the back with the map. Annabeth held it in both hands, jaw set with the concentration of a girl who could plan a siege but did not trust the faded road markings.

"You're doing fine," Percy said.

"I know."

"You don't have to grip the map that hard."

"I know."

He let the silence sit. The road curled through trees, then opened to the coast. With each mile the air changed. Salt crept through the vents. The ocean's presence grew stronger in Percy's chest, familiar as a voice calling from another room.

When they reached the public beach, Argus parked between a minivan full of toddlers and a truck with surfboards on the roof. He pointed at the agreed pickup time, then settled into his seat to keep watch while Annabeth sat very still.

"You okay?" Percy asked.

"Yes. I just haven't done this before."

"Navigated with that terrible map?"

"Taken a whole afternoon in Montauk with you here. No quest. Just us." She glanced at him. "I remember learning to love you. It’s strange to have all that history and still be this nervous about an ordinary afternoon."

Percy nodded. "We don't have to do anything the memories say. We can make today ours."

Annabeth's shoulders relaxed. "That's what I want."

They stepped out into the sun.

The beach was busy but not crowded. Children built sandcastles near the waterline. A couple walked a golden retriever. Two teenagers carried a surfboard toward the break. Percy stood for a moment, taking it in: the salt wind, the sound of waves, the ordinary noise of people spending an afternoon together.

In Atlantis, the sea was home. Here, it was something else. Wider. Louder. Full of strangers who didn't know what lived beneath it.

Annabeth watched his face. "You miss it."

"Atlantis?"

"The ocean."

"I do. But this is nice too. Different nice."

They walked along the shore, shoes in hand, letting the water wash over their feet. Annabeth asked him about the palace gardens. He told her about the anemones that changed color when someone lied and the old coral arch where Triton had recently practiced diving with him and told stories of teaching him as a child. She told him about the Athena cabin in the life she remembered, the smell of old books and owl feathers, and how she'd always thought the marble columns were too thin.

"You were planning a renovation?" Percy asked.

"I was planning three. The roofline was inefficient."

"Of course it was."

She nudged him with her shoulder. "You think I'm predictable."

"I think you're consistent. There's a difference."

"And what am I consistent about?"

"Being right about buildings. Being wrong about how much you can carry by yourself. And pretending you don't like strawberry ice cream."

Annabeth stopped. "I do like strawberry ice cream."

"I know."

"How?"

"Because I remember."

Her expression softened, but she didn't let the memory take over the moment. "Then you can buy me some. And I can decide whether I still like it."

They found a small stand near the boardwalk. Percy bought strawberry; Annabeth chose lemon and then asked for a taste of his. They sat on a weathered bench and watched the surfers.

"I don't always know what to do with the old memories," Annabeth said. "They're mine. But the childhood around them belongs to everybody else, and that gets confusing."

"You don't have to decide today."

"I know my feelings are real. I love you. I want to work out how to live with these two histories: the one I remember, and the childhood everyone here knows and I don’t."

Percy let the question stand between them. He wanted to reassure her. He wanted to tell her what he knew. But she was asking him to listen, not to solve it.

"What do you feel when we're here?" he asked.

Annabeth looked at the ocean. "I feel happy. I feel safe. I feel annoyed when you get ice cream on your sleeve. I want to tell you things before I tell anyone else. I want to hold your hand. That seems like enough to hold onto today."

"It is."

"And what do you feel?"

"I feel lucky. I feel nervous that I'll say the wrong thing. I want to keep making you laugh. And I want you to have room to be whoever you're becoming, even if that's different from the person I remember."

Annabeth leaned her head against his shoulder. "That's a good answer."

"I practiced."

"You did not."

"I practiced in my head."

"I believe that."

---

They walked out to the tide pools near the rocky point. Percy showed Annabeth how to spot the little crabs hiding under ledges. She found a tiny sea star and crouched beside it, careful not to touch.

"Does it feel strange that I have to come up for air?" she asked.

"Sometimes. I can stay under, but I like swimming beside you better."

"Could you teach me?"

"To breathe underwater?"

"No, I mean the other parts. How to move through the water without fighting it."

"Sure. The important thing is not to panic."

"Easy to say."

"I can hold your hand."

She looked up. "Would you?"

"Of course."

They found a sheltered cove where the water was calm and no one was swimming. Percy showed her how to float on her back and let the tide lift her. Annabeth was tense at first, her hands gripping his wrists, but gradually her shoulders loosened.

"You're doing it," he said.

"I'm trying not to think about what could be under me."

"There's mostly sand."

"Mostly?"

"A few small fish. One crab with a grudge."

She splashed him. He laughed and pulled her upright before the next wave arrived. They stood waist-deep, hands still joined, both grinning.

"That was fun," she said, sounding surprised.

"I told you."

"Don't get smug."

He brushed a strand of wet hair from her forehead. "Wouldn't dream of it."

For a moment neither moved. The ocean lapped around their legs. Annabeth looked at him, then at their hands, and leaned in to kiss him. It was unhurried and gentle. Percy kissed her back, careful not to pull her closer than she wanted. When they parted, she rested her forehead against his.

"Still good?" he asked.

"Still good."

"Want to keep swimming?"

"A little longer."

They stayed until the afternoon began to cool.

---

On the drive back, with Argus watching the road, Annabeth rested one hand on the seat between them, palm up. Percy set his hand beside it. Their fingers found each other at the next red light.

"So," she said. "Was it a good date?"

"I think so. The van survived. We didn't get attacked. You learned to float."

"And I got strawberry ice cream."

"You did."

"Then yes. It was good."

At the camp gate, Thalia waited with her arms crossed. Clarisse stood beside her holding a watch and pretending she hadn't been checking it every two minutes.

"You're late," Thalia said.

"By four minutes," Annabeth replied.

"Four minutes is late."

Clarisse looked at the watch. "Three minutes and fifty seconds."

"Thank you," Annabeth said.

Thalia's eyes moved to their joined hands. She raised an eyebrow.

"Good date?" she asked.

Annabeth smiled. "Good date."

"Good." Thalia stepped aside. "Dinner's waiting. Silena saved you both a plate."

They walked in together, hands linked between them. Percy didn't hide it. Annabeth didn't let go.

The camp kept moving around them: the clatter of dinner dishes, the call of the harpies, Clarisse arguing with a younger camper about spear safety. Nothing about the world had changed.

They had added another happy day to the relationship they carried with them. The choice was theirs today too.

At the Poseidon table, Silena slid the saved plates toward them. Clarisse stole a piece of Percy’s bread. Thalia sat down across from Annabeth and asked how the water had been.

Annabeth told her all of it.

Percy listened, still holding her hand under the table, and felt that the day had been exactly what it was supposed to be: not a rehearsal for a remembered future, but a day they had made themselves.
