## Chapter 104: A Day That Was Theirs

Annabeth called it a date.

She did it at breakfast, between passing Percy the honey and telling Thalia the Zeus cabin's rain gutter was still leaking.

"We should go somewhere today," she said.

Percy looked up. "Where?"

"Somewhere that isn't camp. Not a quest. Not a patrol. Just a day out."

Thalia lowered her spoon. "You mean like a date?"

Annabeth's cheeks colored. "Yes. A date. If Percy wants."

Percy felt suddenly aware of everyone at the table. Clarisse was watching him with the expression she usually reserved for an opponent who'd just volunteered to spar. Silena looked delighted. Thalia was trying very hard to appear casual.

"I want," he said.

Annabeth smiled. "Good. Chiron said we can go to Montauk for the afternoon if we stay together and keep our armor hidden. We can take the camp van."

"The van?" Clarisse asked. "The one that makes that noise when you turn left?"

"That one."

"Sounds romantic."

"It's a vehicle," Annabeth said. "It doesn't need to be romantic."

Silena sighed. "That is not how dates work."

"I thought dates were about the people, not the vehicle."

"Both can be true," Percy said.

Annabeth pointed at him. "Exactly."

Thalia leaned across the table. "Take the emergency drachmas. And tell Chiron what time you'll be back. And don't go anywhere near the water if the weather turns."

"We're going to the coast," Percy said. "The water is the point."

"Then don't go anywhere near the *dangerous* water if the weather turns."

"Got it."

Clarisse slid a small bronze knife across the table. "Take this too."

Annabeth looked at her. "We don't need a weapon for a date."

"You don't need one until you do. Keep it in your bag."

Percy tucked it away. "Thanks."

"Bring it back sharp."

The entire table watched them go over their preparations: emergency drachmas, water, snacks, a map, a jacket in case it cooled off. The normal things demigods packed before leaving camp, except that this time the plan was to eat fries and look at the ocean.

They did not leave immediately. Annabeth had brought the map, but not the rest of the plan, and Chiron had asked for one before he approved the van. The paper was spread across the table between the honey jar and a plate Clarisse had pushed out of elbow range. Annabeth had drawn a route from camp to the highway, then another line along the coast. There were circles around a public beach, a place to get food, and a shop where they could buy postcards if it was open.

"You made three possible routes," Percy said.

"Two. The third is the first one if the road is closed."

"That sounds like three."

"It isn't three plans. It's one plan with a contingency."

Clarisse leaned over the page. "Why is the beach circled twice?"

"Because there are two entrances. The southern one has a longer walk from the parking area, but it looks less crowded. The northern one is near the public facilities and the food stands."

"So we take the short walk and get fries sooner," Percy said.

Annabeth tapped the map with her pencil. "We decide when we get there. If the beach is crowded, we use the other entrance. If the weather changes, we don't go into the water. If it changes badly, we leave."

"And if it changes a little?" Thalia asked.

Annabeth gave her a look. "Then we look at the sky and ask Argus what he thinks."

"Argus isn't a weather service," Percy said.

"He has eyes all over his head. That's better than a weather service."

"That depends on whether they're all looking at the same cloud," Silena said. She moved the honey jar off the folded route and found the bottom corner, where Annabeth had listed a return time. "You wrote four thirty for pickup?"

"That's the time we leave the beach. Chiron asked for us at the gate before dinner."

"Five thirty at the latest," Thalia said. "The van takes longer if traffic is slow."

"We told him five thirty. Argus said the drive is usually forty-five minutes, but we gave it extra time."

Percy glanced at the little clock face drawn beside the time. "And if we miss it?"

"We call camp from a pay phone and tell Chiron where we are. We don't just decide that five thirty was more of a suggestion."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking it."

"I was thinking that four minutes late was apparently a felony."

Thalia slid the map back to Annabeth. "Four minutes is how a worried person gets to turn a normal outing into a search."

That quieted the table for a beat. Percy had heard Thalia's worry in the sharpness of her words before. He knew better than to treat it as a joke just because Clarisse was there to make one. Annabeth had heard it too. Her thumb pressed the map's edge flat.

"We'll be back by five thirty," she said. "If something changes, we'll call."

Thalia nodded. "Good."

Silena circled the food stop. "You may want actual lunch before you go down to the water. Fries don't count as a complete meal."

"They count as part of a meal," Percy said.

"That's not the same thing."

"It is if there are enough fries."

"What if the stand is closed?" Annabeth asked, pencil ready. "Do we eat before the beach or after?"

"Before," Clarisse said. "Nobody makes good decisions when they're hungry."

"You make terrible decisions when you're hungry," Silena told her.

"I make efficient decisions."

"You once challenged a vending machine because it kept your drachma."

"It gave it back."

"After you kicked it."

"It understood the point."

Percy drew a small square next to the food stop. "We eat first. Then we can decide if we're still hungry enough for fries."

Annabeth looked at him. "You just want a second lunch."

"I don't see why we have to make the plan worse by pretending otherwise."

She marked the square. "Fine. Lunch first, possible fries later."

The practical list kept getting longer. Sunscreen, a towel, a change of socks, a paper bag for anything wet, drachmas in a separate pocket in case the first set got lost. Clarisse wanted Percy to carry her knife in the outside pocket rather than at the bottom of Annabeth's bag. Annabeth said she had already assigned it a place where it would not catch on the map. Clarisse told her that was the first useful thing she had said all morning.

"It was all useful," Annabeth said.

"The part about postcards wasn't."

"Postcards are useful if you want to remember where you went."

"You have a map."

"A map is not the same as a postcard."

"A postcard doesn't show the road."

"It shows what someone thinks the place is worth remembering."

Clarisse took a bite of toast. "Then get the one with the biggest lighthouse."

Annabeth wrote that down, though Percy suspected she was making a note to look for something else.

The plan went with them to the Big House after breakfast. Chiron was in the study with a stack of camp forms and a cup of tea. Argus stood by the door, one hand on the keys. He had agreed to drive before the date was proposed; he had also made it clear by looking at the clock that this did not mean he was their chauffeur for as long as they pleased.

Annabeth handed Chiron the route. "We will stay in the public areas. The beach and boardwalk are within walking distance of one another. We'll keep our phones on, and Argus will stay with the van. We have emergency drachmas and a landline location marked if we need one."

"And the water?" Chiron asked.

"We check the posted conditions and stay in the shallows. We don't swim if the water is rough or if the weather changes. We leave if there's thunder."

Percy added, "Even if it's far away."

Chiron looked at him over his spectacles. "Especially if it is far away. Thunderstorms do not need to be directly overhead to be dangerous."

"I know."

"Then you will not mind hearing it twice."

"I don't."

Chiron turned the map around and checked the return time. "You leave Montauk at four thirty and return here no later than five thirty. If the road delays you, call. Argus knows where to stop for a working phone. You will not split up, and you will not follow anyone away from the public beach."

"Understood," Annabeth said.

"Yes," Percy said.

"The outing is approved. Keep the armor out of sight. Your ordinary clothes are sufficient, and if you find yourselves uncertain about any condition, come back to the van."

Annabeth folded the route and put it away. Chiron's approval did not make the plan feel like a mission. It made it feel more like the kind of ordinary thing that had rules because ordinary things could still go wrong.

At the door, Argus held up the keys. One eye blinked toward the clock. Another looked at Percy.

"We're coming," Percy said.

They went back to Cabin Three to collect the things they had already packed. Clarisse was waiting on the steps. She had moved from breakfast in the same way she moved from a sparring match: apparently without wasting a motion.

"Knife," she said.

Percy opened the bag and showed her where it was.

"Not there. If the bag gets wet, it sits under your towel. Put it in your jacket pocket."

Annabeth frowned. "The knife is wrapped. It won't be damaged."

"I'm not worried about the knife. I'm worried about you digging through a bag when you need it."

"We are not expecting to need it."

"Good. Keep it where you can find it anyway."

Percy moved it to the inner pocket of his jacket. Clarisse checked the clasp with one tug, then let go.

"Bring it back," she said.

"I will."

"And don't get sand in it."

"I'll do my best to protect it from the beach."

"Do that."

Silena appeared behind Clarisse, carrying a paper sack. "I made sandwiches because I know what happens when you two try to live on a bag of chips and one apple."

"We planned to eat lunch," Annabeth said.

"You planned to eat lunch if the place was open and if you weren't already at the beach and if the line wasn't long. That's not lunch. That's a possibility."

"We have a food stop."

"Then you'll have extra food. It's not a problem." Silena gave Percy the sack. "Turkey and cheese, no tomato. Annabeth's is egg salad. There are two apples and the little bag of pretzels Clarisse pretends she doesn't like."

Clarisse took the sack from Percy and peered into it. "Why are they in here?"

"Because you like them."

"I don't."

Silena turned to Percy. "If she doesn't eat hers, you can have it."

"I heard that," Clarisse said.

"That was the point."

Annabeth tucked the food beside the map and the water bottles. The bag was heavier now, but she didn't complain. Percy offered to carry it, and she handed him the towel instead.

"You can have the bag when my shoulder gets tired," she said. "I'm not going to pretend I'm made of glass."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were thinking it."

He paused, then smiled. "I was thinking I could carry the towel."

"That is a very convenient thought."

Thalia walked them to the van. She had dressed for a morning of camp work, boots and an old jacket, and her hair was still damp from the shower. She stood beside the driver's door while Argus opened the back.

"One more thing," she said.

Annabeth waited.

"If you want to come back early, you come back early. You don't have to fill the whole afternoon because we planned a whole afternoon."

"I know."

"Good. And if the water looks wrong, it doesn't matter how long you waited to see it."

"We won't go in if it looks wrong."

Thalia's gaze stayed on Annabeth for a moment, then shifted to Percy. "And if you hear thunder?"

"We leave the beach and tell Argus."

"Good."

"We're not going to swim in a storm," Percy said. "I like the ocean. I don't have to prove it by doing something stupid."

"I didn't say you did."

"Your face did."

"My face has excellent judgment."

Annabeth bumped her shoulder against Thalia's. It was a small contact, not a request for permission or a promise to obey everything. Thalia let it happen without pulling her into a hug.

"We'll call if the plan changes," Annabeth said.

Thalia nodded once. "Have a good date."

"We will."

"And don't let Percy buy every bad postcard he sees."

"I can choose my own postcards," Percy said.

"That is exactly what I'm afraid of."

He climbed into the van with the towel, and Annabeth followed with the food. The door shut. Through the window he saw Silena give Thalia the little wave they used when neither wanted to make a whole goodbye out of it. Clarisse had already gone inside, though she was standing where she could still see the road.

Percy held up the bag with the sandwiches. Silena gave him a thumbs-up.

The van pulled away.

"You two are making this a bigger deal than it is," Annabeth said.

Silena leaned close. "It's your first Montauk afternoon date here. Of course it's a big deal."

"We already know how we feel about each other."

"I know," Silena said gently. "And this is your first Montauk afternoon date here. Let it be special."

Annabeth went quiet. Percy reached under the table and found her hand.

"It can be both," he said. "We can remember what we had and still make something new."

She squeezed his fingers. "Okay. A date, then."

---

The camp van smelled like pine cleaner, old fries, and the rubber mat that covered the floor. Argus drove, leaving Percy and Annabeth in the back with the map. Annabeth held it in both hands, jaw set with the concentration of a girl who could plan a siege but did not trust the faded road markings.

"You're doing fine," Percy said.

"I know."

"You don't have to grip the map that hard."

"I know."

He let the silence sit. The road curled through trees, then opened to the coast. With each mile the air changed. Salt crept through the vents. The ocean's presence grew stronger in Percy's chest, familiar as a voice calling from another room.

When they reached the public beach, Argus parked between a minivan full of toddlers and a truck with surfboards on the roof. He pointed at the agreed pickup time, then settled into his seat to keep watch while Annabeth sat very still.

"You okay?" Percy asked.

"Yes. I just haven't done this before."

"Navigated with that terrible map?"

"Taken a whole afternoon in Montauk with you here. No quest. Just us." She glanced at him. "I remember learning to love you. It’s strange to have all that history and still be this nervous about an ordinary afternoon."

Percy nodded. "We don't have to do anything the memories say. We can make today ours."

Annabeth's shoulders relaxed. "That's what I want."

They stepped out into the sun.

The sand shifted under their shoes as they crossed from the lot to the public path. Annabeth stopped once to check the posted board. The notice listed the tide, a warning about a rip current farther down the beach, and a red flag for the stretch beside the jetty. The water was calm where they stood, but the beach patrol had roped off the northern end.

"There," she said, pointing. "We stay south of the marker."

Percy read the sign. "The flagged water is farther down than the rocks."

"We stay south of the marker," she repeated.

"I heard you."

"I know. I'm making sure I also heard myself."

He had been about to say that he could feel the nearest current and that the water was not pulling hard. The correction reached him before the words did. His senses didn't replace the signs, the rules, or Annabeth's right to decide what felt safe to her. He looked at the flag and then at the wide path leading to the boardwalk.

"South of the marker," he agreed. "And out if the weather changes."

Annabeth nodded. She tucked the folded route into her pocket instead of keeping it open. They had made it to the beach. She didn't need to supervise every step with a map.

They walked toward the boardwalk first. It ran above the sand on weathered planks, the boards worn smooth in the middle by years of shoes and strollers. A bicyclist rang a bell behind them. Percy moved aside, and Annabeth did too, but she stepped onto a loose board that dipped under her heel.

"That one moves," Percy said.

She shifted her weight and tested it once more. "It isn't loose. The support underneath is uneven."

"So it moves."

"It flexes."

"That's a very architectural way of saying it moves."

She crouched to look through the gap between boards. A family came toward them with a stroller, so Percy stepped off to give them room. Annabeth took a second to finish her inspection, then caught up.

"What did you see?" he asked.

"One board is cupped. It channels water to the edge instead of letting it drain between the planks. The support is a little lower on the left. It needs replacing before the wood splits."

"Can you tell all that from one board?"

"You can tell a lot if you look at where the wear is."

He looked back. There was a dark line along the grain and a shallow groove where shoes had passed over it. He would not have noticed either without her pointing. "Do you want to write it down?"

Annabeth touched the pocket with the map. For a moment, he thought she would pull out her pencil. Instead she shook her head. "Someone who works here probably already knows. I don't have to make a record of every thing I notice."

"That's new."

"It isn't new. I'm practicing."

They went on. At the end of the boardwalk, a row of small shops and food windows opened toward the street. There were saltwater taffy boxes in the windows, postcards, cheap sunglasses, little lighthouse figures and beach towels printed with cartoon dolphins. The air smelled of oil from a fryer and sugar from a candy shop. Percy looked at the menu board outside a sandwich place while Annabeth looked at the postcard rack.

"Lunch before fries," she said. "This place has sandwiches and soup."

"Soup at the beach seems like a mistake."

"They also have sandwiches."

"I just wanted to be sure the soup wasn't the whole plan."

She read the menu, taking in the prices and the small sign about the daily special. Percy watched a gull land on the railing, try to take a paper napkin, and give up when it caught on a bolt.

"What do you want?" Annabeth asked.

"Turkey sandwich. Maybe chips."

"You have a turkey sandwich in the bag."

"That's a later turkey sandwich."

"You could have the sandwich now and save the other one."

"That's a reasonable plan."

"And then you can get fries later if you're still hungry."

"You make it sound like I have to earn them."

"You have to eat something that isn't fried. Silena packed us lunch. It would be rude to bring it home untouched."

That was true, and they both knew it. They found a picnic table behind the shop, on a strip of grass between the boardwalk and a row of low dunes. Annabeth set the bag down in the shade. Percy took out the sandwiches, then carefully checked that the wrapped knife stayed tucked in the inside pocket of his jacket and not under the food. The apples had shifted to one side. Nothing was bruised.

"Silena would be pleased," he said.

"Silena is pleased when people eat."

"Not everybody. Clarisse threatened me when I took one of her fries last week."

"She threatened to break your fingers. She didn't say you couldn't eat the fries."

"That distinction seems important."

They ate with their elbows off the table because a family with two small children was using the next one and the children kept watching them. Annabeth opened her sandwich and removed a piece of lettuce, laying it on the wrapper. Percy ate his before the wind could blow the napkin away. The bread was fresh but thick, and the cheese stuck to the roof of his mouth.

"Silena made this?" he asked.

"She packed it. She didn't bake the bread."

"I know. I'm saying it's good."

"You can tell her that when we get back."

"I'm going to tell her now." He pulled out the paper from his pocket and wrote a quick note with the pencil Annabeth had lent him. The words leaned downhill on the page. He folded it around a drachma, which he knew she would not need, and tucked it back into the bag for later.

Annabeth looked at his handwriting. "That says 'sandwich good.'"

"It gets the point across."

"You could have written 'thank you.'"

"She knows I'm thankful."

"That doesn't mean she doesn't like hearing it."

He considered the note. He could already imagine Silena laughing at his handwriting and setting the drachma aside for Clarisse to steal. He added, beneath the first two words, *Thank you.*

"There," he said.

"Much better."

"You sound like a teacher."

"I learned from one."

After lunch, Annabeth wanted to see the postcard rack. They picked through the cards together. Some showed the lighthouse, some showed a sunset too orange to be real, and one had a cartoon clam wearing sunglasses. Percy held up the clam.

"This is clearly the best one."

"It's a clam with sunglasses."

"Exactly. It understands the assignment."

"The assignment is to remember the place."

"I'll remember the clam."

Annabeth picked out a card with a simple drawing of the harbor and a fishing boat. She examined the print quality, the border, and the paper stock. Percy waited for her to say whether she liked it. She turned it over and read the blank lines.

"You want to send it to someone?" he asked.

"I don't know. Maybe Thalia. She'll say the harbor is not as good as ours."

"You could write that we had a good day."

"She already knows we're having a good day."

"She knows we left. She doesn't know what we found."

Annabeth took a small breath. She had wanted a day away from camp, but she still wanted to bring a piece of it back to her sister. That didn't seem like a contradiction. "Maybe I'll get two. One for her and one for the cabin."

"The cabin?"

"The one at camp. We can put it on the wall."

"That's allowed?"

"I don't know. We can ask."

They bought two harbor cards and one clam card. The clerk slid them into a paper sleeve. Percy paid with a drachma, and the clerk asked if it was a souvenir token. Annabeth said yes before Percy could explain. They walked away, and he looked at her.

"I thought we were using emergency drachmas for emergencies."

"That was a normal drachma. I used the change from the bus stop."

"We didn't take a bus."

"The stop is marked on the route. I picked up the change from the bench when we sat down."

He glanced at her. "You found money just sitting there?"

"Three quarters. I asked the woman next to the bench if they were hers. They weren't."

"And you didn't tell me?"

"You were choosing a clam postcard."

"Important work."

"I didn't want to interrupt."

He laughed. They slipped the cards into the safest pocket of the bag and took the long path back toward the beach.

At the corner, a rack of cheap sunglasses turned slowly in the wind. Percy stopped to look at a pair with blue lenses. Annabeth checked the price tag, then gave him a look.

"I don't need them," he said.

"I didn't say you couldn't get them."

"You did the face."

"I was trying to see if they were polarized."

He picked them up. They were scratched at one temple, and the hinge had a pale mark where the paint had come off. He put them back. "They're not worth it."

"They might still work."

"I already have sunglasses."

"Then why were you looking?"

"They were blue."

"That's fair."

They kept walking. He didn't feel embarrassed that he had wanted something and then changed his mind. The afternoon had room for small choices without turning each one into evidence that either of them had changed.

The beach was busy but not crowded. Children built sandcastles near the waterline. A couple walked a golden retriever. Two teenagers carried a surfboard toward the break. Percy stood for a moment, taking it in: the salt wind, the sound of waves, the ordinary noise of people spending an afternoon together.

In Atlantis, the sea was home. Here, it was something else. Wider. Louder. Full of strangers who didn't know what lived beneath it.

Annabeth watched his face. "You miss it."

"Atlantis?"

"The ocean."

"I do. But this is nice too. Different nice."

They walked along the shore, shoes in hand, letting the water wash over their feet. Annabeth asked him about the palace gardens. He told her about the anemones that changed color when someone lied and the old coral arch where Triton had recently practiced diving with him and told stories of teaching him as a child. She told him about the Athena cabin in the life she remembered, the smell of old books and owl feathers, and how she'd always thought the marble columns were too thin.

He had left out the parts he couldn't be sure of. Triton had told him stories about being small, about the first time Percy tried to swim past the boundary buoys and came back with a shell stuck to his hair. Percy had no image of it, only Triton's amusement and the sound of his voice on the last visit. He could repeat the story, but he couldn't claim it as a memory.

"You said the arch was where Triton taught you to dive," Annabeth said. "Was that your memory, or something he told you?"

Percy slowed. It wasn't an accusation. She was trying to keep the kinds of knowing straight, same as they both did when the edges blurred.

"He told me. I remember the last time he talked about it, not the first time I tried."

"What was it like, hearing a story about yourself that you couldn't remember?"

"Weird. He said I used to kick too hard and splash him in the face. I believe him. It sounds like something I would do." He kicked a little spray of water toward her. "But I don't have the picture in my head."

"Does he know that?"

"No. I haven't told him. He just thinks I don't remember all the details because I was little."

Annabeth nodded. She didn't ask whether Percy planned to tell him. There were reasons not to; there were reasons he might, someday. The beach offered them no answer, and neither demanded one.

"I don't remember the Athena cabin the way I remember our old one," she said. "I know what it was like because I lived there. Thalia has told me little things. I remember the other cabin more clearly. The one that isn't here anymore."

"We can make the place here different."

"It is different. It's mine now, and I'm not going to make it into a copy of something I lost."

"Good. I was going to say the old one had a terrible roof."

"It had an excellent roof."

"It leaked."

"The storm was unusually bad."

"It leaked during a regular rain."

"You never checked the gutter."

"I was busy trying not to fall off the roof."

"And that is why I said the roofline needed work."

He looked at her. "You remember a lot about buildings."

"I remember things that mattered to me."

The directness of it made him glance down at their feet. The water was cold on his ankles, warmer where the sun reached it. He could feel a pull out beyond the roped area, slight but steady. The tide was turning. He kept to the dry side of the damp sand and let the waves come to them.

They passed a couple setting up a folding chair. Annabeth stepped around a little boy's trench in the sand. The boy was building a channel from his castle to the water, but every small wave filled it faster than he could scoop it back out.

"You need a wall on that side," Annabeth told him.

His mother looked up. "He'll figure it out."

"I know," Annabeth said quickly. "Sorry."

They walked on. Percy didn't tease her for intervening. She had noticed a problem and wanted to offer a fix. The mother hadn't been angry, just protective of the boy's chance to work it out. Annabeth had accepted the boundary and let them continue.

"You were right about the wall," he said.

"That doesn't mean I needed to say it."

"I don't think you did anything bad."

"I know. I still don't want to assume people want my help because I see a way to improve something."

"You ask me all the time if I want your help."

"You're different."

"Because you know me?"

"Because you know what I mean when I say no. And because you usually need help finding the thing you just dropped."

"That's a separate skill."

"A very important one."

At the next break in the dune line, they stopped to put on sandals. Annabeth brushed sand from the straps before putting her shoes on. Percy shook his feet, trying to get the grains from between his toes. He had one stubborn grain stuck under his heel. He hopped once, lost his balance and caught himself against the rail.

Annabeth waited. "Are you all right?"

"Yes. The sand attacked."

"Did you win?"

"I think it retreated."

"Then we'll call it a draw."

They went on toward the stand. The sun made the windows gleam, and the ice cream sign had been turned around to face the boardwalk. There were no lines at the moment. Percy looked at the board and then at Annabeth.

"Strawberry?" he asked.

"Lemon."

"You said you wanted to try the strawberry."

"I said I wanted to decide whether I still like it. I can do that and choose lemon."

"Good point."

The woman at the counter gave them paper cups. Annabeth asked for one scoop, then asked if they had a small spoon for tasting. She tried lemon first, then leaned over to take a careful bite from Percy's cup. The strawberry was sweeter than she expected. She made a considering face, which Percy knew was different from a disapproving one.

"Still like it?" he asked.

"I like it. I also like the lemon."

"That's allowed."

"I know. I don't have to pick one for the rest of my life."

"I wouldn't make you."

She set her cup in the shade on the bench before the melting edges ran down her fingers. The bench faced away from the main boardwalk and toward the beach. A pair of gulls argued over a piece of cracker near the trash can. One gave up and flew to the roof. The other carried its prize to the sand and dropped it.

"They're bad at sharing," Percy said.

"They don't need to share. They need to stop fighting over one cracker when there are crumbs everywhere."

"That sounds like advice for the whole camp."

"The whole camp is bad at sharing."

"So are you."

"I share my books."

"You make people ask first."

"That's reasonable."

"You make me ask first."

"You keep borrowing my pencils and returning them without erasers."

"I don't know where the erasers go."

"They don't walk away."

"You can't prove that."

Her laugh was small but real. She finished her lemon ice cream, then ate a spoonful of strawberry from his cup without asking. He nudged the cup closer to her. She took another small bite and let him have the last of hers.

"I liked the strawberry more when I was little," she said.

"The other little or this little?"

She paused. "The one I remember being."

"I remember you liking it then. I don't know if you like it now."

"Now I do. Not as much."

"That's all right."

"It's strange how you can remember a version of something and still have to check it."

"I had to check if you still liked my jokes."

"I don't."

"You laughed at one this morning."

"I was surprised."

"That counts."

"It doesn't."

He let the argument end there. They didn't have to turn every memory into a decision about what it meant. Annabeth could like strawberry ice cream a little less than she used to. She could like him now, in this body, with the people who had made her current life. Both things could remain true without anyone forcing them into one story.

They took the paper cups to the trash and checked the time. They had more than two hours before they had to leave. The sky remained clear and the wind had picked up, but the beach flags stayed green at their end. Annabeth checked the weather notice once more, not because she expected it to change but because that was the agreement. Percy waited without telling her it was fine.

"Still green," she said.

"Good."

"We can walk to the rocks for a while. We stay on the public trail and don't climb over the barrier."

"We stay on the public trail."

"If there's a posted closure, we turn back."

"Yes."

"You don't have to repeat everything I say."

"I thought it was our plan."

"It is. I'm just making sure it stays one."

He bumped his shoulder against hers, gentle enough that she could ignore it if she wanted. She leaned back into him once before they started walking again.

"You were planning a renovation?" Percy asked.

"I was planning three. The roofline was inefficient."

"Of course it was."

She nudged him with her shoulder. "You think I'm predictable."

"I think you're consistent. There's a difference."

"And what am I consistent about?"

"Being right about buildings. Being wrong about how much you can carry by yourself. And pretending you don't like strawberry ice cream."

Annabeth stopped. "I do like strawberry ice cream."

"I know."

"How?"

"Because I remember."

Her expression softened, but she didn't let the memory take over the moment. "Then you can buy me some. And I can decide whether I still like it."

They found a small stand near the boardwalk. Percy bought strawberry; Annabeth chose lemon and then asked for a taste of his. They sat on a weathered bench and watched the surfers.

"I don't always know what to do with the old memories," Annabeth said. "They're mine. But the childhood around them belongs to everybody else, and that gets confusing."

"You don't have to decide today."

"I know my feelings are real. I love you. I want to work out how to live with these two histories: the one I remember, and the childhood everyone here knows and I don’t."

Percy let the question stand between them. He wanted to reassure her. He wanted to tell her what he knew. But she was asking him to listen, not to solve it.

"What do you feel when we're here?" he asked.

Annabeth looked at the ocean. "I feel happy. I feel safe. I feel annoyed when you get ice cream on your sleeve. I want to tell you things before I tell anyone else. I want to hold your hand. That seems like enough to hold onto today."

"It is."

"And what do you feel?"

"I feel lucky. I feel nervous that I'll say the wrong thing. I want to keep making you laugh. And I want you to have room to be whoever you're becoming, even if that's different from the person I remember."

Annabeth leaned her head against his shoulder. "That's a good answer."

"I practiced."

"You did not."

"I practiced in my head."

"I believe that."

---

They walked out to the tide pools near the rocky point. Percy showed Annabeth how to spot the little crabs hiding under ledges. She found a tiny sea star and crouched beside it, careful not to touch.

"Does it feel strange that I have to come up for air?" she asked.

"Sometimes. I can stay under, but I like swimming beside you better."

"Could you teach me?"

"To breathe underwater?"

"No, I mean the other parts. How to move through the water without fighting it."

"Sure. The important thing is not to panic."

"Easy to say."

"I can hold your hand."

She looked up. "Would you?"

"Of course."

They found a sheltered cove where the water was calm and no one was swimming. Percy showed her how to float on her back and let the tide lift her. Annabeth was tense at first, her hands gripping his wrists, but gradually her shoulders loosened.

"You're doing it," he said.

"I'm trying not to think about what could be under me."

"There's mostly sand."

"Mostly?"

"A few small fish. One crab with a grudge."

She splashed him. He laughed and pulled her upright before the next wave arrived. They stood waist-deep, hands still joined, both grinning.

"That was fun," she said, sounding surprised.

"I told you."

"Don't get smug."

He brushed a strand of wet hair from her forehead. "Wouldn't dream of it."

For a moment neither moved. The ocean lapped around their legs. Annabeth looked at him, then at their hands, and leaned in to kiss him. It was unhurried and gentle. Percy kissed her back, careful not to pull her closer than she wanted. When they parted, she rested her forehead against his.

"Still good?" he asked.

"Still good."

"Want to keep swimming?"

"A little longer."

They stayed until the afternoon began to cool.

They did not leave the cove right away. Annabeth wanted to get her feet dry before putting her sandals back on, and Percy had discovered that the inside of one shoe had a seam that rubbed against his heel. They sat on a flat stone above the high-water line, shoes between them, towels across their knees. The tide had drawn back from the sand, leaving the shallow pools in the rocks connected by thin channels.

Annabeth watched one channel fill, drain, then fill again. "The water is doing more work than the rock."

"You say that like it's a competition."

"It is. The rock has been here longer. The water still gets to choose where it goes."

"The rock doesn't get a vote?"

"The rock gets time."

He followed the water from one pool into another. It moved around a small patch of weed and found a lower crack in the stone. The pattern changed with each wave, but not in any way he could predict from a single glance. He thought of the tide tables Annabeth had checked, of the posted current warning and the flag. A little knowledge made the water legible; not enough to make it simple.

"You could make a map of it," he said.

"It would be wrong in ten minutes."

"You could make another one."

"I could. But maybe it doesn't need to stay put to be worth drawing."

She pulled the harbor postcard from its sleeve and opened the flap. The card had a ruled address side. She had bought a pencil from the shop, small and blunt, and now she sketched the cove's outline in the blank space, adding the barriers, the trail, the shallow water, and the marker beyond which they had not gone. It wasn't a map someone else could use. It was a record of this point in the afternoon.

"Are you sure you want to write on it?" Percy asked.

"It's ours."

"It could still be a postcard."

"If I send it, I'll copy the picture onto the other one."

She left a space at the bottom and wrote the time. The pencil barely marked the glossy surface, so she pressed harder and made the line darker. Percy watched her take the cove apart into shapes and distances. He noticed that she didn't draw the little sea star she'd seen earlier. She had looked at it, then let it stay where it was.

"That part isn't for the map?" he asked.

"I don't know what it is. We can leave it alone."

"You don't want to know?"

"I want to know. I don't want to pick it up because I want to know."

He looked back at the pool. The sea star had shifted under a ripple, its small arms settled against the rock. He knew it was not in danger. The temptation to touch it was his own curiosity, not a need. He kept his hands in his lap.

"That's probably better," he said.

Annabeth finished the sketch and laid the pencil across the card. "I like that we can come somewhere and not have to collect it."

"We could take a shell from the sand. There are a million."

"We could. But I'd rather leave it here."

"Okay."

The answer came out easy. She looked at him, as if expecting a joke or a complaint. When neither arrived, her shoulders loosened.

"You don't mind?" she asked.

"No. I got to see it. I don't need to own it."

"You wanted to bring one home this morning."

"I wanted to find a shell. I didn't say I had to keep it."

"You said you were going to put it in the Poseidon cabin."

"I could have changed my mind."

"You could have."

It was a small thing, but she seemed to be testing whether his agreement meant he was following her or simply didn't care. He cared. He liked finding things and showing them to her. He also liked that she could refuse to take one home without making that refusal about him.

She folded the postcard carefully along its sleeve and put it away. Percy pulled the towel over his feet and rubbed the water away. The sun was lower but not low enough to make the beach cold. He could hear people on the main stretch, their voices rising whenever a wave covered the sand.

"Do you want to go back to the boardwalk?" he asked. "We have time for one more thing."

"What did you want to do?"

"I don't know yet. That's why I asked."

She thought about it. "We could walk to the harbor. I want to see if the public path goes all the way around."

"The map says it does."

"The map is old."

"Then we find out."

They packed the towel and the empty water bottles. Percy offered his shoulder under the bag. Annabeth passed him the snacks and kept the map. They followed the public trail past the picnic area, where a man was trying to unfold a beach umbrella and the wind kept snapping it back into its closed position. He had one foot on the base and one hand on the runner, but it wouldn't lock.

Annabeth slowed. "Do you want help?" she asked him.

The man looked at her, then at the umbrella. "If you know how to make this thing work."

"I can try."

Percy set down the bag so he could hold the base steady. Annabeth examined the runner, turned the pole a few degrees and found that the locking pin had caught on the fabric. She moved the cloth clear and asked Percy to keep the base from tipping. The man pushed the runner up. It clicked into place.

"There," Annabeth said. "It needs a little room around the hinge."

"Thanks. I thought it was broken."

"It might be, eventually. But not yet."

The man laughed and thanked them again. Percy picked up the bag. They continued along the trail.

"You asked first," he said.

"I did."

"And he wanted help."

"He did."

"Good outcome."

"It was an umbrella."

"Good outcome for the umbrella, then."

She shook her head, though she was smiling. "You're impossible to impress."

"I was impressed. I just can't tell if you want me to say it."

"You can say it when you want to. You don't have to turn it into a question first."

He thought about that. He had been trying to give her room, which could turn into making her do the work of prompting him. "You're good at seeing where things are stuck."

"Thank you."

"And you fixed the umbrella."

"I didn't fix it. I moved the fabric."

"You made it work."

"That's not the same as fixing it."

"You can decide that."

They reached a small lookout where the trail ended at a rail above the harbor. It was less crowded than the beach. Boats moved in and out of the channel; a few were tied along the dock, rocking gently against their lines. Annabeth took the postcard out again, comparing the printed harbor to the one in front of her.

"The boat on the card isn't there anymore," she said.

"It could come back."

"Or it could be gone. The picture is still accurate about the shape of the harbor."

"So it's like the map."

"Not exactly. It shows one day."

"This one?"

She looked at the card, then at the water. "Maybe."

Percy stood beside her at the rail. He had seen harbors before, but not this one. The boats made little sounds against their moorings, and a gull rode the wind without flapping. He wondered what the underwater part looked like, where the bottom dropped away and where the boats' shadows ended. He didn't dive or reach for it. He was here with Annabeth, on the path, with a return time and a driver waiting at the van. The water could be familiar without inviting him to leave the place where she was.

"Do you miss swimming?" she asked.

"I can swim later."

"You always say that."

"It's usually true."

"And if it isn't?"

He didn't know how to answer without pretending he could calculate the future. "Then I'll miss it. I can be homesick for something and still be glad I'm here."

Annabeth rested her forearms on the rail. "I think that's what I wanted today. To do something we remember doing together, but not make it a test of whether we still feel the same."

"We don't have to prove it."

"I know that. I also know things can be real and still change."

"They can."

"I love you," she said. "I don't need to say it because I think the old memories require it. I want you to know it's a thing I mean now."

He felt the words land without the urgency he had expected, a fact offered to him rather than a promise meant to protect them both from uncertainty. He turned his hand palm-up on the rail. She slipped her fingers through his.

"I love you too," he said. "I don't need you to keep saying it so I can believe you."

"Good. Because I'm not going to."

"That was the one time?"

"For today."

He looked at her. She was teasing, but not hiding behind it. "Then I'll take it."

They stayed until the wind brought the smell of rain from somewhere inland. It wasn't thunder. The sky above the water was still bright, but a thin layer of cloud had moved over the sun. Annabeth checked the time, then the beach notice she had copied onto the folded route. The flag remained green; the notice showed no change. She looked to the west, where the clouds were darker than they had been before.

"We should go back toward the main beach," she said. "Not because we have to leave. I want to be closer to Argus and the posted warning."

"Okay."

"If we hear thunder, we leave."

"Yes."

"If the flag changes, we leave."

"Yes."

"And we don't go in again."

"We don't go in again."

She gave him a searching look. "Are you disappointed?"

"No. We got to swim."

"We didn't swim very long."

"It was enough."

"You don't have to say that to make me feel better."

"I'm not. I'm saying we did what we came to do and stopped when we meant to."

She studied him, then closed the postcard sleeve. "All right. Let's go back."

They returned along the path. The clouds stayed beyond the harbor, and there was no thunder. At the main beach, the flag was still green. The water had not changed in any way they could see, but the sun was behind cloud now, and a breeze pushed sand in low sheets over the path. They didn't go back into the cove.

Instead, they sat on the bench above the sand and shared the pretzels from Silena's bag. Annabeth poured some into Percy's hand and kept the rest in the paper sleeve. They watched a couple try to teach a dog to fetch a floating ball. The dog ran out, barked at the ball, and swam back without it. Its owners laughed and tried again.

"That's a bad dog," Percy said.

"It's a good dog. It just doesn't understand the game."

"The game is bring the ball back."

"The game is what they think it's doing. The dog is swimming. That's probably enough."

"I'd bring the ball back."

"You'd steal it."

"I might keep it for a minute."

"You'd hide it under a rock."

"Now you're making things up."

"Am I?"

He could not say for certain that she was wrong. They ate the last pretzels and found a trash can before the wind could take the bag. Annabeth checked her watch. They had enough time for one more pass through the shops and a stop for fries, then they needed to be at the van by four thirty.

"Fries?" she asked.

"Definitely."

"Then we should decide which place."

They chose the stand nearest the boardwalk. The line was short. Percy ordered a small portion to share, and Annabeth asked for two paper napkins. The fries were hot enough that they waited before picking them up. Percy had to blow on one and still burned the tip of his tongue.

"You could have waited," Annabeth said.

"I thought it had cooled."

"It was steaming."

"I thought it was dramatic steam."

She handed him her drink. "Here."

He took a sip. It was lemonade. He returned it before he could drink more, and she saw the brief surprise on his face.

"You remembered I like it," she said.

"I remember you liked it."

"And you checked."

"I asked the guy what was in it."

"That's not what I meant."

He looked at the cup. "Do you still like it?"

"Yes."

"Then I remembered right."

"You remembered a fact. You didn't decide what it meant for me."

"Okay."

She stole one of his fries. "You can remember things and still let me tell you what I want."

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm saying it because I like when you get it right."

He handed her another fry. "Then I'll try to keep getting it right."

"You don't have to be perfect."

"Good. I'm not."

"I noticed."

The fries were salty, and the paper tray softened at the bottom. They ate slowly because neither wanted to burn their mouth again. When they finished, Annabeth folded the tray into itself and found a bin. The clouds moved off the sun. The air remained cool, but the ocean was calm, and the weather notice still held.

They bought the lighthouse postcard Clarisse had suggested, though Annabeth selected one that showed the actual lighthouse rather than a cartoon. They got the clam card too. Percy wrote his name on its back, then stopped before adding anything else.

"What?" Annabeth asked.

"I don't know what to write."

"You could write the date."

"We don't know the date."

"The day of the week, then."

"We don't know the day of the week?"

She narrowed her eyes. "Do you?"

He tried to remember what Thalia had said at breakfast, but the answer had not mattered then. He hadn't checked. "No."

"Then leave it blank. We can write 'Montauk, with you.'"

"That sounds like a title."

"It is a note to ourselves."

He wrote the words. His handwriting slanted downhill again. Annabeth corrected the first letter, then gave him the card back. "That's readable."

"High praise."

"I didn't say it was good."

He tucked it away with the harbor card. They walked back to the van at the agreed time. Argus was already waiting, one hand on the driver's door. He checked them in the mirror before they climbed in, then pointed to the clock and held up four fingers.

"Four thirty," Annabeth said. "We're on time."

Argus nodded. The doors shut, and the van pulled away from the beach.

Percy checked the bag once more. The folded map was dry, the sandwiches were gone, and the postcards had no bent corners. Annabeth leaned her head back against the seat and closed her eyes. She was tired, but not worried. He let the road carry them toward camp and kept the harbor card safe between his hands.

---

On the drive back, with Argus watching the road, Annabeth rested one hand on the seat between them, palm up. Percy set his hand beside it. Their fingers found each other at the next red light.

The van smelled more strongly of sand now. It had worked into the floor mat and the seam of Percy's shorts. The paper bag with the postcards rested between his shoes, protected from the damp towel. Annabeth had the map folded on her lap, not open, one corner tucked under her hand so it would not slide when Argus turned.

"You don't have to keep that closed," Percy said.

"I know."

"You can look at it if you want."

"I know."

He watched the side of her face. "You're doing it again."

"Doing what?"

"Making sure you know you can do something."

She looked down at the map. "I wanted to keep it in my bag when we got to the beach. I thought I'd feel better if I didn't have to check every turn. Then I wanted it at the harbor because I wanted to compare the card. Now I don't want to look at it."

"Okay."

"That doesn't mean I should never bring a map."

"I didn't say that."

"You might think that."

"I think you're good at maps. I don't think they have to be open all the time."

She rubbed the folded edge with her thumb. "I was worried this morning that the day would feel too planned. I made a route, I listed the places, and then I kept adding conditions. I didn't want to make it impossible to be spontaneous."

"We changed the plan a few times."

"We did."

"We went to the harbor because you wanted to see it. We stopped for the umbrella. We skipped the water when the clouds came over. We ate fries before we went back."

"That all fits on a map."

"Not the umbrella."

"I could draw an umbrella."

"You could."

"I could add an annotation."

"You don't have to."

"I know."

She looked at him this time. "I liked today because you didn't keep trying to turn everything into proof."

"Proof of what?"

"That I like you. That we still want the same things. That the old life is still here. You let the day just be a day."

"I wanted that too."

"The first time we went to the beach in the other life, you tried to race me into the water."

"I did not."

"You absolutely did."

"You started running first."

"Because you said you were faster."

"I was faster."

"You slipped."

"The sand was uneven."

"You fell in a puddle."

"It was a very large puddle."

Her laugh was quiet, and this time he knew it didn't matter which history had supplied the first version of the joke. They were laughing at it now. She remembered that other beach. He remembered her remembering it. They also had wet shoes, a clam postcard, and a camp return time. The present did not have to erase the past to make a new memory.

"I'm glad we did it here," he said.

"Me too."

"Even though we already had a first beach day."

"That was a different beach day."

"Right. This one had the bad map, the umbrella and the possibly hostile crab."

"You did not meet the crab."

"I sensed hostility."

"You sensed a crab."

"A crab with intent."

"That's not a scientific conclusion."

"You weren't there."

"I was standing right next to you."

The road curved. Annabeth put her palm on the seat for balance but did not open the map. Percy kept his fingers under hers. Argus glanced at them in the rearview mirror, then returned his attention to the road. One of his eyes looked at the clock, and another watched a truck coming the other way.

"Did you have fun?" Percy asked.

"Yes."

"The whole time?"

"Not every minute. My shoes hurt after we crossed the boardwalk. The lemonade was too sweet. The cove was colder than I expected."

"Those sound like good parts of the review."

"They are facts. The day doesn't have to be perfect to be good."

"I agree."

"What was your least favorite part?"

"The sand attack."

"That was your fault."

"The sand was in my shoe."

"You could have taken the shoe off."

"It was busy."

"It was a beach. People take off their shoes at beaches."

"I didn't want to make a scene."

"You hopped into a rail."

"I didn't hit it."

"You grabbed it."

"That was a controlled maneuver."

"Of course."

He squeezed her hand, and she squeezed back. The red light ahead changed. Argus eased the van forward.

"When we get back, do you want to show Thalia the postcard?" he asked.

"I'll give it to her after dinner. If I hand it to her now, she'll ask me about every detail before I've had a chance to take my shoes off."

"She'll ask anyway."

"Yes, but she can wait until I sit down."

"What about the camp one?"

"I'll ask Chiron if we can pin it on the board. If he says no, I'll keep it in my notebook."

"And the clam?"

"You can have the clam."

"I thought it was ours."

"It is. You can carry it."

He was pleased by the distinction. "I'll protect it."

"From what?"

"The crab."

She sighed, but kept hold of his hand.

"So," she said. "Was it a good date?"

"I think so. The van survived. We didn't get attacked. You learned to float."

"And I got strawberry ice cream."

"You did."

"Then yes. It was good."

At the camp gate, Thalia waited with her arms crossed. Clarisse stood beside her holding a watch and pretending she hadn't been checking it every two minutes.

"You're late," Thalia said.

"By four minutes," Annabeth replied.

"Four minutes is late."

Clarisse looked at the watch. "Three minutes and fifty seconds."

"Thank you," Annabeth said.

Thalia's eyes moved to their joined hands. She raised an eyebrow.

"Good date?" she asked.

Annabeth smiled. "Good date."

"Good." Thalia stepped aside. "Dinner's waiting. Silena saved you both a plate."

They walked in together, hands linked between them. Percy didn't hide it. Annabeth didn't let go.

The camp kept moving around them: the clatter of dinner dishes, the call of the harpies, Clarisse arguing with a younger camper about spear safety. Nothing about the world had changed.

They had added another happy day to the relationship they carried with them. The choice was theirs today too.

At the Poseidon table, Silena slid the saved plates toward them. Clarisse stole a piece of Percy’s bread. Thalia sat down across from Annabeth and asked how the water had been.

Annabeth told her all of it.

Percy listened, still holding her hand under the table, and felt that the day had been exactly what it was supposed to be: not a rehearsal for a remembered future, but a day they had made themselves.
