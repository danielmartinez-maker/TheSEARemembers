## Chapter 139: Storm Season

Percy Jackson woke to the sound of the sea arguing with the sky.

It was not a loud sound, not yet. It was the low, restless version, the one he felt in his back teeth before he heard it with his ears. Rain tapped the shell windows of Cabin Three in uneven bursts. Wind moved over the roof in long pulls. Somewhere far out over Long Island Sound, thunder cleared its throat and decided to wait.

Percy lay in his bunk for a minute with his eyes open, listening. The fountain in the corner trickled a little faster than usual, as if it had heard the weather too. Across the room Clarisse snored once, turned over, and went quiet. Silena's bunk was already empty, her blanket folded with navy precision, her seaweed ribbon gone from the pillow where she always left it.

Summer storm. A big one.

He knew it the way he always knew water weather, by pressure and tug and taste. The air in the cabin smelled faintly metallic, ozone leaking in around the doorframe. His hair lifted a little when he sat up, static crawling along his arms. Salt and electricity, mixed. Storm and Sea in the same breath.

By the time he had pulled on jeans and a camp shirt and shoved his feet into sneakers without tying them, Silena pushed back through the door with damp cheeks and bright eyes, carrying three mugs.

"You're awake," she said, pleased. "Good. I made extra."

"You're psychic," Percy said, taking one. Hot chocolate. Even in summer, even with the cabin already warm. Bad feeling equals hot chocolate first, questions later. Silena said it had been their rule since they were small enough to spill it. Percy had learned it again here.

"I'm eldest," Silena said, as if that explained meteorology. She handed the second mug to Clarisse, who sat up scowling with her dark hair stuck flat to one cheek. "Drink. Chiron Iris-messaged. Drill at ten on the north shore. Zeus and Poseidon cabins together."

Clarisse took the mug, drank half of it in one go, and wiped her mouth. "Drill? In this?"

"Because of this," Silena said. "A summer storm is tracking right over camp by noon. Chiron wants a practice before it hits. Controlled. No monsters, no border crisis. Just us."

Percy felt his stomach do two things at once, excitement and memory. Spring last year, the reservoir. Summer last year, the real storm that had tried to tear the borders open from both sides. He could still feel that river of floodwater under his hands, still hear Thalia laughing wild in the rain with her spear smoking.

Clarisse was thinking the same thing. She swung her legs over the bunk and looked at Percy over her mug.

"Storm and Sea weather," she said.

"Storm and Sea weather," Percy agreed.

Silena smiled into her chocolate. "Thalia and Annabeth are already up. Annabeth left a note on the door. She says bring water shoes and don't eat a big breakfast if you get dizzy holding current."

"When do I ever get dizzy holding current?" Clarisse demanded.

"That time with the whirlpool and the beach ball," Silena said.

"That beach ball was aggressive."

Percy laughed and drank his chocolate too fast and burned his tongue, which somehow made the morning feel normal and right. Through the window the sky over the Sound was piling itself into gray towers, white at the edges where the sun tried to get through. Not angry yet. Just big. Just waiting to be worked with.

He thought of Annabeth up in the Zeus cabin, probably already diagramming wind shear while Thalia tried to steal her toast. Annabeth at fourteen could read a thunderhead the way other kids read comics. Last fall she had started holding static on her fingertips for a full minute. Last month, two. She still tired if she pushed too long, but she was no longer the kid who had dropped the stream after five seconds in the sand.

They had all grown. Today was for proving it safely.

---

The first argument began with a length of rope.

Clarisse had brought it from the equipment shed and wound it twice over one shoulder. Percy wanted to know why Chiron's training markers weren't sufficient.

"Because stakes can come out," she said.

"Not the ones I planted."

"That's what somebody says right before one comes out."

They stood in the shelter of the Big House porch. Rain ticked against the awning. Grover was protecting a clipboard beneath his coat, and Thalia kept looking toward the far horizon as if the clouds had made a personal appointment with her. This was meant to be a controlled demonstration. Chiron had promised no uncontrolled discharge, no heroics and an independent observer who could stop the exercise.

"Confidence isn't a safety plan," Annabeth said. She held a diagram flat with her elbow. "The rope shows people where to stand. Even if the water stays within the channel, the observers need a boundary they can recognize."

"Thank you," Clarisse said.

"She's not agreeing because you're frightening," Percy told her.

"Good. Means I still have room to improve."

Thalia looked over the diagram. "Who's supposed to stop me?"

Everyone turned.

"If I draw too much charge," she said. "If the wind moves and I think I can handle it anyway. Who tells me to stop, and what happens if I don't hear them?"

"You'd listen to Chiron," Percy said.

"I intend to. That wasn't the question."

Chiron arrived as she spoke. He listened, then took two cards from Grover's clipboard. On one he wrote STOP in block letters. The other stayed blank.

"One verbal order," he said, "repeated by everyone observing. And one visible signal." He tied a strip of bright orange cloth to a wooden staff. "When Grover raises this, the exercise is over. Not after a final demonstration, not once someone has finished proving a point. Over."

"Can we ask why?" Thalia said.

"After you've stopped."

She examined the cloth and nodded. He had not lectured her about having better judgment than the average demigod. He'd given her a rule she could follow even when her judgment was under pressure.

Annabeth wrote the signal beside her measurements. Clarisse checked the rope knots and handed one end to Percy. He fastened it around a post on the practice map Chiron had set up in the porch's sheltered corner. It held when she pulled against it.

"At least you can tie a knot," she said.

"My childhood was very educational."

He regretted the phrase as soon as he said it. In this life, other people remembered his undersea childhood better than he did. Clarisse had never seen the rope lessons Triton had apparently given him. She only watched him test the knot and told him to leave an extra loop for the guard's hand. He did.

At breakfast Jason had already claimed a place beside Ella. His visit had been arranged for the demonstration, and he wanted to help take readings. Ella gave him a separate sheet for cloud cover while she kept the official measurements herself. Jason looked disappointed until Percy pointed out that two independent records would be useful if the wind changed quickly.

"Can I watch from the nearest marker?" Jason asked.

"The marked observation point," Thalia said.

"That's what I meant."

"No, it wasn't."

They could have fought about it for another twenty minutes. Instead Annabeth showed him the map and asked where he could stand without blocking Grover's view of the orange cloth. Jason worked it out for himself. He moved his name on the diagram, then asked Chiron to approve the position.

Silena poured tea while they checked the equipment lists. She noticed that the spare wool blankets had been placed beside the wet-weather crates, where their wrapping could get soaked. Clarisse helped her move them beneath the Big House overhang. It took two minutes and felt humiliatingly ordinary compared with the storm Percy hoped to touch.

On the walk to the coast, Annabeth came alongside him. Her sketchbook was tucked beneath her jacket.

"You wanted today to be spectacular," she said.

"I'd settle for interesting."

"You don't sound convinced."

"I've spent half the morning looking at rope."

"You're going to spend the rest of it working near electricity and moving water. It's all right to prepare."

He smiled. "You sound like Chiron."

"Don't tell him. He'll make me supervise the next drill."

Their hands brushed as they walked. She left her fingers against his for a moment, then withdrew them to hold her paper out of the rain. Nothing dramatic happened. Percy began to understand why that might be worth protecting.

The north shore at nine forty-five looked nothing like the beach during the big storm last year, and Percy was grateful for the difference.

Then, the waves had been house-tall and sideways, the rain had come in sheets, the wind had screamed. Now the Sound was choppy and slate-gray, whitecaps marching in ordinary rows. The sand was damp and firm. The sky was dimming by degrees, clouds thickening overhead, but there were still patches of blue to the west. Gulls wheeled and complained. A couple of naiads watched curiously from the surf, elbows on the water.

Safe weather to practice in. Big enough to feel real. Small enough to stop.

Chiron waited by the equipment shed in his wheelchair form, map case across his knees. Beside him stood Grover with his stopwatch and a new whistle, and Ella with wax tablets in a waterproof satchel. Will Solace leaned against the shed with a healer's kit, looking deliberately bored in the way that meant he intended to enforce rest breaks.

And by the waterline stood Thalia and Annabeth Grace.

Thalia at eighteen had her spear slung across her back, hair tied back. She waved, sharp and glad. Annabeth at fourteen stood beside her with her notebook open, ponytail whipping, gray eyes flicking from cloud to sea to Percy and back. When she saw Percy she smiled and mouthed hi.

Percy mouthed hi back and felt his whole morning settle.

"Right," Chiron said. "This is not an emergency. No borders failing, no monsters, no prophecy. This is a Storm Season drill."

"And about time," Thalia said. "We've talked enough theory. I want to get wet."

"You will get wet," Chiron said dryly. "The purpose today is practice of the joint technique you developed at the reservoir and used last summer under duress. Poseidon children anchor and move water. Zeus children call and ground lightning. Coordination through Annabeth."

Annabeth flipped to a fresh page. "Same principle as last time, smaller scale. Circulating channel from surf to grounding pool, keep it moving so charge never pools. Thalia and I feed pulses. Percy anchors. Clarisse drives. Silena smooths."

"Reservoir rules?" Percy asked, looking at her.

Annabeth met his eyes. "Reservoir rules. You hold. I aim. Nobody plays hero."

It was their old line, worn smooth by repetition, and saying it now in calm weather with Will rolling his eyes and Ella scribbling felt like putting on a favorite sweatshirt. Percy felt Clarisse and Silena straighten on either side of him, the way they always did.

Thalia bumped Annabeth's shoulder with her own. Static snapped faintly. "And beacon rules too. Cadence, shoes, one hand behind back for sky team, stop means stop."

"Ella wrote safety," Ella announced, holding up a tablet. "Ella remembers. Water team wears shoes. No bare feet. Sky team touches only fingertips. Left hand behind back. If anyone yells stop, everyone lets go. Grover blows whistle twice. Will has blankets and honey tea. Ella has dry styluses. Ella does not want pink science again."

Grover nodded solemnly. "Attempt one showed us pink is not science. We've grown."

Clarisse snorted. Silena pressed her lips together to keep from laughing. Percy remembered Attempt Two all too well, the drowned enchiladas, Ella's smeared reds, Grover sitting in the surf with foil stuck to his horns.

Chiron tapped the map. "You will work in the cove, not across camp. Short channel, twenty yards, waist-deep pool at the end lined with salt water. No beacon attempt today. No hill work. Malcolm is supervising the hill ward, with Jason helping monitor the grounding cables. Jason is here on an approved summer visit from Camp Jupiter, not for a live draw. This is for the five of you to shake off the rust."

"And for Annabeth to show off her new stamina," Thalia added, slinging an arm around her sister's shoulders and squeezing. "Tell them, baby."

Annabeth blushed to the tips of her ears, which Percy found deeply unfair this early in the morning. "It's not showing off. I've just been practicing. Thalia's been helping. I can hold a smoothing field longer now without dropping. And I can initiate a small draw myself, not just shape Thalia's. Small. Practice-sized. Will timed me."

Will held up his thermos in confirmation. "Sixty-eight seconds, steady hum, no nosebleed, no pale thing. I checked her pulse after. She still has to rest between, and if she goes white I'm pulling her. That's my job."

"That's his job," Annabeth agreed, rolling her eyes with affection. "He's very good at it. He has a whistle too."

"I don't need a whistle," Will said. "I have a voice that carries across battlefields."

"You have a mom voice," Clarisse said.

"I have a healer voice," Will corrected. "Bigger."

Percy watched Annabeth while they talked. She looked healthy, color good, notebook steady in her hands, hair crackling only a little. Last year after her fever she had buckled in the sand trying to smooth Thalia's spark. Last summer on the hill she had called timing but had not touched the river herself for long, conserving strength for command. Now she stood on damp sand in water shoes talking about initiating draws like it was arithmetic. He felt pride swell so large it almost embarrassed him.

Annabeth caught him looking and raised an eyebrow. "What?"

"Nothing," Percy said. "Just glad you're here."

"Where else would I be?" she said, but her ears went pink again, and Thalia made a gagging sound that was entirely theatrical.

"Okay, lovebirds," Thalia said. "Save it for after we don't electrocute ourselves. Poseidon huddle?"

"Zeus huddle after," Annabeth said promptly.

Chiron smiled, ancient and fond. "Take your positions when ready. Grover will call time. Ella will record. Will will hover. I will observe. Remember, children. Today the storm is your partner, not your enemy. Listen to it."

---

Grover's whistle cut through the wind, bright and absurdly cheerful.

"Drill One in one minute! Channel only, no charge! Ella, mark time!"

"Ella marks ten oh four," Ella said, stylus poised over blue wax. "Sky gray layering. Wind east-southeast fresh. Sea choppy but friendly. Hippocampus absent. Harpy dry so far. Satyr professional."

The three Poseidon siblings waded in to their ankles and then their knees, the cold spring water biting through water shoes. Percy took center automatically, Clarisse to his right, Silena to his left. They joined hands for a second, instinct, skin cold and familiar.

Percy felt them settle alongside him in the water-sense the way he always did, Clarisse like iron wrapped in heat, Silena like silk over steel. His own power sat deep and bright in his chest, eager after a winter of indoor pools and controlled fountains. The sea reached for him, chirpy and restless with storm coming, and he reached back with pressure and intent. Not words. Promise. This way. We'll give you somewhere to go.

"On three," he said. "One. Two. Three."

They lifted.

Water rose from the surf in a thick, glittering rope, as wide as Percy's waist, arching from the breakers to the shallow grounding pool Grover and Juniper had dug that morning and lined with seawater. Silena held the intake, swaying gently, breathing with the waves. In, out. Percy held the crown with both hands high, keeping it aloft and moving. Clarisse fed power from behind, jaw set, eyes on Percy's shoulders, pushing flow fast enough to carry charge when it came.

The rope hummed, steady, shedding spray.

"Hold!" Annabeth called from the dry sand, notebook forgotten in her hand, eyes narrowed at the water. She walked the length of it slowly, checking edges. "Silena, beautiful intake. Clarisse, ease off five percent, you're over-driving the bend. Percy—"

"Yeah?" Percy grunted. His arms trembled faintly with the newness of it, winter muscles waking.

"You're doing three jobs again," Annabeth said, not unkindly. "Let go of the tip. Let Clarisse aim. Just hold the middle and trust us."

Percy exhaled and loosened his fingers. The tip wobbled, then steadied as Clarisse caught it with a grunt and aimed it down into the pool.

"Good," Annabeth said. "Good. That's twenty seconds, steady. Grover?"

"Twenty-four seconds!" Grover called, delighted. "No wobble! No satyr drenching!"

"Ella records: water snake healthy," Ella said, scratching in blue. "No red yet. Ella hopeful."

They held for a full minute, then let the rope fall back with a soft rush. Percy shook water from his hands, breathing hard, grinning despite himself. Clarisse rolled her neck until it cracked. Silena laughed, breathless, and wrung out her braid.

"Okay!" Annabeth said, bouncing once on her toes. "Okay, that was clean. Better than last spring already. Winter training worked."

"Winter training was carrying buckets in the snow because you said flow rate matters more than volume," Clarisse said, but she sounded proud.

"It does matter," Annabeth said. "And you proved it. Ready for pulse?"

Thalia stepped forward, cracking her knuckles, sparks popping between her fingertips. "Ready when you are, baby. Small pulses, like we practiced. I'll start, you smooth?"

Annabeth hesitated, pencil tapping her notebook. Percy saw the flicker of old habit cross her face, the instinct to stay in command and let Thalia take the risk. Then she straightened.

"Actually," she said. "Can I start? Just a small one. I want to test my initiation while the channel's fresh and everyone's rested. Thalia spots me."

Thalia blinked, then grinned wide and proud. "Hell yes. Do it. I've got you."

Percy felt his chest tighten. He trusted Annabeth completely, he knew how hard she had worked, Will was right there with his healer voice, Chiron was watching calmly. Still, his hands curled a little tighter on the water. Old fear, reservoir and hill and fever, the memory of her knees buckling in the sand.

Annabeth saw it. She walked to the water's edge, close enough that he could see the spray on her cheeks, and lowered her voice so only he heard.

"Hey," she said. "I'm okay. Promise you'll tell me if it tingles wrong?"

It was their old pinky promise, inverted and returned. Percy felt something warm and steady settle through him.

"I promise," he said. "You promise you'll stop if you go dizzy?"

"I promise," she said. She held out her pinky, solemn as an oath on the Styx, rain starting to speckle her sleeve.

Percy linked his pinky through hers with wet fingers. "Then I'm good."

"Then I'm good," Annabeth echoed. She stepped back to dry sand, tucked her left hand behind her back in proper safety form, and raised her right hand with two fingers extended.

"Channel up!" Grover called. "Three! Two! One! Lift!"

They lifted again. The rope rose, smooth and fast this time, muscle memory kicking in. Silena breathed. Clarisse drove. Percy held the middle, steady, whispering to the water. Almost there. Keep moving. The sea is waiting.

"Pulse one!" Grover called. "Three! Two! One! Touch!"

Annabeth stepped forward and touched the base of the water rope with her fingertips.

Light ran up the water.

Percy felt it arrive as warmth, not shock. A tingling rush that sang through the stream from base to tip, lighting it from within blue-white-gold. The air sang. The hair on his arms stood up, but his hands did not burn, his arms did not jerk. He could feel Annabeth in the current, distinct as a voice. Where Thalia's power had always been hot and sharp, wild sky cracking down, Annabeth's was steady, humming, even. A held note. She was not throwing lightning. She was inviting a little of it to ride.

It was the most beautiful thing he had ever felt, and he had felt it before, but never like this. Never initiated by her, never sustained.

The pool at the end hissed and steamed where the charged water poured in, venting harmlessly into wet sand.

"Five seconds!" Grover shouted, forgetting cadence in excitement. "Six! Seven! Ella, are you seeing this!"

"Ella sees!" Ella shouted back, scribbling furiously in yellow. "Sea glows! Sky hums! Small Grace holds! Big Grace spotting! Poseidon steady! No red! No red!"

At ten seconds Annabeth lifted her fingers cleanly away. The glow faded. The rope stayed up, clean and unbroken.

Percy whooped before he could stop himself. Clarisse yelled so loud the gulls fled. Silena laughed with relief and delight, keeping the intake perfect even through it.

Annabeth stood staring at her fingertips, breathing fast, color high, eyes wide. A tiny spark popped on her nail and died.

"I did it," she said, disbelieving. "I initiated and held and released clean."

"You did it!" Thalia shouted, grabbing her around the waist and spinning her in a circle while Annabeth shrieked and laughed. "That's my sister! Sixty-eight seconds in the infirmary, ten seconds live with a whole ocean attached! Did you feel how clean that release was?"

"I felt it!" Annabeth gasped, dizzy and grinning. "Percy, did it tingle wrong? Tell me true."

Percy let the channel dip just enough to look at her properly. "Truth? It felt like soda bubbles. Nice. Yours feels different from Thalia's. Hers is hot. Yours is—like humming. Steady."

Annabeth absorbed this like data and treasure at once, nodding slowly. "Good. That means smoothing held through initiation. That's what we've been training for."

Will pushed off the shed, arms crossed, trying to look stern and failing. "Color good. Breathing good. Pulse later. Rest ninety seconds, hydrate, then we go again. That's an order."

"Yes, sir," Annabeth said meekly, accepting the water bottle Silena tossed her. She drank, wiped her mouth, and beamed at Percy over the bottle. He beamed back, water still held aloft over his head, arms starting to ache in the best way.

Chiron made a note on his map case, his expression soft. "Well done, all of you. Ella, note the time and condition for the record."

"Ella notes Drill One success," Ella intoned. "Small Grace glows. Sea carries. Sky listens. No one fried. Straw Bob the Third alive and well on beach as target dummy, relieved."

Everyone looked to where Straw Bob the Third stood staked by the pool in his old helmet, untouched and slightly bedraggled in the rising wind. Clarisse saluted him.

"Your day will come, Bob," she said. "Not today."

---

They took the scheduled break in the rain shelter at the end of the third exercise. Percy found that his hands were shaking slightly. He tried to hide it by tightening the knot on his jacket cuff, but Clarisse noticed.

"Eat," she said, handing him half a roll.

"I'm not hungry."

"I wasn't collecting votes."

He accepted the bread and discovered he was hungry enough to finish it in three bites. Silena brought the canteen round, making sure Jason and Ella drank before anybody started arguing about the readings. The salt air had left a fine film on everyone's lips. Percy could taste it when he spoke.

Annabeth spread the wet paper across the bench. The current had moved within the marked channel for the first two exercises. In the third, the water had risen briefly at the downstream end, against the direction Percy thought he'd given it.

"That's not what I asked for," he said.

"That's why I've marked it," Annabeth answered.

"Could have been a gust."

"Could have. But the gust would have reached the upper flags too, and they stayed nearly still."

Grover leaned over the map. "There's a small drain beyond that point. A freshwater line from the camp irrigation system."

"The Sound is saltwater," Percy said. "The drain shouldn't affect much."

"Shouldn't isn't the same as won't," Annabeth said. "The fresh stream might change the way the surface moves."

He felt a familiar flash of irritation. He knew water. He knew the tug of currents and how to push against them, how to lift a crest without breaking it. Every time they made a chart, somebody found one more thing that his power wasn't supposed to do.

Then he looked at the paper. Annabeth hadn't written *Percy failed*. She had drawn two arrows, one for the command he'd given and one for the movement that followed. The arrows disagreed. Neither could be corrected by his pride.

"All right," he said. "Let's trace the drain."

"Only from the dry side," Chiron said.

They walked to the inspection point. Clarisse carried the measuring line. Percy knelt at the bank and watched a narrow freshwater runnel meet the saltwater channel, clouding it for a few feet before the current dispersed it. The mixing point wasn't dangerous by itself. It was simply something they hadn't accounted for.

"I'll keep the next exercise above it," Percy said.

"No," Annabeth said. "If we move the test section without checking the boundaries, we've changed the experiment."

He sat back on his heels. "Then what do you propose?"

"Record the mismatch. Continue with the next approved pattern only if Chiron thinks the observation doesn't invalidate the safety limits."

Grover looked at the orange flag and then at Chiron. He had the odd expression of someone realizing the job he'd agreed to do actually mattered. The centaur asked Ella to record the drain's position, then checked that the warm section of the channel remained inside the observation line.

"The anomaly is outside the energized part of the next drill," he said finally. "We will not increase charge. We may continue with the original plan and treat that downstream measurement as unverified."

Percy wanted to argue for a second chance to make the water behave properly. He could feel the shape of the current now; repeating the maneuver would have been easy. But he'd agreed that this wasn't a contest. The point of keeping the record was that no one had to erase a failed observation to believe in a successful day.

Thalia sat on the low wall beside him while the others collected equipment.

"You don't like leaving it unresolved," she said.

"Neither do you."

"I hate it."

"So why aren't you arguing?"

"Because Jason's watching."

Percy turned. Jason stood beside Ella, copying the drain location into his own notes with painstaking care. His tongue stuck out of one corner of his mouth. He had missed nothing of the exchange.

"He knows you're good at this," Percy said.

"That's not the part I want him to learn."

Thalia drew her wet sleeve back from her wrist. There were faint pale marks there from old training injuries, not new wounds, and Percy wondered how often she'd been told that the sky belonged to her and therefore she ought to use it every time someone asked. She had always sounded more confident than he felt. He'd never asked whether the confidence was exhausting.

"Zeus used to arrive with a different face every time," she said quietly. "Some days he'd tell me I was the only one who could hear the storm. Next time he might act as if he didn't know me. I spent years trying to do something he couldn't ignore."

Percy kept still. This wasn't a story about the erased life. It was hers, lived here, and she had chosen to tell it.

"And today?" he asked.

"Today I can stop before the clouds ask me to."

He looked toward Annabeth, who was examining the charge meter with the technician. "You do know she worries about you."

"I worry about her."

"That's not the same thing as asking what she wants."

Thalia gave him a sideways look. "I didn't say it was."

She got to her feet when Annabeth called for the observation sheets. Percy handed the damp roll wrapper to Grover for the rubbish bag. He felt less strong than he had before the break, but more certain of what the next exercise was meant to show. A controlled attempt could end with a question. They did not need to risk another attempt simply to turn the question into a cleaner story.

By Drill Four the spring storm had moved overhead in earnest, and practice felt deliciously real without ever tipping into danger.

The sky had gone the color of old bronze, then bruised purple-gray. Rain fell steadily, then sideways in gusts. Thunder rolled continuously far above, not cracking close, just growling as charge moved through high cloud. On the hill Malcolm managed the grounding cables while Jason checked the readings beside him, so the air never went sharp or scary. The hair on Percy's arms lifted, then settled, lifted, then settled, as the system breathed.

It was perfect drill weather. Big enough to draw from. Calm enough to stop.

They ran the channel again and again, each pulse a little longer, each handoff a little smoother. Thalia took Pulse Two, a neat crackling arc that rode Annabeth's smoothing field like a surfer, twelve seconds full illumination. Annabeth took Pulse Three alone, fourteen seconds, release so clean Ella stood up and cheered. Pulse Four they did together, sisters side by side, right hands extended, left hands behind backs, gold and white braided, running up the rainbow of water while Grover screamed cadence and forgot to click his stopwatch and had to estimate.

Percy held the middle through all of it.

That was his job, then and now. The anchor. He took Clarisse's hammering drive in one hand and Silena's gentle shaping in the other and blended them, keeping the channel alive but contained. He felt every rock the water passed over, every dip where it wanted to pool, every gust that tried to tear spray loose. He whispered to it constantly, promises and directions. Keep moving. Almost there. The sea is waiting.

Between pulses they checked each other with nudges, keeping triangle formation. They had trained for this in the cove, in winter with buckets, on the hill holding a river. Work had become easy the way breathing was easy, by doing it together.

The last drill was the one they would talk about, not because it was dramatic, but because it was perfect.

Grover called lift. The rope rose, glittering, humming with steady flow. Silena fed. Clarisse aimed. Percy held, relaxed, trusting. Annabeth stepped forward alone while Thalia watched with arms crossed and pride all over her face.

"Pulse five!" Grover called. "Three! Two! One! Touch!"

Annabeth touched the stream with two fingertips and held.

Light ran up the water and stayed. Blue-white-gold, steady as a lamp. The pool hissed. Percy thought of the beacon, green-gold fire while they held hands in the surf at thirteen.

He could feel Annabeth humming in the current, Clarisse driving, Silena smoothing, himself anchoring. Five wills braided, none pulling too hard.

Twenty seconds. Twenty-five. Thirty.

Annabeth lifted her fingers away, clean. The glow faded. The water fell back at Grover's double whistle, soft rush, no bang, no spill.

Then Will started clapping. Grover blew his whistle, hopping. Ella danced, chanting.

"Thirty-one seconds!" Grover shouted. "Full illumination! No wobble! No spill! New spring record!"

"Ella records no red!" Ella shouted back, holding up a tablet with big blue and yellow lines and, in the corner, a small heart she pretended was data. "Ella records glow lasted long! Ella records Small Grace steady! Ella records Sea remembers Sky! Ella saw it! Ella wrote it!"

Annabeth stood staring at her hand, rain on her lashes, chest heaving. Then she looked at Percy, and her face broke open into joy so pure it made his throat tighten.

"Did you feel that?" she shouted. "Was it clean?"

"It was perfect!" Percy shouted back, splashing toward the edge, channel forgotten. "Annie, that was perfect!"

She met him halfway and threw up her hand for a high-five. Percy raised his hand to meet it.

Their palms slapped, stinging, perfect. Then their fingers curled. Annabeth's hand caught Percy's. They stood in cold spring rain, holding hands, both blushing, neither letting go.

It was chaste and simple. They were fourteen now, and the hold meant more for lasting, but still just that.

"Hi," Percy said stupidly.

"Hi," Annabeth said back, squeezing his fingers. Rain dripped off her ponytail onto his wrist. "We did it. I held it."

"You held it," Percy said. "You were amazing."

Thalia saw it and covered her mouth with both hands to stifle a squeal, failing completely. Silena saw it and pressed her hands to her heart. Clarisse saw it and pretended to gag, but she was grinning so hard it ruined the effect.

"Young love," Grover sighed happily, clicking his stopwatch at last. "Plus excellent grounding technique. Ella, underline that."

"Ella underlines three times," Ella said, doing so. "Ella draws heart around hand-hold. Ella is romantic. Do not tell Ares."

Chiron wheeled forward, smiling. "Well done. Practice made play, play made prayer. Sea and sky by choice. Go get dry."

They walked back up the beach together, dripping and glowing. Percy and Annabeth held hands the whole way, swinging them slightly.

At the top twelve-year-old Jason waited with hot towels, delighted to be helping his sisters instead of unpacking in the Big House guest room. "Heard you lit up the cove. Hill never flickered. Clean work."

"Clean work," Thalia agreed, leaning into her brother's side for a second before straightening. "Baby sister initiated three times. Tell Mom."

"I will," Jason said solemnly. "She'll burst with pride."

---

Chiron ended the drill when the next gust shifted the rain toward the outer markers.

The change was small, but it mattered. The exercise was meant to test how Percy and the Grace siblings could work in difficult weather under supervision. It was not meant to turn a developing storm into a contest about who could keep going longest.

"All charge work stops," Chiron called. "Everyone to the sheltered marker. Walk, don't run."

Annabeth lowered her hands. The faint static around her fingers disappeared, but she still looked irritated. She had been building a controlled path toward the grounding plate, and she wanted another attempt before the rain grew heavier.

"One more," she said.

Thalia shook her head. "No."

"I was close."

"That isn't the safety criterion."

Annabeth looked toward Chiron. The centaur had already given the stop order. She exhaled, nodded and went with her sister toward the marked shelter.

Percy had been holding a shallow channel across the sand to keep water away from the equipment. As he released it, the runoff began finding its own path. The channel had kept one side of the practice area dry, but now there was a little pool beside the grounded conductors, and the people responsible for removing them would have to step around it.

"Can I drain that?" he asked.

"Not with a power surge," Chiron replied. "Use the ordinary runoff cut. Clarisse, please help him clear the gravel."

They took a shovel and worked while Grover kept the other campers behind the rope. Percy could feel the temptation to make the sand behave instantly. But the drain was blocked by grit, not by a magical problem. Five minutes of work moved the water into the safe collection trench. It was less impressive than a wave, and the equipment crew thanked them more warmly than Percy had expected.

Will checked the trainees before they left the shore. Annabeth's hands were cold and trembling a little. Jason had a headache from the noise and wind. Thalia was exhausted in a way she tried to disguise with jokes. Percy felt a dull ache behind his eyes after concentrating on the channel through several drills.

"Nobody goes back into the storm to prove they're fine," Will said.

"Do I look like I need to prove something?" Clarisse asked.

"Yes."

"Rude."

"Accurate," Silena said.

The checks took longer than anyone wanted. Will asked about dizziness, vision and numbness. He made sure everybody could walk steadily and that nobody had strained a shoulder or slipped near the water. Jason needed a quiet half hour away from the noise. Annabeth was told to rest her hands before training again. Percy promised not to try a second current exercise that evening.

Chiron called the group together under the shelter and laid their record sheets on a dry board.

"What succeeded?" he asked.

"Controlled discharge remained inside the marked path," Thalia said. "Until the wind shifted."

"The change in weather didn't cause an electrical failure," Annabeth added. "We stopped before it could become one."

"And what do we need to improve?"

Percy pointed to the runoff pool beside the conductors. "Our water plan protected the equipment during the drills but didn't account for what happened after we released the channel."

"Good observation."

Silena wrote it down. Clarisse suggested making the collection trench deeper before the next session. Jason wanted the check-in calls repeated twice because he had missed the first warning beneath the sound of thunder.

Chiron accepted the suggestions and made the next drill provisional on those repairs and the trainees' recovery. He did not set a date immediately.

By the time they left the beach, the rain had begun to ease. The storm was no longer a challenge to master. It was weather, moving according to forces larger than any of them, and the camp had done what a training exercise should teach: practiced, recognized limits and stopped.

Percy walked up the hill beside Annabeth. Their hands were too tired to hold for long, so they kept brushing shoulders instead.

"Are you upset we stopped?" he asked.

"A little."

"Me too."

She looked at him. "That doesn't mean Chiron was wrong."

"No."

"Then tomorrow we can write a better plan."

"Tomorrow," he said. "After we rest."

She smiled faintly. "You're learning."


---

The drill did not end when the wind dropped. Chiron called everyone back to the Big House porch to review the readings, and Percy discovered that he had written down only the exciting part: the moment the water moved as ordered. Annabeth's page held a second column of measurements, including two they could not explain.

"The charge rose before I gave the signal," she said.

Thalia looked at the numbers. "Then the water wasn't responding only to you."

"Which is why we stop using the channel until we know what happened," Chiron said.

Percy had been about to suggest another quick trial. He closed his mouth. He'd seen the way Annabeth's fingers trembled afterward, and he could still feel a dull ache behind his own eyes. They hadn't failed, but neither had they demonstrated a method that was safe to repeat.

Grover reported the boundary markers he'd checked. Two had been obscured by blown branches; none had fallen. Clarisse assigned a repair pair to the markers and Silena recorded the location so they wouldn't have to rely on someone remembering which tree looked familiar in the rain.

"What about our practice?" Percy asked.

"Suspended for now," Chiron answered. "Until we have a clear isolation plan and someone independent of the exercise can end it."

Annabeth nodded. She looked disappointed, not defeated. She had already begun drawing a new diagram with a dry section separating the conductive channel from the observation point.

"We'll need a cutoff," she said.

"And a reason to believe it works," Thalia replied. "Not just a drawing."

Percy offered to help her test the barriers when Chiron approved a safe procedure. He didn't volunteer to be the test subject. Annabeth noticed and squeezed his wrist once.

By the time they returned to the cabin, the storm had become ordinary rain. The observations stayed with Chiron in a dry folder. That was the difference between surviving one attempt and learning from it.

The Poseidon cabin smelled like salt and chocolate and wet wool.

Someone, probably Silena, had dragged blankets into a nest before the fireplace, which Percy lit with shaking hands. Socks steamed by the door. No one cared.

Making chocolate had been an operation. Percy dripped into the pot, hands trembling. Clarisse took the tin, Silena found milk and shell marshmallows. Thalia stole three from the bag. Annabeth stole one back, bickering like ordinary teenagers while rain drummed the roof.

Now they were all on the floor. Clarisse against the couch, Silena chin on pillow, Percy between them. Thalia in the armchair in Percy's blanket. Annabeth next to Percy, shoulders touching, hair damp, cheeks pink.

For a while they compared notes. Clarisse showed pruned fingers. Silena showed a shin bruise like a map of Greece. Thalia showed clean palms and bowed. Percy showed tingling hands and Annabeth checked his grip until he squeezed back and she nodded. Will had pronounced them tired but uninjured.

No real injuries. That had been the rule. Reservoir rules. Nobody played hero, so nobody got hurt.

Katie reported fields fine. Malcolm reported hill at one hundred percent. Outside the storm moved out to sea, rain easing, sunlight breaking through.

No one spoke for a long time. There was nothing to say the fire didn't say better.

Finally Silena lifted her head. "Say it," she mumbled.

"Say what?" Percy asked, though he knew.

"The thing. The promise thing. From after the reservoir. And the beacon. And last summer."

Clarisse snorted into her mug, but her eyes were soft. "She means the corny thing."

"It's not corny," Silena protested weakly. "It's important. Especially when it's easy to forget after practice. We have to say it after practice too, so we remember."

Percy knew what she meant. Van after the reservoir. Beach after the beacon. This floor last summer. Pinky-promise to back each other. No one fights alone. No one practices alone.

They had promised. They kept it today.

Percy held out his pinky. It trembled with tiredness. "Storm and Sea," he said.

Clarisse linked hers through his immediately, strong and sure. "Storm and Sea," she repeated.

Silena added hers, smiling sleepily. "And Mind."

From the armchair Thalia extended her pinky without getting up. "And Sky, you idiots. Don't leave out Sky."

Annabeth leaned her head against Percy's shoulder, just for a second, just to reach, and linked her pinky through the knot of theirs. Her hand was warm from the mug.

"Mind's here," she murmured. "Always."

They sat like that for a moment, five pinkies tangled over a cooling mug, while the fire popped and the last of the storm dripped off the eaves.

"Okay," Silena said, satisfied, and flopped back onto her pillow. "Now we can sleep for a year."

Clarisse drained her mug and set it down with a clunk. "I'm too tired to make it to my bunk. I'm sleeping here. If Dad floods the cabin in my dreams, I'm killing him."

"He wouldn't dare," Percy said. "Not today. Today we saved his shoreline by practicing."

Silena was already half-asleep, mug tipping dangerously. Percy gently took it from her hands and set it aside, tucking the blanket up around her shoulders. She mumbled something that sounded like love you, stupid fish boys and girls and sky girls and went still.

Clarisse's eyes drifted shut. Within minutes her breathing went deep and even, head tipped back against the couch, hand still resting against Percy's ankle where she had left it for balance during the promise. Sister. Anchor. Hammer.

Percy let his own eyes close. He was so tired his bones ached, his palms buzzed pleasantly, his ears rang faintly with leftover hum. But underneath exhaustion was something warm and steady, like hearth fire. They had done it. Not by being stronger than the storm, but by giving it somewhere to go. Together. Safely. For fun, almost. That felt like growth he could hold in his hands.

He felt Annabeth's head grow heavier on his shoulder.

He looked down. She was asleep. Really asleep, mouth slightly open, eyelashes damp against her cheeks, gray eyes hidden, strategist finally off duty. Her blanket had slipped. Her fingers were still curled loosely around nothing where their pinkies had linked. Her other hand rested in her lap, fingertips faintly red from work, but relaxed, unclenched.

Percy went very still, afraid to wake her. His heart did something complicated and tender in his chest. Fourteen and chaste and certain, he just sat straighter against the couch so she had a better pillow, and did not move his shoulder a fraction, even as his arm started to fall asleep.

Thalia watched from the armchair, and her usual teasing grin softened into something almost sisterly. She stood quietly, wincing at her own stiffness, and pulled the spare blanket, the soft blue one Silena knitted last winter, from the back of the chair.

She draped it gently over both of them, tucking it around Annabeth's shoulders and Percy's arm where it rested against the floor for support.

"Reservoir rules," Thalia whispered, brushing Annabeth's damp hair back from her forehead with surprising gentleness. "You hold. She aims. Nobody plays hero."

Percy met her eyes over Annabeth's sleeping head. "Thanks," he mouthed.

Thalia winked, her own eyes already drooping. She curled back into the armchair, pulling the corner of Percy's stolen blanket up to her chin. "Storm and Sea, Kelp Head," she murmured, half-asleep. "Storm and Sea."

Outside the Sound lapped gently against the shore. The borders hummed, steady and strong. Peleus snored somewhere on the hill, smoke curling from his nostrils in peaceful rhythm. Summer rain ticked softly against the windows, no longer arguing, just talking.

Inside Cabin Three, with fire low and chocolate cooling, the Poseidon siblings and the Grace sisters breathing in sync around it, the sea finally rested.

And so did they.
