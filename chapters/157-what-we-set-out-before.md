## Chapter 157: What We Set Out Before

Percy woke because the cabin door sighed.

Not the pool, not the gulls, not the morning bell from the Big House. The front door of Cabin Three had a particular sound when the lake wind found it, a low wooden sigh through a gap Clarisse swore she would fix and Silena secretly liked, and Percy had learned it in a month the way you learn a sibling's breathing in the bunk above you.

He lay still a moment with his eyes open. The high window over the pool end showed a sky the color of wet slate, early, the sun not yet over the pines. September did that now. It came later every morning and left earlier every night, and the air through the gap had an edge it hadn't had in August, thin and clean and smelling faintly of pine sap and turned earth. First frost was still talk, not weather — the older campers arguing at breakfast about when it would come, Beckendorf saying third week, Katie saying second — but the mornings had started to ask for a second layer, and Percy pulled the quilt up to his chin and counted the sounds of the house.

Water moving under tile. Silena's bare feet, quick and certain, kitchenward. Clarisse's boots, one thump and then the other being laced hard. Somebody's spoon against a pot.

The week the panel sat in Chiron's office had been the longest quiet week of Percy's summer.

He sat up and swung his legs over the bunk edge. The tile was cold enough through his socks that he felt it in his teeth. He dressed the way September was teaching everyone to dress, in layers that could be shed by noon: the faded orange camp shirt with the trident washed nearly white across one shoulder, sleeves cut short back in June by somebody's borrowed scissors, over a long-sleeved thermal gone thin at the elbows that had been Silena's before it was his, the cuffs already shoved up past his wrists out of habit. His jeans were the ones with the worn knees and the lake-dark hem that never quite dried, and his sneakers were the same salt-soft pair, laces double-knotted, a hair tie of Silena's looped around one lace and a smooth white pebble from the canoe lake in his left pocket where it had lived since July.

He folded the quilt back the way Clarisse had taught him, which was corners tight enough to bounce a coin, and stood looking at the bunks along the back wall.

Three bunks for the permanent residents. That was the rule of Cabin Three, unwritten and absolute: Percy, Clarisse, Silena, claimed and staying. The two spare bunks against the far wall, the ones with the towels folded on the hooks above them, were visitor bunks. Everyone at camp knew that. Tyson had slept in the corner one for three weeks in July, making the bed every morning with hospital corners and leaving a round white rock on the pillow like an egg, and Triton had taken the other for a weekend in August, formally and briefly, with gifts and a departure by Monday and tea he did not like but drank politely anyway. Neither of them lived here. Neither of them ever would; they belonged to the sea, and Cabin Three belonged to a lake, and Triton himself had said so over that tea, kindly, as if to make sure nobody built a hope on a weekend.

But the towels stayed on the hooks. Percy had hung them, and nobody had taken them down, and this morning somebody — Silena, by the fold — had replaced the top one on the corner bunk with a fresh one, thicker, the blue one with the frayed edge that dried fastest.

Percy touched the edge of it with two fingers. It was dry and clean and smelling faintly of lemon oil from the cabin shelf, and his chest did the tight, bright thing it had been doing for days now whenever anyone said the word brother where Chiron or the schedule board could hear.

Tyson was coming. Not weeks. Days.

The word had come down through the usual channels, which meant an Iris-message that Clarisse had taken standing in a rain barrel with her arms crossed, and a confirming note from Chiron in his neat centaur hand, and then three days of the whole cabin walking around the fact the way you walk around a dog that has finally gone to sleep in the doorway. Tyson. Percy's half-brother. Son of Poseidon. Big hands, bigger heart, the boy who wept over ponies and built things out of scrap that should not have stood up and did. Coming up from the forges, where he had been apprenticed since August, for a long stay before the weather turned.

Percy went down the hall to Tempest Hall with the towel's lemon smell still in his nose.

The hall was half building and half morning sky. Light came through the high windows in long pale shafts full of slow dust, and the pool lay in the middle of the tile like a held breath, deep and green-clear, warm at the top and cold underneath, salt on the air the way a shell holds salt long after the sea has left it. The mosaic hippocampus slept blue-green at the bottom, undisturbed, patient as furniture.

Clarisse was already at the workbench end with the canoe paddle across two sawhorses, running a thumb along the blade edge. She wore her cutoff camp shirt over a gray thermal with the sleeves pushed up, her dark braid hanging wet down her back from an early swim, her cutoff jeans frayed at the thigh and her socks mismatched — one blue, one gray — because the laundry room had eaten the mates and she refused on principle to buy new ones. Her boots stood by the door, worn at the heel, laces loose, waiting.

"You're up," she said without looking. "Good. Hold this."

Percy crossed the cold tile and held the paddle shaft where she pointed. The wood was smooth and smelled of oil and varnish. It was the repaired canoe's paddle — the green hull they'd pulled half-sunk out of the willow cove back in August, the one he and Annabeth had found the panel in, thirty-nine tally marks cut into a hidden board in the dark under the bow seat. Hull-good, rope-good, fine, as he had made Chiron write down. The panel itself sat now in Chiron's office under lock, wrapped in oilcloth, known to Clarisse and Chiron and, in the careful partial way these things went, to Thalia. A week in the drawer. A week of Percy not asking to see it, because there was nothing new to learn from looking and everything to lose from worrying it like a tooth.

"Tip it," Clarisse said.

He tipped it. She sighted down the blade, grunted, and set her sanding block to a rough patch near the throat with short, hard strokes.

"Chiron says the forge sends him Friday on the morning tide," Clarisse said to the paddle. "Gets in before lunch. Stays through Monday. Sleeps here." She jerked her chin toward the bunks without looking up. "Corner bunk's his. Same as July. Don't move his rock. He left it on the shelf when he left and Silena's kept it there and if you touch it I'll know."

"I wasn't going to touch his rock," Percy said.

"You were thinking about dusting it."

"I was not thinking about dusting it."

"You had the face." She blew dust off the blade and ran her thumb over it again. "He eats at the pavilion with us, he drills with the forges kids Saturday if Beckendorf wants him, and Sunday he's ours. Whole day. Cabin Three. No schedule." A pause. "I told Chiron. He wrote it down."

Percy felt the grin start before he could stop it. "You put Tyson on the schedule as ours."

"I put Sunday down as cabin business. Chiron knows what that means." She set the block down and looked at him finally, level and dark-eyed and entirely serious. "He's bigger than July. Forge work does that. The bunk'll hold him — Silena checked the slats — but the blanket won't cover his feet, so she's making the quilt thing longer. And he gets cold on buses. He told me once. Falls asleep against the window and wakes up with his ear frozen." She said it flatly, the way she said everything that mattered, as if daring him to make it sentimental. "So somebody meets him at the cove with the spare blue hoodie. Not the shirt. The hoodie. The one with the pocket torn."

"The one you said you'd throw out."

"I said I'd throw it out and then I washed it. That's different." She picked up the oil rag and worked it along the shaft in slow circles, and her voice dropped half a register, grudging even with the paddle. "He likes the pocket torn. He keeps — he kept his bus ticket in it. July. Folded it small. Showed me." Her thumb pressed hard into a grain line. "Told me pockets with holes are good because then the ticket's always half out and you can't lose it without knowing."

Percy held the shaft steady and didn't say anything, because there were moments with Clarisse when silence was the only correct offering and this was one of them.

"He's my brother too," Clarisse said to the wood. "People forget. They look at you and him and they do the math. Same father, same — " She stopped. Started again, blunter. "He cried on my shoulder in July when a hippocampus spooked at his shadow. Full weight. Nearly took us both into the lake. And I stood there with lake water up to my knees holding him up and thinking, this is the strongest person I've ever met and he's afraid I'll laugh." She looked up, and her eyes were fierce and wet and furious about being wet. "I didn't laugh. I'd cut my own hand off before I laughed at him."

"I know," Percy said quietly.

"Do you." It wasn't a question. She wiped the blade down with the rag and set it against the sawhorse with exaggerated care. "Then you know why the paddle has to be right. He'll want to take the canoe out. First thing. He always wants the water first. And I'm not sending my brother out in a boat with a sticky paddle."

Percy nodded. His throat had gone tight in a way that had nothing to do with the cold tile under his socks.

"Percy." Clarisse's voice went flatter still, which meant she was about to say the thing she least wanted to say. "You wrote him?"

"Three times. He wrote back twice. Once was a drawing." Percy's hand found the pebble in his pocket and turned it over. "The drawing was a bridge. With us on it. All four — me, him, you, Silena. The bridge was holding."

Clarisse absorbed this with her jaw working. Then she nodded once, sharp, and picked up the sanding block again as if the conversation were over, which it was, except for the last part, which she said to the paddle and not to him.

"Good. Then he knows the way."

---

Silena measured the corner bunk with a length of twine the way other people measured with rulers, which was to say precisely and while humming.

She knelt on the cold tile in the bunk alcove with the morning light coming sideways through the high window and striping the blankets, her hair still damp from the pool and twisted up with the pencil that lived in her bun, a smudge of lemon oil on one cheek she hadn't noticed. She wore her camp shirt tied at the waist over a long-sleeved white tee gone gray at the seams from a hundred washes, the sleeves shoved up to show forearms scattered with small pale scars — rope burns, a forge kiss from July, the thin white line where a fishing hook had caught her at nine — and her cutoffs with the frayed hem, her feet bare despite the cold because she claimed she thought better with her toes on tile, her sandals abandoned by the door with a spare hair tie looped through one strap.

Percy leaned in the doorway watching her run the twine from headboard to footboard, mark it with her thumb, fold it, mark it again.

"You're making the quilt longer," he said.

"I'm making the quilt longer," she confirmed, without looking up. "He grew two inches since July. Clarisse measured his shadow against the doorframe last time like a detective and wrote it on her hand. The blanket comes to here on him now." She touched a spot on the twine. "Which means cold feet, which means no sleep, which means a grumpy Cyclops at breakfast, which means Beckendorf cries into his eggs. So." She folded the twine into a neat loop and tucked it into her shirt pocket. "We lengthen the quilt. I found the blue fabric in the craft loft. It's not a match, but it's the right weight, and he'll like the color. Boys who build things like true colors."

Percy came in and sat on the edge of his own bunk, facing her. "You kept his rock."

Silena glanced at the shelf above the corner bunk. The round white rock sat exactly centered, pale as an egg in the morning light, dustless. "Of course I kept his rock. He left it on the pillow for the next person. That's what it means. You leave something round and white for whoever sleeps there next so they know somebody was happy there." She said it simply, the way she said true things, warm and without apology. "Triton left a shell on the other one. August. A conch with the tip broken. Same idea, fancier brother."

She stood, brushing tile dust off her knees, and went to the hooks. The towels hung in a neat row — two for each resident, one spare each for visitors — and she straightened the blue one with the frayed edge, then, after a moment's consideration, swapped it for the thicker green one from the shelf.

"Blue dries fast but green's warmer," she explained to Percy's raised eyebrow. "He'll swim first thing, whatever the weather. You know he will. He gets in like it's a reunion. And then he'll stand on the dock dripping while Clarisse yells, because he forgets towels exist when there's water." Her smile went soft and wide at the thought. "So we put the warm one where he can see it. And the spare blue shirt on the pillow."

"The spare blue shirt," Percy repeated.

"The spare blue shirt." Silena went to the folded stack on the shelf and lifted the top one — faded blue camp cotton, sleeves cut off in Clarisse's unmistakable jagged lines, hem frayed, smelling of lemon oil and lake and the bottom of the canvas satchel. Everyone in Cabin Three knew that shirt. It had been Clarisse's, then the cabin's, then Percy's for one afternoon on the north beach when the wind turned. "Clean. Folded. On the pillow." She laid it precisely, smoothing the shoulders flat. "So he knows before anyone says a word that there's something here already his. That's the whole trick with shy people, Perce. You don't tell them they're welcome. You show them where their things go."

Percy watched her hands on the shirt, quick and sure, tucking the hem once the way nurses tucked sheets.

"You're good at this," he said.

"I'm good at people." She didn't look up, but her voice had the particular warmth it got when she was pleased and trying not to show it. "Somebody has to be. Clarisse is good at spears and you are good at water and I am good at knowing where the extra blanket lives." She stepped back and surveyed the bunk — quilt to be lengthened, shirt on pillow, rock on shelf, green towel on hook — and nodded once, satisfied. Then her expression shifted, perceptive and suddenly serious, and she sat down beside him on his bunk, close enough that their shoulders touched.

"You're nervous," she said. Not a question.

Percy turned the pebble over in his pocket. "A little."

"About what, love? Tell me the shape of it."

He thought. The cabin breathed around them — water under tile, Clarisse's sanding block resuming in the hall, the far-off bell for chores. The light moved a fraction across the floor.

"It's stupid," he said.

"Try me."

"It's — he's my brother. My full — you know. Same father, same — Cyclops, same — " Percy stopped, started over. "What if he's different? Forge does that. Fire and hammers and older kids. What if he comes back and I'm still the same lake kid and he's — " He made a vague gesture. "Bigger. In every way. What if he doesn't need the canoe anymore. What if he'd rather be at the forges."

Silena listened the way she always listened, completely still, hands folded in her lap, eyes on his face.

"And," Percy said, quieter, "what if he asks about Atlantis."

The name sat in the small space between them. Atlantis. The palace under the sea. The roof that hummed. The father who taught him everything and then let him go. Percy hadn't said the word aloud in a week, not since the evening on the north beach with Clarisse, and saying it now felt like opening a door he kept meaning to lock.

"He won't ask to hurt you," Silena said gently.

"I know. He'll ask because he misses it. Because it's home. And I'll — " Percy looked at his hands. "I don't know what I'll say. Some days I miss it so much I can't breathe, and some days I stand on the dock here and the lake's so — it's so small, Silena. You can see the other side. And I think, that's the whole of it, that's all the water I get now, and I'm glad, and then I'm so guilty about being glad I want to throw up."

Silena was quiet a moment. Then she reached over and took his hand, lacing their fingers the way she did when words were the wrong tool, her palm warm and faintly sticky with honey from breakfast prep.

"Listen to me, little brother," she said. "You left a palace to live in a cabin with two girls who fight over the good spear and sing in the pavilion for honey. That is a very strange choice, and it is yours, and you get to keep making it every day without apologizing for the days it's hard." She squeezed his fingers. "If Tyson asks, you tell him the true part. That you miss the reef and you love the lake. Both things fit. Hearts are bigger than maps."

Percy leaned his head against her shoulder. She smelled of honey and lemon oil and lake damp, and her shirt was soft where his forehead rested.

"Is the food ready?" he mumbled into her sleeve.

She laughed, bright and sudden, and ruffled his hair hard enough to hurt. "The food is never ready. The food is a process. Come on. If you're going to mope, you can mope while pitting peaches."

---

The pavilion kitchen at ten in the morning smelled of yeast and brown butter and the particular green sweetness of September peaches going soft in their crates, and Silena moved through it like a tide through a familiar channel.

Percy sat on the upturned crate by the prep counter with a paring knife and a bowl, pitting peaches the way she'd shown him — thumb in the seam, twist, coax — while she worked the big board beside him, slicing bread from yesterday's loaves into thick honest slabs, her hands quick and flour-dusted, her tied shirt loosened now against the kitchen heat, the pencil still in her bun with a streak of flour across one cheek to match the lemon oil on the other.

Ms. O'Fee stood over them both like a thundercloud with opinions, wings furled tight indoors, arms crossed, eyes narrowed at the growing pile of peach halves.

"That's the good honey," the harpy announced, as Silena reached for the jar on the high shelf. "That's the apiary jar. That's the treaty jar."

"It's for Tyson," Silena said without turning. "He's coming Friday."

"I know who's coming Friday. The whole camp knows who's coming Friday. The forges have been insufferable about it." Ms. O'Fee unfolded one wing just enough to tap the jar with a primary feather. "Treaty honey is for camp business."

"This is cabin business," Percy said around a peach pit, "which is bigger."

"Don't talk with pits in your mouth, boy. You'll choke and I'll have paperwork." But the wing-tip softened, and when Silena sang — quietly, under her breath, the little sailing song with no words she trusted, while she drizzled honey over the bread and layered peach slices on top — the harpy turned her head away and pretended to count the butter pats, and when they left, the jar was wrapped in brown paper with a second pat of butter laid on top like a seal, and a heel of cheese pressed into Percy's hands "for the road, and don't tell the Ares table."

They carried the crate up the hill between them, Silena humming, Percy balancing the cheese, the morning going gold at the edges as the slate burned off and the sun came over the pines. The path switched back through shade that smelled of cold needles and sun-warmed bark, and every opening to the left showed the lake lying flat and gray-blue and enormous, freshwater-clean, the far shore a low green line under a sky going blue by inches.

"Question," Percy said, shifting the crate to his other hand. "What does he eat for breakfast? I mean — does he still do the — "

"The peanut butter by the spoonful with the honey on top, standing in front of the open pantry like a bear at a stream?" Silena grinned. "Probably. Forge kids eat like furnaces. Beckendorf says he has three breakfasts." She nudged him with her elbow. "Which is why we're making the bread-and-peach packets, and the cheese, and the cookies if the oven behaves, and why Clarisse is pretending she's not smoking fish for him tonight."

"She's smoking fish?"

"She's 'checking the smokehouse.' With wood chips. And brine. And a string she measured." Silena's eyes danced. "She told me it was patrol maintenance. I told her patrol maintenance doesn't smell like brown sugar."

Percy laughed, and the sound startled a jay out of the pines ahead, and they walked on in the companionable quiet that had become, over the summer, one of the languages of Cabin Three.

At the top of the switchback the path opened onto the green, and there, coming down from the Big House with a clipboard and a smudge of charcoal on her jaw, was Annabeth.

Percy felt it first in his hands — the crate suddenly lighter — and then in his face, which did the loosening thing Silena had once described as his lake face, except this was his Annabeth face and everybody in his cabin knew that one too.

She wore the gray camp shirt with the blind-faded hem she'd been living in all week because it had real pockets, sleeves shoved up past her elbows, the collar up against the morning chill, her jeans cuffed once over worn boots scuffed white at the toes, her hair pulled back in a knot that was coming down on one side, a pencil behind her ear and another in her hand and a third, somehow, clipped to the clipboard. Olive Nakamura trailed half a step behind her, small and earnest, clutching a rolled map and a fistful of colored markers, eyes wide at the sight of the crate.

"Cartography break," Annabeth announced, stopping in the path. Her eyes went to the crate, to the brown paper, to the honey seal visible at the corner. Precise as ever. "That's treaty honey. You sang for it."

"I sang for it," Silena confirmed gravely. "Worth it."

"Smells worth it." Annabeth's gaze flicked to Percy, and the clipboard lowered a fraction, and her whole posture did the small, specific softening it only ever did for him. "Hi."

"Hi," Percy said, and suddenly the peaches and the cheese and the whole morning were too much to hold politely. "You're — mapping?"

"Drainage. North path. Olive found a seep I missed." Annabeth tilted the clipboard so he could see — neat contour lines, blue ink for water, a red X where the seep was, Olive's smaller handwriting in the margin in green. "She's good. She sees downhill the way you see current."

Olive glowed. "Annabeth says water always tells the truth about a hill."

"Annabeth says a lot of things," Percy said, "and most of them are right, which is annoying for the rest of us."

"Damn right." Annabeth stepped closer, close enough that he could smell charcoal and pine soap and the faint lemon she kept in her pocket for tea. She looked at the crate, then at Silena, then back at him, planning already. "Friday. Bus. What time?"

"Before lunch," Percy said. "Somebody meets it with the hoodie."

"Somebody," Silena said innocently, "with the torn pocket."

"I'll come," Annabeth said. "If that's — " She glanced at Silena, quick and careful, the official cartographer suddenly fourteen and uncertain at the edge of someone else's family business. "If it's okay. Not the landing. The — after. To say hi. Thalia wants to come too. She said — " Annabeth's mouth did the dry, fond thing it did around her sister. "She said somebody has to make sure the Cyclops knows which Grace sister is the dangerous one."

Silena laughed and reached out and squeezed Annabeth's wrist once, warm and certain. "Of course you come. Both of you. Sunday's ours, but Friday's everybody's. He'd be hurt if you didn't. He asked after you in the drawing. He drew you with a — what was it, Perce?"

"A compass," Percy said. "A big one. Like a spear."

Annabeth's ears went pink. She covered it by flipping the clipboard and making a note in the margin — cove, Friday, hoodie — in her small exact hand. "Tell Clarisse I'll bring the north-beach survey. He might like to see where — " She stopped. "Where his rock came from. Olive and I mapped the shelf."

Percy looked at her. She looked at him. Olive looked tactfully at the trees.

"Come here," Silena said, and took the crate from Percy's hands, and took Olive's markers before they could fall, and turned both herself and Olive around with the efficiency of a girl who had managed a hundred such moments. "Olive, love, show me this seep. Is it the one by the root stair?"

And Percy and Annabeth stood in the middle of the path in the morning sun with the pines on both sides and the lake flashing through the trees, and he took her hand — flour on his, charcoal on hers — and she leaned in, and they kissed, brief and warm and certain, the way you kiss someone you have kissed a hundred times and intend to kiss a hundred more, her free hand coming up to rest against his jaw, his thumb against her wrist where her pulse ran quick.

"Hi," she said again, quieter, against his mouth.

"Hi," he said. "I missed you. It's been — "

"Six hours."

"Six long hours of peaches."

She laughed, and rested her forehead against his for a second, and the clipboard pressed awkwardly between them, and neither of them moved it.

"I'm glad he's coming," she murmured. "You light up when you talk about him. It's — " She searched for the precise word, found it. "It's structural. Like a load-bearing wall."

"That's the most romantic thing you've ever said."

"I know. Don't let Thalia hear. She'll put it on my plaque." Annabeth pulled back just enough to look at him properly, gray eyes clear and planning and fond all at once. "Percy. About Sunday. I know it's cabin-only. I know the rule — "

"There is no rule," Percy said quickly. "There's a — Clarisse says — it's free for Poseidon cabin, always was, and everybody else gets ten minutes retroactive, except — " He stopped. "Except you don't. You never did. You're not an occurrence."

Annabeth's eyebrows went up. "I'm not an occurrence."

"Her words. Last month. On the dock." He held her hand tighter. "You can come Sunday if you want. Anytime. You don't need — "

"I know." She squeezed back. "But I'm not going to. Not Sunday." At his expression she shook her head, quick. "Not because I'm not — Percy, I want to. But some days are load-bearing for a family, and I'm good at maps, and I know when a structure needs to stand on its own." Her voice went very precise, which on her meant she was feeling something large and handling it carefully. "Sunday's yours. All four of you — you, Clarisse, Silena, Tyson. No audience. I'll see him Friday and Saturday. Sunday you don't have to share."

Percy stared at her. "When did you get so — "

"Wise? Mature? Devastatingly reasonable?" She smiled, small and crooked. "Thalia says it's the gray shirts. They add five years." She kissed him once more, quick, on the corner of the mouth. "Go. Your sister's giving Olive the full hydrology lecture and Olive's taking notes. If you don't rescue her she'll be carrying the crate by noon."

He went, reluctantly, hand sliding out of hers finger by finger, and Silena handed back the crate with a look of warm triumph, and Olive showed him the seep mark on the map with great seriousness, and Annabeth watched him go with the clipboard against her chest and the charcoal on her jaw and the sun coming through the pines in shafts, and Percy carried the image down the hill like a second crate, lighter than the first and heavier.

Clarisse smoked fish the way she did everything that mattered, which was as though the fish had personally insulted her mother and she intended to preserve the evidence.

The smokehouse stood behind the pavilion in a hollow of pines, low and dark and smelling of old fires and brine, and by late afternoon the thin blue smoke was rising through the trees in a steady column, carrying the smell of brown sugar and salt and applewood down the hill toward the lake. Percy found her there with her sleeves rolled to the shoulder, her braid coiled up under a bandana, her cutoff shirt dark with sweat at the collar, turning fillets on the rack with a pair of long tongs while Beckendorf leaned against the doorframe with his arms crossed, giving advice she was ignoring on principle.

"You're crowding the rack," Beckendorf said.

"I'm spacing for airflow."

"You're crowding for spite."

"I'm spacing for airflow and crowding for spite. They're not exclusive." Clarisse flipped a fillet with a flick that sent a bead of brine hissing onto the coals. "He likes the brown-sugar ones. July he ate four and then fell asleep on the bench with the bones in a pile like a cat. Silena wrapped the leftovers and he carried them in his pocket all afternoon." She didn't look up. "So I'm making double. One batch for Friday night, one for Sunday. The Sunday ones stay in the cold box till morning. Nobody touches them. I counted."

"Counted and wrote it down," Beckendorf confirmed to Percy, pushing off the frame. His forge shirt was dark with soot at the cuffs, his hands black at the knuckles, his smile tired and fond. "He's excited, man. You should hear him at the forge. Talks about the lake like it's — " Beckendorf searched. "Like it's a person. Says the water here's different. Says it holds you different."

Percy's chest did the tight bright thing again. "It does."

"Yeah?" Beckendorf studied him a moment, then nodded, as if something had been confirmed. "He built you something. Won't show me. Says it's for the canoe." He clapped Percy's shoulder once, hard enough to stagger him, and headed back up the hill toward the forges with his soot-cuffed sleeves flapping. "See you Friday, little brother."

Little brother. From Beckendorf, who was eighteen and built like a door, it landed like a medal. Percy stood in the smoke with his eyes watering, watching Clarisse turn the fish with her jaw set and her bandana slipping.

"Need help?" he asked.

"Hold the brine bowl." She thrust it at him without looking. "And don't breathe on the coals."

He held the bowl. The smoke stung and sweetened everything — his shirt, his hair, the pebble in his pocket, probably. His thermal clung at the back of the neck. The late sun came through the pines in low gold bars, and the air had the particular September quality of warmth that knew it was borrowed. Somewhere up the hill someone was practicing the war horn, badly, in short uncertain blasts.

"He built something for the canoe," Percy said.

"He builds something for everything." Clarisse's tongs paused. "July he fixed the loose cleat on the dock with a horseshoe nail and a piece of his own belt. It's still there. Chiron tried to replace it and I told him to leave it." Her voice went grudging and low. "You know what he said when he did it? He said, 'So it holds.' That's all. Like the dock was a person who needed holding."

Percy turned the brine bowl slowly in his hands. "Clarisse. Can I ask — "

"No."

" — what you're afraid of? About Friday?"

She went very still. Then she set the tongs down across the rack with exaggerated precision and turned to face him, arms crossed, bandana slipping further, smoke curling between them.

"I'm not afraid," she said. "I'm — " She stopped. Started again, blunter, the way she always did when the truth cost her. "He's coming from fire. Forges. Hammers. Older kids who swear and stay up. And we're — " She gestured at the smokehouse, the fish, the hill, the whole small green order of camp. "Quilts and towels and peaches. What if we're boring now? What if he walks in and we're still the same lake kids and he's — " She made the same vague gesture Percy had made that morning to Silena, and the resemblance landed between them like a dropped tool. "Bigger."

Percy stared. "That's what I said. This morning. To Silena."

"Well." Clarisse picked up the tongs again, examining them as if they were at fault. "We're siblings. We share a father and a lake and bad ideas." A beat. "What did she say?"

"She said hearts are bigger than maps."

"That's a Silena answer." But some of the wire went out of Clarisse's shoulders. "She's right. She's always right about that stuff and it's infuriating." She turned a fillet, decisively. "He's not bored by quilts, Jackson. July he cried because Silena put a flower on his pillow. A dandelion. Full tears. Said it was the prettiest thing in the cabin. And our cabin has a pool with a mosaic horse at the bottom of it."

Percy grinned. "He's — he's Tyson."

"He's Tyson," Clarisse agreed, and the name in her mouth sounded like something held carefully, the way you'd hold a bird. "Gruff — no. I'm gruff. He's — " She searched. "He's big and he's gentle and he snores like a landslide and he eats the bones if you don't watch him, and he's my brother, and I'm smoking double because last time there wasn't enough, and that's the whole of it, and if you tell Thalia I got sentimental over fish I'll deny it and make you run laps."

"I won't tell."

"Good." She handed him the tongs. "Turn those. I need to check the cold box."

She went, boots crunching on needles, bandana trailing one tail, and Percy stood alone in the smoke turning fish with exaggerated care, the brine bowl at his feet, the gold light going amber through the pines, thinking about brothers and quilts and the particular, unrepeatable luck of being prepared for by someone who loved you gruffly enough to count the fillets.

Thalia found him there.

She came down the hill through the smoke like something the smoke had decided to deliver, tall and pale-eyed and moving with the loose, predatory ease of a girl who had spent the day at the archery range and won. She wore her black camp shirt with the sleeves cut off, silver bracelets stacked to the elbow, her jeans torn at one knee and her boots laced loose, her dark hair pulled back in a ponytail going frizzy at the edges from range dust, her bow unstrung over one shoulder and a quiver of blunted arrows rattling at her hip. Eighteen and utterly certain of it.

"Jackson," she said by way of greeting, wrinkling her nose at the smoke. "You smell like breakfast."

"Brown sugar," Percy said. "It's for Tyson."

"I know. The whole hill smells like your brother's visit." She leaned against the smokehouse wall, arms crossed, protective edge already in her voice even through the teasing. "Annabeth says you're meeting him Friday at the cove with a hoodie with a torn pocket. She made a note. In ink. It's on the official camp schedule now. Nothing says family like documentation."

"She's — she's good," Percy said. "She said Sunday's ours. No audience."

Thalia's eyebrows went up. Then she nodded slowly, dryly. "That's my sister. Fourteen going on forty. She once gave me a seating chart for breakfast." A pause. "She's right, though. Sunday you don't share. Friday you share with everybody, because Beckendorf's kids will mob him, and Saturday you share with me, because I have plans."

"What plans?"

"Secret plans." Thalia pushed off the wall and stole a brine-soaked fingertip of brown sugar off the rack edge, ignoring Percy's protest. "No. Not secret. I'm taking him to the range. He asked. July. Said he wanted to see the bows. I'll put a bow in his hands bigger than he is and let him snap three strings and then tell him he's a natural." Her grin went wide and unguarded for a second. "He laughed last time, Percy. Full-body. Like — like thunder deciding to be nice."

Percy turned a fillet to hide his face. "You like him."

"Everybody likes him. That's his whole deal. He's the only person at this camp Clarisse has never once threatened, which, given Clarisse, is basically sainthood." Thalia's voice dropped, teasing giving way to something older-sibling and level. "You okay? About Friday?"

It was the third time that day someone had asked him some version of the question, and Percy found, to his surprise, that the answer had changed shape since morning.

"Yeah," he said. "I think — yeah. This morning I was — what if he's different. Now I'm — " He gestured with the tongs. "What if we're ready. We made the quilt longer. We smoked double. Silena put the shirt on the pillow. Clarisse oiled the paddle. It's — it's a lot of love for one brother."

Thalia studied him a moment. Then she reached out and ruffled his hair the way Silena had that morning, hard enough to hurt, spearing-violent and sisterly all at once.

"That's the job, sea boy," she said. "Preparing for someone you love is how you find out how much room you made for them without noticing." She took the tongs from him, turned the last fillet herself with practiced ease, and handed them back. "And for the record? He's going to walk in and see the towels and the shirt and the fish and he's going to cry. Full tears. Onto Clarisse. And she's going to stand there with lake water — or fish smoke — up to her knees and hold him up and pretend she's annoyed. And you're going to stand there with your pebble in your pocket and your sister's shirt on your back and think, this is my family, and you're going to be right."

Percy blinked smoke out of his eyes. "When did you get — "

"Wise? Mature? Devastatingly — " Thalia grinned. "It's genetic. Grace sisters. We come that way. Don't tell Annabeth I said anything sincere. She'll put it on my plaque."

Clarisse returned with the cold-box key on a string, stopped dead at the sight of Thalia, and said, "Ten minutes," without a flicker.

"Retroactive?" Thalia said.

"Always." But Clarisse was already turning back to the rack, hiding the small, involuntary smile. "Touch the fish and die, Grace."

"Wouldn't dream of it, La Rue. Your fish is load-bearing."

Percy caught Thalia's eye over Clarisse's bandana'd head. Load-bearing. He mouthed it. Thalia winked, unrepentant, and stole a second fingertip of sugar on her way out, dodging the tongs Clarisse threw — blunt end first, sister-style — with the ease of long practice.

The boathouse at dusk smelled of varnish and rope and the green cold breath of the freshwater lake, and the repaired canoe sat on its sawhorses in the middle of the floor like something waiting to be believed in.

Percy ran his palm along the hull. The green paint was new over old — you could see the ghost of every patch under the gloss if you looked slantwise in lamplight — and the port gunwale still showed the pale scar where the rot had been cut out and new cedar scarfed in, smooth under his thumb. Hull-good, rope-good, fine. He had made Chiron write it down, and Chiron had, in his neat hand, with the date, and Percy had touched the ink to make sure it was real.

The locker stood against the back wall under the bronze plaque that said LAKESIDE, 1978, two padlocks through the hasp, the check-in slate and the spare tally sheets in oilcloth inside — Annabeth's hand, thirty-nine marks, kept for the pair rule. Percy looked at it, nodded to it the way you'd nod to a dog asleep in a corner — acknowledgment without disturbance — and turned back to the canoe.

"You're talking to furniture again," Annabeth said from the doorway.

She stood in the last of the daylight with her clipboard finally abandoned somewhere, her gray shirt untucked on both sides now, sleeves still shoved up, charcoal still on her jaw, hair coming further down from its knot, pockets full — he could see the shapes — of map tools and a folded survey and, unmistakably, the heel of cheese Ms. O'Fee had pressed on him at noon, which he had given her on the path and which she had kept. Behind her the lake lay flat and rose-gold at the edges, the willows at the cove end gone dark, the heron — he couldn't see from here, but he knew — still on the bow of the half-sunk skiff in the shade, gray and still and witnessed.

"I'm not talking," Percy said. "I'm communing. It's a Poseidon thing."

"It's a Percy thing." She came in, boots quiet on the worn boards, and stood beside him at the hull, shoulder to shoulder, looking at the scar in the gunwale. "Olive asked me today if the canoe was new. I told her it was old and repaired, which is better. She asked why. I told her repaired things have biographies."

Percy looked at her. "That's good. That's — did you make that up?"

"I read it somewhere. Doesn't matter. It's true here." She ran one finger along the scar, precise and gentle. "He'll like this. Tyson. Beckendorf says he built something for it."

"So I've heard." Percy leaned his hip against the sawhorse. "Nobody will show me. It's a conspiracy of Cyclopes and forges."

"It's a surprise. Let it be a surprise." Annabeth tucked the cheese further into her pocket and looked at him properly, gray eyes clear in the lamplight. "Show me the rest. The preparations. You have the face."

"What face?"

"The face where you've inventoried everything three times and you want to inventory it out loud to someone who'll listen." She took his hand, charcoal and flour and varnish all together. "Inventory me, Percy. Start at the bunk and end at the lake."

So he did. Walking the boathouse floor in the lamplight with her hand in his, he laid it out the way she'd taught him to lay out a survey — in order, with landmarks. Corner bunk, slats checked, quilt being lengthened with blue fabric the right weight, rock dustless on the shelf, spare blue shirt folded on the pillow, green towel on the hook because warm beats fast-drying in September. Paddle oiled, blade sanded, sighted down like a spear. Bread-and-peach packets in brown paper with treaty honey and butter seals. Cheese. Cookies if the oven behaved. Double fish, brown-sugar, Friday batch and Sunday batch, counted and written down, cold box locked. Hoodie washed, pocket torn, ready for Friday. Sunday cabin-only, no audience, whole day, Chiron wrote it down.

Annabeth listened the way she always listened, completely, head tipped, thumb moving slowly across his knuckles.

"That's good," she said when he finished. "That's — Percy, that's thorough. That's love with a checklist."

"It's Clarisse's checklist. And Silena's. I just — hold things."

"You hold the paddle. You hold the ankle." She said it simply, and the Group Float rose between them without either naming it — Percy's summer education, Silena's invention, Clarisse's claimed credit, the true thing with ears under. "That's not just. That's the job."

The lamplight flickered. Outside, the frogs had started in the marsh, one confident voice and then fifty. The air through the open doors had gone cool enough that Annabeth's collar was up and Percy's thermal suddenly felt like foresight.

"Walk me to the lake?" he said.

"Always."

They left the canoe in its lamplight and walked down the ramp to the water with their hands joined and their shoulders touching, past Annabeth's good skiff with the cord she always tied because he always forgot, to the end of the dock where the planks were still warm from the day. The lake lay before them glassy and enormous, freshwater-dark, holding the last of the rose in its middle and the first stars at its edges, the far shore a black cut-paper line, the boundary rock out in the middle with its white X just visible. The air smelled of wet stone and leaf-fall and the faint mineral breath of a lake letting go of heat. No wind. The flag on the Big House pole, invisible up the hill, would be hanging straight down its rope.

They sat with their feet in the water. It was cold — September cold, honest cold — and warm underneath where the day lingered, the way the lake held its summer deep down and gave it back slowly. Percy rolled his jeans higher. Annabeth uncuffed hers and recuffed them narrower, precise even here.

"Tell me about him," Annabeth said. "Not the inventory. Him."

Percy thought. The water moved against their ankles. A loon called once from the cove direction and didn't call again.

"He's — big," Percy said. "You know that. Everybody knows that. But he's — he's careful. He picks things up like they might break, even rocks. Especially rocks." He turned the pebble over in his pocket, then drew it out and held it flat on his palm between them, white and smooth in the starlight. "July he found this shelf on the north beach — you mapped it — where the stones come up white. He sat there for an hour sorting them. Not building. Sorting. By — I don't know. By something only he could see. And when I asked he said, 'So they have friends.'"

Annabeth looked at the pebble, then at him, her face doing the softening thing it only ever did for him.

"He gave me this one," Percy said. "Said it was the bravest. I asked what made it brave and he said it was the smallest and it came anyway." His voice went thick. He cleared it. "I carry it everywhere. It's — it's stupid."

"It's not stupid." Annabeth closed his fingers over the pebble gently and held her hand over his. "It's structural."

He laughed, wetly. "There's that word again."

"It's a good word. It's staying." She leaned her head against his shoulder, and he leaned his cheek against her hair, pine soap and charcoal and lake, and they sat that way while the stars came out over the far shore and the water moved against their ankles and the dock posts stood around them like patient animals.

"I keep thinking about Atlantis," Percy said at last, quietly, to the lake. Not a speech. Just the thing surfacing, the way things surfaced in September water. "Not — not going back. Just — he'll smell like the forges. Fire and iron. And I'll smell like — " He lifted a handful of lake water and let it fall. "Like this. And he'll ask about the reef, and I'll tell him, and it'll be true, and then we'll go out in the canoe, and the lake'll hold him, and — " He stopped. "Does that make sense? That I'm afraid of a conversation and looking forward to it at the same time?"

"Yes," Annabeth said. Simply. Precisely. "That's what missing people feels like when they're about to stop being missed." She lifted her head and kissed him, slow and warm, her hand against his jaw, his hand against her wrist, the lake moving under them and the stars doing their work overhead. "You're allowed to miss the reef and love the lake. Somebody wise told you that this morning."

"Silena tells everybody that."

"Silena's right. She's always right about that stuff." Annabeth smiled against his mouth. "Don't tell her I said so. She'll put it on my plaque."

They sat a while longer, cuddled close on the end of the dock with the quilt — Silena's quilt, the one that didn't smell like the boathouse except it did, but it was a good smell — pulled over their shoulders, all warmth and lake-breath and shared weight. Percy told her about the horseshoe-nail cleat that held, and the dandelion on the pillow, and the bus ticket in the torn pocket, and Annabeth told him about Olive's seep and the compass Tyson had drawn her with, big as a spear, and the north-beach survey she'd bring Friday, and the evening went blue around them by inches.

When the cold finally found their feet, they stood, folded the quilt, tied the skiff cord — Annabeth, because Percy always forgot — and walked up the hill hand in hand through the pines, past the smokehouse with its dying column of blue, past the pavilion with its lanterns ticking on, to the fork where the paths divided for the cabins.

"Friday," Annabeth said at the fork. "Bus. Hoodie. I'll bring the survey."

"Friday," Percy agreed. He kissed her once more, brief and certain, forehead to forehead after. "Thank you. For — Sunday. For letting it be ours."

"It's already yours," she said. "I'm just reading the map correctly." She squeezed his hand once, hard, and went up the Zeus path with her pockets full of tools and her collar up and her hair coming down, turning once to wave before the pines took her.

Percy walked the last stretch alone, the quilt over one shoulder, the pebble in his pocket, the lake-smell in his clothes. The sky over Cabin Three was full of stars. The door sighed when he opened it. Inside, the pool breathed its small night breath, the towels hung in their row, the corner bunk lay ready with its shirt on the pillow and its rock on the shelf, and from the kitchen came the smell of cooling fish and honey-bread, and Clarisse's boots by the door, and Silena's sandals with the hair tie through the strap, and the whole small, complete order of a house that had spent a week preparing for someone it loved.

He stood in the doorway a moment, taking inventory one last time — bunk, paddle, fish, hoodie, quilt, rock, shirt, towel — and found everything in its place, and himself, for once, exactly where he belonged inside the list.

Clarisse checked the hoodie twice because checking once was for people who trusted tides.

The cabin had gone quiet in the particular way it went quiet after lights-out, pool breathing low at the end of the hall, Silena's needle hissing faintly through cloth in the bunk alcove, Percy asleep already with one arm flung past his quilt and his mouth open. Clarisse sat on the edge of her bunk in her sleep shirt and her one remaining sock, the spare blue hoodie spread across her knees, working the torn pocket with her fingers the way a medic works a wound — assessing, not admiring.

The tear was exactly as Tyson liked it. She'd washed the hoodie three days ago and hung it on the line behind Cabin Three, and the September wind had smelled it faintly of pine and cold, and she'd folded it twice and unfolded it twice since, and now she folded it a third time, smoothing the shoulders, tucking the strings in so they wouldn't catch. The tide. Friday. Before lunch, Chiron had written, in the neat hand that meant he'd already confirmed it twice. Somebody meets him. That somebody was Percy — first in line, pebble in pocket, hoodie over his arm — with Clarisse three paces behind because little brothers needed room to run at big brothers without an audience of one fierce sister, and Silena beside her with the bread packet because somebody had to think of food.

She'd told Chiron exactly that. He'd written it down. The ink had been real.

Clarisse ran her thumb along the pocket's torn edge one last time and thought about July. About lake water up to her knees and a weight coming down on her shoulder like a felled tree, and arms that could bend iron going around her like she was the thing that needed holding. About a voice in her ear saying her name the way nobody else said it — not La Rue, not Clarisse, the whole thing, careful, like a word in a language he was still learning. About standing there in the shallows with her boots full of water thinking, if anyone laughs at this boy I will end them, and then realizing nobody was laughing, because the lake had gone quiet around them the way water goes quiet for its own.

"He's my brother too." She said it aloud, into the dark, to no one, testing the weight. It held. It had held all summer, through the panel and the tallies and the cold seam in the cove and every night she'd lain awake doing arithmetic on staying. People did the math on Percy and Tyson and forgot there was a third term. Same father. Same lake. Same towels on the same hooks.

From the alcove came the soft bite of thread, the shake of cloth. The quilt, lengthened. Silena's work. Enough for forge-grown inches and cold feet.

Clarisse folded the hoodie a final time, laid it on the foot of the corner bunk where Percy would see it first thing, and set her message money — two drachmas for the Iris-message, one to send and one spare — on top of it like a paperweight.

"Message money's mine," she muttered to the empty bunk. "He loses his in the pocket. That's the system. Don't argue."

The bunk said nothing, which was agreement. She pulled her sock off, threw it at the laundry basket, missed on purpose so morning-Clarisse would have something to be annoyed about, and climbed under her blanket.

Percy was asleep when Silena finished the quilt.

She worked by the single lamp in the bunk alcove long after the pool had gone dark and Clarisse's breathing had gone deep and even in the next bunk, the blue fabric pinned and basted, her needle moving in the small, sure stitches she'd learned at nine from a girl who'd left camp the next summer. The air through the high window had gone sharp enough to raise gooseflesh on her bare arms, and she'd pulled Percy's borrowed thermal — returned, washed, folded — over her shoulders against it, the sleeves too long, the cuffs turned back. September sewing. The season's first real work.

She knotted the last stitch, bit the thread, shook the quilt out once to settle it, and laid it over the corner bunk with the blue panel at the foot, smoothing it flat with both palms. It hung past the frame now by a hand's breadth. Enough for tall boys with cold feet. Enough for forge-grown inches.

On the pillow: the spare blue shirt, folded. On the shelf: the round white rock, centered. On the hook: the green towel, where he could see it.

Silena stood back, needle still between her teeth, hands on her hips, and surveyed it the way she'd surveyed the picnic on the north beach rock a month ago — stage set, players not yet arrived.

From the next bunk Clarisse muttered in her sleep, turning over, one arm flung out past the blanket. "...paddle's... oiled..." she mumbled. "...tell Beckendorf... double..."

Silena smiled, took the needle from her teeth, and crossed to her sister's bunk. She drew the blanket up over Clarisse's bare shoulder, tucked it once the way nurses tucked sheets, and stood a moment looking down at her — fifteen, fierce, gruffly and entirely beloved, braid come loose, mouth open, one sock on and one sock off.

"You're a good sister," Silena whispered. "Don't tell Thalia I said so. She'll put it on your plaque."

Clarisse snored, faintly, like a landslide deciding to be nice.

Silena turned out the lamp. The cabin settled around her — tile cooling, water moving under stone, three breaths and one empty bunk waiting for its fourth. Through the high window the stars stood over the pines, and somewhere down the hill the lake lay flat and dark and patient, holding the canoe and the skiff and the heron's reflection and the whole long summer going quiet.

Friday, the tide. Sunday, the lake. Between now and then, nothing to do but wait, and waiting — Silena knew, folding her needle into its case — was just another kind of holding.

She climbed into her bunk, pulled the quilt to her chin, and slept.
