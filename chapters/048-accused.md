## Chapter 48: Accused

By early June, the snow and the whispers of the winter council were weeks behind them. The whispers had never quite stopped. Percy knew something was wrong before the conch blew because the sky went still.

It was morning, early, the kind of blue-white morning after a night of stars. He had walked down to the lake with a towel over his shoulder and his teeth still unbrushed, meaning to swim before breakfast. The water was flat. Too flat. No ripple, no wind. The naiads were gone from the dock. At the far end, the fish had stopped jumping.

Above the cabins, the clouds had lined up in straight rows, like corduroy.

Tyson found him there. Tyson, visiting from the undersea forges, huge and gentle, with one eye and hands that could bend horseshoes and a look on his face Percy had only seen twice before.

"Brother," Tyson said. "Sky father comes."

Percy stared up. "Zeus? Here? Now?"

Tyson nodded. He held out Percy's shoes, which Percy had left by Cabin Three. "Miss Silena says dress right. Miss Clarisse says bring your knife."


---

Percy sat on the edge of the dock to put on his shoes.

It should not have been difficult. He had tied shoelaces every morning for years, in both lives. But his hands were cold, and the first knot came apart when he pulled it. He stared at the loose ends, angry with them for being such a small problem when a much larger one was on its way.

Tyson waited at the foot of the dock. He was carrying the satchel he used at the forges, its canvas blackened at the corners and its strap repaired with a length of red cord. He had not brought any weapons. Percy knew because he could usually hear the collection of odd metal things his brother considered useful knocking together inside the bag.

"You heard from the palace?" Percy asked.

Tyson shook his head. "Heard from little bronze helper. Helper heard from wind."

"That's a terrible chain of evidence."

"Wind was frightened," Tyson said seriously.

Percy looked out across the Sound. The clouds had not moved. Even the gulls were absent. He had always loved mornings when the water held perfectly still, but this was not calm. This was a person holding his breath, waiting for someone to decide whether he could let it out.

"Does Poseidon know?"

"Maybe." Tyson crouched, looking at the failed knot. "You want me to tie?"

"No, I've got it."

Percy tried again. This time the loops stayed. Tyson nodded as though he had just watched Percy repair an important piece of machinery.

"You have to go back to the forges today?" Percy asked.

"I told master I might be late." Tyson touched the patched satchel. "Had to bring word. You are brother."

Percy felt his throat tighten. The visit had been meant to last another two days. Tyson was staying in the guest cabin, enjoying the chance to meet people who didn't have to shout through water to speak to him. He had spent most of yesterday fixing a bent hinge on the dining pavilion door. Now the ordinary pleasures of his visit had been interrupted by the same divine argument that was interrupting everything else.

"Sorry," Percy said.

Tyson looked puzzled. "For what?"

"For your trip getting ruined."

"Trip is not ruined. I am here." He sat down on the boards beside Percy. They creaked under his weight. "Also, hinge is fixed."

"That's true."

"Door will still work when sky father leaves."

Percy wanted that to be the answer to everything. Fix a hinge, keep the door working, and trust that anybody who wanted to come home could find their way in.

He glanced at Tyson's hands, huge and scarred from the forge. "You know if he starts saying something about Cyclopes, I won't let him talk to you like that."

Tyson studied the motionless water.

"He is king," he said.

"I know."

"King can speak. Brother can also speak."

Percy nodded. He was sure Tyson meant the words simply. That was the remarkable thing about him. He saw no reason to make a complicated problem sound clever.

"Will you stand with us?"

Tyson looked toward the cabin rows, where a conch began to sound. "Chiron asked visitors to wait away from green. Too many people."

Percy hadn't known that. He started to protest, then remembered how many campers there would be and how easily the gathering could become dangerous.

"Do you want to stand by the dock instead?"

"I want to watch door," Tyson said. "If people get scared, they need to get inside."

Percy pictured children running toward a building whose hinge Tyson had repaired only yesterday. It was a better plan than Percy's first impulse, which had been to stay beside his brother and dare Zeus to object.

"Okay," he said. "I wish I could be in two places."

"Nobody has two feet in two places," Tyson answered.

Percy looked down at his shoes, both tied now, and laughed despite the pressure in his chest.

"I think that's the worst thing anybody has ever said to make me feel better."

Tyson smiled. "Worked."

At the path leading uphill, Percy turned back. Tyson was already checking the hinge on the small boathouse door, testing it twice with his shoulder before looking for a way to brace it if the wind rose.

Percy climbed toward camp. He hadn't asked Tyson to protect him, but his brother had found something useful to protect, and for the moment that was enough.

---

By the time Percy got back up the hill, Camp Half-Blood had turned itself inside out trying to look neat.

The paths had been swept. The Hermes kids had been chased out of the rafters of the dining pavilion. The Ares cabin had actually made their bunks, which Clarisse said later was proof the world was ending. Chiron stood in front of the Big House in his full tweed jacket, wheelchair form, hooves polished. Mr. D sat in a lawn chair with a diet soda, wearing a shirt that for once did not have a stain on it, which frightened Travis Stoll so badly he walked into a post.

All the campers stood in rows on the green. No one talked above a whisper.

Cabin Three stood together. That was habit. Clarisse on the left, spear grounded but in her hand. Silena in the middle, fourteen and calm, hair pulled back tight, her shawl folded square over her arm. Percy on the right, twelve, hair still wet from splashing his face, jacket zipped wrong.

"You zipped it crooked," Silena whispered, and fixed it without looking, fingers quick. "Breathe."

"I am breathing," Percy whispered back.

"You are breathing fast. Slow down."

Clarisse did not look at either of them. She stared at the sky. Her jaw was set. "Whatever he wants, we stand together. Got it?"

"Got it," Percy said.

Silena squeezed his wrist once. "Got it."

Across the green, Cabin One stood together too. Thalia Grace, sixteen, tall, her black choppy hair tied back, her silver bracelet polished. Annabeth Grace, twelve, braid neat, notebook nowhere in sight for once, hands folded. Sisters. Daughters of Zeus.

Annabeth caught Percy's eye across the grass. She lifted her eyebrows a fraction. Percy tried to smile. It did not work right.

Then the air pressed down.

Percy had felt his dad arrive before. That felt like being underwater and held up at the same time, pressure in his ears and salt on his tongue. This was different. This was dry. The hairs on his arms stood up. The grass seemed to lean away. Somewhere a metal shield rang without being touched.

Light gathered over the Big House porch. Not warm light. White-blue, too sharp to look at.

Zeus stepped out of it.

He was not huge, which was somehow worse. Percy had expected giant. Instead he was a man, tall and broad, in a dark suit that looked mortal until you saw the way the seams sparked. His beard was clipped short, gray and black. His eyes were gray, like Annabeth's, like Thalia's, but colder, with no soft edge. A faint smell of hot metal and rain followed him.

Chiron bowed his head. "My lord."

Mr. D stood up, which Percy had never seen him do for anyone. "Zeus."

Zeus did not sit. He looked out over the rows of kids like a general counting troops he did not trust.

"Camp Half-Blood," he said. His voice carried without shouting, bouncing off the porch beams. "I come with grievance."

Nobody moved. Even the Stolls were still.

Percy felt Silena's hand settle lightly on the back of his shirt, the way she did when he was small and needed to stay put.

Zeus turned his head, slow, toward Cabin Three.

"Poseidon stirs," he said. "Atlantis wakes. Old roads open that were shut. Tides answer a hand I cannot see. Storms gather without my leave and break without my word."

Clarisse's fingers tightened on her spear. Percy could hear the leather creak.

"My brother builds in the deep," Zeus went on. "He gathers his court. He arms his borders. He teaches his young ones star-names that are not ours." His gaze settled on Percy, heavy as stone. "And he sets his son among my daughters like a tide stone among reeds."

Two hundred heads turned to look at Percy.

Percy's face went hot, then cold. He thought of the hill, four blankets, Deneb high and white while Annabeth whispered She Who Draws Lines. His stomach turned.

He stepped forward before he decided to.

"My dad is not building for war," Percy said.

A ripple went through the rows. Chiron closed his eyes briefly. Mr. D mouthed something that looked like oh no.

Zeus looked at him fully now. The pressure doubled. Percy's ears popped.

"Percy Jackson," Zeus said. "Son of Poseidon. Second-born in this age to my brother's house."

"Third," Percy said. His voice shook, but he got it out. "Silena is oldest. Then Clarisse. Then me. You should get that right if you are going to accuse us."

Clarisse made a small sound beside him, half warning, half pride. Silena's hand pressed firmer against his back. Steady.

Zeus's eyes narrowed. "You defend him quickly."

"He is my dad," Percy said. "Of course I defend him."

"You are twelve."

"I know how old I am."

Someone behind him — Connor Stoll, probably — sucked in air through his teeth.

Thalia shifted at the Zeus table line. Percy saw her out of the corner of his eye, shoulders up. Annabeth stood very straight, chin up, the way she did when she was scared and refusing to show it.

Zeus took two steps down off the porch. The boards did not creak. The air crackled.

"Then hear me, son of Poseidon," he said. "Your father withholds tribute. The sea gives less and takes more. Wrecks rise that should sleep. Messengers do not return from his halls. He summons his blood to Atlantis in dreams and tells them stories to turn them from Olympus."

That last part hit Percy like a slap. Stories. The Diver with his lamp. The Net Mender. The Shield Rack. The Lighthouse. Dad's low voice through fountain water in spring, telling Clarisse she remembered right, telling Silena the mending counted, telling Percy the drawer saw how things fit.

Those were not war plans. Those were bedtime.

"They are just stories," Percy said. "He tells us where we come from. That is not turning us."

"It is seeding loyalty below," Zeus said. "Loyalty that should rise."

Clarisse stepped forward then. She could not help it. Percy knew that set to her shoulders. Thirteen and furious and done being looked at like a problem.

"With respect, my lord," Clarisse said, and her voice was anything but respectful, shaking with anger, "we are loyal. I have bled for this camp. Three summers on patrol. Two banners taken fair. I held the west woods last winter when the hellhounds broke through and I did not run."

She lifted her chin. Her spear tip caught the hard light.

"Do not call me traitor in front of my brother and sister," she said. "Do not stand there in a clean suit and say our dad is using us. You do not know him. You do not know us."

Silence. Total. Even the pines seemed to hold still.

Zeus studied Clarisse the way he might study a wave that dared to stand up.

"Daughter of Poseidon," he said. "Bold before this entire house."

"I am not bold," Clarisse said. "I am accurate."

Silena inhaled sharply. Percy wanted to grab Clarisse's sleeve and pull her back and stand in front of her at the same time.

Zeus did not strike her down. That surprised Percy more than anything. Instead the god tilted his head.

"You take his part too?" he asked Silena.

Silena stepped up even with her siblings. Fourteen, dark hair pulled back, hands empty, shawl folded. Her voice was quieter than Clarisse's, but it carried.

"I take my family's part," Silena said. "My mom writes me every month and tells me to be kind and mend what I can. My dad visited in spring and brought shell buttons for my coat because I lost one. He asked about my grades. He told Percy a star story so Percy could give it to a friend." She looked straight at Zeus. "If that is conspiracy, then I do not understand the word."

A few campers laughed, short and nervous, and stopped.

Percy felt his throat get tight. He thought of Silena refolding blankets, corners lined up. Take the green ones. They are thickest. He thought of her kissing the top of his head in the dark. That one was always yours to give.

Zeus looked from one face to the next. Percy, jaw set. Clarisse, shaking. Silena, calm and pale.

Then he turned away from them, dismissing them with the turn, and looked at the two girls in gray.

"Thalia," he said. "Annabeth."

Percy's heart dropped straight through his shoes.

Thalia stepped forward with Annabeth half a step behind her, the way she always did, older sister first. Sixteen and twelve. Both in camp orange, both with their father's eyes. The wind lifted Thalia's tied hair. Annabeth's hands were clenched at her sides.

"Father," Thalia said. She did not bow. Annabeth did not either.

Zeus regarded them. His expression changed, just slightly. Still hard, but with something else under it. Ownership.

"It grieves me to doubt my own blood," he said. "But times require watching. Poseidon's house grows close. Too close. I would know what passes there."

Percy did not understand at first. Then he did, all at once, and his hands went numb.

"Late talks. Fountain messages. Dreams of Atlantis," Zeus said. "You stand near them. You eat near them. My younger daughter shares blankets on hills to learn foreign stars."

Annabeth flinched. Percy felt sick. Someone had told him. Someone had watched the hill.

"I ask loyalty of my daughters," Zeus said. "Mark what the sea children say when they think none hear. Tell me what Poseidon whispers to his son. This is not betrayal. This is duty. You are Zeus-born. Act it."

The green was so quiet Percy could hear the flag rope ticking against the pole.

He looked at Annabeth. She stared at her father, face white. Thalia stared too, jaw working. Percy could see Thalia's bracelet flashing as her hand shook.

He thought of Thalia on the hill that night, dropping down on the blankets without asking, drinking the last of the cocoa. Good stories, Percy Jackson. Thanks for giving my sister the sky. He thought of Annabeth's hand finding his under the blanket, fingers lacing properly. He thought of the Girl Who Ran walking off the chair, footprints trailing.

Do not sit pretty. Walk off.

Chiron took half a step forward. "My lord, perhaps—"

"Be still, centaur," Zeus said without looking.

Mr. D opened his mouth, closed it.

All eyes on the Grace sisters.

Thalia spoke first. Her voice was rough.

"No."

Zeus blinked. "Repeat."

Thalia reached back without looking and found Annabeth's hand. She pulled her sister up even with her, shoulder to shoulder.

"I said no," Thalia said, louder. "We will not spy for you. Not on them. Not on anyone in this camp."

Annabeth drew in a breath. Percy could see her gathering herself, the way she did before a hard dive. She lifted her chin.

"No, Father," Annabeth said. Her voice was clear, twelve and steady. "I will not watch Percy for you. I will not report Clarisse or Silena. They are my friends. They shared chocolate and stories. They walked me back when it was cold. That is not conspiracy. That is family."

"Family is Olympus," Zeus said, cold. "Family is obedience."

"Then you do not know what family is," Thalia said. The words rang out. A couple of Ares kids gasped. "With respect."

Percy stared. His chest felt too full to breathe.

Zeus's face darkened. The sky darkened with it. The straight-row clouds bruised at the edges. A low rumble rolled over the Sound.

"You defy me," he said softly. "Before this host."

"We love you," Annabeth said quickly. Percy could hear the tears under it she would not shed. "You are our dad. But we will not do this. You ask us to trade friends for favor. We will not."

"We are Grace sisters first," Thalia said. "Daughters of Zeus second, if that is what being daughters means today."

Percy thought Annabeth might shake apart. Instead she did the bravest thing he had ever seen.

She walked away from her sister, across the grass, to Cabin Three.

Two hundred campers watched her cross the empty middle ground in silence. Her shoes scuffed. Her braid swung. She stopped beside Percy, close enough that their sleeves touched.

Then she took his hand.

Openly. Fingers through fingers, palm to palm, ink-stained and sandy and salt-rough. She lifted their joined hands just enough that Zeus could not miss it.

Percy's heart slammed so hard he thought everyone could hear it.

"I choose him," Annabeth said to her father, voice shaking but loud. "Not against you. With me. He is my friend. I like-like him, chaste and appropriate, and I am not ashamed of it. I will not pretend I do not. And I will not spy on him."

Silence. Then Thalia laughed, short and startled and proud.

Thalia walked across too. She stopped on Percy's other side and put a heavy older-sister hand on his shoulder. She put her other hand on Annabeth's shoulder.

"Backing her," Thalia said to Zeus. "Both of us or neither. That is how Grace works now."

Clarisse made a choked sound. Percy glanced at her. His fierce big sister had tears in her eyes she would kill anyone for mentioning. Silena was smiling through tears, openly, hand pressed to her mouth.

Zeus stared at their linked hands. At Thalia's hand on Percy's shoulder. At three Poseidon kids and two Zeus girls standing in one line.

"You would bind sky to sea against me?" he said.

"We are not against you," Percy said. His voice came out hoarse. Annabeth's hand anchored him. He held on. "We are just not apart. Sir."

He added sir because Silena had taught him manners would save his life one day.

Percy forced himself to keep talking, because if he stopped his knees would give. He thought of his dad's hands, large and scarred, tying a shell button onto Silena's coat in spring rain. He thought of Triton laughing through fountain static when Percy told him about capture the flag. He thought of Amphitrite's letter, read aloud in Cabin Three, telling Clarisse to oil her spear and telling Percy to eat greens.

"You say Atlantis arms its borders," Percy said. "Have you been there? Lately? It flooded twice in my dad's letters. The palace kitchens flooded. The eel pens broke. Triton spent a month fixing pipes. That is not an army. That is plumbing."

A shocked laugh burst from the Hermes rows. Chiron closed his eyes like he wanted to sink through the porch.

"My dad sends us dried kelp candy that tastes like chalk," Percy went on. "He sends Silena thread. He sends Clarisse whetstones. He sends me notes in the margins about tides. If he was planning war, he is terrible at hiding it. He would not tell bedtime stories first. He would — he would tell us to train harder. To stop sharing chocolate with Zeus kids. He never said that. He said take care of your sisters. Take care of your friends."

Clarisse stepped up beside him, shoulder hard against his. Her voice shook with fury barely held.

"You want traitors?" Clarisse said. "Look somewhere else. I stood in the west woods with a broken spear haft and held while the younger kids ran. Ask Chiron. Ask the Ares kids who saw. I did not ask whose dad was stronger first. I just held. Like the guard in the story. Like the Shield Rack. That is what Dad teaches. Stay. Hold the door. You call that disloyal?"

Silena took Clarisse's free hand, lacing tight to keep her from shaking apart. "We are not your enemy, my lord. We are twelve and thirteen and fourteen. We fold laundry. We lose buttons."

The sky rumbled again. A thin fork of lightning split a far cloud over the Sound. A few younger kids ducked.

Chiron moved then, stepping between, half-bow to Zeus, broad back to the kids, buying a second.

"My lord," Chiron said quickly, calm trainer voice, "these are children. Loyal children. What you saw as distance is merely — new closeness. It can serve Olympus, if steered. Divided cabins serve no one."

"Steered?" Zeus said.

"Taught," Chiron said. "Trusted. Give them a task together, rather than one watching the other. Let them prove loyalty by deed, not whisper."

Zeus was quiet. The pressure did not lift, but it stopped growing.

Mr. D stood and brushed off his shorts. "They are good kids, Zeus. Even the fish ones. Especially the fish ones. They tip well at the camp store. Do not smite the customers."

A startled laugh broke from somewhere in the Hermes cabin. Zeus did not smile, but the edge of the storm paused.

He looked at Annabeth's hand in Percy's.

"Release him," he told Annabeth.

"No," Annabeth said.

Percy squeezed her hand tighter so she would know he was there. Twelve and terrified and holding on in front of a god.

Zeus held her gaze a long moment. Annabeth did not look away. Thalia did not move her hand.

At last Zeus turned his eyes to Percy.

"Son of Poseidon," he said. "You claim your father plans no war."

"Yes, sir," Percy said. "He plans family dinners that go bad because Triton eats everything. He plans new currents for whales. He is not — he would not use us like that."

"And Atlantis?" Zeus said. "The waking roads? The missing messengers?"

Percy swallowed. He thought of Triton's static-filled conch message. Whale singing behind. Tell your little owl friend the sky is clear where you are. That did not sound like war. That sounded like homesick older brother.

"I do not know about messengers," Percy said honestly. "But Atlantis waking is not attacking. It is just — home waking up. Like camp in June. Doors open. Lights on. That is not against you."

"Pretty words," Zeus said.

"True words," Clarisse snapped. Then, quieter, forcing it out, "Sir."

Silena stepped forward half a pace. "My lord, if you fear our house, come see it. Not with spears. Come to dinner. My mom makes fish stew when she visits. Dad tells bad jokes. Percy snores. Clarisse steals blankets. It is not a war room. It is a cabin with laundry."

That drew real laughter, scattered and relieved. Even Chiron smiled.

Zeus did not laugh. But he looked at Silena longer than the others, as if her calm puzzled him.

"You are not afraid," he observed.

"I am terrified," Silena said simply. "But my brother and sister are behind me, so I can stand still."

Percy felt that like warmth up his spine.

Zeus lifted his face to the sky, listening to something none of them could hear. Thunder muttered far off, consultative.

When he looked down again, his decision was in his face.

"I will not punish refusal today," he said. "Blood is hot at this age and Chiron pleads soft." His gaze hardened on Thalia and Annabeth. "But hear me, daughters. Defiance has cost. You will not receive sky-gifts this season. No storm aid. No eagle escort. You stand with sea. See if sea holds you when winter comes."

Thalia lifted her chin. "We will."

Annabeth nodded, throat working. "Yes, Father."

Then to Cabin Three: "And you. Poseidon's brood. I watch Atlantis. Tell your father the sky sees. Tell him tribute is due and silence will be read as answer."

Clarisse opened her mouth. Silena trod lightly on her foot. Clarisse closed it, jaw clenched.

"Yes, sir," Percy said for all of them. "We will tell him."

"See that you do."

Light gathered again. The air thinned. Zeus stepped back onto the porch and into the glare. For an instant his shape was not a man but a vast brightness with wings of cloud. Then he was gone. The pressure snapped off like a rubber band.

Everyone staggered. One of the Demeter kids sat down hard. The flag rope clattered.

The sky over the Sound was blue again, with normal ragged clouds. The pines dripped. A gull cried.

No one moved for three heartbeats.

Then noise broke like a wave — whispering, exclaiming, half a hundred conversations at once. Chiron clapped his hands sharp.

"Campers! To breakfast! Quickly now! Day proceeds!"

Mr. D waved his soda. "Nothing to see! Go eat! Extra syrup for anyone who does not gossip!"

Nobody believed that. Gossip ignited as rows broke.

Percy did not move. He was still holding Annabeth's hand. He realized it at the same time she did. They both looked down.

Her fingers were white-knuckled. His were too. Neither let go.

"You okay?" he whispered.

Annabeth nodded fast, eyes bright. "Are you?"

"No," Percy said honestly. "My knees feel like jellyfish."

Thalia snorted and ruffled both their heads hard enough to hurt. "Breathe, babies. You just told off the sky. You can breathe now."

Clarisse turned on them then. Percy braced for teasing — gross, down boy, Lord Jealousy strikes again. Instead Clarisse grabbed Annabeth in a fierce one-armed hug, careful of her spear.

"You did good, Grace," Clarisse muttered gruffly into Annabeth's hair. "Stupid. Brave. Good."

Annabeth clung back, surprised and pleased. "Thank you."

Clarisse let go fast, ears pink, and punched Percy's arm. "And you. Third? You should get that right? To Zeus? Are you trying to die?"

Percy grinned shakily. "It worked."

"It worked this time, Seaweed Brain." But she was smiling, shaky too. She slung her arm around his neck and squeezed. "My baby brother talks back to gods. I am so proud and so mad."

Silena gathered all of them with her eyes, then with her arms, pulling Annabeth in on the other side, reaching for Thalia too.

"Come on," Silena said softly. "Breakfast. Before the good bacon goes. We sit together today. All five."

Thalia blinked. "We cannot sit together. Cabins—"

"Today we can," Silena said firmly. "If anyone objects, they can argue with Clarisse's spear."

Clarisse hefted it. "Happy to discuss."

They walked to the pavilion in a clump, still half-dazed. Percy and Annabeth's hands swung between them, still linked. Percy thought about letting go to carry his tray. He did not want to. Annabeth did not let go either until they reached the steps, and then only to fix her braid with shaking fingers.

"You held my hand," Percy said quietly, amazed all over again. "In front of Zeus."

Annabeth's cheeks went pink, but she met his eyes steady, gray to green.

"I chose you," she said simply. "Openly. I meant it."

Percy's ears burned. "Good. Because I choose you too. Openly."

Thalia gagged loudly behind them. "Babies! Chaste! Appropriate! Walk faster!"

Clarisse laughed, real and rough, breaking the last tension. "Come on, Lord and Lady Hand-Hold. Bacon before feelings."

---

Breakfast was strange and loud and too fast.

They pushed two tables together — Poseidon and Zeus — despite the harpies screeching about seating charts. Silena talked the harpy down with extra toast and a smile. Chiron pretended not to see, trotting past with his okayest centaur mug. Mr. D pretended not to see while seeing everything.

Tyson brought over a whole plate of pancakes and set it in the middle. "For brave," he announced. He patted Percy on the head, patted Annabeth on the head, patted Thalia on the head hard enough to rattle her teeth. "Good standing."

Grover slid in with apples. "That was amazing! Terrible! Amazing! Do not do it again! My hooves shook!"

Travis Stoll leaned over from Hermes. "You told Zeus no. To his face. You are legends. Also dead. But legends."

Theo, the Hermes kid with the graph paper, came up shy, holding his tray. He looked at Annabeth, then Percy, then their still-close shoulders.

"That temple model?" Theo said to Annabeth. "It held. Overnight test. It held twelve washers." He glanced at Percy. "Thanks for — for holding the ruler that day. It helped."

Annabeth smiled warmly. "Good. I told you the stylobate would do it."

Theo nodded and went back, satisfied. Percy felt the old hot flicker behind his ribs and recognized it and let it pass like a small wave. Annabeth nudged his foot under the table.

"Still my favorite person to explain things to," she murmured, for him only.

Percy nudged back. "Still your favorite structural integrity consultant?"

"Always."

Clarisse across the table rolled her eyes so hard Silena laughed into her juice.

Chiron came by after offerings. He rested a hand briefly on Percy's shoulder, on Annabeth's, on Thalia's.

"Well stood," he said quietly. "Foolishly, bravely. We will speak later of tribute and messages. For now — eat. And stay together today. The camp needs to see you whole."

"Yes, sir," they chorused.

After breakfast, duties dissolved. No one wanted to drill. The arena stood empty. Instead kids clustered, rehashing. Did you see Grace say no? Did you see Jackson correct him? Did you see Clarisse call Zeus inaccurate to his face?

Cabin Three and Cabin One ended up by the lake, because water calmed Percy and sky calmed Thalia and Annabeth liked to be where she could see both.

They sat on the dock, five in a row, feet dangling. Clarisse with her spear across her knees. Silena with her shawl around Annabeth's shoulders without asking. Thalia with her boots off, toes skimming. Percy in the middle beside Annabeth, shoulders touching. Tyson stood in the shallows, happy to be anchor.

No one talked for a while. The lake lapped. Dragonflies stitched. The far pines swayed.

Finally Clarisse spoke, low, staring at the water.

"I am angry," she said. "I am so angry I could put my spear through a tree." Her hands gripped the shaft. "He looked at us like we were knives our dad hid. Like I would turn on camp. I bled for this place."

Silena leaned her head against Clarisse's shoulder. "I know."

"I would never," Clarisse said fiercely. "I am Poseidon and I am camp. Both. He does not get to make those different."

"You told him," Percy said. "You said it to his face. I was proud."

Clarisse glanced at him, eyes shiny with anger unshed. "I was scared."

"Me too," Percy said. "I thought my voice would crack and it did."

Thalia swung her feet. "My dad scares everyone. Even me. Especially me." She looked at Annabeth. "You were steadier than I was, Bee. You said it clean."

Annabeth picked at the shawl fringe. "I was thinking of the Girl Who Ran. And She Who Draws Lines." She looked up at Percy, small smile. "I had somewhere to stand."

Percy took her hand again, easier this time, practiced. No god watching now, just sisters and lake. Their fingers linked.

Thalia saw and sighed dramatically. "Oh gods, again? In daylight?"

"Openly," Annabeth said, chin up, but smiling.

"Openly," Percy agreed.

Clarisse threw a pebble at them, missing on purpose. "Gross. Astronomical. Same thing."

Silena laughed and threw a second pebble, hitting the water with a clean plip. "Let them be. They earned it."

Thalia stood up, balancing on the dock, arms out. Static lifted her hair. She looked like a storm deciding to be kind.

"Listen up, fish and sky," she announced. "New rule. Grace sisters do not spy. Poseidon kids are not traitors. Anyone says different answers to me. And to Clarisse. We will take turns."

"Deal," Clarisse said, standing too, clasping Thalia's wrist forearm to forearm, warrior style. Daughter of the sea and daughter of the sky, holding on.

Silena stood and wrapped an arm around Annabeth, who leaned in. Percy stood last, still holding Annabeth's hand.

They stood there, five across the dock, while Tyson splashed happily and the lake reflected them wobbling and true.

Later, when Chiron called Percy to the Big House to dictate a message to Atlantis — respectful, careful, tribute due and silence not intended — Percy went with dry mouth and came back with heavier shoulders.

"Dad answered?" Silena asked, waiting on the porch steps with Clarisse.

"Not yet," Percy said. "Chiron sent it by rainbow. Said Dad will take time. Said to tell you fish stew invitation is noted in the official record."

Silena laughed wetly. Clarisse snorted.

Annabeth and Thalia waited too, by the railing, pretending not to wait. Annabeth ran down when she saw him.

"What did he say?" she asked. "Chiron, I mean."

Percy told her. She listened full-attention, temple door open, and nodded.

"Then we wait together," she said. She took his hand again, like it was the most natural thing, which now it was. "Walk me to Cabin One? Thalia says I have to learn to accept escort."

"I can escort," Percy said solemnly. "I am trained in structural integrity and monster fog."

Thalia groaned. Clarisse gagged. Silena clapped.

They walked up the path as the sun went low, gold on wet leaves. Behind them Cabin Three's lamp glowed warm. Ahead Cabin One's windows lit. The camp breathed around them, shaken but whole.

At the fork under the lamppost where blankets had once been divided, they stopped. Moths bumped the light. Stars were just starting.

Annabeth did not kiss his cheek this time. She just held his hand a moment longer, then squeezed and let go.

"Goodnight, Percy," she said.

"Goodnight, Annabeth."

Thalia caught Annabeth up in a one-armed hug and waved at Percy over her head. Clarisse waited with arms crossed, spear leaning, exactly like before. Silena stood beside her with a blanket already open.

Percy walked to his sisters. Silena wrapped the blanket around his shoulders. Clarisse ruffled his hair hard.

"You did good today, little brother," Clarisse said quietly.

"You did better," Percy said.

Silena kissed his forehead. "You both did. Come inside. Salt and laundry. Home."

They went in. The cabin smelled like sea and clean wool. Percy climbed into his bunk in his clothes again, too tired to change. Outside, thunder muttered far over the Sound — not angry now, just weather.

He fell asleep with his hand curled like it was still holding someone's, and dreamed of a dock with five kids standing, sky above and sea below, holding the line together.
