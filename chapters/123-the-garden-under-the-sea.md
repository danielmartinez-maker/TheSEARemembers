## Chapter 123: The Garden Under the Sea

Before going underwater, Clarisse checked her weapons with salt-stiff fingers, then checked them again, leather and bronze clicking faintly in the morning air.

“The sea is not going to steal your spear,” Percy said.

“The sea has stolen bigger things.”

“It gives some of them back.”

“Name one.”

“A small ship.”

“That is not comforting.”

Silena laughed, warm and breathy in the cool air, and tucked a wind-loose curl behind her ear. Her cloth bag smelled faintly of pencil shavings and dried apricots, and held their notebook and the half-finished camp map they had started for Amphitrite.

“We are visiting a garden,” she reminded Clarisse. “Not raiding one.”

“I can do both.”

“Let’s aim for the first.”

At the shoreline of Long Island Sound, the four of them gathered. Chiron had walked them down from camp and waited on the path, leaving the visit to them. This was a short garden visit before the longer palace stay they were planning. No court or procession awaited in Atlantis: Amphitrite and Triton would meet them at a sheltered reef garden beyond the city’s outer terraces, screened from the stronger currents and usually quiet except for fish and the soft knock of water on coral.

Percy had been trying to help all morning. He had explained the route twice, described the current that would carry them down, and answered Silena’s questions about the reef’s flowers. He had also carefully not said the word *home* too often. The invitation was small by design, but the place beneath the water still carried the weight of all the years he had lived there and all the years since.

Annabeth stood beside him, turning a pale-green sea-glass charm over between her fingers. Amphitrite had sent it with the directions; it would let Annabeth breathe underwater. Annabeth had inspected it before breakfast, then asked Percy how to tell if it was working. His answer—that she would know—had not satisfied her.

Now Annabeth pressed the charm to her throat. A faint light moved through the glass, green as sunlight seen through a wave. She took a careful breath, held it for a moment, then let it out.

“All right,” she said. “No choking, no burning, no sudden desire to become a clam.”

“I’ve observed no other side effects,” Percy said.

“A compelling safety record.”

He offered her his hand. “Want to hold on when we start down?”

“Yes.” She took it, then glanced toward the others. “That doesn’t mean I’m going to need you to tow me the whole way.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

The water just beyond the shore darkened, not with shadow but with depth. A smooth current began to turn beneath the surface. It circled their ankles, cool and gentle, then climbed around their legs as if the sea were remembering how to welcome people. The tug answered the water in his blood. A path opened where there had been none, the Sound’s surface bending into a clear blue corridor that led out to deeper water.

Chiron watched from the shore. “I will be here when you return.”

“You don’t have to wait,” Percy said.

“I know,” Chiron said. “I would like to.”

Clarisse glanced back at him. “If we’re late, don’t send a search party.”

“I shall do my best to restrain myself.”

“Good.”

Silena stepped into the current first. It rose to her waist and held her without effort, gathering over her shoulders in a clear sheath. Her red hair lifted in slow ribbons.

“It’s like the water is putting on a dress,” she said.

“It’s a current-shell,” Percy said. “It keeps the water from pulling at your clothes.”

“I know. It looks like a dress.”

“A practical one.”

Clarisse took one step into the water, then another. Her shoulders had gone tight. The change in her reached him before she spoke: that familiar tension when she tried to turn a feeling into a job she could perform.

“It’s all right,” he said. “The current will take us down slowly.”

“I know how currents work.”

“I know.”

She looked at him, frowning. “Then don’t explain it like I’m about to fall over.”

“You’re not.” He paused. “I just want you to know we can stop whenever you want.”

Clarisse’s expression did not soften, but something in it shifted. “I’ll tell you if I want to stop.”

“Okay.”

“Don’t tell Silena to stop unless she’s about to swim into a rock.”

“I may tell Percy that,” Annabeth said.

“That seems fair,” Clarisse agreed.

The current rose around them. Percy kept Annabeth’s hand in his and let the water lift the four of them from the shore. The first few feet followed the shallow coastline before the cooler, deeper sea took hold. Then the surface closed overhead.

The world did not go dark. It changed its mind about light.

Sunlight broke into bands, moving around them in long, blue-green ribbons. The air in Annabeth’s charm shimmered in a thin halo. She was still holding Percy’s hand, her other arm tucked close to her side as the current carried them down. He turned his palm beneath hers so she could feel the direction of the flow. She squeezed once in answer.

Silena tilted her head back as a silver fish with a yellow tail circled her shoulder.

“It’s following us,” she said.

“It thinks you have food,” Clarisse replied.

“I have apricots.”

“You are not feeding it your lunch.”

Guiding them along the descending path, Percy pointed out the currents and ridges. The current felt familiar in his muscles, though he followed the route Amphitrite had sent rather than trusting a childhood he could not recall. He checked each ridge against her directions, wondering how it looked to someone seeing it for the first time.

Annabeth stayed near his shoulder, her charm pulsing softly. She studied the currents and rocks, mapping them in her head, working not to turn the unknown into a problem she had to solve before she could enjoy it.

“The water feels less like swimming and more like being carried,” Annabeth said.

“You can swim out of the current if you want.”

“I’m happy to be carried for a while.”

“Then we’ll be carried.”

Clarisse was staring past him into the open water.

At the shore, depth had been an idea. Here, it was a presence. It stretched beneath them in a vast, blue-black distance, interrupted by scattered lights that could have been fish, plants, or far-off windows. The seafloor was too far away to see. There was no visible bottom to tell her where the water ended. Percy saw her grip tighten around the strap of her spear.

“We can go back if you want,” he said quietly.

“I didn’t ask to go back. I’m not afraid of the water.”

“I didn’t say you were.”

“It’s the not knowing where it stops.” Her eyes stayed on the dark below. “The arena has edges. A forest has edges, even if you can’t see them. This just keeps going.”

Percy understood. His body treated the sea as a room without walls. Since arriving here he had been learning its shape consciously, the currents that bent toward home and the ones that led away.

“There’s a shelf under us. The reef sits on it. The path doesn’t cross open water after that.”

“You can see it?”

“Not yet. I know the route.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“No. But it’s enough for this part.”

Clarisse looked at the current flowing around her boots. “All right.”

Percy moved closer, careful not to crowd her, and offered his forearm. Clarisse stared at it as if he had handed her a weapon by the wrong end.

“For balance,” he said.

“I know what balance is.” Still, she took his arm. Her grip eased a little as they continued down. Silena drifted closer to Clarisse without comment.

The seafloor appeared in pieces: ledge, sand, then a broad shelf of coral lit with gold and blue. Branching coral softened the current around the protected shallows.

Silena forgot to speak.

That silence said more than any exclamation; she was impressed to her core.

---

The garden sat in a sheltered hollow between two ridges. Coral grew in terraces of red-orange, lavender, pale pink, and deep blue. Fish moved through it in shifting patterns, from thumb-sized flashes to lazy shapes as long as a camp canoe.

Sea lilies opened and closed with the tide. Ribbon-kelp floated above stones like green banners, while star-shaped blossoms shone gold along the reef’s upper ledges.

Silena turned in a slow circle. “It’s a garden.”

“That’s what Amphitrite calls it,” Percy said.

“It’s more than a garden.”

“Amphitrite says I told her that when I was little. She told me the sea has more than one kind of growing thing.”

“A queenly answer through and through.”

Clarisse released Percy’s arm but remained within easy reach, studying the reef’s edges and the current’s path. Percy didn’t ask her to stop checking for exits. Peace did not arrive as a command.

“The paths are wide enough for a chariot,” he said.

“Why would there be a chariot?”

“It seemed like a useful measurement.”

“You measured a pool with cannonballs once.”

“I got a number.”

Annabeth leaned against his shoulder, smiling. “I’m glad you’ve found a way to make geometry worse.”

“I contain multitudes.”

“Most of them need supervision.”

“That’s why I brought you.”

“You brought me because you wanted me here.”

“That too.”

She squeezed his hand. Her charm made a green circle at her throat. Curious and quietly happy, she drank in the reef. She had chosen to see it with him, not because old memories or family names had placed her at his side.

At the far end of the garden, Triton and Amphitrite waited beside an arch of white coral, without guards or heralds. Triton wore a dark blue cloak; Amphitrite’s robe flowed around her like a current.

Percy recognized their manner now: the way Amphitrite waited for the last guest to reach her, the way Triton stepped aside to give the others room. Their stories of blue stone halls, childhood swimming lessons and races to a pillar were still stories to him, not memories he could retrieve. But the affection between them was something he'd come to know since arriving at camp.

Triton lifted a hand. “You made it without racing the current.”

“I’ve matured.”

“Since when?”

“Since the current started carrying Annabeth.”

“I hope I’m not responsible for your maturity,” Annabeth said.

“You’re not.”

Amphitrite came toward them, the water opening easily around her. Her warm gaze moved from Percy to Clarisse and Silena, then to Annabeth. She did not reach for anyone until they chose to close the distance.

“Percy,” she said. “Welcome back.”

He had expected that simple phrase. It still caught him in the ribs. “It’s good to see you.”

“And you.” Amphitrite looked at his sisters. “Clarisse. Silena. I am glad you came.”

Silena dipped her head. “Thank you for inviting us.”

Clarisse gave a short nod. “This is a beautiful place.”

“It is one of my favorite places in the sea,” Amphitrite said. “There is no throne here, and no one expects anyone to speak in a particular order.”

Triton looked at her. “I believe there is a sign near the east path about the order of speaking.”

“The sign asks people not to interrupt the sea turtles.”

“They’re particular about their meetings,” Triton said.

“They are not holding meetings,” Amphitrite replied.

Silena’s smile widened. Clarisse’s mouth twitched. The moment relaxed around them, not because the water changed, but because no one was waiting for them to perform.

Amphitrite turned to Annabeth. “And you are Annabeth. Percy has told me about you.”

“I hope he mentioned the useful things.”

“He says you make plans and remind him to use them.”

“A generous summary.”

“It was said with affection.”

Percy’s ears burned. “I didn’t know this was going to be a report.”

“It was a conversation,” Amphitrite said. “I left out the cannonballs.”

Annabeth looked at him. “There were cannonballs?”

“She says I was six.”

Amphitrite’s laughter moved through the water. For a moment Percy imagined the boy in their stories beside Triton, then looked at his sisters now, surrounded by people with different histories. The past did not vanish. It made room, sometimes awkwardly.

“We did not come to keep you from seeing the garden,” Amphitrite said. “Would you like to walk?”

“Yes,” Silena answered before anyone else could.

“Good.” Amphitrite gestured toward the first terrace. “Then we will walk.”

They followed a sandy path between coral beds and a pool of waving anemones. Triton explained which plants were native to the reef. Silena stopped at each new color, asking how long the coral had grown and whether the fish recognized people.

“Triton says the blue ones used to follow me,” Percy said. “Apparently I fed them crumbs.”

“That’s not a good diet,” Silena said.

“The fish have been under review ever since,” Triton added.

Silena bent close to a cluster of blue fish. They swam in a quick loop around her fingers. She didn’t touch them, though the wonder in her face suggested she had to remind herself not to.

“They’re beautiful,” she whispered.

“They’re also thieves,” Clarisse said. “A parrotfish stole my buckle.”

“It was scraping algae,” Triton said.

“It stole my buckle.”

Clarisse rolled her eyes, but her gaze had shifted from the dark beyond the ridges to the fish. Her shoulders remained high, though not as rigidly. She let Silena pass through a narrow stretch of coral before following, without drawing her spear.

Amphitrite led them to an open space at the center of the reef. A shallow pool lay there, edged with smooth gray stone. The sand at its bottom was pale as cream, and a single coral tree rose from its middle. Its branches were small, no taller than Percy, and curved in the shape of an umbrella. A scattering of pearl-colored shells had settled beneath it.

“This is the quiet part of the garden,” Amphitrite said. “People come to sit or decide they are not required to talk.”

“That last one sounds useful,” Clarisse said.

“It is.”

They sat on the stones around the pool. Annabeth leaned against Percy’s shoulder and linked her fingers with his.

For a time, no one spoke. The silence made room for the fish and the soft clack of shells beneath the coral tree.

“Is this where I used to hide when I was little?” Percy asked.

“Yes. You were supposed to be napping,” Triton said.

“That counts as hiding.”

Amphitrite watched him tenderly. “You wanted to know the name of every fish and whether the little flowers had roots.”

“Did they?” Silena asked.

“Some do,” Amphitrite said. “Others hold to the coral or drift until they find a place that suits them.”

Annabeth watched the reef. “It changes, but you don’t command it to stay exactly as it was.”

“No. We care for it, but the reef has a life of its own.”

“Places change whether anyone gives permission or not,” Annabeth said. “The trick is deciding what to protect and what to let move.”

Percy understood what she meant. Camp had become home without asking his permission; Atlantis had stayed home even after he left it. He did not have to choose which one was real by pretending the other was not.

“You can come back here,” Amphitrite said, quietly enough that it felt like she was speaking to all of them. “Not because you owe me a visit, and not because this place belongs to me. The garden will be here when you want it. It will also be here if you do not.”

Clarisse had been examining the coral tree. “Does anything in this garden belong to visitors?”

“The paths, while you are here,” Amphitrite said. “The place to sit. The time you choose to spend. Nothing living needs to be taken to prove you came.”

Clarisse nodded once. “Good.”

“There is something I would like to offer you,” Amphitrite said. She reached into a fold of her robe and brought out three small tokens. They were pieces of coral, each polished smooth but not carved into any particular symbol. One was white and threaded with a fine silver cord. One was a muted rose color, with a hole through the center. The third was dark red, flecked with blue.

“They were gathered from the garden’s own fallen branches,” she explained. “Nothing was broken for them. If you would like one, you may choose. They are not marks of rank or promises. They are reminders of a place you have visited.”

Silena leaned forward to look, not reaching. “They’re lovely.”

“You may take one,” Amphitrite said. “Or none.”

Clarisse stared at the red-and-blue piece. Its surface was rough in a few places, the coral’s old pattern still visible beneath the polish. “Why that one?”

“It came from a branch at the outer edge of the garden,” Amphitrite said. “It was darker than the others and grew more slowly. It stayed sound through several seasons of strong current.”

“You’re saying it’s stubborn.”

“I am saying it endured.”

Clarisse’s eyes narrowed a little. “That’s not the same thing.”

“No,” Amphitrite agreed. “It isn’t.”

The quiet between them was different now. His hands tensed before he could stop them. He wanted Amphitrite to say the right thing. He wanted Clarisse to accept something without having to turn it into proof. He wanted every old wound to recognize that no one meant to reopen it.

Those were not things he could make happen by wanting them.

Clarisse looked from the coral token to Amphitrite. “If I take it, what does it mean to you?”

“That you wanted something from this place. No more than that.”

“Not that I’m pledging myself to Atlantis?”

“No.”

“Not that I owe you?”

“No.”

“Not that I have to call you anything?”

Amphitrite held her gaze. “No. You may call me Amphitrite. You may call me Your Majesty, if that feels right. You may choose another name someday, if it feels right then. You do not have to call me mother. You do not have to call me anything you have not chosen.”

Clarisse’s jaw worked once. “All right.”

She reached out and took the red-and-blue token. Her fingers closed around it carefully, as if a small thing could still be heavy. She turned it over once, studying its uneven surface. Then she threaded the silver cord through the hole and looped it around her wrist.

“It fits,” Silena said.

“It’s not armor,” Clarisse replied.

“I didn’t say it was.”

“You were thinking it.”

“I was thinking it looks good.”

Clarisse looked back at the coral. “It does.”

Amphitrite did not ask for thanks. She only smiled. Percy watched Clarisse’s thumb move across the token once, then again. The gesture reminded him of the old carving she had made for him at camp, a panther with its wings spread. Clarisse knew how to make things with her hands. She knew what it cost to keep something from breaking.

“You don’t have to make it mean more than it means,” he said.

Clarisse looked at him. “I know.”

“Okay.”

“And you don’t have to look like you’re waiting for me to fall apart.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You were.”

“Maybe a little.”

“I’m fine.”

“I know.”

She held his gaze for a moment, then gave the coral token a light tap with her thumb. “I like it. That’s all.”

“That’s enough,” Amphitrite said.

---

They stayed beside the quiet pool while the light shifted across the reef. Silena sat close to Clarisse, turning the white token over in Amphitrite’s palm. She had not chosen one for herself. The rose-colored piece remained on the stone between them.

“Do you want one?” Clarisse asked.

“I’m not sure yet.”

“You can wait.”

“I know.” Silena set the white token down. “There’s something I wanted to ask.”

Amphitrite’s face became attentive. “You may ask.”

Silena glanced at Clarisse, not for permission but to make sure she was still with her. Clarisse’s shoulder touched hers.

“Our mortal mothers,” Silena said. “Do you know either of them?”

The question settled over the pool like silt. His sisters had separate mortal mothers, separate homes far above the reef. Percy had never met either woman and could not answer for them.

Amphitrite was quiet before she replied. “I have not met either of them. I know that they raised you, and did so without the help you should have had. I know that each worked to keep her daughter safe. I know their names; I do not know their stories.”

“He told you both names?” Clarisse asked.

“Yes. But names are not enough to tell me who they are. You may ask Poseidon what he remembers, or ask your mothers directly. Their stories belong to them, and to you.”

Silena looked down. “Did Poseidon talk about my mom?”

“He told me you existed,” Amphitrite said. “He did not tell me enough about either mother for me to claim I know her. I will not fill his silence with a story that makes the hurt easier for me to explain.”

Clarisse’s fingers stopped on the coral token. “Did you know he had children and just wait?”

“I knew he had children, but I did not know you as children. I did not come to find you, and I cannot offer an excuse. I did not make those choices, but I know my place in the sea did not stop them from affecting you.”

Silena’s eyes shone. “Did you ever want to meet us?”

“Yes,” Amphitrite answered. “But wanting was not the same as having the right to arrive in your lives. I did not want either mother to feel the gods were claiming what she had carried on her own.”

“So you stayed away,” Clarisse said.

“I did.”

Amphitrite offered no defense. Her honesty did not make the past gentler, but it did not ask them to pretend it had been simple.

Silena rubbed her thumb over the side of the rose-colored token. “I don’t know what I wanted you to say.”

“You do not need to know yet.”

“I thought maybe you’d know what she was like with Poseidon. Whether she was happy.”

“I don’t know whether she was happy,” Amphitrite said. “I would like to know. Whatever she felt about your father, whatever he did or failed to do, none of it measured your worth.”

Silena’s breath trembled. The charm at Annabeth’s throat caught the light as she shifted closer to Percy, but she did not interrupt. Beneath hers, his hand stayed still. He could not fix the question. He could only let it be asked and let the answer remain honest.

Clarisse looked away from the pool. “My mom never said Poseidon didn’t care.”

“Mine didn’t either,” Silena said softly.

“She said he was busy.”

“Mine said the ocean had its own rules.”

“She said we were lucky to have each other.”

Silena swallowed. “We were.”

“We are,” Clarisse corrected.

Silena leaned her head against her sister’s shoulder. “We are.”

Amphitrite watched them, her expression full of tenderness but not pity. “You are family here, all of you. That does not change the family you already have. You do not have to feel differently about your mothers or father, and you do not have to call me mother. You may call me that someday if you choose; you owe me no title.”

Clarisse took in a breath that sounded almost like a laugh. “You keep saying that like people usually do require it.”

“People often want to choose the name for the place they hold in another person’s life,” Amphitrite said.

“And what do you want?” Silena asked.

“I want you safe and free to decide what you feel. I would be glad if you came back or wanted to know me. Neither is a debt.”

Triton rested his elbows on his knees. “For the record, the garden has never demanded a title from anyone.”

Silena let out a laugh that wavered into a sigh. “I want to talk to her about it someday. My mom, I mean. Not because I think that will answer everything. I want to ask her what she remembers, in her own words.”

“That makes sense,” Annabeth said. “Ask in your own time.”

Silena nodded. “I don’t want the answers to be perfect. I just want them to be hers.”

“Then that is what you should ask for,” Amphitrite said. “Not a story from someone who was not there.”

Percy looked at his sisters. Their questions did not disappear, but here they could carry them together. No one was being replaced.

“You can ask your mom when you’re ready,” Clarisse said. “I can be with you. And maybe I’ll ask mine.”

Clarisse looked down at the token on her wrist. “Maybe.”

Silena smiled. “Maybe is a start.”

“You’re beginning to sound like Percy.”

“That’s an insult,” Percy said.

Amphitrite offered Silena the rose-colored token. “Would you like this one?”

Silena looked at it. “I think I’d like to wait. Maybe next time.”

“Then it will wait here.”

“It won’t mind?”

“It is a piece of coral. It has been waiting a long, patient time.”

“That’s reassuring.”

Silena rested her hands in her lap. She seemed lighter, though not because her question had been answered in a way that could close it. It had been met without being turned aside. Sometimes that was its own kind of answer.

---

The quiet was broken by a young attendant arriving from the eastern channel with a basket of pale coral fragments.

Amphitrite looked up before the girl spoke. "What happened?"

"One of the anchoring lines came free in the current change, my lady. The nursery shelves are shifting. We have secured the outer end, but the seedlings will be buried if the next tide comes through."

The attendant stood perfectly still, as though the news needed to be delivered without a trace of alarm. Percy had heard that tone at camp when a counselor reported something going wrong in front of Chiron. Usually it meant someone had been trying to fix the problem before admitting there was one.

"How long have you known?" Amphitrite asked.

"Since the morning turn."

"Then the shelves have been loose for hours."

"Yes, my lady."

Amphitrite set down the rose-colored token. She didn't scold the attendant. "Thank you for telling me. Ask the gardeners to close the inner path. We will look at it before the next shift."

Clarisse was on her feet. "Can I help?"

"You don't know this garden's anchors."

"I know what a failed line looks like."

Amphitrite studied her for a moment. "Then you may come with us to examine it. You will not touch anything until the gardener explains the structure."

"Fair."

Silena put the coral token on the stone. "I know how to tie a line that won't cut into a soft branch. Is that useful?"

"It may be. Come and see."

Annabeth had already taken out her notebook. Thalia caught her arm before she followed.

"Ask first," she said under her breath.

Annabeth turned. It was a mild reminder, not an order, and she treated it that way.

"May I sketch the anchoring layout?" she asked Amphitrite. "I won't record private areas you don't want mapped."

"Yes. The nursery is no secret, but the young coral is fragile. We should stay on the marked path."

They went through an arch hung with kelp ribbons and entered a lower terrace where the current moved more quickly. Rows of small branching corals sat on shallow shelves fixed into the stone. A cord stretched from each shelf to a broad iron ring, keeping the planting surfaces from tipping when the flow changed. The eastern cord had frayed where it rubbed against a sharp edge. With one side unsupported, the shelves had begun to lean toward the sand.

Percy could feel the current pressing them. His instinct was to flatten the water and lift the shelf with a thought, as easily as bringing a cup to his hand. He held back. Amphitrite had made the rules of the garden clear. Even his control might create a sharp shift in pressure the young corals couldn't tolerate.

A gardener arrived carrying a narrow basket of tools. Her hands were dark with sediment, and she wore a belt threaded with small bone hooks.

"We need to move the weight off the damaged line," she said, "then replace it without pulling the shelf through the seedlings. If it swings at all, we lose the nearest row."

"Can I keep the water steady?" Percy asked.

"Only within the outer channel," the gardener said. "The nursery needs the flow. Don't stop it completely."

He nodded. The distinction mattered. Still water might make the repair easier for him while starving the small organisms whose lives depended on a moving current.

Clarisse took the gardener's spare line and examined the knot. "This one's tied like it was meant for a boat."

"It was."

"Then it works under tension, but the cut end needs protecting. You should wrap the ring."

Silena looked at the frayed cord. "A soft sleeve would help. Something between the rope and the edge."

The gardener considered them. "We have strips of woven kelp in the tool box."

"They're strong when wet," Silena said. "I can make one into a sleeve."

She didn't reach for the materials until the gardener offered them. Percy noticed. He'd seen Silena take over a dozen repairs at camp because she was the person most likely to finish them. Here she asked what was needed and waited for an answer.

Annabeth crouched beside a stone marker and drew the shelves from above. "The second ring is closer to the incoming flow than the first. Does that make its line take more strain?"

"Yes," the gardener replied. "The old arrangement was built when the channel was narrower. We have been meaning to move that ring."

"Could the new line go around the outer post instead?" Annabeth asked.

"That post is carved into a living reef. We mustn't use it."

She crossed out the suggestion. She didn't argue. The map would reflect what could actually be done, not simply what made the geometry look tidy.

They divided the work carefully. The gardener directed the repair. Clarisse held the shelf against the frame with both hands, bracing her feet on the solid path. Silena threaded the woven kelp around the exposed ring. Percy shaped a narrow current around the outside so the shelf could be lifted an inch without the full force of the incoming water pushing against it. Thalia stayed a little farther back with Annabeth, watching the edges of the work area and warning two curious fish away with gentle movements of her hands.

"Do not use lightning," Annabeth told her.

"I had absolutely no intention of electrifying a coral nursery."

"Your hair is standing up."

"My hair does that when I am offended."

Clarisse grunted. "Can we argue about hair after I put this down?"

"Yes," Thalia said.

The damaged line loosened suddenly. For a second the shelf tilted in Clarisse's grip. Percy felt the water he was holding slide toward the gap and steadied it without increasing the pressure. Clarisse shifted her weight and caught the frame against her forearm.

"Got it," she said. Her voice was strained but controlled.

"Hold there," the gardener instructed. "Silena, pass the new line behind the iron ring. Not over it."

Silena obeyed. The woven sleeve stayed in place as the cord was drawn through. The gardener made the final knot and tested it with a sharp tug. Only then did she tell Clarisse to let go.

The shelf settled level. The young coral remained where it had been planted.

For a few moments nobody said anything. Percy could hear the faint ticking sounds of the nursery as its branches moved with the current. The gardener inspected the nearest row, touched one loose planting gently back into place, and stood.

"We'll watch it through the next tide," she said. "That is a repair, not a guarantee."

Clarisse rubbed her forearm. "I like that answer."

"Most visitors prefer guarantees."

"Then most visitors haven't repaired anything."

Amphitrite looked around the group. The current moved through their hair and clothing, carrying away the last suspended grains of sand.

"Thank you," she said. "I invited you to see the garden, not to be pressed into its work."

"You didn't press us," Silena said. "We offered."

"And the gardener still decided what we could do," Annabeth added.

The gardener nodded. "Which is why the shelf is still there."

They walked back along the marked path. Percy remained beside Clarisse, who was flexing her hand discreetly.

"Is your arm all right?" he asked.

"Yes. It just got tired."

"You could have told me to take over."

"The shelf didn't need another person jumping in. It needed me to hold still."

He smiled. "You sound like Chiron."

"Insult me again and I'll teach you what the word *hold* means."

Silena caught up with them. A length of unused kelp weave hung from one finger.

"The gardener gave me this," she said. "She thinks I could use the pattern to make a better strap for my sewing bag."

"You'll have to ask how long it lasts out of water," Annabeth said.

"I did. It dries stiff, but it can be rewetted."

"And you can't take living coral home," Percy said.

"I am aware," Silena replied. "I took a strip that was cut already, not an entire reef."

Amphitrite slowed at the top of the terrace. "Would you like to see the old records for the garden? They contain the plans made before the channel shifted."

Annabeth's eyes lit. "Yes. If the gardener is willing."

"She is the one who suggested it."

They spent part of the afternoon in a small workroom built into the stone above the nursery. Scrolls with pressed seaweed samples lay beside etched plates showing the channels as they had been at different times. Annabeth compared the shapes with her own sketch, making notes about the water's route and the way the structures had been changed as the living reef grew.

Clarisse sat near the doorway and watched the gardener demonstrate how to test a line without loading it with the full weight of a shelf. Silena asked about the different textures of kelp fiber. Thalia inspected the safety markers, not because she distrusted the gardeners but because she wanted to know how a person unfamiliar with the place would recognize a closed path.

Percy ended up with Amphitrite at the window overlooking the repaired terrace. He had thought she would be distressed by damage in a place she loved. She was concerned, certainly, but she didn't act surprised that a living garden required maintenance.

"You couldn't have prevented that line from failing forever," she told him.

"I might have noticed sooner."

"Perhaps. Or you might have been somewhere else, as you were this morning. The garden survived because the people who care for it knew what to do."

"I keep thinking I should be able to fix things when they happen around me."

Amphitrite watched the gardeners test the new knot. "That feeling may never disappear entirely. It doesn't have to decide how you behave."

He looked at her. She was not speaking as a god giving a command. She seemed to be considering the problem as carefully as he was.

"Did you worry about me like that?" he asked. "When I was little here?"

"Yes."

"Even when you knew the water wouldn't hurt me?"

"The water was not the only thing that could." She placed her palm on the stone ledge. "I knew you were safe in some places and vulnerable in others. I made arrangements. Sometimes I wanted to make every arrangement myself."

"What stopped you?"

"Triton, on occasion. He was very young when he first told me I had to let you fall off a training float."

Percy laughed, then grew quiet. It was a story about a childhood he couldn't remember. He knew she understood that, but he still had to make himself listen as though he were learning about someone he had never met.

"Did I fall?" he asked.

"You did. You were furious. You stood up and tried again."

"Sounds like me."

"It does." Her smile was small. "But I tell you because you asked, not because you ought to remember it."

He nodded. That made a difference.

When the others emerged from the records room, they carried copies of nothing. Annabeth had written down what the gardeners permitted her to use, not drawn the protected nursery's exact location. Silena had a sample of finished kelp cord. Clarisse knew a new knot and had a tired arm she would eventually admit needed rest. Thalia had identified the exit signs and the staffed routes.

The visit had become something different from a tour. They had discovered how the garden worked, who maintained it, and what its beauty cost in ordinary attention. Percy liked that none of those discoveries required the place to turn into another version of camp. Atlantis could stay itself, and his sisters could remain themselves, while they learned enough to return someday without being helpless guests.

The visit ended without a bell or summons. The current turned toward the upper water, and Amphitrite walked them back to the garden’s outer arch.

“We can come back?” Silena asked.

“You can,” Amphitrite said. “There is no need to decide today.”

Clarisse’s hand moved to the coral at her wrist. “I’ll think about it.”

“That is all anyone can ask.”

Percy hugged Amphitrite before he could turn the thought into a question. For a moment his body settled into the hug as if no years had passed, though his mind held only this afternoon, with his sisters nearby. When she let him go, she did not ask him to stay or say when he should return.

“I’m glad you brought them,” she said.

“Me too.”

She turned to Clarisse and Silena. “Thank you for sharing the afternoon with me.”

Silena hugged her. Clarisse hesitated, then leaned in and touched her shoulder to Amphitrite’s. It lasted only a moment, but she did not pull away as if she had made a mistake.

“Thank you for the garden,” Clarisse said.

“You are welcome here.”

Clarisse’s chin lifted. “I know.”

The words were less than a promise. They did not need to be more.

Annabeth said goodbye with a smile and thanked Amphitrite for the charm. “It worked exactly as described.”

“I am glad. Keep it as long as it is useful; it is yours now.”

Annabeth looked at Percy. “You hear that? A magical object with a clear ownership policy.”

“A miracle.”

“No, a good host.”

“Maybe both.”

Triton clasped Percy’s shoulder. “Next time, perhaps the eastern channel.”

“We’ll check its boundaries first,” Annabeth said.

“Of course.” Silena waved goodbye to the small blue fish that had followed them to the garden’s edge.

The current lifted them from the shelf. Percy looked back once: Amphitrite and Triton stood beneath the coral arch, family saying goodbye after an afternoon together.

The path upward was easier, the water thinning to pale gold around them. Clarisse stayed near Percy without holding his arm; her coral token tapped at her wrist, a small hard rhythm. Silena looked back until the reef’s colors dissolved into blue. Annabeth’s hand remained in Percy’s, steady and warm despite the cold.

At the surface, the Sound opened around them. Sunlight lay across it in bright, ordinary pieces. Chiron waited on the bank, exactly where he had promised to be.

Clarisse stepped onto the grass and took a long breath, grass and pine sharp in her nose. “The air feels thin as paper up here.”

“It’s the same air,” Percy said.

“I was underwater for half an hour. I saw a fish steal a buckle.”

Silena laughed and sat in the grass. Annabeth took off the charm; its green light had faded, though the glass held a thread of brightness. Percy sat beside her, their shoulders touching.

“How was it?” he asked.

“Beautiful,” Annabeth said. “And not yours alone.”

He looked at her. “What do you mean?”

“You showed it to us. That doesn’t mean it stopped being your home. It means you let us see inside it.”

Percy looked toward the water, where the current had already returned to its ordinary course. Clarisse turned the coral token on her wrist. Silena opened her notebook to draw the garden before its colors blurred in memory. Annabeth had chosen to see this place from his earliest years, and Amphitrite had welcomed them without asking them to become anyone else.

His families had not become one family in an afternoon. They did not need to. A place had been made for each to see the others; a current joined two homes without making him leave one behind.

Clarisse glanced at the coral. “It doesn’t mean anything.”

Silena leaned against her shoulder. “Of course not.”

“It’s only coral.”

“And you like it.”

“I do.”

Clarisse’s fingers rested on the token. “She didn’t make me call her anything.”

“No,” Percy said. “She didn’t.”

“Good.”

Silena opened her notebook. “I’m drawing the garden. I’ll leave space for the rose coral.”

“For next time?” Percy asked.

“Maybe.”

Annabeth set the charm in his palm, then took it back and tucked it beneath her shirt. Her hand found his again.

Beyond the camp, the sea kept its secrets. Beneath the surface, the garden kept growing. And on the shore, with his sisters on one side and Annabeth at his shoulder, the distance between his two homes changed shape for Percy. Not gone. Not conquered. Simply crossed, one small visit at a time.
