## Chapter 14: Letters From Under the Sea

The gulls woke Percy before the conch did.

Percy listened until he recognized the surface gulls fighting over crumbs near the canoe lake. In the weeks he'd spent back in Atlantis, he'd heard siren-gulls above the undersea gardens and silverfin darters tapping against palace windows. Amphitrite had named the birds and explained the city currents. Those were memories from his recent visit, not proof he remembered growing up there. He could miss her without knowing how to answer her stories about the little boy she'd raised. In Cabin Three, homesickness sat just behind his teeth.

It was a Tuesday. Triton's letters said Tuesdays in Atlantis still meant dumpling soup and combat drills. Amphitrite had tied protective knots into his hair before he left and quizzed him on the tide charts she'd shown him. He remembered that last morning clearly; the years before it remained someone else's stories. Tuesdays at Camp Half-Blood meant Capture the Flag drills at nine and archery with the Apollo kids at eleven and, if the rumor Grover had whispered at dinner last night was true, mail call from underwater.

Percy sat up. The cabin was quiet. The other Poseidon bunks were empty except for yesterday's abandoned towel mountain and a single sock that had somehow ended up on the ceiling beam. Sun stripes came through the shells set into the walls. The whole cabin smelled like salt and cedar and the lemon soap Silena insisted they all use because, and Percy quoted from memory, "just because we are children of the sea god does not mean we have to smell like low tide."

On his pillow was a note in Clarisse's blocky handwriting:

*Gone to sword practice early. If the mail comes DON'T OPEN IT WITHOUT US. — C.*

Under that, in Silena's loopy cursive:

*Good morning, little brother! I left you honey cakes on the counter. Don't let Clarisse eat yours. Love, S.*

Percy smiled so hard his cheeks hurt. He'd only been at camp a few weeks and already somebody was stealing his breakfast and somebody else was saving him breakfast, and both of them threatened anyone else who tried either.

He ate one honey cake standing up and saved the other in his pocket for later, pulled on his Camp Half-Blood shirt and shorts, and shoved his feet into sneakers that were already full of sand. On the desk by the door, next to the conch shell his father had left for him on his first day, was a second note, this one in a hand he didn't recognize — neat, slanted, adult.

*Perseus — J. will open a channel at 08:00 by the lake. Be prompt. — Chiron*

J. Juniper? Percy squinted. Chiron's handwriting was dreadful, and he had no idea who had volunteered to organize the message.

His heart kicked up fast.

He'd sent three letters down already. The first had been very formal because he'd been nervous: *Dear Mother and Father and Triton, Camp is adequate. Training is adequate. Food is adequate.* Silena had read it over his shoulder and said, "Percy, you sound like a tax form," and made him rewrite it. The second had been better. The third had been three pages about Annabeth's laugh and Grover's panpipes and Clarisse suplexing a hellhound dummy and then asking if the palace kitchen still made that sea-plum jam.

He hadn't seen an answer yet. The sea didn't do fast mail, and Percy refused to turn the delay into a story about Amphitrite forgetting him: she had written first and asked him to tell her about camp. Waiting for a reply was new enough to make him restless without accusing anybody of neglect. You had to seal letters in sharkskin, give them to a hippocamp courier, hope they didn't get distracted by a school of tuna on the way down the continental shelf. Or you waited for an Iris-message slot, which cost drachmas and good weather and somebody on the other end who knew how to aim a rainbow through five thousand feet of water and a palace ward.

08:00 by the lake. Percy checked the clock nailed to the wall. 07:42.

He ran.

---

The lake was glassy and gold at that hour. Mist sat low over the water. The canoes were pulled up and dripping. At the edge of the dock, Chiron in his wheelchair form was guiding Nico di Angelo through how to toss a drachma correctly. Nico had volunteered to help with the Iris-message after Zoe told him the old records included an account of a deep-sea channel.

"No, my boy," Chiron was saying patiently. "You do not skip it like a stone. You offer it. With respect."

"I am offering it with respect," Nico said, holding a gold coin like it might bite him. "Respectfully skipping."

Annabeth sat cross-legged on the dock with a notebook, her blonde hair in a messy braid, her gray eyes tracking everything. Thalia sat next to her, knees up, black hair spiky with sleep, a leather jacket over her pajamas against the morning chill. They were both watching Nico fail at Iris-messaging, amused in spite of how nervous Percy was.

Grover was there too, half-asleep against a piling, his reed pipes tucked into his shirt pocket. Ella the harpy perched on the lifeguard chair with a book, muttering synonyms under her breath. Zoe, daughter of Hecate, stood a little apart with her arms crossed, pretending she wasn't interested.

And Clarisse and Silena were front and center, waving Percy over like he'd been late to his own birthday.

"You came," Silena said, bright. She wore a blue sundress over her swimsuit and her dark hair was already in two braids with little shells tied in. "We saved you a spot."

"You almost didn't come," Clarisse corrected. She had her spear slung across her back and her helmet under one arm and a smear of sunscreen on her nose that Silena had clearly applied against her will. "Move. Sit."

Percy dropped down between them. On his left, Clarisse elbowed him hard in greeting. On his right, Silena leaned her head briefly against his shoulder. Across the dock, Annabeth caught his eye and smiled — small, private, a little nervous for him.

"Is this — are they actually going to get through?" Percy asked quietly.

Chiron smiled. "Amphitrite is queen of the sea for a reason, Percy. If anyone can hold a rainbow steady under the sea, it's her."

Nico finally managed to toss his drachma without concussing anyone. "O Iris, goddess of the rainbow, accept my offering," he recited. The coin flashed gold and vanished. The mist over the lake shimmered, caught light, and stretched into a faint rainbow, wavering at the edges.

Nothing happened for three heartbeats.

Then the rainbow steadied, deepened, and cleared like a window being wiped.

And there they were.

---

Amphitrite filled the center of the image, beautiful and harried, her dark hair piled up with two chopsticks and a starfish, her royal shawl slipping off one shoulder to reveal a plain work tunic underneath. Behind her was the palace kitchen — Percy recognized the copper pots and hanging glow-kelp from his short stay after returning. The squid mosaic behind them was familiar from the photograph Amphitrite had tucked into her last letter.

"Mom!" Percy shouted, scrambling forward so fast Clarisse had to grab his shirt to keep him from falling in the lake.

"Perseus!" Amphitrite pressed both hands to her mouth, then to the edge of whatever was projecting her. "Oh, my baby. Oh, look at you. You cut your hair."

"I didn't," Percy said, touching his head self-consciously.

"You did. Who cut it? Was it the camp? Do they have a proper barber or did one of those Ares boys do it with a hunting knife?"

"It was Silena," Percy said. "She trimmed the ends. It looks good, right?"

Silena waved shyly at the rainbow. "Hello, Your Majesty."

Amphitrite blinked. "Oh! Oh, are these — are these the girls? The sisters?"

Clarisse sat up straighter than Percy had ever seen her. Even Thalia took her feet off the dock railing out of respect.

"Clarisse," Clarisse said stiffly. "Daughter of Poseidon. Thirteen."

"Silena," Silena said, softer. "Fourteen. Daughter of Poseidon. It's such an honor — I mean, we've heard — Percy talks about you all the time —"

"He does?" Amphitrite's face softened all at once. "Oh, girls. Come closer, let me see you. Clarisse, you have your father's chin. Silena, those eyes — oh, you look like my cousin Pelagia. Perseus writes that you have been kind to him."

"We're trying," Clarisse muttered, ears red. "He's annoying."

"He is," Amphitrite agreed warmly. "He leaves wet towels on thrones."

"Mom!"

Laughter rippled around the dock. Percy felt his face go hot, but it was the good kind of hot, the kind that meant everybody was looking at him with love instead of pity.

Then a tall shape ducked into frame behind Amphitrite and slung an arm around her shoulders. Triton. Adult, golden-eyed, smirking, his hair in long braids with shark teeth woven in, wearing a training cuirass with a burn mark Triton said Percy had put there during childhood training.

"There's the surface brat," Triton said. "You look pale. Are they starving you up there? Blink twice if you need a care package of actual food."

"I'm fine," Percy said, grinning so wide it hurt. "You look ugly. Did a jellyfish sting your face?"

"Jealousy doesn't suit you, little minnow."

"I missed you too, you overgrown sea horse."

"Language," Amphitrite said automatically, but she was smiling through tears. "Triton, be nice."

"I am being nice. I refrained from commenting on his haircut." Triton leaned closer to the projection. "Hey, surface kids. I'm the favorite brother. The handsome one. If Percy talks about me, subtract thirty percent for exaggeration."

"He says you cry at turtle hatchings," Clarisse said bluntly.

The dock went silent. Silena covered her mouth. Nico whispered, "He's going to regret that."

Triton stared. Then he barked a laugh. "I like her. Keep her. Mom, can we keep the short angry one?"

"No stealing other children," Amphitrite said. "Perseus, sweetheart, I have so little time — the rainbow only holds a few minutes through the wards — listen. Are you eating? Are you sleeping? Are the nightmares —" She stopped, glanced at the crowd, recalibrated. "Are you sleeping well?"

"Better," Percy said honestly. "Chiron gave me this kelp tea. And Grover plays pipes when it gets loud in my head."

Grover sat up straighter. "It's just nature stuff. Frogs. Crickets. Not a big deal."

Amphitrite's eyes shone. "Thank you, Grover. Perseus wrote about you. Thank you for looking after him."

Grover turned bright red under his fur. "Anytime, Your Majesty. He's — he's my best friend."

Percy looked away fast so no one would see his face.

"And Annabeth?" Amphitrite asked, lowering her voice conspiratorially. "Is Annabeth there? The girl with the plans?"

Annabeth froze like a deer. Percy waved her over. "Come on."

She scooted forward reluctantly, notebook clutched like a shield. "Hi. I'm Annabeth. Daughter of Zeus. It's — it's nice to meet you."

Amphitrite studied her for a long moment. Percy held his breath. His Atlantean mother and his girlfriend, the girl who remembered their shared life as clearly as he did and was learning these new family habits beside him.

"So you're the strategist," Amphitrite said at last. "Perseus says you redesigned the entire capture-the-flag defense in an afternoon and then made the Ares cabin cry."

"I didn't make them cry," Annabeth protested. "Clarisse helped with the demonstration. One of their fighters teared up a little."

"Good," Amphitrite said firmly. "Make them work for it. And thank you for sitting with my son when the dreams are bad. He mentioned it. He doesn't mention things unless they matter."

Annabeth's ears went pink. Thalia, behind her, made a exaggerated gagging face, then softened and squeezed her sister's shoulder.

"Okay, lovefest over," Triton announced. "Dad's here. Act dignified."

The kitchen went quieter. Percy felt Clarisse and Silena go still on either side of him.

Poseidon stepped into view.

He was not what camp expected, Percy could tell by the way Nico straightened and Zoe uncrossed her arms. He wasn't huge and bellowing and trident-waving. He was tired. His beard was neatly trimmed, his deep blue robe was salt-stained at the hem, his eyes were Percy's eyes — sea-green, ancient, sad around the edges. He put a hand on Amphitrite's shoulder and nodded at the rainbow.

"Perseus."

"Hi, Dad."

A beat. The water lapped at the dock.

"You look well," Poseidon said. "Chiron reports your control is improving. Your shield held for six minutes in the pool yesterday."

"Seven," Percy corrected. "Seven minutes. And I made a whirlpool that could hold a boulder. Clarisse helped by throwing rocks at me."

Clarisse blinked. "That was training."

Poseidon glanced at her. "You throw well, I hear."

"Thank you, sir," Clarisse said, her voice cracking as she tried to sound composed.

"She means she appreciates it," Silena supplied gently. "We train together every morning, sir. Percy shows us Atlantean forms, and he's a good, patient teacher."

Poseidon nodded slowly. "Good. That is good. Percy —" He hesitated. Triton looked at the floor. Amphitrite twisted her shawl in her hands. "The council keeps me late. The western trenches stir. But I think of you. Be — be brave. Be kind. Write to your mother."

It was so formal. So distant. So exactly like every letter Poseidon had ever written, every blessing he'd ever given with one hand already on a war map.

Percy felt something sink in his chest. "Yes, sir. I will."

"Is there —" Poseidon started, then stopped. For a second something raw crossed his face, something that looked almost like the dad Amphitrite described carrying Percy through the coral markets, letting him steer hippocamps while she pretended not to notice. "Is the jam to your liking? I sent sea-plum."

Percy's throat closed. "I haven't gotten it yet. But — but I'll save you some honey cakes. From camp. They're really good."

Poseidon's mouth twitched. "I would like that."

"Triton, wrap it up, the coral's dimming," someone called off-screen.

"Time!" Triton said, suddenly brisk to cover the wobble in his voice. "Okay, brat, listen. Mom packed you a box. Real box. Hippocamp courier. Should arrive today or tomorrow if Barnacle doesn't get distracted. There's jam. There's your blue blanket — don't argue, Mom insisted, she says surface nights are cold. There's my old training manual with the good diagrams, and don't let Clarisse steal the knife chapter, she'll get ideas."

"I already have ideas," Clarisse said.

"Good. Sharpen them." Triton grinned, then softened just for a second. "Hey. Minnow. The hollow's boring without you. The new sparring partner screams when I do the whirlpool slam. You never screamed."

"I screamed once."

"You squeaked once. There's a difference. Miss you, kid."

"Miss you too, squid-face."

Amphitrite blew kisses with both hands. "Be safe. Listen to Chiron. Wear sunscreen — you burn, don't argue, you're half-surface now in the sun — and girls, take care of my boy?"

"We will, Your Majesty," Silena whispered, eyes wet.

"We've got him," Clarisse said fiercely. "Nobody messes with him."

The rainbow flickered, thinned, and snapped out with a sound like a soap bubble popping. The mist settled flat over the lake again. The dock was silent except for gulls and water.

Percy stared at the empty air where his family had been. His hands were shaking. He tucked them under his thighs so no one would see.

Then Silena's arm was around his shoulders and Clarisse's heavy hand landed on the back of his neck, warm and grounding, and Annabeth's knee knocked against his, steady, there.

"You okay?" Grover asked softly.

Percy breathed in, out. Salt air. Sunscreen. Honey cakes in his pocket going soft. "Yeah," he said. "Yeah. They — they look good, right? Mom looks tired but good?"

"She loves you so much it's embarrassing," Thalia said bluntly, standing and stretching. "It's gross. I'm going to throw up."

Everybody laughed, and the tight thing in Percy's chest loosened a notch.

Chiron cleared his throat gently. "Percy, when the courier arrives, do come find me. Hippocamps need clearance at the shore. And —" His kind brown eyes were knowing. "I'm glad you got to see them."

"Thanks, Chiron."

The group broke up slowly. Nico tried to Iris-message the library's supply officer and reached a confused pizza shop in Ohio. Ella flapped down to ask Annabeth what "conchomancy" meant and whether it had an antonym. Zoe lingered just long enough to say to Percy, very quietly, "Your mother holds a deep-sea Iris channel for four minutes. That is not a small magic," before walking off.

But Clarisse and Silena didn't move. They stayed flanking Percy on the dock, watching the mist burn off the lake.

"So," Clarisse said at last. "Your mom's the queen."

"Yeah."

"And your brother's annoying."

"Yeah."

"And your dad —" She stopped.

Percy picked at a splinter on the dock. "He's busy. It's — it's not that he doesn't — the trenches are bad right now. And the council —"

"Percy." Silena took his hand. Hers was warm, ink-stained, a little calloused from her bowstring. "You don't have to translate him for us. We know what distant dads look like. Ours lives in a palace under the sea and only shows up for solstices."

Clarisse snorted. "Ours sends tidal waves instead of birthday cards."

"Mine sends formal letters that start with 'Perseus' and end with 'be brave,'" Percy said, trying to smile. "I got one last week. Three sentences. I read it like, twelve times."

Silena squeezed his fingers. "Do you want to show us? The box when it comes? And — and Atlantis stuff? I want to know what they've shown you. What it's like now. If you want to tell."

Percy looked at them — his sisters, thirteen and fourteen, fierce and soft, both trying so hard not to look too eager, both leaning in anyway.

"Yeah," he said, voice thick. "Yeah, I'd like that."

Clarisse stood, hauled him to his feet by the front of his shirt, and shoved him lightly toward the path. "Then come on. You can tell us over sword practice. And if you cry, I'll tell everyone you got sunscreen in your eye."

"I wasn't going to cry."

"You absolutely were going to cry."

Silena linked arms with both of them. "He wasn't going to cry, Clarisse. He was going to get sunscreen in his eye. There's a difference."

Annabeth watched them go from the end of the dock, notebook forgotten in her lap, Thalia's hand heavy on her shoulder.

And for just a second, Percy looked back and caught her eye, and smiled at her — real, wet, grateful — and she smiled back, even though it hurt in a place she didn't have a name for yet.

---

Clarisse La Rue did not get nervous. That was a rule. She had written it in permanent marker on the inside of her shield when she was nine: *CLARISSE DOES NOT GET NERVOUS. SHE MAKES OTHER PEOPLE NERVOUS.*

She was nervous.

It was 3:47 in the afternoon and she was standing on the beach with Silena and Percy waiting for a hippocamp courier named Barnacle who, according to Percy, had the attention span of a goldfish and the dramatic instincts of a theater kid.

"How will we know it's him?" Silena asked, shading her eyes. She'd brought a tote bag with towels, snacks, a first-aid kit, a book on marine mammals "just in case," and three extra hair ties.

"You'll know," Percy said. He was barefoot in the surf, jeans rolled up, a green Camp Half-Blood shirt sticking to his back with sweat. He looked — Clarisse frowned at the thought — he looked happy and sick at the same time, like someone waiting for a birthday party after a funeral.

Clarisse understood that feeling. She'd felt it every time her dad's tidal wave birthday present had arrived without a note. Happy someone remembered. Sick no one stayed.

"There!" Percy pointed.

Far out past the buoys, the water bulged. Then it broke — a huge, shining shape leaping in a silver arc, mane flying, horse head tossed back, fish tail slapping spray twenty feet high. The half-horse, half-fish creature bellowed something that sounded like a trumpet crossed with a neigh and charged toward shore at full gallop, towing a watertight kelp-woven chest on a rope behind it.

"Holy —" Clarisse grabbed her spear out of instinct.

"Don't stab him!" Percy shouted, running into the waves. "Barnacle! Hey, buddy! Easy! Easy!"

The hippocamp — Barnacle — skidded to a stop in chest-deep water, snorting, rolling his huge dark eyes, stamping his front hooves and churning up sand. He was dappled gray-green with a scar on his nose and barnacles (hence the name, Percy explained later) crusted along his fins. When he saw Percy, he let out a sound halfway between a whinny and a sob and shoved his massive head into Percy's chest, nearly knocking him over.

"Whoa, whoa — I missed you too, pal. Yeah. Yeah, I know. No, I didn't forget the sugar kelp. Hold on —" Percy dug in his pocket for the honey cake he'd saved since morning, now slightly squashed. "It's not sugar kelp, but —"

Barnacle inhaled it. Then he sneezed honey crumbs all over Percy and licked his face with a tongue the size of a bath towel.

Silena squealed. Clarisse laughed so hard she had to stab her spear into the sand to stay upright.

"He likes you," Percy said, wiping slime off his cheek, beaming.

"He's slobbered on you," Clarisse corrected. "There's a difference."

It took all three of them to drag the chest up the beach. It was heavier than it looked, woven tight with eel-grass and sealed with wax stamped with the trident of Poseidon. Percy knelt in the sand and broke the seal with his thumbnail, hands shaking just a little.

Silena put a hand on his back. Clarisse, after a second, put her hand next to Silena's. Percy glanced up, grateful, and lifted the lid.

The smell hit first — salt and lavender and hot copper and home. Clarisse didn't know how she knew it was home, but her chest ached anyway.

Inside, packed in layers of soft glow-kelp that pulsed faintly blue, was:

- A jar of deep purple sea-plum jam, labeled in Amphitrite's hand: *For Perseus, Clarisse, and Silena — plenty to share!*
- A folded blue blanket, soft as water, smelling of lavender. Tucked into it was a note: *Surface nights are cold. Love, Mom.*
- A battered leather training manual with TRITON'S — DO NOT TOUCH — MINNOW, IF YOU STEAL THIS I WILL FEED YOU TO A KRAKEN scribbled on the cover, and underneath in smaller writing: *(okay you can borrow it, love you, don't die)*
- Three braided cord bracelets, blue and green and gold, with little shark teeth and pearls.
- A conch shell, smaller than Percy's big one, with a note tied to it: *Press to your ear when you miss us. — A.*
- A flat, formal envelope with a wax trident seal. Percy's name in Poseidon's precise script.
- And at the very bottom, a whole bag of sugar kelp treats for Barnacle, who immediately tried to eat the bag.

Percy stared at it all for a long moment without speaking. Then he picked up the bracelets and held them out.

"Here," he said hoarsely. "Mom made these. One for each of us. She — she must have made them after my letter. Blue for me, green for — Silena, you like green, right? And gold for — Clarisse, gold suits you. You're all — you know. Fierce."

Clarisse took the gold bracelet like it might explode. No one had ever made her anything. Ares kids got weapons. They got armor. They got scars. They did not get braided bracelets from a queen.

"You don't have to —" she started.

"Shut up and put it on," Percy said, suddenly fierce himself. "You're my sister. Mom says so. The jam says 'and his sisters.' That's you. So shut up and put it on."

Silena was already crying and laughing at the same time, tying the green one around her wrist with shaking fingers. "Help me, Clarisse, I can't —"

Clarisse tied Silena's, then held out her own wrist. Percy tied hers, tongue sticking out in concentration, knot neat and double-looped the Atlantean way. Then Silena tied Percy's blue one, and the three of them sat there in the sand staring at their matching wrists like they'd just sworn a blood oath.

"Okay," Clarisse said gruffly, blinking hard. "Okay. Jam. Let's try the jam."

Percy unscrewed the jar. The jam inside was dark as twilight, glittering faintly. He'd talked about it for days — how sea-plums only grew in the midnight trenches, how they tasted like berries and lightning. He dipped in a finger, tasted, closed his eyes.

"Okay?" Silena asked anxiously.

Percy held out the jar. Silena dipped, tasted. Her eyes went wide. "Oh — oh, that's — that's sour and then sweet and then — is it fizzy?"

"That's the lightning," Percy said solemnly.

Clarisse dipped, tasted. It was sour enough to make her jaw ache and sweet enough to make her eyes water and fizzy enough to make her laugh in surprise. "That's disgusting," she announced. "Give me more."

They ate jam with their fingers on the beach while Barnacle dozed in the surf and Silena read Triton's manual out loud in a terrible Triton voice — "LESSON ONE: DO NOT DIE. THIS IS THE MOST IMPORTANT LESSON. LESSON TWO: IF YOU ARE GOING TO DIE, TAKE THE OTHER GUY WITH YOU. YOUR BROTHER, TRITON, WHO IS HANDSOME AND CORRECT" — and Percy laughed until he had sand in his mouth.

Clarisse watched him over the rim of the jam jar and thought about the rule on her shield. She thought about how Percy had said *shut up and put it on, you're my sister* like it was the simplest thing in the world. She thought about Poseidon's formal letter still unopened in the chest, and how Percy's hand had skipped over it twice.

She didn't mention the letter. Instead, when Percy wasn't looking, she tucked the blue blanket around his shoulders because the wind had picked up and his lips were a little blue, and pretended she was just adjusting her spear when he glanced over.

Silena saw. Silena always saw. She smiled at Clarisse over Percy's head and mouthed *thank you*, and Clarisse mouthed back *shut up*, and that was that.

---

Silena Beauregard had kept a journal since she was eight, filling it with pressed flowers, ticket stubs and sketches of her siblings. On the first page, in careful letters, she'd written *Things I Want To Remember When I Am Old.* That evening, after the beach, she opened it to record what she wanted to keep.

*Today Percy shared his family with us. His mom sent jam and bracelets. His brother sent a book that says DO NOT DIE. His dad sent a letter Percy hasn't opened yet. I think he's scared it will be short. Clarisse pretended not to care about the bracelet but she hasn't taken it off and she keeps turning it around her wrist when she thinks no one is looking. Barnacle the hippocamp sneezed on Percy. Percy laughed louder than I've ever heard him. I want to remember the sound.*

She closed the journal and went to find her brother and sister.

She found them in the Poseidon cabin's back room, which the campers called Tempest Hall — half training room, half pool, all water. The floor was mosaic tile, the walls were lined with training weapons and Atlantean coral carvings, and in the center was a deep, clear pool fed directly from the sea through channels under the camp. Chiron had told Silena once that Poseidon himself had shaped it, so his children would always have a place to breathe easy.

Percy was already in the water, floating on his back, blue blanket abandoned on a bench (but folded neatly — Clarisse had folded it when Percy wasn't looking). Clarisse sat on the edge with her feet in, turning her gold bracelet around and around.

"Okay," Percy said when Silena came in with her swimsuit on and a towel and, because she couldn't help herself, snacks. "You wanted Atlantis life. I'm going to teach you an Atlantean game."

"Is it violent?" Clarisse asked hopefully.

"Is it fun?" Silena asked.

"It's both," Percy promised. "It's called Maelstrom."

He explained: one person makes a whirlpool in the middle of the pool. The other two have to swim across without getting pulled in. If you get pulled in, you join the whirlpool-maker and make it bigger. Last person swimming free wins. Atlantean kids played it from age five. Triton had sent diagrams and a note claiming Percy once beat him by swimming under the spin. Percy had practiced the move in the pool before offering to teach it.

"That sounds dangerous," Silena said.

"That sounds awesome," Clarisse said, already sliding in.

Percy made the whirlpool first to demonstrate. Silena watched from the steps, hugging her knees, as her little brother — twelve years old, small for his age, scar on his lip, sea-green eyes bright — raised both hands and *pulled*.

The water obeyed him like a dog obeying its favorite person. It spun up fast, a white-capped funnel in the middle of the pool, humming with power. Percy stood in the shallows at the edge of it, hair plastered down, grinning, completely in his element in a way Silena had never seen at sword practice or archery or even at dinner. This was his first language.

"Okay!" he shouted over the rush. "Go!"

Clarisse dove in like a shark. She powered toward the far wall with huge, furious strokes, jaw set. The current caught her at the halfway mark, tugged her sideways. She fought it, snarling, muscles standing out in her shoulders — and got sucked in with a shriek that turned into laughter halfway through.

"Ha!" Percy crowed. "Join me! Make it bigger!"

Clarisse, spinning in the whirlpool, swearing and laughing, added her own pull. The funnel doubled. Spray hit the ceiling.

Silena, on the steps, clutched her towel. She was a good swimmer — all Poseidon kids were — but she was bookish, careful, the one who read the safety scroll three times before getting in the canoe. She liked the *idea* of whirlpools more than the reality.

Percy saw her face. He eased the spin just a fraction, held out a hand. "Come on! I'll keep you safe. I promise. Swim under, like I told you — deep breath, dive low where it's slow, kick hard. I've got you."

Silena looked at Clarisse, who was now riding the whirlpool like a bull, whooping. She looked at Percy, twelve years old and holding back a maelstrom with one hand while reaching for her with the other.

She took a breath and jumped.

The water was cold and loud and fast. It grabbed her ankle, yanked. Panic flared white behind her eyes — *I'm going to hit the wall, I'm going to —*

Then Percy's hand closed around her wrist under the water, steady as an anchor, and his voice cut through the roar, muffled but clear: *Under! Kick!*

She kicked. She dove low where the water moved slow and sweet, felt Percy's pull guiding her, felt the current slide off her back like oil. She burst out on the far side, gasping, free —

"I did it!" she screamed, clinging to the pool edge. "I did it! Did you see?"

Percy surfaced next to her, laughing, water streaming off his face. Clarisse crashed into both of them, wrapping them in a wet, breathless hug that nearly drowned all three.

"You swam like an Atlantean!" Percy said. "Triton would've — he'd have said —" His voice wobbled suddenly. "He'd have said 'not bad for a surface girl,' which from him is basically a parade."

Silena hugged him tighter. "Then I'll take it."

They played three more rounds. Silena got pulled in twice and won once by hiding behind the ladder until the whirlpool collapsed from exhaustion (Clarisse called it cheating; Percy called it strategy; Silena called it reading *The Art of War*). Clarisse won twice by sheer stubbornness, brute-forcing her way across while screaming sea shanties. Percy lost on purpose once so Silena wouldn't feel bad, and Clarisse caught him and dunked him for it.

By the end they were all pruned and panting, sprawled on the warm tiles around the pool, sharing Silena's snacks (honey cakes, apples, the last of the sea-plum jam on crackers).

"Okay," Clarisse panted. "My turn. Surface game. Truth or dare, camp edition."

"Oh no," Percy groaned.

"Truth," Silena said promptly. "I'm not doing dares in a swimsuit."

"Fine. Truth: what's the weirdest thing about Atlantis that you miss that isn't a person?"

Percy thought. Water dripped off his nose. "The lights," he said at last. "At night, the whole city glows. Glow-kelp on every roof, lanternfish in the streets, the palace dome shining like a moon. And it's quiet — not silent, but soft. Water muffles everything. I miss the nights I had there before coming to camp, falling asleep with the city humming through the walls. Camp is so — loud. Crickets and wind and people. I lie awake sometimes because it's too quiet and too loud at the same time."

Silena wrote that down in her head to put in her journal later: *Camp is too quiet and too loud at the same time.*

"Your turn," Percy said to Clarisse. "Truth or dare."

"Dare," Clarisse said immediately.

"I dare you to tell us something nice about yourself."

"That's not — that's a truth — that's cheating —" Clarisse spluttered while Silena laughed.

"Do it," Silena urged. "Something nice."

Clarisse scowled at the ceiling. The gold bracelet glinted on her wrist. "Fine. I'm — I'm good at mending armor. I learned from the Hephaestus kids. I fixed Chris Rodriguez's breastplate last week and didn't tell anyone because he'd never shut up about it. And I — I like poetry. Okay? I like the loud kind with battles in it. There. Are you happy?"

Percy and Silena stared.

"Clarisse," Silena breathed. "That's lovely."

"Don't tell anyone or I'll kill you."

"We won't," Percy promised solemnly. "I like that. The armor thing. That's — that's secretly kind. Like you."

Clarisse threw a cracker at him. He caught it in his mouth. They all cracked up, and the sound echoed off the wet tile like bells.

Later, while Clarisse toweled off and complained about prune fingers, Silena floated on her back in the settling pool next to Percy, both of them staring at the ceiling painted with waves and stars.

"Percy?" she said quietly, so Clarisse wouldn't hear. "Your dad's letter. You don't have to open it alone, if — if you want company."

Percy's smile faded just a little. He stared up, unblinking. "I know what it'll say. It'll say he's proud and busy and to be brave. Three sentences. He — he doesn't know how to — in Atlantis, he was always in meetings. Triton says he used to be different, before the war with Oceanus heated up again. Before — before I was born, he —" He stopped. "It's okay. Mom's letter was four pages. Triton drew me a diagram of how to beat him in a rematch. That's plenty."

Silena bumped her shoulder against his. "It's okay to want more from him."

Percy was quiet for a long time. Water lapped softly. "Triton said I used to wait outside the council room," he said at last, very quietly. "Just to see Dad walk out. I don't remember it. But during the days before camp, I caught myself doing the same thing. Waiting for him to have a minute."

Silena's throat hurt. She thought of her own father — the god who'd claimed her with a trident flash over her head at age nine and then sent a hippocamp with a coral comb for her birthday every year but never once showed up. She thought of Clarisse, who kept every tidal-wave-smoothed stone her dad's waves left on the beach in a box under her bed.

"We're lucky," she said softly, "in a weird way. We have each other. A lot of demigods don't even get that."

Percy turned his head to look at her. "Yeah," he said. "Yeah, we do."

Clarisse flopped down on the tiles beside them, dripping all over both of them on purpose. "Are you two having feelings without me? Absolutely not. Scoot over. Group float."

They floated in a row, the three Cabin Three siblings, shoulder to shoulder, staring at the painted stars while the pool rocked them gently. And for a little while, the ache eased.

---

Before the cooking began, Silena took the delivery inventory back to the stores desk. It would have been easy to leave it until morning. There was a crate full of strange food, three new bracelets, and a little brother who looked as though he might start laughing or crying whenever somebody said the word *home*. She wanted to join the others. She also knew how quickly gifts stopped being gifts when nobody could agree who was responsible for them.

The night steward was a sleepy satyr named Doron, who had once told Silena that camp paperwork existed so that future campers would know exactly whom to blame. Today he was trying to reconcile six orders for bandage cloth and a box of screws that had appeared without a shipping label.

"Please tell me that's not another unidentified package," he said when she arrived.

"It's family mail." Silena put the courier's manifest on his desk. "Some of the things are for Cabin Three. Some are food we have to store properly. The blankets are already issued, but I want the delivery noted before we use anything."

Doron examined Barnacle's signature, which was a damp imprint of a hoof. "I suppose this is legally binding under whatever maritime law the hippocamps observe."

"Amphitrite sealed the manifest herself."

"Then I'd advise against misplacing it."

They listed the contents together. The food required a cool shelf, the blankets could go straight to the cabin, and three small envelopes were to stay with Percy unless he authorized someone else to handle them. The steward looked up when Silena asked for an extra copy of the last rule.

"Your brother doesn't want the letters read?"

"He hasn't said. That's why I'd rather nobody assume."

Doron marked the entry. "You understand the distinction better than most adults."

"I've worked the stores desk long enough to learn that people take things they think they're entitled to."

He handed her the signed sheet. Silena carried it outside, where the paths were filling with campers returning from afternoon training. She could hear shouting near the lake: Clarisse was trying to convince Percy to race her across the dock, despite having spent half the morning telling him not to exhaust himself before the message from Atlantis.

Near the bathhouse, Annabeth intercepted her.

"Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"Does Percy always react that way when Amphitrite calls?"

Silena thought of the boy staring at the rainbow after the call had ended. He had seemed delighted to see his family. He had also looked painfully uncertain when she asked whether the palace still kept his place set at dinner.

"I've only seen them talk a few times," she said. "I'm not qualified to say what he always does."

Annabeth smiled reluctantly. "Fair."

"Why?"

"He was happy. I know he was. But after the image disappeared he looked as though he'd been waiting for another question, one he wasn't sure how to answer."

Silena folded the manifest into her bag. She hadn't known Percy in the years Annabeth remembered. She was beginning to understand that both of them carried worries they couldn't explain without revealing something larger. The obvious solution would be to ask Percy directly, but that wouldn't tell her whether Annabeth was asking for his sake or her own.

"Did you ask him?"

"Not then. Clarisse was hugging him hard enough to make his ribs complain."

"She calls that hugging?"

"Apparently."

Silena leaned against the wall. "Percy's allowed to miss people when he's with us. He's also allowed to enjoy himself here without proving he doesn't miss them. We can't make him pick one family to be happy about."

"I'm not asking him to pick," Annabeth said, too quickly.

"I know. I'm asking you not to decide what he's feeling for him."

Annabeth looked toward the lake. Silena had begun to notice that whenever somebody offered her a small piece of advice, she wanted to test the whole argument as though it were a bridge about to fail. She wasn't being unkind. She wanted the answer to hold.

"You're right," Annabeth said after a moment. "I'll ask him when we're alone."

"And if he doesn't want to tell you?"

"Then I'll stop asking."

"Good."

They walked to the edge of the practice yard together. A younger child from the Hermes cabin was carrying a damp towel by two fingers, holding it away from herself like something poisonous. She stopped to ask Silena where the clean ones belonged.

Silena explained the laundry bins and watched the girl hurry off.

"How do you keep track of everybody's things?" Annabeth asked.

"Badly, some days. That's why I make people sign forms."

"Percy says you keep an entire cabin running on lists."

"Percy says that because he thinks if I have a list I'll forget to make him put his shoes away."

Annabeth laughed. "Does it work?"

"Not once."

Their conversation should have ended there. Instead Annabeth remained beside the fence, twisting the end of the ribbon at her wrist.

"Amphitrite remembers a childhood Percy can't describe back to her," Silena said quietly. "He goes quiet when she talks about a room or a game from when he was little. I don't know what happened to his memory. I know he gets to decide what to tell her."

Silena turned to her. The words could mean a dozen things, and none was a question she could reasonably answer. Percy had arrived with the kind of gaps in his recent history that frightened anyone responsible for a child. Silena knew he sometimes looked blank when Clarisse mentioned a family outing, then tried to disguise it with a joke. She had learned to let him have that moment.

"I don't know what happened before he came here," she said. "I know she loves him. I know he loves her. If he wants to explain the rest, he can decide when."

Annabeth pressed her lips together. "I know."

Silena could hear the part Annabeth wasn't saying, but refused to fill it in for her. She wasn't going to demand an account of somebody else's memories because she was old enough to recognize the shape of a secret.

"Come eat with us tonight," she said. "You can help us ruin whatever dinner we're attempting."

"I thought Amphitrite sent actual food."

"She did. Percy and Clarisse are already trying to improve it."

"That sounds dangerous."

"It probably is."

They reached Cabin Three to find Clarisse inspecting the little bracelets again. She was holding the gold one near the window, where the last light caught the thread and made the seal's pattern visible.

"Is this supposed to be for fighting?" Clarisse asked without greeting them.

Silena looked at the bracelet. "It's decorative."

"Everything is for fighting if it gets caught in the wrong place. I don't want it snagged on my spear."

"Then take it off when you train."

"That defeats the purpose of a protective gift."

"It is not a ward, Clarisse. It is jewelry from a queen who wanted us to have something of hers."

Clarisse rubbed a thumb along the knot. "So I'm supposed to keep it safe."

"You're allowed to enjoy it."

"I enjoy useful things."

Percy came out of the small washroom in time to hear the argument.

"It is useful," he said. "If you wear it, Amphitrite can pretend she has convinced you to stop getting into fights."

Clarisse glared. "No one can convince me of that."

"Exactly. It makes her optimistic."

She couldn't maintain the glare. The smile that escaped was small and angry at being seen. Silena let her have it.

Percy moved toward the supply crate and took out a pair of neatly folded cloth napkins. He set them beside the bracelets without opening the envelopes in the chest.

"I've got something to ask," he said. "When I send a reply, do you want to put your own notes in it? Or should I tell her how today went?"

Clarisse glanced at Silena. "You can write it."

"Maybe I can. I want you to have the choice."

That changed the room. Silena had been treating the gifts as a statement Amphitrite made to Percy. Clarisse seemed to have been treating them as a test she was meant to pass. But a letter they wrote themselves would be different. They could decide what to say rather than having Percy describe them as additions to his life.

"I'll write something," Silena said. "Not tonight. I want it to sound like me."

"I can tell her I beat you in the shield game," Clarisse said.

"She watched the result," Percy reminded her.

"Then I'll explain the circumstances."

"Which circumstances?"

"The ones that demonstrate I should have won."

Silena fetched clean paper from her writing case. She didn't insist Clarisse use it immediately. Percy took the paper anyway and began making a list of the things the courier could carry safely: letters, small drawings and perhaps a spare photograph if somebody at camp could take one.

"Can we send food back?" Annabeth asked.

"Not without checking the courier's route," Silena said. "Some things spoil before they get down to the palace."

Percy looked at the crate. "I could send her a jar of strawberry jam."

"That might be the first food an Atlantean queen receives from the surface that's actually made for people," Clarisse said.

He gave her a look. "She sent us perfectly edible food."

"You know what I mean."

Silena made a note to ask the kitchens about preserves. It wasn't a promise. It was the first step in finding out what was possible.

A knock at the cabin door interrupted them. Chiron stood outside, carrying a short printed notice from the Big House. The message channel they'd used that morning had been stable, he explained, but repeated openings would require approval because the rainbow could be unreliable in poor weather. He'd arranged for the next available courier dispatch instead.

"There's no need to answer everything tonight," he told Percy. "Your family can receive a letter when the route allows it."

Percy looked at the unopened envelope still waiting in the chest. His father's seal seemed very large for a single piece of paper.

"I'll write after I read the last one," he said.

Chiron nodded. He did not ask why the letter was still sealed, or offer to interpret it. When he'd gone, Silena noticed Percy had stopped moving the paper around the table and was looking toward the door.

"Do you want us to leave?" she asked.

"No. I just don't want to open it with everybody waiting for my reaction."

"Then don't."

Clarisse set down the bracelet. "We'll be in the kitchen. Come when you feel like it."

She said it as an instruction, which made it easier for Percy to accept. Silena collected the manifest and the reply paper, then followed her sister outside. She had expected the afternoon to end with everyone celebrating a queen's gifts. Instead it had reminded her how much work went into making a person feel they could accept something without owing an immediate performance in return.

At the kitchen door, Clarisse stopped.

"Do you think Amphitrite will mind if I write that the bracelets are nice?"

"Why would she mind?"

"Because it sounds stupid."

"It sounds grateful."

Clarisse shrugged. "I might also ask why she didn't tell us about the palace sooner."

"Then ask."

Silena went inside to put the preserves inquiry on the supply board. Behind them the cabin remained quiet. Percy had time to decide when to open his father's letter. His sisters could cook something dreadful without requiring him to be cheerful while they did it.



---

The cooking attempt was Silena's idea, which meant it was ambitious, under-supplied, and doomed.

"We have sea-plum jam," she announced at dinner, spreading Triton's manual open to a page titled *FIELD RATIONS: WHAT TO EAT WHEN YOU ARE LOST AND YOUR BROTHER IS USELESS.* "We have sugar kelp from Barnacle's treats — Percy, did you steal those? — we have camp kitchen access for one hour, and we have heritage. We're making Atlantean dumpling soup."

Clarisse, mouth full of pork chop, said, "I'm in."

Percy, who had nearly set fire to palace kitchen curtains during his short stay before camp, said, "Maybe we should invite Annabeth. She's good at following instructions."

"Invite everyone," Silena said grandly. "It's culture."

So at seven o'clock, the camp kitchen — usually the domain of harried dryad cooks and the Demeter kids on dish duty — was invaded by: Percy, Clarisse, Silena, Annabeth, Thalia, Grover, Ella (who had brought her own apron that said KISS THE HARPY), Zoe (who claimed she was only there to supervise but had tied her hair back), and Nico (who had been invited because Thalia said he looked hungry).

"Okay," Percy said, standing on a stool to address his troops, Triton's manual in one hand, a wooden spoon in the other. "Dumpling soup. Step one: broth. In Atlantis we use deep-current broth with glow-kelp and crab. Here we have —" He peered into the pantry. "Chicken stock and seaweed snacks. That'll work."

Annabeth, sleeves rolled up, already chopping scallions with terrifying precision, said, "Define 'work.'"

"It'll be soup," Percy said. "That's the bar. Soup."

What followed was chaos of the finest kind.

Grover tried to grate ginger and got it in his eye and had to be led to the sink by Ella, who recited antidotes while patting his head. Thalia attempted to knead dough and ended up with it stuck to her jacket, her hair, and Nico's eyebrow. Nico, trying to help, misread "fold gently" as "punch repeatedly" and was banned from dough duty by Annabeth. Zoe, to everyone's shock, turned out to be excellent at pleating dumplings — "Hecate's daughters learn nimble fingers," she said primly, producing a perfect crescent while Clarisse's looked like a deflated football.

"Yours looks like it's been in a fight," Percy told Clarisse.

"It has," Clarisse said, wrestling her dough. "With me. And I'm winning."

Silena floated between stations, tasting broth, adjusting salt, tying back hair, laughing until she cried when Barnacle's sugar kelp, added to the broth on Percy's insistence, turned the whole pot faintly blue and fizzy.

"It's supposed to do that," Percy insisted, though his ears were red. "It's — it's the lightning. Sea-plum jam dumplings, blue broth — it's festive."

Annabeth leaned against the counter next to him, flour on her cheek, watching Silena show Thalia how to pleat properly, watching Clarisse threaten Nico with a ladle for stealing a dumpling, watching Grover and Ella dance around the sink to the radio.

"You okay?" she asked quietly, bumping his shoulder.

Percy bumped back. "Yeah. Mom sent instructions with the manual. She wrote that she used to let me stir, and Triton stole dumplings raw and got his hand smacked. I can't remember that kitchen being full of us. But I can try making it full of people now." He glanced around. "It's nice. Having everybody here."

Annabeth was quiet for a second. The kitchen steamed around them, blue broth bubbling, flour dusting the air like snow. "Can I tell you something weird?"

"Always."

"Watching you with Clarisse and Silena today — on the dock, at the beach — it made me feel —" She picked at a cuticle. "Jealous isn't right. It's not that I want your family. It's that — Thalia's my sister, really my sister, same mom, same dad, same everything, and since coming back, we haven't found much of that. Easy. She's sixteen and grieving and I'm twelve and I remember being seventeen and we orbit each other and we're polite and we love each other so much it hurts and we don't know how to just — make dumplings. Does that make sense?"

Percy looked at her — really looked. Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus in this life, daughter of Athena in the last, twelve years old with gray eyes too old for her face, flour on her cheek, hands shaking just a little.

"Yeah," he said softly. "It makes sense."

"Thalia doesn't remember the life we do," Annabeth whispered. "She remembers raising me. All the little things I can't give back when she asks. And now she's got this whole temple of expectations and our dad — Zeus — he's not —" She laughed, short and wet. "Your dad sent jam and a letter and you think he's distant. My cabin records have a note from Zeus that says 'excel.' One word. Excel."

Percy didn't know what to say to that. So he did what Silena had done for him on the dock — he stayed. He bumped her shoulder again and held it there.

"You're good at this," Annabeth said after a second, nodding at the chaos. "Making people family. You did it with dumplings."

"My mom says soup fixes most things," Percy said. "Triton says soup fixes hunger and everything else needs a sword. They're both kind of right."

Annabeth laughed, real this time. "Come on. Let's finish before Clarisse kills Nico."

The soup, when it was finally served in chipped camp bowls at the big kitchen table, was blue, lumpy, too salty, slightly fizzy, and — as Grover declared through a mouthful — "amazing!"

"Really?" Silena asked anxiously, perched on the bench with her bowl cupped in both hands. "Honestly?"

Thalia slurped loudly. "Honestly? It's weird as hell. I love it. Give me more fizzy dumplings."

Clarisse held up her bowl. "To Percy. And his weird glowing soup. And his mom's jam. And Triton, who is handsome and correct."

"To Triton!" Nico echoed, mouth full.

"To Amphitrite," Zoe said quietly. "Queen who holds rainbows steady."

"To sisters," Silena said, eyes bright, clinking her bowl against Clarisse's and Percy's. "And brothers. And the people who choose to stay."

"To staying," Percy said.

They ate until the pot was empty. Ella collected everyone's favorite words for the soup ("effervescent!" "briny!" "brothy!" "blue!") and wrote them on a napkin for her lexicon. Grover played a soft tune on his pipes while they cleaned, and even Thalia hummed along, her arm around Annabeth's shoulders, both of them flour-dusted and laughing.

Percy washed dishes next to Clarisse, who elbowed him every time he tried to hand her a wet plate, and across the sink Silena dried and stacked, and for a minute Percy closed his eyes and listened — water running, sisters bickering, friends laughing, Annabeth explaining to Nico why you don't put metal in a microwave while Thalia pretended not to listen — and it sounded, just for a second, like the palace kitchen at home. Loud and warm and full.

He opened his eyes to find Clarisse watching him.

"What?" he asked.

"Nothing," she said gruffly. Then, quietly, so only he could hear over the clatter: "Thanks. For today. The bracelets. The game. All of it. I —" She scrubbed hard at a pot. "I've never had a little brother before. It's — it's not terrible."

Percy grinned. "High praise from Clarisse La Rue."

"Shut up and dry."

He dried.

---

Later, much later, after Barnacle had been sent off with a belly full of sugar kelp and a note tied to his mane (*Thank you for everything. We ate the jam. Percy cried a little but don't tell him I told you. Love, S. + C. + P.* — Clarisse had objected to the crying part and been overruled), after the kitchen was clean and the campers had drifted off to their cabins, Percy sat on the steps of Cabin Three with the unopened letter from his father in his lap.

The night was warm. Crickets sawed. The lake glinted through the trees. His blue blanket was around his shoulders, smelling of lavender and home.

He turned the envelope over and over. Wax trident. His name in precise script. Three sentences, probably. *I am proud. Be brave. Be kind.* Or maybe just *Be brave.*

Footsteps in the grass. Annabeth sat down next to him without asking, Thalia a shadow behind her — Thalia nodded once at Percy, then kept walking toward the Zeus cabin, giving them space. Which, from Thalia, was basically a hug.

"Hey," Annabeth said. She had changed into pajamas and a sweatshirt, her braid loose. "You opened it yet?"

"No."

"Want company?"

Percy looked at her. Gray eyes, steady. Flour still on her cheek from the kitchen — he reached over without thinking and wiped it off with his thumb. She went still, then leaned into his hand just for a second before pulling back, ears red.

"Yeah," he said. "Stay."

He broke the seal.

Inside was a single sheet of thick palace paper. Poseidon's handwriting, neat and slanted:

*Perseus —*

*Your mother tells me you have found sisters. Good. Protect them, as Triton protects you. I am proud of the seven-minute shield. Chiron says you will manage ten by summer. I believe him.*

*The sea-plum jam is from the trench garden your mother planted when you were born. I helped carry water. Do not tell Triton; he thinks I am too dignified to carry water.*

*Be brave. Be kind. Come home when the currents allow.*

*— Father*

Percy read it three times. Then he handed it to Annabeth without a word. She read it, carefully, then folded it back exactly along the crease.

"That's more than three sentences," she said softly. "And he carried water."

Percy laughed, wet and surprised. "He carried water. He — he never tells anyone stuff like that."

"He tells you."

"Yeah." Percy pulled the blanket tighter. The gold bracelet — no, the blue one — glinted on his wrist. Green for Silena, gold for Clarisse, blue for him. "He tells me."

They sat in silence for a while, shoulders touching, listening to the camp settle. From the Poseidon cabin behind them came muffled voices — Clarisse snoring, Silena turning pages, the splash of the pool in Tempest Hall.

"Annabeth?" Percy said at last.

"Yeah?"

"Thanks. For today. For — for sitting with me. For the dumplings. For —"

"Anytime, Seaweed Brain."

He smiled at the old nickname, made new again. "You know, Triton's manual has a whole chapter on surface sisters. It says they're stubborn and scary and will dunk you if you lose on purpose."

"Accurate."

"It says to keep them close and feed them jam."

"Also accurate."

Percy stood, stretched, held out a hand to pull her up. She took it. They stood on the cabin steps for a second, two twelve-year-olds with seventeen-year-old memories, salt and flour and sea-plum jam still sticky on their fingers.

"Goodnight, Annabeth."

"Goodnight, Percy."

She walked toward the Zeus cabin, braid swinging, and Thalia was waiting on the steps for her — Thalia, sixteen and grieving and trying, who opened her arms and let her little sister lean in, who listened while Annabeth told her about blue soup and longer letters and a queen who held rainbows steady.

And Percy went inside, to his sisters, to his too-loud, too-quiet cabin that smelled of lemon soap and lavender.

Clarisse was asleep on top of her covers with her spear across her chest and her gold bracelet glinting. Silena was awake in the bunk next to his, journal open, pen in hand.

"How was the letter?" she whispered.

Percy climbed into his bunk, pulled the blue blanket up to his chin, and smiled at the ceiling.

"He carried water," he said.

Silena didn't understand, but she wrote it down anyway: *He carried water. Percy smiled. Goodnight.*

Outside, the gulls had finally gone quiet. Percy lay awake a little longer while Silena finished her journal entry. Clarisse turned over in the next bunk and demanded that he stop smiling in the dark because she could somehow hear it.

"Not possible," Percy whispered.

"Then stop making it possible."

He turned toward the wall. He was still smiling when he fell asleep.
