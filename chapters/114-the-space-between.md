## Chapter 114: The Space Between

The first time Percy and Annabeth disagreed about a familiar sound, it was about a song.

Annabeth hummed it while they walked back from the library. Percy stopped so abruptly she nearly walked into him.

"Where did you learn that?"

She paused. "I don't know. It was in my head."

"It's an Atlantis song. Triton sang it on my last visit. He says he used to sing it when I was little."

"Maybe I heard it from you."

"I never taught it to you."

Annabeth's face closed. "I meant I could have heard somebody humming it here."

"Silena knows sea songs. You could have heard her. I just don't remember her singing this one."

"I don't remember both childhoods, Percy. A tune sounding familiar doesn't change that."

Percy heard the edge in her voice and realized he'd pushed too hard. "Sorry. I didn't mean—"

"You asked where I got it. I answered."

"I know. I just got surprised."

She continued walking. Percy let her get several steps ahead before he followed. The path curved around a stand of maples, and the library's stone wall blocked the breeze for a few yards. Annabeth tucked her hands into her sleeves. He could have filled the quiet with another explanation, but none of them changed the question he'd already asked.

At the next turn, she glanced back. “Are you coming?”

“If you want me to.”

“I wouldn't have asked if I didn't.”

“Right.”

She resumed the walk. He matched her pace without moving close enough to crowd her. The gravel clicked under their shoes. A pair of campers ran past toward the pavilion, arguing about who had lost a wooden token. Annabeth stepped aside to let them through, then looked toward the lake path rather than the library.

“I was going there,” she said. “You can come if you want. I don't need to talk about it yet.”

“Okay.”

He wanted to understand the childhood he'd been told was his, but he couldn't turn every familiar sound into a test of what Annabeth knew. Walking beside her without asking the question again was something he could do before he knew what the song meant to either of them.

At the lake, Annabeth sat on the dock and looked out over the water. Percy sat a few feet away, giving her room.

"I don't like feeling as if I need to prove where every little thing came from," she said.

"You shouldn't have to."

"Sometimes you ask me questions like you want me to be the older me. And sometimes you say you don't expect that. I don't know which version of you I'm supposed to answer."

Percy took in her words. He didn't want to defend himself. He wanted to understand what he'd done.

"You're right," he said. "I don't want you to prove anything. I got caught up in the song because it reminded me of home. I should've said that instead of asking where you got it."

Annabeth's shoulders eased slightly. "I don't know where I got it. Maybe it was from somebody here and I wasn't paying attention. Maybe my fingers know the tune better than I do. It doesn't give me back a childhood."

"It can just be a song you like."

She looked at him. "That's allowed?"

"I think so."

Annabeth smiled faintly. "Then I'm keeping it."

"Good."

He sat beside her, leaving a little space between their shoulders. She looked at that space, then at him.

"You can come closer."

Percy shifted nearer, slowly enough that she could change her mind. Annabeth rested her head against his shoulder.

"Thanks for listening," she said.

"Thanks for telling me."

They watched the water until the song came back to her. This time she hummed it softly, and Percy listened without trying to claim it.

They did not solve where Annabeth had first heard the melody. The next morning, that fact kept finding Percy whenever he tried to put his attention elsewhere.

He made it through breakfast without asking her again. That was not the same as being fine. The pavilion was all scrape and clatter, a bench leg squealing against stone while one of the younger campers tried to balance a bowl on his head. Percy found himself listening for the tune between voices, as if the song might arrive on someone else's breath and provide an answer he could use.

It didn't. The sound he heard was Clarisse telling a boy to stop leaning back in his chair before he cracked his skull. Silena was sorting the small painted labels Katie had asked her to consider for the winter herb bed. She had set the samples beside her plate, each one marked in neat black letters. Percy picked at a piece of toast.

“You look like you lost an argument with breakfast,” Silena said.

“Breakfast started it.”

Clarisse glanced over. “Breakfast is toast.”

“Exactly. Hard to trust something that gets its personality from being set on fire.”

Silena turned one of her labels so it faced him. “This is thyme. It isn't supposed to be exciting.”

“That's what it wants you to think.”

Clarisse huffed a laugh, then noticed that he was looking past them. Her smile thinned. “You do have a question.”

He could have lied. He almost did. “That song Annabeth hummed yesterday. Do you know it?”

“Which one?”

Percy tapped the tabletop once, then caught himself. “I don't know what it's called. It has a little rise at the end, like this.” He hummed the last three notes, quietly. “Triton sang it the last time I visited Atlantis.”

Silena's expression changed. Not to recognition. To care, which was worse in a way; it made him feel that he had brought her a problem she ought to help carry. “I don't know that one.”

“You know sea songs.”

“Some. Not every song that exists under the sea.”

“I know. I just wondered if you'd heard it here.”

Clarisse set down her cup. “Are you asking because you want to know where Annabeth learned it, or because you want to know whether your song is yours?”

“It's not mine.” He stopped. “I mean, it isn't a song I wrote. But Triton said he used to sing it when I was little.”

“He said that to you?”

“When I was there. On the visit. He didn't say Annabeth knew it, or why.”

Clarisse nodded once. “Then the part you know is that Triton sang it for you, and he said he used to sing it when you were little.”

“That's the part I know.”

“And what you want to know is how the melody got to her.”

Percy folded the edge of his napkin. “Yeah.”

Silena gathered the herb labels into a stack. The painted boards clicked together. “There are songs people bring from home. There are songs they hear once and keep. There are ones somebody hums while doing chores and nobody remembers teaching anyone. A tune can travel through camp without anybody marking the route.”

“That doesn't mean that's what happened.”

“No. It means we don't have enough to say.”

He looked at her. “You haven't heard it?”

“I don't remember hearing it. That isn't the same as proving I never did.”

There it was: a careful answer with no hook in it. Percy couldn't argue with it, and he didn't want to. He had already made a mistake once by turning Annabeth's answer into evidence against a gap she had never offered to fill.

Clarisse leaned her forearms on the table. “If you're trying to find out whether she remembers something she shouldn't, quit.”

“I'm not.”

“You did yesterday.”

He looked down. “I asked where she learned it.”

“You asked like the answer could make the song safe.” Clarisse's voice wasn't angry, but she had stopped eating. “You wanted a reason it was in her head. You wanted it to be from somebody here, or from you, or from some other thing you could point at. Then you wouldn't have to wonder.”

“I was surprised.”

“Doesn't make it better for her.”

Silena touched the corner of Clarisse's wrist, not stopping her, only marking that she was there. “He knows.”

“He says he knows.”

Percy didn't protest. She had earned the right to be blunt with him. He knew how it felt when people expected him to remember a childhood other people had watched him live. The tune had touched his own uncertainty, and he'd pushed it toward Annabeth as if her answer would settle his.

“I don't think she's lying,” he said.

“Then don't make her keep proving it,” Clarisse replied.

That landed harder than the first accusation. Not because it was a new rule, but because it pointed at a habit he had tried to pretend was only one question.

Silena's eyes moved briefly to the labels, then back to him. “Would you like me to ask Katie if she recognizes it? Only if that helps, and only if you aren't asking me to make a search party out of the camp.”

“No,” Percy said, then corrected himself. “I mean, you don't have to. If she happens to know it, fine. I don't want people quizzing each other about whether they've heard it.”

“All right.” Silena slid the labels into her satchel. “I can tell you if it comes up while I'm working on the garden sign. I won't turn it into a hunt.”

“Thanks.”

Clarisse nudged his toast with one finger. “And you can tell Annabeth the song reminds you of home. Start there next time.”

“I did.”

“After you made her defend herself.”

“I know.”

She raised a brow at him. “Are you going to keep saying that, or are you going to do something different?”

“Something different.”

“Good. Start by eating. You get worse when you skip breakfast.”

“I don't get worse.”

“You get louder.”

“That's not worse.”

Silena pushed the toast plate closer. “It is for everyone trying to talk.”

He laughed, and it came easier than he expected. The question was still there, but no one had given him a false answer to take home. That was better than a tidy story built to quiet him.

When they stood, Silena tucked the samples into her bag and Clarisse took the empty cups. Percy reached for them first. Clarisse let him carry two, then kept the third for herself.

“A compromise?” he asked.

“A test.”

“Of what?”

“Whether you can make it to the wash basin without turning the cup into a spear.”

“I have never done that.”

“Yet.”

She walked off before he could answer. Silena fell into step beside him, her satchel tapping lightly against her hip. The painted boards had shifted inside it; Percy heard their soft clack at every few steps. He did not ask again about the song.

That promise became harder to keep when the library opened and a pair of campers began practicing a round on the steps outside. Their voices never quite met. One boy rushed the second line, and the girl beside him kept stopping to correct the start instead of finishing the tune. Percy found himself matching the intervals under his breath, then shut his mouth.

There was no reason to hide the melody from everyone. There was no reason to hand it around like a clue, either.

He ducked through the library doors before the campers could ask if he knew the song. Inside, the air was cool and papery, with a faint smell of polish from the shelves. A chair scraped in the back room. Someone had left an open atlas on a table, pages pinned down by a smooth river stone. Percy went to the desk and took out a blank sheet.

He wrote one sentence: *Triton sang the melody during my last actual visit to Atlantis. He told me he used to sing it when I was little.*

Then he stopped.

That was all he knew. He did not remember hearing it as a child. He did not know if Triton had sung it directly to him, or only near him, or whether the words had changed across years. He did not know whether Annabeth had heard it from Silena, another camper, a story, or nowhere he could trace. He only knew it had sounded familiar to her in a way that did not give her a missing memory.

The neatness of the sentence bothered him. It looked like the beginning of a record, the kind he could carry to someone else and ask them to add a line. If he wrote enough facts down, maybe they would stop feeling slippery. He held the quill over the page, then set it aside.

The note was not for a case file. It was for him.

He folded the page once and slid it into the pocket of his jacket. The words could stay small. If Annabeth wanted to ask about them, he'd answer. If she wanted to hum without explaining, he could listen. Neither choice required him to collect witnesses.

Outside, the two campers had reached the end of the round. The girl laughed at the failed harmony and the boy argued that he had been singing the correct version. They started over, slower this time. Percy paused on the threshold long enough to hear them find the same note, then walked toward the cabins.

---

Annabeth found Thalia on the Zeus cabin porch with a length of cord looped over one knee and a screwdriver balanced across her palm. A shallow puddle had collected on the top step. Water dripped from the temporary gutter support in a slow, irregular beat, hitting the same patch of dirt over and over.

“It's supposed to drain toward the barrel,” Thalia said. “It has developed a different opinion.”

Annabeth set down her satchel. “How long has it been doing that?”

“Since the rain stopped.”

“That's not a time.”

“Long enough for me to notice. Not long enough for the whole cabin to float away.”

The temporary support Charlie had fitted still held the gutter away from the wall. It was a plain piece of wood, braced with two screws and a strap that kept the loose end from tilting. The runoff had found a seam at the joint and slipped down the outer edge instead of following the channel to the barrel. Not a collapse. A nuisance with a predictable path.

Annabeth studied the slope from the porch. “We can shift the barrel closer and put a catch board under the seam until Chiron approves a permanent bracket.”

“Or we can wait until the ground dries and put the barrel back where it belongs.”

“If we wait, the next rain uses the same path.”

“And if we put the barrel there, people trip over it coming out.” Thalia gave the porch a glance as if the offending people might appear on cue. “There's a reason I had it against the side wall.”

Annabeth looked again. The narrow path ran from the steps to the cabin's side entrance. Moving the barrel into it would solve the drip and create a worse problem. She had been too quick to turn a visible failure into an easy fix.

“You're right,” she said. “I didn't account for the door.”

Thalia blinked once. “You can say that again.”

“I said I was wrong. Don't make me repeat it for the record.”

“I wouldn't dream of it.” The answer came lightly, but Thalia set the screwdriver down instead of tightening her grip on it. “Could make a shallow channel into the bed. The rain already runs that direction once it leaves the steps.”

“That could work if we keep it away from the foundation. Does the bed have anything planted there?”

“Not on that edge. But Katie's planning winter herbs for the other side.”

Annabeth remembered Silena's painted labels, then caught the thought before it turned into certainty about the garden plan. “I'll ask Katie before we dig.”

“Good.” Thalia stood and tested the porch board with her heel. “I was about to do that before you got here.”

“You were about to dig without asking Katie?”

“I was about to ask Katie.”

“With the shovel already out?”

“I like to be prepared.”

“You like to decide and then ask whether anyone objects.”

Thalia's face sharpened. “That's not what I was doing.”

Annabeth stepped back from the puddle. She could feel the conversation turning away from the gutter, and she had a choice: soften what she meant until it disappeared, or say it plainly and risk the look on Thalia's face.

“You came up with the solution before I got here,” she said. “Then you told me what was wrong with my first one. That doesn't mean you can't listen. It means I want to be part of the decision before there's a shovel in the ground.”

Thalia glanced at the shovel leaning by the post. “I hadn't moved it.”

“I know.”

“I thought you'd want to help.”

“I do. That's not the same as wanting the task picked for me.”

The rain had left the porch smelling of wet cedar and the metallic bite of the gutter. A drop slipped from its lip and struck the dirt between them. Thalia watched it land.

“Okay,” she said at last. “Then let's ask Katie before we choose the channel.”

“And decide together after we know what she wants.”

“You have a way of making a normal sentence sound like a contract.”

“You have a way of making a contract sound like an ambush.”

That got a short laugh out of her. “Fair.”

They found Katie at the garden plot, tying back a vine that had slumped against its support. She listened to the problem, looked at the slope and tapped the damp earth with the end of her trowel. The herb bed would be farther along the opposite side, she said; a shallow runnel near the cabin would be fine as long as it emptied away from the foundation and did not cross the path.

“I can mark where the bed will go,” Katie offered. “Then you can keep the water on the open side.”

Thalia looked to Annabeth. “That work?”

Annabeth took the offered trowel and drew a line in the soil with its blunt end. “If we leave this strip clear and test it with a bucket before the next rain.”

Katie nodded. “I'll put a stake at the future bed edge.”

They worked without rushing. Thalia measured the distance from the foundation while Annabeth marked a low channel that bent around the corner of the porch. Katie set the stake and pulled a length of twine taut to show where the herb bed would end. There was no digging until they checked the slope with water. Annabeth asked to try the first pour; Thalia handed her the bucket without commenting on how full it was.

The water ran along their shallow test line for a few feet, then pooled where the dirt dipped. Annabeth crouched and pushed a little soil aside with the trowel. Thalia put her finger at the lower edge and traced the intended direction. They adjusted the line by a handspan and tried again. This time the water made it around the turn and sank into the grass, clear of both the path and the foundation.

“That's the route,” Annabeth said.

“That's a route,” Thalia corrected. “We should see what it does after more than half a bucket.”

“We have to wait for rain to know that.”

“We can ask Chiron whether we can borrow the hose from the stables.”

“I'll ask him.”

Thalia tilted her head. “You want to ask him?”

“I want to do my part of the repair. You can ask if he has concerns about the support.”

“All right.” Thalia wound the twine around the stake. “We can tell him both things.”

The agreement did not smooth every edge between them. Thalia still looked at the cabin whenever someone came near the wet step, and Annabeth still had to stop herself from making a plan before she had asked who else would live with it. But the next decision had not been made in advance. They had a route to test, Katie's boundary line, and one task each to carry to Chiron.

When they returned to the porch, Thalia picked up her spear and began rubbing the blade with an oil cloth. Annabeth sat on the step beside her. She did not ask what Thalia was thinking. Thalia did not ask if Annabeth had remembered something else.

After a while, Thalia said, “You used to like making little streams in the dirt. You'd build a wall out of pebbles, then get mad when the water went around it.”

“You remember that?”

“I remember a lot of things from when you were small.”

“I don't.”

Thalia rubbed the cloth along the spear shaft. “I know.”

“Does it bother you?”

“Sometimes.” She did not look up. “Sometimes I want to tell you everything, and sometimes I want to let you have a day where I don't bring up who you used to be.”

“I don't want you to stop telling me things.”

“I know. You also don't have to act like you remember them.”

Annabeth watched a drop of water hang from the edge of the gutter and finally fall. “I can hear the story and care about it. I just can't give it back to you as if I was there.”

Thalia's cloth stopped. “That makes sense.”

“It can matter to you and still be something I learned from you.”

“Yeah.” Thalia resumed polishing. “That's probably better than me quizzing you about the damn laundromat every time a photograph turns up.”

“You can tell me about it when you want.”

“I can do that.”

They sat for another few minutes, each watching a different part of the cabin. A pair of campers came up the path carrying a stack of bowls. Thalia moved her spear out of their way; Annabeth shifted the bucket so they could pass. Nobody asked them what they'd fixed. The work was still unfinished, but it had become a shared plan instead of a silent test of whether either sister could guess what the other needed.

When Annabeth stood to go, Thalia called after her. “Tell Chiron about the hose before you get pulled into the library.”

“I'll tell him.”

“And if the ground holds, we finish the channel.”

“Together?”

“If you want.”

“I do.”

Thalia nodded as if that settled the practical part. It didn't settle everything else. It was enough to give them a next step.

Annabeth found Chiron near the Big House steps and asked about borrowing the hose. He listened while she described the gutter joint, the proposed channel, Katie's boundary stake and the half-bucket test. He asked where the foundation sat relative to the low point. She showed him with her hands, then realized she was describing a slope she'd only estimated from the porch.

“I should measure it properly,” she said.

“That would help,” Chiron said. “You may use the hose for a short test after you mark the foundation line. Keep the flow low. If water begins to pool against the cabin, stop and bring me back to look.”

“We'll do that.”

“You and Thalia?”

“Yes.”

He gave a small nod. “Good. Tell me before you dig. The temporary support is doing its job, so there is no need to rush the permanent fix.”

Annabeth carried the answer back to the cabin. Thalia had laid the spear across the porch railing and was oiling its point. When Annabeth told her what Chiron had said, she asked to see the foundation line first, just as Chiron had.

“I hadn't marked it,” Annabeth admitted.

Thalia handed her a piece of chalk. “Then let's mark it.”

They followed the cabin wall and made a faint line on the dry boards where water should not reach. Annabeth measured from the lowest point to the corner and wrote the numbers on a scrap of paper. Thalia held the end of the line while Annabeth checked the distance twice. They did not agree immediately on the channel's curve. Thalia wanted it to turn sooner; Annabeth thought that would put the runnel too close to Katie's future bed.

“If we cut here, the water goes wide of the stake,” Annabeth said.

“And runs toward the path.”

“Only if the ground slopes the other way.”

“Which is what we need the hose to tell us.”

Annabeth looked at her. “You're right.”

“Write it down. Twice in one day; we should keep a record.”

“Don't make me regret admitting things.”

“Too late.”

They borrowed the hose and stretched it from the spigot, keeping the coil clear of the steps. Percy passed on the path and offered to hold it. Thalia thanked him and asked if he could keep the hose from kinking at the corner. He did, then went on when she said they had the rest handled. Annabeth watched him leave without feeling that his departure meant anything beyond the job ending.

The first test ran too quickly. Water cut a bright line through the shallow dirt and pooled against the grass near the path. Thalia shut the spigot. Annabeth crouched at the low point and scraped a little soil toward the channel, careful to keep the foundation line dry.

“We need a wider turn,” she said.

“And less water at once.”

“The rain won't ask permission.”

“No, but we can shape the dirt so it doesn't all hit the turn at the same time.” Thalia pointed at the patch they'd left flat. “A little shelf, then the runnel.”

“That might make it spread too close to the herb bed.”

“Only if Katie's stake is the bed's final edge.”

“It is what she asked us to respect.”

Thalia leaned back on her heels. “Then we don't move the water closer to it. We move the turn away.”

They marked a new curve with the trowel handle, then tried the hose again at a slower flow. The water followed the channel, reached the low patch, and spread across the grass instead of crossing the path. It was not a finished repair. It was a better answer to the question they'd asked.

Annabeth wrote the result beside the measurements. “This is still only a test.”

“I know.”

“We need Chiron to see it before anyone digs.”

“I know.” Thalia wiped wet dirt from her palm onto her jeans. “You don't have to repeat the rule to prove I'm listening.”

“I'm repeating it because I want to remember the boundary too.”

That made Thalia pause. “Okay.”

They coiled the hose, then carried it back together. No one had to win the disagreement for the channel to improve. Thalia had offered a different route; Annabeth had held to Katie's boundary; the water had made the decision more precise than either of them could have done by staring at the dirt.

Back at the porch, Thalia picked up her spear again. “You want to sit for a bit?”

“I have a library errand.”

“After.”

Annabeth looked at the scrap paper, then tucked it into her notebook. “After.”

“You can bring your book. I'll sharpen this.”

“And we can talk?”

“If we have anything to say.”

“That sounds like your version of an invitation.”

“It's the version you've got.”

“I'll take it.”

At the library, Annabeth returned two books and checked a reference on drainage. She found a better way to estimate the ground's pitch and copied the method into her notebook. The pages smelled faintly of dust and ink. Her pencil pressed hard at the first calculation, then lighter as the figures settled into place. She did not need Percy to translate the problem or Thalia to decide what she should ask. The task was hers, and the people around it could still matter.

When she returned, Thalia had finished sharpening the spear and was threading the oil cloth through a small loop in its sheath. Annabeth sat one step below her, notebook open on her knees.

“I checked the slope,” she said. “The channel should stay clear if we cut along the line we marked. But we need to see how the ground moves water after a longer flow.”

“Chiron said low flow.”

“Yes. We can do a longer test with the same low flow next time. If it starts pooling, we stop.”

“Good.” Thalia gave the sheath a final tug. “We can finish the repair when Chiron looks at the plan.”

Annabeth set the notebook aside. “You asked whether I wanted to sit.”

“I did.”

“I don't have a question ready.”

“You don't need one.” Thalia rested her forearms on her knees. “We can sit without turning it into a summit.”

“You make it sound easy.”

“It isn't. That's why I have a spear to clean.”

Annabeth smiled. “What were you thinking about before I got here?”

“Whether I could get away with putting the barrel back in the path and telling the next person to watch where they walk.”

“That would've been a bad plan.”

“A little.”

“And now?”

“Now I think Katie's going to want a clear bed edge, and you were right to ask before changing it.”

Annabeth looked at the numbers in her notebook. She could not remember being four, making a watercourse around pebbles. She could imagine the dirt under her hands and the frustration of the water going somewhere she hadn't planned. She could imagine it without pretending the image belonged to her.

“If you tell me a story about that,” she said, “I might ask you to tell it again later.”

Thalia's gaze stayed on the spear point. “You might.”

“Would you mind?”

“No. I like telling you about you. I just don't like when it feels like I'm trying to lend you a memory you can't keep.”

“You can't lend it to me.”

“I know.”

“But you can tell me what you remember.”

“I can do that.”

They didn't promise to remember every distinction correctly. Annabeth turned back to her notes; Thalia ran a cloth down the spear shaft. The space between them held the work they had done and the things they could not exchange. It did not need to be filled to let them sit there together.

The next day, Percy asked if she wanted to hear the full song.

Annabeth considered. "Yes. But tell me what it means to you. Not what it means for me."

He sat beside her in the shade and sang it in Atlantean. His voice was rough and untrained. The melody rose and fell like a current moving through a narrow channel. The words were about a small fish that left the reef, learned the deep sea, and returned with a different name.

The shade came from the broad oak beyond the path to the beach. They had chosen it because the bench was empty and the branches muffled the afternoon noise. A few campers crossed the green with baskets of laundry; their talk drifted away before reaching them. Percy had asked whether Annabeth wanted the full song, then waited while she considered. He had not said she would recognize it. He had not asked her to close her eyes or listen for anything hidden. She had agreed because she wanted to hear what the tune meant to him.

Before he sang, he gave her the little he had been told. Triton had performed it during Percy's last visit. At some point, he had said he used to sing it when Percy was little. Percy did not have a memory attached to that statement. He could remember Triton's voice from that recent visit, the way the words had sounded as they crossed the room, and his brother's easy teasing afterward. He could not turn those details into a scene from the childhood he had been told about.

“Did he say it was a lullaby?” Annabeth asked.

“No.”

“A song he sang to you?”

“He said he used to sing it when I was little. I don't know if he meant to me or around me.”

“And you don't remember it.”

“No.”

She nodded. “Thank you for keeping those separate.”

He wasn't sure why that made his throat tighten. Maybe because the separation was the only honest shape he could give the story. “I can tell you what the words mean, as far as I know them.”

“You don't know exactly?”

“I know the song. The translation isn't word for word. Some of the turns are more like a saying than a sentence.”

“Then tell me the version you know.”

He started with a quiet breath, and the first notes came out uncertain. The Atlantean sat differently in his mouth than English; he had learned its sounds by listening and getting corrected, not by any memory of a childhood lesson. He shaped each phrase slowly. The song moved up, curved down, then paused before its last part. At one point he lost the note and stopped.

“Sorry,” he said.

“Do you want to start again?”

“I think I can get that bit.”

“Okay.”

He tried the phrase a second time, rougher and more confident. Annabeth stayed still, eyes on the path beyond his shoulder. She wasn't scanning his face for recognition. That helped him keep singing.

He finished on a note that wavered, then let the silence settle without hurrying to fill it. Somewhere nearby, a hammer struck once, then twice. The sound left a small ring in the air.

“Is that all of it?” she asked.

“That's all I know. Triton might sing it longer. There may be verses I haven't heard.”

“Do you want to know them?”

“Maybe.” He turned the question over. “I don't want to ask just so I can prove you heard it from him. Or because I want to find out if I remember it right.”

“You can still ask Triton sometime because you want to know more about the song.”

“Yeah. I can.”

She rested her hands in her lap. “Can you tell me the fish part?”

“Small fish leaves the reef. Goes out into the deep water. Learns its way around there, then comes back. But it comes back with a different name.”

“Why?”

“I don't know if the song says. There are a few possible translations. It might mean the fish has changed. It might mean other fish see it differently. Or it could just be a way to say that leaving and returning don't cancel each other.”

“Is that what Triton told you?”

“No. That's what I think when I hear it.”

Annabeth's fingers moved as if she was counting the phrases. “You heard it on a visit, and Triton told you it had been part of your childhood. Those are the facts. The rest is yours.”

“That's fair.”

“And I hummed it because it was in my head. I don't have to know where it started.”

“You don't.”

She gave him a direct look. “I want you to know I wasn't trying to hint that I had some secret memory. I don't have a hidden piece of your childhood I can hand back.”

“I know.” He had to correct himself. “I mean, I believe you. Yesterday I heard the song and got excited and scared at the same time. I acted like if I asked the right question, I could make that feeling go away.”

“It didn't.”

“No.”

“The question made me feel like there was a right answer you needed me to give.”

“I get that.”

She looked down at the grass, where tiny seed heads caught in the sunlight. “I can remember loving you. I can remember places and years we lived through together. That doesn't mean I know what happened in the childhood everyone here remembers. Sometimes I hear a tune, or someone tells me a story, and it lands in a place that feels familiar. I still can't tell you whether that is a remembered thing, a learned thing, or just a tune I like.”

“You don't have to name the feeling before you can keep the song.”

“I know. I need you to believe that when the song makes you think about home.”

“I will.”

She held his gaze a moment longer, looking for the reflex that would make him ask one more question. He felt it arrive and chose not to feed it. The choice cost him a little, which was useful. It meant he could recognize the impulse before it became her responsibility.

“Do you want to hear it again?” he asked.

“Yes. But this time, can you sing it without stopping to explain every word?”

“I can try.”

“Good. You can tell me about the lines afterward, if I ask.”

“Deal.”

He sang it once more. The notes went steadier, though not perfectly. Annabeth leaned back against the tree and closed her eyes—not as a test, but because the sun had slipped through a gap in the leaves. The tune moved between them without either one claiming to know why it had arrived there. When he finished, she opened her eyes again. For a moment, she said nothing.

"That's beautiful," she said. "And sad."

"It's supposed to be both."

"I think I like the part about returning."

"Me too."

She took his hand. "I remember us. I don't need the childhood everyone else remembers to be here with you."

"I know."

"But I might ask questions. I might get things wrong."

"I'll tell you what I remember. You can decide what to do with it."

Annabeth leaned over and kissed him, a small, deliberate kiss. "That's what I want."

Percy found Clarisse at the Cabin Three table after lunch, rubbing oil into the handle of her practice spear. The movement was careful and slow. She had been cleared to return to ordinary practice, but she still took more breaks than she wanted, and Silena had begun placing a cup of water within reach without asking first. Clarisse pretended not to notice. She drank it anyway.

Silena sat opposite her with a board across her knees, penciling the letters for Katie's winter-herb label. Three samples lay in a row: one narrow enough for the thyme, one broad for the mint, and a third she hadn't decided how to use. A small jar of green paint stood open beside her. The smell was sharp and earthy, and a ribbon of dried pigment marked the side of her thumb.

“Can you hold this end?” Silena asked.

Percy set down the folded note he had been turning over in his pocket and took the board. “Sure.”

“Not too tight. I don't want the fresh line to smear.”

“I can be delicate.”

Clarisse snorted. “You snapped the last brush.”

“It was a bad brush.”

“It was a twig.”

“Exactly.”

Silena drew the last upright stroke and lifted the brush. “There. You can let go.”

Percy eased his fingers away. The word *winter* sat beneath *herbs*, a little crooked but legible. Silena blew across the paint, then turned the board toward Clarisse.

“Think Katie will like this size?”

“Ask her.” Clarisse reached for the water and took another drink. “It's her sign.”

“I will. I'm trying to make a few shapes so she can see what fits the bed.”

“Then make the shapes. Don't make the whole plan for her.”

“I'm not.”

“I know. I'm reminding myself too.” Clarisse's fingers tightened around the cup, then relaxed. “I did that with the assessment. Decided what the station needed before I knew what the kids could actually do.”

Percy recognized the gap between wanting a good result and arranging every detail around it. “You changed the sheet.”

“After I saw what went wrong.”

Silena rinsed the brush in a tin cup. “And now you're letting Katie pick the label.”

“Maybe I'll learn from one example,” Clarisse said. “Don't expect a miracle.”

“I wouldn't.”

They returned to their work. Percy kept the finished board level while Silena set it near the window to dry. Clarisse tested the spear grip with her good hand, turned it once and put it down again. She wasn't supposed to train hard, and no one pretended this little movement counted as practice.

“I asked Silena if she'd heard that song Annabeth hummed,” Percy said.

Clarisse kept her gaze on the spear. “I know.”

“How?”

“You asked at breakfast. I was sitting there.”

“Right.” He had asked the question as if it had belonged only to him and Silena, forgetting how little privacy a breakfast table contained. “She hadn't.”

“She said she doesn't remember hearing it.”

“Yeah.”

“And you're going to stop asking people?”

“I am.”

Clarisse watched him for a beat. “Good.”

He pulled out the folded note. “I wrote down what I know. It was starting to feel like the facts kept moving around.”

Silena put down the brush. “What did you write?”

“That Triton sang it when I visited Atlantis, and he told me he used to sing it when I was little. I don't remember hearing it then. I don't know if he sang it to me or just around me.”

“That's a careful note.”

“It still feels like a question.”

“It can be both,” Silena said. “You know what Triton told you. You don't know what Annabeth heard before yesterday.”

“I know.”

Clarisse shifted on the bench, then stopped when her legs complained. “People tell stories about you all the time. Doesn't mean you remember them.”

“I know that too.”

“Knowing it and acting like it are different.”

“Yeah.”

Silena watched him fold the page again. “Do you want to keep it?”

“Yes.”

“Then keep it. You don't have to make it into something anyone else has to answer.”

He slipped the note back into his pocket. “That's what I told myself.”

“Sounds like a good start.”

Clarisse picked up the spear again, then reconsidered and rested it across her knees. “Does the song make you miss Atlantis?”

“A little. Mostly it made me think about the visit. I miss the people there whether or not I hear a song.”

“You don't have to explain that to us.”

“I wasn't.”

“Good.” Clarisse reached for the cup. “Because if you're going to go, you have to decide that for yourself. We already had that conversation.”

“I know. I haven't decided anything.”

“Then you haven't.”

The plainness of it helped. No schedule, no assumptions, no story about the tune making the choice for him. His letter to Amphitrite had been sent, and whether she'd received it or answered remained unknown. The song didn't change that. It didn't turn a possible visit into a promise or a childhood story into a set of instructions.

Silena picked up the narrow label. “Katie might want this one for the rosemary, if she wants one at all.”

“You could ask her,” Percy said.

“I will.”

Clarisse gave the board an appraising look. “The letters are crooked.”

“They're handmade.”

“They can be handmade and crooked.”

“You said to make the shapes, not the plan.”

“I didn't say the shapes should be bad.”

Silena's laugh escaped before she could contain it. “I'm going to let the paint dry before either of you offers art advice.”

She laid the label flat on the table and pushed the remaining samples together. The unfinished choice belonged to Katie. Silena had done her part and didn't have to keep painting until everyone was satisfied. Percy gathered the rinse cup and carried it to the basin, leaving the dry boards where she had put them.

At the doorway, he looked back. Clarisse had returned to oiling the spear, making slow circles over the grip. Silena was sketching a smaller version on scrap wood. They were talking about whether the letters could be read from the path, not about the song.

He was glad. The sound could remain in his pocket for a while without making the entire cabin turn toward him.

Annabeth found Thalia at the Zeus cabin porch again as the sun lowered behind the trees. The temporary gutter support was quiet now; no rain pressed against it, and the damp mark on the step had dried to a dark outline. Thalia had a sharpening stone in one hand and the practice spear laid across her knees. She was not sharpening the blade. She was smoothing a rough patch along the wooden grip.

“Is that part bothering you?” Annabeth asked.

“It catches my glove.”

“You could wrap it.”

“I could.”

“But you want to sand it.”

“I want it to stop catching.” Thalia tested the spot with her thumb. “Sanding's faster than finding a strip of leather and borrowing Beckendorf's awl.”

Annabeth sat across from her. “Can I see?”

Thalia passed her the spear, grip first. Annabeth ran a fingertip along the rough place. It was smaller than it looked, one raised sliver of wood where the finish had worn through. “If you take too much off, it could loosen the binding.”

“I know.”

“Then why not the wrap?”

“Because I want the grip smooth, not bigger.” Thalia took the spear back. “You don't have to solve it.”

“I was asking.”

“You were designing.”

“That's a kind of asking.”

“Not always.”

The blunt reply stung, though Annabeth could see the difference. She had wanted to be helpful and had moved immediately into the shape of the fix. “Okay. Do you want advice, or do you want to sand it?”

“I want you to hold the light while I sand it.”

“That's a task.”

“It's a very important task.”

Annabeth moved to the porch post and lifted the small lantern from its hook. She angled it over the spear while Thalia worked the stone across the grip. The scrape was dry and steady, interrupted when Thalia turned the wood to inspect the surface. Annabeth watched the dust gather in the lines of her sister's knuckles.

“You do that a lot,” Thalia said.

“Hold a lantern?”

“Take the job in front of you and start improving it before anyone tells you what they need.”

“I asked what you wanted.”

“After you gave me two options.”

Annabeth lowered the lantern slightly. “You could have said you wanted me to hold it.”

“I did.”

“You said that after I asked.”

“And now you're doing it.” Thalia's mouth twitched. “I get to be annoying about it once.”

“Once?”

“For today.”

Annabeth raised the lantern again. “Fine.”

Thalia kept sanding. When she finished, she rubbed the grip with the heel of her hand, then held the spear out for Annabeth to inspect. The rough patch had softened without changing its shape. Annabeth nodded.

“That's good.”

“You can say so without looking like you want to measure it.”

“I could measure it if you want.”

“I don't.”

“Then I won't.”

Thalia set the spear against the wall. “You came back.”

“I said I would.”

“People say things.”

“You thought I wouldn't?”

“I thought you'd find something more urgent. You usually do.”

Annabeth gave her a look. “I had a library errand. I finished it.”

“I'm not criticizing you.”

“I know.”

Thalia leaned her shoulders against the wall. “I don't know what to do when you're here and not here.”

“What do you mean?”

“You remember some things I don't know. You don't remember things I do. Sometimes you look at me like I'm familiar in a way that doesn't include the stuff I thought we shared.”

Annabeth set the lantern down carefully. “I can't give you the exact feeling you want.”

“I know.”

“But I can be here now.”

“That's what everyone says.”

“I know. It sounds too simple.”

“It sounds like a thing you can say without having to answer the harder part.”

Annabeth looked at the dry gutter seam. She could not supply a memory of the laundromat or of building streams in dirt. She could not tell Thalia what it had felt like to be a four-year-old sister in this life. But she could hear the complaint under the words: Thalia had been there, and Annabeth's absence from those memories left her holding the whole account alone.

“I don't want to pretend I remember,” Annabeth said. “I also don't want you to think I don't care because I can't remember.”

Thalia kept looking at the spear. “Those aren't the same thing.”

“No.”

“Sometimes I have to remind myself.”

“You can remind me too. Just don't make it a quiz.”

“I won't.” Thalia paused. “I might make it a story. You can tell me to stop if it's too much.”

“I might ask you to keep going.”

“You might.”

The answer stayed between them without being fixed into a plan. Annabeth looked toward the path. In the cabins below, voices rose and fell around dinner preparations. Someone called for a missing bowl. A door thumped shut. The camp was beginning its evening routine without waiting for them to finish a conversation.

“About the song,” Annabeth said. “Percy told me where he heard it. He didn't tell me that I had to explain why I knew it.”

“He asked you yesterday.”

“He did. I told him how it felt.”

“And he listened?”

“Today he sang it for me. He explained what he knows and what he doesn't.”

Thalia watched her face. “Does it make you feel like you're supposed to remember something?”

“Not exactly. It feels familiar, and then I start wondering whether I have to account for the feeling. I don't. It can be a tune I heard somewhere. It can be something I like. I don't need to make it prove anything.”

“Good.”

“You don't have to ask whether you heard it either.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“I know.”

“But you wanted to tell me.”

“Yes.”

Thalia gave a small nod. “Thanks for telling me.”

Annabeth wondered whether she wanted Thalia to sing the melody with her. She didn't yet. The song had been hers to keep without needing to share its route, and she was allowed to decide that the moment belonged to Percy and her. “Maybe another time,” she said, though Thalia hadn't asked.

“Okay.” Thalia picked up the spear and rested it across her lap again. “You don't have to include me in every part of your life to prove I'm your sister.”

Annabeth breathed out. “I don't want you outside it either.”

“Then tell me when you want me in.”

“I can try.”

“I'll try not to decide for you first.”

It was not an effortless promise. Thalia had already offered to let her choose, and Annabeth knew her sister would sometimes move faster than her own words. But she had caught herself here, with the spear and the lantern, and Annabeth had asked for a specific thing. For tonight, that was enough to hold.

Thalia stood and set the spear inside the cabin. “You staying for dinner?”

“I told Percy I'd meet him later.”

“Then go.”

“You're not upset?”

“I can want you here and still let you go see your boyfriend.”

“You could say it without that face.”

“This is just my face.”

“It is not.”

Thalia shrugged. “Then I can work on my face another day.”

Annabeth smiled. “I'll come by tomorrow to check the gutter plan.”

“You can. We'll take it to Chiron before we dig.”

“And after that, maybe we can do something that isn't fixing the cabin.”

“If you think of something.”

“I might.”

“Then tell me.”

Annabeth returned the lantern to its hook and walked down the porch steps. She didn't feel as if she'd solved what Thalia carried, or given her the childhood she remembered. She had chosen to come back after her errand, to hold the light, to tell her about the song and keep part of it private. The choices did not replace the years that one sister could recall and the other could not. They made one evening belong to both of them.

The following evening, they walked back to the cabins together. They passed the Zeus cabin first. Thalia was on the porch, sharpening her spear.

"Everything okay?" she asked.

"We had a disagreement," Annabeth said. "We talked about it."

Thalia nodded. "Good."

She looked at Percy. "You listened?"

"I did."

"Good." Thalia went back to her spear. "Keep doing that."

Annabeth squeezed Percy's hand and continued toward the Poseidon cabin with him. At the path split, she leaned in and kissed his cheek.

They slowed before the fork. The cabin paths ran apart between the trees, one toward the Poseidon cabin and the other curving back to Zeus. Someone had left a pair of muddy boots beside the trail, toes pointed in opposite directions. Percy shifted to avoid them. Annabeth was still holding his hand, though her attention had drifted to the last bright strip of sky between the branches.

“You liked the song,” he said.

“I did.”

“What part?”

She didn't answer right away. He felt the old urge rise—ask whether the melody had reminded her of something, whether Triton had sung it to her, whether she recognized a phrase. He let the questions pass without voicing them.

“The pause before the ending,” she said. “It feels like the fish is deciding whether to go back.”

“I thought it was a breath.”

“It can be both.”

“Or I might have held the note too long.”

“That too.”

He laughed. “So the best part might be a mistake.”

“It happens.”

“I was told the song is old.”

“You were told Triton used to sing it when you were little.”

“That's true.”

“It's different from knowing when it was written.”

“Also true.”

They began walking again, slowly enough that the last campers heading to dinner passed them. Percy had expected that singing the song would settle something. It hadn't. It had made the edges clearer, though: what he had heard recently, what Triton had said about an earlier time, and what neither of them could prove about Annabeth's first encounter with it.

“Do you think the fish has the same name when it comes back?” he asked.

“The song says it returns with a different name.”

“Maybe that's only because it went somewhere else.”

“Or because it changed.”

“Could be both.”

“You keep doing that.”

“What?”

“Leaving room for two answers.”

“I'm trying to stop making one answer carry everything.”

Annabeth's thumb brushed his knuckle. “That's a better habit.”

“I'm working on it.”

“I know.”

This time he could hear the difference in her voice. It wasn't an invitation to ask another question, and it wasn't a test. It was an acknowledgment of what he had changed since yesterday. He took it without asking her to explain what it meant.

“Would you sing it again sometime?” she asked.

“Yeah.”

“Not right now. I think I'd like to keep the version I have in my head for a bit.”

“Okay.”

“And if it changes when I hear it again, that's fine too.”

“You don't have to get it right.”

“I know.” She turned toward him. “I want to hear it because I like it. Not because I need to find out where it came from.”

“Then that's why I'll sing it.”

“Good.”

They reached the path fork. Their hands separated naturally as Annabeth turned toward Thalia, then she paused and leaned in to kiss his cheek.

“I think you rushed the last note,” she said.

“I held it too long under the tree.”

“You did.”

“You said it was the best part.”

“I said I liked the pause. The note came after it.”

“That's a very specific distinction.”

“You asked.”

Percy tried the ending under his breath, giving the pause more room and letting the note fall instead of stretching it. Annabeth tapped the beat against his sleeve with one finger. He tried again, a fraction slower.

“Better?” he asked.

“Different.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It's the one I've got.” She smiled. “I like this one.”

“The slower one?”

“The one we just made.”

He glanced toward the Zeus cabin. Thalia had gone inside to put away the spear. No one else was close enough to hear them, but Annabeth did not look around to check. She had chosen to sing with him here, on the path, without offering an account of where the notes began.

“So if I sing it this way next time, that's ours?” he asked.

“It's this version.”

“That's what I meant.”

“Then yes.”

He repeated the ending once, quietly. Annabeth joined on the last two notes, not matching him exactly. The sounds met, separated, and settled. There was no need to decide which voice carried the tune or who had brought it to the path.

"See you tomorrow."

"Tomorrow."

She walked back to Thalia, who shifted over to make room on the porch. Percy watched them sit together, shoulders touching, then headed home to his own cabin.

The cabin was quieter than the path. Clarisse and Silena were out; a cup sat upside down on the drying cloth beside the basin, and the open window let in the smell of damp soil from the garden. Percy took the folded note from his pocket and set it on his bunk. The page had softened along the crease. His single sentence was still there, written in the same careful hand.

He could add the translation. He could write that the song seemed to be about a fish that left the reef, learned the deep water and returned under a different name. He could write that Annabeth liked the pause before the last phrase. Those were things he had heard and things she had told him. But the paper wasn't a ledger, and he didn't have to preserve every moment before he was allowed to keep it.

He folded the page along the same line and put it back in the pocket of his jacket. Maybe he would ask Triton more about the song the next time they were together. Maybe he would forget to ask. Either choice could wait until there was a reason that belonged to him, not to an answer he wanted Annabeth to produce.

Outside, the evening breeze pressed the cabin door inward and let it fall back against the frame. Percy sat on the edge of the bunk and listened. The song did not come back to him whole. Only a few notes stayed, enough to remember the sound, not enough to make it a test.

He did not take the paper back out. Tomorrow, the words might still be there, but they would not tell him what Annabeth's first memory of the tune had been. That answer was not his to demand. He could ask Triton more about what Triton knew, when the time came. He could tell Annabeth what he learned, if she wanted to hear it. Neither would make her responsible for matching the song to a past she could not access.

For now, the melody had a pause he had sung too long, a quieter ending they had adjusted together, and a meaning neither had to agree on. Percy leaned back and let the cabin go still around him. He didn't need to decide whether it belonged to Atlantis, to Annabeth, to Triton's story, or to the minutes they had spent under the oak. It had room to be more than one thing.

The song stayed with him. It no longer felt like something he'd lost or something Annabeth had to explain finding. It was a song they shared now, with room for both of them inside it.

He could carry the tune without carrying an answer. By morning, it might sound different in his head. That would not make the version they'd sung together less real.

He left the note folded in his pocket instead of adding a title to it. Some things could stay unfinished until someone had a reason to return to them.

That was enough for tonight, and he let the quiet remain quiet.

He would sleep without turning it into a question for anyone else.
