## Chapter 146: The Long Way Back

The patrol left at noon, and the noon was golden the whole way down the lake.

They went in pairs, mostly because patrols went in pairs and partly because Clarisse had assigned the pairs herself, standing at the boathouse door with the assignment board in her hand and a sunburn across the bridge of her nose and a look on her face that said she had already decided who was going to say something stupid and had pre-annoyed herself about it.

"Jackson," Clarisse said, "and Chase, you take the east shore. Beauregard, with me, west. La Rue, solo, perimeter." She paused, glanced at the wall behind her, corrected nobody, and said, "Thalia, you're with Percy and Annabeth, because Zeus cabins wander, and if I let the Zeus cabin loose without a leash it'll end up in Canada by accident again."

"That was one time," Thalia said.

"It was twice."

"It was once and a half."

Percy took the second skiff with Annabeth. Thalia took the bow seat of the first one, which put her the perfect distance to see everything either of them did and pretend she wasn't looking, and Clarisse took the west shore with Silena, and the whole patrol pushed off into the water at nearly the same time, four oars and eight hands and the sun coming down on the whole thing so evenly bright that the lake looked like it had been polished.

---

The west shore was the east shore's older, grumpier sibling.

Where the east had its swimming buoys and its gentle slope and its list of things that were almost always fine, the west had a retaining wall with a crack in it, a set of submerged stairs that ended six inches below the surface, and a stretch of rocky shallows where the lake had been slowly eating the bank since before any of them were born. Clarisse's list for the west shore was shorter than Annabeth's had been for the east, but each item had more opinions.

"Retaining wall," she said, reading from the clipboard she'd taken from the boathouse. "Crack check. Submerged stairs. Rock line. Drain pipe. And the old oak root that keeps tripping people."

"That's a very aggressive list," Silena said.

"It's a very aggressive shore."

They beached the skiff on the strip of gravel that passed for a west shore landing, and Clarisse climbed out first, boots finding the rocks with the ease of someone who had been walking on uneven ground since before she could walk. Silena followed more carefully, her sandals gripping the wet stone, her free hand finding Clarisse's shoulder without either of them commenting on it.

The retaining wall ran along the top of the bank, a low stone thing that had been built by somebody's grandfather and maintained by the camp's general fund of "we'll get to it eventually." The crack was new — or at least, it was new since the last patrol had checked it, which at camp counted as new. Clarisse crouched and ran her thumb along the gap, feeling the edges, and Silena crouched beside her with the clipboard and the pencil and the kind of patience that made her good at this and bad at admitting it.

"How bad?" Silena asked.

"Bad enough to write down. Bad enough to not be the only thing writing it down." Clarisse sat back on her heels and looked at the wall like it had personally offended her. "We need to tell Chiron. This isn't a 'get to it eventually' crack. This is a 'get to it before the next hard rain' crack."

"Noted," Silena said, and wrote it down, and underlined it twice, because some things deserved underlining.

The submerged stairs were next. They went down into the lake at the angle of a deck that had sunk, four steps visible and a fifth that was a suggestion, and the algae on them was the kind of green that meant you could see it from the surface but not once you were in it. Clarisse stood at the top of the stairs and looked down into the water and said, "I'm going in."

"You're not going in," Silena said.

"I'm going to put my foot on the fourth step and see if it holds."

"You're going in," Silena said, "and I'm writing it down."

Clarisse went in. The water took her to the knee, cold and silty, and she put her foot on the fourth step and leaned her weight on it and the step held, and she said, "Holds," and Silena wrote it down, and then Clarisse's foot went through the algae and found the gap where the step had crumbled at the back, and she went in to the thigh, and the lake closed over her boot with a sound like a mouth closing.

Silena did not laugh. She wrote it down.

Clarisse came out of the water with her jeans dark to the knee and her expression the kind of neutral that meant she was deciding whether to be annoyed or impressed with the lake. "The step's gone at the back," she said.

"I noticed."

"Somebody could miss it in the dark."

"Somebody could miss it in the light."

"That's why we write it down."

"That's why we write it down," Silena agreed.

They did the rock line next, which was a matter of walking the shallows and noting which rocks had moved since the last patrol, which was a matter of Clarisse's memory and Silena's pencil and the two of them building a map of a shore that kept rearranging itself when nobody was looking. The drain pipe was next, and it was fine, and the old oak root was where it had always been and was still tripping people, and by the time they were done the sun had moved a meaningful distance across the sky and Clarisse's jeans were dry to the thigh and damp again from the knee down and Silena's clipboard had a page full of notes that looked like the handwriting of someone who took her job seriously and her sisters more seriously.

"The east shore's got nothing on this," Clarisse said, looking at the page.

"The east shore's got a swimming area and a canoe launch," Silena said. "The west shore's got a retaining wall that's trying to leave."

"Fair."

They pulled the skiff back into the water and pushed off, and Clarisse rowed with the kind of efficiency that made the boat move like it had been told to, and Silena sat in the bow with the clipboard on her knees and her hair coming loose in the wind off the water, and the west shore retreated behind them with its cracks and its steps and its opinionated rocks, and neither of them said anything for a while because there was nothing to say that the lake wasn't already saying.

Then Silena said, "You went in."

"I put my foot on a step."

"You went in."

"The step was gone."

"The step was always going to be gone. That's why we check."

Clarisse rowed. The oars dipped and lifted, dipped and lifted, and the lake took the sound and kept it.

"You went in because you wanted to," Silena said.

Clarisse did not answer for three strokes. Then she said, "I went in because if I didn't, somebody else would have to, and it was my patrol."

"It was our patrol."

"It was my list."

"It was our shore."

Clarisse looked at her. Silena looked back. The kind of look that passed between sisters who had been given the same father and different childhoods and had decided, somewhere along the way, that the difference was a thing they could hold instead of a thing that held them.

"You went in," Clarisse said, "because you're reckless and you like the lake and you wanted to know."

"I went in," Silena said, "because you were going to and I wasn't going to let you do it alone."

Clarisse rowed. The lake moved. The shore went by.

"That's worse," Clarisse said.

"I know."

"That's so much worse."

"I know."

Clarisse put the oars in the water and rowed harder, and the skiff went faster, and the wind came off the water and pushed Silena's hair into her face and she left it there because it was the kind of day where you let the wind do what it wanted.

The east shore was a list. The list was in Annabeth's sleeve, copied in her small square hand: the swimming area buoys, the canoe launch, the drain grate below the steep bank, the age-old fallen birch, the willow cove, and the rock with the white X that marked the boundary — the last legal thing before you were, technically, outside camp.

They did the list in order, and Annabeth did the checking, and Percy did the rowing and the pointing, and Thalia did the making of comments.

"You're rowing with your wrists again," Thalia said, from the bow of the other skiff, four lengths off.

"He rows fine," Annabeth said.

"He rows with his wrists," Thalia said. "It's an insult to show me a lake with all his strength and none of his wrists."

"Chase, does she have to be along?" Percy asked.

"She doesn't," Annabeth said, "and she knows it," and Thalia said nothing more, but she rowed on nicely and nobody died, and that was what the military calls a functioning patrol.

"Drain grate," Percy said, at the fourth stop, and the drain grate was fine.

"Canoe launch," Annabeth said, at the fifth, and the canoe launch was fine.

The fallen birch was fine. The rocks at the steep bank were fine. The willow cove came up at the end — a green half-mile of shade where the trees dipped over the water and the lake went suddenly from sun-warm to cold-shadow cold — and Annabeth checked the mooring stakes and the floating fence section and the new crack in the ramp, and wrote everything down, and Percy watched the little pencil move and the light flicker on her wrist as she wrote, and Thalia said, from the bow of the other skiff, "You're not checking anything."

"I'm resting my eyes," Percy said.

"You're staring at her wrist," Thalia said.

"I'm resting my eyes on her wrist."

"Disgusting," Thalia said, with the exact warmth she used for anything true. "Do it anyway. Just check the next buoy first."

He did.

The white X came at the very end, on the small grey rock that sat out in the water like a punctuation mark, marking the edge of everything legal. Annabeth checked it, said the paint needed doing, wrote it on the list, and then, instead of turning back for the boathouse like you do when a patrol ends, she asked Percy, very casually, if he'd ever been around the outside of the boundary rock at this time of day, and looked at him, and Percy turned the skiff, and they passed the rock at eleven miles an hour while Thalia, in the other boat, said "Called it" so quietly that only Annabeth heard it and only Annabeth was supposed to.

The willow cove was not the boundary. The willow cove had never been the boundary. But the water the other side of the white X was the outside of camp, and the way Annabeth looked at Percy when he passed the rock suggested that maybe the cove's shade had been the whole rule about going too far in the first place, and that some rules were just a line in the water drawn by whoever got to the shore first, and if you were patient and quiet and quick, you got to redraw them in your own hand.

The cove itself was the ugliest bargain at camp. Willow crowns too heavy for their trunks. Half-sunk skiff argyle-freckled with rot. Mud with an opinion. Every kind of bird. And at the far end, just where the water got too shallow for the skiffs and the reeds closed in, a pebble wreck with a flat top which, when the water was low, was the best flat screening rock on the whole lake, and the reason that Percy would, this evening, have to explain to Clarisse how the two of them had made it back to camp wet up to the knees in the middle of a scheduled patrol without swimming.

"Anchor off," Annabeth said, and Percy paid out the anchor line, and the skiff swung its nose gently into the willow shade and went still, and the water sat around them as a room, quiet in the way that lake rooms get, sun-warmed at the top, cool at the bottom, green light in pieces on the surface, willow smell at the edges.

And at the bow, where there was nobody but him and her and the willows, Thalia stopped rowing.

She let the oars sit. She looked at the skiff. She looked at the shade and the water. She said, "This is good," with a nod, and tipped her head back against the gunwale, and closed her eyes for a second and did not go to sleep, and Annabeth took her hand off her list because there was nothing else to check, and the cove took them all three, each in their chosen privacy, for nearly a minute, breathing.

Then Thalia opened one eye. "This is the good detour?"

"This is the good detour," Annabeth said.

"Clarisse doesn't know you come here."

"Clarisse knows everything," Annabeth said, "and comes nowhere."

Thalia laughed — the real one, which even she didn't hand out freely — and tipped her head back again, and the water was so quiet you could hear the willow leaves moving against each other above them, a dry small sound like a held breath, and Percy sat with his hand on the anchor line and let the quiet do the talking.

"Okay," Thalia said. "So the rule is nobody's in the woods alone."

"That's the rule," Percy said.

"So technically you two need a supervision." Thalia crossed her arms, not to be difficult, but because her elbows had jobs. "I'm supervising. Verbally. From a legal distance."

"You're going to nap in the sun," Annabeth said.

"I'm going to supervise the concept of napping," Thalia said. "For the record. Because nobody can say the Zeus cabin wanders."

The water rocked the skiff gently against the willow shade, so gently that Percy could feel it in his hand on the line, and Annabeth shifted her weight in the boat without disturbing it, and the whole pocket of water arranged itself around them, and it was the good detour, and the sun found gaps in the willow crown to put down stripes of it on the surface between them, and the day, which had been going to be a patrol, became instead the best kind of unaccounted time, which is the kind nobody can bill you for.

---

Annabeth had a rule about lakes and boys, and it was not on any list.

The rule was: the lake is the one place a person can be still without being accused of planning something, and a still girl is the most dangerous thing at camp if the stillness is deliberate, and the willow cove turned stillness into an art form, and if you were going to be deliberate about anything today, this was the water to do it in.

She had brought the notebook. She always brought the notebook. The notebook was cover for everything, because if you were sketching, you could get away with watching someone and nobody could prosecute the watching.

The water was calm on the outside of the skiff and full of broken sun on the inside, and Percy sat at the oars with his hands loose on the handles and the bow pointed nose-in at the reeds, and beyond him the willows did what willows do, which is lean and dip and make shade and drop leaves onto everything you own, and because he was Percy, one of the leaves had come down and landed in his hair, and because she was Annabeth, she was not going to tell him, because you got to be efficient like that only once or twice a summer and today was the day.

"You keep looking at me," he said, without turning around.

"I'm sketching your hair."

"From memory?"

"From a leaf," she said, and he reached up to feel for it, and she laughed before he touched it, and he turned, and the laugh gave her away, and he laughed too — not the show-out-loud laugh he spent on Thalia or the steady one he spent on Clarisse, but the other one, the one that had a little bit of embarrassment underneath it, as if being caught looking for a leaf were the same order of embarrassment as being caught looking at her.

"You had one chance to be cool about that," he said.

"You had one chance to check your hair."

"Cool is overrated."

"Cool is very overrated," Annabeth agreed. "That's why I like you instead."

He didn't answer that, but his hand did — found the gunwale between them, felt for her hand, found it, and held on with the thumb across her knuckles, and Annabeth reminded herself, for the thousandth time, that her body knew him even though her childhood didn't; that she had to relearn a lot of things in this life and he was not one of them; that whatever girl she had been at seventeen, in New Rome, designing cities and eating breakfast off the scale of an empire, she had held his hand across a table and across a war and the muscle memory had survived the timeline surgery, somehow, the way some things do, and the surviving language was, apparently, hands.

"You're doing the quiet thing," Percy said.

"You're counting my quiet."

"You do it when you're thinking of something you won't say."

"I always think of something I won't say."

"Not this quietly."

She turned the page of the notebook, so that the sketch was facing him and the blank page was facing her, and wrote, in capitals, with the pencil in her left hand because her right was busy, I THINK OF YOU.

He read it without moving his head much and said nothing, which was the correct answer, because there wasn't one.

The willow light came through the leaves in little coins and moved on the water, and the water moved the coins again, and the coins touched his cheek and her knee and the page of the notebook and the side of the skiff, and Annabeth thought, in the language of sketches, that she could draw this light for the rest of her life and never capture the actual day of it, and that it was better to be in the actual day than to capture it, and she put down the pencil.

"Hey," she said.

He looked at her.

"The cove, not the notebook," she said. "For a minute."

"For a minute."

She moved over the bench toward him. Not fast. There was no reason to hurry; the willow light wasn't going anywhere. She slid the last few inches when the skiff rocked, and then her shoulder was against his shoulder and her knees against the underside of the thwart and her hand still in his, and the lake was the biggest quiet thing either one of them knew.

He said, low, "Thalia's asleep."

"She's supervising the concept of napping."

"Concept's going well."

She felt the laugh go through him before he made the sound — the laugh you feel rather than hear, the one that lives in the ribs — and she tipped her head, very slightly, until her cheekbone rested against the top of his shoulder, and his arm came up around her back, and the cove sat under its willow ceiling with the sun leaking through in coins, and they were fourteen in every legal sense and older in every bone, and the water rocked them a degree every few seconds like an old chair.

"Annabeth," he said.

"Mm."

"That first summer," he said, "before everything, before the timeline — I used to sit on the breakwater and watch the lake and think, this is the biggest water I'll ever know."

She said, "The sea."

"The sea," he agreed. "Atlantis' sea was bigger, but I didn't know it. The lake was the first big water I loved." A pause. The skiff rocked gently against the shade, and Annabeth felt the shape of the rest of the sentence before it arrived. "And now the lake is the last big water I don't remember being a sea."

She turned her head, so her cheek was on his shirt instead of her forehead, so she could see his face.

"Is that sad?" she asked.

"It's the whole thing," he said. "It's the happiest sad thing I own."

Annabeth sat up, because the sentence deserved her full attention and NOT her sleepy weight, and took his chin in her hand, and turned him so he was looking at her over the top of his own shoulder, and she kissed him.

It was not a hard kiss. It wasn't the dawn-dock kind, which broke open under first light and approaching footsteps. It was the willow-shade kind: slow, sun-freckled, careful, unhurried, the kind where you have all day and you know it and nobody knows what time it is, the kind where you take your time finding the angle because the angle is the point. He made a small sound and kissed her back with his hand at her jaw and the other still around her back and the lake air coming in cool off the surface past both their faces.

Her hair fell forward. His thumb found her ear, felt for the small earring she wasn't wearing in this lifetime, and followed the absence of it down to her throat like it was a line he'd drawn once and could still trace. Somewhere in the willows above them a bird moved, and the shadow broke for a second, and went still.

They stayed with it. Long enough for the lake to rock the skiff twice. Long enough for the coins of light on the water to move. Long enough for her to feel the place where his heartbeat lived under her hand on his shirt and to think, with a clarity that almost embarrassed her, that she had known this boy in a war and a city and an airplane and a kitchen, and the lake might be the last place she'd get to know him as something new.

She pulled back — because Thalia's breathing was real and even only ninety percent of the time and the other ten percent was Thalia faking, and because she had promised herself one minute of the cove and not a whole missing afternoon — and pressed her forehead against the corner of his jaw, and breathed.

"Okay," she said. "Back to the notebook."

"Back to the notebook," he agreed, and his voice was wrecked in the good way, the way it got after a real laugh, and Annabeth picked up her pencil, and looked at the page, and drew three lines that were nothing, and wrote, underneath the three lines, in the capital letters she used for anything true, AND AGAIN, LATER.

He read it.

He grinned.

---

The cove had a swimming beach that was technically part of the willow shade, because the willows had annexed it over three centuries of leaning, and because the willows were not impressed by jurisdiction.

It was warm. The sun had come all the way around to the mouth of the cove, so the shallows out there held the heat the way a stone holds a hand, and the lake was glass out to the boundary rock, and Percy stood in the skiff and took his shirt off at exactly the speed of a boy who has decided to be casual about it and is not.

"Bold," Annabeth said.

"Strategic," Percy said. "The patrol deserves a swim check."

"You're making it up."

"I'm making it up," he agreed, and went over the side like a seal, pinwheel of white limbs, splash the size of a small appliance, and came up two feet from the boat shaking his head like a dog, and the cove rang once with the sound and settled again.

Annabeth sat on the gunwale with her feet out of her sandals and her jeans rolled to the knee, and he was treading water there, in the coins of light, with his hair flattened and the lake beading on his shoulders, and he put one hand on the edge of the skiff and one on the side of her knee and said nothing at all for a second, which from Percy was a speech.

"Not yet," she said.

"I wasn't—"

"You were," she said, "and the answer is not yet and also later, so get out of the way, because I'm going in the water and you're going to either hold the boat or come in behind me, and I know which one you'll pick."

She went in off the other side, clean, no show, an entry you could have taught from a diagram, and the cold came up her like a held note, and she surfaced with her hair slicked back and her breath going and her eyes the exact color of the deep water under the willows, and Percy had already come around the bow and was there, treading water in front of her, in the strip of sun between the shade and the deep.

"Swim check," he said.

"Concluded," she said.

They had a rule by now about water and talking, which was, mostly, that they didn't: water had been the oldest thing between them, older than the timelines, older than the kisses, and in it they did not need to be quick or clever. They hung in the warm layer, side by side, her shoulder touching his, and the lake rocked them, and a fish came up under a leaf eleven feet off and put a ring on the whole surface that reached them a second later like a soft word.

"Tell me something true," Annabeth said, because in the water they allowed themselves one.

"Under the dock is still the best morning in camp," he said. "Even before today."

"That's not true, that's favoritism."

"It's both," Percy said.

She laughed, which in the water was a different laugh — bigger and rounder — and swam her hand along the gunwale without meaning to, and Percy watched her laugh the way he watched weather come.

They stayed until the sun came through the willow crown at the wrong angle, which meant twenty minutes had gone somewhere, and then Annabeth climbed aboard first and sat dripping on the bench with her hair flat and her jeans dark to the knee and goosebumps along her arms that Percy noticed and did not comment on because he had a documented record of commenting, and Percy went in once more, alone, off the transom, a long dived glide under the shade, coming up in the very darkest part of the cove where the lake was ten degrees cooler and the world was green as bottle glass, and he stayed down there as long as his lungs allowed, in the quiet, with the willow roots like dark chords around him, and thought, nothing.

Thought nothing at all.

When he came up, Annabeth had the towel already thrown over the seat to dry it and had wedged herself against the far gunwale, and a leaf was in her hair, which was her own fault, and he got aboard and sat next to her and did not take it out, because turnabout.

"You're the worst," she said.

"I'm the driest," said Percy, and wrung out his hair over the side, and the sun took both of them at its own pace after that, and the cove waited with them without being asked.

---

Thalia had been quiet for a while.

Not the performing quiet she put on when she was deciding whether to say something, and not the sleeping quiet she'd been doing in the bow. A different quiet. The kind that had weight to it, the kind that meant she was carrying something and had not yet decided whether to set it down.

Annabeth noticed first, because Annabeth always noticed first. She shifted on the bench, turning so she could see Thalia past Percy's shoulder, and said, "You're doing the thing."

"What thing."

"The thing where you go somewhere I can't follow."

Thalia opened one eye. "I'm in a boat."

"You're in a boat and you're somewhere else. It's a talent."

Thalia closed the eye again. "I'm supervising."

"You're brooding."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive."

Percy, who had been toweling his hair and pretending not to listen, said, "She's been like that since the boundary rock."

"Since before the boundary rock," Annabeth said.

"Since the patrol started," Thalia said. "Since before the patrol started. Since breakfast. Since —" She stopped. Opened both eyes. Looked at the willow crown above them, the way the leaves moved against each other in the small wind off the water. "Since I started thinking about something and haven't stopped."

"Want to share?" Annabeth asked.

"Not particularly."

"Good. I'll guess." Annabeth settled back against the gunwale, her notebook closed on her knee, her pencil tucked behind her ear. "You're thinking about the fact that we're all here. In this cove. On a patrol that was supposed to be work. And it's not work. And you don't know what to do with that."

Thalia was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "That's annoyingly accurate."

"I'm annoyingly accurate about most things."

"Most things," Thalia agreed. "Not everything."

"Name one thing I'm not accurate about."

"The time you said you didn't like the lake."

"I never said that."

"You said it the first week. You said it was 'fine.' You said it was 'a body of water, functionally.' You said —"

"I was twelve and pretending not to be impressed."

"You were twelve and pretending not to be impressed," Thalia repeated. "And now you're fourteen and you still pretend not to be impressed, but you keep a notebook full of drawings of it and you wake up early to sit on the dock and you have a rule about lakes and boys that you think nobody knows about."

Annabeth's mouth opened. Closed. Opened again. "That rule is private."

"That rule is the reason we're in a cove right now instead of back at the boathouse."

"We're in a cove because it's on the list."

"The cove is not on the list."

"It's adjacent to the list."

"It's adjacent to the list," Thalia said, "because you put it there."

Annabeth looked at Percy. Percy looked at the willow crown. "I'm not getting involved," he said.

"You're already involved," Thalia said. "You're the reason for the rule."

"I'm the reason for the rule?"

"You're the reason for the rule."

Annabeth's face did something complicated. Then she said, very quietly, "The rule is that the lake is the one place I can be still without being accused of planning something. And I'm still. And nobody's accusing me of anything. And that's —" She stopped. "That's new."

Thalia sat up. The skiff rocked with the shift, and the water slapped gently against the hull, and the willow leaves moved in their small wind, and Thalia looked at her sister with an expression that was too big for the space between them.

"Yeah," she said. "That's new."

Percy, who had been very carefully not listening, said, "I'm going to dunk my head again."

"You just dunked your head," Annabeth said.

"I'm going to dunk it again," Percy said, and went over the side, and the cove took him in, and the two girls sat in the skiff with the sun coming through the willows in coins and the kind of quiet that meant something had been said that couldn't be unsaid.

When he came up, Annabeth was looking at the water. Thalia was looking at Annabeth. And neither of them said anything, because some things didn't need saying, and the lake was keeping them anyway.

---

They were late.

Not dangerously late, not miss-the-mess-hall late, but the bell had rung three minutes ago for the end of patrol, and Clarisse had a way of being at the boathouse when the bell rang and a way of being at the boathouse when the last skiff came in, and the second way always included commentary.

They rowed back in no particular hurry. Thalia had woken up at the sound of Percy's voice — she always did, he could never figure out how she timed it — and had said, with great ceremony, that she had been awake the whole ten minutes and had seen nothing, heard nothing, and would report nothing, and that this was called professional restraint, and that the Zeus cabin would want it noted for the record.

"Noted," Annabeth said, writing in the margin of the list, for the record: stays awake longer than the person napping, still sees nothing.

The boathouse came out of the trees. Clarisse was on the ramp with her hand on the assignment board and the sunburn across her nose, and Silena was on the dock beside her with her hair down and her wet feet bare, sitting with her legs dangling into the water like a girl in a painting, and when the two skiffs came in, Clarisse looked at the pair of them, and at the willow light still in Annabeth's hair, and at Percy's untucked shirt, and made a sound like somebody summing up a case.

"The east shore," she said. "Tell me about the east shore."

"Done," Percy said. "Buoys, launch, grate, birch, rocks, ramp, boundary rock. Annabeth has the list."

"And how is the boundary rock."

"Needs paint," Annabeth said.

"Did you paint it."

"No."

"Where did the patrol go after the boundary rock, Chase."

Annabeth, with the beautiful honesty of someone who is going to be accused either way, folded her list and held it against her chest. "We went a hundred yards past it," she said. "To the willow cove."

Thalia, climbing out of the first skiff with pure innocence: "I supervised."

"You supervised," Clarisse repeated, in the voice reserved for the worst possible uses of the word.

"The cove," Thalia said, "the small one, willows, nice shade, I sat in the bow and did not drown. It was the most useful I have been in two weeks."

"Nobody," Clarisse said, "asked you to go past the rock."

"Nobody asked you to yell," Silena said, from the dock, without opening her eyes, because the water was warm on her feet and she was going to sit there until the sun made her leave.

"I recruited myself," Thalia said. "The Zeus cabin cannot be left unsupervised to wander."

"You wandered into a cove."

"The Zeus cabin," Thalia said, "did not so much as put a toe out of the skiff, and would like her patrol credit."

Clarisse looked at Annabeth. Annabeth looked at the willow leaf that had followed them all the way back and was still, incredibly, in Percy's hair, and reached over and removed it, and held it up, evidence, and gave it to Clarisse.

Clarisse took the leaf. Looked at it. Looked at the lake. Put the leaf in her pocket, which was how she booked evidence, apparently, and said, "East shore patrol extends ten minutes per occurrence."

"That's a rule," Annabeth said.

"It's a rule I'm making now," Clarisse said. "It's retroactive."

Percy caught Annabeth's eye over Clarisse's shoulder, and they had one of those exchanges that happen in a fraction of a second and take a whole paragraph: they were both thinking of the cove, and the coins of light, and the promise in capitals on the notebook page, and both of them were thinking that ten minutes per occurrence was a rate they could afford, and neither of them said any of it, because the other one already had it.

"Fine," Clarisse said, to the pair of them, to the lake, to the willow thirty yards off, "ten minutes to change and eat. Jackson, you smell like pond. Chase, you have leaves in your collar."

"Proof of cover story," said Silena, eyes still closed, feet in the water, and Clarisse turned her dry look on her, and Silena's mouth did a slow-traveling smile, and the patrol broke up into the last hours of the day.

It nearly did. Silena returned from the equipment shed with the west-shore checklist folded beneath her arm and stopped Clarisse at the ramp.

"We still have to give Chiron the report."

"I know."

"You said you would take it."

"I was going to."

"Before dinner?"

Clarisse looked toward the pavilion. The dinner bell had begun to ring, and Percy could see her weighing the food against the chance to make the broken stairs someone else's problem tomorrow.

"Before dinner," she said.

Percy went with them because his east-shore page needed a signature. Annabeth followed with the map. Chiron was at the Big House sorting cabin duty assignments, and he looked up when the four campers came in at once.

"Something happened," he said.

"The retaining wall," Clarisse told him. She unfolded the checklist and flattened it with her palm. "There's a crack wider than on the previous patrol. The fourth submerged step also has a hole behind the tread. I tested it and went through."

"Are you injured?"

"No. Wet."

"That's not an answer to the question," Chiron said.

Clarisse looked at him. "No injury. My boot got caught for a second. Silena saw it."

Silena showed him the mark on the diagram. "It looks solid from above. Someone could step in the gap before they realized the stone had shifted."

Chiron got up. He took the map and compared it to the older survey kept in the side drawer. The marks didn't line up. The shoreline had moved farther than he'd expected.

"We close the western steps tonight," he said. "No swimming from them and no patrol testing them by standing on the damage."

Clarisse glanced down. "Understood."

"I'll assign the repair crew to examine the wall tomorrow. Until then, the trail above it is restricted. Who can put up the markers before dark?"

"I can," Percy said.

"You have only just returned from patrol," Chiron replied. "Beckendorf is already on the evening equipment shift. I'll ask him. You can bring the supplies to him."

Percy nodded. He disliked that the job had been divided without asking him to do the dramatic part. He liked even less the memory of the hole Clarisse's boot had found.

Annabeth drew a short line across the affected section on her map and wrote the date beside it. "I'll update the shore copy when it's inspected. We don't know the full extent yet."

"Correct," Chiron said. "Report what you saw, and distinguish it from what you suspect."

Silena took back the checklist. Clarisse was the last to leave, and Percy heard her tell Chiron, quieter than before, "Next patrol, I'm not using my foot to check the stairs."

"An excellent improvement to the procedure," Chiron replied.

They went to dinner knowing the broken steps were marked for closure and the wall would be checked, not merely that they'd found something to worry about.

---

Dinner was laid out in the pavilion with the sun going down behind the trees, and the light hit the tables sideways, and the hum of the camp was the evening version, slower and lower, everyone fed and no one in a hurry.

Thalia had claimed the end of the Zeus bench, which was hers by seniority, by force of personality, and by the fact that nobody else wanted the bench closest to the aisle where the Ares table's broad cousins kept accidentally-on-purpose bumping it. Annabeth sat down beside her with a plate constructed entirely of smoked fish and bread, because in this life her favorite things apparently still ran salt-forward, and Percy dropped in across from Annabeth with water and a plate the size of a hubcap, and the Poseidon table, fifteen feet away, was already calling Thalia over by sign language, which was Clarisse pointing at the empty seat beside her once, hard.

"Go on," Annabeth said.

"I'm eating," Thalia said.

"You always eat."

"I'm sitting down."

"You sat down already," Annabeth said. "You ate half of it. You ate with your elbows on the table like a sea captain. Go sit with them before Clarisse comes over here and decides she can guess why you aren't."

Thalia stood up. Had one more thing. Stood up again. Said, over the bench: "She's sitting with her sister."

Clarisse made room without looking, which was the biggest thing she did, and Silena moved the water pitcher three inches, which was the second biggest, and Thalia sat down at the Poseidon table with the three of them on a Thursday evening, and did not explain herself, and was not asked to.

"So," Clarisse said, cutting into the fish. "The Zeus cabin bought us all dessert?"

"The Zeus cabin," Thalia said, "supervised your patrol."

"My patrol was fine."

"Your patrol went to a cove to make out."

"Our patrol went to a cove," Clarisse said, "for shade, and the Zeus cabin made it eighty percent less private and one hundred percent less efficient, and I want it noted that the ten-minute rule is now double for Zeus-cabin accompaniment."

Silena bit into the piece of bread she had been holding against her shoulder like a co-conspirator. "It's cute," she said, "that you two think the cove is the good detour."

Thalia looked at her. Clarisse looked at her. Percy, fifteen feet away, serving himself potatoes, looked at her too late.

"The good detour," Silena said, "is that you four are all sitting at the same table and nobody had to be talked into it." She spread butter on the argument like it needed it. "The cove's fine. The cove's a good detour. The cove's, like, the third-best detour."

Thalia chewed. Considered. Said, "What's the second-best detour?"

"Me," Silena said.

Percy raised his glass without looking up from the potatoes, in saluting, and Clarisse pointed her knife at Silena with a look that meant do not make me laugh at dinner, and Thalia found she was smiling into her fish without permission, and nothing was resolved and nothing needed to be, and the sun finished going down behind the trees and the lamps came on around the pavilion.

After dinner, the four of them ended up on the dock, because it was what they did when there was nowhere to be and enough evening left for it.

---

The walk down from the pavilion was the kind of walk that happened when nobody wanted to be inside and the evening was too good to waste on doors.

Clarisse led, because Clarisse always led, and Silena walked beside her with her shoes in one hand and her hair down and the last of the sunset still caught in it. Thalia and Annabeth walked together behind them, shoulder to shoulder, the way they'd been walking since Annabeth had arrived at camp and Thalia had decided that walking beside her sister was a job she'd been born to do. Percy brought up the rear, because Percy always brought up the rear, because he liked watching all of them at once and because the rear was where you saw things.

"The west shore's got a crack," Clarisse said, over her shoulder, to nobody in particular.

"The east shore's got a list," Annabeth said.

"The east shore's got a swimming area and a canoe launch and a birch tree that's been falling for three years."

"The birch tree is a landmark."

"The birch tree is a hazard."

"The birch tree is both. That's what makes it a landmark."

Clarisse snorted. "You would say that."

"I would say that because it's true."

"It's true because you say it."

"That's not how truth works."

"It's how truth works at camp."

They reached the dock and distributed themselves the way they always did: Clarisse on the edge with her boots off and her feet in the water, Silena on the planks with her legs dangling, Thalia on her back with her hands behind her head, Annabeth sitting with her knees up and her notebook closed on her shins, and Percy in the middle of all of them because Percy was always in the middle of all of them and nobody had ever told him to move.

"Ten minutes per occurrence," Clarisse said, to the lake.

"Retroactive," Silena said.

"Double for Zeus-cabin accompaniment," Claresse said.

"I heard that," Thalia said, from her back.

"You were supposed to."

The lake moved under the dock in its evening way, slow and dark and full of the last light. A fish jumped somewhere out by the boundary rock, and the sound of it carried across the water like a small announcement. The pavilion lanterns were still lit up the hill, warm and yellow, and the smell of somebody's dessert was still hanging in the air, and the camp was going through its evening rituals — doors closing, voices getting quieter, the day putting itself away.

"Do you remember," Silena said, to nobody in particular, "the first time we did this?"

"The first time we sat on a dock?" Clarisse said.

"The first time we sat on a dock together."

"I remember," Clarisse said. "You were crying."

"I was not crying."

"You were crying and you had a juice box."

"I had a juice box because I was thirsty. The crying was unrelated."

"The crying was about the juice box."

"The crying was about being eleven and at a camp full of people who were supposed to be my family and didn't know it yet."

The lake moved. The dock creaked. Clarisse put her hand out and found Silena's shoulder and squeezed once, hard, and let go.

"You're family now," she said.

"I know," Silena said.

"You're stuck with us."

"I know."

"We're not letting you go."

"I know."

Clarisse leaned back on her hands. "Good."

Thalia, still on her back, said, "When I was a tree, I used to think about this."

"About docks?" Annabeth asked.

"About sitting on docks. About having people to sit with. About —" She stopped. "About being a person who gets to be still."

The dock was quiet for a moment. Then Annabeth reached over and put her hand on Thalia's arm, and Thalia turned her head and looked at her sister, and neither of them said anything, because some things didn't need saying and the lake was keeping them anyway.

Percy, in the middle of all of them, with the lake under him and the stars coming out above him and his sisters and his girlfriend and his family all around him, thought: this. This is the thing. This is the thing I crossed two timelines for. This is the thing I'd cross a thousand more for.

He didn't say it out loud. He didn't need to. The lake already knew. Silena went in the water up to her chin with her hair in a bun, and Clarisse sat on the end with her boots off for once, and Thalia lay back with her head on her folded hands and the first stars coming out over the trees.

"You're going to fall in a lake in your boots," Clarisse said.

"I'm not wearing boots."

"Then you're going to fall in a lake barefoot," Clarisse said, "which is still you, being stupid, in a lake."

"I'm being still," Thalia said. "You should try it."

"I'm always still."

"You're always braced."

"Bracing is still."

"It's braced," Silena said, from the water, floating, "and it isn't still."

Thalia put her hand out, blindly, until she found Percy's sleeve on the dock, and tugged, and he came and sat within reach. Not for anything. Just because she'd asked, in the language they'd been building all summer, where the asking is the whole message and the answer is the seat.

"Your sister's still learning to sit down," she said to Annabeth, not loudly.

"Noticed," Annabeth said from the far end, evening on her face.

"Tell her it works."

Annabeth looked at Clarisse. Clarisse looked at the lake.

"There's water in the lake," Annabeth said, deadpan, "if you'd like to test it."

Clarisse put her boots back on, which Thalia was pretty sure was the closest she was going to get to a thank-you, and the lamp at the boat ramp flickered on, and by the time the bait bucket came out — because somebody, inevitably, was going to want to fish, and it was going to be Silena, because Silena loved a rod and never caught anything — Thalia was lying with her eyes closed and her back to the planks, and the lake was doing its evening thing under her, and somewhere in the dark a fish jumped.

She thought, in no particular order: that her sister had made a boy, and that the boy had made her sister, and that neither fact had anything to do with her, and that both facts did. That the fourth arc of her life — the one that had started with a cabin, and a war, and a theft — had somehow arranged itself around a lake. That she was older than all of this by four years and younger by none.

That tomorrow there would be another lake day, and one after that, and that for a girl who had once been a tree, still being a girl on a dock was the best thing she knew how to be.

---

They came off the dock the way they'd come onto it — slowly, and in ones and twos, and with the kind of reluctance that meant the evening had been good and nobody wanted to be the first to say so.

Clarisse went first, because Clarisse always went first, and she paused at the top of the ramp and looked back at the lake and said, "Ten minutes per occurrence. Retroactive. Double for Zeus-cabin accompaniment."

"Noted," Annabeth said.

"Noted," Thalia said.

"Noted," Silena said.

"Noted," Percy said.

Clarisse nodded, once, and walked up the hill with her boots in one hand and her clipboard under her other arm and the kind of walk that meant she was thinking about something and hadn't decided whether to share it.

Silena went next, but she paused beside Percy and said, very quietly, "The cove. The swimming. The sisters. All of it. Thank you."

"It wasn't me," Percy said.

"It was all of you," Silena said. "That's the point."

She followed Clarisse up the hill, and her hair caught the last of the lantern light and held it for a second before she turned the corner.

Thalia and Annabeth were the last two on the dock. They stood at the end of it, side by side, looking at the lake, and the lake looked back at them with its dark and its patience and its keeping.

"Tomorrow," Thalia said.

"Tomorrow," Annabeth agreed.

"Same time?"

"Same time."

Thalia bumped her shoulder against Annabeth's. "Go inside before I get sentimental."

"You're already sentimental."

"I'm always sentimental. I'm just also loud."

Annabeth laughed, and the laugh carried across the water and came back changed, and she turned and walked up the hill, and Thalia watched her go with the kind of look that meant she was keeping something safe.

Then Thalia looked at Percy. Percy looked at the lake. The lake kept its own counsel.

"Good night, Percy," Thalia said.

"Good night, Thalia."

She walked up the hill, and her footsteps faded, and the dock was empty, and the lake was dark, and the stars were coming out, and Percy stayed.

---

Percy stayed at the lake after the others drifted up the hill, because the end of a day at the water is a thing he never wastes, and because Annabeth had gone to get a blanket and either meant to come back or meant to take a long time about it, and he was willing to bet on either.

The last light was coming off the lake in long stripes. The willow cove, way down the shore, was fully dark, its shade saying its own goodnight. Water bugs wrote their endless little sentences in the surface film. The big stone with the white X was out there invisible, holding the line between camp and elsewhere, and somebody, probably tomorrow, would paint it.

Annabeth came back with the blanket and a chemistry-toned grin and sat without asking, under his arm, in the combined smell of sunblock and lake and campfire from the pavilion, and spread the blanket over both their legs, and the two of them sat on the empty dock in the dark the way you can only do when a day has used itself up and the going-to-bed isn't a debt you owe anyone.

For a while, nothing needed saying, and neither of them spent anything on filling it. The dock was warm under the blanket even after dark, because planks give back what they take, and the lake worked below them in its slow night way, and mosquito bats stitched the air over the reeds, and the camp behind them had gone down to its last sounds — a door, a shower running, the pavilion lanterns ticking as they cooled.

"Ten minutes per occurrence," Percy said.

"I heard."

"Retroactive."

"I heard."

"At that rate," Percy said, "the cove pays for itself by next summer."

Annabeth turned her face into his shoulder to laugh where no one could see it, and he felt the laugh, because you feel the small laughs more than the big ones, and she said, against the fabric of his shirt, "We're going to be so bad at being quiet."

"We're going to be so good at being bad."

"Eloquent."

"Thank you."

Her hand had found its way under the blanket to his, and neither of them had drawn a line to mark when that had happened, which was how the best things they did kept working: no ceremony, no committee, just the two of them arriving somewhere without being able to name the mile marker where it started. She laced her fingers through his. Her thumb went once, slow, over the ridge of his knuckles, and he felt it in his whole arm like a struck string.

"Do you remember," she said, "under the dock, when you said the lake was a ceiling?"

"You said it was a room."

"I said it was a room," she agreed. "You said it was a ceiling. We were both wrong. It's a door."

"To where?"

"Everywhere. Today." She tipped her head back against his arm to see more sky. "It opened into today and the cove and the swim and Clarisse catching us and Thalia napping at us and those girls with the inner tubes, and it's going to open into tomorrow, and we get to walk through both."

Percy looked at her. The moon hadn't come up yet, and the lake was at its one dark hour, and her face was a shape without edges, which meant he was seeing her the way you see someone from the inside: temperature, sound of breath, weight of her against his side, facts his body had catalogued before his mind ever weighed in.

"You're a much better architect than I am," he said, which was as close as he could get, out loud, to the size of it.

"Obviously," Annabeth said, and squeezed his hand.

The lake breathed. Some night bird crossed the whole thing in one line. Somewhere behind them, the Hermes cabin started a water-balloon skirmish with itself and lost.

The moon came up out of the trees, not full, not new — the fat crescent that hangs low in September and makes a road of itself on the water — and the road ran the whole lake, from the far town lights they weren't allowed to swim to, up the middle, to the dock, right to the last plank like the lake had drawn them a line home.

"Hey," Annabeth said. "Hey, look."

"I see it," he said, already looking at the moon instead of her.

"I didn't say what it was yet."

"It's the moon. I win."

"You don't win," Annabeth said, "you're just fast," but she was grinning, and he was grinning, and the moon-road lay on the water the whole way out to the far town lights, repairing the night one plank of silver at a time.

"Paint the X tomorrow?" Percy asked.

"I'll get the paint," Annabeth said. "You get the ten minutes."

He turned, and kissed her, and it was the third kind of kiss they owned — not the dawn-dock one, broken open by first light and approaching footsteps, and not the willow one that took its time — but the one that belonged to night water: slow, warm at the top, cool underneath, with the moon-road bright on the lake beside them and the whole day folded into it. Her hand came up and rested at the back of his neck, and his held its place at her jaw like it had been built there, and the lake kept its silence around the dock with the discretion of a good witness.

They came back out of it by degrees, foreheads down, breathing the same cooled air off the surface.

Above them, the stars had been coming out all along, the way stars do — without permission, without hurry — and now they were everywhere, except where the moon-road interrupted them, silver on dark it took itself too seriously to share. The two of them lay back on the dock under the blanket to look at all of it, and Annabeth came in against his side like it was the position a body assumes at fourteen and keeps for life: her head in the nook of his shoulder, his arm around her, her hand flat over his heart, both of them under one blanket in the shape of one thing.

"Name a constellation," she said, in the dark.

"That one," Percy said, waving vaguely. "The Toe."

"The Toe."

"It's a real constellation. I'm looking at it."

"You're looking at a tree."

"Trees can be constellations if you believe in them."

She laughed — the small close laugh, the one that lives in the collarbone, the one he could feel against his side like a second heartbeat — and then the laugh ran out, and they were just looking up, and the sky did what sky does over a lake, which is double itself.

"Do you ever think," she said, quieter now, "that somebody up there had us in the plans?"

"All the time," Percy said. "Tonight especially."

"Tonight especially," she agreed, and you could hear the smile in it without seeing it.

They lay there a long time like that, and the stars came down to the water, and the water held them, and there was no moment at which either of them could have said when her breathing and his had started going at the same speed, or when her thumb had stopped moving on his chest and simply rested there, over the heart, keeping its place.

"Okay," she said, at last, in the voice she reserved for closing arguments. "Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow," he agreed.

He kissed her hair once more — it tasted like lake and sun and the end of a long day — and they lay on the dock in the dark under the blanket and the stars, the whole lake doubled overhead, and the lake did the only thing lakes ever do, which is keep everything, and give you one more day tomorrow.

In the Poseidon cabin, Clarisse noted it in the margin of the duty roster: 10 MIN RETRO., and underlined it twice.

At the arrow of the cove, the heron stood on the bow of the sunken skiff in the dark, patient, holding the boat's place, all night, in case in the morning someone came back to build something.
