## Chapter 212: A Better Route

Leandros saw the camp map on the Big House table and stopped before anyone asked him to move it.

The map had been rolled the last time he saw it. Now it lay open beneath four stones, each one holding a corner flat. The lake was drawn in charcoal and blue ink. Cabin paths were narrow lines, labeled in Annabeth's precise hand. The route to the boathouse bent around the shore instead of cutting straight across the hill.

He had suggested that bend.

The map also marked the low drainage cut with a note: *do not use until ground holds*. The temporary board still sat beside the fence, where Chiron had left it after the soil yielded. The note looked darker than the others, as if someone had gone back over it more than once.

Beckendorf stood at the other end of the table with a coil of dry canvas. Clarisse was counting lengths of rope into separate piles. Chiron had a basket of metal hooks and a list.

“What are you doing?” Leandros asked.

“Getting the water-side equipment under cover before another cold rain,” Clarisse said.

“Which equipment?”

“The canvas rolls, dry line, and the spare mooring hooks.”

“Where are you taking them?”

“The covered shed.”

“That’s farther from the lake.”

“It’s dry.”

“There's a shorter route.”

Clarisse looked at the map. “There are routes. None of them are ready until we check them.”

Leandros kept his hands at his sides. The map was close enough to touch. He did not touch it.

“Can I look?” he asked.

Chiron glanced at him, then nodded. “You may.”

Leandros leaned over the table. He followed the path from the boathouse to the shed with one finger held above the ink. The marked route ran up from the shore, crossed the main yard, and turned behind the forge. It avoided the low ground but went near the forge's wood stack. The spare mooring hooks could snag there. The canvas roll would be worse.

“The shoreline path is better,” he said.

“For carrying the canvas?” Beckendorf asked.

“Not along the lower cut. Along the bench above it. Then down by the boathouse.”

Clarisse set down a rope. “That’s not the route to the covered shed.”

“It could be.”

“Could be is not checked.”

“I know where the current catches the lake path.”

“You know the water. We're moving equipment over ground.”

“They meet at the shore.”

“Not always.”

The words landed like a hand against his chest. Leandros straightened. “I didn't say the current tells you where the path is.”

“You said the shoreline route was better.”

“I said the water-side approach is better.”

“Then say that.”

He looked at the map, not Clarisse. She had gone back to counting rope, as if the correction had been small enough to put away. It was not. He had asked to look. He had said what he saw. She had cut his answer into a smaller shape and returned it to him.

Chiron moved the basket of hooks closer to the table. “If you have a route suggestion, make it precise. Which stretch of water? What have you observed? What remains to be checked on land?”

Leandros disliked the questions because they were reasonable. He disliked them more because they came from Chiron instead of Clarisse. “The east edge of the boathouse landing is exposed. Wind pushes the surface toward the dock, but the lower current moves west along the shore. A canoe would be easier to bring in from the notch north of the shed.”

“Is the notch clear?” Chiron asked.

“I don't know.”

“Is the bank firm?”

“I don't know.”

“Then those are checks we need before anyone carries or launches anything.”

Leandros looked at the map. The notch was a blank line between two small marks. Annabeth had not labeled it as a path.

“I can check the water,” he said.

“You can,” Clarisse said. “If you ask Beckendorf before you go down there. The bank has been soft after rain.”

“I would.”

“You’re asking now.”

“I am.”

“What are you asking?”

He felt the heat climb behind his ears. He had tried to offer something useful. Now the whole room seemed to require exact words before he could move.

“Can I check the water at the north notch and mark the current direction for the equipment route?”

Beckendorf considered the coil in his hands. “You can observe from the dry landing. Don’t step onto the bank. Don’t launch a canoe. Bring the notes back before we choose a ground route.”

“Fine.”

“Ask Annabeth before you draw on her map,” Clarisse added.

“I know that.”

“Do you?”

He looked at her. “I helped draw part of it.”

“And she owns the map.”

That was true. It still sounded like a verdict.

“I'll ask,” he said.

“Good.”

Clarisse went back to the rope. No one told him he had earned a task. No one said they trusted him. Beckendorf put the canvas coil down and went to help Chiron with the hooks. Leandros remained at the map, reading the lines as though the right route might appear if he stared long enough.

He had wanted the shortest path.

The shortest path had no name, no marked surface, and no permission.

---

The north landing stood above the low cut, where the bank flattened close to the water. Leandros stopped at the last dry stone. The earth below it looked dark and packed, but the edge had a shine from last night's rain.

Beckendorf had told him to stay back. He stayed back.

The lake gave him three separate directions. The wind pressed the surface toward the dock. Near the shore, the reeds leaned west. Farther out, the darker stripe of water moved the opposite way from the pale ripples around it. He watched long enough to be sure the movement repeated.

He took out his notebook and wrote the time, wind direction, and the two currents. Beside the shoreline mark, he added: *water approach from north notch may be sheltered; bank not inspected; no launch*. He looked at the words. They were accurate but not useful enough.

He added a small arrow to show where the wind pushed. He did not write *better route*.

On the way back he passed Percy and Annabeth carrying a folded canvas roll between them. They had stopped beside the path where it narrowed around a rain barrel.

“We could take it this way,” Percy said.

“It catches on the lid.”

“We can lift it over.”

“That makes the turn harder.”

“Then we take the longer path.”

“That path crosses the rope piles.”

Percy adjusted his end of the canvas. “I’m not trying to make the path hard.”

“I know. I’m trying to make sure we don't have to put the roll down in the mud.”

“Then we should use the main yard.”

“That’s what I said.”

“You said it was one option.”

“It is the one that doesn't hit the barrel or the ropes.”

Percy gave a short nod. “All right. Main yard.”

Annabeth's expression eased. They lifted the canvas and continued. Percy shifted his grip to match hers without another argument.

Leandros watched them go. They disagreed without making the disagreement into a contest. He didn't know how they did that. They had years of knowing each other's habits, he supposed. That was easy to say and harder to get.

Annabeth came back alone a few minutes later, a small paper roll tucked under her arm.

“Chiron said you wanted to check the water,” she said.

“I did.”

“Did you ask before using the map?”

“I haven't used it. I made notes in my book.”

“Good. What did you find?”

He opened the notebook to the page. He pointed to the two arrows, then explained the surface wind and the lower current. Annabeth read the time and location.

“This suggests the landing may be sheltered from the crosswind,” she said. “It doesn’t show whether the route from the shore is passable.”

“I wrote that.”

“You did.”

“I know what I saw.”

“I didn't say you didn't.”

“You said it doesn't show the route.”

“It doesn't. It shows the water.”

He closed his mouth. She turned the page slightly toward him.

“Your note is clear,” she said. “Could you add one separate mark for the place you observed from? The map is for people carrying the equipment, not only for boats.”

“I'm not putting it on your map.”

“Good. We can decide what belongs on it when we compare the routes.”

“I thought you wanted me to help.”

“I do.”

“You keep correcting the part I helped with.”

“The map isn't finished.”

“Neither am I.”

Annabeth looked at him for a moment. “That’s true.”

He expected her to ask whether he had meant to sound rude. She didn't. She asked him to show her exactly where he had stood. He pointed it out on his notebook. She copied the landmark in pencil onto a separate scrap.

“Is this the mark you meant?” she asked.

“The stone with the split top.”

“Then I’ll call it that until we verify the path.”

“You can just put the current note there.”

“I’ll write what it records.”

“Why?”

“Because someone carrying canvas needs to know where the observation happened. They don't need to read an argument about which route is better.”

“It's not an argument.”

“Then it will be easy to keep the note narrow.”

He felt the flush again, and he looked away. The water beyond the landing had not changed. The current still moved west under the wind.

“Can I add the note to the draft?” he asked.

“To the draft map?”

“Yes.”

“Ask Annabeth,” she said, and then blinked. “I mean, that's me. I can let you mark it on the tracing sheet, not the original.”

He should have felt pleased. Instead the permission made the map feel more like something he could ruin. “I can write the note on the tracing sheet.”

Annabeth unfolded the tracing paper and slid it across the bench. He copied the current arrows and the split-top stone. He wrote: *observed from dry landing; water approach only; land route unverified.*

“That’s clear,” she said.

“Is it enough?”

“For this observation.”

The words were not praise. They were not an insult either. He folded the tracing sheet carefully and handed it back.

---

Clarisse was outside Cabin Three when he returned. She had a bundle of rope over one shoulder and a short list in her hand.

“Can I come with you when you check the path to the shed?” Leandros asked.

“What do you want to do?”

“Check where the canvas roll can fit.”

“On the ground?”

“And near the water.”

“Those are two different checks.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

He shifted the notebook under his arm. “I wrote down what I saw.”

“Good. That doesn't tell us if the path is safe.”

“I know.”

Clarisse read the note. “You can walk the marked path with me. No low cut, no moving the board, no launching anything. If we find a problem, we mark it. We don't fix it on the way.”

“That sounds like a lot of rules for walking.”

“It's a work route.”

“I asked to help.”

“Then help with the route.”

He wanted to ask what else she thought he was going to do. He stopped himself. “All right.”

Clarisse looked at him over the paper. “Do you want to go now?”

“Yes.”

“Then give me a minute. I'm taking the rope to Beckendorf.”

She went to the equipment shed. Leandros waited at the path. He had not been told to wait, but he did not want to follow her and make the request look like a trick. She returned with the rope gone and a pencil behind her ear.

“Ready?”

“Yes.”

“Good. You lead along the marked path. I’ll carry the list.”

He blinked. “Why me?”

“You asked to check it.”

“I thought you wanted to lead.”

“I want the route checked. You asked to help. Lead the part we agreed on.”

He started before he could change his mind. The path ran between the cabins and the practice field, then bent around the old stone basin. The low cut lay below them, still tagged. A board kept the loose soil from slumping farther into the runoff channel. Leandros did not look down at it for long.

At the basin, the path narrowed where the ground had been worn smooth. Two people could pass there if neither carried anything wide. The canvas roll would be awkward.

“Could go behind the bathhouse,” he said. “It’s a little longer, but there’s space.”

“Is that marked?”

“No.”

“Do you know who uses it?”

“Campers.”

“Who?”

“People going to the bathhouse.”

“Which people?”

He looked at the path. “I don’t know.”

“Then we ask.”

“Do we have to ask everyone who walks down a path?”

“Not everyone. The people who use the part we’d block.”

“That could take all day.”

“It won't.”

Clarisse put the pencil to the list and wrote *bathhouse path: ask before moving roll*. She did not turn to face him.

“You could make a list of things you can't do,” he said.

“I could.”

“Would that be useful?”

“More useful than pretending we checked something we didn't.”

He felt the sting of his own earlier words in her answer. “You already know I'm not pretending.”

“I know you want it checked.”

“That's what I said.”

“No. You said the route was better before you knew who used it.”

He held the rope in his hand although Clarisse carried the list. The difference between a water approach and a ground route had seemed obvious to him when Annabeth explained it. Now he had to walk through the other difference: knowing a path existed was not the same as knowing it was available.

They turned toward the boathouse. The main route climbed slightly, then opened into a wider stretch of packed earth. A pair of campers came up carrying empty baskets. Clarisse stepped aside and let them pass. Leandros did the same, though the path was wide enough that they could have gone around him.

“People are going to carry things both ways,” Clarisse said.

“I know.”

“Then route width matters.”

“I'm looking at it.”

The campers passed and gave Clarisse a nod. One of them glanced at Leandros but did not ask who he was. He did not mind. He was still new enough that people looked twice.

The landing appeared ahead. Leandros stopped at the split-top stone where he had stood earlier. “From here, the north notch would give the best water approach.”

“From where do people carry the supplies?”

“From the shed.”

“Then it isn't the water approach.”

“It connects.”

“Where?”

He pointed down the slope. The route narrowed at the lower edge, where rain-dark soil met the reeds.

Clarisse looked at it for a moment. “No.”

“You said we were checking.”

“We did. That bank isn't a route today.”

“It held when I came down.”

“You didn't come down.”

“I could tell.”

“From the stone?”

“From the water.”

“The water doesn’t tell you how deep the soft soil is.”

“It tells me the current.”

“We need both.”

“I know the current won't hold a person.”

“Then why are you arguing?”

“Because you keep acting like I didn't bring anything useful.”

Clarisse's hand went still at her side. “I didn't say that.”

“You keep telling me what the current can't do.”

“Because you keep asking it to do more than it can.”

He heard the answer. He still hated the way it made his work sound small. “If I didn't know the water, we'd be going around the whole lake.”

“No one asked you to map the whole lake.”

“I could.”

“You could. We need this route.”

He glanced at the split-top stone and the damp bank. The water had looked a certain way. It had not looked like a path.

“Then we use the main landing,” he said.

“We haven't checked the roll width there.”

“It fits.”

“Have you tried?”

“No.”

“Then we check.”

“Fine.”

She waited, as if she expected another complaint. He picked up the rope and started toward the landing. The path remained where it was, its width and shape unchanged by his judgment.


At the main landing, Beckendorf waited beside the covered canvas roll. He had tied the ends closed so no loose corner dragged. A second, narrower bundle sat on top of it, ready for the same trip.

“Can the wide roll fit through?” Clarisse asked.

“Probably,” Beckendorf said. “But we should check before adding weight.”

He set the roll down. Leandros walked the route with his eyes: from the landing to the yard, around the basin, past the dry storage, then behind the forge to the shed. The last turn was tight. There was room on the path, but the forge wood stack narrowed the approach.

“Could move the stack,” he said.

“It’s not ours to move,” Beckendorf replied.

“Then take the path on the other side.”

“That crosses the workbench area.”

“We can carry it around the workbench.”

“If the forge crew says so.”

Leandros looked to Clarisse, expecting her to say that was too much asking. She only wrote a note on the list.

“We can ask,” she said.

“So every route depends on whoever is standing there.”

“It depends on who uses the space.”

“That makes it impossible to plan.”

“It makes the plan real.”

The forge crew was away, but one camper was sweeping the workshop threshold. Clarisse asked whether they could carry the roll through the edge of the workbench area. The camper said yes, if they stayed outside the marked line and waited until he moved a crate. Clarisse thanked him. Beckendorf waited with the roll. Leandros listened to the exchange.

The new path wasn't faster. It was available.

They gathered four people for the test. Beckendorf and Clarisse took the front corners. Leandros and a camper named Eli took the back. The roll was heavier than it looked. Leandros felt the weight pull at his hands when they lifted.

“Ready?” Clarisse asked.

“Ready,” Beckendorf said.

“Wait,” Leandros said. “We should turn it the other way.”

Clarisse glanced at him. “Why?”

“The short edge clears the doorway.”

“We haven’t reached the doorway.”

“It will be easier to turn before we’re carrying it.”

“Then say that.”

“I did.”

“No, you said we should turn it the other way.”

Leandros felt the others wait for him. “The short edge clears the turn.”

“Better.”

He swallowed the rest of his objection. “Can I ask you to turn it before we lift?”

Clarisse nodded. They rotated the roll on its supports so the narrow end faced the route. The shift made the next lift easier. Leandros noticed that Clarisse didn't praise the idea. She only took her grip again.

They carried it along the marked path. At the basin, they stopped so a line of campers could pass with laundry baskets. Clarisse chose to wait; the roll filled most of the path. Leandros looked at the basin wall and saw a shortcut across the grass, but the grass was slick and slightly sunk near the drainage channel.

He did not suggest it.

When the path cleared, they continued. The workshop camper had moved the crate, and they carried the roll around the marked line without touching the stacked wood. At the final turn, the back corner scraped the post.

“Stop,” Clarisse said.

They stopped. Leandros held his side steady.

“Could lift the back,” he said. “Then shift the front left.”

“Will that clear the roof?” Beckendorf asked.

Leandros looked at the beam. “No. The canvas catches the edge.”

“Then we need a lower angle,” Clarisse said.

“Lowering the front will drag the back.”

“Only if we keep it level.”

They lowered the front carefully while Leandros lifted the rear corner. The canvas edge cleared the beam. No one had to let go. Together, they moved the roll into the shed and set it on two low supports.

Leandros's palms stung. He rubbed them against his trousers.

“Was the route better?” Eli asked.

“It's longer,” Leandros said.

“But we got the roll through.”

“After we turned it.”

“That's still the route.”

“It's one part of the route.”

Eli grinned. “You're impossible.”

“I'm accurate.”

Clarisse came up beside them. “The route worked. The last turn needs a note. We'll need to turn the roll before we lift.”

“Then write it down.”

“You can write it down,” she said. “On the work copy.”

He reached for the clipboard. “I will.”

“Ask Annabeth before adding the note to the map.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

He set the clipboard back down. “Yes. I’ll ask.”

He had agreed to a route, not to every rule around the route. He was still angry that the others treated each space as a question. He was also aware of how often the route worked because they had asked.

The next narrow bundle fit through without scraping the post. They did not carry the heavy second roll. Beckendorf said it should stay under cover until the next lift plan. Leandros wanted to object that they could do it now, but he had nothing in his hands and no new information.

He let the coil stay where it was.

---

At the end of the afternoon, Annabeth sat at the Big House table with the tracing sheet and the camp map. She had added the landing marker but not the current arrows.

Leandros stopped beside her. “Can I show you the note from the carry route?”

“You can.”

He set the clipboard down. “The roll turned before the shed post. We used the workshop edge after the worker moved the crate. The narrow bundle fit without turning.”

“Did you check the second roll?”

“No. Beckendorf said it should wait.”

“Good. Then we won't label it as tested.”

“I know.”

“Do you want to write the route on the tracing sheet?”

He took a breath. “Yes.”

Annabeth moved the map aside and slid over the tracing paper. “Use the pencil, not the ink.”

“Why?”

“So we can revise the route if the next check changes something.”

“I know how maps work.”

“Then it shouldn't be a problem.”

He stared at her. The route had worked. They had carried a roll without dropping it, and she was already planning what to change. “You can just say you don't want me to add my name.”

Annabeth's hand paused over the edge of the map. “I didn't ask you to sign it.”

“Then why is everything temporary?”

“Because we tested one carry with one roll today. The second roll is still waiting. The path hasn't been checked when it's wet or frozen.”

“Nothing is ever enough.”

“For the map, enough means accurate.”

“I was accurate.”

“You were about the water.”

“I was about the route.”

“You were about one part.”

He felt his jaw tighten. He wanted to tell her that the land route had used his insight too, that they had taken the main landing because he had noticed how the water approached. But that wasn't what had happened. He had said shoreline route, and Clarisse had made him name the part he knew.

“Can I write the tested section?” he asked.

“Yes. Mark only the part we carried today. I'll add the other checks after we do them.”

“Fine.”

He took the pencil and marked the landing, the basin turn, the workshop edge, and the final shed post. He wrote *wide roll test: turn before lifting; keep outside marked forge line; second bundle untested.* His handwriting was cramped.

Annabeth read it once. “That will help.”

“Is it going on the map?”

“On the working copy.”

“Not the public one?”

“The camp copy.”

“Why not the big map?”

“Because that one is for finding places. The working copy is for a task people are doing now.”

He could not find anything wrong with that. He still disliked the difference. “You could have said that before.”

“I did.”

“Not like that.”

“Then I should have been clearer.”

He hadn't expected the concession. Annabeth put a small check beside the note but did not tell him he had done well. She had given him the room to make a useful mark; she had also kept the decision about where it belonged.

Leandros returned the pencil. “The water approach should be on the same page.”

“It can be in the water notes. I’ll connect it to the map once we know which landing is usable.”

“You don't need to keep saying you will.”

“I’ll be clear now: not until we check the landing.”

He looked at the map. The ink showed the camp as if every path had already been agreed on. He knew that wasn't true. The route to the shed had changed twice while they carried one roll.

“Then I’ll leave the note with you,” he said.

“Thanks.”

He walked away before the word could turn into praise or an argument.

Percy was in the yard, rolling the empty supports back toward the shed. “Can you help me with these?”

“I just did a carry route.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you asking?”

“Because I still need help.”

Leandros took one support. “Fine.”

Percy picked up the other. They carried them across the yard, avoiding the wet strip by the rain barrel. Percy didn't ask him about the map, the current, or Clarisse.

“You could have made the route worse if we took the notch,” Percy said.

Leandros looked at him. “That isn't what happened.”

“I said could.”

“I know what you said.”

“I’m not trying to take the useful part away.”

“Then don't.”

Percy adjusted his hold. “You were right about the water.”

“And?”

“And the route on land needed checking.”

Leandros put the support down too quickly. The end hit the packed earth with a dull thud. “So I was right and wrong.”

“That happens.”

“Not to you.”

“It happens to me constantly.”

“Annabeth lets you know.”

Percy glanced toward the Big House. “She does.”

“And you just accept it?”

“Not always.”

Leandros looked at the support. He had been waiting for Percy to explain the secret to a family that trusted him. Instead Percy said he didn't always accept correction. “Then what do you do?”

“Sometimes I argue too long.”

“And?”

“And then I have to decide whether I'm arguing because I'm right, or because I don't want to be wrong.”

Leandros picked up his end again. “That sounds like something Chiron says.”

“Maybe Chiron says it because it's true.”

“It sounds irritating.”

“It is.”

They carried the support the rest of the way. Percy let Leandros choose where to set it down. He did not add a conclusion. The question remained, rough and unhelpful in Leandros's mind.


---

Dinner came later than usual because the equipment had to be moved before the damp set in again. The canvas roll sat on supports under cover. The smaller bundle had gone through the shed route without scraping the post. The second roll waited.

Clarisse set a bowl on the table. Silena brought bread, the rosemary smell familiar from the previous week. Percy sat beside Annabeth, who was drawing a quick note on the back of a paper napkin.

“What are you writing?” Percy asked.

“Where the barrel can sit without blocking the path.”

“You could write it on the map.”

“I could.”

“Wouldn't that be easier?”

“Not if we change the barrel's position.”

Percy tapped the napkin. “Temporary map.”

“Exactly.”

He smiled and passed her the butter. She gave him the bigger half of the bread without looking. The gesture came so easily that it made Leandros aware of the empty space at his own side of the table.

Clarisse put the route list near Beckendorf. “The wide roll made it through after we turned it before the shed post. The other roll still needs a separate plan.”

“Good,” Beckendorf said. “I'll check the cover tomorrow.”

“Why not move it now?” Leandros asked.

“Because we have one tested carry, not two,” Clarisse said.

“I know. It fits.”

“The edge scraped the post.”

“Only a little.”

“That's why we wait.”

He had not wanted to sound like he was pushing. The words came out that way anyway. “I found the current approach.”

“You found a water approach,” Clarisse said. “It was useful.”

“Then why does it feel like you're pretending the rest didn't happen?”

“I'm not.”

“You gave Annabeth the map note.”

“Because she owns the map.”

“I made part of it.”

“You helped with a route note once.”

“And today.”

“And today you checked one part of another route.”

“That's still something.”

“It is. No one said it wasn't.”

Clarisse took a seat. She sounded tired, not angry, which made it harder to argue against her. Leandros sat down too. Silena cut the loaf into even pieces and set them on a plate. She did not look at him to see if he wanted one.

“Can I have the end piece?” he asked.

“Sure.”

She moved the plate to him. The act was simple and did not settle the disagreement.

“I said earlier that you correct everything I say,” Leandros told Clarisse.

“You did.”

“I said it because I didn't like being corrected about the route.”

“That sounds right.”

“I hadn't checked the ground.”

“No.”

“I should have asked before saying the whole route was better.”

Clarisse waited. He did not know what else he was supposed to say. “That’s it.”

“Okay.”

“You don't have to say more?”

“Do you want me to?”

“No.”

“Then I won't.”

He took the end piece and buttered it. The bread was still soft inside. “I know the water.”

“I know you do.”

“You can use that.”

“I did.”

“Then why keep saying it was only one part?”

“Because it was one part.”

He set the knife down. “You could have said it was useful first.”

“I did. At the landing.”

“I didn't hear that.”

“You were arguing.”

“Maybe.”

Clarisse leaned back. “You want me to notice it, but you don't want me to say what still needs checking.”

He looked at his bread. She was right, and the rightness made him resentful. “I want to know that you noticed.”

“I noticed.”

It was the plainest answer she could have given. He could not tell whether she wanted him to thank her.

Across the table, Percy was trying to balance his spoon on the edge of the napkin. Annabeth removed the napkin before it fell. Percy looked betrayed.

“You ruined the test,” he said.

“You were testing gravity.”

“I was testing the spoon.”

“Gravity was involved.”

“It could have been controlled.”

“You didn't control the table.”

Percy took the spoon and set it beside his bowl. Annabeth smiled at him, and he nudged her shoulder with his. She nudged him back without stopping her conversation with Silena. They stayed close in the easy way of people who were not worried a disagreement would end the relationship.

Leandros turned back to his plate. He wanted that ease with his siblings and did not know how to make it appear.

Silena passed the bread to Clarisse. “It’s the rosemary one.”

“I remember.”

“Do you like this one better?”

“I like having bread.”

“That wasn't the question.”

Clarisse considered, then took a piece. “This one.”

“Good.”

No one asked Leandros what he thought. He ate the end piece and said, “It’s good.”

Silena nodded. “Thanks.”

She did not tell him whether she had expected him to like it. He ate the rest without asking for the recipe or offering to change it.

When dinner ended, he carried his plate to the wash basin. Clarisse handed him a towel.

“You can dry.”

“I know how.”

“I didn't say you didn't.”

He held the towel ready. “I know.”

She washed the bowls, and he dried them one at a time. They worked quietly. The task gave him something to do without asking him to fix the distance between them.

When the last bowl was clean, Clarisse took the towel. “The map check is tomorrow. If you want to look at the water approach again, ask Beckendorf before he starts the cover test.”

“I can do that.”

“Good.”

She went to the door. He did not follow. He had been invited to help with a route. He had not been invited to every other task, and the difference still felt like someone moving a boundary while he wasn't watching.


---

The next morning, Leandros returned to the north landing before breakfast. He stayed on the dry stone where Beckendorf had told him to stand. The wind had shifted. It came from the north now, low and steady, wrinkling the lake surface toward the boathouse.

The current below it ran west.

He could see the difference where the two patterns crossed. The water near the split-top stone moved slowly; out beyond the reeds, the lower current carried a line of leaves toward the cove. Yesterday's note would not describe this morning.

He opened his book and wrote the new time, wind, and direction. Beneath yesterday's observation he added: *conditions changed overnight; approach not consistent; check again before any launch.* He did not scratch out the earlier note. It was still true for the conditions he had observed.

At breakfast he gave the notes to Beckendorf.

“The current has shifted,” he said. “I wouldn't bring a canoe in from the north notch today.”

“Did the bank change?”

“I didn't go down.”

“Good.”

“I don't know.”

Beckendorf read the page. “We won't use it for the carry route, then.”

“It might be usable later.”

“It might.”

“That's all?”

“That is all until we have more information.”

Leandros held his jaw tight. “I brought you the change.”

“And I appreciate it.”

The words came too late to feel like praise. Beckendorf set the notebook down beside the work list. “The water note helps with a launch decision. We aren't launching anything today.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

Beckendorf studied him, then returned to the list. “The canvas test is planned for this afternoon. If the ground stays soft, we carry the test roll by the main yard.”

“Not the low path.”

“Not the low path.”

The decision was simple. Leandros felt as if he had lost something anyway.

After breakfast, he found Annabeth at the Big House table. The tracing paper lay beside the camp map. He stopped at the edge and waited until she looked up.

“Can I add a second water observation to the working copy?” he asked.

“Is it in your notebook?”

“Yes.”

“Show me.”

He set the book down, open to the new note. Annabeth read both entries, the one from yesterday and the one from this morning.

“This is useful,” she said. “We can mark the conditions instead of drawing a permanent route.”

“Then add it.”

She turned the tracing paper around. “You can add it here. Keep it beside the north notch mark.”

He took the pencil. “Could I use a different symbol for the shifted current?”

“What do you think?”

“A broken arrow. It changes direction.”

“That can work if you add the time.”

He drew the arrow and wrote the two observation times below it. He had not needed to ask permission for the idea, only for the place it belonged. He still wanted the map to show that he had seen something others had not. The note did, in the only way that mattered: it marked what had been observed and when.

Annabeth checked the lettering. “That’s legible.”

“It's a map.”

“Maps can be hard to read.”

“Not this one.”

She looked at him, then smiled faintly. “Not this part.”

He could have argued that she had taught him the same thing in Chapter 196. He did not. He folded his notebook shut and left the tracing paper on the table.

At the shed, Clarisse had assembled the crew for the test roll. The small canvas bundle was ready. The larger roll stayed inside. The path had dampened again where the yard dipped, but the soil near the basin held.

“Are we using the main yard?” Leandros asked.

“Yes,” Clarisse replied. “The other route needs a separate ground check.”

“Can I check it?”

“Not alone.”

“I didn't say alone.”

“Good. Ask Beckendorf to go with you after the roll test.”

He nodded once. “I will.”

The test began. Four people lifted the narrower bundle and walked it along the route. Leandros had the rear right corner. This time, he watched for both the roll's clearance and the ground under his shoes. He noticed a shallow puddle spreading from the side of the basin toward the path.

“Stop,” he said.

The crew stopped.

“The ground is soft ahead,” Clarisse said.

“The surface is holding,” he replied. “I can see the water coming from the upper path.”

“Do you know that?”

He wanted to say yes. The movement was clear to him, the shallow film running across the packed dirt. But the source could be somewhere he couldn't see.

“I think so.”

“Then what do you need to check?”

He looked at the puddle. “Where it begins.”

“Can we check from here?”

“Yes.”

They set the bundle on two dry blocks. Clarisse and Beckendorf walked the path's edge without stepping into the low ground. Leandros stayed with the load. He could see where the water crossed, but not where it began. A line of wet leaves ran from behind the basin toward the edge of the yard.

When they returned, Beckendorf said the puddle came from a cracked water jug left by the path, not runoff from the hill.

Leandros looked at the jug. It had a split at the base, small enough to leak slowly. He had read the water's direction correctly, but guessed its source wrong.

“Then it isn't the path draining,” he said.

“No,” Beckendorf replied.

“We can move the jug.”

“After we empty it and mark the crack.”

“I can do that.”

“Ask the person who left it before moving it.”

He felt irritation rise. The jug was leaking. Why did he need the person who owned it?

“Who left it?”

“We don't know.”

“Then how do I ask?”

“Leave it in place. I'll mark it for Chiron.”

Leandros gripped the bundle's handle. The path would have been dry if the jug had not cracked. He'd been right about the flow and wrong about the cause. The route was still usable, but only after someone else checked what he couldn't see.

“Fine,” he said.

Clarisse's eyes stayed on the damp ground. “We can carry the bundle around the puddle, but only if everyone keeps to the packed strip.”

“That's the route.”

“That's the route today,” she said. “The next rain could change it.”

He wanted to say she made every answer smaller. Instead he said, “Then we check it again after rain.”

Clarisse looked at him. “Yes.”

The bundle moved on. No one slipped. They reached the shed without scraping the post. Beckendorf recorded the path as usable for the narrow test roll, with a note to check the puddle after the jug was removed and the ground dried.

Leandros helped set the roll on the supports. He had not made the task easy. He had helped make the result accurate.

---

After the roll test, Clarisse asked him to mark the route on the working copy. He wrote the main yard path in a solid line and the alternative behind the bathhouse in dashes. At the bend near the forge, the two routes came together.

“That dashed route isn't checked,” Clarisse said.

“I wrote that.”

“You wrote ‘ask before use.’”

“Because we haven't asked.”

“That isn't the same as checking the ground.”

“It’s a route.”

“It might be.”

He set down the pencil. “You're correcting me again.”

“I'm telling you the note doesn't say what you think it says.”

“It says ask before use.”

“Which tells people to ask. It doesn't say the ground is checked.”

“Then we can ask them to check it.”

“Who is ‘we’?”

“Whoever needs the route.”

“That's not clear.”

“It doesn't need to be. People know.”

“New campers don't.”

He pushed the paper away from her. “You keep turning one line into three problems.”

“Because the line is carrying three questions.”

“It's a map, Clarisse.”

“It has to work when you aren't standing there to explain it.”

He stood before he could stop himself. “Fine. Put whatever you want on it.”

Clarisse stayed seated. “I don't want your line. I want a route that tells the truth.”

The words followed him as he walked from the table. The route had been useful. The note had been clear enough to him. That didn't mean it was clear to someone who had not watched the water.

He stopped near the yard and turned back. Annabeth sat at the far end of the table, speaking with Percy over the layout. She pointed to a narrow space on the page. Percy asked something and moved the paper closer. She did not take the pencil from him. They changed one mark together.

Leandros returned to the table.

“What would you put?” he asked Clarisse.

She glanced up. “I’d separate the water approach from the foot route.”

He hated how simple it sounded. “Then do it.”

“It's your note.”

He sat. “I'll draw two lines.”

Annabeth moved the tracing sheet back to him. “Use one solid and one dashed. Add a legend.”

“Fine.”

He drew the foot route in solid line and the water observation in dotted blue. The key said: *solid: test roll carried today; dashed: needs ground/access check; dotted blue: observed current only, not a land route.* It was more words than he had wanted to write. It was also hard to misunderstand.

Clarisse read it. “That works.”

“Good.”

“Thank you.”

He folded the pencil between his fingers. “You're welcome.”

They did not shake hands. She did not say she trusted him. They had a route note that made the next check possible.

As the work crews packed up, Beckendorf explained that the large roll would wait until they knew whether the narrow passage behind the bathhouse was available. Leandros asked if he could check the ground from the path.

“Tomorrow,” Beckendorf said. “Ask the bathhouse crew before you mark anything.”

Leandros nodded. “I will.”

“Good.”

“Do you think I don't listen?”

“I think you hear the part that feels like a limit first.”

The answer was too close. “You don't know what I hear.”

“No,” Beckendorf said. “I know what you do after.”

Leandros looked toward the shed. “I carried the roll.”

“You did.”

“It passed.”

“The narrow bundle passed.”

“That's what I said.”

Beckendorf glanced at the note. “Then that's what we record.”

Leandros let the correction stay. He did not thank Beckendorf for being accurate. He did not tell him the words sounded different when someone else wrote them down. He put the pencil away and followed the crew back toward the cabins.

On the way, Clarisse walked beside him for half a path. She had the folded work list in one hand.

“You were right about the two currents,” she said.

He waited.

“You were right that the water approach may change. The note will help us decide where not to launch.”

“Okay.”

“That doesn't tell us the dry route.”

“I know.”

“You didn't know at breakfast.”

He almost said he had. Instead he said, “I thought I knew.”

Clarisse nodded. “That's different.”

“Is it?”

“It is to me.”

He had no answer. She continued toward Cabin Three. He stayed on the path, watching the way it widened near the shed and narrowed where the grass met the gravel.

He had learned the route by looking. He had not learned who needed it, what they carried, or what the ground would do after rain. He could learn those too, though no one had said they belonged to him.

---

The following morning, Leandros went to the bathhouse path with Clarisse and Beckendorf. He brought the map copy and his notebook. He did not carry a roll.

The bathhouse crew had two baskets of damp cloths set along the wall. One camper was tying the line higher so it would not brush the ground.

“Can we check the path behind the bathhouse?” Clarisse asked. “We might need it for the larger canvas roll.”

The camper looked at the baskets, then at the path. “You can walk through now. Don't carry anything until we move the baskets.”

“Understood,” Clarisse said.

Leandros took out his pencil. “When will the baskets move?”

“After the cloth is dry.”

He had wanted to ask how long. Instead, he asked, “Can I mark the path as available after the baskets move?”

“Ask me then,” the camper said.

“Okay.”

They walked the path without the roll. It was narrow but level. The ground was firm near the stone wall and soft at the bend by the laurel bush. Leandros crouched to look at the imprint there. The mud held the outline of a shoe. It had not broken through.

“Could still take the narrow bundle,” he said.

“Maybe,” Beckendorf replied. “The large roll needs room to turn.”

“There's room if we carry it lengthwise.”

“Have you tried?”

“No.”

“Then write ‘not tested.’”

Leandros put the note in his book. The words no longer felt like an accusation. They still annoyed him.

At the bend, a line of damp cloth hung across the far side of the route. It blocked the passage. The bathhouse crew had raised it to dry, but the ends still brushed the path.

“We can't take the canvas through there,” Clarisse said.

“After it dries.”

“Yes. If the crew says we can.”

Leandros glanced up at the line. He could have suggested moving it, but the crew had just told them not to carry anything until the baskets moved. “We ask again later.”

Clarisse looked at him. “Yes.”

They returned to the main path and marked the bend as untested. The bathhouse camper came out behind them with another basket. Leandros did not ask when the line would be clear.

“Did you need something else?” the camper asked.

“No,” Leandros said. “We’ll check back after the cloth is dry.”

“All right.”

The answer was not permission for later. It was a response to the question he had actually asked.

Back at the shed, Beckendorf brought out the narrow bundle. “This is the one we can test again if Clarisse says the main path is clear.”

Clarisse looked at the map. “The main path is clear for this bundle.”

“What about the wide roll?” Leandros asked.

“It has not been tested at the bathhouse bend.”

“Then use the main route.”

“That one scraped the post.”

“We know where to turn it.”

“We know for the first roll. We need another check before saying the same method works.”

He felt his irritation rise. “It’s the same shape.”

“Different weight.”

He looked at Beckendorf. Beckendorf waited, not choosing for either of them.

“Fine,” Leandros said. “The wide roll stays on the main route until the bathhouse path is checked and the turn is tested.”

“That's right,” Clarisse said.

He pressed his tongue against the back of his teeth. “I heard you.”

“I know.”

“You don't have to repeat it.”

“I was confirming.”

“Why?”

“Because you said it differently than I did.”

He wanted to walk away. Instead, he picked up the pencil and changed the map note: *wide roll: main route, turn at post; bathhouse alternative untested; ask before use.* He added the narrow bundle's test mark in a separate line.

Clarisse read it. “That is clearer.”

“Good.”

She took the map copy and slipped it into the folder. She did not hand it back to him or give him responsibility for the next check. He noticed the absence. He also noticed that the folder now held enough information for the crew to move the smaller bundle without him explaining each mark aloud.

“Can I keep a copy of the water notes?” he asked.

“Your notebook is yours,” Clarisse said.

“I mean a copy on the work sheet.”

“Ask Annabeth.”

He had forgotten the page was hers. “I can ask.”

“You can.”

The words no longer sounded like a gate closed in his face. They sounded like the next step in the route.


---

Annabeth was adding a label to the camp copy when Leandros returned with the work sheet.

“Can I attach the water notes to this page?” he asked. “The current changes with the wind. It shouldn't look like a route that's always open.”

She read the note he had prepared. “You can attach it to the working copy. Keep the times.”

“I did.”

“Keep the landing mark too.”

“I did that too.”

“Then yes.”

He fixed the small paper strip to the back of the work sheet with two removable corners. It could be lifted if the conditions changed. The main map still showed the camp paths. The work sheet showed where the route was tested and where it wasn't.

“Does the water note belong beside the carry route?” he asked.

“Only as context for the landing. The foot route still needs its own check.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

She gave him a small nod. It was the first time in the last two days that her correction had not come after one of his claims. He felt a little foolish for noticing.

At the shed, Beckendorf laid out the narrow canvas bundle for the final carry. The path had been checked for that size. The wide roll remained under cover until the bathhouse line cleared and the turn was tested again.

“Are we carrying the narrow one along the marked route?” Leandros asked.

“Yes,” Clarisse said.

“Can I take the back corner?”

“If you want.”

“I do.”

They lifted together. The route went from the main landing to the yard, around the basin, past the forge's marked line, and into the shed. There was no water approach today; the current notes told them not to launch. The carry route did not need the lake.

At the basin, they waited for two campers with laundry baskets. At the forge, they paused for the worker to move a crate. At the final turn, they shifted the canvas before lifting it over the post. Every stop added time. None made the trip harder.

When they set the bundle down, Beckendorf marked the test complete for the narrow size and specific path. The wide roll remained separate. Clarisse returned the work sheet to the folder.

“Would you have used the bathhouse route today?” Silena asked from the doorway.

Leandros shook his head. “The line blocked it.”

“Then you didn't use it.”

“No.”

“That’s useful to know.”

“Because it tells us what didn't work?”

“Because it tells us what we have to ask before we try again.”

He looked at the damp line behind the bathhouse. It was still there, lifted high enough to dry. “We'll ask after it's clear.”

“We,” Clarisse said, “or you?”

He took the question too quickly as a challenge. “Whoever needs to move the roll.”

“Good answer.”

“It's not an answer. It's a plan.”

“Plans are answers sometimes.”

He was ready to object. Then he noticed that she was not teasing him. He let it go.

The next meal was at the long table. Percy and Annabeth sat together, sharing a plate of sliced apples. Percy passed her the last red piece; she traded it for the green one. They had the camp map between them, but neither was using it. Annabeth had folded the paper to keep a corner from catching on the bowl.

Clarisse sat across from Leandros. Silena placed the bread basket in reach. He waited until she sat before taking a piece.

“How did the route go?” Percy asked.

“The narrow bundle passed. The wide roll has a different route note.”

“Good.”

“It was longer than my shoreline route.”

“Did your route work?”

“The water approach did. It wasn't usable for the carry.”

“Then you found two routes.”

Leandros looked at him. “Not the same route.”

“That's what I meant.”

“No, it isn't.”

Percy set his apple down. “I didn't mean to claim you knew the whole thing.”

“Then say what you mean.”

“I said what I meant. The water approach and the carry path both matter.”

“They don't matter in the same way.”

“No,” Percy said. “They don't.”

The answer did not soothe him. It did keep the conversation from turning into a fight Percy had decided to solve. Leandros returned to his bread. Percy picked up his apple again.

Clarisse passed him the work list. “Can you copy the two current observations onto your notebook?”

“They're already in it.”

“Then check the times against this sheet.”

“Okay.”

He read the water notes and the separate route record. His first observation had been made from the dry landing before the wind changed. The second was a different morning, with a different surface direction. The carry tests were both on packed ground, with the narrow bundle only. He circled each distinction in his book.

He wanted to put his name across the top. He didn't. The note said what had happened. People who needed it would know what he had contributed by looking at the handwriting.

When he finished, he handed the work list back to Clarisse. “The times match.”

“Thanks.”

“Don't you want to know whether I was right?”

“I know what you were right about.”

“And what I wasn't?”

“You know that too.”

He didn't. Not entirely. But he had begun to see where his observations ended. The map needed more than his eyes, more than the water, more than the shortest route. It needed the parts other people knew.

“I can check again after the bathhouse line is clear,” he said.

“If the crew asks us to move the wide roll.”

“If they do.”

Clarisse nodded. “Then we can ask.”

He looked at the table. “We can ask.”

It still felt strange to say. It no longer felt like humiliation.

---

Before lights out, a camper stopped by the shed with a coil of damp rope.

“Can I take this to the north landing?” he asked Leandros. “It’s closer.”

Leandros looked toward the lake. The wind still came from the north, but the current notes were from the morning. He could not see the lower movement from the shed.

“Not by the notch,” he said. “We only checked the water from the dry landing, and it changed since yesterday.”

“So where should I go?”

“The main path. Ask Beckendorf if the rope needs to be outside tonight. The ground path is marked for the narrow bundle, but the water approach isn't a carry route.”

The camper nodded. “I'll ask.”

Leandros had wanted to tell him the fastest way. Instead he had given him what was known and what still needed checking. The answer took longer, but it would not send the camper down a bank Leandros had never tested.

The next morning, he found Clarisse at the table with the route sheet.

“Can I go with you when the bathhouse line is clear?” he asked. “I want to check the wide roll turn. With Beckendorf, and after we ask the crew.”

Clarisse looked up from the page. “You can ask again when the line is clear.”

“Right.”

“And if they say no?”

“Then we use the main route.”

“Even if it's longer?”

“Yes.”

She studied him for a moment. “All right.”

It was not a promise that the route would be his. It was permission to check a route if the conditions allowed it.

Leandros opened his notebook and wrote the next task without giving it a time. The lines about current remained separate from the lines about carrying. He left space for whatever the ground would say after the next rain, and whatever the people using the path would say before he stepped into it.

A better route, he was learning, was not the one he could see alone.

---

He found Annabeth at the Big House table later, returning the tracing sheet to its folder.

“Did you keep the water notes attached?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Can I see?”

She opened the folder. The two current arrows were still there, marked with observation times. The carry path had its own line and separate legend.

“Could I keep a copy of this part?” he asked.

“Your notebook has the same information.”

“I mean the working map.”

Annabeth checked the folder. “You can take a copy of the water notes. The carry route stays with the equipment list.”

“Why?”

“Because the notes have different users. People moving supplies need the ground route. Someone deciding whether to launch needs the water notes.”

“And I can't keep the whole thing?”

“You can look at it here.”

He felt the old response rise. Instead of giving it voice, he named the part he disliked. “I thought you were trying to leave my work off the map.”

“I wasn't.”

“I know that now.”

Annabeth moved the sheet so he could see the legend. “The map records what we know. If a note changes, we change it.”

“It doesn't say who found it.”

“It doesn't need to.”

“It might help.”

“It might. The main map isn't a record of who noticed every turn.”

“Then how do people know?”

“By asking, or by reading the source note. Your name doesn't make the current stay the same.”

He understood the argument. He didn't like it. The water had not moved because he watched it. The map did not belong to him because he had drawn one arrow. He had still contributed the observation.

“I was wrong to say you didn't want my name,” he said.

Annabeth nodded. “I knew you wanted the work to matter.”

“You did?”

“I heard you.”

He looked at her, unsure whether to believe that she had understood him during the argument or only now. “I don't want to be useful only when something needs fixing.”

“Then don't make every useful thing a claim to a place.”

He almost bristled. “That sounds like a correction.”

“It is.”

He watched for a smile, but she remained serious. “I don't like being corrected.”

“I noticed.”

“That doesn't mean it helps.”

“It can still be true.”

He looked down at the two arrows. “I’ll keep the water notes in my book.”

“Good.”

“And you keep the route sheet.”

“Yes.”

“Maybe I'll check the current again when the weather shifts.”

“If Beckendorf agrees.”

“Right.”

The answer felt less like a door closing when he could see what was on the other side. He closed the folder and slid it back across the table.

“Thanks for letting me check it.”

“Thanks for asking.”

He left the room with his notebook under his arm. The map stayed on the table, open to paths he had helped mark and paths he had not.

That evening, he copied the two observations into his notebook with the times beside them. He left a space for a third. The first note did not cancel the second; the second did not prove the first useless. The lake had moved between them.

He wrote one more line under the arrows: *Check before launch. Ask before changing the ground route.*

He paused over the second sentence. It looked less like a navigation note than a rule for working with people. He left it there.

The next route would depend on the wind, the ground, the roll, and the people carrying it. He could not see all of those from the landing. He would have to ask.

He closed the notebook and set it on the shelf above his bunk. The water notes stayed beside the map in his head, not in place of the people who knew the ground. Tomorrow the wind might turn again. The route might change. He could read what the water did, and then find out what the path allowed.
