## Chapter 130: The Family Photo

The first sign Camp Half-Blood had scheduled a photograph day was a ladder against the Big House.

The second was Chiron on the ladder, clipboard in hand while a Hermes camper steadied the base and two Apollo campers argued about the sun's angle. The third was a hand-lettered notice nailed to the porch post:

**CAMP PHOTO DAY. NINE O'CLOCK. PLEASE ARRIVE CLEAN.**

Somebody had added underneath, in smaller letters:

**CLEAN IS A MATTER OF INTERPRETATION.**

Percy stopped on the path, gravel crunching under his sandals, morning sun already warm on the back of his neck. "Is this a threat?"

"It is an invitation," Annabeth said, joining him, hair still damp from the showers, smelling faintly of lemon soap, droplets darkening the shoulders of her shirt. "Punctuation is friendly."

"The exclamation point sounds like a demand from a very cheerful judge."

"You can appeal."

"To who?"

"The ladder."

Chiron looked down from the porch roof. "I heard that."

"Then I appeal to you," Percy said.

"Appeal denied. Breakfast first, then photographs."

"On what grounds?"

"You have syrup on your sleeve."

Percy looked. He did, in fact, have syrup on his sleeve. "That's not evidence. That's breakfast."

Chiron made a note. "The court has heard enough."

Annabeth tugged Percy toward the pavilion before the centaur could appoint him a lawyer. Faster than rumor about free dessert, the notice had spread, tacked crooked and already smudged with fingerprints. By breakfast campers debated clothes, hair, borrowed belts and combs, and whether hiding behind a taller camper counted as a portrait, the pavilion buzzing like a disturbed hive, smelling of syrup and woodsmoke and warm stone.

"I'm not standing front row," Clarisse announced from the Poseidon table, spear propped against the bench. "I have a reputation."

"For what?" Silena asked, buttering toast.

"Not being in photographs."

"That's not a reputation. It's a gap in the archive."

"Good."

Percy slid in across from them. Only days back from Atlantis. Bag unpacked, palace photograph framed above his bunk, pine air familiar again. He hadn't expected the familiar to arrive with a clipboard.

Silena slid toast his way. "You'll be in the picture."

"Haven't agreed to anything."

"You did walking past the notice."

"Not how agreements work."

"At camp, it is."

"We have strange laws."

"We do," Annabeth said, sitting beside him, knee bumping his under the table. "This one's only a request."

Small relief, disproportionate, loosening the knot between his shoulders. He covered it by stealing a strawberry from her plate, its cool sweetness bursting tart, juice staining his fingertip red.

Grover assured two younger campers a photograph didn't require holding breath. Ella reported surviving one, despite briefly testing whether the camera strap was edible. Mr. D announced he would be absent while standing in the portrait.

The Apollo cabin had produced an old black camera with a brass lens. Kayla held it while Austin wrestled the tripod. "Counselors, cabins, families, then everyone," she said. "Plenty of film."

"How much is plenty?" Percy asked.

"Enough."

"That's not a number."

"It's the number you're getting."

The word *film* snagged Percy's attention. Memory from the life Sally had raised him in unrolled without asking: a small apartment kitchen, afternoon light on cabinets turning dust to gold, the click of an old camera, its plastic body warm from Sally's hands. Sally behind it, laughing as she told him to look up. Missing front tooth, paper crown collapsing over one eye, grape juice sticky on his fingers. Leaning away from the lens and squinting as if it had personally offended him, socks sliding on linoleum.

He remembered the picture too. One of few he still saw clearly: chin tipped up, one hand waving before his face, Sally's sleeve caught at the frame's edge, its cuff frayed from washing. The photo had lived in an album among birthday cards and school drawings, pages soft and swollen with glue, smelling faintly of paper and crayon wax. She used to say she wanted ordinary days remembered, not only dressed-up ones, tapping the page with a flour-dusted finger.

He could hear her saying that. He could also feel the ache of knowing, in this life, she hadn't been there to take any such pictures.

"Percy?" Annabeth asked.

Back came the pavilion, the camera's brass eye, camp chatter. Annabeth had lowered her voice. Not touching him, but her shoulder near his.

"You went quiet," she said.

"I'm fine."

She waited, toast halfway to her mouth.

"Mostly," he amended.

"Want to talk about it?"

He looked again at the camera. "I used to think of photos as something Mom did. Sally. She'd take them when nothing special happened. I remembered them as proof ordinary days happened too."

Annabeth's eyes stayed steady and open. "And this feels like it might replace one?"

"I know it can't. Only hard watching everyone line up like the picture will explain what we are."

"It doesn't have to explain anything."

"Good. Because I don't know how to pose as a family."

"You don't have to pose as anything."

He looked at the table's nicked wood, thumb tracing it. "I can try. Might not manage the whole day."

"Then don't do the whole day." She reached for her cup. "We can leave whenever you want."

She meant it — no test, no favor to repay. That helped more than any order to smile.

"Thanks."

"Also, if anyone orders you to smile, I'll distract them with a math problem."

"That might make everyone look more upset."

"Then I'll pick an easy one."

"Not how math works."

"It is when I'm asking the question."

---

By breakfast's end three campers had changed shirts and one Hermes boy wore a helmet so future historical records wouldn't recognize him. The first portrait, senior counselors, spoiled when an Ares camper sneezed hard enough to shake the tripod. Apollo posed with instruments; Demeter arrived clean and left with green streaking somebody's cheek. Mr. D complained standing beside Chiron made him look stationed at a riding stable. Chiron offered to stand behind him, which worsened matters.

From the pavilion steps Percy watched campers arrange elbows and expressions, shoes squeaking on stone, someone's nervous laugh cracking high and quick. Some grinned at the lens; others stared it down, chins high, ears pink with embarrassment. A younger kid asked if the photograph would capture his soul, clutching his shirt in both fists. Kayla promised it would capture his face, sufficient for the archive, and ruffled his hair, dust puffing faintly in the sunlight.

"A lot of standing around," Clarisse said, appearing beside Percy, arms dusted with training sand.

"Allowed to leave."

"Not standing around. Assessing."

"Verdict?"

"Tripod's crooked."

"Three legs."

"And yet."

She folded her arms. Silena joined them with a silver comb and ribbon, handing the comb to Clarisse.

"Your hair's doing the thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"Wind has won."

Clarisse ran the comb through one side. "Wind can have that side."

Silena tied her own hair back. "We should get our picture."

Clarisse gave Percy a sidelong look. "You too."

"Heard the announcement."

"Good. Saves repeating it."

"Not the only one who can repeat things."

"I can repeat louder."

Silena's smile gentled the exchange. "It can be us three. No speech, no arrangement, nobody deciding what it means."

Percy met her eyes. She'd left him room to refuse. Clarisse had too, though courtesy hid under bluntness. No expectation he'd smooth the picture for them. They wanted him in it without pretending he felt nothing.

"All right," he said. "The three of us."

Clarisse nodded as if she'd expected that answer and was pleased he'd reached it himself. "Now, before somebody poses us beside flowers."

Kayla had moved the tripod into maple shade, low stone wall and blue sky behind, leaves shifting green-gold overhead and dappling the ground. Clarisse stepped onto the wall, was told she'd stand too high, and hopped down scowling, landing hard enough to jar dust from the mortar.

She took Percy's right. Silena stood left, her sleeve brushing his wrist, smelling faintly of lavender from the laundry lines. Percy found himself in the middle, not by arrangement. Silena's shoulder brushed his. Clarisse's arm came close enough to show the small scar near her wrist, pale against skin, a thin silver line he had never asked about and would not ask about now.

"Look at the camera?" Percy asked.

"If you want," Kayla said. "Or at each other. Lens won't mind."

"It's equipment," Clarisse said. "No feelings."

"Been arguing with it all morning," Silena said.

The photographer lifted her hand. Percy's attention fixed on the camera anyway, its brass lens glinting, smelling faintly of oil and old leather. The space before it seemed to narrow. Air sharpened with Sally's kitchen — afternoon flash, paper crown slipping over ears, her voice making him laugh, flour gritty under his bare feet. Image and ache arrived together, warm and sudden behind his sternum.

"We can wait," Silena said quietly, hand light near his elbow.

Percy drew breath. No need to face the lens. He could study ground, sisters, sunlight patching Clarisse's shoulder. Memory didn't require stepping from the present.

"No," he said. "Ready."

Clarisse's arm settled against his. Silena's fingertips touched his sleeve. They stood because they'd chosen to: three Poseidon kids at camp, not legend or council arrangement. Clarisse challenged the camera; Silena looked amused. Percy tried not to think about his face.

"All right," Kayla said. "Hold still."

The shutter snapped.

Smaller click than Percy expected. It didn't split morning open or pull the past away. Kayla lowered the camera; the moment ended.

"That's it?" Percy asked.

"That's it."

Clarisse eyed Kayla. "Did I blink?"

"Don't know yet."

"You should know."

"Film hasn't developed."

"Then develop it faster."

"Not how film works," Percy said.

"Established you know nothing about cameras," Clarisse said.

"I know one button."

"And I know where it should point."

Silena touched Percy's arm before stepping away. "Glad we did that."

He met her gaze. "Me too."

True, though he couldn't say why. The picture wouldn't hold Atlantis arguments or missed years. It would show three people standing close in camp shade. Enough for one picture.

Clarisse gave the camera a hard look. "That's the official one."

"Official what?" Percy asked.

"Us."

No argument possible, her tone said. Then she retrieved her spear from the maple.

"I think she's happy," Silena murmured.

"Can tell?"

"She threatened a camera instead of a person."

"Big day."

"It is."

---

Before they could leave the photo area, the path beside the canoe lake rippled. Water spread over grass in a thin shining sheet, cool over their shoe leather, then drew into a neat circle, rim trembling in the sunlight. A small shell bobbed in the middle, moonlight-colored, inner surface smooth and reflective, pearled light sliding across it.

"Not from the lake," Annabeth said, coming over from the next group, shoes squeaking on wet grass.

Percy crouched and lifted it. Cool in his palm. Its inside gleamed polished silver, then filled with a pale stone balcony beneath blue water.

Triton appeared, hair drifting, tablet tucked under one arm. "Message for Percy, Clarisse, and Silena. Annabeth and Thalia, if present. Atlantis heard you returned safely. I'd like inclusion in the camp photograph, if room allows. This mirror-shell will hold an image beside the portrait. Not a live connection, so camp won't accidentally end up in our dining room again."

"That happened once," Percy said.

"Because you left the mirror open."

"Didn't know it was open."

"On the table."

The shell shimmered. Triton's formal portrait shifted into him smiling faintly, a bright fish drifting into the corner, tail blurred.

"Second one's better," Silena said, leaning in.

"Shell preserves the strongest reflection," Triton explained. "Place it with the group. It will keep that image."

Annabeth studied the rim, turning it slightly. "A mirror storing a picture. A lens with excellent manners."

"It's a mirror-shell," Triton said, with dignity.

"We'll make room," Percy promised.

"Thank you. I'll send another if this one is unflattering."

"You have no control over the photographer," Clarisse said.

"Learning that."

The shell went still. Water beneath Percy's shoes slid back toward the lake, leaving grass damp and shining, smelling of fresh mud. He turned the mirror-shell carefully. On the back, silver pattern curled around the lip like a small tide.

"We can set it on the wall for the group portrait," Annabeth said.

"If it falls, it's going in the lake," Clarisse said.

"I'll keep it put."

The next portrait belonged to Annabeth and Thalia. Beneath the maple they stood shoulders touching, Annabeth with a pencil behind her ear, Thalia's jacket tied around her waist.

"You can put your arm around her," Percy said before stopping himself.

Thalia glanced at him. "I know."

"Didn't mean—"

"I know what you meant." Expression softening. "Thanks."

Annabeth tilted her head. "And I can put mine around you."

"Not how it works," Thalia said.

"Why not?"

"I'm older."

"Not made of glass."

"Didn't say I was."

Annabeth wrapped an arm around Thalia's waist. Thalia's hand settled on her shoulder. Annabeth murmured something too quiet for Percy to catch; Thalia rolled her eyes, fighting a grin.

"Look here, please."

Thalia looked at Annabeth instead. Annabeth noticed and smiled, sudden and real. The shutter clicked.

"Good," Kayla said.

"Did you get it?" Thalia asked.

"Yes."

"Then good."

Annabeth stepped back to Percy, brushing leaves from her sleeve, maple grit catching under her nails. "Felt strange to pose." Her cheeks still held color from standing in the sun, and her pencil had left a faint gray smudge behind her ear.

"You looked fine."

"Not what I meant." She took his hand, fingers dusty. "How are you doing?"

"Better than expected. Worse than hoped."

"Very you."

Around them camp carried on while Kayla changed film: a conch sounded, low and brassy, campers crossed the lawn with sun-warmed dust puffing under sandals, Grover talked with a dryad beside water, her leafy hair rustling. An ordinary day, easier for that to trust, cicadas stitching the heat. Yet the camera's clicks kept bringing Sally — her hands on the old camera, thumbs smudged with ink, her laugh when a print came crooked, dates inked on backs in her looping hand. Those memories didn't belong to the surrounding camp, but that didn't make them false. Nor did they have to be his only belonging.

"I keep thinking the picture will make me choose which life was real," he said.

"It can't."

"I know. Just stand here?"

"Can do that."

They stood together through the wait. Percy felt the sun on his neck, warm and steady, Annabeth's thumb moving once across his knuckles, dry and reassuring, grass prickling his ankles through his socks.

Tyson arrived from the undersea forges by the beach path, visiting in a work apron, soot smudging one cheek in a soft gray thumbprint, leather stiff with heat. He carried a paper sack and rolled canvas, twine biting into his broad fingers. His single eye brightened spotting Percy, crinkling at the corner.

"Brother!" Tyson said.

Three strides crossed the lawn, grass flattening under heavy boots, dew shaking free in bright drops, and Percy left the ground in a hug that smelled of forge smoke and cinnamon, heat still clinging to Tyson's apron. Annabeth stepped aside to avoid collection as part of the embrace, laughing, hands up, hair lifting in the displaced air.

"Hey, Tyson," Percy said into his shoulder, laughing. "You came."

"Poseidon said family picture day." Tyson set Percy down and opened the sack, parchment crackling. "Brought cinnamon rolls. Some are still round."

"The kitchen made them with me," he told Silena. She accepted one; Clarisse inspected another for structural integrity, then ate half.

"How long staying?" Percy asked.

"Until after photographs. Then forge. A hinge wants to be a door but isn't working hard enough."

"Can I see the canvas?" Annabeth asked.

Tyson unrolled a painting of camp shoreline in broad blue-green strokes, paint thick enough to catch light on its ridges, smelling faintly of linseed and salt. Water took most of it; at the edge stood two figures, tall and short, joined by a pale silver line, gulls suggested in three flicks, white flashing against blue.

"Cabins are small," Clarisse said.

"Looked that far from the water," Tyson said.

"It's a water view," Silena decided, tilting her head.

"If the group stands by the lake, I can be in the painting and photograph," Tyson said, beaming.

"You'll be in the photograph," Percy promised. "Painting can come back to camp too."

He'd come to visit his brother. Tyson was family whether the camera held room: half-brother in gods' old language, whole brother in arriving with food and forge plans.

"Want a picture with us?" Silena asked.

"Yes. Room?"

"We'll make room," Percy said.

Tyson turned to Clarisse. "You too?"

"Already in one."

"Then be in two."

"Fine." Her face softened despite herself. "You can be in the next one."

The schedule had started unraveling: Hermes kids wandered into other cabins' portraits, and a dryad kept pulling branches from the frame.

Chiron checked his clipboard, tail flicking flies. "Running slightly behind."

"How far?" Percy asked.

"Next three portraits waiting."

"That sounds on time," Annabeth said.

Chiron glanced at the shell. "Triton's message arrived?"

"He sent a picture for the group."

"We should place it on the wall," Chiron said. "Light will show it clearly."

"I'll keep it put," Clarisse said, already moving.

Last cabin portraits filed past, sandals slapping, someone humming off-key. During the film change Percy sat on grass with Annabeth, blades prickling through his shirt, holding the shell by its rim, nacre cool against his fingertips. Triton's image had quieted, leaving only blue palace wall and a silver current line, gently pulsing like a slow heartbeat.

"Feel strange having Triton here as an image?" Annabeth asked, chin on her knees.

"A little. I can miss him and still be glad we came back."

"You can miss Sally and be here too."

Percy watched clouds cross the shell's silver surface. "Maybe the photo can happen while I remember her."

"Seems possible." She brushed his fingers. Then film was ready, and photographers carried the camera to the lawn beside the canoe lake, tripod legs sinking slightly in soft ground.

---

Before Chiron called the campers together, the photographer discovered that nobody had agreed what the finished picture was for.

The question came from a Hermes girl named Miriam, who had been carrying the boxes of photographic plates. She set one down in the shade and asked where the copies would go after they were developed.

"The Big House," an Apollo camper said.

"Which copies?"

"All of them."

"That's a lot of plates."

The photographer checked his list. There were cabin portraits, senior counselors, camp projects, visitor photographs and the final group picture. He had marked several for the public noticeboard, but the names of the people in those photographs weren't always written down.

Annabeth arrived with a pencil and looked at the list.

"We should label them before anyone forgets who stood where."

"We remember," Clarisse said.

"For now."

Percy studied the camera. He knew the photograph might turn out badly. He might have his eyes closed, Clarisse might be scowling at somebody, and Thalia could be halfway through a joke. That didn't bother him. What bothered him was the idea that somebody decades from now would look at the picture and decide the people standing close together must have belonged to the same god or lived in the same cabin.

"Can you write the relationships accurately?" he asked.

Miriam looked up. "Like whose siblings?"

"Yes. But not just the biological part. Some of us came as visitors. Some of us are friends. And some people in the picture don't want their names written where everyone can see them."

Annabeth nodded. "A public caption can say which event it was and the date. The full names can go in Chiron's archive."

"That's two sets of labels," the photographer said.

"One photograph can have different information depending on where it goes."

Silena examined the list. "I want a copy for Cabin Three. The Poseidon siblings and our guests. I also want you to ask the people in it before you hang the copy publicly."

Clarisse raised an eyebrow. "Who wouldn't want a photograph?"

"Someone who doesn't like the way they look. Someone who has a reason to stay out of the camp noticeboard. Someone who just doesn't want strangers deciding who they are."

Percy thought of the way his first years at camp had been written into palace records he couldn't remember helping create. A picture wasn't a legal record, but it could become a story other people repeated. Silena was right to ask how it would be used.

Chiron joined them. He listened while Annabeth explained the difference between archive copies and public display. He approved the plan and asked Miriam to leave an empty space beside every print request for permission to display.

"What if people change their minds?" Miriam asked.

"Then we take it down," Chiron replied. "A photograph is a memory, not a contract."

The camera crew spent the next few minutes fixing a crooked tripod leg and marking the places where the larger groups would stand. Annabeth checked that the sun wouldn't shine directly into people's eyes. Thalia moved a bench so the shortest campers could stand at the front without being hidden by older counselors.

Tyson, who had been watching the preparations, approached Percy with his painting rolled under one arm.

"Am I in the family picture?" he asked.

"Of course."

"I thought maybe I was only visiting."

"You are visiting. That doesn't stop you being my brother."

Tyson considered that. "Then do I stand with Cabin Three?"

Percy looked at Clarisse and Silena. The three of them had spent enough time discussing who belonged where that he didn't want to decide for them without asking.

"Do you want him with us?" he said.

Clarisse stared. "It's his family too, idiot."

Silena took Tyson by the wrist and guided him toward the place they'd marked. "Stand here. You'll block the sun for half the people behind you, but at least everybody will know where you were."

Tyson laughed and bent his knees until his head was closer to theirs.

Annabeth came to stand beside Percy. She had a spare copy request in her hand, already labeled with the date.

"Are you putting me in your cabin photo?" she asked.

"Only if you want."

"I want to. Then I'm going to put you in the Grace-sisters picture too."

"Does Thalia know?"

"She suggested it."

He looked at Thalia. She was arguing with the photographer about whether her spear counted as a prop. Percy had never doubted that she'd find something to object to.

The two families arranged themselves without trying to erase the distinctions between them. Clarisse and Silena took the places they'd chosen. Tyson ducked to fit. Annabeth stood close to Percy, while Thalia stayed within reach of her younger sister. The photographer waited until everyone had settled, then checked the names and the number of plates one more time.

Percy realized the photograph would not prove that they had been happy every minute before it was taken. It wouldn't settle every question about belonging or remember every argument. It would show who had stood together, on this particular afternoon, and who had chosen to be seen.


---

"This is the final group picture," Chiron announced, voice carrying. "Please gather by cabin, leaving room for satyrs, dryads, and anyone else in the camp community."

"Does the canoe count?" someone called.

"Canoe has not completed registration."

"It's been here longer than I have!"

"Still overdue on safety inspection."

Campers formed a loose crowd, jostling and laughing, sunscreen and cut grass scenting the warm air, skin sticky with heat. Ares claimed the front row without trying to look pleasant, elbows out, sandals scraping. Demeter kids still held their flower basket, petals wilting in heat, bees circling with a drowsy hum. Grover and Juniper stood near the center, his arm around her shoulders, her bark-perfume sweet when the breeze turned, Ella perched on Grover's shoulder, claws careful, Chiron taking the back row where he could see over heads, clipboard finally lowered, tail swishing once.

Mr. D emerged from the pavilion only after Chiron said the group was incomplete.

"Was not informed this was compulsory," Mr. D said, smoothing his shirt.

"It isn't," Chiron replied mildly.

"Then I choose to be elsewhere."

He stayed, arms folded.

Tyson carried his painting to the wall's edge and set it on a wooden stand, canvas fluttering. Clarisse placed Triton's mirror-shell beside it, wedging it with a pebble. The shell glowed, displaying his portrait in a narrow oval of water-colored silver. Triton's face looked toward camp, serious and faintly amused.

Percy stood near the center, between Clarisse and Silena. Tyson rested a careful hand on his shoulder, heavy and warm; Annabeth stood at his other side, Thalia close beside her. People shifted until they could see the camera and stand near the people they wanted, elbows nudging.

"We should have a count," Chiron said.

"No," Clarisse replied. "I want a candid. One where everyone's laughing for real, not politely."

"You can't make it candid if you tell us," Austin said.

"Then don't tell them."

"I can hear you," Percy said.

"Good. Included."

"Take a regular one first," Clarisse told Austin. "Then catch the laugh."

The first group shot held orderly for half a second. Campers adjusted hair; a dryad pulled a leaf from a satyr's collar. Percy looked into the lens. Memory of Sally's kitchen stirred — flour on the counter, her grin — but he didn't leave the moment. Annabeth beside him, Thalia beside her; Clarisse and Silena close, Tyson's hand warm on his shoulder.

The picture wouldn't explain family or settle different histories. It would catch them here, by the lake, with Triton's shell beside them.

"Ready," Austin said.

The shutter clicked.

Done.

"Fast," Tyson said, blinking.

"One advantage of cameras," Annabeth said. "They don't ask you to stand for hours."

Austin lowered the camera. "Need one more. Clarisse asked for a candid."

"I did," she said, chin up.

"How to make everyone laugh?" a camper called.

"Tell a joke," Clarisse said.

"You named a baby dragon," Silena reminded her. "It ate your boot."

"Teething."

"How do you know?"

"Looked at its teeth."

"Not a diagnosis."

"Enough of one."

Familiar enough Silena began laughing before finishing her sentence. Clarisse tried to look offended and failed, mouth twitching. Percy heard the first laugh travel, then another. Grover leaned toward Tyson to whisper something. Tyson looked confused, then laughed because Grover had. Ella flapped wings and nearly lost her perch; the dryad beside her reached up to steady her, laughing. Mr. D shook his head, but his mouth's corner turned up.

"Wait," Austin said. "Everyone's moving."

"They're laughing," Clarisse said. "That's what cameras do."

"Trying to frame the picture."

"Then frame the people."

"Wall is behind the people," Austin said.

"Exactly," Clarisse replied. "That was your joke."

"It wasn't."

Tyson considered the wall solemnly. "It is a very good wall. Doing its job."

"Wall's job is to be a wall," Percy said, shoulders shaking.

"Good job."

Tyson's solemnity broke Silena. She laughed harder, leaning against Clarisse. Clarisse steadied her, then made the mistake of looking at Percy. He was laughing too, sound escaping before he could stop it. Annabeth turned toward him, smile wide and unguarded. Thalia laughed at her sister, then the rest, one hand covering her mouth. Even Chiron smiled, eyes crinkling. The group grew louder, less orderly, more itself.

For a second Percy thought of Sally laughing as he ducked from her picture, flour on her cheek, then taking another anyway, camera clicking like a small, happy insect. Ache remained, dull and familiar behind his ribs, but it wasn't asking him to look from the people beside him, sun warm on his neck and laughter ringing off the water.

"Now!" Clarisse shouted.

The camera snapped.

No chance to arrange it. Tyson's grin huge; Silena's eyes closed; Clarisse laughing despite herself. Percy's mouth open mid-laugh, Annabeth turned toward him. Ella's wings spread, and Mr. D looked as if the camera had been rude.

Austin lowered the camera. "That one might be good."

"It is good," Clarisse said firmly.

"Haven't seen it."

"Heard everyone."

"Doesn't tell you how it looks."

"Tells me what it was."

Certain. A picture could hold seconds without passing judgment on anything outside them.

---

The picture lineup had nearly dissolved when Nico realized no one had written down who stood where.

"That's not a problem," Percy said. "We know who everyone is."

"Right now," Annabeth replied. "What about ten years from now? Or someone who wasn't there?"

She borrowed the back of Chiron's clipboard and sketched the rows while people were still in sight. Clarisse, already halfway toward the pavilion, returned when she heard her name.

"You're putting labels on us?"

"On the photograph," Annabeth said. "Not on people."

Silena checked the order from left to right. Tyson had been standing behind the taller campers, and the photographer had asked him to lean forward so his face would show. His shoulders appeared between two people he had only met that morning. Annabeth wrote his name and noted that he was a guest, not a permanent Cabin Three resident. Tyson looked over her shoulder and corrected the spelling of a forge apprentice's name.

Percy expected the exercise to be tedious. It wasn't, exactly. Nico pointed out Bianca at the end of the second row. Grover identified the counselor behind him. Two younger campers disagreed over which one had borrowed the hat in the photograph, and Chiron settled it by checking who was wearing it now.

"What about the palace picture?" Silena asked.

"Different caption," Percy said. "Different place. Some of the same people."

"Not the same relationships," Annabeth agreed. "A caption can say who's present without deciding who belongs to whom."

Clarisse examined the draft. "Don't call me a court princess."

"I wrote your name."

"Good."

The final note would go into the photo album with the date and the photographer's name. They could add corrections when the prints arrived, but nobody would need to guess which birthday, visit, or family title a smiling face was supposed to prove.

The crowd drifted toward pavilion and cabins, chatter fading. Grover lifted Ella from his shoulder; Mr. D returned to coffee as if never leaving it.

Tyson rolled his canvas. "I'll take the painting to the forge."

"You can leave it in my cabin," Percy said.

"You want it?"

"If you're okay with that."

"Painted it for camp. I'll paint another, with the canoe."

"Canoe might not stay still."

"Then I will paint fast."

Silena joined them, ribbon coming loose. "Family pictures should go where everyone can see them."

"Camp group can go in the Big House," Percy said. "Others in our cabins."

"We'll find room," Annabeth said, already measuring wall space with her eyes.

Percy picked up the mirror-shell, cradling its edge. "Can this stay with the camp picture?"

"Archive keeps items accompanying portraits," Chiron said. "We can ask Triton to send a copy for your cabin."

"Tell him this one's good," Clarisse said. "No need for another."

"Haven't seen it," Percy said.

"Saw his face. Enough."

The photographer promised prints by next day. Camp resumed interrupted business. Percy carried Tyson's painting to Cabin Three and set it beside the framed palace picture. It leaned left. He adjusted twice, then left it crooked — livelier that way.

Annabeth stood in the doorway, arms dusty. "Not going to fix it?"

"Water view. Horizon's supposed to move."

"Not how horizons work."

"Can't prove that."

"Can."

"Don't have to."

She smiled and came in, looking from painting to Atlantis photo to empty space above his bunk. "You'll put the camp portrait here?"

"Maybe. Don't know yet."

"No need to decide today."

"I know."

He sat on the bunk's edge, mattress dipping, springs creaking, canvas cover rough under his palms. Camera clicks still echoed, but softer, like distant taps on glass. New photographs wouldn't replace memories of Sally — flour on her hands, gritty and white, the kitchen radio low and crackling — any more than the Atlantis picture replaced camp. Room on the shelf for frames to crowd together. Room in the day for both grief and laughter without apology.

Annabeth sat beside him, close but not leaning until he shifted toward her. He did. Shoulders touching, steady.

"Want a copy of the three of us," he said. "And one of you and Thalia. And the group."

"Many pictures."

"Many people."

She leaned her head against his shoulder, hair brushing his chin, smelling of sun and lemon soap, a few strands catching on his shirt's rough weave. "You did well today."

"Stood still for a few seconds."

"More than that."

He took her hand, thumb resting over her knuckles, her skin warm and faintly dusty from the day. Outside camp voices passed, woodsmoke drifting on the breeze. Someone shouted the canoe had stolen a sandal; someone else argued canoes lack hands, laughter trailing after.

"Canoe could have stolen it," Tyson called from outside. "Near the water."

Annabeth laughed. Percy looked at empty space above his bunk and remembered the old apartment kitchen, sunlight on cabinets, Sally's raised camera. He could remember without leaving this room.

"Want the one where everyone's laughing," he said.

"Hard to miss."

Outside the lake shone under afternoon sun, gold on blue. Pictures weren't developed yet; nothing needed to be ready before they arrived.
