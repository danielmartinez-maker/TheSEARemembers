# Chapter 216: The Faces We Remember

Thalia laid the lost-property cards in a row across the Big House table and reached for the pencil on her left. It rolled away from her fingers, struck a cup, and spun to the far edge.

Annabeth caught it before it fell.

“You’re using the wrong hand,” she said.

“I’m using the nearest hand.”

“That is your right hand.”

“Which is nearest.”

Annabeth handed the pencil back. Her mouth had tightened around a smile. “If you keep reaching across yourself, you’ll knock over the cup.”

“Then move the cup.”

“I could.”

“Problem solved.”

Annabeth moved it.

The cards had started as Chiron’s practical idea. A basket of misplaced things had accumulated on the Big House porch: one wool mitten, two dull pencils, a narrow belt, a book with no name inside, and a small wooden horse somebody had left beside the steps. Chiron had asked Thalia to make a few simple labels while she sat out the longer walks. Annabeth had offered to help, and Ianthe had asked whether there was another task she could do.

The labels were written. The objects were sorted into two boxes: things that could be recognized by their owners, and things that would need to be described at supper. The spare cards had become a game because Thalia drew a crooked face in the corner of one, Annabeth corrected the nose, and Ianthe said both of them were wrong.

Now the three girls were trying to draw one another from memory without looking up.

Thalia held the pencil in a loose grip and studied Annabeth for exactly three seconds.

“Ready?” Annabeth asked.

“No.”

“You said to start.”

“I said look at you. I didn’t say I was ready.”

Ianthe tapped the card with her pencil. “This is the part where you stop stalling and draw.”

Thalia looked at her. “You’re drawing too.”

“I’m keeping track of the time.”

“There is no time limit.”

“There should be.”

“Why?”

“So no one gets to adjust the picture every time the subject moves.”

Annabeth lifted her eyebrows. “A fair point.”

Thalia pointed the pencil at her. “Don’t encourage this.”

“I’m not. I’m making an observation.”

“Those are two different things,” Ianthe said, and slid the little brass kitchen timer toward the middle of the table. It had been set for five minutes. “We can use this.”

Thalia examined the timer. “You found the timer.”

“It was beside the flour jar.”

“And you decided that meant it belonged in a drawing game.”

“It was on the table.”

“Everything on the table is now part of the drawing game?”

“If it is useful.”

Thalia glanced at Annabeth. “She’s been at Camp for months and has learned to claim any object she can reach.”

“I asked before I took it.”

“That’s true,” Thalia admitted. She turned the card sideways. “Fine. Five minutes. But no rules about how many lines we get.”

“That would make the results inconsistent.”

“The results are faces we’re drawing on spare cards. Nobody is submitting them for a cabin inspection.”

Ianthe’s pencil paused. “You make the rules sound silly when you don’t want them.”

“I make them sound silly when they are.”

Annabeth rested her forearms on the table. “I’m sitting still. Start the timer.”

Ianthe pressed the brass knob. The timer gave a soft, uneven tick.

Thalia began with Annabeth’s hair, which was easier than her expression. She made a slanted line, then another, then a shape that seemed to belong to a leaf rather than a head.

“That’s my hair?” Annabeth asked.

“It is hair.”

“It is also attached to something shaped like a pear.”

“Your head is not a pear.”

“You drew it.”

“I’m drawing fast.”

Annabeth looked at her own card. “I haven’t drawn yet.”

“Good. Then your pear can remain your problem.”

Ianthe’s card had already taken shape. She had drawn a small, careful face with a straight nose and dark eyes, then marked the angle of Annabeth’s chin. The lines were neat enough to look like they had been measured.

Annabeth leaned slightly toward it. “You’re drawing me?”

“You said we were.”

“I said you could draw whatever you wanted.”

“You said to draw the person sitting there.”

“That was not the same thing.”

“It is close enough.”

Thalia looked between their cards. “She’s right. That’s your face.”

“You’re both looking at the paper,” Annabeth said. “You’re supposed to draw me from memory.”

“We are,” Ianthe replied.

“Then why is yours better?”

“Because I remember details.”

There was no boast in the sentence. That did not make it less like one.

Annabeth held her pose. “What details?”

“You press your lips together when you’re deciding whether to correct somebody.”

“Do I?”

“Yes.”

Thalia snorted. “She does.”

“And you lift one eyebrow first,” Ianthe added. “You do it when you think the person should have noticed something already.”

Annabeth’s mouth changed before she could stop it. The left eyebrow moved.

Ianthe pointed with the pencil. “That.”

“That’s because you’re staring at my face.”

“You told me to draw you.”

“I didn’t tell you to inventory me.”

Thalia’s pencil hovered above her card. “Maybe use the other details.”

“There are other details.”

“Good.”

Ianthe looked back down. She added a small curve to Annabeth’s hair. “She turns her head toward whoever is speaking, even if she’s angry.”

“I’m not angry.”

“I didn’t say you were.”

“You drew the angry eyebrows.”

“They are the eyebrows you have.”

Annabeth folded her arms. “This is less a portrait and more a list of charges.”

Thalia laughed. The sound came out too loud for the quiet porch, and she touched the brace at her heel as if she could keep the laugh from jarring anything. It did not hurt. She let her hand fall away without testing the joint or asking it a question.

The timer rang. Ianthe stopped it before the second chime.

They exchanged cards.

Thalia’s picture of Annabeth had a long dark sweep of hair, two mismatched eyes, and a mouth that looked like it had just heard a bad idea. It did not look exactly like her sister. It did look like a person who had spent a long time deciding which part of a problem to solve first.

Annabeth’s drawing of Thalia had a sharp chin and a crooked line of hair, with one eyebrow raised high enough to become a roof.

“I don’t look like that,” Thalia said.

“You do when somebody tells you what you can’t do.”

“You drew a roof.”

“It was an ambitious eyebrow.”

Ianthe tilted the card toward the window. “It is recognizable.”

“Thank you,” Annabeth said.

“That does not mean it is flattering.”

“I wasn’t asking for flattering.”

Thalia put Ianthe’s card next to the others. Ianthe had drawn Thalia with the brace visible under the table and the cane leaning against the chair. Her hair was rougher than the lines around her eyes. One boot sat at an angle. The face looked more tired than Thalia felt, but she recognized the set of her jaw.

“You drew the brace,” Thalia said.

“It was there.”

“You could have left it out.”

“Then the legs would have been uneven.”

“Faces don’t need the whole body.”

“Sometimes the body explains the face.”

The sentence was not unkind. It still made Thalia want to put the card away.

Annabeth looked at it again. “It does explain something. It doesn’t explain everything.”

Ianthe considered that. “That’s fair.”

“Is it?” Thalia asked.

“I think so.”

“You don’t have to agree because Annabeth said it.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Ianthe’s pencil tapped once against the table. “I can agree with the point without agreeing with everything she says.”

Annabeth’s smile came and went. “That’s more than most people manage before lunch.”

“It is before lunch,” Ianthe said. “I’m not making any promises.”

The three cards remained between them. The empty cards waited under the timer. Annabeth reached for a fourth, then paused.

“What are you doing?” Thalia asked.

“I want to draw the horse.”

“Why?”

“Because it’s next to the cards.”

“The horse is in the box.”

“It’s on the table.”

“That’s the entire system now.”

Annabeth picked up the small wooden horse. It had a chipped red saddle and a short tail carved too thick. She set it beside the pencil jar and turned it until the missing piece of its ear faced away.

The task had been meant to help the owner find it. The drawing had become a better description than “small wooden horse.” Thalia wrote that on a label anyway.

Ianthe watched her. “You didn’t draw yourself.”

“I wasn’t asked.”

“You drew both of us.”

“Those are faces I can see.”

“Your face is here too.”

Thalia touched the blank cards. “I know what it looks like.”

“That’s not the same as drawing it from memory.”

“I’m not sure I want to.”

“Why not?”

“Because I get to choose what I put on a card.”

Ianthe studied her for a moment. “That’s true.”

Annabeth moved the wooden horse a little farther from the cup. “If you want to draw your own face, do it. If you don’t, you don’t owe the game one.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

Thalia took the next blank card and turned it over. It had a faint gray mark from the bottom of the pencil cup. She drew a quick line, then another, and made a crooked little storm cloud above a wide mouth. It wasn’t a portrait. It could have been one if the person looking at it wanted it to be.

Ianthe looked down. “Is that supposed to be you?”

“No.”

“Who is it?”

“Someone who complains about the weather.”

“The weather has been clear.”

“That’s why they’re complaining.”

Annabeth picked up a pencil and added two drops beneath the cloud. “Maybe it started to rain.”

“It didn’t.”

“It could have.”

“If we change the drawing every time we change the story, it will never be finished.”

Ianthe looked pleased. “That’s a useful rule.”

“Don’t write it down.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

The three of them laughed again, quieter this time. Annabeth drew a small pair of boots beneath the cloud. Thalia added a scarf. Ianthe put a neat square of ground under the boots so the figure would not float.

“You made a line of earth,” Thalia said.

“So it has somewhere to stand.”

“Now it looks like a warning sign.”

“It looks like a person standing on a hill.”

“On a very square hill.”

“The hill is in the background.”

“The hill should not be square.”

Ianthe frowned at the card, then erased the bottom of the line with her thumb. It smudged into a dark band.

“That’s worse,” Annabeth said.

“I can fix it.”

“It’s not ruined.”

“I can improve it.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I want to.”

Thalia watched Ianthe work at the smear until the gray spread farther. At last she set the card down and took another.

The small decision seemed to irritate her. She did not say why. She began again, this time with a hill that sloped sharply into the corner. The figure stood on it without tipping over.

“Better,” Annabeth said.

Ianthe glanced up. “You don’t have to praise it.”

“I wasn’t praising the person who drew it. I was saying the hill works.”

“That’s a distinction?”

“It is if you want it to be.”

Thalia leaned back in her chair. “I think this one is going to need a label too.”

“‘Person on hill,’” Annabeth said.

“‘Person who refused to be a square.’”

“That is not accurate.”

“It's more accurate than a square hill.”

Ianthe kept her eyes on the card, but her mouth shifted. “I could write both.”

“No,” Thalia said. “That would be too many words for a label.”

A noise came from the porch steps. Percy appeared with a shallow bowl in one hand and a folded cloth over the other. He stopped in the doorway when he saw the table.

“Am I interrupting?”

“You’re interrupting the quality-control process,” Ianthe said.

“That sounds serious.”

“It is.”

Thalia held up her own drawing. “We’re making portraits.”

Percy looked at the card. “Of whom?”

“Someone who complains about the weather.”

“That’s your specialty.”

“It’s not me.”

“I didn’t say it was.”

Annabeth reached for the bowl. Percy set it by her hand. A few sliced pears lay in it, damp where the cloth had covered them.

“You brought these to the quality-control process?” she asked.

“I brought these to you.”

“Why?”

“You skipped the pears at breakfast.”

“I had bread.”

“And coffee.”

“I don’t drink coffee.”

“That was tea. I’m losing track of which argument we’re having.”

“Maybe because you keep starting them.”

“I bring fruit and get accused of starting an argument.”

Annabeth turned a slice over in the bowl and offered him the first bite. “You can eat one before I decide whether to accuse you.”

He took it. “Thank you.”

The exchange was small, almost absentminded. Annabeth did not move away when his elbow touched hers. He did not ask her to explain her face or make room for his opinion. He waited while she chose a pear, then held the bowl steady so it would not slide over the sketch cards.

Ianthe watched them. “You could have drawn each other.”

“We already did,” Thalia said.

“I mean a real portrait.”

“Those were real portraits.”

“You made Annabeth look angry.”

“I made her look like she’d heard a plan.”

“I had heard a plan,” Annabeth said.

Percy leaned closer to the cards. “I can draw one.”

“Do you know how?” Thalia asked.

“No.”

“Then why offer?”

“I thought the game might be about trying.”

Annabeth moved the bowl out of his way. “Use the spare card.”

He picked up the pencil. “What should I draw?”

“Me,” Annabeth said.

He turned to her and studied her face. “You’re sure?”

“I said me.”

“Do you want me to ask if you mean it?”

“Not in the middle of the game.”

“Fair.”

He drew three lines, stopped, and erased one. The pencil made a soft rasp across the paper. He drew her hair as a dark curve, the angle of her chin, and a small line at the corner of her mouth.

Annabeth studied the card. “Why do I have one eyebrow?”

“You were moving.”

“I was not.”

“You moved your eyebrow.”

“Both of them work.”

“I know. I didn’t have room.”

“You made my nose too small.”

“I can fix that.”

“Don’t.”

Percy lowered the pencil. “All right.”

She studied the drawing again. “I like the mouth.”

“Good.”

“You remembered the corner.”

“I look at your face.”

Thalia noticed how he said it, easy and matter-of-fact, without asking anyone else what it meant. Annabeth gave him a look that would have been stern if she had not smiled at the same time.

“You can keep it,” she said.

“Can I put it in the bowl?”

“No.”

“I’ll protect it.”

“You’ll get fruit on it.”

He put the card beneath the timer.

Ianthe took the pencil back. “I want to draw Percy.”

“Do I get to approve?” Percy asked.

“No,” she said. “You get to sit still.”

“That sounds more comfortable.”

Annabeth set her cup on the far side of the table. “He can move if he wants.”

“Then the game won’t work.”

“The game will survive.”

Ianthe looked at Thalia. “You made the first rule.”

“Which one?”

“That the subject can move.”

“I didn’t make that rule.”

“You said it.”

“I said faces move.”

“Same thing.”

“Not exactly.”

Percy sat down. “I can stay still for five minutes.”

Thalia frowned at him. “Why would you offer that?”

“Because she asked.”

“She didn’t.”

“I thought she might.”

Ianthe looked annoyed with them. “I can draw a face without a model.”

“Then do that,” Annabeth said.

“I prefer not to waste the cards.”

“You’re not wasting them.”

“I don't want to draw something I won't use.”

The other three looked at the pile. Thalia glanced at the wooden horse, the cloud-person, the labels, and the portraits. “What would you use this one for?”

Ianthe lifted the blank card. “I don't know yet.”

“Then it can stay blank.”

“I was going to draw Percy.”

“You can still draw Percy.”

“No, I don't want to if he moves.”

Percy turned his hands palm-up on the table. “I can stay still. I don't mind.”

Ianthe drew in a breath as if to answer, then closed her mouth. She looked at the card, measured its edges with her thumb, and turned it over.

“Maybe I’ll draw the horse,” she said.

“It’s not looking at you,” Thalia said.

“Neither are you.”

“I’m sitting right here.”

“Which is why I can see you.”

“Do you want me to hold still?”

“No.”

“Then I’ll draw the horse.”

“That seems fair.”

Ianthe made two quick lines. The horse’s head leaned toward the pencil jar. Her next line captured the thick carved tail.

“That's good,” Percy said.

“I didn’t ask.”

“I know.”

Ianthe stared at the card, then at him. “You can still say it.”

“I can.”

She added the missing ear.

Thalia watched the way Ianthe took praise: she wanted it, then resisted it, then kept it once it came without a demand attached. A father’s praise had left a deeper mark. Thalia did not reach for it. She could not make those marks even by choosing the right words.

“Do you think he remembers what I draw?” Ianthe asked.

No one answered at once.

She looked down at the horse and shifted the card so its edge was parallel with the table. “I mean the notes. He asked about them last time.”

“He read the page,” Annabeth said.

“He paid attention.”

“Yes,” Annabeth said. “He did.”

Ianthe’s eyes stayed on the card. “You say it like there’s a difference.”

“There can be.”

“Between reading a page and paying attention?”

“Between paying attention to a thing and knowing a person.”

Ianthe gave her a quick look. “I didn’t say he knew me.”

“No,” Annabeth said. “You didn’t.”

Thalia saw Annabeth's fingers curl once beside the cup. She kept quiet. The question had not been about her, and she didn’t need to finish Annabeth’s answer.

Ianthe took the dark pencil and shaded under the horse’s belly. “He remembered my notes from home. He remembered what the arrows meant.”

“That mattered to you,” Thalia said.

“Yes.”

Thalia waited for the next question. It did not come. Ianthe had not asked them to say whether the memory proved anything. She had only put the fact down.

Percy reached for a pear slice. “That part makes sense.”

Ianthe glanced at him. “What part?”

“That it mattered.”

The simple answer sat with them better than an explanation would have.

A wind moved through the trees without touching the porch. It caught the loose corner of one label and lifted it. Annabeth pinned it under the bowl, careful not to cover the drawing of the person on the hill.

“Someone left a coat in the basket,” Percy said. “Chiron wants the label to say ‘green.’”

“Is it green?” Thalia asked.

“More brown than green.”

“What did you call it?”

“Green.”

“Why?”

“Because the label says green.”

Annabeth slid the cloth over the fruit. “Take us to see it.”

“We finished the labels.”

“Then we can see if the coat is green.”

“I don’t think you need to inspect it.”

“I want to.”

Percy smiled. “All right.”

He reached for the bowl, then paused. “Should I wait until you finish the drawings?”

Thalia looked at the cards. Ianthe was still filling in the little wooden horse’s front hoof. Annabeth had started a second portrait of the horse, smaller than the first and with a crooked, expressive eye.

“Take the basket,” Thalia said. “We’ll come after.”

Percy looked at Annabeth.

“I’ll come,” she said. “I’m not asking you to wait.”

“Okay.”

He gathered the cloth and the bowl. As he passed her chair, Annabeth caught his wrist lightly, then let him go.

“I’ll see you in a minute.”

“I know.”

He left the porch without asking whether the drawing game had become something more important. Annabeth watched him go, then returned to the card.

“Do you want him to wait?” Thalia asked.

“No.”

“Do you want me to go with you?”

“After the horse.”

“All right.”

Annabeth drew the horse’s second eye.

Ianthe lifted the card she’d drawn. “That is not what its eye looks like.”

“It’s facing the window.”

“You can’t see it.”

“I can see the edge.”

“That’s not enough to say whether it has a white mark.”

“It doesn’t need a white mark.”

“It is a real horse.”

“It is a wooden horse.”

“It was carved from a real horse.”

“Maybe.”

Thalia looked at the wooden figure. “We need to put that in the label.”

“No,” Ianthe said. “It would confuse the owner.”

“If we knew the owner, we could ask.”

“We don’t.”

“Exactly.”

The timer ticked softly, though it had stopped. Annabeth touched the brass top, checked it, and placed it beside the stack. A little dust had gathered around its feet.

The cards were imperfect and private. None of them belonged in the basket with the lost things.

Ianthe placed the horse between her drawing and Annabeth’s. “It looks different from each side.”

“It’s missing an ear,” Thalia said.

“It’s missing part of an ear,” Annabeth corrected.

“You drew the missing part.”

“I drew what it could have looked like.”

“You changed it.”

“I imagined it.”

Ianthe considered the distinction. “That’s different.”

“Sometimes.”

“Sometimes?”

“Sometimes you make the missing part up. Sometimes you draw what is actually there. The picture can’t tell you which unless you say.”

Ianthe turned the horse in her palm. “If the owner finds it, they won’t need the picture to tell them.”

“Probably not,” Thalia said. “They’ll know it from the one ear.”

“And the red saddle,” Annabeth added.

“And the fat tail.”

“That is not fat.”

“It is.”

“It’s sturdy.”

“It's a wooden tail.”

“Exactly.”

Ianthe set it down. “I think the card should show the ear.”

“That seems reasonable.”

“Because then the owner will recognize it.”

Thalia tapped the drawing of the horse with her finger. “And because the missing piece is part of the thing.”

Ianthe looked at the cards again. “Yes.”

The admission was quiet. Annabeth did not turn it into a lesson. She picked up the stack of unused labels and squared the corners against the table.

At the far end, the porch door moved on its hinge. Chiron crossed the courtyard carrying a tray. His steps were measured and unhurried. He looked toward them once, but did not signal or call.

“Should we help him?” Ianthe asked.

“Do you want to?” Thalia said.

“I could carry the tray.”

“You can ask him.”

“I know.”

She stood, then paused with one hand on the table. “Would you like me to wait until he reaches the steps?”

Thalia looked at the tray and saw Chiron’s free hand was steady. “Yes. He’ll have to turn the latch.”

Ianthe waited. When he reached the porch, she asked if he wanted help. He said he did, and gave her the lighter side of the tray.

She carried it inside without rearranging the cups or deciding where it should go.

Thalia collected the drawings. Annabeth took the labels. The card with the small cloud-person stayed on the table, half-hidden under the timer. She thought of leaving it, then put it with the others.

No one was asked to explain which face was the truest.

A few days after the labels were carried to the dining pavilion, rain brushed the roof of Cabin One and stopped. It had not lasted long enough to test the window through a real storm. Annabeth checked the sill anyway, because she had put the check on her own list and did not want an unfinished mark to become an assumption.

The cloth beneath the lower corner was dry. She wrote down the time and the weather, then added that the rain had been brief. Thalia watched her put a line through the word “tested.”

“Why cross it out?” Thalia asked.

“Because it wasn’t a storm.”

“It was rain.”

“It was six minutes of rain.”

“You counted?”

“I was watching the window.”

“You can write ‘brief rain.’ It’s not nothing.”

“I did.”

“Then why did you cross out the other word?”

“Because ‘tested’ would make it sound like the long check was done.”

Thalia shifted in the chair. Her heel stayed supported, the brace fitted beneath the cuff of her sock. She was not on the route to the rack or the fence, and the room was quiet enough that she could hear the pencil move.

“You were making a point to yourself,” she said.

“I was making a note.”

“Those are different?”

“They can be.”

Annabeth glanced at her. “It mattered that I saw the cloth stay dry. It also mattered that I didn’t call it proof.”

“I know.”

“I don’t want the next person to read it and think the window’s done.”

“Then write that.”

“I did.”

Thalia examined the page. “You wrote it twice.”

“I wanted it clear.”

“It’s clear.”

Annabeth turned the page face-down. “You don’t have to check my work.”

“I’m not.”

“You were reading over my shoulder.”

“I’m sitting in the same room.”

“Also true.”

The small disagreement settled between them without growing. Annabeth placed the pencil near the notebook. Thalia reached toward it, then let her hand rest on the table instead.

“Ianthe asked whether I remembered other faces,” she said.

Annabeth looked up. “When?”

“After the game. She asked if Father always looked different.”

“You told her he did.”

“I told her the faces changed. I didn’t tell her what that meant.”

“Good.”

Thalia watched her turn the pencil once between two fingers. “You don’t want to tell her?”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You always answer the sentence you want.”

“I’m trying to answer the sentence you asked.”

“Which was?”

“Whether I want to tell her.”

“And?”

Annabeth set down the pencil. “I want her to know that we have our own memories. I don’t want to turn them into instructions for what she should feel about him.”

Thalia nodded. “I don’t want to do that either.”

“You also don’t want to let her believe he has never been unfair to us.”

“No.”

“Then we have to be careful.”

Thalia let out a quiet breath. “That sounds like a lot of work for a conversation that hasn’t happened.”

“Most conversations are easier before they happen.”

“That’s not true.”

“It is if you plan them.”

“You can’t plan what Ianthe asks.”

“No. I can plan what I’ll answer.”

Thalia looked toward the window. The rain had left no line on the glass, only a few bright beads caught in the lower track. “What would you say?”

“I’d tell her what I remember from the last visit. That he praised her, then used the praise to compare us. That when I objected, he told me I had become less pleasant.” Annabeth’s voice remained even. “I wouldn’t tell her he meant something he didn’t say. I wouldn’t tell her he didn’t mean it either.”

“He said he was proud of her.”

“Yes.”

“He meant that part.”

“I don’t know.”

“You think he didn’t?”

“I think he said it. I think she wanted to hear it. I think it mattered to her.” Annabeth looked at the face-down notebook. “I can’t reach inside him and sort the words into the ones he meant and the ones he used.”

Thalia’s fingers closed around the edge of the table. “You can tell her it hurt.”

“I can tell her it hurt me. I can’t tell her the praise was false just because the comparison was cruel.”

“That’s right.”

“It might not feel right to her.”

“It doesn’t have to.”

Annabeth’s gaze moved to Thalia’s hand. “What would you say?”

“I’d tell her he called me impatient with correction.”

“Was that the only thing?”

“No.”

She let the answer stand. The other memories gathered in the space behind it: a different room, a different voice, a father who had sometimes called her strong when he meant she should endure what he would not change. She did not know which parts belonged to the same version of him and which parts she had carried across the new life. The details would not make her more reliable than Ianthe. They would only make the account longer.

Annabeth turned her notebook over again. “You can tell her more if you choose to.”

“I know.”

“But you don’t have to.”

“I know.”

“Those can both be true.”

Thalia rubbed her thumb along the table’s chipped edge. “I don’t want to turn silence into a test.”

“Then don’t.”

“I don’t want her to think she has to ask exactly the right way before I’ll tell her anything.”

“You could say that.”

“I could.”

Annabeth left the notebook open, with the word “tested” still crossed through. “We can tell her that we’ll answer questions about what we experienced. We can also say when we don’t know. We don’t owe her every detail, and she doesn’t owe us agreement.”

“Do you think she’ll believe that?”

“I don’t know.”

“She might ask if we’re leaving something out.”

“We might be.”

Thalia looked over.

Annabeth did not turn away. “There are things that are private. There are things that are difficult to talk about. There are things neither of us has decided how to explain. We can’t make every boundary look like a complete answer.”

Some memories belonged to one person, some to another. Thalia would not make Annabeth's story part of her own answer, and Annabeth would choose what to say for herself.

“Do you want to tell her that?” Thalia asked.

“Not the whole sentence.” Annabeth looked back to the page. “Maybe: ‘I can tell you what I remember. I can’t tell you what it means for you.’”

“That sounds like you.”

“It’s what I mean.”

Thalia lowered her voice. “I keep thinking she hears our history as a warning she has to pass.”

“Then tell her it isn’t a test.”

“She thinks we already have an answer.”

“Do we?”

“No.”

Annabeth’s expression softened. “Then say that.”

Thalia looked toward the porch. The walk between Cabin One and the Big House was short, but she had been using the cane on uneven paths and longer stretches. She would not make the distance into a measure of what she could do. “If she asks today, I’ll tell her what I remember. I won’t make her sit through a list.”

“And I’ll speak for myself.”

“Not me.”

“Not you.”

“Not her.”

“Not her.”

The promise was small enough to keep. It was not a pact about what Zeus would do. It was a way to stop one sister from becoming the interpreter of the others.

Annabeth folded the notebook cover over the page. “Do you want me to ask her?”

“No.”

“You could tell her.”

“I will.”

“If you want.”

“I do.”

A shadow crossed the window. Thalia turned her head, but it had come from a bird passing over the porch. Her eyes stayed on it longer than they needed to.

Annabeth noticed. “That wasn’t him.”

“I know.”

“You don’t have to check every time.”

“I know that too.”

“Do you want me to stop saying it?”

“Yes.”

“Okay.”

The air remained still. The promise did not alter it.

They heard Ianthe before they saw her. She came up the porch steps with a folded sheet of paper and stopped when she noticed them.

“Did I interrupt?”

“No,” Annabeth said. “We were talking.”

“About me?”

“Some of it.”

Ianthe stepped inside. The rain had dotted the shoulders of her jacket but not soaked through. She set the folded page on the table without opening it.

“What part?”

Thalia looked at Annabeth. Annabeth did not answer for her.

“I remember different visits,” Thalia said. “He uses a different face each time. I know the voice. I know what the room feels like before he starts talking. That doesn’t tell me what he’ll say or what you should think.”

Ianthe took off her jacket and hung it over the back of a chair. “What does the room feel like?”

“Like pressure before thunder.”

“That is a weather description.”

“It’s the closest one I have.”

“Does Annabeth feel it too?”

“Sometimes,” Annabeth said. “Not always the same way.”

Ianthe sat down. “Do you remember every face?”

“No,” Thalia said.

“Do you remember the last one?”

“The green coat. The narrow face. Brown eyes.”

“I remember the coat.”

Annabeth’s gaze settled on the page Ianthe had brought. “What do you want to know?”

“I’m not sure yet.”

“Then you don’t have to ask now.”

“That’s what you both keep saying.”

“Because it’s true.”

“It sounds like you want me to stop asking.”

“We don’t,” Thalia said.

Ianthe traced a line in the table’s grain. “You did the same thing when I asked if you minded that I wanted him to come.”

“I changed the question,” Thalia said. “You told me that.”

“I did.”

“You were right.”

“I didn’t ask you to agree.”

“No. You said what it felt like.” Thalia rested both hands on the table. “You can ask us about our memories. I’ll answer what I can. I won’t tell you what your own memory should mean.”

Annabeth leaned forward. “I’ll tell you what I remember from my side. I won’t speak for Thalia, and I won’t use my story to make your decision for you.”

“Will you tell me what you’re not saying?”

“Sometimes.”

“That’s not a useful answer.”

“It’s the honest one.”

Ianthe looked displeased. “You could say you don’t want to.”

“I could. There are things I don’t want to talk about. There are things I don’t know how to talk about. There are things that aren’t mine to share. I won’t pretend those are the same.”

“Which is this?”

Annabeth held her gaze. “I don’t know yet.”

“You’re making me ask twice.”

“I’m telling you I haven’t decided.”

“Why?”

“Because you asked whether I’d tell you. I’m not ready to give you an inventory.”

Ianthe’s chin lifted. “You’re both careful about everything.”

“Yes,” Thalia said.

“Sometimes it feels like you’ve decided the truth belongs to you because you were here first.”

Thalia’s first answer was anger. The second took longer. “I’ve been here longer. That doesn’t mean I own what happened.”

“It can look like it.”

“It can.”

“You could have said that before.”

“I could have.”

“Why didn’t you?”

“Because I didn’t think you wanted my history. I thought you wanted me to tell you how to feel about yours.”

Ianthe tapped the folded paper once. “I didn’t ask you to do that.”

“I know.”

“Then stop waiting for me to ask the perfect question.”

Thalia looked at the chair opposite Ianthe, the one she had chosen to sit near the door the first time Zeus came. She remembered Annabeth saying the room belonged to the person who had to sleep there. The memory did not make her more certain about Ianthe’s feelings.

“All right,” she said. “I can do that.”

Ianthe took a breath. “What happened before you met him at the pavilion? Not everything. One thing.”

Thalia stared at the folded page. “Before the visit in the green coat?”

“Yes.”

She could hear the request beneath the words. Not a verdict, not an instruction. One fact, in one sister’s memory.

“I remember one earlier visit when he praised Annabeth for being easy and called me a problem,” Thalia said. “I can’t place the face now. I remember the comparison better than the rest of the conversation.”

“What had you done?”

“I don’t remember the whole exchange.”

“Did Annabeth remember it the same way?”

“I haven’t asked her what she remembers about that visit.”

“Why not?”

“I don’t want to make her confirm my memory before I can tell you what I remember.”

Ianthe nodded, taking in the answer and its limits. “That’s all?”

“That’s what I remember clearly.”

“You don’t have more?”

“I might. I’m not sure.”

“Would you tell me if you did?”

“If I chose to.”

“You might not.”

“I might not.”

Ianthe’s mouth tightened. “That sounds like a wall.”

“It is a boundary.”

“Those are not the same thing?”

“Sometimes they are.”

She watched her sister pick up the folded page. “You could decide later that you want to tell me.”

“I could.”

“You might not.”

“I might not.”

“Then I can’t trust the answer.”

“You can decide what to do with it. I can’t make the past more complete because you want it to be.”

Ianthe folded the paper more sharply. One corner bent. “You make it sound easy to keep your own story.”

“It isn’t.”

“Then why do you tell me to do it?”

“I’m not telling you to do anything.”

“You’re telling me I can choose, every time I ask.”

“Because you can.”

“That doesn’t help if I want to know what you remember.”

“Then ask me.”

“I did.”

“And I told you one thing.”

“I asked one question.”

“I answered it.”

“Not the whole thing.”

“No.”

The bluntness landed. Thalia kept her hands flat on the table. She wanted to soften it, to add a reassuring word, but that would have changed what she meant.

Ianthe turned to Annabeth. “What do you remember?”

Annabeth’s shoulders rose and lowered once. “I remember that he praised you for listening, and then used that praise to make a point about Thalia and me. I remember asking him not to compare us. I remember he called me less pleasant than I used to be.”

“Do you remember what he looked like?”

“A man in a green raincoat. Close dark hair. Brown eyes. Narrow face.”

“And did that feel like him?”

“I knew it was him.”

“How?”

“I heard his voice. I felt the air change. I watched what he did.”

“Was the face part of it?”

“It was part of what I saw.”

“Did it feel familiar?”

“The expression did.”

Ianthe lowered the page. “I thought you’d say something different.”

“I might, another day.”

“Why?”

“Because I don’t have a rehearsed speech.”

“You both talk as if the face doesn’t matter.”

“I didn’t say that,” Annabeth answered. “The face matters. It’s the part I can see. It just isn’t the only thing I remember.”

Ianthe considered this. Her annoyance did not disappear. “I wanted him to look like the person I remembered from home.”

“What did he look like there?” Thalia asked.

“A man with a gray scarf. He had a small scar by his ear.” She glanced at the window. “I used to know what the scar looked like better than I knew his voice.”

“Do you remember him differently now?” Annabeth asked.

“I remember both.”

“That makes sense.”

“It does?”

“Yes.”

“You don’t think I should have expected the same face?”

“I don’t know what you should have expected.”

Ianthe huffed. “There you are again.”

Annabeth’s smile was small and tired. “I know.”

Thalia reached for the folded page, then stopped before touching it. “What did you bring?”

Ianthe pulled it toward herself. “A list of things I noticed in the rain.”

“Do you want to show us?”

“Maybe.”

“Do you want us to check it?”

“No.”

“Then we don’t need to read it.”

“I didn’t say you couldn’t.”

“Do you want me to?”

Ianthe looked down. “Not now.”

“All right.”

A knock sounded at the porch door. Chiron stood outside, his hands clasped behind his back.

“Ianthe,” he said, “your father is here. He has asked whether you are willing to speak with him.”

The room seemed to pause. Thalia felt her own attention turn toward Ianthe’s face. She made herself look at the table instead.

Annabeth stayed still. “You can say no.”

“I know,” Ianthe said.

Chiron waited without urging her.

Ianthe touched the bent corner of her folded paper. “Does he want the two of you there?”

“He asked to speak with you,” Chiron said. “He did not say whether anyone else should come.”

“I want to talk to him alone.”

Thalia kept her gaze on the chipped table edge. “All right.”

“Do you want me to wait nearby?” she asked, then caught the question. “You don’t have to answer.”

Ianthe looked at her. “I want you to go on with your day. I’ll find you afterward if I want to.”

Thalia’s hand tightened on the edge. She loosened it. “Okay.”

“I don’t want you following me to check whether I’m upset.”

“I won’t.”

“Or standing outside the door.”

“I won’t.”

Annabeth’s voice remained quiet. “Do you want the note you brought?”

Ianthe glanced down at it. “Yes.”

“Then take it.”

She picked up the page and stood. Her jacket remained over the chair.

Chiron turned to lead her toward the pavilion. Ianthe paused at the threshold.

“I’ll tell you what I want to tell you,” she said.

“That’s yours to choose,” Annabeth replied.

Ianthe left without answering. Chiron followed her at an unhurried pace.

Thalia stayed at the table. The pencil rolled against the brass timer and stopped. She watched it settle.

“You didn’t look after her,” Annabeth said.

“I wanted to.”

“I know.”

“I wasn’t supposed to.”

“You can want to and still let her go.”

Thalia’s heel rested inside the brace. She did not stand to test it, or to prove that she could follow. “That’s what I did.”

“Yes.”

“It doesn’t feel like doing anything.”

“It is.”

Thalia looked at the doorway. The porch was empty now.

Annabeth touched the back of her hand. The contact was light enough to leave room for her to move away. Thalia turned her palm upward and let their fingers meet.

They stayed there until the rainwater had stopped dripping from the roof.

---

They went to the dining pavilion because the coat was still in the basket.

The distance from Cabin One was short. Thalia used her cane where the path dipped, then set it aside once the ground leveled. The walk did not tug at her heel. She did not count her steps or test how quickly she could turn.

Annabeth walked beside her without offering an arm. She had the folded label in one hand.

“Percy was right,” she said.

“About which thing?”

“The coat.”

“Did he say it was green?”

“He said it was more brown than green.”

“That’s not right either.”

“He said it was green-brown.”

“That sounds like a swamp.”

“It might be.”

“You’re defending him now?”

“I’m repeating him.”

“Same thing, sometimes.”

Annabeth gave her a sideways look. “Sometimes.”

The dining pavilion smelled of cedar smoke and apple skins. Chiron was speaking with one of the younger Hermes campers near the serving table. A brown-green coat lay folded on the bench. Its sleeves had been turned in so the buttons did not scrape the wood.

Percy stood at the counter, sorting fruit into bowls. He saw them and lifted his chin.

“Did you come to inspect the coat?” he asked.

“We came to identify it,” Annabeth said.

“I think it’s brown.”

“It’s green,” Thalia said.

“That’s what the label says.”

“The label is wrong.”

Percy looked from the coat to Thalia. “You haven’t seen it yet.”

“I can see it from here.”

“So can I.”

“Then you know it’s green.”

“I think it’s brown.”

“Your eyes are brown.”

“That’s not evidence.”

Annabeth stepped up to the bench. “It’s mostly brown, with a green cast at the seams.”

Percy nodded. “That’s what I said.”

“You said ‘more brown than green.’”

“It is.”

“You also said ‘green-brown.’”

“Both things can be true.”

Thalia glanced at Annabeth. “You are defending him now.”

“I’m describing the coat.”

The Hermes camper looked up from the serving counter. “That’s mine.”

Chiron turned. “You recognize it?”

“The left pocket has a hole. I tore it on a nail in the canoe shed.” He walked over and pointed to the seam. “There.”

The pocket had been mended with thread a shade darker than the rest. The coat’s collar was stitched twice where the fabric had begun to fray.

“Do you know if it is brown or green?” Thalia asked.

The boy grinned. “It’s the color of being cold.”

“That’s not a color,” Annabeth said.

“It is if you’re wearing it.”

He picked the coat up and shrugged it on. The sleeves came down over his wrists, but he pushed them back to show the patched cuff.

“Does it fit?” Thalia asked.

“It’s mine.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

“It fits enough.”

He turned toward the door, then saw the basket. “Did you find the horse too?”

“It was there,” Annabeth said.

“Was it the red one?”

“Small, red saddle, missing part of an ear,” Thalia said.

“That one’s mine too.”

The camper hurried back to the porch to retrieve it. Chiron watched him go, then looked at the labels.

“Your description was clear,” he said.

Ianthe had written it on the card: “wooden horse, red saddle, one ear chipped, broad tail.” She had not added the owner’s name because they did not know it. Now the boy would carry it back to the Hermes cabin, where it belonged.

“She did the label,” Thalia said.

“I saw. It was useful.”

The praise was ordinary, offered to the work rather than the person. Thalia noticed the difference without using it to calculate what Ianthe would want from a father.

Annabeth handed the remaining labels to Chiron. “The coat should be written as brown with green at the seams.”

“Would you like to change the card?”

“I can.”

“I’ll leave it to you.”

Annabeth took the card and crossed out the word “green.” She added the longer description. The change was small; the coat had already found its owner. Still, the label now said what they had seen.

A gust came through the pavilion entrance and lifted one corner of the paper. Chiron placed a cup on top of it.

“Not much of a storm,” Percy said.

“Not enough to check the window,” Annabeth replied.

“I didn’t say it was.”

“You were looking at it.”

“I was looking at the cup.”

“The cup is on the paper.”

“Also true.”

Thalia watched him make a face at the corrected label. “Don’t try to read the tea leaves.”

“I’m not.”

“You have a look.”

“What look?”

“The one where you’re about to say something helpful.”

“I was going to ask if you wanted a pear.”

Annabeth took one from the bowl. “I’ll decide.”

“I know.”

She bit into it and handed the other half back to him. He accepted it without making the gesture into an answer about the day.

The door opened behind them. Chiron stepped aside to let another camper through. Across the green, near the pavilion steps, a man stood with Ianthe.

Thalia recognized neither his face nor his clothes. He was taller than Chiron, broad through the chest, with a square jaw and pale hair combed back from his forehead. A dark wool coat hung open over a white shirt. One hand rested at his side; the other had lifted, perhaps to greet Ianthe or touch her shoulder. The face was older than the man in the green raincoat, with a small crease between the brows and a scar no larger than a grain of rice near the left temple.

The air did not change enough to make the trees move. Thalia felt the old pressure anyway, faint and immediate, as if the storm had been waiting for her to notice it.

Annabeth followed her gaze. She did not move closer.

“Do you want to go?” she asked.

“No.”

“Okay.”

Ianthe stood facing him. She had set her folded page in the crook of one arm. The man leaned toward her, and she let him kiss her forehead. She said something Thalia could not hear. He turned his head as though listening carefully.

The gesture could have meant affection. It could have been nothing more than a greeting. Thalia had no right to decide which from across the lawn.

The man spoke again. Ianthe nodded once, then shook her head. He did not touch her after that. His hands stayed at his sides.

“Do you think she’s all right?” Thalia asked.

Annabeth looked at her. “I don’t know.”

Thalia kept her eyes on Ianthe. “I said I wouldn’t watch.”

“You aren’t standing outside the door.”

“I’m watching from across the green.”

“That still counts as watching.”

“I know.”

“Do you want to stop?”

Thalia looked down at the cane in her hand. The wood was warm from her palm. She could turn away. She could also remain there without moving closer or building a story from what she saw.

“I want to stop,” she said. “I’m not sure I can yet.”

Annabeth nodded. “Then I’ll stay here with you.”

They turned back toward the table. The coat’s label lay beneath Chiron’s cup, the corrected ink dark on the paper. Thalia let the visitor remain in the corner of her vision, not the center.

Percy passed her another pear slice. She took it. He did not ask what the man had said or whether she had recognized him. The quiet gave her somewhere to put the answer she did not have.

When she looked back a minute later, the man had stepped away from Ianthe. He paused at the foot of the stairs and said something. Ianthe raised her chin, answered, then walked toward the Big House by herself.

The man did not follow her.

Thalia turned the fruit in her fingers. The skin was cool, the middle sweet. She ate it without trying to assign meaning to its color.

---

Ianthe came to Cabin One after the lamps had been lit.

She wore the same jacket she had put over the chair earlier. It had dried at the shoulders. The folded page was in her hand, but she had not opened it.

Thalia was by the window with Annabeth. The raincloth beneath the sill was dry. Annabeth’s note lay closed on the table beside the corrected coat label.

Ianthe stopped in the doorway. “I said I’d find you afterward.”

“You did,” Thalia said.

“I’m here.”

“I see that.”

Ianthe waited, then came inside. She didn’t sit. “Do you want to know what we talked about?”

Annabeth kept her hands on the table. “Only if you want to tell us.”

“I do.”

She put the folded page down but kept one finger on it.

“He looked different,” she said. “Older. Broader. Gray hair, a scar near his ear. He was wearing a dark coat. I knew it was him before he said anything.”

“Was the voice familiar?” Thalia asked.

“After a moment. His face wasn’t.” Ianthe sat in the chair opposite them. “He kissed my forehead. I let him.”

“I saw,” Thalia said.

“You saw that part?”

“From the pavilion.”

Ianthe’s eyes narrowed. “You said you wouldn’t watch.”

“I didn’t follow you. I looked across the green.”

“That is still watching.”

“Yes.”

“You said you wouldn’t.”

“You’re right.”

“I don’t want you to make it sound like it didn’t count because you were far away.”

“I won’t.”

Thalia held her gaze. “I’m sorry. I wanted to know if you were upset. I told myself I was keeping you in sight in case you needed me. You said you would come find us. I should have let that be enough.”

Ianthe’s mouth tightened. “It would have been.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

Ianthe rested her hand on the folded page. “I’m not asking you to feel nothing when he visits.”

“I know.”

“Or to pretend the last time didn’t happen.”

“I won’t.”

“But I don’t want you to take every little thing I do and turn it into a sign that I’ve forgotten what you told me.”

Thalia looked down at the table. “I don’t know how to stop doing that every time.”

“You can stop asking if I’m all right before I tell you.”

“That’s fair.”

“Sometimes you can just talk to me.”

“I can.”

“I don’t mean the weather notes.”

“I understand.”

“Do you?”

Thalia paused. “I don’t know if I do yet. I can try to listen without deciding what you mean first.”

Ianthe looked at Annabeth. “Did you see him?”

“Only from across the green.”

“Did you recognize him?”

“No.”

“Could you tell?”

“Thalia felt him. I couldn’t read that from where I was.”

“Would you have known if I told you he was Zeus?”

“I would have believed you.”

“Would you have known before?”

“I don’t know.”

Ianthe took a breath. “He asked if I was getting settled.”

“Did you tell him about the room?” Thalia asked.

“I told him I was still at the Big House. He said it was taking too long.”

Thalia did not answer.

“Don’t,” Ianthe said. “I’m telling you what he said. I’m not saying he’s right.”

“I wasn’t going to say that.”

“You were going to say something.”

“I was waiting.”

“For what?”

“I don’t know.”

Annabeth folded her hands. “What did you say?”

“I said the cot wasn’t ready and the window check wasn’t finished. He asked if I wanted him to speak to you.” Ianthe’s eyes moved from Thalia to Annabeth. “I said no. I want to know where I’m going to sleep, but I don’t want him to decide it.”

“Thank you,” Annabeth said.

“Don’t thank me. It was my room too.”

“I know.”

“I told him that you hadn’t said I couldn’t join Cabin One. You said you wanted me there, but the bed needed work.”

“That’s still true,” Annabeth said.

“I know.” Ianthe pulled at the edge of the paper. “He said I shouldn’t have to defend you.”

“What did you say?” Thalia asked.

“I said I wasn’t defending you. I was telling him why the room wasn’t ready.”

Thalia looked at the folded page. “That sounds like a difference.”

“It is a difference.”

“I heard it this time.”

“Good.”

Ianthe opened the page. It was a small list, written in an orderly hand: a brief shower, a dry lower sill, wind from the west, no long storm, no route checked. The last two entries had been underlined.

“He asked if I still had the weather notes,” she said. “I didn’t bring the notebook. I brought this.”

“Why?” Thalia asked.

“I didn’t want to have to show him the whole notebook just because he asked.”

“Did he ask?”

“No. I told him I’d written a few observations.”

“Did you want him to see them?”

“I don’t know. I wanted him to ask me something else first.”

“What did he ask?”

“He asked how the rack task went. I told him I made one mistake and Beckendorf had me correct the log. I said I had told Leandros and Clarisse to move something when I had no authority to do it.”

“And what did he say?”

“He said the correction mattered. Then he said I had a good mind for seeing patterns.” Ianthe pressed her lips together. “I liked hearing that.”

Thalia kept her hands still. “That’s yours.”

“I know.”

“It doesn’t make you wrong to like it.”

“I know that too.”

“Do you?”

Ianthe looked up. “You don’t have to keep making me prove that I know what I know.”

Thalia breathed out. “You’re right.”

Annabeth turned her face toward the dark window. “Did he ask about us?”

“Not at first. Then he asked if you were getting along.”

“What did you say?” Thalia asked.

“I said that wasn’t mine to report.”

“Did he get angry?”

“No.”

“Did he ask again?”

“No. He said he wasn’t asking for a report.”

“Did you answer?”

“I said I didn’t know what you would say about that.” Ianthe folded the page along its old crease. “He asked whether I liked living with you. I said I liked the girls, but I was tired of not knowing where my bed would be.”

Annabeth nodded once. “That’s fair.”

“I know it’s fair.”

“I’m not saying it isn’t.”

“You both talk like I’m going to ask for permission to have a feeling.”

“I’m listening,” Annabeth said.

“I know.”

The room had no wind in it. Thalia could still sense where the air was waiting, but the windows were shut and the rain had passed.

“He told me he was glad I came to talk to him,” Ianthe said. “He said he was proud of me for being direct.”

“That sounds good,” Thalia said.

“It did.”

“And?”

“And I wondered if he would still be proud if I’d said the room was taking too long because I thought you were lazy.”

Thalia frowned. “Would you?”

“I might have thought it.”

“That isn’t the same as saying it.”

“I know. But I’ve thought worse things about campers who don’t keep their things in order.”

“Like what?”

“Like they’re stupid.”

“You don’t think everyone who loses a coat is stupid.”

“I didn’t say everyone.”

“But you thought it about somebody.”

“I thought it once.”

“Who?”

“I don’t remember.”

“Then you don’t know.”

Ianthe’s eyes flashed. “I said I might have thought it.”

“That’s not the same as knowing what you’d do.”

“I know.”

Thalia stopped. She had entered the argument because the thought bothered her, but she heard how quickly she had turned the conversation into a correction. “I’m not trying to make you defend yourself.”

“You’re asking me to prove I’m not a bad person.”

“No. I’m asking what you mean.”

“That’s what you always do.”

“I don’t always.”

“You do when you think I’m being unfair.”

“Sometimes I should.”

“Sometimes you shouldn’t.”

Thalia looked at the brace beneath her trouser cuff. The leg had not become a reason to turn every disagreement into a lesson about limits. She looked back at Ianthe. “You’re right that I don’t need to question every thought you have. You’re also the one who said you might think someone was stupid because they lost a coat.”

“I can think something unkind and not say it.”

“True.”

“I can also be wrong about someone without it becoming a family decision.”

“True.”

Annabeth watched them both. “And you can change what you do after you notice.”

Ianthe gave a short, sharp laugh. “You have a way of making even a fight sound like a list.”

“That’s because I make lists.”

“Annabeth.”

“I know.”

She rubbed one thumb across the crease of her palm. “I’m not saying you need to explain the thought to me. I’m saying you don’t have to pretend it was fair.”

“I didn’t say it was fair.”

“No.”

“I’m allowed to think something rude.”

“You are.”

“I don’t have to tell you what I think every time.”

“No,” Thalia said. “You don’t.”

Ianthe looked from one sister to the other. “I also don’t have to agree with you about him.”

“No,” Annabeth said. “You don’t.”

“I can like him.”

“Yes.”

“I can want him to come back.”

“Yes.”

“I can also think he was wrong to offer to decide the room.”

“Yes.”

Thalia nodded. “Those can fit together.”

Ianthe’s shoulders eased. “You’re saying that now.”

“I was saying it before. I wasn’t doing a good job of showing it.”

“That’s true.”

Thalia did not ask her to soften the answer. “You can want his attention and still have your own reasons for what you want.”

Ianthe looked down at the page. “He asked whether I’d show him the weather notes another time.”

The phrase fell into the room without a promise attached. Thalia listened for an appointment that was not there.

“Do you want to?” Annabeth asked.

“I want to show him what I’ve noticed.”

“That isn’t the same thing.”

“I know.”

“Do you want to show him next time?”

“If he asks.”

“And if he doesn’t?”

“I don’t know.”

Thalia folded her hands. “You don’t have to decide before he does.”

Ianthe’s mouth shifted at the echo. “You don’t have to say that every time.”

“Right.”

“I’m not angry that he asked. I’m not sure I like that I waited for him to.”

“You don’t have to explain that either,” Thalia said.

“I’m not explaining it. I’m saying it.”

“Okay.”

“You keep making the conversation smaller.”

“I’m trying to leave it yours.”

“That’s not the same as leaving me alone.”

“I know.”

“Sometimes I want you to ask me something.”

“Then I can ask.”

“Not if I have to tell you first.”

Thalia took a moment. “What part of the visit do you want to tell us?”

Ianthe looked at her. The question had not come quickly enough to be perfect. Still, it was different from “Are you all right?” and different from “What did he mean?”

“I want to tell you that he didn’t mention the last visit until I did,” Ianthe said. “I told him I didn’t want him to compare us again. He said he hadn’t compared us.”

“What did you say?”

“I said he did. He said that was how I remembered it.”

Thalia’s throat tightened.

“He didn’t call me wrong,” Ianthe continued. “He didn’t say I should forget it. He said he remembered it differently.”

“Did he apologize?”

“No.”

“Did he say he was sorry?”

“No.”

“Did you want him to?”

“I wanted him to remember what he said.”

“That’s different,” Annabeth said.

“Yes.”

“What did he do after?”

“He was quiet. Then he said he was glad I had told him. I didn’t know whether to believe him.”

“That’s yours to decide,” Thalia said.

“I know.”

“How did it feel?”

Ianthe pressed the folded page against the table. “Good when he listened. Bad when he said he remembered it differently. Good when he looked at me and said he was glad I’d come. Bad when I realized I still wanted to show him the notes.”

“That’s a lot for one conversation.”

“It was a short visit.”

“Short doesn’t mean small,” Annabeth said.

Ianthe looked at her. “Did you want him to listen?”

“Yes.”

“Even now?”

“Yes.”

“Even when he does that?”

“Wanting and trust aren’t the same thing.”

“Do you trust him?”

“I don’t know.”

“You can’t say yes or no?”

“I can. I don’t want to.”

Thalia felt the answer press against the place she kept her own. “I wanted him to come,” she said. “I also wanted to leave as soon as he started talking. Both were true.”

“I don’t remember you saying that.”

“I didn’t.”

“Why not?”

“I was busy being angry.”

“And now?”

“I’m still angry.”

Ianthe nodded, as if the plainness helped. “I think I was angry too.”

“Were you?”

“I was angry that he came back and asked about the room again.”

“Because he made it a problem?”

“Because the room is already a problem. I want it fixed. I’m not going to pretend I like sleeping in the guest room.”

“That’s fair,” Annabeth said.

“I also said it was taking too long.”

“Is it?”

“I don’t know. It feels long.”

“That’s different from the work being late.”

“I know.”

Annabeth folded her hands. “The cot needs a proper repair. The window still needs the longer check. We haven’t decided how the space will work. I want you in Cabin One. I also have to decide what I’m willing to share in my room.”

“I know.”

“I’m not going to let him decide that for me.”

“I told him not to.”

“Thank you.”

Ianthe frowned. “You don’t need to thank me. I don’t want him deciding where I sleep either.”

“That is true.”

Thalia watched the two of them. They were agreeing, but the agreement did not make Ianthe comfortable with the wait or Annabeth eager to share her room. It did not repair the way Zeus had turned a practical question into a judgment.

“What will you tell me if he comes again?” Ianthe asked.

Thalia considered the question. “I’ll tell you what I remember if you ask. I’ll tell you when I don’t know.”

“What if you don’t want to answer?”

“I’ll say that.”

“What if you want to answer later?”

“I’ll say that too.”

Annabeth said, “I’ll do the same. I won’t use what I remember to tell you whether to see him. I won’t answer for Thalia.”

“And you won’t tell me everything?”

“No.”

“Neither will you?” Ianthe asked Thalia.

“No.”

“Good.”

Thalia blinked. “Good?”

“I don’t want to be handed a full list of every time he made you angry. I want to know if something happens that matters to you. I don’t want you to dump every part of your history into my hands because I asked one question.”

“I can do that.”

“Can you?”

“I think so.”

“I want it to be true.”

“So do I.”

Ianthe touched the corner of the paper. “And I don’t want you to pretend you have no opinion just because you’re trying to leave my choice to me.”

Thalia frowned. “I have an opinion.”

“I know. You make that face.”

“What face?”

“The one where you’re trying not to say a thing you think I need to hear.”

Annabeth put a hand to her mouth.

“I’m not laughing,” she said.

“You are,” Thalia replied.

“I’m trying not to.”

Ianthe looked at her own reflection in the window. “I know I’m still new here. I know you had lives before me. That doesn’t mean I want to live like every room is a decision someone else gets to make.”

“It isn’t,” Annabeth said. “The room is not a measure of whether you belong.”

“It still matters.”

“Yes.”

“I want to be able to want it without having to explain why.”

“You can.”

“And I want you to tell me if you think I’m being unfair.”

“Do you want that in the moment?” Thalia asked.

“Sometimes.”

“That’s going to be hard to know.”

“I know.”

“Then tell me in the moment if I’m asking too much.”

“I won’t always notice.”

“Neither will I.”

Annabeth reached for the folded page. “Do you want us to read this?”

“No.”

“All right.”

“Do you want to keep it?” Thalia asked.

“I do.”

“Then keep it.”

“It’s just a list.”

“It’s your list.”

Ianthe slid it into the weather notebook she had brought under her jacket. She did not open the book or show the pages inside. Annabeth had not asked.

Thalia took her cane from where it rested beside the chair. “Do you want to go back to the table?”

“What table?”

“The drawings.”

“I thought you put them away.”

“We did.”

“Then they’re not on the table.”

“They’re in the box.”

“Why?”

“So they don’t get bent.”

Ianthe stood. “I want the drawing you made of the horse.”

“I didn’t draw the horse.”

“The one with the person on the hill.”

“Annabeth drew that.”

“She added the boots.”

“And the hill,” Annabeth said.

“I drew the hill,” Ianthe replied.

“You drew a square hill first.”

“I fixed it.”

“Then it’s yours.”

“I’d like to keep it.”

Annabeth brought the small box from the shelf. The cards had been laid face down, with the labels in a separate stack. She lifted out the one with the hill and handed it to Ianthe.

The pencil lines had smudged at the lower corner. The figure on the slope had a scarf, boots, and a cloud above its head. There was no face to recognize.

“I didn’t finish it,” Ianthe said.

“You can,” Thalia replied.

“I might.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I know.”

Ianthe slipped the card into the back of her notebook, not among the weather notes. “I’ll keep it here.”

Thalia did not ask why.

Annabeth picked up the card of the wooden horse and tucked it beside the corrected coat label. “This one belongs with the description.”

“Why?”

“Because if the boy loses it again, we’ll know what it looks like.”

“He will know what it looks like.”

“Maybe. He said it was his.”

“He recognized it.”

“Then we agree.”

“About the horse.”

“About the horse.”

The disagreement ended where it had started, with an object too small to carry any larger point.

A bell sounded from the dining pavilion. Thalia checked the light beyond the window. It had become late enough to reach supper without rushing.

Ianthe tucked her jacket over her arm. “Are you going to sit with us?”

“I was planning to.”

“Even if I don’t talk about him?”

“Especially if you don’t.”

Ianthe made a face. “That sounded like a correction.”

“It wasn’t.”

“It might have been.”

“Maybe.”

Annabeth put the notebook on the shelf. “We can sit together without finishing the conversation.”

“Can we?”

“We can try.”

“I don’t like trying things when there’s no plan.”

“I know,” Thalia said. “You can decide at the table.”

“That’s not a plan.”

“No.”

Ianthe considered it. “I’ll sit with you.”

“All right.”

They left Cabin One together. Thalia walked with her cane where the path dipped. Annabeth stayed on her right, not close enough to take her arm. Ianthe carried the folded page in her notebook and the hill drawing behind it.

No one asked her whether Zeus had been kind. No one asked if she wanted another visit. They let the next question remain in her hands.
