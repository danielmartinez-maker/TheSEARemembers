## Chapter 217: Nothing Owed to the Sea

Clarisse had been told Poseidon had arrived before she saw him.

Chiron came up the path while she was tying off the end of a fresh grip wrap. He had an envelope in one hand and his cane in the other. He stopped at the edge of the training square, where the packed earth met a strip of winter grass.

“Leandros is at the upper landing,” he said. “Poseidon has arrived and asked to speak with him.”

Clarisse pulled the leather snug. “Did he ask whether Leandros wanted to speak with him?”

“He asked me to pass along that he would like to meet. I have not answered for Leandros.”

“Good.”

Chiron nodded toward the field. “Would you like me to let him know?”

“Leandros can decide for himself.”

“He can.”

The envelope shifted in Chiron’s hand. A blue seal pressed into the fold caught the weak light. Clarisse recognized the shape before she could stop herself from looking at it. She had not seen it since the last time Poseidon came to camp. She remembered a barbecue, the smell of smoke, and a sentence about asking the next morning whether she wanted him at training. The morning had come. He had gone.

She tightened the leather until it stopped slipping.

“Are you all right?” Chiron asked.

“I’m wrapping a spear.”

“I can see that.”

“Then you know what I’m doing.”

He let the answer sit between them. “I’ll tell Leandros he may meet Poseidon, or refuse, or ask for another time. The choice is his.”

“Tell him that.”

“I will.”

Chiron went on toward the upper path. Clarisse checked the wrap once with her thumb and slid the spear into its rack. Nothing about the announcement had changed the morning’s work. Still, the training square seemed to have acquired a line through it, running from the gravel to the shore, from one child of Poseidon toward another.

She took the second spear down.

Leandros came through the gate before she finished checking its balance. He had his tide notebook tucked under one arm and the dark blue scarf he wore on windy days looped twice at his neck. The notebook’s corners had softened from use. He glanced at the spear in her hand, then at Chiron’s envelope.

“Father’s here.”

“Chiron said he asked to speak with you.”

Leandros’s eyes went to the shore again, though the landing was hidden behind the cabins. “He knows I’m here?”

“Chiron says he asked for you.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“I don’t know how he knew.”

Leandros pressed the notebook against his side. “Did he ask about you?”

“No.”

The answer came too fast. Clarisse saw him notice. His shoulders lifted slightly, then settled back into place.

“Are you going?” she asked.

“He came to see me.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“Yes.” He looked down at the notebook. “I’m going.”

“You can go without making it a test of whether you belong here.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were thinking about it.”

“You don’t know what I was thinking.”

“No,” she said. “I don’t.”

He studied her, waiting for more. Clarisse put the spear back. The answer was true, and she did not need to make it softer.

Leandros’s thumb moved over the notebook’s frayed spine. “Do you want to come?”

“No.”

“Because you’re angry?”

“Because he asked to talk to you.”

“That’s not why you’re angry.”

“Probably not.”

He looked as if he might push. Then the notebook moved under his arm, and he seemed to decide it had somewhere else to be.

“Chiron said I can meet him alone,” he said.

“That sounds like a choice.”

“I’m not asking you to approve it.”

“I didn’t say you were.”

“Good.”

The word was clipped. He turned before she could answer and headed up the path.

Clarisse remained beside the spear rack. The wrapped grip was even. She tested the balance once and set it down. From the hill, a small flash of blue moved between the pines. The air carried salt into the field. No wave rose over the shore. No branch bent. The day remained ordinary in every way that did not matter.

Silena came from the cabin with a basket on her hip and a folded dish towel over her arm. She had been sorting root vegetables for the kitchen; the basket held potatoes, carrots, and two onions with green shoots starting at their tops.

“Was that him?” she asked.

“Poseidon?”

“Leandros.”

“He’s going to meet him.”

Silena set the basket on the low bench. “Did Leandros tell you, or did Chiron?”

“Both.”

“You okay?”

Clarisse reached for the towel and put it beneath the basket so the damp wicker would not mark the wood. “I don’t know.”

“Do you want to talk?”

“Not right now.”

“Okay.”

Silena started to lift the basket again.

“Leave it there,” Clarisse said. “You’ll need both hands when you sort them.”

“I can carry a basket.”

“I know. The bench is dry.”

Silena looked at her, then at the bench. “All right.”

They arranged the vegetables without discussing Poseidon. Silena picked out the soft potatoes and set them in a separate bowl. Clarisse removed the onions’ loose skins. The chore was easy enough to do without turning it into a conversation. The brown paper beneath the basket rustled when they moved the vegetables across it.

A moment later, Amphitrite came along the path from the Big House. She wore a charcoal coat over a sea-green dress, her hair braided at the nape of her neck. She carried no basket or wrapped gift. At the edge of the porch, she stopped.

“Would you mind if I sat with you?” she asked.

Clarisse set an onion down. “We’re sorting these.”

“I can sit without helping.”

“Then sit.”

Amphitrite took the empty end of the bench. She did not ask what the vegetables were for. She did not reach for the basket. Silena gave her a brief nod, and Amphitrite returned it.

“You can take the soft potatoes to the kitchen when you’re done,” Clarisse said to Silena. “They’ll be good for the stew.”

“I know.”

“Just making sure.”

Silena pushed one into the bowl. “I know what a soft potato is.”

“I didn’t say you didn’t.”

“You looked like you were about to.”

“I was checking.”

“You were about to check.”

Clarisse snorted. “Fine.”

Amphitrite watched them work with her hands folded loosely in her lap. After a while she asked, “Would you like me to come back later?”

“Why?”

“To see you, if you want that. I can leave now, or stay while you finish.”

Clarisse placed another onion beside the others. “You’re already here.”

“Yes.”

“You can stay until we take this in.”

“Thank you.”

The answer was simple enough. It still made the air feel different. Clarisse reached for the next onion.

The last few days had been colder. The soil in the gardens had gone stiff at the edges, and the training field held frost until the sun crossed the roofline. The camp had started moving its winter supplies under cover. Everyone was busy with small things: lists, cloth, food, wood. Amphitrite had come to camp more than once in the past weeks. She had sat with Silena while she painted and eaten at their table. She had invited Clarisse to walk, heard no, and let the no stand. She had been in the same room without turning Clarisse’s silence into another request.

Those were not the same as knowing one another. They were, Clarisse thought, enough to recognize the sound of the other person’s footsteps.

Silena tied the paper around the soft potatoes and stood. “I’ll take these to the kitchen.”

“Want me to come?” Clarisse asked.

“No. I can do it.”

“Okay.”

Silena looked briefly at Amphitrite, then at Clarisse. She did not ask either of them to explain the morning. “I’ll be back before the meal.”

She carried the bowl inside.

Amphitrite remained on the bench. The winter light lay across her coat, paling the green of her sleeve.

“Chiron said Poseidon is here,” Clarisse said.

“He is.”

“Did you know he was coming?”

“I heard this morning. I came to see you. I did not know what you would want from the day.”

“What did you think I’d want?”

“I didn’t decide.”

Clarisse pulled another onion toward her. “You can answer for yourself, at least.”

“I can.” Amphitrite’s fingers rested on her knee. “I thought you might want company. I also thought you might not. I wanted to ask.”

Clarisse pared away the loose skin. The knife caught at one green shoot, and she trimmed it without cutting into the onion. “You could have asked before you came.”

“I could have. Chiron passed along a question yesterday, and I sent an answer this morning. I should have asked you directly instead of assuming that was enough.”

Clarisse looked at her. “You didn’t assume I’d say yes.”

“No. But I let someone else carry the question.”

“That’s different.”

“It is.”

Clarisse set the knife down. “You can stay.”

Amphitrite nodded once.

The shore path remained quiet. No one called from the upper landing. Clarisse did not ask how the meeting was going, and Amphitrite did not suggest she should. They finished the onions and waited for Silena to return.

When she did, she brought an empty bowl and two cups of water. She handed one to Clarisse. “Chiron said there’s a spot by the pavilion where the path is dry. He thinks we can take the roots through there without the basket tipping.”

“He said that?”

“He said, ‘If you are going that way, you may find the boards drier by the north post.’”

“That sounds like him.”

“It does.”

Clarisse took the cup. The rim was cold against her palm. She drank, then set it down beside the basket.

“Let’s take the carrots and potatoes through the pavilion,” she said. “Leave the onions for dinner.”

Silena nodded. “I’ll carry the bowl.”

“I’ll take the basket.”

Amphitrite rose but waited. Clarisse glanced at her.

“Do you want to come?”

“If you do.”

“Fine. Walk with us.”

They carried the root vegetables toward the pavilion, taking the dry stone path and leaving the lower cut alone. None of them went toward the shore.

---

The pavilion was cool inside. A few campers had already taken the benches near the serving counter, and someone had left a bowl of apples beside the hearth. Annabeth stood at the far table with a page of measurements under one hand. Percy was trying to stack a crate of empty jars on the edge of the counter.

“Is that meant to stay there?” Clarisse asked.

“It’s balanced,” Percy said.

“It’s leaning.”

“It’s leaning in a balanced way.”

Annabeth crossed the room and pushed the crate two inches toward the wall. It stopped wobbling. Percy watched her hand, then the crate.

“That was the part I was about to do.”

“Then we both did it.”

“You did it before I did.”

“You were still deciding whether the lean was part of the balance.”

“I had a theory.”

“You had a crate.”

Clarisse set the basket down by the dry end of the table. “If that falls, the jars are going to break.”

“Nothing fell,” Percy said.

“Because she moved it,” Clarisse replied.

Annabeth marked something on her paper. “We’re putting the heavy pantry jars on the lower shelf. The empty ones can wait until Chiron brings the new box.”

“Where is the box?” Silena asked.

“Still at the Big House. I’m not carrying it down.”

“Good,” Clarisse said.

Percy gave her a look. “You’re allowed to carry boxes.”

“I’m allowed to choose not to.”

Annabeth’s pencil paused. “She’s right.”

“I know she’s right.” Percy picked up an apple from the bowl. “I was making a point.”

“What point?”

“That I wasn’t going to tell her to do it.”

“You just did,” Clarisse said.

“I said she was allowed.”

“You were waiting for me to prove you were right.”

Percy took a bite. “I was waiting for the apple.”

Annabeth tried not to smile and failed. Percy nudged the bowl toward her. She chose one of the smaller apples and set it beside the measurements before she took a bite.

The little exchange did not ask Clarisse to join it. She felt relieved by that. There were people who knew how to let a conversation pass without making her part of the bridge.

Silena shifted the vegetable bowl onto the shelf. “Chiron said Poseidon and Leandros are at the upper landing. Beckendorf went with them to keep the path clear.”

“Did he ask?” Clarisse asked.

“Beckendorf?”

“Poseidon.”

“I don’t know.”

Silena wiped a smear of dirt from the rim. “Chiron said they’re on the marked path.”

“The lower cut is still closed.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

Annabeth gathered the measurements. “The landing has a rail. It’s the safest place to stand and watch water from here.”

“Watching water is not the same as checking the ground,” Clarisse said.

“I know that too.”

Clarisse looked at her. “You’ve learned to say it before I do.”

“It saves time.”

Percy leaned closer to the crate and checked its position. “It also saves jars.”

The basket was light enough to lift. Clarisse took the vegetables to the pantry, where Silena moved the root bowl to the lower shelf and Clarisse set the onions beside the cooking pot. Amphitrite followed them only as far as the doorway.

“I can go,” she said.

Clarisse glanced over her shoulder. “You can stay.”

“I can stay. I also want to know whether you would like me to.”

Clarisse took a cloth from the hook. “You can stay until I finish setting the shelf.”

Amphitrite accepted the limit with a nod. She waited in the doorway, then asked Silena if she could pass her the empty bowl. Silena did. Nothing else had to be arranged.

The pantry smelled of apple skins and dry grain. Clarisse wiped a damp ring from the shelf. The line of jars stood in the order Annabeth had set, heavy ones low, empty ones high.

“When you were at the pavilion,” Amphitrite said, “you chose to come with me.”

“I asked you to walk with us.”

“Yes.”

“That’s different from asking you to follow me everywhere.”

“I know.”

Clarisse glanced at her. “You say that like I’m testing you.”

“I’m trying not to answer the question you didn’t ask.”

“What question?”

“Whether you are making a permanent decision about me.”

Clarisse set the cloth down. “I’m not.”

“All right.”

“Sometimes I want to see you. Sometimes I don’t. You don’t need to turn it into something else.”

“I won’t.”

“You might.”

“I might,” Amphitrite said. “If I do, you can tell me.”

Clarisse pushed the pantry door closed with her shoulder. “I can. I don’t want you to need a special warning every time.”

“You shouldn’t have to give one.”

“Good.”

They came back into the pavilion. Percy had moved the crate to the wall. Annabeth had circled a note on the paper and was explaining the lower shelf spacing to Chiron. No one asked Clarisse whether she wanted to see Poseidon. No one said her name as if it were the next step in a plan.

She sat at the end of the table and checked the list of winter kitchen supplies. It needed two more sacks of flour and a note to keep the potatoes away from the damp wall. She wrote both down. The task remained ordinary.

Through the open side of the pavilion, the upper landing came into view between the pines. Leandros and Poseidon stood beside the rail. Beckendorf was a few paces back, leaning on one shoulder. The shoreline below them held a narrow band of gray water. Poseidon said something. Leandros answered with his notebook open between them.

The god tipped his head toward the water.

Leandros pointed at a place near the outer rocks. His hand moved once, then stopped. Poseidon said something else, and Leandros looked down to mark it in the notebook. He did not look back toward camp.

Clarisse watched them longer than she meant to. It was easy to stand beside someone when both people were looking at the same thing. It was harder when you had already spent years waiting for the other person to look at you.

“What are you thinking?” Silena asked.

“That he came.”

“Poseidon?”

“Yes.”

Silena looked toward the landing. “Leandros wanted him to.”

“I know.”

“Do you wish he hadn’t?”

Clarisse rubbed the side of her thumb where the leather wrap had left a red line. “I wish there were a version where he came and didn’t make it feel like everyone else disappeared.”

Silena did not answer right away. The kitchen bell sounded once in the distance, signaling that the midday meal would be ready in half an hour.

“I don’t think wanting your brother to have a father takes anything from you,” she said.

“It shouldn’t.”

“It can still hurt to watch.”

Clarisse nodded. “You have the same problem?”

“Not the same one.” Silena looked down at the list. “He can be pleased to have found Leandros. That doesn’t mean he remembers what I had to ask for. Or that I have to make everyone comfortable because the rest of us are supposed to be family.”

“You don’t.”

“I know.” Silena placed the list beside the flour tin. “I wanted to say it out loud.”

Clarisse turned the page. “Then it’s said.”

A shadow crossed the pavilion entrance. Leandros came back down the path first, the notebook closed against his chest. Poseidon followed at an easier pace, with Beckendorf between them. Leandros’s face was brighter than it had been that morning. He did not quite smile; he looked as if he was holding the expression in place until he could decide whether it was safe to let it show.

Poseidon’s hair was dark with water at the ends. His shirt was plain gray beneath an open coat. A line of salt had dried along one sleeve.

“Your notes were good,” he told Leandros. “You saw the shift before I did.”

Leandros glanced at the notebook. “The line on the water was angled. It didn’t match the shore wind.”

“That’s what I noticed after you pointed it out.”

“You could see it from the rail.”

“I could.”

Leandros’s chin lifted slightly. Clarisse saw the pride come through before he could hide it.

Poseidon looked from the notebook to the boy. “You have a good eye.”

“Thank you.”

“I’d like to see the first set of readings you made.”

“They’re in the cabin.”

“Would you bring them next time?”

Leandros hesitated. “If there is a next time.”

“I hope there will be.”

It was not a date. It was not a promise. Leandros still held on to it with both hands.

Poseidon stepped into the pavilion. His gaze touched Clarisse, then Silena, then Percy and Annabeth. “Hello.”

“Hi,” Clarisse said.

“Hello,” Silena answered.

His smile was warm, but it did not stay long enough to settle anywhere. “I’m glad you’re all here.”

Clarisse looked at the salt on his sleeve. “You asked to see Leandros.”

“I did.”

“Then you saw him.”

“I did.”

There was a moment when he could have asked how she was doing, or asked about her training, or remembered that last time he had said he would ask. He looked back at Leandros instead.

“Would you show me where Cabin Three is?” he asked.

Leandros glanced at Clarisse. “It’s down the path.”

“I know,” Clarisse said. “I can point you there.”

Poseidon’s attention shifted toward her again, surprised by the offer. “If that’s all right.”

“I’m not offering to give you a tour.”

“Understood.”

Leandros moved his notebook beneath his arm. “I can show you.”

“You can,” Clarisse said.

He looked at her as if her permission had been withheld and then returned. “I know.”

“You asked the question.”

“I didn’t.”

“You looked at me.”

“I was checking whether you wanted me to say yes.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“It was to me.”

Clarisse let that answer stand. He led Poseidon toward the cabin. The god followed, this time not ahead of him.

Silena came to stand beside Clarisse. “Do you want to go?”

“No.”

“Do you want me to?”

“No.”

“All right.”

Silena turned back to the list. They could still finish the kitchen shelf without watching Leandros receive his father.

---

Cabin Three was warmer than the path, though the door still let in a narrow seam of cold. Percy held it open as Poseidon and Leandros stepped inside. Annabeth followed with Silena and Clarisse, while Amphitrite remained at the threshold until Clarisse turned and said, “You can come in.”

“Thank you,” Amphitrite said.

Poseidon looked at the cabin as if he expected the walls to tell him how long he had been away. He saw the bunk frames, the repaired shelf, the thick blanket pinned across the window. His gaze stopped on the window cord.

“What happened there?” he asked.

“It was loose,” Clarisse said.

“When?”

“A while ago.”

“Was anyone hurt?”

“No.”

He nodded, but his attention had already moved toward the practice spears. “You’re still training with these?”

“When I’m not working.”

“Do you use the heavier ones?”

“When I want to.”

“Can I see the balance?”

Clarisse held his gaze. “You can ask.”

The correction landed. Poseidon drew his hands back. “Would you show me?”

“No.”

“Okay.”

He did not ask again. Leandros watched the exchange with interest, his eyes moving from the spear rack to Clarisse and back.

“Do you want to see the current notes?” he asked Poseidon.

“I do.”

Leandros laid the notebook on the table and opened it to two entries. One had a time written in the corner and a line describing the point where the water turned against the shoreline. The other was a brief note from another day, with a different wind direction and a different path of the current.

Poseidon leaned closer. “You went to the lower cut?”

“No.” Leandros pointed at the small sketch of the landing. “I took both readings from the marked path. The lower bank is still soft.”

“Good.”

“Clarisse told me it was soft.”

“It was a fair correction.”

“I know.”

Clarisse leaned her shoulder against the doorframe. “You didn’t know until she told you.”

Leandros looked up. “I know now.”

“You keep saying that.”

“Because it’s true.”

Poseidon’s finger stopped near the page. “How long have you been recording this?”

“Since the storm.”

“What made you start?”

“Everyone was deciding which route to use without checking the water.”

“Everyone?”

“Annabeth and Beckendorf were checking the land. I wanted to know if the current could support the path.”

“Water doesn’t support a path,” Clarisse said.

“It can make one safer.”

“Or make someone think it’s safe when the ground isn’t.”

“I wrote that down.”

“You wrote it down after we argued.”

“Because you were right.”

“I wasn’t asking for credit.”

“Then why do you keep making sure I know?”

Clarisse pushed away from the doorframe. “Because this is your father’s first time looking at your work. I don’t want him thinking you did it alone.”

The sentence made Leandros’s face close. Poseidon straightened.

“I didn’t think that,” he said.

“You just got here.”

“I heard Clarisse tell you about the lower cut.”

“You heard her say one thing.”

“Clarisse,” Silena said, not sharply.

Clarisse looked at her sister. Silena’s voice stayed low. The words were not a request to stop being angry; they were a reminder that she had said her part.

Poseidon shut the notebook, carefully enough not to bend the pages. “I did ask to see Leandros. I wanted to know him. That doesn’t mean I think the rest of you are unimportant.”

“No,” Clarisse said. “It just means you asked for him.”

“I can see that.”

“Can you?”

His expression changed. The question had not been gentle. He did not turn it into a reprimand.

“Not fully,” he said.

The blunt answer unsettled her more than an excuse would have. Clarisse folded her arms.

Silena moved to the table and set the meal basket down. “We’re eating here. There’s room if you want to join us.”

Poseidon looked at her. “I’d like to.”

“We’re eating,” she said. “I’m not making a separate meal.”

“I wasn’t asking you to.”

“You asked me to make the menu board last time. Before that, you asked me to make sure the table felt easy for everyone.”

“I did?”

“You said the menu would help. Then you asked me to keep the conversation from getting sharp.”

Poseidon remembered. He had not intended to turn a request into a job. That was not the same as not having done it.

“I shouldn’t have asked you to manage the room,” he said.

Silena nodded. “I heard that.”

She did not say it was all right. Poseidon did not ask her to.

Percy took bowls down from the shelf. Annabeth passed them across the table, then stopped when she reached Clarisse.

“Do you want one?”

“Yes.”

Annabeth gave her a bowl. Percy carried the stew over from the small kitchen and set it where it could be reached from either end. He and Annabeth exchanged a quick glance over the steam. She pulled her chair closer to his; he nudged the serving spoon toward her before she reached for it. The ease between them was as familiar as the cabin, untouched by the heavy conversation.

Leandros sat beside Poseidon. Clarisse took the opposite end. Silena sat between Clarisse and Percy, leaving an open chair on her other side. Amphitrite chose it only after Silena moved the basket.

The stew was thicker than it looked. The potatoes had started to break down at the edges, and there was enough rosemary to scent the whole cabin. Silena had not cooked it alone; Chiron and two younger campers had helped. No one made a speech about the meal.

Poseidon asked Leandros where he had learned to read currents. Leandros answered with the name of the marina where he had sailed with his mother and the month Poseidon had left the compass. He explained that the compass pointed toward deep water, not north, and that he had used it only when he already knew where the boat was.

“You still have it?” Poseidon asked.

“At my mother’s house.”

“Does she use it?”

“No.”

“Did she keep it?”

“She put it in a drawer.”

“Why?”

“She said it made the kitchen feel like the sea was coming through the walls.”

Poseidon’s hand rested by his bowl. “I can understand that.”

Leandros’s eyes narrowed, not in anger but concentration. “Can you?”

“I can imagine it.”

“That isn’t the same.”

“No,” Poseidon said. “It isn’t.”

Clarisse kept her eyes on her soup. She did not know what it was like to receive a compass that pointed to deep water. She knew what it was like to wait for a father who could command the sea to come back and still not arrive at the appointed hour.

Poseidon looked at Leandros again. “You learned to sail without me.”

“My mother taught me most things.”

“You did well.”

Leandros’s shoulders eased. The praise hit exactly where he had hoped it would. He did not hide it this time.

“Thanks,” he said.

“Leandros has been learning the camp routines,” Percy added. “He helped keep the current notes separate from the route checks.”

“Did he?” Poseidon said.

Annabeth gave Percy a look. “He wrote his own observations. The rest of us checked the walking route.”

Leandros dipped his spoon into the stew. “That’s what I said.”

“You said everyone was deciding without checking the water,” Clarisse said.

“I didn’t say no one checked the land.”

“You said it like we didn’t know what we were doing.”

“I said I had a different skill.”

“After you said we should have asked for your help before we started.”

“Because I was right about the current.”

“You were wrong about the bank.”

“I was right about the water.”

“Those are two different facts,” Annabeth said.

Leandros looked at her. “I know.”

“Then don’t fold them together when you’re defending yourself.”

He pressed his lips together. “I didn’t.”

“You did,” Clarisse said.

Poseidon looked down the table. “Let him finish.”

Clarisse’s jaw tightened. “He was finishing.”

“I meant—”

“I know what you meant.”

Poseidon lowered his spoon. “I’m not trying to tell you how to speak to each other.”

“You just did.”

He stopped. For a second, irritation crossed his face, small but real. He set the spoon down more quietly than he had to.

“Yes,” he said. “I did.”

Leandros looked between them. “I can speak for myself.”

“Then do it,” Clarisse replied.

He leaned toward the table. “I’m proud of the work I did. The current reading was useful. Clarisse was right about the bank. I can be proud of one thing without pretending I was right about the other.”

Clarisse did not answer.

Poseidon’s attention stayed on him. “That’s a fair distinction.”

It was not a great speech. It did not settle the table. Leandros returned to his soup.

The quiet that followed felt different from the first one. It was not filled with an apology that everyone else had to accept. Percy asked Annabeth whether the marked path would be dry enough to bring the pantry crate in the morning. She said it would need another check after the night frost. Percy said they could look together. She replied that he could look if she was still asleep. He smiled and pushed the bread basket toward her.

They were close enough to have small disagreements without making the rest of them responsible for the outcome.

Clarisse ate three spoonfuls before Poseidon spoke again.

“I said I’d ask you the next morning,” he said. “About training.”

The spoon stopped near Clarisse’s mouth.

Silena’s fingers tightened around her cup. Leandros looked down at the open notebook beside him.

“You did,” Clarisse said.

“I didn’t come back.”

“No.”

“I told myself I would ask later.”

“You didn’t.”

“No.”

“Why bring it up now?”

“Because I remembered it.”

“You remembered it before you said it just now.”

Poseidon looked at the table. “Yes.”

“Did you expect me to be glad?”

“No.” He lifted his head. “I hoped saying it plainly would be better than acting like I forgot.”

“It doesn’t make it better.”

“I know.”

“Do you?” Clarisse set down her spoon. “You ask whether I want you to watch me train. You don’t ask what training looks like now. You don’t ask what I’m doing with my life. You show up and ask whether I want you in the one place where I had to keep pretending I didn’t care that you weren’t there.”

Poseidon’s shoulders drew back. “I didn’t know you were still—”

“Don’t.”

He stopped.

“Don’t make the hurt sound like new information you can fix by hearing it once.”

His mouth closed. He glanced at Amphitrite, then away.

Clarisse saw the glance. “She doesn’t have to answer for me.”

“I didn’t ask her to.”

“You looked at her.”

“I was—”

“Looking for a way out?”

“No.” Poseidon paused. “Looking for help, maybe. I’m used to her knowing what to say.”

“Then you can get used to not having that.”

He nodded, slowly. “All right.”

Amphitrite did not move. She held her cup in both hands and said nothing.

Silena spoke after a while. “When you ask me to help him settle, I hear that as a job. When you come back and praise what he can do, I hear it beside everything you didn’t ask me about. That doesn’t mean he shouldn’t have the attention. It means you don’t get to give him attention and call the rest of us finished.”

“I don’t think you’re finished,” Poseidon said.

“What do you think, then?”

He seemed to search for an answer that was not a defense. “I think you have lives I didn’t see. I think I know some pieces. I don’t know what you need from me now.”

“You could ask,” Clarisse said.

“I am asking.”

“Not just today.”

“I can’t promise I’ll know how to do that every time.”

“Then don’t promise every time.”

Poseidon’s expression held both relief and disappointment. “I won’t.”

Clarisse picked up her spoon again. Nothing in her chest loosened. That was all right. She had spoken the thing as she remembered it. He had heard it, and neither result gave her a new duty.

Leandros looked at the notebook. “I wanted him to see my work.”

Clarisse glanced at him. “I know.”

“I didn’t ask him to ignore you.”

“You didn’t have to.”

He bristled. “You’re acting like I did something wrong by wanting him here.”

“I’m not.”

“Then what are you doing?”

“Being here while he sees you.”

“That isn’t fair.”

“Maybe not.”

“Maybe you’re angry at him and you’re taking it out on me.”

“Maybe you want me to say that so you don’t have to hear the rest.”

Leandros pushed his bowl away half an inch. “You always make it about whether I’ve earned something.”

“Because you keep asking what people owe you.”

“I asked for a chance.”

“You asked to lead the cabin on your first morning.”

“I was trying to help.”

“You were trying to be in charge.”

“I was trying not to be the extra person.”

Clarisse heard the younger edge beneath his anger. It did not make the accusation fair. It made it understandable.

“You aren’t extra,” she said. “You are new.”

“That sounds like the same thing.”

“It isn’t.”

“Easy for you to say.”

“Yes,” she said. “It is.”

He stared at her, perhaps waiting for her to deny the advantage. She did not.

Poseidon turned to Leandros. “You don’t have to prove you belong by being useful to me.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Leandros looked at him. “I want you to think I’m good at something.”

“I do.”

“That’s not what I mean.”

Poseidon fell silent. Clarisse recognized that silence: the gap between what a parent meant to offer and what a child had actually asked for.

Leandros stood, took his notebook, and walked to the window. He did not leave. He looked out toward the shore, where nothing could be seen from the cabin.

Clarisse brought her bowl to the sink. Silena followed after waiting long enough not to make the movement look like a rescue. Percy reached for Annabeth’s plate. She moved it out of his hand.

“I can carry it,” she said.

“I know.”

“Then let me.”

He let go. His hand brushed hers before it fell to his side.

The room returned to the dishes and the meal. No one followed Leandros to the window.

---

Poseidon waited until Leandros came back from the window.

“I should go,” he said.

Leandros closed the notebook. “You just got here.”

“I have other places I’m expected.”

“By who?”

“People who need me to answer something.”

Leandros pressed his thumb against the notebook’s corner. “Will you come back?”

“I want to.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“No,” Poseidon said. “I don’t know when I can.”

The answer was careful, and it still left a space. Leandros did not look away from him.

“Would you write?” he asked.

“If you want me to.”

“I’d read it.”

Poseidon nodded. “Then I’ll ask Chiron where to send one.”

“Ask me. I know where I live.”

“I will.”

It was not a date. It was a next step small enough to be possible. Leandros seemed to keep it without deciding what it meant.

Poseidon turned toward the others. “Thank you for letting me visit.”

“Chiron decides whether the path is open,” Clarisse said. “We decide whether to talk.”

“I understand.”

“You understand the words.”

Poseidon took a breath. “Yes.”

He looked as if there were another answer he wanted to give, a better one than the truth he had. Clarisse was glad he left it unsaid.

At the door he paused beside her. “Would you like me to ask you about training now?”

“No.”

“All right.”

“You don’t have to ask because you remembered the promise.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

He nodded once. “I’ll try not to turn remembering into an expectation.”

Clarisse’s shoulders remained high. “You can go.”

Poseidon went. The door fell shut behind him, catching softly at the latch before it settled.

Leandros stayed near the window. He turned the notebook over in his hands.

“Did you want me to say no?” he asked Clarisse.

“No.”

“Were you hoping he would ask about you?”

“Yes.”

He blinked at the answer. “You could have told him.”

“I did.”

“You told him after he came.”

“I told him the last time. He said he would ask me the next morning. He didn’t.”

“But he couldn’t remember everything.”

“No.” Clarisse gathered the empty bowls. “That’s part of it.”

Leandros looked toward the door. “He listened to me.”

“I heard.”

“He asked me about the compass.”

“You showed him your notes.”

“I know.”

“I’m not saying you did anything wrong.”

“It sounds like you are.”

“Then listen better.”

He gave a short, humorless laugh. “You can’t let one thing be good without measuring it.”

“I can. I just don’t think what was good to you is the only thing that happened.”

The reply made him pause. He set the notebook on the table and turned its cover toward himself.

“I thought you were mad because he came to me,” he said.

“I’m mad because he came to you and didn’t think to ask the rest of us anything.”

“He said he didn’t mean to ignore you.”

“I believe him.”

“You do?”

“I believe he didn’t plan to hurt me. That doesn’t mean he didn’t.”

Leandros’s fingers curled around the notebook. “I wanted to be the one he came to.”

“I know.”

“I don’t want you to feel like you have to give that up.”

“I don’t.”

He watched her. “You have him already.”

Clarisse stopped at the sink. “No.”

“You and Percy—”

“Percy is my brother. He isn’t Poseidon.”

“I know that.”

“Then don’t use him as proof that I got enough.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You were.”

The disagreement had grown teeth. Leandros’s face flushed.

“I don’t know what you want me to say,” he said.

“I didn’t ask you to say anything.”

“You said I keep asking what people owe me.”

“You do.”

“I’m trying to figure out where I fit.”

“I know.”

“You keep acting like wanting something is a crime.”

“No. I act like wanting something doesn’t make it yours.”

He looked down. “I thought if I could show him something, he would want to come back.”

“Maybe he will.”

“Maybe he won’t.”

“Yes.”

“That doesn’t help.”

“I’m not trying to help.”

The words came out too hard. Clarisse set the bowls down.

“I mean I can’t make that part easier,” she said. “I can tell you what I know. I know what he promised me and what he didn’t do. I know he came to see you today. You get to decide what that means for you.”

“You don’t get to decide either.”

“I’m not.”

He pressed his thumb against the notebook’s worn edge. “I told him you’d said the bank was soft. I didn’t tell him you were right before I was.”

“You did tell him.”

“After.”

“After I had to say it.”

“I know.”

“Do you?” Clarisse asked. “You corrected yourself at lunch. Then you made it sound like I was trying to take your moment away.”

Leandros’s jaw moved. “I said you were taking it out on me.”

“You did.”

“I shouldn’t have.”

Clarisse looked at him. The apology did not repair the afternoon or make his first interpretation disappear. It was still a sentence he had chosen to say.

“No,” she said. “You shouldn’t have.”

He waited, then shifted his weight. “I’m sorry I said it.”

“I heard you.”

“Are you still mad?”

“Yes.”

He nodded. “Okay.”

“You don’t have to keep asking.”

“I’m not asking whether you’re done.”

“You just did.”

“I wanted to know if the apology changed anything.”

“It changed the fact that you apologized.”

His eyes dropped to the notebook. He could have argued that it should count for more. He did not.

“Fine,” he said.

“Not fine. True.”

He picked up the notebook and slid it into the front pocket of his jacket. “I need to put this away.”

“Then put it away.”

Leandros walked toward the door. This time he did not ask Clarisse to follow. She let him go. Silena brought the last bowls to the sink, where they washed them without talking.

Amphitrite stayed at the table. She did not ask Clarisse what she felt about the apology, or whether she wished Poseidon had stayed. She waited while Clarisse dried her hands.

“Do you want me to go?” Amphitrite asked.

“No.”

“Would you like to sit?”

“I already did.”

“Would you like to stay here with me?”

Clarisse looked at the chair opposite. “For a while.”

Amphitrite sat. Clarisse remained standing, then dragged out the chair beside her and turned it around so she could lean her arms on the back.

“Are you going to tell me I should be glad for him?” she asked.

“No.”

“Are you going to tell him he owes me an apology?”

“No.”

“Are you going to talk to Poseidon about what I said?”

“Not unless you ask me to.”

Clarisse searched Amphitrite’s face. “You’re careful.”

“I have learned that I can be careless when I think I know what someone needs.”

“Have you always been like that?”

“No. Sometimes I was worse.”

The directness surprised her. Amphitrite’s gaze stayed steady, but there was a tiredness under it that Clarisse had not noticed at lunch.

“Were you a bad mother?” Clarisse asked.

Amphitrite’s hands tightened around the cup. “I was not the mother you needed every time you needed one. That is not the same as being bad in every way, and it is not a reason you owe me anything.”

“Did you leave Percy alone?”

“I let him believe I could be reached more easily than I was. Sometimes I was busy with duties. Sometimes I was impatient. Sometimes I believed a letter would be enough because I had sent one.”

“Was it?”

“Not always.”

“Did you know that?”

“I knew some of it. I learned more from him later.”

Clarisse rested her chin on her forearms. “What did you change?”

“I began asking whether he wanted a visit before I arrived. I tried to listen without using his answer as proof that everything was mended.”

“Did he tell you that?”

“He told me he needed me to ask. He also told me there were things I had not seen.”

“Do you think that makes him trust you?”

“I don’t know.”

“Does that bother you?”

“Yes.”

“Do you try to make him reassure you?”

Amphitrite exhaled slowly. “I have wanted to. I have tried not to.”

Clarisse tilted her head. “That sounds difficult.”

“It is.”

“Do you think it’s hard because you’re a queen?”

“No. I think it’s hard because I want to be loved in the way I want to be loved. That can make me impatient with the way someone else is trying to care for me.”

Clarisse looked toward the window. The sun had moved behind a cloud, and the room’s light had gone gray. She knew what it was to get impatient when someone did not respond to the kind of help she had decided to offer. She knew that was not the same as Poseidon leaving. It was not the same as Amphitrite’s absences. She did not need to pretend it was.

“Do you want a piece of bread?” she asked.

“If there is some.”

“There’s always some.”

“Then yes.”

Clarisse pushed the basket across the table. Amphitrite broke a piece in half and returned the larger half to Clarisse.

“That’s not how sharing works,” Clarisse said.

“I thought you liked the smaller piece.”

“I do.”

“Then it is how sharing works.”

Clarisse ate. The bread had gone cool. It was still soft in the middle.

They sat together until Silena came back from the kitchen and asked if Clarisse would help check the stew pot. Clarisse stood. Amphitrite stayed at the table while she and Silena went to the stove. The visit did not turn into an agreement or a promise. It did not have to.

By the time the meal ended, Poseidon had left camp. Leandros had taken his notebook back to the cabin and did not come to supper. Clarisse did not ask where he was. She did not need to know every place he went.

The next morning, the water at the landing was calm. The wind shifted around noon, and the flag over the dining pavilion changed direction twice. The lower cut stayed closed. No one tested the damp bank. The two rope coils remained on their spacers, and the untested rack loop stayed under cover.

The days after moved at their own speed. Clarisse trained in the morning and worked in the afternoons. Leandros kept to his notes when he was asked; once, he corrected the time without being prompted. Another time, he told a younger camper that the wind would not move the wheelbarrow, then had to help retrieve it when a gust caught its loose canvas cover. He complained about the chore before he did it. Clarisse heard him use the word “obvious” twice and watched a girl his age stop answering him.

The third time, Ianthe corrected his estimate of a gust from the upper path. Leandros said he had seen the flag first. Ianthe said she had read the same flag and disagreed. He stared at her until she asked whether he wanted the readings or an argument. He took the readings. The exchange ended there.

Clarisse did not decide what any of those moments meant.

Amphitrite visited once more after Poseidon’s departure. She did not announce herself to the cabin. Chiron said she was in the garden and had asked whether Clarisse would be willing to walk with her. Clarisse finished sharpening her spear, cleaned the stone, and put both away before she answered.

“I’ll go for a little while,” she said.

Chiron carried the message. Amphitrite waited by the low wall. She had brought a shawl against the cold but no walking stick, no basket, and no reason for Clarisse to carry anything.

“Where would you like to go?” Amphitrite asked.

“Past the greenhouse. The upper path is dry.”

“Then we can take that.”

They walked at Clarisse’s pace. Amphitrite did not try to fill the first stretch of quiet. The ground rose beside the greenhouse, where the last herbs leaned into their covers. Clarisse stopped to check the knots on the canvas. One had come loose. She retied it, then pressed the edge down so wind could not get under it.

“Do you want help?” Amphitrite asked.

“Hold the corner.”

Amphitrite held it while Clarisse tied the knot. She kept her hand away from the cloth itself, taking only the edge Clarisse offered. When Clarisse finished, she released it.

“Thanks.”

“You’re welcome.”

They went on toward the upper field. The air smelled of cold dirt and pine. The sea was not visible from that path, though Clarisse could hear it when the wind stopped.

“Do you miss the palace?” she asked.

“Sometimes.”

“Do you miss being there when you’re here?”

“Sometimes.”

“That sounds annoying.”

“It is.”

“Do you ever wish the people here would come with you?”

“I have wished it.”

“Have you asked them?”

“Not lately.”

“Why not?”

“Because wanting someone to come does not mean they should.”

Clarisse thought about that. “Do you wish Poseidon had stayed with us?”

“Yes.”

“Do you wish you could make him?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because he would be there without being with you.”

Clarisse’s stride shortened. She had expected a simpler answer. “You think that’s all?”

“I think it would not be enough.”

“Did it work that way with Percy?”

“Sometimes he wanted me there. Sometimes he wanted to tell me something after the fact. Sometimes he wanted to know I was not waiting for a particular answer.” Amphitrite glanced ahead, not at Clarisse. “I have had to learn that being invited into a room is not the same as being invited to decide what happens there.”

“Did you make that mistake?”

“Yes.”

“What did you do?”

“I asked Percy what he wanted me to do. He said he didn’t know yet.”

“And then?”

“I waited. Not gracefully every time.”

Clarisse looked at the path. The gravel shifted under her boots. “I don’t want you to wait for me as if I’m going to give you a prize.”

“I don’t.”

“You might.”

“I might want an answer. I will have to manage that myself.”

“You’re good at saying that.”

“I’m less good at doing it.”

“Are you?”

“Yes.”

Clarisse stopped at the split in the path. The lower trail ran toward the water, its bank marked with a strip of red cloth. The sign had not changed. Neither had the mud beneath it.

“Do you want to keep going?” Amphitrite asked.

“No.”

“Then we can turn back.”

“I don’t want to go back yet.”

Amphitrite waited.

Clarisse leaned her elbow against the rail. “I just want to stand here.”

“Then we can stand.”

The wind moved across the field and carried the sound of someone calling from the cabins. Amphitrite did not ask whether Clarisse wanted to talk about Poseidon. Clarisse did not say she had moved on from it. Both things could remain true without being exchanged.

A minute passed.

“Do you know what I remember about the first time you came to camp?” Clarisse asked.

“No.”

“You said you weren’t here to inspect us.”

“I remember saying that.”

“I told you not to.”

“You did.”

“You didn’t get offended.”

“I wanted to,” Amphitrite said. “I thought I had been clear.”

“You had been clear about yourself.”

“That is not the same as making you comfortable.”

“No.”

Clarisse watched the trees bend under a gust. “I liked that you didn’t try to hug me until I leaned in.”

“I was afraid you would not want me to.”

“You could have asked.”

“I could have.”

“You didn’t.”

“No.”

“That was all right.”

Amphitrite nodded. “Thank you for telling me.”

“I’m not thanking you for being decent.”

“I know.”

“You don’t have to thank me every time I answer.”

“I can try not to.”

“Good.”

They turned back when the wind grew colder. Amphitrite did not ask whether Clarisse would come again. At the garden gate, Clarisse said, “You can walk with me after training in a few days if you’re here.”

“I would like that.”

“I might change my mind.”

“Then I’ll ask.”

“Good.”

The exchange was ordinary, and Clarisse did not need it to prove the whole relationship.

Later that night, she saw Amphitrite at the Big House porch speaking with Chiron. Clarisse did not go over. She did not need to turn an afternoon into another meeting before it had settled.

Several days later, Amphitrite returned for the walk. Clarisse had not changed her mind. They followed the same upper path, but the ground had firmed since the last rain. Clarisse stopped by the greenhouse to check the canvas knot. It was still tight.

“Do you want to keep walking?” Amphitrite asked.

“Not yet.”

They went as far as the bench above the field. Clarisse sat first. Amphitrite took the other end. Their shoulders did not touch. The space between them had become familiar; it did not feel like a distance Clarisse had to close.

“What do you do when you are not visiting?” Clarisse asked.

Amphitrite told her about a dispute over the repair of a harbor wall, about a council that had argued over where to place a channel marker, and about a young Nereid who had refused to sign her name to a map she had not drawn. She did not make the story a lesson. She told it because Clarisse had asked what her days looked like.

“Did the council listen to her?” Clarisse asked.

“Eventually.”

“Why eventually?”

“They believed the map was more important than the person who had made it.”

“That’s stupid.”

“Yes.”

“Did you say that?”

“I said it in a way they could hear.”

“Did it work?”

“Not the first time.”

Clarisse smiled. “So you’re not good at it either.”

“No.”

“Good.”

Amphitrite looked at her. “You wanted me to be imperfect?”

“I wanted you to be a person.”

“I am.”

“Sometimes people forget that about gods.”

“They forget it about queens too.”

Clarisse leaned back against the bench. “I don’t.”

“I know.”

A bird landed on the rail and pecked at a seed caught in the grain. Clarisse watched it work at the small black thing until it dislodged. The bird flew away with the seed in its beak.

“I’ve been thinking about a word,” Clarisse said.

Amphitrite did not move closer. “You don’t have to use it.”

“I know.”

“You can tell me the word without using it.”

“I know that too.”

The answer she had carried to the bench was still there. It had not come from Poseidon’s visit. It had not come from the walk or the story about the harbor. Those things had only been part of the time between. The word had gathered slowly, around the meals and the questions and the times Amphitrite had stopped herself from deciding what Clarisse meant.

“I want to call you Mom sometimes,” Clarisse said. “Not every time. Not when other people are listening. Just—sometimes.”

Amphitrite’s expression changed, but she did not smile broadly or reach for Clarisse’s hands.

“If that is what you want, I will answer,” she said.

“It doesn’t mean I’m done being angry at Poseidon.”

“No.”

“It doesn’t mean I’m done being angry about anything.”

“No.”

“And I’m not saying you get to talk to him for me.”

“I won’t.”

“I don’t want Silena to know yet.”

“I will keep it private.”

“Not because I think she’ll be mean about it.”

“I understand.”

“She gets to choose her own things. I get to choose mine.”

“Yes.”

Clarisse rubbed one thumb over the seam of her glove. “I may not use it again.”

“You can decide each time.”

“I don’t want you to remind me.”

“I won’t.”

“Not even if I forget I said it.”

“Especially not then.”

Clarisse studied Amphitrite’s face. There was no triumph in it. There was care, and the uncertainty of someone who knew the answer could change.

“Mom?” she said.

“Yes?”

Clarisse let out a breath. The word did not mend the old promise. It did not make the time apart disappear or move the anger out of her chest. It did not mean the next visit would be easy. It belonged to this bench, this cold afternoon, and the space Clarisse had decided to cross.

“I’m ready to go back,” she said.

Amphitrite stood. “All right.”

Clarisse started down the path first. Amphitrite followed beside her, leaving room for either of them to speak.

They did not tell anyone what she had said.

---

Clarisse returned to Cabin Three alone. Amphitrite had gone back to the Big House by the garden path. The walk had ended at the cabin door because Clarisse had chosen the turn, not because either of them had run out of things to say.

Silena was at the table with the inventory list open. She had counted the root sacks again and written the number in the margin.

“How was the walk?” she asked.

“Fine.”

“Do you want to tell me about it?”

“No.”

“Okay.”

Silena turned the paper toward herself and made a mark beside the flour. She did not look disappointed. Clarisse felt a small, unreasonable urge to explain more, then let it pass. Some choices stayed good when no one asked them to become evidence.

Leandros came in from the porch with two lengths of line draped over one shoulder. His scarf was gone. The wind had reddened his ears.

“Beckendorf asked me to bring these to the lower shed,” he said. “He said not to touch the rack loop.”

“Then don’t.”

“I know.”

“Do you need something?”

“I thought you might know which line was the spare.”

Clarisse looked at the two pieces. One was thinner, with a frayed end. The other had a darker strand woven through it.

“The spare is the darker one.”

“Why?”

“Because the other one’s frayed.”

He looked at the line again. “That’s obvious.”

“Then why ask?”

“To confirm.”

“Ask that.”

He breathed through his nose. “Can you confirm the darker one is the spare?”

“Beckendorf can.”

“You just said it was.”

“I said the other one was frayed. I didn’t say what they were using the dark one for.”

Leandros set both lengths on the table instead of carrying one away. “Then I’ll ask Beckendorf.”

“Good.”

He picked up the inventory list and scanned the page. “Why are there three different counts for the flour?”

“Because we have three stores.”

“There’s no reason to make it complicated.”

“There is if they’re in three places.”

“We could put them all in one place.”

“Not if the kitchen needs two sacks by tomorrow.”

“Then move two sacks.”

“You need to ask before rearranging the pantry.”

“I’m not rearranging anything. I’m making a suggestion.”

“Then make a suggestion.”

He put the list down. “I suggest we keep the two sacks by the stove and count the rest at the Big House.”

Silena glanced up. “That could work if Chiron agrees.”

“Then I’ll ask him.”

“Good,” Clarisse said.

Leandros frowned. “You say good like I’ve passed a test.”

“I’m saying your plan has the right question in it.”

“It’s not a plan. It’s a suggestion.”

“Then it has the right question in it.”

He studied her, then took the line from the table. “I’ll bring this to Beckendorf.”

He left the frayed length behind.

Clarisse checked the door latch after he went. It caught on the first try. The lower window blanket rustled, then settled back against the sill. Nothing in the cabin needed her to make a decision about her father.

Silena gathered the inventory pages. “Do you want to help me take these to Chiron?”

“No.”

“Okay.”

“You can ask Leandros.”

“He’s taking line to the shed.”

“Then wait.”

Silena tucked the page under a book. “I can do that.”

Clarisse went to the rack and lifted her practice spear. The leather wrap still held. She turned the haft once in her hand, tested the grip, and placed it back with the other practice weapons.

She did not call the word back into her mouth. She did not need to rehearse it to keep it. She did not have to use it again tomorrow for it to have been hers today.

A knock came at the open door.

Leandros stood outside, holding the darker length of rope. “Beckendorf says that one is spare. The frayed one goes in the repair bin.”

Clarisse nodded. “All right.”

“I told him you were right about the fray.”

“I didn’t ask you to.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

He set the rope beside the door, away from the list. “Are you still angry?”

“Yes.”

“About today?”

“About today and other days.”

“Okay.”

“You don’t have to keep checking.”

“I’m not checking whether you’re still angry. I’m trying to know what I can ask you.”

“Ask what you want. I’ll tell you if I don’t want to answer.”

“That sounds easier.”

“It isn’t.”

He nodded. “I know.”

“Do you?”

“I know you say that when you think I’m being stupid.”

“I didn’t say you were stupid.”

“You didn’t have to.”

“Then don’t decide I mean something I didn’t say.”

He looked down at the rope. “Right.”

Clarisse watched him take the line and put it on the shelf. He did not ask her whether she would tell Poseidon about the visit, and she did not offer to. Leandros could write to his father if he chose. Clarisse could keep the parts of her own history that she had not wanted him to own.

At supper, he took the seat beside Percy. Silena sat across from him with the inventory list at her elbow. Clarisse sat by the window. The conversation moved from the flour count to a younger camper’s lost glove, then to the wind that had shifted at the field. Leandros described what he had seen at the upper landing without claiming it made the lower bank safe. Annabeth said they could compare the time of his observation with the flag record. He agreed, then added that the current had turned faster than expected.

“Did you check the lower path?” Silena asked.

“No. It’s still closed.”

“Good,” Clarisse said.

Leandros glanced at her. “It is still closed.”

“I heard you.”

“I was agreeing.”

“You sounded annoyed.”

“I’m allowed to sound annoyed.”

“You are.”

“Then why do you keep correcting it?”

“Because the bank is still soft.”

“That’s not what I’m annoyed about.”

The table went quiet for a second, then Percy passed him the bread. Leandros took it.

“No one said it was,” Annabeth replied.

“I know.” He set the basket back down. “I just wanted to say it.”

Clarisse stayed with her stew. The visit had changed the shape of the day, not the old things between them. Leandros had been seen by his father. Clarisse had been angry. Silena had refused another invisible job. Amphitrite had walked beside her, then gone back to the Big House. None of that meant anyone had to draw the same conclusion.

Outside, the sea kept its own distance from the camp. The path remained marked, the low cut closed, the ropes on their shelf. Clarisse washed her bowl and set it upside down to dry.

She owed the sea nothing for the word she had chosen.

She owed Poseidon no answer for the attention he gave Leandros. She owed Leandros no permission to be pleased, and no promise that his pleasure would not hurt. She owed Amphitrite no title after one good afternoon.

The word had been hers to choose, and it could remain hers in the quiet between two people.

That was enough for tonight.
