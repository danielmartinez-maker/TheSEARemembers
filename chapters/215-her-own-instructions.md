## Chapter 215: Her Own Instructions

The morning after Thalia told the family about the new archery limit, Ianthe woke before the hall bell.

The Big House guest room was quiet. A stripe of light from the window crossed the foot of the bed. She had taken the weather sheets out of her satchel and arranged them along the desk, one for each day she had been in camp. The notes were not all useful. Some came from places she had not been permitted to reach alone. Some had been taken during a gust and lacked a second observation. The best page had the same time marked beside the flag line and the cabin eaves.

She had started a new sheet for the drying rack.

Beckendorf had told her the two rope coils were still wet in their centers. The repaired canvas seam was dry, but the separate loop still needed a load test. He had said the weather could help them decide when to inspect the materials again, not whether the materials were ready. That distinction had been simple enough when he spoke it. It was less simple when Ianthe thought about how the wind moved through the rows of trees.

She wrote the word “schedule” at the top of the page. Then she drew a line through it and wrote “observations.”

The paper looked less official after that.

She added three columns: time, place, and what moved. Under the third, she left space for the details. She did not write anyone’s name. She had learned that a name on a page could look like an assignment even when she meant it as a question.

The page was not a route map. It was not her weather chart. It was not an order. It was a small record for one job.

She thought of her father looking down at a set of storm notes, asking what she had noticed. She could still feel the way that question had opened a space beside him. She could also remember the sound of his voice when he praised her by making Thalia and Annabeth smaller. The good part had been real. So had the cost.

Ianthe placed the pencil on the page and stopped herself from adding a fourth column for “who should respond.”

That answer belonged to Beckendorf.

At breakfast, she kept the sheet folded inside her satchel. She sat at the far end of the table from the serving dishes, where she could see the open porch and the weather vane above it. The vane turned once, then settled toward the west. She noted the direction in her head and kept eating.

Clarisse entered with her jacket open and her hair still damp from the mist. She sat opposite Leandros, who was already at the table with a cup of tea and a slice of bread. He had an old coil of twine beside him, separated from the dining things by a folded cloth.

“You’re bringing that to breakfast?” Clarisse asked.

“It’s not wet.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

Leandros looked at the twine. “I’m checking the splice.”

“Beckendorf said he would check it.”

“He said he’d load-test it.”

“That is checking it.”

“He said I could bring it to the rack.”

“After breakfast.”

“He didn’t say after breakfast.”

Clarisse took the coil from the table and moved it to the bench beside Leandros. “There. Now it’s not with the food.”

He looked at the coil, then at Clarisse. His mouth pressed into a line. He did not take it back.

Ianthe watched them. She knew the shape of the exchange: each sentence had a practical point, and each one carried a second argument beneath it. It would have been easy to say that Beckendorf could check it when he was available, or that Leandros could wait. She had no reason to join in.

She poured more tea.

Leandros glanced her way. “You have the wind sheets?”

“I have observations.”

“Same thing.”

“Not exactly.”

He looked at the pages tucked beneath her elbow. “You can tell the wind from the flags. That’s useful.”

“I know.”

“But the rack is under the trees. The gusts near the ground change.”

“Beckendorf knows that.”

“You asked him?”

“He asked me to record what I could see.”

“Then you’re not measuring the wind everywhere.”

“No. I’m observing it at the rack.”

“That’s not the same.”

“I know.”

Clarisse broke her bread in half. “Both of you have made the same point twice.”

Leandros looked away first. He pushed the twine a little farther from the edge of the bench.

Ianthe’s first instinct was to ask him whether he wanted to help with the readings. The words reached the back of her teeth and stopped there. She could ask later, if it mattered. For now, she had a job. There was no need to collect a witness to prove she could do it.

After breakfast, Chiron met her outside the Big House. The air was cool, and the grass held the last of the mist. He had a small slate under his arm. Beckendorf waited near the covered rack, where the coils rested on their spacers.

“The center of each coil is still damp,” Beckendorf told her. “The surface feels dry. That does not tell us what is happening inside.”

Ianthe took the slate from Chiron. It held a few lines in his broad hand: rack checks, wind observations, no movement until inspection. A line below that named Beckendorf as the person to decide when materials could be handled. Her name appeared beside “record conditions.”

“Is this what you wanted me to do?” she asked.

“It is the task,” Beckendorf said. “I want you to note the flags at the marked points and tell me if the cover starts pulling or lifting. You can use your wind notes. You do not move the coils, change their tags, choose a route, or assign anyone to help. If the reading changes, you bring it to me. I decide whether anything moves.”

Ianthe read the slate once more. “Can I ask someone to stand at the far flag?”

“If you want to ask. Nobody is assigned through you.”

“Understood.”

Chiron watched her. “Do you want this responsibility?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because the observations will help you decide when to inspect the rack again.”

“That is one reason.”

“It’s useful work.”

“That is another.”

Ianthe tightened her fingers around the slate. She had expected him to ask why she wanted to prove herself. She could not decide whether she was relieved he had not or disappointed that he had not seen it.

“I want to do it well,” she said.

“Then do the part you have been given.”

The simplicity irritated her. “I know the limits.”

“You do.”

Beckendorf pointed to a small strip of red cloth tied on the inside post. “That’s your line. You can stand there or under the awning. Don’t stand behind the coils when the cover moves. If the gust catches it, call me. Don’t grab it.”

“I won’t.”

“Good. We’ll be on the other side checking the lower fence post and the rack supports. Clarisse and Leandros have separate jobs.”

Ianthe turned toward them. Clarisse had a narrow board in her hands. Leandros carried a small measuring cord and the coil of twine from breakfast. They were moving along the firm path that led to the storage yard. The path map lay in Beckendorf’s hand, its lines marked with soft ground and closed access. He had not given it to Ianthe to direct the work. He was carrying it himself.

“Do I need to tell them when the wind shifts?” she asked.

“You tell me,” Beckendorf answered. “They will hear us if we need them.”

Ianthe thought of all the places a message could lose its meaning. If she told Clarisse, Clarisse might decide the wind was not enough to change her job. If she told Leandros, he might know something about the air off the water that she did not. If she told Beckendorf, he would decide whether either mattered.

“I can do that,” she said.

“Good.”

She set herself at the marked point and took out her paper.

The first reading was simple: the ribbon on the open flag line held west; the small strip inside the rack moved less; the cover lifted at the near edge but did not pull against its tie. She wrote the time, the place, and the movement. She noted that the cover had lifted, not that the wind was “safe.” The word safe did not belong in the observation column.

At the next reading, the ribbon at the open flag line turned southwest. The rack strip stayed toward the west. She looked at the trees and tried to understand what they were doing to the air. The leaves barely moved, but one branch at the north edge bent back and then forward. A gust had moved above the rack without traveling evenly through it.

“Beckendorf,” she called.

He came over, leaving the lower post to Clarisse. “What changed?”

“The outside ribbon turned southwest. The rack strip didn’t. The cover lifted once at the near edge, then settled.”

“Did the tie move?”

“No.”

“Did the coil shift?”

“No.”

“Good. Put down what you saw.”

She did.

“Does it tell us to move anything?”

“No.”

“Does it tell us the rack is safe for handling?”

“No. It tells us the flags differ.”

Beckendorf nodded. “Exactly.”

Ianthe had expected him to say more. He returned to the lower post without praising her. The page carried the observation, and that was what he had asked her to produce. The absence of a response felt like a small refusal, though she knew it was not.

She tried to imagine what he might have said if the reading had been wrong. He would have corrected the method. He would not have asked what she meant to do. She had already been given a job, and part of it was to keep her own conclusions narrow.

The next reading was more useful. The wind shifted at the open line, then rose along the eastern edge of the awning. The rack strip turned only after the cover moved. Ianthe recorded the sequence and brought the slate to Beckendorf.

He read the note. “Good distinction. The cover reacted before the rack flag.”

“That could mean the gust is reaching the awning first.”

“Could.”

“Not that it will lift.”

“Right.”

“Then we should keep the ties.”

“We keep the ties because they are set for the rack. The reading tells me to check the outside tie again. It does not tell us how to repair it.”

Ianthe heard the correction beneath the words. She had used “we should” as if the decision were already hers. She looked at the slate. “I’ll write that the reading prompted a check.”

“Good.”

She crossed out the line she had started and rewrote it. The crossing-out looked ugly. She could have started a new sheet. Instead she left it visible and marked the revised note beside it.

A little farther along, Clarisse tested the fence board. She pressed the toe of her boot into the ground beside the brace and stopped before the mud took the full weight. Leandros stood a few feet away, measuring the board’s angle against the temporary stake. Neither of them needed Ianthe’s reading. They had the work in front of them.

She kept to her position.

The next gust came stronger. The cover lifted at the near edge. A wooden peg tapped against the rack post. Ianthe called Beckendorf’s name. She did not reach for the cover.

He came at a quick walk, eyes on the rack. “How many times?”

“Twice since the last reading. The near edge lifted both times. The tie stayed in place.”

“Did the cover pull against the corner?”

“No. The peg tapped once. The cover settled.”

“Thank you.”

He checked the tie and the peg. The knot had not shifted. He set a second small weight on the corner, then watched the next gust pass. The cover lifted again, less than before.

“Write that I added weight after the third lift,” he said.

“Should I mark the first one as three?”

“Two before. This is the third.”

She wrote it down.

Beckendorf crouched near the rack, then looked at the coils without touching them. “The cover can stay. We’ll inspect the tie before we go inside.”

“The wind has turned,” Ianthe said. “The open ribbon is south now.”

“I see it.”

“The rack strip is still west.”

“That tells me the rack’s shelter is changing the movement.”

“It doesn’t tell you the center is dry.”

“No.”

She looked at him. “I didn’t say it did.”

“I’m confirming the boundary.”

Ianthe felt heat move up her neck. “I know the boundary.”

“Then you don’t have to defend it.”

She lowered her eyes to the paper. Her notes were accurate. The cover lifted. The peg tapped. Beckendorf added weight. The rack flag and the open flag disagreed. There was no need for the work to make her feel either trusted or distrusted. The job was the job.

She wrote one more observation and waited for the next interval.
---

The next set of readings came slower. The outside ribbon turned west again, but the rack strip pointed south. The cover did not lift. Ianthe wrote down each movement and kept the observations in the order they happened.

Beckendorf was on the far side of the yard with Chiron, looking at the tie and the peg. Clarisse had returned to the fence board. Leandros was still measuring the brace from the firm path, his cord stretched between the stake and the lower edge.

A gust ran along the rack. The near edge of the cover lifted, then fell. The coil shifted a little on its spacer. Ianthe was sure of it. She had been watching the center one all morning; its outer loops were darker, the damp deeper where they crossed. The wind might blow grit into the fibers if the cover slipped again. The coil needed shelter.

She stepped over the red cloth line.

“Leandros, move the center coil under the inner cover. Clarisse, take the near tie and hold it down until he gets it there.”

Clarisse dropped the board she had been holding onto the grass, not hard enough to break it. “Who told you to move the coil?”

“I did.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“The cover is lifting. The coil shifted.”

“Then tell Beckendorf.”

“We don’t need to wait for him to see the same thing.”

Leandros let the measuring cord fall against the stake. “I’m not moving it.”

Ianthe looked at him. “You can see it shifted.”

“I can see the coil is still on the spacer. I can see the cover is tied. I can see Beckendorf told us not to move anything until he checks it.”

“The cover moved.”

“Then call Beckendorf.”

“You’re both standing here while the fibers are exposed.”

Clarisse’s expression hardened. “I’m not standing here. I’m doing the fence check he gave me.”

“You’re holding the board.”

“I was holding the board before you gave me a different job.”

“I’m not changing your job. I’m asking you to secure the cover.”

“You told me to.”

The distinction landed. Ianthe had not asked. She had used the shape of the request but not the words. She had expected Clarisse to understand that the cover mattered more than the board. She had expected Leandros to move because he was already near the coil. She had decided which work should take precedence without Beckendorf.

She opened her mouth. The excuse was ready: the wind had changed; the coil had shifted; she was trying to prevent damage. All of it was true and none of it made her the person who could reassign them.

“Beckendorf,” she called.

He came back from the lower side of the rack. “What happened?”

“I saw the center coil shift when the cover lifted. I told Leandros to move it and Clarisse to hold the tie. They didn’t.”

Clarisse lifted the board from the grass. “We were checking the fence.”

Beckendorf looked from her to Ianthe. “Ianthe, your task was to record the change and bring it to me. You were told not to move the coils or assign anyone.”

“I know.”

“Do you know why?”

“Because I don’t decide when the materials are handled.”

“And because you don’t know whether moving it would make the drying or the tie worse.”

“I thought it would be safer under the inner cover.”

“You thought. You were not asked to make that call.”

The words were not loud. Ianthe would have preferred a shout. A shout gave her something to push against. Beckendorf’s voice left the work standing between them.

“Did it actually shift?” he asked her.

“I think so.”

“Did you mark it?”

“No.”

“Then let’s check. No one moves anything.”

He crouched at the rack, inspected the spacer and the outer loops, and compared the coil to a small mark on the board below it. The coil had not moved. The cover had lifted, but the edge of the coil had only looked different when the shadow shifted.

Ianthe stared at the mark. She had been certain. She had been wrong.

“Did the cover pull?” Beckendorf asked.

“No,” she said.

“Did the tie loosen?”

“No.”

“Did the peg move?”

“No.”

He stood. “Then the cover stays where it is. I’ll check it again after this reading.”

“I’m sorry,” Ianthe said.

“Say what you’re sorry for.”

She looked at Clarisse and Leandros. Clarisse had put the board down. Leandros had picked up the cord but was not measuring yet.

“I gave you an order that wasn’t mine to give. I told you to stop your assigned work and move something I wasn’t authorized to move. I said it as if the wind gave me the right to reassign you.”

Clarisse did not nod.

“I won’t do it again,” Ianthe added.

“That’s for you to prove,” Clarisse said. “I’m going back to the board.”

Leandros wound the cord around his palm. “You can ask me to check a reading.”

“I did.”

“You asked me to move a coil.”

“I told you.”

“Yes.”

“I’m sorry.”

He gave no answer. He crossed the path and resumed measuring. The cord snapped lightly against the stake as he drew it tight.

The pause had cost them time. Clarisse had to set the board back into place and check the dirt where it had landed. Leandros had to repeat his measurement because the cord had touched the wet grass. Beckendorf watched the tie again instead of moving to the next section of the rack. No one had been hurt. Nothing had been damaged. The work was still delayed because Ianthe had wanted to act before asking.

She returned to the marked point and picked up the paper.

The last line she had written said: “center coil shifted; move under inner cover.” She crossed it out. Her hand shook enough that the pencil made a dark groove. She could have covered the error with a neat new line. Instead, she wrote beside it: “Observed apparent shift during cover lift. Beckendorf checked against mark; coil did not move. No handling authorized.” She added the time and signed her own name.

That was the record she had wished to produce. It was not as clean as a perfect morning, but it was accurate.

Beckendorf finished checking the tie and returned. “Your next reading is due in ten minutes.”

“Are you assigning me to continue?”

“Yes. You have the observations. I still need the line through lunch.”

“Do you still want me to do it?”

“I need the record. If you don’t want to, tell me now and I’ll take it.”

Ianthe wanted to say she could do it. She wanted him to ask whether she wanted to keep the task because he trusted her. Neither thing was necessary.

“I’ll continue.”

“Then stay on the marked side and report the next change.”

“Understood.”

She did not add that she could handle the rest.

The next interval passed without a gust. The flags shifted lightly. She wrote the times and watched the cover. Her attention kept going back to Clarisse and Leandros, to the way they had paused when she ordered them and resumed only after Beckendorf’s decision. The work had not been hers. The embarrassment was.

She could decide that they were too suspicious, or that Clarisse liked correcting people, or that Leandros had refused because he did not want to take direction from her. She could make each of those things partly true. It would not change that she had told them what to do.

At the following reading, she stayed behind the line.

“Beckendorf,” she called. “The rack strip is turning east, but the cover is still settled. The open flag is west.”

He came over. “Anything lift?”

“No.”

“Any shift?”

“I don’t know. I didn’t check the mark. I was told to report the reading.”

“Good. I’ll check the mark.”

It was an ordinary instruction. Ianthe followed it.

Beckendorf found the coil in the same position. “No movement.”

“Then I’ll record that as your check.”

“Record that I checked. Your observation is the flag and the cover.”

She wrote it down. The job had become smaller again. She wanted to make it larger. That was the part she was learning to notice before it changed into an order.

The wind stayed steady through the next reading. Beckendorf moved to speak to Chiron near the fence. Clarisse carried the board to the next support. Leandros measured the brace. They had resumed their work, but neither had asked Ianthe anything. She did not call them over.

She took her sheet to the bench beneath the awning and reviewed it from the first line. The columns were too narrow for the detail she wanted. She could make a second page. She could add a time to the top. She could write “check” beside every reading that needed Beckendorf. The impulse to build a better system came fast. The harder part was to make a system that did not turn into permission to control the people around it.

At the bottom of the page, she added a separate note to herself: “Observation is not clearance.”

Then she drew a line under it.

The pencil had left a gray smudge along the side of her thumb. She rubbed at it, spreading the mark instead of removing it.

She went to find Beckendorf.

“Would you look at my record before I copy it?” she asked.

“I can.”

“Do you have time now?”

“After I finish the support check.”

“Okay. I’ll wait.”

He returned to the fence. Ianthe sat on the bench with her paper in her lap. She could have walked over and offered the sheet to Clarisse instead. Clarisse had seen the apparent shift. Leandros had been close enough to judge the coil’s position. Neither was required to check her work.

When Beckendorf came back, he read the page without speaking. He stopped at the crossed-out line.

“Why did you keep the wrong entry?”

“Because I wrote it, and it affected the work.”

“Good.”

“I don’t want the crossed-out note to look like I still think the coil moved.”

“Then your correction has to say that. You wrote it.”

“I did.”

“Do you want to copy it cleanly?”

“Would that make it easier to use?”

“It might. But keep the original attached. Anyone looking at the record should be able to see what changed.”

She nodded. “I’ll do that.”

“Then the corrected sheet goes with the rack log. I’ll sign the inspection line.”

“Do you want me to take the temperature too?”

“No. We don’t have a thermometer out here.”

“I could find one.”

“That would be a different measurement.”

“I know.”

“Then don’t invent one because the page looks empty.”

She nearly smiled. “All right.”

Beckendorf returned to the rack. Ianthe set the original sheet beside the slate. She had one more reading before lunch.

She thought of the moment she had ordered Clarisse and Leandros. It had come from a real observation, but she had made the observation into authority. She had treated the fact that she could see something as proof that she could decide what everyone should do about it.

She would need to repair the record. The apology would have to stay smaller than the effect, not wider than it. She did not know whether Clarisse would accept it. She did not know if Leandros would trust her next request. She could work without those answers.

When the next gust came, the rack flag turned east and the cover stayed down. Ianthe wrote exactly that.
---

The lunch bell sounded just after the last reading. Beckendorf checked the ties and left the coils on their spacers. The center of each remained damp. He recorded the time of the check in the storage ledger, then asked Ianthe to bring him her sheet.

She handed over the original and the clean copy. She had kept the crossed-out line in the original and copied the correction onto the second page. The clean copy included the exact times, flag positions, cover movement, and Beckendorf’s inspection of the coil. She had not written that the materials were ready, or that they were not.

Beckendorf read both pages. “You kept the first observation.”

“I kept the first conclusion too. It was wrong.”

“You corrected it.”

“I also gave an instruction that wasn’t mine.”

“That part doesn’t go in the weather log.”

“I know.”

He looked up. “Do you want me to put it in the work ledger?”

“No. I’ll tell Clarisse and Leandros myself. They were the ones I directed.”

“Then you can do that.”

“I don’t need you to tell them for me.”

“I didn’t offer to.”

She held the two papers against one another. The clean copy was easier to read. The original was more honest. “Should I leave both here?”

“Yes. I’ll attach the correction to the storage record. The original shows where the decision changed. The clean copy is what we use for the next check.”

“Do I get the next check?”

“That depends on whether I need another observation and whether you want to do it.”

Ianthe had prepared an answer. She had wanted him to say he trusted her with the next step. She could not tell whether he had chosen not to say it or whether the work simply did not require the word.

“Will you need it?” she asked.

“Possibly. The wind changes as the rack dries. I’ll decide after I look at the coil tomorrow.”

“Then I’ll wait.”

“That’s the right way to handle a possibility.”

He said it without praise. She wondered if that made the instruction easier to remember.

She took the new sheet to the table near the awning. There was room there to rewrite the observation labels, but not room for anyone else’s notes. That was fine. The task had been hers. So was the correction.

The words came slowly. “West flag turned southwest. Rack strip remained west. Cover lifted at the near edge. Peg tapped once. No movement confirmed by Beckendorf.” She wrote the report so that another person could read it without having to know what she had hoped to happen.

A shadow fell over the paper. Clarisse was carrying the narrow board back toward the fence.

“I’m not asking you to review this,” Ianthe said.

“Good.”

“I wanted to tell you I gave you an order that was outside the task.”

“You told me.”

“I was wrong to do it.”

Clarisse stopped. “Yes.”

Ianthe had expected more resistance. She had also expected to feel relieved when the word came. Instead, the answer sat between them, blunt and exact.

“I thought the cover might pull loose.”

“That was a reason to call Beckendorf.”

“I know.”

“You also thought you knew what I should do next.”

“I did.”

Clarisse shifted the board on her shoulder. “The fence check was mine. If you see a hazard, say what you see. Don’t decide my next step for me.”

“I understand.”

“Do you?”

“I’m not sure I do yet.”

Clarisse gave her a narrow look. “That’s better than pretending.”

“I’ll correct the record. I won’t write that the coil moved.”

“It didn’t.”

“I know. I also won’t write that it was safe.”

“Good.”

“I’m sorry I interrupted your check.”

Clarisse held the silence for a moment. “I heard you.”

It was not forgiveness. It did not ask Ianthe to make Clarisse say something warmer. Clarisse shifted the board again and went on toward the fence.

Ianthe watched her reach the brace and set the board down. She could not tell whether Clarisse had gone back to work because she had accepted the apology, or because the work needed doing. She had no right to choose the answer for her.

Leandros was still by the storage path. He had looped the measuring cord over one shoulder and was checking the edge of the temporary post. Ianthe carried her paper over.

“I want to apologize for telling you to move the coil.”

“You already did.”

“I want to say the part I missed. I was responsible for the reading. I wasn’t responsible for the materials, and you were working on the fence measurement. I interrupted you.”

“You interrupted Clarisse too.”

“I did.”

He touched the lower post with the cord. “Then tell her.”

“I did.”

“Did she forgive you?”

“She said she heard me.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No.”

Leandros ran a finger along the cord where the fibers had separated near the knot. “You thought the coil moved because you saw the shadow change.”

“Yes.”

“I thought it moved too, at first.”

Ianthe looked up. “You did?”

“For a second. Then I saw the mark.” He pointed to the small scratch on the rack board. “That’s why we check the same point. But I don’t want you telling me to move things because you think the air means it’s time.”

“I won’t.”

“If you want me to check something, ask.”

“I thought I was asking.”

“You weren’t.”

“I know.”

He tightened the cord around the post. “You can ask me about the current if we’re at the water. You can ask me to check a flag if I’m near it. I can say no.”

“I know.”

“I mean it.”

“So do I.”

He looked at her paper. “You’re fixing the record?”

“Yes.”

“Then write that I didn’t move the coil.”

“I didn’t write that you moved it.”

“Not in the first line.”

“I crossed it out.”

“People read the line they want.” He pulled the cord snug and marked the post. “Write the action. Leandros refused the instruction. No material moved. Then put the reason.”

“Will that make it seem as if you disobeyed me?”

“I did disobey you.”

“I wasn’t your supervisor.”

“Then it’s not disobedience. It’s refusing an order from someone who didn’t get to give one.”

She could have argued that she was not trying to be his supervisor. She had already said that. It did not matter what she had meant if he had heard an order.

“I’ll write it that way,” she said.

He nodded and returned to the measurement.

She wrote: “No coil movement. Leandros declined an unauthorized instruction; Clarisse continued the fence check. Both resumed assigned tasks after Beckendorf confirmed the coil had not shifted.” The line was longer than she wanted. She left it that way.

When she looked up again, Leandros had completed the measurement. Clarisse was checking the board against the stake. Neither had gone. Neither had accepted a task from her.

The fact that they were still working did not mean the disagreement had vanished. It meant they had returned to their work.

Ianthe carried the corrected page toward the dining hall. She was hungry enough to notice the smell before she reached the door: rosemary, hot bread, and the sharper scent of crushed stems. Silena stood near the kitchen entrance with a towel over one shoulder and a bowl in her hands.

“Do you want to taste something?” Silena asked.

“What is it?”

“An adjustment.”

“To the bread?”

“To the herbs. I used less rosemary than yesterday and more salt.”

“Is it a test?”

“It is lunch.”

Ianthe looked at the bowl. “Can I sit first?”

“Of course.”

Silena pointed to an empty place at the table. Ianthe put the record beside her plate and sat down. She could have left it in the Big House and returned later. She wanted to keep it where she could see it, but she did not need to make it the subject of every conversation.

Silena cut a slice of bread and set it on a plate. “Taste it before you tell me what you think.”

Ianthe broke off a corner. The crust was warm. The rosemary came through after the salt, not before it.

“It’s better than yesterday,” she said.

“Better how?”

“The herb doesn’t cover the bread. The middle tastes less heavy.”

Silena nodded, considering. “That is what I wanted to know.”

“Do you want me to compare it to the other loaf?”

“No. I remember the other loaf.”

“Then I don’t have to?”

“No. You can eat.”

Ianthe took another bite. Silena moved back to the kitchen without asking her to carry anything. The choice to sit had not been a test. No one needed a weather reading from her.

She opened the record once the bread was finished and checked the last line. “Clarisse continued the fence check; Leandros completed the measurement.” She had named their actions only because they were relevant to the task’s interruption. The names did not assign them.

Thalia arrived with Annabeth a few minutes later. She wore the splint beneath her boot and walked at the same pace she had used the day before. Ianthe noticed her, then looked at the bread.

She did not ask about the heel.

Thalia sat across from her. “You have the rack sheet.”

“Yes.”

“Is that part of your job?”

“It was. The readings are finished for today.”

“What happens next?”

“Beckendorf checks the coils tomorrow and decides whether he wants another reading.”

“That sounds like his decision.”

“It is.”

Thalia tore off a piece of bread. “Good.”

Ianthe could have told her about the command. She could have asked whether Thalia thought she had handled it well afterward. She could have tried to make her sister decide whether the apology counted. The questions formed in her mind and waited.

“I gave Clarisse and Leandros an order this morning,” Ianthe said.

Thalia looked at her. “What happened?”

“I thought the center coil had shifted when the cover lifted. I told Leandros to move it and Clarisse to hold the tie. They said no. Beckendorf checked it. It hadn’t moved.”

“Did you apologize?”

“Yes.”

“Did they accept it?”

“Clarisse said she heard me. Leandros said to ask next time.”

“That sounds like an answer.”

“It’s not the answer I wanted.”

“What answer did you want?”

Ianthe had to think. “I wanted them to say they understood why I did it.”

“Do they?”

“I don’t know.”

“You can’t choose that for them.”

“I know.”

Thalia took a sip of water. “Are you asking me whether you were wrong?”

“I was wrong to order them.”

“Then you know.”

“I’m asking whether what I did after matters.”

“It matters to what you do next.”

“Does it make it better?”

“It makes the work more accurate. It doesn’t erase the first part.”

The words were not comforting. They were fair.

“I don’t need you to tell Clarisse I’m sorry,” Ianthe said.

“Good.”

“I don’t need you to tell her whether she should accept it.”

“Good.”

“I just wanted you to know.”

“Thank you for telling me.”

Thalia went back to the bread. The answer made room for Ianthe without cleaning up the scene for her.

Annabeth looked over from her plate. “Did you record the observation, or the conclusion?”

“The observation. And the conclusion I got wrong, in the crossed-out line.”

“Good.”

Ianthe glanced at her. “You’re not going to tell me to rewrite it?”

“No. Beckendorf is the one using the record.”

“That makes sense.”

“It does.”

Annabeth returned to her food. She did not make the record a lesson. Thalia did not ask the three of them to talk about the order. The conversation ended when it was done.

Ianthe stayed at the table until the bread was gone. She did not ask Silena whether the recipe had improved enough to keep. She did not offer to take the bowl. When Silena brought the rest of the loaf over, Ianthe asked, “Would you like me to move this plate?”

“Yes, please.”

She moved it.

The task had no connection to the wind. It still felt better than being useful in a way she had assigned herself.
---

The work resumed after lunch, though Clarisse and Leandros had to repeat the measurements they had stopped. Ianthe stayed by the rack and finished her record.

The next two readings showed the same difference between the open flag and the strip under the trees. She noted the direction and the time. When a gust lifted the cover, she called Beckendorf rather than stepping over the red line. He checked the tie, added a second knot, and left the coil where it was.

“Do you need another reading?” she asked.

“Not yet. Keep the next interval, then we’ll stop.”

She recorded the change: the cover lifted once; Beckendorf retied the corner; no coil movement; center still damp, per Beckendorf’s check. She wrote “per Beckendorf’s check” because it was not her observation.

He came back to the bench while she was making the next copy. “What do you want to do with the first line?”

“Keep it attached to the correction.”

“Why?”

“Because I wrote it, and someone could use the wrong conclusion if I hide it.”

“What would you tell someone who saw the first line?”

“That I thought the coil moved. I was wrong. Beckendorf checked it.”

“And the command?”

“I don’t know whether that belongs in the storage log.”

“It doesn’t belong in the storage log. The storage log should say what happened to the material. Your conversation with Clarisse and Leandros is not a property of the coil.”

Ianthe looked at the paper. “Then where should I write it?”

“Do you need to write it?”

“I want to remember what I did.”

“You can make a note for yourself.”

She turned to the blank back of her first page. She wrote: “I saw a cover lift and thought the coil moved. I did not check the mark before deciding. I gave two people a direction that was not mine to give. They declined. Beckendorf checked. No material moved.” Below it she added: “Next time: report the observation and wait for the person responsible.”

The last sentence looked like something she might write on a camp board. She drew a box around the words “for myself.”

Beckendorf read it over her shoulder. “That’s yours?”

“Yes.”

“Then keep it yours.”

“I will.”

He went back to the rack. The last reading was due in ten minutes.

Ianthe waited at the marked point. She did not move to see whether Clarisse had finished. She could hear the thud of the board against the brace and the scrape of Leandros’s cord over the stake. The work had started again in the same order it had been in before she interrupted it. That was a relief and not an absolution.

A gust crossed the clearing from the southwest. The open flag turned first. A few seconds later, the rack strip snapped east. The cover lifted at the far edge and settled. Ianthe saw the peg tap twice.

“Beckendorf,” she called. “The far edge lifted, and the peg tapped twice. The open flag turned first; the rack strip changed after it.”

He came over, inspected the tie, and tightened it. “Anything else?”

“No movement that I could confirm.”

“Good. We’ll leave the coil.”

“Should I mark the cover as secured?”

“You can write that I tightened the tie and it stayed down after.”

She recorded his action, not a general status. When she finished, she brought the sheet to him.

“Does the observation help?” she asked.

“It does. I can see when the cover is moving and when the flags differ. The material check still takes a person at the rack.”

“So the reading changes when you inspect it, not whether it’s ready.”

“Right.”

“I can keep doing it if you need the weather notes.”

“I’ll ask you again if we need another set. Today’s task is finished.”

She had thought the next task would arrive without her asking. She had thought he might say that she had done well and could continue. Instead, he told her the shift was over.

“Thank you,” she said.

“Thank you for staying with the assignment after the mistake.”

She looked at him. “You’re thanking me for finishing the job?”

“I’m acknowledging both things. You made a mistake. You also corrected the record and stayed for the agreed task.”

“Does that mean you trust me with the next one?”

“It means I can consider asking you.”

Ianthe nodded. She wanted a larger answer and did not ask him to make one up.

The crew finished the fence check. Clarisse took the narrow board back to the storage stack. Leandros coiled the measuring cord and returned it to the peg. He checked his splice and left it untouched when Beckendorf said the load test would wait until the loop had dried. The two damp coils stayed on their spacers.

Ianthe could have gone directly to the Big House. Instead, she waited until Clarisse had set down the board.

“Can I ask you a question about the measurement you had to repeat?”

“You can ask.”

“Did my interruption change the reading?”

“No. It made us start it over.”

“Could you tell me where you were when I called you?”

Clarisse pointed to the board. “Here. The base was against the stake. I had the far end lifted.”

“Was the near side already checked?”

“No.”

“Then I shouldn’t write that you had finished the lower support.”

“You shouldn’t.”

“I’ll change the sequence.”

“That is your record.”

“It includes your check.”

“Then put down what you saw.”

“I didn’t see it. I’m asking you.”

Clarisse took a moment before answering. “The board was against the stake. I’d checked the bottom and was about to check the top. Then I stopped.”

“Thank you.”

“You don’t have to make every question an apology.”

“I’m not.”

“You sound like you are.”

Ianthe looked at the paper. “I want the sequence to be right.”

“Then write the sequence.”

Clarisse went to the board stack. Ianthe made the correction and initialed it. This time the note was not a description of what Clarisse had done; it was a report of what Clarisse told her. She recorded that too.

Leandros was still at the rack. Ianthe approached but stopped at a distance that left him room to finish his work.

“Can I ask you to check one line in the record?” she said.

“Which one?”

“The line about the coil. I wrote that you declined an unauthorized instruction. I want to make sure it doesn’t sound like you refused Beckendorf.”

He looked at the page. “Then write that it was your instruction.”

“I did.”

“Write that Beckendorf had told us not to move the coil.”

“That’s true.”

“Then that’s what happened.”

“Do you want me to put your reason in your words?”

“No.”

“Would you like to see it before I give Beckendorf the copy?”

“No.”

“All right.”

He went back to the cord. Ianthe let him.

The clean sheet was no longer perfect, but it did not need to be. It showed what the crew had actually done, who had checked what, and where the work had paused. Her first impulse had been to make a complete plan. The useful record was smaller.

She carried it to Beckendorf and asked him to attach it to the storage ledger. He read the correction, signed his inspection lines, and put the copy in the dry folder.

“Do you need to keep the original?” he asked.

“I want it.”

“Then take it.”

She folded it along the crease she had already made. The crossed-out line remained visible inside.

On her way back to the Big House, she passed the porch where Thalia sat with a book. Thalia did not look at her heel. Ianthe did not ask how it felt. She stopped beside the steps.

“Do you want company?” she asked.

Thalia considered, then closed her book around one finger. “You can sit.”

Ianthe sat on the other end of the bench. The distance between them was easy to measure and not yet easy to cross. Neither tried.

“How was the work?” Thalia asked.

“I gave Clarisse and Leandros an order. They refused. I was wrong to give it.”

“Did you finish the record?”

“Yes.”

“Then you did the next part.”

“I don’t know if it was enough.”

“You don’t have to decide that today.”

Ianthe looked toward the storage yard. “I want to be useful.”

“I know.”

“I want people to notice when I do something well.”

“That’s allowed.”

“I also want to make them do it the way I think it should be done.”

“That part is harder.”

“I know.”

Thalia turned one page in her book. “You don’t have to stop wanting our father to notice you.”

“I don’t?”

“No.”

“He compared me with you.”

“I know.”

“I liked the attention before that.”

“You can like it.”

“Does that make me disloyal?”

“To whom?”

“Everyone.”

Thalia looked over at her. “No.”

Ianthe leaned her elbows on her knees. “I don’t know how to hold both things.”

“You don’t have to do it neatly.”

“That sounds like something you tell yourself.”

“It is.”

“Does it work?”

“Sometimes.”

Thalia reopened her book. The conversation had not fixed Ianthe’s feelings or made her mistake disappear. It had only left a place where she could sit without having to earn the next sentence.

Ianthe sat with her. After a while, she unfolded the weather sheet and looked over the notes. She saw the morning’s command recorded in her own words on the back. She did not add that Clarisse had forgiven her. She did not add that Leandros had trusted her. She wrote that the record had been corrected and that the materials had not moved.

Under that, she wrote two instructions for herself.

“Observe. Report.”

After a moment, she added a third.

“Ask before you decide for someone else.”

The words were small enough to fit at the bottom of the page. They did not make her better. They gave her something to do the next time she wanted to be in charge.

At dinner, she sat at the table with Thalia and Annabeth. Percy was across from them, speaking with Leandros about the load test. Clarisse was telling Silena that the fence board needed another check after the next rain. Nobody asked Ianthe to explain the day. Nobody announced her mistake or her correction.

She unfolded the weather sheet once to check the time of the final gust. The paper remained hers.

Across the table, Leandros said, “The wind turned before the rack flag.”

Ianthe looked up. “Did you see it from the storage path?”

“From the end of the fence.”

“Then it’s not the same observation.”

“No.”

“Do you want to compare the times?”

“I’m telling you what I saw.”

“Then I’ll keep your observation separate.”

He looked at her for a moment, then nodded. He did not offer more. She did not ask him to.

The record had no authority to make a family. It could show who saw the gust, who checked the peg, who moved nothing, who interrupted the work, and who corrected the page. It could not decide what any of them owed one another.

Ianthe folded the sheet and tucked it away. Tomorrow Beckendorf might ask her to take another reading. He might not. The coils were still damp, and the splice still needed its test. Thalia’s heel still had a plan of its own. The wind might shift again.

If someone asked her to observe, she could observe. If she saw a change, she could report it. The next decision would belong to the person responsible for it.

For tonight, she let the page stay folded.
---

She let the page stay folded through the night.

The next morning, Beckendorf asked whether she could take two more readings at the rack. The coils were still damp in their centers. He wanted to compare the flags when the open path stayed calm and the air moved under the trees.

“Do you want me to take them?” Ianthe asked.

“I’m asking if you’re willing.”

“I am.”

“Same marked point. Same interval. Record the flags and the cover. If anything moves, tell me. I’ll decide whether we handle the materials.”

“I understand.”

“Can you repeat the task?”

“Two readings at the rack from the marked point. Flags and cover only. I report a change. You decide what moves.”

“Good.”

The words came more easily than the day before. Ianthe still disliked how small the task was. She disliked more the feeling that she would be tested on whether she accepted its limits. She put the old page inside the folder and took a clean one.

The first reading showed the open flag pointing north while the rack strip stayed still. She wrote the time and both positions. She did not write that the air was safe or that the coils could be moved.

Beckendorf inspected the fibers separately. He pressed the center of one coil and told her it remained damp. She wrote his result in a different line with his name beside it.

“You kept the observation separate from the check,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Good.”

He returned to the rack.

The second reading changed. The flag at the open path moved east; the strip under the trees turned afterward. Ianthe recorded the order, then brought the page to Beckendorf.

“Do you want me to hold this corner while you check the cover?” she asked.

“I can do it. Would you like to hold the lantern?”

“Yes.”

She stood where he pointed, held the lantern at the angle he asked, and did not reach for the tie. Beckendorf inspected the peg and left the coils on their spacers.

“Does the reading help?” she asked.

“It helps me decide when to look. It doesn’t tell me what I’ll find.”

“Understood.”

“That is the whole task.”

“I know.”

“Do you want another interval?”

“No. Two were enough for the comparison.”

“Then you’re done.”

She returned the lantern. The task had taken less than an hour. She had not assigned anyone. She had not decided that the rack was safe. She had not needed to make the work larger to have done it correctly.

Leandros passed on his way to the fence. “Beckendorf asked you again?”

“For two readings.”

“Did the flags change?”

“Yes.”

“Which way?”

She held the sheet against her side. “Open path east. The covered strip turned later.”

“Then it’s different from the water.”

“Of course.”

He nodded. “Good.”

It was not a friendship. It was not a family promise. It was a question about an observation and an answer that stayed within its evidence.

He glanced at the folded sheet in her hand. “Did you write down that I said no?”

“Yes.”

“What did you write?”

“That I declined the instruction. I also wrote that it was mine to give, not Beckendorf’s.”

He considered that. “You could write why.”

“You told me you didn’t want me to put your words in the record.”

“I don’t want my reason copied as if you know it. I told you the reason.”

“Should I write that you said it?”

“Write that I told you to leave the coil. You don’t have to explain me.”

Ianthe nodded. “I’ll add that.”

“Are you angry that I refused?”

“I was.”

“Why?”

“Because I thought I was right about the cover. Because you stopped when I told you what to do.”

“I stopped because Beckendorf told me not to move the coil.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

She looked at him. “I know that now. I didn’t act like it then.”

He looped the cord over his shoulder. “You can ask me to check something. I can tell you if the current is different, or if the wind is pushing against it. But you don’t decide what I do because you saw a flag.”

“I understand.”

“I’m not asking you to agree with everything I say.”

“I know.”

“You can ask the question and decide the answer isn’t useful.”

“I can.”

He studied her face, then looked away. “That’s all.”

It was not an apology from either of them. It was a limit stated clearly enough that she could carry it without guessing. She added his correction to the record: Leandros told her not to move the coil because Beckendorf had said to leave it in place until inspection. She did not call the sentence an endorsement of his judgment. It was what he had said.

A camper from the stores approached while Ianthe was folding the second sheet. He had a short board tucked under one arm and a pair of gloves in his other hand.

“Are the coils ready to move?” he asked.

Ianthe looked at the rack, then at the gloves. “I don’t decide that.”

“Beckendorf asked me to check before I clear this side.”

“Then you should ask him. He checked the center yesterday. It was still damp.”

“Can I move the gloves to the porch while I wait?”

“Yes. That doesn’t touch the coils.”

The camper set the board against the wall and put the gloves on the dry shelf. He went to find Beckendorf.

Ianthe watched him go. She had answered the question with the piece she knew and directed him to the person who could decide. She had not told him to carry anything or wait in a particular place.

Beckendorf came back with him a minute later. “The center is still damp,” he said. “Leave the coils on the spacers. You can clear the side path after I finish the cover check.”

The camper nodded and returned to the shelf.

“Do you want that in the log?” Ianthe asked.

“Write that he asked and that I checked. Don’t record it as a readiness test.”

She noted the time, the request, the source of the answer, and the fact that no material moved. She did not write “coil not ready” because that could mean someone had tested it and failed. She wrote “center damp on Beckendorf’s check; remains on spacer; no move.”

The sentence felt exact. It also felt unimportant. No one had praised her for it. She let it stay.

When the next gust crossed the open path, she saw the upper flag move first and the rack strip follow. She reported the sequence to Beckendorf. He came, checked the cover, and said the tie held.

“Do you want a new marker at the far edge?” Ianthe asked.

“What would the marker tell us?”

“Where the cover starts to lift.”

“It would only tell us where it lifted today.”

“Then it might confuse the next check.”

“It might. We can decide after we see whether the same place lifts again.”

Ianthe had an idea for a different marker. She could set one at the same height on both sides and compare how far the cloth rose. The answer came quickly in her mind, already arranged like a diagram. The work did not belong to her. She had not been asked to improve the setup.

She caught herself before she spoke. “I have an idea about the marker, but it’s not part of the observation. Do you want to hear it?”

“After the next check,” Beckendorf said. “For now, keep this one simple.”

“All right.”

The answer was not a rejection of her. It was a boundary on the task. She folded the note and set it under the first sheet.

At breakfast, Ianthe put both records in her satchel. She did not need to take them out unless Beckendorf asked. Thalia was at the table with Annabeth, reading a book whose pages had been softened at the corners. Thalia had not looked at Ianthe’s feet or the splint. Ianthe was grateful. She also noticed that relief could turn into a new kind of watching if she let it.

“Would you like me to sit here?” Ianthe asked.

Thalia looked at the empty place beside her. “Yes.”

Ianthe sat.

“I’ve been trying not to ask you about the heel every time I see you,” she said.

“I noticed.”

“Is it all right if I ask once in a while?”

“If I want to tell you, I’ll tell you. You can ask, but I can say no.”

“I can do that.”

“Good.”

Ianthe glanced at the book. “What are you reading?”

“An old mystery. Annabeth says the ending is obvious.”

“It might be.”

“Have you read it?”

“No.”

“Then you can’t say.”

“I can guess.”

“You can guess privately.”

Thalia held the book closer. “I’ll tell you about the beginning instead.”

Ianthe listened while Thalia described the first page: a locked door, a wet footprint, a missing key. She did not ask whether the clue proved anything. She did not try to solve the story before Thalia had finished. When Annabeth returned with two cups, Thalia reached for one and offered the other to Ianthe.

“Thank you,” Ianthe said.

The cup was warm between her hands. No weather note was needed to explain it.

For a few minutes, she let the conversation go where Thalia took it.

At breakfast, Ianthe folded the new sheet and placed it with the first. She did not write her name in a column for “responsible person.” She wrote the date only as “the morning after the first rack record,” because no calendar date had been set. Beneath it, she noted: “Second set requested by Beckendorf. Two readings. No handling. Coils remain damp by Beckendorf’s check.”

She turned the page over. Her private note to Zeus remained where she had left it, separate from the weather records. She did not take it out.

She still wanted her father to ask what she had seen. She still wanted to answer. Nothing in the two days at the drying rack had made that want disappear. It had only shown her that she could be asked to do a job without being made the boss of everyone nearby. The notes had been hers before her father asked about them; the questions had made the habit feel like a path toward him, but they had not created the weather she loved to watch. She did not know whether she could separate the pleasure of noticing from the hope that Zeus would ask again. She did not have to settle that before breakfast. The sheet stayed in her satchel, with its observations and corrections intact.

At the table, Thalia was describing a book Annabeth had lent her. Ianthe waited until there was a pause.

“Would you tell me what it’s about?” she asked.

Thalia looked up. “Do you want the whole plot?”

“Not if it spoils the ending.”

“Then I’ll tell you the beginning.”

Ianthe listened. She did not correct the order of events or ask how the story should end. The book belonged to Thalia. The description was hers to give.

When the hall bell rang, Ianthe carried her own cup to the washing basin and returned the two sheets to her satchel. She would not decide whether Beckendorf needed another reading. If he asked, she could do the task. If he did not, the work would move on without her.

That was still a place to stand.
