## Chapter 99: Mothers

The morning came in blue and gold.

Percy woke before the conch horn, which almost never happened. The light in Cabin Three was watery, filtered through the shell-chimes Annabeth had hung by the window last summer and never taken down. They chimed faintly though there was no wind. Tyson, on an approved weekend break from the forges, was snoring in the guest bunk across the room, one huge arm hanging off the side, fingers almost brushing the floor. His bedspread — lovingly stitched by a harpy with questionable taste — had slid half off. On the floor between their bunks lay a half-finished game of Mythomagic cards and a peanut butter sandwich Tyson had saved for later and then forgotten.

Percy lay there for a moment, listening to the surf.

Camp Half-Blood in late August had a particular sound. Cicadas in the strawberry fields. The far-off clang from the forge. Somebody already arguing good-naturedly at the climbing wall. And underneath it all, the sea, steady as breathing.

He thought about his mom.

It wasn’t an anniversary or anything. It was just one of those mornings when Sally Jackson, alive in the old life he remembered and gone since he was two in this one, felt very close. Maybe it was the light. She’d always loved mornings like this, when the apartment kitchen would fill up with sun and she’d stand at the stove in her blue bathrobe making blue pancakes and humming off-key. She’d burn the first one every time. She’d declare it a sacrifice to the gods and eat it herself.

Percy smiled into his pillow.

He got up quietly, pulled on shorts and a Camp tee, and slipped outside. The steps of Cabin Three were cool under his bare feet. The lake glittered. Far out past the canoe dock, something large broke the surface and sank again, smooth as oil. He didn’t think much of it.

Annabeth was already awake, of course. She was sitting on the steps of Cabin One with her laptop balanced on her knees and a pencil stuck through her bun. She saw him and lit up in that way she still did, after everything, like his appearance was a pleasant surprise even when it was completely predictable.

“Hey, Seaweed Brain,” she called softly, so as not to wake the rest of her cabin.

“Hey, Wise Girl.” He crossed the grass, still damp with dew, and sat beside her, shoulder to shoulder. “You’re up early.”

“Daedalus laptop overheated again. I woke up thinking about load-bearing walls.” She leaned her head against his shoulder for exactly three seconds — their morning ration of affection before the camp woke up and made them be responsible counselors. “You okay? You have that face.”

“What face?”

“The missing-her face.”

Percy didn’t bother denying it. Annabeth always knew.

“Yeah,” he said. “Just woke up thinking about her. Blue pancakes.”

Annabeth closed the laptop and set it aside. She laced her fingers through his. “Tell me one thing about her I don’t know.”

He thought. After years together in the life they remembered, Annabeth knew most of the Sally stories. The seven-layer dip. The time she’d taken on Gabe with a frying pan just by threatening to. The way she could quote whole passages of books from memory.

“She used to leave notes in my lunch,” he said finally. “Even when we had no money. Even when it was just, like, stale crackers and an apple. There’d be a napkin in there that said ‘You’re my hero’ or ‘Be kind today’ or ‘Extra blue luck.’ In high school, I was embarrassed. I’d crumple them up fast so nobody’d see. Now I’d give anything to find one I missed at the bottom of an old backpack.”

Annabeth squeezed his hand hard. “She sounds like she’d have strong opinions about my filing system.”

“She loved your filing system,” Percy said. “She loved you.”

Annabeth looked down quickly, but not before he saw the pink climb her cheeks. She never quite knew what to do when he talked about Sally like that — like that past could be reached again. He didn’t push it. He just bumped his shoulder against hers and stayed there until the conch blew.

---

Breakfast was loud.

That was normal. The dining pavilion buzzed with end-of-summer energy, everybody trying to cram in one more prank, one more race, one more late-night council meeting before the year-rounders were left with the quiet. The harpies swooped and shrieked. At the Poseidon table, Tyson was happily eating an entire platter of bacon and explaining to a wide-eyed new kid why peanut butter belonged on everything.

“Even on pizza?” the kid asked.

Tyson nodded solemnly. “Especially on pizza.”

Percy laughed and ruffled his brother’s hair as he sat down. Annabeth slid in beside him — Zeus and Poseidon tables had long since given up enforcing seating — with Thalia right behind her, trailing pine needles and attitude.

Thalia Grace looked like she’d slept in a tree, which she probably had. Her black hair was pulled back in a messy ponytail, her eyes lined dark, her camp shirt cut off at the sleeves to show the faded camp bracelet and the scatter of scars she never explained. She dropped her tray with a clatter and stole three of Percy’s grapes.

“Morning, Kelp Head. Morning, Annabeth.”

“Morning,” they chorused.

“You get the Iris-message?” Thalia asked, lower, glancing toward the head table where Chiron was talking quietly with Argus. “About, uh, visitors?”

Percy shook his head. “No. Who?”

Before Thalia could answer, the wind changed.

It came off the water, salt-sweet and cold even in the heat, with a sound like a conch shell held to the ear. The torches around the pavilion flickered green-blue. Conversations stuttered and died.

Everyone turned toward the beach path.

She walked up from the sea as if the hill were level ground.

Amphitrite, Queen of the Sea, wife of Poseidon, the mother who had raised the child everyone remembered Percy being, looked different here in the crowded pavilion. He was used to her recent lessons and letters; carrying those into camp still caught him by surprise. She looked kind. And tired, in the way of mothers who hold entire households together while their husbands are off being storms.

She wore a peplos the color of deep water, embroidered at the hem with hippocamps in silver thread. Her dark hair was braided back with pearls. Her skin glowed faintly, as if lit from within by phosphorescence. In her hands she carried a woven basket overflowing with cloth-wrapped shapes. Behind her, half-hiding behind a dune, was Triton, her son, green-skinned and scowling in a way that was clearly armor.

Chiron rose to greet her, bowing deeply. “My lady. Camp Half-Blood is honored.”

Amphitrite smiled. “Thank you, old friend. I will not keep your campers from their breakfast long. I have come on a mother’s errand.” Her eyes found Cabin Three’s table unerringly. “May I?”

The whole pavilion held its breath as she approached.

Percy stood so fast he knocked his knee on the table. Tyson stood too, beaming, bacon still in hand. Annabeth stood with them, automatically, hand finding Percy’s elbow. Thalia straightened, respectful despite herself.

“Lady Amphitrite,” Percy said. His throat felt dry. He’d faced Kronos. He’d held the sky. This was somehow worse. “Hi.”

Her smile softened. “Perseus. May I sit?”

He nodded, dumbly, and the visitors at the Poseidon table scrambled to make room; Clarisse and Silena were still on morning duty. She sat with impossible grace on a sticky camp bench, set the basket on the table, and looked at each of them in turn — Percy, Tyson, and then Triton, whom she beckoned forward with a flick of her fingers.

“Come, Triton.”

Triton slouched forward, arms crossed, twin tails of his armor clinking. He was taller than Percy, older in god-years, and currently wearing the expression of a teenager forced to attend a family reunion.

“Mother,” he muttered.

“Be polite,” Amphitrite said mildly, but with steel underneath. She turned back to Percy and Tyson. “I have let palace duties crowd out visits here. And I want to do better by you too, Tyson. A queen should make room for family. A mother should make room still.”

Tyson blinked his one big eye. “You brought food?” he asked hopefully, eyeing the basket.

Amphitrite laughed, bright as bells. “Among other things, dear Tyson.” She reached into the basket and drew out the first parcel, wrapped in sea-silk dyed gold. “For you, Tyson, son of my husband, who is my son in all the ways that matter. Open it.”

Tyson tore it open with massive, careful fingers. Inside was a set of tools — not Celestial bronze weapons, but tools. A hammer with a coral handle. A set of chisels that shimmered like fish scales. A leather apron stamped with a cyclops eye surrounded by waves.

“For your building,” Amphitrite said. “Hephaestus tells me you have gifted hands. These will not break, and they will remember the shape of what you intend, even when you doubt. And —” she added another smaller bundle, “peanut butter. From Atlantis. Made with sea peanuts. Which are actually — well. Try it.”

Tyson’s eye went shiny. “Thank you, Mother,” he whispered. He said the word like he’d been saving it. “I will make you a palace! Or a birdhouse! Whichever you want first!”

“A birdhouse,” Amphitrite said gravely. “Seabirds need homes too.” She turned to Triton and her expression turned wry and fond. “Triton, my barnacle. Come here.”

Triton edged closer.

“You have been prickly with your brothers,” she said, not unkindly. “You will apologize and you will mean it. But you are also my son and I love your prickly heart. This is for you.” She handed him a parcel wrapped in dark green.

Triton unwrapped it awkwardly. Inside was a new conch — smaller than his war horn, polished to a rose-gold sheen. When he lifted it, it played a faint tune on its own, something briny and brave.

“A calling conch,” Amphitrite explained to the table. “Blow it when you are lonely and do not know how to say so. It will call me. Or, if you prefer —” she glanced at Percy and Tyson, “— it will call them.”

Triton stared at the shell. His green cheeks darkened. “Mother —”

“Hush.” She kissed his forehead. He allowed it, which for Triton was equivalent to weeping with joy. Then she turned to Percy.

Percy’s heart hammered.

“Perseus Jackson.” Amphitrite studied him. “My husband speaks of you constantly. You have his stubbornness and his temper and, fortunately, your mother’s heart, which saves you.”

At the mention of Sally, Percy’s throat closed.

“I never met Sally Jackson,” Amphitrite said. “But your father told me about her, and I know how carefully you keep the things she left behind. You were two when we lost her. You are allowed to ask about her now, or to miss her on days when you cannot quite explain why. I have been here for the years since, Percy. Neither love needs to push the other away.”

She pushed the last parcel across the table. It was wrapped in plain blue cotton, soft with age. It looked mortal.

Percy unwrapped it with shaking hands.

Inside was a shawl. Hand-knitted, deep blue, with a pattern of waves at the border that shifted almost imperceptibly, like real water. It smelled faintly of lavender and salt.

“It belonged to a mortal woman who loved the sea and was loved by it,” Amphitrite said quietly, so only their end of the table could hear. “I blessed it long ago. It will keep whoever wears it warm — truly warm, down to the soul — even in the coldest depths. I thought —” she glanced at Annabeth, with a queen’s knowing eyes, “— it might be for someone you wish to keep safe when you cannot be there.”

Percy couldn’t speak. Annabeth’s hand tightened on his arm.

“Thank you,” he managed. “My lady — thank you. This is —”

“You can bring Sally’s memory to me,” she said. “You don’t have to choose between us.” She looked at Tyson, who was already strapping on his apron. “And that door is open to you too. To your sisters, whenever they wish.”

Tyson had started to cry openly, great happy tears splashing into his peanut butter. Triton looked away, jaw working, but he shifted his chair half an inch closer to Tyson. Percy felt his own eyes burn.

“Thank you — Mother,” Percy said, letting the familiar word carry the old grief too. It still felt strange and right, a place in the family that he was learning to inhabit.

Amphitrite’s eyes glistened. She rose, composed again in an instant. “Now. I will take tea with Chiron and frighten the harpies, and then I will return to the sea before your head counselor Dionysus accuses me of upstaging him.” A ripple of laughter. “Be kind to one another, my sons.”

She left the way she came, Triton trailing after her, blowing experimentally on his new conch and producing a startled seagull squawk that made Tyson roar with laughter.

The pavilion exhaled and burst back into noise.

But at the Poseidon table, the three of them — Percy, Annabeth holding Percy’s hand, Tyson clutching his hammer — sat quietly around the gifts, feeling like the ground had shifted under them in a good way.

Thalia, who had watched the whole thing with her arms crossed and her expression unreadable, finally reached over and fingered the edge of the blue shawl.

“Nice,” she said gruffly. “Sea queen’s got taste.”

Percy folded the shawl carefully and set it in his lap instead of putting it away. He didn’t want to let go of it yet.

---

The letter arrived at noon.

Percy knew something was wrong because Annabeth came to find him at the forge, where he was helping Tyson test his new hammer, and her face was doing the thing it did when she was bracing for someone else’s pain.

“It’s Thalia,” Annabeth said without preamble. “A letter came by wind spirit. From California.”

Percy set down the tongs. “Beryl?”

Annabeth nodded. “She’s at the Big House. Chiron’s with her. She asked for you.”

Thalia Grace did not cry. That was the rule. She’d cried exactly twice in front of Percy that he knew of — once on the Princess Andromeda when she thought Annabeth was dead, and once after a patrol when she had thought she might lose Annabeth again. Both times she’d been furious about it.

When Percy and Annabeth pushed open the Big House parlor door, Thalia wasn’t crying. She was sitting rigid in Chiron’s armchair, a single sheet of lined paper clutched in both hands, knuckles white. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry.

Chiron nodded to them and withdrew tactfully to the porch, giving them privacy.

“Hey,” Percy said gently, sitting on the ottoman in front of her. Annabeth perched on the arm of the chair, close enough that her knee pressed Thalia’s shoulder.

Thalia held out the paper without a word.

It was short. Brutally short, in a shaky hand Percy recognized from old grocery lists Thalia kept folded in her jacket pocket like talismans.

*Thalia —*

*60 days sober today. They gave me a chip. I thought you should know. I’m at the place in Ojai again, the nice one not the — well you know. Your brother helped arrange the ride with a trusted camp adult. Jason’s good. You looked after him when you could, which is more than I did.*

*I’m sorry. I know I say that. I mean it this time but I know that doesn’t fix it. A lady here says sorry is a door not a house. You don’t have to live in it. I liked that.*

*Don’t write back if you don’t want. Just wanted you to know I’m trying.*

*— Mom (Beryl)*

Percy read it twice before he realized Thalia was watching him read it twice.

“She read it twice,” Annabeth murmured later, telling it like a detail that mattered. And it did. Thalia read everything once, fast, like ripping off a bandage. Twice meant she was letting it sink in.

“It’s sober,” Percy said carefully. “That’s — that’s good, right? Sixty days?”

Thalia laughed, a short, harsh sound. “Yeah. It’s good. It’s the sixth or seventh time it’s been good. Last time she was ‘good’ she showed up at my school with a cheetah-print coat and no shoes and told my principal she was Hera.” She pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes. “Gods, I hate this. I hate that my hands are shaking over four sentences.”

Annabeth put her hand on the back of Thalia’s neck, solid and warm. “It makes sense they’re shaking.”

“She calls herself Mom in the letter,” Thalia said muffled. “She never calls herself Mom. It’s always Beryl. Like we’re sisters. Like she’s embarrassed to claim it.”

Percy didn’t know what to say. His instincts were all wrong here — he wanted to fix it, fight it, stab it with Riptide. He settled for putting his hand over Thalia’s boot, grounding.

“Do you want me to hold onto it?” Annabeth asked. “Or — do you want to answer?”

Thalia dropped her hands. Her eyes were bright, angry. “I don’t know. What am I supposed to say? ‘Congrats on two months, sorry you set the house on fire when I was seven’? ‘Thanks for forgetting Jason’s birthday three years running’?” Her voice cracked. “She says Jason arranged the ride. He’s still a kid. He shouldn’t have to arrange help for his drunk mom.”

“No,” Percy agreed quietly. “He shouldn’t.”

Thalia looked at him then, really looked. “Your mom never — she never did this, right? From what you’ve told me, Sally was — she was perfect.”

Percy flinched. “She wasn’t perfect —”

“She was good,” Thalia said. “She was there, in your stories. You said she packed you blue lunches and fought Gabe to keep you safe. My mom —” She gestured helplessly at the letter. “She loved Zeus more than she loved us. Or she loved the idea of him. And when he left, she just… dissolved. And I had to be the mom. For Jason. I was nine.”

Annabeth’s fingers tightened on Thalia’s neck. Percy saw the flicker cross Annabeth’s face — the old Athena memories and the missing Beryl memories, the complicated thing she rarely talked about.

Thalia saw it too. “What about you?” she asked Annabeth. “What’s it like remembering a goddess as your mom, when you’re my sister here? Someone who remembered your birthday but sent you a book on architecture instead of a hug?”

Annabeth gave a wry, pained smile. “We’re really doing this? Trauma bonding in Chiron’s parlor?”

“We’re good at it,” Thalia said. “It’s practically a camp activity.”

Annabeth sighed and leaned back, staring at the ceiling beams where a stuffed hippocampus head stared back. “Athena, in the life I remember — she wasn’t cruel. That’s what people don’t get. She’s not Beryl. She’s not neglectful in a human way. She remembers everything. She sent me a letter when I was seven explaining why spiders are perfect geometric predators and why I shouldn’t be afraid of them, with diagrams. She visited in dreams and quizzed me. She was proud of me when I built something well. I could feel it.” She paused. “But it’s like being loved by a library. It’s vast and quiet and it doesn’t tuck you in. When I ran away at seven, part of me was waiting to see if she’d stop me. She didn’t. She respected my autonomy. That’s very Athena. And very lonely.”

Thalia nodded slowly. “Memory and reality,” Thalia said slowly. “You remember a mother I never knew that way. And our mother is here, trying again, but you can’t remember those years. I wish I could forget half of them.”

“That’s — yeah,” Annabeth whispered. “That’s exactly it.”

Percy listened, feeling out of his depth and honored to be allowed in. These were the two strongest girls he knew, daughters of Zeus, one carrying memories of a different mother, reduced to kids wondering why their mothers couldn’t love them right.

“My mom used to say,” he offered hesitantly, “that parents are just people who got there first. That they’re making it up too.”

Thalia snorted, but softer now. “Sally Jackson, philosopher queen.”

“She was smart,” Percy said. “Smarter than me, anyway.”

Annabeth reached down and took his hand, threading their fingers together over Thalia’s shoulder, making a chain. “What are you going to do about the letter?” she asked Thalia.

Thalia stared at the paper for a long time. Then she folded it, carefully, along its original creases, and tucked it into the inside pocket of her jacket — the one over her heart, where she kept Jason’s photo and a broken arrowhead Zoë had given her after a camp drill.

“I’m going to keep it,” she said. “I’m going to wait a week. If she’s still sober in a week, I’ll write back. Something short. Something that doesn’t give her everything. A door, not a house.” She looked up, almost shy. “That lady in Ojai’s right.”

“A door, not a house,” Annabeth repeated. “I like that.”

Thalia stood abruptly, rolling her shoulders like she was shaking off a weight. “Okay. Enough feelings. I’m going to go shoot something. Annabeth, you in? Loser cleans the Pegasus stalls.”

Annabeth glanced at Percy, asking silently if he’d be okay left out. He nodded, smiled. “Go. I’ve got forge duty.”

Thalia paused at the door. “Hey, Percy —” She hesitated. “Thanks. For not saying ‘it’ll be okay.’ I hate when people say that.”

“It might be okay,” Percy said. “Or it might not. But you’ve got us.”

Thalia’s grin was quick and real. “Cheesy, Kelp Head. But — yeah.” She left, Annabeth close behind her, and Percy sat alone in the quiet parlor for a moment, holding the echo of Sally’s wisdom and Beryl’s shaky handwriting in his head at the same time.

---

Silena Beauregard found him by the lake that afternoon.

He was sitting on the dock with his feet in the water, the blue shawl folded beside him, watching Tyson and Triton out in the deeper water. The two brothers — half-brothers — were taking turns blowing the calling conch and laughing every time it made a rude noise. Triton kept pretending he wasn’t having fun and failing spectacularly. Tyson had already decided Triton was his best friend forever and was telling him so at top volume.

Silena sat down beside Percy without asking, swinging her legs, her long dark hair pulled back with a pink ribbon that matched her shirt borrowed from the Aphrodite decorating crew. She’d been crying, Percy realized. Her eyes were puffy, but she smiled when he looked.

“Amphitrite was here,” she said. “I heard. That was nice. A mom showing up.”

Percy nodded. “Yeah. It was — a lot. In a good way.”

Silena was quiet for a moment, watching the waves. Then: “Can I ask you something weird? What was she like? Your mom. Sally.”

Percy blinked. “Yeah. Of course. What do you want to know?”

“Everything,” Silena said simply. “What she smelled like. What she cooked. If she sang.”

Percy studied her. Silena rarely talked about her mortal parent. Everyone knew her father was Poseidon, but her mortal mom was a subject she avoided. He’d heard that she had cut contact with Silena, moved abroad, remarried, and had mortal children who would never know they had a demigod half-sister.

“My mom smelled like lemon cleaner and books,” Percy said. “She cleaned other people’s houses to pay rent, so she always smelled faint. And she cooked blue food. Blue pancakes, blue cookies, blue spaghetti once which was a mistake. She sang, yeah. Terrible voice. She’d make up lyrics when she forgot them. She used to sing ‘My Favorite Things’ but replace all the things with blue things. ‘Blue umbrellas and blue noodles and blue…’ I don’t remember. It was bad.”

Silena laughed wetly. “That sounds amazing.”

“She was — she was stubborn,” Percy went on, warming to it. The words came easier by the water. “Gabe — my stepdad, he was awful. He hit me once. Just once. She stood between us with a kitchen knife and told him if he ever touched me again she’d kill him and make it look like an accident. I was eight. I believed her. I think he believed her too.”

Silena’s breath caught. “Good.”

“She stayed with him to keep me safe, which is messed up when you think about it. She married a loser so his smell would hide my demigod scent. She sacrificed years of her life to keep monsters off me. And she never made me feel guilty about it. Not once. She just — she saw me. Even when I was getting kicked out of every school, even when I couldn’t read right, she’d look at me like I was the best thing she’d ever made. Which, objectively, I wasn’t. I was a punk kid who got into fights.”

“You were her kid,” Silena said. “That was enough.”

Percy nodded, throat tight. “Yeah.”

Silena picked at her ribbon. “My mom — her name’s Maria. She was beautiful. Like, real model beautiful, not Aphrodite beautiful. She did pageants. She met Poseidon and it was whirlwind and then I came along and I was… too much. Too emotional. Cried too loud. Needed too much. When I was six she told me I was ‘exhausting to love.’” Her voice wobbled. “I remember the exact words. Exhausting to love. After I came to camp she moved to Spain. She sends a Christmas card every year. Just signed ‘M.’ No note.”

Percy felt sick. “Silena — I’m sorry. That’s —”

“It’s okay,” she said quickly, wiping her eyes. “I have Beckendorf. I have my sister and my brother. I have friends in Aphrodite’s cabin, who are… a lot, but they try in their way. They gave me a mirror that only shows me how my friends see me. Did you know that? So I’d stop hating my nose.” She smiled shakily. “Families are complicated. Even the kind you make for yourself.”

Percy thought of Amphitrite’s shawl, Beryl’s letter, Athena’s diagrams.

“Do you ever write back?” he asked. “To the M cards?”

Silena shook her head. “I stopped. It hurt more to try. Maybe that makes me a bad daughter.”

“No,” Percy said firmly. “It makes you someone who’s protecting herself. There’s a difference.”

Silena leaned her head on his shoulder, sisterly and tired. They sat like that while Tyson attempted to teach Triton how to do a cannonball and Triton attempted to maintain divine dignity while doing a cannonball and failed.

“Thank you for telling me about Sally,” Silena said after a while. “It helps. To know some moms get it right. That it’s possible.”

Percy put his arm around her shoulders and squeezed. “She’d have liked you. She loved everybody’s kids. Our apartment was always full of stray friends. She’d have fed you blue cookies and asked about your love life and embarrassed me.”

Silena laughed for real this time. “Tell Annabeth I said thanks for sharing you for mom talk.”

“Will do.”

She stood, brushing off her shorts, more composed. “I’m going to go help my friends with the bonfire decorations. We’re doing lanterns tonight. For — you know. For absent mothers. And present ones. Clarisse thought of it. Don’t tell her I told you she’s sentimental.”

Percy grinned. “Secret’s safe.”

Silena walked back up the beach, ribbon fluttering, and Percy stayed on the dock, feet trailing in the cool water, thinking about mothers who stay and mothers who leave and mothers who visit across the sea with gifts.

---

Dinner that night was different.

Someone — rumor said Clarisse, with Piper and Silena conspiring — had set paper lanterns all around the pavilion. No electric lights. Just warm gold glows bobbing in the evening breeze, each with a name written on it in marker. Some said MOMS. Some said specific names: SALLY. MARIA. BERYL. ATHENA. AMPHITRITE. MAY CASTELLAN. Even GABE got crossed out and replaced with PAUL, which made Percy laugh so hard he snorted milk.

Chiron didn’t give a speech. He just raised his glass and said, “To mothers — mortal, divine, and chosen. To those who raised us, those who failed us, and those who are still trying.”

“To mothers!” the camp echoed, and the sound rolled out over the hills.

The food tasted better by lantern light. Tyson had saved seats for Triton, who actually came, sitting stiffly at the Poseidon table and pretending not to be pleased when Tyson piled his plate high. Amphitrite’s tools glinted at Tyson’s belt. The blue shawl was draped carefully over Annabeth’s shoulders now — Percy had insisted when the evening turned cool — and she kept touching the edge of it absently, like she couldn’t quite believe how soft it was.

Thalia sat beside the Iris-message bowl, showing Jason at Camp Jupiter Beryl’s letter. Percy watched Jason read the lines twice from the other side of the rainbow, lips moving silently, then place his palm against the image of his sister’s hand. Siblings separated for years could still recognize the weight.

Silena sat with her Aphrodite friends, laughing at something Drew said, her eyes brighter than they’d been that afternoon. When she caught Percy’s eye across the pavilion, she lifted her cup to him. He lifted his back.

After dinner, instead of the usual sing-along, the campers drifted down to the beach in small clusters. Someone built a low bonfire. Someone else passed out sparklers. The waves were gentle, phosphorescent at the edges. Tyson and Triton took turns telling increasingly exaggerated stories about Atlantis — Tyson’s involved a giant peanut butter statue, Triton’s involved defeating a kraken single-handedly, both were clearly lies and everyone loved them.

Percy found himself sitting a little apart with Beckendorf’s old crew, listening to Clarisse — of all people — talk quietly about her mom.

“She sends me caramel corn,” Clarisse was saying, almost defensively. “Every Halloween. With a note that says ‘Don’t share with monsters.’ Like I’m still six. Ares thinks it’s stupid. I save every note.”

“It’s not stupid,” Chris Rodriguez said, taking her hand. “My mom still cuts the crusts off my toast when I visit. I’m hardly little anymore.”

Found-family warmth settled over the beach like the blanket of stars overhead. These were kids who’d been abandoned, hunted, used as pawns by gods and titans, and here they were, trading mom stories like trading cards, laughing and crying and passing the marshmallows.

Percy felt Annabeth settle beside him in the sand, the blue shawl wrapped tight around both of them now. She smelled like vanilla and campfire smoke and the lemon soap from the Zeus cabin showers.

“Hey,” she whispered.

“Hey.”

They sat in comfortable silence, watching the firelight dance on the water. Thalia was nearby, whittling as she talked to Jason through the rainbow bowl set beside the fire. Silena was braiding a younger Aphrodite kid’s hair. Tyson had fallen asleep with his head on Triton’s shoulder, and Triton — miracle of miracles — hadn’t moved, just sat very still, looking out at the sea like he was memorizing it.

Annabeth tugged the shawl closer. “Thank you for letting me wear this,” she said. “It really is warm. Like — deeper than warm.”

Percy looked at her. The lantern light caught the gray in her eyes, turned them gold-green. Sand in her hair. A smudge of marshmallow on her chin. The girl who’d held the sky, who’d outwitted spiders and sphinxes, who’d sat with Thalia that afternoon and talked about being loved by a library.

His chest ached with how much he loved her.

“Annabeth,” he said quietly, so only she could hear under the crackle of the fire and the surf. “I mean this. My mom — Sally — she did love you, in the life we remember.”

Annabeth went very still. “Percy —”

“No, listen.” He took her hand under the shawl, where it was warm. “Not just liked you. Loved you. She took one look at you organizing my sock drawer by color and threat level and decided you were the best thing that ever happened to me. She tried to feed you every time you came over and asked you a million questions about your projects and pretended not to notice when we held hands under the table. She told you embarrassing stories about me and then winked at you like you were co-conspirators. She —” His voice broke. “She always wanted me to find someone who sees me. Really sees me. The dyslexic punk kid and the hero and all of it. That’s you. You’ve always seen me.”

Annabeth’s eyes filled. She blinked hard, but a tear escaped anyway, tracking through the sand on her cheek. She didn’t wipe it.

“Percy Jackson,” she whispered, “are you trying to make me cry in front of the whole camp?”

“A little,” he admitted. “Is it working?”

She laughed wetly and leaned her forehead against his. “Athena, the mother I remember — she never really got this. She didn’t get why I’d choose —” She gestured at him, at the fire, at the messy, loud, loving chaos around them. “A boy who can’t sit still. A family that’s not logical. But sitting here, with your mom’s — with this shawl that smells like the sea and safety — I think I finally understand what Sally knew. That love isn’t a theorem. It’s a choice you make every day. To show up. To stay.”

Percy kissed the tear away, gentle as a promise. “Stay,” he murmured. “Stay with me.”

“I’m here,” Annabeth said. “I’m not going anywhere.”

Around them, the bonfire popped. Someone started a soft song. The lanterns swayed. Thalia caught Percy’s eye over the flames and raised an eyebrow at how close he and Annabeth were sitting, then grinned and deliberately looked away, giving them privacy in the middle of everyone. Silena blew them a kiss. Tyson snored. Triton finally, carefully, rested his cheek against the top of Tyson’s head and closed his eyes.

Percy wrapped the blue shawl tighter around Annabeth’s shoulders and tucked her head under his chin, breathing in vanilla and smoke and salt. He thought about Sally in her blue bathrobe, about Amphitrite crossing a dining pavilion with a basket of gifts, about Beryl’s shaky *Mom* in blue ink, about Athena’s diagrams and Maria’s initial-only cards and all the ways mothers love and fail and try again.

He thought about the family he’d been born to and the family he’d chosen and how, tonight, they felt like the same thing.

Above the beach, the stars wheeled bright and cold and ancient. Below, the sea murmured to the shore, patient as a mother humming off-key in a sunlit kitchen.

Annabeth’s hand found his under the shawl and held on.

And Percy — son of Poseidon, son of Sally, brother, friend, boyfriend — held on back, and stayed.
