## Chapter 99: Mothers

The morning came in blue and gold.

Percy woke before the conch horn, which almost never happened. The light in Cabin Three was watery, filtered through the shell-chimes Annabeth had hung by the window last summer and never taken down. They chimed faintly though there was no wind. Tyson, on an approved weekend break from the forges, was snoring in the guest bunk across the room, one huge arm hanging off the side, fingers almost brushing the floor. His bedspread — lovingly stitched by a harpy with questionable taste — had slid half off. On the floor between their bunks lay a half-finished game of Mythomagic cards and a peanut butter sandwich Tyson had saved for later and then forgotten.

Percy lay there for a moment, listening to the surf.

Camp Half-Blood in late August had a particular sound. Cicadas in the strawberry fields. The far-off clang from the forge. Somebody already arguing good-naturedly at the climbing wall. And underneath it all, the sea, steady as breathing.

He thought about his mom.

It wasn’t an anniversary or anything. It was just one of those mornings when Sally Jackson, alive in the old life he remembered and gone since he was two in this one, felt very close. Maybe it was the light. She’d always loved mornings like this, when the apartment kitchen would fill up with sun and she’d stand at the stove in her blue bathrobe making blue pancakes and humming off-key. She’d burn the first one every time. She’d declare it a sacrifice to the gods and eat it herself.

Percy smiled into his pillow.

He got up quietly, pulled on shorts and a Camp tee, and slipped outside. The steps of Cabin Three were cool under his bare feet. The lake glittered. Far out past the canoe dock, something large broke the surface and sank again, smooth as oil. He didn’t think much of it.

Annabeth was already awake, of course. She was sitting on the steps of Cabin One with her laptop balanced on her knees and a pencil stuck through her bun. She saw him and lit up in that way she still did, after everything, like his appearance was a pleasant surprise even when it was completely predictable.

“Hey, Seaweed Brain,” she called softly, so as not to wake the rest of her cabin.

“Hey, Wise Girl.” He crossed the grass, still damp with dew, and sat beside her, shoulder to shoulder. “You’re up early.”

“Daedalus laptop overheated again. I woke up thinking about load-bearing walls.” She leaned her head against his shoulder for exactly three seconds — their morning ration of affection before the camp woke up and made them be responsible counselors. “You okay? You have that face.”

“What face?”

“The missing-her face.”

Percy didn’t bother denying it. She didn't make him explain the face.

“Yeah,” he said. “Just woke up thinking about her. Blue pancakes.”

Annabeth closed the laptop and set it aside. She laced her fingers through his. “Tell me one thing about her I don’t know.”

He thought. After years together in the life they remembered, Annabeth knew most of the Sally stories. The seven-layer dip. The time she’d taken on Gabe with a frying pan just by threatening to. The way she could quote whole passages of books from memory.

“She used to leave notes in my lunch,” he said finally. “Even when we had no money. Even when it was just, like, stale crackers and an apple. There’d be a napkin in there that said ‘You’re my hero’ or ‘Be kind today’ or ‘Extra blue luck.’ In high school, I was embarrassed. I’d crumple them up fast so nobody’d see. Now I’d give anything to find one I missed at the bottom of an old backpack.”

Annabeth squeezed his hand hard. “She sounds like she’d have strong opinions about my filing system.”

“She loved your filing system,” Percy said. “She loved you.”

Annabeth looked down quickly, but not before he saw the pink climb her cheeks. The old life stood between them in a way nobody else at camp could see. She had known Sally there; in this life she knew her through Percy’s stories, and neither kind of knowing could be handed over whole.

“I wish I could remember the notes,” Annabeth said. “Not what you’ve told me. The actual paper. The handwriting. The way the ink looked after it got damp in your backpack.”

“Blue pen, usually. Sometimes she drew a tiny fish if she had time.” He watched the lake through the trees. “I can picture it. I can’t prove to anybody that it happened.”

“You don’t have to prove it to me.”

He knew that. Still, he had spent enough of this life hearing his own history told back to him by other people, neat and certain: Sally had died when he was two; Poseidon had taken him to Atlantis; Amphitrite had helped raise him. The facts fit on a page. The ache did not. In the life he remembered, Sally had been a person who packed lunches, complained about rent, burned pancakes, and stayed. When he woke here, the memory was his and the childhood was not.

“Sometimes I don’t know whether I miss her or miss the boy who got to have her,” he said. “Then I remember those are the same thing, at least from where I’m standing.”

Annabeth shifted closer. She didn’t offer a clean answer. Her thumb moved once over his knuckle, then stopped. “Do you want to tell me another story, or do you want to sit until the conch?”

“Sit.”

They listened to the camp wake. A screen door banged somewhere uphill; a pair of satyrs argued over a bucket. Annabeth’s laptop stayed shut beside her. When the conch finally sounded, she stood first and offered him her hand. He took it. Neither of them made a speech about what the morning had meant.

---

Breakfast was loud.

That was normal. The dining pavilion buzzed with end-of-summer energy, everybody trying to cram in one more prank, one more race, one more late-night council meeting before the year-rounders were left with the quiet. The harpies swooped and shrieked. At the Poseidon table, Tyson was happily eating an entire platter of bacon and explaining to a wide-eyed new kid why peanut butter belonged on everything.

“Even on pizza?” the kid asked.

Tyson nodded solemnly. “Especially on pizza.”

Percy laughed and ruffled his brother’s hair as he sat down. Annabeth slid in beside him — Zeus and Poseidon tables had long since given up enforcing seating — with Thalia right behind her, trailing pine needles and attitude.

Thalia Grace looked like she’d slept in a tree, which she probably had. Her black hair was pulled back in a messy ponytail, her eyes lined dark, her camp shirt cut off at the sleeves to show the faded camp bracelet and the scatter of scars she never explained. She dropped her tray with a clatter and stole three of Percy’s grapes.

“Morning, Kelp Head. Morning, Annabeth.”

“Morning,” they chorused.

“You get the Iris-message?” Thalia asked, lower, glancing toward the head table where Chiron was talking quietly with Argus. “About, uh, visitors?”

Percy shook his head. “No. Who?”

Before Thalia could answer, the wind changed.

It came off the water, salt-sweet and cold even in the heat, with a sound like a conch shell held to the ear. The torches around the pavilion flickered green-blue. Conversations stuttered and died.

Several campers turned toward the beach path. Others leaned sideways to see around the tables.

She walked up from the sea as if the hill were level ground.

Amphitrite, Queen of the Sea and wife of Poseidon, looked different here in the crowded pavilion. Percy was used to her lessons and letters; seeing her at a camp table still caught him by surprise. The smile she gave the campers was warm, though tiredness showed at the corners of her eyes.

She wore a peplos the color of deep water, embroidered at the hem with hippocamps in silver thread. Her dark hair was braided back with pearls. Her skin glowed faintly, as if lit from within by phosphorescence. In her hands she carried a woven basket overflowing with cloth-wrapped shapes. Behind her, half-hiding behind a dune, was Triton, her son, green-skinned and scowling in a way that was clearly armor.

Chiron rose to greet her, bowing deeply. “My lady. Camp Half-Blood is honored.”

Amphitrite smiled. “Thank you, old friend. I will not keep your campers from their breakfast long. I have come on a mother’s errand.” Her eyes found Cabin Three’s table unerringly. “May I?”

The whole pavilion held its breath as she approached.

Percy stood so fast he knocked his knee on the table. Tyson stood too, beaming, bacon still in hand. Annabeth stood with them, automatically, hand finding Percy’s elbow. Thalia straightened, respectful despite herself.

“Lady Amphitrite,” Percy said. His throat felt dry. He’d faced Kronos. He’d held the sky. This was somehow worse. “Hi.”

Her smile softened. “Perseus. May I sit?”

He nodded, dumbly, and the visitors at the Poseidon table scrambled to make room; Clarisse and Silena were still on morning duty. She sat with impossible grace on a sticky camp bench, set the basket on the table, and looked at each of them in turn — Percy, Tyson, and then Triton, whom she beckoned forward with a flick of her fingers.

“Come, Triton.”

Triton slouched forward, arms crossed, twin tails of his armor clinking. He was taller than Percy, older in god-years, and currently wearing the expression of a teenager forced to attend a family reunion.

“Mother,” he muttered.

“Be polite,” Amphitrite said mildly, but with steel underneath. She turned back to Percy and Tyson. “I have let palace duties crowd out visits here. And I want to do better by you too, Tyson. A queen should make room for family. A mother should make room still.”

Tyson blinked his one big eye. “You brought food?” he asked hopefully, eyeing the basket.

Amphitrite laughed, bright as bells. “Among other things, dear Tyson.” She reached into the basket and drew out the first parcel, wrapped in sea-silk dyed gold. “For you, Tyson, son of my husband, who is my son in all the ways that matter. Open it.”

Tyson tore it open with massive, careful fingers. Inside was a set of tools — not Celestial bronze weapons, but tools. A hammer with a coral handle. A set of chisels that shimmered like fish scales. A leather apron stamped with a cyclops eye surrounded by waves.

“For your building,” Amphitrite said. “Hephaestus tells me you have gifted hands. These will not break, and they will remember the shape of what you intend, even when you doubt. And —” she added another smaller bundle, “peanut butter. From Atlantis. Made with sea peanuts. Which are actually — well. Try it.”

Tyson’s eye went shiny. “Thank you, Mother,” he whispered. He said the word like he’d been saving it. “I will make you a palace! Or a birdhouse! Whichever you want first!”

“A birdhouse,” Amphitrite said gravely. “Seabirds need homes too.” She turned to Triton and her expression turned wry and fond. “Triton, my barnacle. Come here.”

Triton edged closer.

“You have been prickly with your brothers,” she said, not unkindly. “You will apologize and you will mean it. But you are also my son and I love your prickly heart. This is for you.” She handed him a parcel wrapped in dark green.

Triton unwrapped it awkwardly. Inside was a new conch — smaller than his war horn, polished to a rose-gold sheen. When he lifted it, it played a faint tune on its own, something briny and brave.

“A calling conch,” Amphitrite explained to the table. “Blow it when you are lonely and do not know how to say so. It will call me. Or, if you prefer —” she glanced at Percy and Tyson, “— it will call them.”

Triton stared at the shell. His green cheeks darkened. “Mother —”

“Hush.” She kissed his forehead. He allowed it, which for Triton was equivalent to weeping with joy. Then she turned to Percy.

Percy’s heart hammered.

“Perseus Jackson.” Amphitrite studied him. “My husband speaks of you constantly. You have his stubbornness and his temper and, fortunately, your mother’s heart, which saves you.”

At the mention of Sally, Percy’s throat closed.

“I never met Sally Jackson,” Amphitrite said. “Your father has told me who she was to you. She died when you were two; that doesn’t mean you cannot miss her or ask about her. I was here for the years after, Percy. You don’t owe me silence to make room for her.”

She pushed the last parcel across the table. It was wrapped in plain blue cotton, soft with age. It looked mortal.

Percy unwrapped it with shaking hands.

Inside was a shawl. Hand-knitted, deep blue, with a pattern of waves at the border that shifted almost imperceptibly, like real water. It smelled faintly of lavender and salt.

“It belonged to a mortal woman who loved the sea and was loved by it,” Amphitrite said quietly, so only their end of the table could hear. “I blessed it long ago. It will keep whoever wears it warm — truly warm, down to the soul — even in the coldest depths. I thought —” she glanced at Annabeth, with a queen’s knowing eyes, “— it might be for someone you wish to keep safe when you cannot be there.”

Percy couldn’t speak. Annabeth’s hand tightened on his arm.

“Thank you,” he managed. “My lady — thank you. This is —”

“You can bring Sally’s memory to me,” she said. “You don’t have to choose between us.” She looked at Tyson, who was already strapping on his apron. “And that door is open to you too. To your sisters, whenever they wish.”

Tyson had started to cry openly, great happy tears splashing into his peanut butter. Triton looked away, jaw working, but he shifted his chair half an inch closer to Tyson. Percy felt his own eyes burn.

“Thank you — Mom,” Percy said, letting the familiar word carry the old grief too. It still felt strange and right, a place in the family that he was learning to inhabit.

Amphitrite’s eyes glistened. She rose, composed again in an instant. “Now. I will take tea with Chiron and frighten the harpies, and then I will return to the sea before your head counselor Dionysus accuses me of upstaging him.” A ripple of laughter. “Be kind to one another, my sons.”

She left the way she came, Triton trailing after her, blowing experimentally on his new conch and producing a startled seagull squawk that made Tyson roar with laughter.

For a few breaths the pavilion stayed quiet. Then a chair scraped, someone at the Hermes table asked for the salt, and the dining hall began its usual racket again. The harpies resumed arguing over plates. Chiron walked Amphitrite toward the porch, speaking low enough that Percy couldn't make out the words.

Tyson turned the hammer over in his hands. He traced the coral handle with one broad thumb, then slipped the leather apron off the table and folded it carefully across his knees. “I can use this at the forge,” he said. “I will ask first before the special chisels touch anything important.”

“That seems smart,” Percy said.

“And the peanut butter is for eating.” Tyson considered the jar. “I will ask before I eat all of it.”

Triton had made it only a few yards up the beach path before he turned and tested the conch again. This time it gave a low, wavering note. Amphitrite paused and looked back. Triton lowered the shell as if he hadn't meant to call her. She lifted a hand to him anyway, then continued toward the porch. He followed, shoulders a little less rigid.

Annabeth's fingers stayed around Percy's wrist. She hadn't tried to take the shawl or decide what he should do with it. “Do you want a minute?” she asked.

“Maybe later.” He refolded the cotton around the wool so it wouldn't catch on a fork. “I want to keep it with me for now.”

“Then keep it.”

One of the new campers at the end of the table leaned over, stopping short of the shawl. “Is she coming back tomorrow?”

Percy looked toward the porch. Chiron and Amphitrite were still talking; he couldn't hear them. “I don't know.”

The kid nodded, accepting the answer without asking for a promise Percy couldn't give. Tyson had already begun telling Triton, who was back at the table, how he planned to test the hammer on a scrap hinge. The conversation moved on to whether sea peanuts tasted better roasted. The moment did not disappear, but it stopped belonging to everyone at once.

At the Poseidon table, Percy sat with the shawl in his lap and watched Tyson show Triton the hammer's weight. Amphitrite had given each of them something they could choose how to use. Percy tried to let that be enough for the morning.

Breakfast resumed in pieces: a request for the salt, a bench scraping against the floor, Tyson laughing over the sea-peanut jar.

But at the Poseidon table, the three of them — Percy, Annabeth holding Percy’s hand, Tyson clutching his hammer — sat quietly around the gifts, feeling like the ground had shifted under them in a good way.

Thalia, who had watched the whole thing with her arms crossed and her expression unreadable, finally reached over and fingered the edge of the blue shawl.

“Nice,” she said gruffly. “Sea queen’s got taste.”

Percy folded the shawl carefully and set it in his lap instead of putting it away. He didn’t want to let go of it yet.

---

The letter arrived at noon.

Percy was at the forge with Tyson, testing the new hammer on a piece of scrap bronze, when Annabeth appeared in the doorway. She waited until Tyson had put the hammer down.

“Thalia’s at the Big House,” she said. “A letter came from California. Chiron has it. Thalia asked if you’d come over.”

Percy set down the tongs. “Beryl?”

“That’s what the envelope says.” Annabeth glanced at Tyson. “She asked for you. She said you can come too, if you want.”

Tyson wiped his hands on his apron. “I can keep testing this. It can wait.”

Percy looked at the hammer. He wanted to stay and make one more swing, something with a result he could see. “We’ll come back.”

The envelope was on the Big House table when they reached it, still sealed. It had a California postmark and Thalia’s name written across the front. Chiron stood by the window. Thalia was in a chair near the wall, her right palm bandaged and tucked inside her sleeve. The letter had not been opened.

“I asked Chiron to wait,” she said. “I wanted to do it with you two here.”

“You want the door closed?” Percy asked.

“Not locked. Just… less of an audience.” She looked at Annabeth. “You can stay.”

Chiron checked her face. “Would you like me to leave the letter here and step outside?”

“Yeah. Thanks.”

He moved to the porch, leaving the door open a few inches. Percy sat on the ottoman; Annabeth took the chair beside Thalia rather than the armrest. Thalia used her left hand to turn the envelope over. She checked the postmark, then the flap.

“It’s from her,” Percy said, though Thalia could read the name herself.

“I know.” She broke the seal and removed one sheet of lined paper. Her eyes traveled to the signature, then back to the first line. “Do you want to read it?”

“Only if you want me to.”

“I do.” She set the page on the table between them and held its lower edge with her left hand. “Read it out loud. Don’t make it sound nicer than it is.”

The note was short. The handwriting looked like the earlier letter Thalia had once shown him, each line tilting slightly down toward the right.

*Thalia —*

*60 days sober today. They gave me a chip. I thought you should know. I’m at the place in Ojai again, the nice one not the — well you know. Your brother helped arrange the ride with a trusted camp adult. Jason’s good. You looked after him when you could, which is more than I did.*

*I’m sorry. I know I say that. I mean it this time but I know that doesn’t fix it. A lady here says sorry is a door not a house. You don’t have to live in it. I liked that.*

*Don’t write back if you don’t want. Just wanted you to know I’m trying.*

*— Mom (Beryl)*

Percy read it once, then put the page down. The lines made claims about where Beryl was, how long she had been sober, and who had arranged a ride. The letter could tell him what Beryl said. It could not tell him what would happen next.

Thalia asked, “Did she say anything about a visit?”

“No.” Percy kept his voice level. “She says she’s at a place in Ojai. She says Jason helped arrange a ride with a camp adult. That’s what she wrote. She doesn’t say she’s coming here.”

Thalia nodded once. She read the note herself, slowly this time, and set it back on the table. The fingers of her left hand had started to tremble. Her wrapped right hand stayed tucked against her ribs.

“Sixty days,” she said. “Last time, she wrote thirty-eight. I remember the number because it was in the first letter.”

“That’s a longer stretch, if it’s accurate,” Annabeth said. “You don’t have to decide what it means today.”

Thalia looked toward the open door. “I know she’s trying. Or she says she is. I don’t know what I’m supposed to do with that.”

Percy had no answer that would make the paper weigh less. “You can keep it without answering.”

“Or I can throw it out without deciding whether she’s a bad person.” Thalia's mouth twisted. “I hate how every option sounds like a verdict.”

“Then don’t make it a verdict,” Annabeth said. “Make it one choice about one letter.”

Thalia leaned back and rubbed her forehead with the heel of her left hand. “When the first letter came, I wanted to believe it. I wanted to tell Jason Mom was okay. Then I thought about the Pine Tree. We waited there for six hours. Annabeth got sick. I can still see the road.”

“You told me,” Percy said. “I remember.”

“And you remember Sally.”

“I do.”

Thalia looked at him, eyes bright but steady. “Your stories make her sound like she never dropped anything. Like she was always there.”

“She wasn't perfect,” Percy said. “She could be stubborn and she got tired. She let me eat blue candy for breakfast once because I was sick and miserable and she didn't have the energy to fight me. But she came back when she said she would.” He paused. “That mattered more than her being right every time.”

Thalia turned the letter over. “I don’t know what my mom was like before the drinking. I have pieces. I know the person I got most of the time. I know I tried to keep Jason fed and out of trouble because somebody had to. I don't know if she loved Zeus more than us. I know what it felt like when he left and she wasn't there either.”

Annabeth's fingers were folded in her lap. “In the life I remember, Athena was my mother. She could be proud of me and still leave me alone with things I didn't understand. I used to think if I did something well enough, she'd come closer.” She looked at Thalia. “I can't give you a memory of Beryl that I don't have. I can tell you the one I do have, but it won't explain yours.”

“Good,” Thalia said. “I don’t need you to make them match.”

Annabeth nodded. “I won't.”

For a moment, Percy heard only the porch boards creaking under Chiron's weight and the wind stirring the leaves. The three of them had different mothers in different ways: one remembered a woman who had died when he was two and a woman who had raised him afterward; one carried a goddess from a life that Thalia had never lived; and Thalia had a letter from a mother who might be sober today. None of it lined up into one answer.

“The note says ‘Mom,’” Thalia said. She touched the signature with her left index finger. “Usually she signs Beryl. She used to do that even when we were little. I don't know if writing it now means anything.”

“It can mean she wrote it,” Percy said. “You don't have to decide more than that.”

Thalia gave a small, humorless laugh. “I asked for you because you don't jump to the part where everything is fixed.”

“I still want to,” he admitted. “I'm trying not to.”

“I can tell.”

Annabeth reached for the water pitcher and filled a glass. She set it within Thalia's reach without making her take it. Thalia drank, then moved the letter away from the damp ring on the table.

“I'm keeping it,” she said. “I'm not answering today. Maybe in a week I'll know whether I want to write one sentence. That doesn't mean I'm checking whether she's still sober every day. It's not my job to certify her.”

“That sounds like a choice you can change later,” Annabeth said.

“Yeah. A week is just a week. It isn't a test she knows she's taking.” Thalia folded the page once along its crease, then stopped. “Can you put it in the inside pocket? This hand's being useless.”

Percy stood and took the paper only after she offered it. He slid it into the pocket of her jacket, careful not to touch the bandage. She watched him do it and zipped the pocket herself with her left hand.

“Thanks.”

“Do you want us to leave?”

“Walk with me to the steps. Then I want a little time alone.”

They went outside together. Chiron remained on the porch, giving them the path without leaving the house unattended. At the top of the steps, Annabeth asked, “Want me nearby?”

“Nearby is good. No pep talk.”

“Understood.”

Thalia headed toward the path that curved around the Big House, not toward the archery range. The bandage still covered her right palm. She kept that hand close to her side and used her left to adjust the jacket pocket. Percy watched until she reached the bend, then turned back toward the forge with Annabeth.

“You okay?” she asked.

“I don't know.”

“That's allowed.”

He nodded. They didn't discuss what Beryl would do next, or what Thalia would decide in a week. They went back to Tyson and the hammer, where the scrap bronze still waited for another test.

Tyson had set the new tools in a row on the workbench, apart from the forge's usual kit. The apron lay folded beside them. He had wrapped the chisels in a square of cloth so their edges wouldn't touch.

“You don't have to put them away like museum things,” Percy said.

“I know.” Tyson turned the hammer once. “But I want to remember the first day I have it. Later, it will be a hammer I use every day. Today it is a gift.”

He chose a narrow strip of scrap bronze and clamped it in the vise. The hinge he had been testing sat beside it, seven tiny grooves visible along the pin. Tyson pointed to the first one. “This is from when I changed the angle. It clicked, but the hinge leaned.” He tapped the second. “This one was straight but too stiff. I don't want to make it beautiful before it works.”

“That sounds like a good order,” Percy said.

“Can you hold the scrap here?” Tyson asked. “Not near the edge. I will hit the other side.”

Percy took the scrap where Tyson indicated and braced it against the bench with his left hand. Tyson lifted the coral-handled hammer. It looked ordinary enough until it met the bronze; the ring that came back from the anvil was low and clear, not the usual sharp clang. Tyson paused to listen to it, then checked the strip for a bend.

“It didn't twist,” Percy said.

“Not yet.” Tyson made two more measured strikes. The handle fit his palm. He didn't need to strain his shoulder to control the head, and the bronze stayed where he wanted it. When he finished, he ran a finger along the edge without touching the hot part. “This is good.”

“The hammer?”

“The hammer. The work. Both.” Tyson smiled and set the tool down. “It doesn't make the choice. It helps my hand do what I chose.”

Percy thought of the letter folded in Thalia's pocket, of how badly he wanted an object that could take a hard problem and shape it into something simple. “That's a pretty useful gift.”

“Yes.” Tyson put the bent strip in the scrap bin rather than keeping it as a failed test. “The birdhouse will need to be strong enough for seabirds and not too heavy for a tree. I have not picked the tree. I will ask Chiron before I build it.”

“Good plan.”

“Do you think Mother will like it?”

The word came easily from Tyson. Percy still felt it catch in his own chest. “She asked for one.”

“She asked because I offered. She could have said a palace.” Tyson brushed bronze dust from the apron. “She knew I wanted to make something. She brought things for the work. I think that means she will like what I make, even if the first version is crooked.”

“Probably.” Percy picked up the hinge and tested it once, carefully, without forcing the pin. It moved, then stopped where Tyson had designed it to stop. “This one is better than the last.”

“It is number eight.” Tyson wrote the number on a scrap tag and tied it to the hinge. “I want to keep the tests until I know which one works.”

He asked Percy to hold the next strip. Percy did. Tyson took his time, checking the position before he lifted the hammer. When he was ready, he glanced at Percy and waited for the nod. The blow landed true. Neither brother said anything for a moment; there was only the soft tick of bronze cooling on the bench.

“You can go help Thalia again if you need,” Tyson said. “I can work by myself.”

“I know.” Percy let go of the strip. “I'm here because I want to be.”

Tyson's smile showed in the crease beside his eye. “Then hold this next one too.”

They finished the test without fixing anything that wasn't broken. Tyson marked which strip he would keep, washed the tool before the salt air could dull it, and set the hammer beside his apron rather than locking it away. When Percy stepped out of the forge, he could still hear Tyson explaining the eighth hinge to someone on the other side of the door.

---

Silena Beauregard found him on the sandy bank that afternoon, above the tide line and well clear of the dock. Percy had chosen a patch where the ground was firm. He sat with the blue shawl folded beside him, watching Tyson and Triton splash in the water farther down the shore. Triton had the calling conch tucked under one arm while Tyson tried to show him how to make a cannonball without soaking the shell. Neither brother seemed to be following the plan.

Triton came out of the water first. He stopped a few feet from Tyson and looked back toward the pavilion, where Amphitrite had gone with Chiron.

“Mother told me to apologize,” he said. “She was right. I've been rude to you and Percy. I'm sorry.”

Tyson's smile faded, but he didn't move away. “I accept.”

“You don't have to say that just because I said it.”

“I said it because I accept.” Tyson lifted the conch from the wet sand and checked that no water had collected inside. “Now you can help me not soak it.”

“I can do that.” Triton took the shell, held it level, and followed Tyson back into the shallows. The apology didn't change the way Triton stood or make him suddenly easy with his brothers. Percy watched them argue over the pebbles again, quieter than before. When Silena arrived with the lantern box, he turned his attention to her.

Silena carried a flat box of paper lantern sleeves and a coil of twine. She set them between them, then looked toward the water. Her eyes were pink around the edges; she offered no explanation, and Percy didn't ask.

“Clarisse wants these ready before dinner,” she said. “She had the idea. I got the box from the art cabinet. Piper's bringing clips.”

“Do you need help?”

“In a minute.” She sat on the grass rather than the sand and folded her legs beneath her. The ribbon in her hair was pink, tied unevenly. “Can I ask you something strange?”

“Sure.”

“What was Sally like? Not the big stories. The little ones. What she smelled like, what she cooked, whether she sang.”

Percy turned toward her. “I can tell you.”

“I heard about the blue cookies.”

“Those were good. The blue spaghetti was a mistake.”

“What made it blue?”

“Food coloring. She said the sauce was too red and needed balance.”

Silena smiled. “That sounds like someone who was committed to a bit.”

“She smelled like lemon cleaner and books,” Percy said. “She cleaned houses to pay rent, so the cleaner stayed on her sleeves. And she sang when she cooked. Terrible voice. She'd forget a line and make up something about blue umbrellas or noodles until she remembered the real words.”

“Did you hate it?”

“I pretended to. I used to put my hands over my ears. Then I'd ask her to sing it again.” He smiled at the water, where Tyson had emerged dripping and was explaining a new technique with both arms. “She left notes in my lunch, too. Even if all she could pack was crackers and an apple, she'd write something on a napkin. ‘Be kind today.’ ‘Extra blue luck.’ I used to crumple them up before anyone saw. I wish I had kept them.”

Silena ran one finger along the lip of the box. “I like that she wrote something. My mom mostly wrote her name.”

“Do you want to tell me about her?”

Silena watched Triton try to shake water from his hair without letting go of the conch. “Her name is Maria. When I was little, I thought she was the most beautiful person in the world. She did pageants. She met Poseidon and, from the story I was told, everything happened quickly. Then I was born, and she said I was too much. Too loud, too sensitive, too needy.”

She stopped and pressed her lips together. Percy waited.

“When I was six, she said I was ‘exhausting to love.’ I remember the words exactly. I didn't know what exhausting meant, so I asked her. She said I used up all her patience.” Silena tugged at the ribbon, then let it go. “After I came to camp, the cards started coming from Spain. At least, the postmarks were from Spain. They arrive around Christmas. They have her first initial, nothing else. I don't know what her life looks like now. I don't know whether she has other children. I don't want to guess.”

She took a breath and went on before Percy could answer. “My friends gave me a mirror that only shows me how they see me. It doesn't show me what Maria thinks. It doesn't make those cards hurt less. But sometimes I look at it and remember that the people who know me now don't look at my face and see a list of things to fix.”

“That sounds like a good gift.”

“It is. I don't use it every day.” She pushed a loose strand of hair behind her ear. “Some mornings I'm still mean to myself before I even get dressed. But I have it.”

“I was about to say Sally would've told Maria off,” Percy said. “I don't know that. I shouldn't make her into the answer to your family.”

Silena's shoulders lowered. “Thank you. I wanted to hear about her. I don't want to borrow her to explain mine.”

“That makes sense.”

“You can still tell me what she was like.” Silena tapped the lantern box. “Just don't tell me what she would've done about a person she never met.”

“I won't.” Percy looked back toward the water. He had told Silena about Sally before, and she had listened without pretending the memory belonged to her. That mattered more than having a perfect thing to say.

“You don't have to tell me anything you don't want to,” he said.

“I know. I wanted to tell you this much.” She looked at the box again. “The lantern thing is my limit for today. I can help set it up. I don't want to read a note or explain my family to a crowd.”

“Then don't.”

“And no one writes Maria's name for me.”

“Okay.”

Silena gave him a quick, assessing look. “You're not going to tell me that I'm protecting myself?”

Percy felt his first answer forming and held it back. “No. You're telling me what you want. That's enough.”

She nodded, once. “Thanks.”

He remembered the day Sally stood between him and Gabe with a kitchen knife. He had told the story before; it usually came out as a joke, because the alternative was explaining the fear underneath. “Gabe was my stepdad. He was awful. He hit me once. Sally stood between us and told him if he ever touched me again she'd kill him and make it look like an accident. I was eight. I believed her.”

“Good,” Silena said. Her voice was firm, not shocked. “You should have had someone between you and him.”

“She stayed with him for a while because his smell covered mine from monsters. I understand the reason. I still hate that she had to make that choice. She never made me feel like the cost was mine to repay.” He picked up a loose bit of twine and looped it around his finger. “She could get tired. She could be wrong about stuff. But she kept showing up.”

Silena's gaze dropped to the twine. “That's the part I don't know how to picture. Not perfect. Just there.”

Percy took a breath. “Yeah.”

“I get a card from Maria every year. I used to write back. Then I'd spend days wondering if I wrote the right thing or if she would answer more warmly next time. I stopped.” She didn't sound proud or ashamed. “Sometimes I wonder if I should try again. Then the card comes and I remember why I stopped.”

“You don't owe her a reply because she signs a card.”

“I know that when I say it out loud.” Silena brushed grass from the paper sleeve. “I don't know it every December.”

“That's fair.”

She rested her shoulder lightly against his for a second, then shifted away to open the box. It wasn't an invitation to make the moment larger. Percy didn't. She removed a paper lantern frame, checked its folded seams, and handed him the twine.

“Can you help me make sure these don't blow into the fire?”

“That I can do.”

They went over the lantern pieces together. Silena explained which folds were already creased and which needed clips. Percy tied the first one too tight and bent its frame; she fixed it with two careful turns, then showed him how much slack to leave. She planned to use the pavilion's outer rail, away from the cooking area, and keep the loose ones stacked until the campers were ready. When he asked what the lanterns were for, she said, “Whatever each person wants. A name, a blank, a drawing. Nobody has to tell a story.”

“That sounds better than a speech.”

“Chiron said there won't be one.” She packed the repaired frame back into the box. “Clarisse wanted something to do with her hands. I think she wants the others to have that option too.”

Silena stood, brushing sand from her shorts. “I'll see you at dinner. And Percy?”

“Yeah?”

“Don't tell Annabeth I cried.”

“I won't.”

“You can tell her we talked about Sally. She already knows I ask questions.”

“Okay.”

Silena lifted the box against her hip and walked up toward the pavilion. Percy stayed where he was. The water reached the edge of the sand, then slid away. Tyson and Triton had turned the conch into a measuring cup for a game involving pebbles; neither seemed sure of the rules. Percy picked up the shawl and carried it with him when he left the bank. He did not step onto the dock.

By the time he reached the pavilion, Piper had arrived with a bundle of clips and a spool of thin wire. Clarisse was standing on a bench to reach the outer rail. She leaned over to fasten one lantern frame, tested the clip twice, and climbed down before she moved to the next.

“Chiron wants them on this side,” Silena said, pointing away from the serving tables. “The wind pushes in from the beach. If we put them by the kitchen, they're going to knock into the dishes.”

“Then we put them on the inside of the rail,” Clarisse said. “Nothing gets hung over the path.”

Piper held up the clips. “I brought extras in case the paper folds tear.”

“Keep them in the box,” Silena said. “Don't patch a tear until the person who made it says it's okay.”

Piper gave her a quick nod. One of the first lanterns had split along a crease. Instead of repairing it, Silena placed it flat on the table with the others and marked it so its maker could find it.

“Can you hold this end?” Clarisse asked Percy.

He set the shawl on a clean bench and took the frame from her. “Here?”

“A little higher. No, your other left.”

“I only have one left.”

“Then use it.” She reached around him and shifted the paper a fraction of an inch. “The frame's crooked.”

“The rail is crooked.”

“The rail has been here longer than you.”

She was right; he could see the difference once she pointed it out. He held the frame steady while she clipped the top and bottom. The paper stopped flapping. Clarisse gave it a sharp tug. It held.

“That's one,” she said.

“How many are there?”

“Enough.” She marked the count on a scrap of cardboard and checked the next frame. One lantern at a time, no one standing under a loose clip, no writing without the maker's choice.

Thalia came up the path from the direction of the Big House. She walked at an easy pace, right hand tucked inside her jacket sleeve. The bandage still showed at the cuff. She stopped at the table rather than reaching for the paper.

“Need another person?” she asked.

“Only if you want a job,” Silena said. “You can count frames or watch the clips. No lifting.”

“I'll count.” Thalia picked up the cardboard list with her left hand. She read the penciled marks and compared them to the stack. “You have twelve, and the paper says eleven.”

“I miscounted,” Clarisse said.

“The last one's behind the box.”

Silena found it and set it beside the others. “Good catch.”

Thalia returned to counting. Her right hand stayed close to her side. Percy didn't ask whether she had heard from Will or whether the palm hurt. The bandage was still visible; no clearance had been mentioned.

Piper picked up a clean lantern. “Do we want to write names on these before we hang them?”

“No,” Clarisse said. “People can write what they want when they pick one. Don't put somebody's name on a thing they didn't choose.”

“Some people won't want to write anything,” Silena added. “That's fine too.”

Clarisse nodded and attached another clip. She did not ask why Silena had said it.

“Are you writing one?” Annabeth asked Percy when she joined them, laptop tucked under her arm.

“I haven't decided.”

“You can decide later.” She set the laptop on the end of the table and helped Piper sort the frames by size. She left the shawl where Percy had put it.

Thalia glanced at the paper and said, “I might not take one.”

“Then don't,” Clarisse said. She attached another clip. “Nobody's checking.”

Thalia's shoulders lowered a little. She looked at the cardboard list again and continued counting.

The setup took longer than Percy expected. Wind slipped under the rail and lifted the loose paper; Clarisse moved the whole row inward. A clip failed, and Piper replaced it only after the camper who had made the lantern handed her the spare. Silena kept the damaged frame on the table and let its maker decide what to do with it. Chiron walked by twice, each time checking that the aisle to the serving tables stayed clear. He didn't ask what the lanterns meant.

When they finished, six empty frames hung along the rail. The rest were stacked where campers could reach them. Percy picked the shawl back up. Annabeth held the table steady while he tied off the last bit of twine. Thalia read the count back to Silena. Clarisse put the marker down and wiped her fingers on her shorts.

“It's just paper,” she said.

“It's a lot of paper,” Percy said.

“That's not what I meant.” She turned toward the pavilion entrance. “Come eat before the harpies decide we made a mess.”

Thalia folded the count sheet and set it beside the lantern box. She didn't take one. Annabeth carried nothing but her laptop. They walked inside together, leaving the blank frames for anyone who wanted to claim one.

---

Dinner started on time, but the pavilion looked different by the time the campers sat down. Silena and Piper had hung the paper lantern frames along the outer rail, away from the serving tables. Some were empty. Some had a name or a word written in marker. A few had only a drawing: a blue line, a crooked house, a row of stars. Nothing was labeled with someone else's story.

Clarisse stood near the entrance with a marker in her left hand. Her right hand was free, but she kept her shoulders squared and her face set like she'd rather be in the arena. She had written MOM on one lantern. No name beneath it.

“No one has to take one,” Chiron told the campers as he moved from table to table. “No one will be asked to explain one. If you want to leave it blank, leave it blank.” He didn't raise his voice or ask everyone to answer together. He simply moved on to make room for the next camper at the rail.

Percy stopped in front of a lantern still lying flat on the table. He could have written Sally. He held the marker for a while, watching the point gather a bead of ink. Then he wrote her name in the center, letters larger than he meant them to be. He did not add Jackson. He folded the bottom edge of the paper over the string and tied it himself.

Annabeth stood next to him, looking at the lanterns. “Do you want me to write it?”

“No. I want to.”

She nodded and chose a lantern without asking him to interpret the empty space. She drew a small grid of squares in one corner, then returned the marker. Percy recognized the care in the straight lines; he didn't ask whom she had in mind.

Silena added one lantern to the rail with no writing at all. She pressed the clip closed, tested it with a small tug, then handed Piper the marker. When Percy caught her eye, she gave him the slightest nod. He nodded back. Nothing more needed to be said in the middle of the pavilion.

The names he could see from the table were few. Sally. MOM. One lantern had a hand-drawn bowl of soup. Another had a sun with a face. The campers who didn't make one kept eating or found places at the far end. Nobody stopped them.

Tyson had saved a seat for Triton. Triton arrived with the calling conch tucked into the crook of his arm and chose the chair beside him. He sat stiffly until Tyson set down the peanut-butter jar and asked if the sea peanuts needed bread. Triton took a piece of toast and tried the spread. He considered it seriously, then took a second piece.

“It is acceptable,” he said.

“That's almost a compliment,” Percy said.

“It is a very good compliment.” Tyson turned the hammer over beside his plate, careful not to put it near the food. “I made another hinge test.”

“You are supposed to be on leave,” Percy said.

“I chose to stop by the forge for a little while. It was not a shift.” Tyson set the tool down. “The test is better. I will show Mother when I have one that works.”

“If you want to,” Percy said.

Tyson nodded. “I want to.”

The blue shawl stayed folded beside Percy until the air cooled. He turned to Annabeth. “Want to borrow it?”

“For tonight?”

“For tonight. You can give it back when we go in.”

She looked at him for a beat before accepting. “Thank you.” She draped it over her shoulders herself, settling the wool around her neck. Percy let her decide how to wear it. She tugged the edge once, testing its weight, then returned to her meal.

Thalia sat with her left hand around a cup. The bandage still covered her right palm; Percy hadn't seen anyone clear it for use. The letter stayed in her jacket pocket. She did not set it on the table or show it around. When she used the Iris-message bowl later, Percy was seated several places away. He saw Jason appear in the water's surface and heard Thalia say, “A letter came from Mom. I'm keeping it for now.”

Jason asked something Percy couldn't make out. Thalia shook her head. “Not tonight. I haven't decided what I want to say.” She listened while Jason answered, then asked him a question of her own. After a minute, Jason's image faded. Thalia returned the bowl to the counselor and went back to her food. She didn't ask Percy whether she'd done the right thing.

Silena stayed with her cabinmates. Drew leaned over to say something Percy couldn't hear; Silena laughed, then reached across the table to straighten a napkin that had fallen into someone's drink. When she caught Percy's eye, she lifted her cup. He lifted his in return.

After dinner, the campers who wanted to go down to the beach gathered near the marked path. Chiron waited until the group had formed, counted them, and reminded everyone to stay on the firm stretch of sand. The dock's third board still flexed; nobody was to use it. A few campers chose to remain by the pavilion. The rest walked down in loose pairs, carrying blankets, food, and the lanterns they had chosen.

The bonfire was small, built on a clear patch of sand away from the lantern frames and the path back uphill. Percy sat with Tyson, Triton and a handful of counselors, listening to the brothers argue about the right way to describe a sea serpent. Tyson claimed a sea serpent had once stolen a whole jar of peanut butter. Triton said that was a sea dragon and had been the size of a warship. Tyson asked how he knew. Triton started a very long answer that ended with the same story and a much larger jar.

“That's not how it happened,” Percy said.

“You were not there.”

“Neither were you,” Tyson said.

Triton folded his arms. “I was there in spirit.”

“That isn't a thing,” Percy said.

“It is now.” Tyson laughed so loudly that the people on the other side of the fire looked over. Triton tried to hold his frown and lost it.

Silena sat on a blanket with two of her friends. A younger camper asked whether she had written on her lantern. Silena answered, “I put it up the way I wanted,” then asked the camper what she had drawn. She did not name her own mother or explain the blank page. The younger girl showed her a sketch of a kitchen table, and Silena asked how many chairs were supposed to fit around it.

Clarisse sat with Chris Rodriguez and several of the older campers. Percy found a place a little way off, close enough to hear if someone spoke to him but not in the center of the circle. He was turning a marshmallow on a stick when Clarisse's voice carried over the low conversation.

“She sends me caramel corn,” Clarisse said. “Every Halloween. With a note: ‘Don't share with monsters.’ I keep the notes.”

Chris rested his hand beside hers on the blanket. “My mom still cuts the crusts off my toast when I visit. I'm not a little kid anymore.”

Clarisse glanced at him. “I didn't say I was.”

“I know.”

“People would call it babyish if they knew I saved those notes.” She looked at the fire. “Doesn't matter. I keep them.”

“Then they're yours,” Chris said.

Clarisse gave a small nod. She didn't turn the moment into a story about her childhood or invite questions. Someone passed her the bowl of marshmallows, and she took one.

Percy rolled the marshmallow over the heat. “I used to throw away notes from my mom.”

Clarisse glanced at him. “The blue lunch ones?”

“Yeah.”

“You remember what they said.”

“Some of them. I remember the handwriting. Most of the paper's gone.”

“Then you kept something.”

He watched the sugar darken. “I don't know if I kept enough.”

“Enough for what?”

“To make her real when I talk about her. People hear the big parts. The monsters, Gabe, the blue food. They think that's the whole thing.”

Clarisse turned her attention back to her own marshmallow. “I don't think the notes prove everything was fine. I keep them because she wrote them. That's it.”

“That's probably all I need too.”

“Good.” She lifted the stick and checked the underside. It had turned black. “This is burnt.”

“You got distracted.”

“I was talking.” She handed it to Chris and reached for another. “You can eat this one.”

Chris looked at the charred sugar. “I can?”

“You said you like them like that.”

“I said my mom cuts the crusts off my toast.”

“Same thing.”

Chris laughed and took it. Clarisse selected a fresh marshmallow. The folded lantern count sheet stuck out of her pocket, pencil marks visible in the firelight. She didn't take it out. Percy didn't ask.

The night stayed uneven. A few campers talked about their mothers. Others talked about school or the best way to toast bread. One of the youngest boys left before the first song started; his counselor walked him up without asking him to explain. Nobody called him back. Percy could hear the surf under the voices, a steady sound that kept the silences from feeling staged.

Annabeth came to sit beside him after the lanterns had been collected from the rail. She wore the blue shawl around both shoulders. Percy had not pulled it closed for her; she had done that herself. A streak of ash marked her sleeve, and a marshmallow had left a white smear near the cuff.

“Hey,” she said.

“Hey.”

“Thank you for lending me this.”

“I'm glad it fits.”

She rubbed the fabric between finger and thumb. “It's warmer than it looks.”

They watched Tyson settle against a folded blanket. Triton sat beside him, holding the calling conch in both hands. When Tyson's head tipped against Triton's shoulder, Triton shifted to make space. He didn't look toward Percy to see if anyone had noticed.

“I wrote Sally's name,” Percy said.

“I saw.”

“I wanted to. I don't know what I thought would happen when I did.”

“Did anything happen?”

“No.” Percy watched a coal collapse in the fire. “I didn't feel better. I didn't feel worse. It was her name on paper.”

“That counts.”

“Maybe.” He rested his hands on his knees. “Sometimes I worry that when I talk about her, people hear a story I'm using to explain why I'm like this. She wasn't a lesson. She was my mom. She made bad spaghetti and wrote notes on napkins and came back when she said she would.”

Annabeth kept her shoulder against his. “I remember her doing those things. I remember her making room for me at the table, even when we were all pretending we weren't dating yet.”

“We were terrible at pretending.”

“You were terrible at pretending.”

“Fair.”

He turned the last marshmallow on his stick. “She loved you. I don't want to say that because you need it to be true. I say it because I remember it being true.”

Annabeth's eyes stayed on the fire. “I know. I remember it too.” Her voice thinned on the last word. “I miss her, but it isn't my memory to give you back. You don't have to make it easier for me.”

“I don't want to make it easier. I just want to say it out loud.”

“Then say it.”

Percy looked at her. “My mom loved you.”

Annabeth closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them, she leaned her forehead against his shoulder. “I loved her too.”

They stayed that way until the cold reached through Percy's shirt. Annabeth shifted under the shawl, and he asked, “Do you want to keep it until we go inside?”

“Yes, please.”

He put his arm around her, careful not to tug the wool from her shoulders. She laced their fingers together under it. A few yards away, Clarisse was telling Chris he had burned his marshmallow. Silena had started braiding a younger camper's hair after the girl asked. Tyson snored once; Triton didn't move. No one turned toward Percy and Annabeth, and they didn't need the camp to look away for them to be alone together.

Above the beach, the stars showed through the gaps in the trees. The lanterns remained on the pavilion rail, their names and drawings visible when the wind shifted them. Percy didn't try to arrange them into one story. The shore held the water, the fire burned down, and Annabeth's hand stayed warm inside his.

When Chiron called the group back up, the campers gathered blankets and followed the marked path toward the pavilion. Percy waited until Tyson had woken enough to walk beside Triton. The dock lay dark to their right; nobody stepped toward it. At the top of the path, a harpy counselor collected the unclaimed paper frames while Silena checked that the rail was clear.

Annabeth stopped under the pavilion's outer light. She lifted the shawl off her shoulders and folded it once, then again, keeping its edge from dragging against the ground.

“Here,” she said. “Thank you for lending it.”

Percy took it. “It kept you warm.”

“It did.” She touched the blue wool with two fingers. “I'll remember that part.”

He folded the cotton around it the way Amphitrite had wrapped it at breakfast. The shawl was still his; Annabeth had worn it for the evening, and now it rested in his hands again. The distinction felt simple, and good.

Annabeth leaned in and kissed him, brief and familiar. “Come on. The cabins are still a walk away.”

They went as far as the fork in the path together. Annabeth headed toward the Zeus cabin, and Percy carried the shawl back toward Cabin Three. He didn't imagine what either of them would say the next morning. He could ask her then whether she wanted to hear another story about Sally.

When Percy opened Cabin Three, Tyson was already asleep in the guest bunk. One broad arm hung over the side, fingers close to the floor. The tools from Amphitrite's basket were not there; Tyson had left them at the forge after their test. Percy folded the shawl around its cotton wrapper and set it at the foot of his own bed, where it wouldn't fall onto the floor. In the blue-green light, the shell-chimes at the window moved without making a sound.
