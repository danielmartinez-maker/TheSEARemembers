## Chapter 28: Sparring

Clarisse La Rue knew when someone was pulling their punches.

She knew because her father was Poseidon, and the sea did not pull punches. She knew because she had been at Camp Half-Blood since she was eight years old, and she had sparred with every kind of kid — the scared new ones, the show-off Ares kids who came at her yelling, the Hermes kids who tried to cheat, the Apollo kids who apologized every time they landed a hit. She knew what holding back looked like. Shoulders tight. Blade slowing a half-second before contact. Eyes flicking away like you were sorry in advance.

Percy Jackson fought like that, and it was driving her absolutely insane.

It was Saturday afternoon in the sword arena, sun high and brutal, dust hanging in the air and smelling like hot bronze and cut grass. The Ares cabin had challenged Poseidon to a three-on-three — friendly, which at camp meant nobody would need the infirmary for more than an hour. Clarisse, Silena, and Percy versus Sherman, Ellis, and a new Ares kid named Mark who was eleven and still shouted when he lunged.

Clarisse loved team fights. She loved the noise and the press of it, shield on arm, spear in hand, calling short orders. Silena beside her with her dagger and that bright, fearless grin she got when she was having fun. Percy at her left with Riptide uncapped, his stance loose and easy.

And Percy was losing on purpose. Not obviously. Not enough that anyone else would call it. But Clarisse saw it.

Sherman came in heavy with his sword, the way he always did, telegraphing from the shoulder. Percy could have sidestepped and tapped his wrist and ended it in two moves. Clarisse had seen him do it in drills, fast as a fish turning. Instead he blocked straight-on, let Sherman drive him back two steps into the dust, and gave ground he did not need to give.

Ellis tried to flank Silena. Percy saw it — Clarisse saw him see it — and instead of cutting Ellis off with a rush, he hesitated, waited, shouted a warning instead of moving.

Mark, the little new kid, got brave and charged Percy screaming. Percy grinned, knocked the kid's sword aside so gently it was practically polite, and let the kid bounce off his guard and run away to brag about it.

After, by the tool shed, Clarisse grabbed Percy's wrist.

"What was that?" she said.

Percy blinked sweat out of his eyes. His hair was plastered to his forehead. "What was what? We won. You pinned Sherman, Silena got Ellis—"

"You fought like you were made of glass," Clarisse said. "Like you were scared you'd break someone if you actually swung."

Percy looked away, toward the strawberry fields. "Mark's eleven."

"Mark's an Ares kid. He wears a helmet to breakfast in case someone stabs him with a fork. He can take a real parry." Clarisse crossed her arms. She was thirteen, taller than Percy by half a head, broader in the shoulders, and when she planted her feet like that, even the Ares kids listened. "You do this every time. In drills. In capture the flag last week. You get that look and you throttle down to sixty percent."

Silena came up with two cups of water, handed one to Percy. She was fourteen, hair in a French braid with salt still in it from morning swim, freckles standing out across her nose. "She's not wrong, Perce," she said gently. "You fight like you're apologizing."

Percy drank. "I'm not apologizing. I'm just — I don't want to hurt anyone for real. Especially with water stuff. It gets big fast and I can't always—"

"That," Clarisse said, pointing at him, "is exactly why you need real training. Not arena play-fighting with foam on the edges. Real training. You and me. Dawn. Tempest Hall. No audience, no showing off."

Percy stared. "Dawn? Like, sunrise dawn?"

"Like, before sunrise dawn," Clarisse said. "The pool's warmest then. And nobody will be there to stare while you knock me on my butt learning how."

Silena lit up. "Can I come? I can time rounds. I have a stopwatch and a whistle and I make excellent hot chocolate-adjacent moral support."

"You can come if you bring muffins," Clarisse said.

"I always bring muffins," Silena said, like this was a well-established law of nature.

Percy looked between his two sisters — half-sisters, technically, same dad different mortal parents, but after two months of sharing Cabin Three's leaky back window and stealing each other's socks, the half part had worn off — and sighed in that long-suffering way twelve-year-old boys sigh when they know they have lost.

"Fine," he said. "Dawn. Tempest Hall. But if I fall asleep in the pool and drown, I'm haunting you both."

"You can't drown," Clarisse and Silena said together, and laughed, and Percy laughed too, and Clarisse felt that fierce tug in her chest she always felt around them. Protective and proud and a little bit scared, all mixed up.

She did not say the other part out loud, not yet. The part she had been thinking since the night Percy told her and Silena about Atlantis in halting pieces — about the guards drilling in black water under green light, about waking up knowing forms his hands remembered even when his head did not. About being afraid of his own strength because it had once been bigger than a whole city.

She thought: if you're scared of what you can do, you need someone you trust to let you do it full-out and see that it's okay.

She thought: you trust me. Let me be that person.

"Monday," she said. "Oh-five-hundred. Don't be late."

"I don't even know what oh-five-hundred means," Percy said.

"It means when it's still dark and your bed is very comfortable," Silena said cheerfully. "See you there."

---

Percy learned three facts about Tempest Hall at five in the morning on Monday.

First, it was cold outside and warm inside in a way that made your bones ache with relief. Second, Clarisse before sunrise was somehow even more Clarisse than usual. Third, his sister Silena took timing rounds extremely seriously.

Tempest Hall sat at the far end of the beach path, half sunk into the dunes where the woods met the sea. From the outside it looked like a boathouse — weathered gray wood, double doors, a bronze trident over the lintel gone green with salt. Inside, it was one of Percy's favorite places at camp and also one he barely understood.

The main room was long and high, with exposed beams and windows set high to catch sunrise. The floor was smooth concrete that sloped gently toward the center, where a pool stretched thirty feet long and twenty wide, tiled in blues and greens with a huge mosaic of a hippocampus on the bottom. The water was fed straight from the sea through bronze pipes — salt water, always moving faintly, always smelling like home. Around the pool were weapon racks, benches, coiled ropes, stacks of towels that never quite dried, and at the far end a tiny kitchen nook with a kettle and a cabinet that Silena had already claimed as Muffin Storage.

At 5:04 a.m., with the sky outside still indigo and the windows showing stars, the pool steamed faintly. The underwater lights made it glow turquoise. It was beautiful and quiet except for Clarisse doing push-ups on the deck like this was a perfectly normal way to greet the day.

"You're late," she said without looking up.

"It's oh-four," Percy mumbled, dropping his towel and gym bag. He wore swim trunks and an old Camp Half-Blood t-shirt with the sleeves cut off. His feet were bare and cold on the concrete until he stepped near the pool and felt the warm breath of it. "By one minute. That's basically early."

"It's late," Clarisse said, finishing and popping to her feet. She wore a black one-piece practice suit with shorts over it, her hair pulled back in a tight bun, a faded scar on her knee white against her tan. "Silena's been here since four-thirty."

"I have," Silena said proudly from the bench. She wore flannel pajama pants with seahorses on them, a Poseidon hoodie three sizes too big, and a pink stopwatch around her neck. In front of her was a clipboard, a whistle, a Tupperware of blueberry muffins, and three water bottles lined up like soldiers. "I set up. Also I ate one muffin for quality control. It passed."

Percy sat on the edge of the pool and stuck his feet in. Warmth rushed up his legs. The water curled around his ankles like a cat, happy to see him. He felt his whole body unlock. Salt water always did that — told his muscles they could stop being tired.

"Okay," Clarisse said, sitting across from him, legs dangling in. "Ground rules. This week, dawn sessions, just us. No holding back. You hit me like I'm Sherman. I hit you like you're — well, like you're me. If something feels weird with the water, you say so and we stop. Silena calls time. Three-minute rounds, one-minute rest. Deal?"

Percy swallowed. "What if I hurt you?"

Clarisse snorted. "Percy. I have sparred with the Ares cabin since I was eight. I have a scar from a manticore spike on my back. Last summer Ellis threw a javelin at me and I caught it. You are not going to hurt me by actually trying."

"But the water—" Percy started. In the arena he could keep it small. In the pool, with all that salt around him and his blood humming, things wanted to get big. A flick could become a wave. He pictured the training hall he had watched on his recent Atlantis visit, much larger than this, water rising in columns at a gesture, guards shouting. His hands seemed to know it better than his conscious memory did.

Clarisse must have seen it on his face, because her voice went softer. Not soft like Silena. Soft like Clarisse trying to be gentle and mostly succeeding.

"Hey. Look at me. This pool? It's made for this. The drains can take a surge. The walls are reinforced. And I'm your sister. If you knock me over, I'll get up. If you soak me, I'll get you back. That's the point. You need to know where full-out actually is, so you can stop guessing and pulling short. Okay?"

Percy nodded slowly. The water lapped at his shins. Somewhere high in the beams, a gull landed and complained.

"Okay," he said. "No holding back."

"No holding back," Clarisse agreed, and held out her fist. He bumped it.

Silena blew the whistle — a short, cheerful toot — and shouted, "Round one! No swords! Just water! Try to push each other off the edge!"

"What?" Percy said.

Clarisse was already grinning and gathering water with both hands.

It turned out sitting on the edge of the pool and trying to shove each other off with water was much harder and much funnier than it sounded.

Percy's first instinct was to be careful — a little scoop, a little push. Clarisse had no such instinct. She pulled a smooth sheet of water up and slapped it sideways at him, knocking his legs out from under his grip and nearly sliding him in. He yelped, grabbed the concrete lip, and laughed despite himself.

"Hey!"

"Full-out, Jackson!" Clarisse yelled, laughing.

So he tried. He closed his eyes for a second, felt for the water the way he had watched the Atlantean guards demonstrate on that visit — not as a thing to grab, but as a thing to ask. He breathed in, breathed out with the tide, and pushed.

A swell rose under Clarisse, lifting her, tipping her. She whooped, countered by splitting the swell with her forearms, scattering it into spray that glittered in the pool lights. Droplets hung in the air, and for a second Percy saw each one, bright and separate, and he could have moved any of them.

He moved them all — a curtain, gentle, that nudged Clarisse's shoulders.

She went in with a huge splash, came up sputtering and laughing so hard she could barely tread water.

"Okay!" she gasped. "Okay! That was more like it!"

Silena was on her feet, stopwatch forgotten, cheering. "Time! I mean — time! Round over! Percy wins by splash!"

Percy slid into the pool after his sister, and they treaded water side by side, grinning. The steam curled around them. The windows were turning pink at the edges.

"Again," Clarisse said.

They went six rounds that morning. Percy lost two by getting dunked, won one by lifting Clarisse clean onto the deck like a wave depositing a shell (she pretended to be mad and was clearly delighted), and drew three in laughing splashing messes that Silena declared "too cute to score."

By six-thirty, they were pruned and panting and starving, sitting on towels sharing the muffins. Percy's arms ached in a good way. Clarisse had a bruise blooming on her elbow where she'd hit the edge, and she showed it off proudly.

"See?" she said. "Hurt me. I'm still here."

Percy bumped her shoulder with his. "You're still here."

The doors banged open, letting in cold air and sunrise. Two figures stood silhouetted — tall and lean, with crackling static in the air around them.

"Are you sea kids done being loud at dawn?" Thalia Grace called. She was fifteen, with black choppy hair and electric-blue eyes and a leather jacket over her practice clothes even indoors. Behind her, smaller and bright-eyed with a notebook already in hand, was Annabeth.

Annabeth Grace, twelve, daughter of Zeus, Percy's girlfriend in a life she alone remembered alongside him, even if their campmates had known them together for only weeks, and the only person at camp who took notes on sparring like it was a math test.

"You're early," Annabeth said to Percy, coming to sit by his towel. She kissed his cheek quickly — she did that now, quick and casual like she'd always done it — and stole half his muffin. "You smell like chlorine and salt."

"It's salt water," Percy said. "No chlorine. Very natural stink."

"Very natural," Annabeth agreed solemnly, and leaned against his shoulder. She was warm from running over. Her hair smelled like wind.

Thalia dropped her gear by the far lane. "We need the north end for spear drills. You squids stay in the south end and try not to drown each other too loud. I have a headache."

"You love us," Silena said.

"I tolerate you at dawn," Thalia said, but she smiled, and when Silena tossed her a muffin, she caught it one-handed.

Annabeth laced her fingers through Percy's wet ones. "How was it?"

Percy looked at Clarisse, who was wringing out her bun and pretending not to listen.

"It was good," he said. "Hard. Good-hard."

Annabeth squeezed his hand. She knew about Atlantis and the childhood he could not personally remember, the way he knew about her fear of failing Thalia. They had talked about it on the Zeus cabin roof with blankets around their shoulders. She did not say anything now, just nodded and smiled in a way that said she was proud.

"Tomorrow we do swords in the water," Clarisse announced. "Bring your knife, Chase. You and Thalia can use the shallow steps if you want."

"We will," Thalia said. "Someone has to show you how to actually fight while you two play mermaids."

"You're just mad because you can't make the water do that," Clarisse shot back.

"I'm mad because it's five in the morning and you're yelling," Thalia said, but she was already spinning her spear, and Annabeth was already up with her dagger and a grin, and Silena was resetting her stopwatch with great ceremony.

Percy watched his girlfriend and her big sister square off on the far deck, watched his sisters argue about muffin flavors, felt the warm water lapping at his feet and the cold dawn air on his face, and thought that maybe dawn was not so bad after all.

---

Clarisse had a theory about Percy, and on Tuesday she tested it.

Her theory was this: Percy thought of water as a weapon you threw. Big splash, big push. Atlantean war-forms, tidal fists. He did not think of water as armor you wore.

She thought this because she had grown up in the pool. When she was nine, her dad — Poseidon, who visited in dreams as a man with sea-green eyes and calloused hands — had shown her how to pull water up her arms like sleeves. When she was eleven, she had held a curtain of water around herself for a full minute while Ares kids pelted her with blunt arrows, and not one had gotten through. Water could be hard if you asked it right. Hard and fast and close.

Percy did not know that. He had grown up in Atlantis for the first years of this life — palaces and guards and deep pressure — but not in the pool. He had raw power and no skin for it.

So Tuesday's lesson: shields.

"Round one," Silena announced promptly at 5:02 a.m., whistle in mouth, blanket around shoulders, muffin in hand. Outside, rain ticked against the high windows. Inside, the pool glowed steady. "Clarisse attacks! Percy defends only! Three minutes! Go!"

Percy stood waist-deep in the south end, Riptide in sword form, feet planted wide. Clarisse stood ten feet away with a bucket.

"A bucket?" Percy said. "That's your weapon? A bucket?"

"This bucket," Clarisse said with dignity, "is full of water I am going to throw at your face. And you are going to stop it without moving your feet and without hitting me back. Use the pool. Pull it up."

She threw. Not gently. A full bucket in a flat sheet.

Percy's eyes went wide. He raised his sword like that would help. The water hit him square in the chest and knocked him back a step, soaked his shirt, made him sputter.

Silena winced. "Ooh. Point to Clarisse for enthusiasm."

"Again," Clarisse said. She bent, refilled the bucket with a quick scoop that was itself a lesson — smooth, no wasted motion — and threw again.

This time Percy lifted a hand on instinct. The pool rose to meet him, a wobbling wall that was too thin at the top. The bucket-water punched through and got him in the chin.

"You're thinking like it's a wall of bricks," Clarisse called, refilling. "Don't build a wall. Wear a whirlpool. Keep it moving. Moving water sheds water."

On the north steps, Annabeth and Thalia paused their own drill to watch. Annabeth sat on the second step in leggings and a too-big Zeus shirt, dagger across her knees, hair in a messy ponytail. Thalia stood over her, demonstrating a spear bind.

"She's right," Thalia called across the water. "Static defense breaks. Redirect."

"Thanks, Thalia," Percy gasped, wiping water from his eyes. "Very helpful while I'm drowning."

"You're not drowning," Annabeth and Clarisse and Silena said together, and everyone laughed, and even Percy laughed.

Third throw. Percy breathed — Clarisse saw him do the thing he did, that little inhale like he was listening — and this time the water around his torso spun. Not high, not flashy, just a tight current circling his chest and shoulders, catching the thrown sheet and flinging it sideways in an arc that sparkled.

It worked. He stayed dry from the neck down. His hair was still soaked from before and sticking up.

"Yes!" Silena shouted, dropping her clipboard and clapping. "Yes! That's it!"

Clarisse grinned so wide her cheeks hurt. "Again! Faster!"

They drilled it for an hour. Bucket throws, then hose sprays from the deck washdown hose that Silena gleefully operated ("I'm helping! I'm helping with water pressure!"), then finally Clarisse herself rushing him with a foam practice sword while he held the whirlpool and tried to keep his footing on the slick tiles.

He got it. Not perfectly — twice the shield collapsed and he went under laughing — but he got the feel. By 6:15, he could hold a spinning sleeve around his left arm for a full round while parrying with his right.

"That's Atlantean, actually," Percy said during rest, sitting on the steps with a towel over his head, breathing hard. "On my visit the guards showed me a form called Eel-Skin. Water close to the body, always moving. So blades slide. It clicks better when you say whirlpool."

Clarisse sat beside him, elbows on knees. "Tell me. How did they teach it?"

Percy was quiet for a moment, staring at the mosaic hippocampus under the water. When he talked about Atlantis, his voice went distant in a way that always made Clarisse's chest ache. He was twelve. He should not sound like that.

"They showed me the current hall on my visit," he said slowly. "Deep water, strong flow. No sword at first. Just — stand. Feel where the push is. Turn your shoulder a hair so it misses. Master Ione walked along the edge with a staff and poked a trainee if he braced. 'Don't fight the sea,' she'd say. 'Join it and step aside.' Then later they'd give you a blade and you'd do the same thing while moving."

"Huh," Clarisse said. "That's smarter than how Ares teaches. They just yell 'block harder.'"

Percy smiled faintly. "Different gods."

"Show me tomorrow?" Clarisse said. "That Eel-Skin thing, properly? I want to learn your forms, not just make you learn mine."

Percy looked at her, surprised and pleased. "Yeah? You want Atlantean stuff?"

"You're my brother," Clarisse said simply. "You learned somewhere I didn't. Teach me."

Silena, who had been pretending not to eavesdrop while timing Annabeth and Thalia's round, made a small happy sound and wrote SOMETHING CUTE HAPPENED in big letters on the clipboard.

On the north end, Thalia tapped Annabeth's dagger with her spear. "Again. You're dropping your elbow on the riposte. And stop watching your boyfriend. He's fine. He's learning to be a turtle."

"I'm not watching," Annabeth said, very unconvincingly, while absolutely watching. She flushed when Percy waved at her, and Thalia laughed out loud, a bright crackling sound, and ruffled her little sister's ponytail.

"Focus, little spark," Thalia said. "You want to try the disarm?"

Annabeth nodded, set her feet, and lunged. Thalia sidestepped, caught her wrist, twisted gently — Annabeth rolled out of it, tapped Thalia's knee with the flat of her dagger, and sprang back grinning.

"Better!" Thalia said. "You got me. Mostly. If that was sharp I'd be limping and I'd still kill you, but better."

"Thanks," Annabeth said dryly. "So encouraging."

"That's what big sisters are for," Thalia said. "Now, commentary corner: rate the sea siblings on a scale of one to ten for teamwork."

Annabeth considered Percy and Clarisse, who were now attempting to both hold whirlpool shields while Silena sprayed them with the hose and shrieked with laughter.

"Nine," Annabeth said. "Docked one point because Percy just got water up his nose."

"Fair," Thalia said. "Storm sisters would be ten out of ten, obviously."

"Obviously," Annabeth said, and attacked again.

At the end of the session, shivering and happy, the five of them crowded into the kitchen nook while Silena heated milk on the little stove for practice hot chocolate — watery, but warm — and Thalia produced a bag of mini marshmallows from her jacket pocket like magic.

"For emergencies," she said gravely. "Dawn counts as an emergency."

Percy, with a towel around his neck and Annabeth's head on his shoulder, looked at Clarisse over his steaming mug and mouthed thank you.

Clarisse bumped his foot under the table and did not say anything soppy. She did not need to.

---

Wednesday, Percy was the teacher, and he was terrified.

It was one thing to be taught. To be bad at something while Clarisse yelled encouragement and Silena cheered every tiny improvement. It was another thing to stand in front of his big sister — who had been fighting since he was in diapers — and say, I know something you don't.

What if he got it wrong? What if the movements were clearer than his explanation? What if he looked stupid?

Annabeth found him at 4:50 a.m., sitting on the Tempest Hall steps in the dark, picking at the tape on his practice sword.

She sat down without being asked, shoulder to shoulder, and handed him half a granola bar.

"You're nervous," she said. It was not a question. Annabeth always knew.

"A little," Percy admitted. "What if I mess up the form? I remember it in my body more than my head. Like, I can do it, but explaining—"

"You explain things to me all the time," Annabeth said. "You taught me how to breathe under water without panicking last week. You just showed me. You counted. One, two, out. It helped."

Percy looked at her. In the dim porch light, with her hair still mussed from sleep and her Zeus jacket zipped to her chin, she looked very young and very sure of him.

"What if Clarisse thinks it's dumb?" he whispered.

Annabeth snorted. "Clarisse once spent forty minutes explaining to me why the correct way to coil a rope matters for 'soul health.' She does not think earnest things are dumb. Also, she's your sister. She wants to learn from you. Let her."

Percy bumped her forehead gently with his. "When did you get so smart?"

"I've always been smart. You've just finally noticed." She stood, pulled him up. "Come on. I'll be your demo dummy if you need one."

Inside, the pool was already churning faintly — Clarisse doing laps, hard and fast, leaving a white wake. Silena was setting up with two stopwatches now ("One for rounds, one for rest, I'm very professional") and a fresh batch of banana muffins.

Thalia arrived with Annabeth, carrying two spears and looking half-asleep and formidable.

"Morning, squids," she said. "Teach us something cool, Percy, or I'm going back to bed."

"No pressure," Percy said faintly, and everyone laughed.

He stepped into the shallow end, water to his knees, and tried to recall Master Ione's demonstration on his visit. Low, amused, patient. Salt in the air. Green light through deep water.

"Okay," he said. "So in Atlantis, the palace guards didn't fight like we do here. Not mostly swords. They did — forms. Like dances, but for fighting in water. Because water slows you down if you muscle through it, so you have to — you have to move with it."

Clarisse waded in opposite him, listening intently. This was new for her — being the student — and she wore it well. Focused, open.

"First one is called Tide-Breath," Percy said. "It's just breathing and stance. Feet like this — one forward, one back, knees soft. Not wide like arena stance. Narrow. So you can turn fast."

He showed her. Narrow, knees bent, weight centered. Inhale as the water pushed, exhale as it pulled. Let the surge move you an inch instead of resisting.

Clarisse copied him. Her arena stance was wide and rooted — good on land, stubborn in water. She wobbled.

"Smaller," Percy said, and without thinking, tapped her front foot with his own. "Here. Like you're on a boat."

Clarisse adjusted. Steadied. Nodded slowly. "Oh. Oh, that's — that's easier. Huh."

"Right?" Percy grinned, nerves easing. "Now — Eel-Skin, like yesterday, but moving. You keep the spin while you step. Watch."

He walked through the water, slow, turning his torso with each step, left arm trailing a ribbon of water that clung to his skin and spun. It was not showy. It was smooth. The pool lights caught the ribbon and made it glow.

Silena gasped. Annabeth whispered, "Whoa."

Clarisse tried. Her first attempt splashed apart. Her second held for two steps, then collapsed.

"Don't grab it," Percy said, remembering. "Ask it. Like — like you would a horse. You don't yank the reins."

Clarisse laughed. "You know I hate horses."

"Pegasus," Thalia corrected from the sidelines. "We like pegasi. Horses are the worst."

"Fine. Like a pegasus," Percy said. "Gentle."

Clarisse tried again, slower, breathing with it. This time the ribbon held for five steps, clinging to her forearm, shedding spray.

"Yes!" Percy said. "That's it! That's exactly it!"

Clarisse stared at her arm, at the water spinning there, and her face did something complicated — surprise and delight and something softer. "I felt that. It — it felt like — okay, that's cool. That's really cool."

They drilled Tide-Breath and Eel-Skin for an hour, walking laps in the pool, turning, breathing. Silena joined in the shallow end, giggling when her ribbon fell apart. Even Thalia tried the footwork on the steps, without the water-shield her father's powers could not make, and when she managed three steps, she punched the air.

Annabeth, who could not move water but could read a fight like a book, called observations. "Clarisse, you're holding your breath on the turn. Breathe out there. Percy, your shoulders are creeping up. Drop them."

"You should coach," Clarisse told her, panting, hair wet. "Seriously. You see everything."

"I am coaching," Annabeth said primly, making a note. "I'm also winning our bet with Thalia."

"What bet?" Percy asked.

"Whether you two would hug by the end of the session," Thalia said.

"We're not—" Clarisse started, and then Percy, laughing, splashed her, and she tackled him sideways into the deep end, and they both went under and came up snorting, and Silena blew the whistle and shouted, "Hug! That counts as a hug! Pay up, Thalia!"

Thalia tossed Annabeth a drachma with great disgust while Annabeth caught it and bowed.

After, wrapped in towels, Clarisse was quiet for a long time, turning her practice sword over in her hands.

"That was good," she said finally to Percy. "What you taught. That was — I have been fighting for six years and nobody ever taught me to move like that. To not brace."

Percy shrugged, embarrassed and pleased. "Atlantis stuff. It's not better. Just different."

"It's better in water," Clarisse said honestly. "On land I'd still flatten you. But in water — yeah. Thank you for showing me."

Percy felt warm all the way down to his toes, and it was not just the pool.

"Anytime," he said. "Sis."

Clarisse ruffled his hair hard enough to nearly take his head off, and he laughed and ducked, and Silena took a picture with a waterproof camera she had smuggled in ("For the cabin wall!") and Annabeth leaned over to see and said, "You both look like drowned rats," with great affection.

Thalia looked at the picture over their shoulders and said, "Cute. Storm sisters take better pictures, though."

"Prove it," Silena said, and Thalia and Annabeth posed with spears crossed and lightning crackling faintly between their fingertips, and Silena took that picture too, and everyone agreed both pictures were going on the wall — sea siblings on the left, storm sisters on the right, muffins in the middle.

---

Thursday it rained hard, drumming on the roof of Tempest Hall so loud they had to shout. Friday it was clear and bitter cold, frost on the dunes, the pool steaming like a cauldron.

Clarisse loved both days for different reasons, and both days were disasters in the best way.

Thursday: swords in the water. Real swords — well, real practice swords, bronze blunted — in chest-deep water, trying to spar while moving in Tide-Breath stance and holding Eel-Skin. It was impossible. It was hilarious.

Percy kept slipping. Clarisse kept overreaching because she was used to lunging on solid ground. They clashed, tangled,went under, came up laughing with mouths full of salt.

"Stop laughing!" Clarisse ordered, laughing so hard she could barely hold her sword. "This is serious combat training!"

"You said 'combat training' while you have seaweed on your head," Percy gasped, picking a strand off her bun.

Silena, from her lifeguard chair with a blanket and a thermometer ("Water is eighty-two degrees, air is fifty-four, morale is excellent"), called, "Round four to nobody! Nobody wins! Everyone is wet!"

On the north end, Annabeth and Thalia were doing shield work. Thalia had dug out an old aegis-shield — heavy bronze with a storm-cloud design — and was teaching Annabeth to take a hit without getting knocked flat.

"Bend the knees!" Thalia shouted over the rain. "Not the back! Knees! Like you're sitting in a chair that isn't there!"

Annabeth gritted her teeth, took the padded battering ram Thalia pushed at her, skidded back six inches, and held.

"Good!" Thalia said. "Again! And yell! It helps!"

Annabeth yelled — a high, furious sound that made Percy look up and grin — and held again, and Thalia whooped.

"Storm sisters are loud today," Clarisse observed, circling Percy, swords raised.

"Sea siblings are louder," Percy said, and lunged.

He did not hold back. That was the thing Clarisse noticed Thursday and carried in her chest like a warm stone. He came at her full speed, water curling around his blade, eyes bright and focused. She parried, riposted, caught him on the shoulder with the flat — a hit that would bruise — and he grinned and said, "Nice!" and came again.

No flinch. No apology. Just fighting.

After, in the showers — quick, hot, elbows bumping — Clarisse caught Percy's arm and turned it to see the bruise.

"Sorry," she said automatically, then frowned at herself. "No — not sorry. Are you okay?"

Percy grinned, water dripping from his nose. "I'm great. You got me clean. Show me that twist again tomorrow?"

Clarisse felt something loosen behind her ribs. "Yeah. I will."

Friday: worst and best. They were tired — four dawns in a row will do that to young campers — and cranky. Percy was quiet, moving slow. Clarisse's shoulder ached. Silena fell asleep on the clipboard and whistled in her sleep. Annabeth snapped at Thalia for correcting her grip for the tenth time, and Thalia snapped back, and for ten minutes the north end was frosty in a way that had nothing to do with weather.

Clarisse called a halt. She waded to the center, water to her waist, and said, "Okay. New drill. Everyone in the pool. No weapons. Game is Keep-Away. One ball." She produced a battered rubber ball from the equipment bin. "Sea siblings versus storm sisters plus Silena. First to ten catches wins. Losers make hot chocolate."

"What?" Thalia said. "That's not training."

"It's absolutely training," Clarisse said. "Footwork, teamwork, reading the water, reading people. Also we need to wake up."

It worked. The game was chaos — splashing, shouting, Silena shrieking with laughter as she intercepted a pass meant for Percy, Annabeth climbing onto Thalia's shoulders to catch a high throw (allowed? "Absolutely not," Clarisse ruled, laughing, "do it again"), Percy lifting Annabeth clean out of the water to steal the ball and both of them going under in a tangle while everyone screamed.

Final score: 10-9, sea siblings won when Clarisse faked a throw to Silena and lobbed it to Percy behind Thalia's back.

"Cheating!" Thalia declared. "Sea cheating! Using sister telepathy!"

"You have sister telepathy too," Silena pointed out reasonably, wringing out her braid. "You just didn't use it."

Losers made hot chocolate — real hot chocolate this time, with Thalia's emergency marshmallows and Silena's secret cinnamon — and they drank it in the pool, floating on noodles, too tired to get out, watching sunrise paint the high windows gold and pink.

Percy floated beside Clarisse, eyes half-closed, hot mug balanced on his stomach.

"Thank you," he said quietly, so only she could hear. "For this week. I — I feel different. Lighter. Like I'm not carrying something heavy around in a fight anymore."

Clarisse nudged his noodle with hers. "You're fighting like yourself now. Not like you're scared of yourself."

Percy nodded, eyes still closed, smiling.

Annabeth floated over and pillowed her head on Percy's shoulder, her own noodle tangled with his. Thalia floated on Percy's other side and stole his marshmallows. Silena floated at their feet like a figurehead, humming.

Clarisse looked at the tangle of limbs and noodles and mugs, at her brother, her sister Silena, and the Grace friends they had chosen as family, and thought that this — this noisy, warm, silly dawn — was exactly what the pool was for.

Not just war-forms. Family-forms.

She did not say that out loud. She would never live it down. But Silena caught her eye and smiled like she had heard it anyway.

---

Saturday, Percy learned how the other half lived.

He woke before his alarm — 4:40 a.m., dark outside Cabin Three, the sound of the sea through the screen — and for once did not groan. His body hummed, pleasantly sore, used to dawns now. He pulled on trunks and a sweatshirt, grabbed his towel, and met Clarisse and Silena on the path with flashlights.

"You look awake," Silena said suspiciously. "Are you sick?"

"I'm evolving," Percy said. "Like a Pokémon. Dawn Percy is a new form."

"Keep evolving toward carrying the muffin tin," Clarisse said, handing it to him.

Tempest Hall was coldest just before sunrise, and today the windows showed a sky full of stars fading at the edges. The pool glowed. The air smelled like salt and rain.

Silena set up. Clarisse stretched. Percy slid into the water and sighed happily as warmth closed over him.

"Today," Clarisse announced, "we watch."

"What?" Percy said.

"We've gone five days straight of us hammering each other. Today we learn from the storm sisters. Thalia offered to show us Zeus-style sparring — footwork, spear range. Annabeth's been taking notes all week. Time to trade."

As if on cue, the doors opened and Thalia and Annabeth came in, carrying spears, shields, and a whiteboard.

"A whiteboard?" Percy said.

"I made diagrams," Annabeth said proudly, propping it up. On it, in neat marker, were stick figures labeled SEA (with wavy hair) and STORM (with spiky hair) and arrows showing range. "Thalia's reach versus your lunge. Clarisse, you overcommit against long weapons. Percy, you crowd."

Clarisse peered at the board. "Huh. She's right. I do."

"I am always right," Annabeth said. "It's a burden."

Thalia snorted and ruffled her sister's hair. "Okay, little strategist. Show them the drill."

For the next hour, roles reversed. Percy and Clarisse sat on the steps with Silena, wrapped in towels, sipping cocoa Silena had pre-made in a thermos, while Thalia and Annabeth sparred in the shallow end and narrated.

"Spear wins at distance," Thalia explained, keeping Annabeth at bay with the butt of her spear. "See? She can't get inside without risking a poke. So what does she do?"

Annabeth answered by dropping low, slipping sideways, and tapping Thalia's ankle with her dagger. "Go around. Or wait for the thrust and bind."

"Exactly. Patience. Let them waste energy." Thalia thrust, Annabeth parried with her dagger angled, stepped in, and stopped with the flat touching Thalia's ribs. Both froze, grinning.

"Point to Annabeth!" Silena shouted, blowing the whistle. "Excellent patience!"

Percy cheered so loud it echoed. Annabeth bowed with a flourish, hair dripping, cheeks pink.

They ran the drill again and again — Thalia showing how Zeus kids used static bursts to startle (a crackle along the spear that made Percy yelp even from the sidelines), Annabeth showing how to read shoulders to predict strikes.

"Your sister's good," Clarisse murmured to Thalia during water break, nodding at Annabeth, who was explaining something to Percy with great hand gestures while he nodded solemnly. "Really good. Fast learner."

Thalia's face softened in a way it rarely did outside family. "She is. She thinks three moves ahead. She was doing that at six years old with chess. Now she does it with knives. It's — I worry sometimes. She thinks she has to be perfect to be safe."

Clarisse understood that in her bones. "Percy was like that with water. Like if he wasn't perfect, someone would get hurt."

"And now?" Thalia asked.

Clarisse watched Percy laugh at something Annabeth said, watched him demonstrate a Tide-Breath turn in the ankle-deep water, watched Annabeth copy him clumsily and both of them collapse giggling.

"Now he's letting himself be messy," Clarisse said. "It's better."

Thalia bumped her shoulder. "Good coaching, sea girl."

"Good genes," Clarisse said. "Good sisters."

Later, they swapped — Percy and Clarisse sparring with spears Thalia lent them, terrible and laughing, while Annabeth and Thalia provided extremely biased commentary from Silena's lifeguard chair with the stopwatch.

"And Clarisse holds the spear like it's a mop!" Annabeth announced in her best arena voice. "Percy holds the spear like it's going to bite him! Oh! Oh! They've both dropped them! Stunning technique!"

"We're water kids!" Percy protested, fishing his spear from the pool. "We don't do sticks!"

"It's a spear, not a stick," Thalia and Annabeth said together, and everyone laughed.

Silena called the final round a draw — "Both equally bad at spears! Hug!" — and they did, dripping and laughing, sea and storm mixed up together.

On the walk back for breakfast, Percy and Annabeth lagged behind, hands swinging between them.

"You were great today," Percy said. "The diagrams and everything. You're going to be a general someday."

Annabeth swung their hands. "You're great every day. You're different this week. Looser. Happier."

"I feel happier," Percy said. "Clarisse — she pushes me, but like, safe. I can go big and she just grins and comes back. I didn't know I needed that."

Annabeth leaned her head against his arm for a step. "That's what sisters are for. Thalia does that for me. Lets me try stupid things and catches me."

"Are we lucky?" Percy asked. "Good sisters?"

"The luckiest," Annabeth said firmly. "Even if yours steal your muffins and mine steal your marshmallows."

"Even then," Percy agreed, and kissed the top of her head the way he'd seen Thalia do to Annabeth, and Annabeth pretended to be disgusted and clearly was not.

---

Sunday was the last dawn, and Clarisse woke with a tight feeling behind her sternum that she recognized as not wanting something to end.

She lay in her bunk in Cabin Three for a minute, listening to Silena breathing in the bunk below and Percy snoring faintly across the room with one arm hanging off the mattress. Through the window, the sky was still black. Her practice suit was already laid out, damp from yesterday and smelling like salt. Her shoulder was sore. Her elbow was green-yellow. She had never been happier in a training week in her life.

She got up quietly, shook Silena awake, threw a pillow at Percy.

"Last day," she said. "Make it count."

Tempest Hall at dawn on Sunday was golden. A thin fog hung over the dunes outside, and light came through the high windows in slow shafts full of dust. The pool was glass-calm, the mosaic hippocampus clear and bright.

Silena set up with ceremony — stopwatch, whistle, clipboard with a new sheet that read FINAL SPAR — SEA SIBLINGS SHOWDOWN — plus a fresh tin of chocolate-chip muffins and a thermos of real hot chocolate she had woken the kitchen naiad for.

Thalia and Annabeth arrived early, in sweats, with blankets. No weapons today. "Audience," Thalia declared. "And judges. We brought scoring paddles." She held up two paper plates with numbers drawn on.

"We're very official," Annabeth said, sitting cross-legged with her notebook on her knees. "Silena, what's the format?"

Silena blew the whistle importantly. "Five rounds! Three minutes each! Swords in water! Full contact — well, full practice contact! Points for clean hits, ring-outs, and style! No powers above — uh — medium?"

"Medium-plus," Clarisse corrected, already in the water, rolling her shoulders. "We've done medium all week. Today we go medium-plus. Whatever that means."

"It means don't bring the roof down," Silena said sternly. "I have to clean this place."

Percy slid in opposite Clarisse, Riptide uncapped, bronze gleaming in the pool lights. He wore a rash guard today, hair tied back with one of Silena's hair ties. He looked older than twelve for a second — focused, calm, water already curling faintly around his ankles like it was listening.

Then he grinned. "Ready to lose, old lady?"

"I'm a year older than you, plankton," Clarisse said. "Ready to eat pool floor?"

"Such trash talk," Thalia observed from the sidelines, sipping cocoa. "Six out of ten."

"Seven," Annabeth said, writing it down. "Percy gets an extra point for the hair tie. He looks dashing."

"Focus, judges," Silena said. "Round one! Fight!"

Round one went to Clarisse — she faked high, swept low, caught Percy's knee and tapped his chest while he was off-balance. Clean, fast, arena-perfect.

"Point Clarisse!" Silena shouted. Annabeth held up an 8, Thalia a 9.

Round two went to Percy — he let Clarisse drive him back, breathed with the surge, turned at the last second in Tide-Breath and let her momentum carry her past while he tapped her back. Atlantean footwork, pure and smooth.

"Point Percy!" Silena shouted. "Beautiful turn! I saw that! That was textbook!"

"Paddles?" Thalia held up 9, Annabeth 10. "Biased," Thalia accused. "You're dating him."

"I'm objective," Annabeth said with dignity. "He was perfect."

Round three was a mess — both tried Eel-Skin shields while sparring and both collapsed laughing when a wave Percy raised to block Clarisse's lunge instead soaked Silena's clipboard.

"Foul!" Silena shrieked, shielding the muffins with her body. "Assault on the judge! Minus one point each!"

"Sorry, Silena!" they chorused, still laughing, treading water.

Annabeth wrote SOAKED JUDGE in her notebook and underlined it three times. Thalia held up a 10 for comedy.

Round four was the best of the week. No laughing. Just fighting — clean and hard and joyful. Sword on sword, ringing in the steamy air. Water rising around them in curtains and shields, spinning off blades, catching light. Clarisse using Tide-Breath without thinking, Percy using a shield-bash Clarisse had taught him Tuesday. Trading lessons back and forth in real time.

Clarisse felt her heart pounding, her lungs burning, her smile so wide it hurt. This was it. This was what she had wanted — Percy full-out, not holding back, water high and bright around him, eyes fierce and happy.

She lunged. He sidestepped. She turned. He turned. They ended chest to chest in the center of the pool, swords crossed, each with the edge at the other's throat — frozen.

Silence, except for dripping water and their breathing.

Silena stood up slowly, stopwatch beeping forgotten. "Oh — oh my gods—"

"Draw?" Percy whispered, grinning, sword steady.

"Draw," Clarisse whispered back, grinning harder.

They dropped their swords into the water with twin splashes and hugged — wet and panting and laughing, spinning in a circle while the pool sloshed around them.

The sidelines erupted. Silena blew the whistle repeatedly and screamed. Annabeth was on her feet cheering, notebook abandoned. Thalia whistled through her teeth, lightning crackling happily at her fingertips.

"Ten! Ten! Ten!" Annabeth shouted, holding up both paddles.

"Perfect draw!" Silena declared, writing DRAW!!! in huge letters and circling it. "Sea siblings both win! Everyone wins!"

Clarisse held Percy at arm's length, water streaming from both of them. "You didn't hold back," she said. It came out rough, prouder than she meant.

"You didn't let me," Percy said, beaming. "You never let me. Thank you."

Clarisse pulled him in again, quick and fierce, and ruffled his wet hair. "Anytime, little brother. Anytime."

They climbed out to blankets and cocoa and muffins and sisters. Annabeth threw herself at Percy, nearly knocking him over, and kissed his cheek while he laughed. Thalia clapped Clarisse on the back hard enough to stagger her.

"Good fight, sea girl," Thalia said respectfully. "You both looked like actual warriors for a minute there."

"High praise from the mop-holders," Clarisse said, and Thalia laughed and stole her muffin in retaliation.

Silena produced the waterproof camera and set it on the bench with the timer. "Group picture! Last dawn! Everyone in!"

They squeezed together on the pool edge — Clarisse and Percy in front, dripping and bruised and grinning, Silena between them with her arms around both, Annabeth leaning on Percy's shoulder, Thalia behind with her hands on Annabeth's shoulders — and smiled as the flash went off, with the steaming pool and golden windows behind them.

After, they cleaned up in comfortable silence — racking swords, coiling hoses, wringing towels — while the sun rose fully and turned the fog outside to white gold. No one hurried. No one wanted to leave first.

"Hot chocolate," Silena announced finally, holding up the empty thermos. "Real hot chocolate. Big House kitchen. My treat. To celebrate the draw."

"Seconded," Annabeth said. "With extra marshmallows for the winners."

"Who are everyone," Thalia said. "Because draws mean everyone wins and no one does dishes."

They walked up the hill together in the bright cold morning, five across the path, towels over shoulders, hair wet, bruises blooming, chattering about round four and the soaked judge and whether Thalia could learn the footwork for Eel-Skin by next week.

Percy fell into step beside Clarisse, bumping her elbow.

"Same time next week?" he asked hopefully.

Clarisse pretended to think. "Same time next week. But you're teaching Tide-Breath to Silena from the start. And we're inviting Annabeth to do whiteboard tactics every Saturday. And Thalia has to bring marshmallows every time."

"Deal," Percy said. "No holding back?"

"No holding back," Clarisse said, and held out her fist.

He bumped it, and they walked into the steam and noise and sugar-smell of breakfast together — sea siblings, storm sisters, and Silena with the muffin tin held high like a trophy — while the sun came up over Camp Half-Blood and the sea glittered beyond the dunes, keeping time with their breathing: in, out, in, out, like a tide that finally knew where home was.
