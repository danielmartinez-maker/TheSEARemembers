## Chapter 102: Not a Competition

Clarisse knew Percy was upset before he knew it himself.

He'd been quiet since breakfast, and not his normal quiet. This was the kind where he pushed food around his plate, answered questions half a beat too late, and kept glancing toward the training grounds.

"If you're going to stare at the arena," Clarisse said, "you might as well go over there."

Percy looked down at his pancakes. "I'm not staring."

"You are."

Silena followed his gaze toward the field. A new camper named Noah was practicing with Annabeth. He'd arrived at camp three days ago, a Hermes kid with quick hands and an easy smile. Annabeth was showing him how to brace his feet for a spear thrust. Noah listened closely, then tried the movement again.

She smiled when he got it right.

Percy's fork bent slightly in his hand.

Clarisse reached over and took it from him before he could break it. "You're being ridiculous."

"I'm not jealous."

"Nobody said jealous."

Percy glanced at Silena. She had the tact to look into her tea.

"He's new," Percy said. "He doesn't know the footwork. Annabeth's helping him. That's all."

"Exactly," Clarisse said. "That's all."

"I know."

"Then why are you trying to bend the tableware?"

Percy stared at the fork in her hand. "It was already bent."

"Sure. And I was born yesterday."

Silena set down her cup. "Maybe he's not jealous of Noah. Maybe he's worried about being replaced."

Percy looked at her. "I'm not—"

"You can be worried and still trust Annabeth," Silena said. "Those aren't opposites. Just don't make the worry her problem to solve unless there's something real to talk about."

Clarisse gave her sister a sidelong look. "That's almost sensible."

"I save my nonsense for special occasions."

Percy pushed back from the table. "I'm going to training."

"You could also just go ask Annabeth how her morning is," Silena said.

"That sounds like a trap."

"It's not a trap if you ask a normal question in a normal voice," Clarisse said. "Try it sometime."

Clarisse reached for the pitcher. Her sleeve caught the handle, and the last of the syrup sloshed over the rim. Silena caught the pitcher before it tipped. Percy grabbed the stack of clean plates with his free hand, and the three of them spent a moment saving breakfast from becoming a bigger mess.

"See?" Clarisse said when the pitcher was upright again. "A normal question. We can all do those."

"You were going to tell him what to do next," Silena said.

"I was telling him to talk to Annabeth."

"You told him how to talk to her."

"Because he was about to go over there and say something with that face."

"What face?" Percy asked.

"The one that says you're already arguing with somebody in your head."

He looked at Silena. She was wiping the syrup from her fingers with a napkin, not smiling now. "I have a face for that?"

"You have several," she said. "That one's the most obvious."

"I don't think it helps to catalogue them."

"We aren't doing that," Clarisse said. "We're trying to get you to stop making the fork suffer."

"The fork was fine."

"It was bent."

"It was bent before."

"You keep saying that like metal bends itself."

Percy leaned over to put the saved plates on the table. The pavilion carried on around them: a bench dragged across the floor, someone at the Apollo table testing a note on a flute, the dining nymphs stacking cups. Nothing had happened. No one else had noticed his hand tightening around a fork. The fact that his sisters had noticed was both comforting and irritating.

Silena turned the syrup pitcher so its handle faced away from Clarisse's sleeve. "You don't need us to solve it for you. But you look like you're trying to make the feeling disappear by pretending it's not there."

"I don't know what the feeling is yet."

"Then you can figure it out."

"That's what I'm doing."

"Good." Clarisse pushed the bent fork farther from him. "Do it without breaking the tableware."

Percy looked at the fork again. "I didn't ask you to take it."

Clarisse paused. The answer was too easy to throw back at him. She set the fork down between them. "No. You didn't."

"I didn't mind that you noticed," Percy said. "I mind that both of you started deciding what I should do before I knew what I was feeling."

"I said you should talk to her," Silena said.

"And Clarisse said how. Then you said I was worried about being replaced. Maybe that's it. Maybe it isn't. I don't want to sit here while the two of you take turns figuring me out."

Silena folded the napkin once, then again. "You're right."

"That doesn't mean you're wrong about the worry," Clarisse said, and then she saw his expression. "Fine. That was me doing it again."

"Maybe I'm a little jealous," Percy said. The words felt smaller than the scene they had made in his head. "Maybe I saw her teaching him and got stuck on the fact that he gets to meet her now, in a way I didn't. The version of her he sees doesn't come with all the years I remember. And the version of me everyone here knows has years I can't call up when I want them."

"That's not Noah's fault," Clarisse said.

"I know."

"Annabeth either."

"I know that too."

"Does knowing help?" Silena asked.

"Sometimes. It doesn't turn off the first reaction."

Silena nodded, as if that answer were more useful than a perfect one. "Then maybe the first reaction can sit there while you decide what to do. You don't have to make it a rule for anyone."

"That's what I want."

"Then say that," Clarisse said. "Not the part where you glare at a guy who hasn't done anything."

"I haven't glared at him."

Both sisters looked at him.

"Okay. I might have looked at him."

"Like the fork," Clarisse said.

"The fork was different."

"Was it?"

"One of them had a face."

Silena's mouth twitched, but she waited. Clarisse crossed her arms and looked at him. Percy could tell they were both holding back another answer. It made him want to apologize for putting them in the middle, then remind them that he hadn't asked to be put in the middle. Neither thought was exactly fair, so he tried the simpler thing.

"I don't want you to go talk to Annabeth for me," he said. "And I don't want you to make Noah feel like he's done something wrong. If I need help, I'll ask. You can ask me first."

Clarisse rubbed a thumb over the rim of her cup. "I can do that."

"Me too," Silena said. "And I can stop naming the feeling before you do."

"I might still ask what you think."

"You can."

"Then I'll try to answer the question you actually asked."

"That would be a change."

"It would be an improvement."

Clarisse took up the fork. She studied its bent tines, then set it beside her plate instead of trying to straighten it. "We should tell the kitchen someone brought this back like that."

"Someone?" Percy asked.

"Probably a Stoll."

"Probably."

No one asked which one. Clarisse took the pitcher and carried it to the service counter while Silena gathered the cups. Percy stayed to stack plates. The work gave them something to do that did not require looking at him, and that helped. When they finished, the syrup was wiped up and the bent fork had been set aside with a note for the kitchen.

At the table, Silena picked up her book and slid it into her bag. "I'll be by the pavilion after lunch if you want to find me."

"I'll be at the arena."

"I know. I meant if you want to find me."

He nodded. She didn't repeat the offer. Clarisse waited until Silena had crossed the floor before she spoke.

"You don't have to prove you're okay by going over there."

"I'm going to train. I was going to train anyway."

"Then train."

"I can do that."

"Good." Clarisse's voice had gone rougher, as if the short exchange embarrassed her more than the argument. "I'll be at the lower field with the shields."

"Teaching Noah?"

"He asked yesterday. If he shows up, I'll teach him. If he doesn't, I'll put the shields away. That's all."

Percy looked across at the training grounds through the open side of the pavilion. Noah was still with Annabeth, adjusting the angle of his grip while she watched. He couldn't hear them. He didn't need to.

"Okay," he said.

"And Percy?"

"Yeah?"

"Try not to bend the weapons."

"Only forks?"

"Especially forks. The kitchen gets mad about those."

"Noted."

Clarisse returned to the lower field. Percy took his bag and walked toward the arena without trying to decide, in advance, what he would say to Annabeth.

---

He trained harder than he needed to.

The practice sword felt good in his hands. It gave him something simple to do: block, step, turn, strike. His partner was an older camper from the Ares cabin who didn't mind the extra effort. By the third round, Percy was breathing hard enough to stop thinking about the training field.

His partner reset his feet. "Again?"

"Sure."

"That wasn't a yes."

Percy rolled his shoulder once. "Again."

They started at the center line. The older camper's first strike came high, then dipped toward Percy's side. Percy caught it, turned his wrist and stepped in. The blade stopped a finger's width from the other boy's shoulder.

"Better," the camper said. "You waited this time."

"I was waiting before."

"You were guessing before."

Percy frowned. The practice sword rested along his forearm. "I knew where you were going."

"You knew what I did last time. I changed it."

"That doesn't make it a good move."

"It did what it was supposed to do."

Percy didn't have an answer to that. His partner backed away and raised the sword again.

The next few rounds were slower. Percy tried to pay attention to the opening rather than the pattern he expected. It worked until he got tired. His reach shortened, and he was late turning his wrist. He could feel how much strength the practice required in this body. The motions came back easily, but not always at the speed he wanted. Knowing what a move should feel like was not the same as being able to force his arms to do it.

He didn't like the gap. It made him want to push harder until the body obeyed.

"You're crossing your feet," the Ares camper said.

"I know."

"Then don't."

"Thanks."

The other boy lowered his sword. "You want to take a break?"

"I'm fine."

The camper didn't argue. He stepped back and waited.

That was worse than arguing. Percy wiped his palm on his pants and looked at the two marks they had worn into the packed dirt. He could do another round. He could also tell that his hands were starting to tighten around the hilt.

"One more," he said.

"One clean one."

"All right."

They began. Percy watched the camper's shoulders, not his sword. A twitch on one side, a shift of weight; Percy adjusted his feet and blocked before the strike reached him. He turned the blade aside, kept his balance, and stopped when the other boy raised a hand.

"That one was clean," Percy said.

"Mostly."

"You're supposed to say it was perfect."

"I'm not."

Percy laughed despite himself. His chest still felt tight, but it no longer had a name to hide behind. He set the practice sword on the rack and drank from his canteen. He had not solved anything by swinging faster. He had made himself tired, and the feeling had stayed.

The older camper went to gather the practice blades. Percy began to help, but someone called his partner's name from the far side of the arena. The boy pointed to the return rack.

"Can you put mine back?"

"Yeah."

"Thanks."

Percy wiped the practice sword's handle with the cloth beside the rack and put it in the correct slot. He checked that it sat all the way in before letting go. He was still looking down when Annabeth and Noah crossed the edge of the field.
Then Annabeth and Noah walked past.

Percy's next parry came late. His partner tapped him on the shoulder with the flat of the blade.

"Point," the boy said. "You okay?"

"Yeah. Sorry."

Annabeth glanced over. "Percy, can you help me carry these?"

She had three training spears tucked under one arm. Noah was carrying the rest.

"Sure." Percy sheathed his practice sword and went to take the spears.

Noah handed them over. "Thanks. Annabeth showed me the grip. I kept dropping them."

"It's a weird balance," Percy said.

"You should see him with a sword," Annabeth said. "He's got good instincts. Needs to stop trying to overpower every move."

Noah laughed. "I think she said the same thing about you."

Percy looked at Annabeth. Her expression was innocent. Too innocent.

"She did," he admitted. "A lot."

"Could I try that step once?" Percy asked.

Annabeth looked at Noah first. "Would you be okay with that?"

"Sure," Noah said. "I want to see if I understood it."

Annabeth set the spears down on the padded grass and drew a short line with her heel. "Then both of you start here. No weapon. Noah, do it at the pace you were using before. Percy, match him. Don't add speed."

"I wasn't planning to."

She gave him a look.

"Fine. I might have been planning to."

"Don't."

Percy stood beside Noah at the line. Annabeth had moved several paces away so she could watch their feet. She told them to step forward once, turn, and stop where they could still see the target stand without twisting their shoulders. Noah moved carefully. Percy caught himself trying to step farther. The line ended under his front foot, and he had to pull back to match Noah's pace.

"Reset," Annabeth said.

They returned to the starting mark.

"Why did you stop?" she asked.

Percy glanced down. "I was too far ahead."

"Try again at his pace."

He did. The movement felt slow, almost too small to count as practice. He watched Noah's feet instead of the stand. When Noah stopped, Percy stopped with him. Annabeth nodded and asked them to turn without stepping this time.

Noah turned from the waist and shifted his back foot. Percy did the same, but his heel dragged through the dirt.

"Again," Annabeth said. "Percy, lift your heel. Noah, keep your front foot where it is."

They repeated the turn. This time Percy's foot cleared the ground. He expected Annabeth to praise the improvement, but she was already watching Noah's shoulder.

"What's the difference?" she asked him.

"I didn't move my feet first," Noah said. "I turned my body, then moved."

"Good. That is what you were guessing at earlier."

"Can we do it with the spears?" Percy asked.

"One at a time. You both take a shaft from the padded end. Keep the point down until I tell you to lift it."

She handed Noah one spear and then Percy another. They stood far enough apart that the lengths could move without crossing. Annabeth repeated the sequence. Step. Turn. Stop. Noah's spear tipped toward the stand. He caught it before the point reached the ground.

"Hold," Annabeth said. She walked over, asked if he was all right, then showed him how to use his back hand to steady the shaft. He tried it again. Percy followed the same direction, keeping his own spear low, and found the movement more awkward than he expected. He had to adjust his grip to keep the balance without pulling his wrist tight.

"That better?" Noah asked.

"Yeah," Percy said. "I was trying to make the turn too big."

"Me too."

Annabeth waited until they had both set the spears down before she spoke. "That is enough for today. You've both got a thing to watch next time. Noah, your front foot. Percy, the space between the turn and the stop."

"I didn't know I was taking a lesson," Percy said.

"You asked for one."

"I asked for one pass."

"That was the pass."

Noah looked from Annabeth to Percy. "Thanks for trying it with me."

"Thanks for letting me," Percy said.

Annabeth picked up the first spear and handed it to Noah. "Now we can put these away."


They walked the spears back together. Noah carried the lighter end of the bundle while Percy took the other. Annabeth walked beside them, still talking through the lesson: where Noah's front foot had landed, how the shaft had turned in his hands, what he should watch when he tried the movement without her calling each step.

Noah listened. Once, he asked Annabeth to explain the difference between moving the spear and moving his body around it. She demonstrated with an empty hand, then asked him to show her what he thought she meant. He got the first step right and the second one wrong.

"Good," Annabeth said. "Now you know which part you were guessing at."

Noah frowned at his feet. "I was hoping you'd say I did it right."

"You'll do it right when you can repeat it without me reminding you."

"That sounds like work."

"It is."

He looked at Percy. "Is she always like this?"

"With training? Yes."

"With everything," Annabeth said.

"Not everything."

"Percy, you once asked me to check a map and then argued with every correction."

"It was a bad map."

"It was upside down."

"It was a bad map in a different direction."

Noah laughed, and Percy found himself laughing too. The moment was ordinary enough that he almost missed what his mind tried to do with it. It tried to keep score: Annabeth had smiled at Noah. Noah had made her laugh. Percy had been there too. As if one laugh had to belong to one person at a time.

The path to the weapon rack narrowed where a canvas cover had been laid out to dry. Two younger campers were collecting the pegs along the edge, so Annabeth slowed. Noah adjusted his hold. The butt of one spear slipped against the ground.

"Set it down," Annabeth said.

"I have it."

"I know. Set it down before the point catches the canvas."

Noah froze rather than wrenching the bundle away. Percy lowered his end onto the padded strip beside the path. They put the practice spears down together, keeping the metal ends clear of the cloth and the campers' hands.

"Thanks," Noah said. His ears had gone pink. "I thought if I stopped, they would all slide."

"They didn't," Percy said. "They were leaning on you because you were trying to carry too many at once."

"I didn't want to make two trips."

"Two trips are faster than one trip to the infirmary," Annabeth said.

"I wasn't going to get hurt."

"No. But you could have knocked someone over. That's why we stop."

The younger campers picked up the last peg and carried the canvas away. Annabeth waited until the path was clear before asking Noah if he wanted to carry fewer spears or switch sides. He chose to carry fewer. Percy didn't offer to take all of them. Annabeth divided the bundle into two manageable loads and checked that the lengths cleared each other.

They started again. This time Noah watched the shafts instead of the people behind them. Percy took the rear end of the second load. When Noah slowed at the turn, Percy matched his pace. Annabeth walked ahead to open the rack gate and held it until both boys had passed.

The weapon rack was fuller than Percy expected. There was space for the practice spears, but not if they tried to shove them between the javelins. Annabeth asked Noah to stop short of the slots and look at how the rack was arranged.

"Which place gives you room to put them away without lifting above your shoulder?" she asked.

Noah pointed to the lower row.

"That one has the short handles. What else?"

He leaned in, then stepped back. "The second slot is empty. The first one has something tied to it."

"Good. We'll use the second. Keep the ends together until you're facing it."

Percy shifted his grip to help keep the bundle level. Noah took the front ends and waited for Annabeth's signal. She showed him how to rotate his wrists so the shafts turned as a group. He followed the movement slowly, checked that the points stayed toward the padded end of the rack, and slid them into place.

"Now count," Annabeth said.

Noah looked at the rack. "All of them?"

"All the ones we carried."

Percy named each shaft as Noah touched the butt end. They matched the number they had brought. Annabeth gave the rack a careful push to see that the spears were seated and not resting on the edge. Nothing shifted.

Noah exhaled. "I thought putting things away was the easy part."

"It usually is," Percy said. "That doesn't mean you skip checking."

"Does Clarisse make you do that?"

"Clarisse makes me check everything twice."

"That isn't true," Annabeth said. "She makes you check it twice after you say it's done."

"That's different."

"Not really."

Noah smiled. "I thought she was going to teach me shield turns today."

"She might," Annabeth said. "That's separate from this. Yesterday she showed you one way to turn with a shield. Today you're learning how to handle a spear without letting the shaft decide where your feet go."

"So I don't try the shield turn with the spear."

"Please don't," Percy said. "The rack is not a practice partner."

"Noted."

Annabeth offered Noah one more slow pass without a weapon. He set his feet, turned his hips, and stopped before the place where the spear would have crossed his body. It was better than his first try. She pointed out the improvement and gave him one thing to watch when he repeated it later. She did not call it finished.

Noah picked up his bag from the bench. "Thanks for not just telling me I was doing it wrong."

"I did tell you when you were doing it wrong."

"You also showed me what to do instead."

"That's the point of practice."

He thanked her again, then looked at Percy. "Thanks for carrying them. I was trying to get it done fast."

"Yeah. I do that too."

Noah nodded as if that were a useful answer, then headed toward the pavilion.

For a few seconds, Percy and Annabeth stood beside the rack. There was no more equipment to move and no reason to linger, but Percy did not immediately reach for a new task. Annabeth looked at him, not accusingly. She was waiting to see whether he wanted to say anything.

Annabeth watched him for a beat. "You've been quiet."

"I've been training."

"You were glaring at Noah so hard I thought he might catch fire."

Percy looked at the spears. "I wasn't glaring."

"Percy."

He exhaled. "I know it's stupid. You're allowed to help people. You're allowed to like people. I don't want to be the guy who gets weird whenever you talk to someone else."

Annabeth's face softened. "That doesn't sound stupid. It sounds like you're trying to understand what you feel before you make it my problem."

"Clarisse said something similar."

"Clarisse is occasionally wise. Don't tell her I said that."

Percy managed a small smile. "I know you're with me because you choose to be. I just—sometimes I forget that we’re both adjusting to this. We remember the same years, but people here know a childhood you can’t recall. Maybe I worry that someone else could know you here in a way I don’t."

Annabeth leaned one spear against the rack and took his hand. "Noah knows me as the person who taught him how not to drop a spear. You know me as the person who stole your blueprints, slept on your shoulder through a bus ride, and once threatened to redesign the Argo II so you couldn't find your own cabin."

"You did that?"

"I remember planning it. You looked horrified. I was very proud of the idea."

Percy laughed.

"You don’t have to compete with the childhood I can’t remember," she said. "And Noah isn't competing with you at all. He's my friend. You're my favorite person. Those can both be true."

"Favorite person," Percy repeated.

"Yes. And if that changes, I'll tell you. I won't just disappear into some other friendship and leave you guessing."

"I can do that too. If I get worried, I'll talk to you. Not glare at innocent Hermes campers."

"Good. Because the Hermes cabin has enough problems."

She squeezed his hand. Percy squeezed back.
Annabeth leaned the spear against the rack and kept her hand in his until he let go. "Do you want to go back to the arena?"

"I think I do."

"Okay. I'll see you later."

"Yeah."

She picked up her bag and left along the path toward the pavilion. Percy watched until she reached the turn, then went back to the arena. The Ares camper was putting the practice swords away.

"Thought you left," he said.

"I did. I'm back."

"Another round?"

"One. Slower."

The camper picked up a sword and handed it to him. They returned to the center marks. This time Percy asked him to start with the same high strike, then change the second move without warning. The first attempt went too fast. Percy caught the blade late and had to step back.

"Again," he said.

The Ares camper lifted his eyebrows. "You said one."

"I meant one clean one."

"That's different."

"It is."

They reset. Percy waited for the shoulder shift. The second strike came lower, but he saw the change in time and met it. He turned his wrist, kept his feet under him, and stopped when the other camper called the point.

"Clean," Percy said.

"Better," the camper answered. He put the practice sword back. "You're done?"

"Yeah."

Percy returned his own sword to the rack. He checked the slot before leaving, then went toward lunch. The question in his mind was still there, but he hadn't needed to turn it into a test for anyone else.

---

Clarisse waited for him at lunch.

"You talked to her?" she asked.

"Yeah."

"And?"

"And it was fine. I was being an idiot."

Silena passed him the bread. "You weren't an idiot. You were having a feeling. What matters is what you do with it."

Clarisse grunted. "He's still an idiot. But at least he's an honest one."

Percy sat between them at the Poseidon table. Clarisse moved the bread basket closer without looking up from her plate. Silena set her orange beside her cup, just out of his reach.

Percy looked at it. "Is that for me?"

"No. It's mine. You owe me a whole orange because you took half of this one yesterday."

"I thought you gave me half."

"After you reached for it."

"You handed it to me."

"And I still want a full orange tomorrow."

Clarisse slid the bread basket between them. "You two can settle the orange dispute after we eat."

"There's a dispute?" Percy asked.

"There is now," Silena said. "You owe me one."

He considered arguing that he had not taken the orange so much as accepted it after she put it in his hand. That seemed like an important distinction. But she had not offered him this one now; she was reminding him about yesterday, and he could bring her a whole orange tomorrow.

"Fine," he said. "One orange."

"Tomorrow."

"Why tomorrow?"

"Because the kitchen has the crates then."

Clarisse gave Silena a look. "You planned that?"

"I noticed there were crates."

"That isn't the same thing."

"It's close enough."

Percy took a slice of bread. "I'll bring you one. I don't want to owe you an orange for the rest of my life."

"You wouldn't. I'd eventually forget."

"That isn't reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be."

The three of them ate. Clarisse talked about the lower field schedule and Silena told her the cook needed the clear serving table after lunch. Percy listened, then offered to carry something if they needed another pair of hands. Silena said she would ask if she needed him. Clarisse told him he could stop volunteering for every task he saw.

"I only offered."

"I know. You don't have to."

He let that be the end of it. Silena asked Clarisse whether the shield straps had been checked. Clarisse said she would check them after lunch, and Silena reminded her to leave the cracked practice one aside. Percy asked which one was cracked. Clarisse said it was the one with the broken buckle, not the one with the painted mark. He repeated the distinction so he would remember it, and she nodded.

The table conversation moved on to the kitchen's plans and the training schedule. It did not keep returning to the look on his face that morning.

He had sisters. He had Annabeth. He had a whole collection of people who were willing to call him out and stay anyway.

It was a strange kind of abundance. He was still learning how to trust it.

---

Silena caught up with him on the path from the pavilion.

"Annabeth found the books," she said. "If she's still testing the shelf today, I can bring the ones from the list."

Percy looked toward the shrine. The old structure stood behind the worktable, its new shelf empty and its backing pale against the darker stone. "She said she wanted to check it."

"Then I'll take these over." Silena shifted the paper list under her arm. "You can come if you're going that way. You don't have to carry anything."

"I can carry something if she asks."

Silena glanced at him. "That sounded almost like you heard me."

"I did. I just meant it." He stepped aside so two campers carrying a bucket could pass. "I'll ask her what she wants."

They walked the rest of the path together. Silena had a broad reference book in a cloth sling and a thinner volume tucked into the crook of her elbow. She did not hand either to him. Percy did not take them.

Annabeth had cleared the worktable and set the sketchbook open to the page with the test list. The shelf frame rested in its place against the wall. A pencil mark at either end showed where the board met the stone. Clarisse stood near the path with a bundle of short practice shields at her feet.

"I thought you were checking straps," Percy said.

"I did." Clarisse glanced at the shields. "These are ready. Annabeth asked me to check the frame while she did the books. I'm not touching it until she says where."

Annabeth looked up. "That would help. Percy, can you clear the walkway and bring the level from the repair box?"

"Sure."

He moved the spare board away from the path, then found the level where Annabeth had left it. Silena placed both books on the table and kept one hand on the list.

"These are the same categories we wrote down yesterday," she said. "Thin, broad, heaviest. The thin myth guide is light. The camp reference book is the widest. The building manual is the heaviest of the ones I could find."

"No stacking," Annabeth said. She drew three small squares in the margin. "One at a time, remove it before the next. We check the frame before and after each one. If anything shifts, we stop."

"And nobody catches anything," Clarisse added. "If it moves, we get our hands out of the way and take the book off when it's safe."

Annabeth nodded. "Right. The path is clear?"

Percy checked the space between the worktable and the shrine. "Clear."

"Can you walk the route once?"

He followed the path past the shrine without turning sideways. The narrowed top edge did not reach into the walking space. He took one slow step closer, then another, leaving room for a person coming the other way. No corner caught his sleeve. Clarisse moved back a pace to make sure the space stayed open.

"Good," Annabeth said. "Now from the other direction."

Percy returned. A camper came up behind him carrying a basket, so he stepped aside and let her through. The basket cleared the shelf. She glanced at the open books but did not stop.

"That's more useful than my walking in a straight line," he said.

"It is," Annabeth said. "The route has to work when somebody else is using it."

She checked each empty corner with the level and asked Clarisse to watch the shim. Percy held the sketchbook open while Annabeth compared the measurements. Silena read the marks from her list. They had all been here the day before, but the changed top edge made the frame look different from the drawing Percy remembered.

"Empty check first," Annabeth said.

Clarisse set two fingers against the side, just where Annabeth pointed. Percy watched the pencil marks. Annabeth tested the lower board, then the upper. The frame did not rock. She checked the level once more and wrote "empty: steady" in the first square.

"Thin book," she said.

Silena lifted the myth guide from the table. She paused, waiting for Annabeth to indicate where it should go. Annabeth pointed to the left side of the lower shelf, not the center.

Silena lowered it without dragging the cover along the edge. It fit with room on either side. The frame stayed aligned with the pencil marks.

"That's the depth check," Percy said.

"It's one depth check," Annabeth corrected. She removed the book, inspected the board, then marked the second square. "Thin book fits, no visible movement."

"You really aren't going to mark it done," Clarisse said.

"Not from one book." Annabeth returned the myth guide to the table. "Broad book. Center it over the support."

Silena set the reference book down slowly. Percy held the level against the frame after Annabeth pointed to the place. The bubble stayed between the lines. Clarisse looked along the side to confirm the mark had not moved.

"No shift," Percy said.

Annabeth lifted the book back off. "Now we record that."

Silena read the note as she wrote it. "Broad reference volume, centered. No movement seen."

"You can write smaller," Clarisse said. "The shelf doesn't need its own scroll."

"I can read it later," Silena replied. "That's the point."

The building manual looked heavier than it had on the table. Percy put his hands near it but waited. Annabeth examined the lower shelf and the support marks, then checked that the book could be taken away without pulling the frame forward.

"We'll do this one last," she said. "Silena, can you hold the far edge while Percy supports the bottom? Don't lift until I tell you."

They took positions. Clarisse stood clear of the path, watching the shim. Annabeth crouched beside the mark and looked once more at the level.

"Ready?" she asked.

Silena and Percy nodded.

"Set it down, slowly."

They lowered the manual to the center of the lower board. Its bottom edge made a quiet wooden sound. Nothing fell. The frame held, but the pencil mark at the left corner moved a fraction against the stone. Percy felt the shift through his palms before he saw it.

"Stop," Annabeth said.

Silena lifted her hands away. Percy did the same. Annabeth waited, watching the shim and the narrow gap under the side panel. The shelf did not move again.

"It's still up," Clarisse said.

"That isn't the same as passing," Annabeth replied. "We take the book off together. No one reaches under the frame."

She gave Silena and Percy new positions farther from the corner. They lifted the manual straight off and carried it back to the table. Only after it was down did Clarisse touch the frame where Annabeth indicated. The shim had moved slightly, not enough to tip the shelf, but enough to change the mark.

Percy set down his end of the book. "The heavier one shifted it."

"A little," Annabeth said. She wrote in the third square: "test stopped; shim movement observed; no books to be stored."

Silena leaned over the sketchbook to read the three notes. "If somebody sees the books in storage, they might bring them here when we're gone."

"I'll keep them in the same box," she said. "But maybe there should be a note beside the shelf."

"Not on the frame," Annabeth said. "I don't want it to look like a label for a finished shelf." She found a blank scrap in the sketchbook pocket and wrote across it: *Please leave this shelf empty. The support needs another check.*

Clarisse read it from the path. "You can see that without walking up to it."

"Good." Annabeth set the note on the worktable rather than attaching it to the shrine. "I'll ask Chiron whether we can leave it here until we finish the support check."

Percy looked at the list. "So the thin one and the broad one held. The heavy one moved the shim."

"That's what we saw," Annabeth said. "We don't know whether it would have shifted more. We stopped when it shifted."

"And we don't know if the frame is ready for regular use," Silena added. She folded the list so the warning stayed visible. "I'll keep the books in storage."

Clarisse nodded toward the empty shelf. "It can wait. Nobody needs a book badly enough to test it with their head."

Annabeth smiled at that, then copied the three observations into the margin. She did not add a total or a pass mark. The note and the book list would keep the result visible until Chiron had looked at the support.

"We don't keep loading it to see how far it goes," she said.

"Can you adjust it now?" Silena asked.

"I want to look at the support and ask Chiron before we change it. The plan said to test, not to make a guess at a fix while the book is on the table." Annabeth closed the pencil box. "We know what we know. Empty, thin and broad were steady. The heavy manual was not."

Clarisse nodded once. "Then it stays empty."

"Yes." Annabeth took the manual off the table and returned it to Silena's sling. "No one stacks anything there. We can put the books back in storage until we've checked the shim."

Silena folded the list around the three test notes, careful not to cover the marks. "At least we know which part needs another look."

Annabeth looked at the shelf. She seemed disappointed, but not surprised. "That's why we did the test before moving the books."

Percy returned the level to the repair box. He wanted to say it was almost ready, that the movement had been small, that the other two tests had held. He didn't. Annabeth had already written what happened, and none of those other things changed the last square.

"Do you want me to help with the next check?" he asked.

"When I know what the next check is," she said. "For now, can you put the sketchbook somewhere flat?"

He carried it to the table and set it down without closing it. The three boxes remained visible on the page: two marked, one crossed with a careful note. Silena rolled the book sling around the manual. Clarisse picked up her shields and moved them farther from the walkway.

No one called the test a failure. It had done what they needed: shown them where to stop before a book could fall or a person could be hurt. The shelf was still useful as an idea, still standing in the shrine, but it was not ready for anyone to borrow from.

When Annabeth asked Silena to carry the two lighter books back to storage, Percy reached for the empty sling, then stopped. "Do you want a hand?"

"I have these," Silena said. "Thank you, though."

"Okay."

She carried them herself. Percy waited until she had cleared the path before picking up the level box. Clarisse went with Silena, and Annabeth stayed to compare the mark against her drawing. Percy left her to it. She knew where to find him if she needed him.

---

That evening, Annabeth found him on the dock.

She sat beside him, set down a small stack of index cards, and passed him one.

On it, she'd drawn two stick figures, one with dark hair and one with blond hair. Between them was a slightly crooked heart. Underneath, she'd written: *For the record, I choose you. Even when you are being ridiculous.*

Percy read it twice. "You drew my hair wrong."

"That is your objection?"

"It's pretty accurate otherwise."

He turned the card over and wrote: *For the record, I trust you. Even when I'm being ridiculous.*

Annabeth read it, then tucked the card into her pocket. "Good. We'll keep reminding each other."
She looked at the card again, then at the ink on Percy's thumb. "You're going to smear that."

"I can write with my other hand."

"You can try."

"That's what you said about my sword work."

"And I was right then too."

Percy held out his hand for the card. Annabeth gave it back. He blew on the line until it dried, then added a small drawing in the corner: two spears leaning against a wooden rack. One of them had a tiny speech bubble that read, *Not a practice partner.*

Annabeth leaned closer. "You forgot the canvas."

"I can't draw canvas."

"You drew a rack with four legs."

"That's already better than most of my maps."

"It isn't a map."

"It could be."

She took a second card from the stack and sketched a strip of cloth stretched between two posts. At one end, she added a small figure holding a peg. The figure's hair stuck up at odd angles.

"Is that me?" Percy asked.

"No. It's Noah."

"How can you tell?"

"He's shorter."

"You've never measured him."

"I have eyes."

Percy examined the card. The cloth was long enough to cover half the lake. "I think the canvas is too big."

"I think your rack is too small."

"Maybe we need more paper."

"We have plenty." She turned the card over and started to write a date, then stopped. "I don't know what date it is."

"Neither do I."

She put a small question mark where the date should have gone. "That works."

The little stack had started as something she was doodling on while she waited for him. Now it held two drawings of the same afternoon, neither one a record that needed to be kept straight. Percy liked that the things they remembered didn't have to be equal to be shared. He had watched Noah struggle with a load and seen Annabeth teach. He had helped. She had seen him stumble through a practice round and keep his head. None of that changed the older memories they carried. It added something smaller and entirely theirs.

"You should keep that one," she said.

"Which one?"

"The one with the speech bubble."

"Because it's accurate?"

"Because you drew it."

Percy slid the card between the first one and the rest of the stack. Annabeth tapped its edge into alignment.

"Do you save every card you make?" he asked.

"Not every card. Some I leave in books. Some I throw out when I don't like them."

"What about these?"

"I haven't decided."

"Okay."

She considered him, as if she expected him to ask again. He didn't. She tucked the stack into her pocket, not carefully enough to make it look important and not carelessly enough for it to fall out.

"Deal."

She leaned against his shoulder. Percy covered their joined hands with his free one, and the lake moved softly below them.

The feeling hadn't vanished. Maybe it wouldn't. But it had become smaller now that he'd named it and let Annabeth answer it honestly.

He could live with that.

---

The next morning, Clarisse told him Noah had asked for more time with the shields.

"He's not a bad kid," she said. "Needs work on his stance."

Percy waited for the rest.

Clarisse rolled her eyes. "I don't care that you were jealous. I care that you don't make your feelings everybody else's job. You talked to her. That's the right thing."

"Thanks."

"Don't thank me. Now go help Silena with the breakfast crates. She says you owe her an orange."

"I didn't take an orange."

"You did. Yesterday."

Percy looked at Silena, who was smiling into her cup. "I thought that was mine."

"It was on my plate."

"Then I owe you an orange."

"And an apology."

"I can do one of those."

Clarisse shoved a crate into his arms. "You can do both. Move."

The cook let him set the crate on the counter, then asked Silena to check the fruit before it went to the breakfast tables. Some oranges had been bruised along one side where they had shifted in transit. Silena moved those into a shallow bowl for the kitchen rather than putting them back in the crate.

"They can still be used," she said. "Just not passed out whole."

"Is that why I owe you an orange?" Percy asked.

"No. You owe me an orange because you took half of mine yesterday without asking."

"I thought it was mine."

"You thought wrong."

"I'm sorry."

Silena glanced over at him. "I know. You don't need to keep apologizing."

"Clarisse said an apology."

"Clarisse said that because she likes assigning things. I accepted it."

"Then I owe you an orange and not an apology."

"You can do both. Just don't make me listen to you apologize six times."

"I can do one."

"Good."

She finished the last row of fruit and turned the crate so its open side faced the serving table. Percy passed the oranges to her one at a time. She arranged them by size, saving the bruised ones for the bowl. The work was slower than he expected. If he pushed too many across at once, they rolled into the wrong pile. If he waited for Silena to choose each one, the serving table stayed empty.

"Here," she said, handing him one. "This size."

He looked at the orange, then at the rows. "Where does it go?"

"Middle. The large ones go at the end so they don't push the smaller ones over."

He placed it where she pointed. The arrangement held. They continued until the crate was empty, then Silena checked each side of the table to make sure a camper could reach without leaning over the hot dish.

"Do you want your orange now?" she asked.

"I thought that was my payment to you."

"It is."

"Then you should take it."

She picked the least bruised fruit from the crate and held it out to him. "I don't want the one you owe me to be a bad one."

"I can pay you with one I choose."

"You can. Choose it."

Percy went to the next crate, which had been set aside for the afternoon meal, and found a firm orange with a small green mark near the stem. He gave it to her. She tested its weight, nodded, and took a knife from the counter. She cut it into sections, then set half on a saucer for herself and pushed the other half toward him.

"That's half," he said.

"Yes."

"I owed you a whole orange."

"You gave me a whole one. We can share it."

He sat on the bench by the wall and ate his half. Silena kept the other half for after she finished setting out the fruit. When the cook called her to the far counter, she left the saucer beside Percy's hand and asked him not to let anyone mistake it for scraps.

"I won't."

She came back with a folded towel and used it to wipe orange juice from the table. Percy helped her finish before heading toward the lower field. The crate task Clarisse had given him had taken less time than his sisters' arguments about it. It had also turned out to be exactly one orange, not a debt that followed him through the day.

---

The lower field was quiet when he got there. Clarisse had put one shield against a padded stand and left another on the ground. Noah stood a few paces away, checking the strap with both hands. He looked up when Percy approached.

"Did I miss the lesson?" Percy asked.

"No," Clarisse said. "I told him to check his equipment first."

Noah tightened the strap one notch, then loosened it again. "It felt like it was sliding."

"Because you pulled it too tight at the top," Clarisse said. She showed him how the strap rested across his forearm and where his hand should fit. "You need room to move your wrist. If you lock it in place, you're carrying the shield instead of using it."

Noah flexed his hand. "This feels better."

"Then leave it there until you have a reason to change it."

Clarisse set a line in the dirt with the toe of her boot. The shield stand was beyond it. "Show me the turn you learned yesterday. One slow pass. No speed."

Noah lifted the shield close to his side and stepped forward. He took the turn wide enough to clear the stand, then lowered the shield without striking the rack behind it. He checked where he had placed his feet.

"Again," Clarisse said.

He glanced at the rack. "I thought I was supposed to look past it."

"You are. You looked at it after. That's checking your work. Do both."

Noah nodded. He chose a point beyond the stand, carried the shield through the turn, then set it down on the line. The movement stayed controlled.

"That's the one you did yesterday," Clarisse said. "You remembered it."

Noah looked relieved. "I thought I was supposed to have it perfect by now."

"Who said that?"

"No one."

"Then don't argue with an imaginary teacher. You learned one turn. You repeated it. That's where you are."

Percy leaned against the low fence and watched Noah reset. The boy had already gone from spear footwork to a shield lesson with Clarisse; the two skills were not the same, but both asked him to slow down and notice his feet. Percy knew what it was like to want the next step before the current one felt solid. He also knew what it cost to pretend a body could move faster than it could.

Clarisse picked up the second shield. "You asked about sparring. This isn't a spar yet. Today we can work on approaching and stopping. If you don't want to continue, say so."

"I want to try."

"At what pace?"

Noah thought about it. "Half speed."

"Half speed."

Clarisse walked to the line and demonstrated the movement without a shield. She stepped in, shifted her weight, and stopped with both feet planted, leaving a clear gap between herself and the stand.

"Don't reach across the space," she said. "Move yourself first. The shield follows."

Noah copied her. On the first attempt, he leaned too far and had to step out to catch his balance. He stopped, returned to the starting line, and tried again with shorter steps.

"Better," Clarisse said. "What changed?"

"I stopped leaning."

"And?"

"I looked at the spot where I wanted to end instead of the shield."

"Good. Do it once with the shield."

Percy shifted the second shield away from the training lane so it would not become another thing Noah had to avoid. Clarisse saw him do it and pointed to the open space beside the line.

"Stand there. If he asks, you can tell him whether his feet crossed the mark. Don't coach him."

"Got it."

Noah took the shield and approached the line. He moved at half speed, kept his wrist loose, and stopped before he reached the stand. His back foot crossed the mark by a few inches.

He looked down. "I did it again."

"You saw it," Clarisse said. "Reset."

He reset without looking at her first. The second try ended short of the mark. Percy held up one finger to show the distance. Noah nodded, stepped back, and gave Clarisse the shield.

"That's enough," she said.

"I wanted one more."

"You wanted a spar. You have one clean stop and one clean turn. We'll add a partner when those are ordinary."

Noah didn't argue. He rested the shield against the stand and rubbed his palm. "I kept thinking I'd mess up if I stopped."

"You did stop," Percy said. "It worked."

Noah considered the line in the dirt. "Thanks for watching."

"Sure."

Clarisse rubbed away the mark with her boot. "You'll need to draw it again next time. Don't assume the ground keeps your place for you."

Noah lifted his bag. "Can I come back after breakfast tomorrow?"

"If I have time. Ask me then."

"I will."

He left with the shield where Clarisse had put it, not carrying it off or asking to try the whole movement again. Clarisse waited until he had crossed the edge of the field before she gathered the spare equipment.

"He's getting better," Percy said.

"He's learning."

"That's what I meant."

"I know." She set the shield back against the stand. "You didn't have to come over."

"I wanted to see how the turn went."

"Why?"

"Because he said he'd try it again."

Clarisse looked at him, then nodded once. "Fair."

They checked the shields where they rested against the stand. Percy let Clarisse choose the order, and watched her test each strap before leaving. He thought about Annabeth's spear lesson and Clarisse's shield work, how neither of them tried to make a new camper prove he deserved help by getting everything right at once. He checked the last buckle with her and waited for her nod.

"Done," he said.

Clarisse tapped the rack with her knuckle. "The check is. We still have to put these away."

Clarisse lifted the last shield from the stand and carried it toward the rack. Percy followed with the second one.

"You could have left that for me," she said.

"I know."

She glanced at him, checking whether he meant the answer as a joke. He set the shield in the lower row and let go when it was stable.

Clarisse touched the handle. "You want to try the turn?"

"With the shield?"

"That's usually what a shield turn means."

"I thought you were done."

"I am done teaching Noah for today. That's different."

Percy looked at the open patch of dirt where she had marked Noah's line. "I don't know the turn."

"Then we'll start at the beginning. You can stop if you want."

The offer was so direct that Percy almost answered before he knew what he wanted. He watched Clarisse hold the shield by its rim. It was shorter than the practice sword, heavier in a different way. He could say no without disappointing anyone. That should have made the choice easy. Instead he had to find the answer for himself.

"One try," he said.

"One try."

Clarisse handed him the shield. She showed him where to rest his forearm and how to leave his wrist loose. Percy shifted the strap until it sat against his arm without pinching. She asked before adjusting the buckle. He nodded, and she moved it one notch.

"Ready?"

"Yeah."

"Take it slow."

Percy chose a point beyond the rack. He stepped, turned, and brought the shield across his body. His feet moved too wide, and the edge struck his thigh.

"Stop," Clarisse said.

Percy lowered the shield. "I hit myself."

"You did. Does it hurt?"

"No."

"Then decide if you want another try."

He flexed his leg. The knock had been light. "I do."

Clarisse took the shield back long enough to loosen the strap, then returned it. She didn't tell him what he had done wrong until he asked.

"I stepped too wide," he said.

"Yes."

"Can I try again?"

"Go ahead."

He found the point beyond the rack and moved with a shorter step. The turn was smaller than the one he had imagined, but the shield stayed close and his feet ended where he wanted them. Clarisse watched without commenting until he set the shield down.

"Better," she said. "That was one clean try."

"That felt less impressive."

"It wasn't a performance."

"Right."

She leaned the shield beside the others. "You can keep practicing later if you want."

"Would you show me again?"

"Ask me next time."

He nodded. Clarisse lifted her bag from the ground, and they left the field together. Percy didn't need to turn his practice into a contest with Noah, or his afternoon into a test of how much attention Annabeth gave him. He had done one careful turn because he wanted to learn it. That was enough for now.

At dinner, Noah sat across from Percy and asked if he and Annabeth were together.

Percy glanced at Annabeth. She was listening, waiting to see how he would answer.

"Yeah," Percy said. "We are."

Annabeth's smile warmed the whole table.

Noah nodded. "Cool. You're good together."

"Thanks," Annabeth said.

Noah looked at the empty place where his plate had been a moment earlier. "I wasn't sure if I was asking something obvious."

"It wasn't obvious to you," Percy said. "It's okay to ask."

"I didn't want the training to get weird."

"It didn't," Annabeth said. "You were learning spear work."

Noah glanced at Percy. "And you were there."

"Because she asked me to carry the spears."

"I thought maybe you were waiting to say something."

Percy felt his ears heat. There had been a time when the thought would have made him look away. He kept his eyes on Noah. "I was sorting out my own reaction. That wasn't something you needed to fix."

Noah sat back. "Okay."

Annabeth reached for the bread. "He's a new camper. Everyone's still deciding which table gives them the least trouble."

"That's why I picked this one," Noah said. He looked at Clarisse. "The Ares table throws food."

"Only if you give them a reason."

"That's what I was afraid of."

Silena passed him the bread. "Then you chose the right table for tonight."

Noah took a piece and thanked her. He did not ask Percy another question about his relationship. They talked instead about where the spear rack was supposed to go after practice and whether the canvas would be dry enough to fold by morning. Annabeth said it needed another hour. Clarisse said she would check it before the field opened. Noah offered to help move it, and Clarisse told him to wait until she asked.

Percy let the table conversation carry on without trying to measure who had Annabeth's attention. She asked Silena about the bread, then answered Noah when he had a question about the lower field. He joined the conversation when he had something to add and listened when he didn't.

Clarisse lifted her cup. "To being normal about things."

"You have never been normal about anything," Silena said.

"I said to it. I didn't say I was good at it."

They clinked cups. Percy looked at Annabeth and she looked back. They didn't need to make a big deal of it. They could just sit there, between his sisters and their friends, and let the truth be simple.

They were together.

They were learning what that meant this time.

And they had people around them who would make sure neither of them had to learn alone.
