## Chapter 170: What the Envelope Holds

The mail came up the hill on Thursdays, and it came the way everything at Camp Half-Blood came: loudly, late, and in the hands of somebody who expected to be paid in food.

Percy stood at the rail of the Big House porch with his back against a post and the cold coming through his sleeves, watching the Hermes cabin perform its weekly mythology. Two of them ran the line. A girl with envelopes fanned along both forearms like a card dealer called the names in a carrying voice, and a boy had somehow acquired a satchel twice his size and wore it crossed over his chest like a bandolier, so that every time he turned around the satchel tried to take somebody's teeth out. Campers waited in cabin clumps with their hands in their pockets and their breath going up white. The porch smelled of cold pine pitch and lamp oil and the graphite-and-varnish smell of the parlor door standing open behind Chiron's empty chair.

"Mail day," the Hermes girl announced, to nobody in particular. "Letters out go down at dark. Anything in the chest after the lamp's out waits until next Thursday. That is the whole law. There is no appeal, there is no appeal form, and if anybody asks me for an appeal form I will fold them into one."

Somebody laughed. The line shuffled forward.

Percy read the board by the door while he waited, the way he read water, because both were things that told you where you were. MAIL DAY — THURSDAY. LETTERS OUT BY DARK. Under it, in Chiron's square chalk hand, the autumn list from Monday's speech: CANOES STOWED AND COVERED BEFORE MONTH'S END. BUOYS IN. PRACTICE WEAPONS RACKED, NOT LEANED. ORCHARD AND ROOT CELLAR — SILENA, FULL AUTHORITY OVER PEARS AND THE SOULS OF MEN. And below all of it, a blank line where nothing had been chalked yet. Percy looked at the blank the way he looked at the blank under the ridge line on Annabeth's map wall: a space that was waiting for somebody to decide it meant something.

The line moved. A Hermes kid went past him with a bicycle-wheel of string and a wrapped parcel addressed to the forge. Olive Nakamura got a letter with a stamp on it, read the address twice, and announced to the whole porch that it was from her grandmother and that her grandmother had drawn a frog on the back. Marcus pocketed his without comment and stood with his shoulder against the post, and nobody said anything about it, and that was how you knew he had been at camp a while.

"Jackson, Percy," the Hermes girl called.

One envelope. Cream, the expensive kind, with his name on it in block capitals, each letter the size of a stamp: PERCY JACKSON. Percy took it with both hands, the way Clarisse had taught him to take anything you did not want to drop, and turned it over. There was nothing on the back. No return address, because his brother had written from a forge and forges did not have addresses, only fires.

"Grace, two," the Hermes girl called, at the same moment, and Thalia came up the porch steps with a letter in each hand and a face that said she had been ready for this all week and would be annoyed at anyone who noticed. She had her flannel tied over her camp shirt against the cold and her hair still wet from something. She caught Percy's eye over the envelopes, nodded once, and went back down the steps with her boots already carrying her toward Cabin One, and Annabeth, who had been standing at the rail with her field book under her arm, put her pencil behind her ear and went after her sister without a word.

Percy tucked his envelope inside his fleece, where it would stay warm and flat, and walked out to the top step of the porch to read it, because there were things you did not read standing up.

The morning had turned bright and unforgiving, the way Thursdays did after a cold snap. The frost had gone out of the open ground overnight, but it still held in the shadows under the pines, a faint silver crust on the needle-fall that broke underfoot and stopped existing. The lake lay at the bottom of the slope flat as poured metal, steam lifting off the shallows in long low veils where the colder air met water still warmer than the morning. Somebody's kitchen fire had been lit early; the smoke came over straight and thin and smelled all the way up the hill of wet pine and ash and bread. Percy sat down on the step with his elbows on his knees and opened the letter with his thumb, careful of the seal, because Clarisse said a letter was a small machine and you did not break the machine that had carried it.

The paper inside was heavy. It had a smudge of forge soot along one edge and, in one corner, the print of a thumb the size of a dinner plate.

Dear Percy,

it said, in block capitals, and Percy felt his mouth do the thing it did before the rest of him knew why.

I am home. The forges are the same and also different. This fire is bigger. The hammer is heavier. I like both.

I made you a bolt. It is round and it is the size of your thumb and you can roll it across a table to see if it is straight. Mine rolled straight. I also made a hook for Silena's mending tote, because the old hook is sad. She will say it is fine. It is not fine. It is sad.

Brine says hello. She asked if the lake is still cold. I told her it is cold on the bottom and warm on the top, like the forges are cold on the handle and hot at the end. She understood. She ate an apple. She says you owe her one.

The canoe: is the prow piece still tight? I measured it with my feet before I left, which is the best way to measure. If it moved, put your foot on it and write and tell me, and I will make a new one. I have a lot of room inside for fixes.

Clarisse's whistle: I hear it when I sleep. That is a compliment. Tell her I said so.

Write back. You do not have to write fast. But I would like it if you did.

Your brother,
Tyson.

P.S. Be kind. It is still on my hand.

Percy read it once standing, and once sitting, and then a third time with his thumb over the P.S., and then he sat back against the post and laughed out loud on the Big House porch in the middle of a Thursday morning, a laugh that cracked in the middle and had to be put back together with his sleeve.

"He hear it's good news or bad news?"

The Hermes girl was coming down the steps with the satchel boy behind her, both of them on their way down to the road with whatever the camp was sending out. Percy realized he had been smiling at the letter like an idiot and did not entirely care.

"It's my brother," he said.

"Yeah? What's he say?"

"He says the fire's bigger." Percy folded the letter along its creases and put it back inside his fleece, over his heart, patting it flat the way Thalia patted her notes. "And he hears Clarisse's whistle in his sleep. Which he says is a compliment."

"That is either beautiful or a threat," the Hermes girl said, and went on down the steps, and the satchel boy said, "Threat," with total confidence, and they went off down the hill arguing about it.

Percy sat on the porch a while longer. The sun came over the pines and took the silver off the frost-shadow, and the steam went off the lake in thinner and thinner veils, and down at the bottom of the slope the forge in Cabin Eight glowed orange through its shutter slits and put its iron-musk onto the cold air. Somebody was already working iron. The sound came up in short bursts, stopped, started again.

He touched the letter through the fleece. Then he took it out and read it a fourth time, the way you checked a cleat with your foot.

There was no letter from Atlantis. There never was. Triton did not write; Triton arrived — formally, briefly, like weather that had been invited — and stood in Tempest Hall being dignified and unbearable and gone again, and his visitor towel hung folded on its hook beside the corner bunk with its corners squared, waiting for the next time. Percy had stopped expecting anything else from that direction. What you got from the sea was presence and then absence, and what you got from the forges was a letter with a thumbprint on it, and the funny thing, the thing that sat warm behind his ribs, was that the letter was the one he was going to be able to keep under his pillow.

He thought about the blank page on the Cabin Three counter, weighted down with a tin cup so the draft off the lake would not carry it off. He had been walking past it since Tuesday. He had told himself there would be time — there was always going to be time, that was the lie he told pages — and then his brother's letter had come up the hill in somebody's satchel and landed in his hand, and the page on the counter was not blank anymore. It was owing.

"Today," Percy said, to nobody, to the lake, to the letter. "Yeah. Today."

He got up off the step and stood a minute in the cold sun and looked down at the whole camp laid out under its October breath: the pavilion with its tables folded, the forge glowing, the beach white and empty below the dock, the willow cove green-gold and still on the left where a gray shape stood motionless on the bow of the half-sunk skiff. The heron. She had been waiting all summer for whatever she was waiting for, and nobody had ever once known what it was, and Percy had stopped asking, because some things told you more by not being answered.

The office in the Big House stayed shut on Thursdays. The panel sat in its flat drawer and the number stayed thirty-nine, and Percy had learned the shape of a closed door well enough by now to leave it alone. He went inside instead, through the parlor where the long table stood and the map covered most of one wall, and the lamp was already lit and the good ink was out, and that was when he understood that the afternoon had been decided for him, the way good afternoons at camp always were: by somebody else's kindness, waiting to be walked into.

Annabeth Grace opened the letter with her camp knife, along the crease, and kept the envelope.

It was a habit older than this camp, this life, either life: the envelope was part of the record. You never knew which letter would become the one you kept. She slit it, slid the page out, smoothed it against her knee in the thin cold sun on the steps of Cabin One, and read it with the particular attention she gave to anything in her brother's hand — a small, square, upright hand that pressed too hard and made holes in the paper.

Dear Thalia and Annabeth,

it said, and the sight of those two names together, in that order, did something to her chest that no amount of planning could prevent.

Camp is fine. I am in the Fifth Cohort. The training is difficult but I am keeping up. My cohort leader says my shield work is "tolerable," which is the best thing he has ever said about me.

The valley is cold at night. There are wolves on the ridge. They are not the bad kind. I am not allowed to say more.

How is camp. How is the lake. How is Mother. She writes to me every Thursday and I write back on Sundays. It is good to have a letter due.

Please tell me anything you need me to know.

Your brother,
Jason

Annabeth read it twice. Then she folded it along its creases and held it in her lap with both hands and looked out at the slope below the cabin steps, where the lake lay flat and steaming, and did not say anything for a while.

"He's polite," Thalia said. She was sitting one step down with her own letter open on her knees, a page of loopy capitals and underlines, and she said it in the flat voice she used when she had already read something four times herself. "He's twelve and he writes like he's answering a summons."

"He writes like somebody who doesn't know if he's allowed to take up room," Annabeth said.

Thalia's head turned. Then, carefully, she went back to her own letter, which was the Thalia version of agreeing.

Annabeth turned the page over in her fingers. Cream stationery, the kind their mother bought in boxes when she was not drinking, when she was doing the thing where she reorganized the whole house to prove she was well. Her brother had pressed so hard that his capitals had gone through to the back. She could feel the dents with her thumb.

He was eight when he went. That was four years ago. She was ten. She had the arithmetic of it filed like a bearing, because she filed everything: ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen; eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve. Two lines that had once been a single household and were now two lines that met on a page on Thursdays. He had turned twelve without her. She had turned fourteen without him. Whatever he remembered of her — if he remembered her at all in any detail that counted — was a ten-year-old who had not been ten in four years, and whatever she remembered of him was a small person in a good coat at a road, and she had been trying for four years to find the seam where the memory of a person became the memory of a story.

"I don't remember his voice," Annabeth said, to the lake, precise about it, the way she was precise about contour intervals and her own limits. "I remember his shoes. Red ones, too big. And he held my hand at the water and said it was too cold to go in, and then he went in anyway." She turned her hands over. The envelope crackled. "That's not a person. That's postcards. Four years of postcards."

Thalia was quiet a moment. Then she reached over, not looking up from her own letter, and straightened the collar of Annabeth's fleece with two flat pats, hard enough to be a shove and soft enough to be the exact opposite.

"That's him," Thalia said. "Red shoes and then in anyway. Don't take things off the list because they're small. Small is what's left."

The fleece was orange and had been Percy's in July and had quietly stopped being his, and it was the warmest thing Annabeth owned, and she leaned a half-inch into her sister's hand while it was still on her collar, and then Thalia took her hand away and went back to reading, and neither of them said anything about it, which was how it went between them on a cold morning when something true had been said at low cost.

Annabeth reached the last line of her brother's letter and stopped there, the way you stopped at a seam in the water.

Please tell me anything you need me to know.

She read it again. It was the politest sentence in the letter and it was the loudest one, and she could hear the boy under it, a twelve-year-old in a place that measured everything, standing at a writing desk in a valley full of wolves, asking for instructions. Asking to be told what he was allowed to carry. It is good to have a letter due — he had written that himself, in his own too-pressing hand, and it sat in her chest like a stone in a shoe, small and entirely there.

"They're both from her," Thalia said, and held up the second page, the one that had been folded around the first. "Read the P.S. Out loud. I'm not doing it by myself."

Annabeth took the page. The handwriting changed character halfway down, from her mother's loopy capitals into smaller, slower letters, the way a person wrote when they had started a letter in one mood and finished it in another, and had not crossed anything out, because they had decided to be honest about both.

My darling girls. I dreamt about the lake house. I am allowed to now, it turns out. I stood in the kitchen and nothing was broken. Don't answer that part if you don't want to. I'm only telling you so it's somewhere that isn't just me.

Annabeth read it twice. Then she folded the page along the same creases her sister had used and gave it back, and sat with her hands in the fleece's pockets and her jaw set in the way that meant she was filing something she did not want to file.

"Beryl writes him every week," Thalia said. "Sundays, he says. He writes her back Sundays." She turned her own letter over. The loopy capitals went on and on: a yellow door, a garden club, a sentence about how she applauded herself every morning at six-fifteen. "Every week. He's twelve. He's got a letter due from his mother every Thursday, and we're — what? We wrote him when?"

Annabeth searched. She found it, and it was not recent.

"Spring," she said. "I sent the one about the map. You sent — "

"I didn't send mine," Thalia said, very quietly, to her own knees. "I wrote one. In April. It's in the bottom of my satchel." She sat with it. The static in her flannel ticked faintly in the cold, the way it did when she was holding something down. "Four times I wrote him. I never mailed one."

"Thalia."

"You can't unsay a letter, Annabeth. Once it's out it's out, and I kept thinking — I didn't know what I was allowed to be to him. I'm the one who was standing on the road." Her jaw worked. "He gets a letter every Thursday from the mother who drank herself out of the house, and nothing from us, and I let that go on all summer because I couldn't figure out the address of my own — " She stopped. Started over. "He says don't read anything into the handwriting. That's my brother. That's a twelve-year-old boy apologizing in advance for having two sisters."

Annabeth looked at her sister's profile in the cold light: eighteen, jaw set, flannel buttoned to the throat against the wind off the lake, eyes fixed on the steam coming off the shallows. She had watched Thalia carry a household on her back for as long as she could remember, and she had watched, over five months, her sister begin to put it down. This was not that. This was her sister picking something back up and discovering how heavy it still was.

"Today," Annabeth said. "At the table. We're writing him today, and we're mailing it tonight, and it's going to have both our hands in it, and we're not going to decide it for him in advance." She held up her brother's letter so the creases caught the light. "He asked. He asked us to tell him anything. He gave us the whole page, Thalia. All we have to do is use it."

Thalia looked at the letter in her sister's hand. Then at her own, the loopy one from their mother, the yellow door, the garden club. Something in her shoulders came down a half-inch, the way it did when the surveyor in her admitted that a map she had been refusing to draw was, in fact, drawable.

"The table," Thalia repeated. "The Thursday table."

"The Thursday table," Annabeth agreed. "It's where the ink is."

Thalia stood, folded her mother's letter along its creases with more care than she would have admitted to, and put it in her breast pocket, and picked up Jason's again to read it one more time, standing, the way you read a bearing before you set a course.

"Write that down," she said, gruff, nodding at Annabeth's field book. "You always write it down. Write down that I said today."

"I already did," Annabeth said, and showed her the page: THURSDAY. JASON. BOTH HANDS. MAIL TONIGHT. She had written it in the cold light on the steps while her sister was reading, in her small upright hand, because she had decided the moment she read the word Thursday in her brother's letter, and because she had stopped being surprised at herself for planning tenderness. Planning was how she loved people. "I'm going to get the pen I like from the parlor before somebody else does."

"Genius," Thalia said, "you're terrifying."

"I'm organized," Annabeth said. "There's a difference, and I've had to explain it to my whole family."

The parlor of the Big House had been given over to paper.

It happened on Thursdays and nobody announced it. Chiron put the good ink out on the sideboard, where the incense and the old survey ledgers lived, and lit the lamp against the cold, and laid fresh blotting sheets along the table, and then retired to his office and left the door open a hand's width so that anybody who wanted to be supervised could be. The long table ran most of the room. The map covered most of the wall behind it — lake and ridge and the north shore in clean blue pencil, Olive's culvert in the margin, a canoe drawn small and careful in the corner with her initials beside it. A second sheet of paper was pinned below the map with a fresh corner of blank on it, and Annabeth had written nothing there yet, and it did not look like an oversight.

Percy came in with the blank page in his hand and stood a second in the doorway, the way you stood at the edge of water. Campers were already at the table. Olive sat at the far end with her elbows out, writing her grandmother in round letters and narrating it under her breath. Two Hermes kids shared a sheet between them and kept stealing it from each other. Marcus had a page in front of him and had not written anything yet, which was its own kind of letter. The room smelled of ink and cold lamp glass and the woodsmoke that lived in everybody's clothes after a week of fires.

"You're late," Clarisse said, without looking up. "You and the sea both. Sit down before I save you a seat and then you don't use it."

She had taken the end of the bench nearest the lamp, with a sheet of draft paper in front of her and a ruler. Silena sat beside her with wax paper, twine, and the mending tin open, and she had already taken the pencil out of her bun and put it back in twice, which was what she did when she was preparing to be useful.

Percy sat down between them. The bench was warm from Clarisse's back.

"Blank page," Silena said. It was not a question.

"Blank page," Percy agreed.

"Good. Blank pages are only scary until you ruin one." She slid the ink pot toward him with two fingers. "Go on. Ruin it."

Percy fished the farm pen out of the cup Clarisse kept for pens that had wandered, dipped it, and wrote, in his uphill-leaning hand:

Dear Tyson,

Then he stopped. The rest of the page stayed white.

Clarisse looked over. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you commune with it. You have been communing with a piece of paper for thirty seconds." She tapped her own sheet with the ruler. "Letters are like bolts. You don't think at them. You start at the other end."

"Tell him what he'll want to know," Silena said, warm, cutting her thread with her teeth. "That's the whole trick of your brother. He only ever wants to know things."

Percy wrote. The canoe went out Wednesday and it floats straight. The prow piece is tight. I checked it with my foot.

"Write the number," Clarisse said.

"What number?"

"Forty-eight. The lake. He'll want the number."

Percy added: The lake is forty-eight degrees. Cold on the bottom, warm on the top. Same as the forges.

He looked up. "He told me the forges."

"I know he told you the forges. I was in the cabin." Clarisse's pen scratched. She was drawing — the dock's third cleat, the bolt, the grain of the wood, and a caption in her blocky hand: STILL TIGHT. CHECKED WITH A FOOT. Then, in the corner, smaller, because she could not stop herself: I CHECK IT EVERY DAY. She would have died before explaining why that line was on a diagram of a cleat.

Percy wrote on. Brine says hello. She gets an apple against her debt, and she has been told the apples are coming. The maple by the pavilion turned this week, and Silena pressed a leaf for you, so you will know what color camp is.

"Arrows," Clarisse said, without looking up.

"You haven't drawn arrows yet."

"I don't need to draw them yet to tell you to write them."

Percy put in a line about the arrows anyway, because a diagram was better than a paragraph, and his brother had once written instructions on the back of his own hand. Then he got to the part that was not information, and stalled again with the pen in the air.

"Say it," Silena said, not unkindly. "You've gone quiet in the middle of a sentence. That's where the real letter lives."

Percy set the pen down. "How do you tell somebody you miss them when they can't stay?"

The parlor went on around them — ink, a stolen sheet of paper, Olive muttering — and the three of them sat in the small pocket of quiet that Percy's question made, and it was Clarisse who answered, blunt as a boot on a stair.

"You don't tell him anything about staying. You tell him what's here." She tapped the page with the ruler, once. "Write that the corner bunk stays made. That's the rule. He'll understand it."

Percy wrote it. Clarisse says the corner bunk stays made. That's the rule.

Silena read it over his shoulder. "That's not a rule."

"It's a rule."

"You made it a rule so it wouldn't be a feeling."

Clarisse's jaw worked. She did not look up from her diagram. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"Mm," Silena said.

"Don't mm me. Jackson, take the rest of it down."

Clarisse dictated, flat and fast, in the voice she used for tactics. "Write: she says the laundry lines are straight, and nobody in this cabin has folded a sheet right since you left, and it isn't a skill, it's a religion, and don't come back here acting like you forgot how it's done."

"That's four sentences."

"It's one sentence with ambition. Write it."

He wrote it. Silena read it back and laughed into her hand and said nothing, and Clarisse examined her cleat diagram with the concentration of a girl defusing something.

Then Silena, who had been preparing the letter's cargo all morning without discussion, produced it.

A wedge of honey cake gone firm at the edges, wrapped in waxed paper and tied with blue thread from the mending tin. And a maple leaf, pressed flat and dark red, the first one to turn on the tree beside the pavilion.

"The leaf's from yesterday," Silena said. "The maple. The first one. I pressed it in the back of my notebook, under the tower recipe. Don't tell the others, or they'll all want one and there are only so many leaves."

"Tyson can't read a leaf," Percy said.

"He can keep it. You press a thing so it doesn't blow away." She tied the leaf to the cake with the blue thread, one small bundle, and set it at the top of the page where he would not miss it. "Blue thread, so it looks like what it is. Cabin Three, outgoing."

Percy looked at the little bundle, and at his sister's flour-dusted hands arranging it, and had to look away for a second at the map on the wall, because the kindness of small logistics was a thing that went through him sideways.

"Move," Annabeth said, at his shoulder, and Percy moved, and she sat down on the bench beside him with her knees against his and her field book on her lap.

She had come in from the cold with her hair rebraided tight and the orange fleece over her camp shirt, the fleece that had been his in July and had quietly stopped being his, and she set two envelopes on the table and opened a folded sheet flat between them.

"Your brother can't read," she said.

"Hey."

"I drew him a key." The sheet was covered in her small neat drawings: a wavy line marked LAKE; a square marked HOUSE; a stand of triangles marked PINES; a tiny flame marked FORGE; and, in the middle of the cove, a one-legged bird drawn with great care, marked COVE. In the corner, in her working hand: IF YOU CANNOT READ THE WORDS, READ THE PICTURES. THEY ARE THE TRUE PART. — A.

Percy looked at it for a while. "You drew him a heron."

"I drew him a heron because the heron is the most important thing at the lake." She said it precisely, as a statement of fact, and turned the sheet so he could see the whole of it. "It's a key. Every map needs one. He can hold it up next to the camp when he comes and know where everything is."

"He's going to hold it up next to a lake."

"Then he'll know where he is." She tucked the key into the waxed bundle, folded the flap over it, and went back to her own letter with her pencil behind her ear, and Percy went back to his, and they wrote side by side in the lamplight without talking, the way you could only do beside somebody whose silence was the same shape as your own.

Their feet had found each other under the table without either of them looking, her sneaker alongside his, cold against warm, and stayed there, and neither of them mentioned it, because by now there was nothing about it left to mention.

A folded scrap came across the table under his hand. Her small upright letters. Stop watching me. It's making my handwriting crooked.

He turned the scrap over, wrote on the back, and slid it back. Then you should see the face you're making.

The scrap came back. What face.

He looked at her. She was looking hard at her page, but her ear had gone pink, and he knew that pink, and had five months of it filed. He wrote: The one where you're writing something you're not going to say out loud at this table.

The scrap did not come back for a while. When it did, two more lines had been added below his, in a smaller version of her hand.

P.S. — Friday. Campfire. I'm saving you the log with the good back.

And then, wrapped around the margin of the same scrap, smaller still:

P.S. back. Every log has a good back. I will draw the map of them. — Salt.

He read it twice. Then he folded the scrap along the crease she had used and put it in his pocket, on top of Tyson's letter, and under the table his foot pressed once against hers, and she pressed back, and that was the whole of that.

Clarisse was still drawing. Silena was tying twine. Percy leaned across the corner of the table and kissed his girlfriend — square in the middle of her sentence, unhurried, three seconds of it, cold air and ink between them — and she kissed him back without putting down her pen, and then went back to writing, and her line went crooked, and she scratched it out and began it again with her jaw set.

"Jackson," Clarisse said, to her diagram. "I am sitting right here."

"There's a ten-year-old at this table," Silena said.

"I'm ten and a half," Olive said, not looking up from her letter. "I already saw. Do it bigger so I can see properly."

"Nobody is doing anything bigger," Silena said.

When the page was finally full, Percy read it back to himself, and then out loud to his sisters, because a letter you could not say out loud was a letter that was not finished. Clarisse listened with her arms folded and made one correction.

"Arrows," she said.

"They're in."

"Put more in." She turned her diagram around so he could see it. The third cleat, the bolt, the grain of the wood, four arrows for the direction of tightness, and the caption, and the small corner line she would have denied. "He likes arrows. Arrows are honest."

Percy put the diagram in, and the map key, and the little bundle with the leaf and the cake. He pressed the flap flat with his thumb, and the thumbprint stayed in the wax like a seal, one brother answering another in the only language they had that crossed water. Then he wrote the address the way you addressed a fire:

TYSON.
THE FORGES.

There was no house number for a fire. But the mail found fires. That was what Hermes was for.

The parlor light had gone gold by three o'clock, the kind of light that made ink look black and made everything on the map wall look like a promise. The lamp was still lit against it. Somebody had brought in more wood for the stove and the room smelled of it now under the ink. Olive had run out of grandmother and gone home. Marcus was still writing, one line at a time, like a man laying track. Clarisse had her diagram finished and was now sanding the pencil off it with her thumb, and Silena was counting twine lengths under her breath.

At the other end of the table, Thalia pulled a fresh sheet in front of her and squared it with both hands, the way she squared a field sheet before a bearing.

"I'm starting it," she announced.

"Of course you are," Annabeth said.

"I'm older. I'm allowed to be sentimental first. That's the law."

"There is no such law."

"I'm making it. I'm eighteen. I make laws on Thursdays." Thalia dipped the pen and wrote, in the blocky field hand she had learned on boundary lines: Dear Jason. Then she frowned at the page and crossed out three starts of the next word, one after another, which Annabeth watched with the fascination of somebody seeing her sister struggle with a machine.

"Camp is fine," Thalia said, writing it. "The lake is cold. I walked the boundary line for four days and lost an argument to a squirrel, which you would have enjoyed, because you were always the kind of eight who laughed at animals misbehaving." She sat back. "Good start. Warm. Informative."

"'Camp is fine' is not information," Annabeth said.

"It's the news."

"It's the weather. He asked us to tell him what he needs to know. He gave us the page."

"He gave us half a page."

"He gave us all of it."

Thalia looked at the page. Then she made a small, grudging noise and amended it, and they traded the pen back and forth across the sheet, two hands and one letter, and it was the most ordinary thing in the world and neither of them said so.

He is eight no longer. Annabeth added that under the squirrel paragraph, and then crossed it out, because a boy did not need to be told he was growing.

"He's twelve," Thalia said, watching her cross it out. "You don't have to keep proving it to yourself."

"I'm not proving it. I'm checking the contour interval."

"You're checking the contour interval of your little brother." Thalia did not say it unkindly. She took the pen back. "I want to tell him about the squirrel."

"You are telling him about the squirrel. It's the second paragraph."

"I want to tell him the part where it drops the pinecone on my head."

"Then tell him."

"I'm building up to it." Thalia wrote. The squirrel got a pinecone. Then she wrote: There is a heron who lives at our lake. She stands on the bow of a sunken boat and waits. Nobody knows what she is waiting for. I think she is waiting for a boat that is coming back.

"Good," Annabeth said, quietly.

"It's not good, it's the truth. He'll ask about the lake. He always asks about the lake." Thalia turned the sheet a quarter turn, the way surveyors did, and looked at it from another angle. "Write the bit about the orchard. He'd like that. Silena has full authority over the pears."

"Chiron said it in front of everybody," Annabeth said, writing. "Nobody argues with the pears."

Then the letter got to the part where it was hard, which was the middle, as usual.

"Write to her," Annabeth said, not looking up.

Thalia's pen stopped. "Write what to who."

"To our mother. She writes him every Thursday and he answers her every Sunday. He knows more about her life right now than we do — the yellow door, the garden club, the tomatoes that are, and I quote, present. He's carrying all of that by himself because he doesn't know if he's allowed to mention her to us." Annabeth's hand had gone flat on the page. "He doesn't need us to tell him she's sober. He needs us to tell him we know, and that he's allowed to say it."

Thalia sat with the pen in her hand. The static in her flannel ticked, faint, in the cold room.

"He was eight when they took him," she said.

"I know."

"I was standing on the road."

"I know that too."

Thalia looked at the page for a long moment. Then she wrote, in her blocky hand, one line: Mother's new house has a yellow door. She is well. She writes to us both, and she is very bad at it, and we are glad anyway. It is allowed for you to tell us things about her. We want to know.

Annabeth read the line twice. "That's good."

"That's the whole letter," Thalia said. "Everything else is weather."

"It's still allowed to have weather."

"Yeah," Thalia said. "Yeah, it is." She turned the sheet back around. "Write about the canoe."

"I was going to write about the map."

"Write about the canoe first. You need the practice. The map is harder."

Annabeth wrote about the canoe — Tyson's canoe, repaired and varnished, the hippocampus prow catching the light, the way it sat high and eager in the water — and then she wrote three lines about the boy who had fixed it, and read them, and scratched them out, and wrote them again.

Thalia watched her do it with her arms folded and her boots hooked around the chair rung. "You're putting him in."

"I'm putting in a paragraph about the canoe."

"You're putting in a paragraph about the boy who fixed the canoe. You've written the same three lines twice and crossed them out twice." Thalia reached over and stilled Annabeth's hand with two flat fingers on the wrist, the way she did when Annabeth was about to measure something wrong. "Hey. Write it."

"I don't put people in letters. I put facts in letters. People are — people are a contour interval. You can be wrong about them." Annabeth's jaw was set. Her ear had gone pink. "If I write him down, I can't take him back."

Thalia's mouth did the thing it did. "Annabeth. I had a speech."

"A speech."

"A whole speech. Prepared for a year. For whoever was going to come along and break my baby sister's heart. It was going to be terrifying. I had notes." She held up two fingers. "Notes. I was going to ask Clarisse for help being intimidating and she said yes immediately, which tells you something about my face."

"You're not telling me this to be funny."

"I'm telling you this because I never got to use it." Thalia's voice lost the dry edge and found the flat one underneath. "I had a speech, and I didn't need it. He's good to you. He's good to Clarisse, and Clarisse does not let anybody be good to her. Silena likes him, and he lets Tyson carry laundry like it's an honor. And I have spent a year being ready to be a wall, and it turns out I get to be a sister instead." She squeezed Annabeth's wrist once and let go. "I am not wasting a year of preparation on a boy who turned out to be decent. Write the three lines."

Annabeth looked at her sister. Then she wrote them, and they were not the three lines she had crossed out.

There is a boy named Percy. He fixed the canoe and he is very good to us. You would like him. You don't have to yet.

Thalia read it over her shoulder. "'You don't have to yet,'" she repeated. "That's good. That's — okay. That's going to hold."

Then Thalia said, "Tell him about the bacon," and Annabeth said, "There is no bacon left, Saturday's bacon is gone, this is not a letter about a hypothetical," and Thalia said, "It's not hypothetical. It's a memory. Write it."

So Annabeth wrote: We had bacon on Saturday. You would have eaten it raw off the plate and denied it, and the smell went through the house, and we thought about you.

After that they were both quiet for a minute, because that sentence had four years of arithmetic inside it, and because it was a memory that only two people in the world held, and one of them was in a valley full of wolves, and neither of them knew whether he still had it, or whether four years of being eight-to-twelve had filed it down to a story about a house he used to live in.

"Read it," Annabeth said. "Out loud. We have to hear a letter before we mail it."

"That's not a rule."

"It's a rule I'm making now. Letters are instruments. You don't tune an instrument by looking at it."

So they read it aloud, trading paragraphs, Thalia's voice going dry on the squirrels and catching on the last lines, Annabeth's going precise and then not. Twice through. The second time, Thalia's hand was shaking a little on the pen where she had started to sign, and Annabeth watched the shake travel through the capital letter, and said nothing about it, the way she had learned to say nothing about the things that would stop happening if they were looked at.

"Give me the pen," Annabeth said. "Your hand's gone crooked."

Thalia looked down. It had. The signature had gone from a signature into a gesture. She handed the pen over without a word, and Annabeth copied the letter out fair on a clean sheet, keeping Thalia's blocky capitals for the headings and using her own small upright hand for the body, two hands on one page, which was the entire point, and neither of them said that either.

Across the table, Percy sealed the lumpy envelope for Tyson and held it up so she could see it, and she nodded, and he nodded back, and went back to sitting with his sisters.

Then Annabeth picked up her pencil and went to the wall.

At the top corner of the map, where the drawn country ran out into the blank of the sheet, she wrote, small and neat, in the hand that did the camp's records: JASON GRACE. CAMP JUPITER. And under that, because a map was a promise and this was the promise she had been carrying since Friday: WRITTEN TO EVERY THURSDAY.

Thalia came and stood beside her with her boots crossed in the doorway and her arms folded, watching. She watched Annabeth's pencil make the small precise letters. She watched her sister put their brother on the wall of the house where he did not sleep, in the corner where the country ran out, and then she reached over and took the pencil, and added, underneath, in blocky capitals that she did not bother to make even:

COUNTED.

"Six letters," Annabeth said, thickly. "You had room."

"It's six letters. It's enough." Thalia handed the pencil back. "Come on. The mail goes down at dark, and I want to be the one who carries it."

Thalia Grace carried the mail down the hill at dusk and did not let her sister carry any of it, which was not a chivalrous gesture and was not offered as one.

The sack was canvas and it had been mended twice and it weighed about as much as a kitten. It was the whole outgoing post of Camp Half-Blood for the week: forty-one letters and four parcels, and every single one of them had been written on that long table in the parlor by somebody who had sat down on a Thursday and decided to say something to a person who was not there. Thalia had put the sack over her shoulder at the door of the Big House and had walked out with it before anybody could offer to take it, because the last letter in the stack, under eleven other letters and on top of nothing, was in her own hand in her own blocky capitals, addressed to a boy at a camp in a valley in California, and she was not going to hand that to a Hermes kid.

Annabeth came with her, because of course she did, and because Chiron's rule about the road after dark was a rule about pairs and Annabeth had helped write the rule.

The camp behind them was going down for the night. The lamps came on in the west path, amber, one at a time, and the pavilion took the last of the light off the lake and turned it into smoke, and somewhere up the hill the forge was still going, which it always was, and the sound of it came down through the trees in short bursts and stopped and started. There had been a frost the night before and there would be one tonight, and the path under their feet was already hard where the sun had gone off it.

The road was a quarter mile below the gate, past the last pine and the ruined gatepost that nobody had ever fixed, and it ran in a straight line from nowhere to nowhere in the particular way that country roads do, and at the end of the fence there was the mail post: not a box, a post, a bare creosoted post with a hook on it and a stenciled board that said CAMP US MAIL and under that, in somebody's careful hand from fifty years ago, RURAL FREE DELIVERY ROUTE 3.

The mail did not come to the post. The mail came to the hook. What went on the hook at dusk went into town by six the next morning, because a man in a truck drove that road before the bakery opened and collected it, for reasons that nobody at camp had ever investigated because the answer was obviously that Hermes was involved and the explanation would have been worse than the mystery.

Thalia hung the sack on the hook. It took two hands, and it swung, and she held it a second so it settled straight.

"That's it," she said.

"That's it," Annabeth said.

The two of them stood on the road in the cold with their hands in their pockets and looked at a canvas sack on a post, which is, when you spend a minute doing it, one of the strangest things a person can look at: forty-one pieces of paper weighing about nine ounces, going into a truck at six in the morning and out into the world by the weekend, carrying a pressed maple leaf, and a diagram of a canoe cleat with four arrows on it, and a map key with a heron on it, and a letter in block capitals to a twelve-year-old in the Fifth Cohort.

"Four times," Thalia said.

"What?"

"I wrote him four times and never mailed one. I know what I said. I'm saying it again at the post so the post knows." She looked at the sack. "He's going to get a letter from us on Saturday or Monday, and it's going to be in two hands, and it's going to have the seam of the roof in it and the words your room in it, which she wrote and I read and neither of us has said out loud before, and he's going to read it in a place where nobody's ever going to say your room to him."

"He'll write back," Annabeth said.

"He'll write back on Sunday, because that's when he writes."

"Then we'll have a letter due."

Thalia turned her head and looked at her sister in the last of the light. That was the sentence, she thought later, that had actually done it: not the letter and not the post and not the four unwritten ones in the bottom of her satchel, but her fourteen-year-old sister standing on a county road in the cold saying then we'll have a letter due, in the flat voice she used for measurements, as if the whole vast machinery of a family that had been broken on a road four years ago could be put right by establishing a schedule.

Which, Thalia had to admit, walking back up, was exactly how it was going to be put right.

---

Percy Jackson was sitting on the Big House steps when the two of them came up the road in the dark, and he did not pretend he had been doing anything else, because by October there was no point in any of them pretending about that.

"Post off?" he said.

"Post off," Thalia said. "Forty-one letters, four parcels, one sack, one hook, one truck at six in the morning that nobody at this camp is allowed to ask about."

"You're not allowed to ask about the truck."

"I asked about the truck in June and Chiron told me the answer and I have not slept properly since."

They went down to the pavilion for the last of the dinner, which was the good kind of late dinner: the fire out front, the tables half cleared, Silena's bread going round a camp that had eaten already and was eating again because there was bread, and the whole hill under a sky that had gone the deep flat blue of October at half past six. Somebody had lit the nine lamps along the west path and the tenth was on a porch at the bottom of the hill, and the lake lay below all of it, closed, holding the last of the light the way it held everything.

Clarisse had the winter list out on the table with two lines crossed off and one added, and she did not say anything when the Grace sisters came in, and she did not say anything when Percy sat down between them, and after a while she said, to the table in general, "Post go down?"

"Post went down."

"Good. Somebody wrote a letter to the pear supplier and signed my name."

"That was me," Silena said, from the end of the table, without looking up.

"I know it was you. It's my name and your handwriting and a paragraph about how the orchard deserves a proper ledger."

"It does deserve a proper ledger."

"It does," Clarisse agreed, and wrote something on her list, and that was the whole of the argument, which had been settled, by every account, four days earlier.

Annabeth ate her soup with one hand and wrote in her field book with the other, which was a thing she did at dinner as a rule and which nobody in that family had ever once remarked on out loud, because the rule about remarks was the oldest one they had. Under the table her foot found Percy's ankle and stayed there. Across the table Marcus was copying a heading out of somebody else's book in his cramped careful hand, and Olive had gone home, and the pavilion was doing the thing the pavilion did at the end of a Thursday: the fire going down, the talk going slow, everybody staying past the point of being finished because it was warm and because being finished was not the same as being done.

"Friday," Annabeth said, eventually, without looking up from her book.

"Friday," Percy said.

"The campfire. Don't let it become a whole thing."

"I'm not making it a whole thing. You're the one who wrote it on a scrap of paper and slid it across a table in front of a centaur's open office door."

"I wrote it on a scrap of paper because that is how information is moved at that table." She turned a page. "I'm saving you the log with the good back. It's a structural preference. Don't make it be a feeling."

"I'm not making it be a feeling."

"You're making it be a feeling right now, at this table, with your face."

"This is my face."

"I know," Annabeth said, and wrote another line, and her line went crooked, and she scratched it out and began it again, and Percy, sitting beside her in the last of the firelight with his hands wrapped around a mug, decided that this was the shape of his whole life now and did not have a single thing to say about it that would improve on the moment.

The walk back up to the cabins was Annabeth's, because Thalia had stopped at the forge to return a borrowed file and had said, over her shoulder, in the voice of a person granting a favor while pretending to withhold one, "Ten minutes. She's on the porch by ten twenty."

They took the long way, which is what you did when there were ten minutes and you had decided in advance that you did not want them to be short. The lamps went by overhead, one at the boathouse turn, two at the brake, the odd one at the curve, amber through glass, and the frost on the low ground had already gone stiff and shining where the light fell across it, and the whole camp smelled of woodsmoke and cold leaves and, faintly, from somewhere down the hill, of the salt in the hall.

"Did you read it?" Percy said, at the curve.

"Read what?"

"Your letter. When you finished writing it, did you read it?"

Annabeth was quiet for four or five steps. "Twice," she said. "Once for the words and once for whether they were true."

"And?"

"The second reading is harder than the first and I recommend it to everyone and nobody does it," she said. "I wrote him that when he was eight he was afraid of the deep end and he did it anyway, and that the reason I know is that he told me about it himself, and that the reason he doesn't remember telling me is that he was eight, and that this is what a record is for." She put her hands deeper in her pockets. "That's the whole letter, practically. Everything else is housekeeping. Percy, I wrote my brother a letter that starts with a thing he doesn't remember and ends with a list of what we're going to show him when he comes."

"Does the list have the heron on it?"

"The list has the heron on it first."

"Then it's a good letter." Percy reached over without any ceremony and took her hand out of her pocket, which she allowed, and then held it anyway, and they walked the last hundred yards of the west path at the pace of two people who were not going to be rushed by a rule about being on a porch by ten twenty.

At the bottom of the hill the tenth lantern was on its hook and the door was warm and somebody inside was running water in the kitchen, and above the door, on the hook the lantern hung from, two lines had been scratched into the wood with the point of a nail and inked in with a pen, small and neat, both of them upright, no flourishes.

WRITTEN TO EVERY THURSDAY.

COUNTED.

They stood on the step in the cold and read them for a moment, the way you read a thing that had been carved by people you were going to live with until spring, and then Percy opened the door and the warm went out past them and the whole cabin, the long table and the stove and the quilt on the chair and the corner bunk with the white rock on its folded shirt, was exactly where it had been all day, waiting for its people to come in out of the dark.

Inside, Clarisse was at the long table with the winter list, and she had reached the part of the evening where she copied things she had already copied, which was how she thought. Silena was at the stove with a pot of water going for no reason other than that the stove was lit. Somebody had put the second lamp in the window, which was the ten o'clock rule, and the window with the lamp in it faced down the hill, which meant the hill got to look up at it.

Percy came in last and shut the door on the cold and stood a moment with his back to it and did the thing he had started doing in September without telling anybody: he took the room's inventory the way Clarisse took the boathouse's. The table. The stove. The stove's tick. The chair with the quilt on it, the third row pinned and waiting. The counter with the good knife and the tin cup and the plate of the last of Silena's bread under a cloth, because somebody had thought about tomorrow at some point today. The corner bunk with its folded shirt and its white rock, made up and not used, which was the rule.

His sisters were in it. His girlfriend was on the step outside, shaking the frost off her hood and arguing with her sister about a file that had not been returned properly to a forge. The letter was hanging on a hook a quarter mile down the road with forty other pieces of paper, one of which had a pressed maple leaf tied to it with blue thread.

It had been a Thursday with a very ordinary sort of consequence: the letters were gone, and the people who had written them would have to wait. Percy looked once more at the empty space beside the ink bottle on the table. He couldn't retrieve a line he'd written in haste or make the recipient answer before the mail reached them.

"You're doing the thing," Clarisse said without looking up.

"What thing?"

"Standing in the doorway until somebody tells you the day went correctly." She moved her paper aside to make room. "Sit down. We're checking the fireplace before the night shift."

Percy sat. Silena handed him the little metal poker while she lifted the screen and inspected its hinge. It had begun to drag against the stone, which was exactly the sort of problem Clarisse would put on tomorrow's list if they left it. Percy held the screen in place. Clarisse tightened the fastening, tested it twice, and only then pushed the tool aside.

Annabeth came in, stamping the frost from her boots. Thalia followed, still complaining about a file that had not been returned to the forge. Neither of them asked Percy what he'd written. Annabeth put her field book by the lamp and began looking for a clean pencil.

"Did the mail go?" she asked him.

"Six in the morning."

"You checked?"

"I watched them seal the sack."

"Then you've done your part."

He considered pointing out that writing a letter was an exceptionally small part of most problems. But Silena was bringing tea, and Clarisse was asking him to carry the repaired screen back toward the hearth. He got up to help. The letter would take the road without him; the people in the room still needed hands. He could be useful here while he waited.
