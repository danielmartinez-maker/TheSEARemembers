## Chapter 84: Spring Resolutions

The last day of May came to Camp Half-Blood sideways, in a cool wind off the Sound that rattled the shutters of Cabin Three and pushed thin lines of rain under the door.

Percy Jackson woke to cold on his nose and warmth everywhere else. His blanket was tucked too tight to be his own doing. Someone had come in late and fixed it. The cabin smelled like salt and spring rain and the faint lemon of the cleaning soap his mother had sent in his winter care box. The little blue night-light Annabeth had charmed for him two winters ago still glowed over the door, steady as a lighthouse. Outside, the waves were loud. Storm waves were different, sharper, less patient.

He lay there a minute and listened. No early conch horn on spring resolutions day. With mostly year-rounders here, Chiron let this gathering day’s morning happen slow. Percy could hear the flag rope clanking against the pole on Half-Blood Hill. He could hear the forge chimney ticking as it cooled. He could hear Grover snoring faintly through the wall of Cabin Three's back storage room, where Grover had crashed after late patrol because, he said, your cabin smells like the ocean and it helps me dream right.

Percy smiled up at the dark ceiling. Thirteen, and still learning what ordinary holidays could be. Spring resolutions day.

In the life he remembered, he had spent a New Year's Eve in his mom's apartment above the candy shop, with blue popcorn and the ball drop on their little TV and Sally falling asleep on his shoulder before midnight and pretending she hadn't. In an earlier remembered year, he had been at Yancy Academy trying not to get expelled before Christmas break ended. Back then, he hadn't known gods were real.

Now he was here. At Camp. In spring. With his family.

He sat up, scrubbed his hands through his hair, and pulled on the thick gray sweater Annabeth had knitted him for the last winter Solstice. It was slightly too long in the sleeves and had one uneven stripe where she had lost count and refused to frog it because, she said, perfect is boring and you will wear it anyway. He wore it anyway. He wore it every day.

On his desk, under a chipped mussel shell, was the notebook Amphitrite had given him last Christmas to hold his memories of Sally. Dark blue cover, heavy paper, with a little handwritten card tucked in the front: For the stories only you can tell. Love, Mom. He touched the cover for luck, the way he did every morning, and then pulled on his coat and went out into the gray-gold morning.

The rain had mostly stopped. What was left dripped from the pines. His breath fogged in the cool wind. Camp before the summer arrivals was smaller and truer. Twenty-three campers. Six satyrs. Chiron and Mr. D.

Percy cut across the commons toward the pavilion, hands in his pockets, boots crunching. The braziers were already lit. Someone had swept the steps.

Annabeth was waiting for him on the pavilion steps with two mugs.

She was in her rain coat, her blonde hair in a thick braid, her cheeks pink from cold. Her gray eyes found him immediately and lit.

"Happy resolution day, Seaweed Brain," she said, and held out a mug.

"Happy resolution day, Wise Girl." He took it. Hot chocolate, with cinnamon. He sat down beside her on the cold step, shoulders pressed together.

"You sleep?" she asked.

"Good. Grover fixed my blanket again."

"He says you kick it off and then make sad octopus noises in your sleep."

"I do not make octopus noises."

"You do," Annabeth said solemnly. "I have witness testimony."

Percy bumped his shoulder against hers. "Traitor. You're supposed to be on my side."

"I am on your side. That's why I brought you the mug with the chip on the side you like."

He looked down. It was true. It was his favorite mug. He felt that stupid warm fizz in his chest he always felt when she did things like that, small careful things nobody else would notice.

They drank their chocolate and watched the sky go from gray to pale gold.

"Resolution day," Annabeth said after a while.

"Are we doing that this year?"

"Chiron says we should. A spring tradition. Year-rounders bonfire tonight, resolutions at midnight, first-of-June hike tomorrow." She sipped. "I already know mine."

Percy glanced at her. "Yeah?"

She nodded, but she didn't say it yet. She turned the mug in her hands. "It's the garden. I want to finish it this year. No more I’ll do it next month."

He knew which garden she meant.

---

The pavilion filled slowly for breakfast. A spring holiday breakfast was different from summer breakfast. No rush, no lines out the door, no Hermes kids stealing bacon off each other's plates as a competitive sport. Just the clatter of trays, the hiss of the brazier, Mr. D complaining about the cold from under three blankets at the head table while Chiron poured him tea with infinite patience.

Percy and Annabeth ended up at their usual half-table between Zeus and Poseidon. Grover slid in across from them with oatmeal and toast, his horns poking through his knitted hat.

"Morning," Grover said around toast. "Did you hear? Thalia's coming."

Percy sat up. "Today?"

"She Iris-messaged Chiron at dawn. Her scouting party is three ridges over. Chiron approved her return for the bonfire." Grover beamed. "She said, and I quote, tell Seaweed Brain to save me a seat or I'll shoot him."

"Affectionate," Annabeth said dryly.

"It's how we show love," Percy said, and stole Grover's extra toast.

Clarisse and Silena came in together, cheeks red, carrying a covered basket between them. Clarisse La Rue in the cool wind was all bulk and scowl, her dark hair pulled back under a red hat. Silena looked like spring had been invented to show off her coloring.

"Out of the way, freshmen," Clarisse said to no freshmen, and set the basket down with ceremony. "Blue muffins. Your mom sent them, Jackson. She labeled them. This one says Percy, this one says Annabeth, this one says Grover stop eating Percy's share, and this one says Clarisse, eat one, you look cold."

Everyone laughed. Amphitrite's labels were becoming famous at Camp.

Silena slid in beside Annabeth and leaned her head on Annabeth's shoulder for three seconds. "Morning, loves. Happy Resolution Eve."

"Is that what we're calling it?" Grover asked.

"I just decided," Silena said. "We need a name for the day you spend pretending you're going to be a whole new person by June."

"I'm going to be a whole new person by June," Clarisse said. "I'm going to be a person who drinks this coffee before she kills anyone."

Chiron clopped over then. He looked at them the way he always looked at his year-rounders before summer.

"Good morning, my heroes," he said. "Tonight, bonfire at the amphitheater. Eight o'clock. Dress warm. Bring a resolution and a story from last year. Tomorrow, first light hike to the woodland bells. Annabeth, the path is clear?"

"Almost," Annabeth said. "Percy and I will check it after breakfast."

"Excellent." Chiron smiled. "Percy, Amphitrite Iris-messaged the Big House this morning. She wishes you a happy resolution day and says not to stay up too late, which means you should absolutely stay up to midnight."

Percy grinned so wide his face hurt. "Tell her I said midnight exactly and not a minute more."

"Silena, will you lead the song tonight?" Chiron asked.

Silena's smile flickered, just for a second. "We'll see. Maybe."

Clarisse looked at her sharply, then looked away. Percy caught Annabeth's eye. They had both noticed. Silena hummed constantly, braiding hair, washing dishes, walking patrol, but lately she had hesitated when asked to sing a solo. Hearing about the Silena Percy and Annabeth remembered had unsettled her. Nobody blamed her for choices she had not made here; still, standing alone before a crowd could feel like asking to be judged.

Annabeth squeezed Silena's hand under the table.

"And Grover," Chiron said, "the Council wrote back. You will tell me later?"

Grover nodded, suddenly serious, his ears twitching under his hat. "Yeah. Later."

After Chiron moved on, Percy leaned in. "What did the Council say?"

Grover hesitated. "They said no. To the wide search. They said we don't have satyrs to spare for unconfirmed lights this spring."

Percy felt Annabeth go still beside him. Unconfirmed lights meant possible half-bloods, flickers of mist or wild magic that satyrs sensed on their empathy network. Grover had been tracking three since winter.

"What are you going to do?" Annabeth asked quietly.

Grover looked down at his oatmeal, then up, and his young goat eyes were suddenly old. "I think I'm going to go find one anyway. Just one. Before summer. Even if I have to use my own leave."

Percy reached across and gripped his wrist. "Then we'll help you get ready."

Grover smiled crookedly. "Resolution number one, huh?"

"Write it down for tonight," Silena said softly, and pushed a little card and a pencil toward him from the basket. She had made them for everyone, cream cards with pressed leaves in the corner. "Everyone may have one. You can say it aloud or keep it private. Chiron asked me not to turn people's plans into a public test."

Clarisse snorted. "Who made that rule?"

"I did," Silena said. "Just now."

---

The shrine garden was behind the Athena cabin, in a sheltered elbow where the wall broke the north wind and the sun pooled even on a cool spring day.

Before Percy went there, he stopped at the Big House to ask Chiron about the blue notebook Annabeth had given him for the Sally project.

He hadn't told Chiron every detail of his remembered mother. Chiron knew the two children held memories that didn't match the history their families had lived, but the centaur hadn't used that knowledge as permission to display those memories publicly. Percy was grateful for the distinction.

"Do you want to write something for the memorial archive?" Chiron asked.

"Maybe. Not yet."

"Then you needn't."

Percy unfolded the plan he'd made with Annabeth. He wanted to write about Sally Jackson, the woman who raised him in the life he remembered before the rewrite. He wanted the stories to be true, not just the parts that sounded heroic. He also didn't want the book to make Amphitrite, his mother in this life, think he was accusing her of something she hadn't done. She loved the boy she remembered raising, and she didn't know why he couldn't remember his childhood as the same boy.

"Can I keep it private?" Percy asked.

"Yes."

"Even if it's about somebody who died here?"

"Particularly if sharing it would hurt someone who hasn't consented to be part of the story."

Percy looked at the blank notebook. "What if I accidentally write something wrong?"

"Then correct it, or mark the limits of your memory. The important thing isn't to produce a perfect volume. It is to know what each account can support."

Percy thought of Elena's stories about her mother. A written sentence could preserve an ordinary moment without giving the writer ownership of everyone else's feelings.

"Could I show it to Annabeth?"

"That's your decision. But ask her before you make her responsible for keeping it."

Percy nodded. He wasn't asking her to be the keeper of every secret he carried. He wanted her help spelling difficult words and identifying which parts of the narrative were memories they had lived together and which belonged to Percy's childhood before she knew him.

He went to the garden and found Annabeth testing the soil by pressing a small trowel into it.

"I asked Chiron about the book," Percy said.

Annabeth looked up. "And?"

"It's mine. It stays private unless I decide otherwise. You can read what I show you, not every page by default."

She sat back on her heels. "Good."

"I thought you'd be annoyed."

"Why?"

"Because you offered to help."

"Helping isn't the same as owning it." She wiped mud from her hands. "You can tell me when you want me to read something."

Percy opened the notebook to the first page. He tried to write an outline, then stopped. The headings seemed enormous: *Childhood, School, Family, War.* He had thought he could write Sally's story by remembering a few good afternoons. Now he realized the book would include people who hadn't all consented to have their lives recorded.

"What if I don't remember the exact day?" he asked.

"Then don't invent one. Write 'I think it was spring' or 'I can't place it.' You already know how to do that."

"Do I?"

"You've been doing it with your family here. You know what Amphitrite told you about the palace, but you don't pretend you saw those years."

The words hurt a little, though she meant them kindly. Percy looked toward the camp path.

"I want to remember Sally without making everyone else feel like I'm asking them to disappear."

"Then tell the truth about the people who are here too," Annabeth said. "That's part of your story."

He sat on the wall beside her and wrote his first heading: *Things I remember myself.* Beneath it he made a second: *Things people told me afterward.* He left the rest blank. It wasn't much of a book. It was the beginning of one he could trust.

Annabeth returned to the trowel. Percy watched her move a small stone from the growing bed. She'd told him earlier that a garden needed drainage and enough space for roots, not just flowers lined up for a picture. He understood the comparison without needing to say it aloud.

He closed the notebook, then reopened it to add one more note: *Sally liked blue food. I remember her making it. I don't know the date of every breakfast.*

It was a small fact. He knew it was his.



It had started last summer as an apology. Annabeth had been twelve and furious after a difficult encounter with Zeus, and she had taken a trowel and a bag of marble chips and built a tiny square of order in the pine needles: a low stone border, a flat altar stone, a place for offerings that were not about war. The Athena campers had offered a sheltered corner for her garden. She remembered wisdom and craft and olive trees from her original life; this could hold rosemary and thyme and a place for quiet without changing whose daughter she was.

Percy had helped from the beginning. He carried stones. He hauled soil. He learned the names of plants because she said them while she worked and he remembered everything she said. Thalia had brought a wild olive seedling from a scouting trip. Clarisse had, without a word, left a smooth river stone with a helm scratched on it. Silena had planted violets. Grover had blessed it with satyr pipes so the roots would take.

But it was not finished. The back border was still raw earth. The mosaic Annabeth had planned for the center, a gray and blue owl in pebbles, was only half-set. The wooden bench Percy had sanded last spring still needed sealing. War and quests and summer and school had kept interrupting.

Annabeth stood there after breakfast with her hands in her pockets, looking at it the way Percy looked at the sea when he was trying to figure out its mood.

"The woodland bells are up," she said.

Percy crouched. Sure enough, along the south edge where the sun hit first, there were green noses pushing through the leaf mold, pale and stubborn. Lily-of-the-valley. White woodland bells. Ella had shown him the flowers last winter, very seriously, in her herbal. Hope in a white bell.

"They're early," he said.

"They're brave," Annabeth corrected. She knelt beside him, her knee pressing his. "I remember Athena saying spring flowers don’t wait for permission."

Percy nudged one leaf gently with his fingertip. "So what's the plan? Finish before summer?"

"Finish by summer solstice." She pulled out her notebook, flipped to a sketch. The finished garden in ink and watercolor wash: low stone walls complete, mosaic owl finished, bench sealed and set under the olive, thyme between the pavers, a little bronze plaque that said FOR QUIET WORK. "I keep saying next month. I keep letting capture the flag planning eat my afternoons. This year I want to actually do it. Not for a god. For me. To prove I can finish a growing thing, not just a building thing."

Percy studied the sketch. It was beautiful. Everything Annabeth drew was beautiful in the way that meant you could build it if you were patient.

"Okay," he said. "Resolution. I'll help. I'll carry. I'll learn thyme versus oregano finally."

"You still can't tell them apart."

"They both smell green."

She laughed and shoved him lightly, and he caught her hand without thinking and held it for a second in his glove, warm and small and sure. They had held hands a hundred times, crossing creeks, climbing the wall, walking back from the bonfire when the dark felt too big. It was not dramatic. It was theirs. Warm and steady. At thirteen, with the whole camp watching them grow up, steady felt like a promise.

"We'll do Saturdays," she said. "Just us. No quest talk."

"No quest talk," Percy agreed. "And I'll bring muffins."

"Deal." She looked at him, gray eyes very clear. "What's yours? Your resolution. You haven't said."

Percy glanced toward his cabin, where the blue notebook waited under the shell.

"My mom's story," he said quietly. "Not the god stuff. Her story. How she grew up, how she met my dad, how she worked double shifts and still read to me every night and made blue food when the world was gray. She told me pieces in the life I remember. I want to write it down. All of it. Before I forget the exact words."

Annabeth's expression softened completely. "Percy, that's—"

"I don't know how to do it right," he admitted. "I'm dyslexic. Writing's hard. What if I mess it up?"

"You won't mess it up. You'll do it like you do everything. Slow and true." She bumped her head against his shoulder. "I'll help with spelling if you want. But the words have to be yours."

He nodded, throat tight. Sally's story felt huge and fragile, like one of those snowdrop buds. If he didn't write it, who would? Poseidon had myths. Sally had no temple. Percy wanted to write a private account of the mother he personally remembered, without distributing details that would expose the rewritten timeline to people who weren't entrusted with it.

"Saturdays too?" Annabeth asked. "You write, I plant? Side by side?"

"Yeah," Percy said. "Side by side."

They stayed there a while, planning rows, while the sun moved and the woodland bells seemed to lift a millimeter toward the light.

---

Thalia arrived at noon like a storm front, all crackle and grin and windburn.

Percy heard her before he saw her, her laugh bouncing off the Big House porch, and then she was there, black hair spiked with rain, patrol parka over her Camp shirt, a bow across her back and pine needles in her boots. She dropped her pack, grabbed Percy in a headlock, and ruffled his hair until he yelped.

"Seaweed Brain! You got taller. I hate it."

"You got wetter. I hate it more," Percy gasped, and she let him go and grabbed Annabeth next, lifting her half off her feet in a hug that was all older-sister ferocity.

"Wise Girl. Garden still unfinished?"

"Don't start."

"I'm just saying, Athena kids and deadlines." Thalia slung an arm around each of them and squeezed. Daughters of Zeus, son of Poseidon. Kids kept by the hill, Percy thought. They had all been kept by the same hill in different ways.

They walked the barrier together, just the three of them, the way Thalia had asked. Old habit. Check the wards, kick the boundary stones, make sure winter hadn't loosened anything. The wind was sharp up there. The Golden Fleece glowed faintly from the pine, and Peleus the dragon snored in his coils, puffing smoke rings.

For a while they just patrolled, comfortable. Thalia told scouting stories, Annabeth asked sharp questions about logistics, Percy flicked beads of rainwater at the barrier to watch them sizzle.

Then Thalia went quiet near the Zeus fist overlook. She stopped, looked out at the Sound, her electric-blue eyes distant.

"Can I tell you guys something lame?" she said.

"Always," Annabeth said immediately.

Thalia kicked at a muddy rut. "I almost got lost last month. Not like lost lost. We were tracking a manticore in the Cascades and the snow came down and I split off like an idiot because I thought I saw movement and my radio died. I was out for six hours. Cold. Really cold. I knew what to do. I knew the protocols. I just... didn't do them. I just kept walking."

Percy felt his stomach drop. "Thalia—"

"I'm fine. Phoebe found me. She didn't even lecture me, which was worse." Thalia's jaw worked. "I've been running that night over and over. I was scared. I was actually scared and I didn't call for help until I was half-frozen because I thought daughters of Zeus don't get scared and scouts don't need rescue."

Annabeth stepped closer. "Thals."

"I've been thinking about resolutions because Artemis asked us all to pick a virtue to practice," Thalia went on fast, like she needed to get it out before she lost nerve. "And mine is... I want to ask for help. Before I'm half-frozen. Like, early. When I still have options. I hate it. I hate even saying it. But I think if I don't learn it, I'm going to get someone else hurt, not just me."

Silence. Wind. The flag snapping.

Percy thought about all the times Thalia had been the wall for them, holding the barrier in the storm, standing between Annabeth and a monster with sparks running up her arms, never flinching. How lonely that must be, to always be the lightning rod.

He bumped his shoulder hard against hers. "That's not lame. That's the bravest one I've heard today."

"Yeah?" Her voice was small.

"Yeah," Annabeth said fiercely, and took Thalia's cold hand in both of hers. "And you can practice on us. Right now. This spring. Ask us for stupid stuff. Ask Percy to carry your pack. Ask me to check your maps even when you know they're right."

Thalia laughed wetly. "Okay. Practice. Um. I'm cold and I'm starving and I don't want to walk back alone because my boots are wet. Will you walk back with me the long way so I don't have to admit to the Hunters I got blisters?"

"Yes," Percy and Annabeth said together.

"See?" Annabeth said, linking arms with her. "You're already better at it."

Thalia leaned her head against Annabeth's for a moment, then Percy's, and they walked back the long way, slow, talking about nothing, while Peleus smoked behind them and the sky began to bruise toward evening.

---

Clarisse was in the forge when Percy found her, which was where she always went when she was thinking too loud.

The forge on this cool spring day was dim and red-gold, smelling of coal and hot metal and the oil Clarisse used on her spear. She was not working metal, though. She was sitting on an upturned bucket behind the quench barrel with a battered composition notebook open on her knees, chewing her pencil eraser to shreds.

Percy knocked on the doorframe. "Permission to enter war territory?"

Clarisse startled and slammed the notebook shut. "Jackson. What do you want?"

"Thalia's back. Bonfire at eight. Silena saved you a muffin." He nodded at the notebook. "Whatcha writing?"

"Battle logs." Too fast.

Percy raised an eyebrow and sat down on the floor across from her, uninvited in the way only siblings could do. They had earned that. Seasons of sparring and bleeding and staying up late talking about dads who loved wrong. He had called her his big sister by accident and she had kept it on purpose.

"Clarisse."

"What."

"Battle logs don't make you chew your pencil like that."

She glared at him. Then she sighed, huge and put-upon, and opened the notebook a crack. Percy caught glimpses: lines, broken lines, crossed-out words. Not logs. Verses.

"You write poems," he said softly.

"They're not poems. They're... observations with line breaks."

"Can I hear one?"

"No."

"Okay." Percy leaned back against the wall. He didn't push. He had learned with Clarisse that pushing got you stabbed, but sitting got you invited.

The forge ticked. Outside, someone laughed.

Clarisse stared at her page. "They're stupid. My sparring friends don't do poems. My dad—" She stopped. "My dad would laugh. Or worse, he'd use it."

Percy thought about Poseidon, about how Clarisse had once told him their father's praise could feel like another demand to be strong. He thought about Sally, who taped his awful third-grade poems to the fridge for a year.

"My mom would cry in a good way if she heard yours," he said. "Just saying."

Clarisse huffed, but her ears went pink. She flipped pages, past many crossed-out drafts, to one with fewer marks.

"It's about winter patrol," she muttered. "It's short."

Percy nodded and waited.

She read, low and gruff, stumbling once:

"Snow on the spear-rack, / breath in the dark. / I walk the hill / and hold the spark. / No glory in it. / Just cold, just true. / I keep the watch / for me and you."

Silence. The coals popped.

Percy felt his throat close. "Clarisse. That's good. That's really good."

"It's plain."

"It's true. That's better." He met her eyes. "Are you going to share one tonight? At resolutions?"

She closed the notebook hard. "No. Absolutely not. They'll laugh."

"They won't. Silena won't let them."

At Silena's name, Clarisse's expression shifted, softened in a way few people ever saw. Silena was the only person Clarisse let braid her hair. Silena was the only person who had ever told Clarisse her hands were gentle and made her believe it.

"I was thinking," Clarisse said slowly, "maybe my resolution could be... sharing another one. A different poem, if I decide it belongs at the bonfire. I shared three lines for the memorial board, not the whole notebook."

Percy grinned. "That's a good resolution."

"If anyone laughs, I'm killing you first."

"Fair." He stood and offered her a hand up. She took it, strong and calloused. "For what it's worth, big sis, I think that's the bravest poem I've ever heard."

She punched his arm, but gently, and her eyes were shiny in the forge light. "Shut up, little brother."

---

At five, the private resolutions had already begun changing.

Percy found Ella at the library sorting the cream cards Silena had set out at breakfast. The cards weren't being collected for public reading. Campers could choose to put a sealed note in the box for themselves, give one to a friend or keep it in a pocket. Ella was drawing a divider between the notes people wanted archived and the ones nobody was allowed to open.

"That's a lot of boxes," Percy said.

"Different kinds of promises need different homes," Ella replied.

"How do you decide who gets which?"

"Ella asks. If someone cannot tell Ella, the letter stays with the person who wrote it."

Percy nodded. He wasn't going to give her his Sally notebook just because it existed. The memorial garden and a book about his mother were different projects, even if they both dealt with grief.

Silena came in carrying a bundle of folded cloth. "Has anyone seen Clarisse?"

"Forge," Percy said.

"Still?"

"She was writing."

Silena set down the cloth. Her expression changed. She had helped Clarisse make decisions about public poetry before, and Percy recognized the hesitation.

"She told me she might read another piece tonight," Silena said. "I don't want to stand beside the fire waiting for her to do it. That's pressure."

"Then don't," Ella suggested.

"She asked me to listen if she reads it."

"You can listen without asking for a performance."

Silena considered, then smiled. "Fair."

They went outside together. Clarisse was leaving the forge with the notebook under one arm and a small rolled sheet in her hand. She stopped when she saw all three of them.

"What?"

"Nothing," Percy said.

"You're all standing in a suspicious formation."

Silena held out the folded cloth. "You left this in the cabin. It's your old practice wrap."

Clarisse took it. "Thanks."

"And I won't ask what you're reading tonight."

Clarisse looked at her with sudden relief. "Good. Because I haven't decided."

Percy nodded. He wanted to tell her he was proud, that she should read it no matter what. That was exactly the kind of well-meant push she'd been resisting since the arena, when her private poems had become everybody else's topic.

"You can come just to listen," he said.

"I know, idiot."

"That's what I'm planning."

Clarisse almost smiled. Then she looked at Ella.

"Did you get the words for the memorial board checked?"

"Not the final mounting," Ella answered. "Chiron approved the plan, but the brace repair and cart clearance still have to be inspected."

"Then it stays off the fence."

"Yes."

Percy was relieved to hear the older promise still had practical work attached to it. Annabeth had been careful about the placement since the first draft, and nobody needed the board installed in a rush just because the camp was feeling sentimental.

Clarisse rolled the loose sheet into the wrap. She didn't offer it for anyone to read. Silena asked whether she wanted company on the way to the amphitheater, and Clarisse said yes.

They walked together toward the open green. It was still light, and a few campers were carrying firewood to the supervised stack beside the stage. Chiron had asked for wet-weather covers because the wind was carrying rain. The fire would start only after the perimeter had been checked.

A small girl from the Demeter cabin came running toward Silena, clutching a resolution card with muddy fingerprints.

"I wrote that I want to learn the song," she said.

"Which song?"

"Yours."

Silena looked startled. "You can learn it if you want."

"Will you teach me?"

"Not during the bonfire. Tomorrow, when my voice is rested and we can talk about the words."

The girl nodded, satisfied by a specific answer. She tucked the card back into her pocket and went to find her counselor.

Clarisse watched her go. "You make that look easy."

"It's not always easy," Silena replied. "I wanted to say yes immediately. But then I would have spent the whole evening worrying about whether I remembered the plan."

"See? You're learning."

Percy smiled. The next generation of promises at camp was going to be less grand and more inconvenient. People would need to actually keep them.

At the amphitheater the seats were already filling. A few campers chose not to write a resolution at all. Chiron didn't make them explain. Some brought cards to the box marked *private, return to owner*. Others kept theirs. One older camper asked whether a resolution could be revised tomorrow.

"Of course," Ella said. "It's a plan, not a prophecy."

Percy sat beside Annabeth and watched Clarisse choose a place near Silena. The loose poem was still rolled in the wrap. Whether she read it or not would be a choice she made when the time came.

By seven the amphitheater glowed.

The year-rounders had built the bonfire high and careful, oak and pine stacked in a teepee around a core of birch bark, with stones in a wide ring and logs for benches pulled close. Chiron had hung lanterns. The Apollo kids had strung bronze wind chimes that sang whenever the wind moved. Mr. D had, with great martyrdom, provided marshmallows and declared he would stay for exactly twenty minutes, which everyone knew meant two hours.

Percy got there early with Grover to help light it. The fire caught fast, roaring up into the dark, throwing sparks toward a sky already thick with stars. The spring stars were clear and bright, familiar constellations wheeling overhead. Leo. Virgo. The Great Bear keeping watch.

Campers filtered in, bundled, breath fogging, cheeks red. Annabeth came with Silena, both carrying blankets. Thalia came with her traveling cloak still on, sitting pointedly between Percy and Annabeth like she had claimed them. Clarisse came last, her notebook shoved deep in her coat pocket, her face like she was marching to war. She sat by Silena and didn't take her hand, but leaned so their knees touched.

Ella fluttered in with her archive box, already writing: Bonfire, spring resolutions, attendance twenty-three plus two gods minus one god who says he's leaving early but won't. She sat near the fire so her ink would flow in the chill and beamed at Percy when he waved.

Chiron raised his hands. The chatter settled. The fire cracked. Somewhere out in the dark, the woods creaked with cold.

"Another year," Chiron said. His voice carried without shouting, warm as the fire. "We keep count differently here. School years. Summers. Quests. Winters that test us. This year tested us. We lost friends for a season. We found each other again. We held the barrier through storm and sickness. We learned new songs." He nodded at Ella, who glowed. "Tonight we do what demigods have done since before this camp had a name. We name what we will carry forward, and what we will lay down. Who will begin?"

Silence, fire-pop, wind.

Grover stood. His legs shook a little. Percy knew public speaking terrified him worse than monsters.

"I'm Grover Underwood," he said, "satyr, Lord of the Wild's searcher, Percy's best friend, Annabeth's tracking consultant, Juniper's boyfriend." Laughter, warm. "My resolution... the Council said no to a wide spring search. I get why. We're stretched. But I keep hearing... flickers. On the empathy net. Kids out there. Scared. Alone like I was once." He swallowed. "So my resolution is to find one lost demigod this spring. Just one. I'll use my leave. I'll go alone if I have to. But I'll bring one kid home."

Quiet. Then applause, fierce. Juniper, who had come as a dryad guest with leaves in her hair, wiped her eyes and mouthed I love you. Percy clapped until his hands stung.

Silena stood next. She twisted her hands in her coat. Clarisse, beside her, went very still.

"I'm Silena," she said softly, then louder. "Daughter of Poseidon. Sister to Clarisse and Percy, camp sister to... a lot of you." Laughter, fond. "You all know I hum. I hum when I'm nervous, when I'm happy, when I'm braiding. What you don't know... I used to sing. Really sing. Before... before I let a story about another life make me afraid to be heard." Her voice wobbled. Clarisse's hand found hers in the dark between them and held on tight. "My resolution is to sing openly this year. One song, all the way through, where people can hear. Not in the shower. Not under my breath." She took a shaky breath. "Maybe tonight, if you'll let me. After resolutions."

"Yes," half the camp said at once, and Silena laughed through tears and sat down, leaning hard into Clarisse, who put an arm around her like a shield.

Clarisse stood before she could talk herself out of it. Percy saw her hand go to her coat pocket and shake.

"Clarisse La Rue," she said. "Daughter of Poseidon. Big sister to some idiots." She glanced at Percy. He grinned. "I write... things. With line breaks. Don't laugh." Nobody laughed. The fire was very loud. "My resolution is to share poems. One tonight. More later. If you laugh, I'll kill you. But... here."

She pulled out the notebook with shaking hands, opened it, and read the winter patrol poem, voice low at first then stronger. Snow on the spear-rack, breath in the dark.

When she finished, there was a beat of stunned silence. Then Thalia whooped so loud owls scattered, and the whole amphitheater erupted. Clarisse stood there, red-faced, stunned, while Silena jumped up and hugged her so hard they nearly fell into the fire, and Percy thought he had never seen his big sister look so young and so free.

Thalia went next, still standing, sparks in her hair. "Thalia Grace, daughter of Zeus, camp scout, professional pain in the ass." Cheers. "My virtue this year is asking for help. Early. Before I'm frozen." She looked directly at Percy and Annabeth. "I practiced today. It sucked. I'll do it again." She sat down fast, but Annabeth caught her hand and held it, and Thalia didn't pull away.

Annabeth stood, blanket around her shoulders, notebook in hand but she didn't need it.

"Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus," she said. "Little sister, big sister, architect." She smiled at Thalia and Percy. "My resolution is to finish the shrine garden by summer solstice. Not plan it. Finish it. Thyme between the pavers. Owl mosaic done. Bench sealed. A place for quiet work. Saturdays, if anyone wants to help. No quest talk."

"Quest talk!" someone shouted from the Ares table, and everyone laughed.

"Percy will supervise thyme identification," Annabeth added solemnly, "because he thinks it all smells green."

"IT DOES SMELL GREEN," Percy protested, and the camp laughed harder.

Then all eyes turned to him. The fire warmed his face. The stars pressed close. Twenty-three campers, his siblings, his family, waiting.

Percy stood. His heart hammered worse than before a fight.

"Percy Jackson," he said. "Son of Poseidon. Little brother to too many scary girls." Laughter. "Amphitrite gave me a notebook last Christmas. For the stories only I can tell." He pulled it out of his coat, blue and worn already from being carried. "My resolution is to write my mom's story down. How she grew up. How she survived. How she raised me on blue food and stories in the life I remember, when we had nothing. She's not a goddess. She doesn't have a temple. But she's the reason I'm here. She's the reason I'm... me. So I'm going to write it. Even though spelling is hard and it's scary to get it wrong. Saturdays, with Annabeth planting next to me."

He sat down quickly, ears hot. Annabeth pressed her shoulder hard against his and whispered, "Perfect."

Chiron nodded slowly, eyes bright. "Well spoken, heroes. The gods witness. We witness. So be it." He lifted his tea. "To resolutions kept imperfectly and retried daily."

"To resolutions!" they echoed, with mugs and marshmallow sticks.

Ella wrote furiously: Resolutions, six, all brave, all witnessed.

Then Silena sang.

She stood, Clarisse's arm still around her waist for courage, and closed her eyes. For a second Percy thought she would back out. Then she opened her mouth and sang the old hearth hymn, the one the Apollo kids taught the year-rounders, about doors and hearths and going home by another way.

Her voice was clear and sweet and shaking only on the first note. By the second line it steadied, rose, filled the amphitheater and spilled up toward the stars. No accompaniment. No pipes. Just Silena, heard at last.

Percy felt Annabeth's breath catch beside him. He felt Thalia go still. Clarisse cried openly, not bothering to hide it, pride and love all over her fierce face. Grover closed his eyes and swayed. Chiron bowed his head.

When Silena finished, the silence held for three heartbeats. Then applause like summer thunder.

Silena bowed, laughing and crying, and mouthed Thank you to Clarisse, who mouthed back Told you.

---

By ten Percy had heard enough resolutions to be suspicious of the word *always*.

An Apollo camper had promised never to be late to morning practice again, then admitted he had an early infirmary shift twice a week and couldn't possibly do both. Chiron helped him revise it to arriving on time when he was scheduled, or telling the instructor in advance when his duties conflicted. A younger Hephaestus camper promised to rebuild every damaged shield before summer, then checked the repair list and discovered half the parts were still on order.

The night was full of moments like that. Some campers laughed, some groaned, and a few were relieved when they learned they could make a promise small enough to keep.

Grover stood near the edge of the fire with a folded route sheet in his hand. He had been quiet during most of the announcements. Percy joined him.

"You okay?"

"Trying to work out whether mine is brave or stupid."

"What's yours?"

"To help one Searcher who hasn't come home. Not by breaking an order. By asking for a route I can actually take."

Percy looked at the sheet. "Did Chiron approve?"

"Not yet. I'm showing him tomorrow."

"Then maybe that's the resolution. Make the request properly."

Grover gave him a rueful look. "It sounds less heroic when you put it that way."

"Last spring we would have run into the woods with a bag of tin cans and called it a strategy."

"We almost did."

"We were idiots."

"We were ambitious," Grover corrected.

Percy smiled. He remembered Grover's farewell by the lake, the schedule he'd made for reporting his location and the way Ella had agreed not to treat one late letter as proof he was lost. The resolution was a continuation of that conversation. Grover wanted to do something meaningful, but he wasn't using the word *Searcher* as permission to disappear without warning.

"Do you want me there when you ask?" Percy said.

"No. This one's mine. But can you help me read the route restrictions afterward?"

"Sure."

"Good."

Thalia joined them with a cup of tea. Her patrol parka was still damp from the afternoon. She listened while Grover described the proposed search and asked whether he had a rest and return plan.

He showed her the reverse side of the sheet. It included a list of safe houses and a note about contacting Chiron if weather closed a route. Thalia nodded.

"That's better than my first expedition plan," she said.

"What was your first plan?" Percy asked.

"Go quickly. Come back eventually. Tell nobody I needed help."

"Excellent plan."

"Nearly flawless."

She smiled, then looked at Grover again. "You can ask for somebody to review it. That isn't the same thing as asking them to take over."

He looked down at the paper. "I know."

"Knowing and doing are different," Thalia replied. "I spent all spring learning that."

Percy watched her face. He knew she'd been worrying about her Hunters' obligations, Jason and Beryl's invitation to meet as a family later in summer, and the promise to escort Zoe toward the marked lantern at a future new moon. Those obligations belonged to different people. The fact that she could care about all of them didn't mean she could be everywhere.

"Have you made your own resolution?" he asked.

"Ask for help before I get desperate enough to order somebody around."

"That sounds difficult."

"Terrifying."

She raised her cup in a mock toast. Grover touched his route sheet against it, and Percy raised an empty hand because he hadn't fetched a drink.

"To small promises," Percy said.

"To keeping them," Grover replied.

They returned to the fire. Percy noticed Clarisse sitting beside Silena with the rolled sheet still in her lap. She hadn't read it yet. Silena wasn't looking at it. They were watching the younger campers leave in supervised groups, their lanterns moving toward the cabins.

When a small boy stopped and asked whether he'd missed the important part of the night, Silena answered that tomorrow's breakfast was important too, and he didn't need to stay up past his bedtime to prove he belonged. The boy went with his counselor without argument.

Percy looked at his own card. *Write Sally's story.* It still seemed too large. He scratched out the sentence and wrote instead: *Saturday mornings, one page about a day I remember. Mark what I don't know.*

He showed the revised wording to Annabeth when she sat beside him. She read it and nodded.

"I think that's a better start."

"Less impressive."

"More likely to happen."

That was exactly the point. He folded the card and kept it for himself rather than dropping it into the archive box.

Midnight came slow and then all at once.

The bonfire burned low, coals pulsing red-gold. Marshmallows were gone. Mr. D had, as predicted, stayed two hours and fallen asleep in his blankets, snoring faintly while Chiron kept watch with fond exasperation. The younger campers had been escorted to their cabins by counselors before midnight. The older ones talked low, shoulders touching for warmth. Ella had finally fallen asleep on her archive box, pencil still in hand, and Silena had gently tucked a blanket around her without waking her.

Percy and Annabeth slipped away just before midnight, by unspoken agreement, to the overlook above the amphitheater where they could see both the fire below and the Sound beyond, black and silver under the moon. Their breath fogged together. The wind was softer here, smelling of pine and salt and woodsmoke.

Annabeth had brought two blankets. She wrapped one around both of them and they sat on the cold stone, close, watching the stars wheel toward a new month.

"Hey," Percy said quietly.

"Hey," Annabeth said.

They didn't need more for a minute. Below, someone laughed. A chime sang. The coals breathed.

"Can I tell you my wish?" Percy asked. "For midnight. Or is it supposed to be secret?"

"Ours can be together," Annabeth said. "That's allowed. New rule."

"Silena rule?"

"Annabeth rule." She turned to him, gray eyes silver in the moonlight, very serious and very soft. "Hold my hand?"

Percy took her hand. Bare skin, cold at first, then warm where they pressed palm to palm, fingers lacing the way they had a hundred times on creek crossings and wall climbs. Steady. Their shoulders touched. Their blankets overlapped. Somewhere below, Grover started counting down with the Apollo kids, distant and joyful. Ten. Nine.

"I wish..." Percy whispered, "I wish we get to finish what we started. Your garden. My book. All of it. I wish we stay... us. Even when things get big."

Eight. Seven. Six.

Annabeth squeezed his hand. "I wish the same. I wish for Saturdays. For woodland bells. For us to be brave like them." She nodded toward the fire, where their siblings glowed. "And I wish for your mom's story to be beautiful. Because it will be. Because you're writing it."

Five. Four. Three.

Percy turned his hand slightly so their knuckles brushed the stone, grounding. His heart was loud. It wasn't dramatic like ball drops and kisses in movies. It was quieter and bigger. A promise made with a hand-hold at midnight as May became June, witnessed by stars and spring flowers and family.

Two. One.

"Happy June," they whispered together, and below the camp erupted, horns and pipes and Thalia's wild whoop and Clarisse shouting and Silena singing again, and Mr. D waking with a snort and declaring Happy Whatever.

Percy and Annabeth stayed on the overlook through it, hands held tight, foreheads nearly touching, laughing softly while the sky flashed with the Apollo kids' celebratory fireworks, green and gold and sea-blue.

"Think it'll come true?" Percy asked. "If we wish it together?"

Annabeth leaned her head against his shoulder, their joined hands resting on her knee, and watched the sparks fall.

"I think we already started," she said.

They sat there until the fireworks faded and the cold drove them back down to the fire, where Thalia immediately claimed Annabeth's other hand and Clarisse threw a blanket over Percy's shoulders and Silena pressed hot cider into both their hands and Grover said Happy June, guys, you missed the countdown, and Ella woke long enough to write Midnight, wish made, hand-hold observed, outcome pending but hopeful before falling back asleep.

Percy looked around at them, fire-lit and sleepy and brave, and thought about resolutions, about writing and planting and singing and asking, about finding lost kids and sharing poems. He remembered Sally asleep in her apartment with blue popcorn still on the coffee table, in the life where she had lived to see him grow. Here he could write that life down, even while grieving the mother he had lost. He thought about woodland bells pushing up through cool earth because they didn't wait for permission.

He held Annabeth's hand a little longer before letting go to carry Ella's box for her, and Annabeth smiled at him like midnight was still happening.

---

Morning came pale and gold and impossibly clear, the first morning of June.

Percy woke early, before even Chiron's rooster, and pulled on Annabeth's sweater and his coat. The blue notebook was under the shell where he left it. He tucked it into his coat pocket, along with two pencils, and went out into air so cold it rang.

Camp was silent. Smoke rose thin from cabins. Dew glittered on every rail. His boots left dark prints in the wet grass.

Annabeth was already at the shrine garden, as he knew she would be. She had a thermos and two cups and her sketchbook, her breath fogging as she knelt by the woodland bells.

There were more now. Overnight, impossibly, a whole drift of them had opened along the south edge, white bells nodding over green, dusted with dew, brave and new and perfect.

"Happy June," Percy said, sitting beside her.

"Happy June." She poured him tea. "Look."

He looked. Then he opened his notebook to the first heavy page, uncapped his pencil with cold fingers, and wrote, in his uneven dyslexic print that leaned left:

My mom's name is Sally Jackson and this is her story.

Annabeth planted thyme beside him, humming Silena's song softly under her breath, her shoulder against his. The sun rose. The woodland bells lifted. The garden waited to be finished. The book waited to be written.

Side by side, they began.
