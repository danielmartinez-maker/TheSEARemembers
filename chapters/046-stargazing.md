## Chapter 46: Stargazing

Percy knew it was going to be a clear night because the sunset had been the wrong color.

It went down pink over the Sound, not orange. By dinner the sky was dark blue and the first stars were out over the woods. Tyson pointed them out with a sausage and said they looked like barnacles.

Percy had been thinking about the hill all afternoon.

It was not an idea that had come to him all at once. Two nights before, Triton had left a conch shell message in the fountain outside Cabin Three, full of static and a whale singing in the background. Most of it had been family news.

At the end, Triton had said, tell your little owl friend the sky is clear where you are. Tell her to look for the Diver. She will like that one.

Percy had not known what that meant. He had asked Silena. Silena, who was fourteen and knew everything about everything that had to do with stories, had looked up from braiding blue thread into her shoelace and said, oh. Atlantean stars. Dad's side. Different names.

And Percy had thought of Annabeth.

Annabeth Grace knew every Greek constellation. Of course she did. She was twelve and a daughter of Zeus and she could name them all in order and tell you which king had been vain and which god had put which picture up there as an apology. Percy liked listening to her do it.

He wanted, just once, to be the one who knew the names first.

So after dinner he went back to Cabin Three and pulled the spare blankets off the top of the closet.

Cabin Three smelled like salt and clean laundry in the evening. Clarisse's bunk was made tight. Silena's bunk had three pillows and a book face down on them. His own bunk was a mess.

Percy stacked the blankets. There were four wool ones, blue and green, frayed at the edges but warm.

Silena came in while he was folding the second one and stopped in the doorway with her hands on her hips.

"What are you stealing?" she asked.

"I am not stealing," Percy said. "I live here. I am borrowing from us."

"Borrowing for what?"

Percy felt his ears go hot. "I am going to take Annabeth up to the hill. Past the cabins, where the grass is flat. For stars."

Silena's face changed. Not teasing, not right away. Something softer. She crossed the room and took the blanket out of his hands and refolded it properly, corners lined up.

"Atlantean ones?" she asked quietly.

"Yeah. Triton mentioned the Diver. I thought I could show her. The ones Dad's side uses."

Silena nodded. She smoothed the fold with her palm. When she looked up, her eyes were bright.

"Good," she said. "That is good, Perce. She will like that. She likes knowing how things are built, and our stars are built different."

Percy swallowed. "I do not remember all of them."

"Then I will remind you." Silena sat down on his bunk and patted the spot beside her. "Sit. Tell me what you remember and I will fill in the rest. Mom told us some. Dad told us some when he visited in the spring. You were half asleep for half of it, so I will be generous."

Percy sat. The mattress dipped. Outside, someone laughed by the volleyball court and a conch blew for free time ending.

"I remember the Net Mender," he said. "And the Whale Road."

"Good. What else?"

"The Lighthouse That Sank?"

"That is a sad one," Silena said. "Maybe save that for middle, not first. Start with the Diver. Start kind."

She told him the rest while he folded.

Clarisse came in halfway through with her spear over her shoulder. She looked at the pile of blankets and said, "What is this?"

"Percy is taking Annabeth stargazing," Silena said.

Clarisse snorted. "Gross."

"It is not gross," Percy said. "It is stars."

"It is blankets," Clarisse said, pointing with the butt of her spear. "Plural. Gross."

But she did not leave. She leaned her spear against the wall and sat down. Twice she corrected a detail about the Diver and the Whale Road.

Silena raised an eyebrow. "You remember Dad's stories?"

"I remember everything," Clarisse said, and looked away fast, ears pink. "Somebody has to remember right or you two will make things up."

Percy grinned at her. Clarisse scowled back, but it was the soft kind of scowl she only used in the cabin.

When Silena was done, Percy had four stories straight in his head and two more crooked but close enough. Silena stood and pulled the top blanket off the pile and shook it out and folded it again tighter so it would be easier to carry.

"Take the green ones," she said. "They are thickest. Ground gets cold after ten."

"Are you coming?" Percy asked.

Silena smiled. "Not at first. You should get to start it yourself. I will bring hot chocolate later. The real kind, from the kitchen, if I can talk the harpies into it."

"Silena makes everything better," Percy said, honest and simple, and Silena reached over and rubbed his head hard enough to mess up his hair.

"Go brush your teeth," she said. "And take a jacket for her. She forgets hers."


---

Percy made it as far as the cabin door before turning around.

Silena was putting away the spare blankets. Clarisse had taken up a whetstone and was working on the edge of her spear with the steady, irritating patience she only seemed to possess around weapons.

"Where's the old star chart?" Percy asked.

Silena glanced up. "Which old star chart?"

"The one Dad sent when we were little. The one with the blue edges."

Clarisse stopped sharpening. "Why?"

"Because I want to check the Diver against something that isn't you two disagreeing about where his rope goes."

Silena laughed. "We don't disagree about the rope. We disagree about the person holding it."

"That's a pretty big difference," Percy said.

"It is to the person underwater," Clarisse answered.

The chart turned out to be in the chest beneath the porthole, wrapped in a length of faded linen. Its paper had softened at the folds. Along the edges somebody had drawn little fish with open mouths, each beside a direction or a number. Percy recognized the style from the things Silena kept in her sketchbook, but he couldn't identify whose hand had actually made them.

He unfolded it on the floor. It was much less straightforward than he remembered hearing the stories. The names circled clusters of stars, but lines ran from one cluster to another in ways Greek star charts didn't. Some of the labels were written sideways, following the arc of a current. Near one corner a smear of blue ink obscured the last word of a title.

"That's the part that was in your room in Atlantis," Silena said, pointing.

Percy waited for an image to surface. A lamp, perhaps, or the shape of a wall. He could imagine a room under the sea very easily. He'd visited Atlantis since coming to camp, and he knew what its buildings looked like. Imagining was not the same as remembering.

"I don't actually know that," he said.

Clarisse looked down at him. She had learned, over these months, that there were places in their childhoods Percy couldn't meet her. Sometimes she became impatient about it. Sometimes she became careful. He couldn't predict which version would appear.

"I know," she said. "I'm telling you."

He smoothed the chart and tried to find the Diver. The figure lay between several familiar Greek patterns, but when Percy turned the paper toward the window, the labels seemed to run backward.

"How are you supposed to read this?"

"At sea," Clarisse said. "You don't start from the bottom like a classroom diagram. You start with where you are."

"I am in our cabin."

"Then you're going to need an unusually large boat."

Silena knelt beside them, pulled the chart toward the lamp, and showed Percy the little ring of marks around the border. The marks were bearings, not decorations. She rotated the page until one arrow pointed toward the Sound.

"Look at the Whale Road now."

The seven stars ran diagonally across the page, the same way Silena had described the route in her song. Percy traced them with a finger.

"Oh."

"Exactly." Silena pushed a pencil into his hand. "Take notes. Not on that chart."

Percy found the back of an old inventory sheet. He drew the seven points and labeled the rests in the song. Then he attempted the Diver. Clarisse corrected his first two lines and let the third mistake stand long enough for him to discover it himself.

"That's not a rope," he said. "I've drawn a second arm."

"Finally," she said.

He redrew the line. For a moment the scratch of the pencil and the lap of water under the cabin floor were the only sounds.

Percy had been looking forward to impressing Annabeth. That wasn't the same as being able to tell a story accurately. She would know the Greek stars, and if he made something up without meaning to, she would trust him until the sky itself proved him wrong.

"Would it be stupid to take this with me?" he asked, holding up the inventory sheet.

Clarisse shrugged. "Stupider to pretend you know something you don't."

Silena gave him a different sheet, one without a list of broken training shields on the back. "You can take your notes. The chart stays here."

"Because it's old?"

"Because it's ours," Silena said. Her tone was matter-of-fact, not possessive. "And if you take it onto the hill, the dew will eat it."

Percy folded his new sketch, slipped it into his jacket pocket and put the original away. The chest closed with a soft, familiar thud. He could imagine another version of himself sitting over that chart years ago with his sisters. But that version wasn't a scene he could call back whenever he wanted. It existed in the stories his family chose to tell him, and in the marks other hands had left on paper.

At the door, Clarisse called after him.

"Don't put the Lighthouse next to the Shield Rack. They're nowhere near each other."

"That's what the sketch is for," Percy said.

"Then look at it."

Silena followed him out far enough to straighten the collar of his jacket. "You don't have to get every word right. Tell her where you learned it. That's part of the story too."

Percy tucked the sketch deeper into his pocket. For the first time all evening, he wasn't worried about whether Annabeth would be impressed.

He was worried about dropping the paper.

---

The hill past the cabins was a wide rise, flat on top, with a view of the Sound. At night it was empty and quiet.

Percy got there first and laid out the blankets in layers, two on the bottom, one folded for pillows, one on top. It looked lumpy. He decided lumpy was fine.

The sky was opening up above him.

It was the kind of clear night with no city lights. Stars pressing through, then clusters. The Milky Way overhead. The moon a thin curl. Crickets in the grass.

Percy sat on the edge of the blankets and waited, and his stomach felt tight in a good way, like before a swim race.

Annabeth came up the path with her jacket over her arm and her braid coming loose. She stopped when she saw the blankets.

"You brought four blankets," she said.

"Silena said ground gets cold," Percy said. "And one is for a pillow. So really it is three blankets and a pillow. That is normal."

Annabeth laughed. She sat down beside him, knees pulled up.

"Clear night," she said.

"The best kind," Percy said. "I had a thing I wanted to show you."

Annabeth turned to look at him. The starlight caught the edge of her braid. "What thing?"

"Atlantean constellations," Percy said. "Same stars, different pictures. Different stories. I thought you might like them."

Annabeth was quiet for a second. Then she pulled the top blanket over both their legs and leaned back on her elbows.

"Tell me," she said.

Percy lay back too. "Okay. See the bright one with the two dim ones under it? Greeks call that part of Lyra?"

"Yes," Annabeth said. "Vega is the bright one. The parallelogram under it is the body of the lyre."

"Atlanteans do not see a harp there," Percy said. He pointed. "They see the Diver. The bright star is his lamp. The dim ones are his rope."

Annabeth followed his finger. "What did he lose?"

"A pearl," Percy said. "Not for money. For his sister. She was sick and only a deep pearl would help. So he tied a rope around his waist and went down past where light goes. His lamp never goes out because his sister holds the other end up in the boat. The story is not about the pearl. It is about someone holding the rope."

Annabeth was silent. "That is not how the Greek story goes. Orpheus is about losing someone because you looked back."

"I know," Percy said. "This one is about not letting go."

Annabeth nodded slowly. She reached up and traced the shape in the air, fingertip moving from Vega down to the dim stars.

"The Diver," she repeated. "I can see it. If you turn the harp upside down, it does look like someone diving. Head first."

Percy felt warm. "Right? Once you see it you cannot unsee it."

"Tell me another," Annabeth said, shifting closer. "Please."

Percy pointed to the east, where a long curve of stars rose over the trees.

"That one Greeks call Scorpius, part of it," he said. "The tail, kind of. Atlanteans call it the Net Mender."

He told it the way Silena had told it.

The Net Mender was an old woman who lived on a rock after her village sank. Every night she mended nets for drowned fishermen. When she finished a net, she laid it across the sky to dry, and that was the curve of stars. If you were lost, you followed where the net pointed, because she only hung them to point toward shore.

"She sounds lonely," Annabeth said softly.

"She was," Percy said. "But she liked the work. Dad said Atlanteans like stories where the work saves you. Not the fighting. The mending."

Annabeth turned her head to look at him. Her eyes were serious. "I like that. At camp everyone talks about fighting. No one talks about mending."

"Silena says mending counts," Percy said. "She mends all our socks."

Annabeth laughed again, quieter this time. Above them a shooting star cut across and vanished. Percy did not wish on it. He just watched it go and felt Annabeth's shoulder press a fraction closer to his.

They found the Whale Road next, seven stars in a long line. Atlanteans saw it whole as the path whales took carrying messages between palaces.

"That is practical," Annabeth said. "Like a map you cannot lose."

"Exactly," Percy said. "Silena says sailors used to sing the distances. Like, short jump, long jump, rest. They made it a song so kids could remember."

"Sing it for me?" Annabeth asked.

Percy sang it, low and off key. Short hop, long pull, rest and hold. Annabeth joined in, and the crickets kept time.

---

The hot chocolate arrived in a thermos with three mugs tied together by a strip of cloth.

Silena came up the path with her hair loose, carrying the thermos. "Kitchen harpy owed me a favor. Real cocoa, pinch of salt."

Annabeth sat up, smiling wide. "Silena!"

" scoot," Silena said, nudging Percy's foot with her toe. "Make room, little brother. I am only staying a minute. I am a delivery service, not a third wheel."

Percy scooted. Silena poured into three mismatched mugs. She handed the strawberry one to Annabeth.

"How are the stars?" Silena asked.

"Different," Annabeth said. She wrapped both hands around the mug. "Percy showed me the Diver and the Net Mender and the Whale Road. They are better than the Greek ones."

"Do not let Chiron hear you say that," Silena said, grinning. "He will assign extra myths as punishment."

"I will risk it," Annabeth said, and sipped. Her eyes closed. "Oh. This is good."

"Poseidon cabin secret is the salt," Silena said proudly. "Mom taught us. Clarisse pretends she does not care but she always drinks two cups."

Percy held his mug and let it warm his hands. The chocolate was hot and sweet with just enough bitter underneath. It tasted like winter at camp, like sitting in Cabin Three while rain hit the roof.

"Tell her the Shell Bearer," Silena prompted him. "You tell it better than I do."

Percy looked at Annabeth. "You want another?"

"Yes," Annabeth said immediately.

So Percy pointed up again, to a tight cluster that Greeks called part of Delphinus, the dolphin.

"Atlanteans see a kid there," he said. "The Shell Bearer. After a battle broke the palace, this kid, maybe eight, started carrying shells one by one to build a little shrine on the beach. Grown ups said it was useless. She said, then it will take forever, but I will have somewhere to put flowers. The queen saw and picked up a shell too. Then everyone did."

Annabeth was quiet. Silena reached over and tucked the blanket tighter around Annabeth's shoulders without making a big deal of it.

"That is my favorite," Silena said softly. "Mom told me that one in a letter my first summer. I kept it under my pillow."

"You were homesick?" Annabeth asked. She sounded surprised. Silena always seemed so sure.

"Terrible," Silena said. "First two weeks I cried into my pillow every night. Clarisse pretended not to hear and then left dried apricots on my bunk every morning. Percy was not here yet. It was just us two. That story helped. One shell at a time."

Percy stared at his sister. He had not known that. Silena caught his look and winked.

"You were six when I told you the short version," she said. "You fell asleep in the middle and drooled on my arm."

"I did not," Percy said, but he was smiling.

They drank their chocolate. An owl called from the woods.

Silena stood. "I will leave the thermos. Clarisse is on patrol. The route that goes right past this hill."

"She is on patrol?" Annabeth asked.

Silena's smile turned sly. "She took extra patrol. The route that goes right past this hill. Four times. Very thorough. Very dedicated to safety."

Percy snorted chocolate up his nose. Silena patted his back while he coughed and laughed.

"Be good, babies," Silena said. She bent and kissed the top of Percy's head and then, after a half second's thought, kissed the top of Annabeth's head too, light and sisterly. "Yell if you need another blanket. Or if Orion gets fresh."

She walked back down the path, humming the Whale Road song under her breath, short hop, long pull.

Annabeth watched her go. "Your sisters are nice."

"They are the best," Percy said simply. "Even Clarisse. Especially Clarisse, but do not tell her I said especially."

"I will not," Annabeth said, and her smile in the dark was soft at the edges.

Percy poured them both a little more chocolate and they lay back down, shoulders touching now without either of them moving away.

---

Clarisse was a terrible spy.

Percy heard her coming a full minute before she arrived, spear butt thumping the path in a steady rhythm, trying to sound casual and failing. She wore her camp jacket and her hair was still in its knot from drills. She had a flashlight but she did not turn it on.

"Patrol," she announced loudly to no one, stopping at the edge of the blankets. "Official patrol. Checking for monsters. And curfew violations. And, uh, fog."

Annabeth sat up. "Hi, Clarisse."

"Hi," Clarisse said stiffly. She looked at the thermos. She looked at the blankets. She looked at Percy lying there with chocolate on his chin. "You have blankets."

"We have blankets," Percy agreed. "Silena left chocolate. Want some?"

Clarisse hesitated. Patrol was supposed to keep moving. But the thermos steamed invitingly and the night was cold and her little brother was looking at her with chocolate on his face.

"I am on duty," she said. "I cannot sit."

"You can stand and drink," Annabeth said. She poured the green mug full and held it up.

Clarisse took it. She stood at the edge of the blankets like a guard and drank. After two sips her shoulders dropped.

"Good," she muttered. "Silena always makes it right."

"She said you drink two cups," Annabeth said.

"I drink one," Clarisse said automatically, then glanced at Percy, who raised his eyebrows. "I drink one and a half on cold nights. This is a cold night."

Percy patted the corner of the blanket. "You can sit for the Shell Bearer story. I already told it but I can tell it again. Short version."

"I know the Shell Bearer," Clarisse said. "I do not need to sit."

But she did not leave. She stood there and drank and looked up at the sky like she could not help it. The stars reflected in her eyes.

Percy, who knew his sister, said casually, "We did the Diver first. I remembered the rope in the mouth part."

Clarisse nodded once, satisfied. "Good. People always get that wrong."

Annabeth looked between them. "Clarisse, which one is your favorite? The Atlantean ones."

Clarisse went still. For a moment Percy thought she would deflect with something about patrol. Instead she tipped her mug back, finished it, and pointed with the empty mug toward the south, where a square of four bright stars stood with a dimmer one in the center.

"That one," she said. "Greeks call it part of Pegasus or something. I do not care. We call it the Shield Rack."

Percy blinked. He had forgotten that one.

"Tell it," Annabeth said.

Clarisse shifted her weight. She was thirteen and not used to telling stories in front of people. Her voice came out rough at first.

"There was a guard," she said. "Not a hero. Just a guard at a door in the palace. When the city fell, everyone ran. She stayed at her post because someone had to hold the door while the kids got out through the back. She held it a long time. Too long. The sea took the palace. When they found her spear later, it was still upright against the doorframe. So they put her rack up there. Four posts and the spear in the middle. It is not shiny like Orion. It just stays."

She lowered her arm.

"Silena likes the mending ones," Clarisse said, almost embarrassed. "I like the staying ones."

Annabeth was looking up at the four stars. Percy could see her finding the shape, four posts, spear in the middle.

"She stayed so others could go," Annabeth said quietly. "That is a good story."

"It is true," Clarisse said fiercely, then caught herself. "Or, you know. True enough. Dad said the guard was real. Long ago."

Percy felt a tightness in his throat. He had heard his dad tell that one once, late, voice low through the fountain water. Poseidon had sounded proud and sad at the same time.

Clarisse cleared her throat and poured herself the second half cup, proving Silena right. "Anyway. Patrol continues. No monsters. Only you two being gross with blankets."

"We are not being gross," Percy said. "We are being astronomical."

"Same thing," Clarisse said, but she ruffled his hair as she passed, hard enough to mean affection. She nodded to Annabeth, almost shy. "You cold? You want my jacket? You look cold."

"I am okay," Annabeth said. "Percy gave me the thick blanket. And Silena tucked it."

"Good," Clarisse said. "Good. Stay warm. Yell if a harpy gets grabby. Or if he gets gross."

"I will," Annabeth promised solemnly.

Clarisse walked off, spear over her shoulder, trying to resume her patrol stomp. Percy and Annabeth listened. She stopped just beyond the lamplight, where the path bent. They could hear her stop. They could feel her listening.

Percy raised his voice just enough to carry. "Next is the Lighthouse That Sank! It is sad in the middle but good at the end!"

There was a pause. Then the sound of someone settling down on grass just out of sight, trying to be quiet about it.

Annabeth covered her mouth to keep from laughing. Percy grinned up at the sky.

He told the Lighthouse That Sank loud enough for his sister to hear.

It was a tower keeper who kept his light burning during a storm even when the waves took the stairs out from under him, level by level. He kept climbing up as the water climbed, keeping the lamp lit, so the last ship could find the harbor. The ship made it. He did not. Atlanteans put his lamp up there, a single blue white star that flickered, and they said if you were out late and scared, you should look for it and remember someone stayed lit for you.

When he finished, it was very quiet on the hill. Then, from beyond the bend, there was a small sniff.

Clarisse called out, gruff, "You told that one right, Seaweed Brain."

Percy smiled so wide his face hurt. Annabeth reached over under the blanket and found his hand and squeezed it once, quick and warm.

---

Thalia arrived late, the way Thalia always arrived, like weather.

They heard her boots first, then saw her silhouette against the cabin lights, sixteen, taller than most campers her age, with choppy black hair and a jacket full of pins. Thalia Grace, daughter of Zeus, Annabeth's older sister. She moved like static felt, all sharp edges and tired eyes. She stopped at the bottom of the hill and looked up at the blankets and the thermos and the two kids lying side by side staring up.

"Am I interrupting?" she asked. Her voice was rough, joking to cover something else.

Annabeth sat up fast. "Thalia! You came."

"Silena flagged me down by the Big House," Thalia said. She climbed up and dropped down on the blankets without asking, long legs stretched out. "Said there was illicit astronomy happening. And chocolate. I am here for chocolate."

Percy handed her the chipped blue mug without a word and poured the last of the thermos. Thalia drank and sighed through her nose.

"Good," she said. "Real cocoa. Silena knows what is up."

They lay back, three in a row now. Thalia smelled like pine and cold air and the faint ozone that always clung to Zeus kids after they trained. Percy could feel Annabeth relax beside him in a different way now that her sister was there, like a plant leaning toward sun.

"Your brother told good ones?" Thalia asked Annabeth, jerking her chin at Percy.

"He told great ones," Annabeth said. "The Diver. The Net Mender. The Shell Bearer. The Shield Rack and the Lighthouse."

Thalia whistled low. "All the heavy hitters. He save any for me?"

Percy felt suddenly shy. Thalia was older in the way Clarisse was older, but different. Clarisse was steady tide. Thalia was storm. He wanted her to like the stories too.

"I have one more," he said. "But it is short. The Kettle."

"Tell it," Thalia said. "I like short."

So Percy pointed to a small triangle near the Whale Road and told about the old cook who fed an entire fleeing fleet from one kettle that never emptied because she kept adding water and salt and whatever people brought, a fish here, seaweed there, and refused to let anyone go hungry while there was still fire. The triangle was the kettle's legs, holding steady over the flame.

Thalia laughed, real and surprised. "That is a Grace story too," she said. "Different pot, same idea. Beryl made soup like that once when the power was out. Threw everything in."

The name landed soft and strange on the hill. Beryl. Their mom. Beryl Grace, mortal, famous and flawed, still far away despite the letters she sent.

Annabeth went very still beside Percy. Percy felt her hand, still loosely holding his under the blanket, tighten.

Thalia stared up. Her voice went quieter.

"Mom taught me one star thing," she said. "Just one. I do not know if it is Greek or Grace or something she made up when she was, you know. High on paint fumes and being Beryl."

Annabeth turned her head. "She did?"

"Yeah." Thalia kept looking up. "I was maybe seven. Before camp. We were on the roof of this apartment in LA because the lights went out and it was too hot inside. She pointed up and said, see that crooked line? Everyone thinks it is part of Cassiopeia, the queen on her chair. But we know better. That is the Girl Who Ran."

Percy listened hard. He did not move, so he would not break it.

"She said there was a girl who was supposed to sit still and be pretty on a throne," Thalia continued. "Like Cassiopeia. Smile for painters. But the girl got bored of sitting. So one night she stood up out of the chair and walked off the edge of the picture. The chair is still there, empty. The line of stars trailing off is her footprints walking away. Mom said, that is us, Thals. We do not sit pretty. We walk off."

Annabeth's breath caught. Percy felt it more than heard it.

"She told you that?" Annabeth whispered.

"She was sober that night," Thalia said. "Mostly. She braided my hair and we counted footprints until I fell asleep on the tar paper. I do not know if she ever told you. You were little."

Annabeth shook her head against the blanket. "I don't remember her telling me. The letters I have talk about movie stars, not sky stars."

Thalia was quiet for a moment. Then she reached across Annabeth and found her sister's other hand, the one not holding Percy's, and held it tight.

"Then I am telling you now," Thalia said. "So you have it too. Look. There. See the W? That last bit that drops down? That is not part of the chair. That is her leaving. Girl Who Ran."

Annabeth looked. Percy looked. The W of Cassiopeia was bright and clear. He had always seen a throne. Now, with Thalia's words, he could see the break, the line stepping off.

"I see her," Annabeth said, voice thick. "She is running fast."

"Fastest," Thalia agreed. Her voice was rough too, but steady. "Mom said if anyone tries to put you on a pedestal, you kick it over and run. I think she meant it as parenting advice. Beryl style."

Annabeth laughed wetly. Percy pretended to be very interested in the Kettle triangle so the sisters could have a second without him staring.

After a bit, Thalia said, lighter, "Do not tell Chiron I am teaching unapproved mythology. He will have my hide."

"I will protect you," Annabeth said. "I will build you a hideout. With blueprints."

"My sister the architect," Thalia said fondly. She squeezed Annabeth's hand and let go, sitting up. "Speaking of architect. Seaweed Brain. You were supposed to show her the architect one. Silena told me you were saving it. Do not chicken out now."

Percy went hot from ears to toes. "I was getting to it."

"Sure you were," Thalia said, grinning. She stood and stretched. "I am going to go relieve Clarisse of patrol so she can stop pretending she is not eavesdropping from the bushes. You two finish your illicit astronomy. Keep the blankets. I will make sure Silena does not worry."

She bent and kissed Annabeth's forehead, quick and fierce, and then, to Percy's shock, ruffled his hair hard.

"Good stories, Percy Jackson," she said quietly. "Thanks for giving my sister the sky."

Then she walked off down the path, boots crunching, calling out, "La Rue! I can see your spear! You suck at stealth!"

From the dark came Clarisse's outraged whisper, "I am patrolling!"

"Sure! Patrol with me! Let's go!"

Their voices faded. The hill went quiet again, just crickets and wind and the faint wash of the Sound.

Percy and Annabeth were alone.

---

Percy stared up so long his eyes watered.

"Okay," he said at last. "The architect one. It is not called that, exactly. Atlanteans call it She Who Draws Lines. Or He Who Draws, depending who tells it. Silena says it is both. The drawer. The planner."

Annabeth rolled onto her side to look at him, then up again. "Where?"

Percy lifted his arm. His hand was steady now.

"See up there, above the Diver? That bright white one by itself? Greeks call it Deneb, part of Cygnus, the swan's tail."

"I know Deneb," Annabeth whispered. "It is one of the Summer Triangle."

"Atlanteans see it different," Percy said. "They see a girl on a cliff with a stick, drawing in sand. One star is her eye, bright because she sees clear. The line trailing down is her drawing. Story says the sea was going to flood the city and everyone was running around yelling. She sat down and drew how the water would move, where it would go, and where to build walls to turn it. She was not strongest or fastest. She just saw how things fit. They built what she drew and the city held. So they put her up there with her stick, so anyone building anything can look up and ask her for straight lines."

He lowered his arm. His heart was thumping loud enough he was sure Annabeth could hear it.

"I thought of you," he said simply. "Because you always see how things fit. You draw plans for everything. Even lunch seating. You had a plan for capture the flag with arrows and everything."

Annabeth did not answer right away. Percy risked a glance.

She was looking up at Deneb, at She Who Draws Lines, with tears shining in her eyes but not falling, just starlight caught. Her mouth was soft.

"Percy," she said.

"Yeah?"

"No one ever gave me a star before."

Percy swallowed. "It was already yours. I just knew the name."

Annabeth turned then and looked at him fully. She reached up and, very carefully, traced the line from Deneb down, as if she were drawing too.

"She Who Draws Lines," she repeated. "I like that better than swan's tail. No offense to swans."

"None taken," Percy said. "Swans are mean. They bite."

Annabeth laughed, and the tears went away into laugh lines. She lay back down, closer this time, so their temples almost touched. Under the blanket, her hand found his again, fingers lacing properly, not just a squeeze.

They lay like that and looked at her star.

Percy told her the rest of the small details Silena had given him, because talking was easier than being quiet with his heart so loud. How the drawer used charcoal for big lines and shell edge for fine ones. How Atlantean builders still chalked her sign on cornerstones. How Triton said navigators saluted her when they passed because a straight course was a kind of drawing too.

Annabeth asked questions between, practical and wonderful. How wide were the walls? How did she calculate water pressure without modern tools? Did she use sand tables? Percy answered what he could and made up nothing, saying when he did not know, and Annabeth said that was good, that was right, never build on a guess.

The moon thinned. The Milky Way wheeled slow. The chocolate cooled in their mugs and they drank it cool and did not mind.

After a long while, Annabeth said, very quietly, "I don't remember my mom giving me stars. Thalia has the Girl Who Ran. I have her stories about auditions Mom missed, and phone calls. I am glad Thalia told me tonight. But I am gladder you told me yours first. So I had somewhere to stand while I heard hers."

Percy did not know what to say to that. He was twelve. He did not have words for mothers who missed things. He just turned his hand over and held her hand tighter, palm to palm, and said, "You have somewhere to stand. You have the hill. You have blankets. You have me."

Annabeth nodded against the wool. "I know."

They watched the sky until the crickets slowed and the air turned sharp. Percy knew they should go in soon. Curfew had probably passed. But Clarisse was on patrol and Thalia was with her and Silena had sent them, so they had a net of sisters holding the night for them, and he wanted one more minute under the drawer star.

Finally Annabeth shivered. Percy sat up and pulled the top blanket around her shoulders like Silena had done, clumsy but earnest.

"We should walk back," he said. "Before Chiron sends harpies."

Annabeth stood and helped him shake out the blankets and fold them into a pile. They left the thermos and mugs for Silena to collect. Percy slung two blankets over his shoulder. Annabeth took the other two. Their free hands found each other again on the path without either of them deciding it, just natural as tide.

The path down was dark but familiar. Their shoes scuffed. Above the cabins, the windows glowed warm. Someone laughed inside the Hermes cabin. The strawberry fields smelled sweet and green.

At the fork where the path split to Cabin Three and Cabin One, they stopped under the lamppost. Moths bumped the light. The stars were still out, but dimmer here with the lamps.

Annabeth shifted the blankets. She looked up at Percy, gray eyes clear and tired and happy.

"Thank you for the constellations," she said. "Especially mine."

"You are welcome," Percy said. His face felt hot again. "Thank you for listening. And for singing off key with me."

"I was on key," Annabeth said. "You were off key."

"I was on Atlantean key," Percy said. "It is different."

Annabeth laughed. Then she stepped forward and kissed his cheek, quick and warm and dry, her braid brushing his shoulder. It was quick and familiar, an affectionate goodnight to the boy she loved, and it felt big and calm.

"Goodnight, Percy," she said.

Percy stood very still. His cheek tingled where she had kissed it.

"Goodnight, Annabeth," he said.

She walked backward a few steps toward Cabin One, still holding his gaze, smiling. Then she turned and ran, blankets flapping like a cape, braid bouncing. Thalia was waiting at the Cabin One door, arms crossed, and she caught Annabeth up in a one armed hug and waved at Percy over her sister's head.

Percy waved back. He turned toward Cabin Three.

Clarisse was waiting at the Cabin Three door with her arms crossed exactly like Thalia, spear leaning beside her. Silena stood beside her in pajamas and jacket, thermos back in her hands, smiling soft.

"How was it?" Silena asked.

Percy walked up, blankets piled high. His cheek still felt warm. His hand still felt warm where Annabeth had held it.

"Good," he said. "Really good. I remembered the rope in the mouth. I remembered seven for the Whale Road. I told the Lighthouse loud enough."

Clarisse nodded, satisfied. "I heard. You did okay, little brother."

Silena took the blankets from him and wrapped one around his shoulders. "Did you show her She Who Draws Lines?"

Percy nodded, ears red.

"Good," Silena said gently. She kissed his forehead. "That one was always yours to give."

They went inside. The cabin was warm and dark and smelled like salt and chocolate. Percy climbed into his bunk in his clothes, too tired and happy to change. Clarisse kicked off her shoes and flopped into her bunk with a sigh. Silena blew out the lamp.

In the dark, Percy stared at the ceiling where seashells caught faint starlight through the porthole. He could still see the sky when he closed his eyes. The Diver going down with his lamp lit because someone held the rope. The Net Mender hanging her work to point to shore. The Shell Bearer carrying one shell at a time. The Shield Rack standing. The Lighthouse burning. The Kettle feeding everyone. The Girl Who Ran leaving footprints off the chair. And above them all, bright and white, She Who Draws Lines, seeing how things fit.

He fell asleep with his hand curled like it was still holding someone's, and the sea moved under the floorboards, steady as sisters breathing in the dark.
