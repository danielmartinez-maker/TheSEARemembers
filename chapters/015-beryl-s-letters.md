## Chapter 15: Beryl's Letters

Mail came to Camp Half-Blood on Tuesdays and Fridays, assuming the wind nymphs felt like cooperating, assuming no harpies got possessive over a glossy catalog, assuming Mr. D wasn't in a mood to declare that all post was mortal nonsense and set it on fire.

That Friday a few weeks later, the air smelled like crushed strawberries and cold coming off the Sound. Percy Jackson was already at the breakfast pavilion when the mail bag thumped down onto the Hermes table, trailing oak leaves.

"Mail call!" Travis Stoll shouted, standing up on the bench with the bag upended. "Love letters, blackmail material, and bills!"

The pavilion buzzed. Morning light cut in long slabs across the pine tables. At the Poseidon table — which these days meant Percy, Clarisse with her elbow in the grape jelly, and Silena Beauregard braiding a piece of grass into a bracelet while she stole Percy's bacon — Percy craned his neck to see.

"Anything good?" Silena asked.

"Bill, bill, letter for Beckendorf, package for the Ares cabin that is definitely ticking," Travis announced, tossing things with dramatic flair. "Ooh. Pretty envelope. For Thalia Grace?"

He held it up. It really was pretty, or trying to be. Pale blue stationery, the kind sold in drugstores with little clouds in the corner. The handwriting looped big across the front: *THALIA + ANNABETH GRACE — CAMP HALF-BLOOD, LONG ISLAND (you know where!!!)* with three exclamation points and a lipstick kiss stamped beside it, faded coral pink.

Percy felt his stomach drop before he even understood why.

Across the pavilion, at the Zeus table, Thalia had gone completely still. She was sitting alone — the Zeus cabin was only her and Annabeth, and Annabeth was still at the archery range finishing earlymorning practice, her bow case slung over the back of her chair. Thalia had a fork halfway to her mouth. She set it down very carefully.

"Yo! Catch!" Travis said, and threw it frisbee-style.

Thalia caught it one-handed without looking. Her face didn't change. Not exactly. But Percy, who had been watching her for weeks now in the way you watch weather, saw her thumb press hard into the corner of the envelope, saw her jaw tighten.

"Who's it from?" Chris Rodriguez called.

"Return address says B. Grace, Manhattan," Travis read, squinting. "Your mom send fan mail, Grace?"

Thalia smiled, thin and bright. "Something like that."

She slid the letter into her jacket pocket, zipped it shut, and went back to her eggs like nothing had happened.

Percy looked down at his own plate. Clarisse kicked him under the table.

"What?" he whispered.

"You're staring," Clarisse muttered. She was thirteen, all elbows and cropped dark hair growing out from a bad training cut, her eyes sharper than most adults gave her credit for. "Stop staring."

"I'm not staring."

"You are doing your goldfish face," Silena said gently. She pushed her stolen bacon back onto his plate when he didn't eat. "Percy. Eat."

"I just — did Annabeth know a letter was coming?"

Clarisse and Silena exchanged a look. That was new, that look. Since the three Poseidon kids had started sitting together, they had developed a whole silent language.

"No," Clarisse said. "And Thalia's not going to tell her until she decides if it's safe. So mind your business."

"That's her sister," Percy said.

"Yeah," Clarisse said. "That's why it's her business first."

---

Thalia didn't open the letter until third period, locked in the back of the armory where the spare shields were stacked and smelled like oil and old leather.

She told herself she was being practical. Annabeth had combat drills with the Athena cabin until noon, then lunch, then she tutored the younger Hermes kids in Ancient Greek because she liked feeling useful and because Chiron had asked. That gave Thalia four hours to read it, decide what to do, and — if necessary — make it disappear.

Her hands shook taking it out of her pocket. She hated that.

*Okay,* she thought. *Okay, Grace. Get it together. You've defused a drakon trap with a hairpin. You can open an envelope.*

The paper tore along the top. Inside, two sheets, front and back, covered in that big loopy writing that hadn't changed in five years.

> *My most beautiful girls!!!*
>
> *Hi babies! Hi my stars! It's Mommy! Your mommy who loves you more than all the diamonds in all the stores on Fifth Avenue (ha!). I hope this letter finds you!!! A nice lady at the bodega told me how to address it right this time so hopefully you actually GET it.*
>
> *Thalia, baby, are you keeping your hair brushed? You always hated when I brushed it, you would scream like I was killing you, haha, you were such a wild thing. Are you eating? You look so thin in my mind. Annabeth, my little owl, are you still reading everything? Mommy is reading too! I'm reading a book about clean living, it's got a sunrise on it.*
>
> *I'm doing SO good, girls. Really. 38 days. That's more than a month! I have a chip. It's blue. My sponsor is named Carol and she has very strong eyebrows and she says I have a good heart. I told her, I have THREE good hearts, my three babies, but Jason — well. They won't tell me where Jason is. That camp in California, they won't even answer the phone. If you talk to him, tell him Mommy loves him and I'm sorry about the hotel in Fresno, I didn't mean to leave him with the vending machine money, I thought his dad — well.*
>
> *Anyway! I got a job folding sweaters at a store. I fold them SO pretty, the manager said so. I bought stamps with my first paycheck. Real stamps! So I could write my girls.*
>
> *Are there boys? Thalia, don't let boys be mean to your sister. Annabeth, don't let boys be mean to YOU. You are a princess, both of you, you are movie stars. When I get a car again we will drive to the ocean and get hot dogs and I will buy you those matching bracelets you wanted, remember? With the little lightning bolts?*
>
> *Write back, okay? Even just a picture. Draw me Camp. Is it pretty? Is it safe? Are they nice to you? Thalia, take care of your sister. You were always so good at that, even when you were little, you would bring her crackers when I had a headache. My good girl.*
>
> *All my love forever and ever and one day more,*
> *Mom*

Thalia read it twice. Then a third time, faster, like skimming would make certain sentences unhappen.

*38 days.*

*I thought his dad — well.*

*When I had a headache.*

Headache meant hangover. Hangover meant vodka in the orange juice carton. Hangover meant Thalia, age eleven, standing on a chair to reach the lock on the apartment door so seven-year-old Annabeth couldn't wander out onto Broadway while Beryl slept until three in the afternoon with a wet washcloth over her eyes.

Thalia pressed the heel of her hand hard into her sternum. The armory was too warm. The shields leaned and glinted.

She remembered all too well. The years of checking bottles, counting grocery money, listening for the wrong footsteps on the stairs. Beryl was alive, sober for 38 days, folding sweaters, writing in loopy ink about lightning-bolt bracelets. Thalia wanted that to mean something. She was afraid of how much she wanted it.

Alive meant it could still go wrong. Alive meant Annabeth could still get hurt.

Thalia folded the letter back up, slid it into the inner pocket of her leather jacket, and zipped it. She told herself she would give it to Annabeth after lunch. After training. After she figured out the right way to say *hey, remember Mom?*

She didn't give it to Annabeth after lunch.

---

Chiron found Thalia in the armory before she had decided whether to go to lunch. He didn't ask why the door was shut. The workshop steward had already mentioned that someone had taken the spare-shield room out of use for an hour, and Chiron's expression suggested he knew exactly who was responsible.

"Are you free for a moment?" he asked.

"Depends what you need me for."

"A question, perhaps. We received a letter from your mother this morning."

Thalia felt the envelope against her chest as though it had suddenly grown hot.

"I know."

"Then the mail reached you."

"It did."

Chiron waited. He had the irritating ability to make silence feel like an invitation without turning it into an interrogation. Thalia had spent years resenting and depending on it in equal measure.

"I'm not trying to keep it from Annabeth forever," she said.

"I haven't accused you of doing so."

"I just need to know what to say."

"Then perhaps you should decide what you know, and what you're afraid might happen."

Thalia leaned against the shield rack. It was a sensible distinction, which made her want to throw something.

"Mom says she's thirty-eight days sober. She has a job. She wants pictures. She asks if we're safe. She asks about Jason, like he might be living in the next cabin over and all we have to do is look."

"Those are the things she wrote."

"She also wants to drive us to the ocean and buy us bracelets. She still doesn't have a car. And the last time she tried to take us somewhere without telling anyone, I spent a night outside a motel trying to find a telephone."

Chiron glanced toward the empty doorway. "You're allowed to be frightened by the offer."

"Annabeth will hear *ocean* and *bracelets* and want to believe the good parts."

"She might. She may also have questions you've never heard her ask."

"She doesn't remember half the things that would make her afraid."

Chiron let the words settle. Thalia knew she had made the problem sound simple. Her sister remembered a childhood Thalia couldn't share, and was missing a childhood Thalia had lived through. That made Annabeth more vulnerable to some stories and less vulnerable to others. It did not mean she had become too young to read a letter addressed to her.

"Is there anything in the letter that puts either of you in immediate danger?" Chiron asked.

"Not from the letter itself."

"Then I can help you think about what safe contact might look like without deciding for you how to feel about your mother."

"What if Annabeth asks to visit?"

"Then we discuss it. A parent being sober for a little over a month is encouraging. It is not the same as establishing a reliable home or a safe travel arrangement. You and your sister will not be sent away because an envelope arrived."

Thalia looked up sharply. "You promise?"

"I promise that I will not treat the letter as sufficient reason to change your guardianship or remove you from camp. Any visit would require a separate conversation, consent and practical safeguards."

She had expected a kinder but vaguer answer. She found the specifics unexpectedly relieving.

"Would you tell her that?" she asked.

"I can, if she wants me there. But I would rather you didn't need me as proof that your worries are reasonable."

Thalia thought of the sisters' letters to Jason, still waiting for a route to Camp Jupiter. She had asked Chiron for help without expecting him to speak for her. She ought to be able to offer Annabeth the same choice.

"I can tell her after lunch," she said.

"Would you like me to check on either of you afterward?"

"Maybe. Not right away."

"All right."

He turned toward the door. She stopped him.

"Chiron. Does thirty-eight days mean she's going to be okay?"

He looked at her with a sorrow she wished he'd concealed.

"It means she has made it through thirty-eight days," he said. "That is real progress. I can't promise what happens next."

Thalia nodded. She had expected that answer. The hope still stung.

After he left, she unfolded the letter and read it again. The line about Jason was still difficult. The mention of matching bracelets still made her remember a day in a department store when Beryl had been sober enough to laugh at Annabeth for choosing the ugliest pair. Both memories belonged to the woman who'd written the letter. Neither canceled the night outside the motel.

She took a clean sheet of paper and wrote a response in the margin of an old drill plan, because it was the first paper she could find:

*Mom, I got your letter. I'm glad you're safe and working. Chiron says the mail route is open, but I don't know whether we can visit. Annabeth should decide whether she wants to write.*

She stopped. She didn't want to write on behalf of a sister she hadn't even told.

She crossed out the last sentence and began again.

*I want to answer you. I need some time. Please write to camp rather than asking anybody to take us away without speaking to Chiron first.*

That was true. It was also more direct than anything she'd sent her mother before. She folded it and put it in the same jacket pocket as the unopened original envelope. She might send it. She might rewrite it. At least she had decided what she wanted rather than only what she wanted to prevent.

Outside the armory, the afternoon bell sounded. Thalia went out toward the pavilion. Annabeth was sitting with her books beside the Zeus table, a penciled diagram spread over her plate. Thalia watched her for a moment and nearly went over.

Then she saw Percy speaking to Clarisse, remembered the promise she'd made to Chiron and decided she couldn't hand over the letter with an audience listening. She would wait until they could talk alone.

The delay had a reason. It did not become harmless because the reason was understandable.



---

Annabeth knew something was wrong because Thalia lied about the oatmeal cookies.

It was small. That was how Annabeth knew — Thalia Grace did not do small lies well. Big lies, life-or-death lies, monster-behind-you-run lies? Flawless. Small lies? Terrible.

They were walking back from dinner, the sky gone purple over the lake, the path lit with torches. Dinner had been meatloaf and roasted carrots and the Poseidon table had started a food fight that ended with Clarisse wearing a carrot behind her ear like a pencil and Silena laughing so hard she snorted milk. Annabeth had sat at the Zeus table with Thalia and pretended not to watch Percy laugh, which was stupid because everyone watched Percy laugh — it was loud and total and made his whole face change.

"Half the Hermes cabin is coming to the bonfire," Annabeth said, swinging her book bag. "Luke said he'd save us seats if we want."

"Mm," Thalia said.

"And Chiron wants me to help map the new trail markers tomorrow. He said I could bring Percy if I want someone to carry the stakes."

"Mm."

"Thalia. There are oatmeal cookies in the Zeus cabin. I hid them behind the spare blankets. From last week. When Katie gave me extras."

Thalia glanced down. "Cool."

"You love oatmeal cookies. You once threatened Luke with a spear for the last oatmeal cookie."

"I did not threaten — I just held the spear nearby in a suggestive manner."

"So do you want one or not?"

Thalia hesitated. Half a second too long. "Nah. I'm good."

Annabeth stopped walking. "Okay, what's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong."

"You don't want a cookie. Something is catastrophically wrong."

Thalia laughed, but it came out wrong. "Annie, I just ate like four pieces of meatloaf. I'm fine."

Annabeth stared at her sister. Thalia at sixteen was all long lines and spiked black hair and silver bracelets that chimed when she moved. She wore her leather jacket even in heat. She had a scar on her left knee from a guardrail on a highway in Ohio that she refused to explain to the Apollo kids.

She was also, Annabeth noted with the cold clarity she brought to blueprints and battle maps, keeping her left hand pressed over her jacket pocket like she was holding something in.

"You got mail today," Annabeth said slowly. "Travis threw you something at breakfast. Percy told me."

"Percy needs to shut up," Thalia said affectionately and automatically, then winced. "I mean — yeah. I got a letter."

"From who?"

A beat. The torches hissed. Somewhere down by the cabins, someone was tuning a guitar badly.

"Nobody important. Just — junk. Some mortal charity thing that got our address wrong."

Annabeth felt something cold settle in her stomach. "Thalia."

"What?"

"You're lying."

"I'm not —"

"You touched your pocket. You do that when you're hiding something. You did it when you borrowed my copy of *The Odyssey* last week and when you hid the repair report with the broken window."

Thalia's hand dropped from her pocket like it burned her. "Annie —"

"Let me see it."

"It's nothing, it's —"

"Let me see it."

Thalia unzipped the pocket. Slowly. She pulled out the pale blue envelope, now creased, and held it out.

Annabeth saw the handwriting. Saw the lipstick kiss. Saw *B. Grace, Manhattan* in the corner.

Her fingers went numb.

"You read my mom's letter," Annabeth said quietly, "and you didn't tell me."

"It's addressed to both of us," Thalia said. "And I was going to — I just wanted to read it first, make sure it wasn't —"

"Make sure it wasn't what?"

"Make sure she wasn't — Annie, last time she wrote, two years ago, she said she was coming to get us and she was going to bring Jason and we waited by the Pine Tree for six hours in the rain. You had pneumonia for a week."

Annabeth could not remember waiting in the rain. Thalia had told her about it, just as she had told her about motel promises and unpaid bills. The account was frightening; it still did not make the letter Thalia's alone to keep. In her original life, Beryl had been the mother of her friend, a woman whose death she had learned about later. Here, Beryl was her mother too, alive and asking for an answer.

"You don't get to decide for me," Annabeth said. Her voice shook. "I'm twelve, not five. I'm not — you can't just hide things because you think I'll break."

"I'm trying to protect you!"

"I don't need you to protect me from a letter!"

They were shouting now, right there on the torchlit path. A couple of Demeter kids veered wide around them.

Thalia's eyes were bright. "Someone has to! You don't remember — you were too little, Annie, you don't remember what it was like, waking up and not knowing if she'd eaten, if she'd paid the electric bill, if the men in the living room were safe —"

"I know enough to want a choice!" Annabeth snatched the letter. "She's my mother too!"

"Yeah, and I raised you more than she did!" Thalia shouted, and immediately looked horrified at herself.

Silence. The crickets were deafening.

Annabeth's throat closed. "Wow."

"Annie, I didn't mean —"

"No, it's fine." Annabeth folded the envelope with shaking hands and shoved it into her own pocket. "You did. You're right. You did. So congratulations. You get to keep doing it forever, I guess."

She turned and walked fast toward the Zeus cabin, blinking hard. She wouldn't cry on the path. Not because daughters of Athena or Zeus had rules about tears, but because she wanted to finish the conversation before she let herself fall apart.

Behind her, Thalia didn't follow.

---

Percy found Thalia an hour later sitting on the steps of the armory, picking at her bracelets.

He had debated coming. Clarisse had told him, explicitly, "If you go over there and do your sad puppy thing, I will drown you in the lake myself." Silena had added, more gently, "Just give them tonight, Percy. Sisters need space."

But Percy had spent the bonfire watching Annabeth sit with Luke and laugh a half-second too late at jokes, her hand in her pocket the whole time, and watching the empty space beside her where Thalia usually sat, and he'd felt itchy and useless. So he'd slipped away, grabbed two cans of Coke from the camp store with his last drachmas, and gone looking.

"Hey," he said, holding out a Coke. "Peace offering. Or bribe. Dealer's choice."

Thalia looked up. Her eyes were red. She took the Coke. "You shouldn't be here. Clarisse is going to kill you."

"Clarisse tried to kill me at sword practice today and I survived. I'm evolving." He sat down beside her, leaving careful space. "You okay?"

"No," Thalia said bluntly. She cracked the Coke. "I just told my baby sister I raised her more than our mom did, in the middle of a fight about our mom. So. I'm doing great. Really nailing the big sister thing."

Percy winced. "Ouch."

"Yeah."

He didn't ask what the letter said. He didn't ask to see it. He just sat there, swinging his sneakers against the step, and after a minute Thalia started talking anyway.

"She's sober," Thalia said. "Thirty-eight days. She has a job folding sweaters. She wants us to write back." Her voice cracked. "And all I can think is — okay, so what? So she's sober *now*? What about when Annie was six and Mom left her in the car outside a bar in Yonkers for three hours because she 'just needed to use the bathroom'? What about Christmas when there was no heat? I was nine, Percy. I was boiling water on a hot plate to make mac and cheese while Mom slept."

Percy listened. He thought about his own mom — Sally Jackson, who made blue pancakes and worked double shifts and smelled like vanilla and never, ever left him in a car. He felt guilty for having that, sitting next to Thalia who didn't.

"That sounds really scary," he said quietly. "Being nine and doing that."

"It was," Thalia whispered. "And the worst part is I still want to believe her. Every time. I can list every broken promise and then I see a kiss on an envelope and think maybe this is the one. I don't know how to be mad and hopeful at the same time."

Percy bumped his shoulder lightly against hers. "You can be both. I think. My mom says feelings aren't math. They don't have to add up."

Thalia huffed a wet laugh. "Your mom is smart."

"She is. She also says when you mess up with someone you love, you don't fix it with a big speech. You fix it with showing up and doing the dishes."

"Doing the dishes, huh?"

"Or sorting photos. Or whatever sisters do."

Thalia looked at him sideways. "Who told you about the photos?"

"Annabeth mentioned last week she found a box under your bunk. From before camp. She wanted to look through it with you but you kept saying later."

Thalia closed her eyes. "Gods. Yeah. There's a shoebox. Beryl sent it last year with like, twenty bucks and a note that said *for memories!!!* Most of them are — they're not all bad. There's one of Annie at the beach with ice cream all over her face. There's one of all three of us kids asleep on a motel bed."

"That sounds like a good later to do tonight," Percy said. He stood up, brushed off his shorts. "I'm not saying go barge in. I'm just saying — you're her sister, not her shield. There's a difference. I think."

He left the second Coke on the step and walked back toward the cabins, hands in his pockets, trying not to look back.

Thalia watched him go, then picked up the Coke and held it cold against her forehead.

*Not her shield.* Smart kid.

---

The Zeus cabin smelled like ozone and cedar and old books. It was the biggest cabin at camp — white marble, tall columns, a bronze door that hummed faintly in thunderstorms — and for two girls who had once shared a studio apartment with a broken radiator, it felt cavernously empty.

Annabeth sat cross-legged on her bunk with Beryl's letter spread on her knees. She had read it four times.

*My little owl.*

The nickname belonged to a girl Annabeth could only find in photographs. Beryl's letter said she had stayed up reading under the covers with a flashlight. That part sounded like her. She could picture it, but not remember Beryl leaning over the bed.

Annabeth pressed her palms into her eyes. In the life she remembered, Athena was her mother. Beryl's death had been a sad story about Thalia's family, something Annabeth had heard and tried to offer comfort for.

Here, Beryl was her own mother, still capable of disappointing her in real time. Still capable of getting sober. Of relapsing. Of sending letters with lipstick kisses.

She did not know how much of her fear was her own and how much she had learned by watching Thalia. All of it felt immediate.

The cabin door creaked. Thalia stood there holding a Coke and looking like she'd lost a fight with a harpy.

"Hey," Thalia said.

"Hey," Annabeth said, not looking up.

Thalia shut the door, dropped her jacket over the foot of her own bunk, and stood awkwardly in the middle of the cabin. "You eat?"

"Not hungry."

"Okay." A pause. "I brought you — never mind, you don't want the Coke, it's warm now."

Annabeth almost smiled. Almost.

Thalia sat down on the floor, back against Annabeth's bunk, close enough that Annabeth could see the top of her spiky head. "I'm sorry," Thalia said to the floorboards. "For what I said on the path. That was cruel. And untrue — well, partly true, but cruel to say it like a weapon. You were right. I shouldn't have hidden the letter."

Annabeth picked at the blanket. "Why did you?"

"Because I'm scared." Thalia's voice was small. "Every time she writes, I get — I get nine years old again. Listening for her key in the door. Trying to figure out if she's happy-drunk or mean-drunk or sad-drunk. And I don't want you to feel that. Ever again. I want camp to be the place where nobody listens for keys."

Annabeth's eyes burned. "Thalia —"

"Let me finish? Please?" Thalia twisted to look up at her. "When we were little, I used to tell you Mom was a movie star and she was away filming. I know you can't remember that. I'd make up plots. *Beryl Grace and the Diamond Heist.* You'd ask when she'd be home and I'd say after the premiere. I lied so much I started believing it. And then we'd get to camp and Chiron would say *your mother can't visit* and I'd be relieved. What kind of kid is relieved her mom can't visit?"

"The kind who was tired," Annabeth whispered.

Thalia nodded, eyes shining. "Yeah. Tired." She took a shaky breath. "So when I saw that envelope today, I just — I panicked. I thought if I read it first, I could filter it. Take out the sharp parts. Be your —"

"Shield, not sister?" Annabeth said softly.

Thalia stared. "Did Percy tell you that? That little — I'm going to kill him."

"He didn't tell me. But he would say something like that." Annabeth slid off the bunk to sit on the floor beside Thalia, shoulder to shoulder. For a minute they just breathed. The cabin hummed faintly — Zeus's presence, distant and electric, like a refrigerator left running in another room.

"Can I tell you something weird?" Annabeth said.

"Always."

"Sometimes I remember her being dead." She said it fast, like ripping off a bandage. "In the other — in the before. She died, right? Drunk driving? And I remember being older and thinking I was okay with it. Like, it was sad but it was finished. And now she's alive and writing about folding sweaters and I feel guilty for missing the version where she's dead, because at least then I knew how the story ended."

Thalia was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached over and took Annabeth's hand, lacing their fingers the way she used to when crossing streets in Manhattan, when Annabeth was tiny and the traffic was terrifying.

"That's not weird," Thalia said. "I haven't lived that version. But I know what it is to want certainty. And I think — I think we're allowed to love her and be angry at her and miss her and wish she'd just stay away. All at once. Feelings aren't math."

"Did Percy steal that from his mom?" Annabeth asked, smiling wetly.

"Yeah. Sally Jackson is smarter than all the gods combined."

They sat there holding hands like little kids. Then Annabeth said, "Do you still have the shoebox?"

Thalia blinked. "Under my bunk? Yeah."

"Can we — can we look? Tonight? I don't want to read the letter again right now. I want to see something true."

Thalia got up, knelt, dragged out a battered Nike shoebox held shut with a hair tie. She brought it back to the floor between them. Inside: photos, curling at the edges. A hospital bracelet. A plastic lightning bolt from a party favor. A movie ticket stub, faded.

"Oh gods," Thalia said, laughing through her nose. "Look at this one."

It was Thalia at maybe four, scowling over the edge of a chair and holding a baby that had to be Annabeth, wrapped in a yellow blanket. Thalia was scowling fiercely at the camera. On the back, in Beryl's handwriting: *Thalia LOVES her sister (she does, she just doesn't smile for pictures!!!)*

"I look furious," Thalia said.

"You look constipated," Annabeth said, and Thalia shoved her.

"Shut up. Next."

Annabeth at three at Rockaway Beach, ice cream down her entire front, grinning gap-toothed. Beryl's arm around her, cropped out except for a tanned shoulder and a silver bracelet. On the back: *My owl at the beach!!! She ate sand!!!*

"I ate sand?" Annabeth said.

"You ate everything. You once ate a crayon because you liked the color blue."

"I had taste."

They went through them slowly. Jason as a toddler with a bucket on his head. The three kids asleep sideways on a motel bed, Thalia's arm thrown over both siblings. Beryl young and impossibly beautiful in a waitress uniform, winking. Beryl older, tired, holding a Christmas present with shaking hands.

"This one," Annabeth said softly, holding up a photo of the four of them — Beryl, Thalia, Annabeth, Jason — squished into a photo booth, all making silly faces, Beryl's lipstick bright, Thalia's tongue out, Annabeth cross-eyed, Jason drooling. "She looks happy here."

"She was," Thalia said. "Sometimes she was really happy. That's what made it hard. It wasn't all bad. If it was all bad, it would be easy to — you know. To stop."

Annabeth leaned her head on Thalia's shoulder. "Do you think she's really sober? Thirty-eight days?"

"I think she believes she is today," Thalia said carefully. "And today counts. Carol with the strong eyebrows sounds like she doesn't mess around."

Annabeth laughed, surprised. "Strong eyebrows are trustworthy."

"Exactly. Very authoritative brows."

The tension in the cabin eased, fraction by fraction. Outside, the bonfire crowd sang off-key. Inside, two sisters sat on the floor surrounded by proof they had once been small and loved, however imperfectly.

Which was when Annabeth noticed Thalia wearing her sweater.

"Is that my sweater?" Annabeth said.

Thalia looked down at the oversized gray cardigan she had pulled on over her pajama shirt. "No."

"That is my sweater. That's the one Silena lent me last week because I was cold at the lake and she said it looked better on me and I left it on my bunk."

"It's a communal sweater," Thalia said loftily. "Sweaters in a shared cabin are communal property. It's in the camp handbook. Page forty-seven."

"There is no page forty-seven."

"There should be."

"Thalia Grace, take off my sweater this instant."

"No."

Annabeth lunged. Thalia yelped and scrambled backward, dragging the shoebox with her, photos scattering. They wrestled on the floor, Annabeth trying to pull the sleeve, Thalia tucking her arms in like a turtle, both laughing so hard they could barely breathe.

"Give — it — back!"

"Come — and — take — it!"

"It's stretched out! Your arms are too long! You look like a giraffe in a baby blanket!"

"I look amazing! I am setting trends!"

The sleeve ripped slightly at the seam. They both froze, stared at it, then collapsed laughing harder.

"Great," Annabeth gasped. "Now it's ruined for both of us."

"We'll share custody," Thalia wheezed. "Weekends and holidays."

"You're the worst sister ever."

"I'm the best sister ever and you know it."

Annabeth lay sprawled across the floor, cheek on a photo of her three-year-old self eating sand, laughing until her stomach hurt. It felt like surfacing. Like the fight on the path had been underwater and this — stupid, breathless, sister laughter over a stretched sweater — was air.

Thalia flopped beside her, still wearing the stolen sweater, and bumped her forehead against Annabeth's. "Love you, owl."

"Love you too, pinecone head."

"Did you just call me pinecone head?"

"It's the hair. It's spiky."

They lay there until the laughter faded into comfortable quiet. Then Annabeth's stomach growled, loudly.

Thalia sat up. "When did you last eat? You left dinner early."

"I'm fine."

"Nope. We're fixing that." Thalia stood, clapped her hands. "As your elder and wiser sister who once made mac and cheese on a hot plate, I will now cook us a midnight snack."

Annabeth eyed her. "Thalia. The Zeus cabin does not have a kitchen."

"We have a hot pot, popcorn, and determination."

"That sounds like a fire hazard."

"That sounds like dinner."

---

The cooking disaster took exactly eleven minutes.

It started reasonably: Thalia produced an electric kettle, two packets of instant ramen, and a bag of marshmallows she'd stolen from the Hermes cabin. Annabeth contributed the oatmeal cookies from behind the blankets and a jar of peanut butter.

"We're making ramen," Thalia announced. "And then for dessert, we're making cookie-marshmallow-peanut-butter sandwiches, which I just invented and which will make us famous."

"That sounds disgusting," Annabeth said happily, sitting cross-legged to watch.

Thalia filled the kettle, plugged it in, and while waiting, tried to toast marshmallows over a candle she'd lit for ambiance.

"Thalia, no," Annabeth said.

"Thalia, yes," Thalia said, holding a fork with a marshmallow over the tea light. The marshmallow caught fire immediately.

"Blow it out! Blow it out!"

Thalia blew. The flaming marshmallow flew off the fork, arced across the cabin, and landed directly in the open ramen packet.

"Oh no," Annabeth said.

"Oh no is right," Thalia said.

The kettle boiled over. Water hissed across the desk. Thalia grabbed it, sloshed water into the ramen cups, overfilled both, and one cup tipped, sending shrimp-flavored broth cascading onto the oatmeal cookies.

"My cookies!" Annabeth wailed, laughing.

"Save the cookies! Save the — ow, hot, hot!"

Thalia grabbed the cookie bag with her sweater sleeve — Annabeth's sweater — and got broth on it. Annabeth screamed. Thalia tried to wipe it, smeared it more. The fire alarm — which no one knew the Zeus cabin even had, since gods generally disdained fire codes — began to beep shrilly.

"Turn it off! Turn it off!"

"I don't know how! Wave a towel! Do the thing!"

Annabeth stood on her bunk waving a pillow. Thalia stood on a chair waving the camp handbook. The beeping stopped. They stared at each other, covered in broth, marshmallow in Thalia's hair, peanut butter on Annabeth's nose, the cabin smelling like shrimp and sugar and smoke.

Then the door banged open.

Percy stood there in pajama pants and a Camp Half-Blood t-shirt, barefoot, holding a fire extinguisher, with Clarisse behind him in a Poseidon sweatshirt, hair sticking up.

"We saw smoke —" Percy started, then took in the scene: the spilled ramen, the scorched marshmallow stuck to the wall, the two Grace sisters frozen mid-wave with pillows.

Clarisse looked slowly around. "What in Hades —"

Silena appeared behind them, in fuzzy socks, carrying a first-aid kit. "Is anyone hurt? Is anyone — oh." She saw the marshmallow on the wall. "Oh no."

"It was ambiance," Thalia said weakly.

Annabeth burst out laughing. She laughed so hard she slid off the bunk onto the floor. Thalia started laughing. Percy lowered the fire extinguisher, looked at Clarisse, and started laughing too. Even Clarisse cracked, trying to stay stern and failing.

"You idiots," Clarisse said fondly, stepping over the broth puddle. "You can't make ramen without — you didn't even open the flavor packet all the way. And who puts marshmallows near candles?"

"It was for dessert," Thalia protested.

Silena was already moving, practical and kind. She righted the cups, mopped broth with spare towels, peeled the marshmallow off the wall with a look of deep resignation. Percy grabbed the kettle before it could short out. Clarisse confiscated the candle.

"Out of the way, amateurs," Clarisse said, rolling up her sleeves. "Silena, you do damage control. Kelp Head, unplug that death trap. Grace and Grace, sit."

Like scolded puppies, Thalia and Annabeth sat on Thalia's bunk, feet dangling, watching the Poseidon kids take over their cabin.

Clarisse made ramen correctly — water to the line, flavor packet fully dissolved, chopsticks balanced on top to stop boil-over. Silena salvaged three un-soggy cookies, scraped peanut butter onto them with a plastic knife, added a single clean marshmallow each. Percy found clean mugs, poured the salvaged broth, tasted, added pepper from a packet he'd pocketed at dinner.

"Service," Percy announced, handing Thalia and Annabeth each a mug and a cookie sandwich. "Eat."

The ramen was salty and perfect. The cookie sandwiches were sticky and terrible and wonderful. The five of them, crammed onto two bunks and the floor — ate in the wreckage of the Zeus cabin while the torches burned low outside.

"To cooking," Thalia toasted with her mug, marshmallow still in her hair. "Which we will never do again."

"To never again," everyone echoed.

Annabeth caught Percy's eye over her mug. He smiled at her — not pitying, not prying, just warm. *You okay?* his look said. She nodded, small. *Yeah. Getting there.*

He didn't ask about the letter. He didn't need to.

Clarisse told a story about the time she tried to bake a cake in the Poseidon cabin and flooded it. Silena showed them the grass bracelet she'd finished, now tied around her wrist. Thalia leaned her head against the wall, broth-warm and sister-close, and felt the knot in her chest loosen for the first time all day.

When the Poseidon kids finally left — Clarisse carrying the fire extinguisher like a trophy, Silena with promises to bring stain remover tomorrow, Percy pausing in the doorway to mouth *goodnight* — the Zeus cabin was clean-ish, fed, and quiet.

Annabeth changed into pajamas in the bathroom, came out brushing her teeth, and found Thalia already on the roof.

---

The roof of the Zeus cabin was technically forbidden. It was also, Thalia had discovered at age thirteen, accessible via the trellis beside the back column if you didn't mind splinters and if you bribed the dryads with honey cakes, which she did regularly.

Annabeth climbed up after her, less gracefully, in socks and the reclaimed — broth-stained — gray cardigan. Thalia had left it folded on her pillow with a note: *Sorry I stole you. You look better in you. — T.*

The night was clear and cold. The camp spread below them: the cabins in a horseshoe, the bonfire embers glowing orange, the lake black glass reflecting stars, the strawberry fields silver in the moonlight. The old boundary pine stood dark on the hill, its ward lights glinting faintly. Somewhere, a harpy cried once and settled.

Thalia had spread both sleeping bags on the shallow slope of the marble roof and had the shoebox between them, plus the letter, smoothed flat.

Annabeth sat, pulled her sleeping bag up to her chin, and looked sideways. "Are we going to talk about it?"

"Yeah," Thalia said. "If you want."

"I want." Annabeth took a breath. The air tasted like pine and cold stone. "Do you think we should write back?"

Thalia was quiet. She picked at a loose thread on her jeans. "I think — I think I want to. But I think we should do it together. And I think we should tell Chiron first. And I think we should not promise anything we can't keep. Like visits. Or — or that everything is fine now."

Annabeth nodded slowly. "I want to tell her about camp. Not everything. Just — that I'm learning archery. That I mapped the trails. That you braided my hair before the capture-the-flag game and it stayed in the whole time."

"I did do a good braid," Thalia said.

"You did. And I want to ask about Jason. If she's heard anything. Camp Jupiter doesn't answer mortal phones, but maybe — maybe if we write to him, Chiron could send it?"

"We can ask," Thalia said. "Jason should know she's — trying. He should know we're okay."

They sat with that. The stars were painfully bright. Annabeth could name the constellations from her old studies. Static lifted her hair, but she could not tell whether her father was watching or the cabin wards were settling. She hoped he would read the report Chiron sent.

"Thalia?" Annabeth said. "When I told you I remember a different life where Beryl wasn't around anymore, did that make the letter worse?"

Thalia exhaled. "It scared me. I'm already afraid of the phone ringing with bad news. Hearing it happened somewhere you remember doesn't tell us what happens here." She rubbed the letter's crease. "I have wanted her to stay away. I haven't wanted her dead. Sometimes I feel guilty as if they're the same thing."

Annabeth reached for her hand again. Thalia took it.

"I don't think she was all bad," Annabeth said. "I think she loved us in the way a broken flashlight still makes light sometimes. Flickery. But it was there."

"That's a good way to put it," Thalia whispered. "Flickery."

"I don't want to live listening for keys anymore either," Annabeth said. "But I don't want to pretend she doesn't exist. She's — she's our mom. Even if you did most of the mom stuff. Even if you still do."

Thalia laughed wetly. "I'm retiring from mom stuff. I'm going to be the cool older sister who teaches you to hotwire cars and shoplift candy. Much better job description."

"You've never hotwired a car."

"I have too."

"When?"

"Wouldn't you like to know, owl."

Annabeth smiled, leaned her head on Thalia's shoulder again. The marble was cold through the sleeping bags. Above them, the sky was huge and indifferent and beautiful.

"Will you help me write it tomorrow?" Annabeth asked. "The letter? By the lake? Percy can help us find the good flat stones for skipping while we think of what to say. And Silena can bring snacks. And Clarisse can threaten anyone who interrupts."

Thalia grinned. "That's a solid plan. Very tactical."

"I'm a tactical genius."

"You are. You're my genius."

They lay back, side by side, hands linked, the shoebox of photos and Beryl's letter weighted down between them with a spare sneaker so the night wind wouldn't take them.

"Thalia?" Annabeth said drowsily, half-asleep already, lulled by cold air and ramen and sister-warmth.

"Yeah?"

"Thank you. For the crackers. When I was little. When she had a headache."

Thalia's throat closed. She stared up at the stars so Annabeth wouldn't see her face. "Anytime, Annie. Forever."

Below them, the camp slept. The lake lapped. The boundary wards glowed. On the roof of the marble cabin, two sisters breathed in sync under a sky that, for once, felt watchful in a kind way — not like a father judging, but like a blanket tucked around shoulders.

Annabeth fell asleep first, cardigan pulled tight, broth stain and all. Thalia stayed awake a little longer, watching the Perseus constellation rise, thinking about 38 days, about folding sweaters, about strong eyebrows, about how hope felt a lot like fear but warmer.

Tomorrow they would write back. Tomorrow they would tell Chiron, find Jason's address, skip stones while searching for words. Tomorrow Thalia would buy stain remover from the camp store and apologize properly to the sweater.

Tonight, she tucked the sleeping bag tighter around Annabeth's shoulders and left Beryl's letter on the crate between their beds. If their mother wrote again, they would decide what to do when they had an actual envelope to open.

Thalia had spent years anticipating the worst call or the worst knock on the door. She could not stop herself from listening for one. But she didn't have to wake Annabeth to listen with her. She put down the letter, turned off the lamp, and tried to sleep.
