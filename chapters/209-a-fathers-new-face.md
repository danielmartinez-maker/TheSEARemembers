## Chapter 209: A Father’s New Face

Thalia knew there was a storm before the first cloud showed. The air had thickened over Cabin One, not enough to make the window glass hum, but enough to put a pressure behind her eyes. She set the cards on the table and flexed her right hand once.

The heel under her brace ached in the damp. It had been doing that since she climbed the two steps from the porch. No sharp pull, no new pain. She kept her weight even and sat back down.

Annabeth was bent over a page of measurements. She had written three numbers in the margin and crossed out one of them.

“That one wrong?” Thalia asked.

“Not wrong. I realized I’d measured from the outside of the frame twice.”

“So one right number.”

“One useful number.” Annabeth erased the duplicate. “Chiron says the north window still needs a proper weather check before anyone changes the latch.”

“Which means it’s still on the list.”

“Which means it stays on the list.”

Thalia put the deck into a square stack. “I like it when lists have endings.”

“They do. You just don’t like waiting for them.”

“Not the same thing.”

A knock sounded at the open door. Chiron stood on the path with a folded note in one hand. He had not come inside.

“Thalia. Annabeth. Ianthe. Would you all read this before answering?”

Ianthe looked up from the far bunk. She had been writing in a small weather notebook, her pencil held carefully between two fingers. She put it down before she took the page from Chiron.

The note was short. Zeus would be arriving after lunch. He wished to speak with Ianthe and, if they were willing, with Thalia and Annabeth. He would wait at the Big House pavilion. If they preferred not to meet, Chiron could tell him.

The handwriting looked like Zeus’s only because the name was written under it. Thalia recognized no special pressure, no lightning mark. The paper was plain.

Ianthe read it twice. “He wants to see me.”

“He does,” Chiron said. “He has asked whether your sisters would also be willing.”

“Did he ask because he wanted to see them, or because he thinks they should come?” Ianthe asked.

Chiron rested one hand on his cane. “I cannot tell you what he intended beyond what he wrote.”

Ianthe held the note by its corners. “He said he’ll wait.”

“If you want him to,” Chiron answered.

Thalia watched Ianthe’s thumb press into the fold. She looked pleased, but she also checked the note for some hidden condition, as if the words might rearrange themselves if she read them from another angle.

“I’ll go,” Ianthe said. “He asked for me.”

“Okay,” Thalia said.

Ianthe looked up. “You don’t have to go.”

“I know.”

“I’m not asking you to make it easier.”

“I know that too.”

Annabeth closed her notebook. “I’ll decide after lunch.”

Ianthe lowered the note. “You can decide now.”

“I can,” Annabeth said. “I’m deciding later.”

It was a small exchange. Thalia kept her hands on the deck and let Annabeth have the answer she had chosen. A breath passed. Chiron waited without urging any of them.

“I’ll go,” Thalia said. “If I can leave when I want.”

“You can,” Chiron said. “I’ll make sure the pavilion stays open.”

Ianthe folded the note again and gave it back. “I don’t need someone to stay beside me.”

“No one said you did,” Thalia replied.

“I know how it can sound.”

“You don’t have to explain.”

That seemed to catch Ianthe. She glanced at Thalia, then looked back at her notebook. The page was turned toward the wall. The wind notes from the damaged-canvas day were still there, each marked with the direction and time. Beside them, in smaller writing, she had added *unverified until checked against the flag line*.

Thalia didn’t tell her the note was good. Beckendorf had asked for the source and the comparison. Ianthe had written it down. The data still wasn’t ready to use.

Chiron took his leave. “There is no need to answer anyone else’s questions about the visit. You may choose where to meet, what to discuss, and when to stop.”

Ianthe glanced at Thalia after he had gone. “That sounds like a lot of choices for a conversation with a parent.”

“Some conversations need more.”

“You’ve done this before.”

“Yes.”

“Does it help?”

Thalia gathered the cards into her hands. “It helps me know what I can choose.”

Ianthe did not answer. She turned back to the weather notebook and drew a line beneath the caveat. The pencil left a light groove on the paper.

---

Percy appeared at the cabin porch just before lunch. He carried a small bowl of apple slices, which he offered to Annabeth as if he had brought a treaty.

“I wasn’t sure if you’d eaten.”

“I did,” Annabeth said, taking one. “But I’ll have another.”

“Good. I brought too many.”

“How many is too many?”

“More than six.”

“You have three.”

“I was counting the ones I ate on the path.”

She smiled, and he leaned his shoulder against hers. She bumped him away just enough to keep the bowl level, then used her free hand to straighten the cuff of his sleeve. It was an old, comfortable exchange. Thalia had seen them do versions of it often enough that it no longer felt like a performance of being together. It was one of the ways they moved around each other.

Percy glanced at the note on the table. “Is this happening today?”

“If we want it to,” Annabeth said.

“Do you want me there?”

“I haven’t decided if I want to meet him. If I do, I might want you near the path. Not in the conversation unless I ask.”

“Okay.”

“And if I say I’m fine, don’t decide that means I need rescuing.”

“I have learned that distinction.”

“Have you?”

“I am learning it.”

“Better answer.”

Percy reached for another slice. Annabeth held the bowl just out of his reach, then gave it to him. His fingers brushed hers. He bent to kiss her hair near the temple, and she rested her head briefly against his shoulder before returning to her notebook.

It was not a vote about whether she could face Zeus. It was a way to leave room for her choice.

“I’ll be by the path,” Percy said. “If you want me.”

Annabeth looked at him. “That’s what I asked.”

He nodded, and his smile softened. Then he went to help Chiron with the meal table, taking the empty bowl with him.

Thalia watched Annabeth return to the measurements. “Do you want me to ask him to stay?”

“No.”

“All right.”

“I’ll tell him if I do.”

“I know.”

Annabeth tapped the page with her pencil. “You can want to be there with me without deciding for me.”

“I can.”

“And you can leave before I do if your heel hurts.”

“I can.”

“Do you want to?”

Thalia looked down at her brace. “I’ll tell you if it does.”

Annabeth accepted that without making her repeat it.

---

The wait before lunch stretched. Ianthe came over with a deck of her own, which she set beside Thalia’s.

“Do you play Sequence?” she asked.

“I’ve heard of it.”

“Do you want to learn?”

Thalia looked at Annabeth. “Do we have time to lose to her before lunch?”

“I don’t know,” Annabeth said. “Does she already know the rules?”

Ianthe’s chin lifted. “Of course.”

“Then we’re already behind,” Thalia said.

“I can explain the rules.”

“That sounds like an offer.”

“It is.”

Thalia pulled out a chair. “Okay.”

Ianthe dealt the cards into three equal piles, counting twice. Annabeth watched the distribution.

“You don’t need to count them twice,” she said.

“I like knowing we have the same number.”

“We do.”

“You didn’t check.”

“I watched you.”

“That’s not the same.”

Thalia slid a card across the table. “If we start arguing about the deck, it’s going to be dinner before we get to the game.”

Ianthe lined the board with the edge of the table. “The rules say—”

“The rules say we can play,” Annabeth interrupted, “not that everything has to be perfectly straight.”

“It helps.”

“You can straighten it,” Thalia said. “We’ll wait.”

Ianthe did, though she left one corner a little crooked after the table wobbled. She gave the first turn to Annabeth, who studied her cards with a serious, almost theatrical frown.

“Is there a correct move?” Ianthe asked.

“There are several possible moves.”

“Which is the best?”

“That depends.”

“You’re doing that on purpose.”

“Doing what?”

“Making the answer sound like a riddle.”

Annabeth played a card. “I’m deciding.”

Thalia watched them. Annabeth had been patient with Ianthe’s questions, but not in the way a teacher waited for a student to catch up. She left room for her to figure the rules out, even when Ianthe wanted the instructions to guarantee a win.

Thalia took her turn and blocked one of Ianthe’s lines.

“That wasn’t in the strategy you explained,” Ianthe said.

“No.”

“Then why did you do it?”

“Because you were about to win.”

“That’s not a reason.”

“It’s the whole reason.”

Annabeth laughed, and after a second Ianthe did too. She drew another card and rearranged it in her hand as if the game could still be negotiated.

They played two rounds. Thalia won the first by accident, then lost the second after Annabeth spotted the open line. Ianthe asked whether the order of the rounds should count; Annabeth told her it could if they wanted to keep score. No one decided.

For a while, the afternoon was only a board, warm light on the floor, and the three of them making fun of how seriously they were taking a game. Ianthe’s laugh came quickly when she stopped checking the score. Thalia did not try to guess what that meant about her, or about them.



After the game, Annabeth gathered the cards. Ianthe put the board away before anyone asked her to. She stacked it with the rules booklet and squared the corners against the table.

“You could leave it out,” Thalia said. “We might play later.”

“I know where it is now.”

“That’s true.”

Ianthe slid the booklet under the deck. “Do you think he’ll ask about the weather notes?”

“If you want to show him, you can.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“I don’t know what he’ll ask.”

Ianthe leaned back against the bunk. “He said he wanted to see me.”

“Yes.”

“Does it bother you?”

Thalia looked at the chair by the window. The seat still had a pale mark from the stool Annabeth used when she was fixing the latch. It was empty now. The request to move it had not come from Zeus; Ianthe’s cabin placement remained unresolved. “I don’t know yet.”

“You said you’d go.”

“I did.”

“But you don’t want to.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You look like you’re waiting for something.”

“I usually am when he visits.”

Ianthe’s brows drew together. “You have seen him before.”

“More than once.”

“Then what are you waiting for?”

“For the conversation to turn into the one I already know.”

The answer came out sharper than she meant. Ianthe turned her head toward the porch. Outside, clouds had begun to gather above the cabins, although the ground was dry.

“He said he was proud of me last time I told him about my storm sense,” Ianthe said. “He remembered it.”

“Maybe he did.”

“He has been there.”

“For you,” Thalia said.

Ianthe’s expression closed. “That sounded like an accusation.”

“It wasn’t.”

“You always make it sound like there’s a part of the story I’m not allowed to know.”

“You can know your part.”

“And you want me to know yours.”

“I want you to know it exists.”

Ianthe stood up. “I know you have a history with him. I don’t know what you want me to do with it.”

“Nothing.”

“That doesn’t make sense.”

“You can want to see your father without doing anything about me.”

Ianthe’s face shifted, briefly puzzled. “You really don’t mind?”

“Those aren’t the same question.”

“I don’t know how to answer if you keep changing the question.”

Thalia felt the familiar urge to make the words simpler. To tell her about the face at the pavilion, the way Zeus had praised Annabeth for being easy and called Thalia a problem. To explain every time his attention had shown up as a new voice, a new set of eyes, and a rule about how they were supposed to react.

She did not. Ianthe had asked what she wanted her to do. The answer was nothing.

“I don’t need you to agree with me before you meet him,” Thalia said. “I don’t need you to defend me, either.”

“And if he says something unfair?”

“I’ll answer him.”

“What if you can’t?”

“Then I’ll leave.”

Ianthe looked at the brace on her heel. “You can leave even if it hurts?”

“I can leave.”

“You shouldn’t have to—”

“I decide that. Remember?”

The words landed harder than she wanted. Ianthe’s eyes sharpened. “You think I’m trying to manage you again.”

“I think you are.”

Ianthe folded her arms. “I was asking if you were all right.”

“I know. You can ask. You can’t answer for me.”

For a moment neither of them moved. Then Ianthe’s arms loosened. She sat on the edge of her bunk instead of returning to her notebook.

“Fine,” she said.

It was not an apology. Thalia did not ask for one. The next thing Ianthe said was quieter.

“Do you think I’m being stupid for wanting him to come?”

“No.”

“Do you think I’m being naïve?”

“I think you want him to see you.”

Ianthe glanced down at the note. “That’s not the same as wanting to believe everything he says.”

“No.”

“I’m not a child.”

“I know.”

“Then don’t explain it to me like one.”

Thalia held her gaze. “I won’t.”

Annabeth moved her pencil to the other side of the page. “I’m still deciding whether I want to hear him.”

Ianthe looked at her. “You can come and not talk.”

“I can also not come.”

“I know.”

“What do you want?”

“I want you there,” Ianthe said after a pause. “But if you don’t want to be there, I don’t want you to pretend.”

Annabeth nodded. “That’s fair.”

She didn’t promise she’d go. Ianthe didn’t ask for a second answer.

At lunch, they sat at a corner of the pavilion table while Percy and two campers set out pitchers. The sky was brighter over the dining area, but the pressure stayed under Thalia’s skin. She kept her brace on and chose the chair nearest the aisle.

“I’ll go,” Annabeth said once they had eaten. “I want to hear what he says.”

“I’ll be near the entrance,” Percy said. “If you want me inside, tell me.”

“Thank you.”

He brushed a crumb from her sleeve. She caught his fingers, held them for a second, then let go. Their ease did not erase the choice she was making. It made room for it.

Ianthe brought her weather notebook. “I want to show him the notes. I marked which ones aren’t verified.”

“I saw,” Thalia said.

“Are you going to tell him they aren’t good enough?”

“I’m not responsible for your notebook.”

“You’re responsible for everything else he says?”

“No.”

“Then let me answer him.”

“I will.”

Ianthe gave a short nod. She was still holding the book too tightly.

Thalia stood and waited until her heel settled under the brace. It tugged once, low and familiar. She used the cane Chiron had left near the door and did not turn that small decision into a declaration. She could stand for the visit or leave if she needed to.

Annabeth walked beside her without taking her arm.



The pavilion doors were open. Chiron had cleared one of the end tables and left the center of the room empty. A pitcher of water sat between two cups. No one had set out a place card or put a chair at the head.

Thalia chose the seat nearest the side exit. Annabeth sat across from her, Ianthe beside her. Percy waited outside with Chiron, visible through the open doors if Annabeth looked toward the path.

The clouds bunched above the valley. The air smelled of wet stone before the first drop fell. A brief flash whitened the hill, followed by thunder so close that a few younger campers stopped in the path.

Then the sky cleared.

The man who walked into the pavilion wore a green raincoat with the collar turned down. He looked somewhere in his thirties, with a narrow face, straight dark hair cut close at the sides, and no beard. His brown eyes were unremarkable until they settled on the table. No glasses, no gray beard, no broad-shouldered outline that Thalia remembered from the shore. No face she could match to the last visit.

For a second, Ianthe did not move. Then she stood.

“Father.”

His smile came easily. “Ianthe.”

The air in the room tightened around the syllables. Thalia recognized the pressure before she recognized the voice. The new face did not change the person who chose which daughter to look at first.

He kissed Ianthe's forehead and held her shoulders for a moment. She stayed close to him. Her notebook lay on the table beside her chair, but she did not reach for it.

“It’s good to see you,” he said.

“I’m glad you came.”

“Of course I came.”

He turned toward the others. “Thalia. Annabeth.”

“Zeus,” Thalia said.

His mouth shifted. “You can call me Father.”

“I know.”

Annabeth dipped her chin. “Hello.”

He took the chair opposite Ianthe, not at the head. The raincoat stayed buttoned. He looked at the water pitcher, then at the notebook.

“You’ve been keeping records,” he said.

“I’ve been writing down wind direction and time.”

“Good. Precision is a habit worth keeping.”

Ianthe’s shoulders eased. She opened the notebook and turned it so he could see. “I added the source and marked which readings still need comparison.”

Zeus read the page. His attention seemed to rest on the small note at the margin. “You know the difference between an observation and a conclusion.”

“Beckendorf told me to compare the readings with the flag line.”

“Then you listened.”

“I did.”

“That’s rare.”

Thalia felt Annabeth’s eyes lift from the cup. Ianthe had written the note correctly. Beckendorf had given her the standard. The praise still sounded as if he had found a rare quality in a daughter he had barely watched.

Zeus tapped the notebook with one finger. “A mind that can listen before it decides is useful. You could make something of that.”

“I already am,” Ianthe said.

“I can tell.”

He smiled at her, and for a moment the table held only the two of them. Thalia knew that warmth. It had been there when Zeus praised Annabeth for being agreeable, when he asked about her report before he asked how she was, when he appeared under another face and expected the conversation to begin clean.

“Your sisters have never been very good at that,” Zeus said. He kept his attention on Ianthe as though the remark were a private compliment they could enjoy together.

Thalia felt Annabeth go still across the table. That was the first warning, before the tightness in the air or the familiar ache gathering behind Thalia's eyes. She knew this trick. He could turn any kindness into a contest and somehow leave everyone who objected looking ungrateful. He'd done it when they were small enough to think a father's praise ought to be uncomplicated. Now he was doing it with a weather notebook and a girl who had been at camp barely long enough to know which path flooded after rain.

“Never been good at what?” Thalia asked, though she knew perfectly well.

“Listening when someone has more experience than they do. Ianthe, on the other hand, takes correction seriously.” Zeus turned another page, tapping the penciled caveat with one finger. “She understands that an observation is only as useful as the care behind it.”

Ianthe looked pleased. It was a quick, unguarded expression that Thalia almost wished she hadn't seen, because a moment later the girl caught Thalia looking and folded back into herself. She'd worked hard on the notes. She deserved to hear that they were good, and Thalia resented her father for making even that feel dangerous. Across the table, Annabeth laid down her cup slowly enough that it didn't make a sound.

“Ianthe's done good work,” Annabeth said. “Beckendorf helped her set up the comparisons. It's getting more reliable.”

Zeus's eyes moved to her. “You've always had a gift for finding the flaw in a compliment.”

“I wasn't finding a flaw. I was telling you who helped.”

“And that is precisely what I mean.” His pleasant expression remained in place, which made the dismissal worse. “Some people can accept encouragement. Others insist on making a discussion out of it.”

Thalia remembered Annabeth at twelve, carefully practicing the right answer for a visit she'd pretended not to care about. They hadn't been able to predict whether Zeus would ask about her lessons, her powers, or the way she'd spoken to somebody at dinner. What Thalia remembered most vividly was how Annabeth had gone quiet afterward, convinced she should have found a better sentence. Thalia had spent years trying to make sure no one talked to her little sister like that again. She had failed at it more often than she liked to admit.

“Then talk to her,” Thalia said, gesturing toward Ianthe. “Ask her what she's been doing. You came to see her, didn't you?”

Zeus gave her a brief, cold look. “I was speaking to her until you interrupted.”

“No, you were speaking *about* us. There's a difference.”

It was the wrong thing to say. She knew it when the air shifted and the water in the pitcher trembled against the glass. Annabeth glanced at her, not for rescue, but because she knew the exact moment Thalia had gone from irritated to reckless. Thalia hated that look almost as much as she loved her for it. She unclenched her fist under the table, where Zeus couldn't see what it had cost.

“Thalia,” Annabeth said, quietly enough that only she could hear.

“I'm fine.” The lie came out before Thalia could stop it.

Ianthe closed the notebook, trapping her pencil between its pages. “Actually, I was going to ask you something, Father. About the last pressure reading. You said the sea wind can change before the clouds do, and I wanted to know if you'd—”

“Yes,” Zeus said, without looking at the page. “A sound principle. You'll understand it better once you've worked with people who have more discipline.”

Ianthe's lips parted. She had been about to ask a different question; Thalia could tell from the startled annoyance that crossed her face. But she swallowed it, and there it was again: the old family habit of deciding which parts of yourself were worth showing him. Thalia couldn't blame her. She remembered doing it too.

“I was asking whether you'd seen the change on the eastern ridge,” Ianthe tried again. “The flags didn't match, and Beckendorf said the source might be wrong.”

“Then you should listen to Beckendorf. He appears to know his work.”

“I did listen. I wanted to know what *you* thought.”

For the first time since entering the pavilion, Zeus seemed caught without an answer ready. It lasted hardly a second. “I think you ought to be proud of how much you've accomplished here. Your sisters could learn a little from your willingness to improve.”

The notebook lay closed beneath Ianthe's hand. Thalia watched her thumb move over the cover, tracing the edge of a mark she'd made herself. It was not a great betrayal, what Zeus had done. He'd come when he'd said he would. He'd kissed his daughter and praised her work. There were families for whom that would have been a good afternoon. Thalia could see Ianthe struggling to keep it one.

“Could you stop doing that?” Ianthe asked.

Zeus raised his brows. “Doing what?”

“Bringing them into it. I was telling you about the ridge.” Her voice had tightened. “I wanted you to remember what I'd written. I didn't ask you to compare us.”

Something changed in his face then, although the face itself was new to Thalia. The warmth went away from his eyes. “You are beginning to sound like your sisters.”

“I haven't been around them long enough for that to be their fault,” Ianthe said, and Thalia was so surprised she nearly laughed. She caught it behind her teeth. Ianthe wasn't trying to be funny. She had started to look frightened of what she'd just said.

Zeus glanced around the pavilion, as though checking who might have overheard. Through the open doors Chiron remained on the path with Percy. A few campers were crossing the green beyond them, heads bent against the wind that seemed to be rising for no reason. “I came because I wanted to know you were well,” Zeus told Ianthe. “I thought you would be glad.”

“I am glad.” Ianthe's reply was immediate. “I told you that when you arrived.”

“Yet here we are, arguing.”

“Because you keep telling me what I think.”

Thalia's throat tightened. It was an ordinary accusation from a fifteen-year-old to a parent. In any ordinary room it would have been the beginning of a sulk or a long argument over dinner. Here, the rafters made a small, dry crackle, and the cup at Annabeth's elbow began to tremble on its saucer. Zeus had not raised his voice. He didn't need to. Thalia rested both hands on her cane, partly to anchor herself and partly because she didn't trust what she'd do with them otherwise.

He turned away from Ianthe and looked at the sisters. “Perhaps you would both care to explain why she is still living in the guest rooms. I was under the impression that my daughters shared a cabin.”

“We do,” Annabeth said. “Ianthe's going to have space in it. We're sorting out where the cot fits, and the window still leaks when the rain comes from the west.”

“A cot.” Zeus repeated the word as if it were an insult. “You're making your sister wait for a cot.”

Annabeth looked at the rim of her cup. Thalia recognized what she was doing: counting the things she could explain, deciding which details he might use against her, trying to keep the conversation narrow enough that it wouldn't break apart. “There's room for her belongings and we want her there. But Ianthe needs to decide what she wants too. I'm not putting her in a bed because you want a quick answer.”

Zeus gave a humorless little laugh. “You have always managed to turn the simplest family matters into a design project.”

“Maybe because I live in the room,” Annabeth said. Her tone was level, but Thalia saw the red rise along the edge of her ears. “It's easy to tell us what should happen when you won't be there for it.”

The cup jumped hard enough to spill water across the table. Ianthe flinched. Thalia felt the impact of that movement more sharply than the sound of the glass. She'd seen Zeus angry before, and she knew how quickly a room could become unsafe when he decided respect had not been offered quickly enough. The old impulse surged through her, to put herself between him and Annabeth, to make him look at the older sister first. She started to rise and felt the heel pull beneath her brace. Annabeth's gaze met hers, steady and furious, and Thalia forced herself back into the chair.

“You should consider how you speak to me,” Zeus said.

Annabeth's fingers were flat on the table. “You asked why the room isn't ready. I answered.”

“And now you're accusing me of neglecting you.”

Thalia could see how badly Annabeth wanted to say *yes*. She knew because she wanted to say it too, wanted to drag every lonely birthday and every carefully phrased letter into the room and make him look at the damage without being able to call it insolence. Annabeth breathed once, then reached for a cloth and began soaking up the spilled water. The act of cleaning it seemed to irritate him more than another argument would have.

Ianthe pulled the notebook clear of the wet patch. “Father, please. This wasn't what I wanted.”

He turned to her, and the pressure lessened just enough that Thalia could unclench her jaw. “What did you want, then? You've been given a place at this camp, capable people to teach you, and a visit from me. I can't make every part of your life easy.”

“I wasn't asking you to.” Her voice caught, and she cleared her throat angrily. “I wanted to show you the notes. That's all. I worked on them because you remembered the last ones.”

Zeus looked at her for a long moment. His expression softened in a way Thalia had learned to distrust, though she knew Ianthe might still want to believe it. “And I did remember. I am proud of the care you took.” He reached for the notebook, but Ianthe drew it back before his fingers touched the cover. He noticed. His hand settled on the table instead. “If you'd rather turn a pleasant visit into a grievance, I won't stop you.”

“That's unfair,” Thalia said. She couldn't bear it anymore.

“Life has never promised to be fair to any of my children.”

There it was, the ancient pronouncement that always seemed to end the argument for him. Thalia felt fifteen again herself, raging at a voice in the sky that could make a punishment sound like a lesson. She remembered how Annabeth used to wait behind her after those encounters, too frightened to be angry until Thalia was angry first. That was part of why Thalia had so much trouble stepping aside now. She was afraid that if she gave up the role of shield, some part of their childhood would happen all over again.

Ianthe's eyes had filled, though she looked determined not to let anyone see. “I'm not saying I hate you,” she said to Zeus. “I'm not even saying I wish you hadn't come. I just wish you would stop telling me what I'm supposed to be grateful for.”

The silence was worse than the thunder. Zeus studied her, then looked at Annabeth and finally at Thalia, as if searching for the person responsible for a conversation he'd lost control of. His new face had made him look younger when he arrived. Now it had the same exhausted contempt Thalia remembered in every one of his disguises.

“You'll understand me better when you're older,” he said.

“I might,” Ianthe answered. “But I'm here now.”

For one ridiculous moment Thalia wanted to cheer. She was also terrified. Those feelings arrived together, and she could do nothing useful with either. Outside the door Percy had turned at the sound of the cup, but Chiron's hand rested lightly on his shoulder and he stayed where Annabeth had asked him to wait. Thalia was grateful for that too, and resentful that she was grateful, and so tired of calculating which kinds of love Zeus might punish.

He rose. The chair legs scraped against the floorboards. “I came because I wished to see my daughter. I hope that much will be remembered.”

Ianthe held the closed notebook against her chest. She looked young in a way Thalia hadn't allowed herself to notice before. “I'll remember it,” she said. Then, after a painful pause: “I'll remember the rest too.”

Zeus's mouth tightened. For a second the air tasted like metal and the shadows beneath the rafters went sharply black. Then the pressure lifted. He straightened his raincoat, made a small, dismissive gesture toward the table, and looked out at the path as though the weather interested him more than his children.

“I will leave you to your conversation,” he said.

Zeus stepped outside. A low roll of thunder moved east over the cabins, quieter than the first arrival. He did not leave a gift, a promise, or a new instruction. Chiron walked with him as far as the path, then stopped. The rain had never started.

Ianthe stayed seated. Her notebook remained closed beneath her hands.

Annabeth lifted her cup. “Do you want us to go?”

“No.” Ianthe’s answer came fast. She looked at Thalia, then amended it. “I mean, you can. But I don’t want you to feel like you have to.”

“We can stay,” Thalia said. “We can also leave.”

“I know.”

Thalia heard the echo of her own phrase and wondered whether Ianthe used it to be kind or to close the subject. She did not ask.

Annabeth stood. “I’ll check on Percy. I told him I might want him nearby.”

“Do you want me to come?” Thalia asked.

“No. I want you to stay if you want to stay.”

Thalia nodded. Annabeth passed the doorway where Percy waited. He did not ask her what had happened. He offered his hand, and she took it as they walked toward the green.

The space she left behind did not feel empty. It felt chosen.

Thalia turned to Ianthe. “I can wait outside too.”

“I don’t want you to disappear just because Father spoke sharply.”

“I won’t.”

“You also don’t have to watch me to see if I’m upset.”

Thalia stopped. “I was doing that?”

“You watched every time he looked at me.”

“I wanted to know if you were all right.”

“I know. It felt like you were checking whether I was going to make the wrong choice.”

Thalia’s hand tightened on the cane. She loosened it. “I was watching too closely.”

“I can tell you when I need help.”

“Yes.”

Ianthe looked down at the notebook. “I did want him to come.”

“I know.”

“And it was good to hear he was proud of me.”

Thalia’s throat pulled tight, though she did not know why the words caught there. “I’m glad he said something you wanted to hear.”

“Then why do you look like that?”

“Because he used it to talk about you and them as if you were proof that they were wrong.”

Ianthe’s mouth pressed into a line. “I didn’t ask him to.”

“I know.”

“But I liked it before he said that.”

“You’re allowed to.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yes.”

“Even if you didn’t get that from him?”

The question did not sound cruel. It sounded as if she were trying to hold the two versions of her father in the same hand. Thalia could not make them fit for her.

“I wanted him to be different,” she said. “I still do.”

Ianthe looked up. “So you still want him to be proud of you.”

“I wanted a lot of things when I was younger.”

“That doesn’t answer.”

“No,” Thalia said. “It doesn’t.”

The room quieted. The notebook sat between them, closed and flat. Thalia thought about the note: *if they were willing*. Those words had been true. They had chosen to come. The invitation had not told them what to feel when he arrived.

Ianthe opened the cover and turned to the page with the wind readings. “I still think the older people here spend too long deciding what to do.”

“Sometimes they do.”

“I think the cot has taken longer than it needs to.”

“Maybe.”

“I think you let your history make every new thing suspicious.”

“Maybe.”

“That’s not agreement.”

“No. It means I can’t answer the whole question right now.”

Ianthe took a breath. “You can’t ask me to treat him like a stranger just because he’s been inconsistent with you.”

“I didn’t.”

“You acted like I was missing something.”

“I was trying to keep from explaining too much.”

“Those can feel the same.”

Thalia looked at the table. “You’re right.”

“I didn’t say you were wrong.”

“No. You said what it felt like.”

Ianthe was quiet. Then she turned the notebook to the page before the wind notes. On it, she had drawn the flag line beside a small arrow pointing north. The mark was neat and unverified.

“I want to show him that again next time,” she said. “If he comes.”

“If he does.”

“You don’t have to say it like that.”

“I’m not making a prediction.”

“You could try to sound less certain he won’t.”

Thalia looked at the page. “I can try.”

It was not reassurance. It was a promise she could keep: to watch her own tone, and let Ianthe want the next visit.



They left the pavilion by different paths. Thalia waited on the steps while Ianthe took the notebook to the Big House. She did not follow. Annabeth had gone to find Percy, and Chiron remained inside to put away the cups.

Ianthe stopped at the path fork. “I don’t want you to tell me what the visit means.”

“I won’t.”

“You say that, and then you watch me like you’re waiting for me to figure it out.”

“I’m sorry. I was doing that.”

Ianthe looked down at the notebook. “At home, he used to ask about what I noticed in storms. He didn’t always stay long, but sometimes he’d ask me to show him a page. He remembered what the arrows meant.”

Thalia listened. She could see the green raincoat disappearing behind the Big House. She could also see the places where Ianthe’s fingers had kept the page flat.

“I liked that,” Ianthe said. “I liked that he paid attention to something I cared about.”

“I believe you.”

“Then don’t look at it like it was a trick.”

“I don’t know what it was for you.”

“That’s why I’m telling you.”

Thalia lowered her eyes. “I made my history the frame for yours.”

“Yes.”

“I didn’t mean to.”

“I know. That doesn’t make it easier.”

“No.”

Ianthe opened the notebook to the page with her wind notes. “When he praised me, it felt good. Then he said I was different from you and Annabeth. I didn’t want to lose the good part just because he used it to be unfair.”

“You don’t have to lose it.”

“What if I still want him to think I’m special?”

Thalia took a breath. “Then you want that.”

“That’s all?”

“That’s all I can say.”

Ianthe watched her, waiting. Thalia did not add a warning or a prediction. After a moment, the notebook closed.

“Sometimes you make it sound like wanting him is a mistake,” Ianthe said.

“I don’t want it to sound like that.”

“But it does.”

Thalia thought of the time she had been small enough to look at the sky for a sign before she had learned that clouds did not tell her whether her father would come. She did not give that memory to Ianthe as proof.

“I’ll try to stop doing that,” she said.

“I want to talk to him again. Maybe alone next time.”

“That’s your choice.”

“Would you come if I asked?”

“Yes.”

“And if I don’t?”

“I’ll stay here.”

Ianthe nodded once. It was not a hug or a promise that they understood each other. It was a plan for what she could ask next time, if there was one.

“Don’t tell Annabeth I said that,” she added.

“I won’t.”

“Good.”

She started toward the Big House. Thalia let her go alone. The storm had moved away from the cabins, leaving the air damp and bright. There was no need to chase the clearing sky.



Annabeth came back to the porch after she had spoken with Percy. She closed the door but did not sit.

“You didn’t step in,” she said.

“I was about to.”

“But you didn’t.”

“No.”

“Thank you.”

Thalia looked down at her cane. “I wanted to.”

“I know.”

“I thought you might not want to answer him alone.”

“I didn’t. I wanted you nearby.”

Thalia nodded. “I can do nearby.”

Annabeth leaned against the wall. “When he called me pleasant, it made me feel twelve again.”

Thalia felt the words press against the back of her teeth. “I’m sorry.”

“It was his sentence. You don’t have to apologize for it.”

“I know. I’m sorry he said it.”

Annabeth’s mouth softened. “That’s different.”

Thalia settled into the chair by the doorway. The brace tugged when she bent her knee; she adjusted her foot and let the feeling pass. “I wanted to tell him he did this before.”

“You have.”

“And it didn’t change anything.”

“No.” Annabeth looked out toward the green. “It still mattered that you said it.”

Thalia watched Percy walk back toward the dining pavilion. He had kept his promise to stay near the path. He had not followed Annabeth into the conversation or tried to fix what she had said.

“He’s learning,” Annabeth said, following her gaze.

“He is.”

“So are you.”

“I’m trying.”

Annabeth sat across from her. “You didn’t need to choose between defending me and letting me answer.”

“I know that now.”

“Do you?”

Thalia smiled faintly. “I’ll know the next time.”

“That’s more accurate.”

They sat for a few minutes without talking. Annabeth’s shoulder rested against the wall. Thalia’s hand lay open on her knee. Neither tried to turn the quiet into a solution.

When Annabeth finally stood, she held out her hand to help Thalia up, then waited.

Thalia took it. The pull through her heel stayed dull and familiar. She put her weight through her left leg first and rose without making the moment about the injury.

Annabeth did not ask if it hurt.

Thalia did not pretend it didn’t.

---

At dinner, Ianthe brought her notebook to the table but kept it closed. Percy had saved a seat beside Annabeth. Thalia chose the one at the end, where she could stretch her leg without blocking the aisle.

Ianthe sat across from them. She listened to the meal conversation for a while before turning to a younger camper who had set a bowl between the water pitcher and a stack of plates.

“You can’t put that there,” she said. “It blocks the serving line.”

The camper looked at the bowl. “It’s empty.”

“It will be full in a minute.”

“I’m the one carrying it.”

Ianthe reached for the bowl anyway. “Then let me move it.”

“Please don’t.”

Her hand stopped above the rim. Ianthe glanced at Thalia, as if she expected her to tell the younger camper that moving the bowl was sensible.

Thalia kept her mouth closed.

The camper carried the bowl to the far end of the table and set it down. The path cleared. Nothing spilled.

Ianthe withdrew her hand. “That would have been faster.”

“Maybe,” the camper said. “I knew where I was taking it.”

He walked away to fetch the food.

Ianthe turned to her plate. “People here turn everything into a negotiation.”

“Sometimes it keeps us from moving something that isn’t ours,” Annabeth said.

“It was a bowl.”

“It was his bowl to carry.”

Ianthe’s cheeks colored. She opened the notebook, then closed it again.

Thalia wanted to make the moment smaller, to tell her that they all got things wrong. She did not. Ianthe could decide whether to speak to the camper again. No one had made the mistake impossible to repair.

Percy leaned toward Annabeth. “Do you want the last piece?”

“Which one?”

“The one that’s not burned.”

“That’s all of them.”

“I checked the bottom.”

Annabeth took the piece he offered, broke it in half, and returned a smaller piece to him. Percy accepted it without comment. Their knees touched under the table; neither shifted away.

The camper came back with the bowl. Ianthe moved her notebook to make space, then pulled it back before it touched the rim.

“Sorry,” she said. “I’ll keep this on my side.”

“Thanks.”

That was all. He served himself and carried the bowl to the next table.

Ianthe’s shoulders were still tight. She had apologized for the notebook, not for moving his bowl. Thalia did not ask her to finish the thought. A mistake could be partial without becoming nothing.



Ianthe came back to Cabin One the next morning with a folded page in her hand. The Big House guest room had not changed; the bed and window-side place in Cabin One remained an unfinished discussion.

“Can you read this?” she asked.

“Do you want me to?”

“Yes. But don’t tell me what I should say.”

Thalia took the page. Ianthe had written only four lines. She wanted to speak with Zeus again. She wanted him to ask about something other than her wind notes. She did not want him to compare her with her sisters.

The words were plain. The last sentence had been underlined and then crossed out, lightly enough that it remained legible.

“Do you want help with the wording?” Thalia asked.

“No.”

“Then it says what you mean.”

Ianthe took the page back. “Do you think it sounds rude?”

“I think it sounds direct.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“No.”

Ianthe considered the crossed-out line. “I was going to ask if you’d pass it to him.”

“Do you want me to?”

“I don’t know. If I ask Chiron, it’ll become official.”

“It doesn’t have to become anything today.”

“You always say things can wait.”

“I’m trying not to tell you what to do.”

“Sometimes waiting is how people decide for you.”

Thalia could not argue with that. She had wanted to leave room for Ianthe’s choice and had not considered how often an open question could feel like another delay.

“You’re right,” she said.

Ianthe folded the note once, then unfolded it. “I’ll keep it for now. I want him to hear it. I don’t know if I want you to send it.”

“Okay.”

“Don’t tell Annabeth.”

“I won’t.”

“And don’t tell yourself I changed my mind about him.”

“I won’t.”

Ianthe slid the page between two blank sheets in her weather notebook. The message stayed with her, unread by anyone else.

They stood together by the table. The cards from the game were put away, but the board still sat on the shelf with one corner slightly bent. Thalia reached up to straighten it, then stopped.

Ianthe looked at the board. “It was crooked.”

“It was.”

“You let it stay that way.”

“It still worked.”

Ianthe laughed once. “That’s a terrible reason.”

“It’s one reason.”

She did not take the board down. The small argument was over. Neither of them needed to win it.



The next morning Ianthe came back to Cabin One before breakfast. She carried the weather notebook, but she did not open it.

“Can I sit here for a while?” she asked.

“You can,” Annabeth said.

Ianthe looked past her at the bunks. “I mean, in the room. Not just for a game.”

Thalia heard the question beneath it. It was not the same as asking for the window-side bed. The room had a history before she arrived, and the bed still needed a decision. Neither fact answered whether she could sit with them now.

“You can stay for breakfast,” Thalia said. “The room is open.”

“I know you let me in.”

“Okay.”

“I don’t want that to sound like I’m accusing you.”

“It didn’t.”

Annabeth set her notebook aside. “Do you want breakfast here, or do you want to talk about the room?”

“Breakfast. I don’t want to go to the Big House every time I want to be with you.”

“That makes sense,” Annabeth said.

The easy answer did not solve anything. Ianthe still had a guest room. The cot, latch, and window-side space remained unmeasured. But she sat at the table and unwrapped a piece of bread from a napkin.

Thalia poured three cups of tea. Ianthe moved the notebook to the far edge, then left the center of the table clear.

“You don’t have to make the table symmetrical,” Annabeth said.

“I’m not.”

“It’s fine either way.”

Ianthe looked at the cups, then at the line she had made between them. “It helps me think.”

“Then keep it,” Thalia said.

They ate. No one asked Ianthe to explain Zeus again. No one asked whether she had decided to send the note. Her decision remained with her.

After breakfast, Annabeth went to return the cups. Ianthe opened the notebook to the page she had shown Zeus and ran a finger along the margin where she had written *unverified*. She did not cross it out.

“Do you want to check the flag line with Beckendorf?” Thalia asked.

“I want to know when he’s available.”

“That’s a question you can ask him.”

“I know.”

“Sorry.”

“For what?”

“Making it sound like I thought you couldn’t.”

Ianthe closed the notebook. “You do that sometimes.”

“I’ll watch for it.”

“I don’t need you to watch me.”

“I mean I’ll watch what I’m doing.”

“That’s different.”

Thalia nodded. “Yes.”

The difference mattered. It was small and still easy to miss. Thalia did not turn the moment into a lesson or ask Ianthe to agree that she had changed. Ianthe did not give her credit for trying.

When Annabeth returned, she looked from one to the other. “Are we going to the work list?”

“I am,” Ianthe said. “I want to ask about the wind check.”

“We can walk with you,” Thalia said.

Ianthe pulled the notebook closer. “You can come if you want. I don’t need an escort.”

“We know.”

The three of them left Cabin One together. Thalia took the path at an easy pace and kept her cane in her right hand. Annabeth matched her stride without taking her arm. Ianthe walked half a step ahead, then slowed when she noticed she had pulled in front.

No one pointed it out. She glanced back once, adjusted her pace, and continued toward the equipment shed.

She had not turned into an easy sister overnight. The cabin arrangement was still open, her notes still needed comparison, and she still wanted Zeus to come back and tell her she mattered. Thalia could not decide what those things meant for her.

For now, the girl walked beside them and kept her own notebook. The path was wide enough for all three.


At the equipment shed, Beckendorf was recording the latest moisture check. The two coils remained on their spacers, the outer fibers dry and the center layers still damp. He wrote the result in the margin rather than turning the line into a completion mark.

Ianthe waited until he put the pencil down.

“Could I compare my wind notes to the flag line now?”

“Not while I’m checking the coils.”

“It only takes a minute to look at the flag.”

“It takes longer to compare the time, direction, and source.”

“I already wrote the time.”

“I know. I’m checking the line against the record.”

Ianthe’s notebook was open in her hands. “If we wait until the coils are dry, the weather may have changed.”

“The old notes will still be there.”

“It would be more useful to do it now.”

Beckendorf glanced at her, then at the coils. “I have two wet centers, one rack cover out of service, and an open fence inspection. I can’t leave those to check your page.”

The words were even. They did not make Ianthe’s suggestion wrong, but they did not give it priority.

Thalia stood near the door. She could feel Ianthe waiting for her to agree that the work list was crowded or that the check should be quick. She kept her hand on the cane and let Beckendorf answer for his own time.

Ianthe shut the notebook. “When will you be available?”

“When the coils pass their next check and the rack cover is safe. Ask me then.”

“Do you have a day in mind?”

“Not yet.”

Ianthe started to speak, stopped, and looked at the pencil mark beside the damp center. “I’ll write down that it’s still pending.”

“Good,” Beckendorf said. “Keep the source with it.”

“I did.”

“Then we’ll have what we need when we compare.”

She wrote the line beneath her earlier note. The pencil moved slowly, and she erased one word before settling on *ask again after moisture check*. It was not the answer she wanted. It was still more accurate than adding her wind reading to the work list and calling it verified.

She turned the page so Thalia could see. “I didn’t ask you to tell him to do it.”

“No.”

“I wanted you to know that.”

“I know.”

“I don’t need your approval either.”

“I didn’t give any.”

Ianthe lowered her notebook. “You’re good at that.”

“At what?”

“Not answering.”

“I can answer if you ask me what I think.”

“I don’t know if I want that.”

“Then you don’t have to ask yet.”

She looked toward the drying rack. The repaired canvas seam was folded and stored, the separate cover loop laid flat where it would not carry weight. The board remained outside, still waiting for firmer ground. None of those jobs had become urgent simply because she wanted a clear line on the page.

“Let’s go,” Ianthe said.

Thalia followed her out. She did not tell her that asking the question had been the right choice. Ianthe had not asked for a grade.

---

That evening the rain finally came. It tapped against the Cabin One window, then ran in a thin line along the lower edge of the frame. A draft lifted the corner of Annabeth’s map.

Annabeth weighted the page with her pencil. “The latch still isn’t holding.”

“I thought Chiron said it could wait until the next weather check,” Thalia said.

“He did.”

“It just got one.”

“He said the frame needed a second person to test it. This isn’t a test.”

Thalia watched the water trace down the glass. The room was warm. The map corner lifted again.

Ianthe stood in the doorway, her bag on her shoulder. She had returned from the Big House after dinner. “I can put a towel there.”

“We can,” Annabeth said. “But we shouldn’t press anything into the frame until we know where the gap is.”

“It’s coming from the bottom.”

“It might be. I don’t want to guess.”

“I’m not asking to change the latch. I’m trying to keep the floor dry.”

Thalia felt the air between them tighten. Annabeth looked at the lower sill; Ianthe watched her.

“You can help me hold the towel,” Annabeth said. “Don’t press it under the latch.”

Ianthe came in and set her bag down. “Show me where.”

Annabeth pointed to the dry strip of floor beneath the window. Ianthe held the towel there while Annabeth checked the water path from the outside edge. The cloth caught the first drops. No one put pressure on the latch. The work took less than a minute.

“Thanks,” Annabeth said.

“I didn’t fix anything.”

“You helped keep the floor dry.”

Ianthe folded the wet corner of the towel inward. “The repair still needs a second person.”

“Tomorrow, if the ground near the frame has dried enough.”

“Are you going to ask Thalia?”

“If she’s free.”

Thalia had already decided she would help, but she waited. Annabeth had not asked her yet. Her sister turned from the window.

“Will you be around in the morning?”

“Yes.”

“Then we can check it together.”

“Okay.”

Ianthe stood beside the table. “I can help too.”

“After we see what the frame needs,” Annabeth said. “I don’t know yet whether another set of hands will help.”

Ianthe’s face tightened. For a second, Thalia thought she would tell Annabeth that she had already held the towel and could do the rest. Instead she picked up her bag.

“I’ll ask in the morning.”

“That works.”

She left for the Big House without saying whether the answer pleased her. The room stayed dry. The latch stayed unresolved.

Thalia shut the door behind her, then sat down carefully. Rain moved over the glass in uneven lines. Annabeth set the map back on the table and turned it so the damp edge faced away from the window.

Neither sister tried the latch again that night.


In the morning the window was dry, but the latch still sat a fraction above its catch. Annabeth checked it from inside without forcing it down. The towel remained folded on the sill.

Ianthe arrived carrying her notebook and a cup of tea. “Do you want another person to hold the frame while you look?”

“Maybe,” Annabeth said. “I need to see where it catches first.”

“I can hold it steady.”

“Not yet.”

Ianthe set down the tea. Her fingers hovered over the lower sash before she pulled them back. “I wasn’t going to move it.”

“I know.”

“You said not yet.”

“I did.”

Ianthe looked at the latch, then at Annabeth. “Okay.”

The restraint was visible in the way she kept her hands at her sides. Thalia saw her look toward the toolbox, where the small screwdrivers were stored. No one had asked her to get them.

“Chiron said it needs two people to test the frame,” Annabeth said. “One to hold the sash and one to check the catch.”

“I can do either.”

“Let’s see what we have to measure first.”

Ianthe’s mouth tightened, but she picked up the notebook instead of the tools. “I can record the measurements if you want.”

“After I make them.”

“Of course.”

Annabeth looked through the gap by the latch without touching it. The gap was too narrow to see where the water had traveled. She took out the paper strip and used it to mark the position.

“Can you hold the lamp?” she asked Thalia.

Thalia took it from the shelf. Its light warmed the lower frame. Annabeth leaned closer and checked the catch with a small wooden gauge. The measurement came out uneven.

“Could be the hinge,” Ianthe said.

“It could,” Annabeth replied. “I don’t know yet.”

Ianthe’s eyes moved to the hinge. “The top edge is lower.”

“Maybe. I’m recording it before I decide.”

Thalia held the lamp steady. She did not tell Ianthe she was right or wrong. The note would be useful if the source was clear; the repair could wait until they knew what the frame needed.

Annabeth finished the measurement and wrote it down. “That’s enough for this morning.”

“Are you going to fix it?” Ianthe asked.

“Not before I know which part is causing the gap.”

“Could the sash be pulled into line with a wedge?”

“Maybe. That would still leave the latch and hinge to check.”

Ianthe sat back. “It seems like more steps than necessary.”

“It might be.”

“Do you want me to help?”

“Not yet. I’ll ask if that changes.”

Ianthe nodded, though the answer did not satisfy her. She picked up her tea and moved to the other side of the table.

Thalia set the lamp down. “Thanks for bringing the notebook.”

“I wanted to be included.”

“You can be included without doing every part.”

“I know.”

She looked at the page with the wind observations. Her note to Zeus was folded between blank sheets, hidden under the cover. She did not take it out.

Annabeth put the gauge away. The window remained open a thumb’s width, held by a wedge so it would not swing. The frame had a measurement, not a repair. The next step could wait until they had the right tool and enough time to do it properly.

Ianthe stayed for breakfast. She did not ask whether Zeus would come back. Thalia did not ask whether she had decided to send the note.

They could leave those questions with her for now.


At breakfast, Annabeth set the measurement page beside the empty plate. Ianthe moved her notebook away from it, leaving the page in the center.

“Do you want this on the repair list?” Ianthe asked.

“After Chiron checks it.”

“I can mark that.”

“Could you put ‘measurement pending review’?”

Ianthe wrote the words at the bottom of her notebook, not on the shared list. She paused, then copied them to a loose strip and set it beside Annabeth’s page.

“Is that okay?” she asked.

“Yes.”

She didn’t add her name to the work list or draw a priority column. The strip was only a note, and it said what they knew.

Thalia poured another cup of tea. Ianthe took it without asking which cup was hers. Annabeth thanked her and continued eating.

“Do you think the latch will be easy to fix?” Ianthe asked.

“I don’t know yet,” Annabeth said.

“You say that a lot.”

“Because I don’t know.”

Ianthe lifted her cup. “I suppose it’s better than pretending.”

“Usually.”

The answer earned a small, reluctant smile. The page stayed on the table. The room assignment stayed open. Zeus had left without a promise to return, and Ianthe had not decided what to do with the note she had written.

Thalia could not make any of those things simple for her. She could sit at the table while Ianthe chose whether to talk. She could let Annabeth measure the window at her own pace. She could believe that Ianthe wanted their father to come back, even if she did not trust what he would do when he arrived.

For that morning, the three of them shared breakfast and a page of measurements. It was not a verdict on who belonged. It was the work they had chosen to do together.
