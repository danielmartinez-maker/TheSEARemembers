## Chapter 50: Half-Blood Hill

Percy woke before the conch.

It was still dark in Cabin Three, the kind of blue dark that came just before dawn, when the porthole over his bunk showed a square of sky going pale at the edges. The cabin smelled like salt and cedar and the lemon oil Silena used on the footlockers. Somewhere under the floor, the sea moved through the pipes, ticking as it warmed.

For a second Percy lay still and listened to his sisters breathe.

Clarisse was snoring in the bunk to his left, a low steady rumble that shook her blanket. Silena was curled on her side in the bunk to his right, hair in a loose braid, one hand tucked under her cheek, the other hanging off the mattress with fingers brushing the rug. The seashells on the ceiling caught the first light and threw little white sparks across the walls.

Percy stared at them and thought, this is the last morning.

Not forever. Chiron had said three weeks, maybe longer if the Mist was bad on the roads. But it felt like a last morning anyway. His duffel was packed and standing by the door. His spare socks were rolled the way Silena had taught him. Riptide was capped and in his pocket, heavy as a promise.

Today they left camp.

He sat up quietly and swung his legs over the side. The floor was cold. He found his jacket and pulled it on over his pajamas and went to sit by the porthole for a minute, knees up, forehead almost touching glass.

Outside, the strawberry fields were silver with dew. The Big House was dark except for one lamp in Chiron's office. The dragon at the garden gate was curled around the fencepost, smoke curling thin from its nose. Over the woods, the sky was turning pink.

Footsteps creaked behind him.

Silena sat down on the edge of his bunk in her pajamas, blanket around her shoulders. Her eyes were puffy with sleep. She did not say good morning. She just leaned her head against his shoulder.

"You are awake early," she whispered.

"Could not sleep," Percy whispered back.

"Me neither. I kept checking your bag in my head. Did you pack the ambrosia?"

"Side pocket. In the Altoids tin. Like you showed me."

"And the drachmas?"

"Wrapped in a sock. Inside the second sock. Inside the shoe."

Silena snorted softly. "Good. No pickpocket in the world is getting through double socks."

They sat like that. Clarisse snored on. Outside, a harpy cried once over the lake, sharp and far away.

"I made you something," Silena said. She pulled a folded square of blue cloth from her blanket pocket and pressed it into his hands. "Open it later. On the road. When you are bored or scared or Grover is chewing too loud."

Percy unfolded one corner. Inside was a neat packet of sea salt caramels wrapped in wax paper, and under them, a braided bracelet of blue and green thread, frayed at the ends.

"You made this?" he asked.

"This morning. While you were pretending to sleep. I am fast." Silena tied it around his left wrist with quick fingers, double knot, ends trimmed with her teeth. "So you have shore line with you. Blue for us. Green for the lake. Mom used to say sailors wore cord so the sea would know them."

Percy's throat went tight. "Silena—"

"Do not get mushy. Clarisse will wake up and make fun of us." But her eyes were bright. She bumped her forehead against his. "You be careful out there, little brother. Eat regular. Drink water that is actually water. Keep your socks dry. If you get hurt, you yell for Annabeth first and Thalia second and you do not try to be brave alone."

"I will," Percy said. "I promise."

"Liar. You will try to be brave alone and Annabeth will stop you. That is why I like her."

Behind them, Clarisse's snore stopped with a snort. She rolled over, blankets rustling, and sat up with her hair sticking up in its knot, eyes half open.

"You two are loud," she muttered. "It is still night. Go back to sleep."

"It is quest day," Silena said.

Clarisse blinked. Then she was awake all at once, swinging her legs down, bare feet hitting floor. She looked at Percy's duffel by the door. She looked at Percy in his jacket by the window. Her face did something complicated and then settled into a scowl.

"Right," she said. "Up. If you are leaving, you are leaving fed. I am not having the Furies tell stories about my brother starving on Route 25 because he skipped breakfast."

Percy smiled, relief flooding warm through his chest. "Yes, ma'am."

"Do not yes ma'am me." Clarisse stood and pulled her camp jacket over her pajamas and kicked his duffel lightly with her toe to check the weight. "This too heavy? What did you pack, rocks?"

"Three shirts. Two pants. Socks. Ambrosia. Nectar in the little bottle. Toothbrush. The shell Triton gave me. The caramels Silena just gave me. And the extra water bottle you told me to bring."

Clarisse nodded, satisfied despite herself. "Good. Light enough to run with. Heavy enough you will feel it and remember us."

She ruffled his hair so hard it hurt, and then she left it there a second longer than usual, hand heavy and warm on top of his head.

Silena stood and wrapped her blanket around all three of them as best she could, and for a minute Cabin Three was just warm and quiet and blue dark, sisters and brother breathing together before the conch blew.

---

By eight the whole camp knew.

Percy ate breakfast at the Poseidon table with everyone pretending not to crowd him. Beckendorf checked his straps. Tyson hugged him hard and handed him a peanut butter sandwich wrapped in three napkins for the road.

"For later," Tyson said fiercely, eyes wet. "When monsters are rude, you eat and get strong."

"I will," Percy said, tucking it carefully on top. "Thank you, big guy."

Annabeth was at the Zeus table with Thalia. Percy could see her nodding while Katie Gardner handed her trail snacks. Thalia had her arm slung around the back of Annabeth's chair, pretending to be bored while Chiron spoke to them.

Grover sat between tables, which was allowed on quest day, with a plate he was not eating. His legs bounced. His backpack was twice the size of anyone else's. He kept touching it to make sure it was still there.

After breakfast Chiron called the four of them to the Big House porch.

The porch smelled like coffee and old wood. Mr. D was there in his leopard shirt, doing a crossword and pretending not to care. Chiron was in his wheelchair form, blanket over his knees, eyes serious.

He gave them the formal part first. The words Percy had heard in stories but never thought would be said to him. You are sought. The road will test you. The gods will watch and mostly not help. Come back alive. Bring back what was taken. He gave them a leather pouch of drachmas, a folded map with bus routes marked in blue, and a small bronze compass that always pointed to the nearest safe water.

Then he dropped the formal voice.

He knelt to look Grover in the eye. "Trust your nose. Trust your feet. If the wild speaks, listen before you run."

Grover nodded fast. "Yes, sir. I will try. My knees are shaking, but I packed extra socks."

"Shaking knees can still walk," Chiron said gently. "Remember Pan's grove. Remember the music."

He turned to Thalia and Annabeth. His expression softened in a way Percy had only seen a few times, when Thalia had the flu last winter and Annabeth stayed up reading to her.

"Thalia. Annabeth." He took one of each of their hands. "Grace sisters. Look after each other the way you always have. Thalia, your spear is strong, but your steadiness will matter more. Annabeth, your plans are excellent, but allow the plan to bend."

Thalia swallowed hard. "We will."

Annabeth squeezed his hand. "We will come back."

Chiron looked at Percy last. "Percy Jackson. Son of my old friend." His eyes were very old and very kind. "The sea is with you even on dry land. Do not be afraid to ask for rain."

Percy nodded, throat too tight to speak. Chiron pulled him into a hug that smelled like horses and coffee, and Percy clung for a second longer than he meant to.

Mr. D looked up from his crossword. "Do not die," he said. "Paperwork is terrible. And bring me back a souvenir that is not a monster head. A snow globe. I like snow globes."

"We will try, sir," Annabeth said solemnly.

"Try harder for the snow globe than the not dying," Mr. D said, and went back to his puzzle, but as they stepped off the porch Percy saw him slip a second pouch into Grover's backpack when he thought no one was looking, and Grover's eyes went wide.

Outside, everyone who mattered was waiting on the lawn.

Clarisse and Silena were front and center. Tyson held a hand-painted sign that said GO QUEST in blue letters, upside down. Connor Stoll held it right side up for him.

Then Silena stepped forward with the thermos and handed it to Annabeth.

"One cup each at the top of the hill," she said. "Tradition now. We just made it. So it is real."

Annabeth hugged her tight. "Thank you."

Clarisse stepped up to Percy and shoved a small wrapped parcel into his duffel side pocket.

"Extra ambrosia," she muttered. "Do not tell Chiron. And this." She pulled her own red shoelace off her wrist — the one she wore for sparring luck — and tied it around his duffel handle. "So you do not lose your bag. It is ugly enough now you will notice it missing."

Percy laughed wetly. "Thanks, Clarisse."

She grabbed him by the front of his jacket and pulled him into a hug that smelled like iron and soap. "You listen to Thalia on the road," she said low in his ear, fierce. "She knows outside. You listen to Annabeth always, because she is smarter than you. You listen to Grover when he says run. And you come home. That is an order from your big sister."

"Yes, ma'am," Percy whispered.

Clarisse let go and stepped back fast, eyes shining, jaw set. Silena took her hand and held it hard, and Clarisse let her.

Thalia was saying goodbye to her own crowd, all elbows and jokes, but her eyes kept sliding toward the hill.

Percy knew why. He and Annabeth had told the others, though he doubted telling could make them feel it.

Half-Blood Hill. The pine tree. The place where Thalia Grace had died in the other life.

---

Before the walk to the border, Chiron asked them to empty their bags onto the picnic table behind the Big House.

Percy looked at the pile of supplies and felt betrayed. Silena had spent yesterday packing every sock, sandwich and strip of bandage with the confidence of someone who intended to defeat the entire western United States by organization alone.

"Everything?" he asked.

"Everything you plan to carry," Chiron said.

Thalia dumped a coil of rope onto the boards, followed by a bedroll, a first-aid tin and three packets of crackers. Annabeth lined her supplies up according to size, which Percy recognized as a delaying tactic. Grover produced so many small metal objects that Percy lost count somewhere between the cheese knife and the third emergency can.

"Why are you carrying a horseshoe?" Thalia asked.

"It's lucky."

"You're a satyr."

"That doesn't mean I can't appreciate good craftsmanship."

Chiron held it up, weighed it, and set it in the pile marked *leave behind*. Grover watched it go as though somebody had confiscated a friend.

The inspection wasn't about looking prepared. Chiron made Percy shoulder his bag, run the length of the porch and climb the low retaining wall behind the house without his water powers. When Percy landed, the straps had slipped, and the nectar bottle was pressing unpleasantly into his ribs.

"Reach for your sword," Chiron said.

Percy tried. The bag blocked his elbow.

"Again, after you adjust it."

They worked through the problem. A smaller pack made Riptide easier to reach. A higher strap balanced his weight. Percy moved the nectar to a padded interior pocket and secured his sword where it wouldn't fall when he needed both hands.

Annabeth's turn revealed that her mirror for looking around corners could shatter against her knife sheath. She wrapped it in a spare shirt. Thalia discovered her spear was easy to carry on an open field and nearly impossible to maneuver through a doorway without striking the frame. Grover's enormous bag made him stumble when he tried to turn sharply.

"Do we really need to do this in front of everyone?" Thalia asked after nearly breaking a window.

"Would you prefer to discover the problem while a monster is chasing you?" Chiron replied.

She scowled and tried again, shifting the spear point before she moved.

Percy was surprised by how differently Annabeth took the criticism. She didn't mind being told her packing was flawed. She asked how long they might travel without potable water, and which supplies could be abandoned if they had to run. Her notebook came out, followed by the list of capacities she'd prepared the night before.

"Do not write an answer that depends on a power Percy cannot guarantee," Chiron told her. "A lake may help him. An unfamiliar city might not."

"I know," Annabeth said, and changed a line.

Percy felt the little sting of becoming a variable on somebody else's chart. He also knew why she was doing it. A monster wouldn't wait for them to finish a plan. The difference between being able to use a power and promising it would always be available mattered.

Chiron called them around the table again.

"Three travelers named by the Oracle," he said, indicating Percy, Annabeth and Grover. "One authorized guardian." He turned to Thalia. "I have allowed you to accompany them because the alternative appears to be your leaving without permission. That does not give you authority to overrule every decision they make."

Thalia's jaw tightened. "I heard you yesterday."

"Then repeat it."

"Guardian. Not fourth named quester. I can intervene to protect them. I don't get to decide what the prophecy means."

Grover looked down at the equipment. "What if it means we shouldn't take her at all?"

"Then the Oracle may teach us that our interpretation was wrong," Chiron said. "We cannot eliminate uncertainty by pretending we know exactly what the words require."

Percy watched Thalia absorb that. She wasn't happy. Neither was Grover. Annabeth made a small mark beside her sister's name.

"Can we agree what happens if we disagree in a fight?" Percy asked.

"Before a fight," Annabeth corrected. "That's why we're doing this now."

They settled on a few rules. Grover would report what he sensed without being forced to prove immediately that a smell meant a monster. Annabeth would determine routes when there was time to consult a map. Immediate danger meant the first person who saw it shouted a warning. Thalia would take rear guard when the others were moving. Percy would not turn every problem into a challenge to fight alone merely because he had a sword.

"That last one is strangely specific," Percy said.

"Your sisters supplied it," Chiron replied.

From the grass nearby, Clarisse raised a hand without looking away from the shield she was polishing.

"You're welcome!"

Percy felt his ears heat.

They finished with fewer possessions and a clearer idea of what each person could do. Grover still mourned the horseshoe. Thalia accepted a shorter method of carrying her spear. Annabeth repacked the mirror herself, refusing help with the knot until she'd tried twice.

Percy lifted his pack. It felt lighter, but more importantly he knew where everything was. That was different from merely knowing who had put it there.

They started toward the hill only after Chiron had made each of them run the length of the porch one more time. Nobody fell. Grover made the turn without knocking over the potted plants.

"Now," Chiron said, "you may begin."

---

The walk to the hill was short and felt very long.

They went in a loose line up the dirt path past the cabins, past the arena, past the strawberry fields where the satyrs waved. The grass was wet. The air smelled like pine sap and coming rain.

Grover walked in front with Chiron, trying to look brave. Twice he stopped and sniffed the wind and then nodded to himself and kept going.

Annabeth walked beside Percy with her backpack high on her shoulders, her Yankees cap clipped to her belt, her bronze knife at her side. She kept reaching up to check her braid, a nervous habit. Her gray eyes were bright and fixed ahead.

Thalia walked a little behind them, spear in hand. She was quiet. That was unusual enough that Annabeth kept glancing back.

The pine tree came into view over the rise, tall and green against the sky. Beneath it stood the hilltop, and beyond that the highway.

"Hey, boy," Thalia whispered. Her voice cracked.

Peleus crooned and nudged her shoulder. Thalia leaned into him, forehead against scales, eyes closed.

Percy hung back with Annabeth and Grover while Chiron spoke quietly to the dragon and then to Thalia. He could not hear the words, but he saw Thalia nod, jaw working, and saw Chiron lay a hand on her shoulder.

In the other life — the one only Percy and Annabeth remembered, carrying it like a stone in their pockets — this hill was where it had happened. A girl with black hair running uphill with a satyr and two smaller kids behind her, monsters at her heels. She had turned at the top and held the line while the others ran for the border. She had died under this pine.

And now Thalia stood here alive, sixteen years old and tall and breathing, with a younger sister she had raised from a baby in this life instead of dying for as a child in the last one. The pine above her was just a pine, planted by the gods as a marker, not a body.

It felt strange to Percy. He kept seeing the story they remembered beneath the girl standing safely beside them.

Annabeth stepped forward first. She did not say anything. She just slipped her hand into Thalia's and stood beside her under the tree, shoulder to shoulder, looking out at the road beyond.

Thalia looked down at their joined hands. Her mouth trembled. She squeezed hard.

"I keep thinking—" she started, then stopped. She tried again, quieter, so only Annabeth and Percy, who had moved closer, could hear. "What you told me. About this hill, and the other me. I don't remember any of it. I remember getting you both through the border this summer. But now I keep looking at the road and wondering how you can make yourselves walk out again."

Annabeth's eyes filled. "Thalia—"

"And now we are leaving from here," Thalia said. She laughed, shaky. "Walking out together. It feels wrong. Good wrong. Like I am asking this place to trust us outside it again."

"You are not stealing," Percy said softly. "You are just walking."

Thalia looked at him. Her eyes were gray like Annabeth's, but darker, storm cloud to Annabeth's clear sky. "Walking," she repeated. "Yeah. Okay. I can do walking."

Grover came up on her other side and took her other hand without a word, lacing his fingers through hers. Thalia stared at him, surprised, then smiled wobbly and held on.

Chiron cleared his throat. "The border will hold you until you step past the tree line. After that, the Mist thickens. Stay together. Iris-message when you can. And remember —"

"Do not die, bring a snow globe," Thalia recited, trying for lightness.

Chiron smiled. "I was going to say trust each other. But yes. That too."

They drank the hot chocolate Silena had packed, one cup each, standing in a circle under the pine while Peleus watched and the wind moved the needles overhead. It was sweet and hot and tasted like stargazing and winter cabins. Percy saved the last sip and poured it at the base of the tree, the way Silena had told him to, for the spirits of the hill.

"For safe road," he murmured.

Thalia watched him do it. Something in her face eased.

Then Chiron hugged each of them once more, hard and quick, and stepped back inside the boundary line with his hands folded. Peleus curled around the trunk. The camp below was small and bright, smoke from the forges rising straight up.

It was time.

Thalia went first. She always went first when she was scared. She squared her shoulders, lifted her chin, and walked past the pine and down the far side of the hill toward the highway, spear tapping the dirt, without looking back.

Annabeth went second, turning at the last second to wave wildly at the figures on the lawn below — Silena waving both arms over her head, Clarisse with her spear raised in salute, Tyson jumping with his sign.

Grover went third, bleating a little despite himself when his hooves hit the outside dirt. He clutched his backpack straps and muttered, "Okay. Okay. Outside. Grass still grass. Air still air. Good. Good."

Percy went last. He touched the pine bark as he passed, rough under his palm, and whispered, "Take care of them while we are gone."

Then he stepped over the invisible line where the wards ended, and the air changed — thinner, louder, full of car exhaust from the distant road — and camp was behind him.

For the first time in this life, Percy Jackson was outside.

---

The highway was louder than Percy remembered.

In the other life he had walked American roads for years. He remembered heat shimmer and truck stops and the particular smell of hot asphalt. But he had been twelve for a while now in this life, twelve with salt in his shoes and sisters braiding his hair, and his body had forgotten the rhythm of the road. His duffel bounced. His feet hurt after twenty minutes. The sun was too bright off the guardrails.

They walked single file on the shoulder, Thalia in front, then Annabeth with the map, then Grover, then Percy in back. A truck honked and Grover jumped clean over a guardrail post.

"Sorry!" he gasped. "Horns are — they sound like — never mind. I am good. Continuing to be brave."

"You are doing great," Annabeth said firmly, without looking up from her map. "Horns are rude. Everyone agrees."

Thalia dropped back to walk beside him and bumped his shoulder with hers. "First day outside is the worst. Smells wrong. Too much iron. You get used to it."

Grover looked up at her, grateful. "When did you get used to it?"

"I never did," Thalia admitted with a crooked grin. "I just got better at pretending. Ask Annabeth. I complain the whole time."

Annabeth smiled without looking up. "She complains about the smells, the sounds, the seats, the food, the sky being the wrong color—"

"The sky is the wrong color outside camp," Thalia said. "It is washed out. Like someone left it in the sun."

"It is the same sky," Annabeth said.

"It is not the same sky and you know it."

Percy listened to them bicker and felt his shoulders drop an inch. This was good. This was sisters being sisters, steady as tide. He caught Annabeth's eye over Grover's head and she smiled at him, quick and private, and his ears went warm.

At noon they stopped under an overpass to eat. Grover took his shoes off to rest his hooves and sighed. Thalia scouted ahead and came back with word of a gas station two miles on with a bus stop.

Annabeth spread the map on her knees. Percy sat close enough that their shoulders touched, pretending to study routes while mostly enjoying the press of her arm against his. The map showed Long Island curling away behind them, the city a gray smear ahead. Chiron had marked three safe houses — a diner in Queens run by a clear-sighted mortal, a laundromat in Manhattan with a back room, and a bus depot in New Jersey where a son of Hermes drove nights and asked no questions.

"We need to get to the city by tomorrow," Annabeth said, tracing with her finger. "Bus from the gas station goes to the ferry. Ferry to Manhattan. If we miss the four o'clock, we wait until morning."

Thalia nodded, chewing. "We can make four if we do not dawdle. Grover, how are your feet?"

"Good! Good feet. Strong feet. Slightly blistered feet, but I have moleskin. I came prepared." Grover patted his giant backpack proudly. "I also have trail mix. With raisins. I do not like raisins, but they are good for morale, Chiron says."

"I like raisins," Percy said, holding out his hand. Grover poured him a palmful, beaming.

Annabeth rolled her eyes fondly and marked the gas station with an X. "Okay. Gas station, bus, ferry. Easy."

"Famous last words," Thalia muttered, but she was smiling.

They packed up and walked on. The afternoon got hot. Percy offered to carry Grover's backpack and Grover refused with dignity and then accepted after another mile.

Around three, the road curved through woods, oaks crowding close. Birdsong stopped.

Percy felt it in his teeth before he heard it — a pressure change, like diving too fast. The air went still. The shadows under the trees deepened.

Grover froze mid-step. His nostrils flared. His eyes went wide and yellow in the dim light.

"Stop," he whispered.

Thalia was already moving, spear sliding into her hand, body low. Annabeth dropped her backpack and drew her knife in one smooth motion. Percy uncapped Riptide — the familiar shing of bronze unfolding — and stepped up beside her.

"What is it?" Annabeth breathed.

Grover sniffed again, turning in a slow circle. "Sulfur. Old leather. Bat — bat wings. Above. Circling."

Percy looked up. At first he saw nothing but oak leaves and gray sky. Then the light bent wrong between two branches, and a shape dropped through it, shrieking.

Fury.

She was exactly like the stories and worse in person — hag face stretched tight over bone, eyes red as brake lights, hair a nest of hissing snakes, wings like a bat's but charred at the edges, claws outstretched. She wore a tattered black dress that smoked where it touched air. Her scream hit Percy's ears like a nail dragged down glass.

"HALF-BLOODS," she screeched. "THE MASTER WANTS THE GIRLS. THE BOY MAY BLEED."

Thalia shoved Annabeth behind her without thinking, spear up. "Like Hades you will!"

The Fury dove.

What happened next happened fast and Percy would remember it in pieces, the way you remember lightning — not as a line but as afterimages.

Thalia met the dive head-on. She planted her feet and took the impact on her spear shaft held crosswise. The Fury screeched and beat her wings. Thalia dug in and shouted, "Grover, left! Annabeth, now!"

Grover did not run. He planted his hooves and lifted his reed pipes and played.

It was something older, a wild tune that sounded like wind through grass. The sound wrapped around the Fury's wings and tangled them. She faltered. Leaves swirled up and stuck to her smoking dress.

"Percy!" Annabeth shouted. She was already moving, circling right, knife flashing. Her other hand crackled.

Percy saw it — really saw it for the first time outside training. Blue-white sparks crawling up her forearm, gathering in her palm, ozone sharp on the air. Daughter of Zeus. Lightning in her veins.

She was scared. Percy could see it in the set of her mouth. But her hand was steady.

"Water!" she called. "Give me water!"

Percy understood at once. He did not think. He reached — not with his hands but with that other sense, the tug behind his ribs that meant water nearby. A ditch by the road, half full of rain from last night. A puddle under leaves. The damp in the air itself.

He pulled.

Water rose in a shimmering arc, ditch water brown and puddle water clear mixing into a ribbon that hung in the air between his hands, trembling. It was harder than the lake at camp, wilder, less willing. His arms shook with the effort. His vision narrowed.

"Now!" Annabeth cried.

Percy threw the water at the Fury as Thalia ducked low and Grover's music peaked.

Annabeth hurled a burst of lightning toward the airborne Fury just as Percy swept the water forward, but neither had checked whether the wet ground still connected back to their feet.

Light leapt across the streaming water. A stray branch of the discharge snapped against the ditch, alarmingly close to Percy's boots. The wave caught the Fury square in the chest. She screamed and convulsed midair. Smoke poured off her.

Thalia did not hesitate. She drove her spear up through the stunned Fury's wing joint, using the shaft as a lever, and slammed her sideways into an oak trunk with a crack that shook leaves down in a rain.

The Fury slid to the ground, twitching, smoking. Her red eyes fixed on Annabeth with hate.

"The storm girl," she hissed. "He knows your name now. He knows—"

Thalia put her spearpoint to the Fury's throat. "Finish that sentence and lose your tongue."

The Fury laughed, wet and crackling, and dissolved into black sand that blew apart on the wind, leaving only a scorched feather and the stink of sulfur.

Silence fell. Leaves settled. A car passed on the highway beyond the trees, oblivious, radio blaring.

Nobody moved for three heartbeats.

Then Grover lowered his pipes with shaking hands and said, very faintly, "Did — did we — did I do that?"

Thalia lowered her spear slowly. Her arms were trembling. She looked at the black sand scattering, at the scorch mark on the oak, at her sister standing with lightning still flickering faintly around her fingers.

"Yeah, G-man," she said hoarsely. "You did that. You held her."

Grover sat down hard on a root, backpack thumping. "I held her. I did not run."

Annabeth extinguished the last spark in her palm with a flick, the way Thalia had taught her, and immediately turned to Percy. "Are you hurt? Did she touch you?"

Percy was staring at his hands. They tingled where the water had left them, cold and buzzing. "I am okay. Are you? Your hand—"

Annabeth looked down. Her palm was red, a light burn in the shape of branching lines. She flexed it, wincing. "Just feedback. Thalia warned me. I held it half a second too long."

Thalia was already there, pulling a small tin of salve from her belt. "Let me see. Sit." Her voice was rough, big-sister bossy covering worry. She smeared the cool green paste gently across Annabeth's palm while Annabeth tried not to flinch. "You did good, you idiot. Stupid and good. Never channel through standing water without grounding first. I told you."

"You told me," Annabeth agreed meekly. She smiled up at Thalia, eyes shining. "But it worked."

"It worked," Thalia admitted. She tied a strip of cloth around the burn, snug but not tight. "Storm and sea. Dad and Uncle P team-up. The poets will weep."

Percy sat down on the pine needles beside Grover, suddenly shaky now that it was over. Riptide was a pen again in his pocket. His heart thudded against his ribs.

"We really did it," he said. "First monster. Together."

Grover leaned against him, breathing fast, half laughing. "My knees are still shaking. But I played. I did not drop the pipes."

"You were amazing," Percy said honestly. "That music — it tangled her up like vines."

"It was the panic song," Grover said. "Satyr emergency number three. Euphemia taught me. I thought I forgot it, but my fingers remembered."

Thalia finished wrapping Annabeth's hand and sat back on her heels. She looked at each of them in turn — Grover pale but grinning, Annabeth holding her wrapped hand to her chest, Percy with leaves in his hair — and something fierce and proud crossed her face.

"Okay," she said. "Okay. Rules. Percy, that water pull was perfect. Next time, aim for eyes and mouth, not chest. Bigger shock. Annabeth, ground through your back foot. Grover, keep playing even after they fall, until I say clear. Got it?"

"Got it," they chorused.

"Good." Thalia stood and offered Grover a hand up, then Percy, then Annabeth. "Now we walk fast before her sisters show up. Furies travel in threes when they are serious. That was a scout."

That sobered them. They grabbed packs and moved, Thalia setting a brisk pace back toward the road, Grover sniffing every few steps, Annabeth checking her wrapped hand, Percy listening for wings.

After half a mile with no scream from above, the tightness in Percy's shoulders eased a fraction. The woods thinned. The gas station sign appeared ahead, glowing orange through trees.

Annabeth fell into step beside him, close enough that their backpacks bumped. Her face was flushed, hair coming loose, eyes bright with leftover adrenaline.

"That was —" she started.

"Terrifying?" Percy offered.

"Awesome," she finished, grinning. "Terrifying and awesome. Did you see the lightning go through? It branched like a tree. Like the drawings in Cabin Six."

"I saw," Percy said. He could still see it burned on the inside of his eyelids. "You were — you were incredible."

Annabeth ducked her head, pleased and embarrassed. Under the cover of their backpacks, her unhurt hand found his and squeezed, quick and warm. Just for a second. Just long enough to say we are alive and we did it together.

Percy squeezed back. Neither of them looked at each other. Both of them smiled at the road ahead like idiots.

Behind them, Thalia saw.

She saw the hands meet and part. She saw her little sister's ears go pink. She saw Percy stare very hard at a telephone pole like it was fascinating.

Thalia said nothing. She just slowed her pace half a step so she walked beside Grover instead, giving them a little more space, and caught Grover's eye and shook her head once, tiny, with a finger to her lips.

Grover blinked, then nodded solemnly and mimed zipping his mouth shut, which made Thalia snort and ruffle his curls.

Secret kept.

---

They didn't get far before Annabeth stumbled.

Percy had been watching the tree line. He heard her shoe scrape behind him and turned in time to see her put a hand against the guardrail. The cloth Thalia had tied around her palm had darkened in one place.

"Don't say you're fine," he told her.

"I was going to say I needed a minute."

"That's allowed."

Thalia came back at once. Her first instinct was to ask where the next threat was. Then she saw Annabeth's hand and stopped looking beyond the shoulder of the road.

"We need clean water," she said.

"We have some," Grover answered. He pulled the flask out of its side pocket, handed it over, and seemed almost relieved to have an ordinary job.

Thalia didn't unwrap the dressing until they were behind an embankment and out of sight of passing traffic. The burn was small, but the skin around it was red and sensitive. Annabeth hissed when the first trickle touched her palm.

"How bad?" Percy asked.

"Not deep," Thalia said. "And not something we keep testing with lightning." She looked up. "You heard me?"

Annabeth nodded, eyes fixed on the cloth.

Percy looked back toward the trees where the Fury had fallen. They were already several hundred yards away. He could still remember how the electricity had followed the water he'd thrown. It had worked for a moment, but the ribbon had been between him and the monster. It had been connected to the moisture around his hands.

"That could have come back toward me," he said.

Thalia looked at him sharply. "It could have."

Annabeth's face changed. She had been staring at the bandage, and now she looked directly at Percy.

"I didn't check where you were grounded."

"Neither did I."

"We shouldn't do it again," she said.

"Not that way," Percy agreed.

Thalia finished covering the burn with a clean dressing. She didn't praise their combination. She didn't tell them it had been brilliant because it had worked. The problem with a lucky mistake, Percy thought, was how quickly you could turn it into a plan.

"We use each other's strengths," Thalia said, "but that doesn't mean combining them blindly. Electricity doesn't care if the person touching the water is somebody you love."

Annabeth swallowed and looked at Percy again.

"I'm sorry."

"You were trying to stop her."

"That doesn't make it safe."

He crouched beside her until they were level. "Then next time we talk before we throw lightning around. Preferably a long time before."

She gave him a weak smile. "I can add a safety diagram."

"Several. Color-coded. Something Grover can use when he lectures us."

Grover raised one hand. "I would prefer never to be close enough to lightning to lecture anybody about it."

"Reasonable," Thalia said.

They rested while the ache in Annabeth's hand settled. Percy used the time to check the ditch. The Fury's spell had left the water smelling faintly of smoke, and he was unwilling to take any of it for their flasks. They had enough clean water for the next walk, but not enough to waste.

Grover crouched near the base of a pine and listened to the woods. Percy watched him press two fingers into the soil and hold still. A minute passed. Then another.

"Nothing moving toward us," Grover reported. "The animals are coming back. That's usually good."

"Usually?" Thalia asked.

"Better than them running."

Thalia accepted that distinction.

The four of them made a record before they left. It was Annabeth's idea, although Percy held the notebook while she wrote with her unhurt hand. She listed where the Fury attacked, what Grover sensed, what Thalia saw, how the monster moved and why the improvised lightning strike had been risky. Annabeth didn't name the power combination after either of them. She wrote *unsafe unless separated from any conductive route to companions* and drew an ugly circle around it.

"That's very unromantic," Percy said.

"Good."

He passed her the pencil. "Put a second circle."

She did.

By the time they reached the gas station, Grover's knees had stopped shaking, and Thalia no longer looked as though every passing shadow was the second Fury. Annabeth's bandage stayed clean. Percy was relieved by that simple fact more than by anything impressive they'd accomplished in the fight.

He'd wanted their first day on the road to prove they were capable of facing the west. Instead it had taught them how little room there was for mistakes.

The lesson was worth keeping.

---

The gas station was small and bright, smelling of gasoline and fried cheese.

They bought bus tickets with mortal money Chiron had given them — Thalia counted it out carefully, tongue between her teeth — and four bottles of water and a bag of pretzels. The cashier, a tired woman with kind eyes, looked at their dirt-smudged faces and Grover's shaking hands and gave them an extra packet of cookies for free.

"You kids hiking?" she asked.

"School trip," Thalia said smoothly. "Our bus left us. We are catching up."

The woman nodded like she heard that every day. "Bus is running late. Bench outside. Stay off the pumps."

They sat on the bench outside in a row, packs at their feet, watching the sun go low and orange over the trees. The air cooled. Crickets started. The scorched feather from the Fury was wrapped in a napkin in Annabeth's pocket for Chiron.

Grover ate three pretzels and fell asleep sitting up, head tipping onto Thalia's shoulder. Thalia froze, then carefully stayed very still so she would not wake him, mouthing to the others, do not laugh.

Annabeth pulled out her notebook with her good hand and sketched the fight while it was fresh — stick figures with notes in the margins. Fury dive angle. Water arc height. Lightning dispersal pattern. Grounding stance correction. Percy watched her draw, chin on his knees, and thought she looked beautiful when she was working, brow furrowed, pencil moving fast.

"For Chiron's report?" he asked.

"For us," Annabeth said. "So next time we are faster." She tapped the sketch of him pulling water. "You pulled from two sources at once. That is hard. Most Poseidon kids your age can only do one."

Percy shrugged, embarrassed and pleased. "It felt like — like calling. Like they wanted to come."

"You are strong," Annabeth said simply. She tore off a corner of the page and wrote something on it and handed it to him. It said, in her neat architect print: PERCY + ANNABETH — STORM-SEA COMBO v1. WORKS. DO AGAIN BUT BETTER.

Percy folded it and put it in his pocket next to Riptide, where it would get soft from being carried.

The bus came at dusk, headlights sweeping the lot, air brakes sighing. The driver was a big man with a Hermes caduceus tattooed on his forearm. He looked at their orange shirts peeking from under jackets, at Thalia's spear disguised by Mist as a walking stick, at Grover's hooves hidden as sneakers, and winked.

"Half-blood express," he said. "Hop in, kids. Back seats are warded."

They climbed aboard. The bus was nearly empty, seats cracked vinyl, windows rattling. They took the back as told, piling packs in the middle, Grover still half asleep and leaning on Thalia, Annabeth by the window with her notebook, Percy beside her.

As the bus pulled out, Thalia looked back through the rear window toward the hill, now just a dark shape against sunset, pine a black slash on top. Her hand found the pinecone in her jacket pocket — the one Grover's cousins had given Grover, which Grover had given her for luck before the Fury came — and held it tight.

Percy saw her face in the glass reflection. Not scared now. Sad and determined at once.

Annabeth saw it too. She leaned across Percy without a word and took Thalia's hand over the seatback and held it until Thalia's jaw unclenched.

The bus rumbled toward the city, toward ferries and safe houses and whatever had stolen and whatever waited. Outside, lights came on one by one in houses along the road. Inside, the four of them sat close, smelling of sulfur and pretzels and hot chocolate, tired to the bone and still buzzing.

Percy looked at Annabeth's wrapped hand resting on her knee, at Grover snoring softly on Thalia's shoulder, at Thalia watching the road ahead like she could already see trouble coming and meant to meet it standing up.

He thought of Silena's caramels in his bag, of Clarisse's red lace on his handle, of Tyson's sandwich eaten under an overpass, of Chiron's hug and Mr. D's snow globe.

He thought of the hill behind them and the city ahead, and of water rising when he called and lightning running through it like branches.

He was twelve. He was scared. His socks were damp and his arms ached and his heart would not slow down.

But his friends were beside him, and his sisters' knots were on his wrist and bag, and Annabeth's note was in his pocket, and the road was open.

The quest had begun.

---

At the bus counter, their first route plan fell apart.

It wasn't because of a monster. The clerk looked at the map Chiron had given them, slid it back through the window and informed Thalia that the connection to the ferry had been suspended for roadwork three weeks earlier.

"Do you have a newer schedule?" Annabeth asked.

The clerk pointed to a board behind her.

They stood in front of it while a tired woman with two children waited for them to move. Percy would have preferred another Fury. He understood what to do when something attacked him; he had no idea what to do with a bus schedule that contained an entire column of canceled routes.

"We can go by rail," Annabeth said, tracing an alternative. "We'll have to change at a station on the other side of the city."

"That costs more," Thalia answered.

"Not if we buy the day pass."

"The day pass starts tomorrow."

Grover looked between them. "Can we take a bus for part of it and walk the rest?"

"Walking takes time," Annabeth said.

"Time we might have," Grover replied. "We don't have an eagle escort. We don't have an unlimited travel budget. We do have feet."

Percy stared at him. "You sound practical."

"I'm a satyr," Grover said. "We're extremely practical when we're not distracted by shiny things."

Thalia's gaze slid toward his collection of cans.

"Or horseshoes," Percy added.

"That's unkind."

They moved aside so the woman could buy her tickets. When she came back past them, she paused.

"You going to New York?" she asked.

Thalia hesitated. Chiron had told them to be careful about telling mortals more than they needed to know. But the woman had heard their entire argument, and their destination was already printed across the top of the board.

"Eventually," Thalia answered.

"Take the local to the terminal, then the coach. It's slower, but they won't charge you twice for the transfer." She shifted the smaller child higher on her hip. "You kids should eat before you get on. The coach station's food is terrible."

Annabeth checked the route. The prices worked. The travel time was longer than the ideal schedule but shorter than Grover's walk.

"Thank you," she said.

Percy watched the woman go, surprised by the ordinary kindness of a stranger who didn't know anything about gods or prophecies. The Mist hadn't made her blind to four tired children studying a timetable.

Thalia asked the clerk whether their original tickets could be exchanged for the local and coach. The clerk frowned, checked the fare codes and agreed, returning only a few coins in change. Thalia counted it twice before handing it to Annabeth to record. Percy took charge of the water and tickets. Grover stood watch near the windows, trying to distinguish exhaust from anything stranger.

"Do we send Chiron a message?" Percy asked.

"Not every time a timetable changes," Annabeth said. "We'd run out of drachmas before we reached the first real city."

"We had a monster attack."

"That one is worth reporting."

They found a quiet corner and used a shallow cup of clean water to place a brief Iris message. It took several tries before the image steadied. Chiron appeared surrounded by papers. He didn't sound surprised that their first day had included trouble, but became attentive when Annabeth described the near miss with the electrical discharge.

"Do not repeat that method," he said.

"We won't," Percy answered.

"Tell me where you are and whether anyone needs treatment."

They gave the station name and described the burn. Chiron had Annabeth explain what it felt like to move her fingers. His instructions were practical: keep the dressing clean, avoid further electrical use until the injury was assessed, and seek ordinary medical attention if the burn or pain worsened. He did not offer a miraculous cure through a rainbow.

"Should we come back?" Percy asked.

Chiron looked at them through the shimmering water.

"Not merely because the first day frightened you. That is information you should use. If one of you cannot travel safely, you must reconsider the plan. At present I hear that you have shelter and transportation available. Take the rest you need."

Grover nodded so earnestly his curls bounced.

"Rest. Definitely."

"How's camp?" Percy asked before the connection faded.

"Busy. Your sisters have reorganized three border patrol shifts and argued about a canoe's damaged rigging. I believe Silena won the argument."

"She usually does," Percy said.

"That is becoming apparent. I'll send word to Atlantis when there is news worth sending. Until then, keep your observations separate from your guesses."

The rainbow dissolved. Thalia poured out the little remaining water and wiped the cup.

The station was not romantic. The fluorescent lights made everyone look sick, and the benches were bolted to the floor. Percy knew a story about a quest ought to be full of sword fights and narrow escapes. So far it was full of his girlfriend's bandaged hand, arguments about tickets, and a satyr who had been right about walking.

Annabeth tapped the cheaper route one last time.

"We can still make the connection. We just need to move."

The local bus arrived ten minutes later. Percy offered Grover the window seat and took the aisle beside him. Thalia sat across from them, where she could watch both doors. Annabeth leaned her head against Percy's shoulder, her wrapped hand resting on top of her bag.

Percy watched rows of shops move past the window. The road outside camp didn't look like the road he remembered. That was good. It meant he was paying attention to the one beneath his feet.

---

The motel was not a motel yet — just a bus depot with a bench and a vending machine — but Thalia said motel because there was a vacancy sign blinking pink across the street, and that counted as a plan.

"We sleep at the depot tonight," she decided, already in leader mode, spear across her knees. "Bus to the ferry at dawn. Annabeth, you take first watch with me. Percy, you and Grover sleep. We rotate."

Annabeth nodded, already pulling out the map again despite her burned hand. Grover curled up on his backpack and was snoring in seconds, pinecone toy tucked under his chin.

Percy sat beside Annabeth on the cold bench while Thalia bought hot soup from the vending machine that claimed to be chicken. The depot lights buzzed. Moths hit them and fell.

Annabeth leaned her head against his shoulder, just for a moment, heavy with exhaustion.

"We did okay today," she murmured.

"We did better than okay," Percy said. He covered her good hand with his, careful of the wrap. "Storm-sea combo v1."

She smiled against his jacket. "Wait till v2."

Across the depot, Thalia watched them over the rim of her soup cup, Grover snoring at her feet, city glow on the horizon.

She said nothing. She just smiled, small and fierce and proud, and turned back to watching the doors, keeping guard while her sister rested.

Outside, rain began to fall — light at first, then steadier, tapping the awning. Percy felt it like a friend knocking, asking to come in.

He listened to it and to Annabeth breathing and to Grover snoring and to Thalia's boots shifting on tile, and he thought, we are four, and we are together, and the road did not take us today.

Tomorrow would come with ferries and cities and more monsters. Tomorrow they would need v2 and v3 and plans drawn in notebooks.

Tonight there was soup and a bench and rain, and that was enough.
