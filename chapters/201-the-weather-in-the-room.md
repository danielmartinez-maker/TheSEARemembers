## Chapter 201: The Weather in the Room

Annabeth found Ianthe at the Big House work board, moving the list to make room for a second sheet.

The original page was still pinned under the smooth stone. Its notes had accumulated since yesterday: the rail repair and heavy-rain recheck, the fence drainage question, the awning bracket waiting for calmer weather, the north step waiting for frost. The stool remained marked as curing. Ianthe had left the greenhouse soil bank alone.

Her new page had boxes for task, weather, people, and time. The first three lines were filled in. The fourth had a heading but no entries.

“Is that a schedule?” Annabeth asked.

“A proposal.”

“Who asked you to make one?”

“Chiron asked me to help with the list.”

“He asked you to make a clean copy.”

“That was yesterday.”

Annabeth set her folded camp map on the table, keeping it separate from the weather chart and the work list. “Did he ask you to schedule the people?”

“I’m not scheduling them. I’m showing when the work could happen.”

The distinction was clear to Ianthe. The names in the boxes were less clear.

“Beckendorf at the awning,” Annabeth said. “Clarisse and Percy at the fence. Leandros as water lead.”

“Those are the people who know the most about those jobs.”

“Did you ask them?”

“I was going to.”

“Before or after you wrote their names?”

Ianthe’s pencil rested on the blank time column. “If I waited until everyone was available, I’d never finish the schedule.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“I know.”

She said it without a smile. The pencil rolled between her fingers. Annabeth recognized the effort it took not to add another mark.

“Chiron said to separate inspection from assignment,” she said.

“I know that too.”

“Then why are their names there?”

“Because if I leave the page empty, it doesn’t tell anyone what to do.”

“It tells them what needs doing.”

“People don’t follow a list if they don’t know when to show up.”

“Maybe they need to be asked.”

Ianthe turned the new sheet toward her. She had written in a careful hand, each line straight and evenly spaced.

“Look at the weather,” she said. “The awning can be checked while the wind is low. It’s likely to turn before midday. The fence needs a drier ground check, so I put that later. The rail only needs a recheck after heavy rain, so I left that off today. The stool still needs time. The frost check isn’t ready.”

The sequence made sense. Annabeth looked at the weather window. Ianthe had noted the wind easing after breakfast, then rising again from the south. Her forecast could be right. The camp map did not show wind; it showed routes and ground. The weather chart did not show workers. Each paper answered one question, and none answered all of them.

“You got the wind right yesterday,” Annabeth said.

“I know.”

“I mean the forecast was useful.”

Ianthe’s mouth eased. “Zeus told me I notice what other people miss.”

Annabeth remembered the words from the first evening. Ianthe had said them as if they explained both her skill and the authority she expected to have. Annabeth had not known what to do with the connection then. She still didn’t.

“Noticing a thing doesn’t tell you who’s free to work on it,” she said.

“No. But it tells me when.”

“Sometimes.”

“Usually.”

“Those aren’t the same.”

Ianthe’s pencil stopped. “You’re making it sound like I’m wrong about everything.”

“I’m not.”

“You agree the schedule is useful.”

“I agree the weather part is useful.”

“And the order?”

“The tasks depend on conditions. The names depend on people.”

“People also depend on conditions.”

“I know.”

“Then why—”

“Because those conditions are theirs to tell you.”

Ianthe looked at the names she had written. She tapped the one beside the fence. “Leandros noticed the runoff. That’s why I put him there.”

“He noticed it with Beckendorf.”

“He knows how water moves.”

“He knows about the marina and boats. That doesn’t mean he can choose the drainage route.”

“I didn’t say he could choose it.”

“You called him lead.”

“I called him water lead. It’s a title for a task.”

“It sounds like authority.”

“Then what should I call it?”

“Ask him what he can contribute. Ask Beckendorf what the task needs. After that, write down who agreed to do what.”

Ianthe leaned back from the board. “The page is supposed to solve the problem.”

“It can show the problem. It can’t make a solution by filling every box.”

“Sometimes someone has to fill the boxes.”

“Not before they know what belongs in them.”

A knock sounded behind them. Chiron came in, holding a mug with both hands. He looked at the new page but didn’t take it from the board.

“Are you ready to show me what you have?” he asked.

Ianthe touched the first line with her pencil. “I planned the inspection windows. The awning has a calm stretch before the wind turns. The fence is later, if the ground dries. The north step stays pending.”

“And the names?”

“Suggested.”

“Suggested by whom?”

“I picked people with the relevant experience.”

“Did they agree?”

“No.”

“Then they are names to ask, not assignments.”

Ianthe’s shoulders drew back. “The people are still going to need someone to organize it.”

“Yes,” Chiron said. “But the organizer cannot make availability up.”

“I didn’t make it up. I made a draft.”

“A draft that tells people when to be somewhere.”

“Then I can label it draft.”

Chiron glanced at Annabeth. “What do you think is useful here?”

“The weather window and the dependencies. She kept the frost check and stool off the schedule because their conditions aren’t met. The names need confirmation.”

Ianthe looked at Annabeth, then at Chiron. “So, the timing is useful.”

“Yes,” Chiron said. “It is.”

She marked a small check beside the first weather note. Annabeth saw the precise satisfaction in the movement. The page had not been rejected. It had only been limited.

“I can ask them at the morning work check,” Ianthe said.

“Ask each person,” Chiron replied. “And be ready for a no.”

Ianthe lowered the pencil. “If they say no, I can find another person.”

“You can ask whether there is someone else who wants it.”

“That’s what I meant.”

“It may be. Ask first.”

The answer was not quite a correction she wanted. Still, she turned to a blank line and wrote *availability to confirm* above the names.

Annabeth shifted her camp map to the far edge of the table. Ianthe watched the movement.

“Are you measuring the wind route?”

“I’m updating the path notes.”

“The wind route affects the awning.”

“It might.”

“Then you should include it.”

“The map shows where people walk. The chart shows wind. If I combine them, it won’t be clear which part is verified.”

Ianthe lifted her eyebrows. “I was thinking of adding a small overlay.”

“Then ask before you put it on my map.”

“I didn’t say I was going to.”

“You asked me to include it.”

“I was making a suggestion.”

“And I answered.”

Chiron’s mug warmed his palms. He said nothing.

Ianthe studied the two papers. Annabeth waited. The answer could be a yes, a no, or a question. It did not have to become a new version of the bed conversation.

“Could I draw a separate overlay?” Ianthe asked.

“On your chart?”

“Yes.”

“If it stays separate.”

“All right.”

She moved her blank sheet to the side and sketched a small square for the awning, a line for the path, and two arrows indicating the expected wind. No names were attached. No measurements were added.

“That's clearer,” Annabeth said.

Ianthe checked the first line again. “You heard me the first time.”

“I did.”

“Then why did you say it twice?”

“I wanted to make sure you heard my answer.”

Ianthe’s pencil hovered over the page. “I heard you.”

Annabeth nodded. She did not say that hearing a no was the same as agreeing with it. The sketch remained on Ianthe’s own paper.

Chiron took a sip from his mug. “Let’s ask Beckendorf what kind of inspection the awning needs. Then we can decide whether there is a safe window for it.”

“I’ll bring the chart,” Ianthe said.

“Bring the schedule too,” Chiron replied. “With the names marked as unconfirmed.”

Ianthe looked at the page. She erased the word “lead” beside Leandros and replaced it with “runoff note.” The new words were smaller, squeezed into the same box.

Annabeth watched the pencil move. Ianthe had listened to the distinction she could draw: a title became a note. The question of whether another person’s time was hers to organize still sat between the lines.

“Where is Beckendorf?” Ianthe asked.

“Forge,” Chiron said. “He’ll come when he’s finished the job he already has.”

Ianthe’s pencil tightened in her hand.

“Can you tell him to come to the awning after?”

“I can ask him when he’ll be available.”

“Good.”

Chiron set down his mug and picked up the original winter list. “I’ll be at the forge. You can bring the pages when you’re ready.”

He left them at the board.

Ianthe adjusted the paper so the wind chart no longer covered the task list. The names still waited in their boxes. Annabeth took her map and closed it.

“Are you going with Chiron?” Ianthe asked.

“No.”

“You’re not checking the awning?”

“Not without Beckendorf.”

“Then you can come ask him.”

“You and Chiron can ask him.”

“You know the cabin route.”

“Beckendorf knows the repair.”

Ianthe pressed the page flat against the board. “I thought you wanted to help.”

“I do. I’m updating the map.”

“That’s not the same work.”

“No.”

Ianthe looked ready to argue, then turned the pencil once between her fingers. “Fine.”

Annabeth tucked the map under her arm. She could feel the old urge to stay and keep the conversation from turning sharp. She let the room hold the unfinished answer. The work list stayed on its board, with names awaiting permission and a wind window that would close whether or not anyone had asked for it.

Outside, the ribbon on the porch post hung still. For the moment, Ianthe had read the air correctly. The rest of the day would require her to read something harder.

Chiron’s forge stood in a low stone building at the edge of camp. The doors were open, and the smell of coal clung to the path. Annabeth was back at the Big House table when they returned. She had rolled the map open over the edge of the board and was marking the safe walking route to the shed.

Ianthe took the new sheet to the board and pinned it beside the winter list. She had written “DRAFT” at the top in block letters. Below it, she copied the awning inspection window and the people she hoped to ask.

She wrote *Beckendorf — inspect bracket, after forge work.*
She wrote *Clarisse — hold canvas.*
She wrote *Eli — bring ladder.*
She wrote *Leandros — runoff notes.*
She wrote *Annabeth — route map.*

The word *draft* was larger than the names. It was also the only part that was true without a question.

Annabeth checked the walking route against the board. The first mark turned at the stone bench, then crossed the path by the tool shed. She had kept the camp map separate. The dotted shoreline was still unresolved.

“Did Beckendorf agree to the time?” she asked.

“He said he’d check when he finished.”

“That’s not a time.”

“I wrote after his forge work.”

“Did Clarisse agree to hold the canvas?”

“I haven’t asked her yet.”

“Eli?”

“I haven’t asked him.”

“Leandros?”

“He saw the schedule.”

“Did he agree?”

“I haven’t asked him.”

Annabeth put the pencil down. “Then you have the people you want to ask.”

“They’re the people who can help.”

“They might be.”

“If I wait until they all happen to be here, the window could close.”

“Then find out when they can meet before the window closes.”

“I’m trying to.”

“You’re showing them where to be.”

“I wrote draft.”

Annabeth glanced at the page. “The word is there.”

“So?”

“People look for their names.”

Ianthe moved the paper slightly, as if she could make the distinction easier by changing its angle. “I’ll explain it when they come.”

“Why not ask before you put their names up?”

“Because the chart needs people to be useful.”

“It needs people who have agreed.”

Ianthe’s fingers curled around the edge of the sheet. “You say that as if people never help unless they’re asked.”

“Some help because they want to. Some already have something else to do. Some need to know what’s being asked before they say yes.”

“Then I’ll tell them.”

“Ask.”

“I heard you.”

Annabeth took her map from the table. She did not make the word a fourth time.

Chiron returned with a small iron tool in one hand. He set it on the shelf and looked at the board.

“Ianthe, did you bring the draft?”

“Yes.”

“Did the people agree?”

“No. I marked it as draft.”

Chiron followed the names with one finger, careful not to smudge the pencil. “Beckendorf has not given you a time.”

“He said after the forge work.”

“Which could mean several things.”

“He could finish by midmorning.”

“He might. The forge has its own list.”

“I saw the broken hinge.”

“Then you saw the job he was already doing.”

Ianthe looked at the board. “I didn’t know that hinge was on the list.”

“It was added this morning.”

“Then I can change the time.”

“Not until you ask him.”

She picked up the page and studied it. “If I take off the names, what do I show the workers?”

“Show the tasks and conditions.”

“They’ll still ask who is doing what.”

“Then you can tell them we haven’t decided.”

“They don’t like that.”

“You don’t know what they like.”

Ianthe’s eyes moved to Annabeth. “They like knowing what to do.”

“They like knowing what they’ve agreed to do,” Annabeth said.

A door banged at the far end of the hall. Clarisse entered, carrying two leather arm guards and a narrow box of buckles. She put them down on the bench and saw the schedule.

“Why am I holding canvas?”

Ianthe looked at the line. “The awning needs two people to keep it from pulling while Beckendorf checks the bracket.”

“I’m on equipment check.”

“You can do that afterward.”

“No.”

“I thought the rack was already in order.”

“It isn’t.”

“Then you could finish after.”

Clarisse crossed her arms. “I could also not finish it. You don’t get to move my work because you want a clear morning.”

Ianthe had not expected the answer. She looked down at her writing.

“Chiron said to ask.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

“Then why is my name there?”

“Because you know the camp.”

“That doesn’t mean you know my morning.”

“I thought you’d be available.”

“Why?”

“You were in the Big House.”

“I was bringing the buckles to Chiron.”

“That’s why you were here.”

Clarisse looked at the paper. “That’s not the same as being free.”

Ianthe erased the name. The eraser tore the edge of the sheet. She pressed a small strip of tape to the back, then smoothed it with her thumbnail.

“Could you tell me when you’re free?” she asked.

Clarisse regarded her for a beat. “I don’t know.”

“Could you tell me later?”

“If I want to.”

Ianthe’s mouth tightened. Clarisse picked up the leather guards and went through the kitchen door without taking the schedule with her.

Annabeth folded the map and slid it under her arm. “You can ask.”

“I did ask.”

“You asked when she might be available. She said she doesn’t know.”

“So I have no time.”

“You have an answer.”

“What answer?”

“That she doesn’t know.”

Ianthe stared at the line where Clarisse’s name had been. “That isn’t a schedule.”

“No,” Annabeth said. “It’s a person.”

Ianthe turned the page over, as if the blank side might have a different rule.

Chiron picked up the iron tool. “Let’s take the draft to the workers’ check and ask what’s useful.”

“Everyone?” Ianthe asked.

“Whoever is on that check.”

“I’ll explain it.”

“Ask them what they need.”

“I can do both.”

Chiron looked at her. “Start with asking.”

She held the page in one hand and walked toward the open door. Annabeth remained by the table, waiting for her map to lie flat before rolling it again. The edge had lifted near the dotted shoreline.

Ianthe paused at the threshold. “Are you coming?”

“No. I need to finish this.”

“You could bring the map.”

“That isn’t what I’m doing.”

Ianthe glanced at the marked route, then back to Annabeth. “You’re still working on a different paper.”

“Yes.”

“All right.”

She went outside with the schedule.

Annabeth pressed the map edge down with her palm. It stayed flat this time. The wind chart and task list were on the board, close enough to compare but not laid over each other. One described what the air might do. The other tried to describe what the camp might do with the next few hours.

No sheet could tell Annabeth whether Ianthe would hear the difference between a draft and a decision. That answer would belong to the people whose names she had written.

Annabeth finished the route marks while the Big House kitchen warmed around her. The line to the shed was short and straight. The path to the awning crossed a patch of ground that stayed slick after rain, so she marked the edge with a small cross and wrote *muddy after overnight rain*. It was a note about walking, not a reason to move the awning.

She rolled the map and carried it outside.

The work check had formed beside the board. Ianthe stood with her schedule in hand. Chiron was a few steps behind her, talking to a camper who had brought him a box of loose hinges. Clarisse leaned on the far rail of the porch, the leather guards still in her hands. Two younger campers stood near the tool shed, one with a broom, the other with a coil of rope.

“Beckendorf is still at the forge,” Chiron said.

“I wrote after forge work,” Ianthe replied.

“You wrote nine thirty.”

“Because I expected the work to be finished.”

“It isn’t.”

“He can come when he finishes.”

“If he has another task.”

“There’s a calm window.”

“You can tell us when it is. Beckendorf decides whether the bracket is ready to inspect.”

Ianthe’s thumb pressed the lower corner of her schedule. “I know.”

Annabeth stopped near the map board, far enough away that she could listen without joining.

The camper with the broom raised it slightly. “Am I supposed to be at the awning or the west path?”

Ianthe looked down at the page. “The west path is after the awning.”

“I’m already sweeping the west path.”

“Then finish before nine thirty.”

“I can’t do both.”

“Why not?”

“Because one takes longer than you think.”

“What’s left?”

“The leaves by the steps and the mud at the bend.”

“That’s not much.”

“It is if I have to haul the bucket back twice.”

Ianthe stared at him. He shifted the broom to his other hand.

Chiron stepped forward. “What’s your name?”

“Eli.”

“Ianthe, did you ask Eli to take the awning job?”

“I put him down for the ladder.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

“No,” she said. “I didn’t.”

“I was going to,” Eli said. “I’ve got the path first.”

“You can finish later,” Ianthe replied.

“Then I’ll be late for the cabins.”

“Could someone else finish it?”

Eli looked at the broom. “I don’t know.”

“You can ask.”

“I can ask. I don’t know who’s free.”

Ianthe gave a short breath, almost a laugh but not quite. “That’s what I’m trying to find out.”

“By writing my name down?”

She looked at her schedule again. The word *draft* was clear at the top. His name was clear below it.

“I meant it as a suggestion.”

“It doesn’t look like one.”

The other camper, still holding the coil of rope, set it down beside his boot. “My name’s there too. I didn’t hear about this.”

Ianthe turned the page toward him. “You’re on rope duty.”

“I’m on the practice field this morning.”

“Could you switch?”

“I have a lesson.”

“Can you switch it to the afternoon?”

He glanced toward Chiron. “I’d need to ask.”

“You can ask.”

“I’m asking you. Do I have to switch?”

“No. I’m not—” Ianthe looked at the board, then back at him. “No. You don’t have to switch.”

He rubbed the rope between two fingers. “Then I’ll tell Chiron I’m not available.”

Ianthe’s mouth moved but no words came. He picked up the coil and carried it toward the arena path.

Annabeth wondered if the word *unavailable* would become another box to fill. She hoped not.

Clarisse looked at the schedule. “You put my name on the awning too?”

“I crossed it out.”

“You put it there first.”

“I thought you knew the canvas.”

“I do. I’m not available.”

“Then why are you at the Big House?”

“I brought Chiron the guards.”

“I meant—”

“I know what you meant.” Clarisse looked at the two workers. “You don’t have to explain your morning to her.”

“I was asking what they could do,” Ianthe said.

“You put their names down before asking.”

“I told them it was a draft.”

“You told them where to go.”

Ianthe unfolded the schedule. She ran her thumb over the word *DRAFT*, as if checking that it hadn’t disappeared. “If I don’t write a time, no one has a plan.”

“A plan can have blanks.”

“Then it isn’t finished.”

“It doesn’t have to be.”

“People need instructions.”

“People need work they can do.”

“Those are both true.”

“Then ask what they can do before you give them instructions.”

The rope holder had gone. Eli set the broom against the porch and carried his bucket toward the west path.

“Could you still come later?” Ianthe asked him.

“Maybe. I have to ask the cabin lead about the path.”

“That takes a minute.”

“It takes as long as it takes.”

Ianthe watched him walk away. “If they come late, the wind window is gone.”

Chiron didn’t answer. He turned to the hinge box and checked the loose metal. The other camper who’d arrived with the rope paused near the arena.

“Can you tell me when practice ends?” Ianthe called.

“I can tell you what time the lesson ends,” he replied. “I can’t promise I’ll come.”

“That’s fair,” Chiron said.

Ianthe’s eyes stayed on the schedule. Annabeth stepped closer.

“Do you need the map route?” she asked.

Ianthe lifted her face. “I need to know whether the awning path is safe.”

“It’s muddy at the bend. I marked it.”

“Could you check the surface?”

“I already did.”

“Did you check the ground underneath?”

“No.”

“Then we don’t know if it’s safe to put a ladder there.”

“I didn’t say we did.”

“You marked it.”

“I marked where it’s muddy.”

“Then I’ll add the ladder note.”

“Don't add it to my map. Put it on your schedule.”

Ianthe pressed the paper flat against the board. “Fine.”

Annabeth handed her the small note she had written. The line showed the bend and the muddy edge. Ianthe compared the map with her weather chart, then added a warning beside the time slot.

“Where’s Beckendorf?” Annabeth asked.

“Forge.”

“Has he said when he can come?”

“Not yet.”

“Then the time isn’t confirmed.”

“I wrote that.”

“No, you wrote nine thirty.”

“I wrote after forge work too.”

“Which one should people follow?”

Ianthe looked down. She erased the number and wrote *check with Beckendorf*. The correction took three strokes. The space left behind was clean enough to see.

“Will the window hold?” she asked.

“The weather chart says the wind turns south around noon,” Annabeth replied. “That’s what you predicted.”

“I know.”

“Then a time could still be useful, once you know who can come.”

Ianthe kept the pencil over the blank. “You think I should add a column.”

“I think you should ask them. A column could record the answer.”

Ianthe looked at the sheet. “Availability to confirm.”

“Sure.”

She added the words under the names. The letters pressed hard into the paper.

Annabeth looked toward the path. Eli’s broom moved in short strokes along the bend. He worked around the mud instead of into it. The other camper had disappeared toward the practice field.

“Would you like me to take a look at the ladder spot?” Ianthe asked.

“I can check it.”

“You’re the one who made the map.”

“I’m the one who can tell you where the path goes. I haven’t checked the ground for a ladder.”

“Then neither of us knows.”

“That’s right.”

Ianthe glanced at Chiron.

He was testing a hinge against the box lid. “Beckendorf should check the bracket and the ladder location before anyone uses it.”

“After he finishes at the forge,” Ianthe said.

“Correct.”

She wrote the note without a time. The schedule had grown more accurate and less decisive.

Clarisse lifted the leather guards. “I’m going back to the equipment shed.”

“Could you tell me whether the awning needs two people?” Ianthe asked.

“Beckendorf can answer that.”

“You know the canvas.”

“I know it’s ripped.”

“Could you look at the way it’s tied?”

“Not right now.”

Ianthe looked as if she might ask again. She turned the page instead. Clarisse left through the porch gate.

Annabeth wanted to say that one more question would not make Clarisse more available. She did not. Clarisse had answered. The answer did not need Annabeth to make it official.

Ianthe stayed beside the board, holding the schedule against the wind. A gust lifted one edge. She put her palm over it. The note about the muddy path stayed visible; the names curled toward the page.

“Your weather window is useful,” Annabeth said.

Ianthe looked up. “You’ve said that.”

“I’m not saying the schedule is.”

“I heard you.”

“Okay.”

Ianthe’s eyes moved to the two empty names. “I can’t make the wind wait.”

“No.”

“I can’t make people be available.”

“No.”

“Then what am I supposed to do with the time?”

“Tell people when the window might be. Ask whether they can meet it.”

“And if they can’t?”

“Then the task waits.”

“What if the task matters?”

“Then Chiron decides whether to find someone else or wait.”

Ianthe folded the schedule once, then opened it again. The crease crossed the blank availability column.

“I don’t like unfinished work,” she said.

“I know.”

“You don’t.”

Annabeth didn’t argue. “You’re right. I don’t.”

Ianthe looked at the name Leandros. The word *runoff* sat beside it, smaller than it had on the first draft.

“He might be available,” she said.

“Ask him.”

“Then the window could close.”

“Then it closes.”

Ianthe did not answer. She kept the page pressed against the board until the wind eased, then let it hang from its tack.

The schedule had not gotten anyone to the awning. It had sent two people back to the jobs they had already been doing. The quiet morning was still quiet, but the camp’s time had been spent waiting for a plan that had not asked anyone to join it.

Annabeth took the camp map to the path bend after the work check broke apart. The mud was darker there, but it held her boot. She put a small stone at the edge of the slick patch and added it to the map as a temporary marker. A ladder would need to stand beyond it, on the firmer side.

The wind still came from the west. The porch ribbon pointed level with the post. Ianthe’s chart had the wind turning south closer to noon; the timing seemed right.

A few minutes later, Leandros came down the path with his hands in his pockets. He stopped beside the marker.

“Are you mapping the awning?”

“I’m mapping the path.”

“Those are connected.”

“They are. They’re still different.”

He looked at the folded edge of her paper. “Where’s the schedule?”

“At the Big House.”

“Why?”

“Because it’s the work list.”

“It has names on it.”

“Names that haven’t agreed.”

“Mine’s there.”

“Your note is there.”

“Did she ask you to put it there?”

“No.”

“Did Chiron?”

“No.”

Leandros took his hands from his pockets. “Then it’s not your note.”

“It’s Ianthe’s schedule.”

“Same thing.”

“It isn’t.”

He looked back toward the cabins. “You all keep saying that. The list is not a rank. The task isn’t a title. The note isn’t an assignment.”

“Those are different things.”

“Not to the people writing the names.”

“Then tell Ianthe that.”

“I did.”

“When?”

“Yesterday.”

“Yesterday you met.”

“And today she put me on a new list.”

“I don’t think she meant it as an insult.”

“You keep telling me what people mean.”

“I didn’t say what she meant.”

“You just did.”

Annabeth shifted the map to her other hand. “You’re right.”

He blinked once, caught off guard by the agreement.

“I don’t know what she meant,” Annabeth said. “I only know she wrote you there without asking.”

“Maybe you should tell her I don’t want it.”

“You can tell her yourself.”

“Why is that always the answer?”

“Because you’re here.”

He looked at the mud marker. “What if I don’t want to talk to her?”

“Then you don’t.”

“I want to help with the water. I don’t want her choosing the job for me.”

“Tell her that.”

Leandros crouched and nudged the stone with his boot. It moved only an inch. “This ground is too soft for the drainage check.”

“Beckendorf said they’d wait until it firms.”

“I know what he said.”

“Then you know it can’t happen today.”

“I didn’t say I was going to dig.”

“Good.”

He straightened. “Could still look at it.”

“Beckendorf said no load. He didn’t say no looking.”

“He said wait until the soil is firmer.”

“For the drainage route.”

“Which means you don’t know where the water goes.”

“Exactly.”

Leandros studied the slope toward the fence. “It goes under the path.”

“You don’t know that.”

“I can see the runoff.”

“From yesterday’s rain.”

“I can see where it ended.”

“That isn’t the same as knowing where it started.”

He turned to her. “You’re good at saying no.”

“I’m good at knowing what I know.”

“And what you don’t.”

“Sometimes.”

He smiled once, not warmly. “You all have a phrase for everything.”

“I have one phrase for this. The list says we wait.”

The wind shifted. A cold gust swept across the hill, tugging the map from her hand. Annabeth caught it before it folded back on itself. Leandros caught the loose corner.

“South,” he said.

“Your sister was right.”

“She’s not my sister.”

“You just called her that.”

“I meant yours.”

“She’s yours too.”

He released the corner. “We’re not related.”

“Not as half-siblings.”

“Then don’t say it that way.”

Annabeth rolled the map over her arm. “I didn’t.”

“Everyone hears it that way.”

“No one asked you to be my sister.”

“You keep acting like I’m supposed to take the job.”

“Your name is on a note.”

“That’s a job.”

“It’s a suggestion.”

“Then why is it written down?”

“Because the page is a draft.”

“And the paper doesn’t know the difference.”

He left the path before she answered. Annabeth watched him return to the Big House, his shoulders square and his pace measured. She did not follow him. The map still needed a route marker, not a brotherly conversation.

When she reached the porch, Ianthe was there with the schedule, checking the ribbon. “The wind is turning.”

“Your chart said it would.”

“It has.”

“Has Beckendorf arrived?”

“Not yet.”

“Then you can’t inspect the bracket.”

“I know.”

“Did anyone confirm?”

“Eli might be able to come after the path. The other camper has practice.”

“Did you ask Leandros?”

“He’s listed.”

“That isn’t the same.”

“Annabeth.”

“I’m not telling you to remove him. I’m asking whether you asked.”

Ianthe pressed the page between her palms. “Not yet.”

“Then ask.”

“Do you think I’m trying to control everyone?”

“I think you want the work to happen in the order you planned.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“No. It isn’t.”

Ianthe’s eyes shifted toward the hill. “I thought you wanted me to be here.”

“I do.”

“Then why does every person I ask want something else?”

“Because they’re not one person.”

“That’s not useful.”

“It may still be true.”

Ianthe looked down at the chart, where the lines crossed near noon. “The wind window is now.”

“I see that.”

“Beckendorf is at the forge.”

“Yes.”

“He could leave.”

“Could he?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then you have to ask him.”

“Chiron said he’d tell him.”

“He might.”

“You don’t believe he will?”

“I don’t know.”

Ianthe folded the page along its center. “I don’t like how many things nobody knows.”

Annabeth watched the ribbon. It lifted again, pointing south. “That’s one thing we know.”

A door opened behind them. Chiron stepped onto the porch with Beckendorf. Beckendorf held the small tool box and a length of metal strap. His sleeves were dark with forge dust. He had come as soon as the hinge repair was stable, but he did not look as if he had promised a time.

“I’m sorry,” he said. “The forge job took longer.”

“Can we still check the awning?” Ianthe asked.

“Not from a ladder.”

“The wind isn’t that strong.”

“It’s pulling the ribbon sideways.”

“It’s easing between gusts.”

“That’s not the same as still.”

“I can see the bracket from the ground.”

“Then you can see it from the ground.”

“What about from the ladder?”

“No.”

Ianthe’s schedule had a thin line drawn through the first slot. Beckendorf examined the awning from where he stood. The lower corner of the canvas flapped once, then went quiet.

“The bracket is still bent,” he said. “The tie is holding. I don’t have the right replacement strap, and I won’t climb while the wind is shifting.”

“Could I help hold the ladder?”

“You haven’t been trained to spot it.”

“I can follow an instruction.”

“I believe you. I’m still not using you as the ladder spotter.”

Ianthe held the page more tightly. “Then the schedule was right, but the people weren’t available.”

Beckendorf glanced at the names. “The schedule didn’t know who was available.”

“I marked it draft.”

“People still came to ask what the names meant.”

“I told them.”

“You told them after you wrote them down.”

Chiron stepped between the list and the awning. “We’ll wait for calmer weather and the right fastener. Nothing needs to be done today.”

“The wind window is gone,” Ianthe said.

“Yes.”

“Then we have to wait again.”

“Yes.”

The answer seemed to irritate her more than a lecture would have. She folded the schedule once, then unfolded it.

Beckendorf set the tool box down. “The inspection wasn’t wasted. We confirmed the tie is holding and the bracket is still bent. I can bring the right strap when I’ve checked the shed inventory.”

“Couldn’t we fix it now if someone held the ladder?”

“No.”

“Even with four people?”

“Especially if you think four people make a ladder safe in a gust.”

The breeze lifted the loose edge of the map under Annabeth’s arm. The wind chart and schedule both moved. She pressed them down with her hands.

“Can you write that the strap needs replacing?” Ianthe asked.

“Already did.”

“I mean on my copy.”

“Put it in the awning row,” Annabeth said. “Leave the time blank.”

Ianthe took the pencil. “And the name?”

“Ask him when he can do it.”

“He can answer that now.”

“Can he?”

Beckendorf looked at the forge dust on his sleeves. “Not until I check the inventory.”

Ianthe’s jaw tightened. She wrote *after inventory check* and left the time empty. There was no name in the slot.

The actual workers had not been there. The wind had changed. The bracket remained bent. They had lost the calm stretch without touching the canvas, which meant the awning still needed a repair.

Chiron lifted the toolbox again. “We’ll meet at the board after lunch.”

“Who will come?” Ianthe asked.

“Whoever can.”

She waited as if he might give her a better answer.

He didn’t.

Beckendorf headed back toward the forge. Chiron followed him. Annabeth stayed on the porch with Ianthe while the ribbon pointed south.

“You were right about the wind,” Annabeth said.

“I know.”

“You were wrong about the people.”

Ianthe turned. “I wasn’t wrong about them. I was wrong about the time.”

“You didn’t know their time.”

“I knew it could work.”

“You guessed.”

“I made a schedule.”

“Those are not the same thing.”

Ianthe folded the page carefully, matching the corners. “You make it sound like I shouldn’t try.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You said I was wrong.”

“I said the time was a guess.”

“And if I hadn’t written it down, no one would have come.”

“Not everyone came.”

“Some did.”

“Some came because their names were there.”

Ianthe said nothing. Her thumb passed over the word *draft*, flattening the ink until it blurred a little.

Annabeth walked down the porch steps with the map. Thalia sat inside by the open window, one hand around her tea. Annabeth could see the splint from the path. It stayed as it had; the morning had not changed the care plan.

Ianthe remained at the board. She did not follow them, and Annabeth did not invite her to. The schedule would still be there after lunch. So would the names that had not agreed. Annabeth folded her map and took it inside, where the kitchen clock was already edging toward noon.

***

By the time the lunch dishes had been cleared, the clouds had thinned but the wind had not settled. The ribbon outside the Big House lifted, dipped, and pulled south again.

Annabeth returned to the work board with her map tucked under one arm. Percy walked beside her carrying two cups. He had brought one for her without asking what she wanted; it was tea, not coffee, and he had remembered the honey.

“I can hold the map,” he said.

“I have it.”

“You also have the cups.”

“I have one cup.”

He looked at his empty hands. “Fair.”

She passed him the rolled edge of the map. “Hold this, then. Don’t unfold it.”

“I can do that.”

“I know.”

He took the paper without trying to see what she had marked. At the table she set down her tea, then turned the map so its folds would stay flat. Percy steadied the corner with two fingers. His hand rested near hers, not over it.

“Did Ianthe ask you?” he said.

“No.”

“Are you going to help her?”

“I’m going to make sure the path on my map is right.”

“Okay.”

“That’s not the same as helping her plan.”

“I know.”

She glanced at him. He had not asked whether she was going to fix it, or if she thought Ianthe was being unfair, or how he could talk to her. He waited.

Annabeth nudged the map toward him. “Can you keep that corner down while I add the marker?”

Percy put his palm there. “I can do that.”

She marked the slick patch with a small circle and wrote *ladder placement: firm ground only*. The letters followed the contour line.

“Your handwriting gets smaller when you’re irritated,” he said.

“I’m not irritated.”

He raised his eyebrows.

“I’m focused.”

“Sure.”

She bumped his shoulder with hers, lightly. He smiled and moved his cup out of the way before she could spill it. The two cups warmed the folded map’s blank corner while they finished the route. For a few minutes, the board and the list belonged to other people.

“Do you want to stay?” Percy asked.

“For the meeting?”

“For whatever this is.”

“I want to see what she does with the page after she hears no.”

“That sounds like staying.”

“It’s observing.”

“Right.”

She looked down at the finished mark. “You can go if you have something else to do.”

“I don’t.”

“You don’t have to sit here just because I’m staying.”

“I know.” He leaned his elbow on the table. “I want to.”

Annabeth slid her hand across the wood until her little finger touched his. He hooked his around it. Neither of them said anything else about the schedule.

Ianthe came around the porch carrying a new sheet of paper. She had ruled columns with a straight edge: *task*, *conditions*, *asked*, *availability*. The last column remained blank.

“I’m trying it this way,” she said.

Annabeth unhooked her finger from Percy’s and stood. “You asked people?”

“I asked Chiron what to put in the columns.”

“That isn’t what I meant.”

“I asked Eli if he could sweep the path before practice. He said yes, but he can’t do the awning.”

“So you wrote that?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

Ianthe put the new page on the board. Her first sheet was beneath it. The penciled names showed through in faint gray. She had not torn it up.

“What does *asked* mean?” Annabeth said.

“Whether I asked the person.”

“And *availability*?”

“What they said.”

“Good.”

“I knew what it meant.”

“I didn’t say you didn’t.”

Ianthe pressed the page flat with her palm. “You correct every word I use.”

“I correct the ones that decide other people’s work.”

“Is there a word that doesn’t?”

“Yes.”

“Which one?”

Annabeth looked at the new columns. “The task. The condition. The time, if it’s known.”

Ianthe followed her gaze. “That leaves out who’s doing it.”

“Until you ask them.”

“You keep saying that as if asking changes whether I’m responsible for the list.”

“It changes whether you’re responsible for the people.”

Ianthe’s mouth tightened. “If no one takes it, the awning stays torn.”

“The awning is waiting on the right strap and calm weather.”

“Those are conditions. Someone still has to do it.”

“Someone will.”

“When?”

“When they can.”

“That isn’t a time.”

“No.”

“It makes every line useless.”

“It makes the line true.”

Percy took the cups to the porch rail. He did not join the argument, but he stayed close enough that Annabeth could see him listening. She appreciated that he did not step between them. Ianthe looked at him once, then back at the paper.

“Do you think you’re responsible for the whole list?” Annabeth asked.

“I’m the one who noticed it.”

“That doesn’t answer me.”

“It means I have to make sure it gets done.”

“Why?”

“Because it’s there.”

“Things can be there without being yours.”

Ianthe’s gaze sharpened. “You say that because there are already people here who do it.”

“I say it because I’ve had to learn it.”

“Learn from who?”

“From being wrong.”

“That’s not very helpful.”

“It wasn’t supposed to be.”

Chiron came out from the side door carrying the toolbox. He had stopped at the board to read the revised page. “This is easier to follow.”

“I still don’t have a time,” Ianthe said.

“Then the time is not known.”

“People may ignore it.”

“They may.”

“Would you leave it blank?”

“Yes.”

Ianthe stared at the board. “You all act as if being asked is the same as being given a choice.”

“It is,” Clarisse said from the porch steps.

She had arrived quietly enough that Annabeth had not heard her. She leaned against the post, arms folded, with no sign of being about to take up a task. Her eyes went from the old page to the new one.

Ianthe tapped the bottom sheet. “You could have crossed your name out.”

“I did.”

“It was already a draft.”

“You wrote it beside *hold the canvas*.”

“I meant it as a possibility.”

“You didn’t ask.”

“I can ask now.”

“You can. I can still say no.”

Ianthe drew a breath through her nose. “Are you saying no?”

“Yes.”

“All right.”

Clarisse’s arms loosened a little, though her expression did not. “You don’t have to make it sound like I failed a test.”

“I didn’t.”

“You looked at the list and decided I was being difficult.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You thought it.”

Ianthe’s eyes flicked to Annabeth, as if asking for a ruling. Annabeth gave none.

Clarisse continued, “I’m not free. I’m not hiding a better reason from you. I’m busy.”

“With what?”

“That’s not yours to know.”

Ianthe’s chin rose. “If we’re planning—”

“You’re writing a list,” Clarisse cut in. “You’re not planning my day.”

The words landed hard. Ianthe looked down at the page, then rubbed the side of her thumb against the pencil mark on the margin.

“Fine,” she said.

“Fine what?”

“I’ll leave your name off.”

“You could’ve done that before I got here.”

“Yes.”

Clarisse studied her as if waiting for the rest. Ianthe did not offer an apology or an explanation. Annabeth could not tell if the pause meant she had understood or was only trying not to answer.

Clarisse pushed away from the porch post. “I’m going inside.”

“I know,” Ianthe said, then caught herself. “I mean, okay.”

Clarisse went through the door.

The gap she left felt wider than the porch. Ianthe watched it for a moment, then turned to the board. She erased Clarisse’s name from the old sheet, careful enough to leave a pale shadow where it had been.

“You could throw it away,” Annabeth said.

“It’s the first version.”

“It’s also wrong.”

“I know.” Ianthe folded it once and put it under the toolbox, where the paper would not blow away. “I want to see what changed.”

“That’s your choice.”

“Do you think it’s useful?”

“I don’t know.”

“You could say yes.”

“I could. I don’t know yet.”

Ianthe’s jaw shifted, but she let the answer stand.

***

The wind did not settle after lunch. It shifted twice while Annabeth was still at the board, once toward the cabin row and once back toward the hill. Ianthe marked each change on her chart.

She had not written anyone’s name beside the awning.

Thalia came out of the Big House with a cup in both hands. She paused just beyond the door and leaned her shoulder against the frame. The splint was visible beneath the cuff of her pants.

“You should sit down,” Ianthe said.

Thalia looked at her. “I am standing.”

“You shouldn’t be.”

“That’s my decision.”

“The hill is uneven.”

“So is the porch.”

“That’s different.”

“Why?”

“It’s closer to the door.”

Thalia glanced from the door to the porch boards. “I know where I am.”

Ianthe started to answer, then looked at the paper instead. “I’m only saying you don’t have to come out here.”

“I came out to get air.”

“You could get air inside.”

“Not the same air.”

Annabeth held her pencil above the map. She knew the words Thalia was waiting for her to say. A year ago, she might have said them before Thalia could; lately she had been learning that being understood did not mean needing someone else to speak first.

“She can answer you,” Annabeth said, “but she already did.”

Thalia’s shoulders eased. She took a sip and stayed by the door.

Ianthe looked between them. “I wasn’t asking you.”

“I know,” Annabeth said. “I’m answering for myself.”

“You always do that.”

“Do what?”

“Turn one sentence into a rule.”

“Sometimes one sentence is a rule.”

Ianthe put the pencil down. “You’re both acting like I’m ordering you around.”

“You told her where she should stand,” Annabeth said.

“I told her the hill is uneven.”

“And she told you why she came out.”

Ianthe’s expression hardened, but she did not speak over her this time. Thalia lowered the cup and set it on the porch rail.

“I don’t need you to check whether I’m allowed to stand here,” Thalia said. “I have a plan. I’m following it.”

“I didn’t know that.”

“You didn’t ask.”

Ianthe’s gaze fell to the splint, then lifted. “I thought you were going to make it worse.”

“That’s between me and Chiron.”

“I was trying to help.”

“I believe you.”

The answer seemed to surprise Ianthe more than a rejection would have. Thalia did not smile or invite her closer.

“Trying to help doesn’t make you in charge,” Thalia added.

“I didn’t say I was in charge.”

“You keep acting like it.”

Ianthe glanced at the schedule. “I thought being the one who notices things means I should say something.”

“Say it,” Thalia answered. “Then let the person decide what to do with it.”

“What if they decide wrong?”

“Then they decide wrong.”

“And if they get hurt?”

“Then we help them. We don’t get to own them first.”

The porch went quiet. Annabeth watched the ribbon twitch on its post. She thought about all the times Thalia had stepped between her and a bad choice before Annabeth had even made one. Thalia had loved her through that. She had also been wrong.

Thalia looked at her. “You okay?”

Annabeth could hear the question behind the question. She kept her voice even. “I’m okay. I want to finish the map.”

“Okay.”

It was a small answer, and it cost Thalia something to leave it there. Annabeth reached over and pressed the corner of the paper flat. Thalia took her cup again.

Ianthe watched them. “You can disagree with each other and still know what the other one means.”

“Sometimes,” Annabeth said.

“Most of the time,” Thalia said.

“Not always.”

“No,” Thalia agreed. “Not always.”

Ianthe folded her arms. “You had years to figure that out.”

“Yes.”

“I don’t have years here.”

“No,” Annabeth said. “You have today.”

“That’s not much.”

“It’s what we have.”

Ianthe looked as if she wanted to object, but the gust moved the paper against the board and she caught it instead. She held it down, palm over the empty availability column.

“Father told me I notice what others miss,” she said.

Annabeth waited.

“He said it like it was important.”

“It might be.”

“That’s all?”

“That’s what you told me he said.”

Ianthe’s fingers curled at the page edge. “He didn’t tell me what to do with it.”

“Then you have to decide.”

“That’s the part I’m trying to do.”

“By deciding what everyone else does?”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You wrote their names.”

“I know.”

Thalia shifted her weight against the doorframe. “You can be useful without being in charge.”

Ianthe turned toward her. “You make it sound easy.”

“I didn’t say it was easy.”

For a moment the three of them stood around the board with the map, the list, and the wind chart between them. Nobody had an answer that made the columns fill. Annabeth thought that might be the honest shape of the afternoon.

“Does he say that to you?” Ianthe asked Thalia. “That you notice what other people miss?”

Thalia’s fingers tightened around her cup. “I’m not going to compare what he says to us.”

“Why not?”

“Because he can make different promises to different people. That doesn’t tell you what the other one got.”

Ianthe looked down at the chart. “I didn’t say it did.”

“You were about to ask.”

“I wanted to know if he tells you I’m special.”

Annabeth watched the words land. Thalia did not soften them by pretending she could answer for Zeus.

“I don’t know what he tells you when you’re alone,” Thalia said. “I know he’s our father. I know that doesn’t make us understand each other automatically.”

“I thought if I was useful, you’d have to let me be here.”

“You’re already here.”

“That isn’t the same as being wanted.”

“No,” Thalia said. “It isn’t.”

Ianthe’s face went still. Annabeth felt an old protective reflex rise in Thalia—the urge to repair the hurt quickly, to wrap it up before it could get worse. Thalia noticed it too. She set her cup down and let the silence stand.

“I don’t know you yet,” Thalia said. “I don’t know what you want from us, or what you think we owe you. But you can sit at the table. You can be here. You don’t have to prove it by putting us in columns.”

Ianthe’s eyes moved from Thalia to the list. “I didn’t know how else to start.”

“You could ask.”

“I did ask.”

“Not about the work.”

The wind lifted the edge of the page. Ianthe pressed it flat again. “I’ll try.”

Thalia nodded once. It was not a promise that everything was fixed. Annabeth was glad she did not make one.

Percy came up the porch steps with the rolled map case. “You left this by the table.”

“Thanks.”

He handed it to Annabeth. Their fingers touched, and he waited until she had a firm grip before letting go.

“Did the wind change?” he asked.

“Twice,” Ianthe said.

“Then it’s doing what the chart said.”

“Mostly.”

“Mostly is not the same as always.”

Ianthe considered him, then marked the second shift in a new line. “No. It isn’t.”

Percy glanced at the board. “Do you need another cup?”

“I’m fine.”

He looked to Annabeth. She shook her head. Percy nodded and stayed beside the railing, close but not in the middle of the conversation.

The afternoon bell rang from the dining pavilion. Eli passed the porch carrying a broom. “I’m doing the west path before practice,” he said, more to Chiron than to Ianthe.

Ianthe started to answer, stopped, then said, “Thank you for telling me.”

“I already told you.”

“I know. I meant—” She paused. “Thanks.”

Eli gave a brief nod and continued toward the path.

Ianthe wrote *west path sweep—Eli, confirmed* in the new sheet’s task column, then crossed it out.

“Wrong column,” Annabeth said.

“I know.” Ianthe moved the note to *availability*. In the task column she wrote only *sweep west path*. “There.”

Annabeth nodded.

Ianthe studied the two lines. “It’s slower.”

“Yes.”

“But I don’t have to erase his name if he can’t do it.”

“Right.”

“Or decide why he can’t.”

“Right.”

Ianthe pressed the page down against the board. The wind tugged at the corners, but the thumbtacks held.

The west path was swept by the time the afternoon practice groups crossed it. The broom left damp parallel lines in the dust and cleared the leaves that had gathered beside the cabins. Eli did not come back to the board for praise or a second assignment.

Ianthe noticed. She did not write that down.

She did record the wind changes: west, then south, then west again. The ribbon’s direction did not make the awning safe to climb. The correct strap was still missing from the forge inventory. Beckendorf checked once and found no replacement; he told Chiron he would look again when the next shipment was sorted.

Ianthe wrote *strap not available*. Then, beneath it, *ask after inventory check*.

“Is that an assignment?” she asked Annabeth.

“It’s a reminder.”

“Whose reminder?”

“Yours, if you wrote it.”

Ianthe left the line in place.

They walked as far as the covered path before turning back. Annabeth stayed beside Ianthe, with enough room between them that neither had to match the other’s pace. Thalia remained on the porch with her tea. No one asked her to follow.

The rain from the night before had left the low ground soft. At the fence, the brace still held, but the soil was not firm enough to inspect the drainage route. Ianthe read the old mark Leandros had made on the stone. She crouched to see the line where the water had passed, then stood without touching the ground.

“He saw the runoff,” she said.

“He did.”

“Does that mean he can do the check?”

“It means he noticed where water moved yesterday.”

“You don’t think that’s enough?”

“I don’t know what the soil is doing now.”

Ianthe looked at the dark patch and then at the map. “We could come back tomorrow.”

“If it firms.”

“If it doesn’t?”

“Then we wait.”

She wrote *after ground firms* on her sheet. The phrase was already on Chiron’s list. She copied it exactly and did not add a name.

On the way back, Ianthe stopped at the board. The old version of the schedule was still under the toolbox. She pulled it out and studied the gray names. Annabeth could see the pressure marks from where Ianthe had pressed the pencil too hard.

“You’re keeping it?” Annabeth asked.

“I said I wanted to see what changed.”

“Do you know?”

“Some.” She folded the sheet smaller, hiding most of the names. “I don’t like that I thought writing something down made it real.”

“You wanted it to be real.”

“I wanted the work to happen.”

“That can be true too.”

Ianthe glanced up. “You don’t think I’m a bad person?”

Annabeth took a moment. She could have made the answer easier. She could have said no quickly, could have given Ianthe a reassurance that cost nothing and asked nothing from her.

“I think you made a bad assumption,” she said. “And I think you’re still deciding whether you care that it bothered people.”

Ianthe’s gaze sharpened. “That’s worse than saying no.”

“It’s more honest.”

“Do you care if I’m here?”

The question came out without the usual command tucked inside it. Annabeth noticed that she had asked, not accused.

“Yes,” she said. “I care that you’re here. I don’t know you yet.”

Ianthe swallowed. “Those sound like opposite things.”

“They’re not.”

“Will they stop being opposite?”

“I don’t know.”

Ianthe looked back at the old schedule. “You say that a lot.”

“Because I don’t know.”

“I thought you were supposed to know everything about maps.”

“I know where the path goes.”

“And not what happens at the end?”

“Not always.”

For the first time that afternoon, Ianthe gave a small laugh. It was brief and a little unwilling, as if she had not planned to let it out.

“You’re not what I expected,” she said.

“What did you expect?”

“Someone older who would tell me where things go.”

“I’m not that.”

“I can see.”

“Can you?”

Ianthe looked at her. Annabeth could not tell whether she was deciding how to answer or deciding whether she was allowed to leave the question unanswered.

At dinner, the room was loud enough that nobody had to fill every silence. Ianthe carried the new sheet to the end of the table and stopped before setting it down.

“Can I put this here?” she asked.

“You can put it on the side table,” Chiron said. “The dining table is for dinner.”

Ianthe looked at the empty space beside Annabeth’s plate. “I meant sit.”

Thalia moved her cup a few inches to make room. “You can sit.”

She did not say *with us*. Annabeth didn’t correct her.

Ianthe sat down. She kept the list folded in her lap until the first plates arrived. Percy asked Annabeth if she wanted the last piece of bread; she broke it in half and gave him the bigger side without comment. He saw the difference and switched the pieces back. Annabeth rolled her eyes, but she ate the one he put beside her.

Across from them, Ianthe watched the exchange.

“Do you always do that?” she asked.

“Do what?” Percy said.

“Argue about bread.”

“We weren’t arguing,” Annabeth said.

“You changed the pieces.”

“He gave me the smaller one.”

“Because she likes that side,” Percy said.

“I didn’t say that.”

“You always take that side.”

Ianthe looked at their plates. “You remember that?”

Percy shrugged. “It’s bread.”

Annabeth nudged his foot beneath the table. He grinned, then returned to his meal. No one explained the history between them to Ianthe. It was not a lesson. It was simply one of the small things they had learned by being there.

When dinner was over, Ianthe carried her list to the board. She pinned up the revised copy, with tasks in one column and no names assigned without an answer. The awning remained bent. The fence remained braced. The north step waited for frost. The rail still needed its heavy-rain check. The greenhouse bank was left alone, and the stool continued to cure.

On the old page, the penciled names remained, faint beneath the fold. Ianthe kept that sheet in her folder instead of tearing it up.

Thalia stood in the doorway, one hand on the frame. “You don’t have to stay here all night.”

“I’m not.”

“Good.”

“I’m checking the ribbon.”

“You can check it in the morning.”

“I know.”

Thalia held her gaze for a moment, then went back inside. The door stayed open.

Ianthe watched the ribbon turn toward the hill. The wind had not settled into the direction she had predicted. She recorded the change, left the next time blank, and carried the pencil inside.
