## Chapter 188: The Second Tuesday

The grass under the canoe racks had worn itself into two silver rings by five o'clock on Tuesday afternoon, one ring under each hull, and the silver was Monday's frost come back a second time in the middle of an afternoon that had otherwise been mild, because October had become the sort of month that did a thing twice and told nobody about the second one.

Percy Jackson stood in the ring on the near side with his hands in his coat pockets and looked at it, which was not a thing he would have done in September. In September he walked past the canoes. In October he stood over silver grass and thought about water in all three of its coats, and about the notice on the Big House board that morning: HARD, EARLIER, HOLDING THROUGH THE WEEKEND, and under that, in the same square hand, NOT AN EVENT.

NOT AN EVENT was, in Percy's growing experience, precisely how Chiron wrote down the things he intended to worry about on his own time.

He went up the path. The cabin had the stove going, and the stove had the towel rail loaded four rows deep, which was a system Silena had worked out inside the first week of October and which nobody had improved on since: you did not take cold towels down to a warm hall, because cold towels made people hurry, and hurry on black tile was how a room that had stood since before the country lost an argument with somebody's elbow.

Silena was at the table with the whole spread folded into a stack, folding them in fours the way other people deal cards, and she did not look up when the door opened, speaking in the voice of a quartermaster who had been at this since lunch.

"Basket's by the door. You are the door."

"I am the door."

"You, the rack, and the shoes. Anybody comes down in sandals, the sandals go in the rack and the person goes in the water barefoot. Anybody argues, the argument belongs to Clarisse and I don't want to hear about it."

"Where is Clarisse?"

"Here," Clarisse said, from the floor by the stove, with the winter list flat on her knee and the slate propped against her boot, which was the position she had been in since Sunday and which the family had learned to read the way other families read a barometer.

She was going down the list in her flat way, not crossing anything off, because she did not cross off; she re-read, and the re-reading was its own kind of weather. GREENHOUSE BANK. TORN AWNING. FALLEN PINE. The three of them sat on the page the way they sat on the hill: waiting, unhurried, and entirely confident that somebody would eventually turn up with a shovel.

"You've read that four times," Percy said.

"I've read it a lot of times." She put the pencil against the greenhouse line without marking it. "Two frosts inside a week, and that bank's still up there on the north slope like it's going to volunteer."

"It might volunteer."

"It's a bank of earth, Jackson. It doesn't volunteer. It sits there until somebody with a shovel shows up and then it's somebody's Thursday."

"Thursday's mail," Silena said, from the stack.

"Thursday's mail and the bank, then. It's a big Thursday." Clarisse put the pencil behind her ear. "He was at the racks when he came in?"

"Two silver rings," Percy said. "Under both hulls."

"Good." That was all she said about the rings, and it was not about the rings: the canoes had been on her winter list since September, under a heading she had made herself, and a hard frost on a Tuesday was the first thing out of that column that nobody had asked her to write down. "Suits in the bag?"

"Bag's on the bench where it lives," Silena said. "Four rolled, and if anybody asks for a fifth, the answer is Thursday, and if they ask on Thursday the answer is ask the bag, and I have said that sentence four times a week since the first of October and I am not saying it a fifth."

"You said it twice tonight," Percy said.

"Then consider it said." Silena shook out the last towel, a big one, the grey one with the repaired corner, and put it on the top of the pile with the repaired corner folded inside where nobody would catch a heel on it. "And you, door, are stalling. You've had the same face on since you came in."

"I haven't got a face."

"You've got the face you had on Saturday before you stood in a stream for a hundred minutes. It's the I-have-a-plan face and it's always the same plan and it always ends with something getting towed."

Percy put the basket on the table and looked at his sisters with the expression of a boy who had been read accurately by both of them in under a minute and had decided to be flattered instead of defeated. "I can tow the float," he said. "The length of the hall. Under the rail and back. With somebody on it."

Silena's hands did not stop. "No," Clarisse said.

"I hadn't said who yet."

"You were going to say you."

"I was going to say you, actually."

Clarisse looked up from the winter list for the first time. "Say it again."

"Somebody sits on the float and I tow it the length of the hall and back, and the somebody is you, because you're the heaviest, which is a compliment in this cabin, and because if I can tow you I can tow anything in the hall and if I can't tow you then you were right and you get to write it down."

"I get to write it down either way," Clarisse said. "I write everything down. That's the whole of my personality and you know it."

"Rules are against it," Silena said.

"There's five rules and none of them are about towing."

"Rule three," Silena said, "the deep end is not a dare, which you have just turned into a dare, in the kitchen, with a basket."

"That's not the deep end, that's a float."

"The float is in the deep end."

"The float goes where I tow it," Percy said, and Clarisse put the winter list down on the floor with enormous care, which was the way she did anything she intended to survive, and stood up.

"He's been like this since the ridge," she said, to Silena, without lowering her voice. "He came off a mountain carrying a rod and he has not stopped being pleased about it. Fine. Tonight, before the count, while I'm still feeling generous. And if the ladder gets a dent in it, you're the one who explains the ladder."

"If the ladder gets a dent in it," Silena said, picking the basket up off the table and putting it into Percy's arms instead, "I am the one who tells Chiron, and you are the one who explains it, and I want that understood in the kitchen while everybody's warm and reasonable." She pushed his coat straight with two quick pulls, which was a thing she did to everybody in the cabin and which nobody had ever asked her to stop. "Tow it. I don't care. Just not with the little ones in the deep end, because that slate goes up to the porch with a number on it, and the number has to mean what it says, and if the number ever stops meaning what it says then the whole thing's a hobby, and the whole thing is not a hobby."

"You're insufferable about towels," Percy said.

"Everybody's insufferable about something worth having," Silena said, and opened the door for him, and the cold came in off the porch and hit the warm kitchen and made the tenth lantern's flame lean sideways on its hook.

---

The passage behind the iron door was already warm at ten to seven, and it was the specific warmth of a room that had been running all afternoon on purpose: not the kitchen's dry stove heat but a damp, mineral warmth with a taste to it, the kind that gets into the shoulders first and works its way down.

Percy carried the basket and Clarisse carried the slate, and neither of them said anything on the way down, because the passage was narrow and because this part of the evening had become, without anybody legislating it, a quiet one. Behind them Silena had Thalia by the elbow, because Thalia had come down from Cabin One at the last minute with her hair still wet from the shower and one shoe on and one shoe in her hand, and was being conducted.

"I'm not limping," Thalia said, to nobody, at the top of the stairs.

"Nobody said you were," Annabeth said, behind her, with the field book under her arm.

"Nobody's going to, either. If anybody down there writes the word limp on anything, the answer is frost."

"The answer is frost," Annabeth agreed, in the flat tone she used for logging a correction.

"The answer is frost and I want it on the record that I agreed with you in a passage with witnesses."

"There's four witnesses and one of them's carrying a basket."

"I'd like the basket's testimony too," Thalia said, and Percy, three steps ahead, held the basket up over his shoulder without turning around, and said, "The basket confirms the frost," and got a laugh out of his own family in a warm stone corridor at ten to seven on a Tuesday, which was, by any reasonable accounting, an unreasonable amount of luck for one week.

Clarisse went ahead and put her shoulder to the iron door and it opened the way it always opened, which was slowly, and without a sound, and then the hall took the whole group of them the way it took everybody: first the noise of the water, then the wall of warm damp air, then the sight of it, black tile going green under a skylight that had gone the colour of weak tea, steam standing in the long brick curve of the ceiling, and the water itself lying in its long patient shape with the warm inlet making its small hills at the far end.

Two lanterns were already burning, low, on their brackets, which was the hall's own light and which nobody had lit tonight, which meant Beckendorf had been through at some point in the afternoon and lit them and left them there. Neither Clarisse nor Silena said anything about it, and both of them looked.

Olive and Marcus were there already, because Marcus was always there already, standing on the block at the deep end in the economy of a fifteen-year-old who had decided in advance how the evening would go, and Olive was on the bottom ladder rung with her goggles on correctly for the second time in a row and a piece of chalk in her hand, which she had evidently been using on the tile wall. Annabeth saw it before anybody else did and went to look, without a word, which was the whole story of how Annabeth Grace spent her evenings.

"It's the marks," Olive said, when Annabeth stopped in front of her, and her voice had the particular flatness of a person who had done something she expected to be in trouble for. "You said they were wrong. On the first Tuesday. The nine inches and then it doubles. So I counted them again with the string Marcus has got and I chalked where the water actually is, and mine says the paint was off by more than you said."

"Show me," Annabeth said, and put the field book down on the bench and did not open it, which was the second half of the same story.

Katie Gardner's line came in at five past seven: Katie first, in her coat, with the practiced forward lean of a girl who had been bringing smaller children down hills since the summer, and four of them behind her in a ragged rope, in coats, in one case in a blanket worn as a cape, and at the back of the rope Rosie, who was seven, who had been the smallest and the fiercest on the first Tuesday, and who was now, unmistakably, the veteran.

The blue bag came out of the bench and Silena opened it in one pull, and the line sorted itself the way it always did, with the small ones stopping on the tile in a self-conscious clump and Silena handing out suits by size without being asked and without appearing to look at anybody while she did it, and Katie doing the coat pile, and nobody anywhere in the room saying the word coats twice.

"Eight," Clarisse said, from the door, watching the room the way she watched everything, with her arms folded and the slate flat against her ribs. "Nine. Ten. Eleven. Twelve."

A Hermes boy came in behind the last of Katie's line with the expression of a person who had walked past on an errand and had been defeated by rule five, and Clarisse said, "Thirteen," and did not move from the door; and Silena handed him a suit from the bag without looking up, and he took it, and that was that, and nobody in the room found it strange, which was the entire point of rule five and the entire reason the slate still only had five lines on it.

Neve from the forge came in at a quarter past seven with her own towel over her shoulder and the expression of somebody who had walked down out of a hot shop into a cold night and intended to enjoy both ends of it, and took the ladder, and said good evening to nobody in particular, and went under, and Clarisse, still at the door, said, "Fourteen," to the room, in the tone of a woman closing a bracket.

Rosie, in her suit, with her hair already coming out of its band, went straight to the edge and put one hand on the tile and looked at the water with the expression of a general returning to a campaign.

"Face in and count three," Percy said, coming past her with the empty basket, because he had said it to her once, in September, and because some things were worth saying twice.

"I know what it is," Rosie said. "I'm *doing* it." And she did, and came up with her hair flat and her eyes bright and said, to the ceiling, "Three," as if reporting it, and Percy put the empty basket on the bench and went in after her and was, briefly and without ceremony, underwater.

---

Annabeth Grace gave the chalk marks four minutes of her attention and not one second more, which for her was a kind of mercy.

Olive's string had been a good idea and Olive's string had been a bad instrument, because a string stretches, and a stretched string in a warm room measures whatever it feels like measuring. But the girl had got herself three good numbers out of it and had written them on the tile in chalk where the whole hall could see them, in a hand so large and careful that it took up a foot of wall per digit, and that, Annabeth thought, watching her copy the third figure into the corner of the field book, was the part that mattered. A wrong answer that a ten-year-old will show you is worth four right ones kept in a pocket.

"Your string's better than their paint," she said. "It's not better than a plumb. In the winter I'll bring a plumb and you can hold the line it hangs from, which is the harder half, and if you tell Marcus I said so I'll deny it in writing."

"Deal," Olive said, and went off to tell Marcus anyway, and Annabeth closed the field book and left it on the bench under her coat and got into the water, which she did off the edge with no sound at all, in one long fold, because somewhere in June spite had become technique and technique had become a habit she would never in her life admit to.

The hall took her the way it always took her: warm at the chest, cooler at the ankles, and loud. It was the loudness that got her every time. The lake in October was a quiet argument. This room was a family. Every sound anybody made came back off the four walls wearing extra weight, so that a laugh arrived a half second late and slightly bigger than it had left, and a splash had opinions, and the low endless talk of the four small ones in the shallow end came back as one thick friendly hum that you could lean your whole back against.

Thalia came off the deep-end block at twenty past seven in one flat dive, and the hole she left in the surface was big enough to read the skylight through, and she surfaced twenty feet down the hall with the steam rolling off her shoulders and the enormous satisfaction of a person who had spent Monday indoors doing drills in a cold gym and had not enjoyed it.

"The heel's fine," she announced, to the brickwork, before anybody had asked.

"Nobody asked," Annabeth said, from the rail.

"Nobody was going to. I got there first." Thalia swam in under the rail and surfaced with her hair pushed back in two handfuls and her elbows on the tile. "Also: watch this."

She went under again and did not come up, and Annabeth, who had been timing things since she was old enough to hold a pencil, put her wrist flat on the tile and counted the seconds in her head, and watched the steam where Thalia's bubble trail had been, and said, at fifty-three, "Out," and Thalia came up grinning.

"Fifty-four," Annabeth said.

"Fifty-six, easily."

"You're a clerk," Thalia said, with tremendous affection, and hauled herself up onto the block to do it again, and Annabeth looked at the back of her sister's head, wet and dark and steaming, and thought: *she counted with me. She could have come up whenever she liked and she came up on my number.*

It was a small thing. She put it in the pile with the others, which was a thing she had started doing in September and had never once told anybody about, and which was, she sometimes thought, the actual field book.

---

Annabeth heard the float war declared at ten to eight, from the shallow end, in the voice of a boy who had been waiting for it since five o'clock.

The float itself was a slab of old dock foam in a canvas skin, four feet by three, the colour of wet sand, and it lived in the deep end on a hook, and it had been the subject of a six-person campaign in early October to move it the length of the hall, which had taken eleven minutes and had been abandoned on arrival. Tonight it was towed into the shallows by Percy with the seriousness of a man bringing in a barge, and Clarisse got on it.

This was already more than Clarisse had agreed to. She had come in from the bench at eight, after twenty minutes of watching the deep end the way a quartermaster watches a loading bay, and because Percy had spent the last forty minutes being insufferable about rope, and she had lowered herself onto the canvas skin with her arms folded and her chin up and the flat expression of a duchess boarding a ferry.

"Deep end's clear," Percy said, from the water, with the rope already looped on his wrist.

"Deep end's clear," Clarisse agreed, looking at the four small ones in the shallows with Katie standing over them in her coat like a lifeguard with a clipboard. "Katie. Deep end's clear until I say otherwise."

"Until you say otherwise," Katie said, without turning around, which was the voice of a girl who had been given a job and had taken it.

"If the canvas splits," she said, "it's yours."

"If the canvas splits I'll buy you a new float."

"Chiron buys the floats."

"Then I'll owe Chiron a float and you'll enjoy that for the rest of your life."

"I will," Clarisse said. "That's the part that makes it worth the wet."

Percy took the tow rope in one hand, looped it over his wrist, and put the other hand flat on the float's near edge, and Annabeth, at the rail with the field book open on the tile beside her, gave them a count, because somebody had to and because she had been born for exactly this.

"Length of the hall and back," she said. "Under the rail and back under the rail. No touching the bottom. Thalia?"

"Fifty strokes is generous."

"Sixty, then, because I want him to have to try." She looked down the black length of the water, at the far inlet laying its small hills, at the steam hanging in one flat sheet under the brick, at the two of them. "Go."

He went. He was not fast, and that was the interesting part: he towed the way he did everything in water, with his whole body agreeing on a direction, his shoulder rolling under the rope, his kick low and unhurried, and the float came along behind him with Clarisse sitting on it in her camp shirt with her arms folded and her hair coming loose, being towed down a warm indoor lake on a Tuesday in October like a figurehead on a barge, with an expression of total imperial calm.

Twenty strokes. Thirty.

"His left shoulder's dropping," Thalia said, at Annabeth's elbow, because Thalia could not watch a physical event without coaching it.

"His left shoulder's fine."

"His left shoulder's been dropping since July and nobody in this family will say so."

"His left shoulder," Annabeth said, "is fourteen and growing, and it's been mentioned, and it's on the list, and the list is not tonight." But she looked, and it was dropping, and she put it in the pile anyway, because being right and being unkind were two separate drawers and she kept them shut separately.

Forty strokes. The rail at the far end came up green and black out of the steam, and Percy reached it, and instead of turning, he put both hands on the rail and hung there for a second, breathing, and said, down the length of the hall, "Clarisse. It's drifting."

"I know it's drifting."

"I want you to know I know you know."

"I've been told," Clarisse said, from the float, in the voice of a woman reviewing a contract, "that this was going to be a demonstration of towing. So far it's a demonstration of hanging on a rail."

And then, with the enormous unhurried calm of a person who had been waiting for exactly this moment since the kitchen, she stood up on the float.

The float did what dock foam does when a hundred and thirty pounds of fifteen-year-old stands up on it in four feet of green water: it left. It went down at one end and up at the other and then it was simply somewhere else, and Clarisse La Rue went into the black water of the deep end of Tempest Hall in her camp shirt and her boots — her boots, she had kept her boots on, over the rail, dry, as a matter of principle — with an expression that had been, until about a third of a second ago, imperial.

The hall made a noise like a door slamming.

"Nobody was standing on it," Percy said, to the surface, in the voice of an innocent. "I want that in the record. Nobody said anything about standing on it. Rule three says the deep end is not a dare and she made it one, with her feet."

Clarisse came up out of the water, hair plastered, shirt plastered, boots full of lake, and looked at him across four feet of churn.

"Tie," she said.

"That's not a countable outcome."

"It's countable. I counted it. Tie." And she put one hand flat on the tile, and Percy, still in the water himself, still breathing hard from the tow, looked at her hand on the tile, and Annabeth watched him make the decision that he had made every day since about the middle of June, and take it.

"You're going to pay for the boots," he said, and splashed her properly, flat and hard, with both hands, and Clarisse, who had been waiting for it and who had the better angle, took the water up in one sweep and gave it back, and then Marcus was in it, and Thalia, from the block, with the flat overhead throw she had raised her sister with, which traveled in this steam the way it traveled outdoors, which was to say unfairly, and Katie Gardner's four small ones came out of the shallow end like otters, and Rosie, who had never in her life been permitted to splash anybody, was handed a target by fate in the form of the Hermes boy's goggles, and took it.

"Silena," Percy shouted, from the middle of it, "the hall is under attack."

"I can see the hall is under attack," Silena said, from the doorway of the dressing room, where she was laying the warm towels out on the rail in fours. "I'm watching the hall be under attack from where the towels are dry. If anybody drowns in three feet of water I'm going to be so embarrassed that I'll move cabins."

"That's not a veto," Thalia said.

"It's not a veto. It's a forecast."

Nobody drowned. The war ran for nine minutes and ended the way the only good wars end, which is that the smallest combatant wins by accident and everybody lets her: Rosie, towed around the float by Percy as a raft with nothing but her fingertips on the canvas, arrived at the far rail ahead of the entire rest of the hall by half a length and went under the rope with her chin up, and the room, which had been deaf for nine minutes, went quiet, and Clarisse, treading water in the deep end with her boots full and her hair in her eyes, said, out loud, to the ceiling, "Good raft," which was not a count and not a time and not a distance, and which every single person in that hall understood perfectly.

---

At half past eight Clarisse got out, and Annabeth felt the hall go a half tone quieter, the way a room does when the person who is keeping it decides to stand up.

She had her jacket on over her swimsuit within about nine seconds of leaving the water, because she was not going to be seen otherwise, not in a hall, not in front of witnesses, and she took the slate down off its nail by the door and stood with it against her ribs and did the count the way she had done it on the first Tuesday, and the two since: not by walking round the room, but by looking.

"Five of ours," she said. "Marcus, Olive, Neve. The boy who came in on an errand. Katie and hers."

"Fifteen," Annabeth said, from the water, helpfully, and Clarisse gave her a look that could have trimmed a wick.

"Fourteen," Clarisse said. "Katie's got four tonight, not five. Rosie's the fourth. Count twice, write once, and don't ever let a count depend on how a line of kids is walking when you happen to turn your head." She wrote fourteen on the slate in her block capitals, under the fifth rule, where the porch could read it, and hung the slate back on its nail for one second, stepped back, looked at it, and then took it down again.

"I have to take it up," she said, to the room, in the tone of a woman announcing a tax.

"You have to take it up," Percy agreed, from the water.

"It's conditions. A grown-up knows who's in the water. I'm the grown-up. The slate goes up to the porch with the number on it and the number goes on the board under the forecast, and if the number's wrong then the whole thing's a hobby, and the whole thing is not a hobby."

"The whole thing is not a hobby," Annabeth said.

"I know you think it's not a hobby. I've read your book." Clarisse went to the door with the slate and the lantern, and stopped, and did not turn around, and said, in the exact same flat register, to nobody in particular: "Fourteen. Second Tuesday under conditions. Nobody's gone anywhere." And then she went out into the cold passage with the lantern swinging, and the iron door shut behind her with its slow patient sound, and the hall was fourteen people and one empty nail.

---

Thalia Grace was hanging on the rail at the deep end, in the warm water up to her collarbones, doing nothing at all on purpose, when the door opened at twenty to nine and a boy she had shot alongside twice came in out of the cold.

He had his own towel. That was the first thing she noticed, and she resented noticing it, because it meant somebody at this camp had told Rhys Calder where the hall was and what it was, and he had gone back to his own bunk and got a towel and come, which was not a thing you did on a rumor. He had his coat over his arm and his hair still wet from outside, and he stopped two steps in the way everybody stopped two steps in, because the room hit you all at once, and he read the slate on its nail from where he stood.

In and out the same door. Nobody swims alone. The deep end is not a dare. No glass anywhere near the tile. And whoever wants to come, comes.

He read the five of them in about six seconds, in the flat scanning way people read rules, and then read the fifth one again, slower, and nodded once at it, the way you nod at a fence you've been told about before you open it.

Clarisse had come back from the Big House porch a few minutes before Rhys Calder came in, with the slate on its nail again and no comment at all, which in this family meant the number had gone up the hill the way numbers were supposed to go up the hill, and Thalia had watched her pass the door without turning her head and had made a mental note of exactly nothing, because a person who keeps things about her family in a mental folder does not report on the folder.

"Katie's kids," Thalia said, from the rail, because somebody had to say something and she would rather it be a fact than a greeting.

"Marcus told me at the pavilion," Rhys said. "He said Tuesdays and Thursdays, and he said the fifth rule, and he said bring a towel."

"Marcus talks."

"Marcus talks like a man handing over a shift." He put his coat on the rack with the shoes, which was correct, and which meant somebody had told him that too, and Thalia looked at the water and did the thing she did when her sister was within twenty feet and had the expression that her sister currently had, which was to be entirely normal at a louder volume. "I'm not diving," she said, to nobody.

"Good," said Rhys, and did not look up from his coat.

"It's not that I can't."

"I know you can't. You did a flat one on the first Tuesday that put a hole in the surface you could read a book through. Marcus still brings it up."

"I can still do it."

"Then do it."

"It's been used," Thalia said, meaning the block, and meaning nothing by it, and he said, "Fair enough," and went to the far end of the room, and got in the water off the edge the way a person gets in when they have been in enough water to know that the ladder is for people in a hurry, and stayed in the shallow end with the Hermes boy, and did not swim a single length of anything.

Annabeth surfaced at the rail beside her sister's elbow with her hair pressed flat and her voice level. "You didn't say it once," she said.

"Say what."

"Who cares. Three weeks running you've said it before he's through the door. Tonight you said it zero times in the first ninety seconds, which is the longest you've gone since September."

"I'm counting to myself."

"You're counting to yourself," Annabeth agreed, with the enormous charity of a person who was not going to push it. "That's fine. That's allowed. Zero is a number."

"I'll say it twice for spite."

"You'll say it whenever you like. I'm going to go and be helpful about towels." And Annabeth went, and Thalia stayed on the rail with her jaw set in the warm water of a room somebody had opened on purpose, and said, quietly, to the far wall, where a boy from the Apollo cabin was showing one of Katie's line where the rungs were, "Who cares. Who cares," and then, after a moment, in a completely different register, to nobody at all: "He brought his own towel."

Katie's four went up at quarter to nine, in a line, in coats, wet-haired and shining, and Rhys Calder held the door for the last of them, which was the smallest possible size of a kindness, and which Thalia saw, and which she did not put into words for anyone, not even later, not even to her sister, not even in the dark of her own bunk; and he went out with them, in his own coat, with his own wet hair, carrying the coat pile Katie had left on the bench, and the door swung shut on four small backs and a cold passage and a lantern going up the hill in Katie's hand, and Thalia let the hall be warm again, and went back to doing nothing at all on purpose.

---

Silena Beauregard had a system for the towels and the system was this: they went down to the hall in the basket at ten to seven, four rows of them, off the cabin stove rail, still giving off heat; and they went into the little dressing room and over the hot rail off the inlet pipe, which was the best radiator in the building and which Beckendorf had pointed out to her on the first Tuesday without appearing to notice that he was telling her a thing; and then, at nine, when the small ones had gone and the hall belonged to the grown-ups and the almost-grown-ups and the water had gone from choppy to flat, she took them down the rack in fours and went round the room with them.

It was, she thought, the third Tuesday, going round with the basket under her arm and eleven warm towels folded over it: the closest thing she had to a language that nobody could argue with. You could not hand somebody a warm towel and have it mean nothing. It was impossible. The heat went through the cloth and into the hands and the shoulders and the wet hair, and the person on the edge of the water took it and said *thanks* in whatever register they had, and for four seconds they were held, and then they went back in, and she went round again.

Marcus took one and said, "You're a saint," without looking at her, and Olive took one and put it over her head like a tent and stayed there, and the Hermes boy took one and said, "Is it always like this?" and Silena said, "It's usually quieter," and he said, "No, I mean the *towels*," and she had to look at the basket for a second.

Neve from the forge got the last of the dry ones, and said, in her quiet way, that the suit with the silk repair was holding, and Silena said, "Good," in her busy voice, and put the empty basket on the bench, and was aware, as she always was at nine o'clock on a Tuesday, that she had one towel left, and that she was holding it back, and that the holding back was not for anybody in the water.

She went and sat on the low bench by the inlet, which was the warmest six feet of tile in the building, with the towel on her knees, and did not look at the door, and looked at the door.

The hall was down to seven in the water. Marcus was teaching the Hermes boy the under-the-rail push-off. Olive had come out of her towel tent to argue with Neve about whether the steam counted as weather. Annabeth and Thalia were at the deep end rail having one of the low running arguments that the Grace sisters had instead of opinions, and Percy was somewhere in the middle of the room being, as far as Silena could tell, towed by the float rather than towing it, and Clarisse was on the bench by the door in her jacket with the list on her knee, which was where she always was at nine.

The outside door opened at five past nine and the cold came in with the smell of the forge on it, and Charlie Beckendorf came two steps into the hall and stopped, in his canvas coat, with a tool roll under one arm and his own towel in his hand, and looked at the room.

He did the thing he always did, which was take the whole room in the order a person takes in a building: the water, the steam, the people, the slate, the racks, the door. And then he read the fifth rule on its nail, the way he had read it the first time, and the way Thalia had read it three-quarters of an hour ago, slowly, and came in, and put his coat on the rack beside the shoes.

"I'm not here for the pump," he said, when he got to the bench, which was four feet from where she was sitting, and which he said before she could ask, and which was, she thought, the fourth sentence he had said to her all month, and each one of them had arrived the same way, which was slightly too early and entirely on purpose.

"The pump's been fine since Sunday."

"The pump's been fine since Sunday. I came to look at the hall when there's people in it." He unrolled the tool roll on the tile beside him, which contained, as far as she could see, nothing that had anything to do with anything in this room, and said, without looking at her, "Also the second lantern's low. The wick's gone soft on the left bracket. I'll trim it on my way out."

"It's low," Silena agreed. "I've been turning it every twenty minutes so it doesn't drown."

"I know. It's been turning at about the rate a person turns it when they're sitting right next to it." He got the little brass snips out of the roll and stood up and went to the left bracket, and did the wick in the dark the way he did everything, unhurried, by feel, and the lantern came up and the tile under it went from brown to honey, and he stood there for a second looking at the colour on the wall.

"Honey," he said.

"That's what I said."

"That's what you said on the second of October, and I've been trying to find the word in the shop ever since. I've got three panels of glass and a boy who cuts them and neither of us can say the colour out loud." He put the snips away and came back to the bench and did not sit down. "I'm going to swim. Is that — the rules don't say anything about whether you've got to have a suit from the bag or whether you can come in as you are."

"Rule four's the glass one."

"Right."

"Rule two's the one where somebody stays awake on the edge." She looked up at him, on the warm tile, with the towel on her knees. "I'm on the edge."

"So you are," Beckendorf said, and went to the ladder, and got in the water in his shorts and his work shirt, the way a man gets into a river on a bet, without any ceremony at all, and surfaced at the rail with his hair flat and his shoulders up and said, with enormous feeling, "That's *warm*."

"It's been warm since the first Tuesday."

"I know it's been warm. I'm telling you it's warm." He put his forearms on the tile and looked the length of the hall, at the steam, at the far inlet, at the Hermes boy arguing with Marcus about a rule that did not exist. "This is what it sounds like with people in it, then."

"That's what it sounds like with people in it."

"I'd only ever heard it empty. Pipes, mostly. And the heater going on and off like a clock." He was quiet a second. "An empty hall's a good room. It's not this, though."

"No," Silena said. "It's not this."

And then, because it was a Tuesday and because the first conversation had happened on a Sunday and had been sitting between the two of them since, in the way a new plank sits between two old ones, slightly proud of the surface, catching everything: because of that, they talked.

It was not a lot. It was the kind of talking that a hall full of steam makes easy, because you could look at the water while you did it and nobody could see your face from four feet away in that light. He told her the west lamps had gone through two frosts and held, and that the ninth one, the one at the cedar brake, had a moth problem that was going to be a January problem and not an October problem, and that he had written it on a card and nailed the card to the post so that whoever got sent up there in the winter would not have to work it out again in the cold. She told him that Katie had asked, at the pavilion, whether the small ones could come on Thursdays too, and that she had said yes before she had finished hearing the question, and that Clarisse had written it on the winter list under a new heading she had made herself, in a different pen, and had not told anybody about the heading.

"What's the heading?"

"I'm not telling you the heading. She writes it in a different pen. That's what a different pen means."

Beckendorf laughed, quietly, into the rail, which was the second time she had made him do that since the second of October, and which she counted, because she counted everything, and because counting things was the only kind of hoarding she had ever been able to afford.

Then he said, "Your mending came back Friday."

"It came back Friday," Silena said.

"I put a note in it."

"I know you put a note in it."

"I'm mentioning it because I've been carrying a thing around all week and I'd like to put it down somewhere." He said it to the water, with his forearms on the tile, in the flat working voice he had, which did not do speeches and had just done one. "That's all. It's put down."

"It's put down," Silena said, and her own voice came out level, and she was so grateful for it that she nearly laughed. "Also the note was a good note. It had a drawing on it. I don't know why you drew the hook."

"Because the hook's the good part."

"The hook is a hook."

"The hook's the part I'd have got wrong in June," he said, and looked at her, and it was the first actual look of the evening, across four feet of warm tile and steam and the whole long hum of a hall that had been opened for people, and it lasted about as long as it takes to say a sentence like that out loud, and then he looked at the water again, and so did she.

"Thursday," Silena said, after a while.

"Thursday," Beckendorf said.

"The hall's open on Thursdays."

"I know the hall's open on Thursdays. I made the heater do what it does." He got out at ten past nine, in the slow unbothered way he got out of things, and stood on the tile dripping into the honey light, and rolled his sleeves down, and picked up the tool roll. "The wick's trimmed. The lantern'll hold till January now, and the moth problem's on a card, and I'm going up the hill before I say anything else about the hook."

"Good night, Charlie."

"Good night, Silena." He went to the door, and stopped with his hand on the frame, the way people did in this family now, apparently, as though the frame were a place you stood to finish a sentence, and said, without turning around, "Sunday. The eight o'clock one. If you're ever past the archery field at eight on a Sunday, it's a good hour to be past it."

"Eight o'clock," Silena said, to the door.

"Eight o'clock," he said, and went out into the cold, and the door shut, and the second lantern burned on its bracket in the low steady way a lantern burns when somebody who knows the trade has been at the wick.

Silena Beauregard sat on the warmest bench in the building with one towel left on her knees, and did not move for a while, and did not need to. Then she got up and went round the room and did the last round, and gave the last warm towel to Annabeth, who was climbing out at the deep end with her hair flat and her shoulders steaming and the field book already under her arm, and Annabeth took it and looked at her for one second with the particular expression she wore when she was choosing, out of a great many available observations, to be kind instead of accurate, and said only, "Your hair's doing a thing," and Silena said, "Everything's doing a thing," and that was the whole of it, and it was enough.

Percy was the last one in the water, which he had not planned and would not have volunteered for, and which happened the way it always happened: everybody else got out, and the room sorted itself into towels and coats and noise, and he stayed, and the hall emptied around him until it was just the black length of it and the steam and the small patient lapping against the ladder.

He went to the far end and back once, slowly, with nothing to tow and nobody racing him, and touched the rail at the inlet end and stayed there with both hands on the tile, and let the room be what it was on a Tuesday at fifteen past nine: the heater ticking down, the two lanterns burning low and even because somebody who knew the trade had been at the wick, the skylight gone black, the warm hills of the inlet making their small endless argument with the cold.

Fifteen people had been in this water tonight. Four of them were seven to ten years old and had run down a hill for it. One of them had stood up on a float on purpose and gone in boots first. One of them had come with his own towel on a rumour and held the door for the smallest of the line.

"Last one out," Silena said from the door, with the empty basket on her hip, "gets the hall, and the hall is going to be cold in four minutes, so make it a fast last one."

He made it a fast last one. He went up the ladder, and the tile was warm under his feet, and the cold of the passage hit him at the door the way it always hit him, which was like stepping off a boat onto a stone quay, and Silena put a towel on his head from the basket without stopping, and he followed her up the passage in his sandals with his hair dripping and his shoulders going steaming cold, and outside the iron door the whole cabin was doing the thing it did on Tuesdays and Thursdays, which was being a family at half past nine in the evening with a stove going and a lamp on and the smell of hot towels in the stone.

They went up the hill together, seven of them, at nine thirty, because that was how the evening had ended and nobody had arranged it. Rhys Calder had gone up at a quarter to nine, with the coat pile under his arm and Katie's four in front of him like a small slow convoy, because he was the sort of person who took a coat pile, and Thalia had watched him take it and had said nothing at all, which Percy thought was worth more than the last four things she had said out loud.

The west path took the seven of them up in single file at the bottom and in threes at the bend, and the lanterns were on, end to end, the way they had been every night since the third of October: nine of them, amber, staggered left-odd and right-off, and the ninth one at the cedar brake burning low in its hood with a moth already in it, which was going to be somebody's January.

"That's the survey from up here," Marcus said, at the bend, with his hands in his pockets, looking back down the slope at the lake, which from the bend was nothing at all except a place where the stars stopped. "My dad asked me about it on Sunday. I told him five names and a running seep and he said, 'That's not a hobby, that's a water board.'"

"It's not a water board," Annabeth said, behind Percy, in the tone she used for accepting a compliment on somebody else's behalf.

"It's got a rod," Marcus said. "Anything with Marcus's rod on it's a water board."

"You didn't bring the rod. Marcus brought the rod."

"Then it's a water board and I'm the mascot." He took the top path at the split with a wave, and the Hermes boy went with him, and Neve went on up toward the forge cabins with her hood down and her hair still dripping, because forge girls were forge girls in any weather, and the seven became four, and the four became three at the Cabin One fork, where Thalia stopped with her hands in her pockets and her chin up and the lantern light doing the flat amber thing it did on her face.

"Quilt held," she said. "Four days of wind off that ridge and it's still on the poles. I walked past it this morning. Sixteen eyelets and the left corner still off an inch, and Clarisse's winter list says leave it, and I think that's the most anybody's ever said about a quilt."

"Leave it," Annabeth said. "That's her whole review of it and she's not wrong."

"Leave it's a five-star review in this family." Thalia looked up the hill, past the cabins, at the dark shoulder of the north ridge, where a blue rag was tied to six stones somewhere that neither of them could see and both of them could point at. "Right. I'm going to go and be asleep, and if either of you tells Rhys Calder I was cold on this path, I'll deny it and then I'll make it true." She went up toward Cabin One, and did not limp, and did not look back, and the tenth lantern, down the hill at the bottom, burned on its hook by the door of Cabin Three, and Percy watched his friend go up the hill in the dark and thought, not for the first time this week, that the pile was filling.

---

Silena went on ahead up the last stretch of the west path with the empty basket on her hip, because she had a stove to bank and a rail of dry towels to put away and a bag on a bench with four suits rolled in it, and she did not look back once, and she did not have to; the two of them behind her on the path were as much a fixture of a Tuesday as the lanterns, and she had decided something about that in the dressing room at nine o'clock and had put it in the pile with the rest of it, unspoken, the way she did everything.

So it was the two of them on the path at the bottom, at twenty to ten, in the coldest air of the season so far, with the frost coming up off the ground faster than it came down out of the sky.

"Walk you to your door," Annabeth said, which was what he had said to her on Saturday, in exactly that register, and which she said now without any emphasis at all, so that it would land as logistics.

"That's my line."

"It's a cabin line. There's a house full of us and we've all used it."

"Your route is forty feet long."

"It's a very important forty feet. It's got a lantern on it."

They went down past the dark cabins with their hands in their own pockets and their shoulders occasionally touching, and the porch lantern ahead of them burned in its hood by the door of Cabin Three, with the slow steady generosity of a flame that has been fed by hands that know the trade, and the porch light over it was off, and the bench under it was beaded with the first of the night's frost, and Percy put his coat sleeve across the bench in the automatic way you wipe a thing you intend to sit on, and then they were sitting, on the porch of his own cabin, in the dark, under a lantern, with the whole hill asleep around them.

Annabeth had the field book on her knee. She opened it at the back, at the last page of Saturday's survey, and he knew that page the way he knew the sound of her boots on a stair; and then she wrote tonight's entry under it, in the same unhurried hand, and let him read it over her shoulder, because letting him was the whole point of writing anything at all.

TUE 17 — HALL. 14 IN. SECOND TUESDAY.

And under it, smaller: R. C. came.

"You put his initials," Percy said.

"I put his initials because there's no rule about initials and there is a rule about names." She did not look up. "And before you ask, Thalia will read this page in November, or she won't, and either way she'll be insufferable about it, and I'd rather she be insufferable about a page that's accurate."

"You could have written *who cares* under it."

"I considered it. I didn't have the space." She closed the book and put her hand flat on the cover, and then, without any hurry at all, said, "The note's still in there."

"I know the note's still in there."

"It's the back cover's note. It's not a temporary note." She said it to the lantern, in the level voice she used for facts that had taken her a year to be able to say out loud, and then she turned her head and looked at him on the bench in the dark, and the look was the sort that arrives with its own weather, and Percy Jackson, who had been kissed on a porch before, in another life, in this life, in the middle of October, felt the whole evening slow down to the rate at which she was looking at him.

"Yeah," he said. "It's in the same place as yours."

"I know where mine is."

"In my coat. Left pocket. It's been in my coat for eleven days and I've stopped being surprised by it, which I think is the actual achievement of the week, and I'd like that noted."

"Noted," Annabeth said, and then she put the field book down on the frosty bench beside them, which was a thing she would never do with a field book, and which told him everything about where her attention had gone, and he put his arm along the back of the bench behind her shoulders, and she came in against him the way water comes back into a footprint, gradually and completely.

They did not start with the kiss. They sat for a while and let the porch be a porch: the stove ticking itself out through the wall behind them, somewhere in the cabin above them Silena's boots on the boards, the frost making its small quiet business on the rail, the lantern hood ticking as the metal cooled. Annabeth's hair had gone almost dry on the walk up and had gone back to its own opinion about itself, and she let it fall across her own face and then across his shoulder, and he pushed it back with two fingers, and she caught his hand and did not let go of it, and that was the language they had been speaking since June: hands, in which a sentence could be six words or six minutes.

"You're freezing," he said, eventually, because his hand was hers and her hand was October.

"I'm sixty percent freezing. The other forty's fine and the forty's the part I'm keeping." She turned her hand over under his so that their palms were flat together, which was a thing she did when she wanted to hold on without it looking like holding on. "Your shoulder's cold too and you're the one who swam last."

"I'm a Poseidon. I'm cold in a warm room. It's a documented—" He stopped, and she looked at him, and he said, with enormous dignity, "I'm not saying it. That phrase is retired. It's had a good summer."

"It's had a very good summer and it's had enough." She put her head against his shoulder, and her voice came up at him from the wool of his coat. "Stay out here until I go up?"

"Annabeth, this is my porch."

"Then stay out here on your own porch until I go up, and don't make it a whole thing."

He did not make it a thing. He put his other hand over both of theirs, on her knee, and let the cold do its work on the rail and the frost and the far dark of the lake, and after a while she tipped her face up and he bent his head down to it, and they kissed on the porch of Cabin Three under the lantern with the whole hill asleep, and it was slow in the way that their kissing had learned to be slow, which was to say it had stopped being a single event and had become a conversation: a first minute that was mostly breath, and then the second, in which the cold of the air and the warmth of the mouth made their usual argument, and her hand coming up to the side of his neck where the collar was, because the collar was cold and she had never in her life let a cold thing stay where it was if she could reach it, and his arm coming round her back so that she was not balancing on a bench anymore but was, simply, held.

The hall had taught them, in its indirect way, what a room with steam in it does to a kiss: it makes it a bigger sound than it is. The porch did the opposite. The porch took the sound and gave it to the frost and the dark and the two of them, and did not repeat it, and did not need to. They broke for air at the kind of distance where you can still feel the other person's breath and said nothing, and then went back in, because there was no reason not to, and the lantern above them went on doing what it had been doing since October, which was burning amber in a hood on a hook and letting the moths argue with it.

"That's the second Tuesday," he said, into her hair, a while later, with his eyes shut and his back against the porch post.

"That's the second Tuesday."

"There was a survey on a mountain and a quilt on a beach and ten lanterns and a note in the back of a book and a first conversation on a Sunday, and now there's a slate with fourteen on it and a boy who brought his own towel. That's the week. That's actually the whole week, and I'm putting it in the pile and I'm going to keep it."

"You're putting it in the pile," Annabeth said, from his shoulder, in the voice of a girl who kept an identical pile in an identical place, "and Thursday's mail, and Thursday's hall, and Saturday if the frost holds, and the bank Clarisse is going to take a shovel to, and the heron that's still on the bow of that skiff, and the fortieth—" she stopped herself, and he felt her grin against his coat, "—which I am not going to finish saying, because you'll be insufferable about it for a decade."

"I'm going to be insufferable about it until the spring."

"I know. I put it in the pile too." She got up, then, the way a person gets up who does not want to, which is slowly and in two pieces, and picked up the field book off the frost and shook the frost off its cover, and put it under her arm, and stood on the top step of his own porch in his own lantern light and looked down at him with her hair going its own way and her eyes bright with a Tuesday.

"Thursday," she said.

"Thursday," he said.

She went up the west path alone, because she had done it a hundred times and because the path had nine lanterns on it and the ninth was at the bend where you could look back, and Percy sat on the bench on the porch of Cabin Three and watched her go up in the dark, and she looked back at the bend, and did not wave, because waving was a performance and Annabeth Grace did not perform on the way home, and then she was past the bend and there was only the hill and the pines and the cold clear sky with more stars in it than anybody had names for, and the frost coming down on the grass at the rate of a thing that had been waiting all week.

Clarisse's stove ticked behind the wall. The porch lantern burned in its hood. Somewhere down the hill the hall was cooling in the dark, four rows of dry towels in a rack, a slate with its fourteen still on it, one nail that had nothing hanging on it, and the last of the warm water lying still enough to hold both the lantern's light and the black square of the skylight at the same time, without mixing them; and the whole camp slept above it, and the week went on being the sort of week that nobody would know the shape of until about four years from now, when somebody would be standing on a porch somewhere in a different country, counting lanterns.
