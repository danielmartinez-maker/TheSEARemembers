## Chapter 101: A Name for It

By breakfast, Percy had decided not to ask Annabeth the question.

He made that decision while watching her across the pavilion. She was drawing something on the back of a meal roster, balancing the paper against the side of her cup because the table wobbled. The pencil kept skidding every time the bench shifted. She pinned the page with one finger, turned the pencil sideways, and drew a straight line anyway.

Percy knew better than to interrupt her when she was measuring something. He also knew the page had nothing to do with his question. It was probably a shelf or a bracket or a note to herself about a shelf or bracket. Annabeth had a way of making several objects look like one project.

The question had been in his head for days, which was one reason he had decided not to ask it. If a thought stayed long enough, it began to sound as if it had a formal title. This one had acquired several: *Are we still together?* was the least bad. *Do you still want to be my girlfriend?* was the one that made him feel as if he had missed the last four months. *Does this feel like ours?* was worse because he could not define what *this* meant without making it sound like a prophecy.

They were together. They had been together before the displacement and had kept choosing each other here. The first current-life kiss had not erased the ones he remembered, and the years they both carried had not made every new thing a copy. He knew that. He still wanted to hear what Annabeth wanted now.

Across the table she used the pencil to tap the edge of her cup, frowned at the wobbling table, then folded the meal roster beneath one leg. The cup stopped moving.

Percy looked down at his own breakfast. Clarisse had already finished, which meant she had eaten half the fruit from the middle platter while claiming she was only making sure it was ripe. Silena was telling her that this was not how fruit ripened. Clarisse had replied that it was how *she* ripened it.

He could ask one simple question. He had managed more difficult things. He had once jumped into the Underworld with less planning than this. That comparison did not help.

Annabeth looked up. She caught him staring and lifted her eyebrows.

Percy made a face that was meant to say *I have a normal reason to be looking at you.* It probably said the opposite.

She pointed to the far side of the pavilion, where the shrine rose beyond the dining tables. Then she held up one finger, finished the line she was drawing and set the pencil down. She was already giving him more time than he had asked for.

Percy waited while she put away the rest of her breakfast. Clarisse watched him wait and made an exaggerated show of checking the pavilion clock.

“You’re going to wear a groove in the floor,” she said.

“I’m sitting down.”

“Your foot isn’t.”

He looked under the table. His sneaker was tapping the wooden brace.

Silena set the fruit bowl between them. “Let him be nervous for a minute.”

“I’m not nervous,” Percy said.

“Then your foot is doing that for fun?”

He stopped moving it. “Maybe.”

Clarisse gave him the look she used when she had proved a point and did not need to say so. Percy found himself smiling despite the heat in his face.

Annabeth came back for the sketchbook she had left beneath her place. She tucked the roster inside and gathered the loose pencils. “Ready?”

Percy stood too quickly and knocked his knee against the bench. “Yep.”

She waited until he had his balance. “You all right?”

“Absolutely. The bench started it.”

“I’ll keep that in mind.”

They left the pavilion by the path that curved toward the shrine. The morning had settled into its ordinary camp sounds: a sword meeting a shield in the arena, a conch note from the lake, somebody calling for a missing shoe. Percy could see the path markers from the previous night’s celebration had been collected into a neat stack beside the steps. The fire on the hill was banked, its smoke gone.

Annabeth carried the sketchbook under one arm. The meal roster stuck out at the top, its penciled line dividing two columns. Percy wanted to ask his question while they were still walking, before he could decide it needed another title. Instead he watched her step over a shallow rut and followed her around it.

At the shrine, she set the book on a flat section of stone. It was the small structure she had designed for the camp, with an inset niche and room for offerings. Fresh flowers rested at the base. Percy remembered the names on the card Ella had helped write for Hades’s children; the damp petals had begun to curl at their edges.

Annabeth opened the sketchbook to a page filled with measurements. “I’m trying to see whether we can fit a narrow bookcase against this side without blocking the niche.”

“What kind of books?”

“Things people are allowed to borrow. Myth references, camp maps, building notes. Nothing private. The old storage room in Cabin One has books nobody opens because they’re buried under boxes.” She touched the edge of the paper. “I want to move some over, but only if the shelves are stable.”

“Do you need a second pair of hands?”

“I need you to hold this end while I check the angle.” She handed him a narrow board.

Percy took it. It was lighter than it looked, and the rough edge caught his palm. He moved his grip to the smooth side.

“Not there,” Annabeth said. “If you put your thumb over the mark, I can’t tell where the edge lands.”

He shifted his hand without comment. She braced a measuring square against the stone and studied the space. Percy held the board while she checked the wall, then crouched to inspect where it would meet the floor.

“It's not level,” she said.

“Is it supposed to be?”

“No. That's usually the point of measuring.” She leaned back on her heels. “The foundation settled a little on this side. If I build the shelf against the wall, the back corner will lean.”

“Could you put something under the short side?”

“Yes. But I need to know how much. If I guess, the whole frame will still shift when it has weight on it.”

She asked him to lower the board, and he set it down against the stones. She used a pencil to mark the corner, then retrieved a slim wooden strip from her tool roll. A narrow strip of pine sat beside her pencil case, brought with the repair materials.

Annabeth slid the strip under the board. The top edge tilted the other way.

“That was too much?” Percy asked.

“That was a demonstration.” She pinched the strip between her fingers, removed it, and measured the difference. “Now I have a number to work with.”

“So the demonstration was successful.”

“It was crooked.”

“Those aren’t mutually exclusive.”

She looked up at him. Her mouth bent toward a smile, then her eyes went back to the measurement. Percy liked the way she could be amused without surrendering a point.

They spent a few minutes checking the alcove from different sides. Annabeth made marks, erased them, and added them again. Percy held the board only when she asked. When he offered to move it once, she told him she had not finished measuring. He put both hands in his pockets until she called for help.

The shelf work should have made his question easier. Instead, the longer they stood there, the more carefully he tried to phrase it.

Annabeth noticed. “You have something to ask me.”

“I do.”

“Is it about the shelves?”

“No.”

“Did you want to start with that?”

“I was hoping the shelves would distract me.”

“They’ve been trying to distract me for the last hour. It’s not a very effective plan.”

Percy set the board down. He wiped his palms on his shorts, then immediately regretted it because he had picked up a streak of sawdust. “Can we sit for a minute?”

They sat on the low stone edge of the foundation. Annabeth closed her sketchbook but kept her pencil between her fingers. The flowers at the shrine shifted in a breeze. Down the path, two campers argued over a practice shield; neither sounded upset enough to need an adult.

Percy looked at the pencil instead of her face. “I wanted to ask you how the words we use feel now.”

“What words?”

“Boyfriend and girlfriend. Us being together.” He swallowed. “I know we were together in the life we remember. I don’t want to act like that decides everything for you here.”

Annabeth's eyes stayed on him. She did not rush to fill the pause.

Percy kept going because stopping would leave the worst part unsaid. “We hold hands. We write notes. We kiss. I like all of that. I like being with you. I just don't want to assume we need the same things we did before, or that the old memories answer every question about how we live now.”

“So you're asking whether I still want to be your girlfriend.”

“That's the short version.”

“You could've started with that.”

“I tried. It sounded too easy.”

“Percy, it is easy.” She turned the pencil between her fingers. “I want to be your girlfriend. I want to be with you. I’m not doing it because I think I owe the girl I was something, and I’m not doing it because I remember how we used to be. I want it now.”

The sentence loosened something in him. It did not fix every possible future worry, but it was an answer to the question he had actually asked.

“I’m glad,” he said.

“Are you asking what I need, or do you want me to give you a list?”

“I'd rather have a list than guess wrong.”

“I don't have a list.”

“That's a very unhelpful list.”

Annabeth smiled. “I don't need every walk to become a meeting,” Annabeth said. “If I take your hand, it means I want to. If I move away, let me move away.”

“Okay.”

“And if something bothers you, tell me. Don't decide it's just the old life and keep it to yourself.”

“I will. You tell me too.”

“I do.”

“I know. I mean, keep doing it.”

“That I can promise.”

He rubbed his thumb along the edge of the stone. “I keep thinking the girl you were already decided this for both of us.”

“She didn't decide it for me.” Annabeth's voice sharpened. “I remember loving you. I also love you now. If I ever stop wanting this, I'll tell you. Don't protect me from the choice.”

Percy let that sit. He wanted to say he would be careful. He wanted to say he understood. Both sounded too much like promises he would make before knowing what he had understood.

“Okay,” he said. “I'll listen.”

“Good.”

The pencil left a gray mark on her finger. She rubbed it once, then set it beside the sketchbook. Percy wanted to reach for her hand. He waited.

Annabeth took his hand and kissed him. It was brief and a little off-center; the stone ledge forced them to turn at the same time. When she drew back, a gray streak from her thumb marked his cheek.

“You got graphite on me.”

“You were in the way.”

“Of the pencil?”

“Of my thumb.”

She wiped the mark with her sleeve. He smiled, and she picked up the sketchbook again.

Annabeth noticed his pause. “You can hold my hand.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

He took her hand. “I know now.”

Her thumb moved once over his knuckle. It was a small touch, familiar and new at the same time.

“I like being your girlfriend,” she said. “But maybe we can have a word that feels like ours here too.”

“Like a nickname?”

“Maybe.”

“‘My person’?”

“That sounds like a prophecy.”

“‘Partner’?”

“That's what we are when we're on a quest.”

“‘Teammate’?”

“That's worse.”

“‘Favorite person’?”

Annabeth looked down at their hands, considering. “That one might work.”

“So we're favorite people?”

“And boyfriend and girlfriend.” She squeezed his hand. “The nickname doesn't replace anything.”

“Good. I would've had to return all the boyfriend paperwork.”

“There is no paperwork.”

“You don't know that. I could have signed something without reading it.”

“You didn't.”

“I don't remember signing it.”

“That's because it doesn't exist.”

“Sounds like a design flaw.”

Annabeth shook her head, smiling. “Favorite person can be ours. We don't have to use it every time somebody looks at us.”

“That's fair.”

“And we don't have to hide it either.”

“No.”

She let that sit between them long enough for him to hear its difference from the other promise. Their private words could stay private. Their relationship did not have to be.

Annabeth glanced toward the path that led to Cabin One. “I want to tell Thalia what we talked about.”

“Together?”

“If that's okay.”

“Of course.”

“She'll worry.”

“She already worries.”

“She'll worry in a new direction.”

“I can handle that.”

“I know. I still want to be the one who tells her.”

Percy nodded. “Then you tell her. I'll be there.”

Annabeth closed her hand around his, and they stood. She returned the sketchbook to her bag, tucked the pencil behind her ear, and lifted the board again.

“Are we still working on this?” Percy asked.

“We've only established the floor isn't level.”

“That's a beginning.”

“It is if we find the shim that fits.”

She held out one end. Percy took it where she pointed.

---

Percy and Annabeth took the board to the worktable beside the shrine. It was a steady table, built wide enough to hold plans without folding them, and the tools laid out on it belonged to the camp's general repair box. Annabeth checked the pencil marks against her measurements again.

“You sure you don't want me to find somebody who knows how to cut this?” Percy asked.

“I know how to cut it.”

“I know. I meant—”

“I know what you meant.” She ran a finger along the line. “You can help me hold it straight. The cut is mine.”

Percy leaned back. “Got it.”

“You don't have to act like every question is a trap.”

“I wasn't.”

“You were preparing your defense.”

“Maybe I like to know what the charges are.”

She lifted the small hand saw, placed its teeth against the mark, and checked the angle. Percy put one hand at the far end of the board when she nodded. She worked slowly, not because she was afraid but because a crooked cut would ruin the fit. The saw rasped through the wood in an even rhythm. Percy watched the line, ready to steady the board if it shifted.

When the strip came free, Annabeth turned it over and inspected the edge. She held it under the wall shelf, then pushed one end into the gap.

“It fits.”

“Good.”

“It needs sanding.”

“Still good.”

“I didn't say it was finished.”

“You said it fits.”

“That is one requirement.”

“It passed one requirement.”

She slid the shim back out and set it beside the sketch. The shelf frame would need another check after she attached it, but it no longer rocked when they balanced the board over the uneven floor. Annabeth added the new measurement to the drawing and drew a thin line beneath it.

“Do you want to move on to the sides?” Percy asked.

“Not until I decide whether I need to change the depth. I want books to fit without sticking past the edge, but I don't want the shelf to block the niche.”

“Want me to take a look?”

She gave him the page. “Tell me what you see. Don't solve it yet.”

Percy bent over the drawing. At first he saw the shelf, the niche and the numbers. Then he noticed the narrow triangle left between the bookcase and the wall.

“Is this the problem?”

“What do you think?”

“If the shelves are too deep, they'll cover part of the niche. If they're too shallow, the bigger books fall over.”

“That's the problem.”

“Could you turn them sideways?”

“Some could. Not all.”

“Could you make the shelves different depths?”

Annabeth considered it. “Possibly. If the lower shelf is deeper, and the top one is shallower, the face won't line up.”

“Would that be bad?”

“Not if it is intentional.”

“Would it look intentional?”

“That's the next question.”

She took the paper back. Percy had offered a shape, not an answer. He could feel the difference between those things now, although remembering it in the middle of a conversation was harder than understanding it afterward.

Annabeth penciled two versions. In one, the shelf followed the line of the wall; in the other, the lower surface came forward by an inch. She ran her fingertip over each drawing.

“I need to see it in the space,” she said.

“Want to use the board as a mock-up?”

“Yes. We'll lean it against the wall and mark the edge. Don't lift until I tell you.”

“Okay.”

They moved the board to the alcove. Percy held it level while Annabeth shifted the bottom end by a fraction. The piece was small enough that it did not require much strength, only steady hands. She marked where it would sit, then stepped back.

“It blocks the corner by less than I thought,” she said.

“Is that good?”

“I need to look at it from the path.”

“I've heard that's a kind of answer.”

“Only when you need information.”

She went around the shrine and looked from the path, then from the niche, then from the low stone step. Percy held the board in place. The whole design changed depending on where a person stood. From the path it looked like a shelf. From beside the niche it looked like a wall. From the step, it left enough room for a small person to reach the lower row.

A younger camper came up the path carrying a smooth shell. They paused when they saw the board braced against the shrine.

“Are you making shelves?” they asked.

“Maybe,” Annabeth said. “We’re checking whether they’ll fit and stay stable.”

The camper looked at the lowest pencil mark. “Will the small kids be able to reach?”

“That’s what I’m trying to work out.” Annabeth held a scrap of paper against the wall at the planned height. “Would you try this?”

The camper reached up from the path, then stepped onto the stone edge and tried again. “I can get it, but I can’t see the title if the book is back there.”

Annabeth lowered the mark a little and asked them to check once more. This time the camper could see the paper strip and still leave room for the offering niche.

“That's better,” they said.

Annabeth wrote down the change. “Thank you. We won't put books here until the frame is tested.”

“Can I pick one when you do?”

“If the shelf holds and you want to.”

The camper left the shell in the niche and went back toward the cabins. Annabeth kept the lower mark where they had agreed.

“I want the lower shelf reachable,” she said. “If somebody leaves a book here or wants to take one down, they shouldn't have to ask for help.”

“Then the deeper shelf goes low?”

“Maybe.”

She moved the mock-up again. Percy followed her directions. They checked the wall, the path, the place where offerings were set and the space a wheelchair would need to pass. Annabeth was quiet while she did the measurements. Percy knew better than to fill each pause with a suggestion.

After a while she sat on the edge of the step. “I don't want to make the shrine feel like a storage room.”

“Then we don't fill it with every book in Cabin One.”

“That's the obvious answer.”

“It seems useful.”

“It is. I still need to decide which ones belong here.”

“Do you want to decide today?”

“No. I wanted to see if the shelves would fit. That's enough for now.”

She closed the sketchbook. Percy let the board rest on the table, careful to set it flat.

“You don't have to make the project solve everything,” he said.

“I wasn't.”

“I know. I was talking to the project.”

“Good. It looked worried.”

She smiled, then turned back to the sketchbook. “Now, about the shelf.”

Annabeth slipped the sketchbook into her bag. “We should tell Thalia before lunch.”

“Now?”

“If I wait until after, she'll hear about it from Clarisse.”

“Clarisse wouldn't tell her.”

“Clarisse wouldn't tell her on purpose. She'd say something loud at exactly the wrong time.”

“That is different.”

“It is.”

They set off toward Cabin One. Percy walked beside Annabeth, not ahead. She was carrying the sketchbook and the pencil, and the board remained on the table for later. The path passed the arena, where a few campers were already gathering to practice. Percy kept his eyes on the path and did not search for a target.

---

Thalia sat on the Cabin One porch steps with a paper schedule balanced on her knee. She wrote with her left hand, the letters leaning downhill. Her right palm was wrapped and rested against her thigh. Percy noticed the bandage and kept his hands to himself.

She looked up as they approached. “If this is about the shelves, I haven't seen the drawing.”

“It isn't,” Annabeth said.

Thalia set down the pencil. “That's reassuring.”

Percy waited for Annabeth to choose where to begin. She had asked to tell her sister. He was there because Annabeth wanted him there, not because he needed to deliver the message for her.

Annabeth sat one step below Thalia. Percy stood beside the porch rail, close enough to join but far enough that the sisters could look at each other without him between them.

“We talked this morning,” Annabeth said. “About Percy and me.”

Thalia's gaze moved to him and back. “And?”

“We're together. We have been. We want to keep being together.” Annabeth folded her hands in her lap. “We also found a nickname that feels ours. Favorite person.”

Thalia's mouth twitched. “That's awful.”

“It's not.”

“It's a little awful.”

“Then you don't have to use it.”

“I wasn't planning to.”

Percy smiled before he could stop himself. Annabeth looked over at him, and some of the tightness in her shoulders eased.

Thalia tapped the pencil against the schedule. “Did you want me to say something?”

“No,” Annabeth said. “I wanted you to know because you're my sister. I don't want to hide it from you or act like Percy is a stranger.”

“That's not what I asked.”

Annabeth's expression changed. She heard the worry underneath the teasing. “You can say what you think. You don't get to decide for me.”

“I know.”

“You can be worried. You can't make it my job to prove the same thing every time.”

Thalia glanced at the wrapped palm on her knee. She flexed the fingers of her left hand around the pencil and did not touch the bandage. “I don't want to do that.”

“I don't think you want to.”

“That's not the same as not doing it.”

“No.”

Percy looked from one sister to the other. He had the feeling Thalia was trying not to ask him something she knew she should ask Annabeth instead.

She finally did. “Do you think this is what you want because you remember being older?”

Annabeth did not answer right away. “I remember loving him. I remember what we were. Those memories are mine, and they matter. They don't get to make a decision for me now. I like Percy now. I want to be with him now.”

“That's what I'm asking.”

“No. You're asking if the old life is choosing for me.”

Thalia's jaw tightened. Annabeth's voice remained even.

“I don't remember every part of the childhood you remember,” she said. “I don't remember you taking care of me when I was little. I know that happened because you tell me, and because I can see the person it made you. I don't want you to use those memories to tell me what I want either.”

Thalia looked down at the schedule. There was a line through one of the afternoon drills. A small mark had smudged under her thumb.

“I didn't say I would.”

“Not yet.”

Percy shifted his weight. He wanted to soften the exchange, but Annabeth had not asked him to. Thalia was not attacking either of them. She was trying to locate the line between protecting her sister and standing outside a choice she could not control.

Thalia let out a breath. “You're right. I was going to ask you to tell me if he ever makes you feel cornered.”

“I will tell you if that happens.”

“And I don't have to check every time you hold hands?”

“No.”

“Good.” Thalia looked at Percy. “You can tell me too if I get in the way.”

Percy wasn't sure she wanted that answer. “I will.”

“Fine.” She pointed the pencil at him. “Don't make a project out of it.”

“I won't.”

“You two are very bad at not making projects out of things,” Thalia said.

Annabeth laughed once, quiet but real. “That may be true.”

Thalia's expression softened, then drew guarded again. “I can live with it. I can be happy for you. It doesn't mean I'll never worry.”

“I know,” Annabeth said. “I don't need you to stop being my sister.”

“Good. Because I couldn't.”

“Just don't choose for me.”

Thalia nodded. She did not reach to touch Annabeth's shoulder or ask for a hug. The porch step between them stayed empty. Annabeth seemed grateful that she did not have to translate the moment into a gesture.

“Are you all right?” Annabeth asked.

“I'm thinking.”

“You can take time.”

“I know.” Thalia picked up the schedule again with her left hand. “You don't have to wait here while I do it.”

“We weren't going to.”

“Good.” She tilted her head toward the path. “Go eat before Clarisse takes the last of the bread.”

“That's what Clarisse does,” Percy said.

“Exactly.”

Annabeth stood. “We'll finish the shelf plan later.”

“If it fits,” Thalia said.

“If it fits.”

“And if it doesn't?”

“Then I'll change the plan.”

Thalia gave a small nod. “Sounds like you.”

Percy could hear the effort in that answer. Annabeth heard it too, but she didn't ask for more. She slid her hand into his when they reached the path. He held it. Thalia had seen them together before. This time she had heard them name what they wanted, and no one had asked her to make the decision.

They walked back toward the dining pavilion.

---

Silena's orange sat on her plate beside a piece of bread and a cup of water. Percy saw it when he sat down. He thought it was part of the middle platter, reached across, and picked it up.

Silena caught his wrist.

“That one is mine.”

“Sorry.” He set it back on her plate. The orange rolled into the bread.

Clarisse moved the fruit bowl farther from him. “You can't just take whatever's in front of you.”

“I thought it was shared.”

“It's on her plate.”

“I didn't see the plate.”

“You were sitting next to it.”

“Apparently I missed a lot.”

Silena drew the orange back toward herself. “I can give you half.”

“No, I owe you one.” Percy tried to move the orange away from the edge, but she held it still. “I'll bring you one tomorrow.”

“From the kitchen?”

“From the kitchen.”

“Not from my plate?”

“I'll make every effort.”

Clarisse looked at Silena. “Make him bring two.”

“Why two?”

“One for the orange he took and one for making you explain where your plate is.”

“That seems fair,” Silena said.

Percy stared at them. “I haven't actually taken it yet.”

“You reached for it,” Clarisse said.

“I put it back.”

“After Silena stopped you.”

Silena cut the orange in half and pushed one piece across the table. “You can have this one. The debt stands.”

Percy accepted it. “Thanks.”

“Don't turn it into a big apology.”

“I wasn't.”

“You were about to.”

He closed his mouth. Clarisse gave Silena a satisfied look, as if a point had been scored. Percy ate the orange half while the two of them returned to their lunch conversation. No one asked him to describe what he and Annabeth had said at the shrine. He was glad. He also noticed that he wanted to tell them.

“Thalia knows,” he said.

Clarisse looked at him. “She know what?”

“Annabeth and I talked. We told her we're together. And that we want to stay together.”

“Did she ask for a vote?”

“No.”

“Then why do you sound like you brought the results to a council?”

“I don't.”

“You do,” Silena said. “Like you're waiting to hear if she approved.”

“I wasn't asking for approval. Annabeth wanted to tell her.”

“That's different,” Silena said. “But you can still stop carrying Thalia's reaction around for her.”

Clarisse shoveled beans onto her plate. “If Annabeth wants you, Thalia can deal with it.”

“That's not how I think about it.”

“Then don't.”

Silena rested her chin on her hand. “Thalia loves her. She gets scared and sometimes she moves first. That doesn't mean you should turn it into a contest with her.”

“I don't think I am.”

“Good.” Silena took back the orange half she had been cutting for herself. “And you don't have to decide what she feels before she tells you.”

Percy looked down at his food. The two of them had somehow made this sound less complicated without making it a lecture.

Clarisse nudged the serving bowl toward him. “Eat. You have a whole afternoon to overthink in.”

“Do you have to make everything sound like a threat?”

“Would you listen if I made it sound like a suggestion?”

“Probably not.”

“Then yes.”

Silena laughed. “You could try answering people the first time.”

“I answer people.”

“After three questions.”

“I like to be sure.”

“About what?”

“Everything.”

Clarisse snorted. “That's going well for you.”

Percy took another spoonful of beans. The pavilion was bright with movement and noise. Annabeth had left with her sketchbook; he could see her crossing the far path toward Cabin One. She did not look back. He didn't need her to.

He had a future and a past folded into the same relationship, and he did not know how to keep them from getting tangled. At the table, with Clarisse arguing over whose turn it was to carry the bread basket and Silena insisting the orange had not been ripe enough, the tangle felt like something he could hold without pulling it apart.
---

Annabeth was back at the shrine before Percy finished his lunch. She had laid out the drawing, the measuring square and three pieces of wood on the broad table. The sun had moved from the east side of the roof to the center of the path, making a bright square over the work.

“You're early,” she said.

“I had a fast lunch.”

“Clarisse didn't try to keep you there?”

“She tried. I was faster.”

“Did you get the orange?”

“Half of one.”

“That's not the debt.”

“I know.”

Annabeth drew a vertical line down the middle of her sketch. “I changed the shelf depth. The lowest one will be wider than the top two. It leaves room for the niche, and somebody can reach the books without leaning over the offering ledge.”

“That sounds good.”

“It might be.” She set a board along the pencil line. “Can you hold this while I test the corner?”

Percy placed his hand where she showed him. She checked the line against the stone and moved the board slightly toward him.

“Does that feel level?”

“Feels level to me.”

“That's a feeling. Look at the bubble.”

Percy peered at the small level. The bubble sat a little left of center.

“Not level.”

“Right. What would you change?”

“Move the far end up?”

“Maybe. How much?”

“I don't know.”

“Good. We can measure.”

She placed a folded strip of paper beneath the board and watched the bubble shift. They tried another thickness, then a third. The paper bent, which meant it would not work as a permanent shim. She marked the amount on the sketch and took the board away.

Percy tried to remember which piece of the afternoon had been the part that mattered. That was the trouble with turning every action into evidence. He had thought about the name for their relationship, told Thalia, listened to his sisters. Now he was holding a board. It should have been only that.

“Are you thinking about what we talked about?” Annabeth asked.

“Not all the time.”

“That's not an answer.”

“I am. But not in a bad way.”

“What's the difference?”

“I don't know yet.”

“Then say what you do know.”

He let go of the board when she took it from him. “I know I want to be with you. I know I don't want you to have to prove your answer to me every day. I know asking was better than assuming. I don't know how to stop being careful without turning careful into another kind of pressure.”

Annabeth put the board down. “You don't have to stop being careful.”

“I thought you said—”

“I said don't protect me from my own choice. That's different. If you don't know whether I'm asking for help, you can ask. I can answer. The answer can change.”

“Right.”

“And if you make a mistake, you can fix it without turning it into proof that you shouldn't have tried.”

Percy glanced at the floor. “I can do that.”

“I know.”

She made the next mark on the board and went back to work. She had not offered to solve his discomfort; she had answered the question and then returned to the project. Percy liked that she could do both.

He held the other end while she measured the cut. The board's edge was uneven. Annabeth traced a straight line with a pencil, checked it against the square, and moved the mark two millimeters.

“Two millimeters?” Percy asked.

“That's enough to make the front edge look crooked.”

“Would people notice?”

“I would.”

“Then they'll notice.”

She gave him a dry look. “You aren't helping.”

“I can be quiet.”

“You can try.”

He kept quiet while she worked.

The first side piece had to be shortened. The saw moved through it with a rough, regular sound. Percy held the board on the far end, watching the cut instead of the shape of her face. Annabeth stopped twice to clear the teeth and check the angle. When she finished, she turned the piece over and ran a thumb along the fresh edge.

“Better,” she said.

“That's two requirements now?”

“Cut and straight.”

“It's moving up the ladder.”

“Don't encourage it.”

She slid the new piece into place. It fit the drawing, but when they stood it against the wall, the frame rocked again. The board was straight. The floor was still not.

“That's frustrating,” Percy said.

“The floor is lower here. Now we know how much the shim has to make up.”

“Again?”

“Again.”

They lifted the frame away and looked beneath it. One corner of the stone sat lower than the rest. The shim could go under the entire frame, but that would raise the bookcase enough to make the bottom shelf harder to reach. Annabeth crouched and measured the gap with a strip of wood.

“I can make the shim narrower and taper it,” she said.

“Would that hold?”

“Not if I guess.”

She sketched a wedge in the margin.

Percy could have suggested a dozen ways to brace it. He imagined each one being dismissed for a different reason, then realized he was inventing an argument because he wanted to be useful.

“Tell me what would help,” he said.

Annabeth looked up. “Can you hold the left side while I test a wedge?”

“Yes.”

She took a thin piece of scrap and shaved the edge gradually. Percy steadied the frame. She slid the wedge under the low corner, checked the level, then pulled it back out.

“More?”

“Less. The bubble moved too far.”

Percy shifted his grip to keep the frame from rocking.

“Don't lift it,” she said.

“I wasn't.”

“You were about to.”

He stopped. “You're right.”

She measured the wedge again, took off a narrow shaving, and tried it. The bubble moved to the center. The frame stayed put when she pushed gently against the top.

“That's it,” she said.

“It's level?”

“It's level enough for the books. I'll attach it after I check the other side.”

She did not congratulate herself or call the project finished. She wrote down the measurement and moved on to the next corner.

Clarisse came up the path carrying a small stack of boards. “Silena said you were short one.”

“We don't know that yet,” Annabeth said.

“Then I brought one you might not need.”

Silena followed with a paper bag tucked under her arm. “And I brought the list of books we talked about.”

“We talked about books?” Percy asked.

“You said there should be something for people to read.”

“That sounds like me.”

“It was your entire contribution.”

Clarisse laid the boards on the table, away from the tools. “I can carry the heavy part if you need it.”

“The frame isn't heavy,” Annabeth said. “I need the pieces to stay in order.”

“Tell me which one.”

Annabeth pointed to a board. Clarisse picked it up and leaned it against the wall, careful to keep it separate from the one on the floor. Silena opened her list and set it beside the sketch.

“I wrote down three groups,” she said. “Camp reference, myths, and books about building.”

Annabeth scanned the page. “We don't need a group for building.”

“You said people could borrow project notes.”

“That's not the same thing.”

“Then what do you want to call it?”

“I don't know yet.”

“You could call it plans.”

“That's vague.”

“It's a shelf, not a library catalog.”

“Still.”

Silena set the paper down. “I can sort them by what people ask for. We can change the labels after.”

Annabeth considered. “That would work.”

“Good. I didn't want the books to sit in a box while you decided what the category was called.”

Percy picked up one of the shorter boards. “This one goes on the bottom?”

“Not yet,” Annabeth said.

“I was only moving it to the table.”

“It's still part of the frame.”

He stopped with the board in his hands. “Sorry.”

“I said not yet.”

He put it back in the order she had made. Silena watched him, then returned to her list. Clarisse did not comment, which was probably kinder than doing so.

Annabeth took a breath and leaned over the diagram. “The short board goes here, then the wide panel on the left. I need to check the width before we attach the back.”

“Okay,” Percy said. “You tell me.”

“I will.”

The three of them arranged the pieces on the table according to her sketch. Percy made himself wait for each instruction. Annabeth used the measuring square, Silena read the list of dimensions, and Clarisse held the far end when the board needed turning. The work moved more slowly than it would have if Percy had simply picked up the pieces and carried them, but none of the marks got covered and nothing needed to be measured twice.

When Annabeth saw the new frame laid out, her shoulders dropped. “That looks better.”

“Does it fit?” Clarisse asked.

“We'll find out when we stand it.”

Clarisse took one side. Percy took the other, at the end Annabeth pointed to. Silena stayed near the table to keep the loose pieces from sliding.

“Ready?” Annabeth said.

Percy and Clarisse lifted together. Annabeth guided the lower edge to the stone and watched the back corner. It stood without rocking.

“It's level,” Percy said.

“It's stable,” Annabeth corrected. “The level's a separate check.”

She tested each corner with her palm, then marked the position where the shim would go. Percy waited until she told him to lower the frame.

“What's next?” he asked.

“Attach the backing. Then let it set. We can test it with books tomorrow.” Annabeth added a note beneath the measurements: check the frame empty, then try one thin book, one broad reference book and the heaviest manual on Silena's list. She drew a small mark beside the lower shelf and another beside the path.

“Why those three?” Percy asked.

“They answer different questions. The thin one checks whether the shelf is too deep. The reference book tests the wide board. The manual gives us the most weight without stacking everything at once.”

“And if it shifts?”

“We take the books off and adjust it. Nobody needs to catch a falling shelf.”

She added a note to clear the walking path before the test and place each book down separately. The broad reference volume would go near the center, where the support met the side panel. The heavier manual would start on the lower shelf. Annabeth drew a small box beside the list for the measured result, not a check mark.

“I thought you'd mark it done when it held,” Percy said.

“I'll mark what we actually tested. One book doesn't prove the whole frame is ready.”

Percy looked at the three marks. Tomorrow had a shape now, but it still depended on the backing setting and the frame staying steady.

“So today we stop before it's done.”

“Today we stop before it's ready.”

Clarisse looked at the frame. “That's not the same thing.”

“Exactly.”

“I didn't say it was.”

Silena reached for her list. “While you finish the backing, I'll see whether these titles are actually in the storage room.”

“You don't have to do that now,” Annabeth said.

“I want to. I can bring the books when they're ready.”

Annabeth thanked her, then turned back to the shelf.

Percy helped fix the back panel under Annabeth's direction. They used the camp's ordinary repair tools and left the frame resting against the wall while the attachment set. Nobody put the books on it. The empty shelves looked less like a finished project than a promise that could still be changed.

Thalia walked up as they were putting the tools away. Her schedule was folded in her left hand. The bandage on her right palm was clean, and she kept it close to her body.

“Does it fit?” she asked.

“It fits the space,” Annabeth said. “We haven't tested the weight.”

Thalia came closer but stopped before the worktable. She looked at the low shelf, then the empty wall. “You left room to get to the niche.”

“That was the point.”

“I know. I was looking.”

“Do you think it blocks the path?”

“Not from here.” Thalia leaned to see from the other angle, keeping her wrapped hand away from the table. “From the dining path, the corner might catch somebody's shoulder.”

“It's inside the stone line,” Annabeth said.

“Maybe. I couldn't tell from this side.”

Annabeth turned the sketchbook around so they could both see it. Thalia pointed with her left hand. “If you bring the top edge in a little, the corner won't be first thing someone hits.”

“That changes the depth.”

“Only at the top.”

Annabeth took the pencil and checked the line. “It could work.”

“You don't have to use it.”

“I know.”

Thalia nodded, then looked down at the wrapped hand. “I can hold the paper while you measure.”

Annabeth glanced at the bandage. “With your left?”

“With my left.”

“Okay. Just the paper.”

Thalia held the corner down while Annabeth checked the wall distance. It was a small job, but Thalia did it without reaching for the frame or suggesting she knew the whole design. When the measurement was done, Annabeth thanked her and wrote it into the margin.

“I remember you doing that with blocks,” Thalia said. “You'd line them up, then move the top one because you wanted the little figures to see out.”

Annabeth's pencil stopped.

“Do you remember?” Thalia asked, then seemed to hear the difference in her own question. “You don't. Sorry.”

“I don't remember the blocks,” Annabeth said. “I believe you.”

“I used to think it was one of the first things you liked building.”

“Maybe it was.”

“Maybe.”

There was a small quiet. Percy stood beside the table, not sure whether to make a joke or give the sisters room. He chose the second.

Thalia ran her left thumb across the edge of the schedule. “I won't tell you how to design it based on what you used to do.”

“You can tell me what you remember,” Annabeth said. “You don't have to pretend it didn't happen.”

“Does it bother you to hear it?”

“Sometimes. Not because you said it wrong. Because it sounds like it happened to me, and I can't find it in my own head.”

Thalia nodded slowly. “I can try to ask before I tell the whole story.”

“You don't have to hide every story from me.”

“I know.” Thalia smiled without much humor. “That's the difficult part.”

Annabeth put the pencil down. “You can tell me when you want to. I can decide whether I want more.”

“Okay.”

“Today I want to finish measuring the shelves.”

“Then I'll let you.”

The sisters looked at each other. Nothing was fixed by that exchange. Thalia remembered a small child with blocks. Annabeth had no picture of it. They had named the difference without turning it into an argument about which one was real.

Thalia stepped back from the table. “I should take this schedule to Chiron.”

“Want me to walk with you?” Percy asked.

“No. I can carry a piece of paper.”

He nodded. “Okay.”

She left along the path at her own pace, her right hand tucked close but not hidden. Annabeth watched until she reached the turn, then opened the sketchbook again.

“The top edge needs to come in,” she said.

“It does.”

“Do you think that will work?”

“I think it might. You'll know after you measure it.”

“That is not useful.”

“It's honest.”

She huffed a laugh. “I'll take it.”
---

The measurement came out smaller than Annabeth expected. She wrote it down, compared it with the board and shifted the top edge in by less than an inch. The change cleared the path without changing the space beside the niche.

She cut the board herself. Percy held it where she directed and watched the pencil line. When she finished, she ran the measuring square along the edge and checked it twice.

“It's straight,” he said.

“You're not qualified to say that.”

“I can see the square.”

“You can see a square. That doesn't mean you know what to do with it.”

“I've been learning.”

“I noticed.”

The new piece fit along the top of the frame. Annabeth placed it there and asked him to hold the far end. She crouched at the base and looked from the path to the niche.

“It's better,” she said.

“Better than the first plan?”

“Better than the second plan.”

“Should we try a third?”

“No. We know enough to build this one.”

She didn't say the work was finished. She did not need to. The drawing had changed twice, the measurements were written in the margin, and the frame now stood without rocking. They had a plan. Tomorrow—or whenever she decided it was ready—they could test the shelves with books.

Percy went to pick up the measuring square. “What do you want to do with this?”

“Put it back in the repair box.”

“Okay.”

He carried it to the table. On the way, he saw the pencil she had left near the sketchbook and picked it up too.

“Leave that,” Annabeth said.

He put it back. “Right.”

“I didn't say it harshly.”

“I know.”

“You don't have to apologize every time you move something.”

“Then maybe tell me when I actually do something wrong.”

“That's fair.” She looked at the frame and then at him. “You didn't do anything wrong just now. I want the pencil where it is because I haven't finished the drawing.”

“Got it.”

That was the kind of answer he wanted. It gave him something he could act on, not a test he had to pass by guessing. He walked back to her and stayed beside the table while she added the last measurement.

“You can go,” she said. “I don't need a spotter for writing.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yes. I have to make a clean copy before I can order the boards.”

“Then I'll leave you to it.”

She looked up. “Thanks.”

Percy put his hands in his pockets and walked away. He made it to the bend in the path before he realized he had expected her to call after him. He continued toward the arena, feeling faintly ridiculous. Annabeth had said she wanted to be alone with the design. He had listened. The fact that he missed her did not mean he had made the wrong choice.

At the arena, Clarisse stood beside the shield rack with one boot against its lower rail. The rack had sunk into the dirt on one side; every time a camper returned a shield, the top bar tipped toward the path.

“Is that supposed to lean?” Percy asked.

“No.”

“Do you want a hand?”

Clarisse checked the ground, then pointed to the far post. “Hold that. Don't lift.”

He took the post where she showed him. She kicked loose dirt away from the buried foot, worked a flat stone underneath, and pressed the frame back into place. Percy kept still until she tested the rack with one shield. It stayed level.

“Now move your hand,” she said. “I need to see whether it holds without you.”

He let go. The bar dipped a fraction, then settled.

“Good,” she said. “It'll do.”

Two campers came over with their shields. One of them started to stack his on the upper rail. Clarisse pointed to the lower slot instead. “Heavy ones go there. If you leave them up high, the rack tips again.”

The camper shifted his shield. Nothing moved.

“Better,” Clarisse said.

Percy nodded toward the base. “You saw the problem before it fell.”

“That's the point of looking.”

“Most people wait for the noise.”

“Most people aren't responsible for cleaning up the noise.”

She returned the stone to the dirt box and checked that the lane stayed clear. Percy offered to carry the repair tools, but she handed him only the flat pry bar.

“Just that. The rest is mine.”

They walked toward the pavilion together. Silena was at the table near the pavilion's open side with the list she had made. She had added several titles and crossed out two. When she saw Clarisse, she held up the paper.

“I found the building books. Some are old camp manuals, and one is about stonework.”

“Does the stonework book belong in the shrine?” Clarisse asked.

“I'm not sure. It's a little technical.”

“Then it should.”

“That's not how people decide what belongs on a shelf.”

“It's how I decide what I want to read.”

Silena smiled. “You don't read stonework books.”

“I might.”

“You won't.”

“Then put it in the shrine and let me prove you wrong.”

Percy sat beside them. “How many books are there?”

“Enough for one shelf,” Silena said. “Maybe two if Annabeth keeps the building notes separate.”

“She hasn't decided the labels.”

“She doesn't have to decide today.”

“That's what I said.”

“Did she agree?”

“Not in those words.”

Silena folded the list. “I'll wait until she tells me which ones she wants.”

Clarisse took a piece of bread from the basket. “You all keep waiting for people to tell you things.”

“Because it works,” Silena said.

“It also takes forever.”

“Then you can practice waiting.”

Clarisse shook her head, but she did not argue. Percy thought of the question he had asked Annabeth and felt the answer settle more deeply than it had at the shrine. He had wanted the whole conversation to be finished; it was still happening through small choices.

Silena opened the list again. “If the shelves are ready tomorrow, I can help move the books.”

“Don't bring anything until Annabeth has checked the frame,” Percy said.

Silena looked up. “I wasn't planning to.”

“Right. Sorry.”

“It's fine.”

Clarisse rolled her eyes. “You said you weren't going to make it a thing.”

“I wasn't.”

“You're making it a little thing.”

Silena tapped the page with her pencil. “We can just wait. No one is upset.”

Percy put his hands up. “I am waiting.”

“Good,” Clarisse said. “You can practice doing it without announcing it.”

She returned to the shield rack. Silena went back to her list. Percy stayed for a while, listening to the metal noise from the arena and the pavilion conversations around them. They did not need him to direct what happened next. He was allowed to sit with them without having a job.

When Clarisse finished, she returned to the table and reached for a cup. Silena handed it to her without looking. Clarisse drank, then set the cup down on the table instead of keeping it.

“Thalia still has the bandage?” Silena asked.

“Yeah,” Percy said. “She didn't use it.”

“Good.”

“Does she still have to keep it wrapped?”

“I don't know,” Silena said. “No one told me she was cleared.”

“Then it's still wrapped.”

“That's how injuries work,” Clarisse said. “You don't decide it's done because you want it to be.”

Percy looked at Thalia's wrapped hand in his mind, then at the unfinished list under Annabeth's sketchbook. Neither had changed since lunch; he let that be the answer for now.

“I'm going back to the cabin,” he said.

“Bring Silena an orange tomorrow,” Clarisse called after him.

“I know.”

“And don't take it off her plate.”

“I know.”

Silena waved the list at him. “The debt is one orange, not two. Clarisse is inventing fees.”

“I heard that,” Clarisse said.

“I meant you to.”

Percy left them arguing over the orange and the shelf categories. Behind him, Silena was trying to explain why camp reference books should not share a section with myth retellings. Clarisse was making a case for a book she had not read. Neither sounded likely to change her mind.

---

At dinner, Annabeth sat with Thalia at the Zeus table. Percy ate with Clarisse and Silena in Cabin Three's section. He looked over once when he heard Annabeth laugh, then went back to his food.

Clarisse passed him the water pitcher. “You can stop counting.”

“I wasn't counting.”

“Then what were you doing?”

“Listening.”

“To the conversation across the pavilion?”

“Maybe.”

Silena set the bread basket between them. “If you want to know what they're talking about, you can ask later.”

“I don't need to know.”

“Good,” Clarisse said. “Then eat.”

He did. Silena had folded the list in half. Beside the camp manuals and the stonework book, she'd added a short column for titles Annabeth might find in Cabin One.

“I can check the storage room again tomorrow,” she said. “I won't move anything until Annabeth has checked the shelf.”

Clarisse tapped the stonework title. “Save that one. I want to see if it explains why the shrine settled.”

“You want to read a stonework book now?”

“I want to know what made the floor uneven.”

Silena drew a small star beside the title. “I'll ask Annabeth before I bring it over.”

“Fair.”

At the far end of the pavilion, a Hermes camper waited beside the entrance with a practice shield held against his chest. Clarisse noticed him, set down her cup and went over.

“Are you waiting for somebody?” she asked.

“I'm Noah. I wanted to ask about the shield rack.” He glanced at the pavilion tables, then back at her. “I keep turning too early when I carry one.”

“You want to try it now?”

“If you have time.”

Clarisse took him to the arena. Percy followed after Silena gathered the book list. She wanted to leave it on the worktable so Annabeth could check the titles later.

Noah stood with the shield in his left hand. Clarisse watched him take three steps, then stop before he reached the rack.

“Your feet turn before your shoulders,” she said. “Keep the shield close. Don't make the turn with your arm first.”

Noah tried again. The shield shifted against his forearm and knocked the bar.

He stopped. “I thought I had room.”

“You do. You're making the turn too early.” Clarisse pointed at the dirt beside his left foot. “Start with that one. Plant it, then let your shoulders follow.”

Noah looked down, reset his stance and tried without the shield. His feet moved together this time.

“Now pick it up,” she said. “Same steps.”

He carried the shield close, but tightened his wrist as he reached the turn.

“Not so hard,” Clarisse said. “If your wrist locks, you can't change direction.”

Percy moved the rack back a few inches so Noah would have more space. He waited until Clarisse looked over before he let go.

“Thanks,” she said.

Noah tried the turn again. The shield stayed near his side. He took another step, then brought it down without clipping the rack.

“Better,” Clarisse said. “Do it three more times.”

He repeated the turn. On the second try, the shield drifted; he stopped before the edge reached the bar, reset his feet and tried again. The third pass stayed steady.

“That's it?” he asked.

“That's the start.”

Noah lowered the shield. “I keep looking at the rack instead of where I'm going.”

“Then pick a place past it and walk there.”

He looked toward the open lane, chose a spot beyond the rack, and tried once without the shield. His turn widened just enough to clear the post.

“Now with it,” Clarisse said.

Noah carried the shield through the same steps. He slowed at the turn, kept his wrist loose and set it into the lower slot without scraping the bar. Then he checked the rack with his eyes.

“It held,” he said.

“Because you didn't hit it,” Clarisse replied. “Don't make the next turn faster just to prove you can.”

Noah nodded. He lowered the shield and asked, “Should I practice this before I try carrying two?”

“One at a time until this feels ordinary. Then ask me again.”

He placed the shield back in the lower slot and checked that the rack didn't tip. It held.

“Thanks. I'll try it again tomorrow.”

“Do that.”

He left toward the Hermes cabin. Clarisse checked that the shield sat flat, then walked back to the pavilion with Percy and Silena.

At the table, Silena placed the book list beside Annabeth's sketchbook. Annabeth had returned with Thalia and was comparing the titles against the shelf measurements. She looked up when Percy arrived.

“Did you eat?”

“Mostly.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It's a better answer than before.”

She smiled. “I'll accept it.”

“Did you settle the top depth?”

“I have a draft. I'll check it again before I cut anything else.”

“Good.”

“You don't have to approve it.”

“I know. I was saying it looked good.”

“I know.”

That was all. She turned back to the drawing, and he returned to his seat beside Clarisse. The tables filled with evening noise: cups touching wood, benches scraping, people asking for the bread. Annabeth stayed with Thalia, and Percy stayed with his sisters. No one treated the distance between the tables as a problem.

When dinner ended, Annabeth closed the sketchbook and tucked it beneath her arm. She spoke to Thalia for another moment, then looked across the pavilion.

“See you later,” she said.

“Yeah.”

She left with Thalia along the path toward Cabin One. Percy watched them until they turned past the trees, then picked up the book list Silena had left on the table and carried it to the workbench.

“You don't have to do that,” Silena said.

“I know. I want to make sure it doesn't get wet.”

Clarisse stood. “I'll put the shields away.”

“I can help.”

“I'm already going.”

She took the list from him and set it under the sketchbook, then carried the shields back to the arena. Percy stayed with Silena while she checked the book titles. They agreed to leave the labels blank until Annabeth chose them.

The pavilion emptied around them. Percy did not go looking for Annabeth. She knew where he was, and he knew where to find her. When Silena finished, he helped her carry the list back to the table.

The orange was still on her plate. Percy pointed at it.

“Tomorrow,” he said.

“One orange.”

“One orange.”

“Not half of one.”

“An entire orange.”

Silena slid the plate toward herself. “We'll see.”

Percy laughed. Clarisse called from the arena that he could bring the fruit after he helped clear the table. He went to help her. The work was small and familiar: carry the last tray, fold the cloth, leave the table ready for breakfast.

Across camp, a door closed. Percy could not see whether Annabeth had reached Cabin One or stayed with Thalia on the porch. He did not need to know yet. He set the tray down where Clarisse pointed and let the evening finish.
