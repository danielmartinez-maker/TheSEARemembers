## Chapter 182: The First Hot Bread

The stove in Cabin Three had been lit at ten past five.

Silena Beauregard had got up in the dark, stepped past her own boots and then gone back for them, laid a fire with two split sticks of pine and one piece of the oak that Clarisse stacked in threes because oak burned in threes and everything else was a rumour, and closed the iron door on it with the flat of her hand, and by the time anybody else in the room had turned over the stove was ticking the way it ticked on a morning that had a purpose.

Percy smelled it before he opened his eyes.

That was the thing about October: the smell arrived first and the cold arrived second. He lay under his quilt with one arm out and let the cabin tell him the shape of the day without moving. Somebody — Silena, only Silena — had the big bowl on the table already, and the spoon going, and the particular wet slap of a dough that has decided to be a dough happening at intervals of about ten seconds. The two high windows had gone white. Not frosted: fogged, from the inside, which meant the stove was up and the kettle was on and the room had sealed itself against the morning like a hand closing over a match.

He had gone to bed Tuesday night with the hall still in his shoulders — the steam off the water, the lantern hooked on the rail, the long walk up the hill afterward with Annabeth's hand cold in his and then not cold — and woken into a cabin that smelled like a bakery, and for about four seconds he was ten years old in a kitchen he could not have described to anybody without getting the details wrong, and then he was fourteen, in Cabin Three, and the spoon was going again.

"Nobody's allowed up yet," Silena said, without turning around. "The floor's for me and the dough until it says what it's going to do."

"It says it's going to do what you tell it."

"It says that because I've got my hand in it." She put the dough down and slapped the bowl's rim clean with two fingers. "It's the cold. Cold dough lies for the first hour and then tells the truth all at once, and if you're not there when it tells the truth you get bricks. So. You can lie in bed and be useless, or you can get up and be on wood and water."

Percy got up.

That was the other thing about his sister at five in the morning: there was no version of her instructions that did not involve getting out of bed. He pulled his sweater on over his shirt, put his feet in his boots without the laces, and crossed the cabin in the grey light, and the floor by the stove was already warm in one rectangle, exactly the width of the hearth stone.

"How long has it been going?"

"Since ten past." Silena's knuckles went white and then soft in the dough. "Since Tuesday, if you count the sponge. I set it Tuesday at four because Wednesday's the day."

"Wednesday's what day?"

"Bread day." She said it the way Clarisse said *inventory*: as a category rather than an event. "First proper bread since the weather turned. The cabin's been cold a week and the rise has been taking an hour longer every time, and I'm not going into November losing an hour a loaf. So today we bake with the door shut and the stove hot and the windows doing that." She nodded at the white glass. "Which is also, not incidentally, warm. Sit on the warm side and hand me the tin."

The tin was the one with the layers: salt in the top, the good honey in the middle, and a folded square of paper in the bottom that Percy had never once seen her take out in front of another person, and which he knew, because he had lived with her for five years, held a pencil drawing of a room.

He put the tin where she could reach it and went for the wood box and the water bucket, which took two trips out into a morning that had a hard rim of white on every rail and a lake at the bottom of the hill sitting under its own steam like a pot left on a burner. The path between the porch and the woodpile had been walked already. By Clarisse, almost certainly: the logs were in threes, the kindling was under the eaves where the drip could not get at it, and there was a glove on the woodpile edge, left hand, wool, which he took in as evidence.

The cabin had gone from grey to milky while he was outside. By the time he came in with his arms full the whole window wall had turned to a soft white panel with the shapes of pines just visible in it, and the light coming through had changed from the thin blue of an October dawn to the flat glow of a lightbox, and Silena had put two more sticks in the stove and the room was the temperature of a thing that was going somewhere.

Clarisse came in from the porch at twenty past six with her hair wet at the ends and her whistle on its cord and her winter list under her arm, because Clarisse began every day of her life with the list and had once, in September, been seen reading it in her sleep, which Percy had not been able to resist mentioning.

"You're staring," Clarisse said.

"You've got flour on your collar."

"That's not my flour." She shook her coat off onto the hook and put the list on the far end of the table, face down, which was the Friday rule applied on a Wednesday and which nobody had asked her about. "It's the dough's. It's been on me since I got up. It's on the door handle and the wood box and the tap outside, so if you want to be an investigator, go and investigate the woodpile."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to." Clarisse put her hand flat on the stove pipe's elbow, the way she checked the fire without looking at it. "Good. Hot. That's how it should be. If you're in here, you're on trays. The trays are on the shelf and they're cold, and bread that sits on a cold plate goes hard before it reaches anybody."

"I was on wood and water."

"Wood and water is finished. That's what happens when you're fast." She pulled the second stool out with her foot, sat down, opened the list, and put her pencil behind her ear in the same motion, which was a trick Percy had never managed and which she did without noticing she did it. "Trays. Move."

So Percy was on trays, and the cabin went from the quiet of five to the not-quiet of a kitchen that has decided to feed a camp, and the two high windows went on fogging until the pines outside were only a suggestion and the whole room had the light of a room lit from inside itself.

---

The first loaf came out of the stove at twenty to eight.

It was not a big loaf. It was Silena's test loaf, the size of a man's two hands side by side, the one she baked first to find out what the fire was going to do, and she set it on the board with the peel still in her hand and stood back with her head on one side, and the crust made the sound crust makes, a small ticking crackle, like a fire settling.

"Good," she said. "Right. Second's in. That one's ours."

"That one's ours," Percy agreed, and took the end off it.

The heel. The end crust, the torn-off corner where the loaf stops being a loaf and starts being a handle. He had it in his hand and he had a bite out of it before the sentence was finished, and it was hot enough that he had to pass it from palm to palm, and it was, he thought, the best thing he had put in his mouth since the cake in the frost on the ridge, and the ridge had had a nine-hundred-foot view going for it.

"Put that down," Clarisse said.

"It's the heel."

"It's the first heel, which is a different thing entirely, and you know it, and you took it with your mouth." She had the tongs from the hearth in her hand — the long iron tongs, the ones for the coals — and she had picked them up without any visible decision to pick them up, which was somehow worse. "First heel's got a rule. It's in the kitchen code."

"There is no kitchen code."

"There's a code." Silena did not turn around. Her hands were on the second loaf, seams pinched, and her voice had the pleasant blankness of a referee who has been looking forward to this. "Clarisse, read him the code."

Clarisse put the tongs down flat on the table, with a sound like a gavel, and folded her arms. "First hot loaf of a cold-weather bake is cut in three. One for the person who fired the stove. One for the person who carried the wood and the water. One for the person who does the next four hours of standing in front of the fire."

"That's not a code," Percy said. "That's three names and one of them's you."

"It's three names and one of them's me because I did the wood and I fired — " Clarisse stopped. "Silena fired it."

"I fired it," Silena agreed, pinching the second seam. "So the first heel is mine."

"The first heel is hers."

"Then it's split three ways and Percy gives back the bite."

Percy, with the heel in both hands and his back to the counter, looked from one sister to the other and calculated his chances, which were: none, none, and none with the tongs in play. So he did the only thing left to a man with a hot piece of bread and no allies, which was to break the heel in half, take the smaller half, and hold the larger half out toward the stove with the solemnity of an offering.

Silena took the larger half with her floury hand, tore it, gave the biggest piece to Clarisse, took the bite Percy had already taken out of her piece because she was not squeamish about her own kitchen and had never in her life thrown away the end of anything, and said, with her mouth full, "Right. War's over. Percy, you're on trays."

"The war hasn't started."

"You're on trays," Clarisse said, "and the loaf is mine now."

And that was how it started.

---

It started because Percy, carrying the second tray past the board, said, entirely to the room, "She's got it under her arm like a rifle."

Clarisse had not put the loaf under her arm. She had put it on the board and put her forearm over it while she buttered a knife, which is a different thing, and which Percy had described with the accuracy of a cartoon and the timing of a younger brother, and Clarisse's butter knife came down on the board with a clack.

"You want to be fed."

"I want bread."

"Then ask." She turned the loaf so the cut side faced the wall. "Bread doesn't go to people who narrate."

"I narrate because you guard. Guards get narrated. It's in the code."

"There's no code!" Percy said, in exactly the voice he had said it in two minutes earlier, which he knew, and which he did it anyway, and Clarisse looked at him with the flat green stare that had ended arguments since he was nine years old, and then she picked up the loaf.

Not to eat it. To move it. She picked it up, stood, walked the length of the table with it, and put it on the shelf by the back bunks, in the exact spot where Tyson's round white rock had sat before Clarisse moved the rock to his pillow — a place she dusted twice a week and had never let anything else occupy.

"There," she said. "It's up. It's above your reach and above your mouth. If you want it, you come and ask, and I'll consider the request."

"That's a siege."

"That's bread policy." Clarisse sat down, took her butter knife, and began, with enormous calm, to butter the piece she already had. "You started it with the heel. You can end it by being useful. Trays, Jackson."

Percy looked at the shelf, which was seven feet up and against the wall and behind a sister who had once carried a boy her own size off a dock, and then at Silena, who had the third loaf in her hands and who said, without looking up, "The trays aren't going to warm themselves, and neither is the pavilion going to get fed by nine o'clock," and who had thus, in one sentence, ruled on the war and assigned him a side.

So Percy went back to the trays. And the second time he came past the board, his sleeve — the left one, the one that had been on the counter — came up against the flour pile, and he did not notice, and Clarisse did notice, and did not say anything, and that was the mistake.

Because Annabeth arrived at seven, and the flour on his sleeve became a thing that Annabeth saw, and what happened after that was not, in the strictest sense, a kitchen war at all. It was a thing much older and much better organized than that, and it began with a girl in a grey coat putting her bag down on the bench, looking at the flour on his cuff, then at him, and saying, in a voice of total neutrality, "You've been in a fight."

"I've been on trays."

"With the flour."

"The flour was there first."

Annabeth Grace took off her coat, folded it over the back of the chair, rolled her own sleeves to the elbow, washed her hands at the basin, and came to stand beside him at the counter, and said, quietly, so that only he could hear it, "Then we'll win it together," and picked up the tray he had been about to take out, and carried it as far as the door, and put it in his hands, and left her fingerprints in the flour on the wood where she had held it, which Clarisse, from the far end of the table, saw, and which she recorded by taking a bite of her bread, chewing it thoughtfully, and writing nothing at all on the list.

---

Annabeth had come down the hill with a ruler in her pocket for reasons she could no longer remember.

That was the honest accounting of it, and she made it on the path, halfway down, with the smell of woodsmoke coming up at her in waves: she had been at the table in Cabin One with the north-spring fair sheet and the pencil she kept meaning to sharpen, and she had got as far as taking the cap off, and then the wind had turned and brought the hill up to her in one whole piece — pine, cold water, and underneath it, faintly, the particular gold smell of a stove that has been fed since five in the morning — and she had put the pencil down and said, to nobody, "Bread," and Thalia, from her bunk, had said, without opening her eyes, "Go. Take the bag so you have an excuse."

"It's not an excuse."

"It's the bag, Annabeth. It's a whole bag with nothing in it. I've seen it. It's got a hole."

Thalia had come down with her, after all, because her sister's version of not being involved was to arrive early and stand in a doorway with her arms crossed, and she had stayed eleven minutes, which Annabeth had counted, because Thalia counted things too and never admitted it. In those eleven minutes she had tested the stove pipe elbow with her palm the way Clarisse does, been handed two rolls and a paper twist, and gone back up the hill holding them in the crook of her arm like a small animal, saying over her shoulder that somebody had to eat the evidence.

And then Annabeth had taken off her coat and rolled her sleeves and said the thing about winning the war together, and found herself at a counter in a fogged cabin at seven in the morning with a bowl in front of her and Percy beside her and the flour between them like a territory.

The room had settled into the shape it took on a baking day. Silena at the stove end with the peel and the hot cloth, working loaves in and out with the unhurried economy of a woman running a rail line. Clarisse at the far end of the table with the wood ledger, her pencil moving in short administrative strokes between bites. Percy at the counter with the trays, which had turned out to mean: Percy standing where the trays were, which was beside her, and which neither of them had mentioned.

"You've got flour on your sleeve," Annabeth said.

"I've been on trays."

"The left one. It's in the seam." She took his forearm and turned it, and there it was, a pale line of it ground into the cotton from wrist to elbow, and she brushed at it with the flat of her hand, the way you wipe a plan clean, and the flour came off in a small white cloud that hung between them for a second and went up toward the window. "That's most of it. The rest is structural."

"Structural flour."

"It's load-bearing at this point. If I take any more out, the sleeve collapses." Annabeth looked up at him with her hand still on his forearm and the bowl between them and the whole window wall gone white behind them, and said, with a perfectly level face, "Also, for the record, the flour war is going badly for you. Clarisse has the loaf on a shelf. You have a tray, a sleeve, and a narrative."

"I have morale."

"You have a narrative about morale." She let go of his sleeve and picked up the dough scraper and put it down again in a different place, which was not work, and they both knew it was not work, and neither of them said so. "Percy. What's the actual plan? Because I've been here eight minutes and I've counted two loaves, one heel in three pieces, one guarding sister, and you with your back to the room."

"The plan is trays."

"The plan is trays, and then what? The pavilion at nine, the shop at ten, the small ones at three — Silena told me the whole order on the path in ninety seconds, which means she's thought about it, which means there's an end to it, and I'd like to know where it is."

Percy looked at her with the expression he wore when she had asked a question that was not the question she was asking, which she had done since she was seven and which he had never once been fooled by, and then he said, "The end is bread for everybody and nobody cold at the fire. The end of the middle is an hour, between the shop and the small ones, where nothing happens, and Silena's going to sit down in it, and I was going to spend it being useless somewhere warm."

"Somewhere warm."

"There's a rectangle on the hearth floor the width of the stove door, and it's the best real estate in the camp, and I've had a claim on it since August and nobody's ever enforced it."

Annabeth considered this for four seconds, during which she picked up the scraper again and put it back, and turned the bowl a quarter turn, and wiped her own sleeve, which had not needed wiping since Tuesday.

"The hearth floor is small," she said.

"It's the width of the door."

"It's a one-person rectangle, Percy."

"It's a one-person rectangle with a second person's claim on it, which is a different thing." He took the scraper out of her hand and set it down properly, squared to the edge of the board, which was the thing he did with everything he set down and which she had once told him was the most attractive habit in the camp and which he had therefore never stopped doing. "After the forge trays. That's the plan. Bring the bag so you have an excuse."

"That's what Thalia said."

"Then it's two votes." He picked up the loaded tray and went out past her toward the door, and as he went he put his hand, briefly, flat, completely absent-mindedly, against the small of her back, which was the sort of thing he had been doing since they were thirteen and which still, in the middle of a warm kitchen at seven in the morning in front of his entire family, landed somewhere under her ribs and stayed there while he walked out into the cold with the bread.

Clarisse did not look up. Clarisse, who had not looked up from the wood ledger once in four minutes, said, to the ledger, "The rectangle is two people. It's been two people since August. Silena put a second rug under the big chair in September and neither of you noticed, which is the single most impressive thing anybody in this cabin has ever done." Then she turned a page. "Forge trays are on the board and they need ten minutes. They need to be hot when he gets them, so if you're going to stand in my kitchen doing nothing with your hands, do it for nine minutes and then be useful."

Annabeth, who had been caught, and knew she had been caught, and knew that being caught by Clarisse was a species of being seen and had been since they were twelve, put the scraper back in her hand anyway and went on working, and the flour on the counter went on coming up in small pale clouds toward the white window, and outside the whole camp went about its Wednesday with the smell of hot bread running down the hill ahead of it like a rumour.

---

The kitchen emptied at nine.

It emptied the way a room empties when a plan is working: Silena with the first full load under a cloth, Clarisse carrying the second, because she had weighed the distance and decided that her sister's arms were worth more than her own dignity, and the two of them out the door and down the path with the cold closing in behind them like water. Percy watched them go from the window — that had been Silena's parting instruction over her shoulder, *watch the second rise and go nowhere near the shelf loaf* — and then the cabin was the two of them and the stove and four loaves in various states of becoming.

"You're going to look," Annabeth said.

"I'm going to look."

"You've been told not to look."

"I've been told not to touch. Looking's not on the list." He crossed the room and put his hand flat on the shelf loaf's crust the way he had tested the stove pipe, and took it back like a man testing a fence. "It's still going. You can hear it if you shut up."

"I can hear it from here."

"That's what I mean about you." He came back to the counter, and there was flour on his wrist now from the shelf test, and he looked at it, and then at her, and Annabeth put down the scraper.

What happened next took about a minute and a half and would, she thought much later, be the thing she remembered about the first hot bread of the year, rather than any of the loaves.

He reached across her for the cloth, because the cloth was on the far side of the board and he was taller by an inch he had gained in September and had never once mentioned, and she did not move out of the way, which was not an accident and which both of them knew was not an accident, so that he ended up leaning on the counter with a hand on either side of the bowl and her exactly where she had been, and the distance between them went from the width of a bowl to the width of nothing.

"Flour," he said.

"Where."

"On your sleeve. The right one. That's mine. It came off me."

"That's my flour now. I took it in the war." She held the cuff up between two fingers, and then she wiped it, deliberately, down the front of his shirt, in one slow stripe, and looked up at him with her chin up and her face doing nothing at all. "There. Now it's your flour again. It goes back and forth. That's how a trade works."

"That's not a war, that's a trade deficit." He caught her hand before she could pull it back and held it flat against the stripe on his chest, and his thumb went over her knuckles once, and the stove ticked, and the loaf on the shelf gave its small settling crackle, and Annabeth felt the whole morning arrange itself around those two seconds.

"Percy."

"Annabeth."

"We have about eight minutes."

"We have eight minutes, and then they come back, and then we've got the small ones at three, and then we've got the whole evening." He said it quietly, with his hand over hers on his chest and his thumb not stopping, and she could feel her own pulse in her own wrist and his under the flour. "Yesterday was good."

"Yesterday was the hall."

"Yesterday was the hall, and the walk up, and the porch light going off in the middle of a sentence." His voice had gone lower and slower, the way it did when he was setting down something he had been carrying around for a day. "I've been holding on to it all morning. The porch light went off and we just stood there in it."

"We stood there in the dark."

"We stood there in the dark with our foreheads together, and for the record I have been thinking about that during the entire wood-and-water portion of my day." He lifted his free hand and put a strand of hair behind her ear, and his fingers stayed there at the edge of her jaw, light, the way you hold a thing you have been trusted with. "You've got flour on your face."

"I've got flour on my face because I've been working."

"You've got flour on your face because you put your face near a bowl at seven in the morning like a person who wanted flour on her face." His thumb went from her jaw to the spot, just below the ear, where the flour was, and he wiped it off, slowly, and did not take his hand away, and Annabeth stopped doing arithmetic about the minutes.

"You missed some," she said, which was not true.

"Then I'll get the rest of it." Percy leaned down and kissed her.

It was the kind of kiss that starts because two people have run out of other ways to stand in a room. Soft at first, unhurried, his hand still at her jaw and hers still flat against the flour stripe on his chest, and the stove going on ticking under everything, and the high window fogged so completely that the room had no outside in it any more — no hill, no pavilion, no eight minutes — just a kitchen with the light turned to milk and two people in it who had been doing this for two years and still, in the middle of it, occasionally had to stop and work out how.

They stopped. They breathed. His forehead came down against hers and the breath went between them, warm, and she could feel him deciding something, and then he kissed her again, and that one went longer and slower and had its hands in it: her hair, the back of her neck, the collar of her shirt; and hers in his, in the flour stripe, in the damp hair at the nape where he had not dried it properly that morning. It went on past the point where either of them was watching the clock and came out the other side into the place where nobody was watching anything, and it ended the way these things end, which is slowly, in stages, with a last small kiss that is really an apology for stopping, and then foreheads again, and the two of them standing in the flour with their eyes shut while the loaf crackled on the shelf above them like applause that had been told to keep its voice down.

The door latch moved.

They were apart by two feet, and Annabeth had the scraper in her hand and was looking at the bowl with the intensity she usually reserved for contour intervals, and Percy had a cloth and was wiping the counter in a slow arc that covered, with remarkable thoroughness, the entire flour stripe on his shirt. Silena came in out of the cold with her cheeks red and her eyebrows up and her hands empty, looked at the two of them, and at the bowl, and at the counter that Percy had wiped to the point of dampness, and said, in the voice of a woman reading a weather report, "The second rise is fine, by the way. I came back for the cloth." She took the cloth out of his hand. "Also, the forge trays are on the board and they're hot, and Charlie Beckendorf is not a patient man before noon, so I'm taking them up. Both of you: out of my kitchen for eight minutes. Go and be useless somewhere warm. I mean it."

On the path, going up toward the cedar brake with her bag on her shoulder and her hat pulled down against the wind, Annabeth said, without turning around, "She knew."

"She's known since July."

"She put a second rug under the chair."

"That's Silena." Percy took her hand and put it in the crook of his arm and held it there against the cold, and the hill went up in front of them through the pines with the lake showing silver through the trunks below, and behind them, four hundred yards downhill, the whole camp was eating the first hot bread of the year and talking about very little else.

---

Silena Beauregard carried the forge crew's trays up the hill at twenty past nine with a cloth over one arm and her bag over the other shoulder, and the bag had his coat in it, folded, with the paper band around it that she had put there on Sunday and taken off on Monday and put back on Tuesday night, and which she had now taken off again that morning and then, at the last moment, standing in the passage with her boots on, put back.

Four times up the hill with it. Four times back down with it still folded.

She had told herself a different reason each time, which was how she knew the real reason, which was that the real reason was the sort of thing that did not survive being said out loud, even in the inside of your own head at five in the morning with a stove to light.

The forge was the warmest place on the hill that was not a kitchen, and in October it announced itself from forty yards off: the shop door stood open all weathers because the chimneys wanted the draw, and the warm air came out and met the cold coming down off the ridge, and where the two met, about at the woodpile, there was a line that smelled of hot iron, coal, and the linseed oil they used on the benches. Silena walked through it with the trays and pushed the door open with her hip and took the whole heat of the room in one breath, the way you take a drink.

Charlie Beckendorf was at the back oven with his back to her.

She knew it was him before she could have made out his face, which was a thing she had stopped noticing a month ago and had started noticing again last week: the shoulders, the black shop shirt with the sleeves rolled past the elbow, the way he stood at a fire with his weight even and his head slightly back, watching the color rather than the flame. Two of the small forge kids were at the long bench on the left, filing something in a slow two-handed rhythm, and neither of them looked up, because filing was filing.

"You're early," Beckendorf said, to the oven.

"Trays," Silena said. "They're hot."

"I know they're hot. I could hear them." He turned around with the long tongs in one hand and came across the shop floor at the walk he used indoors, unhurried, and took the top tray out of her hands with the cloth gathered in his fingers so that his knuckles did not touch the metal, and set it on the bench, and then took the second one and set it beside the first. "Kitchen's sending bread up to the shop?"

"Kitchen's sending bread everywhere. You've got the second."

"Right." He looked at the two trays under their cloth for a second, in the way he looked at anything that arrived from outside, which was to say, completely, and then he looked at her bag. "That's a heavy walk. Sit down a minute."

"I've got to get back."

"You've got to get back for the third rise and that's not for an hour." He had already gone past her to the bench by the forge mouth and was clearing it, with his forearm, the way he cleared a bench, which was to move everything four inches and then put two things back. "Sit. There's a stool. There's water. I'll drink the water if you don't, and then you'll be angry."

The shop was exactly as she remembered it from Friday, which meant it was exactly as she had been remembering it all week without deciding to: the low orange room, the light off the oven mouth moving on the ceiling like something underwater, the anvil block she had watched him carry in one hand like a sandwich, the long rack of tongs hanging in order by size, because a man who hung his tongs in order was telling you something about himself that he would never say with his mouth.

The tin of eyelets sat on the corner of the bench. He had left the lid off, and inside, on top of the brass, there was a folded square of paper.

She did not pick it up. She looked at it, and then she looked at the ceiling, and then she sat down on the stool and put her bag on her knees and said, in a voice that was entirely about bread, "The second rise is better than the first. Cold weather dough lies, and then it stops lying."

"First hot bread of the year," Beckendorf said. He had gone back to the oven and was standing in its light with his hands on his hips. "The shop's been asking. Two of them asked me at six this morning, in the dark, at my bunk, with a question about rivets that was not about rivets."

"Ask them what they want. Loaves, twists, or the flat rounds with the salt. I can do all three by Thursday."

"They'll want the rounds." He turned back to the fire, put the tongs in, and drew out a shallow iron tray of small parts — fittings, half a dozen of them, the brass going a dull red at the edges — and carried it across the floor at the walk, and Silena, who had spent her whole life in kitchens and knew exactly what a hot tray did to a person who was not looking, stood up without deciding to and put her hand out to steady the far end of it.

Her fingers found the cool corner of the iron at the same moment that his found it, and their hands brushed over the tray, the back of his against the side of hers, from the knuckles to the wrist, and it lasted the length of two steps, and then the tray was down on the brick and their hands were not on it, and neither of them said anything, and the parts on the tray ticked as they cooled, one after another, like a clock being wound in the wrong direction.

Beckendorf put the tongs back on their hook. Then he did the thing she had seen him do a hundred times and never in front of her: he wiped his hands on his own shop shirt, flat, once down each side, the way a man does when he has decided that his hands need to be clean for the next thing and there is no cloth to hand.

"Give me a minute," he said, went to the far bench and said something to the two small ones about the filing, and came back with a tin cup of water which he put on the brick beside her stool; then he got his own lunch out of the shelf cubby, a wrapped bundle on a board, put the board on the bench at her elbow, and unwrapped it, and there in the middle, going soft at the edges from the heat of the room, was one of the pavilion's rounds with the salt on it, from that morning's first load.

"That's mine," Silena said, before she could stop herself.

"I know it's yours. I watched a kid at the pavilion try to take two and get caught by your sister." He broke a corner off his own bread and ate it and did not look at her. "I only took one. It's still warm. Shop rules say if it's still warm it doesn't count as taking."

"Shop rules say a lot of things."

"Shop rules say everything. That's what rules are for." He went back to the oven, and at the door of it he said, without turning around, "The whole shop smells like your kitchen. It has since seven."

The compliment went through her and out again and sat down somewhere behind her ribs, and Silena, who had an answer for most things and had never once in her life not had an answer for a thing about food, sat on a stool in a forge with a cup of water in her hand and did not say anything at all, and looked at her own fingers, which were still faintly warm along the side where the back of a hand had gone past them.

---

She stayed twenty minutes, which she had not planned and would pay for at the other end of the morning, and she knew it while she stayed, which was new, and which she decided not to examine. He worked. That was the thing about Beckendorf in a shop: he did not perform being busy, and he did not stop working to keep her company. He went on at his bench with the thing in front of him and let the room be a room with two people in it, and every so often — at the fittings, at the far bench, at the fire — he said one flat sentence about what he was doing, so that she knew where she was.

"These are the last of the lantern parts," he said, at the fittings, with a small hammer going tap-tap along a rivet line. "The ninth is up. The tenth is the one for your porch. And there's one more I'm building for the top of the west path, at the switchback, that nobody's paid for."

"Nobody's paid for it?"

"Chiron paid for nine." Tap. Tap. "The last one's mine. Somebody's going to want to walk up that path in the dark in November and not go off the bank at the switchback, and it may as well be a lamp I built." He turned the fitting over. "That's not charity. That's materials planning."

"It's charity with a materials story on it."

"It's the only kind of charity this shop does." He said it flatly, into the work, and Silena looked at the side of his face in the orange light and did the inventory she did at the pavilion: who needed seconds, who was quiet, who was performing fullness. He had been up at six. He had eaten half a round and put the rest of his lunch back without finishing it. His hands were steady and his shoulders were not, quite, and there was a line on his forearm where a hot thing had grazed it, weeks ago, healed to a pale seam.

"Your coat's in the bag," she said.

He stopped tapping. He did not turn around. "Is it."

"Cuff's done. Pocket's done. There was a button off the left side and I put on one that doesn't match, because the button tin hasn't got the same, and if you want it matched you'll wait until November when the trading cart comes."

"I don't need it matched."

"It'll bother you."

"It'll be a button that works." He put the hammer down, turned around, came over to the bench, and stood a little way off, with his hands open at his sides the way a man stands when he has been given a thing he did not expect, and looked at the paper band around the coat in her bag, and then at her, and Silena held the bag up by its strap with what she hoped was the ordinary expression of a person handing over laundry.

He did not take it.

He took the band off, instead, and unfolded the coat, and looked at the cuff, and ran his thumb along the seam she had sewn in the candlelight of a storm with eleven kids asleep around the table, and the room went quiet in the way a forge room never goes quiet, with the fire in it and the wind against the chimney.

"The original seam on this was machine," he said. "Two years ago, in a shop in Albany, and it went in the first month I had it." He turned the cuff over. "This one won't."

"It's the same thread."

"It's not the same stitches." He put his thumb flat on the line and left it there for a second, and then folded the coat back along its own creases, exactly, the way you fold a thing you intend to keep, and put it down on the bench between them, and said, in the voice he used for the strength of materials, "Whoever did this knew what it was for."

And that was the compliment that landed too hard, because he said it that way — *whoever* — knowing full well that she was sitting on the stool with her hands in her lap, and said it anyway, because saying it that way was the only way he knew how to say a thing that big without either of them having to stand in it. Silena's ears went hot. She looked at her hands, which were the hands, and then at the far wall, where the tongs hung in order by size, and she said, in a voice she did not fully recognize, "It's thread. It's what the thread's for."

"Right," Beckendorf said. He did not smile. He did not need to; the whole flat surface of his face had changed the way a room changes when a lamp is turned up one notch, and he picked up the hammer and went back to the fittings, and after four taps he said, "Leave the coat. I'll wear it up here at night. The shop's cold after six and I keep meaning to bring one."

"It's got a button that doesn't match."

"I'll get used to the button." Tap. "The shop'll get used to the button. The shop gets used to everything."

Silena put the empty bag on her knees and sat with it there, and the fire moved in the oven mouth, and the wind went over the chimney, and the tin of eyelets sat at the corner of the bench with the folded square of paper still untouched inside it, and she did not pick that up either, and thought about the fact that she had walked up a hill with a coat four times in order to not hand it over, and had now handed it over, and was going home without it, and that this was, when you stripped the whole thing down to the materials, exactly what she had wanted since a storm in October with a needle in her hand.

"Your tray's going cold," Beckendorf said, from the bench. "Eat your bread, Beauregard. That's shop rules too."

"It's the pavilion's bread."

"It's your bread and it's on my bench, which makes it the shop's bread, and the shop feeds its people." He did not look up. "Eat it or the second kid'll find it and there'll be a thing."

So Silena Beauregard ate her own round, with the salt on it, on a bench in a warm orange room with a fire in it and a man not looking at her and every fourth tap of his hammer falling exactly on the beat the last one had, and then she put the board on the shelf cubby herself, and took her bag, and went out through the warm line by the woodpile and down the hill into the cold, and the second rise was fine, exactly as fine as she had said it was, and she did not know until she got to the door of her own cabin how fast she had been walking.

---

The rectangle on the hearth floor was, as advertised, the width of the stove door, and at half past twelve it had two people in it and a blanket over both of them.

The blanket was the old wool one, the summer one, the one that had gone into the lake twice and come out twice and had been retired to the back of the big chair in September, and it smelled of woodsmoke and cabin, and Annabeth had got it down without asking anybody, which meant she had planned this as far back as the forge door and had said nothing on the way up the hill, and Percy decided, lying against her shoulder with his back against the warm hearth stone and her head against the side of his face, that he was going to marry her twice: once for the planning, once for the silence.

"You're thinking loudly," Annabeth said.

"I'm thinking at normal volume."

"You're doing the thing where you breathe in the middle of a sentence. That's thinking loudly. That's how I read your handwriting and your breathing."

"My handwriting's excellent."

"Your handwriting is a structural engineer's fever dream and we've discussed it." She shifted, found the corner of the blanket with her hand, and pulled it up over his shoulder, and then put the same hand back down next to his on the stone, palm up, which was the offer, and he took it, and their fingers did the thing they did, which was to hold on and then not hold on and then hold on again, slowly, like two people talking in a language that only had four words in it and had never needed a fifth.

The stove ticked. The cabin had gone quiet in the particular quiet of a room between jobs: Silena at the far end of the table writing the afternoon's order in the small hard hand she used for recipes, three doughs under cloth behind her, the window wall still fogged to milk. The stone under Percy's back was the exact temperature of a thing that has been at a fire since five in the morning and has opinions about it.

"Your ear's cold," Annabeth said.

"My ear's fine."

"It's not fine. It's the temperature of the lake. Give it here." She turned his face toward her with two fingers on his jaw, the way you square a picture, and then she put her hand over his ear and held it there, and the warmth came into it in stages, and Percy shut his eyes, and said, into her palm, "This is the best day I've had since the ridge."

"It's a day with bread in it."

"It's a day with a rectangle in it." He opened his eyes. She was looking at him with the expression that was not an expression, the one that was just her whole attention with nothing turned down, and he said, "You came down with a ruler."

"I came down with a bag with a hole in it."

"You came down with a bag with a hole in it because you heard the stove from the hill and you wanted to be in a warm room with your hands in something." He said it gently, and she did not argue, and put her forehead down against his temple, and stayed there, and the four words went around their joined hands again, unhurried, for as long as they wanted them.

They were there twelve minutes. Neither of them counted, and both of them knew the number exactly, and neither said it out loud, and after a while Annabeth said, into his hair, "Silena's watching."

"Silena's been watching since seven."

"Silena's been watching since July and she's going to want the rectangle back at one o'clock, so you've got four minutes and I've got to be gone when the small ones arrive, because if the small ones find me in a blanket with you I will never hear the end of it and neither will you."

"I'll hear the end of it anyway. Clarisse has a ledger."

"Clarisse has a ledger with a box drawn around nothing." Annabeth sat up, folded the blanket with two quick passes the way she folded anything — a map, a tent, an argument — and put it back on the big chair, and hauled him to his feet with the efficient strength of a girl who had been hauling him out of things since they were twelve, and then she took the flour handprint off the front of his shirt with her thumb, which had been there since nine o'clock and which they had both left there on purpose, each knowing the other had, and neither saying so.

"One of us is going to have to say it out loud one day," Annabeth said, at the door.

"Say what."

"You know what." She pulled her hat down, gave him a look with the whole flat of her attention behind it, and went out into the cold before he could answer, and Percy stood in his own cabin at one o'clock in the afternoon with flour on his shirt and the rectangle still warm beside him and the distinct, expanding feeling of a person who has just been given a deadline he has not started working on.

---

The small ones came at three, in a herd.

There were eleven of them, and Clarisse counted them from the doorway with her arms crossed and said, "Eleven. Door. Boots off." and then, to Percy, without turning her head, "You're on the door. Nobody eats standing. Nobody eats in boots. If they argue, they queue. The queue is the point."

"They're going to eat the queue."

"They're not going to eat the queue because the queue is where the bread is." She had the shelf loaf down at last, on the board, under a cloth, with her hand on it the way she had guarded it all morning, and then she did something that Percy watched as though it were a ceremony, which it was: she took the bread knife, cut the loaf in half down the middle, and put one half on a plate for the door and one half on a plate for the window, and said, out loud, to the room, "Two loaves are still in the oven and the rounds are in the cloth. Eat. There's more coming. Nobody hoards. Hoarding's a winter-list offence."

"That's not on the winter list," said a Hephaestus kid with his mouth already full.

"It is now." Clarisse put the list on the shelf, face down, and sat down at the table's end and did not eat anything herself for four minutes, which Percy clocked, and which he knew was not discipline but arithmetic: she was watching who went back and who didn't, who took a piece and then stood by the stove holding it like a person waiting to be told it was theirs to keep.

So Percy put a round, torn, still warm, into her hand where she sat, and she looked at it, and then at him, and said, "I've got two hands and a job."

"You've got a job and a mouth and that's a one-hand job." He sat down on the arm of her chair, a seat he had held since he was eleven and which the cabin had never legislated. "Eat. I'm on the door. The door's covered."

Clarisse ate the piece of bread, standing, at four minutes past three, on a Wednesday, and said nothing about it, and put her shoulder against his for the length of one bite, which in Cabin Three was a whole conversation, and then went back to counting the room.

At ten past three Annabeth came back from the pavilion and brought Thalia in with her.

Thalia came in the way she came into every room, which was as if the room had been waiting and she had work elsewhere, and she took the offered round, bit the end off it, looked at it with professional interest, and said, to Silena, who had come out of the kitchen with her sleeves up and her hair coming down, "This is a serious bread."

"It's Wednesday's bread."

"It's a serious bread and I want two rounds of it wrapped on Friday before archery. If I shoot on an empty stomach I'm slow, and I've got a reputation." She ate the second half in three bites and put the plate on the stack with the empties and looked around the room with her hands on her hips: eleven small campers and flour and a stove and four people she had come down a hill for without being asked, and her face did the crooked thing it did when a thing turned out to be more than it had been described as. "Good," she said, to the room, in general, about everything, and went back up the hill alone, carrying the way she carried everything, as though it were somebody else's problem she had temporarily taken on.

---

The last of the bread came out of the stove at five.

The light had gone by then. The window wall had stopped being a white panel and gone grey, and Silena had lit the two lamps, and the cabin had turned into the room it was in the evenings, which was a long table with people at both ends and the stove going at the far side and the smell of the day's work settling into the boards. The last loaf was the flat round with the salt, the one the forge kids asked for, and Silena took it out on the peel and set it down on the board and stood back from it with her head on one side, and the crust did its small ticking crackle, and she said, "Right. That's the lot. Nine loaves, eleven rounds, two twists, and nobody's going cold tonight on my watch."

"And the heel," Percy said.

"And the heel," Silena said. "The heel's yours. It's the last round's and I've been keeping it warm."

She had. She had wrapped the heel of the final round — the end of a bake, the piece a baker keeps for the person who stood in the kitchen all day — in a square of cloth at the back of the shelf, and she handed it to him across the table, and it was still warm, and Clarisse looked up from the winter list and said, "That's favouritism and I want it noted," and Percy said, "Note it," and broke the heel in half and put the bigger half on the board in front of her and ate the smaller half himself, standing at his own stove in his own cabin, and it was warm at five in the evening, which is not how heat works, and which he decided was a Cabin Three phenomenon and not worth investigating.

They fed the five of them at the table that night, the way they had eaten since September: all at once, over each other, the pot going round twice, nobody waiting for anybody to finish a sentence. Silena at the end with her mug in both hands, Clarisse with the list beside her plate doing the arithmetic she only did once a day and always at a table with her family on both sides of her, Annabeth with her shoulder against Percy's and her boots under the chair where the second rug was; and Percy, between bites, doing the arithmetic of his own day: eleven kids fed at three, a shop fed at noon, a rectangle taken and a rectangle given back, four words going back and forth between two hands since half past twelve.

At seven he walked Annabeth up the hill.

The cold had come down off the ridge with real weight in it, the kind that finds the gap between collar and neck and moves in, and the sky had more stars in it than anybody had names for, and the nine lamps went up the west path end to end, amber, in the order Beckendorf had put them, two and one and two, the staggered way, so that every stretch of the path had a light to walk toward. Percy counted them without meaning to, the way he had every night since they went up, and he knew exactly what Annabeth would say about it, and he did it anyway.

"You're counting," she said, at the fourth lamp.

"I'm walking."

"You're counting lamps. I can hear it in your breathing, which is a thing you do, which I have mentioned." She took his hand out of his pocket and held it in both of hers and walked on with it, and the path went up between the pines in the dark, and the tenth lamp was on the hook by the Cabin Three porch, and the eleventh — the one with no pole and no hook and never had been — was the light in Annabeth's window at Cabin One, which was on, because Thalia had gone up ahead and left it burning the way she had since they were children, for somebody walking up a hill in the dark.

"Tomorrow's the sack," Annabeth said, at her own steps.

"Tomorrow's the sack. Down by dark. If you've got anything for it, put it in before supper, because Silena will be at the table with the pen at six and she writes your name on the outside of anything you hand her, big."

"I know how she does it." Annabeth stood on the second step, which put her nearly his height, and did not go up, and Percy put his hands in his pockets, because the second step at the bottom of Cabin One was a place where a thing had happened on a Saturday and on a good number of nights since, and he was not going to hurry it.

"Good night, Percy."

"Good night, Annabeth."

"You've got flour in your hair."

"I've had flour in my hair since nine o'clock."

"I know. I've been looking at it all day." She took her hands out of her own pockets, put them on his collar, and kissed him, and the kiss was unhurried and warm and tasted faintly of salt bread and completely of the last two days, and it went on long enough that the lamp on the Cabin One porch, which was not on a timer, went on shining over the two of them and down the path and out along the nine lamps to the bottom of the hill, and then she went up the steps, and turned at the top with her hand on the door, and did not do anything except stand there for four seconds looking down at him, and then went in, and the window stayed lit.

Percy went back down the path in the dark with his hands in his pockets, and the tenth lamp was on the hook by the Cabin Three door, and through the window he could see the long table and the two lamps and Clarisse at the end of it with the list, and Silena at the stove doing the last of the thing she did every night, which was to leave the kitchen better than the day had found it.

He came in. The cabin smelled of the day all over again, the whole of it at once: hot iron and pine smoke and salt and the particular gold smell of a stove that had been fed since five in the morning and was now down to its coals. On the shelf, under a square of clean cloth, sat the last round of the day with the salt on it, and beside it a paper twist with a pencil line on it that said, in Silena's small hard hand: FOR THE SACK. ANYBODY WHO OWES A LETTER OWES IT TO THE BREAD.

Clarisse looked up from the end of the table. "Shut the door. It's October."

He shut the door and brought the last pan in from the porch. On the shelf, under a square of clean cloth, Silena had left one salted round with a note beside it: FOR THE SACK.

"That's not for you," she said, catching him looking.

"I was admiring your handwriting."

"Then admire it from farther away."

Clarisse moved the winter list aside and held out a clean paper bag. The bread had cooled just enough to be packed without turning the bag damp. Percy lifted it with the cloth, careful not to crush the crust, while Silena found string for the top.

"Who gets it?" he asked.

"Whoever is taking the letters down," Silena said. "They'll be out before any of us get breakfast."

"Except you."

"I'll be out making breakfast."

Percy tied the string and put the parcel beside the outgoing mail instead of carrying it to his bunk. It was such a small addition to the morning's delivery that he might have forgotten it if she hadn't labeled the shelf.

Clarisse put the list away. "Did the front step dry?"

"Mostly."

"Mostly isn't dry."

"I'll scrape it first thing."

"Good. We don't need a mail runner slipping before they reach the road."

Silena wiped the counter, then threw the cloth into the washbasket with the satisfaction of a person who had finally reached the end of her work. Percy carried the bag to the hook where the runner would find it. He checked the string once more before turning down the lamp.

The morning had started with bread for the cabin. It would end with a piece of it going down the road for somebody who had a cold shift ahead. That seemed like the right reason to leave one loaf uneaten.
