## Chapter 51: Medusa's Place

Percy knew the gnome place was going to be trouble because Grover would not stop talking about how good the grass smelled.

It was late afternoon on the second day after the river. The quest van they had borrowed from a friendly nymph depot smelled like hot vinyl and old french fries. Thalia drove with both hands tight on the wheel. Annabeth sat up front with the map open on her knees. Percy sat in the back with Grover, watching cornfields roll past the window.

Grover kept lifting his nose and sniffing.

"That is good grass," he said. "Like, really good grass. Rich. Soft. Someone waters it."

"Everything out here is corn," Thalia said. "You are smelling corn."

"No, under the corn. Grass. Lawn grass. Fancy lawn grass. The kind people trim into shapes."

Annabeth did not look up from the map. She had a pencil behind her ear and three different marks on the paper.

"There," she said, tapping a small square. "That is where the truck driver said to go. Aunty Em's Garden Gnome Emporium. He said a man paid cash to ship a long buzzing box from there two nights ago."

Percy sat up straighter. "Buzzing like the bolt?"

"He said it made his teeth hurt," Annabeth said. "And his radio went to static when he carried it. He put it in his truck and drove it one town over and dropped it at a Hermes Express stop. He said he was glad to get rid of it."

Thalia glanced in the rearview mirror. Her black hair was pulled back and her silver circlet caught the sun.

"And he just told you this?" she asked.

"He told Grover," Annabeth said. "Grover bought two coffees and asked about his route and the guy talked for twenty minutes."

Grover smiled shyly. "He misses his goats. People who miss goats will tell you anything."

Percy grinned. It was true. Grover could get a whole life story out of anyone at a gas station if he listened long enough.

"So we check the gnome place," Percy said. "Ask about the bolt. See who shipped it."

Annabeth folded the map. She looked tired around the eyes. She had not slept well since the river, and Percy knew she was thinking too fast, the way she did when a puzzle had too many pieces.

"Yeah," she said. "But we are careful. A garden place that ships in the middle of the night? That is weird. Real garden stores close at five."

Thalia turned off the highway onto a narrow county road. "Weird is our job."

The road curved past a bait shop and a closed mini golf course with a giant dinosaur. Then the corn opened up and Percy saw it.

A big white house with a wide porch. A gravel lot. A hand painted sign by the road that said AUNTY EM'S GARDEN GNOME EMPORIUM in curling green letters. Under that, in smaller red paint: STATUES! FOUNTAINS! ANIMALS! WE HAVE EVERYTHING!

Behind the house stretched rows and rows of stone. Percy could see them from the road. Gnomes. Lions. Maidens with jars. Boys with flutes. A whole stone zoo baking in the sun.

Grover pressed his face to the window. "Oh. Oh no."

"What?" Percy said.

"The grass here smells scared," Grover whispered. "Like it wants to be somewhere else."

Thalia slowed the van. "Scared grass. Great. My favorite."

Annabeth stared at the rows of statues. Her hand tightened on the map. Percy knew that look. That was her strategy look, the one she got when pieces started to fit and she did not like the picture.

"Percy," she said quietly. "Remember Chiron's lesson? Spring? About stone monsters?"

"Which one?" Percy asked. "There were a lot."

"The one with the sisters. The one where you never look straight at her."

Percy felt cold under his shirt. "You think this is her?"

"I think we park far back," Annabeth said. "And we keep our eyes down. And we have a plan before we go in."

---

Thalia parked the van under a maple at the edge of the gravel lot, far from the house.

No other cars were there. The lot was empty except for dust and a wheelbarrow tipped on its side. A wind chime on the porch moved even though Percy did not feel wind.

"Okay," Annabeth said. She turned around in her seat to face the back. "Rules. This might be nothing. But if it is something, we do not split up stupid. We stay where we can see each other's feet. Feet are safe to look at."

Grover raised his hand. "Can I wear the emergency colander?"

"What emergency colander?" Thalia asked.

Grover dug in his backpack and pulled out a dented metal colander with a strap glued on. "For snow and for looking at bad things without looking. Ella showed me. You put it on and you look down through the holes. It breaks up the bad picture."

Percy tried not to laugh. "You brought a colander for a quest?"

"I bring snacks and safety," Grover said with dignity. He strapped it on. It sat crooked over his cap and horns poked through two holes. "See? Safe."

Thalia snorted, then covered it with a cough. "Okay. Fashion icon. What else?"

Annabeth pulled out her Yankees cap. It looked old and soft. When she put it on, she went invisible. Percy had seen it before and it still made his stomach jump.

"I will scout the porch," her voice said from empty air. "You three look at the statues from the lot. Do not go inside until I wave. And Percy—"

"Yeah?"

"Do not look at anyone's face. Promise me. Even if she sounds nice. Especially if she sounds nice."

Percy nodded. His throat felt dry. "Promise."

Thalia pulled a polished hubcap out from behind the seat. She had picked it up at the nymph depot because it was shiny.

"Rearview mirror," she said. "Poor man's shield. If we need to look around corners, we use this. Not eyes."

Grover lifted his pipes. "And if she talks, I play loud. Satyrs are good at loud."

They got out. The heat hit like a blanket. Cicadas buzzed in the trees. The gnome rows shimmered.

Percy stayed close to Thalia. He looked at feet and gravel. That was the rule.

The statues were closer now. Big ones. Small ones. Some painted bright. Some plain gray.

A gnome fishing by a dry fountain. A lion with its mouth open. A girl holding grapes.

They looked too good.

Percy stopped by a satyr statue. It was a young satyr with a scared face, one arm up like he was trying to block sun. His hooves were detailed down to the cracks. His eyes were wide.

Percy felt his chest go tight.

"Annabeth," he called softly. "Invisible girl. Come see this."

He heard her shoes on gravel. Her hand touched his elbow so he would know where she was.

"What?" she whispered.

"This satyr. He looks like—"

"Like Uncle Ferdinand," Grover said. He had come up on Percy's other side. His voice shook. Through the colander holes, Percy could see his eyes go wide and wet. "That is my Uncle Ferdinand. That is his nose. That is his left horn with the chip from when he fell off the picnic table in '82."

Thalia crouched. She did not touch the statue. She looked at the base.

"There are initials," she said. "F.U. And a date. And look at this moss. No moss on the shoes. Moss everywhere else. Like he has not been here long."

Percy looked down the row. A maiden with a jar. A boy with a flute. A hiker with a backpack still on his shoulders, only the backpack was stone too, straps and all.

Nobody carved backpack straps out of stone for a garden store.

"Nobody buys these," Percy said quietly. "They are too real. Too sad."

Annabeth took off her cap and appeared beside him. Her face was pale. Her gray eyes were sharp.

"This is it," she said. "This is the trap. Chiron warned us. Medusa. She does the veil and the glasses and the nice lady voice. She turns people and sells them as art. Remember the story? Perseus used a shield to look at her reflection."

Thalia stood. "So we do not look at her. We look at feet and hubcaps."

Grover was still staring at his uncle. He reached out and put his palm against the cold stone hand.

"Hey, Uncle," he whispered. "Sorry I laughed when Dad told the picnic table story. You were brave to come this way."

Percy put a hand on Grover's shoulder. He did not know what to say. He thought of his sisters back at camp, Clarisse and Silena. If he saw them like this, turned to garden art, he would feel sick too.

"We will get him out," Percy said. "We will get all of them out."

A screen door creaked on the porch.

"Customers!" a woman's voice called, warm as honey cake. "Oh, wonderful! Come up to the porch, sweethearts! You must be hot!"

Percy froze. Every muscle told him to look up and be polite.

Annabeth grabbed his wrist hard.

"Eyes down!" she hissed.

---

The woman came down the porch steps. Percy stared at her shoes.

Dark sensible shoes. Long skirt. The hem moved strangely, like something under it was shifting.

"Welcome to Aunty Em's!" she said. "I am Aunty Em! Oh, four little dears! Satyr, too! We do not get many satyrs since my poor Ferdinand visited! He loved my art so much he decided to stay forever!"

Grover made a small sound. Percy squeezed his shoulder to keep him still.

Annabeth stepped forward. She kept her eyes on the woman's skirt hem, not higher.

"Hi," Annabeth said. Her voice was polite and steady, the way she sounded when she was stalling for a plan. "We are looking for a package. Long box. Buzzing. A truck driver said it shipped from here."

"Oh!" Aunty Em laughed. It sounded kind. That made it worse. "So many packages come and go, sweetheart. You would need to come inside to check my receipts. All my books are inside. Nice and cool and dark inside."

Thalia shifted. Percy saw her hand tighten on the hubcap.

"We like outside," Thalia said. Her voice was rough. "You can bring the books out."

"Do not be silly," Aunty Em said. "The sun will hurt your eyes. And this handsome boy—" Percy felt her attention turn to him like heat. "This handsome boy with sea green eyes. You should look at me when I talk to you, sweetheart. Where are your manners?"

Her skirt rustled closer. Percy smelled something like old leaves and snake skin.

"Percy, do not," Annabeth whispered.

Percy stared at the gravel. His heart thumped. He wanted to look up. It felt rude not to. It felt like strings were pulling his chin.

That was how she got people, he realized. Not just eyes. Manners. Being nice until you were polite yourself into stone.

"Annabeth is right," he said to the shoes. "We stay outside."

Aunty Em's voice cooled a fraction. "Annabeth? Oh. That is her name? Which one is she?"

"The one talking to you," Percy said. "Annabeth is my girlfriend. We are together on this."

Annabeth's fingers brushed his wrist once, quick. Thanks.

"How sweet," Aunty Em said. "Young love. I love statues of young love. They sell very well as a pair."

Thalia stepped between Percy and the skirt hem. She held the hubcap low, angled up.

"I see you," Thalia said quietly. "In the reflection. Nice try with the scarf."

Percy risked a glance at the hubcap. In the curved metal he saw a warped face, veiled, with dark glasses. Behind the veil, something moved. Snakes. Small green heads lifting.

His stomach turned.

Aunty Em sighed. "Daughter of Zeus. Always trouble. Your father and his court ruin mortal lives. Did your little sister tell you what it feels like to be their monster?"

Annabeth stiffened beside Percy. "Do not listen, Thalia," she said fast. "She is trying to split us."

"I know," Thalia said. But her jaw was tight.

Aunty Em moved fast. Faster than an old lady should. Her hand shot out toward Percy's chin to lift his face.

Annabeth tackled him sideways into the dirt.

They hit hard. Gravel bit Percy's elbows. Annabeth landed half on him, one hand shoving his forehead down.

"Keep your eyes shut!" she yelled. "Shut them!"

Percy shut them. He heard fabric rip. He heard Thalia shout.

"Hey! Snake lady! Over here!"

He heard Grover's pipes blast a horrible loud note, off key on purpose, to break the charm.

Percy kept his eyes shut. Annabeth's weight was warm on his back. Her braid smacked his cheek.

"Are you okay?" she whispered. "Did you see?"

"No," Percy breathed. "I saw gravel. I saw your shoe. I am good."

"Good. Keep them shut until I say."

He felt her shift, but she did not get off. She was shielding him.

Through his closed eyes, he heard Aunty Em hiss. Not like a person. Like a nest.

"You little brats," she said. "You come to my home? You threaten my art? I was a victim once! Did they teach you that in your camp? The gods used me and threw me away! Now I make things that last!"

"Yeah?" Thalia said. "You make garden gnomes? Real proud legacy."

"Thalia," Annabeth warned. "Do not make her mad."

"She is already mad," Thalia said. "I am making her look at me."

Percy heard metal scrape. The hubcap.

"Grover! Now!" Thalia yelled.

Grover played again, louder. A marching song, fast and squeaky. Percy heard hooves running on gravel.

"Left shed!" Grover shouted between notes. "I smell people! Live people! This way!"

Percy opened his eyes a slit, staring only at the ground. He saw Annabeth's knees, Thalia's boots, Grover's hooves kicking dust.

"Go," Annabeth said to him. "Crawl with me. To the truck. We need the side mirror."

They crawled. Percy kept his face down. Gravel dug into his palms. Annabeth held the back of his shirt like she was steering him.

Behind them, Aunty Em laughed.

"You cannot run forever, little hero," she called. "Look at me just once and I will make you beautiful forever beside your girlfriend!"

Percy felt Annabeth's hand shake on his shirt.

"She is lying," Annabeth muttered. "She is lonely and mean. Do not listen."

"I am not listening," Percy said. "I am crawling."

That got a short laugh out of her, scared but real.

They reached the van's back tire. Percy pressed his cheek to the hot metal. Annabeth popped up just enough to angle the side mirror.

"Okay," she breathed. "I can see her. She is by the satyr statue. Veil is slipping. Thalia is keeping her facing the hubcap."

Percy risked lifting his head an inch. He looked only at the mirror.

In the mirror, warped and small, he saw Aunty Em pull off her scarf. Snakes writhed out, hissing. She wore dark glasses but one side was cracked.

Thalia held the hubcap like a shield. She was backing toward the shed, drawing Medusa away from Percy and Annabeth.

"Come on!" Thalia taunted. "You want a Zeus kid? Come get one! Bet you cannot turn me if I am looking at hubcap!"

"You are brave," Medusa said. "Brave girls make good statues. Mouth open, screaming."

Grover ran past the van, dragging something. Percy saw rope and a tarp.

"Found them!" Grover panted. His colander was sideways. "Shed! Three people! Not stone all the way! Legs stone, arms okay! They were wrapped like garden bags! I cut them loose! Thalia helped before she went back out!"

Annabeth stared. "Who?"

"A truck driver! And two campers! From camp! They were on a delivery! She caught them yesterday!"

Percy sat up a little, still eyes-down. "Are they okay?"

"Scared! Thirsty! I gave them water! I told them to stay behind the van and not look! The driver keeps saying black car! Black car took the buzzing box west!"

Annabeth grabbed Percy's arm. "West. Did he say where west?"

"He said LA! The man in black paid double to rush it to LA! To a studio! With dead flowers on the door!"

Percy felt a jolt. LA. The Underworld entrance was under LA. Everyone on a quest knew that rumor. Hades. The bolt went west to the Underworld.

"That is the clue," Annabeth said. "That has to be it."

A crash came from the statue rows. A scream, then stone grinding.

"Thalia!" Annabeth yelled.

Percy jumped up, forgetting, eyes still down but moving.

"Stay!" Annabeth ordered, but Percy was already moving toward the sound, using the van's windows as dim mirrors.

He saw Thalia in the passenger window reflection. She had tripped over a gnome. She was on one knee. Medusa loomed over her, veil gone, glasses slipping.

"Thalia! Your left!" Percy shouted.

Thalia rolled. Medusa's hand caught Percy's forearm as he reached for Thalia. Her nails were bronze and sharp. Pain sliced hot across his skin.

Percy yelled and fell back, clutching his arm. Blood welled in a thin line.

"Percy!" Annabeth screamed.

She ran to him, not looking at Medusa, guided by his voice. She threw her Yankees cap over Medusa's head from behind.

The cap turned invisible on contact, but it covered the snakes. Medusa shrieked and clawed at it.

"Now!" Annabeth yelled. "Thalia! The hubcap! Show her herself!"

Thalia scrambled up, grabbed the hubcap with both hands, and held it right in front of Medusa's covered face. She yanked the Yankees cap away fast and ducked.

Percy stared at the dirt. He heard a gasp.

A horrible scraping sound. Then silence.

Then a thud, heavy and stone.

Grover's pipes stopped.

"Is it—" Percy whispered, eyes shut tight. "Can I—"

"Wait," Annabeth said. Her hand was on his good arm, holding tight. "Wait. Thalia, talk."

Thalia's voice came, shaky and alive. "She looked. She looked at herself in the hubcap. She is— she is stone. All the way. Even the snakes. They look like little stone worms. It is gross."

Percy opened one eye. Dirt. Annabeth's shoe.

He opened the other. Annabeth crouched over him, her face white, her hands already tearing a strip from the bottom of her camp T-shirt.

"Let me see," she said.

---

It was not a deep cut. That was what Annabeth kept saying, over and over, like saying it would make her hands stop shaking.

"It is not deep. It is not deep. You are okay."

They sat behind the van in the shade of the maple. Thalia stood guard with the hubcap, watching the stone Medusa to make sure she stayed stone. Grover was with the freed people, giving them water and talking fast to keep them calm.

Percy sat on the bumper. Annabeth knelt in front of him.

She folded the T-shirt strip into a pad and pressed it to his forearm. The blood spotted through.

"Sorry," she said. "I need to press hard."

"It is okay," Percy said. He watched her face. She bit her lip in concentration. A smudge of dirt crossed her cheek. Her braid had come half undone and hair stuck to her neck with sweat.

"It stings," he admitted.

"I know." She did not look up. "Blow on it if you need to. Silena says that helps. Silena always blows on ours before she wraps."

Percy smiled a little despite the sting. "Your Silena tip?"

"My borrowed Silena tip," Annabeth corrected. "My sister Thalia just tells me to walk it off. A sister and a friend, different medicine."

Percy laughed, then winced. "Ow."

"Sorry, sorry." Annabeth eased pressure, then wrapped the strip around his arm and tied it snug. Her fingers were careful and quick. She tucked the end flat.

"There," she said softly. "Not my best wrap. Chiron would give me a C. But it will hold until we clean it right."

Percy looked at the bandage. Crooked but tight. He could feel her hands still resting lightly on his wrist.

"Thank you," he said. "For tackling me. You saved me. I almost looked."

Annabeth finally looked up. Her gray eyes were bright and angry and scared all at once.

"You promised you would not look," she said. "Then you almost did because she was being polite. Percy, you cannot be polite with monsters."

"I know," he said. "I wanted to be rude, but my chin moved on its own."

She huffed, half laugh, half scold. "Then I will keep tackling you. That is my job now. Professional Percy tackler."

"Good job, then." He bumped his knee against hers. "You are good at it."

They were quiet a second. The cicadas buzzed. The wind chime clinked. Somewhere Grover was saying, "There, there, drink slow, small sips."

Annabeth brushed dirt off Percy's knee. She did not move her hand right away.

"When she said she would make us statues as a pair," Annabeth said quietly, "I got really mad. Like, arena sparring mad. Because we are not art. We are— we are us."

Percy swallowed. "I got mad too. When she grabbed you— I mean, when she talked about you."

Annabeth's cheeks went pink. She looked down at the bandage and smoothed it once more, even though it did not need it.

"You bled because you ran to help Thalia," she said. "That was brave. Stupid, but brave."

"Thalia would do it for me," Percy said. "She is like Clarisse that way. Acts tough, but stays."

Annabeth nodded. She glanced toward her sister.

Thalia stood by the stone Medusa, spear out now, keeping the freed truck driver behind her. The driver was a big mortal man with stone dust on his legs up to the knees. He kept blinking like he could not believe he could move his toes again.

Thalia caught them looking. She raised an eyebrow.

Percy felt his ears go hot. Annabeth pulled her hand back fast like she had been caught passing notes.

Thalia walked over. She moved stiff, favoring her scraped knee. She stopped and looked at Percy's bandaged arm, then at Annabeth's torn shirt hem, then at their faces.

For a second Percy thought she would tease mean, the way older kids sometimes did. The way she had teased Luke about sword drills, sharp to hide soft.

Instead Thalia crouched down so she was eye level with them. Her voice was low and kind, and that was new.

"Hey," she said. "You okay, Seaweed Brain?"

"Yeah," Percy said. "Just a scratch."

"Good tackle, Beth," Thalia said to her sister. "Fast. Clean. Coach would be proud."

Annabeth blinked. "Really?"

"Really." Thalia bumped Annabeth's shoulder with her fist, gentle. "You kept his eyes down. That is hard to do when someone is being nice-creepy. You did good."

Annabeth stared at her sister like she had grown a second head. Thalia never praised soft. She praised like Clarisse, with a shove.

Thalia smiled, crooked. "What? I can be nice."

"You are being nice," Annabeth said suspiciously. "Are you hit on the head?"

"Maybe a little." Thalia touched her temple where a gnome had clipped her. "But I mean it. You two—" She gestured between them, bandage and torn shirt and knees touching. "You look out for each other. I see it. I used to think it was just kid stuff. Following each other around with maps and swords."

Percy held very still. Annabeth held very still.

"But back there," Thalia said, "you did not panic. You covered each other. That is not kid stuff. That is partner stuff. Quest partner stuff." She paused, then added quieter, "And maybe other stuff someday, when you are older and less gross. I am not blind."

Annabeth turned bright red. "Thalia!"

"What? I said someday. And gross is a compliment. Everything at twelve is gross." Thalia stood and ruffled Annabeth's hair, then, after a second, ruffled Percy's too. "Take the compliment, babies. I am accepting your whole tragic bandage romance. Someone has to."

Percy felt warm from ears to toes. It was different from Clarisse teasing him about blankets. That had been brother-sister teasing to cover love. This was Thalia offering something. Permission. Welcome.

"Thanks," he mumbled.

"Yeah," Annabeth mumbled into her knees. But she was smiling.

Thalia winked at Percy over Annabeth's head. It was quick and conspiratorial, like Silena winking at him after chocolate.

"Come on," Thalia said, louder, back to business. "Nurse duty is over. We have statues to move and a driver to question before he faints."

---

The truck driver sat behind the van with a water bottle hugged to his chest. His name was Sam. His legs were gray to the knee and stiff, but Grover had wrapped them in wet towels and he could wiggle his toes.

"I do not remember much," Sam said. "Lady paid cash. Nice lady with a scarf, even in heat. Said do not open the box. Said take it to the Hermes Express drop by the highway. Said a black car would pick it up."

"What black car?" Annabeth asked. She had her notebook out. Pencil ready. Professional.

"Long. Old. No license I could see. Driver wore black. Smelled like—" Sam shivered. "Like a basement in winter. Like flowers left too long. He did not talk. Just loaded the box. It buzzed in his hands and he smiled like it liked him. Then he drove west. Toward the sun. Fast."

Grover sat beside Sam, patting his arm. "You did good telling us. West is good. West helps."

Sam looked at the rows of statues. His eyes filled. "Are they— are they all—?"

"Some are stone a long time," Thalia said honestly. She did not lie to mortals. That was a Zeus kid thing, Percy thought. Blunt. "But you three were fresh. The bags slowed it. And your uncle—" she nodded to Grover, "your uncle is stone, but his heart— Grover says he can hear it. Faint. Like a drum far off."

Grover nodded hard. His colander was off now. His horns shone with sweat.

"I can hear them," he said. "Uncle Ferdinand and two others. Bump-bump. Slow. They are in there. If we get them to camp, to Chiron, to the gods— maybe Apollo can— maybe—"

Percy knelt beside Grover. "Then we take them with us. We load them."

Thalia looked at the van, then at the statues. "That satyr weighs like four hundred pounds. We cannot lift him."

"We can roll him," Annabeth said. She was already planning, pencil moving. "Use the wheelbarrow. Use the tarp as a sled. Tip, do not lift. Like moving a canoe."

Percy grinned. "You have moved canoes?"

"I have drawn canoes," Annabeth said. "Same thing if you believe."

They worked as the sun dropped.

Percy and Thalia tipped Uncle Ferdinand gently onto the tarp. Grover talked to him the whole time.

"It is okay, Uncle. We got you. Sorry about the bumpy part. Annabeth says sorry about the bumpy part in advance. She is an architect. She knows angles."

Annabeth directed from the side, eyes bright. "Left a little! Right! Keep his head up! Do not drag his nose!"

Thalia pulled, Percy pushed, Grover steadied the head like it was a baby bird.

The two campers, a Hermes kid and a Demeter kid, both half stone to the waist, hobbled to the van with help. They cried when Grover gave them nectar water.

"You came," the Hermes kid whispered. "We sent a dream. We did not think anyone heard."

"We hear," Thalia said gruffly. She helped him up into the van. "Zeus kids hear. Even when we pretend we do not."

The Hermes kid stared at her circlet. "You are Thalia Grace? Annabeth's sister?"

"Yeah."

"Annabeth talked about you," he said. "At camp. She said you taught her the Girl Who Ran."

Thalia went still. Then she reached up and tucked the blanket around his stone legs with surprising gentleness.

"She remembered," Thalia said quietly.

"She tells it to everyone who is homesick," the Demeter kid added. Her voice was hoarse. "She says her sister gave it to her, so she gives it away."

Annabeth turned away fast and pretended to check Percy's bandage. Her ears were pink. Percy pretended not to notice because he knew she would hate being noticed, but his chest felt big.

They loaded three statues across the back seats, padded with gnome boxes and blankets. Uncle Ferdinand rode like a king, seatbelted upright, colander now perched on his head because Grover insisted it would keep him safe.

"He looks good," Grover said, stepping back. "Distinguished."

"He looks like a traffic cone," Thalia said. But she adjusted the colander so it sat straight.

Percy walked the rows one last time with Annabeth, covering Medusa's stone face with a burlap sack and turning her toward the wall so even her stone eyes faced nothing.

"Should we smash her?" Percy asked quietly. He did not want to. Smashing felt wrong, even for a monster. It felt like something the old heroes would do and brag about, and he did not want to brag.

Annabeth shook her head. "No. Chiron says stone holds memory. We leave her for the gods to judge. We take her victims to people who can help. That is better than smashing."

Percy nodded. That sounded right. Mending, not fighting. Silena would like that.

Annabeth stopped by the hiker statue. She touched its stone backpack strap lightly.

"We will come back," she promised it softly. "With Apollo or Athena. We will not leave you as lawn art."

Percy heard her and felt sad and proud at once. This was She Who Draws Lines, he thought. Seeing how things fit. Seeing people, not just statues.

The sun touched the corn. Long shadows stretched across the gravel.

Sam flagged down a second trucker on the highway with his hazards. The freed campers piled in with notes for Camp Half-Blood. Grover hugged his uncle's stone hand through the van window.

"Hold on, Uncle," he whispered. "Just hold on."

Thalia started the van. Annabeth rode shotgun with the notebook open to a fresh page. She wrote in big letters: BOLT WENT WEST. HADES? LA. UNDERWORLD STUDIO?

Percy sat in back with Grover, keeping a hand on Uncle Ferdinand so he would not tip.

As they pulled out, Percy looked back at the Emporium. The sign squeaked in the wind. The rows of stone faces stared at nothing. The sack-covered Medusa stood by the porch, stone snakes frozen mid hiss.

He thought about what Medusa had said. About being used and thrown away. He did not feel sorry for her, exactly. She had chosen to hurt people. But he understood why Annabeth had not smashed her. Some stories did not need more breaking.

Grover leaned his head against the window. He looked tired and happy and sad all mixed.

"Do you think Juniper would like Uncle Ferdinand?" he asked suddenly. "When he is unstuck? He tells bad jokes. But he is good with plants."

Percy smiled. "She will love him. Especially if he likes music."

"He snores like pipes," Grover said. "That counts."

Up front, Thalia glanced at Annabeth's notebook.

"West," Thalia said. "Of course it is west. Everything stinks and goes west. LA. Dead flowers. Studio. That is Hades. That is the Underworld."

Annabeth nodded. She turned to look back at Percy. Her eyes met his over the seat.

"We were right to come here," she said. "Even if it was a trap. We got people out. And we know where to go."

Percy touched his bandaged arm. It throbbed dully under the cloth.

"West," he said. "To get the bolt back before war."

"West," Annabeth agreed. She held his gaze a second longer, then turned forward, pencil tapping.

Thalia caught Percy's eye in the mirror and mouthed, tragic bandage romance, with a grin.

Percy stuck his tongue out at her. Thalia laughed out loud, surprised, and Annabeth turned to ask what was funny, and Grover started playing a soft song on his pipes for his uncle, low and sweet, for the road west.

The van rattled onto the highway, heading into the sunset, carrying three stone passengers, one bandaged hero, two sisters, and a satyr who would not stop talking to his uncle about grass.

Behind them, the gnome rows stood empty in the dusk. The wind chime rang once, then still.

Ahead, the sky went orange and pink, and the road ran straight west toward LA, toward dead studios and buzzing boxes and a god in black who smiled when lightning liked him.

Percy watched the sun go down and thought of the Diver holding his lamp because someone held the rope. He thought of Annabeth tackling him into dirt because she held his wrist and would not let go.

He tightened his hand on the tarp so Uncle Ferdinand would not slide, and he stayed awake to watch the road, because that was what partners did.

They held the rope for each other.
