## Chapter 32: Triton Visits

The morning Triton came to Camp Half-Blood, the canoe lake was doing something wrong.

Percy noticed it before breakfast, down at the canoe lake dock with nothing but a hoodie and his sneakers going soggy at the edges. The water was too flat. Not calm-flat, like after a storm passes. Flat like someone had pressed glass over the top of it. No ripple. No gull chop. And the color was wrong too, that deep trench blue you only get a mile out past the shelf, not in a lake that’s usually green and full of naiad gossip.

Then it stood up.

Not a wave. A column. A pillar of water about ten feet tall rose straight out of the middle of the canoe lake without splashing, and inside it, arms crossed and looking extremely annoyed about being damp in the wrong kind of water, was a guy with sea-green eyes and black hair tied back and armor made out of abalone shell.

“Perseus,” he said, in a voice that echoed off the water. “You were supposed to meet me at the shore. Father drew you a map.”

Percy blinked. “I don’t — what map?”

“In the dreams,” Triton said. “The conch dreams. Three nights running.”

“Oh,” Percy said. “I thought those were just weird dreams about soup.”

Triton stared at him. Then the water column collapsed sideways and deposited him neatly on the dock, completely dry from the knees up and soaked from the knees down, which seemed to offend him personally.

He was Percy's brother. His actual older half-brother, and Clarisse and Silena's too. Son of Poseidon and Amphitrite, heir of Atlantis, commander of something with a very long title Percy kept forgetting. Triton had known him for years; Percy had only the recent palace visit to put beside those years. The hippocamp lesson when he was seven, the hurricane off Montauk, the nickname surface barnacle — Triton had supplied those stories. Percy knew the brother telling them better than he knew the boy inside them.

He looked the same. Twenty-ish to look at, though the adult god had commanded patrols long before Percy was born. Tall. Built like a swimmer who also wrestles sharks for fun. Same nose as Percy, which was weird to see on someone else's face. Same grin that meant trouble.

“You're shorter than I remembered,” he said, by way of greeting.

“You're ruder than I remembered,” Percy said. Triton laughed, pulled him into a headlock, and rubbed his knuckles into Percy's hair until Percy yelled uncle in Ancient Greek.

That's how Annabeth found them five minutes later: Percy getting noogied by an Atlantean prince on Dock 3 while three canoes spun slowly in circles around them because the lake was excited.

Annabeth Grace — daughter of Zeus, blonde princess with a gray-eyed glare that could stop a drakon mid-lunge, his girlfriend and best friend in the entire world, who had saved his life more times than he could count — stopped at the end of the dock with her hands on her hips and her camp beads clicking in the wind.

“Percy,” she said slowly. “Why is there a man in shell armor giving you a swirlie with lake water?”

“Annabeth! Perfect timing.” Percy wriggled free, hair sticking straight up with lake weed in it. “This is Triton. My brother. Triton, this is Annabeth. Daughter of Zeus. She’ll probably throw a book at you.”

Triton straightened. All the joking drained out of his face so fast it was like watching the tide go out. He looked at Annabeth the way Atlantean guards look at storm clouds.

“Zeus,” he said flatly.

Annabeth lifted her chin. “Poseidon,” she said back, with exactly the same tone.

Oh, gods. This was going to be a long visit.

---

Triton had prepared for this diplomatic mission with the seriousness it deserved.

Chiron had approved the overnight visit, and Triton had asked his lieutenant what the surface camp required. He had been told: *bring tribute, show restraint, do not challenge the Ares cabin to a duel within the first hour,* and he had remembered two of those three things.

The tribute, a conch chest full of deep pearls and unrustable bronze, was currently sitting at the bottom of the canoe lake because the naiads had declared it “pretty” and refused to give it back.

The restraint was going well so far, in his opinion. He had not blasted anyone. He had not flooded anything. He had only mildly threatened one canoe.

What no one in Atlantis had prepared him for was the stairs.

“Why,” he demanded, halfway up Half-Blood Hill, breathing harder than he would ever admit, “does your camp insist on vertical movement? The palace is flat. The ocean floor is flat. Flat is honorable.”

“It’s a hill,” Percy said, half-jogging backward in front of him to show off that he wasn’t even winded. “You walk up it.”

“It moves,” Triton accused. The gravel was sliding under his sandals. His sandals, made for palace marble and sharkskin saddles, had zero traction. “The ground is unstable. And the air is wrong. Too thin. Too loud with insects.”

“That’s crickets. They’re harmless.”

“They are spies.”

Annabeth, walking a careful three steps to the side like she wasn’t sure if she should offer help or push him down the hill, snorted. “You command a battalion and you’re scared of crickets?”

“I am not scared,” Triton said with immense dignity, just as a grasshopper landed on his shoulder guard and he flinched so hard he nearly dropped his trident. “I am culturally unaccustomed.”

Percy laughed so hard he had to sit down.

It got worse at the cabins. Triton had expected tents? Huts? Honest warrior barracks? What he found were twelve Greek temples crammed together around a green, each one humming with godly power, laundry lines strung between columns, a kid in an Apollo cabin sunbathing on the roof playing a lyre badly.

“Father’s cabin,” Percy said proudly, stopping in front of Cabin 3. It was low and long and smelled of salt and rain, walls built of rough stone with seashells embedded in them, a fountain in the corner that never stopped running. “Home sweet home.”

Triton stared. “It’s… small.”

“It sleeps six.”

“In Atlantis your nursery was larger than this. Your nursery had a whale.”

“Yeah, well, the whale can’t come to camp. Rules.” Percy shoved the door open. Inside, it was cool and dim and perfect — three bunk beds, sea charts on the walls, his old sleeping bag still balled in the corner, Tyson's guest blanket folded with military precision after his last visit. Their Cyclops brother was back at the undersea forges now, but the blanket and his lopsided carved sheep remained.

Triton ran one finger along the edge of Percy’s bunk and came away with dust. He frowned. “You live like a mortal soldier.”

“I live like a camper,” Percy corrected. “It’s better. You get to pick your own blanket.”

“I have attendants for blankets.”

“Exactly. No attendants here. You have to carry your own plate and everything.”

Triton looked genuinely alarmed. “Barbaric.”

Annabeth leaned in the doorway, arms crossed. She hadn’t stopped watching him since the lake. Not hostile, exactly. Calculating. Like she was already three moves ahead in a game he didn’t know they were playing.

“There’s a guest bunk in the Big House,” she said. “Or you can stay in Cabin 3 if you don’t mind smelling like low tide.”

“I am the tide,” Triton said.

“Great. You’ll fit right in,” she said, and walked off toward Cabin 6, already shouting for someone named Malcolm to bring the guest protocols.

Percy watched her go, then glanced at his brother. “She doesn’t hate you,” he whispered.

“She is Zeus’s daughter,” Triton whispered back. “I am keeping count of her knives. She has four visible. There are likely more.”

“She always has more.”

“Good. I respect preparedness.”

---

Clarisse La Rue knew within four seconds of seeing the new guy that she was going to fight him.

It wasn't personal. It was professional. She was a daughter of Poseidon, of Cabin 3, terror of the obstacle course, owner of a well-worn spear that had tasted the hide of everything from hellhounds to the Stoll brothers when they deserved it. Her job was to establish dominance over anything taller than her that walked into camp with armor on.

And this guy walked in with *a lot* of armor on. Plus a trident. Plus hair that belonged in a shampoo commercial.

He was standing in the middle of the sword arena at 10 a.m., turning in a slow circle and frowning at the straw dummies like they’d personally insulted his mother, while Percy tried to explain the rules.

“So no powers,” Percy was saying. “No tidal waves in the arena. Chiron’s rule. And no impaling. Also Chiron’s rule.”

“I do not need powers to defeat straw,” Triton said.

Clarisse cracked her knuckles and stepped into the ring. She was already in full sparring gear — helmet under one arm, spear glinting faintly, a grin that made first-year campers fake a stomach ache to get out of practice.

“Hey, Fish Boy,” she called. “You spar, or do you just stand around looking pearly?”

Triton turned. He looked her up and down, taking in the red plume, the scar, the spear, the stance. Something like delight flickered across his face.

“Sea spawn,” he said. “Excellent. I have been told not to duel you for at least an hour after arrival.”

“It’s been two hours,” Percy offered helpfully.

“Then the waiting period has expired.” Triton bowed, Atlantean style, trident sweeping low. “Triton, son of Poseidon, heir to the Tidal Throne, bane of karkinos. I accept.”

“Clarisse La Rue, daughter of Poseidon, bane of everyone. Try to keep up, Barnacle Prince.”

The circle that formed was instant. Word travels fast at camp — *new godling in the arena, Clarisse is gonna kill him* — and within minutes there were thirty kids on the benches with popcorn from the Apollo cabin’s secret stash.

Annabeth slid in next to Percy on the front bench, notebook already open. Thalia dropped down behind them, boots up, lightning crackling faintly around her bracelet. Even Mr. D paused his pinochle game on the porch to watch.

Chiron trotted into the center, tail swishing. “Rules,” he said sternly. “Sparring blades only. First to three touches. No godly powers. Triton, that means no calling water. Clarisse, that means no — ”

“Yeah, yeah, no cheap shots on the guest,” Clarisse muttered, lowering her spear to salute position. “Spoilsport.”

Triton accepted a standard-issue practice sword like it was a dead fish. “This is unbalanced,” he complained. “The hilt is wrong. The blade is too light. Where is the heft?”

“You’ll manage, Your Highness,” Clarisse said, and lunged.

She was fast. Everyone forgot how fast Clarisse was until she was coming at you. She feinted high, cut low, slammed her shield into Triton’s ribs in the first three seconds and scored a touch on his thigh.

“Point La Rue!” Chiron called.

Triton looked down at the chalk mark on his greave, then up at Clarisse, and grinned — a real grin, sharp and surprised and pleased.

“Again,” he said.

What followed was the best sparring demonstration Camp Half-Blood had seen all summer.

Clarisse fought like a wildfire — aggressive, pressing, shield-first, using her weight. Triton fought like water — which sounds poetic until you realize what it means: he never stopped moving, never met force with force, slid around every bash and let her momentum carry her past him. He was used to fighting with a trident in three dimensions underwater, so on land with a short sword he kept trying to stab in directions that didn’t exist and cursing in Ancient Atlantean when his feet betrayed him.

“Your footwork is terrible!” Clarisse barked, after he tripped over his own sandal strap and nearly impaled a dummy.

“Your ground is terrible!” Triton shot back, hopping on one foot to retie it. “It does not move correctly!”

“Point Triton!” Chiron called, as Triton flicked his wrist and tapped Clarisse’s shoulder while she was laughing.

“Hey!”

“You were distracted. Distraction is death.”

“Oh, it’s on, fish-face.”

Second touch went to Clarisse after a brutal shield rush that sent Triton skidding five feet in the dirt. Third touch — a draw, both blades landing at the same instant, chest to chest — had the whole arena on its feet shouting.

They stood there panting, swords locked, faces six inches apart, both grinning like maniacs.

“You’re good,” Clarisse said grudgingly.

“You’re brutal,” Triton said admiringly. “Our father would be proud. His soldiers fight with more grace and less effectiveness.”

“Wanna go again? Real weapons this time?”

“Absolutely not,” Chiron and Percy said at the same time.

Clarisse pulled off her helmet, shook out her ponytail, and stuck out her hand. Triton stared at it like he’d never seen a handshake before — which, Percy realized, he probably hadn’t — then clasped her forearm, warrior-style.

“Next time,” Clarisse said, “I’m not holding back.”

“Next time,” Triton said solemnly, “I will bring proper footwear.”

From the benches, Thalia wolf-whistled. “Get his autograph, Clarisse! First person to make Barnacle Boy sweat!”

Clarisse flipped her off without looking, but she was still smiling when she walked off to get ice for the bruise blooming on her forearm. Respect, in Cabin 3, looks a lot like wanting to fight someone again tomorrow.

Triton limped over to Percy, practice sword dragging in the dirt, hair plastered to his forehead with actual sweat — a novelty he kept touching with wonder.

“She drew blood,” he announced proudly, showing off a shallow scrape on his elbow. “On land. With sticks. I must tell my war council.”

“Dude, you’re bleeding on the arena sand,” Percy said, handing him a water bottle. “Here. Drink. Don’t bleed.”

Triton sniffed the plastic bottle suspiciously. “What is this vessel?”

“It’s Aquafina. Just drink it.”

---

Silena Beauregard, daughter of Poseidon, had decided, approximately ninety seconds after laying eyes on Triton, that he was a project.

Not in a mean way. In a Cabin 3 way. Which meant: this poor beautiful boy had arrived at camp with seaweed in his hair, shell armor that squeaked when he walked, and absolutely no idea what to do with a cheeseburger, and she could not in good conscience let that stand.

“Triton! Hi! I’m Silena!” She intercepted him outside the dining pavilion at lunch, all smiles and fluttering blue dress and perfected friendliness that had disarmed monsters and new campers alike. “Percy’s told us stories about you, but always his version, which is rude, so I’m going to fix that. You’re sitting with us today.”

Triton blinked. In Atlantis, sea-priestesses — daughters of the sea nymphs who served similar social functions — did not approach princes with such directness. They approached with dances and shell gifts and formal introductions via chamberlains.

“I… have my own table?” He glanced at Percy for rescue. Percy mouthed *just go with it* and abandoned him.

The dining pavilion was chaos to Atlantean eyes. No seating order by rank. No tribute presentation. Children burning bits of food in a fire — *wasteful!* — and laughing. A satyr playing pipes with his mouth full. A girl with spiky black hair and a leather jacket (Thalia, he would later learn) standing on a bench to shout something about seconds.

Silena steered him to the Poseidon table, sat him down, and began what she later called “cultural rehabilitation.”

“Okay, first: cheeseburger.” She slid a plate in front of him. “Camp classic. Beef, cheese, bun, pickles. Do not offer it to the gods yet, just try it.”

Triton picked up the burger with two fingers, like it might bite. Sniffed it. “It smells of… fire and cow.”

“That’s the point. Bite.”

He bit. Chewed. Stopped. His eyes went very wide.

“This,” he said slowly, “is extraordinary. It is hot on the outside and juicy on the inside and the yellow layer is… melting. Why have we not conquered the surface for this alone?”

The Poseidon table erupted. Silena beamed and patted his hand.

“Right? Now try the fries. Ketchup is the red stuff. No, not blood. Tomatoes. Just — just eat it.”

Fries: another revelation. “Salt crystals! On softened roots! Genius!”

Pizza: he tried to eat it with a knife and fork until Drew Tanaka showed him the fold method, and then he ate four slices and declared the pavilion oven “a temple worthy of worship.”

Ambrosia: he sniffed it and went pale. “That smells like the infirmary after a kraken battle. No.”

Nectar: one sip made his ears turn red. “It tastes like lightning and honey. I understand why mortals write songs.”

Then came the Coke.

Silena handed him a red can, already open, condensation beading on the sides. “Soda. Carbonated. Sip it slow, it fizzes — ”

Too late. Triton took a huge gulp, swallowed, and froze. His eyes bulged. A strange high-pitched sound escaped his nose. Then he let out a belch so powerful it rattled the plates and sent two harpies circling hopefully overhead.

Silence. Then the entire pavilion exploded with laughter.

Triton, heir of Atlantis, turned the color of a boiled shrimp. “What sorcery was in that can?” he wheezed.

Percy, from the Poseidon table, was crying laughing. “Bubbles! It’s just bubbles, man!”

“It attacked me from the inside!”

Silena, laughing so hard her mascara ran, handed him a napkin and patted his back. “Breathe through it, prince. Everyone’s first soda does that. You’re officially one of us now.”

And Triton — who had faced down Oceanus’s vanguard without flinching, who had once wrestled a telchine for insulting his mother — sat at a table full of giggling Cabin 3 kids, with ketchup on his gauntlet and powdered sugar from a dessert tray on his nose that Silena insisted on wiping off with a napkin while lecturing him about exfoliation, and felt something warm and unfamiliar settle behind his ribs.

“Your cabin is kind,” he told Silena quietly, after the laughter faded.

She smiled, softer this time, less performance. “We try. Camp can be scary when you’re new. Even princes get nervous. Stick with me this afternoon and I’ll show you the good strawberry fields and where to get extra dessert without Mr. D noticing.”

“I would like that,” Triton admitted. “Though I still do not understand why your hair changes color in the sunlight.”

“It’s called highlights, sweetie. I’ll explain later.”

From across the pavilion, Clarisse caught his eye and mimed a sword thrust. He grinned and mimed a shield block back, nearly knocking over his Coke. Silena caught the can before it spilled.

“Careful! Soda is sticky. You do *not* want to learn what a soda stain does to shell armor.”

“Is that a challenge from the sea cabin?”

“That’s a warning from someone who’s had to clean grape juice out of white chitons three times.”

---

Thalia Grace found Triton on the climbing wall at three o’clock, scowling up at the lava.

“It’s fake lava,” she said, falling into step beside him. She had her bow slung across her back, her punk-rock aura dialed up to eleven, and a new rip in her jeans from that morning’s capture-the-flag planning session. “Well. Fake-ish. It still burns.”

“I know what lava is, daughter of the sky,” Triton said. He was still in his armor, arms crossed, watching a pair of Hephaestus kids rappel down screaming. “We have vents in the deep trenches. Hotter than this. More honest.”

Thalia snorted. “Everything’s a competition with you sea guys, huh?”

“Observation is not competition.”

“Sure.” She offered him a fist bump. He stared at it. She grabbed his fist and bumped it herself. “That’s a surface greeting. Means respect.”

“Ah. Then respect to you as well.” He hesitated. “You are… Zeus’s daughter. The elder one.”

“Yeah. Thalia. Annabeth’s big sister. I also answer to the one with the spear.” There was no bitterness in it, just teasing. “You got a problem with sky kids?”

“I have a problem with your father,” Triton said bluntly. “He throws lightning at my father’s waters and calls it weather. He chained Prometheus to a rock. He is… difficult.”

Thalia laughed — a real, startled bark. “Oh, I *like* you. Yeah, Zeus is difficult. He’s also my dad, so I’m allowed to say that and you’re… tolerated for saying it because you’re Percy’s brother and you just got humbled by Clarisse.”

“I was not humbled. It was a draw.”

“You tripped over your shoe.”

“The ground betrayed me.”

They stood in companionable silence for a minute, watching the wall shift and grind. The climbing wall groaned, boulders sliding, lava sluicing down the channels in bright orange ribbons.

“You want to try it?” Thalia asked.

Triton eyed the wall. “On land, with gravity as my enemy and fire as punishment for falling? No. I will watch. Perhaps tomorrow.”

“Fair. I hate that wall too.” She sat down on a rock, patted the spot next to her. To her surprise, he sat — heavily, armor clanking. Up close, he looked younger. Tired around the eyes. The strain of staying polite in a strange place was starting to show.

“Can I ask you something?” Thalia said. “No politics. Warrior to warrior.”

“You may.”

“Percy says you’re the real deal. Like, actual battles. Not just training. What’s it like down there right now? Honestly.”

Triton was quiet for a long moment. The bravado slipped. “Cold,” he said finally. “Even for us. Old things are waking in the trenches. My father smiles less. My mother counts the guard rotations herself now, which she has not done since the last war. Percy… Percy is safer here, on land, than he would be with us. That is why Father allows it. Do not tell Percy I said that. He thinks Father keeps him here because the air is good for his lungs.”

Thalia nodded slowly. She understood that kind of parental lie. She’d lived it.

“He’s a good kid,” she said. “Brave. Loyal to a fault. Annoying kid when he wants to be, but he shows up when it counts.”

“He is small,” Triton said fondly. “When he was seven he tried to ride a dolphin backwards and fell off and cried and then punched a shark that laughed at him.”

Thalia wheezed. “He punched a shark? I need that story for blackmail.”

“I will trade it for stories of his surface failures. I am told there was an incident with a toilet and a fire hose?”

“Oh, you have *no* idea. Sit down, Fish Prince. Let Thalia educate you.”

They sat on that rock for an hour, trading Percy stories while the climbing wall rumbled and campers screamed and the sun slid lower. By the end, Triton was laughing so hard his shell pauldrons shook, and Thalia had a new ally who understood what it meant to carry a big name and a bigger father and still try to be your own person.

When they stood to go, Triton offered his arm in the Atlantean fashion — elbow out, formal. Thalia looked at it, then slung her arm around his shoulders instead, half-hug, half-headlock.

“We do it like this up here,” she said.

“Barbaric,” Triton said, but he didn’t pull away. “I approve.”

Annabeth had been waiting all day for this.

Not the sparring. Not the lunch chaos, though she’d taken detailed notes on Triton’s reactions for future leverage (weakness: carbonation; strength: surprisingly good table manners when he tried). She’d been waiting for a quiet moment to get Percy’s brother alone and ask the question that had been buzzing behind her eyes since the dock.

*What do you want with Percy?*

Because in her experience, gods and godly relatives never just *visited*. They wanted something. A favor. A message. A test. Especially sons of Poseidon showing up to check on the younger brother who was living on land instead of taking his place in Atlantis.

She found her chance at sunset, in the arena stands. Everyone else had gone to dinner or the campfire setup. Triton was still there, sitting alone on the top bench, turning a practice sword over in his hands and frowning at it like it had personally disappointed him. He’d changed out of his armor into borrowed camp clothes — an orange CAMP HALF-BLOOD T-shirt that stretched tight across his shoulders and swim shorts Percy had lent him — and without the shell and bronze he looked… normal. Young. A college kid, almost. Except for the trident leaning against the bench beside him, humming faintly with power.

“Mind if I sit?” Annabeth asked.

Triton glanced up, then nodded warily. “Daughter of Zeus.”

“Annabeth. Just Annabeth.” She sat a careful distance away, notebook closed in her lap. No weapons visible, though both of them knew that meant nothing. “Good sparring today. Clarisse doesn’t respect many people.”

“She is a worthy opponent. Her shield work is excellent. Her temper is… also excellent.”

“That’s Clarisse.” Annabeth tucked a strand of hair behind her ear. The wind off the Sound was picking up, carrying salt. “So. Why are you here, Triton?”

He raised an eyebrow. “To visit my brother.”

“Try again.”

“I am visiting my brother.”

“Triton.” Annabeth turned to face him fully, gray eyes steady. “I’m twelve, not stupid. Atlantis doesn’t send its heir to the surface for a social call during war councils. My dad doesn’t let Thalia and me have lunch together without three omens and a prophecy. So what does Poseidon want? Is Percy in trouble? Are you here to take him back?”

Triton studied her. Really studied her, the way he’d studied Clarisse’s stance — looking for openings, for tells.

“You are direct,” he said at last.

“I’m a strategist. I don’t like unknown pieces on the board.”

“Percy said you would interrogate me. He said, and I quote, ‘Annabeth’s gonna give you the third degree, just answer honestly and don’t be a jerk about the Zeus stuff.’”

Annabeth felt her ears go pink, but she didn’t look away. “Percy talks too much.”

“He talks exactly enough. He speaks of you constantly in his letters. *Annabeth figured out the trap. Annabeth knew the myth. Annabeth hit a hellhound with a rock from fifty paces.*” Triton’s mouth twitched. “I was beginning to think you were a myth yourself.”

“He exaggerates,” Annabeth said quickly. “I just read a lot.”

“You are his partner,” Triton said. It wasn’t a question. “His closest companion in this camp. His…” He searched for the word. “His anchor to the surface.”

Annabeth swallowed. “My boyfriend. And my best friend.”

“I know.” Triton set the practice sword down. Turned to face her. And when he spoke next, the princely arrogance was gone. He just sounded like an older brother. “I am here because my father asked me to see with my own eyes that Perseus is well. Not in a dream. Not in a report from a naiad. With my own eyes. The deep seas grow restless. There are battles coming that will make the last war look like a border skirmish. Father… Father fears he may need to call Percy home sooner than planned. To train him in Atlantean warfare. To keep him safe behind the reef walls.”

Annabeth’s stomach dropped. “Take him away from camp?”

“To keep him alive.” Triton’s voice was gentle but firm. “You understand duty, daughter of Zeus. You know what it is to be a child of the great powers. The sea claims its own.”

“The sea doesn’t get to claim him without asking him,” Annabeth snapped, louder than she meant to. She took a breath. Lowered her voice. “He’s happy here. He has friends. He has… He has a life. You can’t just draft him because he’s useful.”

“I agree.”

She blinked. “What?”

“I agree with you.” Triton smiled, a little sad. “I told my father the same. I said: let the boy choose. He is twelve. Let him have summers of cheeseburgers and sparring and… what was the red drink that assaulted me?”

“Coke.”

“Let him have Coke and friendship before we ask him to carry a trident into the trenches.” He leaned forward, elbows on knees. “That is why I came. To tell Father the truth: that Perseus is strong *because* he is here. Because of this camp. Because of sisters like Clarisse who humble him and Silena who feeds him. Because of sky daughters like Thalia who teach him irreverence. And because of you.”

Annabeth stared. “Me?”

“You keep his mind sharp. You challenge him. You do not bow to him because he is a prince, and you do not fear him because he is powerful. You throw books at him.”

“I threw a book at him *once*,” Annabeth protested. “It was a field guide. He deserved it.”

Triton laughed. “See? This is what I will report. That my little brother has found his pod. His crew. That to remove him now would weaken him, not strengthen him.” He paused. “And I will also report that the daughter of Zeus who guards him is terrifying and excellent and I am glad she is on his side and not mine enemy.”

Annabeth felt her face go hot all the way to her ears. She looked down at her notebook, suddenly very interested in the blank page.

“I’m not terrifying,” she mumbled.

“You counted my knives this morning,” Triton pointed out. “I counted yours. You have at least six.”

“Seven.”

“See? Terrifying.”

A beat. Then, despite herself, Annabeth laughed. And Triton laughed too, and the tension that had crackled between them since the dock — sea versus sky, old grudges in young blood — eased into something else. Not friendship, not yet. But respect. A truce.

“Don’t hurt him,” Annabeth said quietly. “I mean it. He acts like everything bounces off him, but it doesn’t. He worries he’s not good enough for Atlantis *or* for camp. He tries so hard.”

Triton’s expression softened. “I know. I tease him because he is my little brother and it is my sacred duty. But I am proud of him. More than he knows.”

“Then tell him,” Annabeth said, standing and brushing off her shorts. “Surface custom. We say stuff out loud here.”

She left him on the bench with that thought, notebook tucked under her arm, heart beating a little faster than she’d admit. Behind her, she heard Triton pick up the practice sword again, testing its balance with new interest.

“Seven knives,” he murmured to himself, admiring. “Excellent.”

Dinner was a disaster in the best way.

Triton, emboldened by lunch, insisted on sitting at the Poseidon table with Percy and burning his own offering. Percy tried to explain: you scrape a little of the best part into the fire and say a prayer. Simple.

Triton stood, held up an entire pork chop over the flames, and declaimed in ringing Atlantean Greek: “O FATHER POSEIDON, EARTHSHAKER, LORD OF HORSES, ACCEPT THIS BURNT PIG AND BLESS MY VISIT TO THESE STRANGE DRY PEOPLE!”

The fire flared green and hissed. Everyone stared.

“Dude,” Percy hissed, yanking him down. “You don’t shout. You whisper. And you don’t call us dry people.”

“But you *are* dry people. You are parched. You drink water from plastic and bathe in tiny boxes.”

“Showers. They’re called showers. And you used mine for forty minutes and used all the hot water.”

“It was magnificent. The water fell from the sky *inside the house*. Your mortal plumbing is sorcery.”

Annabeth, passing with her plate, smirked. “Wait till he finds out about water slides.”

Triton’s head snapped up. “Water… slides?”

“No,” Percy said quickly. “No water slides. You have to leave tomorrow. No time. Absolutely no time for water slides.”

“Tomorrow is plenty of time for water slides,” Thalia called from the next table.

After dinner came the campfire. This was sacred. The Apollo cabin led songs, the fire crackled gold, marshmallows were roasted and inevitably burned, and new campers were forced to do introductions. Triton, as honored guest, was forced to the front.

“Sing!” the Stoll brothers chanted. “Sing! Sing! Sing!”

“I do not sing surface songs,” Triton protested. “I sing whale laments and battle hymns in Old Atlantean that cause nosebleeds in mortals.”

“Perfect!” Connor Stoll shoved a marshmallow stick into his hand. “Do the nosebleed one!”

Triton looked at Percy, pleading. Percy grinned and held up his own marshmallow, already on fire.

“Welcome to camp, big brother.”

So Triton sang. Or rather, he *tried* to sing “Down by the Aegean” with the Apollo cabin, got the words wrong in spectacular fashion, substituted what Percy suspected were Atlantean naval commands for the chorus, and ended up with the entire camp shouting “LA LA LA, BRING THE TIDE IN!” while he waved his marshmallow stick like a conductor’s baton and set three marshmallows on fire at once.

Silena cried laughing. Clarisse actually fell off her log. Even Mr. D cracked a smile before remembering he was supposed to be miserable and hid behind his Diet Coke.

Then it was Percy’s turn to get roasted.

“Oh, little barnacle,” Triton said, rising to his full height by the fire, marshmallow goo on his chin. “Shall I tell them about the dolphin incident?”

“No,” Percy said immediately.

“Shall I tell them how you cried when a sea cucumber touched your foot and you thought it was a monster?”

“You said I was *seven*.”

“Shall I tell them about the time you asked Father if fish sleep with their eyes open because you wanted to know if *you* had to sleep with your eyes open to be a good son of Poseidon?”

The campfire *howled*. Annabeth was taking notes. Thalia was taking video on a Mist-concealed camera that definitely wasn’t allowed. Clarisse shouted, “BARNACLE! BARNACLE! BARNACLE!” until the whole camp took it up.

Percy buried his face in his hands, ears burning. “I hate you.”

“You love me,” Triton said, sitting down and slinging a heavy arm around Percy’s shoulders, pulling him in. “I am your favorite brother.”

“You’re my *only* brother here. Tyson’s my favorite.”

“Then I am favorite among the handsome ones.”

“Tyson still counts. Also, you set your own hair on fire with a marshmallow.”

“It was a strategic sacrifice.”

They bickered like that until the fire burned low and Chiron called lights-out and the cabins shuffled off in sleepy lines. Percy and Triton walked back to Cabin 3 together under a sky so thick with stars it looked spilled.

For a while, neither spoke. The crickets — spies, according to Triton — sang. The fountain in Cabin 3 gurgled. Somewhere in the woods, a hellhound barked in its sleep.

“You did good today,” Percy said quietly. “With Clarisse. With Silena. With Thalia. With Annabeth.”

Triton glanced sideways. “You were watching?”

“I always watch. It’s how I learn.” Percy kicked a pebble. “Did you mean what you said? About me being strong because I’m here?”

Triton stopped walking. Annabeth must have told him — that loudmouth, wonderful, terrifying daughter of Zeus must have repeated his words by the fire when he wasn’t listening. He felt a rush of affection for both of them so strong it hurt.

“Every word,” Triton said. He ruffled Percy’s hair, gentler this time. No headlock. Just brotherly. “You are not Atlantean, Perseus. Not entirely. And you are not fully surface, either. You are both. That is not weakness. That is… What is the surface word? Amphibious?”

“Frog-like?”

“Do not ruin the moment.”

Percy laughed, then yawned so wide his jaw cracked. “Are you really leaving tomorrow?”

“At dawn. The tide calls. My patrol — ” Triton hesitated. The war council. The trench reports. The cold places waking. He wouldn’t burden a twelve-year-old with that tonight. “My paperwork calls. Even princes have paperwork.”

“Lame.”

“Agreed.”

They reached Cabin 3. Percy expected Triton to go inside, claim the guest bunk, collapse. Instead, Triton tilted his head toward the Sound, where moonlight laid a silver road across the black water.

“One swim,” he said. “You and me. No armor. No training. Just brothers. The water has missed you. I can tell. You smell too dry.”

Percy didn’t need to be asked twice.

---

The night swim was perfect.

They left their shoes on the sand — Triton stared at Percy’s sneakers like they were artifacts, then lined his borrowed sandals up neatly beside them — and waded in past the breakers until the camp lights were just a warm smudge behind them.

Then they dove.

Underwater at night, with two sons of Poseidon in it, the Sound came alive. Percy felt it the second his head went under: the water recognizing him, wrapping around him like a blanket, healing the bruises from capture-the-flag, clearing the smoke from the campfire out of his lungs. He could breathe. He could *see* — every plankton glowing, every fish asleep in the eelgrass, the naiads peeking from behind rocks to wave at the prince and his little brother.

Triton transformed. On land he’d been funny, clumsy, out of place, tripping over sandals and getting defeated by soda. In the water he was *Triton*. His legs kicked once and he shot forward like a torpedo, hair streaming, a thin membrane flickering between his fingers that Percy had never noticed on land, faint bioluminescent markings lighting along his arms — old Atlantean battle tattoos that only showed in salt water.

*Show-off,* Percy signed in diver’s cant, the hand language Tyson had practiced with him on recent visits.

*Baby barnacle,* Triton signed back, then grabbed Percy’s ankle and towed him twenty feet deeper in half a second until Percy shrieked bubbles and flipped him off, which underwater just made Triton laugh so hard his nose bubbled.

They raced. They wrestled — real wrestling this time, no holding back, spinning in three dimensions, using currents as leverage, until Percy finally pinned Triton by cheating and summoning a jellyfish to drift into his brother’s face. They lay on the sandy bottom at fifteen feet, side by side, watching the moon wobble through the surface, passing a breath of air back and forth just for fun because they didn’t need it but it felt brotherly to share.

*You swim well for a surface boy,* Triton signed. His face was serious now, the teasing gone.

*You walk okay for a fish guy,* Percy signed back. *For someone who’s scared of grasshoppers.*

*I am not scared—* Triton lunged. Percy jetted backward, laughing soundlessly, kicking up sand.

They surfaced together under the moon, treading water, hair slicked back, grinning. The camp was silent. The stars were out. A shooting star — probably an Apollo kid messing around, or maybe just a star — streaked over the hill.

“Thank you for coming,” Percy said quietly, bobbing in the swell. “Even if Dad sent you to spy.”

“I was not spying,” Triton said. “I was… confirming. That you are well. That you are loved. That your pod is strong.” He reached over and squeezed Percy’s shoulder. His hand was warm, even in cold water. “Father will hear that you are thriving. And that if he tries to pull you from this place before you are ready, he will have to answer to Clarisse with a spear, Silena with guilt, Thalia with lightning, and a twelve-year-old daughter of Zeus with seven knives.”

Percy laughed, then sniffed. The salt water stung his eyes. Or something did.

“I’ll come visit Atlantis soon,” he promised. “When… when things calm down. I want to see Mom — Amphitrite. And the whale. Is the whale still in my nursery?”

“The whale is enormous now and still sleeps in your old crib and refuses to move. We have built around him.”

“Tell him I said hi.”

“I will.”

They floated a while longer, not talking. Brothers don’t always need to talk. The tide rocked them. A seal surfaced nearby, looked at them, decided they were gods and therefore not worth barking at, and sank again.

Finally Triton sighed. “We should go in. You need sleep. You have training tomorrow. And I must… practice walking up hills without disgrace.”

“One more race,” Percy challenged. “To the shore. Loser has to drink another Coke.”

“You’re on, barnacle.”

They shot toward the beach side by side — two streaks of glowing water, two sons of the sea, one remembering the palace they had both grown up in, one carrying an erased apartment childhood too, meeting in the middle in moonlit water off Long Island — and if you’d been watching from the dunes, you’d have sworn the sea itself lifted to push them both home.

Percy won by a nose. Or Triton let him win. No one will ever know, and both will lie about it forever.

On the sand, dripping and shivering and laughing, Triton pulled Percy into a rough, one-armed hug and held on a second longer than brotherly teasing required.

“Stay brave, little brother,” he murmured, just loud enough for the waves to hear. “Stay kind. Stay with your pod. Atlantis can wait.”

Then he let go, picked up his terrible borrowed sandals, shook the sand out of Percy’s sneakers like a valet, and walked with him up the beach toward the warm lights of Cabin 3 — a prince of the deep, far from home, with soda in his past and paperwork in his future, whistling very badly the surface song about bringing the tide in.

Behind them, the Sound settled. Flat and glassy no more. Just water, lapping softly, keeping their secret.
