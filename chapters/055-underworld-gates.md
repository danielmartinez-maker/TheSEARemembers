## Chapter 55: Underworld Gates

Percy knew they had reached Los Angeles because the air stopped smelling like anything real.

It was late in the day and the light was white and flat. The highway stretched out in six lanes in each direction and every lane was full. Cars sat bumper to bumper under a pale haze. Palm trees lined the frontage road, dusty and tired. Billboards rose over the traffic, advertising movies and lawyers and a water park that promised cool fun. The heat came up off the asphalt in waves and made the whole city shimmer.

Martin drove with both hands on the wheel. Her black hair was pulled back in a ponytail and sweat stuck the short pieces to her neck. Her silver circlet was pushed up on her head like a headband. She kept checking the mirrors and muttering under her breath.

"If one more guy cuts us off, I am going to ask Chiron why he thought the roads were safer than the monsters," Thalia said from the front passenger seat.

"Please do not," Annabeth said from the passenger seat. She had the map folded open on her knees and a tourist guide to Los Angeles tucked under it. She looked pale and tired, but her eyes were sharp. "We are close. We do not need a police chase on top of the Underworld."

Grover sat in the back next to Percy with his backpack on his lap. His horns were hidden under a green beanie that was soaked with sweat. His sneakers were unlaced because his fake feet hurt. He kept lifting his nose and sniffing, then making a face.

"This city smells wrong," he said. "Like hot metal and old flowers and dry dirt. I cannot smell any happy grass. I cannot smell any goats at all."

"There are goats in Los Angeles somewhere," Thalia said. "They just have the sense to stay out of this traffic."

"There should be goats everywhere," Grover said. "That is the problem."

Percy sat with his forehead against the window. His t shirt stuck to his back. Riptide was in his pocket in pen form, heavy and warm. The backpack at his feet held a copy of the courier records and their notes about the missing bolt, wrapped in an old denim jacket. The weapon itself was still missing. His mother Sally had died when he was two in this history. Hades ruled the place where her spirit had gone. Every mile closer to LA had made that feel more real and more heavy.

He thought about the last phone call and the cold voice on the other line. He thought about his mom making blue pancakes in their small kitchen and laughing when he burned the edges. He pushed the window down an inch and let the hot air hit his face.

"Exit in half a mile," Annabeth said. She pointed at a green sign. "DOA Recording Studios should be off Santa Monica. The driver in Barstow said it is big and black and hard to miss. He said the bands that go in never come out the same."

Thalia snorted. "Comforting."

"He was serious," Annabeth said. "He said his cousin dropped off drums there once. He said the lobby was freezing cold even though it was ninety outside. He said the receptionist did not blink."

Grover shivered. "I do not like that."

"Nobody likes that," Thalia said. "That is how we know it is ours."

The exit ramp curved down into a street lined with low shops and tall fences. A taco truck sat on the corner with music playing. A man washed a sidewalk with a hose and the water ran brown into the gutter. The sun beat down hard enough to make Percy squint.

Annabeth directed Martin turn by turn. Left on La Brea. Right on a smaller street. Past a car wash. Past a building covered in a mural of a singer with huge eyes.

"There," Annabeth said quietly.

Percy sat up.

The drive after the Lotus had not been silent, exactly. Martin kept checking the temperature gauge and asking for directions; Grover kept reminding himself that real roadside grass was ugly but alive. What Percy remembered most was the way the hotel had stayed in the corner of his mind after it disappeared from the mirrors.

At a safe service stop west of the city, they delivered Eva to a trusted nymph contact. The former hotel employee asked Annabeth for a copy of the notes she'd taken just outside the Lotus.

"I don't know whether my family thinks I'm missing," Eva said.

Annabeth had held the pages carefully while the contact checked that the dates matched the receipts Martin had kept. "These are your words. We don't know everything yet. That doesn't mean you have to guess."

Eva looked toward the van. "You aren't coming back with me?"

"We have to keep going," Percy answered.

She nodded without arguing, though he could tell she'd wanted another answer. The nymph contact gave her a place to rest, drinking water that didn't smell like flowers, and an ordinary telephone to use once they found a number she trusted.

Martin had to sign the travel handoff. He read it aloud before putting pen to paper. Nobody made fun of him for doing that anymore.

By the time they reached the Los Angeles traffic, the empty seat where Eva had been felt like proof of two things: they had helped somebody, and they hadn't helped everybody. Percy kept seeing the boy in the sports jersey looking back at his game. He didn't know if the boy had made it to the doors.

Near a freeway off-ramp, Martin pulled into a well-lit service plaza and showed the questers a list of numbers.

"I've told the depot where the van is and where I will be waiting," he said. "I am not taking a vehicle into the Underworld. I'm not following a living child through doors marked for the dead. Your camp authorized the drive, not that."

Thalia folded her arms. "We weren't going to ask you."

"Good. Then we agree."

Percy had come to like the way Martin set limits. Gods usually turned a boundary into an insult; Martin treated it as part of doing a job. He was tired, and his tiredness mattered. The trip had already asked him to drive through cities where the roads changed and repair arrangements dissolved into magic. He shouldn't also have to pretend he could fight Cerberus.

"What happens if we don't come back?" Annabeth asked.

Martin met her eyes. "At the time we agree on, I'll call Chiron. I won't drive off without telling anybody. If your situation changes and you can communicate safely, use the depot contact, not a public number. I can't promise I can go in after you."

Annabeth wrote all of it down.

Grover looked at the list. "Can you tell Juniper I'm alive if the connection from down there doesn't work?"

"I can give Chiron the message you wrote."

"Thanks."

Martin looked at Percy last. "And you. Your sisters have been sending questions through the depot about whether you're eating properly. One asked whether you were wearing the bracelet she made. The other asked if I'd been foolish enough to let you take charge of transportation."

Percy groaned. "That sounds like them."

"I told them you were alive when I last checked and that I remained the driver. I couldn't promise the rest."

Percy put his thumb against the blue-green cord on his wrist. The thread was worn from the road but still tied. He'd wanted to send Clarisse and Silena a confident report after each fight. Now he was grateful someone had told them a smaller, more accurate truth.

They crossed the remaining streets with Martin driving and Annabeth navigating. When the DOA letters first came into view, Percy thought of the family they'd left safe in another person's care and the people waiting for them to make it home. Going below wasn't the same as disappearing. But it could become that unless somebody above knew where they had gone.


On the left side of the street stood a long black building with no windows. It took up half the block. The walls were smooth dark stone that drank the light. Gold letters over the wide front doors read DOA RECORDING STUDIOS. Under that in smaller letters: NO SOLICITORS. NO WALK INS. NO EXCEPTIONS.

A line of black limos waited at the curb, engines running. Nobody got in or out. A pale man in a dark suit stood by the door holding a clipboard. He did not move in the heat. He did not sweat.

"It is cold there," Grover whispered. "Even from here I can feel it. It is like winter leaking out under the door."

Martin pulled the van into a legal parking space across the street beneath a dead jacaranda tree and cut the engine. For a moment nobody spoke. The traffic noise felt far away.

"This is it," Annabeth said. "This is the entrance. In the old stories the Underworld has many doors. This is the one for Los Angeles. The dead come here to be processed."

Percy stared at the gold letters. DOA. Dead on arrival. He should have laughed, but his stomach was too tight.

"So we just walk in?" he asked.

"We walk in like we belong," Annabeth said. "We have the pearls. We have the address from our leads west. We tell them we are dead if we have to."

Grover hugged his backpack. "I am not good at being dead. I am too warm. The dead are cold."

"You will be fine," Thalia said, but her voice was softer than usual. She looked at Grover in the mirror. "You have done scarier than this."

Grover did not look convinced.

---

Thalia made them wait in the parked van and watch first, while Martin called the nearby safe contact to confirm where he would stay.

They watched for twenty minutes. The pale man with the clipboard did not move except to check a name and wave a limo forward. A family got out of a taxi across the street, a mother and two small kids carrying suitcases, and walked up to the glass doors. The doors opened by themselves. Cold fog rolled out onto the sidewalk. The family walked in and did not come out.

A delivery driver tried to bring in a stack of pizzas. The pale man shook his head once without looking up. The driver left the pizzas on the curb and drove away fast.

"Okay," Thalia said at last. She turned around in her seat. Her blue eyes were bright. "The four of us go in together. Martin stays above with the van and our route papers. No splitting up. Percy, you have Riptide. Annabeth, you have the cap and the pearls. Grover, you have your pipes and your courage. Me, I have my shield and spear and an excellent talent for making drivers nervous."

"And your modesty," Annabeth said.

"And my modesty," Thalia agreed. She held out her fist. "Quest team?"

Percy bumped it. Annabeth bumped it. Grover bumped it with both hands.

"Quest team," they said together.

They got out into the heat. The asphalt burned through Percy's shoes. The air smelled like exhaust and hot trash and someone's taco lunch. Across the street, the black building seemed to pull the heat away. The closer they got, the colder it felt.

Percy felt Riptide pulse in his pocket like it knew where they were.

The pale man looked up as they crossed. His skin was the color of skim milk. His eyes were completely brown, no white at all. His suit was perfect. His clipboard was made of bone.

"Names?" he asked. His voice was flat and bored.

Percy opened his mouth, but Annabeth stepped forward.

"We have an appointment," she said calmly. "With Mr. Hades."

The man stared at her. "Everyone has an appointment with Mr. Hades. Do you have a reference number? Are you newly deceased?"

"We are not exactly deceased," Thalia said.

The man sighed like he had heard this a thousand times. "Not exactly deceased. Living visitors. Great. Do you have a pass? A token? A bribe?"

"We have business," Percy said. His voice shook a little and he hated it. "My mother died here when I was two. Sally Jackson. I need to see him, and ask after her."

At the name Sally Jackson, something flickered in the man's brown eyes. He made a mark on his bone clipboard.

"Sally Jackson. Records below. Yes. And you are Perseus Jackson. Son of Poseidon."

Percy felt cold all the way down his spine. "Yes."

The man looked at Annabeth. "Annabeth Grace. Daughter of Zeus." He looked at Thalia. "Thalia Grace. Daughter of Zeus." He looked at Grover. His lip curled. "And a satyr. Unscheduled."

Grover stood up straighter. "Grover Underwood. Searcher. Second class."

"Second class," the man repeated. "Not even first class. Satyrs. Always wandering in where you are not wanted."

Percy saw Grover's hands tighten on his backpack straps. Grover's ears twitched under his beanie. In the back of the van, Grover had told Percy once that searchers were laughed at by the Council. They were given sticks and pipes and told to find a god who had been lost for two thousand years. Most never came home. Grover had been searching for Pan for three years and had nothing to show but blisters and notes.

But Grover lifted his chin.

"I have a searcher's license," he said. His voice was thin but clear. "Issued by the Council of Cloven Elders. And I am on a quest sanctioned by Olympus. You cannot turn away a sanctioned quest without cause."

The man blinked. For the first time he actually looked at Grover.

"You memorized the rule," he said.

"I memorized all the rules," Grover said. "Rule forty two. Any creature bearing a searcher's license and traveling with demigods on Olympus business must be granted passage through neutral doors. DOA Recording Studios is a neutral door."

Annabeth stared at him. Thalia raised her eyebrows. Percy felt a rush of pride so strong it hurt.

The pale man tapped his clipboard. "You are quoting Council law at me."

"Yes," Grover said. His knees were shaking. Percy could see it. But he did not step back. "And I am not leaving my friends. If they go down, I go down."

The cold fog curled around their ankles. The man studied Grover for a long moment. Then he made another mark.

"Fine," he said. "Searcher Second Class Grover Underwood. Noted for bravery. That will go in the record."

Grover let out a breath. "It will?"

"It will. We record everything." The man stepped aside and the glass doors swung open. Freezing air poured out. Behind the doors was a lobby of black marble and low gold lights. "Enter. Charon will process you."

Thalia clapped Grover on the shoulder as they walked past. "Noted for bravery," she whispered. "Did you hear that?"

Grover nodded, eyes wide. "I am going to throw up," he whispered back.

"You can throw up in the Underworld," Thalia said. "Just warn me first so I can move."

---

The person with the bone clipboard looked very disappointed when Grover cited the rule about neutral doors.

"It's a gate," the man said. "Not an invitation."

"A neutral door," Grover insisted. "The council's rules say a searcher may petition for passage while accompanying a sanctioned quest."

"And what does 'petition' mean, second-class searcher?"

Grover blinked. "You listen to my request?"

"It means you may ask. It does not mean I have to grant access to every living creature with an interesting story and a backpack full of cans."

Percy saw Grover's ears droop. He had memorized the rule, and it mattered that the rule didn't solve their problem immediately. The attendant had found the difference between requesting permission and receiving it. Chiron would have approved of the distinction, which made Percy want to argue more.

Annabeth stepped forward, standing close enough to Grover that the man couldn't dismiss him without acknowledging her as well.

"Then we'd like the grounds for refusing the petition," she said.

The man's eyes narrowed. "The grounds?"

"If the rule has conditions, tell us which one we haven't met. We're a sanctioned party. We're asking to reach the court without being processed as dead. We can identify ourselves. If you're saying the petition fails, record why."

Thalia glanced at Annabeth, impressed despite herself. Percy could almost see the older sister thinking of how useful her little sister would be in an argument with a god who relied on people being too frightened to ask for a reason.

The man inspected Grover's searcher's license. It was creased from being carried in his pocket and damp at one corner. He held it by the edge as though a satyr's paperwork might be contagious.

"License current," he said. "But you have four living travelers and only three named in the Oracle's party."

"That's true," Thalia answered.

Percy expected her to go on the offensive. Instead she pulled Chiron's written guardian authorization from her belt pouch and set it beside Grover's license.

"I'm not claiming to be named by the Oracle. My sister's twelve. I have authorization to accompany her as a guardian. That's what Chiron signed."

The attendant took the paper and read the signature twice. He looked up.

"An authorization from a centaur is not an order from Hades."

"No," Annabeth said. "We aren't asking you to treat it as one."

For a moment all four of them stood in the heat, watching the man decide whether the paperwork was worth the trouble. Percy thought about reaching for Riptide. He could imagine slashing through the glass doors and charging inside. He could also imagine how badly that would end when the entire Underworld belonged to the god they wanted to question.

The attendant finally made a mark on the bone clipboard.

"Conditional entry," he said. "You must register your living status with Charon. You are not permitted into the records rooms without separate authorization, and you are not permitted to take souls from the Fields. If you attempt either, the door closes to you."

Percy's stomach dropped at the last condition.

"Even if it's my mother?"

The attendant's brown eyes moved toward him. "Especially if it's your mother. Every living visitor thinks their grief makes them an exception."

Percy felt himself go rigid. He could hear Clarisse in his head telling him to make the man regret speaking like that. He could hear Silena telling him to listen carefully because he might be able to get more information if he didn't waste the chance on anger.

"Then where do I ask about a soul's welfare?" he said.

"Records petitions go through Charon. If you have a court audience, the king may answer or decline. That is his prerogative."

It wasn't enough. It was also more than Percy had known five minutes earlier.

Grover accepted the license back and thanked the attendant, although his voice shook. He didn't thank him for being unkind. He thanked him for stating the condition. Percy noticed the difference.

When the glass doors opened, the cold air felt less like relief than an invitation he might regret accepting. He could cross the threshold as a living guest. Whether he would leave as one was not something the paperwork promised.

---

The lobby of DOA Recording Studios was bigger inside than outside.

Percy had expected a small waiting room with chairs. Instead the ceiling rose three stories high. The floor was polished black marble that reflected their faces back at them, pale and stretched. Gold records hung on the walls in frames, each one labeled with a name and a year. Percy read one as they passed: BUDDY HOLLY. 1959. Another: JANIS JOPLIN. 1970. Another with no name, only a date from last week.

Elevator music played softly from hidden speakers. It sounded like water going down a drain.

Behind a long black desk sat a man in a tailored brown suit. His skin was pale. His eyes were brown like the man outside. A nameplate on the desk read CHARON. He counted coins and stacked them in neat towers. His fingers were long and quick.

The family with suitcases stood at the counter. The mother was arguing quietly.

"But we paid for first class," she said. "We paid extra for the express elevator."

Charon did not look up. "Express elevator is out of service. You get the regular elevator. It stops at every floor."

"How many floors?" the mother asked.

"All of them," Charon said.

The mother opened her mouth, then closed it. Charon held out his hand. She dropped coins into it, one for each of them. He bit each coin and dropped it in a drawer. Then he pointed to a set of wide elevators at the back.

"Last elevator on the right," he said. "Do not press any buttons. Do not talk to other passengers. If something asks your name, do not answer."

The family walked to the elevator with their suitcases bumping. The doors opened with a soft chime. Inside was dark. They stepped in and were gone.

Percy swallowed. His mouth was dry despite the cold.

Annabeth walked up to the desk. She set four golden drachmas on the black surface. Real Olympus coins. They glowed faintly.

"Four living visitors," she said. "Round trip, please. We need to see Hades about a stolen item and a mortal woman."

Charon stopped counting. He looked at the coins, then at them. His brown eyes lingered on Thalia's circlet and Percy's face.

"Living visitors," he said. "You know the rule. The living pay double. And most do not come back."

"We know," Annabeth said. "We have pearls for the way back."

Charon picked up one drachma and bit it. He nodded slowly. "Grace girl. Always prepared." He looked at Percy. "Poseidon boy. Your father does not pay me extra, you know. He thinks the sea covers his debts."

"My father sends his regards," Percy said, because Annabeth had told him to say that if Charon mentioned Poseidon.

Charon snorted. Fog came out of his nose. "His regards. Fine." He swept the coins into the drawer. He stamped four black wristbands with a skull and slid them across. "Wear these. They mark you as living guests. Do not lose them. If you lose them, you become permanent residents."

Grover fumbled his wristband and almost dropped it. Thalia caught it and slipped it on his wrist for him. Her hands were steady.

"Thanks," Grover whispered.

"Anytime, goat boy," Thalia said.

Charon pointed to the back. "Last elevator on the right. Same rules. Do not press buttons. Do not talk to strangers. And boy..."

He looked at Percy.

"Your mother is in the Fields. She is waiting. Do not make her wait too long."

Percy felt like the floor tilted. "You have seen her? Is she okay?"

Charon had already gone back to counting coins. "Everyone is okay until they are not," he said. "Next."

---

Percy reached for the wristband and stopped before touching it.

"What's the difference between a living guest and a person who's dead?" he asked.

Charon looked up from the coins. "Biology, young man. You'll find it's decisive."

"I mean for the paperwork."

"Then say that."

The ferry-master slid a large register across the counter. Annabeth leaned over it before Percy could read the headings. Columns recorded passage, permitted destination, escort requirements and whether a guest had property awaiting inspection.

"Are these the rules?" she asked.

"Rules and consequences. Some people confuse them."

A row near the bottom was marked *living petitioner*. Beside it was a box labeled *court appointment verified*. It was blank.

"We have permission to enter the building," Annabeth said. "But we don't have an appointment with Hades."

"Correct."

"Then how do we get one?"

"You request an audience. If the court has reason to receive you, an usher will escort you from the hall of records. If the court refuses, you leave by the route prescribed for living guests."

Percy looked toward the bronze elevators. "And if we don't agree with the refusal?"

Charon tapped the wristbands. "Then you learn why I keep saying living guests rarely come back."

Thalia's expression hardened. Percy almost expected her to lean across the counter and threaten the man with her spear. Instead she looked at Annabeth.

"Can we petition before going down?"

"You can request a hearing," Charon answered. "It won't be reviewed until you reach the lower court. The king doesn't conduct business in a lobby full of tourists."

Grover shifted from one hoof to the other. "What about the people in line? The dead? Do they get hearings?"

Charon stared at him. "Everybody wants a hearing. Most simply want to know whether somebody is waiting for them. Some insist there's been a mistake. Some don't remember their own names. We process them as best we can."

Percy looked at the family with the suitcases. They hadn't looked like ghosts at first, which had made it easy to treat them as customers in an unusually grim office. Now he noticed the mother holding the youngest child's hand with too much care, and the way the father kept checking a ticket he seemed afraid to lose.

"Are they all right?" Percy asked.

Charon's eyes flicked toward the elevator. "They are no longer in the mortal world. That is the most precise statement I can make."

Percy disliked him a little less for not pretending the situation could be made pleasant by a cheerful answer.

He placed his own drachma on the counter.

"My mother's name is Sally Jackson. She died when I was two. That's in the records here. I want to know whether she has peace. I'm not asking to take her out or to rewrite the judgment."

He hadn't intended to say all of that. The words came out rough and unprepared. Annabeth didn't interrupt. She knew the older memories he carried of Sally alive, the ones the altered world didn't recognize, but she didn't try to put those into an official petition.

Charon took a clean sheet and asked Percy for the details of the altered life: Sally's name, approximate year of death, the city where she'd lived and Percy's relationship to her. He wrote them down in a clerk's swift hand and stamped the top.

"This authorizes a welfare inquiry, not an audience with her," he said. "If the records office has a response for a living relative, it can be included with your court request."

Percy stared at the paper. "Will I get to know tonight?"

"I didn't say that."

"Okay."

"And the stolen weapon?" Charon asked.

"We have evidence that a buzzing package moved west," Annabeth answered. "We need to ask whether anything matching that description came through this entrance."

Charon considered the question.

"You're asking whether I received a particular object."

"Yes."

"The dead don't normally arrive carrying weapons belonging to Zeus. I would have noticed one rattling the walls."

"Would you tell us if it did?" Thalia asked.

"Not without authority."

Percy wanted to groan. Another answer that left them where they started. Annabeth, though, wrote both parts down. *Charon says not routinely received; cannot establish no passage.*

"You're doing that thing again," Percy murmured.

"The useful thing?"

"The thing where you make every sentence less satisfying but more accurate."

Annabeth gave him a tired look. "You're welcome."

Charon almost smiled. Then he handed out the wristbands and took the fees.

"Keep the welfare petition with your registration," he told Percy. "If you have to use a pearl to escape, take the papers with you. I dislike filling out the same form twice."

Percy held the thin sheet as though it could tear if he breathed too hard. He had not found Sally. He had not found the bolt. But he had a request that somebody in the Underworld would have to read. He could carry it without lying about what it meant.

---

They walked to the elevators together, their shoes loud on the marble.

There were six elevators in a row. Five were steel and bright. The last one on the right was different. Its doors were made of dark bronze, dented and scratched. Above it, instead of floor numbers, there was a dial with names: PENTHOUSE. LOBBY. PARKING. FIELDS. PUNISHMENT. HADES.

The dial needle rested on LOBBY.

Annabeth held the four pearls in her palm. They were white and pink and glowed faintly in the gold light. She had kept them wrapped in a cloth since Santa Monica, when the Nereid had given them to Percy. One for each of them. One way out.

"Check them again," she said to Percy. "Make sure they are real."

Percy touched one. It was warm. "They are real."

Thalia stood a little apart, staring at the bronze doors. Her spear was collapsed into a mace canister on her belt, but her hand rested on it. Her shield was a silver bracelet on her wrist. She looked strong and scared at the same time.

Annabeth saw it too. She stepped over to Thalia and stood shoulder to shoulder.

"Hey," Annabeth said quietly.

"Hey," Thalia said.

They had known each other a long time, Percy knew. Thalia had helped raise Annabeth from a baby in this history; they had traveled with Luke before camp. Thalia had all those memories, while Annabeth had only the stories she was being told. Still, they had a language Percy and Grover did not always understand. A look. A shoulder bump. The way Thalia waited for her hand instead of assuming she remembered the next move.

"This is it," Annabeth said. "Down is real now."

"Yeah," Thalia said. Her voice was rough. "Down is real."

Annabeth held out her hand, palm up, fingers spread. "Sister ritual? For luck? Before we go down?"

Thalia looked at the hand. Something eased in her face. "Want me to show you again?"

"I remember the moves you showed me at camp," Annabeth said. "You told me you made it up when I was scared under the highway bridge. Daughters of thunder do not go dark alone."

Percy and Grover stepped back without being asked. This was not for them.

Thalia set her palm against Annabeth's palm. Their fingers lined up. Then they curled their fingers together and tapped twice, knuckle to knuckle. Then they turned their hands sideways and slid them apart with a crackle.

A real spark jumped between their skin. A tiny blue arc of static, sharp and bright. It smelled like rain.

Percy blinked. "Whoa."

Thalia grinned, just for a second, real and young. "Daughter of Zeus. I carry a little weather with me."

Annabeth grinned back. Her eyes were wet but she was smiling. "Daughter of Zeus. I carry the plan."

"Lightning handshake," Grover whispered to Percy. "They did that before the river too, when we thought the Furies were coming. Annabeth told me. It means, I am scared and you are my sister and we go together."

Percy nodded. His throat felt tight.

Thalia and Annabeth pressed their foreheads together briefly, the way siblings did. Thalia was taller, so she bent down.

"Jason would laugh if he saw me doing this," Thalia murmured, so quiet Percy almost missed it. "My little brother. He always said my rituals were dramatic. He was small, but he was stubborn. He wanted to do everything I did. I used to tell him he had to wait until he was taller than my spear. He tried every day. He would stand against the door frame and mark it with a crayon."

Thalia swallowed hard. Her eyes stayed on Annabeth.

"I lost him when he was two," she said. "My mom was not well. Hera... Hera took him. To protect him, she said. To make him a hero somewhere else. I did not know for a long time if he was alive or dead. Now I know he is at Camp Jupiter, but I still hate not being able to check on him myself. But he is my brother. Blood. Son of Zeus like me. Even if the gods split us up, that does not stop."

Annabeth squeezed Thalia's hand. "You will find him," she said fiercely. "After this quest, we will arrange another visit. I will help. I am good at books and maps and making Chiron answer letters. We will see Jason Grace."

Thalia nodded, blinking fast. "Yeah. And then he can laugh at my handshake in person."

Annabeth smiled a little. "My other brothers would laugh too. Bobby and Matthew, in the life I remember. Blond hair, scraped knees, always fighting over the front seat. Frederick used to write that Matthew read my astronomy books and Bobby built traps for monsters that were really just the mailman. They were mortal. I don't have those letters here. I don't know what their lives look like without me."

"Do you write to them?" Grover asked softly.

"When I can," Annabeth said. "I send postcards I never mail. I keep them in my trunk at camp. I tell them to be good and to study math and not to open strange doors. Being a big sister from far away is strange. You love them so much it hurts, but you cannot protect them up close. So you protect them by keeping the monsters away from everyone."

Thalia bumped Annabeth's shoulder. "You are a good big sister. Even from far away. Even when you're the younger sister learning how to tease the elder one."

"You are not a stray," Annabeth said. "You are my sister. Blood and choice. The gods gave us a distant brother and a lot to be scared of. We still get to choose how we stand together."

Thalia and Annabeth pressed their foreheads together briefly, the way siblings did. Thalia was taller, so she bent down.

"Do you think he'll like visiting the lake again?" Percy asked before he could stop himself.

Thalia pulled back. Her face closed a little, then opened again. "Yeah. Jason liked it last time. I lost so many ordinary days with him when he was little. I don't talk about that part much."

Annabeth touched Thalia's arm. "We can give him the elevator button," she said. "Bobby and Matthew used to fight over those in my old life. It's funny, which little things I miss when I think about them."

"Do they miss you?" Grover asked.

"Sometimes," Annabeth said. "I miss them more than I say. It is weird. Being a big sister from far away."

Percy thought about that. In his old childhood he had been an only child. He had wanted a sibling and been glad he did not have to share his mom's blue pancakes at the same time. Looking at Thalia and Annabeth, he understood something new. Siblings were not just people you lived with. They were people you carried.

Thalia rolled her shoulders. "Okay. Enough feelings. We have an elevator to catch."

"Wait," Percy said. "Give me a minute. I need... I need to do something first."

---

Percy walked to the side of the lobby, near a tall pillar where the gold light did not quite reach. He faced the wall so the others would not stare. He pulled Riptide out in pen form and held it tight.

He did not know how to pray properly. At camp they had lessons on offerings and altar fire, but he had slept through half of them. He knew Annabeth would do it with perfect Greek and perfect timing. He just had words.

So he closed his eyes and tried.

Dad, he thought. Poseidon. Father. I am here. I am in LA. I am about to go down.

His hands shook. The cold air hurt his lungs.

I am mad at you, he thought honestly. I remember a life when you left Mom and me alone. Here you came for me after she died, and Amphitrite raised me. You gave me a sea and a name, but I still feel the old loneliness when you go quiet. The bolt was stolen and everyone blames you and I do not know if you did it. I want to believe you did not.

Behind him he heard the soft chime of an elevator and Charon's drawer closing. The music kept playing, water down a drain.

But I need help, Percy thought. Mom is down there. Sally Jackson. She made blue food for me and read to me when I was sick and never once treated me like I was broken. Those are memories of the childhood she gave me before history changed. I can't demand that life back. I need to know she has rest. So if you can hear me in the sea or in the earth shakes or wherever you are, please watch the water. Please keep a path open.

He thought of the Nereid in Santa Monica, green and kind, who had called him little prince and given him pearls. He thought of what she had said about Amphitrite, queen of the sea, who had smiled at him from far away.

And he thought of his mom telling him once, when he was small, that families were bigger than houses. That love did not split, it multiplied.

So he added, quieter, in his head:

And Amphitrite, if you hear this too... thank you for the care you've given me and for every meal and lesson and ordinary day you and the household shared with me. Please keep everyone at home safe. I don't know how this ends, but I want to do right by Sally, by you, and by the family waiting above. Please do not be mad that I am asking.

He opened his eyes. The marble pillar was cold under his forehead where he had leaned without realizing. His pen was warm in his hand. He thought of the sea smell after rain at Montauk, and his mom humming while she stirred mac and cheese blue with food coloring just to make him laugh. He thought of Poseidon standing alone in a throne room of shells, proud and distant, and Amphitrite beside him, gracious enough to lend pearls to a boy who was living proof of a broken promise. Two mothers. Sally had raised him in the life he remembered; Amphitrite had raised him in this one. The memories did not cancel the care he was learning to accept. He wanted to honor both.

He felt silly and less alone at the same time.

When he turned around, Annabeth was watching him, not pretending she had not. Grover gave him a thumbs up. Thalia nodded once, like she understood exactly what he had done.

"You good?" Thalia asked.

Percy nodded. "Yeah. I am good."

Annabeth held out her hand. "Then we go down together."

---

The bronze elevator doors opened with a groan.

Inside, the elevator was larger than it looked. The walls were lined with dark wood and scratched mirrors that did not reflect right. Percy saw his face stretched long and pale. Annabeth beside him looked small and fierce. Thalia looked like a warrior. Grover looked like he might faint.

A Muzak version of a funeral march played from a cracked speaker. A small plaque by the buttons read: PLEASE DO NOT PRESS. WE KNOW WHERE YOU ARE GOING.

The dial above the door swung from LOBBY and began to crawl downward.

The doors closed.

The elevator dropped.

Percy's stomach flew up into his throat. Grover grabbed the handrail with both hands and bleated. Thalia spread her feet and bent her knees like she was surfing. Annabeth grabbed Percy's sleeve to steady herself, then let go quickly like she had not meant to.

They fell and fell. The lights flickered. The mirrors fogged and cleared. Cold air pressed on Percy's ears until they popped.

Floor by floor they passed places Percy could feel but not see. A hot wind with screaming. A cold wind with flowers. A middle place that smelled like clean laundry and sounded like distant traffic.

Grover started to breathe too fast. "It is too deep," he whispered. "The earth is too thick over us. I cannot feel the sky. I cannot feel Pan. I am just a satyr in a box."

Thalia put a steady hand on his shoulder. "Breathe with me," she said. "In for four. Out for four. Like we did in the van when the Furies were close."

Grover tried. In, out. In, out. His shoulders came down a fraction.

"It is okay to be scared," Thalia said quietly. "Bravery is not not being scared. Bravery is quoting Council law at a dead guy when your knees shake."

Grover gave a wet laugh. "Noted for bravery."

"Noted for bravery," Thalia confirmed. "When we get back, you are telling the Council exactly what you said. Word for word. They will have to make you first class."

"First class searcher Grover Underwood," Grover said, trying it out. "That sounds... that sounds good."

The elevator kept falling. The cable creaked.

Percy felt Annabeth shift beside him in the dark. Her hand found his. Not grabbing. Just there, palm to palm, fingers light.

He turned his head. In the dim flickering light her gray eyes were wide. She was looking at the dial, at the names sliding past: PARKING. STORAGE. FIELDS EDGE.

She squeezed his hand once, quiet and firm.

Together, she mouthed. No sound, just the shape of the word.

Percy squeezed back. He felt the pearl cloth tied to his belt and Riptide in his pocket and her hand in his, all at once.

"Together," he whispered, so low only she could hear.

The elevator shuddered. Thalia reached across and put her hand over both of theirs for a second, quick and fierce, a sister and a leader at once. Then she pulled back to steady Grover as the box swayed.

"We are almost there," Annabeth said. Her voice was steady, but her hand stayed in Percy's. "Whatever happens when those doors open, we do not let go of the plan. We find Hades. We ask about the bolt and Mom. We use the pearls only if we have to."

"And if he says no?" Grover asked.

"Then we make him say yes," Thalia said. Lightning flickered faintly around her bracelet. "Politely. At first."

The dial needle trembled near the bottom. The speaker crackled and died. The funeral march stopped mid note.

Silence pressed in, thick and cold.

Percy thought of his mom's hands, warm from the oven. He thought of Poseidon's trident on the beach at Montauk in a dream. He thought of Amphitrite's far smile and Sally's near laugh, both dear to him in different ways, Sally remembered and waiting somewhere below, Amphitrite living above in the world he wanted to return to. He held Annabeth's hand a little tighter and she held his.

The elevator slowed.

---

Halfway through the descent, the elevator stopped between floors.

The music continued. The bronze dial went still, the pointer wavering somewhere between FIELDS and STORAGE. No one spoke at first. Percy could hear only Grover's breathing, the tick of the handrail against the wall and the quiet vibration of machinery that seemed too old to trust.

Annabeth let go of Percy's hand and reached for the emergency instructions posted beside the buttons.

"Don't touch the controls," she read. "Ask an attendant for assistance. The lift will resume when the lower court acknowledges the passengers."

"Doesn't sound like an emergency," Thalia said.

"Doesn't sound like good news either."

Grover crouched and pressed a palm to the floor. Percy watched him shut his eyes. The satyr had spent enough time on the road to know that listening carefully was not the same as guessing what every sound meant.

"Something's under us," Grover said at last. "Not a monster exactly. Too many people moving in one place."

Percy felt a pressure beneath his feet, like a current pressing against a rock. It reminded him of the river outside St. Louis, but there was no water here that he could reach. He disliked that. He had become accustomed to at least one element of a fight behaving like home.

A small brass panel beside the door opened. A voice came through it.

"Living petitioners, explain the extra registration."

Annabeth looked at Percy.

He pulled out the sheet Charon had given him.

"We have four wristbands," he said. "Three questers and one guardian. We also have a welfare petition for Sally Jackson."

"Four bands," the voice repeated. "Three names in the Oracle's party. Is the fourth intended to enter the King's court?"

Thalia stepped forward.

"Yes. Chiron authorized me as a guardian."

"We recognize the centaur's authorization for passage. We do not recognize a guardian's right to speak in a court hearing unless invited."

Thalia's jaw tightened. She glanced at Annabeth, who was watching her with the kind of steady attention that made Percy feel as though the next sentence mattered more than it might seem.

"I can keep my mouth shut until I'm invited," Thalia said.

Percy didn't believe her for a second. The voice through the panel apparently had similar doubts.

"That condition will be recorded."

"Go ahead."

A pen scratched somewhere behind the wall. The panel closed. The elevator shuddered, then began moving again.

Grover stood with relief.

"That was terrifying," he said. "We stopped because of a piece of paper."

"Not because of the paper," Annabeth replied. "Because there were four living visitors and only three names in a prophecy. The paperwork made them notice."

Percy looked down at his band.

"What if they decide we shouldn't all be here?"

"Then they tell us before we reach the throne room," she answered. "And we decide what to do with that information. We don't make a fifth person out of a fourth by wishing."

Thalia looked at her little sister, then toward the dial.

"I won't promise I can stay quiet if somebody threatens you," she said.

"I don't want you to promise that. I want you to tell me when you're about to change the plan."

"That's harder."

"I know."

The elevator passed another floor. Behind its bronze doors they heard distant voices arguing about the location of a filing cabinet. Grover began to laugh, then tried to stop himself because laughing below ground felt wrong.

"What's funny?" Percy asked.

"This is the Underworld," Grover said. "I thought there would be nothing except fire and death. There's paperwork."

"My dad would hate it here," Percy said.

"Your dad has a palace," Annabeth replied. "He probably has paperwork too."

Percy thought of the official message he had helped Chiron send after Zeus's visit. He supposed Poseidon had probably received it from an attendant, read it, and handed it to someone whose job was to make sure the answer didn't start a war. The thought annoyed him. It was easier to imagine a god choosing silence out of indifference than admitting a whole court might be involved.

The elevator continued downward. Percy unfolded Sally's petition and read the words once more. It said almost nothing about the person he remembered. Her laugh wasn't there. Neither were the blue pancakes, the school mornings or the way she would wait until he finished talking before giving advice. A record couldn't contain a mother. It could only tell him whether somebody had found her in a place where living sons weren't meant to go.

He folded it again and put it beside the pearls.

Annabeth watched his hands. "You're going to ask about her."

"Yes."

"And the bolt?"

"Also yes."

"Which first?"

He had no answer. That embarrassed him more than it should have.

"Whichever Hades will answer," he said finally.

Annabeth nodded. She didn't tell him which question mattered more. It was not a problem an architect could solve on paper, and Percy was grateful she understood that.

Thalia steadied Grover when the elevator lurched again. The satyr's hooves scraped against the floor.

"What are we doing if the pearls don't work?" Grover asked.

Percy looked toward the bronze doors.

"We don't know that they won't," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"No," Annabeth said. "It's not. We should identify a second way out if we see one, and we shouldn't spend the pearls because we're frightened before we need them."

Percy counted the four small shapes through the cloth. One for each traveler. Enough for the party they had, but no spare for a soul he hoped to find.

The thought made his throat close.

He had no intention of treating Sally as a piece of cargo. He also couldn't pretend he hadn't imagined bringing her out into the sunlight. He remembered her alive in another life, and some part of him still expected the universe to return her if he asked in the right words. Charon had already told him the rules didn't work that way.

Percy put the cloth away and waited for the last floor.

---

The elevator stopped with a heavy thud that shook Percy's teeth.

For a moment nothing moved. The doors stayed closed. The mirrors were completely fogged now, dripping with condensation. The air smelled like wet stone and smoke and pomegranates.

Ding.

The bronze doors groaned open.

Cold hit them first, then sound. A vast roar like a stadium and a river at once. A howling wind. Distant barking, deep enough to shake bone.

Percy stepped out first, pulling Annabeth gently with him, then letting go so he could draw Riptide. Thalia came next with Grover, shield up.

They stood on a ledge of black rock overlooking a cavern so large Percy could not see the end of it.

Below them stretched the Underworld.

A wide gray river coiled through the dark, crowded with boats. On the far bank rose high walls of black stone topped with bronze. Great bronze gates stood open in the wall, tall as apartment buildings, etched with screaming faces. Torch fire burned green along the walls. Shadows moved along the paths, thousands of them, silent and shuffling.

Above the gates, in letters of fire, were words in Greek. Percy could read them now without trying. He wished he could not.

YOU ARE DEAD. THIS IS ONLY THE BEGINNING.

At the gates, larger than any dog had a right to be, sat a three headed hound. Even from far away Percy could see each head swiveling, sniffing. Teeth like stalactites. Eyes like coals. A low growl rolled across the cavern and made Grover's knees buckle.

Cerberus.

"The Gates," Annabeth breathed. She stepped up beside Percy, shoulder to shoulder. Her hand found the pearls at her belt to count them. One, two, three, four. "We made it. Those are the Gates of Hades."

Thalia moved to Percy's other side. Her spear was now full length in her hand, sparking faintly. Her face was pale in the green torchlight but determined.

Grover stood behind them, pipes in his shaking hands. "Searcher Second Class," he whispered to himself like a prayer. "Noted for bravery. I can do this."

Percy capped Riptide for now and uncapped it again as a sword. Bronze glowed in the dark. He wondered where Sally's spirit rested beyond those gates. He thought of Poseidon and Amphitrite and the sea far above. He thought of Annabeth's hand still warm from his.

He looked at his quest team, his girlfriend and their friends, sea-born, sky-born, and a satyr who had chosen to stand with them.

"Stay together," he said.

Beyond the gates, the dark waited. Hades waited. The bolt was still missing. His mother's name was somewhere in the records of this place.

Grover stared down at the lines of the dead winding toward the bronze gates. Some carried suitcases. Some carried nothing. A child held an old woman's hand. An old man in a business suit kept checking a watch that had stopped. None of them looked up at the ledge. None of them cried. That was the worst part. The silence of so many people not crying.

"What if they see our wristbands?" Grover whispered. "What if Cerberus smells that we are alive?"

Annabeth studied the great hound with narrowed eyes, already planning. "I don't know exactly what Cerberus detects. The wristbands show we're registered as guests, but they won't make us invisible. We walk slowly and follow the posted route rather than testing whether he'll chase us. Percy, your sword stays capped until we are past the river line. Thalia, shield ready but low. Grover, play nothing unless I say. Music calms him in the stories, but wrong music angers him."

Thalia checked the charge on her spear. Small sparks crawled along the tip and died. "I have read that his middle head likes order. Left head likes chaos. Right head just likes meat. If he looks at us, do not meet any eyes. Look at feet. Like Medusa rules, but bigger and furrier."

Percy tried to smile. "Great. Feet. We are good at feet."

The wind shifted. Sulfur and cold flowers washed over them. The green torches along the wall guttered and flared. Far below, the river Cocytus groaned against its banks. The sound made Percy's teeth ache.

He thought again of his prayer in the lobby. Of Poseidon and the open water. Of Amphitrite lending grace. Of Sally's spirit somewhere below, beyond the years he had missed in this history. He wanted news of her. He wanted to know that the mother he remembered had not been left alone in the dark.

The ledge beneath the elevator had a narrow desk carved directly into the rock.

A woman sat behind it with a ledger and a lamp whose flame burned green without flickering. She looked like an ordinary clerk who had worked an impossibly long shift. When the four arrived, she didn't ask whether they were brave. She asked for their wristbands.

"Temporary registrations," she said after reading the marks. "The route to the court crosses the main receiving hall. Stay to the right. Do not join the queue for judgment. Do not follow anyone who says they recognize you unless an usher confirms their identity."

Percy almost asked how a soul could recognize someone it hadn't met. Then he remembered Sally and the kind of voice that had called him from the Lotus phone.

"Does that happen often?" Annabeth asked.

"Often enough that it's posted." The clerk indicated a notice in Greek. Percy read it: *Living visitors may mistake a familiar voice for a lawful summons.*

Grover shivered. "Do you have one about plants?"

The clerk looked puzzled.

"Plants that call people by name," he clarified.

"No plants are permitted on the passage to the court. They're difficult to manage in the Fields."

Thalia looked toward the vast gate and back at the wristbands. "What about Cerberus?"

"He's on duty."

"Does he bite living visitors?"

"He's a guard dog. He responds to breaches."

Thalia opened her mouth, then closed it. Percy could see her choosing not to make the joke she'd been about to make. He thought that counted as real progress.

Annabeth asked for the nearest authorized exit if the court refused an audience. The clerk described a route back to the lift, warned that access required their registration to remain active, and gave them a small map printed on coarse gray paper.

"This isn't a promise of safe return," the woman said.

"I know," Annabeth answered.

Percy looked at the map. The line from the elevator to the court bent around a waiting area and an inspection station. It was not a maze, and the route was clearer than any of them had expected. That made him suspicious. He'd spent the whole journey looking for traps disguised as ordinary places. Here, the dangers were printed on signs in a place that looked like death itself.

"Why give us this?" he asked.

"Because the court doesn't want lost living petitioners wandering through the Fields," she said. "It creates more work."

He almost laughed. Then he looked down toward the lines of souls. The dead were not extras in his quest. They were people arriving in a place they hadn't chosen, needing somebody to tell them where to stand. He wouldn't have wanted to spend eternity being lost because four demigods had distracted the staff.

The clerk signed their map and returned their wristbands.

"One more thing," she said. "If you meet someone you love in the Fields, remember why you're here. Most living visitors want to stay longer than they are permitted. Some try to take someone back without understanding the consequence."

Percy gripped Sally's folded petition until its edge cut slightly into his palm.

"I know the rule," he said.

"Knowing isn't the same as obeying."

He thought of the Arch, where he'd known his promise to Annabeth and broken it anyway. He didn't argue.

They left the desk with the map in Annabeth's hands and the pearls secured at Percy's belt. The sound of Cerberus breathing rolled through the hall. Percy could see the hound's heads turning as the lines of souls moved forward. Nothing about him was tame, but he wasn't lunging at every person who passed.

Grover looked at the stamped map. "What if he doesn't like us?"

"Then we follow the instructions, don't run toward him and ask the attendant for help," Annabeth said.

Percy looked at her. "That's a very ordinary plan."

"I'd like one ordinary plan to work on this quest."

At the last turn, Percy saw an old man standing just beyond the queue barrier, clutching a faded photograph.

He was calling a name. Not loudly. He seemed to be testing whether he could still make the sound. The dead around him kept moving, and the attendant at the post was trying to explain which line the man belonged in.

Percy slowed.

"What's wrong?" Annabeth asked.

"He lost somebody."

"Most people here have," she said. Her voice softened when she saw his face.

The man looked toward them for an instant, his eyes settling on Percy. He didn't ask for help. He held out the photograph as though somebody living might be able to read it. Percy took a step forward before remembering the clerk's warning.

An attendant came between them.

"Living visitors are not permitted to take custody of records or personal objects from unassigned souls," she said. "If you need to report a concern, use the desk."

Percy looked at the old man. The photograph was creased across a woman's face. He wanted to take it and promise to find her, but he didn't know where she was or whether she was in the Underworld at all.

"Could you help him read the name?" he asked the attendant.

She turned and examined the picture. "I can direct him to the correct register."

"Thank you."

The man didn't smile. He simply followed her as she led him toward another desk. Percy stood still until they were out of sight.

"You could have taken the photograph," Thalia said quietly.

"I know."

"Why didn't you?"

"Because I can't find everyone," Percy answered. "And if I'd taken it, he might never have known where it went."

Annabeth checked the map. "That doesn't mean we do nothing. It means we find the person whose job includes helping him."

Percy was beginning to recognize how much he disliked being unable to solve things by force. Even here, no one could rescue the dead with a convincing speech. It was a weakness he would have to carry into Hades's court with his sword capped and his questions ready.

They continued toward the gates, following the route rather than charging at whatever frightened them first. It wasn't heroic. Percy thought it might be the responsible choice.

They walked toward the Gates.
