## Chapter 149: The Argument

It started over nothing.

That was the thing about the best arguments — they never started over what they were really about. They started over nothing, over a dish left in the sink or a towel on the floor or a comment that landed wrong, and then they became about everything.

This one started over the dock.

Specifically, it started over who got to sit where on the dock, and whether the arrangement they'd fallen into was an arrangement or a rule, and whether rules could be broken by people who'd made them.

"I'm just saying," Clarisse said, "that the arrangement is not a constitutional document."

"Nobody said it was a constitutional document," Thalia said.

"You said it was 'the arrangement we'd earned.' That sounds like a constitutional document."

"It's not a constitutional document. It's an arrangement."

"An arrangement you're treating like a constitutional document."

"I'm not treating it like a constitutional document. I'm treating it like an arrangement that works."

They were sitting at the Poseidon table at breakfast. Percy was on one side, Annabeth on the other, and the argument was happening across the table with the kind of energy that meant it was going to be a thing.

"The arrangement works," Thalia said, "because everybody knows where they sit."

"The arrangement works," Clarisse said, "because everybody's afraid to move."

"Nobody's afraid to move."

"I'm not afraid to move. I'm choosing not to move."

"That's the same thing."

"It's not the same thing."

"It's the same thing and you know it."

Percy looked at Annabeth. Annabeth looked at Percy. The kind of look that passed between people who were watching an argument unfold and were trying to decide whether to intervene or let it run its course.

"Let it run," Annabeth said, quietly.

"It's going to be a thing," Percy said, quietly.

"It's already a thing."

The argument continued. It moved from the dock to the training arena to the question of whether Clarisse's spear was better than Thalia's spear (it wasn't, and everybody knew it, including Clarisse, but she wasn't going to admit it) to the question of whether Thalia's lightning was more controlled than Clarisse's aim (it was, and everybody knew it, including Thalia, but she wasn't going to admit it either).

By lunch, the argument had become a camp-wide thing. People were taking sides. The Poseidon cabin was mostly on Clarisse's side. The Zeus cabin was mostly on Thalia's side. The other cabins were split, with the younger campers enjoying the drama and the older campers remembering that this was not the first time Clarisse and Thalia had argued about something that wasn't really what they were arguing about.

---

The Poseidon table at lunch was not the place to be.

Percy realized this about fifteen minutes into the meal, when the argument that had started at breakfast migrated to the pavilion with the inevitability of weather. Clarisse was at one end of the table, Thalia at the other, and the space between them had become a kind of demilitarized zone where nobody sat and nobody ate in peace.

"They're doing it again," said a kid from the Ares cabin — one of Clarisse's usual sparring partners, a stocky boy with a buzz cut and the kind of loyal face that meant he'd picked a side and was going to stick with it.

"Doing what?" Percy said.

"Arguing. About the dock. About who sits where. About —" The boy shrugged. "About whatever it's really about."

Percy looked at Annabeth, who was sitting across from him with her lunch untouched and her attention on the far end of the table. "Is it always like this?" he asked.

"Like what?"

"This. The taking sides. The camp-wide thing."

Annabeth considered. "Sometimes. When it's about something that matters to people. When it's about something that's not really about what it's about."

"And this is about something that's not really about the dock."

"This is about everything that's not the dock."

The argument had by now moved past the dock entirely. It was about the training schedule and who got to use the arena first. It was about the campfire songs and who picked them. It was about the way the camp was run and who got to run it and who got to say how it was run. It was about a hundred small things that had been accumulating for weeks, maybe months, and had finally found a voice in the question of who sat where on the dock.

Percy watched Clarisse. She was doing the thing she did when she was angry — sitting very still, speaking very precisely, every word a small controlled demolition. She wasn't yelling. She didn't need to yell. She had the kind of anger that was more effective quiet.

Thalia, on the other hand, was doing the thing she did when she was angry — getting louder, getting bigger, filling the space with the kind of energy that made people step back. She had her hands on the table and her chair was pushed back and she was doing the thing with her voice that meant she was five seconds from lightning.

Percy looked at Silena, who was sitting next to Clarisse with her lunch also untouched and her face doing the thing it did when she was watching her sisters fight — the wincing, the waiting, the bracing.

"We should do something," Percy said quietly.

"Like what?" Annabeth said quietly.

"I don't know. Something."

"You did something. Last night. On the dock. You stopped them."

"I stopped the argument. I didn't stop the thing that caused the argument."

Annabeth was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Maybe you can't stop the thing that caused the argument. Maybe the thing that caused the argument just needs to run its course."

"And if the course it runs is through the whole camp?"

"Then the whole camp deals with it."

Percy looked at the pavilion. It was doing the thing it did when the camp was tense — the noise level was wrong. Too loud in some places, too quiet in some places. The Hermes cabin was watching with the kind of attention that meant they were enjoying the show. The Apollo cabin was trying to pretend nothing was happening. The younger campers were wide-eyed and passing notes. The older campers were eating quickly and not making eye contact.

A kid at the next table — a small girl from the Demeter cabin, maybe nine or ten — leaned over and said, in a stage whisper that carried, "Are they fighting again?"

"It's not a fight," her older sister said, also in a stage whisper. "It's an argument."

"What's the difference?"

"A fight is physical. An argument is —"

"I know what an argument is. I'm asking about the difference between an argument and the thing they're doing."

The older sister was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "The thing they're doing is an argument that's been going on for longer than today."

The small girl considered this. "How much longer?"

"Since always. Since the beginning. Since they became sisters."

"Oh," the small girl said. "So it's a sister thing."

"It's a sister thing," the older sister confirmed.

The small girl nodded, satisfied, and went back to her lunch. Percy looked at Annabeth. Annabeth looked at Percy. The kind of look that passed between people who had just heard something true and were trying to decide what to do with it.

"A sister thing," Percy said quietly.

"A sister thing," Annabeth agreed.

"And we're going to let it run its course."

"We're going to let it run its course."

Percy went back to his lunch. It was cold. He ate it anyway.
---

The campfire that evening was careful.

Not careful in the way that campfires are usually careful — the Chiron-supervised, nobody-goes-near-the-flames, don't-burn-your-marshmallow kind of careful. Careful in the way that people are careful when they're trying not to set off something that's already burning.

The fire was doing its thing. The flames were doing their thing. The marshmallows were doing their thing. The songs were doing their thing. But the energy was wrong, and everybody knew it, and nobody knew how to fix it.

The five of them didn't sit together.

This was unusual. The five of them always sat together. It was one of the things that was true about the camp the way it was true that the lake was wet and the cabins were crowded and Chiron's lectures were long. The five of them sat together at campfire. They sat together in a row, in the arrangement they'd earned, and it was just what they did.

But tonight they didn't.

Clarisse sat with the Ares cabin. Thalia sat with the Zeus cabin. Silena sat with the Aphrodite cabin. Percy and Annabeth sat with the Poseidon cabin. They were in the same place, near the same fire, but they were not together, and the difference between those two things was the whole problem.

Annabeth watched from the Poseidon cabin's log. She watched Clarisse, who was sitting very still and eating her marshmallow with the kind of precision that meant she was angry. She watched Thalia, who was sitting very straight and talking too loudly to one of her siblings and laughing too hard at something that wasn't funny. She watched Silena, who was sitting very quietly and looking at both of them with the kind of expression that meant she was trying to figure out how to fix something that couldn't be fixed by being nice.

"She's going to burn her marshmallow," Percy said quietly.

"She's not going to burn her marshmallow," Annabeth said quietly. "She's going to eat it perfectly and it's going to be a whole thing."

As if she heard them, Clarisse took a bite of her marshmallow. It was perfectly golden. It was perfectly toasted. It was the kind of marshmallow that would have been a point of pride on any other night. Tonight it was just a marshmallow.

"Thalia's going to set the pavilion on fire," Percy said.

"She's not going to set the pavilion on fire," Annabeth said. "She's going to generate so much static that somebody's hair stands up and it's going to be a whole thing."

As if she heard them, Thalia laughed at something and a small spark jumped from her finger to the log beside her. The log smoldered. The kid next to Thalia moved over. Thalia didn't notice.

"They're both doing the thing," Percy said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where they're trying to prove they're fine by being extra not-fine."

Percy was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "We should do something."

"Like what?"

"I don't know. Something."

"You did something. You stopped them."

"I stopped the argument. I didn't fix what caused it."

Annabeth was quiet. The fire did its thing. The songs did their thing. The camp did its careful, walking-on-eggshells thing.

"The camp knows," Annabeth said.

"Knows what?"

"That something's wrong. That the five of us aren't sitting together. That the argument isn't really about the dock."

"What are they saying?"

"The usual. That Clarisse and Thalia are fighting again. That it's about the dock. That it's about who's been here longest. That it's —" She stopped. "That it's a sister thing."

"It is a sister thing."

"It's more than a sister thing."

Percy looked at her. "What is it?"

Annabeth was quiet for a long moment. The fire crackled. Somewhere in the dark, one of the younger campers started crying — not a big cry, just the kind of small cry that meant they were overwhelmed by the tension and didn't have the vocabulary for it.

"It's about the fact that they both found something they didn't think they'd get," Annabeth said finally. "A family. A home. People who love them. And they're both so scared of losing it that they're willing to fight each other to keep it."

"And the dock is just the thing they're using to fight about it."

"The dock is just the thing they're using to fight about it."

They sat there for a moment. Then Percy said, "We should sit together."

"All of us?"

"All of us. The five of us. The arrangement."

"Clarisse is over there. Thalia is over there. They're not going to come over here."

"Then we go to them."

"Percy —"

"We go to them. We sit with them. We show them that the arrangement isn't the thing, the thing is the thing, and the thing is us sitting together even when it's hard."

Annabeth looked at him. He looked back. The kind of look that passed between them when one of them said something that was obviously right and the other one was going to stop arguing and start helping.

"Okay," Annabeth said. "Let's go."

They got up. They walked across the campfire circle. They sat down — Percy next to Clarisse, Annabeth next to Thalia — and the campfire went quiet for a moment, the way it does when something important is happening and everybody knows it.

Clarisse looked at Percy. Percy looked at Clarisse. "What are you doing?" Clarisse said.

"Sitting with you," Percy said.

"I'm sitting with my cabin."

"You're sitting alone."

"I'm sitting with my cabin," Clarisse said again, but her voice was different now. Softer. Less certain.

Percy didn't argue. He just sat there. Annabeth sat next to Thalia, who was doing the thing with her hands that meant she was generating static and trying not to.

"Stop that," Annabeth said quietly.

"Stop what?"

"The static. The sparks. The thing you're doing where you're trying to prove you're fine by being electric."

Thalia's hands went still. "I'm not doing anything."

"You're doing something. You're doing the thing where you're so scared of losing control that you're controlling everything."

Thalia was quiet. The fire crackled. The camp watched.

"I'm not scared," Thalia said.

"You're scared," Annabeth said. "You're scared that if you're not the lightning anymore, you won't be necessary. You're scared that if you let go, you'll lose us."

Thalia's face did something in the firelight. Not quite a flinch. Not quite a break. Something in between.

"Yeah," she said quietly. "I'm scared."

Percy reached over and put his hand on Clarisse's shoulder. Annabeth reached over and put her hand on Thalia's arm. Silena, from across the circle, got up and walked over and sat down between them, and the five of them were together again, in the arrangement they'd earned, and the campfire went back to doing its thing, and the tension broke like a wave breaks on a shore — not all at once, but enough, and then enough was everything.

---

The real argument started after dinner.

They were on the dock — all five of them, in the arrangement that wasn't a constitutional document — and the sun was going down and the lake was doing its evening thing, and Clarisse said, "I'm just saying that some of us have been here longer than others and that should count for something."

"Count for what?" Thalia said.

"Count for knowing where things go."

"Knowing where things go isn't the same as deciding where things go."

"It's not?"

"It's not."

"Feels like it is."

"Feels like it isn't."

Percy, who had been sitting quietly in the middle of all of them, said, "Maybe it's neither."

Clarisse and Thalia both turned to look at him.

"Maybe it's neither," Percy said. "Maybe the arrangement isn't about who's been here longest or who's afraid to move or who's choosing not to move. Maybe it's just about what works."

"What works for you," Clarisse said.

"What works for all of us."

"What works for all of us is an arrangement that makes everybody comfortable."

"What works for all of us is an arrangement that makes everybody comfortable," Thalia said, "not an arrangement that makes one person comfortable and everybody else accommodating."

"I'm not the one who's accommodating."

"You're the one who's been here the longest."

"Being here the longest doesn't mean I get to decide."

"Being here the longest means I get to have an opinion."

"Everybody has an opinion."

"Some opinions count more than others."

"Do they?"

"Do they?"

The argument continued. It got louder. It got more personal. It got into things that weren't about the dock at all — about Clarisse's need to control things because so many things had been out of her control, about Thalia's need to protect things because so many things had been taken from her, about the way they both loved the same people and the same lake and the same camp and couldn't always agree on how to show it.

Percy sat in the middle and watched. Annabeth sat beside him and watched. Silena sat on the other side and watched.

Finally, Percy said, "Stop."

It wasn't loud. It wasn't sharp. It was just the word, said with the kind of quiet that meant he meant it.

Clarisse stopped. Thalia stopped. The lake kept moving under the dock, and the sun kept going down, and the camp kept doing its evening thing, but the argument stopped.

"You're both right," Percy said. "You're both wrong. You're both scared. You're both trying to protect something. And you're both doing it by trying to control something you can't control."

"We're not scared," Clarisse said.

"You're scared," Percy said. "You're scared that if the arrangement changes, things will change, and if things change, you'll lose something. You're scared that if you let go of the control, you'll lose the thing you're protecting."

Clarisse was quiet.

"You're scared," Percy said, turning to Thalia. "You're scared that if the arrangement changes, you'll lose your place in it. You're scared that if you're not the protector anymore, you won't be needed. You're scared that if you let go of the control, you'll lose the people you're protecting."

Thalia was quiet.

"And you're both wrong," Percy said, "because the arrangement isn't the thing. The arrangement isn't what protects us. The arrangement isn't what makes us family. The thing is the thing. The thing is that we're here. The thing is that we're together. The thing is that we're us. The arrangement is just the shape we give it. And shapes can change without the thing changing."

The dock was quiet. The lake moved. The stars started coming out.

"He's right," Silena said.

"He's right," Annabeth said.

"He's right," Clarisse said, quietly.

"He's right," Thalia said, quietly.

---

They walked back up the hill in the dark.

Not together, exactly — Silena was a few steps ahead, and Annabeth was a few steps behind, and the distance between them was the kind of distance that meant they were both thinking and didn't want to intrude on each other's thinking.

The camp was quiet. The kind of quiet that came after a storm — not the absence of sound, but the presence of aftermath. The crickets were doing their thing. The lake was doing its thing. The cabins were mostly dark, with a few windows still lit by people who weren't ready to sleep yet.

Silena stopped at the top of the hill. Annabeth stopped too. They stood there for a moment, looking out at the lake, which was doing its night thing — the dark water, the star-shimmer, the way it held the sky and gave it back.

"He shouldn't have done that," Silena said.

"Percy?"

"Intervening. Stopping them. He shouldn't have done that."

Annabeth was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Why not?"

"Because it's not his fight."

"It's not his fight," Annabeth repeated. "But he's part of it."

"He's part of the family. That's not the same as being part of the argument."

"Silena." Annabeth's voice was gentle. "He stopped them because he loves you. All of you. He stopped them because he couldn't watch you destroy each other anymore."

Silena was quiet. The lake moved. The stars did their thing.

"He was right," Silena said quietly. "About what he said. About the fear. About the control. About — all of it. He was right."

"He usually is," Annabeth said. "It's infuriating."

Silena laughed. It was a small laugh, but it was a laugh, and in the dark on the hill above the lake it felt like something.

"He was right," Silena said again. "And I hate that he was right."

"You don't hate that he was right. You hate that you were wrong."

"I was wrong."

"You were scared. That's not the same thing."

Silena turned to look at her. "You're on his side."

"I'm not on a side. I'm on the side of you not tearing each other apart."

"That's his side."

"That's my side. I happen to agree with him about this."

They were quiet for a moment. Then Silena said, "You know what the worst part is?"

"What?"

"I don't even know what we were arguing about anymore. The dock. The arrangement. Who sits where. It's — it's stupid. It's so stupid."

"It's not stupid. It's never stupid when it's about the things that matter."

"The dock isn't the thing that matters."

"The dock is the thing you're using to talk about the thing that matters."

Silena was quiet. Then she said, "And what's the thing that matters?"

"You know what the thing that matters is."

"I want you to say it."

Annabeth was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "The thing that matters is that you both love the same people and the same place and you're both terrified of losing it, and you're both trying to hold on so tight that you're crushing it."

Silena's face did something in the dark. Not quite a flinch. Not quite a wince. Something in between.

"Yeah," she said quietly. "That's the thing that matters."

They stood there for a moment longer. Then Silena said, "Come on. Let's go to bed. Tomorrow's going to be a long day."

"Why?"

"Because the argument's not over. It's just paused."

They walked to the cabins together. The lake kept its silence below them with the discretion of a good witness.
---

The next morning, Chiron found Thalia on the hill above the lake.

She was sitting on the boundary rock with her knees up and her arms around them and her face doing the thing it did when she was trying to be alone and wasn't sure if she wanted to be found. The lake was doing its morning thing below her — the silvering, the first ripples, the way it went from mirror to water when the wind found it.

"Thalia," Chiron said, coming up the path with his hooves making their soft sound on the grass.

She didn't turn around. "If you're here to tell me to talk to Clarisse, I already talked to Clarisse."

"I'm not here to tell you to talk to Clarisse."

"Now?"

"No."

Thalia turned to look at him. "Then what are you here for?"

Chiron sat down on the rock beside her. It was a large rock — the boundary rock, the one with the white X that marked the edge of camp — and there was room for both of them, though Chiron took more room than Thalia did.

"I'm here to tell you something," he said.

"What?"

He was quiet for a moment, looking at the lake. Then he said, "When I was young — younger than you, though not by as much as you might think — I had a brother."

Thalia looked at him. Chiron didn't talk about his family. It was one of those things that was known but not discussed, the way everybody knew that Chiron was immortal and everybody knew that it hadn't always been easy.

"What happened to him?" Thalia asked.

"He died. In a war that I couldn't stop. A war that I saw coming and couldn't prevent and spent every day of my very long life wishing I had."

Thalia was quiet.

"I held on," Chiron said. "I held on to the memory of him. I held on to the idea of what we'd been. I held on so tight that I couldn't see what was in front of me. I couldn't see the students who needed me. I couldn't see the camp that needed me. I couldn't see the life that was waiting for me because I was so busy holding on to the life I'd lost."

"That's not what I'm doing," Thalia said.

"Isn't it?"

Thalia was quiet. The lake moved below them. A bird called from somewhere in the trees.

"I'm not holding on to the past," Thalia said. "I'm protecting the present."

"What's the difference?"

Thalia looked at him. "The past is gone. The present is here. I'm protecting what's here."

"And how do you protect it?"

"By holding on. By being strong. By being the thing that doesn't break."

Chiron was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "Thalia. The thing that doesn't break is the thing that shatters."

Thalia's face did something.

"You can't protect people by being hard," Chiron said. "You protect people by being present. You protect people by being there. You protect people by letting them see you — not the lightning, not the protector, not the thing that doesn't break. You. The person inside the lightning. The girl inside the protector. The sister inside the warrior."

"I don't know how to do that," Thalia said quietly. "I don't know how to be the person inside the lightning. I've been the lightning for so long that I don't know what's inside it."

"Then find out," Chiron said. "You have sisters. You have a brother. You have a camp full of people who love you not for your lightning but for your heart. Let them see you. Let them help you. Let them be strong for you so you don't have to be strong for them all the time."

Thalia was quiet for a long time. The lake moved. The bird called again. The camp was waking up below them.

"Clarisse said something like that," Thalia said finally. "On the dock. During the argument. She said —" She stopped. "She said I needed to be needed. And she was right. I do need to be needed. I need to be the protector because if I'm not the protector, I don't know what I am."

"You're Thalia," Chiron said. "You're a daughter of Zeus. You're Annabeth and Jason's older sister, and you have spent years trying to make yourself useful before anyone can tell you they love you. You're a friend. You're a girl sitting on a rock above a lake trying to figure out how much protecting is enough. You don't need to be needed every minute. You need to let people know you."

Thalia's eyes were bright. She looked away, at the lake, at the sky, at anything but Chiron.

"I don't know how to be known," she said.

"Then let us know you," Chiron said. "Start with your sisters. Start with Clarisse. Start with the person who argues with you because she loves you and doesn't know how to say it."

Thalia laughed. It was a wet laugh, a laugh that was close to something else, and on the rock above the lake in the morning light it felt like something.

"She's impossible," Thalia said.

"She's impossible," Chiron agreed. "And she loves you. And you love her. And the two of you are going to argue about the dock until the end of time. But you're going to argue about it as sisters, not as enemies. That's the difference."

Thalia was quiet. Then she said, "Thank you."

"You're welcome."

They sat there for a moment. Then Chiron got up, and Thalia got up, and they walked down the hill together, and the lake kept its silence below them with the discretion of a good witness.

---

They didn't solve it that night.

They didn't solve it the next night, or the night after that. The argument ran its course the way arguments do — it flared, it burned, it left ashes, and then it was over, and they were left with the wreckage and the question of what to do with it.

What they did was this: they sat on the dock, and they talked. Not about the dock — about the thing under the dock. About the fear and the love and the protecting and the controlling. About the way they'd both been given families they hadn't asked for and were trying to figure out how to keep them. About the way they both loved the same people and the same lake and the same camp and couldn't always agree on how to show it.

Clarisse talked about the years when she had started taking responsibility for every younger camper who came through the arena. About the days she had confused being frightening with keeping order, and the times she'd tried to settle an argument by making the other person stop talking. She admitted that she had sometimes treated her siblings like recruits instead of asking what they wanted. The family she loved did not need the dock arranged like a defensive position.

Thalia talked about helping raise Annabeth and being old enough that people sometimes mistook her confidence for permission to decide everything. She missed Jason, away at Camp Jupiter, more than she usually admitted. And she feared that if she loosened her grip on the routines she had built around Annabeth, she would fail to notice a danger until it was too late. This was the life she remembered. There was no lost Hunterhood or tree years to invoke, only a family whose members had begun asking for room to make their own decisions.

And then they listened. They listened to each other. They listened to the lake. They listened to the quiet that came after the argument, when the ashes had settled and the thing under the thing was visible.

---

Clarisse was at the training arena at midnight.

She wasn't training. She was just standing there, in the dark, with her spear in her hand and the kind of restlessness that meant she needed to hit something but didn't want to hit anything that would hit back.

The arena was empty. The dummies were dark. The weapons rack was locked. The whole place smelled like sweat and leather and the particular smell of the camp that came from too many people training in too small a space.

She stood in the middle of the sand and thought about the argument.

Not the argument itself — the argument was just words, and she was good at words, and she could out-argue anybody at this camp if she wanted to. It was the thing under the argument. The thing Percy had named. The fear.

She didn't want to be scared. She didn't do scared. She did angry and she did mean and she did whatever it took to survive, and scared was not part of the repertoire.

But Percy was right. She was scared.

She was scared that if the arrangement changed, things would change. She was scared that if she let go of the control, she'd lose the thing she was protecting. She was scared that the family she'd found — the sisters and the brother and the lake and the dock — was held together by the shape of it, and if the shape changed, the thing would dissolve.

She threw the spear. It went end over end in the dark and stuck in the sand thirty feet away with a sound that was very satisfying and not nearly satisfying enough.

She walked over and pulled it out. Threw it again. It stuck again.

"You're going to pull a muscle."

Clarisse turned. Thalia was standing at the edge of the arena, backlit by the moon, with her hands in her pockets and her face doing the thing it did when she was trying to be casual and wasn't.

"I'm training," Clarisse said.

"It's midnight."

"I'm training."

Thalia walked into the arena. She stood a few feet away, looking at the sand, looking at the dummies, looking at anything but Clarisse.

"I'm sorry," Thalia said.

Clarisse was quiet.

"I'm sorry for — for what I said. About you being controlling. About you being afraid. I shouldn't have — I shouldn't have gone there."

"You went there because it was true."

Thalia was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Yeah. I went there because it was true."

They stood there in the dark. The lake was visible beyond the trees, doing its night thing. The stars were out. The camp was quiet.

"I'm sorry too," Clarisse said.

"For what?"

"For what I said about you needing to be needed. About you being the protector. About — all of it."

"You went there because it was true."

"Yeah."

They were quiet for a moment. Then Thalia said, "We're a mess."

"We're a mess," Clarisse agreed.

"We love each other."

"We love each other."

"And we're terrible at showing it."

"We're terrible at showing it."

Thalia laughed. It was a small laugh, but it was a laugh, and in the dark in the training arena it felt like something.

"I don't want to lose this," Thalia said quietly. "The family. The dock. The arrangement. All of it."

"I'm not trying to lose it. I'm trying to hold on to it."

"I know. Me too. That's the problem."

Clarisse looked at her. In the dark, with the moon behind her, Thalia looked younger than she was. She looked like a girl before the whole camp started depending on her. Like somebody's little sister.

"We're both trying to hold on so tight," Clarisse said, "that we're crushing it."

"Yeah."

"Maybe we should — I don't know. Hold on less tight."

"Maybe."

They stood there for a moment. Then Clarisse said, "Come on."

"Where?"

"The lake. I want to go to the lake."

It was not a question. It was the kind of statement that Clarisse made when she'd decided something and expected the world to accommodate itself.
---

Breakfast the next morning was excruciating.

Not excruciating in the way that Chiron's lectures were excruciating — long, tedious, full of facts you didn't need and dates you didn't care about. Excruciating in the way that things are excruciating when they're trying very hard to be normal and failing spectacularly.

The five of them were at the Poseidon table. This was not unusual — they always sat at the Poseidon table. But the arrangement was different. Not the physical arrangement — Clarisse was still on the edge with her feet up on the bench, Thalia was still at the far end with her chair tilted back, Silena was still in the middle with her hands around a mug, Percy and Annabeth were still on either side. But the emotional arrangement was different. They were trying to be normal and it was not working.

Clarisse ate her eggs with the kind of precision that meant she was angry. Thalia ate her toast with the kind of aggression that meant she was angry. Silena ate her cereal with the kind of care that meant she was trying to be invisible. Percy and Annabeth ate with the kind of deliberate casualness that meant they were trying too hard to make things normal.

Nobody talked.

The pavilion was doing its breakfast thing — the clatter of plates, the murmur of conversation, the sound of a hundred campers eating and talking and being alive. But at the Poseidon table, the conversation was a void, and the void was getting bigger.

A kid from the Hermes cabin walked by with a tray and said, "Morning!" with the kind of cheerfulness that meant they hadn't noticed the tension.

Nobody responded.

The kid kept walking.

Percy looked at Annabeth. Annabeth looked at Percy. The kind of look that passed between people who were watching a disaster unfold and were trying to decide whether to intervene or let it happen.

"We have to say something," Percy said quietly.

"Like what?" Annabeth said quietly.

"I don't know. Something."

"You've been saying that a lot."

"Because it keeps being true."

At the far end of the table, Thalia took a bite of her toast and chewed it with the kind of violence that meant the toast had done nothing to deserve what was happening to it.

"You're going to break your teeth," Clarisse said.

"I'm eating toast," Thalia said.

"You're assaulting toast."

"I'm eating toast aggressively."

"That's the same thing."

"It's not the same thing."

"It's the same thing and you know it."

The words hung in the air. They were the same words they always used — the same rhythm, the same pattern, the same dance. But they were different now. They were said with less anger and more something else. Something that might have been the beginning of a joke.

Thalia looked at Clarisse. Clarisse looked at Thalia. For a moment, neither of them said anything.

Then Thalia said, "I'm sorry."

The pavilion didn't go quiet. The camp didn't stop. The lake didn't pause. But at the Poseidon table, everything stopped.

"You don't have to apologize," Clarisse said.

"I do, though. I said things that were — I went too far."

"You went where you went because it was true."

"Yeah. But I went there angry. I should have gone there — I don't know. Not angry."

Clarisse was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "I'm sorry too."

"For what?"

"For what I said about you needing to be needed. For saying it the way I said it. For — all of it."

They were quiet. The toast was getting cold. The eggs were getting cold. The coffee was getting cold.

"We're both sorry," Thalia said.

"We're both sorry," Clarisse agreed.

"And we're both idiots."

"And we're both idiots."

Thalia laughed. It was a small laugh, but it was a laugh, and at the Poseidon table in the morning light it felt like something.

"We're not idiots," Clarisse said.

"We're a little idiots."

"We're a little idiots," Clarisse agreed.

Percy looked at Annabeth. Annabeth looked at Percy. The kind of look that passed between people who were watching something good happen and were trying not to cry about it.

"We're all a little idiots," Silena said quietly.

"We're all a little idiots," Percy said.

"We're all a little idiots," Annabeth said.

The pavilion did its thing. The camp did its thing. The lake did its thing. And the five of them sat at the Poseidon table and ate their breakfast and were together, and it was enough, and it was everything, and it was theirs. Thalia looked at her for a moment. Then she said, "Okay. Let's go to the lake."

The lake at midnight was a different country.

The water was warm — warmer than the air, warmer than the night, warmer than anything had a right to be in the dark. It was the kind of warm that came from the earth, from the deep place where the lake kept its summer even in the cool of the night.

All five of them were there. Not because they'd planned it, but because the argument had left them all restless and the lake was the only thing that knew what to do with restlessness.

Clarisse was the first in. She went in the way she did everything — decisively, without hesitation, stripping off her shirt and her boots and walking into the water like she was claiming it. The water came up to her waist, then her chest, then she was under and came up with her hair slicked back and her face doing the thing it did when the water touched it — the softening, the letting go.

Thalia was next. She went in the way she did everything — dramatically, with a yelp and a splash and the kind of announcement that meant she wanted you to know she was doing something. She went in up to her shoulders and then floated on her back with her arms out and her face to the stars.

Silena went in quietly, the way she did everything. She waded in up to her knees and then sat down in the shallow water and let it come to her chest and just sat there, with her arms around her knees and her face doing the thing it did when she was happy — the gentle, the at-peace.

Percy went in the way he did everything — like the water was coming home. He walked in and the water opened for him and he was under and came up and the lake was doing its thing around him, the welcome, the recognition, the old friendship.

Annabeth went in last, because Annabeth did everything with deliberation. She took off her shoes and her jacket and folded them on the dock and walked to the edge and tested the water with her toe and then waded in up to her waist and stood there, looking at the others.

"This was a good idea," she said.

"It was Clarisse's idea," Thalia said, floating.

"It was the lake's idea," Clarisse said.

"It was everybody's idea," Silena said.

They swam. Not laps, not training, not the kind of swimming that had a point. Just swimming. Just the water and the night and the stars and the five of them together in the lake that held them.

Clarisse dove and came up with her hair in her eyes. Thalia floated and did the thing with her fingers that made small lightning sparks dance on the surface. Silena swam in slow circles with her eyes closed. Percy moved through the water like he was part of it, which he was. Annabeth floated on her back and looked at the stars and thought about maps and change and the way things moved and stayed.

After a while, they gathered in the shallow water near the dock. The water came up to their waists and the night was warm and the stars were bright and the camp was quiet on the hill above them.

"We're going to be okay," Silena said.

"We're going to be okay," Thalia said.

"We're going to be okay," Clarisse said.

"We're going to be okay," Annabeth said.

"We're going to be okay," Percy said.

The lake moved around them. The stars did their thing. And the five of them stood in the water that held them and looked at each other and knew that the arrangement wasn't the thing, and the thing was the thing, and the thing was enough.

That afternoon, they went to the training arena.

Not because anyone suggested it. Not because anyone planned it. Just because the argument had left them all with too much energy and not enough somewhere to put it, and the training arena was the only place at camp that knew what to do with excess energy.

Clarisse was the first to pick up a practice spear. She picked it up the way she picked up everything — decisively, without hesitation, with the kind of confidence that came from knowing exactly what you were good at and not being shy about it.

Thalia was the next. She picked up a practice sword and did a few experimental swings, and the air around her crackled with the kind of static that meant she was working through something.

"I call Thalia," said a kid from the Athena cabin, who was at the arena for the afternoon session.

"You can't call Thalia," Clarisse said.

"I called her."

"I'm calling Thalia," Thalia said.

"You can't both call Thalia," Silena said.

"I'm Thalia," Thalia said.

"Exactly," Silena said. "You're Thalia. You can't call yourself."

"Why not?"

"Because that's not how teams work."

Thalia looked at the practice sword in her hand. She looked at Clarisse with her practice spear. She looked at Silena, who was sitting on the bench with her legs crossed and her face doing the thing it did when she was trying not to get involved in something that was going to get people hurt.

"Fine," Thalia said. "I'm on Clarisse's team."

"I don't want you on my team," Clarisse said.

"Too bad. I'm on your team."

"You're going to be impossible."

"I'm always impossible."

"That's not a selling point."

"It's not a selling point. It's a fact."

Percy and Annabeth watched from the bench. They were refereeing, which meant they were sitting on the bench and watching and trying not to get hit by stray practice weapons.

"They're going to destroy each other," Annabeth said.

"They're going to destroy each other and then they're going to be fine," Percy said.

"That's optimistic."

"That's experienced."

The training session started. It was supposed to be a friendly sparring match — two on two, Thalia and Clarisse versus two Athena cabin kids who were good but not that good, which was the only reason Clarisse and Thalia had agreed to team up.

It started friendly. For about thirty seconds.

Then Clarisse got competitive. She always got competitive. It was one of the things that was true about her the way it was true that the lake was wet and the sun was hot and Chiron's lectures were long. She got competitive and she got mean and she got the kind of focused that meant she was going to win or she was going to make sure nobody else won either.

Thalia, on the other hand, got creative. She always got creative. It was one of the things that was true about her the way it was true that lightning was fast and storms were loud and the Zeus cabin was dramatic. She got creative and she got unpredictable and she got the kind of energized that meant she was going to do something nobody expected, including herself.

They were a disaster together.

Clarisse went left. Thalia went right. They collided in the middle. The Athena cabin kids took advantage of the confusion and scored three points in a row.

"This is not working," Clarisse said.

"This is working fine," Thalia said.

"We're losing."

"We're not losing. We're —"

"We're losing. We're losing by three points."

"Three points isn't losing. Three points is —"

"Three points is losing when we're supposed to be winning."

They stood in the middle of the arena, breathing hard, covered in sand, looking at each other with the kind of frustration that came from being on the same team and not being able to figure out how to be on the same team.

"Maybe we should try —" Thalia started.

"Don't tell me what to do," Clarisse said.

"I'm not telling you what to do. I'm suggesting —"

"You're telling me what to do. You're suggesting what I should do. That's the same thing."

"It's not the same thing."

"It's the same thing and you know it."

They stared at each other. The Athena cabin kids were watching. The other campers at the arena were watching. Percy and Annabeth were watching from the bench.

Then Thalia started laughing.

It wasn't a mean laugh. It wasn't an angry laugh. It was the kind of laugh that came from being so frustrated that the only thing left to do was laugh at the absurdity of it all.

"We're doing it again," Thalia said. "We're arguing about the arrangement."

"We're not arguing about the arrangement," Clarisse said. "We're arguing about strategy."

"It's the same thing."

"It's not the same thing."

"It's the same thing and you know it."

Clarisse looked at her. For a moment, her face was doing the thing it did when she was about to say something cutting. Then it did something else. Something that might have been the beginning of a smile.

"Yeah," she said. "It's the same thing."

"So what do we do?"

"We stop fighting about the arrangement and start fighting together."

"That's surprisingly wise for someone who just got hit with a practice spear."

"I didn't get hit with a practice spear. I got grazed by a practice spear. That's different."

"It's not different."

"It's different and you know it."

Thalia grinned. Clarisse almost-smiled. And they turned back to the Athena cabin kids, who were standing there looking confused and slightly terrified, and they said, in unison, "Okay. Let's do this."

They did this.

It wasn't pretty. It wasn't elegant. It was two people who loved each other and didn't know how to show it trying to fight together and mostly succeeding. It was Clarisse going left and Thalia going left and them colliding and then laughing about it. It was Thalia generating so much static that Clarisse's hair stood up and then Clarisse throwing sand at Thalia and then both of them laughing so hard they couldn't fight.

It was messy and it was loud and it was imperfect and it was theirs.

By the end, they'd won by two points. The Athena cabin kids conceded with good grace. The other campers applauded. Silena, who'd been watching from the bench the whole time, was doing the thing with her face that meant she was proud and trying not to show it.

Percy and Annabeth walked over as the five of them were gathering their things — water bottles, practice weapons, the detritus of an afternoon spent being too competitive and too loud and too much.

"Good game," Annabeth said.

"It was a good game," Clarisse said.

"It was a chaotic game," Thalia said.

"That's the same thing," Clarisse said.

"It's not the same thing."

"It's the same thing and you know it."

Percy looked at Annabeth. Annabeth looked at Percy. The kind of look that passed between them when they were watching something good happen and were trying not to be too obvious about how happy it made them.

"Come on," Percy said. "Let's go to the lake."

"To the lake?" Clarisse said.

"To the lake," Percy said. "It's what we do."

They went to the lake. The sun was going down. The water was doing its evening thing. The five of them sat on the dock in the arrangement they'd earned and chose and kept, and the argument was over, and the thing under the argument was healing, and the world was whole, and the world was theirs, and the world was enough.

A week after the argument, they sat on the dock again.

Same five people. Same lake. Same stars. But the arrangement was different.

Not different in any way you could point to. Just different in the way that mattered. Clarisse was still on the edge with her feet in the water. Silena was still on the planks with her legs dangling. Thalia was still on her back with her hands behind her head. Annabeth was still sitting with her knees up and her notebook closed on her shins. Percy was still in the middle of all of them.

But the arrangement wasn't a rule anymore. It was a choice. It was a thing they did because they wanted to, not because they had to. It was a shape they gave to the thing, not the thing itself.

"Clarisse," Thalia said.

"Yeah."

"I'm sorry."

"I know."

"I was scared."

"I know."

"You too."

"Yeah."

They were quiet for a moment. Then Clarisse said, "The arrangement's not a constitutional document."

"The arrangement's not a constitutional document," Thalia agreed.

"It's just an arrangement."

"It's just an arrangement."

"And it can change."

"And it can change."

"And we can change."

"And we can change."

The lake moved under the dock. The stars came out. The camp went quiet. And the five of them sat in the arrangement they'd chosen, with the lake in front of them and the stars above them and the whole summer stretching out ahead of them like a promise they intended to keep.

"Percy," Thalia said.

"Yeah."

"You were right."

"I was right?"

"You were right and you're going to be insufferable about it."

"I'm not going to be insufferable about it."

"You're already being insufferable about it."

"I'm not being insufferable about it. I'm being right about it."

"Those are the same thing."

"They're not the same thing."

"They're the same thing and you know it."

Percy laughed. The laugh carried across the water and came back changed, and the lake kept its silence around the dock with the discretion of a good witness, and the stars came out, and the camp was quiet, and the five of them sat together in the arrangement they'd earned and chosen and kept, and the world was whole, and the world was theirs, and the world was enough.

The next morning, Percy woke to the sound of the lake.

Not the sound of the lake doing anything dramatic — just the sound of the lake being the lake. The lap of water against the dock pilings. The call of a bird from somewhere in the trees. The distant sound of the camp waking up — a door, a voice, the first clang of the pavilion kitchen.

He lay still for a moment, listening. The cabin was quiet. The bunks around him were empty or occupied by shapes under blankets. The window showed him a sky that was more blue than gray, with the sun already up and the day already happening.

He got up. Dressed. Went outside.

The camp at this hour was the kind of empty that made you feel like you were the only person alive. The paths were empty, the pavilion was dark, the cabins were all closed up and sleeping. The lake was a blue mirror with the sun touching it, and the air was cool enough that he could see his breath, and the whole place felt like it was holding its breath, waiting for the day to start.

Percy walked down to the dock.

He wasn't the only one.

All four of them were already there. Clarisse on the edge with her feet in the water. Silena on the planks with her legs dangling. Thalia on her back with her hands behind her head. Annabeth sitting with her knees up and her notebook open on her shins.

The arrangement. The choice. The thing they'd earned.

Percy sat down in the middle of all of them, and the lake moved under the dock, and the sun came up, and the camp woke up around them, and the day started, and it was good, and it was enough, and it was theirs.

"Percy," Clarisse said.

"Yeah."

"Thanks. For yesterday. For stopping us."

"You don't have to thank me."

"I know. I'm thanking you anyway."

"Percy," Thalia said.

"Yeah."

"Thanks. For being right."

"I'm always right."

"You're not always right."

"I'm always right about you."

Thalia laughed. The laugh carried across the water and came back changed, and the lake kept its silence around the dock with the discretion of a good witness, and the sun came up, and the camp woke up, and the day started, and the five of them sat together in the arrangement they'd earned and chosen and kept, and the world was whole, and the world was theirs, and the world was enough.

That night, Percy went to the Big House to return an equipment roster and found Clarisse arguing with a piece of paper.

She was alone at the long table, a lamp pulled close, with the roster between both hands. Her braid was coming undone, and the dark bruise on her wrist from the afternoon's sparring had been wrapped in clean cloth. She looked tired enough to insult the lamp.

"Is the paper winning?" Percy asked.

"Don't start."

He set his own clipboard down. There were names in two columns on Clarisse's sheet, one marked *arena* and the other *lake watch*. Several had been crossed out hard enough to tear the paper.

"What's wrong with it?"

"Half the kids who volunteered for training switched to the lake watch after that ridiculous argument. They thought if they picked one, they were picking a side. Chiron told me to sort it out."

Percy recognized two names from the breakfast crowd. He'd seen the younger campers watching Clarisse and Thalia with the breathless excitement reserved for fights they didn't understand. He had laughed along at first. Now he looked at the ragged holes in the roster and wasn't amused.

"Could we ask them what duty they actually wanted?"

"We could. They're all at dinner."

"Then we can fix the sheet tomorrow."

"I have to post it tonight."

A knock sounded against the open doorframe. Thalia was there, carrying a folded notebook and looking almost as displeased as Clarisse. Annabeth stood behind her with a box of spare pencils.

"Chiron sent us," Thalia said.

Clarisse held up the roster. "Wonderful. The opposing party has arrived."

"I'm not opposing you tonight."

"You were opposing me at breakfast."

"I was. It was a bad idea to turn a seating argument into a public sport. I know."

Percy expected Clarisse to have something sharp ready. Instead she looked down at the paper. "I made it a sport too."

Thalia put her notebook on the table. The lists didn't match. Percy could see several people assigned to the same place at the same hour.

"Some of the younger campers offered to cover my watch because they thought Clarisse wouldn't want them on her patrol," Thalia said. "They didn't know I wasn't even in charge of this month's roster."

"You're not," Clarisse said. "I am."

"I noticed."

"Good."

Annabeth leaned over the table. "Before either of you starts, can we separate the jobs from the people? There are two watches that actually need coverage, one group lesson, and a couple of campers who shouldn't be assigned alone. That's a logistics problem."

Clarisse took the pencil Annabeth offered, but not the notebook. "I'll make the assignments."

"You're the coordinator," Annabeth said. "I'm not trying to take that away."

Thalia looked at the two damaged rosters. "My name shouldn't have been on the announcements. I can tell Chiron that."

"Do," Clarisse said, and there was still an edge to it.

The five of them worked with the lamps burning low and the camp outside settling into night. Silena arrived halfway through with a bowl of the last dinner rolls. She took one look at the lists, then pointed out that two siblings from Hermes had been scheduled for separate jobs despite having agreed to help each other carry the equipment. Clarisse changed that assignment. Thalia corrected the watch starting time; it had been copied from the previous season. Annabeth checked the number of people needed at each station. Percy recopied the page because Clarisse had broken the pencil point on the first draft.

It took nearly an hour. Nobody made a speech. They also didn't manage not to argue.

When Clarisse objected to putting one inexperienced camper on the south watch, Thalia said he'd volunteered. Clarisse replied that volunteering didn't give him the training. Thalia started to say something about control. Then she looked at the boy's name and the notes beside it.

"You're right," she said. "Put him with me for the first watch. I can show him what to look for."

Clarisse considered her. "If Chiron approves."

"Ask him."

"I will."

Silena ate a roll and moved another toward Percy. "Progress. The two loudest people in camp just decided to consult the person actually in charge."

"Don't write that down," Clarisse told her.

"I don't need to. Annabeth has three copies."

At last the clean roster lay on the table, with a note for Chiron identifying the assignments that still required his approval. The unconfirmed names remained unconfirmed. Annabeth would rather have a blank than a false certainty, and Percy knew she was right.

Thalia stayed at the doorway when the others began carrying the pencils and rolls out.

"About the dock," she said.

Clarisse did not look up. "Which part?"

"Where everyone has been sitting. I wasn't trying to order people around."

"You did."

"I know." Thalia's fingers tightened on the notebook. "I liked knowing Annabeth was beside me. And I acted like the place was mine to assign."

Percy thought Clarisse might apologize in return. She didn't, not immediately.

"I liked being the one who chose our side of the dock," she admitted at last. "When you moved things, I heard it like you were taking a place away."

"I wasn't."

"I know that now. I didn't then."

The silence was long enough to be uncomfortable. Annabeth remained beside Percy, not hiding behind her sister and not volunteering to interpret her either.

"We could leave the seats unassigned," she said.

Clarisse snorted. "That's what a dock is supposed to be."

Thalia smiled a little, though it didn't quite cover her embarrassment. "I might still sit next to you sometimes."

"Sometimes you'll have to ask," Annabeth replied.

Thalia considered her, then nodded. "All right."

It wasn't an agreement that ended every argument. Percy knew better. Clarisse and Thalia would compete again, and neither would suddenly grow patient. But the roster had changed, the campers wouldn't have to choose factions to do chores, and Annabeth had been allowed to speak for herself without either older girl finishing the sentence.

Outside, the path was cool under their shoes. Silena handed Clarisse the remaining roll and said she'd see her at the cabin. Thalia went toward Cabin One with Annabeth, taking the longer path because her sister wanted to stop by the drawing room. Percy accompanied Clarisse part of the way.

"Was that so terrible?" he asked.

"Yes," she said. "There was paperwork."

He laughed. "Did it help?"

"Ask me after tomorrow's watch."

"Fair."

At the cabin steps she gave him the roster copy for Chiron. She had written the names neatly this time, without crossing them out in anger.

"Put that on his desk in the morning," she said. "And don't announce to everyone that the dock has been liberated."

"Was it imprisoned?"

"Percy."

"All right. No announcements."

She went inside. He stayed on the steps a moment, holding the paper, then turned toward the Big House to put it somewhere he wouldn't forget it before breakfast.
