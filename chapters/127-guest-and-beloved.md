## Chapter 127: Guest and Beloved

Several days after their return from the palace weekend, the second invitation from Atlantis rode a current where no current should have been and beached itself on the canoe lake's shore. Percy reached it before any younger camper could mistake it for a conch and try to blow. Against his palm the shell gave its soft, familiar chime.

"Is it a summons?" Annabeth had a camp map pinned under a bowl of apples. The map curled despite the weight; so far the map was winning. Morning sun slanted across the pavilion table, warming the wood beneath their elbows and gilding the edges of the paper. The air smelled of cut grass and bacon from the braziers, and somewhere behind them a Naiad laughed, bright as water striking stone.

"It's from Triton." Percy turned the shell over, tracing the thin blue light circling its rim. "Mother and Father would like to see us again. No court, no formal dinner, no lecture on which spoon goes with pearlfruit."

"That last part sounds like a promise he's made before."

"He says we can come for the afternoon and leave whenever we want." Percy held the shell to his ear. Beneath Triton's voice ran the faint rush of distant water.

"He also says the west reef is calm, the kitchens overdid the sea-plum cake, and if I tell Clarisse there are no weapons involved she'll demand details."

"There are no weapons involved?" Clarisse called from the path. Hearing her name had summoned her in time to sound insulted.

Behind her came Silena with a basket of clean towels. "He didn't say that. He said no court."

"Not the same thing."

"It is if you aren't planning to fight anybody."

"I don't plan to fight anybody," Clarisse said, shifting her spear to the other shoulder. "I plan to be ready in case somebody else does."

Thalia fell in with them, silver jacket on despite the warmth, a note for Chiron in one pocket and a travel pack over her shoulder. "I've got the afternoon free. If that invitation includes a ride back to the surface, I'm in."

"It does," Percy said. "Triton even promises no practice on the greeting."

"He must be ill," Annabeth said.

"Or he's saving it for next time."

"There's a next time?"

Lightly asked — but her hand had stilled on the corner of the map. Percy had learned her smallest questions could be doors; wiser to let her decide whether to open them.

"There can be," he said. "If we want one."

Annabeth studied him, then smoothed the curled edge flat. "All right. I want to go."

They left after lunch. From the lake a patient current bore the five of them toward the coast, cool water combing through hair and tugging hems loose. Guppies scattered before the prow of their passage, silver flickers darting through sun-shot green. The lake floor fell away beneath them, sand giving way to smooth dark stone, and the water pressed cool against ears and cheeks, muffling the world to a soft, even hush. When water closed overhead Annabeth's green glass charm kindled, green-gold against her throat, and Percy kept hold of her hand until the current steadied, feeling the small, steady pulse at her wrist beneath his fingers. Then she let go, remaining close enough for shoulders to brush, her sketchbook sealed tight in its oilskin, its leather corners darkened by spray. By Atlantis, Clarisse had counted the outer guards twice to be sure and Annabeth had charted the arch splitting the current around the palace entrance, pencil moving even as the water moved her, her brow furrowed in the particular, quiet pleasure of working through a puzzle.

---

Pale stone, coral, and living light opened around them, water tasting of salt and jasmine from the gardens. Guards hailed Triton by name, spears tapping stone in greeting, the sound ringing clear and low through the water; an attendant waved at Percy and asked after the camp map, remembering the last visit's stories. The palace remembered him the way a home does — the particular tug of the side current by the stairs, the warm upwelling near the kitchens that breathed against his skin like air from an open oven, the faint mineral smell of sun-warmed marble carried on the flow. Annabeth it had not yet learned, and Percy felt the difference as a small weight behind his ribs.

In the garden court Amphitrite greeted Annabeth as "Lady Annabeth, daughter of Zeus, honored guest," and Poseidon welcomed each of them before council called him away. Warmth, unmistakable. Even so Percy caught Annabeth glancing his way when Triton and Clarisse traded a joke about some old palace corridor. Everyone else carried memories of this place now. Percy carried their years of stories and his own recent visits. Annabeth carried a sketchbook and a few visits — no slight in that, yet distance all the same.

"I'm glad you came again," Amphitrite said. "The garden has been asking when it might show you the open blossoms."

"How does a garden ask?" Annabeth said.

"It changes the color of the path."

Annabeth glanced at her boots, then smiled as though the answer deserved consideration.

The sea-plum cake ran deep violet-blue, studded with pearled fruit. Clarisse took charge of slicing so everyone got a share. Talk drifted from garden to new sparring ring, which Clarisse meant to inspect. Triton told how eight-year-old Percy had walked into a formal meal with an eel tangled in his belt.

"It was holding on," Percy said.

"The eel was trying to return to its habitat," Triton replied.

"I was taking it there."

"After it bit you twice."

Annabeth laughed with the rest, having missed the beginning. When the story ended she folded her napkin into a neat square. Nothing wrong; nobody had excluded her on purpose. Still Percy watched her take in familiar doors and names, mapping a place where everyone else already knew the routes.

Triton teased Percy about getting lost behind the east gate. Percy blamed the wall; Amphitrite observed the wall had been repaired, not moved. Annabeth smiled, then glanced toward the far doorway — a family story to enjoy without a place inside it.

Once the others stood, Percy said, "Want to see a place I haven't shown the group?"

"A secret passage?"

"Not exactly."

"Does it bite?"

"Only if you insult it."

"Then I'll be polite."

He told Triton they'd be on the western reef. "I'm certain the cove will be pleased you remembered it," Triton said.

"It's not a person."

"You used to talk to it."

"The water answers."

"That is a different problem."

Annabeth smothered a smile and followed Percy toward the garden doors.

---

Quiet lay over the west reef. At a guard post Annabeth offered the formal gesture Triton had taught her. Her eyes checked whether the watchman accepted it, not whether she had performed it flawlessly.

"He did," Percy said once they passed.

"How can you tell?"

"He stopped watching your hands."

"That's a very Atlantis answer."

Between coral shelves the path narrowed, water cooling as the reef shaded it, small shrimp clicking among polyps. Then the way ducked beneath a ribbon-kelp curtain, fronds brushing cheeks and shoulders like beaded strings, cool and slick, leaving beads of moisture on skin and a faint iodine tang in the water. Beyond, the water stilled to glass. The reef hollowed into a cove hidden from the main paths, sound itself seeming to settle into a held breath. Pale shells scattered its floor in drifts, pink and cream catching the filtered light; along the far wall luminous coral burned green and blue, pulsing faintly with the surge, casting moving lace on sand that shifted with each slow swell.

"You found this as a kid?" Annabeth asked.

"Triton says I hid here after I lost his practice trident."

"Where was it?"

"In here."

"Then you didn't lose it."

"That was my argument. It didn't work."

They slipped under the kelp. According to Triton, this cove had been Percy's retreat from lessons, council, anyone addressing him as prince. Since returning he had found quiet here himself. Never before had he brought Annabeth here.

"You don't have to explain why you brought me," Annabeth said.

"I wanted you to see somewhere outside the palace tour. Mine, in a way I don't know how to explain."

She studied the glowing wall. "Somewhere nobody expects you to be the prince."

"Exactly."

They settled on a flat ledge. After a moment Annabeth said, "I'm trying to work out what I'm supposed to be here. Everyone knows who you were as a little boy. The guards know your old routes; Amphitrite knows what you were like sick; Triton knows every story. Clarisse and Silena are making their own memories now. I'm glad. I only don't have any of mine yet."

"You have the garden and the reef path."

"Things I saw. Not years."

"No," Percy said. "Not years. You came as a guest, and you're new here. I won't pretend that's the same as being born to it."

"I don't want you to."

"But you get to decide what Atlantis means to you. No need to fit yourself into my old life. I can show you the cove; the rest can grow between us."

Shoulder to his shoulder, Annabeth leaned in. "I don't need Atlantis for a first home. I only don't want to stand at the edge of yours, waiting to be let in."

"You're here," he said. "Not waiting."

She stayed close. "I want to understand the parts of your life that still hurt."

"Sally used to make blue cookies," Percy said.

"I know. I don't mind hearing it again."

He watched light travel the coral. "Somebody once told her blue food was silly. She decided that meant making more. Even tired, she'd dye the dough till the kitchen looked like the sky had fallen in."

"What did that mean to you?"

"That I didn't have to earn the fun parts. A day could be hard and still come with a ridiculous cookie. She didn't make everything all right. She made clear I mattered while it wasn't."

Vanilla and warm sugar drifted up, rain on a windowsill — the boy from another life surfacing with the smell, ache and all, without swallowing the whole hour. For a heartbeat Percy could feel the cracked linoleum under his socks, hear the radiator's tick, see flour dust hanging gold in the afternoon light while Sally hummed off-key and nudged the tray with her hip to close the oven door.

"I used to think missing Sally was unfair to Amphitrite," he said. "Like room for only one mother. Amphitrite never asked me to stop missing her."

"The cookies remember how Sally loved you," Annabeth said.

"Yeah. Not proof everything was better then, or a recipe for going back. Only proof she was there, and loved me in the small ways she could."

"You're allowed to miss her here and love Amphitrite too. One doesn't thin the other."

"I know."

"Sometimes knowing isn't enough. I can remind you, if you want."

"I'd like that."

After a quiet stretch Percy asked, "What did the Athena cabin mean to you?"

Annabeth told him about the flat roof above its library window. At seven, sleepless, she had found it; from there the camp paths lay visible and the cabins shrank small enough to comprehend. Sketchbook in hand, she had planned renovations — bigger windows, a reading loft, a dumbwaiter for midnight snacks.

"The cabin meant what I noticed could become something," she said. "I belonged because the way my mind worked made sense to people."

"And you miss it."

"Sometimes. I miss the cabin and the people, but I wouldn't trade Thalia or this life for it. I can miss it and still love Cabin One. Carry it without living there."

Percy thought of Sally's kitchen and this cove, the home remembered and the home raising him now. Erasing one to love the other had never been required.

"What would make Atlantis feel more like somewhere you can return to?" he asked. "Not what I decide. What you want."

She considered. "A map."

"Thought you'd say that."

"Not a palace map. A map of what I've seen and want to remember. Paths, gardens, currents. Places people actually use. Not only the important rooms."

"You can draw one."

"I'd like to."

"We can ask Amphitrite for an old garden chart."

"I'll make my own. I'll ask which places to leave out, and I won't draw anyone's private room without permission."

"Very responsible."

"Also interesting. Atlantis is built for water moving through it. I want to see how the paths change."

"And I can show you where to start."

"You could." She met his eyes. "If you wanted."

"I want to."

For the first time since leaving the palace, the tension left her hands entirely. From her tunic pocket she drew a small pencil, short enough to hide in her palm, yellow paint worn smooth where her fingers gripped it, a thin bronze ring circling the end. Percy recognized a sketching pencil, sharpened to a careful point.

"I meant to give you this earlier," she said. "Then the timing felt awkward, and I wasn't sure it would make sense."

"What is it?"

"A pencil."

"I got that part."

"The one I used to draw the garden at camp. The first sketch I showed you after Atlantis."

"The one with Clarisse by the clearing?"

"Yes. I kept the finished drawing, but I thought you should have the pencil." She rolled it once between her fingers. "Good weight. Small enough to carry everywhere. And it works without being perfect."

"You're giving me your pencil so I can make a map?"

"I'm giving you a pencil because I want you to have something from how I see things. No need to use it for a map."

Percy accepted it carefully. Warmth from her fingers still clung to the wood, and the worn yellow paint felt smooth as river stone against his calluses. Small thing — yet it felt like trust with a piece of how she moved through the world, the faint graphite smudge near the tip carrying the smell of cedar and paper.

"I'll try not to lose it."

"You get one loss. After that I charge."

"What if I draw a fish?"

"Then the fish should be accurate."

"That's a lot of pressure."

"You'll manage."

He tucked the pencil into the small pocket inside his belt. Annabeth watched, expression softening. Percy wanted to offer something in return, something more considered than an answer. He reached for the cord around his wrist. At its end hung a pale shell, worn smooth, small as the pad of his thumb, its spiral faintly blue at the center.

Years ago he had found it in this cove, before he understood the place would become his secret. Loose from the reef, carried into the hollow. Through room moves and surface journeys he had kept it, never certain what for. It had waited in his room until this visit.

"I brought you something too," he said.

Annabeth eyed the shell. "From the reef?"

"Came loose a long time ago. Nothing living now. I found it in this cove."

"You kept it?"

"I did." He untied the cord from his wrist. "Thought you might like it for a pendant. If you do. If not, it can stay a shell."

She held out her hand. He laid it in her palm. Her fingers closed, gentle and curious. Light caught the spiral. Nothing marked her Atlantean or asked her to take a title. Only a piece of the cove, offered because he wanted her to have it.

"I like it," she said. "Thank you."

"It doesn't mean you have to come back."

"I know."

"It doesn't mean you have to like every part of Atlantis."

"I know that too."

"It only means I wanted to give it to you."

"Then I'll keep it because you wanted me to have it." She lifted the cord. "Will you help me put it on?"

He moved behind her, careful to brush her neck no more than necessary, his own breath held so as not to stir the fine hairs at her nape. The cord settled beneath her charm, its fibers rough then softening under his fingertips; he tied a simple knot, tugging it snug with a small, deliberate care. Annabeth reached up to feel the shell, its surface cool and ridged beneath her touch. Against the sea-glass it rested, two small pieces of different homes together, clicking faintly when she tilted her chin.

"There," Percy said.

"There."

At the entrance a current stirred the ribbon-kelp. Percy turned. Beyond the opening Triton waited, one hand raised where they could see him. He did not step through until Annabeth nodded.

"I was told this was private," Triton said. "So I have not entered."

"Considerate of you," Annabeth said.

"I can be." His gaze moved to the shell at her throat, then to Percy's belt. "The cove's collection has grown."

"It was already in my room," Percy said. "I brought it today."

"A subtle distinction."

"An important one."

Triton leaned against the coral at the entrance. "Mother asked me to say the training hollow is ready whenever you are. Clarisse has inspected it and declared the floor acceptable, which is high honor."

"It is not high honor," Clarisse called from somewhere outside. "It is barely acceptable."

"She's been waiting ten minutes," Silena added. "She says she isn't waiting, but she keeps checking the door."

"I can hear you," Clarisse said.

"I know," Silena replied. "That's why I said it loudly."

Triton smiled. "I also came to see if you were both all right."

"We're fine," Percy said.

"Good. Then I can return to my duties without accusation of spying."

"Were you spying?" Annabeth asked.

"No. I was standing outside the cove, being extremely respectful."

"That's a very specific defense."

"I have practice."

Starting away, he paused. "Percy."

"Yeah?"

"I'm glad you showed her this place."

Teasing dropped from his voice, only for a moment. Triton included Annabeth in his look. "You don't need my permission to care about my brother. I know you don't. But you have my blessing, if that word matters. Both of you. You've been careful with each other, and you make room for the parts that came before. That's a good way to keep going."

Percy had no ready answer. Until Triton spoke, he hadn't known he needed to hear it. "Thanks."

"And if he ever gets lost on the way out, you may bring him back by the collar. He did it for years."

"I never got lost."

"You did once in the kitchen."

"The kitchen had three doors."

"You went through the pantry."

"It was a door."

Annabeth gave Percy a sympathetic look. "I'm sure that made sense at the time."

"It did."

"Of course." Triton swam off with a shake of his head, smiling still. Behind him the ribbon-kelp drifted shut.

Clarisse and Silena waited with Thalia by the outer path. Silena had brought a small lanternfish in a glass bowl, though Amphitrite assured her the fish swam free in the reef and followed only because it liked her hair. Clarisse stood arms crossed, spear tucked beneath one elbow.

"You found the cove," Silena said.

Annabeth touched the pendant. "I did."

Clarisse looked from it to Percy. "You showed her the hiding place."

"It's not a hiding place," Percy said.

"You hid from Triton there."

"One time."

"You hid from Mother there too," Triton called from farther down the path.

"I was looking at coral."

"You were supposed to be in the dining room."

"I was looking at coral near the dining room."

Silena laughed. "You always make the story worse."

"It wasn't a story till Triton started telling it."

Clarisse met Annabeth straight on. "How was it?"

"Quiet," Annabeth said. "And beautiful."

"That's the short version."

"The longer version is Percy has a secret place where nobody expects him to be a prince."

Clarisse weighed that. "Good. Everyone needs one."

"I'm glad he showed me."

"You should be." Clarisse's mouth twitched. "He doesn't show it to people often."

"I know."

Silena glanced from Annabeth's pendant to Percy's pocket, where the pencil made a small shape under the cloth. She chose not to ask about either. "Training hollow's ready. Clarisse says the new ring is too soft."

"It is too soft," Clarisse said. "They've padded it so nobody can get proper footing."

"That sounds like a safety feature," Annabeth said.

"That's what I told her," Silena replied. "She said safety matters, but people also need to learn how to land."

"Both can be true," Annabeth said.

Clarisse nodded once. "Exactly."

They started toward the palace. Percy fell in beside Annabeth while the others walked ahead. For a while they listened to Silena explain the lanternfish and Clarisse argue against naming it after a weapon. Thalia stayed quiet, yet close enough to hear everything.

At the garden's edge Silena slowed to walk with Annabeth. "Can I tell you something without making it bigger than it is?"

"Yes."

"The first time we came here, Clarisse and I felt like guests too. Not because anyone treated us badly. They were kind. But everybody knew palace and rules, and we didn't know what being daughters of Poseidon meant here."

Over her shoulder Clarisse said, "I knew what it meant. I wasn't sure I wanted it."

"You still don't have to decide."

"I know." Clarisse kept walking, pace easing enough to hold together. "Point is, we didn't arrive already knowing where to stand. We came. Then we got to decide what we wanted."

"And we decided we wanted to come back," Silena said. "Maybe not every time, and maybe not for the same reasons. But we wanted to know this part of Percy's life. Now it's one of the places we have memories."

Annabeth listened, thumb moving over the pendant at her throat.

"You don't have to be Poseidon's daughter to be ours," Clarisse said. "You're already Thalia's sister. You're Percy's person. But we're not saying you count because you're attached to somebody. We choose you because you're you."

Silena nodded. "I think of you as my sister. No need to call me that if it feels strange. But that's what I mean when I say I want you here. Not as Percy's favor. Not as a guest we must be polite to. As Annabeth."

Percy's throat tightened. He looked at Annabeth without answering for her. She deserved room to take the words in herself.

"Thank you," she said. Quiet voice, steady. "I don't know what I'm ready to call everything yet. Still learning what this place is to me. But I'd like to come back. And I'd like to keep knowing you both."

"That's enough," Silena said.

"It's a good start," Clarisse added. "Nobody gets a title for showing up. You have to earn it."

Silena frowned at her. "That's not what I meant."

"I know." Clarisse bumped her shoulder against Silena's. "I'm teasing."

"You're not very good at it."

"I'm excellent at it."

Annabeth laughed, sound carrying into the garden. Beside him Percy felt something ease in her. Not every question answered; the day hadn't made her a child of the sea or handed her a lifetime of memories. It had made room for uncertainty without leaving her to stand alone in it.

---

Before they returned to the training hollow, Percy found a small group waiting at the west-garden entrance. The palace steward had set out a line of wooden tiles and asked them to stand behind it while the maintenance workers examined a cracked stone above the arch. A section of the ceiling had shifted after a hard tide.

"Is that the only route?" Annabeth asked.

"No," the steward said. "The eastern corridor is open, though it takes longer."

"We can take it."

"You are invited guests. We could clear the western entrance for you first."

Annabeth looked at the cracked stone. "Please don't. If it needs checking, it needs checking."

Percy felt a flash of pride in her, followed by an uncomfortable realization: the steward had nearly treated their visit as more important than the people who would use the corridor all week. He'd offered to send workers into a possibly unsafe passage just to spare them a walk.

"Is the eastern route safe?" Percy asked.

"Yes. It was inspected after the last tide."

"Then that's our route."

Triton appeared from the opposite end of the hall. He read the situation quickly, spoke to the steward, then turned back to the group.

"I should have told you the western entrance was being checked," he said. "The damage is minor, but we haven't cleared the arch."

"Then nobody goes through it," Clarisse said.

"Agreed."

They took the longer path. It curved around an old courtyard where pale water moved over stones cut with the names of gardeners who had cared for the reef in earlier generations. Silena stopped to read several. Some were not royal names; they belonged to attendants and workers whose lives had been tied to this place.

"Do people outside the palace know about these?" she asked.

"They can visit when the public garden is open," Triton said.

"Then I'd like to know who decides which parts are public."

"The household gardeners."

"Not your father?"

"He doesn't inspect the visitor routes."

Clarisse snorted. "That much I believe."

Percy walked on with Amphitrite, who had come to meet them at the courtyard gate. She wore no crown. The ends of her sleeves moved softly in the current.

"Are the records from last time corrected?" he asked.

"Yes. I checked them myself. I also asked the steward how often guests were assigned a relationship without being consulted."

"What did he say?"

"More often than he should have."

"Was he offended?"

"He was embarrassed. That is different."

They reached a low bench near a row of sea lilies. Amphitrite paused there, giving the others room to examine the carved names.

"I do not want you to think that the correction means I'm withdrawing my affection," she said.

Percy was surprised by the question. "I don't."

"Your sisters were right to choose their own names. You were right to ask what yours meant. I can love the people you bring here without treating them as mine to claim."

He looked toward Clarisse, who was arguing with Triton about whether the cracked arch should have been caught at the previous inspection. Silena was taking notes on the courtyard plants. Annabeth had gone ahead with Thalia to measure a narrow step.

"I wish I could remember what it was like here," Percy said.

"I know."

"Sometimes I think if I remembered it, everything would feel easier."

"It might make some things easier," Amphitrite answered. "It would make other things more complicated. But the missing years are not a debt you owe me."

The garden smelled faintly of warm stone and salt. Percy stood beside her, uncertain what to say. He was used to adults trying to fill his silences with reassuring conclusions.

Amphitrite didn't. She waited until he spoke.

"I want to come back," he said. "Not because I have to make up for those years."

"Then come when you want to."

"And Clarisse and Silena?"

"They may come when they want to. You needn't act as their interpreter."

He grinned. "They'd be furious if I tried."

"I have met them."

By the time they caught up with the others, the steward had returned with an inspection slip showing that the western arch was still closed. Triton read it and signed the entry as witness rather than ordering the closure lifted.

Clarisse checked the seal. "Good. I don't care how old the palace is. Cracked stone is cracked stone."

"Are you always this cheerful?" Triton asked.

"Only when everyone follows basic safety rules."

Silena nudged Percy. "We're not guests who have to earn affection, are we?"

"No," he said.

"Good. Because I want to see the training hollow, but I also want to be able to go home without someone treating that as rejection."

He glanced at Amphitrite. She'd heard, and her response was a simple nod.

They continued through the safe corridor. Percy could feel the palace opening around him, neither completely strange nor completely familiar. It was a place where old records could be corrected and ancient stone could be closed for repair. He thought he trusted it more for admitting those limitations.


---

At the training hollow Clarisse tested the new floor with two hard steps, dust puffing around her boots, and pronounced it "usable." Silena took Annabeth to see the garden's open blossoms, petals wide as hands and smelling of honey and brine, while Thalia asked Triton how currents would shift with the next tide, arms folded against the cool upwelling. From the path Percy watched, pencil in his pocket and the cove's quiet still with him, the stone warm through his sandals.

After a while Annabeth returned carrying one fallen flower. Instead of pocketing it, she asked Amphitrite whether sketching was allowed. Amphitrite said yes, and showed where the flower had grown. Annabeth opened her notebook and drew carefully, leaving space around it for how the current moved.

Percy sat beside her while she worked. He named two nearby coral branches, then stopped when she said she wanted to guess first. He watched her take her time. When one name came out wrong, he gave her the answer and she wrote it down without crossing out her guess.

They stayed until the outer reef darkened and luminous coral began to show. The return current waited, but Amphitrite invited one more walk along the terrace before departure. The whole family went: Poseidon by then, council behind him; Triton with Silena; Clarisse and Thalia comparing the new sparring ring's strength; Amphitrite talking with Annabeth about the garden chart she wanted to make.

---

When they reached the terrace the others continued toward the palace, sandals slapping and voices thinning with distance. Percy and Annabeth lagged behind together, in no hurry to trade quiet for hall noise, the stone balustrade still holding the day's warmth under their palms. Below, the reef shone in branching lines, pale blue and green and silver, corals brightening one after another as light faded, each colony kindling in sequence like lamps lit down a street. Tiny fish flashed between branches, scale-glints winking and vanishing. Farther out, darkness held the sea's edge, deep and patient, and the current breathed faintly against the terrace foot, cool and steady.

Annabeth stood at the low wall, both hands on its smooth stone. The shell pendant lay over the green charm at her throat. Her sketchbook tucked under one arm; a smudge of blue marked the side of her hand.

"You have the pencil?" she asked.

Percy touched the pocket where he'd put it. "Right here."

"Good."

"I'll use it carefully."

"You can use it however you want. Only don't sharpen it with a sword."

"I have never sharpened a pencil with a sword."

"You looked like you were considering it."

"I was thinking how it could work."

"That's worse."

He laughed. She smiled, and quiet returned, easy this time.

"I still feel like a guest," she said. "But not only a guest."

"That sounds right."

"I think I can be both."

"You can."

"And I can be the person you choose, not only the person who came with you."

"You are."

Annabeth turned toward him. "I choose you too. Not because I need you to make Atlantis mine, or because you're the only person who understands the strange parts. I choose you because you're you. And I like who I am with you."

Percy looked at the reef, then back at her. No promise to fix every distance lay in the words. Something they could keep choosing, one day and one visit at a time.

"I choose you," he said. "I want to show you where I've been, and I want to learn the places that matter to you. No need to become part of my past to belong beside me now."

"Good," she said. "Because I'm not very interested in being a story you already know."

"I like the parts I don't know yet."

"Convenient."

"True."

She set her hand beside his on the wall. Their fingers touched. Percy turned his palm up, and Annabeth slid her hand into his. He held it gently, thumb resting over her knuckles, while the luminous reef brightened beneath them.

From the palace Triton's voice carried. "If you two are done being mysterious, the current is leaving without us."

"The current will wait," Percy called.

"It will not. It has other places to go."

"Then it can come back."

"That's not how currents work."

"I've seen it happen."

Annabeth squeezed his hand. "We should go before he sends a search party."

"He wouldn't."

"He has Clarisse."

"True."

They turned toward the others, still hand in hand. Beneath them the reef shone, a bright place with no throne and no audience, and beyond it the cove waited, no longer his alone. Annabeth carried her own Atlantis memories now — not years, not roots laid before they met, but an afternoon's shape, a shell against her heart, and a place where invitation asked her to become nobody else.

Percy carried her pencil. She carried his shell. Neither gift asked either to stay. Both gave reason to return.
