## Chapter 199: A Bed Beside the Window

Annabeth woke when the blanket over the window shifted.

It was not the bell. The cabin had not reached the hour for that yet. The wind had found the narrow opening in the frame and worried the cloth, puffing it toward the room, then letting it drop again. The uncovered strip of glass showed a low gray sky and the dark end of the ridge.

Thalia was awake too. She sat at the table with her splint-braced foot on the cushion and a cup between both hands. Her hair had come loose from the tie at the back of her head. She looked at Annabeth, then at the window.

“It’s still raining,” she said.

“Not much.”

“That’s usually how it starts.”

Annabeth pushed back the blanket. It was damp at the bottom where the cloth had touched the sill. She lifted the edge, checked the frame, and pinned it higher with the wooden clip they kept on the shelf.

“Is it better?” Thalia asked.

“It’s not dripping.”

“Great.”

“That wasn’t what you asked.”

Thalia glanced at her. “It’s fine.”

Annabeth looked at the splint. The heel rested on the cushion. Thalia’s other foot was tucked under the chair. Annabeth had learned that the details did not tell her how much pain Thalia was in, only that Thalia had chosen the chair and the cushion.

“Do you want me to bring the kettle closer?” she asked.

“I can reach it.”

“I know.”

The words came out in the same tone Thalia had used the morning before, and Annabeth heard the echo. Thalia’s mouth moved at one corner.

“See? You can learn.”

“I learned that from you.”

“That’s not something I’d brag about.”

Annabeth put the kettle on the table between them. Thalia thanked her and poured her own tea. They had not finished the discussion about Ianthe. They had not finished several others. The cabin seemed to hold the parts of those conversations in the spaces between the beds and the table.

On the shelf, the folded weather chart lay beside the map. The paper had taken on the smell of charcoal. One arrow pointed north; the shorter one bent toward the hill. The note beside it said *watch again tomorrow*. Annabeth had not decided whether tomorrow meant today. She let the paper stay folded.

There was still room to walk from the door to the table. A bed would change that. The window side was already narrow. If they pulled the table toward the stove, the door would have a better path, but the chair would scrape against the bed. If they moved the bed toward the wall, Thalia would have to turn sideways to get to her side. Annabeth could make a plan from the room as it was. She could also make a plan that left no room for another person to say no.

A knock came at the door.

Annabeth looked at Thalia. Thalia did not rise.

“Do you want me to get it?” Annabeth asked.

“No. You can.”

Annabeth opened the door. Percy stood on the porch with a paper sack in one hand and his hair wet on the side facing the rain. He held the sack away from his coat as if the food had asked him to keep it dry.

“Hi,” he said. “I brought breakfast.”

“Is it breakfast?”

“It’s food that was near the kitchen.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“It has a roll in it.”

“What kind?”

“I didn’t ask.”

“Percy.”

“I was trying to bring you something before the rain got worse.”

His eyes flicked to her face and back, checking whether she was teasing or irritated. Annabeth took the sack from him.

“Come in,” she said.

He glanced past her toward Thalia. “Is that okay?”

“Of course,” Thalia said.

Percy came inside and shut the door carefully. He set his coat on the empty hook, then stood by the table until Annabeth had made space for the sack.

She opened it. There were two plain rolls and a small paper packet of salted almonds. One roll had a bite missing.

“Did you eat this?”

“I was checking it.”

“You checked the whole side?”

“It’s an irregular roll.”

Thalia took her tea back to the chair. “A serious problem.”

“I can go get another one,” Percy said.

“No,” Annabeth replied. “I can have the side you didn’t check.”

He sat beside her. Their knees touched under the table. He rested his hand palm up near hers, leaving the choice open. Annabeth turned her hand over and fitted her fingers against his.

“The map moved,” he said.

“The map didn’t move.”

“It’s farther from the window.”

“It was getting damp.”

“Then the window moved.”

“Would you like to inspect the window?”

“No. I believe you.”

“Good.”

He squeezed her fingers once. Annabeth lifted the roll from the sack and tore it in two. The inside was still warm. She passed him the larger piece.

“You take it,” he said.

“I chose the smaller one.”

“That doesn’t mean you have to.”

“It means I chose it.”

He accepted the larger piece. No argument followed. She had not handed him a test, and he had not turned the bread into a question about how much she wanted. They ate while the rain tapped the roof and Thalia watched the strip of sky.

Percy looked at the weather chart when Annabeth reached for the almonds. “Did she get the wind right?”

“Part of it,” Annabeth said. “The hill changed the direction.”

“She marked both.”

“After Thalia pointed it out.”

“That sounds like a useful chart.”

“It could be.”

“Could be?”

“It’s one morning.”

“Fair.”

He nudged her foot beneath the table. Annabeth nudged back. Their private jokes usually required less explanation than their maps. That did not mean they agreed about every line on one.

“Chiron asked me to come by after breakfast,” Percy said. “He’s moving the dry blankets out of the west room before the next rain. The path between the shelves is blocked.”

“Do you need help?”

“He asked the older campers to make a clear route. I can carry things.”

“Are you asking me to come?”

“I was going to, if you wanted to.”

She considered the weather chart, then the room. “I can come for a little while.”

“Only if you want to.”

“I do.”

“Okay.”

He lifted her hand and kissed the knuckle nearest his thumb. Annabeth let her shoulder rest against his for a moment. The warmth of his shirt was easier to trust than the gray light outside.

A second knock sounded.

Percy let go of her hand. Annabeth stood and crossed the room. Thalia stayed in the chair. Annabeth opened the door.

Ianthe stood on the porch without her bag. Her hair was tied back again. A folded sheet of paper stuck out of her coat pocket, and she held both hands close to her sides, as if keeping them from reaching for anything.

“Can I come in?” she asked.

Annabeth looked at her, then at the door behind her. The question was small. It did not answer the other one.

“Yes,” she said. “We’re talking about the room.”

“I want to talk about the bed.”

“We can talk about it.”

Ianthe waited.

Annabeth moved away from the doorway. She did not step aside far enough to suggest the room belonged to someone else now.

Ianthe crossed the threshold. “I’ve drawn two arrangements.”

“Did you ask Chiron before changing the room?”

“I haven’t changed anything.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

“No. I didn’t ask.”

“Then we can look at the drawings. We won’t move anything today unless we all agree.”

Ianthe’s eyes went to the window, then to the two beds. “I don’t want to move your things.”

“Good.”

“I want to know what space there is.”

“That’s fair.”

Ianthe took the paper from her pocket but did not unfold it yet. “Can I use the table?”

“After breakfast,” Annabeth said. “We’re still eating.”

Ianthe’s gaze moved to Percy and the paper sack. “I can wait.”

It sounded like she wanted them to notice that she was waiting.

Percy folded the empty wrapper and set it by his plate. “We’ll be a minute.”

“I can wait longer.”

Annabeth glanced at him. He had already picked up the almonds. “We are eating,” she said.

Ianthe nodded once, too sharply, and remained by the door.

Annabeth sat again. Percy stayed beside her. The room had changed without any furniture moving: Ianthe was inside it, and Annabeth was aware of every path between the table and the window.

---

Ianthe remained by the door until Percy had finished the roll. He offered her the almonds. She shook her head, then watched the packet pass to Annabeth.

“You can sit,” Annabeth said.

“I’m fine standing.”

“Then stay there.”

Ianthe looked at the two beds again. She had come without her bag, so the room held none of her things. The folded paper was the only part of her that seemed to have arrived ahead of her.

Percy wiped his fingers on the napkin. “I should go help Chiron.”

Annabeth looked up. “You said after breakfast.”

“It’s after breakfast.”

“You’ve had half a roll.”

“That’s still breakfast.”

“It’s a light breakfast.”

“I can eat more at the Big House.”

“Can you?”

“I’ll see what they have.”

He collected his coat from the hook. Before he left, he leaned down and kissed Annabeth’s temple. She turned her face enough to catch his mouth instead. He smiled against her, then gave her a second, briefer kiss.

“I’ll be back if you want me,” he said.

“I know.”

“Do you want me?”

“Not for the room.”

He did not ask what she meant by that. “All right. I’ll be in the west room.”

Annabeth watched him go, then set the paper sack on the shelf. Ianthe had waited. She had also watched the kiss.

“You two talk a lot without explaining,” Ianthe said.

“Sometimes.”

“Is that because you’ve known each other longer?”

“Some of it.”

“Does that mean I’ll always be left out?”

The question came as if it had a correct answer Annabeth was supposed to find. She could have answered no. She could have told Ianthe that the cabin did not have a fixed number of people, that there were other half-siblings, that no one had to share every conversation. Any of those answers would have asked her to promise something she did not know.

“I don’t know yet,” she said.

Ianthe’s chin lifted. “That’s not very welcoming.”

“I’m not trying to welcome you with a promise.”

Thalia’s cup made a faint tap against the table. Annabeth looked at her. Thalia kept her eyes on the tea.

Ianthe unfolded the page. “These are two options.”

The first sketch had three beds, though only two were drawn in the room. Ianthe had used the table as a rectangle and the window as a line. She had placed a third narrow bed under the uncovered strip of glass, with the head against the wall and the foot toward the door. On the second drawing, the table was moved to the opposite wall and the beds arranged in a row.

“Where did the third bed come from?” Annabeth asked.

“Chiron said we could discuss what you need.”

“He didn’t say there was a bed.”

“He has guest beds.”

“Those are in the Big House.”

“They’re beds.”

“Different rooms.”

Ianthe pointed to the page. “There’s enough space.”

“Did you measure?”

“I used the boards.”

“Which boards?”

“The floorboards. I counted them.”

Annabeth leaned over the page. She counted the boards too. The distance from the window to the end of the bed was smaller than Ianthe had drawn. One plank was cut short by the wall. The next had a dark knot that made it difficult to see where the seam ended.

“You counted this one as full length,” Annabeth said.

“It is a full board.”

“It’s not a full space. The wall cuts it off.”

Ianthe leaned closer. “That doesn’t change the distance to the bed.”

“It does if you’re using the board as your measurement.”

She touched the lines with her fingertip. “I can measure it properly.”

“Good.”

“I didn’t say you were right.”

“You can measure it.”

Ianthe looked at Thalia. “Do you want the bed?”

Thalia set down her cup. “That’s not my decision.”

“You live here.”

“Yes.”

“Then what do you want?”

“To keep my bed where it is.”

“Because of the heel?”

“Because it’s my bed.”

Ianthe’s fingers tightened on the page. “The new one can go by the window.”

“Why there?” Annabeth asked.

“I want to see the weather.”

“From the Big House, you can see the ridge.”

“Not from the guest room.”

“You could open the window.”

“I want to know when the wind changes.”

Annabeth heard the desire in the answer, but it did not make the plan hers to approve. “We leave that window open because it lets us see the weather from the cabin. I sleep under it.”

“You can move.”

“I don’t want to.”

“You don’t own the window.”

“I didn’t say I did.”

“Then what is the problem?”

“You’re planning where I should sleep in a room that isn’t yours yet.”

Ianthe’s eyes sharpened. “It’s Cabin One.”

“It’s also where Thalia and I live.”

“I know.”

“Then ask before you draw our beds moving.”

“I’m trying to make space for myself.”

“You can do that without moving us around.”

“How?”

“By asking.”

Ianthe gave a short, humorless laugh. “You keep saying that.”

“Because you keep not doing it.”

Thalia’s fingers flexed around the handle of her cup. Annabeth glanced toward her, and Thalia met her eyes. She did not come between them.

Ianthe lowered the drawings. “You’re angry.”

“I’m not.”

“You sound angry.”

“I’m telling you what I don’t want.”

“That sounds like anger.”

“It can be both.”

Ianthe looked at the paper. “I thought having sisters meant I wouldn’t have to ask for every inch.”

“Having sisters means you have to ask us.”

“That’s a rule?”

“It’s how this room works.”

“Did Thalia ask you before she moved in?”

“I don’t remember living anywhere else.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the one I have.”

Ianthe held the paper at its edges. The corners had started to curl from the damp in her coat pocket.

Annabeth pointed to the lower drawing. “We can measure it together, if you want. But we’re not deciding a bed today.”

“Why not?”

“Because I don’t know if a third bed fits. I don’t know what Chiron has available. And I don’t know if we can share a room.”

“You don’t know if you want me here.”

“I want to know you.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No.”

Ianthe stared at her. The honest answer was not the gentle one. Annabeth had tried gentleness before and watched people use it as agreement.

“What if I can fit?” Ianthe asked.

“Then we’ll know a bed can fit.”

“And if we agree on a place?”

“Then we’ll know where it could go.”

“You keep moving the answer farther away.”

“I’m telling you what we can decide now.”

Ianthe’s face flushed. “Father said his children belong in Cabin One.”

“He did?”

“Yes.”

“Did he tell you where the bed would go?”

“No.”

“Then he didn’t decide that part.”

Ianthe looked away. Annabeth knew the temptation to say more: that a god’s words could not replace the person whose space he was claiming, that Zeus had never been the one to make a bed feel safe. She kept it to herself. Ianthe did not know the history behind the sentence, and Annabeth would not use it as a weapon.

“I can show you the room measurements,” she said. “If you want to start there.”

“I already have them.”

“You have estimates.”

“I can be exact.”

“Then use the tape measure.”

Annabeth took the small one from the drawer. She set it on the table, beside the drawings, and waited.

Ianthe did not reach for it.

Thalia adjusted her foot on the cushion. “I can hold the other end if you need.”

Annabeth glanced at her. “I’ll ask if I need help.”

Thalia nodded. “Okay.”

Ianthe watched them. “You do that a lot.”

“Do what?”

“Ask before helping.”

“I’m trying to.”

“Does it make everything take longer?”

“Sometimes.”

“Does it make it better?”

Annabeth looked at the strip of glass, the bed, and the window blanket she had clipped that morning. “Sometimes.”

Ianthe picked up the tape. “All right. We can measure.”

She took the end toward the window. Annabeth held the other. The distance was two inches less than Ianthe’s drawing allowed. The third bed would fit only if the table shifted and the clear path from the door narrowed.

Ianthe wrote the number down. “It still fits.”

“It fits on paper,” Annabeth said.

“It fits in the room.”

“It might.”

“What else do you need?”

“I need to walk it.”

Annabeth moved the chair out of the way and traced the path from the door to the table, then from the table to Thalia’s bed. The narrow strip passed between the proposed bed and the chair. She could turn sideways. Thalia would have to step over the corner of the rug.

“Thalia can’t step there easily right now,” Ianthe said.

Annabeth looked at the splint. “She can walk.”

“I didn’t say she couldn’t.”

“You put the bed in the way.”

“I can move it.”

“Not without asking her.”

Thalia stood slowly, using the table for balance. She waited until Annabeth looked at her.

“Do you want me to walk it?” she asked.

“Yes.”

Thalia crossed the room along the current path, placed her sound foot around the rug, then turned back. “The chair can move. The rug can’t.”

“We could fold it,” Ianthe said.

“I don’t want to.”

“Why?”

“Because it’s mine.”

Ianthe’s expression tightened again. Annabeth could see the answer coming, and she could feel herself prepare to interrupt. She pressed her fingers against the table edge and stayed quiet.

“Fine,” Ianthe said. “The bed could go on the other side.”

“Then it blocks the cupboard,” Annabeth said.

“I can draw another plan.”

“Later.”

“Why later?”

“Because you asked to talk about the bed. We did. We don’t have to finish it today.”

“I want to know where I’ll sleep.”

“I understand.”

“Do you?”

“I know what you said.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No.”

The answer stopped Ianthe for a moment. Annabeth could not tell if she had expected an argument or an apology. She folded the tape measure and put it beside the charcoal.

“Chiron can tell you what beds are available,” she said. “We can look at the room again after that.”

Ianthe folded her drawing along the crease she had already made. “I’ll ask him.”

“Good.”

She took the paper to the door. “Can I leave this here?”

“On the table.”

“I know where the table is.”

Annabeth looked at her. “Then leave it there.”

Ianthe set the drawing down. She placed it beside the weather chart, not on top of it.

Thalia resumed her seat. Annabeth moved the tape measure back into the drawer.

“Do you want to go to the west room?” she asked.

“Chiron asked me to help.”

“I mean now.”

“I can help later.”

“Okay.”

Thalia looked at her cup. “You did fine.”

“I don’t know if I did.”

“You didn’t promise her a bed.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“I know.”

Annabeth looked at the drawings. “She might think I don’t want her because of him.”

“Do you?”

“I don’t know.”

“That’s fair.”

“It doesn’t feel fair.”

“It can be fair and still feel awful.”

Annabeth folded the paper more carefully than Ianthe had. She had not decided whether the drawings belonged in the room. For now, she left them on the table where she could see them.

---

The rain was falling harder by the time Annabeth reached the Big House.

Thalia had chosen to stay in the cabin. She had said she wanted to finish her tea and check the window. Annabeth had asked if she wanted company.

“No,” Thalia said. “Go help with the blankets.”

Annabeth had nearly asked if she was sure. She stopped herself.

“Okay.”

Thalia looked up. “You can ask if I want help. You don’t have to ask if I’m sure every time.”

“I’ll try.”

“I know.”

The hill path was slick but not muddy. Annabeth walked with Ianthe and kept to the packed edge where the stones were set closer together. Ianthe carried her drawing inside her coat. The paper stayed dry, pressed against her chest.

At the Big House, Chiron waited beneath the porch roof with a clipboard on his lap. The satyr from the day before was gone. A stack of blankets stood beside the door, each folded into a square.

“Good morning,” Chiron said. “I’m glad you came back.”

“I said I would,” Ianthe answered.

“You did.”

“Do you have another bed?”

“For the guest room?”

“For Cabin One.”

“I don’t know yet. I can ask the house staff to check the storage room.”

“How long will that take?”

“Today, if they’re free. Tomorrow if they’re not.”

“Can I sleep there tonight?”

“You can sleep in the guest room again tonight.”

“I don’t want to.”

“I understand.”

“Then can you get the bed?”

“I can see what’s available. I can’t promise the cabin is ready for it.”

Ianthe’s jaw set. “It’s ready for me.”

“The room is ready for its current occupants.”

“Then they can move.”

“Not without their agreement.”

Ianthe looked at Annabeth. “I don’t want to move anyone.”

“You drew both beds moving,” Annabeth said.

“I drew options.”

“Those options move them.”

“I’m looking for a place where I fit.”

“Then ask where we think that is.”

The answer seemed to pull the fight out of Ianthe’s mouth before it could form. She tucked one corner of the paper tighter beneath her coat.

Chiron tilted his head toward the west room. “There’s a smaller task I could use help with. Some of the spare blankets are in the passage, and the rain is reaching the outer shelf. We need to clear a walking route before the next group comes through.”

Ianthe watched the stack. “How many?”

“Six bundles in the passage. Three shelves available in the room.”

“Who’s moving them?”

“A few campers from the morning work crew.”

“Are they carrying all six?”

“They’re sorting as they go.”

“That will take longer.”

“It may.”

“I can make a plan.”

“You can offer one,” Chiron said.

Ianthe’s gaze went to the hallway. “I know.”

“Do you want to help?”

“Yes.”

Annabeth had already planned to spend an hour in the west room. She could carry blankets. She could also watch Ianthe try to turn an offer into a command. It was not the same as agreeing to be responsible for her.

“I’ll help too,” she said.

Ianthe looked at her. “You don’t have to.”

“I know.”

There was no sign that Ianthe understood why Annabeth had chosen to come. She turned toward the passage.

The west room was a storage space off the back hall. It held spare bedding, a pair of small tables, and a rack of oilskins. Water had slipped beneath the outer door and spread in a thin line across the floor. The crew had already moved two bundles away from it. Percy stood near the end of the hall, stacking dry sheets on the upper shelf. He set his coat on a chair so it would not drag across the blankets. A boy Annabeth had seen with the broom yesterday was stacking smaller bundles beside him. Another camper, taller and older, pulled a narrow table out of the passage.

“Don’t move that yet,” Ianthe said.

The taller camper kept hold of the table. “Why?”

“Because the bundles need to go through.”

“The bundles are going out.”

“Then the table needs to be clear.”

“It is.”

Ianthe looked at the table, then at the gap between it and the wall. “Not enough.”

“We’ve carried them through.”

“You have six more.”

“We can do six more.”

“Not if you keep taking the long route.”

The camper set the table down. “What long route?”

Ianthe took the charcoal from the shelf. “Here.” She drew a rough rectangle on the back of one of her unused sketches. “The wet bundles go out through the side door. The dry ones go to the upper shelf. The large ones stay in the middle, so they don’t block the smaller sets.”

“Why the side door?” Annabeth asked.

“The passage is narrow. If everyone comes in and out the same way, they’ll meet at the turn.”

Annabeth looked at the hall. Ianthe was right. The turn was tight, and the floor was wet by the outside door. A second route could keep people from crossing with full arms. She had chosen the only space wide enough for two people to pass.

“Then we need to move the table,” the taller camper said.

“Yes,” Ianthe replied.

He glanced at Chiron, who remained just outside the room. “Are we moving the table?”

Chiron looked at the gap. “Move it closer to the wall, but leave room to open the cupboard.”

The camper shifted the table. It scraped along the boards and stopped before the cupboard. There was enough room.

Ianthe pointed at the bundles. “You take those two to the side door. You—”

“Who are you talking to?” the younger camper asked.

Ianthe looked at him. “You.”

“I have a name.”

“I don’t know it.”

“Then ask.”

Annabeth set down the bundle she was carrying. Chiron did not interrupt.

“I’m asking now,” Ianthe said. “What’s your name?”

“Eli.”

“Eli, take the smaller bundles to the shelf.”

“Which shelf?”

“The upper one.”

“I’m already putting the smaller blankets up there.”

“Then keep doing that.”

“I was.”

Ianthe’s mouth tightened. “You could have told me.”

“You could’ve asked what I was doing.”

“I did.”

“No, you told me where to put things.”

The boy returned to the blankets. His voice had not risen. That made it harder for Ianthe to turn it into a challenge.

“Why does everyone make an order into an argument?” she said.

“Because sometimes you give an order,” Annabeth replied.

“I gave a direction.”

“Without asking what was already happening.”

“It was obvious.”

“Not to you.”

Ianthe looked at the shelves again. “The plan still works.”

“It might,” Chiron said. “Let them tell you whether it works for the task they’re doing.”

“They’re moving blankets.”

“They’re sorting blankets too.”

Ianthe turned to the taller camper. “What are you moving first?”

“The table and then the wet bundles.”

“Why?”

“Because the table blocks the cupboard. We need the cupboard space for the folded sheets.”

“And after that?”

“We’ll move the damp bundles to the side room. They need air.”

“That’s not what you said.”

“I was answering your first question.”

The boy lifted a bundle from the stack. His hands were wet from the cloth. Annabeth held out a dry towel.

“Thanks,” he said.

He wiped his palms and lifted the bundle again. Ianthe watched him test the weight. “Use both arms. It’s easier.”

He shifted the blanket against his chest. “I know.”

The first bundle passed through the side door without touching the frame. The route worked. The older camper followed with the second, leaving the center walkway clear.

Ianthe looked at the plan in her hand. “See?”

“Your route is useful,” Chiron said. “The way you gave it wasn’t.”

“I didn’t make them do anything they weren’t already doing.”

“You told them what to do before you knew what they had done.”

“They should have explained.”

“They were working.”

“So was I.”

Annabeth felt the answer gather in her own mouth. She could say she had seen Ianthe do the same thing in Cabin One, and the same with the window. But the crew was still moving blankets. She set her bundle on the dry shelf and watched the room instead.

The older camper came back from the side room. “We need one person at that door. The hall floor’s slick.”

“I can stand there,” Ianthe said.

“You can,” he said. “But don’t tell Eli to carry the wet ones. He’s got the dry stack.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were.”

Ianthe stared at him. “You don’t know what I was going to do.”

“I know what you told him.”

The camper took another bundle and carried it to the side room. He did not wait for her answer. The work kept moving without Ianthe directing it.

Annabeth picked up two smaller blankets. The younger camper reached for one at the same time.

“I have them,” he said.

“I can carry one.”

“I know. This is the small stack.”

Annabeth let him have it. “Okay.”

He carried them through the passage and came back empty-handed.

“Do you need another?” Ianthe asked.

“We’re almost done.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the one I have.”

Annabeth recognized the phrase from yesterday. Ianthe did too. Her eyes moved to Annabeth, but she did not claim the words.

The team finished moving the bundles in less time than Annabeth expected. Ianthe’s route had helped. The table stayed where Chiron placed it, the cupboard opened, and the damp blankets had air around them. No one thanked Ianthe for supervising. No one had asked her to supervise.

The older camper wiped the floor near the door. “Could use a second towel here.”

Ianthe found one on the rack and handed it to him without telling him where to put it.

“Thanks,” he said.

She nodded. Her plan remained folded open in her other hand.

“Can I keep this?” she asked Chiron.

“Of course.”

“I mean for Cabin One. I can draw it to scale.”

“Maybe another time.”

“Why?”

“Because you haven’t been asked to plan Cabin One.”

“I was asked to help make a plan.”

“By your father?”

“Yes.”

“Then you can tell me what he said. It still doesn’t decide the room for Annabeth or Thalia.”

Ianthe looked down at the drawing. The rain tapped the side-room window. “I know.”

Annabeth could not tell whether she believed it yet.

---

When the last blanket was folded, the younger camper carried the damp towel to the porch rail and hung it where the rain could not reach it. The taller camper tested the path through the room, then stepped aside for Ianthe.

“You can walk it,” he said. “We’ll see if the turn stays clear.”

Ianthe moved through the passage with her arms at her sides. At the narrowest part, she turned her shoulders and made it through. The route had enough room for one person with a bundle, and the return path stayed open.

“It works,” she said.

“It does,” the camper replied. “We’ll keep it until the blankets dry.”

Ianthe looked at the diagram. “You could have said you were bringing them out.”

“We were going to.”

“You could have said that.”

“You could have asked.”

She folded the paper in half and held it against her side.

The camper pushed the door open with his hip. “We’re done in here.”

Chiron thanked them and asked one of the older campers to check the outer shelf later. The group began to disperse. Ianthe stayed beside the table, looking at the space where the damp blankets had been.

Annabeth took the empty basket from the floor. “Do you want to come back to the cabin?”

“I should check the shelves.”

“Which shelves?”

“Where the dry blankets go.”

“Chiron asked the crew to move them.”

“I can make sure they’re arranged.”

“They’re already arranging them.”

“Someone should check.”

Annabeth put the basket down. “You can ask Chiron if he wants you to check.”

“He asked me to help.”

“He did.”

“Then I can help.”

“Yes.”

Ianthe looked as if Annabeth had made the answer harder than it needed to be. She went to the hall, where one of the older campers was carrying a final bundle.

“Where do you want this?” Ianthe asked.

“Top shelf. We have two left.”

“Which one?”

“The one with the blue mark.”

“I don’t see a blue mark.”

“It’s under the fold.”

Ianthe waited while he turned the bundle. The small blue thread was at one corner. She reached toward it, then stopped when he lifted it himself.

“Here,” he said. “See?”

“I see.”

He carried it to the shelf and placed it beside the others. Ianthe watched until the bundle was aligned with the stack.

The arrangement was straight. It did not require her to adjust it.

Annabeth walked out to the porch. Percy stood under the roof with a basket of folded sheets tucked against one hip. He had gotten wet anyway; rain had darkened the shoulders of his shirt.

“You’re done?” she asked.

“Almost. Chiron says these go to the guest rooms.”

“Are you carrying all of them?”

“I can carry some.”

“Does he know you’re taking them?”

“He asked me.”

“Good.”

He smiled. “You sound surprised.”

“I’m relieved.”

He set the basket on the bench and held the door for her. The two of them stood near the porch post while Chiron spoke with the crew. Annabeth could hear the rain against the roof and the scrape of a chair from the kitchen.

“Did she decide?” Percy asked.

“No.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

“Good.”

Annabeth glanced at him. “That’s not what I expected you to say.”

“I’m trying not to help you decide.”

“You are helping.”

“By not helping?”

“By not turning it into a project you can finish for me.”

He looked toward the west room. Ianthe was still there, hands folded over her paper. “I can carry a basket.”

“You can.”

“I’m also good at carrying baskets.”

“You are.”

“Maybe someday you’ll let me carry an entire basket without reviewing my methods.”

“Maybe.”

He pulled one of the loose threads from her sleeve. “You don’t have to know what you want today.”

“I know that.”

“Do you?”

“Not always.”

She liked that he could ask without making the question into a lesson. He had his own work, his own cabin, his own family. He did not need to turn her answer into his assignment.

“I want her to ask before she rearranges my room,” Annabeth said.

“That’s reasonable.”

“I want to know if she can live with us.”

“Also reasonable.”

“I don’t know whether I want her to.”

“Okay.”

“She’s my sister.”

“She is.”

“I don’t want that to mean she gets to decide what I want.”

“It doesn’t.”

Annabeth looked at his hand, resting against the basket. “You don’t think I’m being unfair?”

“I don’t know yet.”

She looked up.

“I don’t know her,” he said. “I don’t know what Chiron can find for a bed, or what the room looks like to her. I know you live there. I know you said no to moving your bed. That’s enough for me to stay out of the decision.”

He said it without giving her a solution. Annabeth let her shoulder touch his.

“Can I still want you there?” she asked.

“Yeah.”

“Even if I said I didn’t want you to help with the room?”

“Those are different things.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I’m checking.”

He tilted his head, and she kissed him before the next question came. It was brief and familiar. The damp air cooled her cheek where his face had been warm. His hand settled at her elbow, then loosened when she stepped back.

“Can you come by later?” she asked.

“After I take these upstairs.”

“For me.”

“I’ll come.”

“Not to talk about the bed.”

“Then what are we talking about?”

“I haven’t decided.”

He laughed. “I’ll bring no plans.”

“Good.”

“That’s a lot of pressure.”

“Do you need a chart?”

“No. I’ll risk it.”

He picked up the basket and took it toward the stairs. Annabeth watched him go, then looked back at the west room.

Ianthe had left the folded plan on the table. She had not taken it to the shelves or told anyone to use it. The paper was still open enough to show the short path she had drawn.

When Annabeth went back inside, Ianthe was tracing the line with one finger.

“The turn was clear,” Annabeth said.

“Yes.”

“Your plan helped.”

“I know.”

“The crew moved the table because Chiron asked. Not because you told them.”

“They used the path I drew.”

“They did.”

“So it worked.”

“The path worked.”

Ianthe folded the page again. “You don’t give compliments easily.”

“I’m not sure it’s a compliment.”

“It’s true.”

“It is.”

Ianthe slid the paper into her coat. “Then I’ll keep it.”

Annabeth let her.

---

The Cabin One table looked smaller after Annabeth came back from the Big House.

Thalia had moved the weather chart to the far end and set Annabeth’s map beneath it, keeping the charcoal from smudging the paper. She sat with one boot on the floor and the other heel resting on the cushion. The extra chair stood near the stove, turned toward the door.

“Did they need all the blankets?” she asked.

“Almost.”

“Did Ianthe help?”

“Yes.”

“Did she tell everyone what to do?”

“Yes.”

Thalia took a drink from her cup. “And?”

“Her plan worked.”

“That doesn’t sound like the whole story.”

“It isn’t.”

Annabeth set the empty basket on the floor. The rain had followed her in on her sleeves. She hung her coat on the hook and kept the damp hem from touching the map.

“Ianthe saw that the hallway was too narrow for two people with bundles,” she said. “She routed the wet blankets through the side door and kept the dry stack on the upper shelf. It was better.”

“Did she ask them what they were doing?”

“After she told them to move.”

Thalia nodded. “Did they?”

“One of them asked her why. The other asked Chiron whether they had to follow her plan.”

“That sounds like an answer.”

“I know.”

Thalia waited. Annabeth saw her working not to fill the silence. Yesterday she had done the same thing at the Big House. She could ask for help without giving Annabeth the answer she expected.

“Do you want to hear what happened?” Annabeth asked.

“Yes.”

“Do you want to say something about it?”

Thalia looked at her. “Do you want me to?”

“Not yet.”

“Okay.”

Annabeth pulled out the chair across from her. The table was clear except for the charcoal, the weather chart, and two plates from breakfast. She took the larger plate and scraped the crumbs into her palm.

“Ianthe saw the route,” she said. “The crew had already started moving the bundles. She acted like that meant they weren’t doing it right.”

“Did they tell her they were?”

“They told her what they were doing. She said it would take too long.”

“Was she right?”

“Maybe about one part.”

“Then she could have said that.”

“She did. She didn’t say why until she drew it.”

Thalia leaned back. “That’s not useless.”

“No.”

“Or enough.”

“No.”

Annabeth brushed the crumbs into the wastebasket. “She used the younger camper’s name after he made her ask. He was already sorting the small blankets. She told him to do it, then he told her he was already doing it.”

“He’s the same one from yesterday?”

“Yes.”

“Does he know her?”

“No.”

“Then maybe she should stop giving him orders.”

“That’s what I thought.”

Thalia’s eyes had gone to the door. Annabeth followed her glance. The open strip of glass showed the rain moving past the hill in thin gray lines.

“I wanted to tell her that,” Thalia said.

“You didn’t.”

“I wanted to.”

“Why?”

“Because she’s treating people like they’re slow.”

“Is that what you saw?”

“She told the kid to use both arms while he was already carrying a blanket.”

“He might have needed the reminder.”

“Did he?”

“No.”

Thalia rubbed the rim of her cup with one thumb. “I don’t like her deciding who needs help.”

“Neither do I.”

“I’m still trying not to decide for you.”

Annabeth held the plate under the tap. “You can say what you think.”

“I did.”

“You can say it to her too.”

“Do you want me to?”

Annabeth turned off the water. “I don’t know. If I ask you to, I want you to say it. If I don’t, I don’t want you to jump in because it feels like I’m in trouble.”

“That makes sense.”

“Does it?”

“Mostly.”

She dried the plate and set it in the cupboard. The cupboard door did not close at first. A spoon had shifted behind the shelf. Annabeth moved it, then shut the door.

“I don’t want her to think I’ll agree to anything just because she’s our sister,” she said.

“Then don’t agree.”

“I don’t want to make her prove she deserves to be here.”

Thalia did not answer immediately. Annabeth could hear the rain against the window and a cart rolling down the path outside. The pause felt like a space Thalia had left for her instead of a silence she was waiting to fill.

“I don’t know what the difference is yet,” Annabeth said.

“Neither do I.”

Annabeth returned to the table. “I want her to ask before she moves things.”

“That seems fair.”

“I want her to stay long enough that I can decide if I want to share the room.”

“Also fair.”

“I want to be able to say no without having it mean I hate her.”

Thalia’s fingers stopped at the cup. “I can help with that.”

“Can you?”

“I can try.”

“What would you do?”

“I would tell her you get to choose.”

“I know that.”

“Would you want me to say it?”

“Not unless I ask.”

Thalia accepted the answer. Her gaze lowered to the cushion under her heel. “Then I’ll wait.”

Annabeth looked at the old room map she had started on the back of the paper. “Do you want to see the measurements?”

“Do you?”

“I want to see what the space is.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

Annabeth almost smiled. “Yes.”

Thalia pulled the chair closer with her hand, careful not to nudge the cushion. She did not lift the leg or shift the stool. Annabeth spread the paper between them.

The room was a rough rectangle. She drew the two beds first, then the window, the table, and the door. She left the third bed as an outline in pencil, not charcoal.

“Here,” she said. “If the bed goes along the window wall, it leaves this much room.”

“Not enough for the chair.”

“Not if we keep the table where it is.”

“What if the table moves?”

“Then it blocks the cupboard.”

“What if the cupboard moves?”

“It’s built in.”

“Right.”

Thalia leaned over the paper. “You could put the third bed here.”

“That’s where your chair has to turn.”

“I can move the chair.”

“Do you want to?”

“I might.”

“That’s different.”

Thalia looked at her. “I know.”

“Do you want to move your bed?”

“No.”

“Then don’t draw it that way.”

“I was looking at the room.”

“Your bed is not a piece in the room.”

“Neither is yours.”

Annabeth looked at the line she had drawn for the proposed bed. She erased it.

Thalia took the pencil from her hand. “You can keep the room open.”

“Can I?”

“Yes.”

“For how long?”

“As long as you need.”

“And if she thinks we’re making her wait to punish her?”

“Then we tell her what we’re doing.”

“Would you tell her?”

“If you want.”

“I want you to wait for me to ask.”

Thalia set the pencil down. “Okay.”

Annabeth rolled the paper up and slipped it beneath the weather chart. They had not found a place for the bed. They had found a way to draw the room without moving either sister out of it.

A knock sounded at the door. Annabeth glanced at Thalia.

“Do you want me to get it?” Thalia asked.

“Yes.”

Thalia stood. She took the step slowly, with her hand on the table, and opened the door.

Ianthe stood on the porch with Chiron beside her. She held the folded diagram against her coat.

“Can we talk?” Ianthe asked.

“About the bed?” Annabeth called from the table.

“Yes.”

“Come in.”

Ianthe crossed the threshold. Chiron stayed outside.

“Do you want me to stay?” he asked.

Ianthe looked at Annabeth. “I can talk to them.”

Annabeth felt the old impulse to say *we can manage*. She looked at Thalia instead.

“Do you want him to stay?” she asked Ianthe.

“No.”

“Then he can go.”

Chiron nodded. “I’ll be in the Big House if you need me.”

Ianthe watched him leave. “I didn’t need you to answer for me.”

“I asked you.”

“You could have let me answer.”

“You did.”

Ianthe stepped farther into the room. “I have another plan.”

Annabeth moved the map away from the table’s center. “Show us.”

---

Ianthe opened the paper on the table.

The drawing used the same rectangle as before, but the beds stayed where they were. A third bed sat against the wall opposite the window, drawn narrow enough that the door still had a clear swing. The table had moved two inches toward the stove. Annabeth could see the measurements beside it.

“You measured again,” she said.

“I used the tape.”

“Good.”

“The path from the door is clear.”

“On paper.”

“I know you’ll want to walk it.”

“I might.”

Ianthe tapped the edge of the plan. “I didn’t move your bed or Thalia’s.”

Annabeth kept her hands on her side of the table. “You asked me to look.”

“I did.”

“Then I’ll look.”

She traced the route from the doorway to the table, then from the table to her own bed. The pencil line came close to the corner of the rug. It did not cross it. The narrow bed left a foot and a half at the end, less than Annabeth wanted but more than the first arrangement had allowed.

“This might fit,” she said.

Ianthe looked at her. “You said it was too narrow.”

“I said the other plan was too narrow.”

“It is the same bed.”

“It’s not the same arrangement.”

“You’re still deciding.”

“Yes.”

Ianthe sat down. “I can move the table more.”

“That blocks the cupboard.”

“I measured the cupboard.”

“Did you open it?”

“No.”

“Then you don’t know how far it comes out.”

Ianthe glanced at the built-in cupboard. It was an old wooden door with a handle that stuck in damp weather. Annabeth opened it. The door swung wider than Ianthe’s drawing allowed and nearly touched the table.

Ianthe leaned forward. “I didn’t account for that.”

“No.”

“The table can go another way.”

“Maybe.”

“Then I’ll redraw it.”

“Not until we know what bed Chiron has.”

“I’m trying to make this work.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Annabeth looked up from the plan. “You made a better drawing.”

“That’s not the same as wanting me here.”

“No.”

Ianthe folded her hands. “You keep saying that.”

“Because you keep asking questions I can’t answer yet.”

“Would you rather I didn’t ask?”

“I’d rather you let an answer take the time it takes.”

“That’s easy to say when you have a bed.”

Annabeth sat back. There it was: the difference between them made into a charge. She felt Thalia’s attention shift from the chair. Annabeth did not look at her.

“I have a bed because I live here,” she said.

“And I don’t.”

“Not yet.”

“Not yet means you can still say no.”

“Yes.”

Ianthe’s eyes narrowed. “Then you don’t want me.”

Annabeth’s mouth went dry. She had no good answer that could be promised without lying. She could hear the rain on the roof and a cart moving across the gravel outside.

“I want to know whether we can live in the same room,” she said.

“That’s not the same.”

“No.”

“I want a bed.”

“I know.”

“I want my place.”

“I know.”

Ianthe’s gaze went to the window. The blanket lifted, then fell. “If I’m in the Big House, I can’t tell when the wind shifts.”

“You can check the window.”

“It’s on the other side of the room.”

“Then you can ask Chiron if you can open it.”

“I don’t want to ask every time.”

“You can ask once.”

“What if he says no?”

“Then you’ll know.”

Ianthe’s fingers pressed into the table. “At home, my mother wanted me to stop watching the sky.”

“Because of the storm?”

“Because I kept saying it would turn.”

“Did you keep saying it?”

“I told her it would.”

“That’s different?”

“Yes.”

Annabeth waited.

“I thought I was right,” Ianthe said. “I was right about the wind changing. I was wrong about when. The chairs went through the gate before it turned.”

She did not look at Annabeth. The admission came without the satyr there to hear it and without Chiron asking. It was not an apology. It was not a new story that erased the car.

“Do you want to call your mother?” Annabeth asked.

“I said I would.”

“You haven’t.”

“I know.”

“Do you want to use the Big House phone?”

Ianthe’s head turned. “Do I need permission?”

“No. You can use it.”

“Then why ask?”

“Because I don’t know if you want to.”

Ianthe’s hands flattened on the table. “I do. I don’t want people listening.”

“Chiron can find you a room with a door.”

“I know.”

“You can close it.”

“I know.”

Annabeth wondered whether Ianthe wanted the room to be hers or wanted the part of it that could be shut. They were not the same. She could ask, but she did not want to turn every pause into a question.

“If you want to call her, you can ask Chiron to use the study,” she said. “He’ll leave you alone.”

“I can ask him.”

“You can.”

“Do you think he’ll say yes?”

“I think so.”

Ianthe looked back at the plan. “I want the bed beside the window.”

“You said that.”

“I want to see the sky.”

“I understand.”

“I’m not trying to take your bed.”

“You drew it moving twice.”

“I was looking for space.”

“You were looking at our beds as if they were the space.”

Ianthe shut her eyes for a moment. “I can see why you’re saying that.”

Annabeth waited for the next part. It did not come.

“You can’t assume that because we’re sisters, I’ll agree,” she said.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I know the words.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No.”

Ianthe's answer had a familiar shape now. Annabeth was not sure whether it was a lesson or simply the only sentence they both could trust.

Thalia shifted in her chair. “Do you want me to say something?” she asked Annabeth.

Annabeth met her eyes. “No. I’m okay.”

“Okay.”

Ianthe looked at them. “You ask her before you speak?”

“Sometimes,” Thalia said.

“Why?”

“Because she’s the one talking.”

“I’m talking too.”

“Yes.”

“So why don’t you ask me?”

Thalia let that sit. Annabeth noticed her hand move from the chair arm to the cushion and stop.

“Do you want me to say something?” she asked Ianthe.

Ianthe frowned. “I don’t know.”

“Then I’ll wait.”

“That’s what you do?”

“When I don’t know.”

“Does it ever make you miss the chance?”

“Sometimes.”

“What happens then?”

“Then I missed it.”

Ianthe looked back at the plan. The pencil marks had smudged beneath her palm. She rubbed at them with her thumb, making the line less clear.

“I thought being a sister meant there was a place,” she said.

Annabeth felt the room tighten around that sentence. “There is a cabin.”

“That’s not what I mean.”

“I know.”

“You’re sisters already. You know where everything goes.”

“We’ve lived here a long time.”

“Together?”

“Mostly.”

Ianthe looked between them. “You get to have history with him. You get the cabin. You even know which window to leave open.”

The words were not quite a complaint. Annabeth could hear the comparison in them, the thing Zeus had told Ianthe or the thing she had decided for herself. She wondered whether Ianthe thought she had been chosen for something. She could not know.

“We don’t know the same things about him,” Annabeth said.

Ianthe’s gaze sharpened. “You’re his daughters.”

“Yes.”

“Then how can you not know?”

“Because he isn’t here.”

“He came to see me.”

Annabeth did not ask how often. The details were not hers to demand.

“Did he tell you everything?” Ianthe asked.

“No.”

“What did he tell you?”

“That’s private.”

“Why?”

“Because I’m not going to tell you things about Thalia without asking her.”

“Do you think I’d repeat them?”

“I don’t know.”

The answer landed badly. Ianthe pushed the plan away from herself.

“You think I’m a risk.”

“I think I don’t know you.”

“That’s what you keep saying.”

“Because it’s true.”

Ianthe stood. Her chair scraped against the floor. “I can’t make you know me faster.”

“No.”

“I can make a bed fit.”

“Maybe.”

“And you still won’t tell me if I can have it.”

“I won’t decide before we know what Chiron has and before Thalia and I have talked.”

“You’re talking now.”

“We are.”

“You’re both here.”

“That doesn’t mean the answer is yes.”

Ianthe gathered the paper. Annabeth let her lift it.

“Can I leave the drawing here?” Ianthe asked.

“Yes.”

“Even if you don’t agree?”

“Yes.”

Ianthe placed it on the table. The paper lay beside the weather chart, one folded edge touching the map. She moved it until the edges no longer overlapped.

“I want to call my mother,” she said.

“Then go ask Chiron for the study.”

“I will.”

The door opened before she reached it. Chiron stood on the porch with a small list in his hand.

“I found two cots in storage,” he said. “One has a broken leg. The other is wider than the space we measured.”

Ianthe looked at him. “How much wider?”

“Enough that it would narrow the path.”

“Could the leg be repaired?”

“Yes, but not tonight.”

“How long?”

“I’ll know after the carpenter checks it.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Maybe.”

Ianthe stepped back from the door. “Then I’m in the guest room again.”

“For tonight,” Chiron said.

“I know.”

The word came out clipped. She slid past him onto the porch. “Can I use the study phone?”

“Of course. I’ll leave the room empty.”

“Thank you.”

Chiron turned to Annabeth. “She’ll have privacy.”

“Okay.”

He looked at the drawing on the table but did not pick it up. “We can talk about the cot tomorrow.”

Ianthe stood under the porch roof. She had not left the steps.

“Will I be able to sleep in Cabin One tomorrow?” she asked.

“We’ll see what the carpenter finds, and then we’ll talk.”

“That isn’t a yes.”

“No.”

Ianthe looked at Annabeth through the open door. “I’ll come back after I call.”

“You can,” Annabeth said.

“Will you be here?”

“Yes.”

“Will you look at the drawing?”

“If you bring it.”

“It’s right there.”

“Then I’ll look at it.”

Ianthe held her gaze for a moment, then left with Chiron toward the Big House.

Thalia did not say anything until the porch was empty.

“Do you want to talk about it?” she asked.

“Not yet.”

“All right.”

Annabeth turned the drawing so it faced her. The bed beside the window was still too close to the table. The cupboard door still needed room to open. The route from the threshold was clear if the rug stayed where it was, but Thalia had said she did not want to move it.

The plan had improved. It had not become an agreement.

Annabeth took the pencil and erased the line that moved Thalia’s chair. She left the space blank.

Outside, the rain kept falling. The cabin held the same beds it had held the night before, and Annabeth had not promised what tomorrow would do with them.

---

Thalia waited until the rain softened before she stood.

Annabeth had left the two drawings on the table. One showed the bed beside the window; the other, the hallway route for the blankets. The map sat beneath them, its shoreline still marked with a dotted line. Thalia could have turned the bed plan over. She did not.

“Do you want me to move the chair?” she asked.

Annabeth looked from the chair to the paper. “No.”

“All right.”

“You don’t have to ask about the chair.”

“I thought I did.”

“It’s my chair too.”

Thalia nodded. She had expected Annabeth to say that the drawing was hers, or that the room was theirs, or that the new girl had no claim yet. Instead, the answer had been a chair. Small enough to move. Specific enough not to become a decision about whether Ianthe belonged.

Thalia returned to her seat. The cushion had slipped toward the edge. She moved it back with her hand and set her heel on it again. The room smelled of tea and damp wool. The rain kept the window glass gray.

Annabeth picked up the blanket clip and tested it between her fingers. “It keeps falling.”

“I can tie it.”

“Do you want to?”

Thalia looked at the clip. “Not if you’re still using it.”

“I can use it after.”

“Then I can tie it.”

She waited while Annabeth finished checking the edge of the cloth. The narrow strip of glass stayed clear. When Annabeth handed her the clip, Thalia threaded it through the blanket and fastened it to the frame.

“There.”

“Thanks.”

“Want me to close the door?”

“Not yet.”

Thalia left it where it was.

Annabeth studied the window for a while. She liked being able to see the sky before the rest of the camp did. She had not said that was why she kept the strip uncovered, but Thalia had watched her pull the blanket aside every morning.

“Do you want the bed by the window?” Thalia asked.

Annabeth kept looking through the glass. “I want the window.”

“That’s different.”

“I know.”

Thalia did not ask her to explain. Annabeth would say it if she wanted to.

The floorboard creaked near the stove. Thalia shifted her foot on the cushion, then stopped. The dull ache remained where it had been. She had not promised it would be gone by tomorrow. She had not promised anyone she would carry the work crew, either.

“I want her to stop moving things before she asks,” Annabeth said.

“Then tell her that.”

“I did.”

“Then she knows.”

“Does she?”

“I don’t know.”

Annabeth turned from the window. “You don’t have to take my side.”

“I’m not.”

“You sound like you are.”

“I’m on your side. That doesn’t mean I think every answer is yours to make.”

Annabeth sat with that. Thalia was glad she had not said it sooner. The sentence might have sounded like another way to tell her what to do.

“Do you think she should be in the cabin?” Annabeth asked.

Thalia looked at the two beds. Her bed was near the inside wall. Annabeth’s stood under the window. There was a narrow path between them and the table. The room fit two people because they had learned how to fit it around the two of them.

“I think she’s Zeus’s daughter,” Thalia said. “That doesn’t tell me where she sleeps.”

“Is she your sister?”

“Yes.”

“Does that change anything?”

“Probably.”

“Like what?”

“I don’t know yet.”

Annabeth gave a short laugh. “You really don’t.”

“No.”

“Usually you make a list.”

“I could.”

“Would it help?”

“Not with this.”

Annabeth took the papers and stacked them with the bed plan on top. “I don’t want to turn it into a test.”

“Then don’t.”

“I also don’t want to act like nothing changed.”

“Something changed.”

“What?”

“She’s here.”

“That’s not much of a plan.”

“No. It’s all I know.”

Annabeth set the stack on the shelf, close to the weather chart but not over it. “She can come to breakfast tomorrow.”

“Do you want to ask her?”

“I can.”

“Okay.”

“You don’t have to come with me.”

“I know.”

Thalia saw Annabeth glance at her. She had meant the answer as permission; Annabeth had heard it as a reminder. Neither of them corrected the other.

Annabeth pulled the chair back from the table. “Do you want some bread?”

“Yes.”

She broke one of the rolls Percy had brought and put half on Thalia’s plate. The crust had gone soft in the room’s damp air. Thalia ate it anyway.

“Did you like the blanket plan?” Annabeth asked.

“Which one?”

“The one from the west room.”

“It worked.”

“Did you like her plan?”

“I liked the route.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No.”

Annabeth let the subject rest. She took the other half of the roll and ate it while she looked at the window. Thalia had no answer for the bed, the room, or the kind of sister Ianthe might become. For tonight, Annabeth had chosen breakfast. Thalia could stand beside her without deciding what that choice meant.

The cabin stayed as it was: two beds, one table, one window with a strip of clear glass. The drawing remained on the shelf, the line for a third bed still in pencil.
