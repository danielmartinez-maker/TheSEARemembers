## Chapter 106: The Visit That Wasn't a Test

Clarisse knew Poseidon was coming because the lake went quiet.

Not calm. Quiet. The ripples stopped, the naiads vanished beneath the surface, and the usual little sounds of water against the docks seemed to disappear. Clarisse noticed it before Percy did. She was at the forge returning a borrowed shield when the silence slid over the camp.

She found Percy and Silena at the Poseidon cabin, both staring toward the lake.

"You feel that?" Silena asked.

Percy nodded. "Dad's here."

Clarisse stayed by the doorway. For years, Clarisse could count his visits on one hand. Once, when she was small, he’d brought a toy trident she couldn’t lift. Once he’d asked whether she liked camp, then left before dinner. The palace weekend in Atlantis and the barbecue at camp were attempts to change that. She still didn’t know whether a quiet afternoon would follow all the ceremony.

She didn't know what to do with a father who arrived like weather and left like a tide.

The water at the lake's center rose in a smooth pillar. Poseidon stepped out of it in the same broad-shouldered form Percy had described, sea-green eyes and damp white robes. He looked first at Percy, then at Clarisse and Silena.

"My daughters," he said.

Clarisse had prepared for this. She'd thought of a dozen replies, all sharp. None came out.

Silena stepped forward first. "Hello, Father."

Poseidon smiled at her, then turned to Clarisse. "Clarisse. You've grown."

"Even since the barbecue. That happens."

Percy glanced at her, but Poseidon didn't seem offended. He nodded, as if she had given a perfectly reasonable answer.

"I have been away too long," he said. "I will not pretend otherwise."

That wasn't an apology. It wasn't nothing either.

Silena's face was careful. "Why are you here now?"

"To see my children. To speak with them without asking them to travel to Atlantis or Olympus." He looked at Percy. "And because Percy has been missed."

Clarisse heard the *and*. The qualifier. The reason that made this visit make sense to the god. She felt her old anger begin to rise, but Percy was watching her, not Poseidon. He didn't look pleading. He just looked aware.

Clarisse breathed out slowly.

"There's a table by the lake," she said. "If you're staying for lunch, we can use that."

Poseidon looked surprised. "I would be glad to."

"Then help carry the food."

Silena hid a smile.

---

Poseidon did help carry the food. He carried too much of it at once, which made the task less helpful than he intended. Clarisse let him carry the bread basket and took the plates from his other hand.

They ate under the shade trees, with the lake in view. Poseidon asked Percy about Atlantis: his classes, his swimming, whether Triton was still teasing him. He asked Clarisse about her training and Silena about her music. He listened to the answers.

That was the part Clarisse didn't know how to handle. She'd expected the questions to be formal, something a king asked because a king was supposed to ask. But Poseidon remembered details. He knew Clarisse preferred the eastern training ground because the footing was rougher. He knew Silena had started singing in the amphitheater. He knew Percy had been taking lessons in Greek history.

Maybe he'd been watching from far away. Maybe he'd been told. Maybe he had actually been trying, even when he wasn't there.

Clarisse wasn't ready to forgive him. But she could acknowledge that the lunch was not a performance.

When Percy began describing the Atlantean game of currents, Poseidon demonstrated it with four shells and a glass of water. Clarisse and Silena joined in. Clarisse won twice by disrupting the flow with her thumb.

"You cheat," Silena said.

"I adapt," Clarisse said.

Poseidon laughed, then moved one of the shells back to the rim of the glass. "The game changes when someone interrupts the current."

"That's what the thumb was for," Clarisse said.

"I know. I was explaining why it worked."

"You mean you were explaining how I beat you."

"I mean I was explaining how you changed the rules without breaking them."

Clarisse looked at the glass. The water had gone still again. One shell sat in the center, one had drifted to the far side, and the two smaller ones had settled together by the edge. "You can change the rules in a game?"

"If everyone knows what game they're playing."

"That's convenient."

"It can be." Poseidon set the glass back on the table. "The difficult part is noticing when someone else thought the rules were different."

Silena tipped the shells into her palm. "I thought the goal was to keep one shell in the middle."

"It can be," Percy said. "The currents shift depending on which side you press. If you get the water moving in a loop, the shell stays in the center."

"I thought it was a race," Clarisse said.

Percy shrugged. "It can be that too."

"Then why didn't you say so?"

"You didn't ask."

"That's not a defense."

"No, it's why the game changed."

Silena rested her finger beside the glass. "I thought the goal was to keep one shell in the middle. We could try it that way."

"Which one?" Clarisse asked.

"Keeping one in the middle. No racing."

Clarisse looked toward Poseidon. He did not offer to settle it for them. She could make the choice herself. "One round. No score."

"We can try."

"All right."

Silena nodded. "Okay."

The small game had given them something to disagree about without anyone having to win the argument. Clarisse was surprised by how much she liked that. It did not make her feel closer to Poseidon. It did make the lunch feel less like a test she was expected to pass.

Poseidon asked Percy to show him the training pool in the Tempest Hall.

Clarisse had been watching Percy clear the plates. His father had made no announcement about staying longer, but the lunch was ending. The game had run out of water. The soup pot was almost empty, and the cloth around its handle had gone cool.

“Can I show you the pool?” Percy asked.

Poseidon looked at Clarisse first, though she did not know whether he meant to ask her permission or was checking whether she wanted him to stay. “If you have time,” Percy added, “I can take you there.”

“I'd like to see it,” Poseidon said.

“Then go,” Clarisse told Percy. “I'll bring the pot back.”

“I can carry it.”

“I know. I have the basket.”

“Do you want me to wait?”

“No.”

Percy lifted the vegetables and bread. Poseidon put a hand under the soup pot, then looked toward Clarisse.

“You can take it,” she said. “Use both hands. The cloth is on the handle.”

He did. Percy waited until the pot had cleared the bench before he set off toward the hall. Poseidon followed him, keeping pace beside him. He did not touch his son's shoulder or try to put an arm around him. Percy walked as he chose, carrying the food between them.

Clarisse watched until the path took them behind the willows.

“You didn't ask them to stay,” Silena said.

“I didn't know they were going to leave.”

“You said he could go.”

“Percy wanted to show him the pool.”

“I know. I wasn't saying you should have stopped them.”

Clarisse set the basket on the tabletop and picked up the bread bowl. “Then what are you saying?”

Silena stacked the clean plates. “That you let Percy have the afternoon he wanted with Dad, even though you were still figuring out your own.”

“It was a pool. Not an afternoon.”

“It could have been both.”

“They were gone for two minutes.”

“They haven't come back yet.”

Clarisse looked down the path. It was empty. She could hear the camp at a distance, the practical noises of people who had a schedule that did not change just because the god of the sea was visiting. She could not hear anything from the Tempest Hall.

“I'm not keeping score,” she said.

“I didn't say you were.”

“Then don't make it sound like I gave something up.”

Silena wiped a crumb from the tabletop. “You did give Percy room to do what he wanted without making him explain it.”

“He asked.”

“He did.”

“That isn't the same as me giving something up.”

“No.” Silena folded the cloth carefully. “I wasn't trying to say it was. I wanted to say I saw you do it.”

Clarisse nearly told her that watching was not the same as understanding. She didn't. Silena had not claimed to understand. She had only named what she saw.

Clarisse took the empty cups to the pavilion. “You want to help me clear this or not?”

“I do.”

“Then carry the plates.”

Silena did. She did not thank Clarisse for asking. Clarisse was glad she didn't.

At the kitchen counter, they returned the soup pot first. The counselor who had loaned it asked whether the group had eaten enough. Silena said there had been plenty. Clarisse set the pot beside the sink and turned its handle away from the aisle so nobody would catch a sleeve on it.

“We have extra bread,” Silena said. “I can leave it with the kitchen.”

“Ask first.”

“I will.” She went to the counselor and asked whether the loaf could stay in the pantry. The answer was yes, if she wrapped it. Silena took a clean cloth, folded it around the bread, and put it on the shelf with the other food.

Clarisse washed the cups. The water was warm enough that grease slipped off the glass without much scrubbing. She kept her elbows close to the sink to leave room for the next person waiting to use it. Silena dried the plates, checking each rim for a chipped place before she stacked it. They worked without talking for several minutes.

The quiet was different from the lake's. It had edges and motion. A faucet ran. Someone stacked trays at the other end of the counter. A door opened and closed in the kitchen. Their father was not in the room, but every few moments Clarisse found herself listening for his footsteps anyway.

Silena set one plate aside. “This one is chipped.”

“Put it in the repair box.”

“It's not in the red tub.”

“It's on the bottom shelf.”

Silena checked. The box was there, nearly full of cracked cups and loose handles. She made room for the plate by moving a cup with a missing piece out of the way.

“Do you think he's going to ask to hear the song again?” she said.

“He asked once.”

“I know.”

“You didn't say yes.”

“I know that too.”

Clarisse rinsed a spoon. “Then I don't know.”

Silena took the spoon from her and dried it. “I might want him to hear it. I might not. I want to decide when it's closer to being ready.”

“Then tell him that.”

“I did.”

“Good.”

Silena hung the towel over the side rail. “You don't have to check whether I'm okay with him. I can be glad he's here and still not give him something. I can want him to listen and also want the song to be mine for a while.”

Clarisse looked at the remaining dishes. “I wasn't checking.”

“You were about to ask if I wanted him to hear it.”

“No, I wasn't.”

“You looked at the amphitheater path.”

“I was checking whether the path was clear.”

“The path is clear.”

“I know that.”

“Then maybe you were thinking about the song.”

Clarisse put the spoon into the drawer a little harder than she meant to. It struck the side and rattled against the others.

“Maybe.”

Silena didn't press. She set the last clean plate on the shelf, then looked at the schedule board. “I may have a rehearsal coming up. I haven't set the order yet. I'll put it on the board when I know.”

“If you want him to come, tell him when it's on the board.”

“If I want him to come.”

“Right.”

Silena's smile was small. “You don't need to remind me what I said.”

“I wasn't.”

“You were reminding yourself.”

Clarisse leaned on the counter. “What if you want him there and he misses it?”

Silena folded her arms. “Then I'll be disappointed.”

“What if he says he'll come and doesn't?”

“Then I'll be angry. I still won't be wrong for wanting him there.”

Clarisse considered the answer. Silena had said it without turning toward her, as if it belonged to the future and not to a test she needed her sister to score. Clarisse was used to being the one who identified the risk before anyone else could stumble into it. It was harder to stand back and let Silena choose the version of disappointment she was willing to risk.

“Okay,” Clarisse said.

Silena lifted the empty basket. “That sounded like a real okay.”

“It was.”

“Good.”

“Don't turn it into a whole thing.”

“I won't.”

“I'm still not getting my hopes up.”

“I didn't ask you to.”

Clarisse took the basket from her. “You can hope for your own things.”

“I do.”

That answer belonged to Silena. Clarisse did not try to attach herself to it.

They returned to the lakeside table. The game glass still had the shells in it. Sunlight flashed on the water, turning the chipped one white along its rim. Clarisse picked up the glass and carried it to the shore. She did not throw the shells or hold on to them. She crouched above the waterline and tipped them into the shallow water. They washed back and settled among the small stones.

“That's where they came from,” Silena said.

“They can go back there.”

Silena held the empty glass steady while Clarisse poured the last of the water into the lake. The glass was not from the lake, but the water had been. Clarisse rinsed it once in the shallows, careful not to let it slip. The cup was too broad for one hand to keep its grip at the bottom. She passed it back to Silena.

“Take it to the pavilion?”

“Sure.”

Silena started ahead. Clarisse stayed at the edge until she could see the shells had stopped moving. They were only shells. The game had not been about a score. She had still wanted Poseidon to let her win without calling it a favor. He had done that. It didn't change what he had done years ago, but it was one thing that had happened today.

When she turned back toward the table, she saw the glass from the path.

Silena had stopped beside the benches. She was watching the trail that led to the Tempest Hall, waiting without making a show of it. The basket hung from her left hand. The small beads of water on the tumbler made her fingers shine.

“Are you waiting for them?” Clarisse asked.

“I'm going to the pavilion. I'm not waiting.”

“You stopped.”

“I saw you were still at the lake.”

“You don't need to stay.”

“I know.”

Silena continued toward the kitchen. Clarisse followed her at the same pace.

---

When Percy and Poseidon returned, the afternoon had not turned into evening, but the light had shifted across the grass. Clarisse was near the pavilion door, carrying the cloth from the bread basket back to the kitchen. She saw them coming before Percy noticed her.

Percy had been talking. She could tell from the way his hands moved, one describing the height of something and the other cutting across it. Poseidon watched him, his head turned slightly so the conversation did not have to compete with the campers passing along the path.

“Did you show him the pool?” Clarisse asked.

“The pool and the changing room,” Percy said. “I explained why the hall uses saltwater instead of the canoe lake.”

“Did he ask?”

“I told him.”

Poseidon glanced at Clarisse. “The water in the hall is kept separate from the lake. I did not know the camp had made a dedicated training pool.”

“It has,” Percy said. “For a while.”

“It is well designed,” Poseidon said.

“It's a pool,” Clarisse replied.

“An unusually well-designed pool.”

Percy grinned. “I know.”

That was not how Clarisse remembered Poseidon talking about her practice. But Percy had shown him a place he wanted him to see, and Poseidon had listened. Clarisse did not need to turn the compliment into a comparison between them.

“Did you swim?” she asked.

“No.” Percy glanced at the lake. “We talked.”

“About the pool?”

“Some.”

Poseidon did not add to the answer. If they had spoken about something else, Clarisse had not heard it. She had no right to fill in the blanks from a few gestures.

Silena came from the pavilion with the empty basket and a cloth folded over her arm. She had changed from drying dishes to wiping the menu board. One corner of the chalk list had smudged, and she had left it until the board was dry enough to rewrite.

Poseidon looked toward the amphitheater. “Would you like to show me where you practice?”

Silena followed his gaze. “Not today.”

“All right.”

“I might later.”

“I would like that.”

“Then wait until I ask.”

“I will.”

He had said he could wait at lunch. Now he had something more specific to do. Silena did not owe him a date for the invitation, and he did not ask her to provide one.

Clarisse could feel the edge of a question in her throat. Would he really wait? Did he understand that there might not be a later? She swallowed it. Silena could ask him herself if she wanted to know.

They walked together back toward the table. The soup pot was cool. Clarisse lifted the lid and checked that there was no food left inside; there wasn't. She nested the pot against the basket, then reached for the folded cloth.

“Can I help?” Poseidon asked.

“Carry this back to the kitchen.”

“I can carry more than a cloth.”

“You're carrying the pot.”

“Then I can carry both.”

“No. That basket is mine.” Clarisse tucked the cloth into the basket. “You can carry the pot.”

He accepted the division. Percy reached for the basket, then paused.

“Do you want me to take it?”

“No. I have it.”

“Okay.”

Silena raised the menu board to let the breeze pass behind it. “I'll take this in once the chalk is dry.”

“Then I'll take the pot,” Poseidon said.

They headed for the kitchen together, Clarisse with the basket and Poseidon with the pot. Percy stayed beside Silena and the menu board. Nobody had to walk in a single group to make the visit count as a family visit.

The kitchen counselor accepted the pot, checked it, and thanked them. Poseidon said he was glad to help. The counselor asked whether he wanted to leave the cloth with the pot or take it back to the pavilion.

“Take it back,” Clarisse said. “It's Silena's.”

“It's the kitchen's,” Silena corrected. “They gave it to us for the hot handle.”

“Then put it with the clean cloths.”

The counselor nodded. “I can wash it and return it to the basket.”

“Thanks,” Silena said.

Poseidon asked where he should leave the pot. The counselor pointed to a shelf. He placed it there and moved it a few inches back to give the next person room to carry a tray through. He did not make the counselor thank him again.

Outside, the wind had warmed. Clouds that had been gathered over the ridge were beginning to break apart. It was not storm weather, and the water on the lake had gone back to its ordinary movement. A few campers were coming up from the path with towels. They greeted Percy, then Poseidon. One asked whether the game at the table had been a new camp contest.

“It was a cup,” Percy said. “No prizes.”

The camper looked disappointed. “No prize?”

“The prize was getting the water on Clarisse's napkin.”

“I cleaned that up,” Poseidon said.

“Then he got the prize,” Clarisse said.

The camper laughed and kept walking. Poseidon smiled, but Clarisse did not know whether he had found the joke funny or was relieved the moment had not become serious. She did not ask.

At the pavilion, Silena rewrote the menu board. The afternoon snack had been moved to the second line; someone had asked for the ingredients in the oat bars to be clearer. Silena spaced the words out so campers could read them without crowding around the board.

Poseidon stood to the side. He did not lean over her shoulder. Clarisse noticed his eyes move from the old list to the new one and then away.

“Would you like to see it when I'm done?” Silena asked him.

“If you want to show me.”

“I will.”

Silena finished the lettering, erased the chalk dust from the frame, and stepped back. “The second line is hard to see from the path.”

“I can read it from here,” Clarisse said.

“You're standing right beside it.”

“That's where I'm supposed to be.”

“I'll move the board out to the edge.”

She lifted it from the wall, but one foot caught on the ground. The board tilted. Silena shifted her grip without dropping it. Poseidon took one step forward, then stopped.

“Is it heavy?” he asked.

“Yes,” Silena said. “Could you take the lower edge? Not the top. I don't want the chalk to smear.”

Poseidon put his hand where she indicated. They lifted it together and walked the board a few paces to the wider section of wall. Silena chose where to set it. The feet were uneven; one corner rocked when she let go.

“It's not steady,” Clarisse said.

“I know. I can put a folded card under that side.”

“Or a wood shim.”

“I don't want to make a permanent change.”

“Then the card will work.”

Silena found one of the spare menu cards and folded it once, testing the height. The board stopped moving. She stepped back to check whether the lettering could be read from the path.

“Can you see the oat bars?” she asked Percy.

“Mostly.”

“Can you read the ingredients?”

He walked to the edge of the path. “Yes.”

“Good.” Silena looked at Poseidon. “That's the part I wanted you to notice. It's not only that I like writing on the board. It helps people decide what they want without needing to ask someone to read it for them.”

“I understand,” Poseidon said. “You make room for people to choose.”

Silena considered him. “That's a better way to say it.”

He smiled. She didn't add anything else.

Clarisse watched her sister stand beside the board. The chalk had left a blue smudge on Silena's thumb. She rubbed it with the side of her finger and shook the dust loose. She did not seem proud of the board exactly; she seemed satisfied that it worked. It was the same look Clarisse had seen when a practice rack stayed upright after the last shield came off.

Poseidon had asked about Silena's music, not the board. He didn't turn what she'd just shown him into a claim about how he knew her. He had also helped carry it because she asked.

“Do you want him to hear the song?” Clarisse asked.

Silena looked at her. “I said I haven't decided.”

“I know. I'm asking now, not deciding for you.”

“Not today.”

“Then that's what we know.”

Silena's eyes narrowed. “You sound like Chiron.”

“I don't sound like Chiron.”

“You do a little.”

“Take it back.”

“No.”

Poseidon stood nearby, but did not interrupt them. The visit had not made Silena's answer need explaining. Clarisse glanced at him, then at the board. She felt no easier about him. She felt slightly more able to keep the question where it belonged.

They spent the next few minutes returning the rest of the borrowed things. The folded tablecloth went to the pavilion basket. Percy asked whether the shell glass could stay on the counter for the kitchen staff to wash; the counselor said yes. Clarisse checked the lakeside table once more. It had been moved only a little when they carried the food away. One of its legs was still low; the other table remained ready for the next camp meal.

Poseidon waited at the path instead of walking ahead of them. The sun had made the back of his robes dry. Clarisse could see a darker edge at the hem where the lake water had soaked it.

“Are you staying for dinner?” Percy asked.

“I had planned to leave before then.”

“Because of something in Atlantis?”

Poseidon shook his head. “No. I thought a daytime visit might be enough.”

“For whom?” Clarisse asked.

Poseidon paused. “I don't know.”

“You don't have to guess for us.”

“I will try not to.”

“Trying isn't a promise.”

“No. It is what I can offer today.”

Clarisse looked down the path. If she wanted him to stay, she could say so. If she wanted him to go, she could say that too. Neither answer had to represent every year that came before or every visit that might come after. The fact that she could choose did not make the choice easy.

“Do you want him to stay?” Percy asked.

“I haven't decided.”

“All right.”

“Don't ask me again in five minutes.”

“I won't.”

He did not ask what Clarisse would decide. She was glad for that, though she did not tell him.

---

Clarisse carried the basket back to the Poseidon cabin and left it on the table. The panther was no longer there. Percy had put it on his bunk the previous night. The sea horse, ship and owl remained together on the common table. She had not picked a place for them yet.

She noticed them only because she was looking for the cloth. Silena had taken it back to the kitchen, so the basket was empty.

“Do you want the figures somewhere else?” Silena asked.

“Not yet.”

“I was just asking.”

“I know.”

Clarisse turned the owl a little toward the other carvings. The same motion she had made the night before. Then she stopped. The figures could face wherever she left them. She set the owl back down without checking whether it looked like it belonged.

“Will you want to put them on the shelf?” Silena asked.

“I said I'd decide in daylight.”

“This is daylight.”

“It's not enough daylight.”

“There's plenty.”

“Then I'll decide later.”

Silena didn't argue. She sat on the end of her bunk and began to unbraid her hair. The beads in it clicked softly against the blanket. She had braided it back that morning so it would stay out of her face while she wrote; she could have left it in until dinner, but she had not needed to.

Clarisse went to her bunk and opened the drawer where she kept the pieces of sandpaper and the small knife she was allowed to use for carving. The new panther had not needed more work yet, and the figures were not a project she wanted to touch in front of anyone. She closed the drawer again.

“Do you want to go somewhere?” Silena asked.

“No.”

“Do you want me to stay?”

“You're already here.”

“I meant, do you want me to come with you if you go somewhere?”

Clarisse took the shield from her arms and leaned it against the wall. “I don't know.”

Silena glanced up. “That's an answer.”

“I didn't say it was a good one.”

“It doesn't have to be.”

Clarisse sat on her bunk. She was aware of the outside path and the sound of campers coming and going. She was aware of Poseidon somewhere beyond the cabin, and of Percy having taken him away to show the pool. She was also aware of how much she wanted to ask Silena what she thought of the visit, whether she wanted Poseidon to stay, whether she would want to keep a gift if he brought one. Silena had already told her she might want him to hear the song and might not. There was no reason to put the question back in a different form.

“Did you like the game?” Clarisse asked instead.

Silena finished unbraiding the last inch. “Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because it was small. Nobody had to be impressive.”

“You got the smooth shell to the middle every time.”

“I didn't do it because I wanted to win.”

“Then why did you do it?”

Silena set the hair tie beside her. “I wanted to see if the shells could stay in the center.”

“Could they?”

“Not when you all kept moving the water.”

Clarisse looked at the unbraided hair over Silena's shoulder. “You could've told us.”

“I wanted to see how long it would take before one of you asked.”

“Was that a test?”

“No. It was a question I had.”

“That sounds like a test.”

“It wasn't for you to pass.”

Clarisse rolled her eyes. “That's what people say when they're testing you.”

“Then next time I'll tell you before I move the shell.”

“There doesn't need to be a next time.”

“I didn't promise one.”

“You did not.”

Silena was not grinning, but she was closer to it now. She leaned back on her hands. “I liked that Father didn't say the game proved anything.”

Clarisse looked at the figures on the table. “It didn't.”

“I know.”

“I know you know.”

Silena nodded. “He did ask if I wanted to show him the song. I don't know why that made the lunch feel different.”

“Because he asked and then waited.”

“Maybe.”

“He could have asked and then kept asking.”

“He didn't.”

Clarisse stopped. There was a difference between believing a person might change and assuming that a single afternoon had changed them. Silena was not doing either. She was describing what happened.

“Would it matter if he kept asking?” Clarisse said.

“Yes.”

“What would you do?”

“Tell him to stop asking.”

“And if he didn't?”

“I'd leave.”

“That's a lot of trouble over a song.”

“It's not over the song.”

Clarisse recognized the answer in herself. The song was just where the question had found a place to stand. She did not make Silena explain the rest.

“Okay.”

“That's enough.”

“Enough for what?”

“Enough conversation about my song.”

Clarisse nodded. “Fine.”

Silena picked up the hair tie and wrapped it twice around her fingers. “I'm going to see whether the kitchen needs the board back later.”

“You don't have to work on it all day.”

“I know.”

“You can leave it there.”

“I know.” Silena held her gaze. “I want to check it once before dinner, because I moved it and the card under the leg might shift.”

“Okay.”

She stood, then took two steps toward the door. “Do you want to come?”

Clarisse looked at the figures. The permanent place for them could wait. “Not yet.”

“Okay.”

Silena left without asking whether Clarisse had changed her mind. Clarisse stayed in the cabin. She did not tell herself that she had chosen solitude because the visit was too much. She had chosen to stay because the path was loud and the cabin was quiet. That was all it needed to mean.

She sat on the edge of her bunk and retied the leather guard on her wrist. The strap was too tight at the first notch and too loose at the second. She pulled it through the first, then adjusted the buckle so there was enough room for her hand to flex.

A loose strap could make every movement seem like a problem. It could also be a small repair, if she had the time and the right thread. She did not have to decide on the whole guard while she was sitting there. She could take it to the forge after dinner.

When Silena came back, she had the chalkboard cloth folded over one arm. The menu card had stayed under the uneven foot. She set the cloth down and told Clarisse that the board was still readable from the path. A camper had asked about the oat bars; Silena had pointed to the written ingredient list. The camper had chosen one without needing someone else to answer for them.

“Good,” Clarisse said.

“Yeah.”

“Do you want to see if the board still wobbles?”

“I already did.”

“I meant right now.”

“It doesn't.”

“Okay.”

Silena folded the cloth again, taking care not to crease the chalk side. “I wrote a note for Poseidon.”

Clarisse looked at her. “What does it say?”

“Nothing dramatic. I said the rehearsal will be on the amphitheater board when I schedule it. I didn't say he could come.”

“You don't need to tell him anything.”

“I know. I wanted him to know there was a place he could find out if I changed my mind.”

Clarisse made room for the folded cloth on the table. “Are you going to give it to him?”

“If he asks before he leaves. If he doesn't, I'll keep it.”

“That sounds like you already decided.”

“It's a conditional.”

“That's what I said.”

“No, you said I decided. I decided that if he asks, I'll tell him. I haven't decided whether to give him the note. I'll decide then.”

Clarisse waved one hand. “Fine.”

Silena smiled at the corner of her mouth. “You don't have to agree with my wording.”

“I don't.”

“You heard it, though.”

“I heard you.”

“Good.”

Clarisse went to the door and looked down the path. She could see Percy and Poseidon crossing the clearing toward them. Poseidon walked a half-step behind Percy, not because he was being led, Clarisse thought, but because Percy was choosing the pace.

She let go of the door frame. “They're back.”

“I see them.”

“Do you want to give him the note?”

Silena slid it into her pocket. “I don't know.”

Clarisse stepped out of the doorway. She could let Silena make that choice without trying to settle it before Poseidon arrived.

When Poseidon reached the cabin, Clarisse was still at the doorway. Silena had the folded note in one hand, but she had not taken it out of her pocket. Percy walked a little ahead of his father and stopped where the path widened. He glanced at Clarisse first, then at Silena.

“Did you show him the board?” Clarisse asked.

“Yeah,” Percy said. “And the pool.”

“Was the pool still there?”

“Mostly.”

Poseidon looked at him. “The west drain needs clearing.”

“It always needs clearing,” Percy said. “It gets leaves from the ridge.”

“Then you know what to do.”

“I know what the maintenance crew does. I don't know if I should touch it.”

“Then ask them.”

Percy nodded. Clarisse wondered if he was glad his father had answered so plainly or irritated that it took so little to answer. His face did not tell her. She let the question stay his.

Silena drew the note from her pocket, then folded it once more along its existing crease. “Father?”

Poseidon turned toward her. “Yes?”

“I wrote something about the rehearsal.”

She paused. Clarisse could see the little ink mark on her thumb where the pen had slipped. Silena looked at the paper, then at Poseidon. No one moved to close the distance for her. Clarisse kept her hands at her sides.

“I decided I'd rather say it than give you the note,” Silena said. “If I schedule a rehearsal, I'll put it on the amphitheater board. You can look there. I don't know when that will be.”

“I understand.” Poseidon did not reach for the note. “Thank you for telling me.”

Silena put it back in her pocket. “I might change my mind.”

“Then you may change your mind.”

Clarisse kept her expression still. That was easy to say. The harder part would be not treating a changed mind as a promise broken. Silena seemed to be measuring the answer herself. She finally nodded, once, as if she had accepted the words for the moment and nothing beyond it.

Poseidon looked at Clarisse. “I have not asked you enough about your training.”

“You asked about it at lunch.”

“I asked what you were doing. I should have asked what you wanted from it.”

Clarisse shifted her weight. She could have made a joke. She could have said a new shield, better footing, a sharper sword, a target that stayed still. The list of things she wanted from training was not the same as the answer to his question, and she didn't know whether she owed him the honest version.

“I want to be better than I was yesterday,” she said.

“That sounds like you.”

“You don't know what sounds like me.”

Poseidon took that without a flinch. “You're right. I know pieces.”

The answer left her with nowhere to push. Not because he had won the exchange. He had simply stopped pretending that familiarity could be declared. Clarisse glanced at Silena, who was looking down the path rather than at either of them.

“Are you leaving now?” she asked.

“I should.”

“You should, or you want to?”

Poseidon looked toward the lake. Its surface had returned to its usual restless movement. A pair of campers crossed the far shore, arguing about something small and urgent. “I have an obligation in the sea before evening.”

“That's not the same answer.”

“No,” he said. “It isn't.”

Clarisse waited. She did not offer him an easy exit, and she did not ask him to stay. Percy rested his hand on the doorframe. Silena's note made a small square beneath her palm.

Poseidon looked from one child to another. “I can stay a little longer.”

Silena's shoulders went still. Clarisse saw Percy turn his head, but he did not speak for her. She did not speak either.

“Would you like to?” Clarisse asked.

“I would.” Poseidon looked at her. “If you would have me.”

The words were not a test. That did not mean she had to make them easy. “You can sit with us. Don't make it into a speech.”

“I won't.”

“Good.”

They sat outside the cabin, where the shade from the roof reached the bench. Percy took the end nearest the door. Silena chose the other end. Poseidon sat between them, with a space left on the bench that Clarisse could take or leave. She stayed standing for a moment, then leaned against the post. The wood had warmed in the sun.

For a while the conversation was ordinary. Percy told them about a cracked tile near the training pool. Silena asked if it was dangerous or just annoying. Percy said it was both, depending on whether you were trying to run. Poseidon asked whether the hall's caretaker knew. Percy said yes, but the replacement tile had not arrived. Silena said the same thing had happened with the amphitheater steps last season, and the carpenter had painted a warning stripe until the repair was done.

Clarisse listened to them talk about repairs. It was strange how much easier it was to sit through details that had no hidden answer. A tile could be cracked. A stripe could mark it. Somebody could order a replacement. Nobody had to pretend that was the same as fixing the years a person had missed.

Poseidon asked whether the figures on the table were Clarisse's work. She looked at him, then back at the cabin. “Yes.”

“They're good.”

“That's a broad review.”

“I don't know enough about carving to say more.”

“Then don't.”

He nodded. “Would you teach me?”

The question caught her off guard. “No.”

“Fair.”

“You'd need to sit still for hours.”

“I can do that.”

“You'd need to listen to corrections.”

“I can do that too.”

“You'd have to use cheap wood and a small knife.”

“I have no objection.”

Clarisse narrowed her eyes. “You are not getting me to promise a lesson.”

“I didn't ask for a promise.”

“Good.”

“Would you consider it?”

She looked at the figures again. The panther was gone from the table, carried to Percy's bunk. The three remaining pieces stood together in the patch of sunlight. She could imagine Poseidon sitting over a block of wood, making a mess of the first cut. She could also imagine him turning the attempt into proof that he had done something for them. She did not know which version would happen.

“I'll think about it,” she said.

“That's enough.”

“Don't sound proud of yourself.”

“I'll try not to.”

Silena stood. “I need to take the note back to my bunk.”

“You can leave it,” Clarisse said.

“I want it with me.” Silena looked at Poseidon. “I don't want you to answer it or keep it. I just want to have it.”

“Then keep it.”

Silena went inside. Percy watched her go, then looked at Clarisse. “I can walk him to the lake.”

“Do you want to?”

“Yeah.”

“Then go.”

Poseidon stood. Clarisse stepped aside from the doorway. He did not reach for her. She had not decided whether she would want that, and she was glad he did not make her answer with her body.

“Thank you for lunch,” he said.

“You helped carry it.”

“I did.”

“Then thanks for carrying it.”

A small smile touched his face. “You're welcome.”

He turned to Silena's empty place on the bench, as if considering whether to call her back. He did not. Clarisse wondered whether that was restraint or simple uncertainty. It didn't matter enough for her to decide.

At the edge of the path, Percy fell into step beside his father. Clarisse followed several paces behind, not because she wanted to supervise them but because the lake was in the same direction as the forge. Silena came out after a minute and joined her. She had left the note inside.

They reached the shore together. Poseidon stopped where the water lapped at the grass. Percy stood close enough to hear him without having to lean in. Clarisse stayed with Silena near the path.

Clarisse watched Percy absorb the promise. She wanted to know whether the words helped. She didn't ask. The promise was for him to judge, and time would be the only thing that could give it weight.

Poseidon stepped toward the water, then turned back to Silena. “I hope I hear you sing one day.”

“You might,” Silena said. “If I decide to.”

“I hope you do.”

“That’s okay.”

Poseidon reached into the fold of his robe and brought out a small glass vial. It held a handful of sea-glass beads in shades of blue and green, each one worn smooth at the edges.

“I thought you might use these for your jewelry,” he said to Silena. “Would you like them?”

Silena looked at the vial without reaching for it. Clarisse watched her take the time she needed. The beads could have meant a gift, an apology, a request to be thanked, or only a handful of things he had noticed she liked. No one could decide which meaning she had to take.

“Yes,” Silena said at last. “I’ll keep them.”

Poseidon placed the vial in her hand. Silena closed her fingers around it, then tucked it into her pocket. She did not smile for his sake. She did not refuse for Clarisse’s. She simply chose to keep the beads.

Poseidon turned to Percy. “May I?”

Percy nodded. His father drew him into a brief hug, one hand between his shoulder blades. Percy let his arms come up around him. When they parted, Poseidon said he would send word directly to Percy. Percy answered, “Okay.”

Clarisse looked at the beads in Silena’s pocket and then at Percy. She had nothing to accept or refuse. A part of her was relieved he had not brought her a gift to make the day easier. Another part noticed the empty space all the same. She could leave both reactions where they were.

Poseidon faced Clarisse. “Goodbye.”

“See you.”

It was not a promise. It was not an invitation. It was a sentence that left room for the next time without pretending one was arranged.

He stepped into the lake. The water rose around him, then folded over the place where he had been. The ripples spread to the shore and tapped Clarisse's boots. She did not move until they receded.

Percy looked at her. “You okay?”

“That's what you asked me when he left the last time.”

“Did I?”

“Yeah.”

“What did you say?”

“That I was annoyed.”

“Are you?”

“Yes.”

He waited.

“And not only annoyed,” she added.

He nodded. “Okay.”

“You don't need to make a face like you solved something.”

“I didn't.”

“You were going to.”

“I was going to ask what else.”

“Don't.”

“All right.”

Silena came up beside them. “I made tea.”

“Now?” Clarisse asked.

“It's already made.”

“That doesn't answer why.”

“I wanted tea.”

“Fine.”

They went back toward the cabin. Percy walked behind his sisters, neither hurrying nor dragging his feet. Clarisse could see the water on his sleeve where the lake had splashed him. She wanted to tell him to change before it dried stiff. She didn't. He knew where his clothes were.

Inside, Silena poured three cups from the small pot she had left covered on the shelf. She had made nectar tea, the kind that tasted a little different to each person. Clarisse's carried the sharp cinnamon warmth she liked. Percy sat down at the table and wrapped both hands around his cup.

“Are you keeping them?” Clarisse asked Silena.

Silena looked at her. “Yes.”

“Because you like them?”

“Because I like them.” Silena rubbed the rim of her cup with her thumb. “I thought about saying no before he offered. Then I decided I could answer when it happened.”

Clarisse picked up her cup. “You don't have to explain it.”

“I know. I wanted to say it out loud.”

Silena sat across from Percy.

Percy lowered his cup. “I get that.”

Clarisse took a sip. The tea was hot enough to make her pause. She set it down and waited for the warmth to settle. It did not fix anything. It made the cabin feel like a place where the three of them could sit after a difficult afternoon without turning it into a meeting.

“What did you show him in the hall?” Clarisse asked Percy.

“The pool. The diving platform. The channel where the water comes in.”

“Was he impressed?”

“I don't know. He said the channel was narrower than he remembered.”

“Was it?”

“Maybe. I don't know how long ago he saw it.” Percy rubbed his thumb against the cup's handle. “He asked if I liked going there.”

“What did you say?”

“That I do.”

“Do you?”

“Yeah. It's quiet when there aren't lessons.”

“Then tell him that.”

“I did.”

Clarisse nodded. She was glad Percy had his own answer, even if it was small. She could not tell whether he had wanted the visit to be more or less than it was. It seemed unfair to make him report his feelings as if they were a score.

Silena lifted her cup. “He listened.”

“He did,” Percy said.

“That doesn't settle everything.”

“No.”

“I don't want it to have to.” Silena's voice was level. “I don't want one afternoon to become evidence that we have to be happy now.”

“No one gets to require that,” Clarisse said.

Silena's eyes met hers. “You don't have to require it either.”

Clarisse felt the point land, not as a rebuke but as a reminder. “I know.”

“Okay.”

They drank their tea in quiet. The cabin creaked as the afternoon heat began to fade. Outside, someone called for a missing sandal. Another camper answered that it was by the canoe racks. The ordinary noise returned piece by piece, as if camp had been waiting for the lake to let go of its silence.

Percy finished first. He carried his cup to the washbasin and rinsed it. Silena asked him to leave it upside down on the cloth. He did. Clarisse took her own cup but Silena stopped her.

“I'll do it.”

“I can wash one cup.”

“I know. I want to wash them.”

Clarisse set it back. “All right.”

Silena washed the cups slowly, making sure the tea did not stain their insides. She left them to dry on the rack, then folded the cloth around the tea tin and put it away. Clarisse watched her choose the shelf without asking whether the tea should be saved for another day.

“Are you going to give him the note if he comes back?” Percy asked.

“I don't know.” Silena dried her hands. “If he asks about rehearsal, I might tell him to check the board. The note was only for me to figure out how to say it.”

“Then you don't need to decide now,” Clarisse said.

“I know. I'm done deciding about it for today.”

“Good.”

Silena took the note from her pocket, unfolded it, and read the two lines. She did not tear it up. She folded it again and placed it in the drawer with her paper and pencils. It was hers to keep, and it could wait there without becoming a promise.

Clarisse stepped outside. The sky had begun to turn gold above the trees. She could hear the forge from here: a hammer striking metal, someone counting, then a pause while the next piece was turned. She wanted to go back and finish tightening the shield strap she'd taken there earlier. She also wanted to stay on the step for a few minutes.

Percy came to stand beside her. “Are you going to the forge?”

“Later.”

“Want me to come?”

“No.”

“Okay.”

He did not ask if she wanted company instead. He did not take her no as a request for something else. She was grateful and did not say so.

“Did you want him to stay longer?” he asked.

Clarisse looked at him. “You asked whether I'm okay. I answered.”

“I know.”

“Then let that be the answer.”

“All right.”

He leaned one shoulder against the doorframe. He looked tired in the open, unguarded way people looked after spending a day outside. Clarisse wondered whether the trip to Tempest Hall had given him a chance to ask questions he had been saving. She did not ask what they were. If he wanted to tell her, he would.

The last sunlight caught the edge of the chalkboard where Silena had left the menu. The ingredient card was gone; she had brought it inside. The uneven board stood straight now, the small card shim still hidden beneath one leg. Clarisse had seen Silena check it twice. She hoped the card held. If it slipped, the board would wobble again, and Silena would fix it.

“I'm going to ask the kitchen for the card back tomorrow,” Clarisse said.

“Which card?”

“The one under the board. It's from the repair box.”

“Oh. We should probably put it back.”

“We will.”

“We?”

“You're the one who carried the board.”

“Fair.”

The answer was ordinary enough to leave them both smiling. Clarisse did not mistake it for reconciliation. It was simply a shared task they could finish.

When Percy went inside, Clarisse stayed on the step for a little longer. The lake had become noisy again, frogs calling from the reeds and water tapping the dock. No sign remained of the pillar that had brought Poseidon to shore. She could still feel the small swell against her boots, though she knew it was gone.

She had invited him to lunch. He had helped carry the food. He had asked questions and accepted some answers without prying for the rest. He had stayed when she challenged him to choose whether he wanted to, and he had left when it was time for him to go. Those things were not enough to balance everything. They did not have to be enough to count.

Clarisse stood and went to the forge.

---

Percy stayed by the lake after Clarisse left. The cabin door had swung closed behind her, and Silena was inside putting away her writing things. He could have followed Clarisse to the forge. He could have gone to the dining pavilion, or found Annabeth, or waited for the message his father had said he would send. Instead he sat on the dock, where the boards were still warm from the afternoon sun.

The water had returned to its usual sound. A small wave slipped under the dock and pushed at one of the supports. Percy watched it travel along the shore until it broke against a flat stone. For a few seconds he imagined he could still see where his father had stood. Then the reflection changed and there was only sky.

He hadn't asked Poseidon about the message. He hadn't asked how soon it would arrive or what it would say. The promise had sounded simple. Percy had learned that simple promises could still be hard to keep, but he had also learned that it did not help to demand a timetable before one existed. He would wait to see what happened.

He thought of the training pool at Tempest Hall. His father had noticed the cracked tile and the narrow channel, details Percy had passed without comment because the place was familiar. Poseidon had asked about the pool as if the answer mattered. Percy had said he liked it when it was quiet. His father had listened, then told him to ask the caretaker before trying to fix the drain. It was a small conversation. Percy did not know how much to make of it, so he tried not to make anything of it yet.

He heard footsteps on the path and turned. Annabeth came down from the direction of the cabins with a rolled sheet of paper under her arm. She slowed when she saw him, then chose the empty stretch of dock rather than the bench behind him.

“Hey,” she said.

“Hey.”

She sat beside him and set the paper between them. The top edge had a faint blue line drawn along it. “I was looking for you.”

“Did something happen?”

“I wanted to show you the drainage sketch before I forget which measurement I changed.”

“That's why you were looking for me?”

“Partly.” She glanced at his face. “Clarisse said your dad came by.”

“Yeah.”

“Are you all right?”

“Yeah. It was okay.”

Annabeth waited, looking out at the water rather than leaning into him for an answer. Percy noticed the care in that and felt a small loosening in his chest. He didn't have to perform a tidy version of the day for her.

“He came to see all of us,” he said. “We had lunch. I showed him the pool. He said he'd send me a message.”

“Do you believe him?”

“I want to.” Percy picked at a loose thread on his shorts. “I don't know if that means I believe him.”

“That seems fair.”

“I didn't ask when.”

“Do you wish you had?”

“Maybe. But if I asked, then I'd be waiting for a date he hadn't given me.”

Annabeth nodded. “You can ask later if you want to.”

“I know.” He let the silence settle. “Clarisse invited him to lunch. She acted like it was just because he was there and could help carry things.”

“Maybe that was part of it.”

“Yeah.”

“Did she seem okay with him?”

“Some of the time. She was still Clarisse.”

Annabeth smiled. “That usually means she's doing fine enough to tell someone off.”

“Pretty much.”

“And Silena?”

“She told him she'd let him know if she scheduled a rehearsal. She didn't promise he could come.”

“That's a good boundary.”

“Yeah. I think she was relieved after she said it.”

“Did she tell you?”

“No. I just saw her.” Percy looked down at the board. “She might've been relieved. Or just done talking about it.”

“Those can look similar.”

“Right.”

Annabeth rested her hand on the rolled plan. “You don't have to figure out what the day meant for them.”

“I know.” He looked at her. “I kind of want to, though.”

“Because you care.”

“Yeah.”

“Caring doesn't give you the answer.”

“I know that too.”

She did not tell him the visit had gone well, or that Poseidon had proved something. She did not say he should feel hopeful. Percy was grateful she didn't turn the afternoon into a lesson about feelings. She was making space for him to have them without sorting them into a conclusion.

“What was the sketch?” he asked.

Annabeth unrolled it over their knees. It showed a narrow channel running past the shrine base, with measurements written along one side. She had drawn the ground as a gentle slope toward the trees, and the runoff line in blue. A second line marked where the stone lip would keep water from cutting across the path.

“I want to widen the outlet here,” she said. “If the runoff stays in this channel, it won't wash the gravel out near the steps.”

“Is that the spot that flooded last week?”

“Not flooded. It pooled.”

“That's a distinction.”

“It is if you're trying to tell the maintenance crew what to fix.”

Percy leaned closer. “What happens if the water comes in faster?”

“It can spill over the low side.” She pointed to a small notch in the drawn stone lip. “This keeps it from building up. The crew can widen that notch if we get more rain than expected.”

“Could the water just go around the shrine?”

“It could. Then it would run under the path and loosen the edge stones.”

“Would the path move?”

“Not all at once. It might settle unevenly first.”

“Which is worse, because then someone trips.”

“Exactly.”

She held the page while he traced the route in the air, careful not to touch the pencil marks. They talked about the grade, where runoff had collected, and how the crew could set stones without blocking the channel. Percy did not pretend to understand every measurement. He asked questions where the drawing confused him and let Annabeth explain the difference between what she had observed and what she was proposing.

The sketch had a corner folded under where she had changed a number. She opened it and showed him the original measurement beneath the correction. “I thought the ground was flatter. I came back and checked.”

“Do you have to redraw all of it?”

“Not all of it. I can make a clean copy after I confirm the outlet.”

“Can I come with you?”

“Tomorrow, if you want.”

“I want to.”

“Then come.”

She put the pencil behind her ear and gathered the sheet back into a roll. Percy took the loose end to help her fold it neatly. They worked carefully, each keeping a hand on the paper so it would not spring open. When they finished, Annabeth tucked it beneath her arm.

“Thanks for looking,” she said.

“Thanks for showing me.”

“You didn't have to understand all of it.”

“I wanted to.”

“I know.”

Percy looked at her. “I like seeing you work something out.”

She raised an eyebrow. “You like drainage plans?”

“I like you explaining the parts you care about.”

Her expression softened, but she did not laugh it off. She set the rolled sketch on the dock between them and put her hand over his. “I like telling you.”

The contact was warm and familiar. Percy turned his palm under hers and linked their fingers. He could feel the rough edge of graphite on her thumb. He rubbed it lightly with his own, and it smeared a little darker.

“You have pencil on your hand,” he said.

“It's probably on yours now too.”

“It is.”

“Then we're both marked.”

“Very romantic.”

“I thought so.”

He leaned over and kissed her. It was gentle, a familiar pause between them, and she met him without hesitation. When they drew apart, she rested her forehead against his for a moment.

“I'm glad you came to find me,” he said.

“I wanted to.”

“Even though I was sitting here being vague.”

“You weren't vague. You said it was okay, and then you explained what okay meant.”

“I did?”

“You told me the facts and the part you didn't know.”

“That's a lot of explaining.”

“It was useful.”

He smiled. “You're my favorite person to explain things to.”

“Good. I'm your favorite person to look at drainage drawings with.”

“That's a very specific category.”

“I like winning categories nobody else entered.”

He laughed. The sound carried over the water and came back smaller. Annabeth unrolled the plan again and pointed to one final measurement. He listened while she explained why she'd changed it, then asked whether they could look at the site together tomorrow. She said yes, provided he didn't step into the channel before she had checked how deep it was.

“I can follow directions,” he said.

“You can follow some directions.”

“That's still a passing grade.”

“For now.”

The light above the trees thinned to blue. They stayed on the dock until the first cabin lights came on, talking about the shrine plan and whether the maintenance crew had enough stone. The afternoon did not end with a declaration or a decision. Percy did not ask Annabeth to tell him whether his father had changed. He did not ask her to make the day easier to understand.

When they stood, he carried the rolled drawing while she gathered the pencil. They walked back toward the cabins shoulder to shoulder. The lake moved beside them, ordinary and restless, and Percy let the sound accompany them without trying to hear a message in it.
