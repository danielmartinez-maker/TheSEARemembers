## Chapter 122: The Lake Stands Up

The first sign that Triton had arrived was the canoe lake standing up.

Not a wave. The whole lake lifted at its center, a neat blue hill of water that made the canoes slide toward shore and sent three ducks quacking indignantly into the reeds. Percy looked up from the camp schedule he was pretending to read. Beside him, Clarisse lowered the whetstone from her spear. Silena set down the ribbon she had been tying around a basket of strawberry jam.

“That’s dramatic,” she said.

“That’s my brother,” Percy said. “He thinks doors are a suggestion.”

The water hill collapsed outward in a shining ring, cold spray kissing their cheeks and the smell of churned mud rising off the shore. Triton rode the spill in on a crest that should have dumped him on the dock and instead set him neatly on the grass. His bronze greaves touched down without so much as a splash. Sea spray rolled off his shoulders, then gathered itself into a thin floating ribbon and vanished into the lake.

He wore a dark green tunic, a shell clasp at one shoulder, and the expression of someone who had been given a task and intended to complete it before anyone could make a joke.

Clarisse looked him over. “No armor?”

“This is a lesson, not a siege.”

“You came in on a wall of water.”

“The water volunteered.”

“I’m sure it did.”

Silena rose and brushed grass from her skirt. She had a small notebook in one hand already. “Triton. Welcome to camp.”

He bowed to her, a little deeper than the greeting called for. “Silena. Clarisse. Percy.”

His gaze rested on his sisters last, but his greeting had given each of them the same space. Percy noticed. He also noticed Clarisse notice it.

“I’m here to prepare you for Atlantis,” Triton said. “Your departure is confirmed for the end of the week. Mother has sent the passage details to Chiron. Father will meet you at the palace.”

“You’re here to teach us how to bow,” Clarisse said.

“I’m here to teach you enough that no one mistakes your confusion for disrespect.”

“I can disrespect people perfectly well without getting confused.”

“I know. That is one of the reasons I’m here.”

Percy folded the schedule. “Couldn’t this have been a letter?”

“You’re the prince. You should understand how a visit works.”

Clarisse’s face lit up. It was not a friendly light. “The prince.”

“Don’t,” Percy said.

“Prince Percy,” Silena said thoughtfully, testing the sound.

“Please don’t.”

“Your Highness,” Clarisse said, and dipped so low she was nearly looking under his chin. “Would Your Highness like me to polish his shoes?”

“I’d like you to stop before I use the lake to put you in a tree.”

“Threatening the royal family. That’s a strong start.”

“We have not begun,” Triton said. “Stand in a line.”

Clarisse stood exactly where she was. Silena moved beside her. Percy stayed on the bench.

Triton closed his eyes for half a second. “A line, Percy.”

“I can make it interesting,” Triton said. “I can call Father and ask him to conduct the lesson.”

Percy stood. “Fine. But if he calls me ‘Prince Perseus’ in front of everyone, I’m going to walk into the lake.”

“That would be a breach of etiquette,” Silena said, pencil poised.

“You have been taking notes for six seconds,” Clarisse said.

“I’ve taken notes for less.”

Triton guided them toward the flat stretch of lawn between the lake and the Big House. Annabeth and Thalia were coming down from the porch, where they had apparently been revising the guest list with Chiron. Annabeth carried a folded map; Thalia had a mug and the face of someone who had heard the phrase “formal greeting” and immediately suspected a trap.

“The diplomacy class has begun,” Thalia said.

“It has not,” Triton replied. “This is orientation.”

“That’s diplomacy class with worse snacks.”

Annabeth joined the line beside Percy. “I’m coming too. I need to know what the palace expects from a guest.”

“Good,” Triton said. “You will be introduced as Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus, and honored guest of the royal household.”

“That sounds reasonable,” Annabeth said. “What do I do with my hands?”

“Keep them visible, don’t draw a weapon unless there’s a real threat, and don’t touch the coral without permission. Don’t—”

“I’m beginning to understand Clarisse’s concerns about palace furniture,” Annabeth said.

“The furniture is mostly innocent.”

“Mostly?” Percy asked.

“There is one chair that bites.”

“Why is there a biting chair?”

“It was made for an admiral who enjoyed being uncomfortable,” Clarisse said. “That I understand.”

Thalia perched on the edge of the bench, staying close enough to listen. “I’ll be there as Annabeth’s sister and camp representative. I’m not taking notes.”

“You may want to take notes,” Silena said.

“I have a perfect memory.”

Triton raised a hand. “We are beginning with the greeting.”

---

“A greeting is not a performance,” Triton said. “It tells the people you meet that you see them, that you know where you are, and that you mean to enter in peace.”

“So, a performance,” Clarisse said.

“No. A performance is what Percy does when he tells people he beat me at current-racing.”

“I did beat you,” Percy said. “The hippocampus was on my side.”

“That is not what ‘race’ means.”

Triton ignored this, which was probably wise. “In Atlantis, the formal greeting is a hand over the heart, palm turned outward. It means: I come without a hidden weapon, and I recognize your welcome.”

Clarisse looked down at her hands. “If I have a weapon, do I lie?”

“No. You leave it with the guard at the threshold, if the room requires it. The gesture is not a claim that you are harmless. It is a sign that you are not concealing your intent.”

“What if I don’t want to leave my spear with a guard?”

“Then you tell the guard. The rule is not more important than your safety.”

Clarisse studied Triton. “You should’ve led with that.”

“I thought I did.”

“You said ‘formal greeting.’ That’s not the same thing.”

“Fair.” Triton tapped his own chest with his right hand, then turned his palm outward. “Hand here, palm open. A slight inclination of the head. You needn’t bow low. We are greeting family, not surrendering to it.”

Triton demonstrated. His gesture looked simple, though Percy knew every movement in Atlantis carried some echo of the water. Triton’s was controlled, not grand. He could have made the grass bow around him; he didn’t.

“You try,” he said.

Silena went first, hand over her heart, palm outward, and inclined her head. “I come in peace and accept your welcome.”

Triton nodded. “Good. You can say that in Atlantean if you wish, but it is not required.”

Silena’s pencil paused. “How do you say it?”

He said a phrase in the deep, fluid language of the palace. Percy recognized it, and watched Silena write the sounds phonetically.

“Can you say it slower?” she asked.

Triton repeated it slowly.

Silena copied them. “Close?”

“Very close.”

“Do I need to bow at the same time?”

“You can. But the gesture matters more than the words.”

She looked up. “The meaning matters more than getting the pronunciation perfect?”

“Yes.”

“Good.” Silena underlined the phrase once. “I can work with that.”

Clarisse tried next, palm out with the blunt certainty of someone daring the air to comment. “I come in peace.”

“Good,” Triton said.

“And I’m keeping the spear.”

“That is not part of the greeting.”

“Then I’m doing great.”

Percy gave the gesture a quick tap to his chest and a nod that looked like he had spotted someone across the dining pavilion.

Triton stared at him. “You have done this since you were small.”

“My hands know it better underwater. It’s different there.”

“The gesture is exactly the same.”

“The water makes it look more dignified.”

“No, it makes you look like you’re waiting for a fish to get out of your way.”

Annabeth went last. Her motion was precise, hand at the proper angle, head dipped just enough.

“That’s it?” she asked.

“That’s it.”

“No special bow for Zeus’s daughter?”

“Not unless the host chooses to offer one. The palace will recognize your family. It will not ask you to perform it.”

Thalia took a sip from her mug. “I like that.”

“You are not required to use the greeting either,” Triton told her. “You are my guest.”

“If someone makes a fuss about it, I’ll use the version where I show both hands and ask where the exits are.”

“That is a perfectly good version.”

“Triton,” Percy said. “You’re supposed to be teaching formality.”

“Formality without sense is a trap with good posture.”

Clarisse glanced at him. “I like this lesson.”

“You haven’t seen the posture part.”

“I’m still considering it.”

They practiced in pairs. Silena asked whether the greeting worked for guards and servants, too. “Anyone who meets your eyes and means the welcome,” Triton said. She wrote down *everyone, not just important people*. Clarisse tried the gesture, then a forearm clasp. Triton returned it. “That’s better.”

“The greeting?”

“You. The greeting is fine.”

Triton clasped her forearm again, briefly and firmly.

When it was Annabeth’s turn, she faced Triton, pressed her hand over her heart and said, with impeccable confidence, “Your Majesty, I come in peace and accept your—”

“Wrong title,” Percy said.

Annabeth froze. Her cheeks pinked. “I did that on purpose.”

“You did not,” Thalia said.

“I was testing whether he was paying attention.”

“That is not a useful test,” Triton said. “I am a prince. ‘Your Highness’ is correct for me. ‘Your Majesty’ is for the king or queen.”

“Right. Your Highness. I knew that.” Annabeth looked at him. “I apologize. And my respects to Her Highness, Queen Amphitrite.”

Silence held for a beat.

Triton’s eyebrows rose. “You’ve promoted me and demoted my mother.”

“I did?”

“You called me Your Majesty and her Your Highness. You made me king and her a princess in one greeting.”

“I was trying to be efficient.”

“You nearly started a succession dispute with grammar.”

Thalia choked on her tea. Percy laughed hard enough to have to brace a hand on the bench. Even Clarisse grinned.

Annabeth put her hands over her face. “I’m an architect. I can build a stable arch. I can’t apparently address a family.”

“The family is harder to build,” Silena said kindly, jotting down the corrected terms.

“You are all enjoying this too much.”

“That’s because you were the one who said it,” Percy told her.

Annabeth lowered her hands. “Fine. I'm never making a mistake again.”

“Don’t promise that,” Triton said. “You’ve made the first one. It’s best to accept the tradition.”

---

“Titles are for introductions,” Triton said. “They are not the whole person.”

He had moved them beneath the shade of the Big House porch. Chiron brought out a stack of cards and a pitcher of lemonade. He claimed he was helping. Percy suspected he mostly wanted to see whether Triton would make anyone bow to a chair.

Triton held up the first card. It read: *King Poseidon and Queen Amphitrite.*

“When addressing both of my parents in a formal greeting, you may say ‘Your Majesties.’ If you speak to either alone, ‘Your Majesty’ is correct. You may also say ‘Lord Poseidon’ or ‘Queen Amphitrite.’ In the family rooms, you may use their names if invited.”

“And in private?” Clarisse asked.

“Their names, if they invite you to use them. If someone corrects you, you don’t need to apologize for being new.”

“For me,” Triton continued, “the formal address is ‘Your Highness.’ When a herald introduces me, it is ‘Prince Triton.’ In the corridors, it is Triton. You may call me Triton anywhere.”

Clarisse folded her arms. “What are our titles?”

Triton looked at her directly. “You are daughters of Poseidon. At court, you may be introduced as Princess Clarisse and Princess Silena.”

Clarisse’s shoulders went square again. “May be?”

“It is a courtesy, not an office or a claim to succession. You can ask to be introduced by your names instead.”

“Would that be disrespectful?”

“No. Some people may be surprised. They will survive.”

Clarisse gave a short huff. “You could’ve said that first.”

“I am learning to lead with the useful part.”

Silena had been writing the titles in a tidy list. At the bottom she added: *Princess, if we want. Names, always.*

“I like that,” she said. “I don’t mind being a princess in the introduction. I don’t want someone to look at me and think they know what I’m supposed to do.”

“They won’t,” Triton said. “Not if I’m there.”

“And if you’re not?” Clarisse asked.

“Then you tell them what you’re supposed to do.”

“Whatever you decided.”

Silena smiled at the page. Beside her, Percy watched Clarisse’s expression. She hadn’t relaxed exactly, but the hard line of her jaw had eased. Triton was giving them room to refuse, not just a rule to obey. That mattered to her. It mattered to Percy too.

“What about Annabeth?” Clarisse asked.

“Lady Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus, honored guest of Atlantis,” Triton said. “A description, not a job.”

Triton handed her the next card. It read *Prince Perseus of Atlantis.* Percy glanced at it, then at his brother. “You made a card for me?”

“You are the one who keeps pretending it doesn’t apply.”

“It doesn’t apply here.”

“At camp, no. In the palace, the herald may call you Prince Perseus.”

“I’d rather they called me Percy.”

“Some people will use the full name in the formal hall. You can answer, ‘Thank you, I’m Percy.’ You don’t have to become a different person.”

Clarisse leaned toward Silena. “Your Highness, would you like a strawberry?”

Percy grabbed one from the basket and threw it at her. Clarisse caught it in her mouth.

“Perfect form,” Triton said.

“I can do a bow too,” Clarisse said around the berry. “I refuse to waste one.”

“A bow is not wasted if it makes someone feel welcome,” Silena said.

“I said it isn’t wasted if it makes someone feel welcome. I didn’t say I was going to do it.”

“That’s progress,” Percy said.

“You don’t get to grade me.”

“As Prince Percy—”

Clarisse raised a finger. “Finish that sentence and I’m feeding you to your own lake.”

“As your little brother,” Percy said quickly, “I’m proud of you.”

“That’s a much better title.”

Silena tucked the notebook under her arm and nudged Percy with her shoulder. “You’re going to be introduced as a prince. We’re going to call you that at least once.”

“Why?”

“Because we love you.”

“That doesn’t make sense.”

“It doesn’t have to.”

“Your sisters have discovered the title of prince makes you blush,” Thalia said. “This will be a difficult week for you.”

“It will not.”

“Prince Percy,” Annabeth said.

He looked at her, smiling. Her hand rested close to his on the bench. He squeezed it once, quick and warm, while the others argued about whether a title could be used as a weapon.

---

The palace tour happened on the grass, with four benches, serving trays, and a wooden fish Chiron found in a cupboard.

“Why is there a wooden fish?” Percy asked.

“A gift,” Chiron said. “It has been useful for decades.”

“As what?”

“A fish.”

Triton placed the wooden fish at the end of the benches, then set the trays on a low table. “We will practice entering a formal room, receiving an introduction, and taking a seat at dinner.”

Clarisse eyed the arrangement. “This is the etiquette lesson?”

“Yes.”

“Where’s the palace?”

“I have described it.”

“You said the corridors are pale stone and the archways have coral. That’s not the same as seeing it.”

“No,” Annabeth said. “It’s not.” She crouched and spread the map on a bench. “Could you draw the room?”

Triton took a piece of charcoal and sketched the receiving hall: the garden doors, the king and queen’s places, and the prince’s seat to the right. “At formal council, I sit at the far end.”

“Why?” Annabeth asked.

“The room is arranged for the current being discussed. In family, it is a home. In council, a map of duties.”

Annabeth sketched the room in the margin. “Can visitors sit by the garden?”

“Usually.”

“Can we leave during a formal meal if we need to?”

“Yes. No one is trapped at a table.”

Annabeth looked up. “I appreciate that.”

“There’s a second door behind the shell wall. The court uses it when the main hall is full.”

“A hidden door?” Annabeth leaned closer. “That is a terrible use of a door.”

“You may tell the architect.”

“I will.”

Percy tilted his head at the map. “The room changed since I was little. They added the shell wall after the eastern arch flooded.”

Annabeth looked at him. “The hall flooded?”

“One of the outer channels backed up. Water came in knee-high and knocked over two chairs.”

“Was the biting chair involved?”

“It bit a footman and floated away.”

“I’m starting to like the chair.”

“They put it in a gallery after that.”

“Good. Let it reflect on its behavior.”

Triton lifted the wooden fish. “I am the herald. This is the fish.”

“Then why does it have a hat?” Clarisse asked.

“I thought it looked underdressed,” Silena said.

“Fish don’t wear hats.”

“Atlantean fish might.”

“Have you met one?”

“No. Fine, we don’t know.”

Triton plucked the fish from Thalia’s hand. “The herald will announce the guests. You will wait until the name is complete before you step forward. A nod, a greeting, and you may approach the hosts when they invite you.”

“What if they forget to invite us forward?” Clarisse asked.

“Then you can come forward, or wait until they finish speaking.”

“Clarisse,” Percy said gently, “we can ask them when we’re there.”

She glanced at him, then closed her mouth. “I’m asking now so we don’t have to ask there.”

“That makes sense,” he said.

Triton didn’t cut in. He let the answer stand.

They took turns approaching the benches. Triton announced each in a solemn voice that made everything sound like the opening of a trial.

“Clarisse, daughter of Poseidon, guest of the royal household.”

She stepped forward, palm to heart, gave a small nod, and stopped exactly out of arm’s reach.

“Well done,” Triton said. “You can come closer; the hall is not a sparring ring.”

“Habit,” Clarisse said, but she took one step.

Silena followed. “Silena, daughter of Poseidon, guest of the royal household.”

She moved forward, made the greeting, and smiled as if the invitation were personal. It was. Her warmth wasn’t a breach of etiquette. It was the welcome.

“Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus, honored guest of Atlantis,” Triton announced.

She made a formal nod. “I’m grateful for your invitation, Prince Triton.”

“Good.”

“Does ‘honored guest’ mean I’m required to behave honorably?”

“You are required to behave as yourself.”

“That could be interpreted broadly.”

“It usually is.”

“That’s reassuring.”

“Prince Percy of Atlantis,” Triton said.

“No.”

“You have to practice.”

“I know my name.”

“Then use it.”

Percy walked the three steps toward the makeshift receiving hall. He gave the greeting and looked at Triton. “Hi.”

“Your Highness,” Clarisse said from the line.

“Don’t start.”

“It’s the title. I’m being respectful.”

“You are enjoying yourself.”

“That doesn’t make it disrespectful.”

“It might.”

“A good title can carry two things.”

“That’s not a real rule.”

“It is now,” Silena said, writing it in her notebook.

Triton turned to the makeshift table. “Wait for the host to sit before you do. Take the seat offered, and ask if you need another. There is no hidden test in the furniture.”

“That’s good to know,” Percy said. “Triton told me I once sat in the admiral’s chair at council and got a speech about the order of the currents.”

“You put your feet on the table and fell asleep during it,” Triton said. “Grandmother made you take the lesson again the next day.”

“With his feet on the floor?” Silena asked.

“That was the rule.” Triton had described the silver-haired woman correcting Percy’s posture, then slipping him a sweet. Percy wished he remembered her himself. “Will she be at the palace? I’d like to meet her with you.”

“At dinner, use the utensils provided. Ask if you don’t know what they’re for. Wait until food is offered, and say if you can’t eat something. Nobody counts bites,” Triton continued. “And don’t stab the furniture.”

Annabeth glanced at the imaginary biting chair. “A tragedy.”

Silena wrote down *nobody counts bites*. Clarisse eyed the four spoons on the tray. “Why can’t I use one?”

“You can,” Triton said.

“Then we’re finished.” Clarisse picked the largest spoon. “I’ll use this.”

“That one is for soup.”

“Then I’ll have soup.”

Silena chose the shell scoop. “I’ll try the first course.”

“I’ll try it too,” Percy said. “I’m not leaving her alone with the mystery dish.”

“It’s chilled kelp and citrus,” Triton said.

“You called another dish a mistake. I have concerns.”

Annabeth leaned toward Percy. “And to get a servant’s attention?”

“Use their name if you know it. Otherwise, ‘Excuse me’ is enough.”

“Good. I’ve got it.”

“Then announce us,” Clarisse said. “Let’s see if we’re ready.”

Triton stood up straight and took the wooden fish. “The royal household is gathered for supper. The guests are to be welcomed.”

“You’ve made the fish a herald,” Annabeth whispered.

“It has a hat,” Silena whispered back. “It’s official.”

Triton cleared his throat. “Princess Clarisse, daughter of Poseidon, guest of the royal household.”

Clarisse stood. She looked as though her legs had decided they wanted to fight the bench. Her gaze passed over the empty space where the imagined court would be, then returned to Triton.

She did the greeting. “Thank you for the welcome. I’m glad to be here.”

The words were plain. Her voice was not. It had gone a little tight around the edges.

“Good,” Triton said, and did not correct a thing.

Silena stood next. “Princess Silena, daughter of Poseidon, guest of the royal household.”

Her greeting was warmer, and she added the Atlantean words she had just learned. The phrase came out slightly uneven. She caught the stumble, smiled at it, and tried once more. On the second attempt it flowed.

“That was lovely,” Percy said.

“It was close,” Triton said. “Lovely is not the measure.”

“I can have two measures.”

“You can.”

“Then it was close and lovely.”

“Agreed.”

Annabeth rose. “Lady Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus, honored guest of Atlantis.”

She held the pause for exactly the right amount of time. “Your Highness, I accept your welcome.”

“Good,” Triton said. “You have learned.”

“I have memorized.”

“That is a start.”

“I have not forgotten the succession dispute.”

“No one has.”

“Excellent.”

“Prince Percy,” Triton announced.

Percy rose with a dramatic sigh. “I'm Percy. Thanks for inviting me.”

“You have failed to use the title.”

“I acknowledged the title by ignoring it.”

“That is not how acknowledging works.”

“It worked for me.”

Clarisse pointed at him. “I like that one.”

“Of course you do,” Triton said.

Percy looked down at the table. It was strange how many details returned as soon as he let them: the shape of the benches, the open space where the servants would move, a blue glass bowl in the center. He could almost hear the palace again, the soft rush of water through its channels, and the low hum of the city outside.

“You’re worried,” Silena said quietly.

“No.”

“You are.”

“I’m thinking.”

“Those aren’t opposites.”

“They are when I’m doing it.”

Clarisse set her hand on his shoulder, heavy and familiar. “You don’t have to show us every room or explain every rule before we get there. If something is weird, we’ll ask.”

“I know.”

“And if we get lost, we’ll find you.”

“That’s the part I’m worried about.”

“Why?”

“Because you’ll take a wrong corridor on purpose to see what’s down it.”

“That sounds like a good plan.”

“It sounds like you.”

“Same thing.”

Silena’s elbow nudged his. “We’ll stay together. We can be confused together.”

“That’s a strong family plan,” Annabeth said.

“And if the chair bites one of us?” Clarisse asked.

“We’ll put it in the lake,” Percy said.

“Not the lake,” Triton said. “It’s an antique.”

“The lake can appreciate antiques.”

“The lake has excellent taste.”

“Then the lake can decide.”

Silena held up her notebook. “I’ve got the plan. We greet the hosts, ask for names if we’re unsure, don’t eat the mistake fish, don’t touch the coral, don’t let the chair bite us, and remember we can ask to leave a room.”

“And use the titles correctly,” Triton said.

“Mostly correctly,” Annabeth said.

“Correctly,” Triton insisted.

“I reserve the right to one mistake per day.”

“One?” Percy asked. “That seems ambitious.”

“One formal mistake. You get the rest.”

“I accept.”

“You aren’t the host,” Triton said.

“I accept on the host’s behalf.”

“You cannot do that.”

“I have decided to.”

“That is not etiquette.”

“It is architecture. I’m creating a framework for future mistakes.”

Thalia raised her mug. “I support the framework.”

---

By the time the sun leaned toward the pine trees, Triton had reviewed their greetings three times and the table rules until Percy could identify the spoon for pearlfruit.

“What is pearlfruit?” Clarisse asked.

“A fruit,” Percy said. “It grows in shell-gardens.”

“I’ll believe it when I see it.” Silena wrote *pearlfruit* in her notebook and added a question mark. “And the garden plants that change color when someone walks past?”

“Those are real,” Percy said. “One kind copies the visitor’s colors.”

Annabeth frowned. “That is unreasonable.”

“It’s pretty.”

“That’s what unreasonable things say to get away with it.”

Silena closed her notebook and cradled it to her chest. “I want to see the garden. And the kitchens. And whatever room has the family stories. Percy, will you show us your favorite place?”

Percy pictured the training terrace, the garden behind his mother’s apartments, the window ledge where silver fish circled the lamps. He’d never thought of showing those places to Clarisse and Silena.

“Yeah,” he said. “I’ll show you.”

“And the room with the biting chair?” Clarisse asked.

“Watch me ask,” Triton said.

Silena slipped her arm through Clarisse’s. “We should bring the jam and the garden painting.”

“The jam is in Percy’s bag,” Clarisse said. “He can explain it formally. It contains strawberries.”

“He’s going to be unbearable all week,” Silena said.

“He’s already unbearable,” Clarisse said. “Now he’ll be unbearable with a title.”

Triton rolled up the hand-drawn map. With care, his hands flattened the paper before folding it, smoothing each crease with his thumb. Percy knew his brother could be impatient with people, but never with things he valued.

“You did well,” Triton said.

“We stood up and said hello,” Clarisse said. “That’s not difficult.”

“It can be. You don’t know what it will feel like in the hall.”

“Neither do you,” she said. “Not this time.”

He paused with the map half folded. Percy knew Clarisse wasn’t trying to jab at him. Being a child who belonged in the palace wasn’t the same as arriving with sisters who had never seen it.

“No,” Triton said. “I don’t.”

“Then we’ll learn it when we get there. You tell us the rules; we’ll tell you when they make things worse.”

“That seems fair.”

“It is.” Silena leaned on Clarisse’s shoulder. “And I’ll take notes.”

“Of course you will.”

“Someone has to remember which spoon goes with the pearlfruit.”

“Use whichever spoon makes you happy,” Clarisse said. “I’m not fighting a spoon.”

“There’s one more thing,” Triton said.

“A second biting chair?”

“No. I want you to know the titles and customs so you can decide what you want to use. Not so you can pass a test. The court can be loud. Some people may see Poseidon’s daughters before they see Clarisse or Silena. I can’t control what they notice first.”

“You can control how they behave,” Clarisse said.

“I can try.”

“Good.”

He looked at Silena. “I can make sure you know where to find us, where you can go, and who to ask for help. I can’t promise every part of the palace will feel familiar.”

“It doesn’t have to,” she said. “We’ll be there together.”

Clarisse bumped Percy’s shoulder. “You’re doing the thinking face.”

“That’s my face.”

“Your regular face is worse,” she said. Silena hooked an arm through his. “We’ll be fine, little brother. And you’ll be fine, Prince Percy.”

“I’m calling you both ‘Your Most Annoyingness.’”

“That’s not a title,” Triton said.

“It is now,” Silena replied. “The palace can learn.”

“One more practice?” Triton asked.

Clarisse groaned. “No.”

“Seven is lucky,” Silena said.

“Seven is a number.”

“It can be both.”

Percy picked up the wooden herald-fish and set its paper hat straight. “Fine. One more. Her Majesty of the Sea—”

“No,” Annabeth said.

“Prince Triton, who has not been bitten by a chair today—”

“Percy.”

“Princess Clarisse, armed but not concealing it—”

“I have a knife in my boot,” Clarisse said. “That’s concealing it.”

Triton covered his face. Silena laughed; Thalia caught her sliding notebook, and Annabeth rescued the fish before Percy could declare it monarch.

“We’ll be all right,” Silena said.

“Probably,” Percy said.

“Definitely,” Clarisse corrected. “We have a plan.”

“A list,” Silena said.

“Same thing,” Annabeth said.

“A list is shorter.”

The lake settled back into its ordinary shape. The ducks returned to the reeds, still muttering. Triton watched them, smiling faintly.

Triton looked at his sisters. The practiced sternness slipped, showing the older brother underneath.

“I want you to feel welcome there,” he said. “Not like guests who have to earn a place, or titles walking through a hall. You’re my sisters. I want the palace to feel like it has room for you.”
