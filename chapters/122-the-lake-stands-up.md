## Chapter 122: The Lake Stands Up

The first sign that Triton had arrived was the canoe lake standing up.

Not a wave. The whole lake lifted at its center, a neat blue hill of water that made the canoes slide toward shore and sent three ducks quacking indignantly into the reeds. Percy looked up from the camp schedule he was pretending to read. Beside him, Clarisse lowered the whetstone from her spear. Silena set down the ribbon she had been tying around a basket of strawberry jam.

“That’s dramatic,” she said.

“That’s my brother,” Percy said. “He thinks doors are a suggestion.”

The water hill collapsed outward in a shining ring, cold spray kissing their cheeks and the smell of churned mud rising off the shore. Triton rode the spill in on a crest that should have dumped him on the dock and instead set him neatly on the grass. His bronze greaves touched down without so much as a splash. Sea spray rolled off his shoulders, then gathered itself into a thin floating ribbon and vanished into the lake.

He wore a dark green tunic, a shell clasp at one shoulder, and the expression of someone who had been given a task and intended to complete it before anyone could make a joke.

Clarisse looked him over. “No armor?”

“This is a lesson, not a siege.”

“You came in on a wall of water.”

“The water volunteered.”

“I’m sure it did.”

Silena rose and brushed grass from her skirt. She had a small notebook in one hand already. “Triton. Welcome to camp.”

He bowed to her, a little deeper than the greeting called for. “Silena. Clarisse. Percy.”

His gaze rested on his sisters last, but his greeting had given each of them the same space. Percy noticed. He also noticed Clarisse notice it.

“I’m here to prepare you for Atlantis,” Triton said. “Mother has suggested the end of the week for the palace visit, if you're ready. She sent the possible passage details to Chiron. Father will meet you there if you choose to come.”

“You’re here to teach us how to bow,” Clarisse said.

“I’m here to teach you enough that no one mistakes your confusion for disrespect.”

“I can disrespect people perfectly well without getting confused.”

“I know. That is one of the reasons I’m here.”

Percy folded the schedule. “Couldn’t this have been a letter?”

“You’re the prince. You should understand how a visit works.”

Clarisse’s face lit up. It was not a friendly light. “The prince.”

“Don’t,” Percy said.

“Prince Percy,” Silena said thoughtfully, testing the sound.

“Please don’t.”

“Your Highness,” Clarisse said, and dipped so low she was nearly looking under his chin. “Would Your Highness like me to polish his shoes?”

“I’d like you to stop before I use the lake to put you in a tree.”

“Threatening the royal family. That’s a strong start.”

“We have not begun,” Triton said. “Stand in a line.”

Clarisse stood exactly where she was. Silena moved beside her. Percy stayed on the bench.

Triton closed his eyes for half a second. “A line, Percy.”

“I can make it interesting,” Triton said. “I can call Father and ask him to conduct the lesson.”

Percy stood. “Fine. But if he calls me ‘Prince Perseus’ in front of everyone, I’m going to walk into the lake.”

“That would be a breach of etiquette,” Silena said, pencil poised.

“You have been taking notes for six seconds,” Clarisse said.

“I’ve taken notes for less.”

Triton guided them toward the flat stretch of lawn between the lake and the Big House. Annabeth and Thalia were coming down from the porch, where they had apparently been revising the guest list with Chiron. Annabeth carried a folded map; Thalia had a mug and the face of someone who had heard the phrase “formal greeting” and immediately suspected a trap.

“The diplomacy class has begun,” Thalia said.

“It has not,” Triton replied. “This is orientation.”

“That’s diplomacy class with worse snacks.”

Annabeth joined the line beside Percy. “I’m coming too. I need to know what the palace expects from a guest.”

“Good,” Triton said. “You will be introduced as Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus, and honored guest of the royal household.”

“That sounds reasonable,” Annabeth said. “What do I do with my hands?”

“Keep them visible, don’t draw a weapon unless there’s a real threat, and don’t touch the coral without permission. Don’t—”

“I’m beginning to understand Clarisse’s concerns about palace furniture,” Annabeth said.

“The furniture is mostly innocent.”

“Mostly?” Percy asked.

“There is one chair that bites.”

“Why is there a biting chair?”

“It was made for an admiral who enjoyed being uncomfortable,” Clarisse said. “That I understand.”

Thalia perched on the edge of the bench, staying close enough to listen. “I’ll be there as Annabeth’s sister and camp representative. I’m not taking notes.”

“You may want to take notes,” Silena said.

“I have a perfect memory.”

Triton raised a hand. “We are beginning with the greeting.”

---

“A greeting is not a performance,” Triton said. “It tells the people you meet that you see them, that you know where you are, and that you mean to enter in peace.”

“So, a performance,” Clarisse said.

“No. A performance is what Percy does when he tells people he beat me at current-racing.”

“You told me I beat you,” Percy said. “The hippocampus was on my side.”

“That is not what ‘race’ means.”

Triton ignored this, which was probably wise. “In Atlantis, the formal greeting is a hand over the heart, palm turned outward. It means: I come without a hidden weapon, and I recognize your welcome.”

Clarisse looked down at her hands. “If I have a weapon, do I lie?”

“No. You leave it with the guard at the threshold, if the room requires it. The gesture is not a claim that you are harmless. It is a sign that you are not concealing your intent.”

“What if I don’t want to leave my spear with a guard?”

“Then you tell the guard. The rule is not more important than your safety.”

Clarisse studied Triton. “You should’ve led with that.”

“I thought I did.”

“You said ‘formal greeting.’ That’s not the same thing.”

“Fair.” Triton tapped his own chest with his right hand, then turned his palm outward. “Hand here, palm open. A slight inclination of the head. You needn’t bow low. We are greeting family, not surrendering to it.”

Triton demonstrated. His gesture looked simple, though Percy knew every movement in Atlantis carried some echo of the water. Triton’s was controlled, not grand. He could have made the grass bow around him; he didn’t.

“You try,” he said.

Silena went first, hand over her heart, palm outward, and inclined her head. “I come in peace and accept your welcome.”

Triton nodded. “Good. You can say that in Atlantean if you wish, but it is not required.”

Silena’s pencil paused. “How do you say it?”

He said a phrase in the deep, fluid language of the palace. Percy recognized it, and watched Silena write the sounds phonetically.

“Can you say it slower?” she asked.

Triton repeated it slowly.

Silena copied them. “Close?”

“Very close.”

“Do I need to bow at the same time?”

“You can. But the gesture matters more than the words.”

She looked up. “The meaning matters more than getting the pronunciation perfect?”

“Yes.”

“Good.” Silena underlined the phrase once. “I can work with that.”

Clarisse tried next, palm out with the blunt certainty of someone daring the air to comment. “I come in peace.”

“Good,” Triton said.

“And I’m keeping the spear.”

“That is not part of the greeting.”

“Then I’m doing great.”

Percy gave the gesture a quick tap to his chest and a nod that looked like he had spotted someone across the dining pavilion.

Triton stared at him. “You have done this since you were small.”

“My hands know it better underwater. It’s different there.”

“The gesture is exactly the same.”

“The water makes it look more dignified.”

“No, it makes you look like you’re waiting for a fish to get out of your way.”

Annabeth went last. Her motion was precise, hand at the proper angle, head dipped just enough.

“That’s it?” she asked.

“That’s it.”

“No special bow for Zeus’s daughter?”

“Not unless the host chooses to offer one. The palace will recognize your family. It will not ask you to perform it.”

Thalia took a sip from her mug. “I like that.”

“You are not required to use the greeting either,” Triton told her. “You are my guest.”

“If someone makes a fuss about it, I’ll use the version where I show both hands and ask where the exits are.”

“That is a perfectly good version.”

“Triton,” Percy said. “You’re supposed to be teaching formality.”

“Formality without sense is a trap with good posture.”

Clarisse glanced at him. “I like this lesson.”

“You haven’t seen the posture part.”

“I’m still considering it.”

They practiced in pairs. Silena asked whether the greeting worked for guards and servants, too. “Anyone who meets your eyes and means the welcome,” Triton said. She wrote down *everyone, not just important people*. Clarisse tried the gesture, then a forearm clasp. Triton returned it. “That’s better.”

“The greeting?”

“You. The greeting is fine.”

Triton clasped her forearm again, briefly and firmly.

When it was Annabeth’s turn, she faced Triton, pressed her hand over her heart and said, with impeccable confidence, “Your Majesty, I come in peace and accept your—”

“Wrong title,” Percy said.

Annabeth froze. Her cheeks pinked. “I did that on purpose.”

“You did not,” Thalia said.

“I was testing whether he was paying attention.”

“That is not a useful test,” Triton said. “I am a prince. ‘Your Highness’ is correct for me. ‘Your Majesty’ is for the king or queen.”

“Right. Your Highness. I knew that.” Annabeth looked at him. “I apologize. And my respects to Her Highness, Queen Amphitrite.”

Silence held for a beat.

Triton’s eyebrows rose. “You’ve promoted me and demoted my mother.”

“I did?”

“You called me Your Majesty and her Your Highness. You made me king and her a princess in one greeting.”

“I was trying to be efficient.”

“You nearly started a succession dispute with grammar.”

Thalia choked on her tea. Percy laughed hard enough to have to brace a hand on the bench. Even Clarisse grinned.

Annabeth put her hands over her face. “I’m an architect. I can build a stable arch. I can’t apparently address a family.”

“The family is harder to build,” Silena said kindly, jotting down the corrected terms.

“You are all enjoying this too much.”

“That’s because you were the one who said it,” Percy told her.

Annabeth lowered her hands. “Fine. I'm never making a mistake again.”

“Don’t promise that,” Triton said. “You’ve made the first one. It’s best to accept the tradition.”

---

“Titles are for introductions,” Triton said. “They are not the whole person.”

He had moved them beneath the shade of the Big House porch. Chiron brought out a stack of cards and a pitcher of lemonade. He claimed he was helping. Percy suspected he mostly wanted to see whether Triton would make anyone bow to a chair.

Triton held up the first card. It read: *King Poseidon and Queen Amphitrite.*

“When addressing both of my parents in a formal greeting, you may say ‘Your Majesties.’ If you speak to either alone, ‘Your Majesty’ is correct. You may also say ‘Lord Poseidon’ or ‘Queen Amphitrite.’ In the family rooms, you may use their names if invited.”

“And in private?” Clarisse asked.

“Their names, if they invite you to use them. If someone corrects you, you don’t need to apologize for being new.”

“For me,” Triton continued, “the formal address is ‘Your Highness.’ When a herald introduces me, it is ‘Prince Triton.’ In the corridors, it is Triton. You may call me Triton anywhere.”

Clarisse folded her arms. “What are our titles?”

Triton looked at her directly. “You are daughters of Poseidon. At court, you may be introduced as Princess Clarisse and Princess Silena.”

Clarisse’s shoulders went square again. “May be?”

“It is a courtesy, not an office or a claim to succession. You can ask to be introduced by your names instead.”

“Would that be disrespectful?”

“No. Some people may be surprised. They will survive.”

Clarisse gave a short huff. “You could’ve said that first.”

“I am learning to lead with the useful part.”

Silena had been writing the titles in a tidy list. At the bottom she added: *Princess, if we want. Names, always.*

“I like that,” she said. “I don’t mind being a princess in the introduction. I don’t want someone to look at me and think they know what I’m supposed to do.”

“They won’t,” Triton said. “Not if I’m there.”

“And if you’re not?” Clarisse asked.

“Then you tell them what you’re supposed to do.”

“Whatever you decided.”

Silena smiled at the page. Beside her, Percy watched Clarisse’s expression. She hadn’t relaxed exactly, but the hard line of her jaw had eased. Triton was giving them room to refuse, not just a rule to obey. That mattered to her. It mattered to Percy too.

“What about Annabeth?” Clarisse asked.

“Lady Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus, honored guest of Atlantis,” Triton said. “A description, not a job.”

Triton handed her the next card. It read *Prince Perseus of Atlantis.* Percy glanced at it, then at his brother. “You made a card for me?”

“You are the one who keeps pretending it doesn’t apply.”

“It doesn’t apply here.”

“At camp, no. In the palace, the herald may call you Prince Perseus.”

“I’d rather they called me Percy.”

“Some people will use the full name in the formal hall. You can answer, ‘Thank you, I’m Percy.’ You don’t have to become a different person.”

Clarisse leaned toward Silena. “Your Highness, would you like a strawberry?”

Percy grabbed one from the basket and threw it at her. Clarisse caught it in her mouth.

“Perfect form,” Triton said.

“I can do a bow too,” Clarisse said around the berry. “I refuse to waste one.”

“A bow is not wasted if it makes someone feel welcome,” Silena said.

“I said it isn’t wasted if it makes someone feel welcome. I didn’t say I was going to do it.”

“That’s progress,” Percy said.

“You don’t get to grade me.”

“As Prince Percy—”

Clarisse raised a finger. “Finish that sentence and I’m feeding you to your own lake.”

“As your little brother,” Percy said quickly, “I’m proud of you.”

“That’s a much better title.”

Silena tucked the notebook under her arm and nudged Percy with her shoulder. “You’re going to be introduced as a prince. We’re going to call you that at least once.”

“Why?”

“Because we love you.”

“That doesn’t make sense.”

“It doesn’t have to.”

“Your sisters have discovered the title of prince makes you blush,” Thalia said. “This will be a difficult week for you.”

“It will not.”

“Prince Percy,” Annabeth said.

He looked at her, smiling. Her hand rested close to his on the bench. He squeezed it once, quick and warm, while the others argued about whether a title could be used as a weapon.

---

The palace tour happened on the grass, with four benches, serving trays, and a wooden fish Chiron found in a cupboard.

“Why is there a wooden fish?” Percy asked.

“A gift,” Chiron said. “It has been useful for decades.”

“As what?”

“A fish.”

Triton placed the wooden fish at the end of the benches, then set the trays on a low table. “We will practice entering a formal room, receiving an introduction, and taking a seat at dinner.”

Clarisse eyed the arrangement. “This is the etiquette lesson?”

“Yes.”

“Where’s the palace?”

“I have described it.”

“You said the corridors are pale stone and the archways have coral. That’s not the same as seeing it.”

“No,” Annabeth said. “It’s not.” She crouched and spread the map on a bench. “Could you draw the room?”

Triton took a piece of charcoal and sketched the receiving hall: the garden doors, the king and queen’s places, and the prince’s seat to the right. “At formal council, I sit at the far end.”

“Why?” Annabeth asked.

“The room is arranged for the current being discussed. In family, it is a home. In council, a map of duties.”

Annabeth sketched the room in the margin. “Can visitors sit by the garden?”

“Usually.”

“Can we leave during a formal meal if we need to?”

“Yes. No one is trapped at a table.”

Annabeth looked up. “I appreciate that.”

“There’s a second door behind the shell wall. The court uses it when the main hall is full.”

“A hidden door?” Annabeth leaned closer. “That is a terrible use of a door.”

“You may tell the architect.”

“I will.”

Percy tilted his head at the map. “Triton told me they added the shell wall after the eastern arch flooded. That must be the part you showed me in your sketch.”

Annabeth looked at him. “The hall flooded?”

“One of the outer channels backed up. Water came in knee-high and knocked over two chairs.”

“Was the biting chair involved?”

“It bit a footman and floated away.”

“I’m starting to like the chair.”

“They put it in a gallery after that.”

“Good. Let it reflect on its behavior.”

Triton lifted the wooden fish. “I am the herald. This is the fish.”

“Then why does it have a hat?” Clarisse asked.

“I thought it looked underdressed,” Silena said.

“Fish don’t wear hats.”

“Atlantean fish might.”

“Have you met one?”

“No. Fine, we don’t know.”

Triton plucked the fish from Thalia’s hand. “The herald will announce the guests. You will wait until the name is complete before you step forward. A nod, a greeting, and you may approach the hosts when they invite you.”

“What if they forget to invite us forward?” Clarisse asked.

“Then you can come forward, or wait until they finish speaking.”

“Clarisse,” Percy said gently, “we can ask them when we’re there.”

She glanced at him, then closed her mouth. “I’m asking now so we don’t have to ask there.”

“That makes sense,” he said.

Triton didn’t cut in. He let the answer stand.

They took turns approaching the benches. Triton announced each in a solemn voice that made everything sound like the opening of a trial.

“Clarisse, daughter of Poseidon, guest of the royal household.”

She stepped forward, palm to heart, gave a small nod, and stopped exactly out of arm’s reach.

“Well done,” Triton said. “You can come closer; the hall is not a sparring ring.”

“Habit,” Clarisse said, but she took one step.

Silena followed. “Silena, daughter of Poseidon, guest of the royal household.”

She moved forward, made the greeting, and smiled as if the invitation were personal. It was. Her warmth wasn’t a breach of etiquette. It was the welcome.

“Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus, honored guest of Atlantis,” Triton announced.

She made a formal nod. “I’m grateful for your invitation, Prince Triton.”

“Good.”

“Does ‘honored guest’ mean I’m required to behave honorably?”

“You are required to behave as yourself.”

“That could be interpreted broadly.”

“It usually is.”

“That’s reassuring.”

“Prince Percy of Atlantis,” Triton said.

“No.”

“You have to practice.”

“I know my name.”

“Then use it.”

Percy walked the three steps toward the makeshift receiving hall. He gave the greeting and looked at Triton. “Hi.”

“Your Highness,” Clarisse said from the line.

“Don’t start.”

“It’s the title. I’m being respectful.”

“You are enjoying yourself.”

“That doesn’t make it disrespectful.”

“It might.”

“A good title can carry two things.”

“That’s not a real rule.”

“It is now,” Silena said, writing it in her notebook.

Triton turned to the makeshift table. “Wait for the host to sit before you do. Take the seat offered, and ask if you need another. There is no hidden test in the furniture.”

“That’s good to know,” Percy said. “Triton told me I once sat in the admiral’s chair at council and got a speech about the order of the currents.”

“You put your feet on the table and fell asleep during it,” Triton said. “Grandmother made you take the lesson again the next day.”

“With his feet on the floor?” Silena asked.

“That was the rule.” Triton had described the silver-haired woman correcting Percy’s posture, then slipping him a sweet. Percy wished he remembered her himself. “Will she be at the palace? I’d like to meet her with you.”

“At dinner, use the utensils provided. Ask if you don’t know what they’re for. Wait until food is offered, and say if you can’t eat something. Nobody counts bites,” Triton continued. “And don’t stab the furniture.”

Annabeth glanced at the imaginary biting chair. “A tragedy.”

Silena wrote down *nobody counts bites*. Clarisse eyed the four spoons on the tray. “Why can’t I use one?”

“You can,” Triton said.

“Then we’re finished.” Clarisse picked the largest spoon. “I’ll use this.”

“That one is for soup.”

“Then I’ll have soup.”

Silena chose the shell scoop. “I’ll try the first course.”

“I’ll try it too,” Percy said. “I’m not leaving her alone with the mystery dish.”

“It’s chilled kelp and citrus,” Triton said.

“You called another dish a mistake. I have concerns.”

Annabeth leaned toward Percy. “And to get a servant’s attention?”

“Use their name if you know it. Otherwise, ‘Excuse me’ is enough.”

“Good. I’ve got it.”

“Then announce us,” Clarisse said. “Let’s see if we’re ready.”

Triton stood up straight and took the wooden fish. “The royal household is gathered for supper. The guests are to be welcomed.”

“You’ve made the fish a herald,” Annabeth whispered.

“It has a hat,” Silena whispered back. “It’s official.”

Triton cleared his throat. “Princess Clarisse, daughter of Poseidon, guest of the royal household.”

Clarisse stood. She looked as though her legs had decided they wanted to fight the bench. Her gaze passed over the empty space where the imagined court would be, then returned to Triton.

She did the greeting. “Thank you for the welcome. I’m glad to be here.”

The words were plain. Her voice was not. It had gone a little tight around the edges.

“Good,” Triton said, and did not correct a thing.

Silena stood next. “Princess Silena, daughter of Poseidon, guest of the royal household.”

Her greeting was warmer, and she added the Atlantean words she had just learned. The phrase came out slightly uneven. She caught the stumble, smiled at it, and tried once more. On the second attempt it flowed.

“That was lovely,” Percy said.

“It was close,” Triton said. “Lovely is not the measure.”

“I can have two measures.”

“You can.”

“Then it was close and lovely.”

“Agreed.”

Annabeth rose. “Lady Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus, honored guest of Atlantis.”

She held the pause for exactly the right amount of time. “Your Highness, I accept your welcome.”

“Good,” Triton said. “You have learned.”

“I have memorized.”

“That is a start.”

“I have not forgotten the succession dispute.”

“No one has.”

“Excellent.”

“Prince Percy,” Triton announced.

Percy rose with a dramatic sigh. “I'm Percy. Thanks for inviting me.”

“You have failed to use the title.”

“I acknowledged the title by ignoring it.”

“That is not how acknowledging works.”

“It worked for me.”

Clarisse pointed at him. “I like that one.”

“Of course you do,” Triton said.

Percy looked down at the table. He could picture the benches and the open space where servants might pass, because Triton had sketched them and talked him through the hall. The blue glass bowl in the center came from one of Amphitrite's descriptions, not a memory of seeing it himself. The rush of water through palace channels was easy to imagine, but he could not claim he remembered the old room. He still wanted to see it with his own eyes.

“You’re worried,” Silena said quietly.

“No.”

“You are.”

“I’m thinking.”

“Those aren’t opposites.”

“They are when I’m doing it.”

Clarisse set her hand on his shoulder, heavy and familiar. “You don’t have to show us every room or explain every rule before we get there. If something is weird, we’ll ask.”

“I know.”

“And if we get lost, we’ll find you.”

“That’s the part I’m worried about.”

“Why?”

“Because you’ll take a wrong corridor on purpose to see what’s down it.”

“That sounds like a good plan.”

“It sounds like you.”

“Same thing.”

Silena’s elbow nudged his. “We’ll stay together. We can be confused together.”

“That’s a strong family plan,” Annabeth said.

“And if the chair bites one of us?” Clarisse asked.

“We’ll put it in the lake,” Percy said.

“Not the lake,” Triton said. “It’s an antique.”

“The lake can appreciate antiques.”

“The lake has excellent taste.”

“Then the lake can decide.”

Silena held up her notebook. “I’ve got the plan. We greet the hosts, ask for names if we’re unsure, don’t eat the mistake fish, don’t touch the coral, don’t let the chair bite us, and remember we can ask to leave a room.”

“And use the titles correctly,” Triton said.

“Mostly correctly,” Annabeth said.

“Correctly,” Triton insisted.

“I reserve the right to one mistake per day.”

“One?” Percy asked. “That seems ambitious.”

“One formal mistake. You get the rest.”

“I accept.”

“You aren’t the host,” Triton said.

“I accept on the host’s behalf.”

“You cannot do that.”

“I have decided to.”

“That is not etiquette.”

“It is architecture. I’m creating a framework for future mistakes.”

Thalia raised her mug. “I support the framework.”

---

Triton had called a break after the mock dining service, but the break lasted only until Clarisse noticed he was putting the wooden fish back into its box.

"Where does the real fish go?" she asked.

"Which fish?"

"The ridiculous one."

"It does not go anywhere. That was a model of a centerpiece."

"Then I have a question about the centerpiece."

Triton kept the box open. "Ask."

"If something at the palace makes a guest uncomfortable, who is the guest supposed to tell? You spent half the morning showing us how to greet people without causing an incident. You haven't spent five minutes explaining what we do if someone causes one."

The table had gone quiet. Not because Clarisse was being rude, Percy thought, but because the question was a good one. Triton's instruction had assumed the people receiving them would be more knowledgeable and more powerful than his sisters. It had not explained how to disagree with any of those people.

"You speak to me," Triton said.

"And if you're across the palace?"

"To the chamber steward. Or the nearest member of the guard."

"What if the problem is the guard?"

Triton took the fish back out of the box and placed it on the bench. "Then we need to talk about more than the centerpiece."

Chiron, who had been folding the practice place cards, settled into a chair. Annabeth opened her notebook. Thalia moved her feet off the bench in front of her, suddenly paying full attention.

"At camp," Clarisse said, "I can refuse a training partner. I can tell Chiron a drill is unsafe. I can go to my own cabin. What do I get to do underwater?"

"You get to do all of those things except walk straight back to camp without arranging a safe passage," Triton said. "The palace is large. It is not a trap. But leaving it requires us to provide a route."

"Then I want that route explained before we're there."

Triton nodded. His answer came slowly enough that Percy knew he was reconsidering more than the phrasing.

"There are three places you can summon help," he said. "The main family hall, the entrance to the west garden, and the guest chambers. Each has a shell bell. Ring it twice and tell the attendant you want to return to the surface. They notify me or Mother. We arrange an escort to the shore."

"Can any of us ring it?" Silena asked.

"Yes."

"Without explaining why?"

"Yes."

"And if someone insists we stay for dinner?"

"Tell them I authorized your departure."

Clarisse looked unsatisfied. "You haven't actually authorized it."

"I am doing so now."

Thalia leaned forward. "Write that down."

Triton blinked. "What?"

"Not for drama. For the attendants. If the whole palace runs on etiquette, a guest will get farther with something that looks official than with an argument in a hallway."

Annabeth drew a narrow rectangle on her page. "A guest card. Not permission to leave, because permission shouldn't be needed. An explanation that the people staffing the route have already been told to help."

Triton looked at the sketch. He seemed personally offended by the fact that a fourteen-year-old had just identified a gap in his plans. Then he said, "That would be useful. I'll have the steward prepare copies."

"You don't have to be upset about it," Percy offered.

"I'm not upset."

"That tone means you're either upset or considering an invasion."

Triton shut the box. "I am considering how long it took you to learn basic court greetings, and whether we have time for a lesson in silence."

"You'd be surprised how bad I am at that lesson."

"We are all familiar with your limitations, brother."

Silena raised one finger. "There is another thing."

Triton sat down again with great patience. "Of course there is."

"You talk about Mother a lot. I want to know what we're supposed to call her."

"Amphitrite."

"We know her name."

"You may call her Lady Amphitrite if you prefer something formal."

"Not *Mother*?"

The word fell awkwardly into the space between them. Percy felt his chest tighten. He'd called Amphitrite by that name in the weeks since they'd begun speaking. It was not simple for him, but it was becoming natural in its own way. Clarisse had a living mother with a different history. Silena had her own. Triton had spent a childhood with Amphitrite that none of them could reproduce. The same word couldn't be handed to everyone like a place card.

"She would never demand it," Triton said.

"Would she be hurt if I never did?"

"No." He glanced at Percy, as though asking whether Percy could confirm that. Percy stayed silent. This wasn't his assurance to give. Triton straightened. "She will want to know you as you are. You should decide how to address her."

Silena folded her hands around her cup. "Good. I think I'd like to call her Amphitrite."

"Then do that."

Clarisse let out a breath Percy hadn't realized she was holding. "I'll call her that too."

"You can change your mind," Triton said.

"So can she," Clarisse replied. "She's welcome to ask me what she wants. We can have an ordinary conversation."

"Nothing about this family is ordinary," Thalia murmured.

"That," Annabeth said, "is why I'm taking notes."

Later, when the other campers dispersed toward their afternoon duties, Percy followed Triton down to the canoe dock. The water looked flat and unremarkable again. Its sudden lift that morning had sent boats skidding toward the mud, and two Apollo campers were still arguing over whose canoe had been scratched.

"Was the entrance necessary?" Percy asked.

Triton looked out at the lake. "No."

"Then why did you do it?"

"I thought it would make you laugh."

"It did. For about ten seconds."

"After which?"

"After which two boats got stuck in the reeds."

Triton considered the distant canoe rack. "I should speak to Chiron."

"That would help."

Percy sat at the edge of the dock. His brother stayed standing, hands behind his back, looking so much like a prince delivering a formal statement that Percy wanted to push him into the water.

"I want them to like our home," Triton said.

"They might."

"You sound uncertain."

"They might like some parts and hate others. Clarisse will find a training rule she thinks is stupid. Silena will tell a servant not to take her bag. Annabeth will probably decide your palace needs a new drainage system. Thalia will insult a guard if he talks to Annabeth like she's five."

"And you?"

"I'll spend half the visit worrying that they'll think I'm trying to replace camp with Atlantis."

Triton sat beside him. The wood shifted beneath his weight.

"You're not asking them to replace camp," he said.

"I know. But I don't want them to feel like I invited them as part of my life and then left them standing at the door while I go inside."

"They are invited into the rooms we can actually share. Not every room has to belong to all of us."

Percy rolled that over. He had been imagining a perfect visit: no mistakes, no jealousies, no ancient grudges, every relationship resolving into something so clean it could be painted above the dining table. That was not how their ordinary evenings in Cabin Three worked, and it probably wouldn't be how a weekend underwater worked either.

"What happens if Poseidon doesn't make it to dinner?" he asked.

"He has duties."

"That's what people always say when they don't know."

Triton didn't contradict him. "Father makes promises that do not always suit the length of his attention."

"I don't want Silena waiting all evening for him."

"Then I won't tell her to."

"And if he says he'll show up and doesn't?"

"We eat dinner. The food should not go cold for someone who isn't there."

Percy nodded. A simple rule. Useful enough that he intended to remember it.

On their way back up the slope, Triton stopped by the two canoes that had slid into the reeds. One had a new scratch along its blue side; the other had lost the leather binding on an oarlock. He asked the Apollo campers who handled repairs. They directed him to the boathouse.

Percy expected a grand gesture. Instead his brother took a repair sheet from the wall, wrote down the damage and asked where the replacement leather was kept. When one camper explained that the maintenance shifts were already assigned, Triton looked faintly surprised.

"You could pay for it," Percy said, "but the oarlock still has to be fixed by someone who knows how."

"I know how to repair a fitting."

"Then ask whether they want help."

Triton did. A boy from Hephaestus cabin gave him a blunt needle, a strip of leather and a set of instructions. The prince of Atlantis sat on an upturned crate and worked on a canoe oarlock while Percy held the loose timber steady. He did not summon a servant or make the lake do the work. Twice he threaded the leather through the wrong hole. On the third attempt the fitting held.

The Hephaestus boy tested the movement. "It'll do. Check it again after the next practice."

Triton accepted the instruction as though it were a judgment from a royal council. He looked pleased when the boy walked away.

"Did we just repair a canoe?" Percy asked.

"We did."

"I might need a family photograph."

"Tell no one."

"Clarisse will ask why you're covered in grease."

"Then I shall tell her I have been learning how your court functions."

"That's pretty much true."

Triton cleaned his hands at the pump. He still had a piece of blue thread caught on one cuff.

"Percy," he said, "I don't know whether this visit will be easy for you."

"Neither do I."

"But your sisters will never be treated as tests of your belonging. If someone mistakes the situation, tell me."

Percy looked at him. "I will. And if *you* mistake the situation, I'll tell you too."

Triton raised an eyebrow. "I'm sure you will."

They walked back to the Big House while the dinner workers lit the first lamps, and for once Percy wasn't rehearsing what he would say at the palace. He was thinking about a guest card, an ordinary exit, a meal that wouldn't wait for an absent father, and a prince with grease on his sleeve. Those seemed like better preparations than getting a spoon right on the first try.

Thalia found Annabeth behind the Big House at the end of the lesson, standing over the guest-card sketch with her pencil arrested halfway through a line.

"Does it have to be perfect?" Thalia asked.

"Have you met me?"

"Several times. You were very annoying the first time."

"I was seven."

"You were annoyingly precise at seven."

Annabeth made a last mark and sat back on her heels. She had drawn the guest card as a small folded sheet with room for the party's names, the return route, and the steward's seal. It was good, but she could already see what wasn't. They still didn't know how a visitor underwater breathed in places that weren't part of the palace's protected architecture. They didn't know whether a human attendant could escort people all the way to the surface. And there was the little matter of a god who had promised to meet them and could change his mind without warning.

"I should ask Chiron about the guards," she said.

"You should ask Triton."

"He's biased."

"Everyone's biased. You ask him, compare his answer with Chiron's, and decide how much you trust the plan."

Annabeth pushed a loose strand of hair away from her face. "You sound like you trust him."

"I don't know him well enough to trust him with your safety. That's different from deciding he's lying."

"That's a very careful distinction."

"I can be careful."

Annabeth raised an eyebrow. Thalia flicked a pine needle at her.

"Sometimes," Thalia amended.

Annabeth tucked the card into her notebook. "I want to go."

"I know."

"Not just because Percy wants me there."

"I know that too."

"I want to see how they make a building stand underwater without crushing the people inside it. I want to see the channels and the old stone. I want to understand what Triton means when he says the doors obey the tide."

"Then say that when somebody asks why you're coming."

Annabeth gave her a skeptical look. "I sound like I only care about the architecture."

"You don't. You've spent all morning asking how people leave when they don't want to stay. That's not architecture. That's people."

She looked at the guest card again. Thalia waited, hands tucked into her pockets, refusing to decide what her sister would say next.

"Are you coming because you want to?" Annabeth asked.

Thalia looked toward the canoe dock where Triton had gone to fix the oarlock. "Partly. I'd like to see Percy with his family when there's no camp around to tell everybody what their roles are."

"And partly?"

"Because I don't want you to have to handle our father alone."

Annabeth closed the notebook. The word *our* still mattered to her. Zeus had appeared in so many forms over the years that she could recognize his voice more readily than his face, and even that voice could change between affection and a command in a breath.

"He said he wouldn't come," Annabeth said.

"He says lots of things."

"Are you going to tell me not to go?"

"No. I am going to ask what we do if he turns up anyway."

Annabeth thought. "We don't argue with him in front of a room full of strangers."

"Reasonable. But if he addresses you like you're five?"

"You let me answer first."

"And if he won't let you?"

"Then we leave."

Thalia nodded. "That works."

"We have to actually do it, not just say it."

"Annabeth, I have dragged you away from dinner before. I am capable of dragging you away from a palace."

"That's not what I meant. I want to choose when we leave."

Thalia held her gaze for a moment. Annabeth could see the objection forming: what if she made a bad choice, what if waiting made the situation worse, what if there was a threat she'd fail to recognize? Thalia had raised her from childhood; the instinct to act first had kept her safe more often than either of them could count. It was also making it harder for Annabeth to grow.

"Fine," Thalia said. "You give me a signal if you want help. Not one I invent for you. You decide it."

Annabeth considered the possibilities. She could have chosen something elaborate and secret. She picked the simplest thing.

"I'll say your name."

"You say my name all day."

"I'll say, 'Thalia, I want to leave.' No guessing."

"Then I'll help you leave."

"And if I don't say it?"

"I'll ask whether you want to."

The answer was not a magical guarantee, but it was better than an agreement Thalia wouldn't remember when she was frightened. Annabeth wrote the words on the back of her guest card and folded it.

"You're keeping that?" Thalia asked.

"Yes. I keep most of our agreements in writing."

"Terrible habit."

"Excellent habit."

Thalia sat beside her in the shade and took the pencil when Annabeth offered it. She wrote her own name beside the line, more heavily than necessary. Annabeth made a face at the indentation left on the paper.

"Your handwriting is a structural hazard."

"Then ask Triton to repair it."

They were still laughing when Silena approached with the basket of practice napkins and asked whether they wanted to help fold them for storage. Annabeth stood, reached for one end of the basket, and stopped herself before offering to carry the whole thing.

"Do you want help?" she asked.

Silena smiled. "Yes. I asked."

"Right."

Thalia carried the empty card box. They walked toward the pavilion together, the guest card now tucked between the sketches of Atlantis and the measurements of a bridge Annabeth might someday build.

Clarisse had refused to wear a ceremonial cloak. This had been understood by everyone except Triton, who appeared on the training field carrying a length of blue cloth that looked expensive enough to buy several cabins.

"No," she said, before he reached her.

"I haven't said what it is."

"Your face said it."

"Your face says several things. Most of them unprintable."

Silena came out of the weapons shed carrying a practice spear. She saw the cloth and began to laugh.

"It's only for formal arrival," Triton said. "You would wear it during the procession into the receiving hall."

"We're arriving through the water."

"Yes."

"Then it gets wet."

"The fabric is prepared for that."

"And then I drag it around a palace while trying not to trip over it?"

Triton began to explain how the tail of the cloak was weighted to follow its wearer in a current. Clarisse gave him the expression she used when a training partner demonstrated a trick too elaborate to survive a real fight.

"Show me," she said.

He draped the cloth over his shoulders. The blue dropped around him in a perfect line. He walked, turned, stepped backward and knelt. To his credit, the cloak moved with him rather than tangling around his ankles. Silena examined the fastenings, interested despite herself.

"Those clasps are clever," she said. "They release if the cloth gets caught."

"Exactly," Triton replied.

Clarisse put her spear against the outside of his knee.

"Now defend yourself."

"I beg your pardon?"

"If a guest has to wear this, I want to know what happens when a guard mistakes them for a threat."

Triton looked at the spear. Then he looked at Silena, who raised both hands in surrender to the logic of the situation.

"I can take the cloak off first," he said.

"That avoids the question."

"Not every garment needs to function as armor."

"And not every guest needs to be dressed as decoration."

Triton loosened the clasp and let the blue fabric slide into his hands. He folded it across the bench. "You may wear your own clothes," he said.

"Good."

"If you wish, we can arrange a smaller insignia indicating the guest wing."

"Maybe. If it's useful."

Silena came closer. "Do they have to mark us at all?"

"No. It would help staff recognize you."

"Then explain that instead of bringing us a cloak."

Triton glanced at his brother, who had arrived carrying a packet of oarlock leather. Percy lifted his eyebrows, plainly refusing to save him.

"The insignia," Triton said, "is an option. Not a requirement. I apologize for assuming."

Clarisse rested the butt of her spear on the sand. "Accepted."

"Can I ask you something in return?"

"Ask."

"Why do you use a practice spear whose shaft is shorter than your reach?"

Clarisse looked down. The shaft was old and had been repaired near one end. The grip was polished smooth where her palms found it.

"Because it teaches me to close distance," she said. "A longer spear would make the same drill easier."

"At the cost of leaving your front side unprotected when you're too close."

"Only if I let you get too close."

"May I try?"

"Finally," Clarisse said. "A lesson with a point."

They took positions in the circle. Percy stepped back to the fence. Silena leaned the cloak on the bench and offered to keep time. Triton chose a blunted staff from the rack and measured the distance between them with a glance.

On the first pass Clarisse drove forward quickly and caught his staff under hers. Triton stepped sideways instead of retreating. She had to abandon her attack to keep him from moving behind her. He didn't strike. He stopped at the opening and showed her where she'd left it.

"That is what I mean," he said.

"Fine. Again."

The second pass went differently. She shortened her grip, drove the point toward his shoulder and used the butt to guard her other side. Triton was fast enough to keep pace. She felt the weight shift before he moved, caught him with a controlled tap against the ribs, and smiled without trying to hide her satisfaction.

"Point," Silena called.

"To Clarisse," Triton agreed.

"Again," Clarisse said.

Percy expected them to make a competition of it. They did for a few minutes, but not in the way he'd feared. Each exchange ended with a concrete explanation of what had worked and what hadn't. Triton corrected one of Clarisse's angles; she challenged his habit of leaving his legs too narrow when the surface wasn't perfectly level. Silena kept the score on a scrap of paper and ignored Percy's efforts to claim honorary points for cheering.

When they finished, Clarisse had won three of the seven passes. Triton set his staff aside and rubbed his forearm where she'd struck him.

"Your technique is good," he said.

"I know."

"You don't need me to tell you."

"No. But I can still hear it."

Silena handed them water. Triton looked from one sister to the other.

"I was thinking of the palace as something I had to explain before you could enter," he admitted. "I've been forgetting that you already know how to enter unfamiliar places."

Clarisse drank before answering. "You do know things we don't. We came to learn them. I just don't want you to turn every lesson into a test of whether we're good enough to be there."

"Understood."

"And I'm still not wearing the cloak."

"I had gathered that."

Silena picked up the blue cloth and examined one of the fastenings. "Can I borrow it to see how the release works?"

"Of course."

She turned the clasp over in her hand. "I have an idea for a bag strap that should do the same thing if a satyr gets it caught on a fence."

"That is not its intended use," Triton said.

"It would be a better one," Clarisse replied.

Percy followed them toward the pavilion carrying the folded cloak. They passed the repaired canoe, where the Hephaestus boy was already testing the oarlock and finding it held. Triton stopped to ask whether it needed anything further, accepted that it didn't, and joined the others without making another ceremony out of helping.

When the last rehearsal ended, Percy found that Triton had been summoned to the boathouse.

The summons was not a royal affair. An Apollo camper had simply brought over a broken paddle and demanded to know who was responsible for the lake rising under the canoes that morning. The blade had cracked where it knocked against the rack.

"I caused the water to rise," Triton said.

"Then you need to sign the incident sheet."

Triton looked at the clipboard she was holding. "You want my signature?"

"And your name. It's the box here."

She handed him the pencil. He read the form carefully and began writing. Percy had never seen his brother look quite so uncertain about a piece of paper.

"Does this happen often?" Triton asked.

"The paperwork? Every time something breaks."

"I meant a visitor disturbing the lake."

"Not usually before lunch."

Percy laughed. The camper, whose name was Iris, did not. She was responsible for recording the damage before the Hephaestus cabin could begin work, and she was plainly tired of people treating that responsibility like a joke.

"Is anyone hurt?" Percy asked.

"No. We checked the dock first. But one boat lost an oarlock strap and this paddle isn't safe to use. They found another cracked blade at the rack. Chiron said to list everything."

Triton completed the sheet. "Can I replace the paddle?"

"We'll replace it. You can cover the material. Don't rush the forge cabin; they've got jobs already."

"Understood."

"That means waiting," Iris clarified.

"I know what it means."

She looked skeptical. "Good."

They carried the paddle to the shed and watched a Hephaestus camper test the crack. The wood separated along the grain when he flexed it. He marked it as unusable and wrote the measurements for a new blank.

Percy expected Triton to order the sea to return a perfect paddle immediately. Instead his brother asked how the repair queue worked and whether someone could show him the fastening they'd lost.

The boy from Hephaestus cabin found the damaged oarlock and a replacement piece of leather. He handed Triton a blunt needle.

"Thread this through the holes," he said. "The first loop goes behind, not in front. If you do it wrong the strap twists every time someone rows."

Triton knelt beside the canoe. His first attempt was wrong. The boy pointed to the mistake. Triton undid the stitch and tried again. By the third attempt the leather lay flat.

"That'll hold," the boy said. "Check it after the next practice."

Triton nodded. "I will."

Percy held the canoe steady while the fitting was tested. They had finished nothing impressive. There was still a cracked paddle on the repair shelf and another boat waiting for inspection. But a loose piece had been secured, and someone who knew how to fix it had approved the work.

"I had attendants for things like this," Triton admitted when they left the shed.

"We have people too," Percy said. "They're just allowed to tell you you're doing it wrong."

"That seems to be a point of pride."

"It is."

Triton rubbed a smear of oil from his wrist. "I thought the lake rising would make you laugh."

"It did. For ten seconds. Then it moved the boats."

"I didn't consider that."

"You were thinking about a dramatic entrance."

"I was thinking about seeing my brother."

That stopped Percy for a moment. Triton said it without accusation. Percy knew his brother had traveled from a place where people were always looking at him as a prince. At camp, people looked at him because he'd moved their equipment without asking. Neither kind of attention was quite what he'd wanted.

"You can just walk up the path next time," Percy said.

"And disappoint everyone?"

"Clarisse will pretend she is disappointed. Silena will make you tea. Annabeth will demand a briefing."

"Would you come to meet me?"

"Yes."

Triton nodded. "Then I won't need the lake to stand up."

They continued toward the pavilion. A handful of younger campers were carrying practice shields back from the arena. One asked Triton whether he could make the water rise again. His brother looked at the stack of equipment and said no, not without warning everyone responsible for the shore.

"Aw," the boy said.

Percy considered explaining why that was a sensible answer, then decided the answer could stand on its own.

At the Big House, Annabeth had finished the guest-card design. It included each visitor's name and the three palace locations where someone could request an escort to the surface. Clarisse had crossed out a line requiring guests to explain why they were leaving. Silena had drawn a clasp that could detach without tearing the paper. Thalia had written *ASK FIRST* in letters large enough to offend a court scribe.

"Do the attendants actually know about these arrangements?" Annabeth asked.

"They will," Triton said.

"Before we arrive?"

"Yes."

"Will you check, rather than assume?"

He examined her. "You are very thorough."

"You invited us somewhere we can't get out of without help. I have a reason to be thorough."

"Then I will send you the steward's confirmation."

Thalia glanced over her shoulder. "Good. Now about our father."

The conversation chilled. Annabeth lowered her pencil. Zeus had promised not to intrude on their trip. His promises were not always dependable, and they all knew it.

"Has he said anything to you?" Thalia asked Triton.

"No. I would have told you."

"If he arrives anyway, we may decide to leave."

"Then we'll arrange the journey back."

"Even if it embarrasses your father?"

Triton met her eyes. "Yes. I do not speak for Zeus, but I can speak for the hospitality of my home. No guest will be required to remain where they feel unsafe."

Thalia relaxed a little. Percy hadn't realized until that moment how much she needed the answer. Annabeth was still writing down the details, but her shoulders had lowered too.

Silena took the card, folded it once and handed it to Percy. "You'll be holding this with us, right? Not making a separate plan because it's easier for you underwater."

He took it. "Right."

"Then say it."

"I won't make a separate plan without asking."

"Good."

Clarisse gave him a satisfied nod. "You are becoming slightly less of an idiot."

"That should go on the guest card."

"No room," Annabeth said. "We used the back for actual useful information."

Triton turned to Chiron, who had arrived carrying the signed incident sheet. The centaur glanced at the new repair entry, approved it and thanked the prince for taking responsibility.

"You're welcome here," Chiron said, "whether or not you intend to rearrange our lake."

"I'll give advance notice next time."

"That would be appreciated."

The afternoon had been a series of less-than-royal discoveries for Triton. He had learned that a guest could refuse a cloak, that a sister's request for privacy was not an insult, that a spectacular entrance could generate paperwork, and that even an ancient prince might need someone younger to tell him which way to thread a leather strap. Percy suspected his brother would be thinking about those lessons all the way home.

There was still much they didn't know about the palace visit. They hadn't chosen every meal or established who would be present when Poseidon entered the hall. The uncertainty remained. But the guests now had an exit procedure, Triton had accepted that the staff must be informed, and the damaged camp gear was recorded for repair.

Percy walked with his family toward dinner. He didn't feel prepared to do everything correctly in Atlantis. He did feel better prepared to tell the truth when something went wrong.

By the time the sun leaned toward the pine trees, Triton had reviewed their greetings three times and the table rules until Percy could identify the spoon for pearlfruit.

“What is pearlfruit?” Clarisse asked.

“A fruit,” Percy said. “It grows in shell-gardens.”

“I’ll believe it when I see it.” Silena wrote *pearlfruit* in her notebook and added a question mark. “And the garden plants that change color when someone walks past?”

“Those are real,” Percy said. “One kind copies the visitor’s colors.”

Annabeth frowned. “That is unreasonable.”

“It’s pretty.”

“That’s what unreasonable things say to get away with it.”

Silena closed her notebook and cradled it to her chest. “I want to see the garden. And the kitchens. And whatever room has the family stories. Percy, will you show us your favorite place?”

Percy pictured the training terrace, the garden behind his mother’s apartments, the window ledge where silver fish circled the lamps. He’d never thought of showing those places to Clarisse and Silena.

“Yeah,” he said. “I’ll show you.”

“And the room with the biting chair?” Clarisse asked.

“Watch me ask,” Triton said.

Silena slipped her arm through Clarisse’s. “We should bring the jam and the garden painting.”

“The jam is in Percy’s bag,” Clarisse said. “He can explain it formally. It contains strawberries.”

“He’s going to be unbearable all week,” Silena said.

“He’s already unbearable,” Clarisse said. “Now he’ll be unbearable with a title.”

Triton rolled up the hand-drawn map. With care, his hands flattened the paper before folding it, smoothing each crease with his thumb. Percy knew his brother could be impatient with people, but never with things he valued.

“You did well,” Triton said.

“We stood up and said hello,” Clarisse said. “That’s not difficult.”

“It can be. You don’t know what it will feel like in the hall.”

“Neither do you,” she said. “Not this time.”

He paused with the map half folded. Percy knew Clarisse wasn’t trying to jab at him. Being a child who belonged in the palace wasn’t the same as arriving with sisters who had never seen it.

“No,” Triton said. “I don’t.”

“Then we’ll learn it when we get there. You tell us the rules; we’ll tell you when they make things worse.”

“That seems fair.”

“It is.” Silena leaned on Clarisse’s shoulder. “And I’ll take notes.”

“Of course you will.”

“Someone has to remember which spoon goes with the pearlfruit.”

“Use whichever spoon makes you happy,” Clarisse said. “I’m not fighting a spoon.”

“There’s one more thing,” Triton said.

“A second biting chair?”

“No. I want you to know the titles and customs so you can decide what you want to use. Not so you can pass a test. The court can be loud. Some people may see Poseidon’s daughters before they see Clarisse or Silena. I can’t control what they notice first.”

“You can control how they behave,” Clarisse said.

“I can try.”

“Good.”

He looked at Silena. “I can make sure you know where to find us, where you can go, and who to ask for help. I can’t promise every part of the palace will feel familiar.”

“It doesn’t have to,” she said. “We’ll be there together.”

Clarisse bumped Percy’s shoulder. “You’re doing the thinking face.”

“That’s my face.”

“Your regular face is worse,” she said. Silena hooked an arm through his. “We’ll be fine, little brother. And you’ll be fine, Prince Percy.”

“I’m calling you both ‘Your Most Annoyingness.’”

“That’s not a title,” Triton said.

“It is now,” Silena replied. “The palace can learn.”

“One more practice?” Triton asked.

Clarisse groaned. “No.”

“Seven is lucky,” Silena said.

“Seven is a number.”

“It can be both.”

Percy picked up the wooden herald-fish and set its paper hat straight. “Fine. One more. Her Majesty of the Sea—”

“No,” Annabeth said.

“Prince Triton, who has not been bitten by a chair today—”

“Percy.”

“Princess Clarisse, armed but not concealing it—”

“I have a knife in my boot,” Clarisse said. “That’s concealing it.”

Triton covered his face. Silena laughed; Thalia caught her sliding notebook, and Annabeth rescued the fish before Percy could declare it monarch.

“We’ll be all right,” Silena said.

“Probably,” Percy said.

“Definitely,” Clarisse corrected. “We have a plan.”

“A list,” Silena said.

“Same thing,” Annabeth said.

“A list is shorter.”

The lake settled back into its ordinary shape. The ducks returned to the reeds, still muttering. Triton watched them, smiling faintly.

Triton looked at his sisters. The practiced sternness slipped, showing the older brother underneath.

“I want you to feel welcome there,” he said. “Not like guests who have to earn a place, or titles walking through a hall. You’re my sisters. I want the palace to feel like it has room for you.”
