## Chapter 153: What the Sea Owes

Percy Jackson woke to the sound of the pool breathing.

Not the loud way, the way it breathed during drills, when Clarisse had the baffles open and the current running and the water shoved against the tile like something with hands. This was the small sound. The pool at rest. Water moving faintly over mosaic, a trickle somewhere deep in the channels that fed it, and under that the low hush the whole cabin kept while its people slept.

He lay still and counted.

He had started counting at Atlantis, where you counted the days between supply ships. Here the counting meant something else: a way of keeping track of being noticed back. The days since Clarisse had let him carry her spear on patrol without comment: forty-one. The days since Silena had left a cup of something hot on his bunk without being asked: three. The days since he had stopped flinching when somebody said the word brother: he checked for that one every morning, like a tongue checking a loose tooth.

The morning was worth lying still for. Light came through the high window above the pool end of the cabin and laid a long bright plank across the floor. The air smelled of wet stone and lemon oil and the deep mineral damp that Cabin Three kept no matter how dry the summer got. Somewhere out on the green, a radio was playing in the Athena cabin. A bird was announcing itself with more confidence than its size warranted.

Percy sat up and dressed the way you dressed at camp when you meant to spend the day in water. The faded orange camp shirt off the hook, the one with the trident washed to almost-white across the shoulder and the sleeves already shortened by somebody's scissors, taken in June and no longer fitting anywhere. The shorts that had been his in April and were somebody else's by September. The sneakers he had worn so long that the canvas had gone soft as cloth, still faintly damp from yesterday, because drying shoes was a rumor at this camp. He put his pockets in order out of habit: a hair tie Silena had pressed on him, a smooth white pebble from the canoe lake, and the folded wax-paper wrapper of yesterday's cookie, which he kept because it still smelled faintly of honey.

The three bunks against the back wall were made up and empty, with towels folded on the hooks and the patient, waiting look of furniture that gets used twice a year. Tyson had slept in the corner one for three weeks in July, and made the bunk every morning, and left a round white rock on the pillow that looked exactly like an egg. Triton had come for a weekend in August, the way he came for a weekend in August: formally, briefly, with gifts, and gone by Monday, back to a sea with better libraries than this one. Neither of them lived here, and neither of them ever would; they had a sea, and Cabin Three had a lake, and Triton had said so himself, kindly, over tea he did not like. But the towels stayed on the hooks. Percy had put them there, and nobody had taken them down, and that was the whole argument in favor of hope that a fourteen-year-old boy needed.

Tempest Hall stood at the end of the hall, its door open the way it always stood open, because Clarisse said closed doors made the tile sweat.

The hall was half building and half sky. The high windows went up to the eaves, the tile ran cool underfoot, and the pool sat in the middle of the room like a held breath, deep and clear and faintly green, warm at the top and colder underneath, fed by channels that came up under the hill from a sea nobody at camp ever bothered to locate on a map. It had a smell: salt and rain and something older, the way a shell smells before you put it to your ear. Percy had spent his whole life in water that tasted like that, and still, every time, he stopped at the edge and breathed it in like a person standing in a front yard where the locks had been changed.

Clarisse was doing laps. She did them the way she did everything, which was as though the water owed her money: long stroke, unforgiving rhythm, her dark braid hanging wet down her back, her cutoff camp shirt clinging and then floating and then clinging again. Silena sat on the tile with her back against the wall and a spear across her knees and a tin of lemon oil open beside her, working the shaft with a rag in slow circles, her camp shirt tied at the waist over her swimsuit and her hair in a bun that had been haphazard since yesterday evening.

"Morning," Percy said.

"You're late," Clarisse said, surfacing.

"It's eight."

"It's eight-ten."

"Clarisse. It's eight."

"Then you're late by ten minutes. Retroactive." She pushed her wet hair back with the flat of her hand and did not smile, but the corner of her mouth was negotiating. "It's a system I invented. I'm proud of it. You'll hear about it."

Percy sat down on the tile next to Silena and let his feet hang toward the water. "You're oiling a spear at eight in the morning."

"Somebody's got to. The salt gets in the grain and then the shaft goes soft and then it snaps in your hand in the middle of a fight, and then Percy Jackson cries, and I don't want to listen to it."

"That's not why," Silena said mildly, working the rag along the shaft. "I like doing it. It's the quiet part."

"It's the quiet part," Clarisse agreed, which was as close to agreement as she got before breakfast, and she swam to the edge and folded her arms on the tile and looked up at her brother upside down, water running off her jaw. "Good. You're here. We're taking the day."

Percy looked at Silena. Silena looked at Percy. The kind of look that passed between people who had heard this tone before and were checking whether the other one had also caught it.

"Taking it where?" Percy asked.

"Taking it. The day. The whole day." Clarisse hoisted herself up out of the pool and wrapped a towel around her shoulders like a cloak, like armor, like the only thing she owned that she had not had to fight for. "No patrols. No arena. No Athena cabin map committee. No Zeus cabin." She pointed at him with the flat of her hand. "No girlfriend."

"Annabeth isn't a schedule, Clarisse."

"She's got the north path with that map kid. Olive. The one with the drainage."

"Olive's got a drainage problem and you know her name, which you'll deny."

"I deny it." Clarisse flicked water at him, which was her way of being affectionate and also her way of testing whether he would flinch. He did not flinch. "Thalia's at the arena. Thalia and the arena can have a beautiful life together. Today is Poseidon cabin. Three of us. No audience. No supervision. No ten-minute rule."

"You're giving me," Percy said slowly, "a whole day with you and Silena. On purpose."

"Don't make it weird."

"I'm not making it weird. I'm noticing it. It's a documented habit of mine, I notice things."

"Then notice that your mouth is still open."

Silena set the spear down very carefully on its rest and reached for a second tin, a rag, a small soft brush, with the economy of someone who had done this so many thousand times that her hands knew the order better than she did. "He's never done a Group Float," she said, to Clarisse, in the voice of a person bringing a matter before a committee that has already decided.

Percy's head came around. "You've done a Group Float?"

"Every summer for five years."

"You did it here. I've seen you do it here," Percy said, "and every time I've assumed it was a private ritual with, like, rules and oaths."

"Watching isn't floating," Clarisse said.

"It has rules and oaths."

"I assumed if I asked you'd tell me to jump in the lake."

Silena laughed. "You're in the lake half your life anyway."

"Not the point." Percy pulled his knees up and wrapped his arms around them, his damp sneakers squeaking on the tile. "Tell me the rules and oaths."

Silena looked at Clarisse. Clarisse looked at the water. Some negotiation happened that did not involve words, the kind that happens between people who have known each other longer than they have known themselves, and then Clarisse said, "She made it up. It's hers. She tells it."

"I was eleven," Silena said, "and it was the hot week in July, second summer, and the pool was the only cold thing at camp, and I lay back in the water with a life preserver under my head and stayed there until I stopped being hot and started being something else. And I decided that if the water was going to hold me for free like that, the least I could do was say something true while it did."

"And you told her it was stupid," Percy said.

"I told her it was stupid every day for a month." Clarisse examined her cuticles. "And then I did it. Every day for a month. After her."

"So what are the rules?" Percy asked. "And the oaths."

Silena held up one finger, then two, then three, with the seriousness she gave to the few things she took seriously. "One. Somebody floats, one at a time, and somebody holds their ankle so the current doesn't take them. Two. When your ears go under, you say one true thing. Just one. You don't get to plan it. If you plan it, it doesn't count. Three. When you come up, nobody asks. If you explain it, the float is over and you're on dishes for a week."

"And if you don't want to say anything?" Percy asked.

"Then you float, and you hold the ankle, and you listen," Silena said. "That's most of it, anyway. The floating's the point. The true thing's just the ticket price."

Percy looked at the pool. The light came through the high windows in long shafts full of dust, and the water held them and broke them and held them again, and at the bottom of the pool the mosaic hippocampus lay clear and bright and blue-green, a horse of the sea asleep in tile, the way it had been asleep in tile since before any of them had been claimed.

"How does it start?" he asked.

"You just get in," Clarisse said. "Stop asking questions and get in the water, Jackson. You've been in water since before you had bones."

So he got in.

The pool took him at the knee, then the thigh, then the waist, the way it always did, and he went under without ceremony and came up in the middle of the room with his hair flat and the salt on his lips and the whole world reduced to light and breath. The water was warm at the top. It went cold three feet down, the way sea water goes cold, and then it was fine, and then it was better than fine, and it was the oldest feeling he owned. He lay back. The water held him. It held him the way the sea had held him his whole life and the way the lake, lately, had started to, which was differently, and he had not yet found the word for the difference, and he suspected the word was family.

Clarisse waded in and lay back beside him and held his ankle. Silena came in last and took Clarisse's ankle, and the three of them made a line across the pool, three heads and six spread hands and the ceiling of the hall above them, and nobody said anything for a while, because there was nothing to say that the water was not already saying.

Then Silena went under.

She stayed down a long time, long enough that Percy felt Clarisse's grip tighten on his ankle, long enough that he started to count, and then she came up with her hair streaming and her face doing the thing it did when she had decided to keep something.

She did not explain it. That was the rule.

Percy went next. The water closed over his ears and the world went to the low green roar that it always went to, and he said it, one true thing, the ticket price, into the water that would not tell anybody.

He did not explain that one either.

Clarisse went last. She did not go deep. She only put her head under and came straight back up, quicker than either of them had, and shook her hair like a dog, and said, "Oath complete," in a voice that was a little too steady, and lay back, and floated, and looked at the ceiling, and did not explain it.

Percy knew better than to ask. He had been at camp long enough to learn which doors had signs on them, and his sister's face had just grown a sign.

They floated until they got cold, and then Clarisse taught Percy the thing she claimed to have invented, which was lying perfectly still with your hands folded on your chest like a sunken king until the water went glassy over you and you disappeared, and Silena, who had invented at least two things Clarisse claimed credit for, timed them both with the big tin lid, and Percy won, and Clarisse said the timing was rigged, and Silena said everything was rigged, and Percy realized he was laughing with his ears under.

They got out when the light on the tile had moved meaningfully across the floor. Clarisse dressed without drying off, which was a habit she had never explained and never lost: wet camp shirt, boots, socks, the whole of it dripping. Silena rolled her shorts and wrung out her hair with both hands and pinned it up with the pencil that had been living in her bun.

"North beach," Clarisse announced. "I'll get the satchel. Silena, you do the food."

"Do I do the food," Silena said.

"You always do the food."

"I always do the food because you'd bring beef jerky and a spear."

"Those are both food."

"They're one food and one weapon." Silena was already at the door, barefoot, her sandals swinging from two fingers. "Percy, towels. Two each, plus the blanket off the end of my bunk."

"Which blanket?"

"The quilt. It doesn't matter which quilt, there's only the one that doesn't smell like the boathouse, and it does, but it's a good smell."

Percy collected towels from the shelf, two each, plus a third for later, because you always needed the third towel, and found Clarisse waiting in the hall with a canvas satchel over one shoulder and her expression doing the thing it did when she wanted something and was not going to ask for it.

"You coming?" she said, to the satchel, to the hall, to him.

"I'm coming," Percy said.

"Good." She started down the hall at the speed of a girl who had decided the world would keep up. "Your shirt's on inside out."

"It is not."

"It's on inside out, and your hair's full of salt, and you smell like a fish market, and you're my brother, so I'm allowed to say all three." She glanced back, half a second, not enough to slow down. "Get a shirt that fits."

"Nobody's asked you for fashion advice."

"Nobody's asked you for advice of any kind," Clarisse said. "I give it free. That's the problem with me. I'm generous."

Silena Beauregard had a theory about families, and the theory was this: a family is not the people who made the decision to stay. A family is the people who kept making it, quietly, on days when nobody was watching and the decision cost something.

She had made the decision at eleven, in the hot week of her second July, and she had remade it most days since, because she was the kind of girl who did the arithmetic on her own life and then did it again in the morning to be sure.

The pavilion was empty at midmorning, which was the hour Silena liked best, when the breakfast plates had been cleared and the lunch prep had not begun and the long tables sat in the sunlight with crumbs on them like the sparkle of a party that had happened without her. She went behind the counter with the confidence of a girl who had been doing it for five years, found the good brown paper, the wax paper, the three bandanas Clarisse would pretend not to want, and filled a shallow crate.

Cold peaches, because it was September and September peaches were the best ones anyway, the ones that gave a little when you pressed them, the ones that tasted like the last of summer being spent on purpose. Bread, because Clarisse would call it filler and then eat her weight in it. Cheese cut into thick honest wedges by somebody who knew what they were doing. A jar of honey from the apiary on the hill, which Silena took at some personal risk and under the terms of a treaty she had negotiated personally with a bee. Two lemons from the greenhouse, for squeezing into the iced tea, because lemons were her whole personality and neither of her siblings had the good taste to say so out loud. And apples, because a picnic without apples was a meal, and a picnic with apples was a day.

She was debating the honey when a shadow fell across the counter.

"You," said the harpy, folding her arms. She was a big shadow and a sharp voice and a pair of wings she kept carefully furled indoors, and her name was Ms. O'Fee, and she had known Silena for five years and had never once called her by her right name.

"Me," Silena agreed.

"That's the good honey."

"It's for my brother and sister. It's a Poseidon cabin day."

"It's always a Poseidon cabin day with you three. You're going to eat us out of house and home." The harpy said it the way she said everything, which was loudly and with affection hidden somewhere in the middle of it where you had to go looking. "Sing for it."

"It's eight-forty in the morning."

"Sing. Or take the jar and I'll put it on your record."

So Silena sang, quietly, into the empty pavilion with the dust in the light shafts and the smell of coffee gone cold on the burner, a little sailing song with no words she trusted, and the harpy's wings shivered once, and the harpy turned her head away and pretended to find something on the shelf, and when Silena left, the jar was wrapped in a second brown paper with a pat of butter laid on top like a seal.

She met Percy on the path down to the boathouse, and she stopped to watch him for a second before he saw her, because watching was the other half of her job.

He walked the way he walked everywhere, which was as though the ground were a thing he was politely asking permission of, and he had towels around his neck and over one shoulder and a folded quilt bumping against his hip, and every few steps his eyes went to the left, to where the trees opened and the lake showed through, and every time it happened his face did a small thing, a loosening, a letdown of the guard, and then he put the guard back up and kept walking. He had been at camp one summer. He had not yet learned to want water without showing it.

Four years longer at camp than him, she thought, and the arithmetic was still there, waiting for her like it always was.

Cabin Three had a rhythm, and Silena knew it the way she knew her own blood: new children in the spring, claimed or reassigned or gone by summer's end, the towels on the hooks and the bunk made up and the mosaic hippocampus asleep at the bottom of the pool through all of it. Five years of comings and goings, and of learning not to put too much of yourself into a bunk that might be empty by June. Clarisse had learned that as hardness. Silena had learned it as maintenance, and it was the only thing about her sister she had ever been quietly, carefully afraid of.

Percy saw her then, and smiled, and it was the smile he gave the lake when he thought nobody was looking, and the arithmetic did not stand a chance.

"You're early," he said. "I thought you had to negotiate for the food."

"I bribed a harpy with a hymn. It was the most dignified day of my life." She fell into step beside him. "Where's Clarisse?"

"Went ahead with the satchel. She does that."

"She does that."

They walked a while. The path went down through the pines in a long easy switchback, and the lake kept appearing and disappearing to their left, bright and bare, a held coin. The morning was going the way September mornings went at camp: cool enough at the top of the hill that the grass was still wet, warm enough halfway down that you wanted the shade.

"Silena," Percy said. "Can I ask you something serious?"

"You can ask me anything. You might not get an answer."

"Is this a pity day?"

The question landed in the middle of the path and sat there. Silena stopped walking.

"Is it a pity day," Percy said, not looking at her, "because you're doing something nice for the kid who was raised by a god in a palace and can't make friends? Because I want to know. If it is, it's fine. I'd just rather know."

And Silena looked at her brother, fourteen years old, with towels around his neck and a quilt under his arm and his hair still salt-stiff from the pool, and thought, with the clarity she was prone to and not always grateful for: he was working so hard to be easy, and he thought being easy was the same as being enough.

"No," she said. "It's not a pity day."

"You sure?"

"I'm sure. Because I'm the one who said it first. Six days ago, at breakfast, Clarisse said, and I quote, 'Jackson's got a whole cabin of sisters and not one of them has asked him a single thing about his actual life.' Quote unquote. Then she kicked a rock and said, also a quote, 'the ten-minute rule does not apply to Cabin Three business,' and I said 'clarisse, it's not a rule, it's a threat,' and she said 'it's both.'" She started walking again, and he followed, and the pines went by on both sides of them.

Percy was quiet for a few steps. Then he said, "She said that?"

"She said it with her whole chest. Then she tried to take it back by announcing she was only doing it because you're useless at shore patrol and somebody had to teach you." Silena's mouth did the slow-traveling smile it did when she was about to be honest at somebody. "She's terrified. That's why she's leading. Clarisse always leads when she's terrified."

"She's not terrified."

"Percy. She's been terrified since the day you walked into this camp in shoes that didn't fit, and she has been terrified in exactly one direction: that you'll decide we're not worth staying for." Silena stopped at the bottom of the switchback, where the path met the boathouse clearing and the lake opened up gray-blue and enormous and still. "She doesn't know what to do with you. None of us do. You showed up here at fourteen with a whole ocean in your pockets and you were nicer than we deserved, and that's the problem."

"What do I do with that?" Percy asked, very quietly.

"You float," Silena said. "That's literally the entire tradition. You float, and you hold somebody's ankle, and you wait for them to say the true thing, and you don't ask them to explain it." She smiled at him, warm and a little sad and completely without repair in it. "That's all you ever have to do with any of us."

The boathouse was a long low shed of silvered wood tucked into the pines, smelling of varnish and rope and old canvas, with a bronze plaque over the lintel that said LAKESIDE, 1978, and a rack of overturned canoes leaning against the wall outside like a row of patient animals. Inside, the light came in through the open doors in two long slants, and the dust turned in them, and the place smelled of the lake it served, which was a fresh smell, green and cold and clean, nothing like the salt-deep smell of Tempest Hall's channels.

Clarisse was already there, standing on a bench with her arms full, and she had the good satchel. She had also, Silena saw with the particular exhaustion of a girl who had known her sister for five years, acquired a coil of rope, a lantern nobody would need until dark, a first-aid kit the size of a loaf of bread, and a folding camp chair.

"What," Clarisse said, "are you two looking at."

"Where did you get a chair?"

"It was in the loft."

"Why did you bring a chair?"

"Because the rock's flat and the rock's hard, and I have muscles, and I'm not sitting on a rock like a fisher bird." Clarisse dropped the whole armful onto the bench with a clatter that made the lantern swing. "I also brought the good satchel. Rope, knife, line, hooks, and the tin cup, because somebody—" she pointed at Percy, "—forgot that water makes you thirsty and crying makes you thirstier."

"I didn't forget," Percy said.

"You forgot in your heart, which is worse."

Silena set the crate down on the workbench, which was scarred with initials going back decades, and unwrapped the first of the bandana bundles. "Let's do this outside where there's light and I can see what I'm doing."

They carried it down to the lake in a ragged procession, and Silena brought up the rear, because the rear was where you saw things.

That was what she saw. Clarisse ahead with the satchel and the camp chair stacked on top of it, walking like a general who had taken a hill and was refusing to celebrate. Percy beside her with the quilt over one shoulder and the towels around his neck, matching her pace, not looking at her, both of them pretending with enormous effort that they were not both trying to walk in front of the other one.

And Silena, in the rear, with the bandana bundles warm against her shoulder and the smell of peaches and lemon oil and lake on her hands, made the decision again, the way she had at eleven, the way she did most days: to be the one who stayed.

The north beach was the last place at camp where the pines ran right down into the water: a half-moon of sand and warm flat stone that nobody had built anything on, with the glassy shallows and a shelf of sun-warmed rock that the lake had polished over ten thousand years into something that felt, sitting on it, like a bench made for exactly three people.

Clarisse set the chair down on the rock with the ceremony of a queen and immediately, Silena noticed, did not sit in it.

Percy stood at the edge of the rock with his shoes off and his toes in two inches of freshwater and his face doing the thing it always did when the lake found him.

"Say it," Clarisse said.

"Say what?"

"You've been making that face for a month. Say it."

Percy looked at the lake, gray-green and flat and bright with the late sun, the far shore a low green line, the boundary rock sitting out in the middle of it like a punctuation mark with the white X just visible.

"It's the biggest water I've ever been able to see the other side of," he said.

Nobody laughed at him. That was the thing Silena would have told you about her family, if you had asked her in one of the rare moments when she was willing to be proud out loud: nobody laughed at the true things.

Clarisse La Rue swam the way she ran and fought and argued, which was as though the water had personally insulted her mother.

She went in first, off the flat rock, without testing the temperature, because testing the temperature was for people who were undecided about being alive, and she had never once in fifteen years been undecided about anything except her family. The water closed over her head and her braid swung and she was gone, down and out, and when she came up she was twenty feet from the shore and already grinning the grin she would have denied under oath.

"You're slow," she announced, to both of them, to the lake, to the general concept of siblings who had grown up elsewhere.

"Nobody said go," Percy called, wading in behind her with his jeans rolled to the knee and his rent sneakers still on, because Percy did everything in stages: first wade, then swim, then dive, then never come up again if he could help it.

"Then you're slower. Both." Clarisse hung in the water and let her feet touch bottom, and the top of her head steamed a little in the sun, which was now high and doing the work it had come to do. "Race. To the point and back. Loser carries everything up the hill."

"The chair?"

"Especially the chair."

"I call the point," Silena said, and went down the rock ladder into the shallows with the easy competence of a girl who had spent five years learning exactly how much of this lake she could take in one afternoon.

The point was a jut of grey rock a hundred yards out, the finish line of every race ever contested by the children of Cabin Three, because it was far enough to be a race and close enough to be a dare. Clarisse took the outside line. Percy took the inside. Silena, who had no intention of racing anybody before noon, lay back with her hands folded on her chest and announced that she would be the finish line.

"Go," Silena said.

Clarisse exploded off the sand. Percy went under, because Percy cheated, and came up twenty yards ahead in a burst of white foam with his whole face lit up, and then did the thing Clarisse had privately decided was unfair about him: he swam like the water was a friend who was letting him win. Long strokes. No splash. No noise. The kind of swimming that looks like resting from fifty feet away.

She put her head down and made herself into a machine.

The lake came past her in its green cold way, and for a while there was nothing in her head at all, which was the only reason she had ever learned to race in the first place. At the point she slapped the rock with a flat hand, felt the barnacle roughness of it, and turned.

She won by four feet.

She came up the sand like a conqueror, breathing hard, her hair in her face, her cutoff shirt clinging, and she had to stand there for a second with her hands on her knees and her chest heaving before she could say the thing she had planned to say, which was going to be devastating.

"You cheated," Percy said, arriving behind her, also breathing, also dripping, his rent sneakers emerging from the water like a miracle of bad decisions.

"I took the outside line."

"You swam under the first third. I felt the current."

"The current was mine," Clarisse said. "I was using it."

"The lake's not yours."

"The lake," Clarisse said, "is Cabin Three's lake, and I'm Cabin Three, so."

"Remind me who's carrying the chair," Silena said, drifting past them on her back with her eyes closed.

"Nobody's carrying the chair."

"Then it's Clarisse."

"It's absolutely Clarisse."

Clarisse flipped her braid over her shoulder and did the math, which was that a win was a win, and that her sister was floating past with the smug serenity of a person who had never in her life been wrong about anything, and decided, on the whole, to be in a good mood.

They lay on the warm rock while the sun did its work. Silena had laid out the picnic with the efficiency of a person setting a stage: the brown paper, the bandanas, the peaches sitting in a nest of leaves so they would not bruise, the bread torn rather than cut, the cheese in thick wedges, the jar of honey with its pat of butter seal, the lemons, the iced tea in the tin, and the tin cup, which Clarisse had indeed brought, and which she now held in one hand like a woman who had been right about something and wanted it noted in the record.

Percy sat on the rock. Clarisse sat in the camp chair. Between those two facts there was a whole argument that neither of them was going to have out loud.

"Your shirt's inside out," Clarisse said.

"It dried that way."

"Your shirt was inside out when you put it on. That's a different thing. That's a decision you made."

Percy looked down at himself. He was wearing the faded orange camp shirt with the trident washed white across the shoulder, and it was inside out, and it had been inside out since the pool, and now it was drying in the sun with a rime of salt along the seams and sticking to his shoulders in the middle.

"Turn around," Clarisse said. "I'll fix it."

"You'll fix it."

"I'm not going to bite you." A pause, delivered at exactly the right length to be a threat and then a joke. "I bite Silena. She likes it."

"I'll allow it," Silena said, from the peaches.

Percy turned, and Clarisse reached up and gathered the shirt at his collar and pulled it straight and shook it out once, twice, until it hung right, and then flattened the seams at his shoulders with two flat pats, the way somebody's aunt might have done if somebody's aunt had been a soldier.

"There," she said. "Now you look like you belong to somebody."

"That's a creepy thing to say."

"It's a Cabin Three thing to say." She sat back down in her chair and drank from the tin cup and looked out at the lake. "So. Atlantis."

Percy's hands stopped moving on the lace of his sneaker.

"Yeah," he said. "I was waiting for somebody to."

"What was it like?"

This was the question, Clarisse thought. This was the one she had been carrying around for a month like a stone in her pocket, turning over and putting back, turning over and putting back, and now it was out in the sun and it was heavier than she had expected and lighter, and she had no idea which of those was going to win.

"It was," Percy said, and stopped, and started again. "You know how people say the ocean's loud? It isn't. It's the loudest quiet there is." He was looking at the lake and not seeing it. "There's a color. Under there. Not blue. Everybody thinks blue. It's this—" He made a shape in the air with both hands, cupping, like he was holding something round and full of light. "It's green gold violet all at once depending on the hour and how deep you are, and when the sun hits it right, the whole reef looks like a city made of stained glass that somebody dropped."

Nobody said anything. Silena had gone very still, which was how Silena listened.

"And it was built," Percy said. "A city. Not ruins. A city. The market's at the crossroads where four currents meet, and the vendors sell things that are still alive, and there's a street where the houses are made of shell and they sing when the tide turns, and you can hear it from blocks away, this big low hum, like the whole island is purring." He laughed a little. "I used to lie on the roof and listen to it. I thought every kid had that. I thought every kid's dad was a god and their roof hummed."

"Did he teach you to swim?" Clarisse asked.

Percy blinked. "What?"

"Your dad. Did he teach you to swim."

"He taught me everything." Percy said it simply, the way you say a fact about gravity. "He was there. Every day. He'd come up out of the water at the training courts and make me do the forms over until I got them right, and he'd take me out past the reef at night where the whales sing, and he'd tell me the names of the currents, and he'd—" He stopped. "Yeah. He taught me everything."

Clarisse set the tin cup down on the rock.

"Yeah," she said. "He taught me how to drink from a cup. That was about it."

"Clarisse," Silena said.

"No, I'm asking it. You've been loading that question for a month, Jackson, and everybody at this camp can hear you loading it." Clarisse's voice had gone very level, which on her meant the ground was about to move. "Poseidon claimed me when I was nine. There was a storm over the canoe lake. Middle of August. The water in the drinking glasses went the color of wine and the bottom fell out of the sky for four hours, and in the morning there was a trident burned into the sand at the north beach, and Chiron looked at me for a long time and said, 'Welcome to Cabin Three, Clarisse.'"

She picked up a peach and did not eat it.

"And then nothing. He never wrote. He never visited. He never came up out of the water at the courts and made me do anything. For six years I sat in that cabin and I waited, and what I got was a lake, a bunk, and a sister who cried." She turned the peach over in her fingers. "Six years, Jackson. Then you show up. Fourteen years old. A whole palace under the sea, a father who taught you everything, and you can do things with water I've never in my life been able to do, and Chiron watched you pick up a trident like it was a spoon, and suddenly the whole camp's talking about a Poseidon kid like there's only been one."

The silence that came after it was not the kind either of them knew how to hold.

Percy was looking at his hands. Then he looked up, and his eyes were the color they went when he was about to say something he had not planned, and Clarisse braced, because she had learned to brace.

Then he looked around. At the sand. At the little half-moon of beach, and the flat rock, and the pine shade.

"This is the beach," he said.

"This is the beach." Clarisse pointed, out past the shallows, where the lake went over a sandbar two feet down. There was a mark in it: a pale scar in the white sand, faint after nine summers of waves, still shaped like something with three prongs. "That's where he put it. Nine years old, and I remember thinking it was beautiful. And then nothing ever again, and now it's a sandbar, and in fifty years it'll be gone, and that's the whole of our father in one place."

Percy looked at the mark under the water for a long moment.

"I'm going to remember it," he said.

"You don't have to do that."

"I want to."

"Then do it," Clarisse said, and looked away, out at the lake, because her face was doing something she had not cleared with anybody.

"I didn't ask for the ocean," he said.

"I know."

"I didn't ask for him either."

"I know that too."

"I'd trade it," Percy said. "Right now. I'd trade all of it for a single letter."

"Don't do that," Clarisse said sharply. "Don't you dare do that. Don't you make yourself smaller so I don't have to feel like this."

"I'm not making myself smaller. I'm telling you what's true." He leaned forward, elbows on his knees, the wet cuffs of his shirt hanging off his forearms. "You think I got a family and you got a lake. You think I got a father and you got a bunk. And you're right. You're completely right, and I'm not going to stand here and argue you out of it with a speech about how we all have our own gifts."

"Good," Clarisse whispered.

"But." Percy held up one finger, the way Silena had in Tempest Hall, and the resemblance struck her and then did not matter. "You got to grow up here. You got to learn this lake. You got five years of a sister who shows up with a juice box and sits down next to you on a dock and doesn't say anything, and I got a roof that hums, and I've been here one summer, and I don't know the names of anything."

Clarisse opened her mouth.

"My turn," Percy said. "You asked. I answered. Let me finish." He took a breath. "You're not going to like this part. I'm not trying to be nice about it, because you already told me not to."

"Get on with it."

"I hated it there," Percy said. "The palace. The reef. The roof that hums. I hated it, Clarisse. I lay up there at night and I wanted—" He stopped, and started over. "I wanted somebody to fight with. I wanted somebody to race. I wanted a sister here at camp who'd yell at me for my shirt being inside out. I wanted you and Silena in my life, and instead the ocean kept us in separate homes until I came here. And I asked for this. I asked to come. And you've been here the whole time, and I walked in last, and I took your father and half your cabin and the only thing anybody at camp wanted to look at, and I did it by accident, in a week."

Clarisse sat in her camp chair, on the warm rock, at the north beach, at the end of September, with a peach in her hand, and felt something in her chest give way with the particular crack of a thing that has been holding weight it was never built for.

"You noticed," she said.

"I noticed."

"You noticed and you didn't say anything."

"I was waiting," Percy said. "For you."

Silena got up then, quietly, the way she moved when a conversation had come to a place it could not be moved past, and went down to the water's edge and knelt and filled her hands and splashed her face, and left the two of them alone in the sun, which was the kindest thing she had done all day and by a wide margin the bravest.

"Say the rest of it," Clarisse said.

"The rest of it is stupid."

"Say the stupid part. Stupid's usually the true part."

Clarisse set the peach down. She looked at the lake. She said, to the lake, the way she had said the true thing in the pool: "I hated you. For a month. I was the only Poseidon kid at this camp, and I was the oldest, and I was good, and then they put a third bunk in our cabin and a fourteen-year-old boy walked in from the woods with no shoes and a trident scar and the whole camp turned its head."

"I had shoes," Percy said.

"You had shoes that didn't fit. Stop interrupting my confession."

"Sorry."

"I hated you," Clarisse said, "and then you were bad at spears."

"I wasn't—"

"You were terrible at spears. You still are. I could take you with a broom." She was talking faster now, the way she did when the words were coming from further down than she usually let them. "And you cried. In the laundry room, at midnight, in your first month, with your face in a towel, because you thought nobody would hear you over the dryers. And I heard you. And I stood in the hall for ten minutes and I didn't know what to do, Jackson, I had no idea what to do, because nobody had ever taught me what to do when somebody is sad in a place that's supposed to be yours."

"I didn't cry in the laundry room."

"Everybody knows about the laundry room."

"Silena would tell me."

"I told Silena," Silena called, from the water, without turning around, in the voice of a girl with no shame and all the love in the world.

Percy laughed. It came out of him sideways and caught him by surprise, and then it was real, and Clarisse felt the laugh come out of her too, and for a minute the two of them sat on the rock and laughed about a laundry room and a towel and a fourteen-year-old boy crying into a dryer, and the lake moved, and the sun was warm on their shoulders, and it was, Clarisse thought, the closest thing to a family she had ever gotten for free.

Then she said the thing that stung, and she did not take it back.

"You'll go," she said. "That's the part that scares me. You went from a palace to a cabin in one summer. You leave places. You left the ocean, didn't you? You left it. And one day a quest's going to come, or a war, or a road, and you'll be gone, and the third bunk will be made up and the towel will be on the hook, and we'll be here, and you'll be out there, and I will have spent five years learning not to miss people."

The words went out across the water and did not come back.

Percy looked at her for a long moment. Then he said, "Yes."

"That's it? Yes?"

"Yes," Percy said. "I don't know how to stay anywhere. You're right. I've lived in two places in my whole life and I left the first one, and the second one is a cabin with a pool at the end of the hall, and some mornings I wake up in it and I can't remember which ocean I'm near." He wiped his face with the hem of his shirt, which was now right-side out and drying stiff with salt. "You're right, and I'm not going to argue you out of it, and I'm not going to promise you I'll never leave, because that's the kind of promise that gets people killed."

"So teach me the staying part," Percy said. "That's all I'm asking. Not forgiveness. Teach me the staying part."

And Clarisse La Rue, who had a reputation and had earned every tooth of it, sat in her camp chair at the north beach with her arms crossed over her knees and looked at her brother and thought: there was no Thursday thing. She had made that up. There was absolutely a Thursday thing. She had been doing it for five years, he had been here for one summer, and he already knew how to ask.

"Fine," she said.

"Fine?"

"There's no Thursday thing."

"There's absolutely a Thursday thing," Silena said, from the water.

"Shut up, Silena."

"Thursdays," Silena said, floating, "you do the tile. Both of you. On your hands and knees with a brush and the lemon oil. Clarisse pretends it's discipline. It's not discipline. She caught Percy doing it the second week he was here, alone, at midnight, with a rag."

Percy's ears went red. "I was trying to be useful."

"You were trying to be useful," Clarisse said, "in a room that wasn't yours yet, at an hour when nobody could see you, so you could pretend it wasn't a big deal when they found out." She went to the satchel and came back with a faded blue camp shirt, the spare she kept rolled at the bottom of it for exactly this purpose, its sleeves cut off in jagged lines and its hem frayed, and shoved it at Percy's chest. "Put that on. The wind's turned and you're cold and you're going to get sniffly about it, and I'm not nursing a sniffly brother through sparring."

"It's not mine."

"It's Cabin Three's, which means it's yours, which is the entire point of Cabin Three, and if you make it a whole thing I'm throwing you in the lake."

Percy took the shirt. He looked at it, the jagged sleeves, the fray, the smell of lemon oil and lake dried into the cloth, and he put it on over the orange one, and it was too big in the shoulders and right everywhere else.

"You're tiny," Clarisse said.

"You're short."

"I'm two years older and you're built like a fishing pole."

"Put a shirt on, you're shivering," Silena said, and Clarisse realized she was, slightly, and did not bother to deny it, and stood there in the sun with her arms out and her boots on and her socks in her hand, and let herself be one of the three Camp Three siblings at the same rock on the same lake, with a peach she had not eaten going soft in the paper beside her.

---

Percy carried the chair up the hill, because he had lost the race, and he carried it the way you carry something you have been given on purpose.

The walk up went slow, because the day had used itself well and nobody wanted to be the first to get to the top. Clarisse had the satchel on one shoulder and her boots in her free hand, and she was walking barefoot on the pine needles with the unhurried competence of a girl who had done this path a thousand times in every season. Silena had the empty crate and the quilt draped over her shoulders like a cape, and she was singing something with no words that everybody recognized, and every now and then she stopped singing to point at a bird and then started again as if nothing had happened. Percy came along in the middle in a shirt that was too big in the shoulders and right everywhere else.

At the bend where the path came out alongside the water, they could see down the whole length of the lake to where the willows closed over the cove, and Percy stopped.

The heron was still there.

It stood on the bow of the half-sunk skiff in the willow shade, the one the campers had stopped asking about, grey and still and perfectly balanced, one leg folded up under it, the reeds around it thick and green. It had been there for weeks. Nobody knew where it came from. Nobody knew what it was waiting for. It was simply the fact of the place now, the heron and the skiff and the water holding them both, and every time Percy saw it he felt the same thing, which was a kind of attention, as though the lake had posted a witness.

It turned its head and looked at him.

Then it looked away, and smoothed one feather with its beak, and went on being whatever it was being.

"She's still there," Silena said, coming up beside him.

"Yeah."

"Good," Silena said. "I like knowing something's waiting."

They went on up. Halfway, a Hermes kid came down the path in the other direction with a stack of training shields on one arm and a loaf of bread under the other, and he took in the three of them in a second, the wet hair and the emptied crate and the quilt and the camp chair, and said, with total cheerfulness, "Poseidon day?"

"Poseidon day," Clarisse said.

"Dang," said the Hermes kid, and got out of the way, and then, because he was eleven and had not yet learned to leave anything alone: "Is it on the calendar?"

"It's a tradition," Clarisse said.

"It's a religious observance," Percy said.

"It's both," Silena said, and the kid nodded gravely and went on down the hill with his shields and his bread, and the three of them climbed the last of the switchback in the gold late light without speaking, which had become, Percy realized, one of the languages they spoke.

The dock had that end-of-day quality it had when nobody else had thought of it yet. The water was going rose at the edges, the far shore was a dark green line, the pavilion lanterns up the hill were just starting to tick on, and a loon called once from the direction of the cove and did not call again. The three of them put the chair down at the top of the ramp, where it could watch, and walked out to the end of the dock, and sat, and put their feet in the water.

"I saved you a peach," Percy said, and pulled it out of the back of his shorts, where it had been riding against him for the last hour and a half and had not enjoyed any of it. "It's bruised. It's basically a peach smoothie in a skin."

"I didn't say thank you."

"You didn't have to."

"I'm saying it now so you don't get used to it." She took it and bit straight into it, juice running down her wrist. "You're disgusting."

"I'm thoughtful."

"You're thoughtful at Annabeth. You're disgusting at me."

"You're the one who's eating my peach."

Clarisse made a noise that was not a denial.

"You're saving her one too," Silena said.

"Yeah." Percy looked at the peach in his other hand, wrapped in a leaf, the one with the best shape. "Is that okay?"

"Percy." Silena's voice had all the warmth in it that she ever put into two syllables. "You're allowed to have more than one kind of love. That's the whole deal here. That's the entire arrangement." She flicked a wet finger at his knee. "Take it to her after dinner. Tell her the north beach has good rock."

"I was going to tell her it was a patrol thing."

"It's not a patrol thing."

"It's a Cabin Three thing," Clarisse said. "Tell her that. She's official cartographer, she'll want it on the map. Everybody wants Cabin Three things on their map." She wiped her wrist on her shorts and looked out at the lake, and her voice went into the flatter register it went into when she was about to say something true. "She drew our cabin on that wall, you know. Put the pool in. Got the channels wrong, but she got the pool."

"Did you tell her?"

"I told her the channels were wrong. I didn't tell her she got the pool right." Clarisse shrugged, a small one. "She'll figure it out."

The sun went down behind the trees on the far shore and the lake took its one particular evening color, the dark gold that comes after the bright gold, and the water was warm on their feet from the day and cool underneath, the way it always was, the way the lake held its summer deep down and gave it back slowly.

"Float," Percy said.

Clarisse looked at him. "It's September, Jackson."

"It's still warm. It was warm at midnight at the end of summer."

"It's October in two weeks."

"Then we've got two weeks," Percy said, and stood up, and pulled his shirt over his head, the borrowed blue one, and handed it back to her, and folded it, and set it on the dock where it would not blow in. "Float. With me. Ears under. One true thing. I want to do it in the lake, because I've only ever watched, and I want to know what it's like."

Silena stood up. She unlaced her camp shirt at the waist and let it fall, and stepped out of her shorts, and stood on the end of the dock in the last of the light in her swimsuit with her hair down and her feet bare and her whole self arranged like a person who had never once in her life been embarrassed to be alive.

"Clarisse," she said. "Ankle."

"You're both insane."

"You say that every time."

"You do it every time too."

Clarisse dropped her shirt on top of Percy's, and kicked off her shorts, and stood up on the planks, and looked at the lake, and did the thing with her face that Percy was learning to read, which was the softening, the letdown, the small private undoing that only happened when the water was in front of her and nobody was watching and she was about to be held.

"Fine," she said. "But if anybody's out there to see, I'm denying it and blaming you."

"Nobody's out there."

"I know. I'm pre-blaming."

They went in off the end of the dock, three of them, in a spread of ripples that ran the whole width of the light, and the freshwater came up over their knees and their waists and their chests, and Percy lay back, and the lake took him, and it was different from the salt, and it always was: colder, cleaner, somehow more honest, the way a room is more honest than a palace.

They made their line, as the tradition required. Percy on the outside, Silena in the middle, Clarisse closest to the dock, and each of them holding the ankle of the one in front, and the water moving gently under them all, and the sky going violet above the pines, and the first star coming out over the far shore where the town lights they were not allowed to swim to were just beginning to prick on.

Silena went first.

She took a breath and went under, and her hair spread around her head in the green light, and she said the true thing with her ears full of lake, and stayed down longer than she had in the pool, and came up with her face shining and her eyes clear and no explanation whatsoever, because that was the rule.

Then Clarisse.

She put her head under without drama and came straight back up, quicker than either of them, and shook her hair out, and said, "One," and did not explain it, and Percy felt her fingers tighten once on Silena's ankle and then let go.

Then Percy.

He filled his lungs, and went down, and let the lake close over him, and in the green roar with the dock posts going up above him like pillars and the warm and cold layers dividing somewhere near his ribs, he said it. He said the thing he had been carrying since the rock, since the laundry room, since the first summer, the thing that was the price of admission and the whole of his ticket.

He came up. He did not explain it.

They floated for a while in the line, three heads and a spread of hands, and the water held them the way it held the dock and the skiff in the cove and the heron's reflection, which was the same and also not the same, and Percy thought about how much water he had known in his life, and what each of it had taught him, and what he owed it.

The sea had taught him survival. The sea had taught him the shapes of storms and the names of currents and the weight of a kingdom's expectations on a pair of shoulders that had not finished growing. The sea had been a father who was always there and never, somehow, in the room.

The lake had taught him something else. The lake had taught him how to be held by a thing that was smaller than he was.

He said that part out loud, and then he explained it, because Silena was already crying a little, quietly, in the way she had, and because Clarisse was looking at him with an expression that was doing three things at once, and because the rule about not explaining was a tradition and not a law, and traditions, as his sisters had taught him, were things you chose.

"It's a good lake," he said. "That's all. It's a good lake, and you gave it to me, and I don't know how to say thank you for a thing like that, so I'm saying this instead."

"You're a freak," Clarisse said, thickly.

"I'm your freak."

"You're absolutely my freak." She splashed him, a flat-handed handful of freshwater that went in his ear and down his neck, and he splashed her back, and Silena came up in the middle of it with her hair in her face laughing her big round water laugh, and for a minute the three of them thrashed around in the shallows off the end of the dock like children, like siblings, like people who had been given a lake and had decided, on purpose, to be grateful for it.

They crawled back up onto the dock at last, dripping, and sat with their feet in the water and the quilt pulled over their shoulders, all three of them under one quilt, which was the only way to share a quilt and the way Cabin Three had always done it. Percy put the folded blue shirt back on over his head, and it was dry where it had been folded, and it smelled like lemon oil and lake and the bottom of a canvas satchel, and he did not say anything about it, and neither did Clarisse, which was how gifts worked in Cabin Three.

"Ten minutes per occurrence," Clarisse said, to the lake.

"Retroactive," Silena said.

"No Zeus cabin," Percy said. "So the rate's single."

"That's right." Clarisse leaned her head back against the post and looked at the stars, which were out now, all of them, except where the thin moon had not yet come up. "That's the best part of the rule. That's the secret of the whole thing, and nobody's ever noticed it but me."

"What secret?" Percy asked.

"The rule's not about minutes." She said it simply, the way he had said the thing about his father on the rock, the way you say a fact about gravity. "It's about who counts as an occurrence."

Percy looked at her. Silena looked at her.

"You counted me out of the rule from the beginning," he said.

"You're not an accompaniment, Jackson. You're Cabin Three. You're the whole reason the room has a third bunk in it." Clarisse examined the water. "I made the rule to keep people out. I made it in April, when it was just me and Silena and a lake, and I said ten minutes per occurrence so nobody would take our dock and our cove and our midnight. And then you showed up in shoes that didn't fit, and I kept saying it, and every time it got less true, and now it's just a thing I say because you two expect me to say it."

"So stop saying it," Silena said.

"Never."

"So say it right," Percy said. "Say it for us."

Clarisse was quiet for a moment. Then, in her best carrying voice, the one she used for announcements and threats and the occasional prayer, she said:

"Ten minutes per occurrence. Retroactive. Free for Poseidon cabin. Always was."

"Always was," Silena echoed.

"Always was," Percy echoed, and the words went out across the water and came back changed, and the lake kept them, and the stars did their work, and the three of them sat on the dock under one quilt with their feet in the freshwater and the whole long summer going quiet around them.

Somewhere down the shore, in the willow shade, the heron shifted its weight on the bow of the sunken skiff and settled again, and the lake went on holding everything it had been given: two palaces' worth of memory, three childhoods' worth of different water, one dock, and the whole of it, forever, in trust.
