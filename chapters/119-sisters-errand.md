## Chapter 119: Sisters' Errand

Storm-wrack still clung to Camp Half-Blood, as though the sky had tried to rearrange it and grown bored halfway through.

The dining pavilion's awning sagged, strawberry pulp streaked the Hermes cabin roof where a basket had landed, and the shield leaning against a pine by the lake had acquired a branch through its handle. Its owner had promised revenge on the tree.

Overhead, the sky looked clear and innocent. Annabeth didn't trust it for a second.

Cabin One had taken mostly decorative damage. The stone walls were sound, the roof was sound, and the porch columns had weathered the wind. The decorations were another matter. A blue-and-silver banner had ripped down one side. Three little bronze cloud-bells lay bent in the grass. The carved wooden sunburst over the door was missing its center, and its rays had twisted at angles that made it look less like a symbol of the sky and more like a surprised octopus.

Chiron had put the repair assignment on the board beside the dining pavilion. In his neatest hand he'd written *Cabin One: decorations, not structure*, with a small note beneath it: *Please do not turn this into a lightning experiment.*

Annabeth had read the note twice. The second time had been for the pleasure of imagining the exact expression on Chiron's face when he wrote it.

She found Thalia on the porch with the banner folded over one arm and a toolbox in the other hand. Thalia had already changed into work clothes: jeans with one torn knee, an old camp shirt, and boots whose laces had clearly lost an argument with her impatience. Her black hair lifted in the breeze, smelling faintly of rain and pine sap. The cloud cover had gone, but static still feathered around her sleeves, raising the fine hairs on her arms.

"I brought the toolbox," Thalia said.

"I can see that."

"Good. We're ahead of schedule."

Annabeth glanced at the toolbox. It was large enough to hold a small anvil, several hammers, and what looked like the remains of an absurdly ambitious mousetrap. "Are we repairing decorations or rebuilding the cabin from the foundation up?"

"Depends how the afternoon goes."

"Chiron specifically said decorations, not structure."

"Then we should be safe as long as nobody gets inspired." Thalia set the box down. Its contents clanged like a drawer full of angry cutlery. "I got the banner down before it could take someone's head off. The bells are over by the steps. We can straighten the sunburst, put it back, then decide what to do with the rest."

She draped the banner over the nearest porch post.

Annabeth rested her sketchbook against the porch rail. "I thought we might start by deciding what to do with the rest."

"Sure. After the sunburst." Thalia had already opened the toolbox and was sorting through it. "The center pin's probably bent. I can drill the old one out."

"Can I look at it first?" Annabeth asked.

"Of course." Thalia picked up the wooden sunburst. "Here."

She handed it over, though she kept hold of the lower edge, as if Annabeth might drop it or the wood might bolt for the woods. Salt air and old varnish clung to the panel, which had once been painted dark blue, with thin silver lines fanning from a bronze center. The paint was chipped. One ray had split at the tip, and the center disk was gone. Annabeth turned it over, checked the back, and ran a finger carefully over the broken edge.

"The pin isn't bent," she said. "The hole is. If we force a new one into it, the split will keep opening."

"We can clamp it."

"We could. Or we could repair the wood and make a new center that covers the old hole."

Thalia shifted the panel in her hands. "That sounds like more work."

"It is more work. It would also last longer."

"Okay." Thalia put the panel on the porch bench and found a small clamp in the box. "I'll—"

"Thalia."

Her sister stopped with one hand around the clamp. Outside, the camp had returned to its usual afternoon noise. Nothing required Thalia to move faster than Annabeth could follow.

"I want to do the repair," Annabeth said. "Not watch you do it."

Thalia's expression changed by only a fraction. Her mouth stayed level, but her hand tightened around the clamp. "I know you do."

"I don't think you do."

"Beth—"

"If I ask for a tool, I want you to hand it to me. If I make a decision about the design, I want you to let me try it before you decide there's a safer or faster version. I can do this."

Thalia set the clamp down. "I wasn't saying you couldn't."

"You were about to take over."

"I was about to help."

"Those aren't always the same thing."

The words hit harder than Annabeth intended. Sawdust stung her nose. Thalia leaned against the porch post, arms crossing, as if she'd reached for a familiar answer and found empty air.

"I know you care," Annabeth said. "I'm not asking you to stop. I'm asking you to trust me with the hammer."

Thalia looked at the toolbox. "The hammer is one of the least dangerous things in there."

"That's deeply reassuring."

"I'm working on it."

Annabeth drew a line in the dirt with the toe of her shoe, then erased it. "I don't want to spend the afternoon proving I can do basic things. I know you don't mean to make it feel that way. But it does."

Thalia's gaze came back to her. "Okay."

The word was quiet, not a surrender, not quite an agreement. Annabeth waited.

"I can try to ask before I grab something," Thalia said. "But if you're holding a drill wrong, I'm probably going to say something."

"You can say something. You can't take it out of my hands unless I'm actually about to lose a finger."

"What's the official finger-loss threshold?"

"Visible blood."

"What about near-visible blood?"

"No."

"A lot of splinters?"

"You can tell me to use gloves."

Thalia considered this as if Annabeth had proposed a treaty with a particularly devious god. "All right. You get the tools and the design decisions. I'll do the ladder, the carrying, and the parts that require two people."

"You can use tools too," Annabeth said. "Just don't take mine away while I'm using them."

"That seems fair."

Far from a perfect arrangement; the reservation in Thalia's voice remained. Still, Annabeth had helped make it. That mattered.

"First decision," Annabeth said. "We don't put the sunburst back as it was."

Thalia lifted one eyebrow. "That's a bold way to begin the repair."

"The old design has a wood center where wind pressure can split the rays. The storm didn't cause the weakness. It exposed what was already failing."

"So we're improving it."

"We're changing it. Improving is a judgment we can make after it survives another storm."

"You're going to make that the motto, aren't you?"

"I hadn't planned to. But now that you mention it, I could make a little plaque."

"Please don't. Chiron will give us a second assignment."

They carried the pieces into the patch of shade beside the porch, where an old worktable sat under an oak. Annabeth laid out what the storm had left them: the sunburst panel, the bronze center bracket, three bent bells, a length of chain, and fragments from the cracked blue glass medallion that had once hung beneath the eaves. She drew a rough plan in her sketchbook. They would splint the cracked ray from behind, then add a bronze ring around the missing center and suspend the bells and glass drops below it. Wind could move the pieces independently instead of pushing against the whole panel.
Before Annabeth could decide where to start cutting, she set the sketchbook flat and measured the bracket against the back of the sunburst. The ruler caught on a splinter. She lifted it, rubbed the wood dust away with the heel of her hand, then measured again. The mount had twisted when the center came loose. It wasn't ruined, but if they bent it back, the holes wouldn't line up with the peg over the door.

“We need a new strap,” she said. “Something that spreads the weight across the back instead of making the pin do all of it.”

Thalia looked toward the toolbox. “There’s copper sheet in the emergency box.”

“Not wide enough.”

“I can make it wide enough.”

“You can splice two pieces, but the joint would sit right across the split.” Annabeth drew two marks on the sketch. “I want a single piece. The forge might have a strip left from the weather vane brackets.”

“Then we’ll go ask.” Thalia lifted the toolbox by its handle. “I’ll take this.”

Annabeth looked at the box, then at the scattered salvage. “We need the ruler and a sample. You don’t need to carry every hammer we own.”

“I know what’s in there.”

“So do I. That’s why I’m saying it.”

Thalia set the toolbox down. The handle creaked. She flexed her fingers as if the box had made a point neither of them wanted to hear. “What do you want me to bring?”

“The damaged bracket, the ruler, and the sketch. I can carry the rest.”

“You sure?”

Annabeth appreciated the question and disliked how much she appreciated it. “Yes. The bracket is awkward, so take that. I’ll take the sketchbook and ruler.”

They sorted the work into two piles. The bent bells and the glass fragments stayed on the table under a clean cloth; the wind could lift them if they left them exposed. Thalia wrapped the bracket in a spare rag so the twisted edge wouldn't scratch anyone. Annabeth closed the sketchbook around the ruler and tucked it beneath her arm.

At the porch steps, Thalia glanced at the path. Camp's main road was passable, though storm debris had been dragged into its edges. Someone had stacked branches beside the pavilion instead of blocking the way. The shorter cut-through toward the forge crossed a low place where runoff had carved a channel through the dirt.

“Main path?” Thalia asked.

“Main path.”

“It's longer.”

“It’s also wide enough to carry the bracket without walking sideways.”

“Good reason.” Thalia started down the steps, then stopped at the bottom so Annabeth could take the lead. It was such a small change in their usual rhythm that Annabeth almost missed it.

They crossed camp at the pace of the errand. Near the pavilion, two younger campers were sorting bent tent stakes into one bucket and usable ones into another. A third held an awning rope that had come loose from its post. Thalia's head turned at once. Her shoulders followed, already angling toward them.

Annabeth stopped. “Do you want to check whether they need help?”

Thalia's eyes flicked from the campers back to her. “I can ask.”

She didn't take the rope or rearrange the buckets. She went over and asked the camper holding it what the problem was. The knot had slipped, and the post's eye had bent. One of the others had the right replacement stake, but no one could hold the awning up and tie it at the same time.

“I can hold this,” Thalia said. “Do you want me to?”

The camper handed her the rope. Thalia braced one boot against the post while the child threaded a new knot through the loop. It took two tries; on the second, the awning drew taut and stayed. The camper checked the knot, then gave a satisfied nod.

“Thanks,” the child said.

“You did the knot,” Thalia said. “I was a post with arms.”

“That’s useful.”

“Don't tell anyone. I have a reputation.”

The campers laughed and returned to their sorting. Thalia came back to Annabeth without adding a chore to their route. Annabeth hadn't needed to intervene, and Thalia hadn't needed to pretend she hadn't noticed the loose rope. They had both done what they could without taking over the errand.

The forge stood warm even with the sky clear. Its open doors breathed out iron tang and old charcoal. A few campers were cleaning tools along the outer rack, keeping the main work area clear for repair work. Annabeth waited at the threshold until the smith looked up.

“Do you have a narrow strip of bronze or copper we can use?” she asked. “It needs to sit flat across the back of a wooden sunburst. The center mount twisted in the wind.”

“How long?” the smith asked.

Annabeth checked her drawing. “About the width of my hand. Longer than the crack by two inches on each side. Thin enough to bend around the panel, not so thin it buckles.”

The smith glanced at Thalia's wrapped bracket. “You bringing the old piece back?”

“Only as a pattern,” Annabeth said. “We're not leaving it here.”

“Didn't ask you to.” The smith pulled a small tray from beneath the bench. “We have a few offcuts from the roof hooks. Pick one that fits your plan. The thinner stuff will bend. The thicker strip will be harder to shape around that curve.”

Annabeth compared them, then chose the middle-width strip. It was longer than she needed, with a clean edge and two old screw holes at one end. She checked its flexibility against the sketch without bending it. It would fit if she placed the holes outside the split and filed down the corner.

“Can I use the hand vise?” she asked.

The smith nodded toward the cleared bench. “If you know what you're doing.”

“I do. I haven't done it with this exact bracket.”

“Better answer.”

Thalia set the wrapped wood down, then moved to the other side of the bench. She didn't reach for the strip. “Want a hand?”

“Hold the paper under the metal so I can mark where the old holes land.”

Thalia did. Annabeth used the ruler to transfer the bracket's measurements, then marked the two new positions in pencil. The first line was too close to the split. She erased it and started over. Nobody rushed her. When she finally got the spacing she wanted, Thalia angled the paper toward the smith so the holes could be checked against the forge's punch sizes.

“Those will work,” the smith said. “Take the strip to your table and do the shaping there. Don't hammer it over an edge unless you want a crease.”

“Understood.”

“Bring back the other half if you don't use it. Someone may need it for another repair.”

“We will.” Annabeth rolled the strip in a canvas offcut and tucked it under her arm.

On the walk back, they stopped at the pavilion long enough to return the awning rope's spare hook, which had been left on a bench. The campers had finished sorting the stakes. One of them had made a small sign labeling the buckets. It was crooked, and the words were too large for the board, but nobody had asked Thalia to rewrite it.

Annabeth looked at the sign. “That's a good label.”

“It's readable,” Thalia said.

“Those aren't different things.”

“They are when you want it straight.”

“Did you want to fix it?”

“I did.” Thalia nodded toward the children. “But it's their sign.”

Annabeth followed her glance. The crooked letters caught the sunlight. “Maybe it'll stay up better if they made it.”

“Maybe.” Thalia kept walking. “Don't make me sound wise about it.”

“I wouldn't dare.”

By the time they returned to Cabin One, the worktable had been cleared of loose glass and the shade had moved a little farther across the dirt. The ornament still waited in pieces, but now they had a strip that could hold the center without asking the cracked wood to do more than it could.

Annabeth set the supplies down in the order she planned to use them. “This is why we brought the sketch.”

“You made them let you use the vise,” Thalia said.

“I asked.”

“Right.” Thalia looked at the strip, then at Annabeth. “You asked for the right thing.”

Annabeth heard the compliment without trying to turn it into a verdict on the whole afternoon. “Let's see if the measurements are right first.”

Thalia bent over the drawing. "You want to hang glass over the doorway?"

"Small pieces. Rounded edges. The largest one would be no bigger than my thumb."

"And if they fall?"

"They'll be on short chains. The chains are attached to a crossbar. If one breaks, it drops a few inches and gets caught."

"If the crossbar breaks?"

"Then the whole thing falls."

"Glad we covered that."

"The crossbar will be supported at both ends."

Thalia pressed a finger to the corner of the sketch. "What if we use the bronze bells for the crossbar?"

"They're hollow."

"Could fill them with—"

"No. I want the bells to move."

"I wasn't saying—"

Annabeth looked up.

Thalia's finger lifted from the paper. "Right. Sorry. Your design."

The apology was quick, almost too quick. She tucked her hands into the pockets of her jeans as if they had become suspicious objects. Annabeth felt some of the tightness in her chest ease. The effort was visible, which made it harder to dismiss.

"You can still give me ideas," she said. "Just let them be ideas before they become a plan."

"I can do that."

"You say that like you have to remind yourself."

"I do."

Annabeth looked back at the drawing. "Then we can both practice."

Thalia tapped the paper once, gently. "The bells will sound different if they're lower."

"That's useful."

"And you could put the glass pieces on the outside of the ring, so they won't hit the bells."

Annabeth sketched the idea. "That's also useful."

"I have occasional thoughts that aren't orders."

"I'll tell the camp."

"Don't. I have a reputation."

The bench wobbled on the uneven dirt, smelling of warm pine resin, so Annabeth agreed to work on the shaded ground. Thalia didn't say *I told you so*. Her careful silence made Annabeth smile. They sorted the salvage, set the blue glass aside to be smoothed, and brought over a small vise from the forge.
Before putting the strip against the panel, Annabeth tore a clean page from the back of her sketchbook and pressed it over the damaged center. She rubbed the side of a pencil across the paper until the shape of the crack and the old fastener holes appeared. The mark was uneven where the wood had split. She measured from the sound edge instead of trusting the old center, then folded the paper over the new metal strip.

Thalia leaned close enough to see without reaching for the drawing. “You’re making another plan.”

“A pattern. If I punch holes from the first sketch, the strap could pull the split wider.”

“Could it be shorter?”

“Not if it’s going to bridge the crack.”

“Could we put two fasteners on each side?”

“We could. But they’d press on the old holes. I’d rather use the new points I marked.” Annabeth turned the paper so Thalia could see the spacing. “The pin holds the center. The strap takes some of the sideways movement. If we make this part too rigid, it will transfer the strain to the next ray.”

Thalia looked from the page to the wooden panel. “So it needs to flex a little.”

“Exactly.”

“Could you make it curve before you attach it?”

“That’s the idea.”

Thalia shifted onto her heels. “Do you want me to hold the panel while you test the pattern?”

“Yes, on the unbroken side. Not at the ray.”

She braced it where Annabeth indicated. Annabeth laid the paper against the curve and found a gap at the lower edge. The pattern would leave the metal floating over a hollow; tightened down, it would bow and pull against the wood. She marked the gap with a small X, then made another template, narrowing it by a fraction. The second try sat flatter.

“Would that spread the force?” Thalia asked.

“Better.”

“Then use that one.”

“I will, after I test it.” Annabeth lifted the paper. “It’s close. Close isn’t the same as right.”

Thalia's mouth twitched. “I wasn't going to say it was right.”

“I know.”

The sun had warmed the metal enough that it no longer felt cool against her palm. Annabeth marked the template onto the strip with a carpenter’s pencil, keeping the old holes outside the new shape. When she picked up the small punch, she paused over the first mark.

“Can you hold the strip flat?” she asked.

Thalia took the edge of the worktable rather than the metal. “Here?”

“There. It shouldn’t slide, and you won’t be near the punch.”

“Got it.”

Annabeth set the punch on the first mark and tapped it once. The metal dimpled but did not open. She struck again, harder. A clean hole appeared; the punch rang against the table. She checked the back for a raised burr and filed it smooth. The second hole went faster. She filed both corners, stopping to run a scrap of cloth over them. The cloth caught once. She filed the corner again until it slid cleanly.

Thalia watched her test it. “Would that catch on the wood?”

“Not now.”

“Good.”

They fitted the strip to the panel without tightening it. The first side rested flat. The other side lifted a little above the grain. Annabeth pressed gently. The metal gave, then settled against the wood without flattening the cracked ray.

“That looks better,” Thalia said.

“It is better. I still need to see what happens when the center ring's attached.”

“Because the ring adds weight.”

“And wind can pull unevenly on the pieces. If I fix the strap too soon, I won't know whether the imbalance comes from the ring or the wood.”

Thalia picked up the bronze ring from the salvage pile. “Then let's test the two together.”

Annabeth handed her the panel. “Hold it the way it will hang.”

Thalia lifted the sunburst only a few inches from the table. Annabeth rested the ring over the open center and let it find its own balance. The lower edge tipped toward the porch. She moved one chain anchor on her drawing and tried again. The ring stayed level, but the left ray dipped under its weight.

“Too heavy on that side,” Thalia said.

“Or the ray is already weaker.” Annabeth checked the splint line. “The strap can't make this branch stronger than the wood. The ring has to hang from the bracket, not the ray.”

“How do we do that?”

“Separate support.” Annabeth drew a second mark inside the circle. “The bracket carries the ring. The wood holds the shape. That way, if the ray flexes, it doesn't make the center swing.”

“Can the back strap reach the bracket?”

“If I bend it around the back edge.”

“Will it crack?”

“I don't know yet.” Annabeth looked at the metal. “We can make a gentle curve in the vise and stop before it creases.”

Thalia set the panel down. “Want me to bend it?”

Annabeth considered the question and the awkward angle. “I want you to hold the vise steady. I'll shape it.”

“Okay.”

The vise jaws resisted the first turn. Annabeth loosened them, adjusted the strip, and tried again. The bend came slowly, a shallow arc that matched the panel's back. When she fitted the bracket against it, the metal touched along almost the entire length. There was a small gap at one end, but she could correct that with the fastener without pulling the crack shut.

“Now?” Thalia asked.

“Now it might work.”

“That's less certain.”

“It is.” Annabeth laid the bracket over the support. “I don't want to say it does until the parts are together.”

“Then we put them together.”

Annabeth began with the center ring, attaching it to the bracket with two small loops of chain. She used the longer links first, leaving the adjustment open. Thalia held the panel upright while she checked the balance. The ring tilted again, but this time the tilt came from one loop being a link longer.

Annabeth shortened it. The ring leveled. She tested the fasteners by pressing each one with her thumb, then lifted the panel herself. It was heavier than she expected, though still manageable. The pressure rested on the bracket and strap. The split ray moved a little but did not widen.

“I'm going to set it down,” she said. “The strap needs one more fastener before we hang anything below the ring.”

Thalia released the panel at once, then caught the lower edge when it tipped toward the table. “Careful.”

“I have it.”

“I know.” Thalia kept her hands nearby until Annabeth steadied the piece, then withdrew them. “I saw it move.”

“I saw it too.”

The brief jolt left Annabeth's fingertips tingling. She rubbed her thumb over the edge of the panel, not because she was hurt, but because she had expected the prototype to feel more secure than it did. She had accounted for weight and flex on paper; in her hands, the wood still had its own opinions.

“Do you want to stop and get another opinion?” Thalia asked.

“I want to look at it for a minute.”

“Okay.”

Annabeth set the sunburst flat and examined the support from both sides. Nothing had cracked. The fasteners held. She could add the third point without making the strap rigid, but only if she kept it closer to the bracket. The design was still sound. It just needed one more adjustment before the decorative pieces went on.

“I’m not stopping,” she said. “I’m changing the order. We finish the back, then test it again before the bells.”

“Your call.”

“And when I ask you to hold it, you can tell me if it shifts.”

“I can do that.”

“Without catching it before I get a chance?”

“If it’s not going to hit the ground.”

“That seems fair.”

She added the third fastener and left the strap with a little give. When she tested the panel again, it held steady. The lower ray still flexed if she pressed it, but that movement no longer pulled on the center. She made a note in the margin: *do not hang glass from wood; attach to ring.*

Thalia read the note. “A useful reminder.”

“It's for me. I'll remember what we tried.”

“That's what notes are for.”

“They're also for arguing with future me.”

“Does future you ever listen?”

“Only when I use a ruler.”

"Do you want me to hold the dowel?" Thalia asked.

Annabeth glanced at her. "Yes, please."

Thalia held it steady. Annabeth worked the bell back into shape with the flat side of a small hammer. The first strike was too light. The next two were even. On the fourth, the bell gave a clean metallic note and settled into a rounder curve.

"There," Annabeth said. "It'll hang straight now."

"It sounds good." Thalia let go of the dowel. "Not too loud."

"The glass will make a higher sound."

"You can tell that from looking at it?"

"I can tell the glass is going to sound like glass."

"Incredible. Cabin One's finest scholar."

"The distinction is important."

While Thalia straightened the copper, Annabeth smoothed each glass shard on a hand stone until none could snag a sleeve.

Annabeth marked holes in the copper ring and measured the chain. When a strip twisted too sharply, she set it aside. Thalia reached for it, checked herself, then held out her palm.

"Want me to see if the vise can flatten it?" she asked.

"Yes. But don't hammer it. The crease might split."

"I'll use the smooth jaw."

"Thanks."

The breeze swung the torn banner over the porch post. Its loose edge snapped against the wood, a small, persistent sound. Annabeth watched it for a few seconds before looking back down at her sketch.

"That banner can be saved," she said. "The tear's clean. We can reinforce it from behind."

"I have a needle and waxed thread in the box."

"Why do you have sewing supplies in the toolbox?"

"It isn't a toolbox. It's the emergency box."

"You brought an emergency box to repair decorations?"

"The storm knocked a tree over beside the creek. The box has rope, two shovels, a medical kit, and trail mix."

"You packed a rescue operation for banner repair."

"The assignment said storm damage. The note didn't mention the tree."

Annabeth laughed. "Your childhood was a series of worst-case scenarios, wasn't it?"

Thalia's face softened into a crooked smile. "You say that like yours wasn't."

"Mine had more diagrams."

"That's not the defense you think it is."

"My worst-case scenarios had labels."

"Mine had a bus schedule."

"A bus schedule is a kind of diagram."

"It is if you need to know which bus gets you across town before dark."

The joke thinned into something quieter. Annabeth knew Thalia's childhood in pieces: the older girl finding food and shelter, watching their mother's moods, and keeping a younger child moving. It came from Thalia's stories, not from the childhood Annabeth herself remembered. Knowing the facts hadn't given her the days.

"You were eight," Annabeth said. "You shouldn't have had to know bus schedules."

"Neither should you have had to know what happened when the sky got too quiet." Thalia folded the repaired copper strip into a gentler curve. "That was the weird thing about our childhood. We got good at stuff because it was necessary, then everyone acted like being good at it meant it hadn't been hard."

Annabeth watched the sunlight move across the broken glass. "Maybe we could have had a normal afternoon if someone had warned us."

"What would we have done?"

"Complained about normal problems. Like a school project."

"You'd have made a chart. I'd have borrowed the glue without permission."

"That's stealing with a better publicist."

"Your chart would have had too many colors."

"There is no such thing." Annabeth smiled. "What did you think a normal afternoon was when you were little?"

Thalia considered. "One where the most dangerous thing was using too much soap."

"That sounds nice."

"Soap is expensive."

Annabeth thought of the childhood Thalia described and the different one she remembered. Fear had shaped both; knowing that did not make the missing nights return.

"You told me about the fever," Annabeth said.

Thalia's hands stopped on the copper strip. "Which one?"

"The one before camp. You said we were under that awning with the hole in it, and the rain kept getting through. You'd put your jacket over me and then it got soaked too."

"You were maybe four. I was eight."

"You said you kept a wet cloth on my forehead. I can imagine the cold water. And you told me the thunder was a giant rolling furniture around upstairs."

Thalia looked toward the camp, not at her. "I was trying to make you laugh."

"Did I laugh? I hope I called the giant rude."

"You told him to stop stomping. You had a fever and were still trying to negotiate with the sky."

"That tracks."

"I was scared," Thalia said. "I didn't know how high your fever was. I didn't know where to take you. The motel clerk had told us not to come back, and the clinic was across town. I remember you trying to sit up and me telling you no, then you got mad at me."

"I didn't like being told what to do."

"You still don't."

"I was right about the fever."

"You were right about wanting water. You were not right about trying to walk to the clinic by yourself."

Thalia remembered the soaked jacket, the clinic, and the fear of having no adult to call. Annabeth knew the wet cloth and the ridiculous giant only through her telling. They could talk about the same night without pretending both of them remembered it.

Annabeth set the pencil down. "You shouldn't have had to know how to do all that."

"I know."

"I'm glad you did it."

Thalia glanced back at her, one corner of her mouth tightening. "Those can both be true."

"They can."

Thalia returned to the copper strip. "Your turn to tell me something from the other life."

Annabeth blinked. "Why?"

"Because you were about to ask me something. And I know the look you get when you're deciding whether to ask."

"I wasn't."

"You were."

Annabeth turned the pencil between her fingers. Thalia's death pressed on every other memory, but it wasn't the one she wanted to set between them beside a toolbox. There were other memories, less grand and still important.

"I remember you coming back," Annabeth said. "After the tree."

Thalia's shoulders went still. "What do you remember?"

"The fleece. The light on the hill. You stepping out of it and looking at all of us like you were trying to work out how much time had passed. I remember wanting to run to you, but I didn't. I thought you might not want a hug from someone who was still a little kid to you."

"I would have wanted one."

"I know that now. I didn't know it then."

Thalia's fingers rested against the vise handle. "I can picture wanting to catch up. Seeing you taller, looking like someone who'd made it through a lot without me. But I can't tell you what that Thalia thought. I wasn't there."

"I know. You never became a tree here."

"Still hard to imagine."

"It was hard watching you figure everything out."

"Did she try to catch up in one afternoon?"

"Pretty much," Annabeth said. "She didn't have to. I was glad she was there."

Thalia kept her attention on the vise. "I'm glad you got your friend back."

"Even if she wasn't my sister?"

"Yeah." Thalia turned the handle a fraction. "I remember raising my little sister. You remember growing up with a friend. Now you tell me those stories, and I keep wanting to answer for someone I never was. As if being your sister means I should know what to do about all of it."

"They tell me the same thing about you."

"Which is?"

"That you should know what I need before I say it."

Thalia let out a breath through her nose. "That sounds like a terrible instruction."

"It is."

"We should ignore it."

"We can try."

"Trying seems to be the theme of the afternoon."

"It's either that or the theme is decorative storm damage."

"That one has fewer feelings."

"And a worse title."

Annabeth fitted the copper strip into the ring and marked the crossbar for a second support. Thalia held pieces when asked and fetched the requested tools. When she passed over a rasp instead of a file, she corrected the mistake without comment.

As the sun moved past the roofline, they repaired the banner. Annabeth threaded the needle while Thalia held the torn edge flat.
The tear had clean edges, but the cloth had pulled into a shallow wave. Annabeth laid the banner across the worktable and aligned the silver bolt before she began. If she simply brought the two sides together, the symbol would bend where the fabric puckered. She drew a chalk line on the inside of the cloth, marking where a narrow strip of backing would take the stress.

“Can I cut the backing?” Thalia asked.

“Not yet. I need to check how much the cloth moves when I pull the stitch.”

“Right.” Thalia kept the banner flat. “I can hold this corner.”

“Please do.”

Annabeth began with a loose running stitch to keep the tear from widening. The waxed thread resisted her needle, then slid through. She pulled the first loop too tight. The cloth gathered up again, worse than before.

“Stop,” she said.

Thalia lifted her fingers away. “What happened?”

“I pulled too hard.” Annabeth reached for the stitch with a small pick. “If I keep going, the bolt will kink.”

“Can you undo it?”

“I think so. Don't move the cloth.”

The thread had caught under itself. Annabeth worked the pick into the knot, freeing one loop at a time. She felt the urge to hurry before the light changed. The more she rushed, the more likely she was to cut the fabric. She made herself loosen her shoulders and tried again, keeping the stitch wide enough to spread the pull.

Thalia watched the needle. “Do you want me to hold it farther back?”

“Maybe. Put your hand here.” Annabeth shifted her sister's fingers to the smooth section of cloth. “Not the bolt. The fabric. Let it move a little when I pull.”

“Like this?”

“That's right.”

The next stitch went through without gathering the banner. Annabeth finished the short line, then unrolled the backing strip against the seam. It was a leftover piece from an older repair, a different shade of blue, but it would sit on the inside and wouldn't show from the path.

“That's not the same fabric,” Thalia said.

“It doesn't need to be. It needs to hold.”

“We could use part of the torn edge.”

“It's frayed. It would make the patch weak.”

Thalia gave a small nod. “Then use the old strip.”

Annabeth cut the backing to size and pinned it in place. A breeze lifted the far edge of the banner. Thalia reached to catch it, then stopped with her hand above the cloth.

“Do you want me to hold the loose end?”

“Yes.”

She held the corner while Annabeth stitched across the backing, changing from a loose running stitch to short diagonal ones. The work was slower than the first pass. Every few inches, she checked the opposite side to make sure the silver bolt stayed straight. One stitch landed too near the edge. She removed it before the cloth could tear and set it a finger-width inward.

Thalia kept the fabric from drifting without keeping it rigid. When Annabeth paused, Thalia did too. Their hands were close, her sister's thumb holding the pale blue cloth down while Annabeth drew thread through it. Nobody told Annabeth she was lucky to have help. Nobody called the work difficult enough to justify taking it away.

“Do you sew often?” Annabeth asked.

“Enough to keep something from falling apart until we find a better way.” Thalia shifted the cloth. “I used to mend our clothes when the hems went. My stitches were terrible.”

“Were?”

“They're better now.”

“Why did you keep doing it?”

“Because we didn't have the money to replace a shirt every time a seam went.” Thalia flattened the backing. “And because I didn't want you to wear things that didn't fit.”

Annabeth paused. She knew that story; Thalia had told her about measuring sleeves with the edge of a bus ticket. But this was the first time she had heard sewing folded into the same memory.

“Did you have a favorite thing you fixed?”

Thalia considered. “A green jacket. The zipper was broken, so I put hooks down the front. It looked terrible.”

“Did I wear it?”

“For years.”

“Did I like it?”

“You thought the hooks made you look like a pirate.”

Annabeth laughed, then looked down at the blue cloth. “That feels like a version of me.”

“It was you,” Thalia said. “One you haven't met.”

“I know.” Annabeth pulled the thread through. “I don't remember the jacket. But I can imagine how you repaired it.”

“Not very neatly.”

“It worked.”

“It did.” Thalia turned the panel so Annabeth could reach the next part. “You had a habit of refusing to wear anything if you couldn't get in and out of it yourself.”

“That also sounds like me.”

“Yeah.”

Annabeth made three careful stitches. The patch no longer pulled the banner out of shape. Its backing sat flat, and the lightning bolt ran straight across the repair. She kept the stitches small but not invisible. If someone looked closely, they would see where the cloth had been mended.

“Could we cover the seam with another strip on the outside?” Thalia asked.

“We could. But that would make the edge heavy.”

“And we don't need to hide it.”

Annabeth looked up. “No.”

She knotted the thread and pressed the patch between her fingers. The cloth held without puckering. When they lifted the banner, the wind ran through it and the repaired seam moved with the rest.

“That's good,” Thalia said.

“It's good enough to hang.”

“You don't have to pretend it's perfect.”

“I know. I want it to stay up.”

Thalia nodded. “Me too.” The stitch line wasn't perfectly straight, but the patch would hold.

When the banner was whole again, Thalia held it up to the light. "You can't see the patch."

"You can if you know where it is."

"That's true of most things."

"Maybe."

Thalia handed the banner to her. "Want to hang it?"

Annabeth smiled. "Yes." Thalia carried the ladder but let Annabeth set it and test the rungs.

"You can hold it," Annabeth said. "Just don't move it unless I ask."

"Understood."

Annabeth climbed two rungs, lined the stitched edge against the bracket, and fitted the ring over the peg. The breeze lifted the blue cloth with a soft snap, its silver lightning bolt crooked but bright.

"A little higher on the left," Thalia said.

Annabeth looked down. "Is it actually crooked?"

"A little."

"Do you want to tell me or fix it?"

Thalia's mouth opened, then closed. "Tell you."

Annabeth shifted the pole a finger-width. "Better?"

"Yes."

"Thanks."

Annabeth climbed down. Thalia didn't reach for the ladder until she'd stepped clear. The banner settled into place, its patched seam visible if someone knew where to look. It belonged there anyway.

"One thing fixed," Annabeth said.

"One thing repaired."

"What?"

"You said improving was something we had to wait and see about."

Annabeth nodded. "I did."

"I'm learning the rules."

"There are a lot."

"You should make a chart."

"I could color-code it."

"No."

They brought the half-finished ornament to the porch and checked the hanging balance. The bronze ring now sat in the sunburst's open center, its cracked ray splinted from behind. Copper arcs hung at different heights, each supporting one bell or a small piece of blue glass. It looked a little like a sunburst and a little like a rain cloud caught in the act of becoming something else. Annabeth liked that it didn't decide between the two.

"The center needs a final pin," she said. "And the lower chains need to be checked for swing."

Thalia nodded. "I'll get the ladder."

"I'll get the pin."

They reached for the toolbox at the same time. Thalia withdrew her hand.

"You can open it," Annabeth said. "I need the short punch and the bronze pin."

Thalia crouched and selected both. "This one?"

"Yes."

She passed the tools over, handle-first. Annabeth set the punch against the center bracket, adjusted the angle, and tapped the pin into place. The first hit glanced off; the second went true.

"Good," Thalia said.

Annabeth ran a finger around the edge. "It isn't perfect."

"It doesn't have to be."

"The center sits a little off."

"From here, it looks like it sits exactly where it wants to."

Annabeth looked up at her. "That's an extremely suspicious compliment."

"It's a compliment from someone who once put a crooked pinecone on a shelf because it had character."

"Was that one of the pinecones Zeus gave you?"

"No. That one came from a tree."

"A normal tree?"

"As far as I know."

"A disappointing backstory."

Thalia held the ladder steady while Annabeth fixed the repaired sunburst above the doorway. The fasteners held. The bells chimed low and gentle; the glass answered with a thinner note. It sounded like rain beginning somewhere far off.
The first gust came up the path from the water, lifting the banner and turning the glass drops in a bright, uneven arc. One shard swung farther than the others and struck the inside of the bronze ring. The note it made was not the thin reply Annabeth had imagined. It was a sharp tick, almost a snap.

“Stop,” Annabeth said.

Thalia held the ladder steady while Annabeth climbed down. The ornament kept moving overhead, the same shard tapping the ring whenever the wind pulled it toward the doorway.

“Is it going to break?” Thalia asked.

“I don't think so. But the chain is too long.” Annabeth watched it swing again. “And that one catches the ring. If someone comes through while the wind is up, it'll make them think something hit the roof.”

“Can you shorten it from here?”

“Not safely.” Annabeth looked at the ladder. “We take it down and adjust it on the ground.”

Thalia nodded and waited for Annabeth to step away before moving the ladder. They each took one end of the panel. Annabeth kept a hand against the ring while they lowered it, careful to keep the glass from striking the wood. It was heavier than when they had lifted it, now that the pieces hung free and shifted with every step.

When they set it on the worktable, Annabeth examined the links. She could shorten the chain by one, but the glass piece had a rougher edge than she remembered. The smoothing stone had rounded its point, not the whole side. She ran her thumb along it, found the catch, and fetched the stone.

“Do you want me to hold the ring?” Thalia asked.

“Yes. Just steady it while I smooth the edge.”

Thalia held the bronze ring between two fingers, away from the shard. Annabeth worked the stone along the glass in short strokes, stopping to check the edge against the scrap cloth. The cloth glided over it without catching.

“Now the chain,” she said. “Can you pass me the small pliers?”

Thalia chose the right tool and held it out by the handle. Annabeth opened the link, removed one section, and closed it again. The drop now hung closer to the center, with enough room to move but not enough to strike the ring. She measured the three nearest pieces against one another. Their lengths were different by design, but all the glass needed to stay within the circle's safe swing.

“What if it moves more in a storm?” Thalia asked.

“Then we'd take it down before the next storm.”

“Can we do that without unpinning the whole thing?”

Annabeth looked at the mount. “If we use a hook at the top instead of the closed loop, yes. It can be lifted off the peg as one piece. The lower catch can keep it from twisting.”

“Would it stay up in regular wind?”

“It should.”

“Should?”

“That's what a test is for.” Annabeth moved to the doorway and looked at the peg. “We can keep the current fastener for today, then ask Chiron whether the hook is strong enough before we leave it there overnight.”

Thalia tilted her head. “That's sensible.”

“It's also the only option that lets us check it while someone responsible is still awake.”

“You are responsible.”

"Chiron has been checking these things longer than I have."

“That's one way to measure it.”

They carried the ornament outside again, this time with the ladder waiting beside the porch. Annabeth asked Thalia to hold the lower end of the panel while she checked that each chain was tucked away from the bracket. The wind had softened. It lifted the banner once and then let it fall.

“Ready?” Thalia asked.

“Ready.”

Thalia steadied the ladder. Annabeth climbed high enough to reach the peg and tested it with her free hand before lifting the ornament. The hook caught. She guided it into place, then fitted the small lower catch around the support. When she climbed down, they both stood back and waited for a breeze.

The first was barely enough to move the glass. It rang softly, without touching the metal. The next came from a different direction, turning the blue shard outward so it caught the light. The bells answered, lower than the glass but not crowded by it. Annabeth watched each link, counting the seconds between swings until the pieces settled.

“Does it need another adjustment?” Thalia asked.

Annabeth kept her eyes on the center. The glass moved in a small circle, then came back to stillness. “Not yet.”

“That's a cautious yes.”

“It's a yes to leaving it up while we watch it.”

“How long?”

“Long enough to see it settle. Then we ask Chiron to check the mount.”

Thalia looked at the clock tower. “We have time before dinner.”

“Good. I want to write down the hook size while I remember.”

Annabeth went to her sketchbook and added the change: one shortened glass chain, one smoothed edge, one removable hook with a lower catch. She wrote beside it that the mount still needed a second pair of eyes before they considered the repair finished. The note did not make her feel less capable. It made the work more honest.

Thalia read over her shoulder. “You said it was fixed.”

“I said it works. I didn't say it was finished.”

“Good distinction.”

“The hook may need a stronger gauge. If it bends, the whole piece could come down.”

“Then we ask Chiron to look.”

Annabeth glanced up. Thalia wasn't trying to replace her judgment with his. She was agreeing that a repair could be their work and still benefit from a check.

“I'll ask,” Annabeth said.

“I can come with you.”

“If you want.”

“I do. I can explain what we changed.”

Annabeth nodded. “Then we'll go together.”

They waited until the swinging stopped. The ornament stayed in place, no more than a finger's width from the panel. The chime was quiet enough that the breeze had to find it; it didn't demand attention from the whole cabin row. That felt right to Annabeth. A decoration could make a sound without becoming a warning.

They stood shoulder to shoulder as a blue shard turned in the late sun.

"It works," Annabeth said.

"It sounds like the weather arguing with itself."

"That's not bad."

"No. It sounds like home."
Chiron came up the path as they were watching the blue shard settle. He paused at the foot of the porch, his gaze moving from the patched banner to the ornament above the door.

“May I take a look at the fastening?” he asked.

Annabeth glanced at Thalia. “We were going to ask you.”

“Then I arrived at a useful time.” He waited while they brought the ladder over. Annabeth showed him the hook and the lower catch, explaining how the strap bridged the split and why she had kept the glass chains separate from the wooden rays. She pointed out the note in her sketchbook about the shorter chain.

Chiron tested the peg with his hand, then gave the ornament a careful lift. It came free without twisting. He returned it to its place and checked that the lower catch had closed.

“Your mount is sound,” he said. “The hook is removable, and nothing sharp is exposed. After strong wind, take it down and inspect the strap before putting it back. That is sensible maintenance for any decoration that moves.”

“Would the catch hold if the peg shifted?” Annabeth asked.

“It would slow the turn. It is not a second mount.” He tapped the porch support, not the ornament. “If you want it to carry more weight, we would need a different attachment. As it is, keep the pieces light and let the wind move through them.”

Annabeth looked at the ring. “That was the plan.”

“I can see that.” Chiron turned the banner edge gently between his fingers, checking that the thread hadn't pulled the cloth tight. “The patch is neat. It doesn't hide the repair, but it lets the fabric move.”

“That's what we wanted,” Thalia said.

Chiron looked to her. “You both did well to wait for the right material instead of forcing the first piece to work.”

Thalia shrugged, but her hand rested on the toolbox handle. “Annabeth did the design.”

“And you both made it safely.” Chiron returned the ladder to the porch rail. “The remaining strip belongs back at the forge. If you take it there, they'll have it for another repair.”

“We will,” Annabeth said.

He stepped away from the entrance. “I will mark the cabin task as repaired. The next strong wind will tell you more than I can from standing here.”

“That's fair,” Annabeth said.

When Chiron had gone, she wrote the inspection note beside her measurements: removable hook checked; remove after strong wind; inspect strap before rehanging. The list was not a certificate that nothing could go wrong. It was a record of what they had done and what still deserved attention.

Thalia waited until Annabeth closed the sketchbook. “Ready to return the scrap?”

“Yeah.” Annabeth gathered the unused length of metal and the bent bracket they had replaced. “We'll take the main path again.”

“Because it’s wide enough for the package?”

“Because it’s wide enough, and because the other route is still cut up.”

Thalia took the bracket when Annabeth offered it and carried it at her side rather than under her arm. At the forge, the smith accepted the unused metal and put it back in the tray. Annabeth described the hook's final shape and the way it lifted free. The smith inspected the cut edge for burrs and nodded once.

“Good use of the offcut,” they said. “That should keep the next repair from being a guessing game.”

On the way back, Thalia carried the empty toolbox. Annabeth held her sketchbook against her ribs. Neither had to ask the other whether the errand counted as finished. The repair was up, the extra material had returned to the forge, and the cabin could use its doorway again.

Annabeth looked from the repaired banner to the new ornament. Neither erased the storm that had damaged them.

"Do you think the old one was supposed to make a sound?" she asked.

"I don't know. It never did when I was little."

"Maybe the bells were rusted."

"Maybe it was only decoration."

"Maybe decorations can change jobs."

Thalia turned toward her. "That's such an Annabeth way to put it."

"Is that good?"

"It's useful."

Annabeth leaned her shoulder against the porch post. "I want this to be a normal afternoon."

"It was. We did repairs. We had one argument. We only nearly stabbed the banner twice."

"That's not my definition of normal."

"Your definitions are strict."

"I had different childhoods. I only remember one."

"You had the one where your older sister packed a medical kit to sew a banner."

"And one where my friend died and came back as herself."

Thalia looked at the chime. "Yeah."

"Those are not the same childhood."

"No. But we can talk about both, even when only one of us remembers."

Annabeth thought of Thalia's fever story and her own memory of the hill. They hadn't tried to give each other memories; they'd let each other hear what was missing.

"Do you ever wish one of them was the only one?" Annabeth asked.

Thalia took a while to answer. "Sometimes I wish I could hear about that life without wondering where I fit in yours now. That isn't the same as wishing you wouldn't tell me."

"No."

"Do you?"

Annabeth watched a blue shard swing from its chain. "I wish I didn't have to explain why I'm different from the girl you raised. I also don't want to lose the girl you raised. And I don't want the friend I remember to become a story that doesn't belong to me."

"I don't want that either."

"But you still treat me like I'm going to break if you don't catch me."

Thalia's eyes lowered. "I know."

"Knowing doesn't make it stop."

"No." Thalia rubbed her thumb over a bit of dried paint on her palm. "It doesn't."

The honesty didn't solve the problem. It made it less lonely.

"I don't need you to stop worrying," Annabeth said. "I need you to ask me what I need before you decide."

"I can try."

"And I can tell you when you're doing it."

"You already do."

"I can do it without yelling."

"You weren't yelling."

"I know. I said it sharply."

"You were clear."

Thalia picked up the box of bent scraps. "We should put these away before the harpies decide the copper is shiny enough to steal."

"They already stole the hammer last week."

"That was a different hammer."

"It had your initials on it."

"There are many T.G.'s in camp."

"There is one Thalia Grace."

"You have a lot of faith in record keeping."

"I have a lot of faith in evidence."

They packed the tools together. When the breeze flipped Annabeth's sketchbook open, Thalia almost took it from her, then stopped and looked at her instead.

"Want me to hold that while you put the tin away?"

Annabeth passed it over. "Yes, thanks."

The small question made the afternoon feel different. Not fixed. Different. They swept the porch and collected the last scraps of glass. Annabeth slid her changed design into her notebook.

When the work was done, the cabin's entrance looked less battered. The banner lifted and fell along the post. The new ornament shifted under the eaves, its bells and glass pieces making a small, layered sound. Their shadows joined on the porch floor, long and slightly uneven.

"If we had a normal afternoon, we could sit around doing nothing," Thalia said.

"We can sit and listen to the decorations. That's technically something."

"You make doing nothing sound like homework."

"I make homework sound like doing something."

Thalia smiled. Then, after a moment, she said, "I'm sorry I tried to take over."

Annabeth's hands rested on her knees. "I know you were trying to help."

"That's not the same as saying I didn't do it."

"No."

"I'm sorry I made you feel like you had to prove you could do it," Thalia said. "You don't."

Annabeth studied the cracks in the porch stone. "I believe you. I might still get annoyed next time."

"That's fair."

"And you might do it again before you remember."

"Probably."

"That's hardly an inspiring promise."

"I can promise to listen when you tell me. I can promise not to argue that you didn't mean it."

Annabeth looked up. "That would help."

Thalia nodded, a small movement. "I don't want to be the person who makes you feel like a kid when you're trying to be my sister."

"I'm your kid sister."

"You're also Annabeth."

"Good. I was hoping you noticed."

"I notice everything." Thalia let the old boast dissolve into a grin. "That's my problem."

"One of them."

"You have a list?"

"I have a chart."

"I knew it."

Annabeth leaned against her sister's shoulder. Thalia waited, then leaned back. The contact was warm, solid without trapping her.

The chime moved above them. A little bell answered the wind, and a shard of blue glass turned slowly in place.

"That's nice," Annabeth said.

"It is."

"It isn't the way it was before."

"No."

"Do you miss it?"

Thalia watched the ornament. "I remember it. I don't think I miss the sound it didn't make."

Footsteps scuffed the path outside the porch. Percy came around the corner carrying a paper sack in one hand and a jar tucked under his other arm. He had a bit of grass in his hair and the wind had tugged his camp shirt crooked.

"I brought snacks," he announced. "There are crackers, apple slices, and something Silena called honey cakes. I was told not to ask what was in them until after trying one."

"That sounds ominous," Thalia said.

"It sounded encouraging when she said it." Percy set the sack on the porch rail. "Chiron said you two were still at work. I didn't want to interrupt."

"We finished," Annabeth said.

Percy looked over her shoulder at the ornament. The chime gave a soft, bright note. "That's new."

"It's a replacement," Annabeth said. "Not exactly the same as the old one."

"It sounds better."

She looked at him. "You don't know what the old one sounded like."

"I know what this one sounds like. That's enough."

Annabeth smiled. Percy slid the sack toward her and lingered only long enough for her to take a honey cake. Thalia took one too. He waved, then headed back along the path, leaving the snacks, a warm patch of afternoon, and a crumb on the porch step.

Annabeth brushed the crumb aside with the toe of her shoe. Above her, the new chime shifted in the breeze. Thalia reached for the paper sack, then stopped and looked at Annabeth.

"Want to choose first?" she asked.

Annabeth picked out an apple slice. "Yes."

Thalia took a honey cake. They sat side by side on the top step, listening to the cabin's new decoration find its own weather.
