## Chapter 119: Sisters' Errand

Storm-wrack still clung to Camp Half-Blood, as though the sky had tried to rearrange it and grown bored halfway through.

The dining pavilion's awning sagged, strawberry pulp streaked the Hermes cabin roof where a basket had landed, and the shield leaning against a pine by the lake had acquired a branch through its handle. Its owner had promised revenge on the tree.

Overhead, the sky looked clear and innocent. Annabeth didn't trust it for a second.

Cabin One had taken mostly decorative damage. The stone walls were sound, the roof was sound, and the porch columns had weathered the wind. The decorations were another matter. A blue-and-silver banner had ripped down one side. Three little bronze cloud-bells lay bent in the grass. The carved wooden sunburst over the door was missing its center, and its rays had twisted at angles that made it look less like a symbol of the sky and more like a surprised octopus.

Chiron had put the repair assignment on the board beside the dining pavilion. In his neatest hand he'd written *Cabin One: decorations, not structure*, with a small note beneath it: *Please do not turn this into a lightning experiment.*

Annabeth had read the note twice. The second time had been for the pleasure of imagining the exact expression on Chiron's face when he wrote it.

She found Thalia on the porch with the banner folded over one arm and a toolbox in the other hand. Thalia had already changed into work clothes: jeans with one torn knee, an old camp shirt, and boots whose laces had clearly lost an argument with her impatience. Her black hair lifted in the breeze, smelling faintly of rain and pine sap. The cloud cover had gone, but static still feathered around her sleeves, raising the fine hairs on her arms.

"I brought the toolbox," Thalia said.

"I can see that."

"Good. We're ahead of schedule."

Annabeth glanced at the toolbox. It was large enough to hold a small anvil, several hammers, and what looked like the remains of an absurdly ambitious mousetrap. "Are we repairing decorations or rebuilding the cabin from the foundation up?"

"Depends how the afternoon goes."

"Chiron specifically said decorations, not structure."

"Then we should be safe as long as nobody gets inspired." Thalia set the box down. Its contents clanged like a drawer full of angry cutlery. "I got the banner down before it could take someone's head off. The bells are over by the steps. We can straighten the sunburst, put it back, then decide what to do with the rest."

She draped the banner over the nearest porch post.

Annabeth rested her sketchbook against the porch rail. "I thought we might start by deciding what to do with the rest."

"Sure. After the sunburst." Thalia had already opened the toolbox and was sorting through it. "The center pin's probably bent. I can drill the old one out."

"Can I look at it first?" Annabeth asked.

"Of course." Thalia picked up the wooden sunburst. "Here."

She handed it over, though she kept hold of the lower edge, as if Annabeth might drop it or the wood might bolt for the woods. Salt air and old varnish clung to the panel, which had once been painted dark blue, with thin silver lines fanning from a bronze center. The paint was chipped. One ray had split at the tip, and the center disk was gone. Annabeth turned it over, checked the back, and ran a finger carefully over the broken edge.

"The pin isn't bent," she said. "The hole is. If we force a new one into it, the split will keep opening."

"We can clamp it."

"We could. Or we could repair the wood and make a new center that covers the old hole."

Thalia shifted the panel in her hands. "That sounds like more work."

"It is more work. It would also last longer."

"Okay." Thalia put the panel on the porch bench and found a small clamp in the box. "I'll—"

"Thalia."

Her sister stopped with one hand around the clamp. Outside, the camp had returned to its usual afternoon noise. Nothing required Thalia to move faster than Annabeth could follow.

"I want to do the repair," Annabeth said. "Not watch you do it."

Thalia's expression changed by only a fraction. Her mouth stayed level, but her hand tightened around the clamp. "I know you do."

"I don't think you do."

"Beth—"

"If I ask for a tool, I want you to hand it to me. If I make a decision about the design, I want you to let me try it before you decide there's a safer or faster version. I can do this."

Thalia set the clamp down. "I wasn't saying you couldn't."

"You were about to take over."

"I was about to help."

"Those aren't always the same thing."

The words hit harder than Annabeth intended. Sawdust stung her nose. Thalia leaned against the porch post, arms crossing, as if she'd reached for a familiar answer and found empty air.

"I know you care," Annabeth said. "I'm not asking you to stop. I'm asking you to trust me with the hammer."

Thalia looked at the toolbox. "The hammer is one of the least dangerous things in there."

"That's deeply reassuring."

"I'm working on it."

Annabeth drew a line in the dirt with the toe of her shoe, then erased it. "I don't want to spend the afternoon proving I can do basic things. I know you don't mean to make it feel that way. But it does."

Thalia's gaze came back to her. "Okay."

The word was quiet, not a surrender, not quite an agreement. Annabeth waited.

"I can try to ask before I grab something," Thalia said. "But if you're holding a drill wrong, I'm probably going to say something."

"You can say something. You can't take it out of my hands unless I'm actually about to lose a finger."

"What's the official finger-loss threshold?"

"Visible blood."

"What about near-visible blood?"

"No."

"A lot of splinters?"

"You can tell me to use gloves."

Thalia considered this as if Annabeth had proposed a treaty with a particularly devious god. "All right. You get the tools and the design decisions. I'll do the ladder, the carrying, and the parts that require two people."

"You can use tools too," Annabeth said. "Just don't take mine away while I'm using them."

"That seems fair."

Far from a perfect arrangement; the reservation in Thalia's voice remained. Still, Annabeth had helped make it. That mattered.

"First decision," Annabeth said. "We don't put the sunburst back as it was."

Thalia lifted one eyebrow. "That's a bold way to begin the repair."

"The old design has a wood center where wind pressure can split the rays. The storm didn't cause the weakness. It exposed what was already failing."

"So we're improving it."

"We're changing it. Improving is a judgment we can make after it survives another storm."

"You're going to make that the motto, aren't you?"

"I hadn't planned to. But now that you mention it, I could make a little plaque."

"Please don't. Chiron will give us a second assignment."

They carried the pieces into the patch of shade beside the porch, where an old worktable sat under an oak. Annabeth laid out what the storm had left them: the sunburst panel, the bronze center bracket, three bent bells, a length of chain, and fragments from the cracked blue glass medallion that had once hung beneath the eaves. She drew a rough plan in her sketchbook. They would splint the cracked ray from behind, then add a bronze ring around the missing center and suspend the bells and glass drops below it. Wind could move the pieces independently instead of pushing against the whole panel.

Thalia bent over the drawing. "You want to hang glass over the doorway?"

"Small pieces. Rounded edges. The largest one would be no bigger than my thumb."

"And if they fall?"

"They'll be on short chains. The chains are attached to a crossbar. If one breaks, it drops a few inches and gets caught."

"If the crossbar breaks?"

"Then the whole thing falls."

"Glad we covered that."

"The crossbar will be supported at both ends."

Thalia pressed a finger to the corner of the sketch. "What if we use the bronze bells for the crossbar?"

"They're hollow."

"Could fill them with—"

"No. I want the bells to move."

"I wasn't saying—"

Annabeth looked up.

Thalia's finger lifted from the paper. "Right. Sorry. Your design."

The apology was quick, almost too quick. She tucked her hands into the pockets of her jeans as if they had become suspicious objects. Annabeth felt some of the tightness in her chest ease. The effort was visible, which made it harder to dismiss.

"You can still give me ideas," she said. "Just let them be ideas before they become a plan."

"I can do that."

"You say that like you have to remind yourself."

"I do."

Annabeth looked back at the drawing. "Then we can both practice."

Thalia tapped the paper once, gently. "The bells will sound different if they're lower."

"That's useful."

"And you could put the glass pieces on the outside of the ring, so they won't hit the bells."

Annabeth sketched the idea. "That's also useful."

"I have occasional thoughts that aren't orders."

"I'll tell the camp."

"Don't. I have a reputation."

The bench wobbled on the uneven dirt, smelling of warm pine resin, so Annabeth agreed to work on the shaded ground. Thalia didn't say *I told you so*. Her careful silence made Annabeth smile. They sorted the salvage, set the blue glass aside to be smoothed, and brought over a small vise from the forge.

"Do you want me to hold the dowel?" Thalia asked.

Annabeth glanced at her. "Yes, please."

Thalia held it steady. Annabeth worked the bell back into shape with the flat side of a small hammer. The first strike was too light. The next two were even. On the fourth, the bell gave a clean metallic note and settled into a rounder curve.

"There," Annabeth said. "It'll hang straight now."

"It sounds good." Thalia let go of the dowel. "Not too loud."

"The glass will make a higher sound."

"You can tell that from looking at it?"

"I can tell the glass is going to sound like glass."

"Incredible. Cabin One's finest scholar."

"The distinction is important."

While Thalia straightened the copper, Annabeth smoothed each glass shard on a hand stone until none could snag a sleeve.

Annabeth marked holes in the copper ring and measured the chain. When a strip twisted too sharply, she set it aside. Thalia reached for it, checked herself, then held out her palm.

"Want me to see if the vise can flatten it?" she asked.

"Yes. But don't hammer it. The crease might split."

"I'll use the smooth jaw."

"Thanks."

The breeze swung the torn banner over the porch post. Its loose edge snapped against the wood, a small, persistent sound. Annabeth watched it for a few seconds before looking back down at her sketch.

"That banner can be saved," she said. "The tear's clean. We can reinforce it from behind."

"I have a needle and waxed thread in the box."

"Why do you have sewing supplies in the toolbox?"

"It isn't a toolbox. It's the emergency box."

"You brought an emergency box to repair decorations?"

"The storm knocked a tree over beside the creek. The box has rope, two shovels, a medical kit, and trail mix."

"You packed a rescue operation for banner repair."

"The assignment said storm damage. The note didn't mention the tree."

Annabeth laughed. "Your childhood was a series of worst-case scenarios, wasn't it?"

Thalia's face softened into a crooked smile. "You say that like yours wasn't."

"Mine had more diagrams."

"That's not the defense you think it is."

"My worst-case scenarios had labels."

"Mine had a bus schedule."

"A bus schedule is a kind of diagram."

"It is if you need to know which bus gets you across town before dark."

The joke thinned into something quieter. Annabeth knew Thalia's childhood in pieces: the older girl finding food and shelter, watching their mother's moods, and keeping a younger child moving. It came from Thalia's stories, not from the childhood Annabeth herself remembered. Knowing the facts hadn't given her the days.

"You were eight," Annabeth said. "You shouldn't have had to know bus schedules."

"Neither should you have had to know what happened when the sky got too quiet." Thalia folded the repaired copper strip into a gentler curve. "That was the weird thing about our childhood. We got good at stuff because it was necessary, then everyone acted like being good at it meant it hadn't been hard."

Annabeth watched the sunlight move across the broken glass. "Maybe we could have had a normal afternoon if someone had warned us."

"What would we have done?"

"Complained about normal problems. Like a school project."

"You'd have made a chart. I'd have borrowed the glue without permission."

"That's stealing with a better publicist."

"Your chart would have had too many colors."

"There is no such thing." Annabeth smiled. "What did you think a normal afternoon was when you were little?"

Thalia considered. "One where the most dangerous thing was using too much soap."

"That sounds nice."

"Soap is expensive."

Annabeth thought of the childhood Thalia described and the different one she remembered. Fear had shaped both; knowing that did not make the missing nights return.

"You told me about the fever," Annabeth said.

Thalia's hands stopped on the copper strip. "Which one?"

"The one before camp. You said we were under that awning with the hole in it, and the rain kept getting through. You'd put your jacket over me and then it got soaked too."

"You were maybe four. I was eight."

"You said you kept a wet cloth on my forehead. I can imagine the cold water. And you told me the thunder was a giant rolling furniture around upstairs."

Thalia looked toward the camp, not at her. "I was trying to make you laugh."

"Did I laugh? I hope I called the giant rude."

"You told him to stop stomping. You had a fever and were still trying to negotiate with the sky."

"That tracks."

"I was scared," Thalia said. "I didn't know how high your fever was. I didn't know where to take you. The motel clerk had told us not to come back, and the clinic was across town. I remember you trying to sit up and me telling you no, then you got mad at me."

"I didn't like being told what to do."

"You still don't."

"I was right about the fever."

"You were right about wanting water. You were not right about trying to walk to the clinic by yourself."

Thalia remembered the soaked jacket, the clinic, and the fear of having no adult to call. Annabeth knew the wet cloth and the ridiculous giant only through her telling. They could talk about the same night without pretending both of them remembered it.

Annabeth set the pencil down. "You shouldn't have had to know how to do all that."

"I know."

"I'm glad you did it."

Thalia glanced back at her, one corner of her mouth tightening. "Those can both be true."

"They can."

Thalia returned to the copper strip. "Your turn to tell me something from the other life."

Annabeth blinked. "Why?"

"Because you were about to ask me something. And I know the look you get when you're deciding whether to ask."

"I wasn't."

"You were."

Annabeth turned the pencil between her fingers. Thalia's death pressed on every other memory, but it wasn't the one she wanted to set between them beside a toolbox. There were other memories, less grand and still important.

"I remember you coming back," Annabeth said. "After the tree."

Thalia's shoulders went still. "What do you remember?"

"The fleece. The light on the hill. You stepping out of it and looking at all of us like you were trying to work out how much time had passed. I remember wanting to run to you, but I didn't. I thought you might not want a hug from someone who was still a little kid to you."

"I would have wanted one."

"I know that now. I didn't know it then."

Thalia's fingers rested against the vise handle. "I can picture wanting to catch up. Seeing you taller, looking like someone who'd made it through a lot without me. But I can't tell you what that Thalia thought. I wasn't there."

"I know. You never became a tree here."

"Still hard to imagine."

"It was hard watching you figure everything out."

"Did she try to catch up in one afternoon?"

"Pretty much," Annabeth said. "She didn't have to. I was glad she was there."

Thalia kept her attention on the vise. "I'm glad you got your friend back."

"Even if she wasn't my sister?"

"Yeah." Thalia turned the handle a fraction. "I remember raising my little sister. You remember growing up with a friend. Now you tell me those stories, and I keep wanting to answer for someone I never was. As if being your sister means I should know what to do about all of it."

"They tell me the same thing about you."

"Which is?"

"That you should know what I need before I say it."

Thalia let out a breath through her nose. "That sounds like a terrible instruction."

"It is."

"We should ignore it."

"We can try."

"Trying seems to be the theme of the afternoon."

"It's either that or the theme is decorative storm damage."

"That one has fewer feelings."

"And a worse title."

Annabeth fitted the copper strip into the ring and marked the crossbar for a second support. Thalia held pieces when asked and fetched the requested tools. When she passed over a rasp instead of a file, she corrected the mistake without comment.

As the sun moved past the roofline, they repaired the banner. Annabeth threaded the needle while Thalia held the torn edge flat. The stitch line wasn't perfectly straight, but the patch would hold.

When the banner was whole again, Thalia held it up to the light. "You can't see the patch."

"You can if you know where it is."

"That's true of most things."

"Maybe."

Thalia handed the banner to her. "Want to hang it?"

Annabeth smiled. "Yes." Thalia carried the ladder but let Annabeth set it and test the rungs.

"You can hold it," Annabeth said. "Just don't move it unless I ask."

"Understood."

Annabeth climbed two rungs, lined the stitched edge against the bracket, and fitted the ring over the peg. The breeze lifted the blue cloth with a soft snap, its silver lightning bolt crooked but bright.

"A little higher on the left," Thalia said.

Annabeth looked down. "Is it actually crooked?"

"A little."

"Do you want to tell me or fix it?"

Thalia's mouth opened, then closed. "Tell you."

Annabeth shifted the pole a finger-width. "Better?"

"Yes."

"Thanks."

Annabeth climbed down. Thalia didn't reach for the ladder until she'd stepped clear. The banner settled into place, its patched seam visible if someone knew where to look. It belonged there anyway.

"One thing fixed," Annabeth said.

"One thing repaired."

"What?"

"You said improving was something we had to wait and see about."

Annabeth nodded. "I did."

"I'm learning the rules."

"There are a lot."

"You should make a chart."

"I could color-code it."

"No."

They brought the half-finished ornament to the porch and checked the hanging balance. The bronze ring now sat in the sunburst's open center, its cracked ray splinted from behind. Copper arcs hung at different heights, each supporting one bell or a small piece of blue glass. It looked a little like a sunburst and a little like a rain cloud caught in the act of becoming something else. Annabeth liked that it didn't decide between the two.

"The center needs a final pin," she said. "And the lower chains need to be checked for swing."

Thalia nodded. "I'll get the ladder."

"I'll get the pin."

They reached for the toolbox at the same time. Thalia withdrew her hand.

"You can open it," Annabeth said. "I need the short punch and the bronze pin."

Thalia crouched and selected both. "This one?"

"Yes."

She passed the tools over, handle-first. Annabeth set the punch against the center bracket, adjusted the angle, and tapped the pin into place. The first hit glanced off; the second went true.

"Good," Thalia said.

Annabeth ran a finger around the edge. "It isn't perfect."

"It doesn't have to be."

"The center sits a little off."

"From here, it looks like it sits exactly where it wants to."

Annabeth looked up at her. "That's an extremely suspicious compliment."

"It's a compliment from someone who once put a crooked pinecone on a shelf because it had character."

"Was that one of the pinecones Zeus gave you?"

"No. That one came from a tree."

"A normal tree?"

"As far as I know."

"A disappointing backstory."

Thalia held the ladder steady while Annabeth fixed the repaired sunburst above the doorway. The fasteners held. The bells chimed low and gentle; the glass answered with a thinner note. It sounded like rain beginning somewhere far off.

They stood shoulder to shoulder as a blue shard turned in the late sun.

"It works," Annabeth said.

"It sounds like the weather arguing with itself."

"That's not bad."

"No. It sounds like home."

Annabeth looked from the repaired banner to the new ornament. Neither erased the storm that had damaged them.

"Do you think the old one was supposed to make a sound?" she asked.

"I don't know. It never did when I was little."

"Maybe the bells were rusted."

"Maybe it was only decoration."

"Maybe decorations can change jobs."

Thalia turned toward her. "That's such an Annabeth way to put it."

"Is that good?"

"It's useful."

Annabeth leaned her shoulder against the porch post. "I want this to be a normal afternoon."

"It was. We did repairs. We had one argument. We only nearly stabbed the banner twice."

"That's not my definition of normal."

"Your definitions are strict."

"I had different childhoods. I only remember one."

"You had the one where your older sister packed a medical kit to sew a banner."

"And one where my friend died and came back as herself."

Thalia looked at the chime. "Yeah."

"Those are not the same childhood."

"No. But we can talk about both, even when only one of us remembers."

Annabeth thought of Thalia's fever story and her own memory of the hill. They hadn't tried to give each other memories; they'd let each other hear what was missing.

"Do you ever wish one of them was the only one?" Annabeth asked.

Thalia took a while to answer. "Sometimes I wish I could hear about that life without wondering where I fit in yours now. That isn't the same as wishing you wouldn't tell me."

"No."

"Do you?"

Annabeth watched a blue shard swing from its chain. "I wish I didn't have to explain why I'm different from the girl you raised. I also don't want to lose the girl you raised. And I don't want the friend I remember to become a story that doesn't belong to me."

"I don't want that either."

"But you still treat me like I'm going to break if you don't catch me."

Thalia's eyes lowered. "I know."

"Knowing doesn't make it stop."

"No." Thalia rubbed her thumb over a bit of dried paint on her palm. "It doesn't."

The honesty didn't solve the problem. It made it less lonely.

"I don't need you to stop worrying," Annabeth said. "I need you to ask me what I need before you decide."

"I can try."

"And I can tell you when you're doing it."

"You already do."

"I can do it without yelling."

"You weren't yelling."

"I know. I said it sharply."

"You were clear."

Thalia picked up the box of bent scraps. "We should put these away before the harpies decide the copper is shiny enough to steal."

"They already stole the hammer last week."

"That was a different hammer."

"It had your initials on it."

"There are many T.G.'s in camp."

"There is one Thalia Grace."

"You have a lot of faith in record keeping."

"I have a lot of faith in evidence."

They packed the tools together. When the breeze flipped Annabeth's sketchbook open, Thalia almost took it from her, then stopped and looked at her instead.

"Want me to hold that while you put the tin away?"

Annabeth passed it over. "Yes, thanks."

The small question made the afternoon feel different. Not fixed. Different. They swept the porch and collected the last scraps of glass. Annabeth slid her changed design into her notebook.

When the work was done, the cabin's entrance looked less battered. The banner lifted and fell along the post. The new ornament shifted under the eaves, its bells and glass pieces making a small, layered sound. Their shadows joined on the porch floor, long and slightly uneven.

"If we had a normal afternoon, we could sit around doing nothing," Thalia said.

"We can sit and listen to the decorations. That's technically something."

"You make doing nothing sound like homework."

"I make homework sound like doing something."

Thalia smiled. Then, after a moment, she said, "I'm sorry I tried to take over."

Annabeth's hands rested on her knees. "I know you were trying to help."

"That's not the same as saying I didn't do it."

"No."

"I'm sorry I made you feel like you had to prove you could do it," Thalia said. "You don't."

Annabeth studied the cracks in the porch stone. "I believe you. I might still get annoyed next time."

"That's fair."

"And you might do it again before you remember."

"Probably."

"That's hardly an inspiring promise."

"I can promise to listen when you tell me. I can promise not to argue that you didn't mean it."

Annabeth looked up. "That would help."

Thalia nodded, a small movement. "I don't want to be the person who makes you feel like a kid when you're trying to be my sister."

"I'm your kid sister."

"You're also Annabeth."

"Good. I was hoping you noticed."

"I notice everything." Thalia let the old boast dissolve into a grin. "That's my problem."

"One of them."

"You have a list?"

"I have a chart."

"I knew it."

Annabeth leaned against her sister's shoulder. Thalia waited, then leaned back. The contact was warm, solid without trapping her.

The chime moved above them. A little bell answered the wind, and a shard of blue glass turned slowly in place.

"That's nice," Annabeth said.

"It is."

"It isn't the way it was before."

"No."

"Do you miss it?"

Thalia watched the ornament. "I remember it. I don't think I miss the sound it didn't make."

Footsteps scuffed the path outside the porch. Percy came around the corner carrying a paper sack in one hand and a jar tucked under his other arm. He had a bit of grass in his hair and the wind had tugged his camp shirt crooked.

"I brought snacks," he announced. "There are crackers, apple slices, and something Silena called honey cakes. I was told not to ask what was in them until after trying one."

"That sounds ominous," Thalia said.

"It sounded encouraging when she said it." Percy set the sack on the porch rail. "Chiron said you two were still at work. I didn't want to interrupt."

"We finished," Annabeth said.

Percy looked over her shoulder at the ornament. The chime gave a soft, bright note. "That's new."

"It's a replacement," Annabeth said. "Not exactly the same as the old one."

"It sounds better."

She looked at him. "You don't know what the old one sounded like."

"I know what this one sounds like. That's enough."

Annabeth smiled. Percy slid the sack toward her and lingered only long enough for her to take a honey cake. Thalia took one too. He waved, then headed back along the path, leaving the snacks, a warm patch of afternoon, and a crumb on the porch step.

Annabeth brushed the crumb aside with the toe of her shoe. Above her, the new chime shifted in the breeze. Thalia reached for the paper sack, then stopped and looked at Annabeth.

"Want to choose first?" she asked.

Annabeth picked out an apple slice. "Yes."

Thalia took a honey cake. They sat side by side on the top step, listening to the cabin's new decoration find its own weather.
