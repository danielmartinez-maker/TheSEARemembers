## Chapter 132: A Month Beneath the Sea

The shell had rested on Percy's desk for three days, catching morning light through the cabin window. Across its pale spiral ran a thread of blue that brightened whenever the tide turned, pulsing faintly as though keeping time with distant surf. Poseidon had asked whether Percy might consider a month in Atlantis, training with him and Triton. No deadline. No command. Only an invitation, offered in a voice that had sounded careful.

Morning and night, Percy had listened to it once each, then again yesterday while Riptide waited half-polished on his bunk. Brine still clung to the shell's curve when he lifted it, and the faint hum of distant surf rose from its mouth before fading. He had not answered. He had not told Annabeth.

A month beneath the sea could teach him the currents that moved through Atlantis without need of a map. He could learn to fight in water deep enough that every stroke carried weight, where pressure rang in the ears and light thinned to blue-green shadow. Triton had told him plainly that he moved the sea on instinct, strong but undisciplined, burning through strength where control would serve him better. Beneath the critique lay another offer: daily meals, shared work, the slow accumulation of belonging between lessons.

Percy wanted that.

Nor did wanting it make leaving any easier.

Pocketing the shell, with Riptide at his hip, he left the cabin into cool morning air. Dew silvered the grass and the pine scent hung low off the hill, mingled with woodsmoke from the breakfast fires and the clean mineral breath of the lake below. His shoes darkened where the grass ran damp against the path. Down by the lake, Annabeth usually worked before breakfast, pencil moving against the murmur of water. Something in his chest already knew she would be there today, sure as tide-pull. The certainty proved correct.

She sat on the low stone wall beside the water, notebook open across her knees, hair drawn back with a bronze clip. A folded map lay beside her, its edges lifting in the breeze, corners softened from frequent handling. Pencil shavings dusted the stone near her shoe, and lake-light freckled her forearms. At his approach she glanced up and smiled, attention dropping at once to the shell in his hand.

"That came from Atlantis?" she asked.

"A few days ago."

Her pencil stilled. "A few days ago?"

Settling onto the wall beside her, he left a hand's width between them. Salt and lake-water clung to the stone, and a damselfly rested a moment on the map's edge before lifting away. "It's from my dad. He asked if I want to come back for a month. Train with him and Triton."

"A month."

"Yeah."

Along one crease she folded the map with deliberate care. "And you haven't answered."

"No."

"Or told me."

Gentle as her tone stayed, its levelness made evasion impossible. Percy set the shell on the stone between them. Its blue thread caught the sun and held it.

"I wanted to understand what I thought before I brought it to you," he said.

Annabeth held his gaze. "You can tell me before you've sorted out what you think."

"I know."

"Do you?"

No adequate answer came. Only the obvious one, and the obvious one fell short. "I wasn't hiding it."

"I didn't say you were." One fingertip brushed the shell, then withdrew. "I'm glad they asked you. I wish you had told me when the message arrived."

"I'm sorry."

She nodded, though the quiet afterward told him the apology had not closed the distance.

Across the lake, sunlight laid a thin bright path toward the far shore, breaking where minnows dimpled the surface and reeds nodded along the shallows. A dragonfly stitched the air above the wall, wings catching light. "I think I want to go," he said.

Beneath her hand the folded map creased. "For the month?"

"I don't know yet."

"But you lean toward yes."

"Yes."

Lowering her eyes to the map, she said, "Okay."

Measured, not angry. He had braced for anger. Without it, the knot in his chest had nowhere to rest.

"I want to get better in deep water. I can shove a current around, but once I let go I don't always grasp what it does next. Triton says I could learn to accomplish more without emptying myself in the first minute," he said.

"That sounds useful."

"It is."

"And you want to see your family."

Turning the shell over in his fingers, its ridges cool against his skin, he said, "Yeah. I do."

Annabeth pressed her lips together. "I'm glad you want to go. I'll miss you, too. A month is long, Percy."

"I know."

"You keep saying that, though it lands as though the month were a footnote."

His voice edged sharper than he intended. "I'm not treating it that way. I'm trying to explain why this matters to me."

"I understand why it matters. I'm asking you to allow that a month away matters, too." She shut the notebook, the slap of the cover soft in the morning air. "I'm not asking you to stay. I'm asking you not to reason me out of missing you merely because the training has value."

The ready replies crowded his throat and died there: she had Thalia and camp, he could call daily. Comfort for an hour, weight for the rest of the month.

"I don't want you feeling abandoned," he said instead.

"I might feel that way regardless."

Stated without accusation, the truth struck harder. Drawing his knees up, heels scraping the stone, he said, "I thought if I waited until I understood what I wanted, I could spare you worrying over something that might never happen."

"It didn't spare me."

"I see that now."

"It left you worrying alone."

"I believe you," she said. "You don't need to shield me from the unfinished parts. I'm already here."

He nodded. Between them the shell had gone quiet. "I'm sorry I waited."

Tucking the map beneath her notebook, its paper whispering, she said, "Thank you."

"If you came for the month —" He paused. "What would you want from me?"

"What do you mean?"

"Would you want to visit, or write, or prefer I leave you room to focus? I can't guess what you'd need."

"I'd want to talk with you. How often, I don't know yet."

"I don't want you coming because you feel obligated."

"I wouldn't come for the whole month, I don't think. A visit, though — yes. I want to watch the garden shift and ask Triton why his star map shows three norths."

"They're different kinds of north."

"I told him that wasn't an answer."

Her mouth lifted at one corner, then settled. "I also want to remain here. I like this place. You know that."

"I do."

"If you go, I don't want to choose between supporting you and admitting it will hurt."

"You shouldn't have to choose."

"Neither should you."

"What do you mean?" he asked.

"Don't choose between wanting your family and wanting me. I refuse to turn my worry into a test you can fail."

"I don't want to pick one of you over the other," he said.

"You don't have to."

"If I go, you might feel left. If I stay from fear of that, I might resent it — resent you — even though you never asked me to stay."

"I want neither outcome."

"We should pause for a little while," she said.

His stomach tightened. "Because you're mad?"

"Because we're answering what we fear the other person means, rather than what's actually said." Standing, she tucked the notebook under her arm; lake-light moved across her face. "I need to consider what I truly want to ask you."

"Okay."

"And you need to consider what you want, apart from what keeps me from hurting."

"I can do that."

A small, tired smile touched her. "I know."

Rising too, he wanted to touch her hand and checked himself, uncertain whether the gesture would comfort her or ask her to steady him. He stayed where he was.

"I'll see you later?" he asked.

"Yes. Later."

She took the path back toward the cabins, shoes brushing damp grass. Percy watched until the trail bent behind the Athena cabin, then pocketed the shell. Its weight seemed greater than before.

---

Beyond the canoe dock, Clarisse and Silena had claimed a narrow strip of beach. Cross-legged in the sand, Silena balanced the family photograph from Atlantis on her knees, shielding the gloss from blowing grit with the edge of her sleeve. Clarisse worked a whetstone along a practice spear that showed no visible need for sharpening, steel whispering with each pass. Both looked up as Percy came down the path, pebbles shifting under his shoes.

"If you've come to report the current changed again, spare me," Clarisse said.

"The lake doesn't have a current."

"Then no problem exists."

Sliding the photograph back into its sleeve to shield it from grit, Silena said, "You carry a problem regardless."

"Trying to decide about a month in Atlantis."

Clarisse drew the stone down the blade's length. "Training?"

"Yeah. Dad and Triton offered."

"You want to go?"

"I think so."

"That's one part." She turned the spear to inspect the edge. "What's the other?"

"Annabeth."

Clarisse glanced up and waited, allowing the silence to stand. Percy settled onto a driftwood log a few feet from Silena, its sun-warmed bark rough through his shirt.

"She's worried I'll be gone," he said. "I'm worried she'll feel abandoned. No shouting. We came close enough, though."

"A disagreement isn't a disaster," Silena said.

"I know."

"You both deliver that line as though someone died," Clarisse said with a grunt.

"I didn't tell her about the invitation for three days."

"Bad call," Clarisse said flatly. "You know better next time."

"I thought figuring it out first could spare her worrying over a maybe."

"Did that strategy succeed?" Silena asked.

"No."

Rubbing his palms across his knees, sand rasping faintly, Percy said, "I don't want her forced between being supportive and being honest. I also don't want to stay behind merely because I'm afraid she'll miss me."

"Both halves sound reasonable," Silena said.

"And both can hold true at once," Clarisse added. "Wanting to go doesn't equal wanting to leave her. Her sadness doesn't equal asking you to stop. You have to decide whether the training merits the cost."

"You think I should go?" he asked.

"No."

Immediate. Clarisse lifted her chin. "I think you should quit asking me to decide for you."

"You want a clean answer because sitting inside a messy one feels worse," Clarisse said.

Turning toward him, Silena said, "Our invitation doesn't obligate you to give Atlantis a month. We love having you as our brother. We'll rejoice if you return, and we'll understand if the timing proves wrong."

"I want to see you both," he said.

"Then say exactly that. Don't disguise it as training if what you hunger for is a month with us."

"I do want the training," Percy said. "And ordinary time with you, Dad, and Mother. It arrives tangled together."

"Then let it stay tangled," Silena said. "No need to sort it into tidy boxes before you can comprehend it."

His gaze dropped to the photograph: Clarisse squinting at the camera as though prepared to argue, Silena leaning against her, sunlight catching the sea-polished beads at her wrist, the palace bright behind them. One frame contained all their worlds without erasing the differences.

"Was being there hard?" Percy asked.

Resting the spear against the log, Clarisse said, "Sometimes. I wanted to see where you grew up, wanted to know our father somewhere he wasn't already leaving. I also wondered what my life might have held had he been present. Wanting the visit didn't dissolve that."

"Amphitrite made being there easier," Silena said. "That helped, though it didn't rewrite Clarisse's past."

"I'm sorry," Percy said.

"Not your task to make it stop aching," Clarisse replied. "Quit trying to make everyone comfortable before you ask what they need."

Silena laughed lightly. "We can want you in Atlantis without demanding you come. You can want to come without pledging never to be busy elsewhere. The remainder belongs to you and Annabeth, not to us."

"I thought you might feel hurt if I didn't go."

"I'd miss you. Missing you differs from holding it against you," Silena said.

Lifting the spear, Clarisse said, "If you go, don't make us the reason Annabeth must perform being fine. Talk with her. And don't use her as your excuse to stay away."

"I won't."

Leaning her shoulder against Clarisse's, Silena said, "Ask for more information. A month need not mean thirty unbroken days, or it might. You lack the facts yet."

"An invitation isn't a contract," Clarisse said. "Ask what training involves. Ask whether a shorter version exists. Then decide whether you desire that, not what looks most sensible on paper."

"Talk with Annabeth before you answer," Clarisse said. "Not for her approval. Because she shares your life."

Blunt, not unkind. Percy studied the shell in his palm, its edge catching morning light.

"Thanks."

"Don't thank us for telling you to be honest," Clarisse said, swinging the spear onto her shoulder. Then she tossed it toward him. "Show me whether you can move this."

Sand whispering underfoot, Silena whisked the photograph clear before they took positions, tucking the sleeve tight against blowing grit. Clarisse closed fast. Percy blocked, pivoted, redirected her third strike so momentum carried her past his shoulder.

She turned, eyes bright. "Better."

"That sounded like praise."

"Don't grow accustomed." They traded a few more passes, corrections flying while Silena named the faults neither wanted to hear. The shell no longer weighed like a stone in his pocket. The question remained, but it had edges now.

---

Thalia was at the archery range when Percy found her. Bow resting on the rail, she repaired a strap on her bracer with a small leather punch. Downrange, a target bore a neat cluster near its center, straw whispering where the latest shafts had bitten. The resin smell of pine and oiled leather hung in the warm air. Without looking up as he stopped beside her, pine sap and leather sharp in the air, she waited.

"Annabeth told you," he said.

"She told me you received an offer. She didn't ask me to talk you out of anything."

"Didn't figure she would."

Thalia bit off the loose thread-end and tested the strap. "Good. I won't."

"Clarisse and Silena said the same."

"They're smart."

"They didn't phrase it that way."

"Unnecessary."

Setting the bracer aside, she freed one arrow from the target. The shaft released with a dry click. "You and Annabeth nearly argued?"

"We did argue. A little."

"That happens."

"I know."

"You possess considerable knowledge about things that still surprise you."

Her dryness drew a reluctant breath from him. "Noticing that pattern myself."

Twirling the arrow between her fingers, Thalia said, "What do you want from me?"

Direct enough to halt him. "I guess I wanted your take."

"On whether you should go?"

"Yeah."

"Annabeth will miss you. You'll miss her. You want to learn from your father and spend time with your family. I see no contradiction."

"The contradiction is what I feel."

"Feelings rarely arrange themselves around facts."

She slid the arrow home into the quiver and stepped back, boots crunching on fallen straw and feather clippings. "I dislike the thought of her sad. I also refuse to wield that as a reason to dictate where you go. I raised her. That doesn't appoint me her voice, nor yours."

"I know."

"Do you?"

"That question keeps surfacing today."

"Perhaps record the answer once you find it."

He leaned against the rail, wood warm beneath his forearms. "She said she doesn't want to become my reason for staying."

"She wouldn't be."

"I'm afraid I'd stay regardless, then resent her."

"Then don't stay without naming why."

"And if I go and she feels abandoned?"

Thalia lowered her eyes to the bow. "Then listen when she says so. Don't argue her out of the feeling or remind her she has a sister and therefore cannot be lonely. And don't pledge to call every hour, then feel trapped once training begins."

"I wasn't planning hourly calls."

"You send 'I'm fine' and omit mention of being thrown into a wall."

"That happened once."

"Twice."

"The second was a post. Different."

"Weak defense."

He let the teasing pass, watching two younger campers struggle to fit a shield over one another's heads. "You think she'll be okay?"

"Wrong question."

"I don't want her forcing herself to be okay with it because it benefits me," he said.

"She doesn't have to."

"Then how do I handle this?"

"You ask what she needs. You state what you want. Together you decide the next step, not whether one person may have a feeling." Thalia slung the bow over her shoulder. "And quit using me as an oracle. I don't know Annabeth's needs better than she does. You carry a shell. Perhaps consult it."

"The shell offers no advice," Percy said.

"Then it surpasses most oracles," Thalia replied.

"She was the first person I wanted to tell," Percy said. "I waited three days."

"Don't convert your apology into leverage for permission to go."

"I won't."

Starting toward the path, she halted. "Don't stay because you fear I'll make Annabeth miserable if you leave. I maintain enough hobbies."

"Like menacing the target?"

"It knows its offenses." She bumped his shoulder passing. "Talk with her when you're prepared to listen."

The mended bracer still lay on the rail. Percy picked it up and followed.

---

Percy went to Chiron after speaking with Thalia, carrying the shell and an empty page from Annabeth's notebook. He didn't know what answer he'd give his father, but he had finally decided that guessing about a month's absence was not the same as planning one.

Chiron was at his desk sorting the next training rotation. Outside the open window the camp moved through its afternoon chores, hammering from the armory and the distant clatter of dining tables carrying through the heat.

"A month in Atlantis," Chiron repeated. "Your father did not propose dates?"

"No. He said I could choose when."

"That is generous in one respect and incomplete in several others."

Percy sat. "I was afraid you'd just say no."

"I might," Chiron said. "But first I would want to know what exactly you would be doing, who would supervise you, and how your return would be arranged."

"Triton would teach the deep-current training."

"Every day?"

"I don't know."

"Would your father be present?"

"He said he wanted to train with me."

"That does not answer the question."

Percy looked down at the shell. He had imagined daily lessons with both his father and his brother. But Poseidon had never promised a calendar; Percy had supplied the rest because he wanted it to be true.

Chiron drew a simple table on the empty page: dates, training objectives, supervision, communication, return arrangements, and obligations at camp. He left every column blank.

"You and Annabeth have commitments here," he said. "Your cabin has duties. You are still a child under camp's protection. The prospect of training with a god doesn't cancel those facts."

Percy examined the blank table. "So if I get the answers, would you let me go?"

"We could discuss whether a full month makes sense. We might agree to a shorter trial. I won't promise before I know the risks."

A shorter visit hadn't occurred to him. He had treated a month like a package he could either accept or reject, even though his father hadn't demanded that.

"I could ask for two weeks," he said.

"You could ask for five days. The duration should follow the work, not the other way around."

Percy laughed faintly. "Triton will think I'm negotiating a contract."

"Then he'll be pleased you learned something from his etiquette lessons."

They wrote a request together. Percy asked for a proposed training schedule, the names of the people who would actually teach, a defined return date, weekly contact with camp, and a route out if he wanted to end the visit early. He also asked whether any of the lessons required exposure to pressure or currents beyond the protected palace channels.

Chiron read the draft and pointed to the line about Poseidon.

"You should say plainly that you would like time with your father. Don't make it sound as though you only want technical training."

Percy hesitated. "What if he agrees to the technical part and not that?"

"Then you will have learned something about the offer before you've committed yourself."

That stung. It was also the answer he needed.

He added: *I'd like to know when you can actually be there, not just when the palace will be open to me.* It was difficult to write. He'd become used to treating his father's attention as something to receive gratefully whenever it appeared. Asking for a schedule felt demanding, though it was a perfectly ordinary thing to want from a parent.

Chiron didn't praise him for the sentence. He simply waited until Percy finished.

"What about Annabeth?" Percy asked.

"She should not have to approve your training."

"I know that."

"But if you decide to go, she deserves accurate information about how long you'll be away and when you can speak. She isn't responsible for managing your father's schedule, and you aren't responsible for preventing her from missing you."

Percy thought of the conversation by the lake that morning. Annabeth had said almost exactly that, though he'd been too frightened of losing her approval to hear it at first.

"I'll talk to her again tonight," he said.

"Good."

He took the letter to the saltwater shore rather than try to send it through the canoe lake. There he placed the shell in a shallow pool beyond the breaking waves and read the question aloud. The blue thread brightened. For a moment a warm current touched his hands, and the shell lifted just enough to show the message had been accepted.

There was no immediate reply. Percy was grateful. He didn't want to make a decision before he had the answers, and he didn't want to pretend his father had supplied them.

On his way back he found Silena in the cabin, repairing a strap on one of his bags. She looked at the new training table and raised her eyebrows.

"You're becoming organized."

"Against my will."

"Did you tell Annabeth?"

"Yes. It didn't go perfectly."

"That doesn't mean it was wrong to tell her."

"I waited three days."

"Then next time tell her on the first day. You can still change your mind afterward."

Percy folded the letter copy and put it on his desk. "Chiron suggested a shorter visit."

"Would you want one?"

"Maybe. I don't know if a month is what I need or just what I was offered."

Silena gave the repaired strap a final tug. "Then that's what you should figure out."

He listened to the sound of the cabin fountain and looked at the still-blank calendar. The fact that he wanted to go had not changed. He was beginning to distinguish what he wanted from what he assumed he had to accept in order to receive it.


---

That evening, he found Annabeth on the dock.

Honey-colored light lay across the lake, gilding each ripple and warming the dock planks beneath their weight. Swallows stitched the air overhead, and the far treeline stood dark against the brightness. Boots hooked on the lower rung, one hand resting on the morning's map, its margins now threaded with fresh notes, she watched the water. Percy halted several steps behind, planks warm beneath his shoes.

"Can I sit?"

"Yes."

Beside her he settled. Boards creaked under his weight, warm from the afternoon sun and thrumming faintly where small waves struck the pilings. For a while they watched gold gather along the lake's edges, swallows stitching the air above.

"I've been thinking," he said.

"Me too."

"I'm sorry I held the message apart from you. It mattered enough to occupy my thoughts, and you deserved to know."

"Thank you. I don't require every half-formed thought, but when something starts to matter, let me in before you've decided."

"I wasn't trying to decide without you."

"I know. It felt as though you waited to tell me until you'd chosen. I want your thoughts, not your request for permission."

"That is what I want, too."

"A month stretches long. I would miss you, and hard days would come. Don't interpret those as proof you did wrong, but don't assume I'll be fine and depart without shaping a plan together."

"I wouldn't vanish."

"I know. I don't want to become something you managed in advance."

He let the impulse to defend himself pass like a wave under the dock. "Understood. I want you to hear what I want, not a version edited for your comfort."

"I want to train there. I want to handle water with more skill. And I want ordinary hours with my family. I want to remain long enough that every meal stops feeling like a visit. I want to discover whether I can belong there, not only arrive when invited."

Never before had he phrased it thus. Spoken aloud, the want grew larger and less like a simple choice. Annabeth listened without interruption, breeze stirring the loose strands at her temples.

"I don't know whether a month is the right shape for that," he added. "I don't know how much training I need, or whether I'll excel. But I want to learn."

Annabeth nodded. "That sounds like you."

"I'm afraid going will feel to you like choosing them over you."

"I won't believe that. I might feel lonely, or furious at the entire ocean for taking you. Loneliness differs from doubting your love."

Quiet as the admission came, Percy recognized its cost, offered without a demand folded inside.

"And if I don't go, I fear letting fear decide — telling myself I stayed for you when you never asked."

"I don't want that either."

He exhaled. "I'm sorry you discovered it by spotting the shell."

"And I'm sorry I made my fear sound like a verdict. Hearing you wanted to go, I assumed you'd placed me on the far side of the decision."

"I was omitting parts."

"You don't need to apologize for missing me. Don't make me guess what you want."

He turned his hand palm-up on the dock between them, going no farther. Annabeth regarded it, then laid her hand in his, calluses familiar against his skin.

"Thank you," he said.

"You're welcome."

They watched the darkening lake. Her fingers held warmth against his. Neither squeezed as though a grip could guarantee anything. Holding on sufficed.

"We need not decide tonight. Ask what the month entails: how much training, whether rest days exist, whether a shorter stay is possible. Not because I'm maneuvering to shorten it. You should comprehend what you'd accept."

"And if only a full month works?"

"Then we discuss that."

"And if you still prefer I not go?"

Down at their joined hands she looked. "I won't pretend I'll desire it. I want you near, but that cannot outweigh everything else."

"You matter. The training matters. I refuse to rank them."

"Good."

He threaded his fingers between hers. "I thought I could protect you from fear. Mostly I was shielding myself from hearing yours."

"Understandable. Still unfair."

"I'm sorry."

"I forgive you."

The words neither erased the invitation nor answered it. They widened the air between them.

"I don't want you shrinking your world to prove you love me," she said.

"I don't want you shrinking yours either."

"Good. Then we should act as though we mean it."

He smiled. "How?"

"No answering invitations for each other. We tell each other when something matters, even unfinished. No unilateral decisions about things that reshape both our lives."

"I'll talk with you before I accept. You'll tell me honestly how you feel without reducing it to yes or no. And we'll listen."

"That's the plan."

"Solid one."

"Chiron will ask us to write it down."

"You can draw the map."

"A map of the plan?"

"You love maps."

"Not that much."

Laughter loosened the tightness in the evening. Annabeth rested her head against his shoulder, hair smelling faintly of cedar and sun, the dock boards radiating the day's stored warmth beneath them. He tipped his cheek lightly against it. Neither declared the month fine. Neither called it impossible.

After a while, Percy drew the shell from his pocket. "May I answer him now? Only to say we want more detail, and we'd like to visit together for a few days before any decision?"

"That suits me, provided 'together' means you invite me because you desire my company, not because you need me to inspect the offer."

"I desire your company. I also value your inspection."

"Accurate."

"You can decide whether to come. No pressure."

"I'd like to. A short visit. Not agreement to the month. I want to see the palace again, and hear about the training from those who'd conduct it."

"That matches what I want."

She lifted her head. "Then tell them."

Cradling the shell between his hands, Percy pressed the small ridge near its opening. Blue light traced the spiral. Moments later Poseidon's voice arrived, softened by the distance between palace and lake.

"Perseus. I hope you have had time to consider the invitation. There is no hurry. Triton has set aside a place in the training hall if you wish to use it. We could begin with a month and adjust the schedule as we learn what you need."

A faint rush of water closed the message.

Percy looked at Annabeth. She nodded once, granting him room to answer in his own words.

"Father," he said to the shell, "thank you. I want to learn more about the training and what a month would entail. I haven't decided. Annabeth and I would like to visit for a few days before I decide, if that suits everyone. I want to see you and Mother and Triton, and to talk with Clarisse and Silena about the training. I'll write again once we've talked."

He paused and glanced at Annabeth. "Did I omit anything?"

"No. You might add that you're not asking them to reserve the place yet."

"Right." He turned back to the shell. "Please don't hold the training place for me. I don't want arrangements made around a decision I haven't reached."

The shell's light dimmed. The message departed with a soft chime.

Annabeth watched it in his hands. "That felt different."

"Because I didn't need to resolve everything before speaking."

"Exactly."

"I should have told you sooner."

"You have now."

"I'll strive to do better next time."

"I know."

"That one I can demonstrate."

"You can begin by telling me when the shell answers."

"I will."

She shifted closer. Percy turned toward her slowly enough that she could change her mind. She didn't. Their kiss stayed soft and familiar, without urgency. It lingered a moment, then another. Parting, Annabeth's forehead rested against his.

"I'm sorry I made you guess what I wanted," he said.

"I'm sorry I made wanting to go feel as though it would wound me beyond repair."

"It won't."

"I know. It will ache sometimes. Different thing."

"I'll remember."

"And should I forget — should you forget — I'll remind you."

"Deal."

She kissed him again, brief answer to his answer. His hand remained in hers upon the old boards while dusk spread over the lake, cicadas tuning up along the shore.

---

Before dinner Percy took his proposed calendar to Chiron.

There were thirty boxes on the page. He had shaded five of them for lessons he would miss at camp, and another four for the nights he wanted to speak to Annabeth if she had time. They looked harmless until Chiron spread the actual training roster beside them.

"You've included the autumn safety review," Chiron said.

"I can make it up."

"It isn't an examination. You're assigned to help younger campers prepare the boats. Somebody will need to cover your station."

Percy looked down at the roster. He'd imagined a month underwater as a choice between himself and Atlantis. It was also a choice that made more work for people who stayed behind.

"Could we move the visit?"

"Possibly. But first establish whether the palace means thirty consecutive days or a training block with permitted visits home."

"I thought that was obvious."

"That's why we write it down."

Chiron lent him paper. Percy drafted two questions to Poseidon and Triton: the proposed dates and whether he could return for camp obligations without disrupting the course. He did not add a third asking whether he was disappointing anyone by requesting a shorter visit. That question belonged to him, and he wasn't ready to give it to his father as though Poseidon should answer it.

At the other end of the table Annabeth calculated the likely dates from her own lesson schedule. She'd brought her notebook, but not to design Percy's month for him.

"If they answer tomorrow, we can compare the options," she said. "Not before."

"Do you want to come for part of it?"

"I might. I'm not promising a week I haven't checked."

"Fair."

Silena came in to collect the garden gloves she'd lent Chiron and glanced at the schedule. She saw the empty boxes where Percy hadn't written dates yet.

"You're not leaving tomorrow, then?"

"No. I'm finding out what the invitation actually allows."

"Good," she said. "Then I can ask you to help with the laundry without feeling I'm stealing you from the king."

Percy folded the questions and placed them under Chiron's seal for the outgoing current. The month wasn't canceled. It wasn't confirmed either. It had begun to look like something he could plan without turning other people's lives into assumptions.



Clarisse and Silena had saved two seats at the Poseidon table, although Clarisse insisted the open places were coincidence. Steam rose from the serving bowls, carrying rosemary and roast, and the long hall buzzed with clatter and talk. Across from them sat Thalia, boots hooked around the bench, silver jacket draped over its back. Her gaze traveled from Annabeth to Percy, then to the shell's outline in his pocket.

"You sent an answer," she said.

"We requested more information," Percy said. "And we might visit for a few days."

"Together?" Silena asked.

"If timing allows." Annabeth reached for the water pitcher, condensation cool under her fingers. "I want to see the palace again. That alone is decided."

Clarisse passed her the cup. "A few days allows the eastern channel."

"We'll see the training hall first," Annabeth said.

"The eastern channel holds more interest."

"You haven't reviewed the training plan."

"I have seen the channel."

"Then you can show us afterward."

Clarisse considered. "Fine."

Silena leaned in. "And the garden?"

"If open," Percy said.

"It remains always open," Silena replied. "The path may have altered."

"Then we'll map it." Annabeth took a bite of bread, crust crackling. "I'm not pledging the whole month. I'm pledging a visit."

"Enough," Silena said. She glanced at Percy, and he caught the second meaning. "No need to resolve the rest tonight."

"I know." He caught himself. "I mean, I'm practicing remembering that."

"Better," Clarisse said.

Thalia stole one of Percy's roasted potatoes, salt sharp on the air. Annabeth called it practically affection; Thalia countered that Percy kept too many. Leaning back, Clarisse said, "You could train in Atlantis and still hold this argument here."

"The potatoes wouldn't travel well," Percy said.

"Better than camp stew," Thalia replied, and Silena rose to the stew's defense.

Conversation drifted onward: a cracked shield in the armory, its boss split from a mistimed block, a new path cut through the woods where roots still showed pale, the enduring question of whether Grover had counted the breakfast rolls correctly. Nobody pressed Percy to promise a departure date or Annabeth to promise she would be fine. The month waited ahead, unchosen, but no longer a shape Percy carried alone.

After dinner, he walked Annabeth to the Zeus cabin. On the steps, lamplight spilling across old stone silvered with mica, she brushed a crumb from his shirt, flour still fragrant on her fingers from dinner bread.

"I'll help you review the training details when they arrive. If you go, we'll shape a plan to stay connected. If you don't, we'll discuss what you want instead. And should we change our minds later, we talk again."

"No unilateral decisions."

"Right."

Rising onto her toes, she kissed him. Gently he returned it, one hand resting at her shoulder. Stepping back, some tension had left her face.

"Good night, Seaweed Brain."

"Good night, Wise Girl."

He watched her go inside, then started back toward Cabin Three, gravel crunching underfoot and pine scent deepening in the cooling dark. Inside his pocket the shell lay cool and silent. By morning it might bear an answer from Atlantis. What he would decide once details arrived, he didn't know. The not-knowing no longer rose like a wall. It stretched like a path they could walk without either pretending to see its end.

At the lake, night breeze ruffled the surface into crossing ripples, cool against his face after the hall's warmth. Crickets stitched the dark with steady sound. Percy paused long enough to watch moonlight break and reform, then continued toward the cabin lights.
