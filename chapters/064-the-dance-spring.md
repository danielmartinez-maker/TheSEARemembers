## Chapter 64: The Dance (Spring)

Spring arrived at Camp Half-Blood in a single bright rush.

One morning frost still silvered the strawberry fields. By afternoon bees hummed over the same rows and the smell of thawed earth rose warm into the air. The lake turned blue again. The dryads wove pale blossoms into their hair. Pegasi shed tufts of winter coat that drifted across the pasture like snow that did not melt.

Percy noticed it first in the water. He was at the canoe lake before breakfast, skipping stones, when the naiads surfaced and told him the deep-water flowers had bloomed overnight.

"Spring tide," one said. "Everything wakes up."

She dove and came up with a dripping garland of tiny white buds and draped it over a half-submerged rock. The buds trembled and then, in the thin morning sun, began to open one by one. Percy watched, crouched on the dock in his jacket, breath puffing. He had seen coral bloom in Atlantis with Tyson, whole fields lighting up at once when the current turned warm, but this was quieter. More mortal. A dock plank creaked under his sneakers. A heron eyed him from the far reeds.

He tried a flatter stone the way Tyson taught him, sidearm and low, wrist loose. It hopped four times before sinking. The naiads clapped politely. One told him the lake liked him because he said please to the water. Percy was not sure water worked that way, but he said please anyway, out of habit from Atlantis, and the next stone hopped five times.

Percy grinned and skipped another stone. He was thirteen now, tall enough that his camp shirt always looked a size too short in the sleeves. His black hair was shaggy again. Annabeth had threatened to trim it with her architect scissors.

Thinking of Annabeth made his stomach flip the way it had all winter. On the bridge they had promised to make time for each other even when classes and palace duties got difficult. They had loved each other before returning; living that love in ordinary camp days still made him happy enough to feel foolish.

Chiron had called it young and sweet and had given them the talk about respect and friendship first, which had made Percy's ears burn. Poseidon had laughed loud enough to rattle the Atlantis windows and hugged him. Thalia, Annabeth's older sister, both daughters of Zeus, had stared at Percy and said, "If you make her cry, I will throw you off Half-Blood Hill."

So they were dating. They ate together. They did homework on the Big House porch. They had their own bench by the lake to talk about currents and bridge math and bribing Blackjack with sugar cubes.

It was not dramatic the way mortal movies made dating sound. There were no grand gestures. It was small things. Percy saved her the corner brownie because he knew she liked the crisp edge. Annabeth saved him the extra pencil because he always lost his. They walked each other to cabin forks at night and said goodnight in the same words every time, and it never got old. When Percy had a bad morning in Atlantis school with algebra that hung glowing in seawater and would not stay still, Annabeth listened and redrew the problem in her notebook in straight mortal lines until it made sense. When Annabeth stayed up too late redrawing the stable roof and got cranky, Percy brought her lemonade and sat quietly whittling until she sighed and leaned her head on her hand and admitted she was tired.

Chiron had told them the most important part was remaining good friends. They were good at that. They had been friends since they were twelve in the life they remembered, and they had survived years together after that. Here, they had shared a blanket on the Big House floor during a winter storm while Thalia told terrible jokes to keep them awake. Friendship was familiar. Handholding could still make Percy's ears warm in front of the whole camp, but even that felt like friendship, just closer.

But the Spring Dance was different.

The notice went up Monday on the Big House porch in Chiron's neat ink.

SPRING DANCE
Big House — Saturday, 7 o'clock
Music by Apollo Cabin
Punch and Cookies Provided
Nice Clothes Encouraged, Armor Discouraged

Underneath, Travis Stoll had added in pencil: BRING A DATE (OR DON'T. WE HAVE PUNCH.)

Percy stood staring at it until Grover bleated behind him.

"Dude. You're staring."

Grover's horns had grown over winter and he kept polishing them when nervous. He was polishing them now.

"I'm reading," Percy said. "We're already going together, right? Me and Annabeth? That's assumed?"

"Yeah. You're dating."

"Right. But I can't just assume. I have to ask her properly."

Grover nodded sagely around a mouthful of tin can lid. "The big formal ask. Kayla said Austin tried to ask Pollux by shooting an arrow past his head."

"I am not shooting anything past her head," Percy said. "Her sister would kill me."

"Just ask simple," Grover said. "Say, will you go with me? And mean it."

Percy stared at the vines drawn on the notice. "What if she thinks dances are illogical?"

"She redesigned the Big House floor plan for dance traffic flow," Grover said. "For fun. She wants to go."

Percy knew that was true. He had seen the sketches with PUNCH TABLE — KEEP AWAY FROM DOOR. Still, saying the word date out loud felt bigger than fighting monsters.

---

The Big House was already full of people who had opinions about furniture.

Annabeth was on the floor with a tape measure, trying to show Travis why the punch table couldn't stay directly beside the doorway. Travis kept moving it back because the table was heavy and he'd already carried it across the room twice.

"People will stop here as soon as they come in," Annabeth said. "Then nobody behind them can get through."

"Then people should keep walking," Travis answered.

"Have you ever watched people enter a room with free cookies?"

Travis considered that. "All right. Fine. Move it."

He put his shoulder against the table. Percy took the other end without being asked, and they pushed it toward the side wall. Annabeth watched the feet, pointed out the crooked leg and made them stop before the wood scraped the floor.

The actual problem was the room's shape. It had been built for meetings, not dancing. Two doors opened inward. One wall held the old fireplace, and the other had windows that needed to remain accessible. The Hephaestus kids had offered to set up a small raised platform for the musicians, but the platform would narrow the path between the chairs and the exit.

"We don't need a raised stage," Annabeth said.

Austin from Apollo looked wounded. "We're performing."

"You can perform from the floor. People will still hear you."

"But the audience needs to see the band."

"Then use fewer chairs in front. Or put the musicians near the far wall."

He looked over the diagram. "We'd have to move the speakers."

"Yes."

Percy watched the argument. He knew Annabeth didn't care about winning it for the sake of being in charge. She had spent winter sketching the bridge because people needed to cross a muddy creek safely. Here the problem was a room full of children who would arrive at the same time, spill punch and move unpredictably. It was a small building problem and therefore exactly the kind she liked.

The discussion grew louder when the dryads arrived with vines.

"We can't fasten them to that beam," a dryad told Annabeth. "The wood is too dry. We can hang them along the window frames."

"Not across the window latches."

"Then lower, near the wall."

"People will catch their clothes."

The dryad sighed. "Do you want flowers or not?"

"Yes," Annabeth said. "I want flowers that don't make anyone fall."

Percy was beginning to suspect the dance might be canceled by sheer exhaustion.

Chiron arrived and examined the new layout. He asked how the musicians would reach the door if they needed to leave during the dance. Annabeth pointed to the clear path she'd marked. Then he asked whether children in chairs or anyone with an injured leg would have space to move around the room without crossing the dancing area.

She looked down at the drawing.

"I made the path wide enough," she said.

"Have you tested it?"

"Not yet."

"Then let's test it."

They borrowed a wheelchair from the infirmary and rolled it along the proposed route without anyone seated in it. The corner by the fireplace was too tight. Annabeth measured again, moving the nearest chairs and the punch table farther from the turn. The second attempt worked.

"Good," Chiron said.

Annabeth smiled for perhaps half a second. Then she noticed the cookie table had become the new obstruction.

"Oh, come on."

Percy laughed so loudly Travis looked offended.

"Do you think this is funny?" Annabeth asked.

"Only a little."

"Then come help with the cookies."

They finally reached an arrangement that allowed room to dance, room to sit and a way out that didn't require moving three tables. The musicians accepted a floor-level performance area with good sightlines. The dryads kept their vines near the ceiling, safely away from lamps and handles. Mr. D claimed the punch table would be his responsibility because he'd heard the Stolls planned to use it for a practical joke.

As the others began carrying the furniture into position, Annabeth rolled up the layout.

"I might not have time to do my hair on Saturday," she said, mostly to herself.

Percy looked at her. "Silena said she'd help you."

"That doesn't mean I can arrive late to my own planning job."

"Maybe you don't have to be here for every chair."

She glanced toward the band and the tables. Her instinct was plainly to say she did.

"You can assign somebody to check the layout at the end," Percy suggested. "Milo knows how to measure. Chiron can check the exits."

Annabeth thought about it.

"Then Milo will need the drawing."

"Give it to him."

She did. He asked two questions about the window clearance, wrote the answers directly on the plan and went to find a pencil sharpener. Annabeth stood watching him go, surprised at how difficult it was to hand over something she'd planned.

Percy took her empty measuring tape and coiled it.

"You know the dance is supposed to be fun for you too," he said.

"I know. I just don't want everybody tripping."

"You've made that very clear."

She laughed. "All right. I'll stop before the entire camp starts hiding the furniture from me."

They left the Big House with one job actually finished. Percy was still worried about asking her formally. He also knew exactly why he wanted to go with her: even when she made ordinary arrangements complicated, she cared about people having a good time rather than merely being told one had been arranged for them.

He'd better not spend three more days rehearsing a speech about that.

---

At lunch the pavilion curtains were tied back to let the breeze through. Little jars of wildflowers sat on the tables.

Percy kept glancing at the Zeus table where Annabeth sat with Thalia. Annabeth's blond hair was in a lopsided braid Thalia had done. She wore a light blue sweater Silena had lent her and talked with her hands, drawing rectangles in the air. Thalia stole grapes off her plate. They looked like sisters, which they were, same stubborn chin and narrowed gray-blue eyes when thinking.

"You are staring again," Tyson rumbled happily beside him.

Tyson, eight feet tall with one big eye, was eating spaghetti with a spoon. A noodle hung from his chin.

"I'm thinking," Percy said.

"You are thinking about asking Annabeth to the dance," Tyson said. "Grover told Juniper. Juniper told Ella. Ella told the harpies. Everyone knows."

Percy put his forehead on the table. "Great."

"You should ask with flowers," Tyson said. "In Atlantis mermen bring sea flowers for the coral dance. They stay fresh if you sing to them. I can get pink ones. Like sunrise."

Percy lifted his head. "You'd do that?"

"Of course! You are my brother. She is my almost-sister. I will not eat them this time."

Across the room Annabeth looked up and waved two fingers. Percy waved back so fast he knocked over his nectar cup. Grover caught it.

"Real smooth," Grover whispered.

---

Percy spent three days trying to ask.

On Tuesday he walked to Cabin One with a speech ready, but Thalia answered in pajamas and said Annabeth was at the library, and Percy forgot everything and said, "Cool book," and left.

On Wednesday he tried at their bench, but Annabeth started explaining coral-lattice for the dock with such bright eyes that he just listened for an hour and never got the words out.

On Thursday Connor Stoll fell into step beside him.

"You're hovering," Connor said. "Like a nervous seagull."

"I'm patrolling," Percy said.

"You're pining," Travis said, appearing with toast in his mouth. "Just ask her."

"I want to do it right," Percy muttered. "Not just assume."

Connor clapped his shoulder. "Then pick a good moment. Just you two. Say what you mean."

"Today," Percy said. "After dinner. At our bench."

After dinner the sky was pink and gold. They walked toward the lake together, sneakers crunching, crickets starting.

"Hey, Wise Girl?" Percy said. His voice cracked.

Annabeth looked up. There was pencil lead on her cheek. "Yeah?"

Percy had planned a speech. It vanished. He just said it plain.

"Will you go to the Spring Dance with me? As my date? I know we're already — you know — but I wanted to ask for real."

She blinked. Then her face lit like sunrise over water.

"Percy. Yes. Of course yes."

"You sure?"

Annabeth laughed. "Seaweed Brain, I've had my dress planned since February. Silena's been helping me."

"You have?"

"Girls plan ahead," she said, grinning. "And boys panic for three days and ask at sunset, apparently."

"You noticed?"

"Everyone noticed. Thalia took bets. I said Thursday."

"It's Thursday."

"I know." She bumped his shoulder. "You did it right."

They walked to their bench shoulder to shoulder. Percy's hand brushed hers three times before Annabeth laced her fingers through his and held on like it was natural.

It was.

---

Percy celebrated for one hour before realizing his next problem.

He did not know how to dance.

The only dancing he had done was falling into a marshmallow bowl during a satyr reel. Mrs. O'Leary still wagged her tail at music, expecting comedy.

"You're doomed," Travis said at breakfast. "Just sway."

"You need professional help," Connor said. "Clarisse."

Percy choked on juice. "Clarisse La Rue? Spear-in-your-face Clarisse?"

"She takes ballroom. Mortal school. Got an A," Travis said. "Bring chocolate. Tell no one."

Percy found Clarisse behind Ares cabin threatening a dummy in boots and a TRY ME shirt.

"Jackson. What?"

Percy held out sea-salt chocolate. "I need dance lessons. For Annabeth. Please? I'll do two patrol shifts."

"If you tell anyone I know ballroom, I'll bury you behind the forge," Clarisse said. Then she snatched the chocolate. "If you step on my feet, I'm stepping back. With boots."

They met in the empty archery range after dinner. Targets stood pale in the dusk. The grass was damp and smelled cut. Somewhere beyond the fence a dryad swept blossom petals off the path with a willow broom.

Clarisse unwrapped the chocolate with her teeth.

"Box step," she barked. "Right back. Left side. Right together. Left forward. Right side. Left together. Even you can draw a box."

Percy tried to picture the box the way Annabeth would draw it, neat lines with arrows. It did not help. He went the wrong way into a target, bowstring twanging against the wood. A loose arrow rattled in its quiver.

"Your other right! No wonder Poseidon keeps you from naval formations."

She played a staticky waltz on a little speaker and demonstrated, surprisingly graceful. For a moment she did not look like a spear-first daughter of Poseidon at all. She looked light. Her boots, huge and scuffed, found the beat without effort. Her shoulders dropped. Percy realized she must have practiced for hours in mortal school gyms with taped floors and folded bleachers, embarrassed the whole time, getting good anyway through sheer stubbornness. That was very Clarisse.

Percy's mouth fell open.

"You're good," he said.

"Shut up," she said, pleased anyway. "Hands up like a beach ball. Not strangling it. Follow me. I'm leading."

What followed was comedy. Percy stepped left when she stepped right. He stepped forward when she stepped back. He apologized so much she told him to count instead.

"One-two-three. Stop looking at your feet! Look over my shoulder! Not like that, it's creepy!"

"I am looking!" Percy said, stepping square on her boot. The speaker crackled. A string of lights from the distant Big House porch flickered on, gold in the blue dusk.

"OW! Jackson!"

"Sorry — I mean one-two-three!"

She made him do it without music, just counting, then with music, then without looking down. She counted with him, then made him count alone. She corrected his hands three times. Too high, too low, too tight. "You're holding a dance partner, not a hydra," she said. She showed him where to rest a hand for a slow dance so it was respectful, high on the back, no sliding. She made him practice asking — "May I have this dance?" — until he could say it without mumbling at his shoes.

Mrs. O'Leary wove between their legs trying to join. Two Apollo kids watched from the path, fists in mouths to stifle laughs.

By the end Percy could manage a wobbly box without killing anyone. His shirt stuck to his back. Clarisse's boots were scuffed.

"Not terrible," she admitted. "For slows you just sway. Here."

She showed him the hold. Percy practiced on a fence post while she poked his spine with a spear butt.

"Don't hunch. You're not ashamed of her. Don't yank her. And Jackson—"

"Yeah?"

She scuffed the dirt, suddenly awkward. "Annabeth's good people. Don't be an idiot Saturday."

"I'll try."

"If any guy bothers her, come get me," Clarisse added. "I'll handle it."

"Deal. Thanks."

"Get out before someone sees us being nice. Ruins my reputation."

Percy left counting under his breath, only tripping over his own feet twice. The path home ran blue with dusk. Bats flicked over the fields. Pavilion light glowed warm. He held his hands up in slow-dance shape like an idiot and grinned when a firefly landed on his sleeve like applause. One-two-three, he told the firefly. It blinked and drifted off toward the lake.

---

While Percy suffered, Annabeth had a nicer time.

Silena had claimed the Aphrodite back room Friday afternoon. Lights were strung. Lemonade and shell-shaped cookies were out. The air smelled of honeysuckle.

Silena, daughter of Poseidon and guest in her Aphrodite friends' workroom, brush in one hand and ribbons in the other, pointed at a chair. "Sit."

Annabeth sat, shy to be fussed over. She was used to notebooks, not salons. The chair was draped with a soft blue cloth that smelled faintly of lavender. On the table beside it Silena had laid out everything in neat rows the way Annabeth herself liked to lay out drafting tools — brushes by size, ribbons by shade, clips in a little ceramic dish shaped like a shell. It made Annabeth relax a fraction. This was just another kind of planning.

Through the open window came the sounds of camp getting ready. Someone hammered something on the Big House porch. An Apollo kid ran scales on a trumpet, cracked a note, swore, started over. The strawberry fields buzzed. A pegasus whinnied far off.

"Nothing fancy," Annabeth said. "Just neat. It gets in my face when I talk—"

"When you talk with your hands," Silena finished, smiling. "Half-up. Best of both. Trust me."

Thalia lounged in the doorway, arms crossed, self-appointed guard.

"Keep it simple," Thalia warned. "She's thirteen."

"When do I ever make anyone look like someone else?" Silena said patiently. "I enhance."

She worked gently, smoothing Annabeth's thick blond hair without pulling, twisting two sections back and pinning them with tiny silver owl clips from Thalia last Christmas. She added a pale blue ribbon.

When Silena turned the mirror, Annabeth whispered, "Oh."

It was still her. Just shinier. Gray eyes bigger. Hair soft around her shoulders.

"You look beautiful," Silena said firmly. "Not grown-up beautiful. You beautiful. There's a difference."

Thalia came close, tough face melting. As sisters, daughters of Zeus, they shared the same chin, the same eyes. Thalia squeezed her hand.

"Mom would make a fuss if she could see you," she said quietly. "Percy's going to forget how to count."

"Thalia!" Annabeth blushed to her ears.

"What? Clarisse says he counts now. One-two-three. Cute. Dorky, but cute."

Silena laughed and gave Annabeth a cookie and a tiny vial smelling of vanilla and rain. "One drop behind each ear. Not perfume. Just you after rain."

Annabeth hugged her carefully. "Thank you."

"That's what camp sisters are for," Silena said.

Thalia blinked fast. "Okay, mush over. Who's doing my eyeliner tomorrow? I'll bite people without eyeliner."

---

Percy survived lessons. Now he needed a corsage.

He had seen a movie where a boy gave a girl wrist flowers, and Chiron smiled warmly when he asked.

"Lovely tradition," Chiron said in his stable office. "What flowers?"

"Sea flowers. Tyson's getting pink ones. Like sunrise."

Before Chiron could lend preservation mist, the door banged open. Thalia stalked in, static crackling, bow over shoulder.

"Jackson. A word. Outside. Now."

Percy's blood ran cold. "Am I in trouble?"

"Yes. No. Maybe. Outside."

On the porch the sky was lavender. Thalia pointed at his chest, sparks at her fingertips.

"Listen, Seaweed Brain. Annabeth's my little sister. If you step on her toes, I'll step on yours. If you ditch her for punch, I'll drown you in punch. If you make her cry—"

"You'll throw me off Half-Blood Hill," Percy finished. "Six times now."

"Six," Thalia agreed. "With love. Lightning love."

Percy stood his ground. "I won't. I promise. I'll be careful with her."

Thalia studied him, blue-gray eyes so like Annabeth's but older. Sparks faded. She ruffled his hair.

"I know, Kelp Head. You're good. Stupid, but good. Here."

She shoved a small box at him. Inside on tissue lay a silver wrist band with a flat disk engraved with waves and lightning bolts.

"Silena and I fixed it up," Thalia said gruffly, looking away. "Little water vial underneath. Atlantean meets camp craft. Annabeth helped design it last year."

Percy's throat tightened. "Thalia—"

"Don't get mushy. Small side up. And tell her she looks nice. Even smart girls like to hear it. Especially them."

"I will," Percy promised, clutching the box.

Thalia punched his arm lightly. "If you get nervous, count. One-two-three. Clarisse told me."

"Everyone tells you everything?"

"I'm terrifying. It's a gift."

Tyson arrived an hour later through the lake portal, dripping, holding a cloth bundle high. Water pooled under his huge sneakers on the Cabin Three floorboards. The fountain in the corner gurgled, happy to see him.

"Percy! I did not eat them!"

He unwrapped the cloth with enormous care, tongue sticking out. Inside were sea blooms, petals translucent pink veined with gold, glowing faintly from within, smelling of salt and honey. Droplets of light clung to them. They pulsed very faintly, the way jellyfish pulsed, as if they still remembered the tide.

Percy had seen a lot of beautiful things in Atlantis — vaulted pearl ceilings, gardens of living coral, his father's trident lit with sea-fire — but these small flowers made his chest ache in a different way because his big brother had carried them all the way up through cold portal water, singing the whole time, hands cupped to keep them safe.

"They only open in spring tide," Tyson whispered. "I sang about the brave little clam all the way up."

"You sang about a clam," Percy said, touched.

"It worked!"

With Tyson's huge gentle fingers and Chiron's mist, they fixed three blooms onto the silver wristband Thalia had given Percy for Annabeth's corsage, with sea grass behind. Pink and silver and green. Perfect.

"She will love it," Tyson said. "It matches her brave smart heart. Now practice counting. One-two-three!"

---

Saturday dawned bright and nervous.

The Big House was closed for decorating. Apollo kids snuck in with instruments. Stolls snuck out with cookies. Mr. D yelled about punch stains.

Percy failed at normal. He put armor on backward. He poured juice on cereal and ate it. He held a book upside down until Grover turned it over.

"Breathe," Grover said at lunch, watching Percy arrange carrots into a box diagram. "It's Annabeth. Two hundred breakfasts."

"Not in nice clothes," Percy said. "What if I trip? Punch on her dress? Thalia throws me off the hill?"

Grover patted his arm. "Clarisse says you're adequate. Clarisse never says adequate. And Juniper and I are more nervous. Ella has a poem to read before the slow songs."

Ella, the russet-feathered harpy, clutched poetry like a shield and nodded fast. She and Grover were close friends, but Grover was dating Juniper, and Ella had volunteered to introduce the first slow song.

"Grover read ninety-three dance books," Ella recited. "'Festooned with crepe paper.' Ella never danced. Grover says swaying counts. Juniper says it does. Does it count?"

"Swaying totally counts," Grover said. "Juniper and I will try it. You can read your poem, Ella. You don't have to dance to enjoy the music."

Percy loosened a little. "One-two-three."

"That's the spirit," Grover said.

In Aphrodite cabin, preparations were calmer.

Annabeth sat still while Silena re-pinned the owl clips and ribbon. She wore a pale blue dress with white flowers embroidered at the hem, skirt swirling when she turned — tested three times in the mirror — and her battered sneakers because Silena declared no blisters.

"Spin," Silena ordered.

Annabeth spun, skirt flaring. She felt pretty and happy and fizzy like cider.

Thalia wolf-whistled from a bunk in black jeans and boots and a lightning-stitched top, her formal, with extra eyeliner. "My little sister going with Kelp Head. Where did time go?"

Annabeth threw a pillow. "Stop." She smiled so hard her face hurt.

A knock came. Grover stood stiff in a shirt and grape-leaf bow tie beside Juniper, who wore pale blossoms threaded through her hair. Ella had come with them, her russet feathers decorated with little white flowers, holding the page of her poem.

"Pretty. Ella is pretty. Juniper is pretty too," Ella announced, ducking in.

"Both of you," Grover said, then looked shyly at Juniper. "Especially you."

Silena fussed over blossoms while Thalia taught Grover to untie his tied-together shoes. Annabeth watched, heart full. Family.

Then a softer knock. Everyone froze.

"Him," Thalia sang. "Places! Hair! Breath! He has the corsage! Open!"

---

Percy stood on the porch, heart hammering loud enough for dryads to hear.

He had showered. Tyson had combed his hair neat with sea gel that smelled of kelp. He wore dark pants, white shirt sleeves rolled up, good sneakers. His palms sweated around the corsage box.

He knocked. Heard squealing. Considered running.

Silena opened, smiled kindly, stepped aside. There was Annabeth.

Percy forgot counting. Forgot box steps. Forgot everything except Annabeth in blue with flowers on the hem, hair half-up with owl clips, smiling like he was the only person alive.

"Hi," she said softly.

"Hi." His voice squeaked. "You — you look really nice. Pretty. Pretty-nice. Both."

Thalia snorted. Silena elbowed her.

Annabeth did not look away, cheeks pink. "Thanks. You look nice too. Tyson do your hair?"

"Yeah. Sea gel."

"It looks good," she said loyally, nose wrinkling.

Percy thrust the box forward. "Corsage. Tyson got flowers from Atlantis. Thalia helped with band. Chiron gave mist. Can I put it on?"

Hands shaking, he opened it. Sea blooms glowed pink-gold even in daylight.

Annabeth gasped. "Oh, Percy."

"They only open in spring tide," Percy said. "Tyson sang about a clam to keep them fresh."

She laughed, eyes shining. "Thank him. Yes, put it on."

Tongue out in concentration, Percy slipped the band on her wrist. Perfect fit. Small side up. Waves and lightning glinting.

"Perfect," he whispered.

Annabeth admired it. "Best corsage ever. Excellent load distribution. Great engineering."

That was his Annabeth, romantic and structural. Relief flooded him.

Thalia loomed, two fingers to her eyes then to Percy. "Remember, Jackson."

"Thalia!" Annabeth protested.

Thalia straightened Percy's collar, smiling. "Have fun. Back by ten. Chiron's orders. One-two-three."

"One-two-three," Percy agreed.

Grover and Juniper came out together, his bow tie crooked and the blossoms in her hair bobbing. Ella walked beside them reading her poem under her breath. The five headed to the Big House as the sun sank gold across the grass.

---

The Big House meeting room had become a garden indoors. Someone had swept the old wooden floors until they shone. Someone had borrowed every spare chair cushion in camp so no one would have to stand all night.

Green and blue streamers hung from beams. Jellyfish lanterns glowed gold. Real vines trailed walls, coaxed by dryads to bloom exactly that evening, tiny white buds opening even as campers arrived and stared. Tables held cookies — chocolate chip, oatmeal, lemon bars dusted with sugar — and a crystal punch bowl Mr. D guarded like a dragon. Beside it stood a handwritten sign in Chiron's ink: ONE CUP FIRST, THEN SECONDS. HE MEANS IT. LOVE, THE STOLLS (FORGED).

"No punch without a cup," Mr. D warned each arrival. "No baptizing friends. Stolls, looking at you."

Chiron stood at the door in his best jacket, horse half brushed shiny. "Percy, Annabeth. Lovely. Excellent posture. Practicing?"

"Yes, sir," Percy said straighter. "Clarisse taught me. Don't tell."

"Lips sealed," Chiron twinkled.

Apollo set up guitars and flute and dented trumpet. Kayla cued Austin. A fast sun-chariot song filled the room. Stolls dragged Hephaestus kids into chaotic jumping with one lost shoe.

Percy and Annabeth stood at the edge of the room, hand in hand.

"Loud!" Annabeth leaned close.

"What?" Percy shouted.

She laughed and squeezed. They got punch under Mr. D's watchful eyes and watched Juniper teach Grover the steps while Ella rehearsed beside the stage.

"Step, slide, step," Juniper said, moving at Grover's pace. "Don't look down. I'm not going anywhere."

Grover tripped. Juniper caught his arm, and they laughed. Ella marked down the misstep as a possible line of poetry rather than announcing it across the room.

Percy's nerves turned good-buzzy. Then music changed.

Austin strummed soft. Kayla lifted flute. A slow sweet melody drifted like water over stones.

Chiron clapped. "Slow dance! Find partners! No dipping unless practiced! Stolls, means you!"

Floor cleared. Travis bowed to Connor. Pollux found a partner. Dryads swayed with satyrs.

Percy turned to Annabeth, remembering: stand straight, don't hunch. He held out palm up as practiced eleven times in Cabin Three mirror.

"May I have this dance?" Voice wobbled only a little.

Annabeth set her hand in his, corsage glowing pink on his fingers. "Yes."

They stepped on floor. Percy held one hand up like a beach ball, other lightly on her back, not grabbing. Annabeth's free hand rested on his shoulder. They left respectful space as Chiron taught. Close to talk, comfortable to move. Percy remembered Clarisse poking his spine — stand tall — and Chiron's talk — be kind, be steady — and Tyson's advice — sing if nervous, but only inside your head.

"One-two-three," Percy whispered, swaying. The floorboards were smooth under his sneakers. The lantern light moved softly across Annabeth's ribbon. Someone's shoe squeaked. Someone laughed. The punch ladle clinked.

He stepped on her foot at once.

"Ow," Annabeth laughed.

"Sorry — one-two-three — sorry—"

"It's okay. Forget counting. Feel it. Like waves."

Like waves. Percy knew waves. He stopped counting, listened to guitar and flute and Annabeth humming. Shoulders dropped. Steps steadied. Left, side, together. Sway.

They circled slowly, imperfect but together. Her dress swirled round sneakers. Sea flowers brushed his shirt with salt-honey scent.

"You did good," Annabeth murmured. "Clarisse taught well."

"Threatened me with boots. Motivating."

She laughed through his hand. "You smell like kelp and cookies."

"You smell like rain. Good spring rain."

Around them lanterns glowed. Vines swayed. Mr. D smiled faintly, tapping time. Chiron watched soft-handed.

Nearby Grover and Juniper swayed with his bow tie coming loose and her flowered hair brushing his shoulder. Ella sat by the Apollo musicians, waiting for the verse she had offered to read during the next interval. Grover looked over and gave her an encouraging thumbs-up.

Percy caught his eye. Grover mouthed, SWAYING COUNTS.

It did.

The song ended too soon. Last guitar note hung like firefly, faded. Clapping. Apollo launched fast song. Stolls stampeded back.

Percy and Annabeth stepped off hand-linked, flushed.

"Too much stepping?" Percy asked, leading to cool window chairs.

"Only twice. Once mine. Left when you went right."

"Clarisse says unclear leading."

"Clarisse says everything's your fault," Annabeth said fondly. "Love language."

They split warm oatmeal chocolate chip. Outside evening deepened blue, stars pricking, fireflies over strawberries.

Annabeth lifted glowing wrist. "Tyson sang?"

"About a brave little clam."

"Tell him clam did good." She leaned head on his shoulder one second, sweet and simple, friends tired-happy. Percy sat still, smiling cheeks aching.

Thalia arrived with three punches, gave two away. Menace gone, almost soft in lanterns.

"How was it?" she asked, straddling chair backward. "Count out loud?"

"No," Annabeth said. "Felt waves."

"Nice. Cheesy Poseidon. Approve," Thalia said.

Silena floated by checking hair, beaming. "Adorable. Box step?"

"Wobbly," Percy admitted.

"Perfect from door," Silena said, moving on.

Evening blurred warm. Line dance Percy survived by watching Connor's feet and moving half a beat late. Chiron danced with Mrs. O'Leary who stepped on all four hooves and barked with joy. Mr. D after punch told actually interesting spring story about Persephone's garden that made even the Ares kids listen. Ella recited whole set list from memory between songs — "'Track three, tempo moderato, key of G'" — and Grover applauded each like heroics. Pollux and Austin attempted a spin and ended tangled in a streamer to huge cheers. Travis tried to dip Connor and dropped him. Connor got up bowed anyway.

At quarter to ten Chiron rang bell.

"Last song! Lovely evening! Clothes hung up, cups in bins, no unsupervised pegasus rides!"

Last song slowed softer still. Percy and Annabeth danced again, easier. He stepped once, she stepped back in revenge, both laughing too hard to sway, standing mid-floor hand in hand while music played round them.

When it ended they thanked Apollo, stacked two chairs for tired Chiron, waved to Grover and Juniper still swaying without music, and to Ella gathering her poem beside the stage, and walked into spring night with Thalia steps behind pretending not to chaperone. The porch boards were cool underfoot. The air rushed fresh after the warm crowded room. Frogs sang down by the lake. The Big House windows glowed behind them, gold and laughing, music fading to a single guitar tuning down.

Air cooled grass and night flowers. Torches and fireflies lit path. Owl called.

At the fork to Zeus's cabin Thalia tactfully checked for monsters behind a bush where she could see. Crickets stitched the dark. A torch sputtered. The path stones still held a little daytime warmth through their shoe soles. Annabeth's corsage gave off its soft pink glow, lighting her fingers from below like a lantern.

"Thanks for going," Percy said, scuffing dirt. "Had fun."

"Me too," Annabeth said. Owl clips glinted torchlight, corsage glowing faint. "You did it right. Ask. Flowers. Dance. All."

Percy's ears burned. "Clarisse dancing. Tyson flowers. Thalia band. Silena — you look great."

"But you showed up and tried," Annabeth said simply. "That's what matters."

She squeezed his hand firm-warm, let go, stepped toward her path.

"Goodnight, Seaweed Brain."

"Goodnight, Wise Girl."

Thalia emerged, arm round Annabeth, pointing at Percy. "Ten out of ten! No hill-throwing! Watching you, Jackson!"

Percy laughed, waved, waited till Cabin One window glowed yellow in blue night. Then turned to Cabin Three counting without meaning — one-two-three — sea-flower scent on shirt, crickets singing, spring soft over camp.

Best dance ever. Only stepped twice.

He passed the forge, dark now, embers banked. He passed the cabins, most windows warm. In Cabin Eleven someone still laughed. In Apollo someone still hummed the slow song on guitar, soft through shutters. The night felt kind and watchful.

Above stars burned steady. The pine on Half-Blood Hill rustled, guarding them all asleep.
