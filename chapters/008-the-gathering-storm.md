## Chapter 8: The Gathering Storm

Percy discovered that the fastest way to lose an argument with Silena was to insist that he was capable of doing two things at once.

"You can carry the letters or you can carry the toolbox," she told him. "If you drop the bag in the creek, the post doesn't get to tell Amphitrite that you meant well."

"The creek is about a foot deep."

"Then your mother's going to get a letter that smells like a foot-deep creek. Which do you think she'll like better?"

Percy looked at the canvas mailbag she'd placed on the bench. His sealed letter to Amphitrite was inside, along with several others that needed to go out with the satyr messenger before noon. The letter had occupied most of the previous evening, and he'd spent enough time thinking about it that even the possibility of its getting wet made him uneasy.

He handed the toolbox to Clarisse.

"Fine. You win."

"I didn't realize we were competing," Silena said, counting the envelopes a second time.

"We weren't," Clarisse told him. "You invented that part."

It had been two days since the storm damaged the Hall's already vulnerable ward. The paths around the building were still cordoned off. Chiron had tightened curfew, assigned extra border patrols and warned campers against guessing which god might be responsible for the trouble. Percy understood the warning. He also knew that a rule against speculation was not going to stop the speculation.

He helped Silena take the mailbag to the messenger's collection point. On the way they passed two Hephaestus campers carrying fresh wooden panels toward the Hall. A third camper stood by the path, making a list of everyone going in and out of the work zone. She was taking the job seriously enough that a senior counselor had to explain why his name was missing from the roster.

Silena watched the exchange with approval.

"At least somebody's recording who goes into the building now."

"You don't think they did before?"

"I think they did sometimes. Then people stopped thinking it mattered because they knew one another. That's the problem with a place where everybody knows which drawer the keys are in. Eventually nobody remembers who last opened it."

Percy thought of the attic window in the Big House, which had been left loose long enough that he and Annabeth already knew how to use it. He looked away before Silena could notice the expression.

"You found anything new about the delivery signature?" he asked.

"Not yet. The stores keeper remembers the crate because it was heavier than the invoice said it ought to be. That's something we can check. He doesn't remember the person who signed for it, and I'm not going to make up an answer because Nico wants the ledger finished."

"Have you told Chiron?"

"Of course. He asked for the original invoice to be sealed while we compare the copies. It's still in the stores office, and this time somebody's actually signed the borrowing register."

She paused at the messenger's desk and deposited the mailbag. Percy watched the satyr open it, count the envelopes and place Amphitrite's letter in a separate pouch for sea delivery. The sight gave him an odd mixture of relief and dread.

"She'll write back," Silena said.

"I know."

"You look as though you're not sure you want that."

He thought about the childhood the letter had mentioned without describing. He hadn't lied, exactly. He had chosen what he could tell.

"I want to hear from her," he said. "I'm just not always sure I know what to say when she writes about things from before camp."

Silena looked at him for a moment. "Then ask her about the things you want to know. You don't have to pretend you remember every detail before you're allowed to talk to her."

Percy nodded. He'd been given that advice before. It still hadn't become easier to follow.

At breakfast, Annabeth claimed the end of the Poseidon table for their notebook. She had kept the bandage on two fingers, which made her pencil grip awkward, and had arranged a small stack of loose pages beside her bowl. Percy brought two plates and sat opposite her.

"You know Chiron's going to put you back on an early bedtime if he finds out you skipped breakfast to make another chart."

"I didn't skip breakfast. I'm eating right now."

"That's an empty bowl."

"I had an apple before you got here."

"That's the same defense you used yesterday, and Thalia ate the apple."

Annabeth looked at the plate he pushed toward her. "You brought bread?"

"And eggs. I have been informed that they're good for keeping a person alive."

She took the plate and began eating, though she kept one hand on the notebook so the morning breeze wouldn't lift the pages.

They had agreed to keep two sets of notes. The first contained what they remembered from their erased life: the wars they had survived, the families they had known, the version of camp where Annabeth was Athena's daughter and Clarisse belonged to Ares. The second contained things anybody in this camp could check, such as the Hall's construction dates, the purchase orders, the damaged wards and the conflicting prophecy records.

They could not afford to mix the two.

"The problem isn't that we're getting nowhere," Annabeth said, using the side of her thumb to turn a page. "It's that we're asking different questions without realizing it. We keep acting as though proving the Hall had a bad installation would prove why our lives changed. It wouldn't."

"Agreed. We could have a badly installed ward and somebody rewriting time. Camp could be unlucky enough to get both."

"And the Oracle's silence might be another separate problem."

Percy looked toward the Big House. "Or they might be connected."

"Exactly. But if we're going to test the connection, we need a reason other than both things frightening us."

He leaned over the notebook. She had drawn three boxes and connected them with dotted lines.

*Hall maintenance.*

*Prophecy transcripts.*

*Oracle silence.*

Under the first she'd written *physical evidence and witnesses*. Under the second, *dated copies, origin uncertain*. Under the third, *Chiron confirms no new prophecy for three weeks*.

"Where do our memories go?" Percy asked.

"On the other page. We know those memories exist, Percy. We don't have to prove them to each other. But Nico can't inspect what we remember. If we ask him for help, we have to give him something he can actually examine."

He looked at the margin. There she'd written a question about the cabin layout, then crossed it out and written *not independent proof*.

She had been doing that more often. A detail from their old lives would occur to her, and she would reach for the easy explanation before remembering that the camp around them held a very different set of records. Percy knew why it frustrated her. She had spent years solving problems by comparing what she knew against what the world told her. Now even that comparison required an argument about which world had been real.

"You're going to wear a hole through the paper," he said, pointing to the place where she'd been pressing her pencil.

"I've noticed."

"Want me to write for a while?"

She hesitated, looking at the bandages, then handed him the pencil.

He wrote *check original dated copies* beneath the prophecy box.

Annabeth nodded approvingly. "Good. And we need to ask about the Oracle without making it sound as though we've decided somebody gagged her. Chiron knows she's been silent, but he hasn't said why."

"Do you think he'll let us speak with her?"

"I think he'll tell us that he's still investigating the records and the ward damage. But I want to ask properly before we do anything else."

Percy lifted an eyebrow. "You mean before we do something he tells us not to do."

"Preferably before we even decide whether we're going to do that," she replied, reaching for her bread. "I'm trying to improve my methods."

"You could start by asking your boyfriend whether he's finished his own repairs before volunteering him for a burglary."

"I haven't volunteered you for anything."

"Yet."

Annabeth smiled faintly. It had been an exhausting week, and he was glad she still found him funny.

They took the notebook to the Athena cabin to compare two public maps. The older one showed a path that the Hall's builders had rerouted three years earlier. Annabeth pointed out where the new foundation had overlapped the previous supply road. Percy marked the difference in pencil, then asked why there were so many crossed-out measurements along the southern edge.

"The ground was probably unstable," she said. "Or the survey team changed the design. There's a note here about drainage work, and this area gets water from the hill after heavy rain."

"So yesterday's flooding wasn't exactly a surprise."

"No, but the amount was worse than the plans expected. That's something the repair crew can use, even if it has nothing to do with the prophecy."

He could see the distinction she was making. The mystery was not the only reason to ask a good question. Sometimes a drainage map mattered because somebody might slip in a flooded entrance during the next storm.

At midday they took their public notes to the Big House and requested an appointment. The secretary at the front desk was a harried satyr with a stack of forms taller than his horns.

"Lord Chiron is handling the border patrol reports," he said. "If you have information about the Hall, leave it with the maintenance team. If it's about the Oracle, put your request in writing."

Annabeth produced a page from her satchel.

The satyr examined it. "You've already written it."

"I had a feeling you might ask."

"I can send it upstairs. That doesn't mean he'll say yes."

"We understand," Percy said.

They waited on the porch while the satyr carried the request away. Annabeth ran her thumb over the edge of the notebook, catching herself when it pressed against a healing burn. Percy wanted to tell her to stop, but he'd heard enough people telling her what to do with her hands this week. He shifted closer so she could rest the book on his knee instead.

Chiron returned their request that afternoon with a handwritten note: the attic remained restricted because its protections were under review, and no one was permitted to question the Oracle until he and Mr. D determined that it was safe. He would consider a supervised meeting when the inspection was complete.

That was a refusal, but not an unreasonable one.

Annabeth read the note twice.

"How long is the inspection going to take?"

"It doesn't say."

"He should at least tell us what he's checking. We're the ones who found the conflicting copies."

"Maybe the next step is to ask for the dates and stop trying to go into the attic," Percy said.

She looked at him sharply. "I don't want to stop. Something is wrong with the Oracle, and if we wait for everybody to finish repairing every bracket in camp, we could be waiting until the next disaster."

"That's possible," he said. "But there are people who can help us without climbing through a window. Nico knows how to trace the records. Zoe knows the Hall's ward marks. Thalia can ask about Olympus's purchase orders. We could spend one day listening to what they find."

Annabeth looked away toward the Hall. The new canvas was tied down tightly, and a worker on a ladder was measuring the distance between two mounting points.

"You want to wait," she said.

"I want us to have a reason to break the rules that isn't just being frightened somebody will get there first. I've broken plenty of them. I don't want to do it automatically because we're worried."

She turned back, a little surprised by his answer.

"I hate that you're making sense," she said.

"It's upsetting me too."

They agreed to ask the others what they knew before making another decision. For once, the notebook went back into Annabeth's bag without a new midnight plan written in the margin.
---
Nico had taken over one end of the Hall workshop without asking anyone's permission.

There were three ledgers spread across the table when Percy and Annabeth arrived, each opened to a different year. Beside them lay a folder of copied receipts and a narrow wooden case holding the damaged fitting from the storm. The fitting was sealed beneath a glass lid. Nico had written a note on the case: *Not to be handled until the ward team finishes testing.*

Percy read it aloud. "You sound like Silena."

"Silena made me write it. Somebody tried to pick the thing up with his bare hands yesterday."

"Was it you?"

"It was a counselor who ought to know better."

Nico moved his notebook out of the way so Annabeth could sit. She had brought the public section of their investigation, with her more personal notes left in Cabin One. That distinction mattered more to Percy now that he'd watched how seriously Nico treated the provenance of even a damaged invoice.

"We asked Chiron for an interview with the Oracle," she said. "He won't let us go into the attic until the ward inspections are finished. Have you found anything about the old transcripts that could justify an earlier review?"

Nico opened the oldest ledger.

"Not a reason to go in without permission. But I did find something odd about the way the transcripts are stored. They used to be copied for the camp archives as soon as Chiron approved them. After the Hall was built, the records show a second copy being sent to the Hall's custodian."

"Why?" Percy asked.

"Probably for the official displays. The Hall has an exhibit on the history of prophecy. A custodian would need source copies to check the inscriptions."

Annabeth leaned forward. "Who signed the transfer?"

"That's the odd part. The first two years have signatures from a known assistant. The last one has the custodian's seal but no name on the receipt."

"Does that mean the copy was stolen?"

"It means the receipt is incomplete. I'd like to ask the custodian before I make the story bigger."

Percy looked down at the year. It matched the period when the prophecy records had begun disagreeing.

"Could we compare the Hall's display copy with the original in the Big House?"

"Only if Chiron allows it. Right now the Hall copy is in conservation because the mosaic room got flooded. The original is still with the archivist." Nico hesitated. "I did ask to see the earlier catalog. Chiron said I could check the public index but not the restricted transcripts."

Annabeth made a note.

"Then the public index should tell us whether there's a dated copy earlier than the one we found."

"Maybe. But a catalog entry doesn't always describe what's actually in the folder. I used to think it did." Nico looked rather offended by his own discovery. "Apparently archivists make mistakes like everyone else."

Zoe arrived carrying a rolled diagram, which she laid over the end of the table without disturbing Nico's ledgers. She was younger than Thalia but had the weary expression of somebody who'd been answering the same questions for two days.

"I heard somebody wanted to compare the ward marks," she said.

Annabeth opened her notebook to the drawing she'd made after the storm.

"We saw these marks near the northern column. Are they from Hecate's protections?"

"Some of them. That one isn't." Zoe pointed to three hooked strokes meeting at a circle. "The Hall borrowed a common motif from older boundary inscriptions. It means containment in several different magical traditions. You can't identify the caster from it."

"Could the same symbol be used in the attic?" Percy asked.

Zoe thought for a moment.

"Possibly. The Oracle has protective inscriptions of her own, and the Big House is older than the Hall. You'd have to compare an actual inscription, not just a sketch, before you could say the marks were the same."

Nico looked up. "We have old survey drawings from before the Hall was built. We could check whether the motif was copied into the new design."

"Those surveys weren't complete," Zoe warned. "The builders changed several sections during construction. I can tell you what the motif was meant to do in the plan, but I can't promise the finished building matches it."

Annabeth nodded. "That's still useful. We can compare what the design intended with what the repair team found."

Percy watched them work through the drawings. Annabeth asked about line thickness and orientation. Zoe answered with a careful explanation of how protective inscriptions could look similar without being magically identical. Nico kept interrupting to ask where every measurement had been recorded and whether they could cite the actual page.

It was the sort of conversation Percy normally enjoyed only when he could be somewhere else. Today he listened. They were showing him how to build an answer from things another person could verify.

At the end of an hour, Nico had drawn a smaller diagram of the records chain. Zoe had identified three common ward patterns that might be mistaken for one another, and Annabeth had produced a list of questions she wanted to put to the archivist.

None of it told them who had rewritten their lives.

"It doesn't mean we wasted the morning," Annabeth said, as though Percy had complained.

"I didn't say that."

"You looked like you were going to."

"I was thinking that if the Hall borrowed its symbols from the Big House, the old protection could be the useful starting point. Not because it's suspicious. Because it's older."

Zoe glanced at him with new interest.

"That's actually a reasonable observation."

"Thank you. I'll remember this day."

She ignored the joke. "If you want to learn why a symbol was copied, check the architect's design notes. But don't assume the person who copied it knew how the original enchantment worked. People have been decorating sacred buildings with symbols they no longer understand for centuries."

Nico tapped the ledger. "The Hall custodian might have those notes."

"Then I'll ask," Annabeth said.

"You'll ask politely, right?" Percy said.

She gave him a flat look. "You are enjoying this far too much."


They took their questions to the Hall office. The custodian was unavailable, but the assistant promised to forward their request and showed them which catalog numbers referred to the original construction survey. It was a small success. The assistant also explained that the older transcripts were being checked for water damage and that access would be restricted until the work was finished.

Percy wrote down the dates.

When they left, Annabeth was walking faster than he was.

"You're upset," he said.

"I'm frustrated. There are answers in those files, and every person we ask has another reason why we can't see them."

"Some of those reasons are good. The archive isn't a puzzle box somebody left for us to solve. There are people trying to keep it from falling apart."

"I know. I hate that I know. But I still don't like waiting."

He didn't tell her to be patient. She'd heard more than enough of that word since the fever, and he wasn't going to turn it into an argument about whether she had a right to want answers.

They walked back toward the cabins with an appointment request, two new names and a list of catalog entries. Percy counted those as real progress. Annabeth looked at the Hall's sealed doors as though she was measuring the distance between the information they needed and the time somebody would finally let them touch it.

---

Percy and Annabeth hadn't made it far along the cabin path when a sharp metallic clatter sounded behind them, followed by several voices speaking at once. Annabeth stayed with their copied notes while Percy turned back through the repair-yard gate. One of the replacement racks had toppled against the fence, scattering practice shields into the dirt. A boy from the Hermes cabin was standing with both hands raised, as though the rack might accuse him personally.

Nobody was hurt. Percy established that before he went to help lift the frame.

A younger girl was collecting the shields. She kept looking at the closed doors of Tempest Hall.

"Don't put them back against that wall," she told him.

"The rack wasn't attached to the wall," Percy said. "That seems to be the problem."

"That's where the lightning came through."

"The lightning didn't come through the practice-yard wall."

"My brother said the Hall's cursed. He said anybody who goes near the north side gets marked."

Percy set the frame down. The brackets had been fastened through rotten timber, and one screw had pulled out with a damp curl of wood still caught in its threads. That explained the fallen rack much better than a curse. He showed her the screw.

"See? This is what happens when someone puts a heavy rack on a board that needed replacing last year."

She studied it without appearing convinced. "So it's safe?"

"Not until we fix it."

The answer seemed to surprise her. Percy understood why. She had probably been getting a mixture of assurances that everything was fine and warnings that she ought to stay away from half the camp. Neither told her what the adults were actually doing.

He asked the Hermes boy to find someone from the repair crew. The boy hurried off. The girl remained beside him, turning a loose strap over in her hands.

"Was Annabeth the reason the storm got so bad?" she asked.

Percy went still for an instant. "Why would you think that?"

"Somebody said she was making lightning inside. I heard she burned the doors."

"She was helping another camper. The ward was already damaged, and the repair team hasn't finished working out what happened."

"She's Zeus's daughter, though."

"She is."

The girl waited, expecting a more satisfying answer. Percy could have told her what Annabeth had done during storms in another lifetime, how many people she'd rescued and how often she'd walked into danger without asking whether she could manage it. None of that belonged to this girl's understanding of her camp. It would only turn one rumor into a different one.

"Being Zeus's daughter doesn't mean every lightning strike is hers," he said. "And if you hear someone blaming a person before the inspection's done, you can ask where they got their facts."

The girl gave the damaged screw another look.

"Can I help fix this?"

"If the instructor agrees. We need another bracket, and you'd be useful holding the board steady."

When the repair camper arrived, he found them sitting beside a tidy stack of shields with the failed screws laid out on a scrap of cloth. Percy explained what they'd seen. The camper checked the back of the rack and confirmed that the wood had split well before the storm.

"We'll move the racks to the dry shed until we replace the uprights," he said. "Could you carry those?"

The younger girl took two shields and marched toward the shed before anyone could stop her.

Percy followed with the rest, thinking about how fast fear had crossed the camp. A rumor about Annabeth had taken less time to reach a child than the repair team's account of the damaged ward. He wished the second could catch up with the first.

Silena was waiting at the stores desk when he came back. She had a bundle of notices in one hand and an expression that suggested he was about to be assigned more work.

"You're supposed to bring the revised tool list," she said.

"Nobody gave me a revised tool list."

"I know. That was the problem I was about to tell you about. The requests are coming in faster than anyone can check what's already been issued."

She spread the notices on the counter. Each came from a different cabin. Someone needed spare lantern oil for patrol. The archery team wanted new targets after the flood. The dining pavilion had requested heavy cloth for the tables near the leaking roof. All of them were reasonable demands. Taken together, they were more than the stores could supply that afternoon.

"I thought Chiron had put you on the Hall orders," Percy said.

"He's asked me to keep track of them. That doesn't mean camp stops needing everything else." She slid a page toward him. "Look at the quantities. Half these people are guessing because they haven't checked what they already own."

"Maybe you should tell them."

"I have. Three times. Then two counselors told me their requests were special, and a third asked whether I could simply do the inventory for him."

"Could you?"

Silena stared at him until he realized how badly he'd phrased the question.

"I mean, you probably could. I wasn't saying you should."

"Thank you for the distinction."

She sat on the stool behind the counter and took off the pencil she'd tucked into her hair. There was a smear of graphite behind one ear. For once, she looked less interested in making the situation pleasant than in making somebody else understand how much work was hidden in a well-stocked room.

"I like knowing how stores works," she said. "It's useful, and when I make a mistake I can usually find it before someone goes without something important. But every time people see that I'm good at it, they give me another person's job. Yesterday one of the senior campers asked me to put his equipment away because I 'always know where everything goes.' I spent an hour finding space for gear he could have carried himself."

Percy considered the requests. "Could we put the quantities on the noticeboard? What we have, what we don't, and who's responsible for checking their cabin before asking for more?"

"That would help. But I don't want it to turn into a public argument about who's taking too much. The cabins already think the Hall repairs are stealing their supplies."

"What if we give everybody a form to return with a count instead of posting their orders? You can publish what stores can issue, and the counselors have to do the checking."

Silena turned the idea over. "You're volunteering to help with the count?"

"For as long as you need me. Except I have a rack to finish."

"An hour," she decided. "We'll make the forms. You can distribute them while I'm at the delivery inspection. But I want the counselors' signatures on the requests before they come back. They need to own what they ask for."

They went to work. Percy ruled columns on spare paper while Silena dictated the categories. The task was dull in the way that made a little concentration matter. A misplaced number could send the wrong kind of rope to a patrol or leave a cabin waiting for blankets it had already been issued. He wrote slowly, making sure the cramped lines could be read.

Clarisse arrived carrying the damaged rack brackets. She looked at the forms and then at Percy.

"How many punishments do you have now?"

"Just the one. Apparently repairing things is a transferable skill."

"You were supposed to finish the rack before dinner."

"I will. I'm helping Silena make sure nobody takes every spare screw in camp first."

Clarisse glanced at the notices, then took the form meant for Cabin Three. "We used two rolls of binding tape this week, and the spare buckles are almost gone. I'll count the rest."

"You actually read the form," Silena said.

"I can read."

"Some of the other counselors seem to think that's optional."

Clarisse's mouth twitched. She studied a request from the Ares cabin, with a quantity circled three times in red.

"They want how many practice spearheads?"

"Twice what stores has," Silena said. "I sent it back."

"Good. They'll keep breaking them if you keep replacing them." Clarisse set down the bracket. "I can ask their counselor what happened to the last shipment. He might tell me something honest if he knows I'm going to check the yard."

Silena looked doubtful. "Ask him without turning it into a challenge."

"You're taking away all the enjoyable parts of my afternoon."

"Yes."

Clarisse collected the list and went. Percy watched her go, struck again by how differently his sisters dealt with the same problem. Silena made a system that would survive a bad mood. Clarisse went to the place where the bad decision had happened and made sure someone answered for it. Either one could make a day harder when she got impatient. They also needed each other.

By the time the forms were finished, there were fewer complaints at the counter and a neat pile of signed requests ready for the next supply run. Percy delivered the copies to the cabins, stopping to explain to the girl from the practice yard why the repair team wanted the shield racks moved.

When he returned, Silena was closing a ledger.

"Your letter went out this morning," she said without looking up. "The sea pouch was sealed before the messenger left."

"I saw."

"Good. I thought you'd ask me to check a fourth time."

Percy leaned against the counter. "I might still ask tomorrow."

"Then I'll tell you the same thing."

He smiled. She didn't smile back immediately.

"Percy, you don't have to fix every frightened person's opinion of Annabeth on your own," she said. "I heard what happened by the rack. I can make sure the counselors know the repair team's actual finding."

"I wasn't trying to start a fight."

"I know. But one of the reasons people listen to you is that you'll talk to them like they're worth answering. Don't lose that by arguing with every rumor you hear."

He looked toward the path where the younger campers had disappeared. "I hate that they're scared of her."

"Then help us give them something reliable to be less frightened of. And let Annabeth answer for herself when she wants to."

Percy nodded. He wanted to tell Annabeth about the girl, but not as a problem he'd already solved for her. He could ask whether she wanted to speak to the younger campers during the next safety demonstration. She might say no. That would be her decision.

Silena handed him a final sheet. "Take this to the workshop. It's the list of tools that actually came back from the Hall."

"One more errand?"

"Consider it the price of having volunteered so enthusiastically."

"I was tricked."

"You were useful," she said. "There's a difference."


---

Annabeth was sitting on the floor of Cabin One when Thalia came back from the evening patrol.

She had spread three large sheets of paper beside the bed so she could compare the Hall's new layout with the older survey without bending her injured fingers around a pencil for too long. Her hand was getting better. The healer had removed one bandage that morning and told her to leave the other until the skin stopped pulling when she closed her fist.

Thalia closed the door behind her and stepped carefully around the papers.

"Did I miss the part where our cabin became the records office?"

"You're welcome to reclaim the floor if you can help me move the drawings without folding them."

Thalia set her spear on its hooks and crouched beside the nearest page.

"Is this the map we worked on the other day?"

"Some of it. Nico found a public index showing how the prophecy records were copied after the Hall opened. The original was supposed to stay in the Big House, but there was also a display copy sent to the new custodian. I want to check which drawings belong to the original construction and which were added later."

Thalia traced the edge of one diagram with a finger, careful not to smear the pencil.

"Why do you need that for the Oracle?"

"Because some of the ward marks appear in both places. Zoe says they're common motifs. I want to know whether the Hall borrowed the pattern from the Big House or whether both buildings are using a much older design."

"That sounds like something she'd know."

"She knows what the motifs mean in the plans. She doesn't know who installed every piece or whether the drawings match what's there now."

Thalia sat back against the bedframe.

"You're doing the thing where you list all the missing information until somebody offers to help you get it."

Annabeth looked up.

"I wasn't trying to make you volunteer."

"I know. I'm telling you I can hear how frustrated you are without pretending I can solve it."

Annabeth smoothed the edge of a page. The reply was a little different from the ones she'd grown accustomed to hearing from Thalia, and she didn't quite know what to do with it.

"I asked Chiron for supervised access to the attic," she said. "He said no until the inspections are finished. That could be days."

"Have you asked for a specific appointment instead of a general permission?"

"Yes. The secretary said he'd forward it."

"Then let me talk to Chiron tomorrow. I won't ask him to open the attic just because we want to look around. But maybe he'll let us inspect one catalog entry at a time with the archivist there."

Annabeth felt a small, irrational surge of annoyance. Percy had suggested nearly the same thing that afternoon. She knew it was sensible, and she hated how easily other people could say it when they weren't carrying memories of a future that nobody else could remember.

"You make it sound like I haven't tried," she said.

"I don't think that. I think you get so focused on the thing you're missing that you stop counting everything you've learned while you were looking."

Annabeth nearly laughed. It was a strange thing to hear from Thalia, who had spent years deciding that anything she couldn't control was a threat. She wondered whether the ward accident had frightened her sister enough to make her more cautious or whether the evacuation drill had taught them both something about acting on information rather than fear.

"Did Clarisse put you up to that?" Annabeth asked.

"Clarisse thinks I should use fewer speeches and more practice drills. She may have a point."

"She usually does, when she's talking about practical things."

Thalia pointed to the copy of an older camp plan. "I still don't understand where that one came from."

Annabeth looked down. It was the same drawing she'd been unable to explain in the previous chapter. She had brought it out again because she needed to compare it with the public survey, but she hadn't thought about what Thalia might ask.

"I know," she said. "I'm sorry. I didn't mean to put it where you'd see it and then refuse to answer questions."

"Then why is it here?"

"Because I trust you not to take my notebook apart looking for answers I can't give you. I can't do this work if I'm always afraid you might come into the cabin and read everything while I'm out."

Thalia's face tightened.

"Have I ever done that?"

"No. You haven't. I'm telling you I appreciate it."

Annabeth had chosen the words carefully. She wasn't going to hand Thalia a lie about the old map. She also wasn't going to pretend there were no consequences to keeping it.

Her sister looked at the drawing again.

"You keep saying this is about more than the Hall. I believe you. But I don't want to find out what it is because some monster makes you tell me while you're hurt."

"Neither do I."

"Then promise me you won't go into the attic alone."

Annabeth's first instinct was to say yes and let Thalia think the matter settled. She looked at the page in her lap instead.

"I can promise not to go alone. I can't promise I won't ever need to go back."

Thalia noticed the difference immediately.

"Annabeth."

"I said I'd ask for permission first. I did. I asked for an appointment. I'm still asking other people for records they can share. But I won't promise that if everything keeps getting delayed, I'll stop caring about the Oracle."

"That's not what I asked."

"You're asking me to promise something I don't know I can keep."

Thalia stood. For a moment Annabeth thought they were about to have another argument ending with a slammed door. Instead her sister walked over to the window and checked that the latch was secure.

"I wish you'd talk to me before you make the decision," Thalia said at last. "Even if you think I'll disagree. I don't want the first thing I hear about another trip to be Chiron telling me you've been caught."

Annabeth didn't know how to give her everything she wanted without promising a future she might not be able to deliver.

"I'll try," she said.

Thalia looked over her shoulder. "That sounded carefully worded."

"It was."

"I'll take it for tonight."

She came back to the floor and helped Annabeth stack the drawings in order. The pages were too large for the little desk, so Thalia found a piece of spare cardboard and slid it beneath the stack. Annabeth wrote the catalog numbers on the top sheet while her sister held it steady.

"Does your hand still hurt?" Thalia asked.

"Only when I grip the pencil too tightly."

"Then stop doing that for the rest of the evening."

Annabeth looked at her.

"You can say that without starting a war," Thalia added. "I'm not taking the pencil away."

"I was about to say I agree with you."

Thalia appeared genuinely surprised.

"Well. That's a pleasant change."

They put the drawings away. Annabeth left the public catalog on the desk and the private notes beneath her mattress. Thalia did not ask what was in the second notebook. She was learning to let the question sit there without making Annabeth answer it every time she came home.

After dinner, Annabeth found Percy near the practice yard, working on the last of the repairs his instructor had assigned. He had a screwdriver in one hand and was trying to steady a board with his knee while a younger camper held the other end.

"You're doing that the difficult way," she said.

"That's because I'm trying to finish before somebody changes the instructions again."

The younger camper laughed and held the plank straighter. Percy tightened the screw, checked that the rack didn't wobble and passed the screwdriver back to its owner.

He followed Annabeth away from the tools.

"You look as though you've been talking to Thalia."

"I have. She offered to ask Chiron for a supervised look at the records tomorrow."

"That sounds good."

"It is." Annabeth hesitated. "I also told her I couldn't promise never to go into the attic again."

Percy stopped beside the fence.

"Are you thinking about going tonight?"

"I don't know. I want to check the earlier transcript before the catalog is moved into conservation. The archive index says the original should still be in the attic, and I think there's a chance the dated copy Chiron made is filed with the older patrol records."

"That's a reason to ask for the record tomorrow, not necessarily to climb through a window tonight."

"I know. But I keep thinking about Chiron saying the Oracle's been silent for three weeks. What if something happens before we get permission?"

Percy put both hands on the fence rail. The bandage on his forearm had been reduced to a small dressing, but he could still see the red mark beneath it. He'd been very sure of what he was doing when he ran toward the Hall, and he'd discovered that certainty wasn't the same thing as a safe plan.

"If we go back," he said, "we leave the Oracle alone. We look only for the earlier copy, and if anything feels wrong, we get out. No touching wards, no experimenting with the chair. I don't want to repeat what happened in the storm."

Annabeth nodded. "Agreed."

"And we should leave somebody a note about where we're going. Not the whole story. Just enough that if we don't come back, somebody knows where to look."

"Thalia would come after us the moment she found it."

"Then maybe that's a reason to think harder about whether we're doing something sensible."

She studied him. He wasn't refusing to help her, but he wasn't willing to let the decision happen just because she was frightened. That made her both irritated and grateful.

"Can we at least look at the public catalog once more before curfew?" she asked. "If the entry isn't there, we'll have to wait."

"That's a plan I can agree to."

They went to the Athena cabin. The catalog entry was there, but its description was incomplete: a bundle of patrol reports filed near the year of the stolen prophecy, with Chiron's private notes listed separately. The bundle was due to be moved into protected storage the following morning. The date caught Annabeth's attention.

"That's the only place the earlier margin copy could have survived," she said.

"Or it could be a bundle of ordinary reports."

"Yes. But after tomorrow, we might not be able to compare it with the other copy without another formal request."

Percy read the catalog entry twice, trying to decide whether the time pressure was real or simply the result of a storage procedure. In the end he agreed to one limited trip, not because the rules had stopped mattering, but because he knew Annabeth would keep looking until she found a way to answer the question.

They made the plan carefully. They would go after the midnight patrol passed, take a shaded lamp, copy only what they found and leave the originals in place. Percy would watch the corridor while Annabeth checked the indexed bundle. If they heard anyone coming, they would leave through the window instead of hiding among sacred objects and hoping nobody noticed them.

They wrote a note with the time and location and sealed it in a plain envelope addressed to Chiron. Percy kept it inside his jacket. If they got caught, he wanted to be able to hand it over and say exactly what they'd been doing.

The plan did not make the trip right.

It made them accountable for the decision, at least to each other.
---


Past midnight, they climbed for the attic again.

The Big House slept. Pine sap and old wood met them at the same loose window, the same groaning stair, the same attic door swollen with summer humidity.

Inside, the Oracle's mist had thickened. It banked in the corners and threw back iridescent light that stung Percy's eyes. Ozone prickled his nose. The floorboards seemed to vibrate, the way the dock did before lightning struck the Sound. Somewhere under the wax and dust lay a sour trace, like old pennies left in rainwater, and every breath felt slightly too thick to finish.

Annabeth knelt beside the archive chest and sorted the loose pages by their dates. Percy held the shaded lamp; neither of them touched the Oracle's chair.

"What are you looking for?" he whispered.

"A copy made before the theft."

"You think someone left it in here?"

"I think whoever changed one page might have missed a boring one."

Quiet settled, broken by the tick of cooling shingles and paper rasping beneath her fingers. Then she paused over a sheet clipped to a patrol report.

"Percy."

He brought the lamp closer. The margin held four lines in Chiron's cramped hand, dated before the transcript they had found. A question mark followed the last one.

Annabeth copied them into her notebook before she read them aloud:

*The oath shall break, the king shall fall,*
*The son of Poseidon shall face the call.*
*One throne shaken, one sister bound,*
*In darkness and fear, the truth is found.*

Annabeth set the page back under its clip. Her hand shook slightly as she underlined the differences in her notebook.

"An earlier version," she whispered. "'One throne' and 'one sister' — not 'three thrones' and 'two sisters.'"

"So somebody upsized it."

"Or this is a bad copy." She swallowed. "We need to know which. What if someone is *building* toward the altered wording?"

Cold spread from his sternum outward. "You mean they might be answering a prophecy."

"Might. A date and four lines aren't proof of who changed our lives."

"Who does that?"

"Someone who profits when it happens."

They stared at each other while dust sifted through the mist around them.

Percy exhaled. "Then we decode the altered version. 'Three thrones shaken, two sisters bound.' Which thrones? Which sisters?"

"No idea." Annabeth pushed upright, wincing. "But I doubt we'll have to wait long to be introduced."

Floorboards creaked below — footsteps, voices. Both of them went rigid.

"Someone's coming," Percy breathed.

He hauled Annabeth upright and towed her behind a tower of sheet-draped furniture. They wedged into the shadows, chests barely moving, as the attic door swung inward and lamplight spilled across the floor.

"— telling you, something's wrong." Chiron's voice, low and urgent. "Three weeks silent. That has never happened."

"Oracles are theatrical." Mr. D sounded sharp, irritated, a glass clinking. "They sulk. They return. They spout riddles. It's the job description."

"This isn't temperament. Her last prophecy — the stolen one — broke every pattern in her record. Now silence." A pause, hooves shifting on boards. "That's not drama. That's a gag."

"Placed by whom?"

"I don't know. But I mean to learn."

The voices thinned. The door shut. Darkness resettled, smelling of dust and lemon polish.

Percy counted his own heartbeats to five hundred before he moved.

"Silent," Annabeth murmured. "No new prophecies. We don't know whether she can't or won't."

"Or won't. Maybe she knows something she can't afford to say aloud."

"There might be a connection between the records, the silent Oracle, and the monsters at the border. But we still don't know if one cause explains all three."

"And we're sitting in the middle of the web."

"Yes."

They exited the way they entered — window, ivy, damp grass — and held their silence until the Big House windows had shrunk behind pines and the lake air replaced attic dust in their lungs.

Near the water, Annabeth stopped. "We need to speak with her. Directly."

"The Oracle who hasn't spoken in three weeks?"

"The only witness who was in the room when it happened."

Percy weighed exposure against ignorance: the watcher they hadn't identified, the questions that would mark them. Annabeth was right anyway. She usually was.

"Okay." He bumped his shoulder against hers. "Careful. And not solo."

"Not solo," she agreed.

They walked back toward the cabins without talking, the decision settling damp and heavy as lake fog on their shoulders.

Far off, thunder worked its way along the hills.

Rain was gathering.


---

By morning, Clarisse found Percy helping replace a weapon rack at Tempest Hall. She waited until the instructor walked away before speaking.

"You smell like attic dust," she said.

"Good morning to you too."

"Chiron doubled curfew. Somebody's been leaving footprints under the Big House windows anyway."

Percy set down the bracket. "You watching the windows?"

"Somebody has to. Nico's watching the records. Silena's finding out who got paid to fix the Hall. I'm watching our idiot little brother so he doesn't get himself expelled before he's learned where the clean towels go."

"I haven't been expelled."

"That is not an achievement yet."

He turned the bracket over in his hands. He wanted to tell her why the prophecy mattered so much. If he did, he'd have to explain the memories he and Annabeth had already agreed to keep between themselves. Clarisse had been his sister for three weeks. Her concern was genuine. It wasn't a reason to hand her something that could frighten her for the rest of her life.

"What do you know about Zoe?" he asked instead.

Clarisse frowned. "Why?"

"She was the first person who told me about the missing prophecy."

"She knows the Hall's wards better than most counselors. Hecate kids study that kind of thing. She's spent a lot of time around the Hall since it opened, and she's been quiet since the break-in."

"You think she's responsible?"

"I think she has a reason to care. That isn't the same thing." Clarisse took the bracket from him and held it against the post. "Hold that steady."

Percy obeyed.

"If you're going to ask questions about her, ask Nico what he's found," she added. "And maybe ask Zoe herself. You're good at guessing motives from somebody's face. Doesn't mean you're right."

Percy started fastening the bracket. "Did Silena tell you to say that?"

"She said I should use fewer threats."

"Did it work?"

"I'm trying." Clarisse took the wrench back before he could strip the bolt. "Now get out of the way."

---

The meeting in the dining pavilion was supposed to last fifteen minutes. Annabeth knew because Thalia had written *fifteen minutes* at the top of the notice in letters large enough to see from the doorway.

By the time Annabeth arrived, eleven younger campers were sitting at the breakfast tables. Another three hovered near the entrance, debating whether they were prepared to attend a meeting held within sight of the damaged Hall. Bea stood near the chalkboard with a stack of blank cards. Clarisse had borrowed a low crate from stores so everyone could see the broken bracket from the practice-yard rack.

"This is a briefing," Thalia told Annabeth. "Not a demonstration of anyone's powers."

"I wasn't planning to throw lightning at breakfast."

"I know. I'm saying it where the others can hear." Thalia lowered her voice. "Chiron asked us to answer questions. I don't want you feeling as though you have to stand up there and defend yourself."

"Then don't make me stand up there. Let me help answer what they actually ask."

Thalia regarded her. She was dressed for patrol and still had her sword at her hip. Annabeth almost asked whether her sister ever got a morning that didn't begin with somebody else's problem. She didn't. Thalia would hear the question as criticism of work she had chosen.

"All right," Thalia said. "Bea's leading. We stop if it turns into people shouting about who's to blame."

"An excellent rule for the entire camp."

"Don't start."

Bea asked the campers to write down what they wanted to know. Not their names, just the questions. Some used the cards, while others spoke aloud. One boy wanted to know whether he could reach archery practice without passing the Hall. A girl asked why the repair crew wore heavy gloves when the weather was clear. Someone at the back asked whether a broken ward could let monsters through, which brought a brief, unhappy silence.

Thalia wrote three headings on the board: *What we saw*, *What we know*, and *What needs checking*.

Annabeth watched her hesitate over the last one. Her sister disliked uncertainty where safety was concerned. But she wrote it anyway.

"Can we put the answers somewhere people will be able to read them later?" Annabeth asked.

"If they're correct," Bea said.

"Date them. The repair team can change the notice when it learns something new. That's better than leaving an old answer up as though it stays true forever."

Thalia added a date to the board.

Milo sat in the second row, feet not quite reaching the ground. He had been the missing camper during the evacuation drill and had spent part of the storm trapped near the archery shed. Annabeth remembered helping guide him and two others away from the flooded entrance. Today he wore a sweater despite the warmth.

"What if somebody goes to the wrong place again?" he asked. "I didn't know the Hall was closed until I got there."

"That's on us," Thalia said. "We changed the assembly point and didn't get the new signs up everywhere. The notices will include a map, and counselors will check the routes before practice."

Annabeth drew the lower path on the board. She marked where the runoff had collected, then drew an alternative around the upper bank. She stopped short of labeling it safe.

"Bea, can the maintenance crew check the slope before we put this up? I haven't walked it since the rain."

"Of course. I'll ask this afternoon."

Milo looked at Annabeth's hand.

"Does it still hurt?"

"The burn? When I hold the chalk too tightly. The healer says it's mending."

"Did you get it because you were making lightning?"

She heard the question beneath the question. Several campers had watched her arrive with expressions that made their guesses clear. Annabeth had spent the morning preparing an explanation of the difference between a failing ward and a demigod's powers. It suddenly seemed more important to answer the child who had been there.

"I was at the Hall when the protection started failing," she said. "I was trying to help people get out. I got burned when the damaged ward reacted. My powers can affect electricity, but that doesn't mean I know everything about them yet. The repair team is still investigating. I can't give you their conclusion before they have one."

Milo twisted his sweater hem. "So it could happen again?"

"There's a reason the Hall is closed. Nobody should go inside until the inspections are finished. That isn't because you did anything wrong, or because the building is angry at you. It's because the crew found a problem and needs to make sure it's fixed."

A girl in front raised her hand. "My brother said you set the ward off. Because of Zeus."

Thalia stiffened beside Annabeth. Ordinarily Annabeth might have answered too quickly, especially with her sister ready to fight for her. Instead she set down the chalk. Her fingers had started to ache.

"Your brother wasn't with me," she said. "I don't know what he heard. I didn't enter the Hall to practice lightning. I went because somebody needed help. If the inspection shows I made a mistake, I want to know what it was so I don't repeat it. But none of us should invent an answer before the evidence is in."

The girl looked uncertain whether that was a reply she could take home. Bea handed her another card for the questions the repair team still needed to address.

Someone asked how to tell a damaged ward from a harmless one. Clarisse took that opportunity to lift the broken bracket from the crate.

"This isn't magic," she said, displaying the split wood where a screw had pulled free. "It's part of a rack that nearly fell on somebody. People kept leaning shields on it because the rack had always been there. Nobody looked closely enough until the wood gave out. A ward can fail for reasons you won't be able to see, but the rule is the same. If something looks wrong, keep clear and tell the person responsible. Don't touch it to find out what happens."

"Especially if somebody tells you it's cursed," Percy said from the doorway.

Annabeth turned. He was carrying repaired practice shields and had apparently stopped when he heard the discussion. Clarisse pointed him toward an empty bench.

"You have a job," she informed him.

"I'm doing it. The instructor approved the rack."

"Then you can help Bea put up the map afterward."

He accepted this with an exaggerated expression of misery. Several younger campers laughed, and the room became a little less tense.

Thalia resumed the briefing. "If you report a problem, you're not getting someone in trouble. And if a counselor says we're still checking, it means we don't have the answer. It doesn't mean there's a secret list of people you're supposed to be afraid of."

She glanced toward Annabeth, then back to the campers.

"If somebody dares you to touch a closed ward to prove you're brave, you get an adult. You don't have to win an argument to stay safe."

"Even if the person telling us is older?" asked a boy near the back.

"Especially then," Clarisse said. "Older campers can be idiots. I have extensive evidence."

The meeting lasted forty minutes. By the end they had a draft map, an ordered pile of questions for maintenance, and three changes to the emergency notices. Bea gave Milo the task of carrying the revised route instructions to his cabin counselor. He accepted the paper and left with two other children, arguing about whether the longer path would make them late to archery.

When the pavilion emptied, Thalia sat on a bench and rubbed her eyes.

"I said fifteen minutes."

"You did," Annabeth said. "They wanted to talk."

"I know. I just wasn't expecting so many sensible questions."

"They're frightened, not stupid."

"I didn't say they were."

"No, you didn't." Annabeth picked up the chalk again and erased the unfinished sketch.

Thalia watched her work. "You handled that better than I would have. I'd have found whoever started the rumor and made them wish they'd kept quiet."

"You still might."

"I might," Thalia admitted. "But Milo left with the map instead of sitting here staring at the Hall. I think that's better for him."

Annabeth stacked the remaining cards. "I wish I could tell you why the Oracle matters so much."

Her sister looked at her but didn't press for the reason.

"Even if you find what you need, it might not be the answer you want," Thalia said. "Chiron has to think about everyone who sleeps in these cabins. If he keeps the attic closed, you have to account for that."

"I do think about them."

"I know. It's part of why I worry. You'll take responsibility for the entire camp and then be furious when somebody tells you there are things you can't do alone."

Annabeth folded a corner of the rough map, noticed it, and smoothed it flat.

"I'm not going alone."

Thalia's gaze moved to Percy, who was helping Bea straighten the noticeboard. Her expression suggested that she knew very well who counted as *not alone*.

"That wasn't what I meant."

"I know."

Neither sister made the other finish the argument. That was new enough to be a relief and difficult enough that Annabeth refused to call it a solution.

Percy came back for the clean copy of the map. Annabeth held one edge while he kept the paper flat and Bea fetched pins. He noticed her flexing her sore fingers and took over the side she was holding without announcing it.

"Silena told me about the girl by the rack," Annabeth said.

"She asked whether you caused the storm. I told her what the repair crew knows. I didn't try to tell her what you thought."

"Thank you. Everybody seems to expect I can either stop every storm or make the next one. I can barely make a spark go where I intend."

"We could put that on the noticeboard."

"Under the heading *Please stop asking me to do the weather*?"

"Exactly. Your father can take the complaints."

"He's more likely to send a thunderbolt than an apology."

"Then we'd better charge admission."

Annabeth laughed, then looked toward the Big House beyond the pavilion roof. She was relieved that Milo had been willing to ask a question, and glad Thalia had let her answer without stepping between them. Neither feeling settled the matter of the Oracle. The forbidden attic still sat there, full of documents they could not inspect in daylight.

Bea returned with the pins. Together they posted the revised route and left a space beneath it for the maintenance team's approval. Annabeth copied the catalog numbers from the previous day into her public notebook and made a separate list of what the children still deserved to know.

When Percy left for the final rack inspection, she remained a little longer to help Bea sort the cards. Several were badly spelled. None was frivolous. Whatever answers the Oracle held, those questions were real, and somebody had to take them seriously without pretending to know more than they did.

---

At dusk, Annabeth found Percy on the dock reviewing their notes. The night before, the Oracle's mist had gathered around the chair without producing a new prophecy. They had found a dated copy in Chiron's handwriting, not an explanation.

"I spoke to him," she said.

"To Chiron?"

"He confirmed the Oracle has been silent for three weeks. He wouldn't say whether he thinks that's connected to the stolen prophecy from three years ago."

"That's not quite the same as knowing someone's stopped her from speaking."

"I know. I hate it." Annabeth sat beside him. "We have two things now: an older copy and a pattern of silence. Both matter. Neither tells us the rest."

Percy closed the notebook. "So what now?"

"We try asking her directly, with Chiron there if we can persuade him. If that fails, we'll decide whether going back at night is worth it."

"That sounds suspiciously sensible."

"You make me sound ill."

He nudged her shoe with his. "I like you sensible. I also like you when you're sure everyone else is wrong."

"That's most of the time."

"I noticed."

She leaned her shoulder against his, just for a moment. Then she stood and took the notebook.

"Come on. Curfew. We can get arrested tomorrow."

They made it back before the horn. Percy counted that as an achievement.
