## Chapter 184: Friday, and the Porch

The archery field in October belonged to whoever got there first, and between four o'clock and the light going off the top of the ridge that was Thalia Grace, by an arrangement that had never once been stated out loud and was now, after two Fridays, load-bearing.

The grass had gone brown and hard. The targets stood out at twenty paces in a row, the straw greyed down to the colour of old rope, the faces pale where the weather had got at them and dark in rings where somebody's arrows had. The wind came off the ridge in long flat pieces and laid them across the field one after another without ever quite stopping, and the whole place smelled of cold dust and cut hay and, faintly, somewhere downwind, the outside of a forge. She liked it in the specific way she liked anything that did not care who she was. Straw did not care. Wind cared, but wind was honest about caring, and after four years on this hill Thalia Grace had developed a real taste for the things that were honest about what they were.

Five arrows, all of them inside the gold if you were generous about the outside ring, and she pulled them one at a time and put them back in the quiver and allowed herself the private satisfaction of counting twice, which she would have denied doing.

"You're early," said a voice from the road.

"I'm on time. You're early. I've been here since four."

Rhys Calder came through the gate with his bow unslung off his shoulder and a canvas coat with the collar up and his hair doing what cold did to it, and in one hand, unexpectedly, two tin mugs with steam off them, walking the way he walked everywhere, which was as though the ground had been laid out for a job and he was glad of the work. "Four's early for a Friday," he said. "Most of this hill is standing in a kitchen at four on a Friday."

"Most of this hill doesn't have an elbow to maintain."

"Your elbow's been maintained." He held out the nearer mug. "This is for the rest of you."

She took it, because not taking it would have been a conversation. The tin was warm clean through and the heat went into her fingers and stayed there, which was, she noticed a second later, the entire trick of the object: he had carried a mug of something hot out to a cold field without commentary, in the flat way he carried everything, as though the thing being delivered was the point and the person holding it was only the transport.

It was far too sweet — sweet the way a thing is sweet when somebody has guessed at the number of spoons and guessed high and kept going out of commitment to the guess.

"Calder, that's three spoons."

"Four."

"That's not tea. That's an opinion about tea."

"It's Friday," he said, which answered nothing at all, and set his own mug down on the target butt and strung his bow, and did not look at her while he did it, which was the other thing about him, the thing she had not been able to get a line on in three weeks: he paid attention to her the way he paid attention to a badly hung door. Completely, immediately, and with no apparent interest whatever in whether she enjoyed it.

They shot. The arrangement, also unstated, was five arrows each, alternating, with the count kept out loud, because Thalia had said on the first Friday that she wanted the count out loud and he had said *fine* and had kept it out loud ever since, in a flat monotone that made a good score sound like a delivery.

"Nine."

"Ten."

"Nine."

"Nine."

"You're enjoying that."

"I'm recording it," Calder said.

She put her last arrow into the gold by two inches and turned around with her hands on her hips in the middle of the brown field in the cold, and he did not say well done, which was correct, and did not say anything else either, which was less correct, and she looked at where he was looking and found that he was looking at her draw hand.

"Set your fingers before you raise," he said. "You set them on the way up. When the wind's off the left you lose the low ones."

"I don't lose the low ones."

"You lost two on Sunday and you lost one today. Low left, both times. It's half the wind and half the fingers, and the fingers are the half you can fix in an afternoon."

It was the kind of correction that was worth more than the arrow, and they both knew it, and Thalia felt the familiar and completely unwelcome sensation of being seen accurately by somebody who was not going to make anything of having done it. Four years she had been shooting with an elbow that dropped, fingers that set late, and a heel with opinions, and not one person on this hill had said a word about any of it. Every one of those people had a good reason for keeping quiet about Thalia Grace, and not one of those good reasons had been as useful to her as a sentence.

"Again," she said, and they shot again. She set her fingers before she raised, and the low left went where the low left should have gone, and the field took the arrows back one at a time and gave nothing else while the light went on down the grass toward the road.

At the end of it he sat down on the grass at the end of the line, his back against the straw of the last target, his knees up, which was what he had done the first Friday, like a man who had walked a distance and meant to be there a while, and she stayed standing, because sitting down would have meant the heel, and the heel was nobody's business but its own.

"How's third place," she said, to be pleasant, to be a problem.

"Full," he said. "Bench at six, and a shop that's warm by eight, and nine lanterns on a path I helped hang. I've got a letter in my pocket from a kid at the other forges about a pump housing. It's a good life."

"You get letters from kids about pumps."

"Tyson. He writes like a telegram and he's right about the pump, which is the annoying part." He turned his mug around on the grass with one finger, slowly, the way he did everything slowly and everything at once. "He writes to you too, doesn't he."

"Everybody writes to Tyson," Thalia said, which was true and also not an answer, and she let the not-answer sit there, and the wind went over the field, and down the line a target rope tapped its post in the small regular rhythm of a thing that had been tapped ten thousand times and expected ten thousand more.

Then, up on the road, two people came from the direction of the Big House: one with a folder of fair sheets under her arm and her hair coming out of its knot in the particular way it came out when she had been drafting at a table for three hours and had then walked outside and remembered wind, and one behind her with his hands in a coat that had been sized for a person a size bigger, his chin down, listening to something. Thalia watched them come up the road for the length of a breath, and then found, to her own complete annoyance, that she had stopped watching the road.

She was watching the man beside her stand up, shake the grass off the back of his coat, and walk down the line to where her fifth arrow had gone in low and left, and pull it out, and look at the entry angle in the light, and put it in the quiver with the others. And she was watching all of this from a position of total stillness in the middle of a field, with her bow in one hand and her mouth, she suspected, slightly open.

"Grace," said Annabeth Grace, from the gate, in the pleasant and entirely lethal voice she used for facts.

"Don't start."

"I haven't started."

"You're doing the start. That's the start. You did the start in June about the canoe and the start in August about the map and I know the start when it's being started."

"The start," Annabeth said, "was going to be that the light's going, that you have three arrows in the butt, and that you have not pulled them."

"They're pulled."

"There are three in the butt."

"She's been counting them for two minutes, Annie," said Percy, in his coat, in the voice he used for buoy lines. "Let her have the group. She's got a face on."

"I do not have a face on."

"You've had a face on since the gate," Annabeth said. "It's a specific face. It's the face you had in June when you said you didn't care about the canoe race."

"I didn't care about the canoe race."

"You cared about the canoe race."

"I cared about *winning* the canoe race, which is a completely different category of caring and which you would know if anybody in this family read anything I said out loud." Thalia pointed the bow at her sister with the whole enormous conviction of a girl who had been winning arguments in this family since her sister was small enough to be carried. "I pulled the arrows. Watch. I'm pulling arrows. This is a person pulling arrows, in the cold, on a Friday, for America."

They stayed, which was the worst possible outcome and also, she found when she looked at the finding, the one she had wanted, and the four of them went down the line together in the going-dark: Thalia first with the arrows, Percy behind her counting them, Annabeth walking the row with her folder under her arm and saying nothing whatsoever, and up at the road the last man on the field picking up two mugs and following at the distance a person follows when he has understood the situation completely and has decided to be useful about it instead of discuss it.

At the gate Thalia gave Annabeth the whole of her face: bright, wide, entirely empty, the face of a girl with nothing on it anywhere.

"Sunday," she said. "Eight o'clock. Elbow. You can walk up with me if you like and you can tell whoever you want on the way. Tell Clarisse. She'll write it on a list and it'll still be on the list in November and I'll still be denying it."

"You don't have to tell me anything," Annabeth said.

"Correct. And yet here we all are, in a gate, being a family in the dark."

Calder came up behind them with the two mugs and handed the empty one to Thalia, who took it, because by then taking it was simply what her hands did, and he said to Annabeth, with the flat courtesy he used on everything from a broken hinge to a centaur's paperwork, "She's setting her fingers late. She's fixed the elbow. It's a good arm."

"Thank you," said Annabeth.

"You're welcome." He nodded up the road. "Twenty minutes of light. It's all uphill."

And that was the whole of it, and nothing was said about it, which was the correct way to end a Friday on a brown field. Thalia walked up the hill beside her sister in the dark with a borrowed tin mug in her hand, her heel saying what it said, her elbow fixed, a group she had counted twice; she did not mention any of it, was mentioned by nobody, and was, by the private arithmetic she did not do on the path, completely fine.

Silena Beauregard had been carrying a glove around for six days.

It had started on the morning after the storm, when Beckendorf had come down for porridge in a room full of other people's drying coats and she had put one mended glove on the kitchen corner of the long table and he had picked it up and looked at it for slightly too long. She had said *bring me the other one* out loud in front of her sister, and he had said *that's what I was going to say*; then he had gone up the hill, she had gone on with the porridge, and the left glove had come down to Cabin Three in his coat pocket and stayed there.

She had done it on Wednesday night, at the end of the table, after the bread was out and the dishes were done and the room had gone to the particular quiet that a stove makes when it is the last thing awake. Two finger seams split at the index and the middle, which was the way a work glove died, and the cuff gone soft in the wrist where the hand went in a hundred times a day. She had taken the seams apart properly rather than tacking them, and put a facing in the cuff out of the grey flannel off the quilt pile, and pressed it flat under the bread board with a bowl of water on top of it, and left it there the way you leave a thing that has decided what it is.

So she had had it for six days, and for six days she had done what any reasonable person would do with a work glove, which was to leave it on the shelf by the recipe tin, and then move it to the shelf by the door, and then move it back, twice, and to tell herself on Thursday that the forge would be busy, and on Friday that the forge would be busy.

The stove was going, and the soup was on at the back with a whole ham bone in it that had been Clarisse's doing — Clarisse had produced it at noon from somewhere in the woodshed with the air of a woman who had been carrying a ham bone around since September and had finally found the room to put it down. The cornbread was mixed and resting under a cloth. It was half past four and the light was still in the high windows, thin and October-coloured, and nobody in the cabin needed her for forty minutes, and the forty minutes were the problem, because forty minutes was exactly long enough to walk down a hill and back.

She put on her coat, took the glove off the shelf wrapped in the square of clean cloth she had put it on on Wednesday, put the square in her coat pocket, and went out into a cold that had come up under the door while her back was turned.

The west path was empty and the lamps were not lit yet, which meant she had ten minutes of grey to get down and ten minutes of grey to get up, and she walked it fast, past the boathouse turn and through the cedar brake where the smell went from cold leaf to cold pine all at once, with her hands in her pockets and the cloth square bumping against her knuckles the whole way, which she found she could feel through her coat and which she did not move to the other pocket.

The forge was warm before she reached the door, the way it was always warm before she reached the door, and it came out at her in a long low breath that smelled of coke and hot scale and the sharp clean oil on the bench. Two kids were at the far end doing something to a gate hinge. Beckendorf was at his own bench with his sleeves rolled and his head down over a bracket that had been marked out in chalk, and he looked up when the boards creaked, and looked at her, and put the chisel down, which was, she had decided in September, the only compliment in the language of that room.

"You're in my light," she said, from the doorway, brilliantly.

"You're standing in the door."

"I'm here for a second." She crossed the shop in four steps and took the cloth square out of her pocket and put it on the bench between the chalk and the oil can, and unfolded it, because a glove presented in a cloth was a glove presented like an occasion and she had decided at the gate that it would not be one. "Left hand. The wrist was gone and both finger seams were open at the stitch line, so I took them apart instead of tacking them, and I put a facing in the cuff because the flannel was in the tin and the flannel was the right weight, and that's the mending, start to finish."

He picked the glove up and turned it over and put it on, which she had not planned for and was not ready for, and flexed the fingers twice, and looked at the cuff where the grey sat neat and flat against the old brown wool.

"That's the second one that's better than it started," he said.

"Bring me a third one."

"I will." He said it in the flat voice, the one that reported the strength of materials, and took the glove off and put it down on the bench, and then reached past her, to the shelf above the bench where the drawings lived in a clip — and took down a folded square of workshop paper, grey, cut square with a knife, and held it out to her between two fingers the way he held a washer.

"Here," he said.

It was not a large thing and it was not a heavy thing and it weighed nothing, and Silena took it, because the alternative to taking it from Charlie Beckendorf in his own shop on a Friday afternoon was a conversation, and the conversation was not what she had walked down a hill for. She put it in her coat pocket without opening it, and her hand stayed in that pocket for a moment longer than a pocket needs, and she was aware of every degree of the temperature in that room.

"Right," she said.

"Right," he said, and picked up the chisel, and that, as far as the shop was concerned, was that.

There was a sentence she could have said. There were about four of them and she had them all in her head in a row on the way down, and on the way back up she had them all in her head in a row again, and she said none of them, because the door of the forge had closed behind her and the path was grey and the lamps were starting to come on down at the boathouse turn, and a girl with a folded square of paper in her pocket walked up a hill in the last of the light and did not open it until the cedar brake, where nobody could see her from anywhere at all.

She opened it there, with her back against a pine and the cold coming down the path at her, and read it standing up, in the blue part of the evening, with her thumb on the edge of the fold so it would not tear.

*Glove's back. It's better than the one I've got. I wore the other one today to hold cold stock and took it off twice to look at the stitching, which is not a thing I have ever done with a glove.*

*That drawing of yours. I measured the shop wall on Thursday to check myself, which is a habit and not a comment. Your window's fine and my sink was wrong, same as I said on Saturday. But I went back and looked at where the light falls at four in the afternoon, and you had it right on the card before anybody said anything to you about it, and I've been thinking about that more than I've been thinking about the sink.*

*Four letters on a wall over a bench is the whole of what I've written in my life. This is the fifth. There's a bracket in the corner. It's how I sign things. That's a good drawing. I'd build it. It's on paper now.*

She read it twice in the brake, and then a third time, slowly, in the way you read a number you have already read correctly and want to be sure of anyway, and then she folded it along its own crease, which was a knife-fold and not a soft fold, and put it back in her pocket, and stood under the pines for a moment with her hand over it, and then walked up the rest of the hill at a completely normal speed.

The cabin was warm and smelled of the ham bone, and Clarisse had the winter list in front of her at the table with a pencil laid beside it, in the posture she reserved for the part of the afternoon where she was not going to do anything about the list but was going to be near it.

"You went down the hill," Clarisse said, without looking up.

"I went down the hill."

"In the cold."

"It wasn't cold."

"It's cold. It's been cold since Tuesday. I've got a thermometer and a stove and an opinion." Clarisse turned a page she did not need to turn. "Dinner's at six. The bread's on the board and don't touch it, it's for the table. And the third lamp down the path wants its wick trimming, and I noticed it four times today, so if you're going out anyway you can take the snuffers."

"I wasn't going out again."

"Right," Clarisse said, in exactly the voice, and Silena went into the kitchen corner and put the soup spoon in her hand and did not throw anything at her sister, which was its own form of losing an argument gracefully.

At ten past five she took her coat off the hook to hang it properly and felt the folded square in the pocket, and took it out, and read it again at the window in the last of the grey with the soup going behind her, and this time she read the third paragraph twice on its own, which was the paragraph about the letters, and the bracket, and how a person signs a thing.

Then she opened the recipe tin on the shelf. Salt in the top layer, the good honey in the middle, and in the bottom, under the folded square of paper with the pencil drawing of a room on it that had been living there since the summer, a gap exactly the size of a folded square of grey workshop paper. She put the note in the gap, flat, and closed the lid, and put the tin back on the shelf in the exact spot the tin always sat, and went to get the cornbread, and had the whole dinner on the table by six with nothing on her face at all, which was, she thought, the single most impressive thing she had done all week, and which nobody in the entire camp would ever know about.

---

Cabin Three's long table took twelve on a plain night and took fifteen on a Friday, and the extra three knew exactly who they were.

Two of them came in together from up the hill, the way they had been arriving since September: Annabeth with the field book gone flat and tired under her arm and her collar up, and Thalia behind her with her coat still on and her bow left standing in the rack by the door, which was the same rack where two tin mugs now sat, set down without ceremony by a girl who had walked up a hill in the dark carrying them and had clearly been asked about them on the way and had clearly won.

The third was Marcus, who came in at ten past six with Olive on his shoulder like a sack of flour, both of them red to the ears from the path, both of them already talking, Marcus reporting and Olive contradicting, in the state they had been in since the mountain, which was the state of two people who had spent nine hours on a ridge with five other people and had come home related to them.

Silena put the pot on the table herself, which meant nobody helped, which was the rule. White beans and a ham bone going to pieces in it, cornbread black at the rim of the skillet and pale gold in the middle, a dish of squash from the Big House garden that had come down with Thalia in exchange for nothing, bread from the morning, honey, and the tea in the big brown pot that tasted the way Friday tasted.

"The stove's been going since two," Percy said, sitting down.

"The stove's been going since two," Silena agreed, "and the stove has not once told anybody about it, which is the difference between the stove and the rest of this cabin."

"I helped."

"You carried wood."

"Two loads. In the cold. With commitment."

"Sit *down*, Percy."

He sat down. That was the thing about his sister's orders: they arrived with a ladder in them and there was no version of the third rung that did not involve doing the thing, and he had discovered in the last month that he did not mind this at all, that he had in fact started to find it the most restful sound in the building, the way anyone finds the sound of a door that is being kept shut against the weather restful.

The windows had gone black at twenty past six, all three of them, and the room had turned itself into a room: the stove's iron door glowing at the low orange it held when it had been fed and left alone, the two high windows doing nothing but reflecting the lamp back at itself, the table gone to noise. Fifteen people, three pots, two baskets of bread, and the particular October dark outside that was not a darkness so much as a wall, standing a yard past the glass, waiting without interest.

Marcus reported the road: he had walked down at seven that morning, and the sack had been off the hook, which meant the truck had come at six like it always came at six, and whatever forty people had put on that hook at dusk on Thursday was by now in a county office with a stamp on it.

"So in town," Thalia said, "right now, at this exact moment, a man is holding a letter from my sister and does not know he's in danger."

"Your sister doesn't write dangerous letters," Annabeth said.

"My sister writes *observations*. My sister once wrote a man a paragraph about a hinge and I watched him read it twice." Thalia took the bread basket with the air of a person claiming a prize. "Letters are power. That's all I'm saying. It's Friday, the sack went, and this hill has a hinge opinion going out into the world."

"It was a well-made hinge," Annabeth said, with enormous calm, and Percy put his face in his soup, and the table went over, and Clarisse, at the head, said, "If the mail's finished, the squash isn't," and started the pot going round again.

Olive, halfway through her second bowl, looked up with the frank directness of a ten-year-old who had walked nine hours on a leg the length of a chair leg, and said, "Is it true your name's up on the wall in the Big House with a bracket around it?"

"It's not a bracket," Percy said. "It's a qualification."

"It's a bracket," Annabeth said.

"Tell her what it says," Clarisse said, to Percy, in the voice that meant the entertainment was going to come from his direction whether or not he volunteered.

"It says *Jackson, P. — load, enthusiasm*."

"What's enthusiasm?" Olive asked.

"It's the part she ran out of ordinary words for," Clarisse said, without any change of expression whatever, and then, into the pause that opened up in front of her like a doorway, walked the sentence that had apparently been waiting in her all evening: "He didn't volunteer. She read out a list of names in a tone of voice and his name was the one that came out of her sounding like a bridge. Load is the job. Enthusiasm is what he added by standing in a creek with his socks off and calling it warmer than the lake."

"I *was* warmer than the lake."

"Everything's warmer than the lake," said Clarisse, "in October," and then Percy laughed so hard he had to put his spoon down, and Marcus laughed because Percy laughed, and Olive said "load, enthusiasm" into her bowl twice to get the shape of it, and Annabeth sat at the far end with her chin in her hand and let the whole thing happen to her face without defending herself once, which Percy let stand.

Thalia came in from the porch at that point, coat off now and draped over the back of her chair in the specific way that made it Annabeth's chair's problem, and sat down and took the squash dish without anybody offering it and said, "I've had a day."

"You've had a field," Annabeth said. "You came up the hill with two mugs."

"One mug."

"There were two on the rack."

"One of them is a *concept*," Thalia said, and the table went again, and Percy, who had been watching his girlfriend's sister fight a losing war against a room she had started, said, kindly, "Two mugs, though."

"One of them was for the man who brought the tea," Thalia said, with the tremendous dignity of a person who had run out of road, "and the tea was a crime, and I am never discussing it again, and if anybody at this table says the word *date* I will tell Chiron about the canoe."

"You didn't lose a canoe," Clarisse said, from the head of the table, without looking up, in the flat and entirely peaceful voice of a woman laying a trap that had been baited since June.

"So you were on a date," said Clarisse.

The room did not fall about it. The room detonated. Percy's soup went the wrong way entirely and Annabeth made a sound she had never once made in public and Olive asked what a date was and was told by three people at once not to worry about it, and Thalia Grace, eighteen years old, daughter of Zeus, who had stood in a gate all summer and told the weather what to do, put both her elbows on the table and her face in her hands and said, into her own palms, in a voice of absolute murder, "It was a *field*. It had *straw* in it. There were *targets*."

"Four spoons of sugar," Annabeth said, to the room, in a voice of perfect lightness, and Clarisse, who had not moved a muscle, poured the tea.

It took another four minutes to get Thalia's elbows off the table and another ten to get her to eat, at which point Annabeth, without being asked and without any change of expression, moved the dish of squash to her sister's side of the table and put the second helping on her plate and went back to her own soup.

"I didn't ask," Thalia said.

"You never ask."

"You moved it like a waitress."

"I moved it like your sister," Annabeth said, and Thalia looked at her for a second too long, and then ate the squash, and said nothing, which in that family was a sentence all on its own and got filed as one.

The cornbread went round and then went round again, and the argument that had been simmering at the near end of the table since the skillet came out finally came to a head over the corner piece, the one with the black edge and the soft middle, which was the piece that everybody at that table had been pretending not to look at for eight minutes.

Percy got it first, which was his mistake, because Clarisse had been watching the corner the way she watched everything she intended to have.

"That's mine," she said.

"It's on my plate."

"It was on the *table* on your plate, which is a different location." She held out her hand, palm up, the way a woman collects a tool off a bench. "I made the fire. I split the oak. The corner goes to whoever carried the wood in."

"I carried wood in."

"In September. The code's daily." Clarisse's hand did not move. "Silena. Rule."

Silena, who had been waiting at the stove for precisely this and had a spoon in each hand like a woman with a pair of scales, said, without turning around, "The corner goes to whoever's been outside the most today."

"I was outside for *hours*," Percy said.

"You were in the Big House."

"The Big House has a porch."

"Porch doesn't count," Silena said.

"Since when does the porch not count?"

"Since I made the cornbread, and since it's my stove and my table and my corner piece by descent, and since you have been in that building since ten o'clock this morning doing paperwork for a woman with a clipboard, and I know because I put the lunch in your bag." She turned around and pointed the serving spoon at Thalia. "Give it to the archer."

"Thank you," said Thalia, and took the corner off Percy's plate with two fingers and ate it in front of him, in the middle of the sentence he was starting, with her eyebrows up, and Percy looked around the entire table for an ally and found, in order: his other sister, holding the bread basket like a shield; his girlfriend, who had put her pencil behind her ear and was watching with the expression of a person reading a well-built mechanism; Marcus, who had learned in September that the Poseidon table was not a place for volunteers; and Olive, who said, "I'd have given you mine," and was told by everybody simultaneously that she was the only decent person in the cabin.

After that the table did what a Friday table did, which was to go quiet in the middle without anybody deciding to. The pot went round a third time, because there was a third time in it. Marcus told the story of the gate hinge he and two Apollo kids had got stuck at four o'clock and it got worse each time he told it and he told it twice on purpose. Rosie, from the far end, wanted to know whether the tally up in the Big House had found its fortieth mark yet, because there had been a rumour in September and October was half gone, and Annabeth said, "It says thirty-nine," and left it there the way you leave a stone where it lies, and nobody picked it up, and the rumour went on being a rumour.

Silena wrote to Tyson on Wednesday, she reported, at the end of the meal, when the plates were going back into the pile and the tea was on its second pot, and Percy said he had written his own line in the same envelope, and she said, "I saw your line. It says *come back and the stove's yours, also Clarisse says hi, she doesn't, I wrote that part*."

"I wrote that part myself."

"You wrote the stove. I wrote the rest because I have the handwriting and you have the nerve." She stacked the last two plates and stood up with the pile against her chest and looked at the room the way she looked at a room she had fed: fast, and sideways, and with her hands already deciding what came next. "Tyson writes back on Sundays from the forges when the fires are out. That's the arrangement. He'll come when he comes and the stove'll keep."

"The stove'll keep," Percy agreed, and meant it about more than the stove, and did not say so, because his sister had a pile of plates and a look on her that meant she wanted the kitchen to herself for eight minutes, and he had been raised — twice, in two different kinds of weather — by women who counted in minutes.

Clarisse went out to the porch at twenty past seven with the snuffers, because the third lamp down the path had been bothering her since Wednesday in the way a lamp bothers a person who has hung it themselves, and came back in a minute and a half with the wick trimmed and the glass wiped and the cold all over her sleeves, and reported, to nobody, "Nine lit. Ours is on the hook. All good."

"That's everything?" Percy said.

"That's the whole report. Everything else is on a list and the list is face down, and it's Friday, and nobody's reading the list to a table." She sat down and put her hands around the mug Silena had put in front of her without being asked, and the stove ticked, and outside the three black windows the hill went on being a wall with nine points of amber laid along it, and the room got, in the way rooms get on a Friday at half past seven in October, about two degrees warmer and about four years quieter, without anybody's permission.

"Porch," said Marcus, from the doorway, in the tone of a man making a motion at a meeting, and Olive seconded it, and then fifteen people got into approximately seven coats, because in a family with one stove and a porch there is no such thing as going out; there is only being sent.

Annabeth Grace had a rule about Fridays that she had never told anybody, which was that Friday was the only night of the week the day was allowed to stop.

The rule had not existed in June. In June she had not needed it, because in June the days stopped themselves, out of some arrangement between the lake and the weather that October had clearly not been told about. Now, in the middle of the month, the days ran on past six and past the soup and into the dark without any sign of having finished, and she had noticed, about three Fridays ago, that she was the only person at the table still holding a spoon at half past nine, and that the reason was not hunger.

So the rule: Friday. Somewhere between the last of the tea and the point where the hill went completely quiet, the day got put down, on purpose, and she was permitted — she had decided this herself, in the field book, in the margin, where all her real decisions were made — to stop holding it.

The porch of Cabin Three was the tenth lantern's porch, and the tenth lantern was on the hook by the door, and it was, she thought, stepping out into the cold with the blanket from the end bunk over her arm, the best-made object in the whole camp. She had measured eleven lamps that autumn, and this one's glass was the thickest of them, and its wind-gate was cut a quarter inch proud so it could not flare, and she had never once said so to anybody, because Beckendorf knew it and because saying so would have made it a compliment instead of a fact.

The others were out there in the dark at the far end of the boards: Marcus with his back to the rail and Olive asleep against his shoulder with her mouth open and absolutely no dignity about it; two of the cabin kids sitting on the step below with a cup between them; Clarisse standing at the rail in her coat with her arms crossed, doing the thing she did at night, which was to look down the hill and count the lights without moving her lips. Percy was on the third step, in his coat, with his hands in his pockets, in the posture he had of late taken to describing as *not a lighthouse, just standing here*, which was a description that fooled nobody who had ever watched him walk a girl up a hill in the dark and then stay.

"You brought the blanket," he said.

"I brought the blanket."

"Which one."

"The grey one off the end bunk. Not the quilt. The quilt lives at the ring now, Clarisse's ruling, and I'm not going to be the person who brings the north beach fire ring up the hill in a sack."

"Good instinct." He moved over on the third step, which was not quite enough room for a blanket and two people, and they both knew that, and neither of them said anything about the arithmetic, and she sat down beside him and put the blanket over both their knees, and the cold came up off the lake at the same moment, on schedule, the way it did every night at about the time the fire in the stove decided it was finished.

Down the path, in order, the way they had been hung: one at the boathouse turn, two in the cedar brake, the odd one at the curve, and then the long straight stretch down toward the road, and the frost was already writing itself into the low ground at the edges where the light did not reach, the thin silver lace it had been putting down every night for a week. She counted them without meaning to. One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, and the tenth over her head, and the dark between each of them, and none of the darknesses the same size, which was the part she liked: the lamps had been set by somebody walking, not by somebody measuring, and the result was a rhythm instead of an interval, and she had decided she preferred the rhythm and had not told Clarisse, because telling Clarisse would have produced a second set of intervals on paper by morning.

"Thursday's note is in the back cover," she said, because it was Friday and because the rule said the day got put down and putting it down meant saying the true thing at the end of it.

"I know. I saw you put it there."

"It's behind the survey sheets, under the oilcloth flap, and the oilcloth flap is not a security system."

"It's a *system*," Percy said. "It's a flap. It's got tension."

"It's got a curl. If someone wanted your note they'd have it in four seconds and you'd never know."

"If someone wants my note they can have my note, Annie. It says the same thing it always says. It's not a secret, it's a habit." He took his hands out of his pockets and put one of them, palm up, on the blanket between his knee and hers, in the small plain way he had been doing since June, which was not a request and not a demand and not even, exactly, an invitation: it was more like a sentence being set down on a table where both of them could reach it.

She put her hand in his. Her fingers were cold and his were colder, which was wrong by every law of their family tree and right by every law of the two of them, and his closed around hers slowly, the way he did everything that mattered, and then his thumb moved once across her knuckle, one pass, unhurried, and then again, and the second time it was not a caress so much as a check, the way you check a thing you are holding to find out whether it is still there.

"Your hands are always cold," he said.

"My hands are always cold in October."

"Your hands are always cold in a room with a stove in it, and that's the part I don't get, because I've stood in that room, and that stove is a *presence*."

"A presence with a door that leaks at the bottom left corner, which is why the left side of the room is warm and the right side is where the bread goes to get a skin." She leaned into him, shoulder to shoulder, and the blanket took her weight and gave it back, and she felt his shoulder move under her own and then settle. "I'm going to fix the door."

"You're going to file the door."

"I'm going to do both. I'm going to file it and then fix it and then write down that I fixed it, in the margin, in pencil, where nobody looks."

"That's my girlfriend," Percy said, in the voice of a man proudly describing a bridge.

She laughed, which surprised her, which was the whole point of the rule, and she turned her face into the side of his coat because it smelled like the woodpile and the stove and the cold, and stayed there for a moment with her eyes shut, and felt him tip his head until it rested against hers, and neither of them spoke for long enough that the two kids on the step below went quiet too, and the quiet went out across the dark like something being poured.

The kiss, when it happened, happened the way the good ones did now, which was not at all like the first ones. The first ones had been events. This one was a decision that two people had already made separately and were now making together at the same speed, and it started with her turning her face up and his hand coming off the blanket to the side of her jaw, cold fingers and all, and her saying nothing at all, and him saying nothing at all, which was the part she could not get over, that they had gotten to the point where the sentence was unnecessary.

It was slow. It was slow in the way that a long thing is slow, with its own weather inside it: the first part, which was soft and certain and over too soon; and then the part where he did not move back, and she felt his breath go out through his nose against her cheek, and his mouth found hers again at an angle, and the second half began, the half that was its own beginning. The lantern above them burned steadily in its grey-surrounded amber. The cold climbed up the steps and gave up about a foot short of their knees. Somewhere down at the bottom of the hill a lamp in the brake flickered in a gust and came back, and neither of them heard it, and the blanket lay over both their knees with nobody holding either end of it.

When they came apart they did not go far. They went the distance of one breath and then half of another, and stopped with their foreheads together, and she kept her hand where it was, flat on his chest under the coat, feeling the ordinary enormous business of him breathing, and he kept his thumb on the line of her jaw, not moving, not doing anything except being a place where his hand lived.

"You're smiling," he said.

"I'm exhaling. It's a Friday. It's the exhale."

"Same thing, at your end." His hand slid from her jaw to the back of her neck, under the hair, warm, and stayed there, and she let her eyes close again, because the rule allowed it and because the weight of his arm arriving across her shoulders was, after fourteen years of a life she remembered and thirteen and a half of one she did not, one of about six sounds in the world she had never had to translate.

They sat like that for a while. The blanket went down over her knees and his, and her head came to rest against his shoulder, and his came to rest on top of hers, and the position that resulted was bad engineering by any measure she respected, because it put both their necks at an angle and one of them would pay for it in the morning, and the two of them sat on the third step of a cabin at the bottom of a hill and did the thing she had been letting herself do since the ridge, which was nothing, for a length of time she did not measure and would not have written down.

Below them the lamps went on. Marcus carried Olive up the hill at twenty past nine without waking her, which was a feat of engineering she would have written down if she had not been under a blanket; the two kids on the lower step finished their cup and went in; Clarisse said, at the rail, without turning around, "Stove's banked. Door's latched. Don't be an hour," which was a permission and an instruction and a goodnight, in the order Clarisse kept such things, and went in and shut the door on the cold.

And then there were two people on the step and ten lights on a hill, and the lake below them doing what it did in October, which was to lie there in the dark being the largest thing for miles and not mentioning it.

"Tomorrow's the harvest," Annabeth said, eventually.

"Tomorrow's the harvest," Percy agreed. "Chiron said the word *harvest* at lunch in the voice he uses for paperwork, which means there will be ladders, and games with rules, and something with cider in it, and Clarisse will win at least two of the games and will describe it as the cabin's result."

"Clarisse wins everything. Clarisse won the *quiet* game last week and then told us the score."

"That's my sister." He was quiet for a moment, and then said, in a different register, lower, the one he saved for things he had decided long ago and never revisited: "I'm going to check the heron in the morning."

"You check the heron every morning."

"I check the heron most mornings. There's a difference and it's called honesty." He turned his hand over under the blanket and laced his fingers through hers again, and this time it was the slow way, the way that took a second and a half and made a small sound against the wool. "It's been on that bow since August. It hasn't moved. It hasn't nested, it hasn't eaten anything I've seen, and it stands on a boat that's underwater, Annie. I've decided that's a tenant, and I've decided the rent is me noticing."

"The rent is you noticing," she said, and could not keep the warmth out of it, and did not try.

"It's a good lease. It's the most reliable thing I do." He lifted his head and looked at her, and the lantern above them caught the side of his face in the way it had no business doing at ten o'clock on a Friday in the cold, and she looked back at him steadily, because she was fourteen and she was tired and she was in love with him in a way that had survived two sets of parents and a mountain and a year that had not been kind to anybody, and because looking was free. "Annie."

"Mm."

"Stay out here a bit."

"That wasn't a question."

"It wasn't meant as one." His arm tightened across her shoulders, one notch, the way you take up slack in a line, and she made herself heavy against him on purpose, and felt him breathe out, and the lamps went on being ten lamps, and the frost went on coming up the path toward the boards at the rate it had been coming up the path every night for a week, and neither of them was counting it, and neither of them was going in.

Thalia Grace walked up the west path at ten past ten with her hands in her coat and her bow at home in the rack two hundred yards behind her, and let the lamps do the work.

That was the new thing about the hill, and she had not decided whether she liked it. In June the path up from the cabins was a thing you did in the dark with a hand on whatever was nearest, and you learned it the way you learned a face: by getting it wrong a few times. Now it was nine points of amber laid end to end down the slope, and a person could walk it at any hour with her boots and her opinions and not put a foot wrong, and the only thing she had to do was pass each one in turn and be, for about four seconds at a time, a lit person.

She passed the boathouse turn. She passed the brake, where the smell went pine and then cold and then pine again. She passed the odd one at the curve, the one Beckendorf had hung crooked and then re-hung twice on a Wednesday with Silena holding the ladder and telling him it had been straight the first time, which it had not, and which neither of them had ever mentioned since.

The frost was doing its thing at the edges. She could see it laid into the grass either side of the path in the thin silver way it came down, and she could hear it, which was the part people forgot: frost had a sound, a small tight creak, like a rope taking strain, and you only heard it walking alone at ten at night with nothing else going.

She had left the mugs on the rack by the Cabin Three door. Both of them. She had put the second one down beside the first without making a thing of it and had then, before she could decide not to, watched her own sister's eyes go from the mugs to her face and back, and had decided, at the door, in the cold, in front of witnesses, not to pick either of them up, because picking them up would have started a conversation and leaving them was a fact, and Sunday was two days away, and the rack was where mugs lived.

This was fine. This was a completely ordinary way for a Friday to end, and she was eighteen years old, and she had shot thirty-two arrows in a brown field and eaten two helpings of beans and the corner piece of a cornbread she had not had to fight for, and she had been called a word at a table by a girl with a serving spoon and had not thrown anything, and it all sat in her the way a good day sat, which was: warm, and slightly too big for the room it was in, and impossible to report.

Cabin One was dark when she got to it, which meant Annabeth was still down at the bottom of the hill, which meant Annabeth was on a step under a blanket, and Thalia knew this the way she knew the frost, by sound and by absence, and did not think anything further about it, except that she herself was going to sit on her own porch for ten minutes and then go in and check the window latch.

She sat on the top step instead of the rail, because the rail was a standing thing and the step was a sitting thing and the heel had opinions. Then, because there was nobody within two hundred yards and because she had been carrying it since the field, she took off her left boot and put it on the step beside her and looked at the heel of it, which was what she did instead of saying she was tired.

It was a good boot — a boot that had gone up a mountain in September and come down again, with a quarter inch of wear on the outside edge that no cobbler on this hill could talk her out of, and when she pressed the sole with her thumb there was an ache in it that went up into the ankle and stopped there, politely, the way a well-behaved ache stops. Hot cloth. That was the deal she had made with herself in the Big House porch on Sunday morning: hot cloth, and nothing else, until Thursday. It was Friday. She had shot twice on it and said nothing to a man who had corrected four years of her arms in one hour and had then brought her a mug of something with four spoons of sugar in it and called it Friday, and she had told her sister nothing, and Clarisse had written a line on a list about a heel and had not said it out loud once, and the ache sat in her ankle and stopped, politely, at the top.

"You're sitting in the dark with your boot off."

Thalia did not start, because a Grace sister does not start, but the boot came off the step and back onto her foot with a speed that would have embarrassed her in front of a witness.

"I'm sitting in the dark with my boot *on*," she said. "You're walking up a hill at ten past ten in October with a blanket over your arm like a woman carrying a rug out of a fire."

"A blanket. Singular." Annabeth came up the three steps and sat down on the top one beside her, in her coat, with her hair down and her field book under her arm and the grey blanket folded over it, and her hands, as always, cold, and her face, as always, doing the thing where it looked like nothing and reported everything. "It came back up the hill with me. It wasn't going to carry itself."

"Mm." Thalia waited exactly as long as was kind, and then said, "Your coat smells like woodsmoke and his coat."

"It's a porch."

"It's a *verdict*," Thalia said. "It's ten past ten and you've got a fold line across your sleeve where the blanket was and your hair's come down on one side, and I want you to know that I noticed all of it and that I am being extremely good about it."

"I noticed that you're being extremely good about it," Annabeth said. "It's worrying."

"I'm turning over a new leaf. New leaf. Big leaf. Big sister leaf." She put her elbows on her knees and looked down the hill, and the hill lay there in the dark doing the thing she had come out to look at, which was the whole nine lamps at once, laid out end to end below her like something spilled on purpose, with the tenth burning down at the bottom by a cabin door, and between each of them the frost-lace and the black grass and the small tight creak of a world getting cold in the right order.

"Good field?" Annabeth said, after a while.

"Good field."

"Elbow's holding?"

"Elbow's holding." Thalia let the answer sit for three seconds and then added, because her sister had asked in the voice that asked, and because the Friday rule went both ways: "He brought a mug out to the line. Two mugs. He walked out with two mugs at four in the afternoon because he'd been told — no, because he'd *worked out* — that a person who shoots for an hour in October needs the hands done first. And he didn't say anything about it. He said *this is for the rest of you*, and put it in my hand, and then told me my fingers were setting late, and he was right, and I have been thinking about the mug for six hours, which I consider a failure of character."

"Six hours," Annabeth said.

"Six hours. On and off. Between the soup and the cornbread and the part where Clarisse destroyed me at my own table, there was the mug. There was a lot of the mug." She was quiet a moment, and the lamps went on being lamps, and then she said, without turning her head, in the voice she used for the small true things: "Nobody's ever done that. Brought me a thing I needed before I'd said I needed it. Everybody on this hill has needed things from me since I was twelve, Annie. I'm the one who goes out. I didn't know there was a version of it where somebody comes out with a mug."

Annabeth did not say anything for a moment, which was the correct thing to do and which her sister had, in the last year, gotten very good at doing.

"You could tell him that," she said, at last.

"I could *shoot* a man. That doesn't mean it's a plan." Thalia put her boot back on properly and stood up and stretched like a person who had been sitting down on purpose and not because of anything. "It's Friday. It's your rule and I'm not going to ask what it says because knowing you it says something in the margin in pencil. Go and be fourteen. I'm going in."

"Thalia."

"Hm."

"The mug's a good leaf," Annabeth said, from the top step, in the flat warm voice that had been hers since about June, and Thalia Grace, who had been told all her life that she was Zeus's daughter and had spent this whole year finding out that she was somebody's older sister, put her hands in her coat and went inside and checked the window latch, and the stove, and the lamp, and did not stand at the window afterward for any longer than it takes to look at a hill and be sure of it.

Below her, on a step at the bottom of the path, two people and a blanket went on not going in. Nine lamps burned between her and them and a tenth burned over their heads, and the frost came up the low ground at the edges at the rate it had all week, and somewhere past the boathouse turn and the brake and the curve the whole camp lay down under a sky that had more stars in it than anybody in it had names for, and the week — the wet one and the ridge one and the survey one and the mail one, all four of them carried up a hill and put on a wall and written down in three different kinds of handwriting — went on turning, slowly, the way a heavy thing turns, toward Saturday.
