## Chapter 79: Elena's Tale

The fire had burned down to coals the color of orange peels.

It was that kind of late at Camp Half-Blood when dinner was long over and the conch had never sounded for dismissal, when nobody wanted to be the first to stand up and break the circle. The April night was cool despite the afternoon warmth, the air smelling of pine smoke and lake water and marshmallow sugar. Sparks lifted straight up into a sky crowded with stars. From the woods came the steady sawing of crickets, and from the Big House came the faint clink of dishes being washed, and from somewhere down by the cabins somebody laughed in their sleep.

Percy Jackson sat with his shoulders hunched forward, his hands loose around his knees. His girlfriend Annabeth sat close enough that their shoulders touched. They did that without thinking anymore, the way people who have survived years together do when they are tired. Percy’s beat-up camp necklace glinted in the firelight. Annabeth’s Yankees cap was pushed back on her head, her blonde curls pulled up and frizzed by the heat.

Across from them, Elena Reyes, a visiting archivist helping Chiron sort the winter records, twisted her notebook in her lap. The camp's harpy Ella had retired to the library; tonight belonged to the human girl with the similar name.

She was fourteen, small for her age, with dark brown skin and close-cropped black hair and glasses that kept sliding down her nose when she got nervous. She pushed them up every seven seconds. She was the daughter of Mnemosyne, the Titaness of Memory — one of those cabins that was never quite full and never quite empty, tucked at the end of the row where the godly parent’s name made new campers go, “Wait, who?”

Everyone helping with the winter records knew Elena. She cataloged everything.

She cataloged the contents of the Big House attic on index cards. She cataloged which naiad liked which offering. She cataloged sparring scores, and meal rotations, and the exact dates the strawberry fields bloomed for the last six years, and who borrowed whose shield and whether they returned it polished. If you lost a sock, you asked Elena what dryer you left it in. If you forgot what Clarisse had threatened to do to you last Tuesday if you touched her spear again, Elena could look it up, then ask whether the person wanted the entry read aloud. She was learning that knowing private details did not give her permission to repeat them.

She did it smiling, and she did it fast, and she never bragged about it. The notebooks were just always there — three in her messenger bag, one in her back pocket, one half-falling apart in her cabin.

Tonight Chiron had left the fire early. Mr. D had vanished after two songs, muttering about paperwork. It was just campers. Grover sat on Elena’s left, his legs crossed, his crutches laid beside him in the dirt, the fur on his goat legs brushed clean for once. He kept glancing at her notebook like he wanted to make sure it didn’t fall in the fire.

Clarisse was there with her arm around Silena, both of them sunburnt from the afternoon’s capture the flag. Thalia sat a little apart the way she always did, one boot up on a log, her black hair full of woodsmoke, her silver lightning bracelet catching the light after a long afternoon on border patrol.

It was Silena who started it.

“Elena,” she said softly. “Will you tell it tonight?”

Elena looked up. She pushed her glasses up. “Tell what?”

“Your tale,” Silena said. “Why you write everything down. You’ve never told it all at once.”

A log popped. A spray of sparks went up.

Percy glanced at Annabeth. Annabeth glanced back. There was a whole conversation in that look that came from years of quests, arguments, and learning when the other needed room to breathe. *Should we push? Should we let it be?* Annabeth gave the tiniest nod.

Percy leaned forward. “Only if you want to,” he said. “No pressure. Campfire rule.”

Elena looked around the circle. Nobody was staring. Grover smiled at her, quick and warm.

“I want to,” she said after a moment. She took a breath. “I just — I’ve never said it straight through. It comes out in lists usually. So if I mess up —”

“You won’t,” Grover said.

She smiled at him, then down at her notebook. She didn’t open it.

“My mom’s name is Marisol,” she said. “Marisol Reyes. She’s a librarian in Baltimore. Not like — not a fancy library with marble. A little branch library with a leaky roof and a storytime rug with dinosaurs on it.”

---

Marisol Reyes remembered everything until she didn’t.

That was how Elena told it, and the fire seemed to lean in to listen.

Marisol had the kind of memory that made other librarians laugh in disbelief. She could remember which kid liked books about tornadoes and which one would only read if the dog on the cover looked friendly. She remembered due dates without the computer. She remembered the exact shelf where a misfiled book should be. Patrons would come in and say, “It was blue, and there was a boat, I think?” and Marisol would walk straight to it.

She met Mnemosyne on a rainy Tuesday.

Elena told this part the way her mother had told it to her, which was the way you tell a story you have polished smooth from handling. Her mother had stayed late to reshelve after a pipe burst. The basement was flooded an inch deep. A tall woman in a gray coat was standing in the 398s with water around her ankles, holding a book of folk songs and crying.

“She wasn’t crying loud,” Elena said, staring at the coals. “My mom said it was like she was leaking. Like she had too much inside.”

Marisol waded over and asked if she needed help. The woman said, “I remember every song ever sung and I cannot remember who taught me the first one.”

Marisol, practical, kind, twenty-nine and already tired, said, “Then let’s find a new first one,” and made tea on a hot plate in the break room even though it was against the rules, and taught the strange woman the lullaby her own grandmother used to sing whileshelling beans.

The woman stayed through the winter. She shelved books. She learned the Dewey Decimal System in an afternoon and then reorganized the biography section when no one was looking because, she said, “chronology is a kindness.” She and Marisol talked late while the radiators clanged. In the spring, she was gone. On the break room table she left a note in perfect cursive: *Thank you for lending me a beginning. I will return what I can.*

Marisol found out she was pregnant in May.

“She named me Elena because it was short and it fit on library cards,” Elena said, and there was a ripple of soft laughter. “And for the first six years it was just us. And my abuela. And the library.”

Elena’s early childhood was cataloged not in notebooks but in her mother’s voice. Marisol told her everything. Who her grandmother had been. What street her grandfather grew up on. What Elena’s first word was — *luz*, light — and what the weather was that day. Marisol kept a baby book so detailed it was practically a novel. Height and weight and first tooth and also: *Elena hid the remote in the laundry basket, 3:14 p.m., laughed so hard she hiccuped.*

“My mom said remembering is how you keep people with you,” Elena said. “Even if they leave. Especially if they leave.”

When Elena was seven, Marisol started forgetting small things.

At first it was jokes. Keys in the fridge. A storytime theme left at home. Then it was bigger. She called Elena by her sister’s name — Elena had no aunt by that name, not living. She got lost driving to the grocery store she’d gone to for ten years. She shelved picture books in adult nonfiction and then cried when she found them, because she knew she had done it and didn’t know why.

The doctors said early-onset Alzheimer’s. Rare. Aggressive. No family history that made sense, except — Elena paused here, and Percy felt Annabeth’s shoulder press harder against his — except her mother sometimes said, in the clear moments, “She warned me. Your — she warned me it might be like this. To carry so much and then pay for it. She said memory is a river and I drank too deep just loving her.”

Whether Mnemosyne had meant it as warning or price, no one knew.

Elena was eight when her abuela moved in. Elena was nine when her mother stopped working at the library. Elena was ten when her mother looked at her across the kitchen table and said, bright and polite, “Are you one of my storytime kids?”

“I cataloged everything after that,” Elena said simply. “Because she couldn’t.”

Elena rubbed the worn cover of her notebook. “My first binder was terrible,” she continued. “Not because the handwriting was bad. Because I mistook it for an instruction manual.”

She had been ten. Her abuela had brought home a binder from a discount store and a pack of colored tabs. Elena made a section for food, another for medicine, and a third for family stories. She wrote everything she could remember about Marisol. Her favorite tea, the order in which she shelved library books, the songs she had sung while she cooked. She copied old appointments onto a calendar and labeled the photograph albums so nobody would need to guess who the people were.

For a while it made Elena feel useful. Her mother could forget where she put her slippers, but Elena could check the binder and point to their usual shelf. The woman who had once remembered every library patron's book preference had started asking the same question three times in a single meal. Elena could answer every time.

Then one Tuesday morning Marisol asked for tea without lemon.

“It's supposed to be lemon,” Elena told her.

Her mother looked at the mug. “I don't want lemon today.”

“But you always have lemon.”

“Not today.”

Elena went to the binder. She showed her mother the page with the neat blue handwriting: *Tea: lemon, no honey.*

Marisol looked at the words, then at her daughter. Her expression changed. She didn't look confused. She looked hurt.

“Maybe I don't want what the book says,” she replied.

Elena was furious. She'd written the entry herself, and it was correct. The taste hadn't changed just because the disease was making everything else uncertain. If the record could be contradicted, what was the point of making it?

“Mom,” she said, “I remember.”

“I know you do.”

Marisol went to her bedroom without taking the tea. Elena sat at the kitchen table with the binder open and cried until her grandmother came home from the pharmacy.

Abuela read the entry, then asked who the binder was for.

“So Mom can remember,” Elena said.

“Does a notebook get to decide what she wants for breakfast?”

“If she doesn't remember what she likes, someone has to.”

“Your mother is allowed to change her mind,” Abuela said. “Even if she remembered everything, she could decide she didn't like lemon today.”

Elena stared at the page. Her throat was sore from crying.

“But I did the work,” she said.

“Yes. You did. You were trying to help. That doesn't make the choice yours.”

It was an answer Elena hated. She had begun treating remembering as a job nobody else could do. If she couldn't be useful, she wasn't sure who she was allowed to be.

Abuela sat beside her. She explained that the doctors would handle the medicine, that the medical papers weren't meant to sit in a binder visitors could read, and that she would take responsibility for appointments. Elena could help with stories or meals when she was asked, but she was still a child.

“That doesn't seem fair,” Elena protested.

“It isn't fair that your mother is sick,” Abuela agreed. “You don't need to make it less unfair by taking on everything.”

That night Elena crossed out the food rules. She wrote a new heading: *Things Mom has liked before. Ask what she wants today.* She kept the information she'd collected, but left blank lines to record a new answer if one came.

She separated the family stories from the medical notes. She stopped writing down every conversation in the house just because she could. When Abuela asked for help with appointments, Elena helped. When she didn't, Elena went outside and played basketball with the girl next door, badly enough that the ball spent most of the afternoon rolling into the street.

A few weeks later Marisol and Elena sat with a photograph album. One picture showed her mother at a picnic table beneath pine trees, years before Elena was born. Marisol couldn't remember when it had been taken.

“Was I happy?” she asked.

Elena checked the picture. The woman in it was laughing, her face turned toward someone outside the frame. There were cups on the table and a plate of strawberries.

“You look happy,” Elena said. “I wasn't there. I don't know what happened before or afterward.”

Marisol smiled. “Then maybe I was.”

They stayed with the picture. Elena didn't fetch the binder and try to prove anything. She could say what she saw without pretending she knew what her mother felt.

At the campfire Elena took a breath.

“That was the first time I understood the difference between remembering and owning,” she said. “I still want Mom's stories. I still want every ordinary day we had. But she gets to be a person today, even if it doesn't match the notes I made yesterday.”

Grover nodded. He'd been watching her with his hands folded on his knees.

“What changed in your notebooks?” he asked.

“More questions.” Elena counted them on her fingers. “Who saw it? Who told me? Is it still true? Can I share it? And does anyone need to know?”

“That last one sounds particularly difficult,” Annabeth said.

“It is.” Elena smiled faintly. “The old binder was easier. A perfect list always tells you what to do. People don't.”

She closed the notebook on her lap without opening it. For a moment all Percy could hear was the fire and the rustle of someone adjusting their blanket. He thought of the unfinished letters he wrote to his family, and of how much harder it was to ask a question without knowing whether he would like the answer.

---

The fire cracked. Nobody moved.

Elena’s voice had gone flat in the way people’s voices do when they are trying very hard not to cry and doing a decent job of it. Grover shifted, just slightly, so his knee touched the edge of her blanket. Not crowding. Just there.

“I started with her,” Elena said. “I wrote down what she liked to eat before she forgot she liked it. Oatmeal with brown sugar, not white. Tea with lemon, no honey. The dinosaur rug — she loved that rug. I wrote down the songs. I wrote down the stories she told me about abuela, because abuela would cry if she had to repeat them and Mom forgot. I carried a notebook to school. Teachers thought I was — you know. That kid.”

“You *are* that kid,” Clarisse said, but her voice was gruff-kind, not mean. “Continue.”

Elena smiled a little.

“I thought if I wrote it fast enough, I could keep her. Like — if I got every detail, the forgetting wouldn’t count. Stupid, I know.”

“It’s not stupid,” Annabeth said quietly. Percy looked at her. Her gray eyes were bright with firelight and something else. She had her arms wrapped around her shins now. “It’s not stupid at all.”

Elena nodded, grateful.

“When I was eleven, weird things started. I would remember things I shouldn’t. Like — I’d walk into the library basement and know exactly where a book was even though I’d never shelved it. I’d dream whole hallways full of scrolls. Once I brushed Mrs. Henderson’s hand at storytime and felt as if I knew her husband’s name and when he died. I told her without asking. She got scared, and she had a right to be. Even now I can't prove what I sensed, or that it was mine to say.”

Monsters found her that winter. A empousa posing as a substitute librarian. Elena remembered the smell — burnt sugar and old pennies — and ran before the woman even showed her teeth, because she remembered a story another demigod kid at school had whispered once and never repeated. She ran to the branch library and locked herself in the staff bathroom and called her abuela, and her abuela called the number Elena’s mother had made her memorize years ago and made her promise never to lose: a number in New York with a strawberry painted next to it in the baby book.

“She remembered that,” Elena said. “My mom wrote it down when she was still — when she could. Next to my immunization record. *If Elena starts remembering too much, call this.*”

Grover had been the one sent to collect her. He told that part with a glance at Percy, who nodded — Grover had described that winter to him, the endless rain, arriving at camp with a shivering girl in a too-big coat clutching three notebooks and refusing to let go of his hand on the bus. Elena had been eleven then, years before Percy's return here.

“I cataloged the whole trip,” Elena said. “Bus number. Rest stops. What Grover ate —”

“Three bags of tin cans,” Percy put in, trying to lighten it. “He told me. He was very proud.”

“Aluminum,” Grover corrected, mock-offended, and Elena laughed — a real laugh, wet at the edges but real — and the tension broke just enough to breathe.

---

“My abuela brings my mom to visit when she can,” Elena said. “Not often. It’s hard to travel. My mom — last time, she didn’t know me. She thought I was a nice volunteer. She asked me to read to her. So I did. I read her own baby book back to her. About the remote in the laundry basket. About *luz.*”

She stopped. She pushed her glasses up. Her hands were shaking.

“She fell asleep with her head on my shoulder. And I thought — okay. Maybe cataloging isn’t keeping. Maybe it’s just — lending. Like she lent that woman a beginning. I can lend my mom her middle. Even if she can’t keep it.”

Silence. The coals pulsed.

Percy felt his throat close. He stared hard at the fire so he wouldn’t do anything embarrassing. Next to him, Annabeth was very still. He knew that stillness. It was her thinking-stillness, but also her sad-stillness, the one she got when she talked about Luke or about her remembered childhood, running away at seven with nothing but a backpack and a hammer. That history remained hers even though Thalia had raised Annabeth Grace here.

Annabeth reached out without looking and found Percy’s hand and squeezed it once, hard. Their old signal. *Remember?*

He remembered.

He remembered his mom in the original life — the Manhattan kitchen that belonged to those memories, not this altered childhood. Sally Jackson had raised one stubborn, impossible boy and welcomed the girl he brought home from camp. She made blue pancakes and sang off-key and kept his drawings in a trunk at the foot of her bed because, she said, “I’m building evidence that you were happy.” Here she had died when he was two. Remembering her care did not make that loss less real.

Percy remembered sitting with Annabeth on the fire escape after they had met at camp, while Sally worked late. Annabeth had quizzed him on quest details because she was terrified that if they didn’t practice remembering, the Mist or monsters or just growing up would take things away. He remembered her after the Labyrinth, sitting up late writing down its turns on graph paper so she wouldn’t forget the passage that had almost killed him. He remembered his mom forgetting — not like Elena’s mom, not disease, just the ordinary mortal forgetting that demigod life forced on her: names of friends she’d met only once, the order of stories Percy told, small details lost while she worked to keep him safe.

He remembered thinking that his girlfriend’s brain was like Elena’s notebooks. Endless shelves.

“I get it,” Percy said hoarsely. He cleared his throat. “The — writing it down so it counts. In the life I remember, my mom kept — she kept all my stuff, and things Annabeth gave us. Even dumb stuff. Report cards with C-minuses. Annabeth’s first architecture sketch, which was — sorry —”

“It was a terrible drawing of his apartment building with lasers on it,” Annabeth said, her voice thick but steady. The circle laughed softly. “It was avant-garde. You were jealous.”

“I was jealous,” Percy agreed. “Mom kept it on the fridge for three years. She said — she said if we ever felt like we were just — monster bait, or mistakes, we could look at the fridge and see we were also kids who lost teeth and drew lasers. Evidence.”

“Evidence,” Elena repeated. She liked the word. She mouthed it like she was filing it.

Annabeth leaned forward, her elbows on her knees. The fire lit the planes of her face, the same familiar concentration Percy loved, sharpened by different worries.

“My boyfriend snores,” she said to Elena, deliberately casual, and Percy squawked in protest while everyone laughed. “No, listen. On a trip in the life we remember, we shared a room — separate bunks, and Percy took the top because he said being higher meant he could see danger coming, which made no sense —”

“It made perfect sense,” Percy muttered.

“And after a bad dream he’d make me retell the same story. About a day at the beach, when I dropped my ice cream and he tried to blame the ocean. He’d correct me if I missed a detail. ‘No, you yelled *before* the wave came, not after.’ He needed something ordinary to stay the same. I thought he was being annoying.”

“I *was* being annoying,” Percy said. “It was a skill.”

“I get now he was cataloging,” Annabeth said. She looked at Elena. “You do what I used to do in my head. You just — you’re braver. You write it where other people can use it.”

Elena stared at her. Then she ducked her head, but not before they all saw her smile.

Grover cleared his throat. He’d been quiet, turning a marshmallow stick over and over in his hands without putting a marshmallow on it.

“Can I — Elena, can I say something?” he asked.

She nodded.

“You lent me your notes once,” he said. “Last winter. When I couldn’t find — when Juniper and I had that fight, and I couldn’t remember what I’d said wrong. You asked Juniper which parts of the notes she was willing to let me hear, and then you read them back to me. You didn’t hand over the rest. You said, ‘Page forty-two. You were tired and you weren’t listening.’”

More laughter, gentle. Grover’s ears went pink at the tips.

“You were right,” he said. “Page forty-two. I apologized. It helped. You help like that all the time and you act like it’s just — filing. It’s not just filing. You — you keep us honest. You keep us kind, because we know someone’s paying attention.”

He stopped, realizing everyone was looking at him. He rubbed the back of his neck.

“What I’m saying is — I’m glad you write things down. And if you ever want help carrying the notebooks, I’m — I’m good at carrying things. I have — upper body strength. From crutches.”

Elena giggled through tears. “You have excellent upper body strength, Grover.”

“I do,” he said solemnly. “Ask anyone.”

He offered her his sleeve to wipe her eyes, the way satyrs do when they don’t have tissues and don’t care about laundry. She took it, laughing, and blew her nose loudly, and the whole circle groaned and laughed, and Grover looked absurdly pleased with himself, like he’d just slain a drakon.

Percy caught Annabeth’s eye again. *Nice kid.* Annabeth mouthed back: *Good kid.* The way old friends quietly approve of someone helping another friend.

Chaste and sweet and exactly enough — Grover didn’t put his arm around her. He just moved his blanket so it overlapped hers by two inches, to block the wind. Elena noticed. She didn’t move away.

---

The fire needed another log. Clarisse got up to get it, dragging a half-charred oak branch from the woodpile and heaving it on with a grunt. Sparks exploded upward. She sat back down hard next to Silena, who immediately checked her hands for splinters out of habit.

“Your turn,” Elena said suddenly, wiping her glasses on her shirt. “You guys said — campfire rule is everybody shares if they want. Why do *you* stay? I’ve cataloged your schedules and your weapon preferences and your godly parentage, but I don’t actually know — why here? Why not leave?”

Clarisse snorted. “Who says I stay? I’m stuck here. My dad —”

Silena elbowed her. “Be honest. It’s Elena’s night.”

Clarisse glared at the fire. She was big, scarred, her dark hair in a practical ponytail, her eyes narrowed like a fighter who’d learned to look mean before anyone could look past it. Percy had seen her cry a few times — when Silena got hurt in capture the flag, when a younger camper called her on a borrowed phone and asked when she was coming back. Clarisse denied the tears afterward, every time.

She picked at a scab on her knuckle.

“Fine,” she said. “You want honest? I stay because out there I’m — I’m the girl who got expelled twice and punched a teacher. My mom’s boyfriend calls me ‘the problem.’ My school file is like Elena’s notebooks but all bad. ‘Disruptive. Aggressive. Unmanageable.’ Here —” She jerked her chin at the cabins dark on the hill. “Here I’m — I’m good at something that isn’t just hurting people. Patrols. Training the little kids. You know Michael from the Demeter cabin? He couldn’t hold a spear last summer without crying. Now he can disarm Percy —”

“Hey,” Percy said. “Once. He did it once and I was tired.”

Laughter. Clarisse almost smiled.

“I stay because nobody here flinches when I walk in a room,” she said quietly. “Out there they flinch. My mom flinches. Here Silena just says, ‘Move your big feet, you’re blocking the fire,’ and Thalia throws a marshmallow at my head. I’m — normal here. I’m useful. My dad might be a jerk, but at least he claims me. At least Chiron looks at me and sees a leader, not a lawsuit.”

She stopped, embarrassed. Silena took her hand, laced their fingers together, and didn’t make a big deal of it.

“I cataloged that,” Elena whispered, writing fast in the margin of her notebook even though she’d promised herself she wouldn’t. “*Clarisse: stays to be useful, not feared. Sees herself as leader.*”

“Don’t make it sound nice,” Clarisse muttered, but she was blushing.

“It *is* nice,” Silena said. She tucked her head against Clarisse’s shoulder. Her voice was softer than her sister’s, but steady. “I stay because — people think it’s because I’m pretty or because I like clothes or whatever. It’s not.”

She looked at Elena.

“The relatives who took care of me love me. They do. They send care packages with the good chocolate and call every Sunday; Mom sends less, and Poseidon is harder to pin down. But with them I’m — the pretty one. The nice one. The one who smooths things over when people fight. Nobody asks me what I think about anything important. Here —” She gestured at the circle. “Here I talked Beckendorf into redesigning the Pegasus stables even though he’s Hephaestus and I’m Poseidon and everyone thought I just wanted them pink. I *did* want them pink, but also I wanted better ventilation because the Pegasi were getting sick. And he listened. And Chiron listened.”

She smiled, a little sad.

“I stay because here I’m not decoration. I’m — I’m smart in a way that counts. I can tell when someone’s about to break before they break. I knew Elena was having a bad day last Tuesday before she did, remember?”

Elena nodded, eyes wide. “You gave me tea. Chamomile. Two sugars. Page — I wrote it down.”

“See?” Silena said. “Someone has to notice. That’s my job. Out there, noticing just makes you nosy. Here, noticing saves lives on quests. So I stay.”

Thalia was next, and everyone turned to look at her. She’d been so quiet they’d almost forgotten she was there, which never happened with Thalia.

She stared into the fire for a long time. The silver in her hair — a streak that hadn’t been there before last winter — caught the light.

“I stay because I left,” she said finally.

Percy frowned. Annabeth tilted her head.

“I took the long scouting assignment,” Thalia said. “You all know that. I thought — if I was always responsible for someone, I could at least choose a job with an end date. Camp bunks, family arguments, all left behind for a while. And it was good. It *was* good. Chiron trusted me to help the satyrs. The people out there became friends.”

She pulled her knees up, jangling her bracelets.

“But then I started counting the days until I came back. For — for Percy’s birthday. For Annabeth’s stupid architecture presentations —”

“They’re not stupid,” Annabeth said automatically.

“They’re incredibly stupid and I love them,” Thalia said, and Annabeth threw a marshmallow at her, and Thalia caught it in her mouth to applause. “And I realized — I missed the noise. A scouting camp is quiet. It’s clean. Nobody leaves socks in the shower. Nobody argues about whose turn it is to muck the stables. And I missed arguing. I missed —” She looked at Elena. “I missed being remembered wrong. The scouts know me as Thalia the Brave, Thalia Who Doesn’t Miss. Here Percy knows me as the girl who ate an entire blue birthday cake with her hands and threw up in the lake —”

“You begged me not to tell!” Percy said.

“I’m telling on myself now for narrative effect,” Thalia said loftily. “Point is. Here I’m not a statue. I’m — I’m a person who messes up and comes back. I stay — I come back — because this is the only place that lets me leave and still keep my bunk. You all kept my bunk. Even when I was away for weeks. Elena, you dusted it. I saw your log.”

Elena blushed furiously. “Cabin inspection log, March 14th. *Thalia’s bunk dusted, blanket folded. Pine needles removed. Welcome home whenever.*”

Thalia’s face did something complicated. She crossed the circle and offered her arms. Elena stood and hugged her willingly, laughing, and Thalia released her when she asked for room. A few campers clapped softly.

“Okay, okay, ribs,” Elena gasped, laughing. Thalia set her down, ruffled her hair, and went back to her seat, eyes suspiciously bright.

---

It got late. The coals sank lower. Someone — probably Travis Stoll — started snoring in the back row. The younger kids had been herded to bed an hour ago, but the older circle remained.

Percy added the last log himself. He was good at fires now; Sally had taught him how to lay one without lighter fluid in his original childhood, back when they couldn’t afford it. Annabeth watched him work, handing him bark without being asked, the old rhythm.

Elena looked at them.

“Can I ask — why do *you* stay? Both of you? Percy, you could live in Atlantis. Everyone says Amphitrite sends enough sweets to end wars.”

Percy and Annabeth exchanged another look. This one was longer.

“I can go there,” Percy said. “It's the home everyone else remembers for me. Best of both, if we can manage it. But why do I come *back* —” He glanced at his girlfriend.

Annabeth picked it up. “Because out there we have to pretend we’re not — weird. At school Percy pretends he can’t read Greek and I pretend I don’t already know calculus. We pretend monsters aren’t real and the Mist didn’t just make our principal forget a minotaur attack. It’s exhausting. Our mortal families don't know every part of that. Beryl is trying to understand, but she can't stand guard while we sleep.”

Percy nodded. “Here we don’t have to translate. Annabeth can talk about load-bearing walls at breakfast and nobody blinks. I can talk to the fish in the lake and nobody calls a psychiatrist. And —” He hesitated. “And we get to be ourselves without explaining every strange thing. You know? Out there people hear ‘demigod’ and think we’re lying or crazy. Here everyone’s got a messed-up family. Elena’s mom forgets. Clarisse’s mom flinches. Amphitrite remembers a childhood I can't remember, and sometimes that hurts us both. Here that’s normal. Everyone’s got something.”

Annabeth’s voice dropped. “I stay because Percy stays. And he stays because I stay. Is that dumb?”

“No,” Elena said immediately, writing it down. “*Annabeth and Percy: stay for each other. Stay to not translate. Evidence of happiness.* That’s — that’s good data.”

Percy threw a stick at her. She dodged, giggling.

“Don’t catalog us,” he said.

“Too late,” she said. “You’re in volume seven. Pages twelve through — a lot.”

Grover stood, stretched, winced as his hooves popped. He offered Elena his hand. She took it without hesitation and let him pull her up.

“Walk you back?” he asked. “It’s dark past the cabins. I have night vision. Satyr perk.”

“And excellent upper body strength,” Elena reminded him solemnly.

“And that,” he agreed.

They walked off together, Elena’s bag bumping against her hip, Grover’s crutches clicking faintly on the path, their heads bent close as she showed him something in the notebook by moonlight. Not hand in hand. Just close. The way friends walk after a hard story when neither wants the other to be alone.

Silena sighed happily. “They're good friends.”

“Then let them be friends,” Clarisse said.

“You were crying at their cuteness five seconds ago.”

“I was not. Smoke in my eyes.”

Thalia stood, groaning. “I'm too old for logs. Literally. I'm like — ancient in patrol-roster years.”

“You're barely older than the rest of us,” Annabeth said.

“Exactly.”

One by one the circle broke. Clarisse banked the fire with practiced hands. Silena collected abandoned marshmallow sticks. Percy poured the last of the water from his canteen onto the embers, hissing steam rising up smelling of ash and sugar.

It was just him and Annabeth for a minute, the way it started.

Annabeth bumped his shoulder. “You okay?”

“Yeah,” he said. “You?”

“Yeah.” She was quiet. “Do you think — do you think Beryl could — like Elena's mom, one day? Forget?”

Percy thought about it. He thought about Sally’s hands, floury with blue cookie dough, tucking a drawing into that trunk. He remembered her voice on the phone in his original life: *Tell me everything, baby. Even the scary parts. I’ll remember the good for you when you can’t.*

“I don't know,” he said. “But what happened to her mom doesn't tell us what will happen to yours. We can keep the letters. Keep answering when you and Thalia want to. We can remember what she tells you.”

Annabeth nodded, satisfied. She stood, pulled him up with surprising strength for someone who spent all day drawing.

“Come on, Seaweed Brain,” she said. He was a little taller than her now. She still took his hand and tugged as if he needed help finding the path, and he still pretended to hate it.

“Coming, bossy,” he said.

They walked up the hill together, past the cabins where lights were going out one by one, past Elena’s window where a lamp still burned — she was writing, of course, getting it all down before sleep took the edges off — past Grover’s door where a pair of crutches leaned neatly against the frame.

At the top of the hill, Percy stopped. He looked back. The campfire was a dull red eye in the dark, smoke rising straight up to join the stars. Somewhere an owl called. Somewhere the lake lapped against the dock.

Annabeth stopped with him. She didn’t ask why. She just stood there, shoulder to shoulder, the way they had by the fire, the way they had on the fire escape in their remembered life, the way they stood when there was nothing to say and no need to say it.

“Evidence,” Percy said softly.

“Evidence,” Annabeth agreed.

They parted for Cabin One and Cabin Three, holding hands until the path divided.

And in Cabin Mnemosyne, Elena Reyes opened a fresh page, dated it in her neat hand, and wrote:

*Tonight I told my tale and they listened. Percy cried a little and pretended he didn’t. Annabeth held his hand. Grover said I keep people kind. Clarisse stays to be useful. Silena stays to notice. Thalia stays because we kept her bunk.*

*Mom, if you ever read this: you taught me. Remembering is keeping. I am keeping all of us.*

*Tomorrow I will catalog the stars. Tonight was enough.*

She closed the notebook, turned off the lamp, and slept.
