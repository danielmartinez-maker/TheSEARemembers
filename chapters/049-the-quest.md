## Chapter 49: The Quest

Percy woke before the conch.

That almost never happened. In Cabin Three the mornings ran on noise — the conch from the Big House, Clarisse's alarm clock that she hit like it owed her money, Silena humming while she hunted for her other sandal. Percy usually surfaced somewhere in the middle of all that, salt-mouth and tangled in his sheet, with the sun coming through the porthole windows in bright slats.

This morning the cabin was blue-dark and quiet. No conch. No humming. The air smelled like the sea, the way Cabin Three always did, varnish and rope and clean laundry, but underneath it there was something else. Ozone. The way the air tasted before lightning hit the lake.

Percy sat up.

Clarisse was already awake. She sat on the edge of her bunk with her spear across her knees and a whetstone in her hand, drawing it slow down the edge. Shink. Shink. She did that when she was thinking too loud to sleep. She wore her camp shirt and her hair pulled back hard, and her face had the flat, focused look she got before capture the flag.

Silena was awake too. That was stranger. Silena loved sleep the way otters loved shellfish. But she sat cross-legged on her bunk with her blanket around her shoulders, three pillows stacked behind her, a book face-down and forgotten on top. She was braiding and unbraiding the same blue thread around her finger.

"You're both awake," Percy said. His voice came out rough.

"You're awake," Clarisse said.

"Something feels weird."

Silena nodded. She wound the thread tight enough to whiten her fingertip, then let it go. "I dreamed about the attic," she said. "The Big House attic. I have never even been up there. But I dreamed the trapdoor was open and green light was coming down the stairs."

Percy felt the back of his neck prickle. "I dreamed about thunder with no sound. Just flashing. Over and over."

Clarisse set the whetstone down. "I didn't dream. I slept like a rock. Somebody has to in this cabin." She stood and slid her spear into the rack by the door, too careful about it, and Percy understood she had also felt it. The wrongness in the air. Clarisse never fussed with her spear unless she needed her hands to stop shaking.

A knock hit the cabin door. Three fast raps, then a pause, then two more. Grover's knock.

Percy was already pulling his shirt on. "Come in."

Grover slipped inside, shutting the door fast behind him. He wore his camp shirt too big over his jeans, his sneakers laced to hide his hooves, his horns filed down but visible under his curls. He had his pipes stuck through his belt loop and his backpack half-zipped and bulging with reeds and tin cans and string. His eyes were wide.

"Chiron wants you," Grover said, looking at Percy. Then at Clarisse and Silena. "All three of you. And Annabeth and Thalia. Big House. Now. Rachel's —" He swallowed. "Rachel's upstairs. In the attic. She went up at dawn and she hasn't come down and Chiron says the mist is leaking under the door."

Silena pressed her hand to her mouth. Clarisse grabbed her jacket.

Percy shoved his feet into his shoes. His heart beat hard against his ribs. "Is she okay?"

"She asked for lemonade and a second pillow," Grover said. "So I think yes? But her voice sounded echoey. You know. Like down a well."

The path to the Big House was empty. Usually at this hour there were early runners and harpies dragging laundry bags and somebody half-asleep heading to the forge. Now the gravel crunched too loud under their shoes. The sky over the Sound was pale gray with a thin yellow seam at the horizon. The flag on the Big House porch hung limp. Even the strawberry fields on the hill seemed too still, leaves turned up silver-white.

Annabeth and Thalia were already on the porch.

Annabeth stood by the railing with her arms crossed tight over her stomach, braid over one shoulder, notebook clutched under one elbow like she had grabbed it without thinking. When she saw Percy she straightened a little, and Percy felt his chest ease half an inch just seeing her upright and alert and herself. Daughter of Zeus, twelve, gray eyes sharp even with sleep still in them.

Thalia stood beside her, tall and leather-jacketed despite the warmth, black hair spiked with static. Daughter of Zeus, older sister in every sense that mattered, the one who had raised Annabeth when their father proved too vast and crackling to be a parent. She had her hand on Annabeth's shoulder. When she saw the Poseidon kids coming up the steps, she lifted her chin.

"Took you long enough, Fish Face," Thalia said to Percy, but there was no bite in it.

"Nice bedhead, Pinecone Face," Percy said automatically. It was their greeting. It steadied him.

Chiron opened the door before anyone knocked. In his wheelchair form, blanket over his knees, beard braided, eyes kind and grave. Behind him the Big House parlor was dim, lamps still lit. Mr. D was conspicuously absent, which told Percy more than anything else that this was serious. Dionysus never missed drama unless someone had told him to stay away.

"Come in, children," Chiron said. "Quietly. Rachel is preparing."

---

The downstairs of the Big House smelled like lemon polish and old wood and Chiron's tea. The upstairs hallway was narrower, lower-ceilinged, lined with hero photos that watched them climb.

Percy had never been to the attic. Nobody went to the attic except to store war trophies. The staircase at the end of the hall was steep and dusty. Green light seeped around the edges of the trapdoor at the top, thin as paper cuts.

Chiron stopped at the foot of the stairs. He could not climb them in the chair, and he did not try. He looked up at the five of them — Percy, Annabeth, Thalia, Clarisse, Silena — and Grover hovering a step below with his pipes clutched white-knuckled.

"Rachel Elizabeth Dare came to us this summer as a clear-sighted mortal," Chiron said low. "She has carried the spirit of the Oracle since the vessel was unsealed. Until now it has spoken only in whispers and dreams. This morning she woke and asked to be taken to the attic. She said it was time. Whatever she says now carries full authority."

Silena took Percy's hand. Clarisse took Silena's other hand. Annabeth reached over Thalia and caught Percy's sleeve between two fingers, just a pinch of fabric. Percy held still so she would not let go.

The trapdoor creaked open from above.

"Come up," Rachel said, and her voice echoed.

Percy went first because somebody had to, and because his knees were already moving. The attic air hit him warm and thick, smelling of cedar and dust and something green and ancient, like crushed bay leaves. Boxes and old shields and a dusty taxidermied hydra head crowded the eaves. In the center, on a wooden stool, sat Rachel.

She wore her paint-splattered camp shirt and shorts, feet bare, hair wild red around her face. Her eyes glowed green. Mist coiled off her shoulders and pooled around the stool legs and drifted toward the single round window. Behind her, the shadows seemed deeper than they should be.

"Hi," Rachel said in the echo voice. "Sit. This will be fast because bodies are blunt instruments and mine wants toast."

Nobody laughed. They sat on crates and the floor. Grover sat so close to Percy their shoulders pressed together. Annabeth sat on Percy's other side, knees touching his. Thalia stood behind Annabeth with her arms crossed. Clarisse stood behind Percy. Silena knelt beside Clarisse, hands folded.

Rachel's head tilted. The green in her eyes brightened until Percy could see it reflected on the dust in the air.

She spoke, and the attic seemed to lean in:

*Three shall go west where the sky was torn,*
*Sea-born and storm-born and wild hoof-worn.*
*The bolt that was stolen sleeps chained in stone,*
*And the thief wears a face that is not his own.*
*Trust not the road, trust not the sign,*
*Till the owl and the trident share one line.*

Silence fell hard. The mist thinned. Rachel blinked, and the green faded out of her like water draining. She swayed on the stool.

"Toast," she said faintly in her normal voice. "Did I get toast?"

Grover was up the stool in a second, steadying her. Thalia grabbed the spare pillow and shoved it behind her. Silena produced a juice box from somewhere — Silena always had snacks — and stuck the straw to Rachel's lips.

Percy barely noticed any of it. The words rang in his skull.

Three shall go west. Sea-born and storm-born and wild hoof-worn.

He looked at Annabeth. She looked back. Her gray eyes were huge, but her mouth had set in that straight planner line that meant she was already building lists in her head.

Chiron's voice came from below. "Children? What did she say?"


---

They were halfway down the attic stairs when Annabeth stopped.

Percy nearly ran into her. Grover was behind him, still helping Rachel with her blanket, and Thalia had taken the lead to make sure the steps were clear. The air had begun to smell like ordinary dust again, but the words from the Oracle stayed sharp in Percy's ears.

"What is it?" he asked.

Annabeth turned her notebook over so that the page was hidden against her chest.

"I want to check what she said about the owl."

Percy stared at her. "Right now?"

"Before everybody starts telling us what it means."

Thalia called from the landing. "Beth, Chiron's waiting."

"One minute."

Something in her voice made Thalia pause. Percy expected a protest, but his girlfriend's sister kept moving, taking Grover and Rachel down the final flight. Clarisse waited a few steps below with Silena, apparently guarding the space without having been asked to.

The moment they were alone, Annabeth unfolded the notebook.

"Sea-born, storm-born, wild hoof-worn. That's Percy, me, and Grover if Chiron agrees. But the end is *owl and trident*. I'm Zeus's daughter here, Percy."

"I know."

"An owl isn't his symbol."

Percy remembered a life in which Annabeth's gray eyes had been proof she was Athena's child. He remembered her wearing an old Yankees cap, studying blueprints, arguing with gods who expected gratitude simply because they had bothered to speak. He remembered the owl on an offering he had once helped her make. Those memories were real to him. They were not proof that the present gods had preserved the same relationships.

"Maybe the prophecy is talking about our other life," he whispered.

Annabeth looked toward the open stairwell. Voices carried from below, Chiron asking for a clear account of Rachel's words, Thalia asking him to wait until everybody was safely down.

"Maybe," she said. "But the Oracle didn't say Athena's daughter. She said owl."

"Could mean you. Could mean somebody else."

"I know. I don't want us to decide it's about me just because it used to be."

Percy felt a cold pinch of fear. In the world they remembered, prophecies had managed to be both accurate and misleading. Sometimes the trouble came from knowing too much and assuming the rest would follow.

"Then we don't decide," he said.

Annabeth examined the page again. She had written the six lines exactly as Rachel spoke them, then underlined the last one. Beside *owl* she put a question mark.

"There's also the thief with somebody else's face," she said.

"Lots of monsters can pretend to be people."

"And gods."

"And mortals with the Mist."

"And people we think we know," Annabeth said.

Percy watched her write three possibilities, leaving space below them. She wasn't listing suspects. She was preventing them from settling on one too soon.

He thought of the stolen bolt from the quest he remembered. The old sequence of events had taught him not to trust the obvious explanation. But the words about stone and a false face were new, and he had no reason to believe his memories supplied the missing answer.

"I don't think we should tell Chiron what we remember about the old bolt," he said.

"Not as if it happened this time," Annabeth agreed. "If we tell him a theory, it should have evidence from this life."

Below them, Chiron called their names.

Annabeth folded the page closed.

"This isn't us forgetting," she said. "It's us checking."

Percy nodded. He took her hand for the last few steps, not to lead her and not to be led, just because the stairs were steep and they were both shaken.

When they reached the landing, Thalia looked at their joined hands and the closed notebook but didn't ask. She was already arguing with Clarisse about whether Rachel should be allowed to walk down unaided.

Rachel, wrapped in her blanket, rolled her eyes.

"I can walk," she said. "I have legs. Also, I would really like toast."

Percy stepped aside so she could pass. For once, nobody tried to turn her into a voice that existed only to tell other people what they should do.

---

They told him downstairs around the ping-pong table, which had been cleared of paddles and covered with a map of the United States instead.

Rachel sat wrapped in a blanket on the couch with toast in both hands, color coming back to her cheeks, insisting she was fine and that everybody stop staring. Mr. D had appeared at some point, sunglasses on indoors, Diet Coke in hand, leaning against the doorframe like he had been there all along.

Chiron listened to the prophecy repeated twice — once by Annabeth word-perfect, once by Percy to check — and his face grew longer with each line.

"The bolt," Chiron said. "Zeus's master bolt. Stolen, as we feared. Sleeps chained in stone — west. The thief wears a face that is not his own." He glanced at Mr. D.

Mr. D sighed. "Daddy's spear is missing and he blames Fish Boy's daddy. Classic. Next he will blame the mailman."

"It is not Percy," Annabeth said hotly. "Percy has been here all summer. Everybody has seen him."

"Of course it is not Percy," Chiron said gently. "But Zeus's anger rarely waits for evidence. The bolt must be found and returned before the solstice, or there will be war between sky and sea. The Oracle has spoken clearly on the party. Sea-born —" He looked at Percy. "Storm-born —" He looked at Annabeth. Thalia straightened, but Chiron's gaze settled on Annabeth. "And wild hoof-worn — a satyr. Grover, you have proven yourself brave and loyal."

Grover squeaked. "Me? Brave? Are you sure the Oracle said brave? She said hoof-worn. That could be any satyr. There are many satyrs with excellent hooves —"

"Grover," Percy said, bumping his shoulder. "You're my best friend. I want you."

Grover's ears pinked. He clutched his pipes. "Well. If you put it like that. Okay. Okay, yeah. I'll go. Somebody has to keep you two from walking into traffic."

Annabeth reached across the map and squeezed Grover's wrist. "Best hooves for the job."

Percy felt something settle in his stomach, heavy and bright at once. This was it. The classic shape he remembered from another life pressing through — him and Annabeth and Grover on the road west. Only this time he was twelve again, after remembering his life through seventeen, and Annabeth was Zeus's daughter, not Athena's, and the bolt slept chained in stone instead of the backpack he remembered from the first quest. Still, the shape fit his shoulders like a jacket he had worn before.

"I'll do it," Percy said. "I swear on the Styx — no, I swear on my mom —" He stopped. Sally was gone in this history. Amphitrite had raised him here, but it was Sally's blue cookies and laugh that rose in his memory. His throat closed. Silena's hand found his back. "I swear I'll bring it back. Sea and sky. Owl and trident, one line."

Annabeth looked at him, and her eyes shone. "Me too. I swear on Thalia. I'll bring it back with him."

"Absolutely not," Thalia said.

Everyone turned.

Thalia had her arms crossed so tight her jacket creaked. Her blue-streaked black hair stood up with static, which meant she was sparking. A faint smell of ozone clung to her.

"You are twelve," Thalia said, pointing at Annabeth, then at Percy. "Twelve. Going west, alone, after a weapon that can level mountains, with a thief who can wear anybody's face. No. No way. Chiron, you cannot be serious."

"Thalia —" Annabeth started.

"No," Thalia said. "You are my sister. I raised you. I taught you to hold a knife and read a map and check for monsters under the bunk. I am not sending you out past Half-Blood Hill with a backpack and a hope."

Chiron folded his hands. "The Oracle named three."

"Then I go as backup," Thalia said. "Fourth. Unofficial. I stay in the shadows, I watch the rear, I do not touch the prophecy until it needs me. You know the old stories. Quests always pick up extra swords. Somebody has to be the sword."

Percy saw Annabeth's face flicker — annoyance and relief tangled together. Thalia the protector, Thalia who hovered at the edge of every sparring match, Thalia who had once chased a harpy three fields over for stealing Annabeth's sandwich. Annabeth loved her fiercely and chafed under her fiercely, both at once.

"Thalia, you can't —" Annabeth said.

"I can," Thalia said. "Try to stop me and see what happens. I will follow at a hundred paces with sunglasses and a fake mustache if I have to."

Mr. D snorted into his Diet Coke. "I would pay to see that."

Chiron was quiet for a long moment. Then he nodded, slow. "A guardian, not a quester. It has precedent. If the Oracle does not object —" He glanced up the stairs. No green light. No echo. "I will allow it. Thalia Grace may accompany as far as needed in defense, but the three named must be the ones to face the trials."

Thalia exhaled. Her shoulders dropped an inch. She ruffled Annabeth's braid. "Good. Then I'm coming."

"I want to come too," Silena said suddenly.

Every head turned again.

Silena stood with her hands knotted in front of her, fourteen and lovely and brave, daughter of Poseidon, sister to Percy and Clarisse. Her voice shook, but she lifted her chin.

"If Percy is going west, I should be there. I can heal. I can talk to horses. I can — I can pack really good lunches —"

"Silena," Clarisse said.

Silena turned on her. "Don't you dare say no kindly."

Clarisse's jaw worked. Daughter of Poseidon, thirteen, spear-fighter, the middle sister who acted like the oldest when it counted. She looked at Silena, then at Percy, then at the map with its red pins already marking the westward roads.

"Somebody has to guard camp," Clarisse said roughly. "If the bolt is gone and war is coming, camp is exposed. Somebody strong has to stay. Somebody who can drill the younger kids and hold the border if monsters test it." She swallowed. "I volunteer. I stay. And you —" She pointed at Silena. "You stay with me. Because if both of us go, Cabin Three will be empty and Dad —" She stopped. Their father in Atlantis, too far to help. "Cabin Three stays standing. That is how we help Percy."

Silena's eyes filled. "But —"

"Silena." Clarisse took her sister's face in both hands, fierce and gentle. "You pack the best lunches in the world. Pack his. Pack it so good he has to come back to tell you what he ate last. That is your quest."

Tears spilled over. Silena nodded, laughing wetly. "Okay. Okay. I can do that."

Percy felt his own eyes burn. He stood and hugged both his sisters at once, arms around their waists, face pressed to Silena's shoulder and Clarisse's elbow. They smelled like sunscreen and rope and home.

"Best cabin," he mumbled.

"Damn right," Clarisse said, and kissed the top of his head hard. "Don't die, Kelp Head. I will kill you."


---

The question of Thalia being a guardian lasted longer than her victory had made it seem.

Chiron called the four travelers back to the map after Clarisse and Silena went outside for air. The ping-pong table was covered with roads in blue ink, folded schedules, and three empty circles he had drawn on a sheet of paper to represent the named questers.

Thalia pointed at the circles. "You could draw a fourth."

"That would not change the Oracle's words," Chiron said.

"I know. I'm saying it would make your diagram accurate."

Grover was staring at the map with an expression Percy recognized from whenever their satyr friend smelled a monster and could not decide which direction it was coming from.

"Could I say something?" he asked.

Everybody turned. Grover fidgeted with the reed pipes tucked into his belt.

"If the Oracle says three, and we take four, isn't that exactly the sort of thing we tell campers not to do when we teach them old quest stories?"

Thalia's face reddened. "I'm not planning to steal anybody's place."

"I know. But if a fight happens, and you win it for us, then maybe we're not the three who did what the prophecy said."

Annabeth leaned forward.

"And if she doesn't help when one of us is about to die, we're being ridiculous about the wording."

"Also true," Grover said faintly. "I was hoping there'd be an easier answer."

Chiron set down the pen. Percy had noticed that adults sometimes looked delighted when a child found a problem, as though finding the problem were the same as fixing it. Chiron didn't. He looked tired.

"There is no rule saying a fourth companion immediately cancels a quest," he said. "There is also no guarantee that the Oracle's number will have the meaning we first assign it. A guardian may travel near a party without taking charge of the tasks named for that party. But once danger comes, all of you will have to decide what your obligations require."

"So you can't give us instructions that make it safe," Percy said.

"I can give you sensible precautions. I can't promise you a safe interpretation of a prophecy."

Thalia glanced at her sister. "Fine. Precautions. Start with the obvious: I'm not letting anyone separate Annabeth from the group in a fight."

"Good," Chiron said. "But do not interpret every difference of opinion as a reason to take command of her."

Thalia looked as though he'd accused her of something appalling.

"She's twelve."

"So is Percy," Annabeth said. "Nobody keeps saying his age every time he picks up a weapon."

"Believe me," Percy muttered, "Clarisse does."

Annabeth didn't look away from Thalia.

"I want you with us," she said. "I really do. But I need you to be my sister, not a second commander who can overrule me whenever you're frightened."

Thalia swallowed.

"I'm frightened all the time about you," she answered, very quietly.

Percy felt a little ashamed of how often he'd heard that sentence without noticing what it cost her to say it.

Annabeth reached over the map and turned the pencil between her fingers.

"Then if you think I'm doing something stupid, say so. I'll listen. Don't decide that because you're older you get the last vote."

Thalia considered. "What if it's a monster coming at you with a sword?"

"Then hit it. I'm not asking you to schedule an argument while it swings."

Grover made a helpless little noise, half laughter, half relief.

Percy thought they were finally agreeing on something, but Chiron held up a hand.

"The protections Zeus withdrew yesterday matter," he said. "There will be no eagle escort assigned to you, and you should not count on the court's aid if weather turns dangerous. That includes you, Thalia. Your own talents are yours to train and govern; they are not a substitute for an escort the god has explicitly refused."

Thalia nodded once. Percy knew she hated hearing her father's sanction turned into a practical travel constraint, but it was one. Chiron wasn't inventing it.

"We'll use roads with places to shelter," Annabeth said. She looked at Percy. "Can you handle water if we get stuck without a storm escort?"

"Sometimes. Depends how much there is."

"Good. That's what I'll write. Sometimes."

"That's not very reassuring," Grover said.

"Would you rather have me promise it always works?"

"No. Please don't."

Chiron marked the map at several public rest stops but didn't hand it over yet.

"Tomorrow morning you will receive the formal route guidance and the drachmas available to the camp. Tonight your job is to pack for what you can carry, not what you wish you could carry. A bag too heavy to run with can turn a small problem into a fatal one."

Grover immediately began to look guilty about his backpack.

"And you," Chiron said to him, "must decide when the wild is giving you information and when fear is merely making every noise seem dangerous."

Grover nodded. "I'm not always good at telling."

"That is precisely why you should tell the others what you sense without declaring certainty."

Percy watched them. Thalia was angry, Annabeth was thinking, and Grover looked as if he had been assigned a final exam on a subject nobody had taught him. None of them seemed especially like the heroes of a prophecy. That made the meeting feel more useful.

He placed his finger between the three drawn circles.

"I don't want to spend the whole trip counting whether we're three or four," he said. "I want to know who needs help and who's able to give it. If the prophecy has a problem with that, I guess we'll find out."

Chiron looked at him for a long time. Then he folded the paper with the circles and put it away.

"That," he said, "may be the most responsible answer available to us."

Thalia gave Percy a look that was half gratitude and half irritation. He suspected she wasn't done arguing about command. Neither was Annabeth. But they could leave camp with the disagreement spoken aloud rather than buried beneath everyone's attempts to sound brave.

---

Chiron dismissed them to prepare. Departure at dawn — not today, tomorrow, to allow packing, blessings, and messages to Olympus. One day to get ready for the road west.

One day felt like no time at all and forever.

The news spread through camp by lunch the way news always did — via the Stolls, who knew everything before Chiron finished saying it.

Percy could not walk ten feet without someone stopping him.

At the dining pavilion, the Poseidon table was mobbed. Younger kids he barely knew pressed ambrosia squares and drachmas and lucky shells into his hands. A nine-year-old Demeter girl gave him a seed packet. "For emergencies," she whispered. "Plant it and run."

Tyson, Percy's Cyclops half-brother, still visiting from the undersea forges, arrived with a waterproof satchel he had stitched himself from old sailcloth, seams crooked but strong, and presented it with both hands. "For quest," Tyson said proudly. "Waterproof. I tested in lake. Peanut butter sandwich survived."

Percy hugged him. "Best satchel ever."

At the forge, the Hephaestus kids sharpened Percy's sword and checked the balance three times without being asked. At the arena, the Ares kids — who normally jeered at everything — formed a silent honor line when Clarisse walked Percy through, and each one tapped their practice blade to her spear as she passed. Camp respect, wordless.

Grover was mobbed by satyrs. They gave him reed charms and extra socks and a tiny emergency block of cheese "aged in the woods, very stinky, monsters hate it." Grover accepted it all with wide eyes and packed and repacked his bag four times.

Annabeth was mobbed worst of all. Everyone wanted the storm-born daughter to look at their maps, bless their blueprints, promise to bring back a postcard from the Hoover Dam. She handled it with brisk kindness, answering questions while her fingers worried the edge of her notebook, until Thalia bodily extracted her.

"Enough," Thalia announced, slinging an arm around Annabeth's neck and steering her toward the cabins. "My sister needs to pack, not sign autographs."

Percy caught Annabeth's eye over Thalia's arm. She smiled tiredly. He mouthed, you okay? She mouthed back, meet me later.

He nodded. Later. They would need later.


---

Grover found Percy at the edge of the strawberry fields with a list of questions he'd written on a scrap of bark.

Percy had been trying to count the gifts stuffed into his jacket pockets. He had two drachmas, a lucky shell, three pieces of ambrosia somebody had slipped him without an explanation and a small packet of seeds. Silena was going to make him take most of it out again when she checked his bag.

"Why bark?" Percy asked.

"I ran out of paper," Grover said. "And this was already on the ground."

The list was scratched deeply enough that Percy could read it. It contained *water*, *food*, *train*, *monster smell*, *money*, and *Juniper?* The last word had been underlined twice.

"Does Juniper need to come?" Percy asked.

Grover looked horrified. "No. She needs to know I'm not going to disappear without saying goodbye. I told her I would tell her when I was leaving camp. That was supposed to mean something."

"Then tell her. Now."

"I did tell her. She wants to know whether I'll be able to write."

Percy hadn't thought that far ahead. He'd imagined heading west and returning with the bolt, all the difficult days compressed into a single adventure. From the look of Grover's list, their actual problems would begin much sooner.

"Can we send messages from the road?" he asked.

"Sometimes Iris messages work. Sometimes the connection is lousy. Sometimes you don't have a drachma, or water, or time." Grover tapped the first word on the bark. "I want to know what happens when we can't."

Annabeth arrived carrying two empty water flasks and a roll of string. Thalia followed with a small tin of sewing supplies she'd taken from Cabin One.

"I heard the word messages," Annabeth said.

"Grover wants a way to tell Juniper he's alive," Percy explained.

"Not every hour," Grover said quickly. "Just when I can. I don't want her sitting by her tree thinking I've forgotten."

Annabeth set down the flasks. "Then we should decide how often we'll try. And what counts as a missed message."

Thalia frowned. "If we promise every night, we'll end up taking risks just to keep the promise."

"Exactly," Grover said, relieved. "I don't want that either."

They sat in the shade at the edge of the rows. Annabeth drew three columns on the back of an old practice schedule: what they could carry, what they could ask Chiron to provide, and what they simply couldn't guarantee. Percy pointed out that Chiron's map and drachmas were due in the morning. They could plan questions, not pretend to have the supplies already.

Grover explained what satyrs could sense on the road: plants under stress, disturbed soil, the smell of monsters when the wind was favorable. He admitted the smell of traffic and hot asphalt made those senses harder to use. He didn't always know whether a strange odor meant danger or merely a mechanic working late in a roadside garage.

Percy had assumed Grover would be their early-warning system. Listening to him now, Percy realized he'd been treating his friend's ability as if it were a machine that could be switched on and trusted without a second thought.

"So if you say something smells wrong, we stop and look," Percy said. "But we don't assume you know what it is."

"And if I say I don't know, you don't argue me into saying I do."

"Agreed."

Thalia picked up the bark list. "What's this about train?"

"If we have to take public transport, I need to know where I can hide my hooves," Grover said.

"Your jeans are fine," Percy said.

"Until somebody asks me to take my shoes off, or we get dragged into a station with metal detectors and I'm carrying pipes and three knives."

"You're carrying three knives?" Annabeth asked.

"One is a plant knife. One is a cheese knife. One is for cutting rope."

Thalia sighed. "We'll check what actually belongs in the bag."

Percy glanced at her. "Same goes for your spear."

"My spear is completely reasonable."

"It is longer than Grover."

"That is an argument in its favor."

They argued about the spear until Annabeth made them write it on the list for Chiron to review. None of them knew how the Mist would behave around a security checkpoint. A plan based on everybody seeing what they wanted to see wasn't much of a plan.

Finally Grover took the bark back. He crossed out the question mark beside Juniper and wrote *when safe*. Beneath it he added *tell her before we go*.

"That's enough," he said.

Percy thought so too. They couldn't know where the thief was, whether a message could reach Atlantis, or whether Zeus would punish his daughters more severely tomorrow. But they could agree not to sacrifice a companion's safety to a promise of a postcard.

As they separated for packing, Annabeth handed Percy one of the flasks.

"Fill this before tomorrow," she said. "Actual water, not the sea. Grover needs to drink it too."

Percy nodded and carried it to the cabin, where Silena was already waiting with a growing mountain of sandwiches.

---

Silena packed like war was coming and lunch was the weapon.

By mid-afternoon Cabin Three smelled like a bakery and a first-aid tent had collided. Silena had commandeered the spare bunk as a staging area and covered it with neat piles — folded socks, rolled bandages, a coil of blue rope, a water flask etched with a trident, a smaller flask etched with a lightning bolt.

"That one is for Annabeth," Silena said when Percy picked it up. "Tell her I remembered. She likes hers with lemon."

Percy stared. "How did you know that?"

"Girls notice," Silena said primly, and went back to wrapping sandwiches.

She made them assembly-line: turkey and cheese, peanut butter and honey, tuna with pickles on the side in a separate wax paper so it would not get soggy. She labeled each in Greek with the day it should be eaten. She added apples, cheese sticks, dried seaweed from Atlantis that she swore restored energy, ambrosia in a wax-sealed tin, nectar in a tiny stoppered bottle with strict instructions tied to the neck in blue thread — sip, do not gulp.

"Silena," Percy said, watching the pile grow. "We have to carry this."

"You will eat it," Silena said firmly. "You are twelve and growing and going west to fight a face-stealer. You will eat." Her hands stilled. "Promise me you will eat."

Percy crossed the cabin and hugged her from behind while she sat, chin on her head. "I promise. Every crumb."

"Good." Her voice wobbled. She kept wrapping.

Clarisse sat on her own bunk cleaning her spear and Percy's spare dagger side by side, oil and rag, methodical. When she finished she held the dagger out hilt-first.

"Sharp," she said. "Do not stick yourself."

"I won't."

"Do not lend it to Annabeth unless she asks nicely, which she will, and then do, because she is better with it than you."

"Hey."

Clarisse grinned, quick and fond. "Truth hurts, Kelp Head." Her grin faded. "Listen. Out there — you watch her back and she watches yours, yeah? That line about owl and trident sharing one line. That is you two. Stay on the same line. Do not go lone hero."

Percy nodded, throat tight. "I will."

"And Grover —" Clarisse shook her head. "Feed him. He thinks straight when he is fed. Thalia will handle rear guard, but do not rely on her to sleep. She will not sleep. Make her eat too."

"Yes, ma'am."

Clarisse stood and pulled him into a headlock and rubbed his hair until he yelped. "Best little brother," she muttered into his hair, so low only he heard. "Come back with all your limbs."

---

Ella cried.

Percy found her by the lake at sunset, where the harpies roosted in the pines. Ella the harpy, small and feathered and red-headed, his friend who memorized books and spoke in quotes and loved him with fierce, fluttering loyalty. She sat on the dock with her wings wrapped around her knees, a stack of books beside her tied with string.

When she saw him she burst into tears.

"Oh no," Percy said, sitting fast beside her. "Ella, hey, don't —"

"'Parting is such sweet sorrow,'" Ella sobbed, quoting and crying at once, feathers shaking. "Romeo and Juliet, act two! But it is not sweet! It is awful! Percy is going west! West is far! Far has monsters! Monsters have teeth!"

Percy put his arm around her wings, careful of the feathers. "Ella, I'm coming back. It's a quick trip. Find the bolt, say hi to the Hoover Dam, home by fireworks."

"'Home is the place where, when you have to go there, they have to take you in,'" Ella wept. "Robert Frost! Camp will take you in! I will take you in! But what if road takes you first?"

Around them the other harpies pretended not to listen while absolutely listening, preening a little too hard.

Percy thought of everything Ella had survived — cages, hunger, fear — and how she still gave away books like treasure. His chest ached.

"Ella," he said. "Will you do a quest for me while I'm gone? A really important one."

Ella sniffled. "Quest? Ella quest?"

"Yes. I need you to remember things for me. Keep a list of every book Annabeth wanted to read but hasn't yet, so when we get back I can surprise her. Can you do that?"

Ella straightened. Tears still on her cheeks, but her eyes focused, bright and sharp. Memorizing was her gift, her joy. "Ella can remember. 'I have always imagined that Paradise will be a kind of library.' Jorge Luis Borges! Ella will make library list!"

"Perfect. And one more." Percy took one of the blue hair ties Silena had given him and tied it gently around Ella's wrist, where feathers met skin. "Hold this for me. So I have a reason to come back and get it."

Ella stared at the blue tie. She cradled it to her chest. "Ella will guard with life. 'Guard with life,' many books. Ella understands."

She hugged him, wings beating, and Percy hugged back hard. Over her shoulder he saw the other harpies turn away to give them privacy, one of them wiping her beak on her wing.

---

Zoe found him after dark by the Hecate cabin torchlight.

Zoe Nightshade, daughter of Hecate, dark-eyed and grave, not warm the way Silena was warm, but steady the way stone was steady. She waited on the path with her hands folded, torchlight catching the silver in her black hair. Percy had fought beside her in capture the flag and seen her magic hold a bridge of mist together while the other team screamed.

"Jackson," Zoe said.

"Hey," Percy said.

She held out a small pouch. Inside, on a cord, lay a charm — a tiny silver torch crossed with a tiny bronze trident, backed by a flat black stone that drank the light.

"Hecate's daughters learn doors," Zoe said. "And crossroads. This is a door-charm. If you are trapped in stone —" She paused. Prophecy: sleeps chained in stone. "If you are trapped, hold it and speak my name. Once. It will open one door for you. Only one. Choose well."

Percy closed his fingers around it. The stone felt cool, then warm. "Zoe — this is — thank you. How can I ever —"

"Come back," Zoe said simply. "My magic works best on the living." Her mouth twitched, almost a smile. "And keep thy owl safe. She talks overmuch about architecture, but her wards are neat."

Percy laughed wetly. "I'll tell her you said that."

"Do not." Zoe tucked her hands back into her sleeves. "Go with torches lit, Percy Jackson."

She left before he could hug her, but he felt hugged anyway.

---

He met Annabeth on the hill past the cabins, where the grass lay flat and the Sound spread black and silver below.

Same hill as the blankets and the Diver. Different night. Tonight the sky was hazy, stars veiled, moon wrapped in cloud. Crickets sang. The camp lay behind them, lamp-lit and murmuring, preparing for sleep before a quest dawn.

Annabeth sat with her knees up and her notebook closed beside her, which told Percy how tired she was. She never closed the notebook. She wore the lemon flask Silena had given her on a cord around her neck already, and her camp jacket over her pajama shirt, and her braid loose for once, hair curling around her face.

Percy sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched. He handed her the door-charm to look at. She studied it, turning it over with careful fingers, and handed it back.

"Zoe's work," she said. "Good. Really good. Crossroads magic sticks to stone."

"Yeah."

Silence, comfortable and crackling at once. Below, a conch blew for lights-out. Neither of them moved.

"I packed," Annabeth said. "Thalia checked my bag and repacked it and then I repacked it when she wasn't looking because she thinks books are dead weight and I need my books."

Percy smiled. "How many?"

"Three. Down from seven. Compromise." Annabeth picked at a grass stem. "Grover packed cheese. I packed rope and chalk and a mirror. For looking around corners. AndAmbrosia. And your spare socks, because Silena said you lose socks like you lose —" She stopped.

"Like I lose what?"

"Like you lose track of time when you're in water," Annabeth said primly, but her ears went pink. "Her words. Mostly."

Percy bumped her shoulder. "Thanks for packing my socks."

"Someone has to."

More silence. The moon slid out from cloud, laying a road across the water. Percy watched it and thought about west, about stone, about a thief with no face. Fear moved in his belly, cold and quick, like a fish turning. He let himself feel it. Twelve was old enough to be scared and go anyway.

"I'm scared," Annabeth said quietly, as if she had heard him. Maybe she had. They did that sometimes, spoke at the same seam.

"Me too," Percy said.

"I keep thinking about that line. Owl and trident share one line. That's us. That's —" She looked at him. Gray eyes silver in moonlight. "I don't want to mess it up. You're my — you're my best friend, Percy. Besides Thalia. Besides Grover, different category. You're my —"

"Same," Percy said. His voice cracked. "You're my same."

Annabeth laughed, shaky. "Good. Then we agree. We go together, we come back together."

"Promise?" Percy held out his pinky, the way Silena had taught him when he was small and promises needed string to hold.

Annabeth hooked her pinky through his, solemn as an oath on the Styx. "Promise. No heroics without the other. No leaving."

"No leaving," Percy repeated.

They stayed like that, pinkies linked, shoulders pressed. Then Annabeth leaned forward and rested her forehead against his. Just that. Forehead to forehead, breathmingling, eyes closed. The world narrowed to the warm point where they touched and the sound of crickets and the smell of lemon and lake on her skin.

Percy closed his eyes. He memorized it the way Ella memorized books — pressure, warmth, the tickle of her hair, the steadiness of her breathing evening out with his.

Behind them, on the path, gravel crunched. Thalia cleared her throat loudly, pointedly, and said to the night at large, "Wow, the moon is really mooning tonight. I see nothing. I am blind. I am a blind guardian who sees nothing and will remember nothing."

Percy and Annabeth jerked apart, faces flaming. Thalia stood with her back pointedly turned, arms crossed, staring at a pine tree like it owed her money.

"Thalia!" Annabeth squeaked.

"What?" Thalia said to the pine tree. "I came to tell you curfew is in five and if you are late Clarisse will do push-ups with you on her back. I saw nothing. Foreheads are very platonic. Very sister-approved."

Percy covered his face. Annabeth threw a grass clump at her sister's back. Thalia grinned without turning around.

"Two minutes," Thalia said, holding up two fingers to the tree. "Then I'm dragging you both back by the ears. Questers need sleep."

She stayed turned. Percy and Annabeth looked at each other, embarrassed and laughing silently, and Percy mouthed promise again, and Annabeth nodded, and they touched foreheads once more, quick and fierce, while Thalia lectured the pine tree on the importance of rest.

---

Curfew came. Camp slept, or pretended to.

In Cabin Three the lamp burned late. Silena finished the last sandwich label and tied the food bag shut with blue thread in a bow. Clarisse set her spear by the door, ready for morning drills she would lead alone. Percy lay in his bunk with Zoe's charm on its cord around his neck, Tyson's satchel packed by his feet, Ella's blue tie memorized around a harpy wrist by the lake, his sisters breathing on either side.

Across camp, Cabin One's tall white roof was barely visible through Percy's porthole. He imagined Annabeth awake over a last check of the map and Thalia lecturing her about sleep, but he couldn't know. He would see them both at breakfast.

On the hill, mist drifted over the border. Percy wondered whether the attic trapdoor was closed again, and whether the thing waiting in the west wore a face he had already seen.

But that was tomorrow.

Tonight Camp Half-Blood held its breath together — Poseidon and Zeus, satyr and harpy and Hecate's daughter, toast crumbs and blue thread and promises kept forehead to forehead — and prepared to send its own into the dark, fed and armed and loved half to death.

Percy turned on his side and whispered to the dark, to no one and to Annabeth across the green, "One line," and fell asleep with the words like a rope in his hand.

Silena, still awake, whispered back from her bunk, "Come back."

Clarisse, half-asleep, finished it: "Or else."

Outside, thunder rolled far out over the Sound, soundless no longer, and the sky answered with heat lightning, and the camp slept on beneath it, ready.
