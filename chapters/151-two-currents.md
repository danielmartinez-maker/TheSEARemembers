## Chapter 151: Two Currents

The arena at half past nine was the hottest place at camp, which was saying something, because the sun had been up for hours and the rest of camp had spent them trying to stay in the shade.

Clarisse was already there.

She'd come down before the conch, before breakfast, before the part of the morning that belonged to anybody else, with two practice spears under her arm and a thought she couldn't shake. She set the spears in the sand, butt-first, side by side like twins, and stood there in her worn boots and her cut-off camp shirt and let herself think it, because she'd been not-thinking it for weeks.

She had been training Percy wrong.

Not the Atlas. The Atlas had nearly killed him and then made him better, which was a normal Clarisse lesson plan — she'd learned how to teach from people who taught her to survive by almost dying first. No, the Atlas was the best thing she'd ever taught anybody.

The wrong part was everything around the Atlas. Every time she drilled him on footwork, she drilled it slow. Every time she let him hold a live weapon, the yard was empty. She talked about real fights like weather — stuff that happened to other people, somewhere else, while Percy stood very still and waited for it to be over. She had been building him a training program where nothing could touch him, and it had taken an argument about a dock to see what she'd built. The shape of it. The thing she could hold.

Percy had said the word out loud on the dock, three mornings ago, and then said it again more kindly: *control*. And she'd wanted to hit something, and then she'd wanted to be hit by something, and then she'd gone to the arena at midnight and thrown a spear into the sand until her shoulders ached, because those were the only three things she knew how to do with a feeling that big.

She picked up one of the practice spears. Turned it. The wood was warm from the sun already, worn smooth where a hundred hands had gripped it, taped at the grip with the black electrical tape that Chiron bought by the case.

"You're going to wear a hole in the sand," said a voice from the gate.

Clarisse didn't turn around. "You're late."

"I'm fashionably late."

"You're wearing sneakers."

Percy came through the gate — sneakers, damp cuffs on his jeans from where he'd been down at the lake first, a green camp shirt untucked on one side, hair that looked like he'd run his hands through it while thinking about running his hands through it. He had two oranges in his hand, held up like a peace offering, which was funny, because Clarisse was not at war with him and had never once managed it for longer than five minutes.

"Breakfast," Percy said. He tossed her one. She caught it without looking. "You weren't at the Poseidon table."

"You weren't either."

"I was at the lake."

"Planning your victory."

"I was planning my morning."

"Same thing." She peeled the orange in one strip, the way Silena had taught her years ago, no juice wasted. "Sit down. Stretch. Then we're going to talk about how you fight like you're apologizing."

Percy sat down on the sand, not the bleachers, because Percy had never once in his life used a bench when the ground was available.

"The Atlas again?" he asked.

"No."

"Spear forms?"

"No."

"Then what?"

Clarisse finished her orange and dropped the peel in the bucket by the gate, which somebody had painted POSEIDON CABIN on the side of in letters that had begun to chip. She picked up the second spear and held it out to him, butt-first, the way you handed somebody a job.

"Sparring," she said.

Percy looked at the spear. Then at her. Then at the spear again.

"You mean — sparring."

"I mean sparring. Where I try to win. You can hit back. That's what makes it sparring."

He took the spear, hefted it, checked the balance, did the two quick test swings she'd taught him without ever noticing she'd taught him. "I've sparred before."

"You've danced before. You've done forms. You held the sky for eleven minutes and made Silena cry about it." She walked backward into her corner of the sand, her boots scuffing two lines. "I'm asking different questions today."

Percy's chin came up, the stubborn rising through him like heat. "What kind of questions?"

"The kind where I beat you."

The first exchange lasted about four seconds.

Clarisse came in low and fast, the way she always did, her spear sweeping a flat arc at his knees, and Percy hopped it — he'd never in his life been slow on his feet — and then she was already turning the shaft in her hands, reversing the arc, and she tapped him twice in the time it took him to understand what direction he was being attacked from. Once on the ribs. Once, gently, on the side of the head, where the practice tip kissed his ear.

"Point," she said. "Point."

"You went fast."

"I went war-god fast. There's a difference."

She reset. He reset. He was breathing already, too fast, and his guard was up where she'd taught him to put it, which was high and too tight, and that was the problem, because a guard that tight could only defend one line at a time and the whole rest of him was an invitation.

She came in again. He blocked. She rode the block, turned it, took his front foot out from under him with the flat of the shaft, and when he stumbled she didn't tap him — she just waited, spear down at her side, and let him catch his balance, because she was a war god's daughter in this life too, and even war gods had a line they didn't cross to win a practice match against their baby brother.

"Four seconds," she said. "You want to know why?"

"My guard's too high."

"Your guard's too high, you're breathing like you're climbing stairs, and you're watching my spear." She tapped her own chest. "Watch me. The spear does what I tell it. I tell it what to do because I know where I want it to go. You keep watching the weapon and you'll never see the person."

"Knowing isn't the same as doing. That's the whole problem with you." She grinned, not a nice grin, the grin he'd been answering with a middle finger since he was nine. "Again."

Again.

He came at her this time, which was something — Clarisse never had to tell him twice to try, which was the thing about her little brother that scared her most, because he had never met a situation he didn't charge into with everything he had.

She let him come. She let him throw a straight thrust that spent itself against her spear, then she stepped inside his reach, the oldest trick there was, and stopped the butt of her shaft against his sternum and pushed, just enough to move him, just enough to teach him.

"Point," she said. "You're dead."

"I had you."

"You died."

"I had you before I died."

"Nobody's judging your essay. You're dead."

He reset without arguing, which worried her more than if he'd argued. He did the thing where he arranged his face into carefulness, and Clarisse knew that carefulness. It was the face he made when he was about to do something enormous.

He came at her again. Not fast. Patient, which was against every law of his nature. He feinted high — badly, but it was a feint, which was new — and when her spear ticked up to meet it, he changed levels and swept her front ankle, and she hopped, and he was already pressing a two-hit combination at her shoulder and ribs, and she took the first on her spear and the second on her forearm because the angle was wrong and her body knew it before her mind did.

She stepped back. Breath out. Point to Percy, in her head, silent.

"Well," she said. "You've been practicing when I wasn't looking."

"Who taught you to feint?"

"You did. Last month. You said I telegraph everything."

"I said you telegraph everything *and* you lead with your face." She circled. He circled. The sand gritted under their boots and sneakers, two different sounds, one heavy and one quick. "Who told you to be patient?"

"Nobody." His eyes were the color of the lake in August, the deep part, the part past the buoys. "I just got tired of losing."

"Good." She came in. He met her. "Get tired of it every time."

They worked.

That was Clarisse's word for it and it was the right one. They worked through the morning, and the sun climbed, and the sand got warmer underfoot, and little by little the arena filled up — a few Athena kids at a knot-tying station in the shade, a pair of Hermes kids racing the length of the stands, and eventually a knot of younger campers who had come to watch the Poseidon siblings hit each other with sticks, because the camp had decided, in the days since the argument, that watching Clarisse be gentle with somebody was the best show going.

She ignored them. Percy did not. Every time he glanced at them, she tapped him.

"Point. Point. Point."

"They're watching you," Percy said, between a block and a reset.

"They're watching *you*. You're the one they came for. Little Percy Jackson, sea prince, holds up the sky, gets beat up by his big sister." She spun her spear in a flashy arc that was pure theater and something she'd practiced in a mirror when she was nine. "That's why they're here. They want to see the hero bleed."

Percy's eyes went to the front row — a kid from Hephaestus, maybe ten, cross-legged in the sand with a juice box. The kid waved. Percy waved back with the hand that wasn't holding the spear, which cost him a tap on the shoulder.

"Point," Clarisse said. "See what being nice gets you."

"I'm not being nice. I'm being seen."

"Same thing in this family."

"Again," she said. "And this time when I come high, you don't flinch."

"You flinch. I've watched you flinch at a loud noise since you were small. Now stop flinching."

Round three was not a round. It was a conversation.

She came high. He didn't flinch. She went low, and he was already moving, not away but into it, the way water moves into a rock's shadow, and his spear came up under hers and turned it aside and he stepped inside her guard with the softness of somebody who had stopped thinking about winning and started thinking about the sand under his feet.

The tip of his practice spear touched her ribs. Light. Almost nothing.

He looked as shocked as she felt.

"Point," she said, and her voice did something she wished it hadn't, so she made it flat and mean. "Don't look so surprised. You set that up four minutes ago. I was just too busy showing off to notice."

Percy stared at her. Then the grin broke across his face, the real one, the one that made him look twelve and not fourteen, and he reset his stance, and something happened between them that had nothing to do with points.

She came at him with everything. She drove him back three steps, four, and he gave ground like it cost him something, blocking one, deflecting two, taking a third on the shoulder, and then he planted his feet and stopped her cold, and the two spears locked, and they stood there nose to nose in the middle of the arena, breathing hard, wood straining against wood.

"You stopped," Percy panted.

"Beginners' luck," Clarisse lied.

"Again."

She spun out of the lock and came around wide, and Percy came with her, and they traded — she took one, he took one, she took two, he took one that was meant and not an accident — and that was when he overreached.

He went for a low sweep, too deep, too committed, the way he did everything, and her shaft slammed down across his forearms, and his own spear turned in his hands, and he went down hard: one knee, then a palm skidding across the sand, then his weight coming onto a wrist that bent the wrong way with a sound that made Clarisse's stomach drop through the floor of the world.

The practice spear clattered. She didn't remember dropping hers.

"Ow," Percy said quietly, cradling his hand against his chest, and then, when she dropped to her knees in the sand in front of him: "No, no, I'm fine, don't —"

"Give it here." Her voice came out rough enough to strip paint. She took his wrist in both hands, gentle the way she was only ever gentle with him and Silena and nobody else living, and turned it into the light, and went cold all over, because it was already swelling, purple blooming at the bone like a tide coming in.

"Clarisse —"

"You went too deep."

"I know."

"You went too deep and you went too fast and you didn't set your recovery." Her thumbs moved carefully, feeling for the wrongness, and her eyes stung, and she was furious at herself, and at the sand with its soft forgiving surface that had still managed to hurt him. "Numb or tingly?"

"A little. A little tingly."

"Then it's not broken. But it's going to be purple for a week, and you're going to tell everybody you got it in a fight with a monster, and I'm going to let people believe it, because I'm generous." She reached into the cargo pocket of her shorts — she'd put the roll of gauze there that morning without admitting to herself why — and started wrapping his hand and wrist in quick, competent loops, the way she'd learned to wrap Silena's hands a hundred summers ago, before Silena got nicer about things and started doing the wrapping herself.

Percy watched her work with a small, crooked, embarrassed expression, and the younger campers had gone quiet in the way of people witnessing something they knew they shouldn't speak about.

"Hey," he said softly.

"What."

"You're shaking."

"You're twisting your wrist the wrong way."

"Clarisse."

She stopped. The gauze ran from her fingers. She looked down at the knot she was tying — white, tidy, unbecomingly neat — and she thought about the dock, and the argument, and the shape of things she had built to keep people safe until they turned into cages.

"I used to do this in the other life," she said. "Wrap my own hands. Nobody ever wrapped them for me. You got hurt, you kept going, because Ares's kids didn't get the luxury of lying down." She tucked the end of the gauze in and tapped the finished knot with two fingers. "And then I got a little brother who charges into everything with his whole stupid heart, and I forgot how to let him get hurt at all."

Percy was quiet a moment. The arena murmured around them — footsteps, voices, the distant *thock* of an arrow finding a target.

"I did charge in," he said.

"You did."

"Like, full charge. Face first."

"That's the style we discussed."

"I got a point earlier."

"I saw it." She sat back on her heels. The sand was hot even through her jeans. "You got a point. You also hit like you were writing a letter about it — 'Dear Clarisse, I would like to hit you, here is one hit, please return serve.' When you commit, Percy, commit. Do the thing halfway and the thing will eat you."

"That's profound."

"That's Tuesday. Shut up."

He flexed his wrapped hand experimentally, wincing, and she wanted to take the wince back from him and put it somewhere else, in herself, the way she'd always wanted for everybody, the way that was the whole flaw of her.

"Thalia's on the ridge with Annabeth," she said, because she wasn't going to look at his face while she said the next part. "Behind the Zeus cabin. Your girlfriend's learning lightning from your sister."

Percy got up and tested his wrist again, making a face at it. "Annabeth has lightning?"

"Annabeth has *static*," Clarisse said. "It's all in how it holds together. She spent last week shorting out every doorknob in the Zeus cabin."

"That's horrifying."

"That's your girlfriend. She's very romantic." She gathered up both practice spears and started for the gate, and he followed, sneakers dragging slightly now that his wrist hurt. "If Thalia's teaching like Thalia teaches, she's already been yelled at once and cried once and is currently being yelled at again."

"Should we —"

"We should go get them when I'm done with the peels." Clarisse stopped at the gate and let one of her real faces show, the one under the mean one. "You did good today. The point was real. Next time I won't have to hold back at all, and then we'll see what you're actually made of."

Percy's ears went red. He kicked at the sand and looked off toward the lake, and the red reached the back of his neck, and Clarisse noted all of it for later ammunition.

"You're blushing," she said.

"I'm hot."

"It's ninety degrees and it's got nothing to do with it."

"Siblings don't say nice things."

"I do. I say them constantly. You bring it out in me." She started walking again, up the path. The lake glinted through the trees, bright and freshwater-clean. "Go put ice on that wrist, and tell Annabeth the Zeus-cabin accompaniment rule is still in effect."

"The dock rule?"

"Ten minutes per occurrence, retroactive. Double for Zeus-cabin accompaniment." Clarisse counted it out on her fingers, slow and sarcastic. "She's on my dock beyond her allotment right now, and I'm letting it slide only because I'm in a good mood."

"The rule's dead. You said it in front of everybody."

"The rule's dead to *me*," Clarisse said. "The rule lives in my heart. That's where rules go when they die." She clapped his good shoulder. "Now go find something cold for that hand, and when Thalia's done teaching Annabeth to throw lightning like a girl throwing a tantrum, I want to see casualties."

The ridge behind the Zeus cabin was the driest place at camp, and Annabeth had chosen it on purpose, because she chose everything on purpose.

She'd gotten there twenty minutes early, which was another word for half an hour of being terrified, and she'd spent it setting up: the flat shelf of granite, warmed all day by the sun; her canteen at the top of the slab where she couldn't kick it; the borrowed hoodie — Thalia's, gray, sleeves past her knuckles, cuffs frayed — folded once at the edge, because it was too hot to wear and she was determined to be sensible for at least the first hour of her life.

Then she'd sat down in the dry grass with her back against the rock, sketchbook on her knees, and waited by drawing, which was how she waited for everything.

From the ridge you could see the lake behind and above the cabins, through a screen of pines: a bright blue shape pressed into the green of the valley, the buoys tiny white dots, the dock a long brown line where the water met the sand. Annabeth drew it fast — three strokes for the shoreline, four for the dock — and then, because she could not help herself and because this was the flaw of her mind the way charging was the flaw of Percy's, she labeled it. *Lake. Canoe dock. Buoys move seasonally. See annotations.*

A grasshopper went off in the grass beside her like a sneeze, the cicadas were up in the pines doing their long hot sawing, and the whole ridge smelled like resin and dust and the hot-metal smell of a sun going down on stone. The camp below sounded like it had finally, in the past few days, stopped holding its breath.

She turned the page and started a second drawing: a rough diagram of what Thalia had told her, three days ago, to expect. Lightning wants ground. You are not the ground. You are the path. Offer the path; don't hoard the charge; don't fight it, because fighting is how it turns on you. She drew it as a little map — an arrow coming down, a figure standing with her arms at her sides, a second arrow going out sideways into the earth. Labeled: *Grounding. Like a river finding the sea.*
"It looks like a plumbing diagram."

Annabeth's pencil stuttered. She hadn't heard the footsteps, which never happened, which meant whatever else was true, her sister had learned quietly in the last year and was going to be unbearable about it.

Thalia came up the ridge with her spear across her shoulders and her hair in its usual state of rebellion — the black of it lifting at the ends, not from the wind but from the static clinging to it, the way it did whenever Thalia had been practicing and not telling anyone. She had two water bottles in one hand, one obviously stolen from the Poseidon cabin's shelf by the paint on the cap, and she was wearing jeans with a hole in one knee and her old leather jacket, unzipped, because Thalia wore the jacket in every kind of weather and no argument on earth had ever moved her.

"It's a schematic," Annabeth said.

"It's a schematic of a drain." Thalia dropped onto the rock with the bonelessness of somebody who was eighteen and had decided that morning that today's job was being a sister rather than a protector, and had put the armor on over her camp shirt anyway, because habits were hard. She handed Annabeth the canteen. "You drew a diagram. Of lightning. As if lightning were a sewer."

"It's how I think."

"That's the problem." She tugged Annabeth's ponytail, the way an older sister measures time. "You're early, there's grass on your boots, and you've labeled the lake. Twice."

"Okay," Thalia said. "Here's how today goes. You've got the theory, because you get everything in theory. What you don't have is the part where your body stops arguing with every nerve ending it's ever had and just lets the electricity go where it's supposed to go."

"Fight the current, it turns on you," Annabeth recited. "Offer the path. Don't hoard the charge."

"Perfect. Textbook. Now forget the textbook. Show me your hands."

Annabeth looked at her own hands — sun-brown across the knuckles, a smear of graphite on the left one, nails bitten to the quick. She held them out, palms up.

"These are holding themselves," Thalia said. "Look at them. Your whole self is doing that. You plan everything, Annabeth. You label everything. You map it all down to the millimeter, and then when something comes at you that can't be mapped, you go rigid, and whatever you're holding — lightning, a friend, a sister — you hold so tight it has nowhere to go."

"That's — that's not fair."

"Fair's over here." Thalia touched her own temple. "Honest's over here. You called me rigid. On the dock. Drink your own medicine."

Annabeth looked at her hands a moment longer. Then she unclenched them, one finger at a time, and tried to breathe like a person who had nowhere to be.

"Fine," she said. "Teach me."

"Good." Thalia walked out onto the flat part of the ridge, a shelf of bare granite and dry yellow grass where nothing overhead but a single scrawny pine stood anywhere close. "Rule one: not near the lake. Water conducts. Drop a charge in this lake, everybody in it feels it, and Clarisse will personally throw me into the west shore. Rule two: nothing overhead —" She pointed up. "Nothing to strike. Rule three: ground goes down and out, never through you. Your arms are not the ground. Your body is a hallway. The lightning is a guest, and you let it out the back door."

Annabeth stood, the dry grass hissing against her boots. She pulled the hoodie on anyway — rolled the sleeves up twice over her elbows — because she got cold when she was nervous, and because Thalia's jacket-think was maybe catching.

"Rule four," Thalia said, and her voice went quiet. "You don't aim at anything you couldn't survive aiming at. Lightning doesn't forgive being confused about what it wants. And neither will I."

The first try was not a lightning bolt. It was barely a spark.

Thalia built it in her own hands first, the way she did — a pale thread of it, no bigger than a birthday candle, wound between her fingers — and Annabeth watched with her whole body gone still and her cartographer's brain went quiet for once, because there was no map for this, no drawing of the way the light crawled from one finger to the next like something alive and curious and old.

"It wants to leave," Annabeth said.

"Everything alive does. That's not a reason to trap it. Take it."

"How?"

"I don't know. That's your part." Thalia opened her hands, and the thread stretched and reached, and Annabeth reached back, both hands cupped, and the spark jumped the gap and landed like a cat landing — a soft thump of presence in her palms — and her whole body shouted for her to close, to grab, to hold, and she did none of those things, and for about four seconds the spark sat in her hands, bright and new and hers.

Then she breathed.

The spark died like a candle in a gale.

"Sorry," she said immediately, to the spark, to Thalia, to the ridge, to the idea of competence. "I moved. I breathed and it moved and I —"

"You held it," Thalia said. "Four seconds. That's four more than yesterday."

"That was nothing."

"That was four seconds. That's not nothing." Thalia flopped back onto the rock, arms behind her head. "Do it again. And breathe anyway. It can learn to breathe with you. That's the whole trick nobody tells you — it's not about not moving. It's about moving and being held anyway. That's what the sky is, on a bad day. That's what people are."

The second spark came slower, and on the third pass Annabeth almost fumbled it by flinching in advance, which was worse than flinching, and Thalia laughed at her, out-loud eighteen-year-old laughter rolling off the rock into the dry grass.

"Stop anticipating," Thalia said. "You keep being scared before anything happens. It's my least favorite thing you do."

"It's nobody's favorite thing I do."

"It's in the top three." Thalia sat up, and her face went serious, and the static retreated from her hair a little. "It was in mine, for a while. When I first came back — after the tree, after the Hunters, all of it — everything was so loud, and I kept getting ready for the bad part before it even arrived. I scared somebody I hadn't meant to scare, and I had to learn, the slow way, that getting ready isn't the same as being ready."

Annabeth sat down on the rock beside her, not too close, and let the second spark die quietly before she tried again.

"I'm scared I'll hurt Percy," she said, to her hands, because that was easier than the ridge and the sky. "I threw it at him once, during the lightning catch, and it left a mark on his arm, and he said it was fine, and it wasn't fine, and I keep —" Her throat went tight. She took the breath anyway. "I keep getting ready for the bad part. With him. With all of it. I get ready so hard that sometimes I forget to let it be good."

The ridge was quiet. Cicadas. Far below, the lake doing its afternoon thing, bright and freshwater and honest, having no idea that all this was happening above it.

Thalia was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "You know my little brother?"

Annabeth looked up. "Jason."

"My little brother. At Camp Jupiter. Half a continent away and, I'm told, currently trying to teach a garden spirit to appreciate his collection of yo-yos." Thalia's mouth did the thing it did when Jason came up, half a smile and half a wound. "He writes me letters. Real ones, on paper. And in every single one he tells me something true about himself that he's scared to. He's fourteen, same as you, and he's got lightning in him like you've got lightning in you, and it comes out sideways when he's scared, and he tells me about it anyway."

"Because fear isn't the problem. Fear arrives. It knocks. The problem is only what you do when you open the door." Thalia's gray eyes — her father's eyes, which were Annabeth's eyes — went direct and steady. "You hurt Percy once, with a thing you hadn't been taught, and you told me about it, and you're up here learning. Do you have any idea how rare that is?"

Annabeth's throat hurt. "I'm scared it'll happen again."

"It will."

Thalia said it plainly. No cushioning. She'd learned that Annabeth could smell a cushion from a mile off.

"It'll happen again," Thalia said. "Not the mark — you've been practicing. But one day your nerve will go, or you'll be tired, or somebody will scare you at the wrong moment, and a little of it will go where it shouldn't. That's not failure. That's a Tuesday. The question is whether you tell the truth about it afterward, and whether you keep coming back up here." She poked Annabeth's shoulder, gently, the way Percy poked at a stuck door. "Same with Percy. Same with me. You can't love people and be certain."

Annabeth looked at the ridge. At the pine. At the lake glinting below. At the sketchbook lying open where she'd left it, the labels suddenly looking not like facts but like prayers.

"Okay," she said. "Again."

"Good girl. And this time, tell me what you feel. Not what it's doing. What it does to you."

"It's cold. No — it's the idea of cold. It's the second before you jump in the lake." She held her hands up. Thalia reached, and passed it across, and this time when it landed Annabeth made herself keep breathing, made herself be a hallway, a hallway with a guest in it. "Oh. It's listening."

"Everything wants to be listened to," Thalia said, and there was the whole of her in it, the girl who had been a tree for six decades and had learned patience from someone she'd loved and lost. "Now don't grab it. Let it stand there. Be the hallway."

So she was the hallway.

The hallway was warm stone and dry grass and the smell of her own sunscreen, and at the far end of it there was a door. The spark stood in the middle of the hall and looked around, and Annabeth did not grab it. She counted, because counting was a thing her mind could hold while the rest of her held nothing.

Five. The spark dimmed, steadied, found its shape — no longer a nervous thread but a small bright knot, the size of a marble, the size of a heart.

Eight. The knot began to fray at the edges. Not dying this time. Fraying, coming apart in slow curls, and every instinct Annabeth had shouted for her to close her hands and save it.

Eleven. She did something else. She sank her weight — one foot back, knees soft, the stance Clarisse would have called a good recovery — and opened the hallway's back door, and the fraying charge went down out of her palms and through her boots and into the warm rock of the ridge with a sound like a held breath let go. The stone smoked, a scorch mark small as a thumbprint.

The ridge smelled like a struck match. Annabeth stood in it with her hands empty and her heart going like a drum, and she had never been prouder of a thumbprint.

"That," Thalia said, "was grounding."

"It was three seconds of not dying."

"It was eleven seconds of holding and a controlled discharge. That's a real thing with a real name." Thalia was off the rock and coming across the granite, and her face was doing something Annabeth did not see very often and always wanted to see more: pride, unguarded, the thing under the protector, plain as day. "Say it back to me."

"I held it. And when it started to come apart, I put it down on purpose."

"Say the name."

"Controlled discharge."

"Faster, next time. And don't just put it down — tell it where you're putting it. Down, out, away from anything standing downrange." Thalia pointed at the lake, bright below them. "Never aim at a door you can't open. Water, people, houses, the Ares cabin — anything that can't dodge. You especially don't get to be careless with it near him."

Annabeth nodded. The wind came up the ridge and brought the lake-smell with it, cool water and sun-warmed pine.

"One more thing," Thalia said. "Shape it."

"Shape it."

"Don't just hold it. Move it. I can't teach you this part — it's like teaching somebody to dance by describing the song. You feel where the charge wants to go, and you say yes, gently. Like a river going around a stone." She made a ball in her own hands, the one that had taught Percy the lightning catch, and Annabeth watched it the way you watch a person, not a phenomenon. "Stop trying to make it do what you planned. Make it do what it wants, with you watching where it goes."

Annabeth held her hands out. Thalia passed the charge across, a bigger one this time, and Annabeth felt it land like a thing with legs, and she said yes to it, gently, the way you'd steer a canoe with your paddle instead of dragging it.

It moved.

It moved up her wrist and around her elbow and down again, a slow bright braid that went where she looked the way water goes down a slope, and she laughed — she couldn't help it — and the spark jumped, startled, and unraveled into a dozen tiny threads that ran up her sleeve and popped, one by one, against the inside of the borrowed hoodie's cuffs, and the last of them went straight up and singed a thin black line into her left eyebrow.

The ridge went very quiet.

Thalia looked at her. Annabeth looked at Thalia.

"You," Thalia said carefully, "have got a — right there, on your —"

"I can feel it. It's on my eyebrow."

"I'm fine, Thalia, I'm fine —" Annabeth pressed her sleeve to her face and smelled the scorch, that exact hot-penny smell, and then, because her sister was making a face like the world was ending, she started to laugh too — a wet, helpless laugh that came up out of the fear she'd been carrying all afternoon, and once it started it would not stop, and behind her Thalia began laughing too, the two of them standing on a hot rock in the late afternoon laughing until a blue jay down the slope lifted off in a panic at the noise.

"Your eyebrow," Thalia gasped, wiping her eyes, "is having a much worse day than your wrist —"

"My what?"

"Earlier. I saw Percy. On the path, with Clarisse, with his hand wrapped up like a snowman. Broken, is he? Did she break him?"

"He says it's not broken. It's purple." Annabeth pressed her sleeve to her mouth, giggling into the gray cotton, and thought of Percy coming down the path with an orange in his hand and his sister's gauze on his wrist, and her whole chest went warm in a way that had nothing to do with lightning. "He's probably down at the dock right now."

"Good." Thalia sat back down on the warm rock and patted the stone beside her, and Annabeth sat, and their shoulders knocked together, and Thalia reached over and tucked the singed strand of Annabeth's ponytail behind her ear with absent, familiar fingers. "Then you'll have somebody to incriminate."

"I set it down on purpose," Annabeth said quietly, not looking at her. "The controlled discharge. I put it down. Thalia — it worked. It actually worked."

"I know. I saw." Thalia's hand stayed, a moment, at the side of her head, and then came away. "You're going to be dangerous, Annabeth Grace. You already are. There's no world where you're not. But you're going to be dangerous on purpose or dangerous by mistake, and today you chose on purpose. That's the whole difference between us and monsters."

Annabeth looked at her. "Say that again."

"No, you'll get a big head." But she said it again, softer, and she let Annabeth hold her sleeve for a second, and the static in her hair settled and buzzed and settled.

Then, because Thalia was, among her many qualities, a person who could not let a moment stay tender for more than eleven seconds, she pulled a coil of thin copper wire out of her jacket pocket and held it up.

"Arm," she commanded.

"What's that?"

"Grounding charm. Wound it last night. It gives the charge somewhere to go if you ever get surprised. It won't save you — nothing saves you — but it buys you a second, and a second is usually the whole game." She took Annabeth's right wrist and wound the coil around it, three turns, neat as a map legend, and tied it off with a knot that looked exactly like the kind of knot Annabeth would have tied, because they had learned their knots in the same places. "There. Now you're officially a Grace sister with hardware. Don't tell Clarisse. She'll want matching ones."

"I want matching ones," Annabeth said, and Thalia snorted, and Annabeth turned her wrist in the sun and watched the copper catch the light, bright and orange-red, the color of the thing she'd done today that nobody could take back from her.

She picked up her sketchbook. On the back page, where the plumbing-diagram lived, she added, in her small careful hand: *Ridge. Grounding practice. 11 seconds held. Discharge clean. Note: water conducts. Never near the lake.* And then, because Thalia leaned over to look and she wanted her to look, she added one more line and let her see it: *Dangerous on purpose or dangerous by mistake. Today: on purpose.*

Thalia read it, and read it again, and made a big show of not being moved, and failed.

"Write that on the map," Thalia said. "On the real one. The Big House wall. Put a little note on it — 'Here there be lightning.' And put my name on it, and yours. People should know who works there."

"It's not a map of —"

"It's a map of everything you've touched." Thalia stood, pulled Annabeth up with her, shouldered her spear, and started down the ridge path, and Annabeth grabbed her sketchbook and pulled the hoodie's sleeves down over her hands, because the evening had turned gold and the air was cooling the way it did when the lake let go of the day's heat. "Come on. Clarisse said your boyfriend's got a wrapped hand and a lot of opinions, and I want to see both."

---

Percy had not gone to get ice for his wrist.

Camper Percy Jackson did not, as a rule, put ice on things. Camper Percy Jackson sat on the end of the dock with his feet hanging over the water and his wrapped hand in his lap and his eyes on the ridge path, waiting for a girl in a gray hoodie to come down it.

The lake did its afternoon-into-evening thing around him: turquoise going long and gold at the edges, the chop picking up as the wind came across from the far shore, the water talking to the pilings under him the way it had talked to every version of him since before he could walk — hello, hello, you're back, I kept your place. So the waiting wasn't hard. What was hard was not checking his wrist every eleven seconds to see how far the purple had spread.

He got up when he saw them. Two figures on the ridge path, coming down through the long gold light between the cabins. One in black, one in gray. He made it down the planks and onto the sand just as they reached it.

Annabeth was already grinning. Percy felt the grin go straight through him and out his own face before his brain had even arrived.

He stopped a step away from her and took stock: the singed eyebrow, the scorched cuff on the borrowed hoodie, the smell of struck match that came off her like a wake, and the new coil of copper wrapped at her wrist, bright as a match head every time the sun found it.

"I want to kiss you," he said carefully, "but you're crackling."

"Don't — I mean, you can — I controlled it. Mostly. Thalia, tell him."

"I'm telling you," said Thalia, dropping onto the sand with her back against a piling, "that you are both going to be insufferable about this for a week, and I would like it noted for the record that I predicted it." She tipped her head back and called up toward the pavilion. "Clarisse! We have casualties!"

"They're not casualties," Annabeth said. "We're present."

"Casualties," Thalia said. "Fifteen minutes ago I had a girl with a scorched eyebrow and a smile on her face. If that's not a casualty, what is?"

Percy took Annabeth's wrist and turned the new copper coil over in the light. Three neat turns, tied off with a knot that could only have come from people who'd learned their knots in the same places.

"It's you," he said. "You made it nerdy."

"It's not nerdy, it's functional. Grounding charm." She opened the sketchbook and angled it toward him, and there on the back page was the plumbing diagram from the morning — still a plumbing diagram, in Percy's opinion — with a new column of small neat writing under it. "I logged everything. Eleven seconds on the hold. Controlled discharge, clean, down and out. And Thalia made me write down that water conducts. In my own handwriting."

"I also made her promise she'd never try it in the lake," Thalia said from the sand. "Because her face does something when she's excited, and the lake does not need that."

"I am standing right here," Annabeth said.

"I know. I can see your face."

"Clarisse," Thalia called. "We're fine! Come down!"

A fast-moving orange camp shirt came over the path, and behind it, at a distance that said she had learned caution, Silena, in her blue summer dress, carrying the faded blue blanket over one arm and a jug of cold lemonade in the other hand.

Clarisse stopped in front of Percy and did a slow, deliberate inspection tour of him: his hair, his sneakers, the damp cuffs of his jeans where they'd dried twice and gotten wet again, the sleeve he'd re-wrapped himself without admitting it.

"What happened to my bandaging?" she said.

"Nobody liked it," Percy said. "I improved it."

"You strangled it."

"You tied it like a boat hitch."

"I tied it like it needed to not fall off in a fight." She reached out and had the wrap adjusted in eleven seconds flat, without even looking at it, the way a mother fixes a collar at a dinner table. "That's because you're going to have a hand tomorrow. Be grateful."

They sat — Clarisse on the edge of the blanket with her boots in the sand, Thalia sprawling on one elbow in the last of the gold light, and Percy and Annabeth on the end of the dock, which was where they had both ended up by instinct and entirely, because the five of them had long since stopped doing the math.

Silena poured lemonade into paper cups that read CAMP HALF-BLOOD WOODLAND AWARDS in letters fading at the folds, and the lemonade was cold and sour and nobody had thought to bring sugar, and it was perfect.

"To the injured," Silena said, raising her cup.

"To the injured," Clarisse agreed, and they drank, and Annabeth spilled a little on her chin because it was so cold, and Percy reached over with his injured hand and caught the drop with his thumb, and the handholding happened the way it always happened, which was all at once and then forever, and neither of them remembered consenting to it and neither of them would ever need to.

Clarisse saw it happen. Clarisse never missed anything.

"Zeus-cabin accompaniment on Poseidon territory," she announced, holding up her bare wrist and tapping it. "The clock starts now."

"That rule is dead," Percy said.

"The rule is dead to me, and undead in my heart. Double for Zeus-cabin accompaniment. Retroactive. Ten minutes. I'll be rounding down."

"Clarisse," Silena said mildly, reaching for the notebook she kept in the blanket pocket for exactly these calamities, "do you want me to keep the tally?"

"Yes," Clarisse said.

"No," Percy said.

"Yes," Thalia and Annabeth said, together.

So it was decided. The tally was kept, and Silena's pen made a small scratching sound against the page, and the lake did its thing under the dock, and the sun went down a little further into the trees on the far shore.

It was then that Annabeth did the thing with her hands.

She'd been holding the cup between her knees, telling Clarisse about the log — eleven seconds, clean discharge, water conducts, in her own handwriting — and she got excited, and when Annabeth got excited the coil on her wrist lit from the inside, a small gold thread of light crawling up the back of her hand, and she was already bringing her hand up over the lake to show them, the way a girl shows a scar, when Thalia's voice cracked across the evening like a whip.

"Water. Conducts."

Annabeth's hand stopped in midair. The coil went out. The lake lapped.

"Water conducts," Thalia said, standing up. "Annabeth. We had one rule."

"I didn't throw it," Annabeth said, small. "I was just going to — show you. It was in my hand."

"But you reached for it," Thalia said. "You reached toward it."

Annabeth looked at her own hand. Then at the lake — bright, innocent, freshwater, lapping at the pilings under them the way it had for a thousand years — and something crossed her face: not fear, exactly. Something more careful.

"I know," she said. "I put it back."

"Say the whole thing."

"I put it back, and I sank it, and it went into the ground." She turned her palm up, and there in the middle of it was a scorch mark, fresh and small, the size of a penny. "Seeped. Gone. Before I even thought about it. Thalia, I didn't even think about it this time."

Thalia looked at her sister for a long moment. Whatever she saw there made her sit back down, slower, with her shoulders dropping half an inch.

"That," Thalia said, "is the first time I've seen you do that. Not the spark. The not-doing. You reached for it, and you put it back, and you didn't even notice doing it. That's what I've been trying to teach you all day. That's the lesson."

Percy looked from one sister to the other, and then at Annabeth's hand — the scorch mark, the copper coil, the graphite stain on her knuckle — and felt a warmth in his chest that had nothing to do with static and everything to do with the sight of the two people he loved most in the world being good at something together.

"Okay," he said, "but also: you reached for it."

"Over a lake."

"Percy," Thalia and Clarisse said, together.

"Water conducts," Percy said, and started laughing, and then everyone was laughing, and Silena wrote something in the tally book that made Thalia lean over to read and then cover her face with her hand.

---

Silena declared the tally void, on the grounds that she had miscounted the start time and that clocks were a social construct, and Clarisse declared war on her over it, and the war lasted about as long as it took for the lemonade to run out. Then the arguing ran down the way arguing ran down around the lake — not into a conclusion but into a quiet, and the quiet spread out over the dock like a blanket the five of them had agreed to share.

The lake went gold, then rose-gold, then the deep blue of that part of evening when the water looks deeper than it is. A loon called once from the far shore and did not call again, and the whole sound of the place narrowed down to water on wood and the five of them breathing.

Annabeth turned the page in her sketchbook and started to draw, and Percy, who had long ago learned the grammar of her working, sat where she could look at him without admitting she was looking.

"Draw the lake," he said. "You've labeled it twice today."

"Three times."

"It's not a record, it's careful." Her pencil moved. "Your hand's purple."

"It's handsome. Clarisse said purple is a color of strength."

"Clarisse said your wrist was 'purple' and that you were 'going to have a hand tomorrow.'" Annabeth's mouth quirked. "Do you want to talk about it? The sparring. How it felt."

"I got one on her. A real one. My point counted."

"She told everybody. She announced it on the path like a decree. 'My little brother got a point on me today and it wasn't luck.' She actually used the word 'decree.'"

"She's going to make me pay for it."

"Forever." Annabeth's pencil ticked along the page. "She's been teaching you for weeks, and today was the first day she actually taught you to lose."

"That's what it was. It was like the Atlas. She wasn't teaching me a form. She was teaching me that I can get hit and keep going." He picked up a pebble and turned it over in his wrapped hand. "And she told me about her other life. How nobody ever wrapped her hands. You got hurt, you kept going, and there was never anybody who would have —"

"Who would have wrapped them for her," Annabeth finished quietly.

"She has people now."

"I keep telling her that." Percy chucked the pebble at the lake. It skipped twice, badly, and sank. "She's got you now, too. She calls you 'girl,' she says you short out doorknobs, and she does this thing where she's protective of you now, which is terrifying, because Clarisse-protective means she'll tackle the sun on your behalf."

Annabeth set her pencil down. The sketch had come out well — the two of them on the end of the dock, drawn the way she drew everything that mattered, from the inside: legs hanging over the water, the outline of the far shore behind them, one wrapped hand and one copper wrist.

"Percy."

"Yeah?"

"After the lightning catch, when I hurt you — I remember thinking that being dangerous meant being a monster." She turned the coil on her wrist. "Thalia sat me on a hot rock for hours today and told me fear isn't the problem — that the problem is what you do when you open the door. Then she humored my plumbing diagram. Then she gave me this." She held up her wrist. "It's not a very good knot."

"It's an excellent knot."

"It's your knot. She used your knot."

"Then it's an excellent knot twice."

Annabeth smiled, small and real, and put her hand over his, and the coil was warm from her skin, and the last of the sun was going down at the far shore, and the lake took the sky's color and gave it back changed, the way it always did.

"I have something for you," she said.

"You have a lot of things. You're carrying a whole art supply."

"Shush." She dug in the front pocket of the borrowed hoodie — the deep one, big enough to lose a hand in, which was the entire reason she'd stolen the hoodie in the first place — and came up with a folded piece of sketchbook paper, soft at the edges, and put it in his palm.

*Percy —* it began. *For the person who let his sister teach him to lose, the way you let Thalia teach me to be dangerous. You got a point and you didn't brag, except in the appropriate ways. Love, A.*

He read it twice. Then he refolded it — carefully, wrong, the long way, so that it came out a narrow strip instead of a square — and put it in his shirt pocket, over his heart, the way she'd watched him do with the blue shell when they were small.

"I'm putting this over my heart," he said.

"You're putting the wrong end out."

"I'm putting my heart in it."

"You're insufferable."

"I'm practicing."

Percy got up.

"Okay, what?" Annabeth said.

"Swim."

"Percy, it's evening."

"Exactly. It's the best hour. It's the hour the lake likes." He pulled his shirt over his head and dropped it on the planks. "And I've been sitting still since nine in the morning because somebody threw me in the sand like a sack of flour."

"I threw you for your own good," Clarisse said, from the blanket, without opening her eyes.

"Clarisse," Silena said.

"I may have slightly contributed," Percy said. "By charging. Again."

"Like a person who thinks a training yard is a dare," Thalia said.

"I do not think it's a dare. I think Clarisse thinks it's a dare, which is the same thing."

"It's not the same thing," Thalia said.

"It's the same thing and you know it," Clarisse said, and then clapped a hand over her own mouth, because the phrase had come out of the argument, out of the dock, out of the old arrangement, and everybody heard where it came from, and silence held for exactly one second.

Then Percy bent double over the dock planks laughing, and Annabeth pressed her sleeve to her mouth, and Thalia kicked sand at Clarisse, and Clarisse, who did not kick sand at people, kicked a little bit back, and the fight was over before it started, which was the whole point.

Percy went off the dock the way he did everything in the water, all at once, no hesitation, clothes and all: sneakers off first — one, then the other, landing on the planks with a clack — jeans dark from the knees down in half a second, shirt in his hand and going into the lake like it had always been lake property. The cold hit him first, always did, the honest freshwater cold of a lake that had baked in the sun all day, and then the warmth underneath it, the deep summer place the lake kept even after the air cooled, and the lake said hello to him the way it always said hello: not in words. In the shape of it. In the way the water made room.

He came up in the gold light and shook his hair out like a dog, and the lake spread out around him and held him the way it always held him, which was: loosely, and completely.

"I'm going to assume," Thalia said, from the sand, "that this is the part where somebody tells us water conducts."

"Water conducts," Clarisse said solemnly.

"I'm standing on the shore, Annabeth, like a sensible adult, and I'd like everyone to appreciate the discipline it takes not to be in that lake right now."

"She's twenty," Clarisse said.

"Eighteen. I'm eighteen. I'm old and I have principles." Thalia crossed her arms. "I'm not getting in the water while your brother is in there negotiating directly with the lake."

"Why does everyone think I'm negotiating with the lake?" Percy called. "I'm swimming. It's a lake. Swimming is in the name."

"Clarisse says you fight like the lake," Annabeth said.

"Exactly," Clarisse called, delighted. "Impossible. That's the word."

"TEACHING," Percy yelled, over the water, to everybody, to nobody, to the lake. "She was teaching me to listen to the current before I throw myself into it! That is called learning! It is called sibling growth!"

"It's a dare," Thalia said.

"It's a training!"

"It's both!" said Clarisse and Silena and Annabeth, together, and then everybody was laughing, including Percy, and he swam back to the dock and came up beside it in the shallows with one hand over the lip of the planks and water streaming off him, and Annabeth was sitting on the end of it with her legs hanging and her hoodie sleeves pushed back and the copper coil catching the last light, and she looked down at him, and he looked up at her, and the lake was all around them and the sky was all above them and there was nowhere in the world he would rather have been.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi."

"You labeled the swim?"

"I labeled the swim." She turned the page so he could see, and sure enough, in the corner of the sketch of the two of them, in tiny careful letters, there was a new annotation: *Dock, dusk. Lightning grounded. Lake: also grounded. Lake: in his possession, apparently.*

"Erase 'apparently,'" Percy said.

"Never."

He took her hand where it hung, the one with the coil, and turned it over, and pressed his mouth to the fresh scorch mark in her palm, the one she'd put there by sinking a charge on purpose, and then to the inside of her wrist, and then, because the dock was warm and the evening was almost done and the tally book was closed and the lake did not mind, he got up onto the first rung of the ladder and kissed her properly — a second and a half, a water-dropped, sun-warmed, fully annotated kiss, her hand in his wet hair, his bandaged wrist carefully between her shoulder blades so that it touched her only where she welcomed it, and the four people on the shore made the kind of noises that four people make when they are deliberately not watching.

"Zeus-cabin accompaniment," Clarisse announced, to the sky in general, "is hereby declared retroactive forever, for the record."

"Declared void," Silena said. "The clock was a social construct."

"The clock," Clarisse said, "was never a clock."

---

They stayed until the first stars, which at that time of year came early and came all at once, like a camp full of people deciding at the same moment to go outside.

Percy came up the ladder with water still running off him and sat down on the planks with his back against a piling, and Annabeth, without being asked, handed him the edge of her hoodie to dry his hair with, which was the most intimate thing anybody did all evening, and everybody left them to it the way waters part around a stone.

Silena closed the tally book. The verdict written in it was long and squeaky-voiced and fair: no rules had been broken, the clock had been a noble experiment, and dinner was, according to the conch that was even then beginning to blow down at the pavilion, happening with or without them.

"Walk up with us?" Thalia said, to Clarisse, which was not the same as asking, and they both knew it, and both of them had learned to say it anyway.

"Walk up," Clarisse said.

"You have a name, you know."

"Clarisse."

"There it is." Thalia grinned. "That's growth."

The five of them put out the lantern — actually a jar with a candle in it, from the Poseidon cabin, which Clarisse had carried down without mentioning it — and started up the hill in the dark, the way they had so many times that summer, the path worn smooth by the traffic of them, the cabins dark ahead and the pavilion lights on, the lake behind them going quiet.

Somewhere on the hill, where the path bent and the trees opened onto the water, Annabeth stopped. The others went on ahead, Clarisse and Thalia in the middle of their slow, comfortable, post-argument bickering about nothing, Silena between them.

Percy stopped with her. He was wearing his damp shirt now, the cuffs dark with lake water, his sneakers squelching softly with every step, and she was in the gray hoodie with her hands in its deep pockets and the copper coil on her wrist and the sketchbook under her arm, and between them the path fell away down to the water.

"Tomorrow," she said, "I'm adding the note. On the map in the Big House, the official one. *Here there be lightning.* And our names on it, because Thalia says people should know who works there."

"And do they?"

"They do now." She looked at him, and the last of the evening was on her, and the scorch of a struck match in her hair and the smell of the lake on his shirt, and her eyes did the thing they did when she was about to say something that cost her. "I used to think being good at things meant being finished at them. That one day I'd know enough not to get it wrong anymore. My sister spent three hours on a hot rock today teaching me that the wrong is coming anyway, and that the day's job is only to be careful on purpose."

Percy thought about the arena, and the sand, and the point he'd earned, and his big sister's hands knotting gauze around his wrist with the tenderness she only ever spent on two people in the world.

"She spent the whole morning teaching me the same thing," he said. "Backward. She let me lose on purpose. An hour of it. It was the best morning I've had all summer."

"Did you tell her that?"

"I will. I'm building up to it."

"You're ridiculous."

"I'm practicing."

Annabeth laughed, small, and bumped him with her shoulder, and they started up the path again, and the camp opened around them: the pavilion with its lights and its low noise, the cabins in their rows, the smell of dinner on the evening air, the lake below doing its evening thing, the same as it had been since they were small, which was the trick of it — it held them all the same way, and asked nothing, and remembered everything.

"Percy," Annabeth said, when they reached the top of the hill and the others were waiting, Silena with her chin on her hand and Clarisse making an obviously sarcastic comment about the sunset.

"Yeah?"

"Thank you for letting her put the gauze on."

"I let her do lots of things. I let her call me minnow."

"She's going to call you worse tomorrow."

"I know." He took her hand — the coil, the graphite knuckles, the small scorch mark — and swung it once between them, gently, the way he'd never do if anybody could see him. "That's how you know it's home."

---

The lake kept its silence below them, and the camp did its evening thing, and five people climbed the hill together in the dark, sisters and brothers by blood and by choice, all of them the same thing, which was: a family that had learned, that day, to trade skills the way other people traded gifts.

The lake gave the sky back, star for star. The map on the wall in the Big House said so, in small careful handwriting, under an annotation that read *Here there be lightning*, and the summer went on around them, and it was theirs, and it was enough.
