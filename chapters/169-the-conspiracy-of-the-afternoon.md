## Chapter 169: The Conspiracy of the Afternoon

Percy woke to the sound of his sisters failing to whisper.

It was a specific sound, and it had a shape. Somebody hissed the word *shh* with far too much breath in it. Somebody else's bunk creaked under the sound of a person shifting weight they had forgotten they were holding. A third person made the small punished noise of somebody trying to keep a clipboard from rattling. In Cabin Three, on the first clear morning after the cold snap, every honest noise carried: the window glass still cold to the touch from the night, the indoor channel in Tempest Hall moving slow and mineral-smelling up through the floor grates, Clarisse's bunk stripped and empty from early laps, the corner bunk made up tight with the round white rock still centered on the pillow where Tyson had left it Tuesday.

Percy lay still and listened, because the lake had taught him to listen before he opened his eyes.

"—told him half past," Silena was murmuring, close and low. "He wakes at the conch. The conch is seven. So we have half an hour and you're using it badly."

"Then stop saying his name," Clarisse said, in the flat register she used for tactics, which happened to be the same register she used for hiding tenderness. "You say his name, he wakes. It's sonar. I've told you this."

"I'm being careful."

"You're being loud. You put potatoes in the bread on Thursday and blamed the harpy."

"That harpy was hovering, Clarisse. It had intent."

Percy sat up in his bunk and let his feet find the cool floor. He was in the faded orange camp shirt he'd slept in, sleeves already cut short back in June, and the gray sweatpants gone thin at both knees. Sleep still sat on him warm and heavy. He rubbed his face with one hand and looked at the room, at the three of them arranged in a casual cluster around the little worktable by the spear rack, and understood, with the unhurried certainty he trusted about weather and people, that a conspiracy was in progress and that it concerned him.

"Morning," he said.

Nobody jumped. But Clarisse's hand moved to cover a clipboard on the table with a speed that would have been invisible to a slower boy.

"Morning," Silena said, too brightly, sixteen and warm and perceptive, hair pinned up with a pencil through it, flour already on one forearm though it was barely past seven. She was in her lake-gray sweater over her sleep shirt, and she had a satchel open at her feet that said *day trip* in a language older than words.

Thalia stood in the doorway with two mugs of tea and four days' worth of survey habits still half on her, though she'd been back since Friday. Eighteen, black hair wild and pushed behind her ears, her gray flannel tied around her waist over a clean camp shirt, boots caked at the ankles with the red mud of the north ridge. She had been out to the granite shoulder before breakfast. She always was, now that the spring was hers to visit.

"You're awake," Thalia said.

"Am I?" Percy took the mug she offered with both hands, lemon and honey and steam coming off it into the cold. "Or am I part of an operation?"

"An operation," Clarisse said immediately. "No. Nothing. There's no operation."

"Clarisse."

"There's a *chore*," Clarisse said. Her jaw did the thing it did when she was committing. "It's a chore morning. You have chores. Canoes need scrubbing. Spears need counting. Go be a useful member of this cabin and butt out."

"Chores," Percy repeated. "On the clearest Wednesday of the year. On the one day nobody has patrol, nobody has survey, and the whole lake is empty." He drank the tea. "Can I use the clipboard? I want to write a note."

Clarisse hesitated. That was the tell, and it filled the room like a wave coming in.

"Sure," she said.

He picked it up. It was her real work clipboard, the one she used for hull inspections and spear-drill rotations, and clipped inside it, under the metal clamp, was a sheet of paper in Silena's careful hand with a title that Silena had clearly underlined twice for emphasis.

*OPERATION SHORE LEAVE.*

Beneath it, three columns of times and initials. *0900 — stage the vessel. 0915 — clear the shore. 0930 — depart. 1200 — provisions delivered, warm rock secured. 1600 — assistance permitted if requested. 1830 — whistle. 1900 — debrief.* In the margin, small and pleased with itself, a note in Thalia's blocky capitals: REMEMBER TO PRETEND.

And at the bottom, in the space for assignments:

*ASSET: SALT. PRINCIPAL: PENCIL.*

Percy read it twice. He read it a third time, slowly, the way he read water.

"Salt," he said. "I'm Salt."

"You weren't supposed to see that," Clarisse said.

"I'm Salt and she's Pencil."

"It was a decoy," Clarisse said, with the total commitment of a girl who had never told a lie in her life and was furious at having to try. "That's a fake document. Fake op. We run them sometimes. Psychological."

"Psychological," Percy said.

Clarisse looked at Silena. Silena looked at Thalia. Thalia, who had known about the operation since Monday and had been enjoying herself enormously, looked at the ceiling and drank her tea.

"It's not psychological," Silena said, giving up with the grace of a person who had never once won an argument about flour. "It's a nice thing, Percy. We were going to tell you at breakfast."

"You told me at breakfast," Percy said. "You told me by failing to whisper at me."

Thalia set her mug down on the shelf by the spear rack, next to the lemon-oil tin. "All right. Fine. The jig is up. The Wednesday operation is compromised on the morning of execution." She said it like a survey report. "Asset Salt, do you require a briefing, or are you going to stand there being smug?"

"I require a briefing," Percy said. "I require the full briefing. I want to know how long this has been in the works and whether Annabeth is in on it."

"Annabeth is not in on it," Silena said. "Thalia's briefing her at nine. We were going to get you out of the cabin first so you couldn't coordinate."

"You packed for a Tuesday once and brought a lantern with no batteries," Clarisse said. "You are not a coordinator. You are a liability with good intentions." She took the clipboard back, snapped the clip shut over the operation sheet, and squared her shoulders into business. "So. Since you know. Since you're *Salt*, which is your own fault for having the eyes of a sea kid and the timing of a thief — the rules still stand. Listen once, because I'm saying them once."

"Here we go," Percy said, and drank his tea, and waited, warm all the way down, because this was the thing nobody had warned him about when he'd learned what a family was: that they would go to work for him behind his back, and that the going-to-work would be the loudest, worst-guarded, most obvious thing in the room.

Clarisse held up one finger. It was blunt and sun-browned and had a new scar across the knuckle from Tuesday's cold snap day.

"One. The lake is forty-eight degrees now. Tuesday took it down and it hasn't come back up. Warm on top, cold underneath, like always, but the cold is deeper now, so you get in stages and you get out before you get stupid. Knees, waist, chest. Then out. And you do not swim past the boundary rock. Not today, not this week. The far water is colder and further and I'm not fishing anybody out of it in October. The buoys are your world. The buoys are beautiful. Love the buoys."

"Two. The canoe." She pointed the finger at the window, where through the pines the canoe beach showed bright. "That canoe is Tyson's canoe. He fixed it in June, he carved the prow, he varnished it twice, and the hippocampus on the bow is the only good carving at this camp. You treat it like a person. No dragging it on stones. No standing in it. No testing whether it will tip, which I know is a thing you think about."

"I have never once—"

"You tested it with an apple."

"That was a scientific test and the apple survived."

"Salt," Clarisse said flatly, "if you scratch my brother's canoe, I will throw you in the lake, and I will not help you out of it, and I will tell Silena it was your fault."

Percy set the empty mug down. "Understood."

"Three." She hesitated. Her face did the thing it did for the true things, the flat covering, the brief exposure. "Three is you be decent. She's fourteen. You're fourteen. Wring out on the rock before you carry the blanket. The blanket carries dry people. I'm not saying why I'm saying it."

"I know," Percy said quietly.

"I know you know. That's why I said it once." She reached out and straightened his collar with two flat pats, the way she had straightened Tyson's collar the night before the ferry, hard enough to be a shove and soft enough to be the exact opposite. "Four. Have a good day. You've had two weeks of people leaving and cold coming in. You've earned a warm rock."

Silena had gone back to the satchel during the lecture, and now she hoisted it onto the table with a thump that sounded like something with real weight. She began the unpacking with the ceremony she gave to all her food, laying items out in a row on the cloth like evidence.

"Eleven pounds," she announced. "I weighed it twice. Four honey cakes, still warm-ish. Bread. Cheese in triangles, because triangles are correct and everyone knows it. Two peaches, which is the last of the season, so savor them, and don't tell the Hermes cabin or they'll get ideas. Thermos of lemon water. Cold chicken, because you two get hungry in the afternoon and pretend you don't. Apples. And the fleece blanket, which is extra, and the tin cup, and this—" She set a small wax-paper packet on top, folded square. "—which is emergency chocolate. Not for the afternoon. For the walk up the hill when you're cold and pretending you're not."

"I don't pretend I'm not cold," Percy said.

"You pretend about everything thermal," Clarisse said. "It's your whole personality. Put the fleece in the bag. Take it out when she starts rubbing her arms. That's your cue, Salt. Girls get cold first because they're smarter about noticing."

Thalia had been leaning in the doorway with her arms folded, watching the operation with the dry delight of a girl who had spent four days in the woods and come home to find a parlor. Now she pushed off the jamb and came into the room, boots loud, and stopped in front of Percy. Her eyes were gray-green and very serious and her mouth was fighting a grin so hard it looked like a physical battle.

"Asset Salt," she said. "Mock-serious warnings. This is the part of the operation that is actually about me."

"Proceed."

"Warning the first." She held up a finger to match Clarisse's. "He cannot be trusted with a schedule. The principal is aware of this. The asset is not. I am now making the asset aware." She looked at Clarisse. "This isn't a betrayal. This is damage control."

"Noted," Clarisse said, magnanimous. "Filed."

"Warning the second," Thalia went on. "If he tries to improve the afternoon, that is a warning sign. If he draws up an itinerary, that is a red flag. If he asks her to rate the day on a scale of one to ten, she is to walk into the lake of her own accord." She paused. "The boy builds when he's happy. He builds because he thinks good things need scaffolding or they'll fall down. She knows this better than we do, and she will humor him, and I want it on the record that I predicted it."

"This is an elaborate way of calling me annoying," Percy said.

"I've called you annoying for five months, Jackson. This is an elaborate way of loving you." Thalia clapped him on the shoulder, hard. "Warning the third. When it gets dark, the rock is cold and the water is colder and you will both be tired in the way that water makes you tired. That's the good tired. Stay inside it as long as it lasts. Don't rush up the hill."

Percy looked at the three of them: Clarisse with her clipboard and her scarred knuckles and her spear leaning against the table; Silena with flour on her forearm and a satchel that weighed eleven pounds; Thalia with red ridge mud on her boots and a mock-serious finger still in the air.

"You're all insane," he said.

"Yes," Silena said, pleased. "Sit down and eat a honey cake before you go. You always skip breakfast on days that matter."

He sat. He ate the honey cake standing over the table because sitting sounded like an event. It was warm and dense and tasted like the inside of a good week. On the shelf above him the corner bunk sat made up tight, the round white rock centered on the pillow, and he touched it once on his way past, one finger, the way you check a pulse.

"Tyson's letter's still not here," Clarisse said, from the doorway.

"Comes next week," Percy said. "Maybe Thursday."

"Maybe Thursday," Clarisse agreed. "Corner bunk stays made till he's actually home anyway. That's the rule." She paused. "And no panel tonight. Chiron's closed the office early. Thirty-nine's still thirty-nine."

"Nobody asked you about it," Percy said.

"You were going to."

Silena pressed the satchel into his hands, and the fleece over that, and hooked the tin cup on the strap. Then she stepped in and fixed his collar herself, two seconds after Clarisse had already fixed it, and the two of them bumped shoulders and neither of them mentioned it.

"Go," Silena said. "She's at the dock. We told her quarter past. It's half past and you're late early, which she'll pretend annoys her."

Percy went out of Cabin Three into the cold bright morning with the satchel on his shoulder and the fleece under one arm, conch shell silent on its post, the whole camp smelling of frost-melted pine and woodsmoke and the far mineral breath of the lake. Behind him, immediately, the whispering started again — urgent, urgent, the sound of three people re-plotting a compromised operation at speed.

At the bend in the path, where the pines opened onto the slope down to the canoe beach, he stopped. He unclipped the operation sheet from the clipboard he had not actually given back, folded it once along the crease Clarisse's clip had left in it, and put it in his pocket.

Then he went down to the lake grinning like the day had personally signed for him.

---

Annabeth reached the canoe dock at nine-ten and found the north end of camp running a covert operation in broad daylight.

She had come down the switchback from the Athena cabin with her bag over one shoulder, in her sturdy olive shorts with real pockets, a faded orange camp shirt loose over a dark blue one-piece, and Thalia's gray flannel tied around her waist against the morning chill. Her hair was rebraided tight for water and tied with the hair tie Percy had handed her without being asked, the way he always did. Functional clothes for a cold lake, and she had told herself she would not map today, and had brought the field notebook anyway, because she was herself.

The lake lay flat and bright and autumn-cold, silver-blue under the clear sky, the storm gone out of it from Tuesday and the whole shore steaming faintly where the sun hit cold stone. The canoe beach below the dock was empty in the way a stage is empty: the two green skiffs tied at their pilings, the paddle rack neat, the swimmers' buoys bobbing out at their rope line, and mid-lake the boundary rock with its white X still readable from the shore.

And in the middle of this arrangeable nothing, the conspiracy was executing itself badly.

The green canoe was already staged at the top of the ramp, and it was a sight: Tyson's canoe, repaired and varnished and carved, the hippocampus prow catching the light on its horse head and curling fish tail, the whole bow of it so careful and eager it looked like it wanted to be taken out. Percy had told her about the repair in the slow way he told her about Tyson, late at night on the Hermes cabin steps, and she had run her own hand over the smooth seam once in August and understood what a brother's work was worth.

The problem was the eleven pounds.

Silena was standing in one of the skiffs, mid-transfer, loading the satchel and the fleece and the thermos out of the skiff and into the canoe, and the canoe was tied at the dock and the skiff was tied to the canoe and neither of them was going anywhere. Every so often Silena would surface from the transfer with a strand of hair in her mouth, mutter something about the schedule, and duck back down. Thalia stood on the dock with a rope over her shoulder and the expression of a girl who had been assigned "decoy" and was taking the assignment extremely seriously, scanning the shoreline for civilians. And Clarisse was on the ramp with her clipboard, directing traffic.

"Left side," Clarisse said. "No, her left. The other left. Silena, the thermos goes on the thwart, it falls in the water and then we're drinking peaches."

"It's a sealed thermos, Clarisse."

"It's an unsealed thermos of my personal experience, is what it is. Left side. Gently. Imagine you're putting a baby into a bath."

Annabeth stopped at the top of the ramp. The three of them saw her at the same moment and all three made the face of people who had been caught.

"Morning," Annabeth said.

"Morning!" Silena called, bright as a bell, from inside the canoe. "Lovely day. Clear. Cold. We're — moving things. This is a thing that happens on Wednesdays."

"You're staging the green canoe."

"We're staging *a* canoe. There are canoes. They need staging." Clarisse had put the clipboard behind her back, which fooled nobody, because a girl holding a clipboard behind her back looks like a girl holding a clipboard behind her back. "Coastal maintenance, Chase. Nothing to do with you. Go enjoy your — whatever you're doing. Somewhere else. Far away from here."

"Percy's coming," Annabeth said.

"We know," Thalia said. "He's Salt."

"Thalia."

"He read the sheet! His sister handed him the actual operational document with her own two hands. I told her to clip it under the hull-inspection pages, but no, she wanted it accessible." Thalia was delighted. "Accessible. Directly to the enemy."

"I thought he'd need the hull-inspection pages," Clarisse said, mortified. "He was asking about the canoe. How was I supposed to know he'd turn it over?"

"He turns everything over," Annabeth said. "He's Percy. Turning things over is his, um." She searched for the word. "It's his hobby. It's his architecture."

Clarisse groaned into her clipboard. Silena surfaced from the canoe with the satchel secured and the fleece folded on top of it, and held up a hand for the dock. Annabeth took it and stepped down into the canoe, which rocked under her with a good solid feel, deep and steady in the way of well-built things. The varnish was still new enough to smell of pine sap and sun, and under it the faint forge-smoke of a forge that had made good work in June.

"Thalia," Silena said, from the thwart, "you're supposed to brief her. The mock-serious warnings. That was your assignment."

"Mine? Mine is decoy and recon." Thalia hopped down onto the dock and settled on the edge with her boots hanging, entirely unbothered. "But I'll do it. I'm excellent at it." She turned to Annabeth, and her whole face went grave, and her voice dropped into the register of a town crier reading a death notice. "Principal. Listen well, for this may be the last time you see the open water. Ahem."

"Don't you dare."

"Principal. You are about to spend an entire afternoon with a boy who is fourteen, who swims like a fish with ambition, and who — I cannot stress this enough — builds schedules when he is happy." "Warning one. If he makes an itinerary, humoring him is permitted. Pretending the itinerary is a good idea is not. Warning two. If he tries to improve on the afternoon, walk into the lake. I will retrieve you. Warning three." Her voice lost a fraction of the mockery, went real underneath. "He's had a hard two weeks. We all have. If today he's too much, or too loud, or too much of a boy with a plan — tell him. He can actually hear you. That's new, and it's yours, and I want you to use it."

Annabeth stood in the canoe with the satchel at her feet and the fleece blanket on the thwart and the hippocampus prow bright under her hand, and felt the morning arrange itself around her the way water arranges itself around a stone.

"Warning three isn't a warning," she said.

"No," Thalia agreed. "It's the true one. The other two were to make the true one land. I learned that from Silena. She does it with raisins."

"I do it with raisins," Silena confirmed, from the boat. "Annabeth. Come here. I have provisions for you."

She pressed the fleece blanket into Annabeth's arms — heavy fleece, warm even indoors, smelling of lemon soap — and a wax-paper packet that she folded into Annabeth's hand with a specific pressure, the way gifts were passed in this cabin.

"What's the emergency chocolate for?" Annabeth asked.

"Sixty-three percent," Silena said. "It's for the walk up the hill. When you're cold and pretending you're not, and for when he's being a boy and you need something sweet that isn't him." She glanced off toward the pines. "Also, this is the part where I give you the whole-hand wave and pretend I'm not being sappy, so look at the water while I do it and then we'll never speak of it."

"I'll look at the water," Annabeth said, and looked at the water.

Silena waved with her whole hand. Annabeth saw it out of the corner of her eye and felt it land in her chest like a small warm stone, and then Clarisse coughed loudly on the dock and said that she had never in her life seen so much sentimentality before nine in the morning, and the moment passed the way these moments did in Cabin Three, quickly, like something handed over and already put away.

Percy came down the path at half past with the satchel on his shoulder and the fleece under his arm, and the three sisters froze in their various stages of operation, and he stopped on the ramp and looked at all of it — the staged canoe, the loaded satchel, the clipboard behind Clarisse's back, the absolute sham of the whole morning — and grinned.

"Morning," he said.

"Morning," Annabeth said.

"Morning, Salt," Clarisse said, and then caught herself. "I mean — Jackson."

"You called me Salt," Percy said. "You called me Salt to my face. The whole operation is compromised. You're running a compromised operation."

"Load the vessel," Clarisse ordered, pointing at the canoe with her whole hand. "Vessel. It's a word for canoe. Boats are vessels. Everybody load the vessel and stop making it weird."

"Vessel," Thalia repeated, delighted. "It has a code name now."

"It's not a code name, it's a boat word. Get in." Clarisse pushed the canoe off the ramp with one boot and caught the painter, and Percy stepped in with the easy care he used on anything that floated, and held out a hand for Annabeth, cold fingers and warm, and she took it and stepped down beside him on the thwart with their knees already touching.

"For the record," Percy said, settling the satchel in the bilge and the fleece between them, "I read the whole sheet. Including the part about assistance permitted if requested."

"We're pretending you didn't," Clarisse said.

"I'm pretending I didn't," Percy said. "I'm very good at pretending. Ask my report card."

"Nobody asked your report card, Salt, because you don't have one, because you don't have school, because you live at a summer camp and get kidnapped by mythical alliances twice a year. Now untie. Untie, untie, vessel, go."

Annabeth leaned against Percy's shoulder as he untied the painter, and Silena shoved them off the ramp with the flat of her hand against the green hull, and Clarisse pushed at the stern with the clipboard still crushed behind her back, and Thalia on the dock raised both hands in a seeing-off gesture that was half benediction and half mock-salute, and the green canoe slid off the ramp into the cold bright water and took them.

---

The lake was so clear it felt dishonest.

Percy took the oars in the bow because Annabeth had claimed the bow seat with her knees drawn up and her braid over one shoulder, on the grounds that she had a better view for "navigating," which meant watching the shoreline go by in sectors out of pure love, the way she'd been doing since June. The canoe came up under him light and eager, a good boat, and the oars dipped and pulled and whispered in a rhythm he had in his wrists now, easy as breath. The bow sat high. The hippocampus prow cut the small chop clean, and every few strokes he could feel the little forward push of the carving reading the water, the way a good prow always did.

"You're doing the thing," Annabeth said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you're pleased with the boat."

"I'm not pleased with the boat. I'm pleased with the boy who fixed it." He pulled through a stroke, let the oar feather, pulled through again. "There's a difference. Clarisse says there's a subtle distinction. She's been quoting herself a lot lately. I think she's studying."

"She's been quoting herself since July. She thinks it's oratory." Annabeth shifted forward, elbows on her knees, watching the water slide past the gunwale. The shoreline went by: the swimming buoys at their rope line, the empty canoe launch, then the white X on the boundary rock coming up slowly off the bow, painted bright enough to see from the dock. Past the rock the lake opened into its real blue, and the far shore lay low and blue-black and pine-dark under the clear sky. "Shore's empty."

"Shore's cleared," Percy said. "Nine-fifteen, shore cleared. Her handwriting's terrible but she's thorough. She probably did the Hermes cabin herself. Personally. With threats."

"She sent the whole camp on a supplies run. I saw the list. Chalk, rope, and every lost sock on the property." Annabeth said it with the flat fondness she used for Cabin Three's management style. "She took the word *operation* as a challenge. She thinks a schedule is a form of love."

"She's not wrong."

"She's extremely wrong." Annabeth tipped her head back, squinting up at him. "You're not going to make an itinerary, are you?"

Percy pulled a stroke. "That's Thalia's accusation. You've been talking to Thalia."

"I packed the fleece myself and she still briefed me twice." She settled back against the thwart and pulled the fleece blanket onto her knees, winding the edge of it around one hand. "For the record. I'm not briefing you back. I'm just saying it once, so if it happens, it's not my fault. I don't love a schedule. I love a hole in the day with you in it."

"I heard you the first two times," Percy said, and let it go, because the lake was doing the kind of morning that didn't need help. He rowed them out past the boundary rock, and the water went from green-brown to a deep clean blue, and he watched the bottom drop away below the clear of it and thought that he could do this for hours, and then thought that he could do it forever, and that the second thought didn't frighten him, which was new.

They passed the mouth of the willow cove on the left, green-gold and still, the cattails closed in. The heron stood on the bow of the sunken skiff, motionless, one leg folded.

"Bird's still there," Percy said, quietly, the way you'd speak at a shrine.

"Of course it is," Annabeth said. "It doesn't have anywhere better to be."

At the north beach the shallows ran shallow and glassy over white sand, and beyond them the warm flat stone lay in full autumn sun, big as a cabin floor, still holding the heat of the morning because stone holds heat the way it holds everything, slow and faithful. Percy beached the canoe above the waterline on the half-moon of sand with the painter furled around the bow cleat the way Annabeth would have liked it done, and shipped the oars, and lowered the satchel and the fleece down onto the thwart with a thump.

It was the private spot. The north end of camp had been cleared by whatever method Clarisse had chosen, threats or supply-lists, and the whole beach was theirs: the empty picnic tables up under the pines, the rope of buoys, the flat stone with its seam of white sandbar showing pale below the surface where the trident scar had healed into the bottom. The lake smelled of cold wet stone and hot pine. A jay went by low over the water with a flash of blue.

They changed in the way you change after five months of knowing someone: efficiently, without discussion, without looking away either. Annabeth pulled her camp shirt off over her head and set it on the fleece with her shorts folded on top, revealing the dark blue one-piece, functional and plain, and Percy pulled his own camp shirt off and stood in his swim trunks with the sun already on his shoulders, goosebumps coming up and going down again in the cold air. Her hair went into a knot she secured with the hair tie he'd handed her down at the dock. He ran his hands through his own hair, wet his fingers in the shallows, and slicked it back, and she watched him do it the way you watch a scene you'd prefer to remember exactly.

Percy climbed up onto the flat stone, and Annabeth came up beside him. The rock was warm under their bare feet, gritty and hot on top, and the air on their shoulders was cold enough to make the warmth a pleasure rather than a fact. The whole lake lay open in front of them: buoys, boundary rock, the far shore, the cove behind them to the left.

"That looks like it goes on forever," Annabeth said quietly, her shoulder touching his arm.

"It kind of does," Percy said. "It did in June."

She looked at him. He was watching the water, and his face had gone into the unguarded thing it did when something big moved through him and he let it.

"Yeah," she said. "It kind of does."

They stood on the warm rock and let the cold wind go past them both.

---

Annabeth had forgotten how cold the lake could get, which meant she had forgotten summer.

They went in off the flat rock the way Cabin Three went in — knees, waist, chest, no testing, no counting — and the cold took her in three separate shocks that arrived like arguments and were answered by her whole body going bright and awake. She came up gasping and streaming, hair already dark with it, and laughed, and the laugh cracked a little in the cold air. Percy went in beside her with a shout that sent a jay up out of the pines, and came up shaking his head hard, water flying everywhere, announcing to no one that the lake was "extremely rude" and that he was "a scorpion, actually," which was a thing he said every autumn, and which she had filed the first time as a joke and the second time as a promise about who he was.

The water had its autumn layers in. Warm at the very top from the sun on the shallows, a skin of it, and then the cold coming up under the arms like a door opening in a cellar, and then, three feet down, the deep layer that forgot the sun entirely and stayed at its own year-round temperature, honest and enormous. Annabeth swam out slow and found the seam where the waters met and dove it twice to feel the temperature change on her face, and Percy showed her the seam from underneath, and she put her hand flat into it and felt it move past her fingers like a wall that allowed you through.

They swam side by side, no hurry in it, no count, no race. He turned on his back and floated with his hands folded on his chest the way he did when his eyes were closed, and she floated beside him with her hair spread out in the cold water, and they lay on the lake together with their shoulders almost touching, the cold on their faces and the warm skin under their chins, the sky huge and clean and moving above them. Then his hand found hers under the water and held it, cold fingers and warm, and they floated that way, anchored only to each other, for a long time, while the buoys knocked softly on their rope line out past the sandbar.

Annabeth turned her head in the water to look at him. "Say it," she said. "The thing you say. In the lake."

"The water holds me," Percy said, quietly, out of the old stubbornness and the new tenderness both, because it was still true, and because saying it in the cold water was the same as saying it in the warm, and she had learned that the difference between a ritual and a cliché was whether the person saying it had kept it alive.

"Held twice," she said, and squeezed his hand once under the water.

"By lake and by me," he said. "Don't wear that one out. You're the only person alive who's allowed to say it and you're running it into the ground."

"It's in the file. It's a filed tradition. I'm saving it." She let go of his hand and turned over and swam, and he swam with her, and they came out of the water onto the rock together, streaming and steaming, and lay on the warm stone with their feet hanging over the edge and the sun coming down heavy and gold on their skin, and the rock gave back the morning's warmth until Annabeth could feel it through her whole back, the way a good rock does after a clear night and no clouds.

They spread the fleece out on the hot stone, not for warmth yet but because Silena had packed it and the rule was that Silena's work got used. Annabeth unpacked with the methodical pleasure she brought to every Silena supply: bread in cloth, cheese in triangles — the rotation was permanent now, Clarisse had made it a policy — cold chicken in the tin, apples, and two peaches, the last of the season, and the thermos of lemon water, and the honey cakes, and the emergency chocolate, which she set aside on a corner of the fleece under a square of wax paper, unopened, honored.

Percy flopped backward onto the fleece with a groan and announced that eleven pounds was not a weight but a grudge, and that his sister had gamed the afternoon in advance and he was both a victim of it and grateful.

"Guests first," he said, without opening his eyes.

"There are no guests."

"Then you first," he said. "Annabeth first. Clarisse's rule, cabin law, no appeals."

She poured the lemon water into the tin cup — cold and sweet, sharp with lemon — and handed it to him, and he drank, and handed it back, and she drank, and they shared the bread, passing it back and forth with their heads bent and their knees touching, the way they ate everything now. The honey cake left sugar on her fingers. The cheese triangles were, in fact, better, which was not true but which had become law anyway. Percy did Clarisse's flat tactician voice with fair accuracy, and Annabeth laughed into his shoulder and got honey on his camp shirt and did not apologize.

After the food they lay on their backs on the fleece with the tin cup between them for ballast, looking up at a sky that was going slowly from hard blue to the softer blue of afternoon, and the far pine-dark shore low along the bottom of everything. Percy's hand found hers and held, thumb moving once across her knuckles and then resting, the way he had learned from Silena and the way he did it now without thinking. Her hair dried loose and wavy over her shoulders in the sun and the wind. The lake moved at the edge of hearing. The rock warmed the air under the fleece until it came up through their backs and they had to shrug the fleece down off their shoulders.

Annabeth turned her head. He was watching the sky with his eyes half closed, sun on his skin, lake drying in his hair, fourteen and sun-browned and entirely unhurried, and she leaned up on one elbow and kissed him — slow and warm, honey and lemon and peach between them — and he kissed her back with his free hand coming up to cup her jaw, thumb warm against her cheek, and they stayed like that in the sun with the wind coming cold off the water onto their bare shoulders, and the kiss went on, unhurried, patient, warm on the outside and cool underneath, exactly like the lake.

When they broke apart they did not break apart. Their foreheads stayed together, breathing the same sharp cold air, and she put her hand flat over the place where his heartbeat lived under his shirt and felt it going the way it went — fast, then steadying.

"Hi," he said, against her forehead.

"Hi," she said back, and kissed him once more, brief this time, because she had exactly forty more minutes of wanting to just look at him, and she took them.

---

The friction came out of fullness, the way frictions with Percy did, and it came out of a piece of paper.

He had been lying on his back with his hand in hers, pleasantly heavy with lemon water and honey cake and the particular good tiredness that swimming makes, and at some point his free hand had gone into his pocket and come out with the operation sheet, folded and refolded, and he had started turning it over and over against the fleece the way he turned stones over when he was thinking. Annabeth had let him. She was busy being comfortable — her hair drying in the sun, her bare toes stretched out toward the warmth of the rock, her head tipped back so the light came through her eyelids red — and for a few minutes the sound of the paper and the soft knock of the buoys and the wind were the only things in the world.

Then she heard the pencil.

It was the sound of pencil on paper, the small dry scrape she knew better than her own voice, and she opened one eye.

Percy had a stub of pencil from Silena's satchel — the one Clarisse used for inventories and love notes and logistics — and he was writing on the back of the operation sheet, filling it in, his handwriting leaning uphill the way it did when he was concentrating.

"What are you doing?" she said.

"Planning," he said, pleased with himself. "Improving."

"Improving."

"Clarisse's plan is a skeleton. It has logistics but no soul. So I'm adding the — the weekend-warrior element. The human element." He was writing fast, grinning at the paper. Annabeth sat up. Not fast. Not alarmed. Just up, and watched him write, because Annabeth had plans, and she knew what plans looked like from the inside, and she had told herself a hundred times that she would not be the girl who got crabby about somebody else's notes.

Percy turned the sheet around and showed it to her with both hands, like a man unveiling a bridge.

*AFTERNOON,* it said, in his uphill letters. *12:00 — swim to buoy. 12:30 — back. 1:00 — lunch part two. 1:30 — race to boundary rock and back. 2:00 — cove. 3:00 — race to cove. 3:30 — long kiss, rescheduled for 4. 4:00 — rate the day out of ten. 4:30 — one more thing. 5:00 — list continues.*

"See?" he said. "Structure. Momentum. I even gave us a long-kiss slot. That's generosity, Annabeth. That's grown-man scheduling."

She read it twice. She read it a third time, the way she read everything she was afraid she was about to feel something about, and then she felt it.

It was small. That was the honest thing about it afterward: it was small. A hairline crack, the kind you only see because you're standing at the angle you're standing at.

"You don't have to entertain me," she said.

The words came out flat and precise and wrong, the way her words did when she had felt something before she had named it, and she heard them land wrong and could not get them back.

Percy's face did the thing it did when he had been hit and was deciding whether it counted. "I'm not entertaining you," he said. "I'm making the day good."

"I know that's what you're doing." She sat with the fleece over one knee and her hands curled into it, and she looked at the paper instead of at him, because his face at that moment was very open and very young, and she knew that whatever she said next would land somewhere permanent. "That's not — I'm not crabby about the racing. We raced in August. You won. It was fun."

"So what is it?"

She was quiet a second. The lake knocked the buoys. Somewhere out past the boundary rock a fish turned over in the sun.

"Thalia said it this morning. You build when you're happy. You build because you think good things need scaffolding or they fall down." She looked up at him. "I don't want scaffolding. I wanted the hole in the day. I said that on the water, and you nodded, and twenty minutes later you're writing *4:00 — rate the day out of ten.*"

"I was joking," Percy said. "Mostly."

"Mostly," she said.

The word sat between them.

Percy set the pencil down on the fleece. He looked at the paper, at the whole architecture of the afternoon he had spent twenty minutes building, and his jaw worked the way it did when he was working out where the true thing was. Then he said it, and the word was the clumsy one, and it came out of him before he had dressed it: "I didn't want you to be bored."

Annabeth felt the flinch go through her and recognized it for what it was, the thing you recognize because you have met it before, in a different jacket.

"Bored," she said.

"Not — not bored of me. Of the day." He ran a hand through his hair, the one he had slicked back for the water, and left it standing. "You like things. You do things. You map and you plan and you walk the north path and you draw cairns with the rag going the right direction, and I like watching you do it, and today you sat here and you soaked up sun and you didn't want to do anything except float, and part of me thought — I thought I'd better bring enough afternoon for two people, is all."

"Enough," Annabeth said.

"Yeah."

"Enough." She was quiet again, and then, because precision was the only virtue she fully trusted, she went at her own flinch with the pencil and took it apart. "There are two feelings in here. I'm going to tell you both of them, and you're not going to interrupt, because if you interrupt I'll lose the thread. Okay?"

"Okay."

"One." She held up a finger, and it was Clarisse's finger, and she had learned it from Clarisse, and neither of them would ever admit it. "I wanted the empty hours. The unmapped ones. I have a whole life of people giving me useful afternoons, Percy — useful things, useful tours, useful lessons I'm very grateful for. I wanted one afternoon that was useless. Just you and the rock and the cold water. That feeling is mine and it's fair."

"Fair," he said.

"Two." She drew a breath. "The other one's worse."

"Tell me."

"I watched the three of them this morning," she said. "Plotting. Whispering. The sheet with the columns and the operation name and my initials on it. You could tell they'd been doing it since Monday. They had a whole — a language. Code names, Salt, Pencil, *the vessel*." Her voice had gone smaller without her permission. "And I stood on the dock and I got briefed, and I laughed, and then you came down the path and you fit into it so easily, you just got in the canoe like you'd never fit into anything in your life, and I realized —" She stopped. Started again, which she almost never did. "I was jealous. For about four minutes. Of a clipboard. Of a conspiracy I was the guest at instead of a member of."

Percy had gone very still, the way he went when something true went past without armor.

"I didn't want to be *in* it, exactly," Annabeth said. "I wanted to be *inside* it. I wanted the three of them to have plots that already had me in them, that they'd been running since Monday without asking, the way their plots have you in them. And then you started scheduling, and I got handed a schedule, and I felt like a thing on the list instead of a person in the plot." She looked at her hands. "That's all. That's both of them. I told you it was stupid."

"It's not stupid," Percy said.

"It's a little stupid."

"It's not stupid." He picked up the operation sheet — hers now, the sheet with his itinerary on the back — and looked at it for a long second, at the uphill handwriting and the times and the generous long-kiss slot. Then he turned it over to the front, to Clarisse's three columns of operation logistics, initialed and underlined. "Look at it," he said, and held it so she could see.

"I'm looking at it."

"Your initials," he said, and pointed. "*Principal.* Not *guest.* Not *visitor.* In my sister's handwriting, planned since Monday." He tapped the line. "They built a whole operation around you before they ever met you for the morning. Silena packed eleven pounds of food for you and didn't say so, because she does that. Clarisse cleared the whole north shore in your name and then pretended she had chores. Thalia briefed you twice, and the second time she wasn't joking, and you know it." His voice had gone into the flat register he used for true things. "You were never the guest. You're the principal. You're the reason the sheet exists at all."

Annabeth looked at the paper. At *PENCIL* in Silena's careful hand.

"And I'm not scheduling you," Percy said. "I'm sorry. I was doing the thing where I fill up the spaces because I don't trust the quiet. The quiet and I have a whole history, and most of the history is me finding out people left in it." He turned the paper back over to his own side, the itinerary, the structure, the grown-man scheduling. "So. Cross it out."

"Cross it out?"

"Every line," he said, and handed her the pencil. "Take the pencil. Cross out all of it. Except one."

She took the pencil. She looked at him — fourteen, sun-pink at the temples, his hair standing where he'd pushed it, his eyes steady and a little frightened and entirely hers — and then she looked at the list. And she crossed it out. She crossed out *1:30 — race to boundary rock and back.* She crossed out *3:30 — long kiss, rescheduled for 4,* which made a noise in her chest that was half laugh and half something she was not going to cry about. She crossed out every line, every time, every generous slot, until the back of the operation sheet was a mess of graphite and good intentions.

Then she stopped. One line was left, because she had kept one on purpose, because she was precise: *5:00 — one more thing.*

"That one's not mine," Percy said. "I put it there so there'd be a reason to keep going to five."

"I know why you put it there," Annabeth said. She turned the sheet over and wrote, on the front, under the three columns of Clarisse's careful operation, in her own square hand: *ONE MORE THING: THE HOLE IN THE DAY. NO TIME SPECIFIED.* Then she pressed it flat against the fleece with both palms and looked up at him. "There. Fixed. One item. No times."

Percy looked at the amended operation for a long time. Then he laughed, wet-eyed, the laugh that lived in the ribs, the one she had been chasing since June. "That's the worst plan I've ever seen," he said. "That's a plan with one item and no logistics whatsoever. Clarisse would have a field day."

"Clarisse would hate it."

"I'm never showing her. This is ours. Principal's privilege." He lay back down on the fleece beside her, shoulder to shoulder, their knees touching, and picked up her hand and laced his fingers through hers. "Annabeth. The thing I said. I didn't want you to be bored. That's not it exactly. What I meant is I didn't want you to figure out you could have a whole day without filling it up, and then wonder why you needed me in the hole."

She turned on her side to look at him. "You're the reason the hole is good," she said. "You're the only person who'd get in the hole with me and not be bored. Everyone else wants to climb out of the hole and show me the top. That's — that's the job I've had my whole life. Sit in the hole, get lifted out, be shown the sky, be grateful." She reached up and put her hand flat on his chest, over the heartbeat. "You get in the hole. That's the whole thing. That's why you're the fixed point."

Percy turned his head and looked at her, and whatever he saw there made his breath go out of him, and he put his hand over hers on his chest and held it there, and they lay in the sun and let the long quiet come.

Then he kissed her.

It was the long kiss of that afternoon, the one that had its own weather, and it came the way good things did when nobody was managing them: slowly. It started with their foreheads still touching, and then his hand at her jaw, his thumb warm against her cheek, and her hand in his hair where the lake had dried it salt-stiff and it came loose under her fingers. He tasted like lemon and cold water and the last of the honey. She tasted like peach. The wind came off the lake onto their bare shoulders and neither of them moved away from it; if anything they leaned into the cold, because the cold was what made the warmth real.

It stayed unhurried. It stayed patient. It went on long enough that the world narrowed down to breath and attention — not urgency, attention, the thing they had both been starving on and were now full of — and long enough that Annabeth could feel his heartbeat under her palm change its tempo, and stop trying to hide it, and just go, fast then slow then fast, which was the most honest thing his body ever did. She matched her breathing to his the way she matched pencil strokes to a straightedge, deliberately, and felt his arm tighten once around her back, noticed, kept, and then stay.

They kissed in the cold sun on the warm rock until the light went from gold to rose at the edges, and neither of them counted anything, and there was no bell, and there was no one above the pines who cared what two fourteen-year-olds did in the middle of an empty lake on a Wednesday, and the whole enormous clear sky seemed to have made itself available for exactly this and nothing else.

When they finally came apart, they came apart by degrees, the way you come out of water: a little at a time, and reluctantly, and then all at once. Their foreheads stayed together. Their hands stayed laced on the fleece between them. The wind moved. The lake moved. Percy's thumb found her pulse and rested there for a second, then moved again, then rested.

"Hi," he said, against her forehead.

"Hi," she said, and kissed him once more, small and sure, because there was time, because the day had been built for lingering, and because lingering was allowed when it was tenderness and attention and nothing else, and this was the day they had figured out that this was allowed at all.

The sun kept going down. Neither of them moved to stop it.

---

After a while Annabeth sat up, and pulled the fleece over both their shoulders, and climbed back down against the rock so that she was sitting with her back to his chest and the fleece around them both like a tent, which was a position she had decided in August was allowed and had been perfecting since. The rock gave up its heat into their legs. The lake came in over their feet, warm on top from the day and cold underneath, and she held his hand in the shallows with both of hers and let the water take and give it back, take and give it back, which was the only clock either of them wanted.

Percy's arm was around her under the fleece, and her hand was over his heart, and she could feel it going slow and steady under her palm, and her breathing found his without either of them deciding it, the way breathing does when two people have been paying attention to each other for six hours.

"Pencil," he said, into her hair.

"Salt," she said back.

"You're warm now."

"I'm warm now." She meant more than the temperature. He knew she meant more than the temperature; that was the whole education of the day, that the sentences had gotten shorter and truer without either of them planning it. She lifted her head and looked out at the lake. It had gone rose-gold at the edges, and the cold was coming up the water now, a visible line where the warmer skin gave out and the deep cold took over, and the buoys knocked on their rope line in the hush. "We should go in once more," she said. "Last swim. Before it's just bragging."

"Clarisse's rule," Percy said.

"Clarisse has a rule for everything. It's why we like her."

They shed the fleece and the flannel and the camp shirts in the way of swimmers — no foolishness in it, no modesty in it, just the practical business of two people who knew each other's hands from water — and went in off the flat rock for the last time that afternoon, and the cold closed over their heads with its low green roar, and they swam a slow length of the shallows side by side with their eyes open to the rose sky, and came out streaming and gasping and laughing at the same moment, which was its own kind of arithmetic.

They dried the way you dry when you're going to walk in cold air: quickly, thoroughly, the towel competence of people who had done it a hundred times. Annabeth pulled her camp shirt and shorts back on over the blue one-piece damp and didn't care, and Percy pulled his on over his trunks, and they put the fleece back over the rock and sat on it to eat the emergency chocolate in two squares, which Silena had clearly intended for a much smaller portion, and which was going to be logged against them later with total accuracy.

Then Annabeth reached for the operation sheet.

It had been on the fleece between them since the repair, squares of crossed-out graphite and the one surviving item, and Percy watched her pick it up and unfold it, and hold it flat against her knees, and take up the stub of pencil from beside the tin cup.

"Don't laugh," she said. "It's not to scale."

"I have never laughed at anything you've drawn," Percy said, which was a lie of long standing, and which she ignored with the ease of long practice.

She drew on the margins of Clarisse's sheet, in the empty spaces where the columns ran out. The canoe from above, with the hippocampus prow gone slightly lopsided in the drawing because she liked it that way. The willow cove, its crowns in a scribble of green that was really just the shadow of the leaves drawn from memory. The flat rock. Two figures on the rock, P and A, so small that they were barely two dots with a blanket between them. And at the top, over the three columns of Clarisse's careful operation, she wrote, in her square hand, the amendment: *PRINCIPAL PENCIL KEPT THE DAY. ASSET SALT KEPT HER IN IT. LOGISTICS CANCELLED.*

Then she folded it along the same crease Clarisse's clip had made and pressed it flat against her palm for a second, and put it in his hand.

"There," she said. "Pockets and paper."

Percy unfolded it, and looked at it a long time, at the cove and the canoe and the two dots on the rock. "Annabeth. This is — Silena's sheet."

"Amended," Annabeth said, ears pink in the cold light. "Clarisse's operation, minus the schedule. Your itinerary, crossed out. The one item, kept." She bumped his shoulder with hers. "You keep yours in your pocket. I keep mine on paper. Now we both have the same paper. That seemed — the correct plan."

"The correct plan," Percy said, hoarse and delighted, and put it in the pocket of his camp shirt with the lake pebble and the folded vellum from Tyson's morning, patting it flat. "Staying things."

"Staying things," Annabeth agreed. "Tools checked?"

"Tools checked." He took her hand. "Boyfriend checked."

"Boyfriend's in the hole," she said. "With me. Where the schedule can't get him."

"Where the schedule can't get him," Percy agreed, solemn, and stood, and folded the fleece into a square so it would not hurt anyone, and they packed the satchel in the comfortable silence of people who had said everything important: tin cup rinsed, apple cores saved for the compost, the satchel hoisted, the canoe beached higher, the painter secured, the oars shipped. Annabeth checked the canoe's prow with her palm, one pass over the hippocampus carving, the way you'd check a friend's forehead for fever.

Then they rowed back at dusk, Annabeth in the bow and Percy at the oars, the lake going silver-blue under the first stars, the far shore dark and low, the cold wind coming straight off the water into their damp hair. Their hands found each other across the thwart without looking, cold fingers and warm, and they held on all the way in.

---

The dock was lit.

Not with one lantern. With five, which was the wrong number, and which they discovered when the green canoe came around the point of the north beach and the whole canoe dock blazed up out of the blue dark like a birthday somebody had been saving. Clarisse stood at the dock's root with a lantern in one hand and her spear over one shoulder and her braid rebraided for night. Silena sat on the end of the dock in her lake-gray sweater with her feet hanging, a thermos of tea in her lap and a plate of honey cakes beside her, warming her hands around a mug. Thalia sat on the rail with her flannel tied at the waist and a thermos of her own, boots dry at last. And beside them, of all things, sat Olive with a map tube and a face of total confusion, and two Hermes kids who had been drafted into something and clearly did not know what.

Percy shipped the oars and let the canoe come alongside the dock under its own momentum, and Annabeth stood up in the bow and took the painter from Clarisse's hand, and tied it off without thinking, the way she always tied it, because he always forgot.

"Good evening," Clarisse said.

"Good evening," Percy said.

"You're late."

Percy looked at the sky. "The operation said debrief at seven."

"The operation said debrief at seven," Clarisse agreed. "It's eight-ten."

"You lost the whole afternoon," Thalia said, taking a sip of her tea. "It was terrible. We waited and waited. Somebody tell me you at least got the landings in."

Percy tied off the last knot. "We did the landings."

"And the column?"

"We were column-adjacent."

Thalia pointed at him with the mug. "You hear that, Clarisse? Column-adjacent. That's a Prince. That's an admiral." She turned her head and addressed the lake at large: "I predicted the itinerary. There was an itinerary. There was a whole list with a long-kiss slot. I want that on the record with witnesses."

"Who are your witnesses?" Percy asked.

"Olive, unfortunately, and two Hermes kids who heard a whistle and thought it was a fire drill and came running, which is the story of how this check-in got bigger than we planned." Clarisse's face did the scowl that meant she was both furious and happy. "The whistle," she said. "It was supposed to be *our* whistle. The private one. I had it made on a string. And then it turned out to be the same whistle Silena uses to call the whole camp to dinner, because apparently there is one whistle at this camp and it belongs to dinner."

"I use it for muffins," Silena said, unrepentant. "You're welcome to buy your own whistle, Clarisse."

"I ordered my own whistle. It comes Thursday. Thursday, we will have whistles." Clarisse turned the lantern she was holding, and it lit up the two of them on the dock — Annabeth in her camp shirt with her hair half-dried and her ears pink, Percy with the satchel on his shoulder and the fleece under his arm and the folded operation sheet in his pocket, and both of them with their hands already finding each other. She looked them over with the quick inventory of an older sister: dry enough, warm enough, fed, whole. Then her voice dropped out of the mockery entirely. "Good day?"

"Good day," Percy said quietly.

"Best one," Annabeth said, and meant it with her whole voice. "Water cold. Current kind. Rock warm."

"Rock warm," Clarisse repeated, and nodded once, as if logging it, and looked away fast, out at the lake, and blinked hard.

Silena had already poured the tea and cut the honey cakes, and she pressed a mug into Annabeth's hands and one into Percy's, and they sat on the end of the dock with their feet in the evening-dark water and the fleece over their knees, and the Hermes kids and Olive were thanked and waved back up the hill with honey cakes in their hands and a story they were going to be telling all autumn.

"The letters," Thalia said, once the dock was theirs again. She had her map tube across her knees and a sheet of good paper on top of it, and she set both aside and leaned forward with her elbows on her knees. "I'm writing Jason Thursday. Courier goes to Camp Jupiter Friday, Chiron says. I need one line from each of you — something true, not a weather report. Percy, you're writing something about the lake. Annabeth, do the headings, because your headings are better than mine. Clarisse, you're doing the diagram of the cleat, because you drew it once and it made me laugh for four days. Silena —" She paused. "Silena's doing the rest. Obviously."

"Obviously," Silena said, serenely. "It's the book of Jason now. It's mostly evening moods and excuses."

"I'll write something tonight," Percy said. "After the steps."

"And I'll do the headings," Annabeth said, "and I'll bring vellum, because vellum survives a courier who drops things."

"Vellum survives everything except Clarisse," Thalia said. "It's decided, then. Thursday we write. Friday it goes." She sat back and picked up her tea, and her face in the lantern light was eighteen and dry and protective and very softly pleased. "Everybody contributes. That's the rule. You don't write to a friend at the other camp alone. That's how letters go wrong. You write with five hands."

Percy looked at the lake flashing dark-silver below the dock, and at the five lanterns standing around the dock like a small defended village, and at the satchel and the fleece and the folded sheet in his pocket, and felt the day close the way water closes over a dive: cleanly, completely, without losing anything.

"Five hands," he said.

"Five hands," Annabeth agreed, and squeezed his fingers once under the fleece, and neither of them let go.

They sat on the dock until the tea went cool and the stars came down over the pines, and then Clarisse stood and lifted her lantern and started up the dock toward the path, counting heads out of habit, one, two, three, four, five, all present, all kept, and Silena gathered the honey-cake plate and followed her, and Thalia walked last with her flannel flapping and her boots soft on the boards.

Percy and Annabeth came up behind them hand in hand, the fleece over both their shoulders and the satchel between them, and at the bend in the path, where the pines opened onto the slope down to the canoe beach, they stopped, the way they always stopped, and looked down at the lake. The green canoe lay at the top of its ramp, dark and patient, the hippocampus prow bright in the starlight. Out past the boundary rock the water was flat and enormous and honest, freshwater and autumn-cold and going quietly about the business of turning over, the deepest water coming up to meet the cold air, the lake changing its skin for the season without any comment or complaint at all.

"She'll hold us in November," Annabeth said quietly, leaning her head against his shoulder as they started up the hill.

"Yeah," Percy said. "She will."

At the top of the switchback, Percy stopped at the sight of the white stone on the corner bunk. It had been placed almost exactly at the center of Tyson's folded shirt. Nobody in the cabin had moved it since the farewell. Clarisse had moved a lot of other things, but not that.

"Did somebody dust his bunk?" Percy asked.

Silena glanced back, the cake plate balanced on one palm. "I did the window. Didn't touch the stone."

"The blanket's straight," Annabeth said.

"Clarisse straightened it yesterday," Silena replied. "She'll deny it if you ask."

From inside the cabin Clarisse's voice cut through the open door. "I can hear you."

"Then you know what I said."

Percy set the satchel by the entrance rather than beside the spare bunk. He had been carrying it across his shoulder since the dock and was abruptly tired of the strap. Annabeth followed him in, putting the canoe notes where they'd be found when someone checked the equipment again.

Silena turned to Thalia. "You still have the lantern count?"

"Five came back. Five go in the book."

"Good. Put your name beside it."

Thalia opened her mouth, prepared to complain that she didn't need a ledger for every good evening, then took the pencil and signed. Annabeth watched with an expression Percy recognized: she was already working out how she'd mark the return of the canoe and the condition of the landing before the next cold morning.

He didn't stop her. Instead he picked up the empty cups. Clarisse had the stove going and was looking for a clean pan. Somebody had to deal with the ordinary aftermath of their conspiracy. If the afternoon had worked, the evidence was the pile of dishes and a family disagreeing about whose turn it was to wash them.

Percy volunteered before the argument could start. Clarisse handed him a towel, Silena offered to help, and Annabeth stayed by the table to finish the note. The bunk remained exactly as it was.
