## Chapter 181: Whoever Wants to Come

The slate came off its nail at a quarter past six and went down face-up on the Cabin Three table, which was Clarisse's way of announcing that the evening had started and that anybody who wanted to argue with it could read the five rules and take the matter up with the wall.

Percy counted the towels out loud. He did it with his chin up and the flat ceremonial voice he used for counting anything his sister had a stake in — eight, nine, eight, nine — and Silena, on the far side of the table with the blue bag open and the suits going in smallest on top, did not look up once.

"There are nine towels," he said.

"There are eight towels."

"There were nine before you moved one."

"There were eight before you started being a weather system. Bag."

He took the bag. It was heavier than it looked, because the jar of goggles had gone in with the suits, and the tin of pins and the spool of silk thread had gone in with the goggles, because a swim bag in this cabin carried a sewing kit as standard equipment and nobody had ever put it to a vote. Nobody mentioned the thread. Anything that got noticed out loud got charged back to you at a later date, and Percy had lived with these two long enough to know the tax code by heart.

"The count line's empty," he said.

"The count line's empty because there's no count yet. I'm not writing a number in advance like a man betting on a horse."

"You wrote a number in advance last Tuesday."

"Last Tuesday I wrote nine, because last Tuesday I knew what nine was." Clarisse put the chalk down and looked at the slate the way she looked at a fence post that had come out of the ground. "Tonight it's whoever. That's the fifth rule and it's the whole rule. The number doesn't exist until half past eight, and then it exists permanently."

"Funny how that works."

"Funny works for me." She turned the page of the winter list, read the top of it, wrote nothing, and closed it, which in the language of this cabin was a form of grace being extended to the entire evening. "Katie's got six. Marcus is coming unless the pump argues, and if the pump argues he'll come anyway and complain about it in the doorway. Olive's coming because you told her on Sunday the depth marks were pending, in a tone, and she has spent the week waiting to watch a wall get corrected."

"I said pending. I didn't say painted."

"You said it the way a man promises fireworks." Clarisse stood up and pulled her jacket on over her camp shirt and took the slate under her arm, and looked at the two of them with the expression of a woman handing over a room she had personally resurrected. "Hall. Seven. Shoes in the racks. Percy, you carry the bag and you don't set it down on the wet tile, because that bag has her handwriting on the inside flap and it answers to nobody in this cabin except her."

"It says BLUE BAG," Percy said.

"It says a great deal more than BLUE BAG," Silena said, from the far side of the table, in the mild voice of a woman who had been rolling spare suits into a beach bag since she was eleven, and who had apparently decided at some point to stop pretending the bag was neutral equipment.

Down the west path the lamps had been burning since five, because Beckendorf's lamps did not wait for anybody's permission, and the nine of them went ahead of the three in the order they came to them: the odd one at the curve, two at the cedar brake, one at the boathouse turn, left-odd and right-off exactly as she had told him on a Monday in the rain. Each lamp laid its own flat gold rectangle on the frost, and Percy walked through all nine of them carrying a swim bag while the cold came up off the lake in a thin moving sheet that you could see if you looked along the water the way you look along a road.

It was the second proper frost of the month. It stood on the bank in the lee where Saturday's sun had not got at anything, and it crunched under the three of them in the particular way that frost crunches when it is not apologising, and at the foot of the bank, below the boathouse turn, the hall's own door stood shut with no light around its frame and a thread of steam coming off the top hinge into the dark.

"That's the door on a Tuesday," Clarisse said. "The top way's for towels and for people who can't sleep."

"The top way's for towels, and it's also for me at three in the morning, and I'd like it noted that both of those are the same errand."

"Noted." She opened the door, and the cold went in front of her like a curtain being taken down, and the hall took all three of them in at once.

It was exactly as it had been and completely different, which was the thing about a room that only got used twice a month. Black tile floor, black tile walls, the barrel ceiling of Roman brick patched in three places with brick that was the wrong colour and proud of it, the skylight gone to black glass with the night lying on the outside of it. The water moved at the far end in small slow hills where the hot line came in, and the heater ticked behind the inlet box, and the steam hung in the long curve of the roof in one flat sheet, and the whole room breathed out at them the way a room breathes when somebody has opened the door it has been waiting behind.

Silena went straight to the inlet end with the towels. Four of them she laid on the stones: the flat old pieces of the original floor that the hot line ran underneath, worn round at the corners, older than any tile in the building, the only thing in the room that had been there when the place was still a bathhouse and somebody had heated it on purpose for the first time. She laid them out in a row with the folded edges down, so that anybody who got out at half past eight into a cold October night would find a towel the temperature of a kitchen.

"You're warming the towels," Percy said.

"I'm warming the towels," Silena agreed, without turning around. "It's not a philosophy. It's a stone that's hot."

"You could put that on the slate."

"Rule six: Silena warms the towels." She straightened the last corner with two fingers. "Don't put it on the slate. He'll find out and he'll want a footnote."

Clarisse hung the slate on its nail by the door and stood back from it with her arms crossed and her jacket still on, and read all five of them again, the way she had read them every night for a week, out of what Percy had come to think of less as anxiety and more as a foreman's habit of checking that the sign was still on the building.

ONE: IN AND OUT THE SAME DOOR.
TWO: NOBODY SWIMS ALONE. SOMEONE ON THE EDGE, AWAKE.
THREE: THE DEEP END IS NOT A DARE.
FOUR: NO GLASS ANYWHERE NEAR THE TILE.
FIVE: AND WHOEVER WANTS TO COME, COMES.

"Percy."

"Sir."

"Rule five goes up to the porch with the count at half past eight. Condition one. Chiron wrote it and I'm not re-litigating it in a warm room." She said it to the slate rather than to him, which was how she said most things she meant. "An adult knows who is in the water on any given evening, and the only reason I agreed to that sentence is that it made him write down the second half."

"Which half?"

"The half where the hall is not Cabin Three's hall." She turned around at last and looked at the two of them in the steam, and the corner of her mouth did something that on any other face in the world would have been a smile and on hers was simply the fact of the evening going the way she had planned it. "Seven o'clock. Open the door."

So Percy opened the door, and the cold came in from the bottom of the bank, and up the frosty path in the dark there were voices coming down in a bunch — a woman's voice doing a head count and six small ones doing a head count badly over the top of her — and the first Tuesday of the second week of Tempest Hall began with Katie Gardner saying, at the threshold, in the voice of a person who has walked children down a hill in the dark before and intends to complain about it later, "There's steam coming out of the ground down here. I want it on the record that I said there's steam."

"There's steam," Clarisse said. "It's a bathhouse."

"It's October."

"It's a bathhouse in October. Come in and shut the door, you're letting the whole ridge out."

---

Katie Gardner did not swim. Katie Gardner came down the hill in a coat with a scarf on and a canvas bag of her own, sat on the second bench, and became, by eight o'clock, the most effective piece of furniture in the building, because she took coats and hung them and took wet ones off small ones and put dry ones on, and never once raised her voice above the level of a person who expected to be obeyed, which meant that nobody ever had to.

Her six came in in a clump the way they had the first time and stopped in the doorway to breathe, because a black-tiled hall full of steam at seven o'clock at night is a large thing when you are six or seven or even ten, and every single one of them did the same thing: coat off, shoes off, toes in the water, hands flat on the tile, and the small intake of breath that Percy had learned in September was not fear exactly, but arithmetic.

"Face in and count three," Rosie said to the smallest of them, a Demeter boy of six who had evidently not been told the method. "That's the whole thing. Face in, count three, head up, look at something you can see. Everybody's watching but they're not watching *at* you."

"Where'd you learn that?"

"I learned it in here." She said it the way somebody says *I learned it in Rome*. "From a boy. He's here tonight, he's the tall one with the bag."

"I'm not tall," Percy said, going past them with the goggles jar. "I'm correctly assembled."

Rosie did not turn around. "That's the one," she said, to the six-year-old, with the calm of a tour guide, and Percy had to set the jar down on the rack and busy himself with something else for a moment, because being cited as a historical source by a seven-year-old was a category of feeling he had not been prepared for at any age he had ever been.

Then the door kept admitting people, because rule five kept working.

Marcus came at ten past seven with his hair still wet from somewhere else and a mark of machine oil on his forearm, and stopped two steps in and looked at the water with the flat suspicion of a man who had been arguing with plumbing all day. "The pump's fine," he said to nobody. "It's fine until Thursday. I'm just saying it out loud so that when it isn't fine on Thursday nobody writes my name."

"Nobody writes your name," Clarisse said.

"My name gets written."

"Your name gets written when you do something." She jerked her chin at the deep end. "The pump's not your problem tonight. I'm on this bench, which is condition two, which means the deep end is open and you are welcome to it. The pump dies on Thursday, and then it's yours."

Olive Nakamura came down with a drawing tube under one arm, in a coat, and stood at the edge of the tile and looked at the painted numbers climbing the wall in their wrong white line the way another child would have looked at a cake, and said, without preamble, "Where's your pencil?"

"It's in the boat house."

"It's in your sock," Olive said, and was right, and Percy gave her the pencil, and she took it into the dressing room with the tube as though she were being handed a commission.

And Neve from the forge came, and two of the Apollo kids who had heard the door, and a Hermes boy who had come out of pure nosiness on the opening night and had, by now, the unmistakable air of a person for whom Tuesday had become a fixed institution.

By a quarter past seven the room had twelve people in it and the water had stopped being still.

That was the part Percy never got used to. He had grown up in water and he had arrived at this camp assuming, without ever once examining the assumption, that everybody came to it the way he did — as the thing you did instead of standing up. What he got instead, twice a week, in a black-tiled room with the heater ticking, was six kids working up to it an inch at a time with their hands clenched on the edge, and then the exact moment it broke: somebody's grip loosened, somebody laughed because their feet came off the floor, and the room went from a held breath to a riot in about four seconds flat.

The small ones splashed the way small ones splash, which is to say with no aim, no mercy and no awareness that they were doing it, and the hall gave every one of them back twice, so that a single flailed arm became weather. Marcus went under the ladder and came up on the far side like a man arriving at work. Olive, who had spent twenty minutes of her life learning to float on her back in this room and had never once been told she was good at it, did it again under the skylight with her goggles on the right way round and her ears under, and said, to the ceiling, to nobody, "It holds you up better," and somebody behind her said "yeah" and nobody else in the room understood the sentence and everybody in the room agreed with it.

Percy went down the shallow end on his hands and knees with his eyes open, because the water was clear of the tile tonight and the light from the lamp came through it in one long green coin, and he stayed there with his lungs easy and his hair floating in front of his face and thought, with the part of his mind that was always doing this whether he asked it to or not, about a corridor.

The smell got him there, the way it always did, before the water did. Clean mineral salt in warm air, not sea salt and not the lake, which smelled of pine roots and cold mud and reeds — the other thing, the deep-palace thing, the corridor outside his mother's rooms where the pools were lit from below and the water was kept at the temperature of a held breath and somebody's voice came down the length of it at six o'clock to say that dinner was on.

He had put that room where it belonged a week ago tonight, in front of a slate with five rules on it, and he could feel it now through the back of his ribs anyway, the way you feel a stair in the dark that you have already counted. The hall was not the palace. The hall was a Tuesday in October with a broken pump and a boy of six learning to count to three and a woman on a bench warming towels on hot stones, and both of those things were true at once and neither of them cancelled the other, and he chose — the way he had chosen a week ago, on purpose, in front of Annabeth — to push off the tile and come up.

Steam. Lantern. The ceiling's flat sheet of it, and the ladder ticking softly against the tile where somebody had just gone past it, and the whole shallow end arguing at once about a thing that had happened four seconds earlier and was already being disputed by two people who had not seen it.

He came up laughing for no reason he was going to give anybody, and Clarisse, on the bench by the door with the winter list, looked over at him with the expression she reserved for a spear she had decided not to check, and said, "Don't get sincere in the pool, Jackson."

"I'd never dream of it."

"You're doing it with your face."

---

Annabeth Grace came down the west path at seven-forty with the field book under her arm and her sister four steps ahead of her, which was Thalia's walking pace and had been since they were children — not rudeness, exactly, just a woman who had spent her life arriving somewhere before anybody else had agreed to leave.

They passed the lamps in reverse order on the way down: the odd one at the curve first, then the two at the cedar brake, then the single one at the boathouse turn, each of them throwing its flat gold rectangle across the frost and each of them, Annabeth noticed without deciding to, exactly where Beckendorf had said on a Friday that it was going to be. Left-odd, right-off, the odd one at the curve. She had not expected to be able to recite a lighting plan from memory and found she could, which she blamed on three years of reading plans for a living.

"You brought the book," Thalia said, without turning around.

"I brought the book."

"You're not going to open the book."

"I'm not going to open the book."

"That's worse than opening it," Thalia said. "Opening it is a decision. Carrying it is a hobby."

Annabeth adjusted the book under her arm. "You were on the porch for ten minutes with your boots off, which is a thing you do when you're deciding whether or not to be somewhere, and then you came down the hill at a speed I have only ever seen in a woman who had already decided and was annoyed about it."

"I'm deciding whether to be somewhere right now."

"You're already down the hill. The decision's been made; you're just arguing with it in the car."

Thalia made a noise that was not a word and went through the cedar brake ahead of her, and at the bottom of the bank, where the frost stood in the lee, the hall's door was open with steam coming out of it in a slow white rope that the cold took and pulled flat.

The room took Annabeth the way it always took her, which was by subtraction: cold gone, wind gone, the whole black box of tile and brick filled with warm salt air and the noise of sixteen people's worth of argument coming back off four walls wearing everybody's own voice. The steam hung in the long curve of the ceiling in one unmoving sheet. The water at the inlet end was making its small slow hills. Somewhere at the far end a pair of heels went slap-slap-slap across wet tile and then there was a dive — Thalia, off the deep-end block, flat and hard, in one motion, and the hole she left in the surface was clear enough to read the tile through, and she came up twenty feet down with the steam rolling off her shoulders and her hair laid flat behind her like something drawn.

"Thirteen!" she announced at the rail.

"Thirteen what?" Clarisse said, from the bench by the door, without looking up from the winter list.

"Lengths. Since Sunday. I've been counting."

"Nobody asked you to count."

"Nobody asked me to count any of the things I count, and the camp's still standing." Thalia hauled herself up onto the rail and sat there with the water running off her, looking at the door. "Calder asked me at mess whether this hall's open to everybody."

"And what did you say?"

"I said it's written on the slate. He said he'd read it, then, and went off for his towel." She said it to the water, in the flat register of a report being filed by a woman who did not intend to be asked about it.

"That's the most interesting thing you've said since Saturday," Annabeth said.

"It's logistics. I'm doing logistics."

"You're doing logistics about a boy with a towel, and you'd like me not to mention it."

"Yes," Thalia said. "Explicitly. Put it in the book."

"I'm not putting it in the book."

"Good," Thalia said, and then, because being agreed with was apparently the one thing she could not handle, she got off the rail and went under again in one flat dive that made the whole room blink.

Annabeth put her field book on the second bench, next to Katie's canvas bag, and took her boots off and her outer layer off, and sat on the tile with her feet in the water at the shallow end, which was the way she had done it every time, and which she had decided in advance, on the walk down, that she was going to do again tonight.

For one evening she was going to measure nothing. She had said that on the opening night and she had meant it then and she meant it now, and the fact that the wall of painted white numbers was climbing the tile in front of her in their wrong nine-inch line did not change the meaning of the decision, only its cost.

She looked at them anyway. Third from the shallow end was a full nine inches out, and after that the mistake compounded, the way small errors do when nobody stops them. Somewhere behind her eyes a plumb and a straightedge were already lining up for a night in the winter when nobody would be swimming. Not tonight. Tonight the numbers were paint on a wall in a warm room, and she was a girl with her feet in the water.

The small ones had begun to take the room over. That was the thing Annabeth had not expected to love about Tuesdays and now could not do without: the way a hall full of black tile and old brick turned into a room owned by seven-year-olds the moment somebody let them in. Rosie had graduated to instructor and was running the six-year-old through the count at the ladder with her hands on her hips, and when he came up spitting and furious she said, in the mild voice of a doctor, "Now do four," and Annabeth had to look away from the two of them for a second, because there was only one way that sentence had got into the world and it had come through this room a week ago.

Katie, on the second bench in her coat with the canvas bag on her knees, caught Annabeth looking and said, quietly, "She told her mother she learned to swim in a bathhouse. Her mother asked what a bathhouse was. She's been explaining it since Thursday."

"Is she right?"

"She's roughly right, which for her is a landslide." Katie took a coat off the hook and folded it. "I've got five at nine."

"Take five at nine. The count doesn't close the door."

Clarisse's voice came across the room, flat and unhurried: "No. It doesn't."

Then there were two of the Apollo kids at the door with their towels, in and out in eight minutes because they had something on at the pavilion, and the Hermes boy, who came down at ten past with a lamp and a satchel, read the room like a clipboard, decided he was staying until half past, and then changed his mind at a quarter to and went off up the path with the lamp swinging, calling over his shoulder that he would be back on Thursday and that somebody should leave the door unlatched, which was a request that nobody answered and everybody heard.

So the room settled at fifteen heads with the water moving in it, and Annabeth put her feet in deeper and rested her wrists on the tile and felt, through the warm water, a hand come to rest over hers.

Percy had swum over without a sound, which he could do and did, and now he was beside the edge with his arms folded on the tile and his hair flat to his head and his wet hand laid over her hand where it sat in two inches of water, and he did not say anything for a moment, and neither did she, and the room went on without them.

"You're not in," he said.

"I'm supervising the tile."

"You're supervising a wall you've decided not to fix."

"I'm being *generous* to a wall I've decided not to fix, which is a different activity and a much harder one." Annabeth turned her hand over under the water so that her fingers were against his, which was a thing they had been doing for so long that neither of them had any memory of the first time. "How many."

"Eighteen through the door since seven, and fifteen at the count, once the two Apollo kids go back to the pavilion and the Hermes boy goes to run his evening."

"When's the count?"

"Half past." He squeezed her fingers once, the way you squeeze a tool to check it's seated. "Clarisse does it and then somebody carries the slate up, and Chiron's condition is that a grown-up knows who's in the water, and since apparently nobody in this room qualifies as a grown-up, it goes up the hill on my face."

"On your face."

"It's a short errand and I'm the one with the coat. She asked me in the tone she uses for firewood."

Annabeth looked at him, in the steam, with the lamp making a green coin on the tile behind his shoulder, and thought about the field book on the second bench and the folded note in its back cover, which she had not opened since Monday night and which she could feel the location of the way a person can feel a key in a pocket.

"You'll freeze on the way up," she said.

"I'll freeze on the way up and I'll come back and I'll be warm in four minutes, because this room is a kettle."

"That wasn't a complaint."

Clarisse counted at half past eight from the bench with her jacket on and her eyes on the room, in one sweep rather than one by one, the way you count heads you have already counted twice in your head: one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen. She wrote 15 in the count line in her blocky capitals, under the five rules, where it sat like a receipt.

"Fifteen," she said, out loud, to the room. "Thirteen in the water, two on the bench, and if anybody wants to argue with that, argue with the wall."

"Nobody argues with the wall," Thalia said, from the deep-end rail with her hair wrung out in one hand. "The wall's got a pencil waiting for it."

"I know," Percy said, and held Annabeth's hand a second longer under the water, and then let go and went out through the dressing room to find his coat, and Annabeth sat with her feet in the edge of the warm and watched him go up the west path through the lamps with the slate under his arm, a boy in a coat over a swimsuit climbing a frozen hill to hang a number on a porch, and thought that if ever a thing had been built out of one specific person's stubbornness, that was the exact shape it came in.

She did not take Thalia's bait. She took her feet out of the water, and the cold came up her ankles and made her laugh at nothing, and the hall threw the laugh back at her from all four walls at once, and it sounded, she thought, like the room agreeing with somebody.

---

Silena Beauregard turned the towel row over at the inlet end, one at a time, because the four flat stones under the hot line held their heat for about an hour and then gave it back slower than they ought to, and a towel that had gone cold on a stone was only wet cloth with an opinion.

She had been doing it all evening between other things: laying the fresh ones down, taking the damp ones up to the rack in the dressing room, checking the jar of goggles, which was still minus the two cracked pairs in her pocket, pairs she was going to fix on Thursday with the needle and the silk thread and the strip of rubber from the kit under her bunk, a plan she had now had for a week and had not once advanced. The stones were the best idea in the room and nobody had had it on purpose. They were the old floor, the bathhouse floor, the only part of the building that predated the tile, and the hot line ran underneath them, and the towels went on them in a row with the folded edges down, and by half past eight anybody who got out could wrap themselves in something the temperature of a kitchen.

The door opened at twenty to nine and the cold came in with it in one long low breath, and Percy came through with the slate under his arm and his ears red at the edges and his hair still wet under the coat hood, which meant he had run most of the way up and all of the way back.

"He was on the porch before I got to the bottom step," he said, dropping the slate on the bench beside her. "Standing there with the lamp on and the door open, like he'd been listening to me come up the hill in sandals."

"And?"

"'Fifteen.' He said it before I did." Percy pulled the coat off and dropped it over the hook. "'It's in the book,' he said. 'Fifteen.' Then he asked me whether the stones at the inlet end were still hot, and I said yes, and he said, *good, that is the part that matters*, and then he went inside and shut the door on me standing there in a swimsuit."

"He asked about the stones."

"He asked about the stones. He didn't ask about the water, or the count, or the harpy. The stones." Percy grinned, the way he did when a thing had landed exactly where he had hoped it would. "I'm putting that on the slate. Rule six: the stones stay hot."

"Rule six is spoken for. Silena warms the towels."

"Then rule seven."

"There's no rule seven." She set the dry towel down at the end of the row and did not look up, and heard, in the room behind her, the whole evening going on without either of them in it: the small ones at the shallow end, Marcus's voice doing arithmetic about nothing, the ladder ticking against the tile, the heater's slow tick through the stone. "She'd let you write one. You know she would."

"I'm not writing a rule. I'm just going to keep saying it until she does."

"That's how all of them got written," Silena said, and Percy laughed and went back into the water, and the hall gave the laugh back off four walls larger than he had spent it.

At ten to nine the outside door opened again and the cold came in a second time, and this time it came in wearing a canvas coat over work clothes with a tool roll under one arm.

Charlie Beckendorf stopped two steps in, the way he always stopped, which was not hesitation but inventory: the water, the towels, the slate with its five rules and the new number under them, the rack, the benches, the small ones being collected into coats by the second bench. He read the slate. He read all of it, and the count line last, and then he looked at Silena by the inlet end with the folded towel still in her hands.

"Fifteen," he said.

"Fifteen."

"That's a lot of people in a room with one pump." He crouched at the inlet box and got the roll open on the tile beside his knee, and the whole business of the evening rearranged itself around the fact of him being in it, which Silena noticed the way you notice a lamp being lit in a room you were already reading in. "Thursday's the day. I said last week it'd hold about a week, and it's a week tomorrow, and tomorrow's Wednesday, so tonight's the last honest evening it's got."

"So fix it tonight."

"That's the plan." He had the box open and his hooded lamp already set on the tile at an angle he had plainly chosen in the doorway, and his hands, which were enormous and unhurried, went in past the fitting and moved it a quarter of a turn and checked it and moved it back, and Silena stood there with a towel and understood that she was going to be standing here for the next twenty minutes whether or not there was any use in it.

There was use in it. The hooded lamp threw a shadow into the box that got worse the closer he leaned, and after a minute he said, without looking up, "The shadow's the problem. Same as last time."

She set the second lantern on the tile low and left, and held it, and said, "There."

"Bit higher."

"Higher."

"Stop." His hand came out of the box and took the lantern by the handle from her, not roughly and not carefully either, the way a person takes a thing from somebody who is holding it for them, and he moved it an inch and gave it back, and their fingers were both on the wire handle for the length of one breath, and then they weren't, and neither of them said anything about it, and the water went on with the one patient sound it knew, under the ladder.

He worked for a while and she held the light. From the shallow end came a splash of the particular size that means a small person has been pushed in by a slightly larger one and has decided to enjoy it, and then Katie's voice doing a count, and Beckendorf's mouth moved in a way that was not a smile and was the same shape as one.

"Fifteen," he said again, to the box, as if he were checking the number against the plumbing.

"Fifteen," Silena said. "Four out of the bag, three of them borrowed and one of them mine, and Rosie's back in the one with the silk in the shoulder, which she has now worn three Tuesdays running and has never once mentioned to anybody."

"That's the one I noticed," he said. "Opening night. Somebody sewed a shoulder like it was a shirt."

"Left shoulder, where a strap takes weight. That's where suits give out first."

"That's not where you'd sew if you were rushing."

"No," Silena said. "It isn't."

He did not look up. He got the strainer out of the inlet and held it under the lantern light, and it was, once again, a flat disc of rust-coloured lint and hair and ancient leaf-mould, and he said, "That's April," and she said, "That's still disgusting," and he said, "That's still plumbing," and the hall gave her laugh back to her from four walls at once, and Beckendorf's face did the thing it did when he was caught, which was to go entirely still and then get busier.

After that he told her what he was doing, in short lines, while she held the light: the new strainer he had cut himself from a finer mesh, because the old one was never going to hold a season; the fitting he had replaced; the gasket he had made out of a strip of the same rubber she used for the goggles, which he knew, he said, because he had watched her mend a pair in the dressing-room doorway on a Thursday and had decided that her rubber was a better material than anything the shop had.

"You watched me mend goggles."

"I watched you mend a lot of things," he said, and then, into the box, without changing his voice at all: "Hold the light where it was. You moved it."

"I didn't move it."

"You moved it a quarter inch when I said the rubber thing." He still was not looking up, and his ears, in the lantern light, had gone the colour of the tile behind him, and Silena Beauregard, who could talk her way through a whole pavilion and had once held a hall of two hundred people with one sentence about a cake, stood on black tile with a lantern in her hand and could not think of a single thing to say, and did not need to, because at that exact moment Clarisse's voice came across the room like a hand clap.

"Nine o'clock. Small ones out, coats on, Katie's got five. Katie, your scarf's in the blue bag, second hook, and if you leave it here again it's going in the lost-and-found I don't have."

Beckendorf laughed, quietly, at the floor of the inlet box. "She counts scarves now."

"She's counted scarves since July. She counts everything that belongs to somebody." Silena set the lantern down on the tile, steady, and stood up, and as she did she looked at the bench under which the basket sat, and the bundle that was not hers was still folded inside it, untouched since Friday, and she was not going to touch it tonight either, and that, she decided, was its own kind of answer; so she picked up the next dry towel instead.

"Stones are hot," she said, over her shoulder, as he was packing the roll. "There's four of them and they stay warm till closing. Take one."

Beckendorf looked at the row of towels on the old stone for a second, and then at her, and then he took one, because it was an ordinary offer and there was no way to decline it that would not be strange, and he folded it over his hands the way you warm hands that have spent an hour in a cold building's plumbing, and said, "That's better than any glove I own."

"They're just towels on hot stones."

"They're towels on hot stones," he agreed, in the tone of a man writing a sentence down somewhere he would not be showing anybody, and picked up the tool roll, and went out into the cold, and the door shut, and the room was the room again.

---

At nine o'clock the hall started handing people back.

Katie's five went out in a line with their coats pulled over their wet hair, and the door opened and shut five times in ninety seconds, and each time the cold came in like an animal that had been waiting politely and the steam went out to meet it. Rosie was at the end of the line, and at the threshold she turned around and said, "Same time Thursday?", and Clarisse, from the bench, without looking up from the winter list, said, "Same time Thursday, and it's the same door," and the small girl went up the frosty bank with her chin up, satisfied that the institution had been reconfirmed.

Then the hall did the thing it always did when the children had gone and the evening was left to the people who meant to stay in it.

It got quieter without getting quiet. The heater ticked. The water lay in its long black shape with the lamp light standing on it in one green coin. The steam had come down from the ceiling's curve and was hanging lower now, at head height, so that you moved through it rather than under it, and the tile held the warmth the way stone holds anything, slowly and without conditions.

"Statue," Thalia said, from the deep-end rail. "Somebody set a number."

It was Clarisse's game, which meant it had rules and no name: shallow end, bottom of the water, sitting, nobody pushing off, nobody surfacing, whoever was still down when the rest of them had come up. Annabeth declined on the spot and took the timing, which she did with the field book balanced on her knee on the dry tiles and her chin up, in the tone of an official who had seen the entries and had views about them.

Thalia went first and went down off the block without a sound and stayed under with her arms folded and her hair lifting and settling around her head like something growing, and came up at fifty-four seconds and said, "Fifty-four," before anybody could speak, "and a week ago I did fifty-one in this same water, and I have since been up a mountain, so call it a fifty-eight adjusted."

"Adjusted by whom?" Annabeth said.

"By me. I'm the only person here who does her arithmetic with her lungs shut."

Marcus went next and did seventy-one and came up grinning with his hair flat and said nothing at all, which was the only acceptable way to win at it, and Clarisse, on the bench, wrote 71 in the margin of her page without comment, which in this family counted as a knighthood.

Percy went down and got to forty-six and came up with his ears roaring and the steam in his face, laughing at nothing he was going to explain, and Annabeth said, "Forty-six," and then, because she could not help herself and had never once in her life wanted to, "Forty-six, if the start was clean, and I'd rather be accurate."

"The start was clean."

"The start was *enthusiastic*. Enthusiastic isn't clean."

"Forty-six is forty-six," Thalia said, hauling herself out onto the tiles with the ease of somebody who had made fifty-four look like a decision, "and the record is mine until Thursday, and I'd like that noted by the person with the pencil."

"Nobody asked for a record," Clarisse said, from the bench. "There's no record. There's a margin, and the margin is mine."

Marcus left at twenty past nine with a nod at the inlet box and a look at the slate, and Olive went with him as far as the bank because she was going up to the Big House to pin her sheet to the wall before Chiron saw the morning, and Neve from the forge had gone ten minutes earlier with her towel over her head like a hood, so the hall belonged to the five of them again, exactly the way it had on the first night, except that this time five had come out of fifteen instead of eleven.

Annabeth got in properly then, entering the water the way she did everything: from the edge, under her own terms, with nothing wasted. She had turned that into a private discipline out of pure spite in June and had since made it look like a manner of birth, and she came up with her hair dark to her shoulders and her hands pushing the water away from her face, and Percy was beside her in two strokes, and the water between them went small.

"You timed me," he said.

"I timed everybody. That's the job."

"You gave Marcus a clean start and me an enthusiastic one."

"Marcus sat still like a man who had done it before and you went down like a man who had something to prove to a shallow end." She said it with her mouth entirely straight, and then the corner of it went, and he felt it before he saw it, because she was close enough that he could have felt a change in her breathing, and that was a thing he had been able to do since he was twelve years old in two different lives.

They floated. That was what they did instead of talking for a while: he put his hands out flat under the water and she took them, one each, and leaned back until the water had her, and her shoulders were against his chest and her hair went on moving with the breathing of the water, and he walked her the length of the shallow end and back again with his feet dragging softly on the floor of it, in bare feet on old tile, and the whole walk took four minutes and neither of them counted a second of it.

The warm salt held her up the way it held everything up. She said, at one point, to the ceiling, "It's warmer than it was last week."

"It's the same water."

"It's a different Tuesday. The stones are hotter, the door's been opened fewer times, and there are fewer people's bodies in it, and I did the arithmetic without wanting to."

"That's the most romantic thing you've said all night."

"It's a heat-loss calculation," Annabeth said, and then, without moving her shoulders an inch: "Say it back to me."

"Your heat-loss calculation is the most romantic—"

"No. The other thing. From the tile, on Sunday."

He was quiet a second, walking her past the ladder, past the green coin of the lamp, through the steam that hung at the height of their chins. "The brackets stay," he said.

"The brackets stay," she agreed, and closed her eyes, and let the water do the holding.

Then Thalia got out and went into the dressing room for her things, and Silena began the towel round at the inlet end with her back to the room, and Clarisse was at the door on the bench with the winter list flat on her knee and her eyes on the page and the whole warm room in front of her, and Percy and Annabeth stood in chest-deep water at the shallow end and did not move, and the hall went on being a hall.

He kissed her first. He had not planned to, and neither had she, and that was the shape of the whole evening: the door had been open and people had come in, and then the door had stopped and it was the two of them in a room that smelled of mineral salt and hot stone.

There was no rush in it anywhere, and no particular order to it either. His hand came up from the water to the side of her face and brought the water with it, a line of warmth along her jaw and her temple, and she turned into it the way she turned into everything of his, without a pause and without ceremony, and her hands came to rest flat on his chest under the surface, and the kiss went on and the room answered from four walls at once, and the answer was larger than the two of them were making.

They breathed. That was the whole of it and the whole of the point: they breathed between one part of it and the next, his hand moving to the back of her neck with the wet hair under his fingers, her thumbs pressing once against his sternum, and then again, and the heater ticked, and the steam stood still, and neither of them was in any hurry to be anywhere else in the world, and neither of them closed the distance until they were ready to, and when they did it was slower still.

"The lamp's going low," she said, against his mouth, without lifting her head.

"Let it."

"The stones are going cold."

"Silena will put more on them."

"Percy."

"Annabeth." He said her name the way she liked it, which was flat and complete, with no question in it, and she made a small sound that was not a word and came back into the kiss as though the argument had been a formality that they were both happy to lose.

Afterwards they stayed where they were with their foreheads together, breathing, her hands still flat on his chest and his arm still around her back, and the water moved around the two of them and nobody in the room looked, and that was not an accident: it was the specific, deliberate courtesy of four other people who had all, at that exact moment, decided to be somewhere else with their eyes, and who had managed it without a word and without a single glance that would have made either of them stop.

"I'm going to get out before I become a raisin," Annabeth said eventually.

"Same."

"The towels are on the stones."

"I know where the towels are." He let go, which took a moment, and then they both went for the edge at the same time and both got there the same way: over the tile in one move, onto the warm floor, into the cold air of a room that had spent three hours being kind.

The towels were on the stones and the stones were still hot, and Percy took one and put it over his shoulders and stood there in the steam with it, and the heat went through the cotton into his back in a slow line, and he understood, standing there, why Chiron had asked about them and nothing else. Out of a hall in October, into a cold room, onto hot stone, under a towel the temperature of a kitchen: that was the whole bathhouse argument, and it had been made four times by four flat pieces of floor that nobody had ever put a rule about.

---

Annabeth came out of the hall's door at ten to ten, with her boots fixed by the second step, and the cold took her the way cold takes everybody in October: all at once, personally, and with an opinion about what she was wearing.

The path above her was lit the whole length of it. Nine, from the foot of the bank to the top: the one at the boathouse turn close enough to touch, the two in the cedar brake above it, the odd one at the curve above those, and five more beyond that which she had never once been able to name and could nevertheless count in order in the dark the way a person counts stairs in their own house.

"Steaming," Thalia said, from two steps up, in the tone of a woman reporting weather.

"I'm steaming."

"You're steaming and you're walking at his pace, which is a third of mine."

"Your pace is a third of a person's and you know it."

"My pace is *efficient*," Thalia said, and went on up ahead of them at exactly the speed she had been walking at since they were children, and did not look back, and did not need to, because she had been able to hear the two of them behind her in the dark since the door.

They went up the bank at the same time as each other, which was the point of banks. The frost had come down harder in the hour they had been indoors and it stood over the grass in one unbroken white sheet, and the lamp at the boathouse turn threw their two shadows ahead of them, long and joined, walking up the hill into their own blur.

His hand found hers between the second and third lamps, where the path bent and the brake closed over it, and neither of them made anything of it, which was how it had been for so long that the not-making-of-it had become the whole language. His fingers were cold and her fingers were cold and they held on anyway, and then, two lamps further up, she put both their hands into the pocket of his coat, which was the only part of the arrangement that either of them had ever suggested improving.

"You're cold," he said.

"You're colder. You're the one who was in the water longest."

"I was in the water longest because I was being walked up and down a shallow end by a woman who was supervising the tile."

"You were being *escorted* by a woman who was supervising the tile," Annabeth said, "and if you say heat-loss calculation I'm letting go of your hand."

"I would never."

"You said it about the stones."

"I said it about the stones because it's true and because you taught me that saying true things is the same as flirting." He stopped at the cedar brake, under the two lamps, where the path went narrow and the frost on the branches came down in a thin dry powder at the movement of them, and the cold had got into her collar and she had stopped noticing it four lamps ago, and he put his other hand on the side of her neck and said, "You're freezing."

"I'm cold. There's a difference and you know it."

"I know it." His thumb went once, slowly, along the line of her jaw, and the cold skin there came up warm where he had touched it, and she looked up at him with her hood fallen back and her hair still carrying the last of the hall's heat in it, and said nothing, and did not have to.

The kiss on the path was a different animal from the one in the water. It was colder and slower and it had a shape to it: her hands under his coat against his ribs, his forehead against hers first, the breath going between them in small white ropes that neither of them bothered to move out of the way, and then her mouth against his and the salt from the hall still on both of them and the frost in the air and the lamp above the brake making their two shadows into one long thing on the white ground. They stopped for breath with their faces close and their eyes shut and went back into it, and the second part of it was slower than the first, and neither of them counted any of it, and the camp above them went on being asleep and the water below them went on being water.

"You'll get a cold out here," she said, at last, into his scarf.

"I've got a documented condition."

"You've got a documented condition and I've got a documented boyfriend who stands in freezing water to be near people."

"That's the same documentation." He took her hand again and put it back in his coat pocket, and they went up the last stretch, past the curve and the last of the nine, to where the path ended at the porch of Cabin Three, where the tenth lantern was on its hook and burning, as it had been every night since a Friday, for anyone who came up the hill late.

It was the warmest twenty feet in the camp, that porch. The lantern threw amber across the boards and the rail and the two of them, and Annabeth sat down on the top step with her back against the post and pulled him down beside her, and he went, and she put her coat around both of them the way you put a tarp over two things that are going to be out here a while, and they sat like that, shoulder to shoulder and hip to hip, with her head against his wet hair and his shoulder going gradually cold and neither of them minding the cold at all, which was the entire reason for the coat.

From where they sat you could see the whole west path going down in nine amber pieces to the dark at the bottom, where the hall's door had just shut with the light going out under it, and a minute later the sound of it, small and final, came up the hill.

"Fifteen," Annabeth said.

"Fifteen."

"Thursday."

"Thursday, and Marcus's pump, and if it makes a noise, nobody writes his name."

"Percy."

"I know." He turned his head and put his mouth against her hair at the temple, which was a place he had found in March and had never once had to look for again, and she closed her eyes and let the whole evening sit in the pile with all the others, and stayed there on the top step of somebody else's cabin with her coat around two people for as long as the budget allowed, which was, on a Tuesday, in October, longer than it had been on most nights.

Thalia was on the Cabin One porch when Annabeth got up there, standing at the rail in her coat with her arms folded, looking down at the nine lamps.

"You were twenty minutes," she said.

"I was twenty minutes."

"You were twenty minutes and you've got frost in your hair and you've still got your hood down." Thalia did not turn around. "There's cocoa in the pot. I made it at the wrong end of your walk, so it's gone over, and I've decided that's your problem now."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Drink it." Then, after a pause, in the flatter register that meant she had been holding a sentence in her pocket for the length of a hill: "He walks the whole way up with you and doesn't say anything on the way. That's not nothing. That's a man who knows the walk is the point."

Annabeth stood at the rail beside her sister and looked down at the lamps, one, two, three, four, and did not say anything for a moment, because some things in a family got said once and were then owned.

"He does know," she said.

"Good. Now drink the cocoa, it's terrible." Thalia went in, and the door shut, and the porch kept one of them in the cold for another ten seconds by choice, looking at the light on the path.

---

Clarisse and Silena came up the hill at half past ten with the basket and the blue bag and the slate, and found Percy sitting on the third step in his coat with his hands in his pockets, waiting for no reason he was going to give them.

"You'll catch your death," Silena said, going past him with the basket.

"I'm guarding the lantern."

"The lantern's not yours."

"It's the cabin's and I'm in the cabin." He stood up and took the slate out of Clarisse's hand before she could hang it on its nail by the door, and read the count line in the lantern light: FIFTEEN, in block capitals, under the five rules, with a line drawn under it the way you underline a thing that is finished with for the night.

"Chiron initialed it," Clarisse said. "He initialed it in pencil, at the porch, at a quarter to nine, and then he said goodnight to the slate and went in."

"He said goodnight to the slate."

"He's a centaur with a clipboard. He says goodnight to paperwork." She took the slate back from him and hung it on its nail and stood under the lantern with her arms crossed, looking at it, and the light went amber over the whole five lines and the number under them, and the west path below went on down the bank in nine flat pieces to the dark.

"Thirteen in the water and two on the bench," Percy said.

"Thirteen in the water and two on the bench," Clarisse said. "Nobody drowned. Nobody broke the tile. The pump's still running, which means Thursday's somebody else's problem." She put her hand on the door. "Bunks. All of you. I'm not carrying anybody up a ladder tonight."

Inside, the cabin had gone cold in the specific way cabins go cold when the stove has been out for three hours and the door has been opened twice: everything at the temperature of the outdoors but without the wind. Percy stripped off the damp things and got into the bunk in the dark with the blanket up to his ears, and the cold came at him from the window and from the boards and from the gap at the bottom of the door, and his hair was still wet at the ends, and his skin still carried the hall.

Percy pulled the blanket tighter and waited for his hair to stop dripping against his collar. The hall had been crowded, and the smell of warm salt still clung to his clean shirt. It had reminded him of Atlantis in a way he couldn't explain to anybody sleeping in this room. He didn't have to invent an explanation for it now.

Across the cabin, Silena got out of bed again. She put her feet into slippers and moved quietly to the window above the sink, where cold air had been reaching them for days. The old strip of cloth in the sill had shifted. She pressed it back into place with the heel of her hand, tested the draft, and went looking for the spare towel.

"Leave it," Clarisse muttered from beneath her blanket.

"I'm almost finished."

"Then finish without redecorating the window."

Silena gave the gap another push, wedged the towel firmly into the corner, and returned to bed. The room was still cold, but the air no longer moved directly across Percy's face.

"Better?" she asked him.

"Much."

"Then sleep."

It was an instruction he could obey. The hall would need its floor checked before Thursday, and Clarisse had already written the pump onto her board. He'd help when it was time. For tonight he folded the damp corner of his pillow under itself and shut his eyes. Down the hill someone finished extinguishing the last lantern. Inside the cabin the stove ticked as it cooled, and the repaired gap in the window held against the wind until morning.
