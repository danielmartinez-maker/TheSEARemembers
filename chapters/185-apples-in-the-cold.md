## Chapter 185: Apples in the Cold

The cold got into Cabin Three on Saturday morning the way it had been getting in all week: under the door, around the left sill, and through the frame over the sink, where Percy had stuffed a rolled towel in September and where somebody with a neater corner than his had since taken it out, folded it again, and tucked the ends down inside the wood.

He lay still with the blanket up to his chin and let the cabin tell him who was awake.

Silena was at the shelf. Apples came out of the wooden crate and into the enamel bowl, one at a time, and she counted them in the low voice she used when she was counting for herself instead of for a room, and every fourth one she turned over in her fingers before she let it go. Two went back into the crate without any comment at all, which was how a person said *cake* without having to say cake.

Clarisse was at the table with her tea and the list.

The winter list had been living on that table for three weeks now and it had grown two halves. The bottom half was written the way she wrote everything she had finished: capitals, flat, one line each, no wiggle in it anywhere. RIDGE - DONE. WATER FOUND AGAIN BY BETTER PEOPLE THAN ME. QUILT - HUNG AT THE RING, LEFT CORNER OFF AN INCH. LANTERNS - 9 UP, 10TH OURS. Above that, in the older writing from before any of it had happened, sat the three she had not got to:

GREENHOUSE BANK.
TORN AWNING.
FALLEN PINE.

Percy read them from under the blanket the way he read most things, sideways and from a distance, and said nothing, because working a page of Clarisse's list was a sport he had lost twice, won once, and did not intend to play again before he had eaten something.

"Fruit's going to freeze," Clarisse said, without looking up.

"Which fruit."

"All of it." She turned a page she did not need to turn. "There's a front coming through Sunday or Monday and I'm not writing a fourth line up there for people to feel bad about. The orchard's today's job. Yours too, since you're being horizontal."

"I'm being horizontal in a supportive role."

"You're being horizontal in my cabin."

"It's my cabin."

"It's Poseidon's cabin and he isn't here, so it's mine." Clarisse drank her tea. "Row one through six are ours. Katie's kids took seven and eight yesterday and did it the way eleven-year-olds do everything, which is badly and with enthusiasm, so leave those alone. Nine and ten are past the wall and nobody's going to fight you for them."

Sitting at the end of the table with her hair still wet from the wash-house, Silena said, without any particular emphasis, "The cake needs eight pounds of the firm ones and the press needs everything else, and if the two of you start on the counting argument before nine o'clock I'm going to sit on the crate."

"What counting argument?" Percy said.

"The one where he says he picked more and I say the crate was on his side of the path."

"The crate was on my side of the path."

"The path moved," Clarisse said, and drank her tea.

She stood up and capped the pencil and put the list face down on the salt, which was the way she said a thing was settled and also the way she said she did not want anybody reading the top half of it, and both meant the same thing this morning: the three untouched lines were staying untouched, and she knew it, and did not intend to discuss it. "Boots. Coats. The big sack's behind the door and the two wooden crates are on the porch, and if anybody has pockets, this is the day to have them."

It had, in fact, been Percy's idea, three days ago, in the ordinary way that a thing became his idea at this cabin, which was that he said it out loud once and Silena wrote it down and Clarisse started building it. The orchard ran eleven rows on the south slope past the greenhouse, and it had been picked twice in September by whoever walked past it, which meant that the middle rows had gone on hanging while the ends emptied out, and that the fruit left on the trees at the second week of October was the fruit nobody had wanted: high, small, shaded, and hard. Hard was what a press wanted. Hard was also what a cake wanted, if you were Silena, who had opinions about starch and had once made Percy hold a ladle at nine o'clock at night to hear them.

"You'll take the ladder up the wall end," Silena told Clarisse, "and you'll take the pole and the basket, and Percy, you're the ground."

"I'm the ground," Percy said.

"You're the ground because you're the only one of us who can be handed a thing from twelve feet without dropping it, and because last time you had the ladder you stood on the third rung for forty minutes and forgot there was a ground." She bit into one of the two apples she had kept back, and it cracked like a board. "That's the row, then. That one's firm."

Clarisse put her coat on the way she put on everything, which was fast and with a small violence toward the buttons, and then she stood with her hands flat on the table and looked down at the page under the salt for a second longer than looking required.

"Leave the pine," she said.

"Nobody's near the pine," Percy said.

"I know nobody's near the pine." She let go of the table. "It's a big dead pine that came down in the storm across the tool fence and it can sit there being dead until there's a day where moving it isn't the worst idea anybody's had. Today's a fruit day. Fruit doesn't wait."

"Does the pine wait?"

"The pine waits." She went for the door, and took the two crates off the porch on the way past, and Percy followed her out into a morning that was, by any honest measure, not a good one: the sky low and colourless, the grass stiff with a frost that had melted at the edges and then thought better of it and refrozen, the lake down the slope gone the flat pewter it went in October when it was not being silver for anybody.

It was, in other words, exactly the sort of morning that a person could spend indoors for, and Percy Jackson, who had spent a hundred mornings indoors in a palace that never got cold and had not liked one of them, put his hands in his coat pockets and walked down the west path toward the orchard with his two sisters, and was, without announcing it, in a very good mood.

Behind him, Clarisse said, to Silena and to nobody, "He's going to steal apples."

"Of course he's going to steal apples."

"I'm going to let him get away with it for about an hour," Clarisse said, "and then I'm going to enjoy myself."

---

Annabeth Grace got to the bottom of the Cabin One steps at ten past eight with her field book under one arm and two burlap sacks over her shoulder, and found the porch already occupied by her sister, who had been there a while, by the evidence.

The evidence was a coil of rope, a folding knife, four paper twists of Silena's that had clearly been acquired by bribery, and Thalia herself sitting on the rail backwards with her boots on the porch boards and her chin in her hand, looking out over the meadow with the expression of a woman who had done a whole morning's work before the sun came up and was now deciding what to spend the day on.

"You're early," Annabeth said.

"I'm exactly on time for something. I haven't decided what."

"You brought rope."

"I brought rope because it's a Saturday in October and rope has never once let me down." Thalia swung down off the rail. She had her good coat on and her bad heel in the flat boot that Annabeth was not supposed to know was a flat boot, and she had put the good boot on the foot that went down the steps first, which was a piece of staging that Annabeth had noticed on a Thursday in September and had never once mentioned. "Orchard. Rows one through six. I heard it at dinner last night between the potatoes."

"You heard it at dinner last night because Silena announced it at dinner last night."

"I heard it at dinner last night because I have ears." Thalia took one of the sacks off her sister's shoulder without asking and put it over her own, and then looked at the field book. "You're not taking that up the orchard."

"I'm taking the book because the orchard's on the slope and the slope's on my map as unimproved, and unimproved means nobody's ever checked whether the terrace wall at the top of row six is standing or lying down, and if it's lying down it's been lying down for a while and somebody ought to have noticed."

"It's a fruit run."

"It's a fruit run on a hill, and hills are my whole thing this month."

Thalia considered her. "You're going to check the wall."

"I'm going to check the wall."

"You're going to check the wall and then you're going to draw the wall and then you're going to stand there being right about the wall." Thalia went past her down the steps, and at the bottom she turned and walked backwards for three of them, grinning, and said, "Same bet as always, wisdom. Full sacks. Loser does the peels."

"There is no *same bet as always*. We've never done that."

"We're doing it now, so in a year it'll be a tradition, and traditions are just bets that won." Thalia set off down the path with the sack on her shoulder and her bad boot on the flat and her good boot taking the slope, and Annabeth followed her, and the camp went past them in the order it always went past: the cabins, the forge with its chimney cold, the pavilion with the tables still out from dinner, the arena, and then the long grass and the wire fence and the smell, which changed.

It changed the way it always changed in October, when you came down the west path and the wind turned and you got the whole orchard at once, which was a smell with three layers in it: the rot under everything, because an orchard in the second week of October was ninety per cent a thing going down; the cold green snap of the grass between the rows, which had been walked flat all summer and was standing up again now that nobody was walking it; and under both of them, faint and sweet and completely unreasonable, the apple itself, which smelled like sugar and bark and, Annabeth had never been able to get away from this, like the inside of a school lunchbox in the second week of September when she had been seven years old and had still believed that Virginia was where she was going to live.

She had mapped the orchard once, in July, from the slope above it, at one inch to forty feet, because she mapped everything, and she had drawn the eleven rows as eleven green bars and had never in her life walked down one of them. That was the thing about a map. A map told you where a thing was, and it never once told you what it was like to stand in it with your hands cold and a sister ahead of you bellowing a count.

"Row six!" Thalia shouted. "Wall first, peels later!"

"Go and pick!"

"I'm going and picking!" Thalia went into the rows backwards, still grinning, and was gone between two trees with her sack swinging, and somewhere further down the slope, out of sight behind the rise, a voice that was not a Grace voice and never had been said, clearly and carrying all the way up the hill, "Jackson. That's in my row."

Annabeth stood at the gate of the orchard with the frost in the grass and the smell of apples coming up the slope at her, and put her pencil behind her ear, and went down to find out what her family had started.

---

The rule, as laid down at ten past nine by Clarisse La Rue from the third rung of an aluminium ladder she had carried down the hill one-handed, was this: a person picked their own row, a person ate from their own row, and anything a person wanted from another row went in the sack and got divided at the press by weight.

"That's not a rule," Percy said. "That's a customs arrangement."

"It's whatever I say it is while I'm holding the ladder." Clarisse reached up into the low spread of row four, which was hers, and came back with three apples in one hand, and looked down at him from the rung with the flat look that meant she was pleased with the morning and was going to spend the morning being pleased in a way that looked like a siege. "You've got the pole and the basket and the ground, Jackson. Ground doesn't get opinions."

"Ground gets the basket."

"Ground carries the basket."

"She's right," Silena said, from the base of row five, where she was kneeling with the sack open and the crate beside her and a twist of paper already full of the small firm ones. "And ground also gets to be the one who walks the sack down to the press every twenty minutes, which is why I let you have it, because I didn't want to carry it and Clarisse couldn't."

"I could carry it."

"You could carry it and you'd drop the sack and we'd lose the second-best row to a slope." Silena folded the twist and set it in the crate. "Go and be the ground somewhere else. If you're good I'll give you the twist with the bruise in it."

"Generosity like that," Percy said, "is why this family's still running."

He went off along the row with the pole over his shoulder and the basket on his hip, and he was, in fact, excellent ground, and he intended to prove it, which was the first mistake.

The pole was a twelve-foot ash shaft with a wire basket on a hinge at the end of it, and the way it worked was that you put the basket under the apple and gave the shaft a short hard twist and the apple came off into the basket and you lowered it, and the whole business took about four seconds if you had done it before and rather longer if you were learning it on a cold morning with your fingers going numb at the second knuckle. Percy had done it before. His hands had known the angle before his head did, which was the sort of thing that happened to him now and then and which he had stopped being surprised by, and by the time he had finished row one he was taking the high fruit off row two without looking at the basket.

"That's my row two," Clarisse said, from twelve feet up and forty feet away.

"That's row two's row two. I work for the tree."

"You work for me. I'm the row captain."

"There's no such thing as a row captain."

"There is today and the badge is coming." Clarisse came down the ladder in three rungs and moved it, and the ladder had barely stopped moving when Percy heard the sound of a pole going into a tree at the far end of row three, and Thalia Grace, who had been told she was row six and had clearly been row six for eleven minutes, began taking the high fruit off row four, which was Clarisse's, with the unhurried efficiency of a woman who had been picking fights on hillsides since before the trees were planted.

Clarisse looked at the far end of row four. Then she looked at Percy.

"Don't," Percy said.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to say something."

"I was going to say that it's a family," Clarisse said, and put her hand into his basket, and took two apples out of it, and dropped them into the front pocket of his coat, one at a time, and said, "There. Now it's in your row. Eat them."

And that was how it started, and it went on for an hour and ten minutes, and it was, Percy thought later, one of the better things that had happened to him in either of his lives.

The shape of it was simple. You could not be caught stealing if the thing you stole was already in the other person's possession, so the war was fought entirely in pockets. Clarisse came down off the ladder twice and put apples in his coat without asking, and both times she did it with two fingers, sideways, like a woman reloading a weapon, and both times she said "Row four's got a scab on it, try it" or "That one's for the press, Jackson, that's a press apple" while she did it, so that on the record, which was Silena's face at the base of row five, nothing had happened. Percy retaliated by filling the pockets of Clarisse's own coat where it hung on the ladder, and then by taking an apple off row four and handing it up to her on the pole, which was so openly a theft that she had to laugh, which she did, briefly, and then threw a leaf at him.

Thalia, meanwhile, was running a completely separate and much more successful operation out of row six, which she had cleared in twenty minutes and then abandoned, and what she was actually doing, Percy worked out at about half past ten, was trading: she had gone down the slope to the little kids' rows with the paper twists Silena had given her and come back up with a sack of the big soft dessert apples, and taken them to the wall at the top of the slope where Annabeth was drawing, and from there the whole orchard could hear the two of them running what was plainly a market.

"I'll trade you four softs for the ladder for ten minutes."

"Six softs."

"Four and I tell you which of Marcus's socks those are." Annabeth's voice came down the slope, delighted and entirely without shame. "And I get the ladder after, because the wall goes past the end of row six and nobody's put a ladder on it since June."

"Six softs," Thalia said, "and I'll hold the ladder."

Clarisse, two rungs up, listened to this with her head on one side and then said, to Percy, "Are they talking about a wall."

"They're talking about a wall."

"On a fruit run."

"On a fruit run."

Clarisse put another apple in his pocket. "Don't tell her I gave you that."

"I'm going to tell her you gave me four."

"I'll tell her you ate nine off the ground."

"Ground doesn't get opinions," Percy said, and Clarisse made the short hard noise that was the inside of a laugh, and the ladder creaked, and the morning went on going past in the way a good morning does, in pieces you do not notice arriving.

Silena worked row five the way she worked everything, which was with a plan and a rhythm and no wasted motion at all, and every twenty minutes or so Percy walked the full sack down the slope to the press shed at the orchard's edge and came back up with an empty one, and on the fourth of those trips he found her sitting at the base of a tree with her back against the trunk and her hands in her lap and a folded square of paper in her fingers, reading it, with her face completely still and completely unlike the face she wore in a room full of people.

She did not put it away fast. She had the small embarrassment of being caught and then, deliberately, did not perform it, which was a thing she had got good at in the last two weeks and which Percy had decided, on the whole, to treat as ordinary.

"Press wants more," he said.

"It wants everything and it's going to get it in two trips." Silena folded the paper along the creases it had already acquired from four separate foldings and put it in her shirt pocket and patted it flat, and stood up, and looked at him with the warm unguarded expression she reserved for the moments when she had been seen and not moved. "You've got a row four apple in your left pocket and one in your right, and the one in your right has got a bruise, so that one's mine."

"How do you know which pocket."

"Because she puts them on the right so that you'll reach across yourself to get them out, and you always reach across, and it makes you fumble, and she's been watching you fumble for six weeks." Silena picked the bruised apple out of his pocket without looking, and put it in her own, and said, "Down you go. I'll bring the last two sacks."

By half past eleven the trees in rows one through six stood picked, which meant stripped, which meant that the orchard looked the way an orchard always looked in the second week of October when somebody had finally been bothered: bare, and thin, and much bigger than it had been at nine o'clock, with the whole slope opened up between the trunks so that you could see the greenhouse below with its glass gone dull and grey, and the fence, and the lake past all of it, flat pewter, going nowhere.

And the fruit was down at the press shed, in crates and sacks and four pockets, in a heap that came up to Percy's knee, and Silena stood over it with her arms folded and did the arithmetic out loud for the benefit of nobody.

"Firm ones, eight pounds for the cake. Bruised, cut, into the press. High ones, into the press. Soft ones, if Thalia has finished trading them, into a bowl for the pavilion." She looked up the slope, where two figures were coming down past row six with a ladder between them and a stack of flat drawings under one arm. "And there's the surveyor. Put the kettle on, Percy. It's going to be a long afternoon and I want everybody in the shed where I can see them."

---

The press shed was the only building on the slope that nobody had ever painted, and it was Annabeth's favourite structure at Camp Half-Blood, and she had never told anybody this because the moment you told people a shed was your favourite they started asking questions you could not answer without sounding like a person who had a type.

It was stone on three sides and board on the fourth, with a slate roof that dripped for an hour after any rain and a floor of flat flagstones laid so well in 1911 or thereabouts that not one of them had heaved, and it sat at the low corner of the orchard with its door facing the water so that on a clear afternoon you got a square of lake in the doorway and nothing else. Inside, on a trestle that had been there longer than the roof, stood the press: a wooden basket on a stone trough, an iron plate, a screw the thickness of Percy's wrist, and a bar four feet long with worn places at exactly the two spots where two hundred Octobers of hands had gone.

She had drawn it in July too, of course she had, from memory of having seen it through an open door, at one inch to a foot, and she had drawn the screw and the bar and the trough and had written in the margin *TWO PEOPLE MIN.*, and she had been right about that since July without ever having tested it, which was the kind of thing she was trying to get better at noticing.

"This is the best building here," she said.

Percy put the kettle on the small iron stove at the back, which Silena kept lit in October with a stick of fatwood and a great deal of disapproval, and did not rise to the obvious bait, which was a way he had of letting her have a sentence without charging her for it.

"It's a shed," he said.

"It's a *press house*. The roof's slate, the floor's laid in random ashlar, and the trough drains to a channel that goes out under the door and down to the grass, and nobody's ever put a mark on that wall that says who built it."

"So it's a good shed with no paperwork."

"It's a good shed with no paperwork," Annabeth agreed, and wrote it down, because he had said it better than she would have and because he knew she was going to write it down and had said it anyway.

The work took the two of them for two hours and forty minutes, and it was, in its way, the best thing that happened that day, and neither of them said so while it was happening, which is how those things go.

The first part was the grinder. You tipped the apples into the hopper at the top and turned the handle, and inside the hopper there were two wooden rollers with teeth cut into them, and the teeth took the apple and gave it back as a wet grey-brown pulp that fell into the bucket below, and the whole business made a sound like a slow boot in mud. Annabeth fed. She fed the way she did everything, which was at a rate she had decided on beforehand and would not be hurried off, three at a time, stems first, with her fingers clear of the rollers by an inch, and every now and then she put in one of the hard high ones that had gone soft in the crate and listened to the sound it made and took it out again and said, "Not that one."

"You've developed opinions about apples."

"I've always had opinions about apples. You've just never stood this close to them."

Percy turned the handle, which was a job for the arms and not the back, at a steady rate without being asked, and the bucket filled, and Silena took it away and came back with another, and at the end of the first hour Annabeth washed her hands under the channel where the water came out cold off the grass and said, without turning around, "Your turn's nearly done."

"My turn's the whole afternoon."

"It doesn't have to be." She shook the water off her hands, picked up the cloth, turned around, and then did not put it down, which was the part of the afternoon Percy would think about later, and considerably more often than he would have admitted at the time. She took his right hand in hers and did it the way you do a thing for a person who has been on a cold handle for an hour, which was slowly, with the thumb, and starting at the outside edge rather than in the middle, and she did the left one after, and his hands were cold and hers were colder and neither of them mentioned it, because mentioning it would have required stopping.

"You get the bar," she said.

"I get the bar."

"You get the bar because the screw wants more weight than I've got and less than Clarisse would give it, and you're exactly in the middle and you hate being exactly in the middle, which means you'll underdo it on purpose and then correct, and correcting twice is what the pomace needs." She let go of his hand and put the cloth over the trough and looked up at the screw with the expression she reserved for problems with a solution she had not yet been allowed to test. "Layers. An inch and a half, cloth over, again. Don't spin the bar. Two hands, walk it, stop when it protests."

"When it protests or when it argues?"

"Protests. It's iron. It argues with everything."

That was the second part, and it took the whole of the afternoon and the whole of Percy's shoulders and would be, he knew within ten minutes, the thing he was sore over on Monday, and he did it anyway and did it properly, and Annabeth built the layers beside him with her sleeves pushed up past her elbows and her hair doing the thing it did whenever she had stopped caring about her hair, which was that it came down out of its band in one long strand at the back and got pushed behind her ear every ninety seconds and came loose again every ninety seconds after that.

The bar went round and the screw went down a quarter turn at a time, and the cloth took the colour of the pulp through it, and the iron plate pressed, and the first cider came out of the trough's lip in a thin brown-gold line that ran into the enamel bowl with a sound so quiet that the whole shed leaned in to hear it.

Annabeth put her face near it. Not in it. Near it, and closed her eyes for the half second that a person closes their eyes when they are finding out whether a thing is what it should be.

"That's it," she said. "That's the September rain and the cold night and the granite. You can taste the frost that hasn't happened yet."

"You can't taste a frost that hasn't happened."

"I can taste the *promise* of a frost that hasn't happened, and it's better than the ones that have." She straightened up and looked at him across the trough with the steam of her own breath going up into the rafters and the iron bar between them, and said, "Try it."

He did. It was cold and it was sour and there was a brown sugar underneath it and a taste of bark and, underneath everything, something green and alive that had spent all summer in the dark on a tree and had come out into a stone shed in October and had not once been sweet for anybody until now. It tasted like the orchard smelled at nine o'clock in the morning, which was a thing he had not known you could do.

"Say something," Annabeth said.

"That's a shed with no paperwork," Percy said.

Annabeth Grace laughed out loud in the press house, alone, with her sleeves pushed up and pomace on her wrist and her field book going damp at the corner on the trestle, and outside in the doorway there was a square of flat grey lake and a slope of bare trees, and inside it there were two enamel bowls filling up, and it was, by any honest measure, the middle of the best day either of them had had in a fortnight, and neither of them was in any hurry to be somewhere else.

Clarisse came in at twenty past one to see about the second crate and looked at the two bowls, and at the layers, and at the bar, and at Percy's shoulders, and said, "You're spinning it."

"I'm not spinning it."

"You spun it twice. I heard it from the gate." She picked up the first bowl, drank from the rim of it, considered it for a long moment with her eyes on the roof boards, and put it down. "It's early. It wants a day. Put it in the corner with a cloth over and don't let Thalia in here."

"Thalia's on the range," Annabeth said.

"Thalia is *always* on the range, and she'll be in here by four." Clarisse went to the door and stopped in it, in the square of grey light, with one hand on the jamb. "Good work," she said, to the trough, to the stone, to nobody, and was gone up the slope with her hands in her pockets before either of them could say anything, which was the whole design of the sentence.

---

They carried the day home at four o'clock with their coats full of it.

That had been Silena's instruction, delivered at the shed door with the authority of a woman who had run a kitchen through a storm and a survey and a mending bee, and which nobody had argued with: pockets. Not bags, not sacks, pockets, because pockets kept the fruit apart and a bag let it bruise itself into cider before it got up the hill.

So Percy went up the west path with nine apples in the two big pockets of his coat and two more in the small one, walking the way a person walks when their coat has quietly become a piece of furniture, and Clarisse went ahead of him with the two crates and the ash pole over her shoulder and did not once slow down, and behind them Annabeth came up the hill with the flat drawings under her arm and her own pockets doing the same job, and every twenty yards or so an apple would knock against her hip and she would put her hand on it without breaking stride, and it was, Percy thought, the least graceful thing he had ever seen her do and he liked it more than he could have explained to anybody.

At the pavilion the tables had been pushed back and the long one nearest the kitchen had been taken over entirely. Silena had done the cake at three, in the big square tin, with the eight firm apples peeled into a bowl and the peels left in a twist on the counter because the peels were a debt that two people had not yet paid, and the cake now sat at the end of the long table under a cloth with a weight on top of the cloth, which in the grammar of this kitchen meant cooling, and meant that anybody who touched it was answering to her.

"Where's Thalia?" Annabeth asked.

"Range, since two. She came in at four with thirty-two arrows and one bad one." Silena did not look up from the board, where she was cutting bruised quarters off a stack of apples with the wooden knife, at speed.

"And Rhys Calder," Annabeth said, mildly, "is back on Friday."

Silena's knife did not stop and her shoulders did not move and the only thing that changed was the rate at which she cut, which went from quick to slightly quicker for about three seconds and then came back down, and she said, "The wind changes four times. It's October."

"I said nothing."

"You said a *name*." Silena put the wooden knife down and swept the quarters into the bowl with the side of her hand, and then she did look up, and she was smiling, and the smile was the one she wore when she was about to say something true and unkind in the correct proportion. "He's back on Friday and she's got him at eight tomorrow and she has —" and here she stopped, laughed at herself, and finished the sentence out loud, with her face going pink at the tops of the cheeks in a way she could not have hidden from a person on the next table, let alone from a sister standing two feet away, "she has *opinions* about his release, and she has been holding them in since the archery field, and I have been listening to them for two days, and that is the entire story, and if you repeat it I will put salt in the cake."

"Salt's good in apple cake."

"Salt is *excellent* in apple cake and that is not the point."

Annabeth put her hands up and went off around the tables, and behind her Silena went back to the quarters, and the tops of her ears had gone pink as well, which Annabeth noted and did not mention, because she had spent four months learning the size of the debt she owed that girl and had decided, quietly and permanently, to pay it back in the same currency.

Beckendorf came in at half past five to ask a question about the press.

He came in the way he came into every room, which was with his shoulders slightly lowered, as though the doorway were always the last inch of a place he had been asked to leave, and he had a rag in one hand and an oil can in the other, and he did not come in looking for anybody. He stood at the end of the long table and said, to Silena, at a distance of eight feet and without turning his head, "The screw's weeping at the collar. It wants packing before the next run or you'll lose a quart down the trough."

"I'll get the flax," Silena said.

"I'll bring the flax down Tuesday."

"Tuesday," Silena said, in a voice with nothing in it at all, "is Tempest Hall."

"Then I'll bring the flax down Tuesday at six and you'll have it packed by seven," Beckendorf said, put the oil can in his coat pocket, and went out past the tables and down the west steps into the blue part of the evening without once looking at the cloth over the cake, which Annabeth thought was a piece of manners so deliberate that it took more work than not looking at all.

Silena stood at the board with her hands flat and her ears pink for a count of five, and then she said, out loud, to the room, in a normal voice, "Flax," as though it were a word she had never heard before and which she intended to have a good long think about.

The game started at dusk, and it started because the little ones started it.

Katie's six had been let out of the Big House after the cake in the state that eleven-year-olds get into after sugar and a warm room, which is to say they went downhill like weather, and by the time the older ones arrived there was a chalk mark at the bottom of the meadow slope, a standing stone at the top of it, and a line of apples on the grass between, and Neve, aged eleven and entirely unbothered by the cold, was explaining the rules to anyone who could not escape.

The rules were these. You stood at the stone. You rolled an apple. Closest to the chalk line won. If your apple hit somebody else's apple, both of them counted and you bought the other person a cider.

"That's it?" Thalia said. She had come off the range at ten to six with her bow over her shoulder and her hair wet at the temples and the flatboot on the foot that went down the slope last, and she had eaten two pieces of standing cake and had not mentioned Maine or elbows or eight o'clock in the morning once. "That's the whole game."

"That's the whole game," Neve said, with enormous dignity. "It's harder than it looks. Apples aren't round."

"Apples are extremely not round," Thalia said, and put her bow down on the grass, and took an apple off the line, and rolled it, and watched it go off at an angle into the dark with all the confidence of a woman who had once rolled a boulder off a mountain.

Clarisse beat her by eleven inches and then insisted on measuring with the ash pole, and then insisted on measuring again, and then said, "It's a tie," with the tone of a person who had won and was not going to be gracious about it but was going to be fair about it, which in this family counted as grace.

Percy and Annabeth played four games and lost three of them, to Neve, to a nine-year-old called Danny who had no idea how he had done it, and to Silena, who rolled an apple straight down the middle without looking and then said she had been watching Thalia's footwork.

At the bottom of the slope the grass went grey and then went dark, and past the grey there was the beach, and past the beach there was the water, and the water had gone the deep flat pewter-black it went when the light came off it early, and down at the far end of the sand the north beach ring had a fire in it.

Somebody had laid it at five. It was Clarisse's fire, and it was built the way she built everything, low and square and completely without drama, and it was burning down already toward its coals, and above it on the two iron poles the storm quilt hung in the dark with its edges lifting, the whole eleven feet of it, a square made of everybody's old shirts going orange and then grey and then orange again as the flames moved, and there was nobody down there at all, and it was doing its job anyway, which was to be there.

Percy stood at the top of the slope and looked at it for a while with his hands in his coat pockets and his shoulders aching in the places where the bar had taught them to ache.

Annabeth came and stood beside him, and did not look at the beach, and looked at him looking at it, and then she said, "Silena sent a blanket down. It's behind the stone."

"She sent it to the stone?"

"She sent it to whichever of us got cold first." Annabeth looked at the fire on the sand for another three seconds, and then at him, and her face did the thing it did when a decision had been made and was going to be presented as a fact. "It's colder down there. That's the point of it. Bring it."

---

The blanket was the Cabin Three wool, the big one, the grey-and-brown stripe that had been on the drying rack since the storm and which Silena had folded twice and put behind the standing stone with a twist of paper on it that said, in her hand, *IF YOU'RE COLD YOU'RE ALREADY TOO COLD, SIT DOWN.*

It took both of them to open it out flat on the grass, because it was heavy in the way that a good blanket is heavy, and the wind wanted a corner of it, and by the time they had it down on the rise above the beach the game had gone quiet behind them and the camp had started going up the hill in ones and twos for the pavilion and the last of the light, and the two of them were, for the first time in eleven hours, sitting in a place with nobody in it.

Annabeth sat with her knees up and her back against the stone and the blanket over both of them to the waist, and Percy sat beside her with his shoulder against the stone and his bad shoulder against hers, and for a minute neither of them said anything at all, because there was a sound in the quiet that they both wanted to hear out, which was the fire down on the sand going crack and settle, and the lake moving on the shingle at the far end of the beach with no wind in it at all, and, far up the hill, the thin sound of a hundred and some kids being let out of a pavilion.

"Say something," Annabeth said.

"You've said that to me twice today."

"You've had two good things to say both times."

He had. He knew he had. And the thing about the third one was that it was not going to be clever, and he was fairly sure she knew that too, so he did not try.

He put his hand flat on the blanket between them, palm up, and left it there, and after about four seconds Annabeth Grace put her hand in it, and that was the whole of it, and it stayed like that for a long time while the fire below went down and the sky over the ridge went through the four or five colours it went through in the second week of October before it committed to black.

Hand-holding was a language and they had been speaking it for a year and a half, and like any language it had more words than anybody ever wrote down. There was the walking word, loose and easy, which happened without either of them noticing. There was the listening word, two fingers and a question, which meant *is this all right* and got answered, most of the time, by a thumb moving once, which meant *yes, and keep going*. There was the cold-weather word, the whole hand and the sleeve pulled down over the knuckles and a squeeze at the end of every third step. And there was the one they used when there was nobody else on a hill and a fire was going down on a beach two hundred feet below, which was all four fingers and the flat of the palm and no hurry in it anywhere, and which neither of them had ever named out loud, because the moment you name a thing like that it becomes smaller than it is.

Annabeth moved first. She came off the stone and sideways, the way you do when you have decided to spend an hour somewhere and are arranging yourself for it, and she got the blanket up over both their shoulders and put her head down against the side of his neck in the exact place it had been about eleven times before and which she found, every time, on the first try, without looking, in the dark.

"You're cold," he said.

"I'm not cold. I'm *becoming* cold. There's a difference and I'll write it in the margin."

"You'll write it in the margin tomorrow, when you can't feel your fingers."

"I can feel my fingers." She took his hand again under the blanket and put both of their hands on his knee and arranged the fingers one at a time, and then, with her mouth about three inches from his ear and her eyes closed, said, "I got the wall."

"Of course you got the wall."

"It's standing at the north end and it's lying down at the south end and the south end's been lying down since before either of us was born, and the footing's on bedrock so it doesn't matter, and I've drawn it in three scales."

"Annabeth."

"Yes."

"That's the best thing anybody's said to me all day and you know it."

"I know it," she said, and did not sound smug about it, which was the only reason he let her get away with it, and then she lifted her head off his neck and turned it and looked at him in the dark, and the light off the fire on the sand came up the slope at them in slow orange pulses, and put one hand on the side of his face with the flat of her fingers the way she did, unhurried, giving him all the time in the world to decide anything, which was a thing she had been doing since the first time and which he had never once needed.

So he did not decide anything. He just went the last three inches.

They had kissed in a lot of places in the last eighteen months: a boathouse, a cove, a fire ring, two porches, and a kitchen doorway, and the thing about this one was that neither of them could have said afterwards what it had been about. It was not a good-night, and it was not a hello, and it was not relief, because nothing had happened today that needed relieving. It was just long. It went on the way a fire goes on, in which you stop counting and then, later, start again.

They broke for air with their foreheads together and their breath going up into the dark in two separate plumes that mixed about a foot above their heads and did not entirely become one thing, and Percy said, "Cold?"

"No."

"Annabeth."

"Colder," she said. "Not cold. Colder, which is a different word, and I'm going to put it in the margin, and you're going to find it in March and be smug about the brackets."

"I am always smug about the brackets." He put his arm properly around her and pulled the blanket up over her shoulder with the other hand and got them rearranged, which took about twenty seconds and one elbow, and then they were the shape they were going to be for the next hour, which was one shape: her against his side with her knees up under the blanket and her head on his shoulder and her hand flat on his chest with the fingers slightly curled, and his chin on the top of her head, and the whole of the rise above the beach in front of them and the whole of the dark behind.

The fire on the sand went down to coals. The quilt on the poles went from orange to grey to a blacker black than the dark around it, and its edges stopped lifting, because the wind went off the water entirely at about ten past six, and the lake came in on the shingle in long flat sets that made a sound like a person turning a page in a very large book.

Annabeth's breathing went slow.

It did not go slow all at once, because she was not a person who did anything all at once, and because the thing that was making her sleepy was not tiredness at all but the specific and total safety of a place with a stone at her back and a person around her and a fire she did not have to tend, and that took about fifteen minutes to arrive. Her hand on his chest went from slightly curled to flat. Her head got heavier against his shoulder in three separate instalments, each one a surprise, and each one followed by a small correcting lift that fooled nobody.

"You can sleep," Percy said.

"I'm not asleep."

"I know you're not asleep. I'm giving you permission to be."

"I don't need permission." She did not lift her head. "I need twenty minutes and a wall I've already drawn."

"You've drawn three walls."

"I've drawn *one* wall in three scales, and the other two are the same wall in a different scale, and you know that, and you're being kind about it because you're kind about everything tonight." Her eyes were shut. "Why are you kind about everything tonight."

"Because it's Saturday and I carried you up a hill."

"You carried *apples* up a hill."

"Same hill."

Annabeth made the sound that was almost a laugh and did not let go of his coat, and then she said, into his shoulder, in the voice that was halfway down already, "Ten more minutes."

"Take twelve."

"Twelve is too many."

"Then take eleven and I'll round."

She took eleven. He counted them, because he was fourteen and because counting something with her in it was the only kind of arithmetic he had ever enjoyed, and during the eleventh one her hand on his chest went still in a way that a hand goes still, and her whole weight settled, and Percy Jackson sat on a cold rise above a beach with a girl asleep on his shoulder and a wool blanket over the two of them and did not move a single part of himself that he could avoid moving, and was, for about nine minutes, entirely and completely happy in the uncomplicated way that he had not been since he was about six years old and had found out what a swimming pool was.

It was the cold that moved them, in the end. Not the cold on him, which he could have sat in until Sunday, but the cold on her, which arrived at her fingers first and then at the ends of her ears and which he found by putting his own hand over hers, and which he then spent about forty seconds deciding how to say out loud without waking her into a thing.

He did not have to. She came up out of it the way she did everything, which was all at once and with her own reasoning intact.

"Fourteen minutes," she said, without opening her eyes.

"I gave you eleven."

"You gave me eleven and then you sat there for three more because you were counting whether I was cold and you hadn't decided how to say it." She sat up, and the blanket fell to her waist, and she put both her hands out in front of her and looked at them in the dark the way a person looks at a tool they have been using for an hour. "That's cold."

"That's cold."

"That's *properly* cold." She put her hands flat on his chest to warm them, one on each side, and looked at him doing it, and then she leaned in and put her mouth about an inch from his and stopped there, and said, "Thank you for not saying anything while I was asleep."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

"You'd dream of it. You had four speeches ready." She kissed him, briefly, twice, and the second one was slower than it needed to be, and the cold came up off the water and went around the stone and did not get in anywhere at all. "Up. If we stay here one more minute I'll go back down and then we'll be the people who slept on a rise in October, and Silena will never let us in the kitchen again."

They got up. It took a while, because blankets at that hour had opinions about being folded, and because Annabeth's hands took four full minutes to come back, during which she stood with them inside the front of his coat, one on each side of his ribs, looking up at him with an expression of complete and entirely unrepentant entitlement, and when he said, "This is a hostage situation," she said, "It's a *warming* situation, and you'll survive," and did not move them until the last possible second.

The blanket went over the standing stone in a folded square, with the twist of paper on top of it, so that the next person who came down the north path in the dark would find it and know that somebody had thought about them.

Below them the beach fire was a bed of coals under a quilt that was now, in the last of the light, only a shape. Percy looked at it for a second. Then he turned and started up the slope with Annabeth beside him, and behind them the fire and the quilt and the empty rise went on being exactly as they were, which was the whole point of leaving a place well.

---

The west path had nine lanterns on it and they were all burning.

They came on one at a time as you walked up, because that was how they had been hung, end to end, from the low hook by the canoe stow to the last one in the cedar brake, and the effect of walking up it in the dark was that the light arrived in front of you in pieces: a post, a puddle, a coil of somebody's rope, the flat of a stone step, and then dark again, and then the next one. Beckendorf had done the glass in September and Silena had done the wicks in October and neither of them had ever said so out loud, and the result was that the path up the hill from the beach was, at half past seven on a Saturday in the second week of October, the best-lit mile of ground at Camp Half-Blood, and also about four feet wide.

Annabeth walked on his left, which was the side away from the drop, and she had her hands in her pockets and her field book under her arm and her hair doing the thing it had been doing since the shed, and she had not said anything for about two hundred yards, which with her was not a silence at all but a receipt.

"Are you writing it up now?" Percy said.

"I'm writing it up in my head now so that I can write it up in the book tomorrow without inventing anything." She took her right hand out of her pocket and held it out, palm up, in the dark, without looking at him, which was the shortest sentence in the language and which he had been fluent in for a year. "Order. Orchard. Rows one to six. Press house. The wall."

"The wall goes in three scales."

"The wall goes in three scales and you are not allowed to be smug about the brackets in a sentence that starts with *the wall goes in three scales*."

"I'm not being smug. I'm being a supportive spouse of the survey profession."

Annabeth stopped walking.

It was not a stop that meant anything bad. It was the particular stop of a person who has been handed a word and is standing in the middle of the dark deciding what to do with it, and Percy, who had spent a year and a half learning the grammar of that stop, stood in the light of the fifth lantern with his hand out and did not take the sentence back, because taking a sentence back was not how you did this.

"I didn't say — " he began.

"You said it," Annabeth said. "I'm not saying you can't say it." She put her hand in his and walked on, and her thumb moved once, which was *yes, and keep going*, and then she said, quite steadily, in the voice she used for things she meant and was not going to repeat, "You can say it whenever you like. I'll take it apart later, and I'll be thorough about it, and you'll hate all of it, and I'll keep it."

"That's the most romantic thing anybody's ever said to me in a wet pine wood."

"It's the most romantic thing anybody's ever said to you *today*," Annabeth said, "and the day has had competition."

They went up the last of the path with their hands in their coat pockets together on the side that had the pockets, which meant that for about sixty yards they walked with one shared hand and two separate coats, which was entirely impractical and which neither of them suggested stopping, and it cost them both a small amount of dignity at the top of the hill, where the path opened out onto the grass between the cabins and there were three people standing around the Cabin Three porch in the lamplight doing nothing in particular.

Those three turned out to be Neve, a girl with apple juice down one sleeve, and a nine-year-old called Danny who was asleep standing up against the porch post with an apple in both hands. Katie's six had not gone to bed. Clarisse La Rue sat on the porch rail in her coat with the winter list on her knee and a pen she was not using.

"Road's closed," Clarisse said.

"Since when."

"Since now. Since I decided it. You've both got pomace on you and the step's been swept." She did not look up from the page. "Say goodnight. There's a queue behind you."

"There's no queue. There's three people."

"There's three and one of them's asleep." Clarisse raised her chin at Danny, and then, for the first time, looked at the two of them standing at the bottom of the steps with one hand each and their coats full of fruit, and her face did the thing it did in the press house, which was to be entirely without any expression for slightly too long and then to move on. "Well?" she said. "Hurry up. It's cold and I've got a page."

So Percy took an apple out of his right-hand pocket, because it was the only one he had left and it was a good one, and put it in Annabeth Grace's left-hand pocket, one at a time, with two fingers, sideways, the way a person reloads a weapon, and said, "Row four's got a scab on it. Try it."

And Annabeth, who had not been surprised by anything at this camp in four months, took it out, looked at it, put it back, and said, "I'll weigh it," and turned and went up the steps of Cabin One, and at the top she did not turn around, because she had done that the week before and was not going to do it every time, and because her hand went to her pocket instead, and because that was enough.

Percy waited until the lamp in the Cabin One window came on, which took about a minute and which he was not going to admit he waited for, and then he turned around, and Neve said, "You've got a bruise on your coat," and Clarisse said, "He's had a bruise on his coat since nine o'clock and he's proud of it, go to bed," and the two of them went off up the path dragging Danny between them in the manner of a length of lumber, and the tenth lantern burned on its hook by the Cabin Three door with the door open behind it and the stove-light coming out through the gap onto the boards.

Inside, the cabin was doing the thing it did at the end of a day, which was to be quiet with four people's breathing in it and a clock that nobody had wound and a smell of apples and woodsmoke and the particular cold-metal smell that a stove has when it has been fed hard all day and is now thinking about stopping.

Silena had gone up an hour ago. The tin was on the counter with a cloth over it and a weight on the cloth, and beside the tin there was a plate with two pieces of cake on it and a note propped against the kettle in her handwriting that said, *ONE FOR THE ROAD, ONE FOR THE MORNING. DON'T TELL CLARISSE THE SECOND ONE EXISTS.*

The crate from the porch stood by the table, and Percy emptied his pockets into it, nine apples, one at a time, counting under his breath in the voice you used when you were counting for yourself and not performing for a room, and every fourth one he turned over in his fingers before he let it go, and two of them he put on the counter next to the cloth instead, and neither of those two was the bruise.

Clarisse came in at ten past eight and looked at the crate and then at him and did the arithmetic that a person does when they have been sitting on a rail with a list and a pen they were not using and had in fact been counting apples out of two coats the entire time.

"You're one short," she said.

"I'm one short."

"I put four in."

"Four went in and one went out, and the one that went out is in a cabin up the hill in a coat pocket, and it's got a scab on it, and it's the best one I picked all day." Percy put the cloth over the tin and put the weight on the cloth. "Row four. High up. I had to use the pole."

Clarisse folded her arms and considered Percy's answer. Row four was hers. She had climbed it twice that day, checked the wind through the branches, and picked the fruit that would survive storing. She also knew which apple he'd given away. It had been the one with a small scab on the skin and the sweetness that made up for it.

"Did you tell her about the bruise?" she asked.

"It's an apple, Clarisse."

"Apples go soft where they're bruised. That one won't keep until winter."

"I told her to eat it tomorrow."

"Then you did the sensible thing by accident."

Percy took the cloth off the tin, checking that the remaining apples had enough space between them. The crate would have to go to the dry shelf in the morning. He reached for it, but Clarisse stopped him with one hand.

"Not tonight. The bottom's damp from the field."

"Then we'll change it tomorrow."

"We will."

She moved the crate clear of the cold window and wrote SHELF, MORNING in the margin of her list. Percy watched her pencil travel across the page and understood that she wasn't angry about the missing apple. She was making sure the others wouldn't go to waste.

"Good row," he said.

Clarisse glanced at him. "Best on the hill."

"You would say that."

"I picked it. I'm allowed."

She carried the list upstairs without looking back. Percy replaced the cloth, left the crate where she'd moved it, and went to wash the sticky sweetness off his hands. The apple up the hill would be eaten before it spoiled. The ones on the shelf would have a better chance of lasting. Clarisse, who hated losing useful things, had managed to arrange for both.
