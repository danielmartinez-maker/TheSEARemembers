## Chapter 171: Loud, Off-Key, and Ours

The planned fire had been on the slate board by the dining pavilion steps since the second week of August, tacked up by somebody with three thumbtacks and no name, and by the last Friday of September it had acquired, in colored chalk, four separate amendments and a small crowd of campers who touched it every time they walked past, the way people touch a bruise to see if it still hurts.

Percy Jackson stood in the middle of the amphitheater with a bench on his shoulder and his opinions about music held strictly to himself.

The stone tiers rose around him in their old worn rows, carved in places with the symbols nobody at camp could translate anymore, and the fire bowl at the bottom had been swept and lined with fresh sand. Beyond the bowl the meadow ran down to the canoe lake, flat and green-gold in the late afternoon, the far shore sharp enough to count the pines on it. Past the far shore, granite. Past the granite, more granite. September had turned the whole valley the color of a held breath, and the air carried the thin edge it had been carrying since the cold snap on Tuesday, which had sent everybody to their trunk for a jacket and then remembered it was only September and let go again.

"Third row," said Annabeth Grace, who had come down the meadow path ten minutes earlier with a pencil behind her ear, graphite on her left cuff, and the particular expression of a girl who had made a list and intended to survive it. "Facing the bowl, angled left, so the fire sits on the audience's right and nobody gets smoke in their eyes. The other two go at the ends, not the sides. Traffic."

"Traffic," Percy repeated. He set the bench down. "Since when do we have traffic at a campfire."

"Since you decided the whole camp was invited."

"I didn't decide that. Chiron wrote it on the slate. In chalk. In blue."

"Chiron wrote what you told him to write, and you told him to write what Silena said, and Silena said it to Clarisse, and Clarisse said it to me on the porch Thursday while she was sharpening a spear and trying to look innocent." Annabeth walked past him toward the woodpile, checking the bench's angle with her boot. "You're running a music night, Percy. Whole camp. All the cabins. That's not a fire, that's a production, and productions have producers, and you're the producer."

"I'm fourteen."

"You're fourteen and you organized the amphitheater." She turned. Sun at the edge of her hair, gray eyes level, the lake green-gold behind her down the slope of the meadow. "Admit it."

"I organized a bench."

"You organized," Annabeth said, "three benches and a fire and a sign-up sheet, and then you stood in the middle of it looking pleased, which is why I can always tell."

He had been standing in the middle of it looking pleased. He put his hands in his pockets, which was how he admitted things.

They had argued the event into shape over two weeks, the way everything in Cabin Three got argued into shape: Silena proposing, Percy agreeing too fast, Clarisse objecting on principle, Annabeth reducing it to a schedule, Thalia arriving late and taking credit. The season was turning. The lake had gone from a summer place to a September place, cold under the shine, and the canoes would be up on racks by the second week of October. Somebody had said, at the Poseidon table, that the summer had been loud and strange and mostly good, and that it ought to go out the way it had come in, and somebody else had said, well, then, music, and the idea had stuck before anybody could object to it.

Now it was Friday, and the fire was his.

It was not a small thing. The fire, at this camp, in this family, was never a small thing, and Percy had been handed the job the way he got handed everything — by Silena deciding, by Clarisse nodding once, by Annabeth writing his name on the list in her small hard hand where it could not be undone.

"Where's the list?" he said.

She produced it from her back pocket without looking, unfolded it along creases it had acquired from four separate foldings, and read. "Wood quota. Split oak, and Chiron's being difficult about pine because of smoke. Lanterns, two hundred, which is a crime against anyone carrying them. Blankets, twelve, minus the four Tyson took to the forge in August and never brought back, which I keep calling a loan so it doesn't hurt."

"He'll send them back."

"He'll send something back. It won't be towels." She folded the list in half, then in half again, then apparently thought better of it and opened it back up. "Sign-ups. Grover with pipes. Apollo cabin doing a three-part, which they claim is under control. Thalia. Me, apparently, which is an error somebody will pay for."

"You're not singing." Percy set the second bench. "You told me on the dock in August that you can't sing."

"I can't sing. I'm on the list as an apology note in advance." She tucked the pencil behind her ear. "Clarisse wrote no and then drew a line through the no. Silena wrote one song, then crossed it out, then wrote it again twice, which means one song and a lot of nerve."

"What about me?"

"Fire." She tapped the paper. "You light it. You feed it. You keep it from eating the meadow, which is the most important job tonight and the only person who doesn't know it is the person holding it."

"Everybody knows it except the guy holding it."

"You hum."

He stopped with a bench in both hands. "I don't hum."

"You hum when you're polishing Riptide. I've heard you through the wall. You do it when you're thinking, and you do it louder when you're worried, which is a tell, and one of these days somebody is going to use it against you." She was already walking, heading back up the rows, and she delivered it over her shoulder in the flat voice of a girl giving a bearing rather than a tease. "Also you're tone-deaf, so it hardly matters."

"I'm not tone-deaf. I'm selective."

"Selective is the word people use for tone-deaf."

Percy set the bench down and let it go, because arguing with Annabeth Grace about music was like arguing with the lake about being wet, and because she was smiling, and because she had stopped at the top of the stone rows with the sun behind her and the meadow going down to green-gold water, and the whole camp had arranged itself for a moment without asking his permission, the way it did sometimes, all at once.

Two Hermes kids he didn't know well came past carrying a bench between them and told him the Apollo cabin had claimed the third row, and he told them the third row belonged to whoever carried it, which they seemed to accept as law.

"Percy." Annabeth had come back down the rows with a rolled paper under her arm, and she held it out. He took it and unrolled a corner and found a sky.

It was a chart in her small hard pencil: the hill, the meadow, the arc of the lake shore, and over all of it a scatter of dots and labels and one shaded band running northeast to southwest. He knew the hand from a hundred maps. He knew the subject from three weeks of her dragging him out of the pavilion after dinner with a notebook under her arm.

"Stars," he said.

"Stargazing. Tomorrow night." She watched his face, precise and planning and quietly, carefully pleased with herself. "Chiron approved it an hour ago. Silena's drawing up a blanket inventory. Clarisse says she's not coming, and then she'll be the first one up the hill with a thermos."

"Tomorrow night," Percy said. "The overnight."

"The overnight." She let the word sit there, the agreed word, the one that had been circling the edges of conversations for a week — passed down the Poseidon table at breakfast, across the forge, out to the boundary line and back. It was late September. The canoes were going up in two weeks. After tomorrow the season would begin closing the way doors close at the end of a long evening, not all at once, but definitely, and the whole camp had decided without a vote that it would not spend the last warm weekend indoors. "We sleep out. Up on the ridge above the spring, where Thalia found the water. Small fire, large blanket pile, and we stay up until we're too cold to keep arguing. Breakfast at dawn, which Silena will describe as a reasonable hour, and Silena will be wrong."

"That's the best thing that's going to happen this year."

"You say that about lunch, too."

"Lunch is frequently excellent." He rolled the chart again, careful with the creases, and gave it back. "Can I bring Riptide? Not to use. Just to own. For the stars' sake."

"No swords at a stargazing night. I just made that a rule."

"That's not even a real rule."

"Rules I make count double, because I'm right more often." She was heading for the woodpile, brisk, when Thalia Grace came down the meadow path and announced herself the way weather announces itself, loudly and with no regard for anyone's schedule.

"— and I'm telling you, if I hear one more hymn about the sun I'm going to go live with the satyrs, and that's not a threat, that's a career advancement." Thalia was eighteen, dusty, in cargo pants and her camp shirt with the gray flannel tied around her waist by the sleeves, boots unlaced, black hair full of pine needles. She had a canteen in one hand and a lute in the other, and she had plainly taken the lute from somewhere.

"That's not yours," Percy said.

"It was abandoned. It was leaning against the pavilion doing nothing with its life for two months." Thalia dropped onto the bench he had just set, dead center, and struck a chord that was approximately a chord. "Tonight it's a career."

"You can't play lute."

"I can play lute badly." She struck it again, louder. "There's a difference. Badly, loudly, and with commitment. My sister taught me that."

"Your sister is standing right there and says you're wrong."

"Annabeth says I'm wrong about everything and she's usually right, which is exactly why I don't stop." Thalia hooked an arm around Annabeth's shoulders when Annabeth came close enough and pulled her down onto the bench, and the two of them sat with their foreheads tipped together for half a second, sisters in late light, and then Thalia spotted the rolled chart and made a delighted noise. "Oh. Have we broken the news? Percy, has my genius sister informed you of the overnight?"

"She has."

"Then we're doomed, and it's going to be cold, and there'll be exactly one blanket between us, and someone will have to share." Thalia said it with enormous significance and then ruined it by adding, "My vote is that Jackson curls up like a distressed seashell and Annabeth gets the good half, because she's taller uncurled."

"I do not curl up."

"You curl up like a shrimp. It's adorable. Everybody has noticed." She stood, slung the lute across her back, and looked down the meadow toward the lake with the expression she got when she was counting something. "The fire isn't lit. Percy. The fire. You've got the fire, right?"

"I've got the fire."

"Then it's a good Friday." She started up the tiers toward the bowl, strumming one wrong note every third step, and Annabeth watched her go and made the small face she made when Thalia was being more tender than she was admitting.

At the bottom of the rows, Chiron had arrived in his wheelchair with a lantern in his lap, checking the sand in the bowl with one finger, and had fallen into conversation with Mr. D, who had appeared with two bags of marshmallows and a declaration that he would remain for exactly twenty minutes. Percy had heard that declaration before and knew precisely what it was worth. Somebody on the crafts table had started a rumor that the tally in Chiron's office would find its fortieth mark before October, and Annabeth had given the rumor the flat stare she gave bad engineering, and the panel sat in its office quietly saying thirty-nine.

Clarisse came down the path from the cabins with a jacket over her camp shirt and her braid redone for the evening, and Percy raised a hand and got a grunt, and he noticed, because he noticed things, that she was carrying a folded paper, and that she was carrying it in the hand she used when she did not want anybody to see what was in it.

"Clarisse!" Thalia called down the tiers. "Are you on the list? Genuinely? Because I want to write it down while I'm winning."

"Shut up, Grace," Clarisse said, and put the folded paper in her jacket pocket, and kept walking.

---

Silena Beauregard was folding blankets in Cabin Three when she decided, without hurry and without much drama, that tonight she was going to sing.

She had been thinking about it for two weeks, the way she thought about most things: sideways, while her hands did something else. Blankets were what her hands were doing. Nine of them, folded in thirds, stacked on the bottom bunk where the quilt normally slept, each one shaken out and counted and smoothed flat so it would not hurt anyone. She hummed while she worked. She always hummed while she worked. The hum had been her voice for a long time — at the washbasin, on patrol, in the kitchen at six in the morning with flour on her forearm — and she had been, for a lot of her life, entirely comfortable with that.

The thing about a hum was that nobody could ask you to sing the rest.

Outside, the amphitheater was filling up. She could hear it from here: the drift of voices, the scrape of benches on stone, somebody's reed pipes warming up two notes at a time, the Apollo cabin's three-part murmur going up and down as they found their shape. In two hours the whole camp would be down there in the dark with the fire burning, and she would be standing up, and everybody would be looking at her, and she would open her mouth and find out whether she still remembered how.

She had done it once. New Year's Eve, in January, in the January cold, standing in the amphitheater with Clarisse's arm around her waist for ballast. One song, all the way through, where people could hear. She remembered the first note shaking. She remembered the second line steadying. She remembered Clarisse crying openly with pride all over her fierce face, and Grover swaying with his eyes shut, and the silence afterward that had lasted three heartbeats and then broken into applause like weather.

She had not done it since.

Not once in nine months. She had hummed. She had sung at dishwater volume while she braided Annabeth's hair before the solstice dance. That was it. The resolution had been real when she made it, and it was real now, and it had not gotten any less frightening with practice, and Silena had learned enough about herself by sixteen to know that this was simply how she was built: the wanting and the fear arrived together, and neither one waited for the other.

"Bag's wet," said Clarisse, coming in.

She came in barefoot with her boots in one hand, her jacket over her arm, her braid dark with lake water, and she was pink from the cold and pretending she wasn't. Silena looked up from the blankets and made the assessment she always made, quick and warm and mostly accurate: her sister was rattled. Clarisse was fifteen, blunt as a cleaver, and the only person at camp who could not lie to her at all, and she had just come back from the lake with a jacket over her arm because the pocket of the jacket was full.

"Lake went in?" Silena said.

"Just the shallows. Jackson's scrubbing the last canoe." Clarisse set her boots by the door, and Silena watched her take the folded paper out of her jacket pocket, and watch it for a second, and put it in the front pocket of her camp shirt instead, close to her ribs.

Silena kept folding. "Silena has decided that tonight you're on the list."

"Silena has decided that tonight she's on the list. I'm not."

"Yes, you are."

"No." Clarisse sat down hard on the bottom bunk, on the wrong bunk, the one that was hers by seniority and by violence, and picked up the stack of folded blankets she had just demolished and put them back on the other bunk. "I don't do this. I do spear. I do volleyball. I do the wood. That's the arrangement. That's been the arrangement since New Year's."

"New Year's was the arrangement since New Year's," Silena said mildly. "It's September. Arrangements are seasonal."

"Silena."

"Clarisse." She smoothed the last blanket, set it on the stack, and sat down on the floor beside the bunk with her back against the frame, which was where the two of them had had every important conversation they had ever had. "You wrote something."

Clarisse went very still.

"You always write in the forge, because the tick of the coals covers the sound of you thinking, and you always come back from the lake when you're asking yourself something you don't want to ask, and you have a piece of paper on you right now that you have been carrying for four days." Silena pulled her knees up and rested her arms on them, warm and unhurried, sixteen and perceptive and completely without apology for either. "I'm not going to read it. I would never read it. But I know it's there."

"It's stupid," Clarisse said, to the wall.

"It's a poem."

"Everything's a poem with you."

"With me, yes. With you, only when you've got something." Silena tipped her head back against the bunk frame. "Are you going to read it tonight?"

"No."

"Because you think they'll laugh?"

"Because it's — " Clarisse stopped, and her jaw worked, and when she spoke again the bluntness had gone thin over something that was not blunt at all. "Because it's about Jackson."

Silena waited. She had five Septembers of practice at waiting.

"It's not for them." Clarisse's hands were flat on her knees, knuckles up, the way they got when she was holding something. "It's for him. It's — it's about the cabin, and the lake, and how he came in here two years ago talking to the water like it owed him money, and now I can't hear the lake without hearing him. And if I read that out loud in front of forty campers he'll go red, and then he'll be nice about it, because he's nice about everything, and then it'll be weird."

"Will it?"

"Yes."

"Percy Jackson has never once been weird with you." Silena picked a thread off her sleeve. "Not when you taught him to keep a spear. Not when you put your jacket over him when he fell asleep on the lake shore. Not when you counted him every single day for a year and then had to stop."

Clarisse made a sound that was not a word.

"And anyway," Silena said, "you are not the only one doing this tonight. I've got a song and I've been sick about it since Tuesday, and you have not noticed, which tells me you've been busy. So. We'll both be terrible. We'll be terrible in front of everybody, together, the way Cabin Three does everything."

"You're not going to be terrible," Clarisse said. "You're going to be good, and they're going to feel it, and it's going to be fine, and I'll hit anyone who says otherwise."

"That's a promise I'm keeping." Silena stood, and gathered the blankets against her chest, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame. "Clarisse."

"What."

"Does he know?"

"No." Clarisse's face did the thing it did. "Nobody knows. Except — " She stopped. "Except maybe Annabeth. She saw me with it Thursday on the porch."

Silena smiled, small and warm and entirely unsurprised. "Then it's already out. Annabeth Grace doesn't keep secrets, she schedules them." She shifted the blankets. "Bring your boots. And bring the paper. I'm not saying you have to read it. I'm saying it'd be a shame to carry it up the hill and then carry it home again."

---

She took the long way to the lake, which was a thing she did when she had something in her pocket.

The path down from the cabins ran behind the forge and then opened onto the north beach, and the beach was quiet, because the whole camp had decided that the last Friday of September would be spent either preparing for the amphitheater or pretending it wasn't happening. The pines stood back from the sand in their usual line. The lake lay out flat and green-gold toward the far shore where the granite ridge rose up behind the trees, and the afternoon light had gone long and low across it, and the whole water was moving, a little, always, in the way it had when it wasn't wind at all but the lake remembering something.

Percy was out in it.

Waist-deep off the flat rock with a scrub brush in one hand and a bucket between his feet, sleeves of his camp shirt cut short, jeans rolled to the knee and dark with wet, hair gone salt-stiff and curling. He had been scrubbing the last canoe for an hour. He had volunteered for it without telling anyone why, the way he volunteered for things now, which was: because he had been a boy who left, and he was still, every day, learning how to be a boy who stayed. He was humming. Off-key. Under his breath, the way a person hums when they think nobody is listening, and he was wrong, because she was listening.

She stood in the pines with her folded paper in her jacket pocket and let the sight of him sit in her chest the way it had sat in her all summer: with a small amount of aggravation, a large amount of pride, and no room to spare for either.

The paper had four drafts on it. Three were in the notebook she kept behind the quench barrel in the forge, where the coals ticked and nobody came and she could cross out whole lives without explaining them. This was the fourth. She had written the first line of it three weeks ago at the forge table with her teeth clamped around a pencil eraser, and she had not known then what she was writing, and she knew now, which was worse.

Observations with line breaks, she had told Percy once, in the forge, when he found out. Not poems. Observations.

These were not observations anymore.

The truth of it was this, and she had worked it out standing where she was standing now, three different times: siblings were the terrifying thing. Not monsters. Not quests. Not the boundary line, which was only stones and could be walked in four days. Siblings were the terrifying thing because they were forever, and forever had a middle in it, an endless middle of ordinary days in which everything that had happened today would slowly become something nobody mentioned. She had watched people drift. She knew how it went. Three years of something, and then a shrug, and you were strangers with shared initials.

Clarisse La Rue was fifteen, and she was not going to let it happen by accident, and she had written forty-four lines of plain speech to nail down something she could not otherwise hold, and she had been carrying it for four days without being able to hand it to the person it was for, because she was Clarisse, and she was frightened of exactly one thing, and it was this.

"Annabeth's coming down the path," said Annabeth, coming down the path.

Clarisse jumped, and would have died before admitting it. "You walk like a heron."

"You're standing in the pines like a trap." Annabeth arrived with her field book under her arm, a stake bundle in one hand, and a tape measure clipped to her belt, and she stopped when she saw the look on Clarisse's face, and her precise expression went careful. "Are you all right?"

"I'm fine."

"Fine is what people say." Annabeth set the stakes down against a pine. "You've been doing the thing with your pocket."

"It's my pocket."

"It's a pocket with a secret in it." Annabeth did not push. She was good at not pushing; she was, Clarisse had decided some months ago, a Grace sister and therefore a person of tactics. "The overnight site's up the ridge above Thalia's spring. Chiron wants the fire ring outside the tree line so the pines don't drop sap on the blankets, and he wants the path checked by one of us who doesn't fly. I volunteered. Percy's scrubbing and won't stop until he runs out of canoes, which at this rate is never."

"He always does the last one," Clarisse said. "The last one. It's like he's afraid somebody will take the last thing away."

Annabeth looked out at the water and did not say anything for a second, and then said, quietly, "I know."

"He's an idiot."

"He's yours," Annabeth said. "He's all of ours."

Clarisse's hand closed on the paper in her pocket. "Grace," she said, "you want to read something."

"I want to read something," Annabeth agreed, instantly, without hesitation, which was the most Annabeth possible answer and the reason Clarisse had picked her and not anybody else. "Is this about the pocket?"

"This is about the pocket."

"Then take it out of the pocket."

Clarisse took it out. She looked at it a second with the sun going rose-gold on the creases, and then she handed it over handle-first, like a weapon, which was the only way she knew how to give anything.

Annabeth read. She read standing up in the sand with her shadow going long in front of her, and she read the whole thing through without skipping, which Clarisse had not expected, and then she started at the top and read it again, quieter, moving her lips on the short lines, and Clarisse stood in the pines and watched her do it and experienced, in full, the sensation of being flayed.

Finally Annabeth lowered the paper. "You're good," she said.

"I'm fine."

"Clarisse."

"Don't."

"I said you're good." Annabeth's gray eyes came up, clear and exact, with the ferocity she used for blueprints and bad arguments. "This is good. The stanza about the ankle is the truest thing I've read since Percy's last note, and I read his last note four times on the dock in August, so that is a high standard."

Clarisse felt her ears go hot. "Which stanza do I cut?"

"None of them." Annabeth tapped the paper against her palm. "Third one's a bit long and you know it. The rhyme on insufferable and week is doing the work of a better word. And the last four lines are the ones you're afraid of, which is how I know they're right, because you only get nervous about things that are exactly true." She handed the paper back. "Read it tonight."

"No."

"Clarisse."

"I said no. I'm doing wood. I'm the wood."

"You're the wood in the same way Silena is the dishes." Annabeth was already picking her stakes back up. "He doesn't know."

"Nobody knows."

"Then it's a surprise." Annabeth shouldered the stakes. "He'll be embarrassed for about eleven seconds and moved for the rest of his life, and then he'll be unbearable at breakfast, and you will have made something permanent out of a middle. That's not nothing. That's the whole job."

"I didn't ask for the job."

"I've never once seen you do anything you didn't ask for." Annabeth started up the beach toward the ridge path, and paused, and looked back. "The fourth line of the last stanza. Nobody tells him to do that. Read it out loud to a hundred people, Clarisse. Somebody should tell him."

She went up the path, and Clarisse stood in the pines with the paper warm from Annabeth's hand, and realized, with a distinct and personal hatred, that she was going to do it.

"Tragic," said Thalia Grace, from directly behind her. "Deeply tragic. I have never seen a war hero reduced to such a state by a piece of notebook paper."

"Are you going to tell anybody you eavesdrop," Clarisse said, "or is it a secret you take to the grave?"

"It's a talent. It's called being interesting." Thalia came out of the pines with her lute under her arm and a grin wide enough to be illegal, and she had clearly heard enough to be entirely certain of her ground. "So that's it. That's the poem."

"You don't know about a poem."

"I know about a poem, because I just heard the word stanza, and Clarisse La Rue only uses the word stanza when somebody is being precise at her, which she is right now, because she just got her feelings destroyed by my little sister." Thalia held out a hand. "Let me see."

"You'll show the whole camp."

"I'll show the whole camp if you don't read it at the fire, and then you'll have to live with that instead." Thalia wiggled her fingers. "Hand it over, Cabin Three. That's an order from somebody who loves you, and you'll have to guess which part is which, because I'm not explaining myself."

Clarisse handed it over. She told herself it was because Thalia would find it eventually, which was true.

Thalia read it standing in the sand with the lute under her arm, and her face did the thing Clarisse had been dreading and hoping for at the same time: it softened, and it went eighteen-years-old and honest, and she looked up at the end with her electric eyes bright and wet at the corners and no trace of a joke anywhere in her.

"Oh," said Thalia Grace, daughter of Zeus, the loudest person at camp, in a voice that was barely carrying. "Oh, Clarisse."

"Don't."

"It's about him."

"It's about the cabin."

"It's about him." Thalia folded the paper along the creases with more care than her looting hands had any right to, and gave it back. "And here's what's going to happen. You are reading it. I am going first, and I am going to be loud and off-key and entirely committed, and when I finish I am going to stand at the front of the amphitheater and announce you by name, and then nobody will be able to leave, because Thalia Grace will be standing in front of the fire with a lute and no shame."

"You're not playing that thing."

"I am playing that thing. Badly. Loudly." Thalia was already walking backward up the path, delighted, the whole performance arranging itself in her head. "You'll go third. Before the fire burns down, while they're still warm and before Mr. D goes home and takes the good cheer with him. Silena has you on the list, by the way. Third. Between Grover and me. We all voted."

"Nobody votes in Cabin Three."

"Silena votes. Silena's always voted." Thalia's shout came back down the path as she went, faint and electric and affectionate, the way thunder is affectionate at a distance. "Bring the paper, Clarisse! I'm not done loving this!"

Clarisse stood alone in the rose-gold light with the paper in her hand and the lake moving a little, always, in the way it moved when it wasn't wind. Out on the flat rock Percy set the brush in the bucket and stood up straight and shaded his eyes toward the shore, and she wanted, with a suddenness that frightened her more than the amphitheater did, to say something across the water.

"Don't make it weird," Clarisse told herself, and he couldn't hear her, which was the only reason it was bearable.

"Jackson!"

He cupped a hand to his ear. "What?"

"Get out of the lake. You're the fire. It's almost dark." She waited until he raised both hands in surrender, and then she turned for the path with the paper folded into her inside pocket and pat it flat over her own heart with her own palm, and walked up toward the amphitheater with the fire unlit and forty-seven lines of plain words in her shirt, and the belief — she knew it was wrong; she believed it anyway — that if she read them out loud, the middle would hold.

---

By the time the light went, the amphitheater was full.

The amphitheater was the oldest stone at camp and nobody knew who had cut it: broad tiers rising out of the meadow's edge in twelve ranks, weathered and worn smooth in places by seventy years of campers' backsides, and down at the bottom the fire bowl, a shallow ring of fieldstone packed with clean sand, with the whole lake laid out below it and the pines standing in a dark ring behind the topmost row. Above the meadow the sky went from gold to rose to the deep blue that comes early in late September, and the first stars came out over the granite ridge, faint and then brave.

Percy stood at the bowl with the split oak stacked beside him and the whole camp arriving down the path, and did the job he had been given.

He did it the way he did everything that mattered. He laid the kindling in a log cabin, four walls and a heart of pine shavings and a curl of birch bark that Thalia had stolen from Tyson's split pile and declared gossip-catching, which was a thing that did not matter and mattered anyway. He set two logs against it and left room for air, because the lake had taught him that much about fire: it wanted a way in as much as it wanted food. Then he knelt, struck the match on the stone where Clarisse had shown him, and held it to the bark.

The flame took. Small at first, orange and uncertain, then running up the shavings and catching in the resin, and then the logs were talking, and the meadow filled with the smell of it, warm dust and oak and September, and the whole turning crowd — Annabeth had counted; nobody had needed to — turned their faces toward the light the way people do.

Percy sat back on his heels and let the heat come up at him and watched the fire find its shape. He had not expected this to feel like a big deal. It was a fire. He was the guy with the matches. But the amphitheater was warm and loud and full, and the lake was silver below him, and the fire he had laid with his own hands was standing up straight and throwing sparks over the heads of everyone he had, and he felt, in his chest, the specific and enormous pleasure of having made the warmth that other people were standing in.

"Stop looking pleased," said Annabeth. "You're feeding it your own head."

"I'm not looking pleased."

"You're looking like a cat on a radiator."

She had come down while he was watching the fire, and she had brought two blankets, and she had been sitting beside him on the stone step long enough for him to notice without looking, which was a way they had of knowing each other: she always took the step below him, an inch lower, so that their shoulders fit.

"One of these is for the overnight," she said, handing him nothing, because she was keeping them.

"One of these is for us."

"That's the same thing when it's the same night." She shook one out over both their laps and tucked the other, folded, behind her back for later, and they settled, and the blanket came up warm on both sides, and her knee found his knee under the wool with the unerring aim of long practice. "You laid the fire good."

"Fire's a professional. Ten minutes per occurrence, free for amphitheaters."

"You stole that from Clarisse."

"I gave it to her and she improved it and I took it back." He leaned sideways so their shoulders slotted, and she leaned back, and her head came to rest on his shoulder with the exact weight he had been carrying around all day without knowing. He put his arm around her under the blanket, careful with the wool, and she took his free hand and laced their fingers together in the dark, cold and warm, and they sat and watched the camp be warm.

It was a good place to be fourteen. The fire was loud and opinionated and throwing off sparks that went up and vanished. The lake was below, black and silver. Above the top row of stone, the pines were black on a sky going the color of ink. Somebody in the back row was already toasting a marshmallow past the point of decency. Clarisse sat two ranks up and to the left with Silena beside her, boots drawn up, arms around her knees, jacket on, and she was watching the fire rather than the people, and she was the only camper present who could sit at a party and look like she had been stationed there by herself.

"So," said Annabeth low, into his shoulder, "who's actually going up?"

"Silena says there's a list."

"Silena says there's a list, and Silena wrote the list, and Silena wrote herself on the list last so nobody would back out first." Her voice was quiet and dry and fond, the way it got when she was admiring somebody and hiding it behind logistics. "She's done this for two weeks. She hums the order of events while she braids."

"How do you know?"

"She hummed it to me Thursday while she was doing my braid, and it went: Grover, Apollo cabin, Thalia, Clarisse, me-as-a-joke, Silena, and a Jackson-something-or-other, and she ran out of breath on the last one and called it a schedule." Annabeth traced something with her thumb on the back of his hand. "The something-or-other is you."

"I'm not on the list."

"You're on the list. She told me yesterday she has no idea what it is, but that you are on it, and that you will do it, and that nobody will let you out of it." She was smiling. He could hear it. "Also, Clarisse's on it. She wrote no and drew a line through it."

"Clarisse doesn't sing."

"Clarisse isn't singing." Annabeth was quiet a moment, and turned her head just enough that her lips were near his ear, so the rest of the camp couldn't hear it. "I read something in the sand on the north beach an hour ago. Don't tell her I told you. Just — if she goes up there tonight, you be the guy who makes it easy."

"I'm always the guy who makes it easy."

"I know," Annabeth said. "That's why I told you."

Chiron stood up at the front of the bowl with his lantern and did not say anything at all for a second, and the camp, which was howling, settled by itself, because that was what happened when Chiron waited. He welcomed them in three sentences, thanked Silena twice by name, called the fire the last of the season with the lightness of a man who knew that being reminded of endings was the price of being reminded of things, and then he sat down on a cushion somebody had provided, with the air of a centaur who had completed his duties and intended to enjoy himself.

Silena stood instead. She had changed into a soft gray sweater over her camp shirt with her hair down, and she had the sign-up sheet in one hand, and she held it up so the whole camp could see it and did not open it, and the whole camp groaned, and she beamed.

"Rules," she said. Her voice carried without effort, warm and serene, the voice of a girl who had fed two hundred people breakfast and would feed them breakfast again. "One: nobody leaves. Two: if somebody's bad, we cheer louder, which is a scientific fact I have proved at a stove. Three: nobody performs twice, which means I'm watching you, Thalia. Four — " She looked down the rows, at the faces turning up toward her in the firelight, and her warmth did something complicated. "Four: this is the last fire of the season, and I want everybody to remember that we chose to be here. It's September. It'll be cold by Tuesday. The canoes are going up on the racks. And tonight we are all standing in the dark together being loudly bad at things, and in twenty years, when somebody asks you about this summer, that is the thing you are going to say. You'll say, I was there, and it was cold, and I was off-key, and I was not alone." She paused, and the fire popped, and she smiled at it. "Also, tomorrow night we sleep outside, so save your voices if you're smart. Nobody ever is. Grover, you're first."

Grover was on his feet before anybody could lose their nerve, horns poking through his wool hat, reed pipes in both hands, and he was so nervous his goat legs were trembling, and he closed his eyes, and he started to play.

Percy had heard Grover play before. He had heard it in the meadow in March, when the pipes were a search, and on the boundary in July, when the pipes had been a warning. This was neither. This was the music the satyrs made when nothing at all was wrong: a low, skipping, complicated thing that did not have a shape so much as a mood, reeds over reeds, a melody that kept almost arriving and then wandering off to look at a flower. The firelight moved differently with it. Halfway up the tiers, a satyr kid he didn't know started a step that turned into another step, and then a dryad with leaves in her hair, who had come down with Juniper, stood up and began to dance in the sand at the edge of the firelight, and by the third verse the whole left side of the amphitheater was moving, and the pipes were not a performance anymore, they were a weather system.

When Grover finished, the pipes went on playing, and the sound of two hundred campers stamping the rhythm out on the stone tiers came up out of the dark like a second drum.

"Told you," Annabeth said, quiet and delighted, and stamped her boot twice, and Percy stamped with her.

The Apollo cabin went up next, three of them, and they did a hymn to the setting sun in three-part harmony that was old and slow and very long, and one of the boys, in the middle, went flat for four bars and then found his way back on pure nerve, and when they finished the whole camp cheered, and the boy sat down and hid his face in his hands while his sisters pounded his back, and he was, until the end of his life, going to remember that this camp cheered loudest for the bar he got wrong, because that was the kind of camp it was.

Thalia went up last of the early ones, with the lute, and she took her time about it, and when she started she was, in fact, terrible.

She played with great commitment and no relation to the strings. She sang a marching song she had learned from somebody, loud, off-key, louder on the choruses, the words arriving half a beat ahead of the tune like a rumor outrunning a town. She was eighteen and a daughter of Zeus and she stood in front of the fire with sparks going up in her hair, and she was having the time of her life, and by the third line the whole amphitheater was singing with her, badly, and nobody cared, and the sound went out over the meadow and across the lake and came back off the granite in the wrong key, which Thalia took as a compliment.

"She's the loudest person alive," Annabeth said into his shoulder, warm and watching. "She's showing off for everybody. She's always shown off for everybody. In January she did the same thing with the resolutions, and she pretended she was joking, and she wasn't."

"She's good, though," Percy said.

"She's bad," Annabeth said. "She's so bad. That's the point of her." And then, very quietly, in a voice that had gone past fond into the place where she kept the things she was too exact to say out loud: "I used to think she was going to leave and not come back, every summer, all summer, for years. And now she's standing there in front of a hundred people being the worst thing I have ever heard, and I can't — "

"I know," he said.

"I know you know."

They held hands under the blanket and listened, and the camp howled the chorus at the lake, off-key and enormous, and the fire stood up and threw its sparks over all of it, and the stars thickened over the granite ridge, and the night arranged itself around that sound the way water arranges itself around a stone.

Thalia finished by striking a final chord that missed entirely, and took a bow that almost went over into the fire bowl, and sat down hard beside Annabeth with her face flushed and her eyes bright, and Annabeth put her arm around her sister without being asked, and Thalia leaned into it, one second, the way a person leans into shelter they had built for somebody else.

"Off-key," Thalia said, breathless. "Did you hear how off-key?"

"Nobody could hear anything but how off-key," Annabeth said.

"Somebody tell me I'm good."

"You're not good."

"Don't care." Thalia took the mug Silena pressed on her, drank deep, and grinned into it, and then her hand found Annabeth's knee under the blanket and patted it twice, and her chin dropped onto Annabeth's shoulder, and the whole amphitheater, from the top row to the fire ring, sat warm and let the fire settle into its second hour.

Percy held on to his girl and looked at the fire and waited for Clarisse, who was, he could see, waiting for herself.

---

Annabeth had read the poem twice in the sand on the north beach, and she had thought about very little else for an hour.

It was not that she was a person who thought about poems. It was that the poem was about somebody she was also thinking about constantly, and that the person who had written it was somebody she had watched for five years build a fortress out of bluntness and then, slowly, begin to open doors in it. Clarisse La Rue was fifteen, and she was her friend, not her sister, and she had been standing on the beach with her shadow going long in front of her, and she had handed over a folded piece of paper with her ears bright red, and she had said nothing at all.

Some things did not need anniversaries. Some things just needed witnesses.

"You're doing the thinking face," Thalia said. "Out loud. With your whole head."

"I'm thinking."

"About what?"

"About nothing that's any of your business."

"Rude." Thalia had her flannel open over her camp shirt and her boots up on the step below her, and the firelight picked out the hard bones of her face and the soot on one cheek where a spark had found her. She tipped her head toward the front of the bowl, where Silena was telling two Hermes kids to stop wrestling for the marshmallow bag, and dropped her voice without changing her posture. "Is this the one?"

"Yes."

"The Cabin Three thing?"

"Yes."

"Does Jackson know?"

"No." Annabeth smoothed the blanket over her knees, once, the planner's pass over a surface she had already smoothed. "And you're going to announce her, and you're not going to tease her, and you're going to do it warmly, and then you're going to sit down and let her have it."

"I tease warmly," Thalia said, deeply wounded. "I'm warm. I'm the warmest person at this fire. Ask anyone."

"I'm asking you."

Thalia's mouth twitched. She reached over and found Annabeth's knee under the blanket and pressed down once, the way she did, the way she had done beside the map table and in the loft and twenty other places, hard and brief and entirely without apology. Then, to Annabeth's complete surprise, Thalia leaned in and kissed her temple, fast, scratchy with soot, and got up, and went to the front of the bowl.

Annabeth sat very still, hand flat over the place on her knee where her sister's hand had been, throat tight, and understood with the special clarity of people who have loved somebody through the exact thing they are now watching, that Thalia was going to be warm. Thalia was always warm, in the end, in the way a thunderhead is warm, after the lightning has gone somewhere else and the rain is just rain.

She reached sideways under the blanket and found Percy's hand and laced her fingers through his, and felt him squeeze back without being asked, and she did not have to explain. That was the whole thing. That was what she had not had, once, at any age, at any distance, in any dark.

"All right," Thalia Grace said to the whole amphitheater, and the fire cracked and the camp went quiet, and Annabeth's sister stood in front of two hundred people with sparks in her hair and her hands in her pockets like she was not about to do anything remarkable. "Next is Clarisse La Rue. And I'm doing this one for her, because she'll throttle me if I don't, and because she wrote me a note yesterday that said, and I quote, tell them or I will kill you, except she wrote it in all capital letters, which means she was serious."

Laughter, fond and surprised.

"Clarisse writes poems," Thalia said. "She thinks nobody knows. Everybody knows. She hides in the forge with a notebook, she chews pencils to death, she tells people she writes battle logs, and she has been doing it for two years, and she once read one out loud at a bonfire in January, and I have never seen anybody look so much like they were going to throw up and so much like they were going to cry at the same time." Her voice dropped into something lower and completely honest. "She's the bravest person at this camp in a way that doesn't show up in any arena, and she never mentions it, and if anybody laughs at her tonight you answer to me, and Thalia Grace has lightning and no shame."

She sat down. The fire popped.

In the second-rank shadow, Clarisse stood up.

---

Clarisse stood up the way she did everything, hard and without transition, knees straight, shoulders back, boots planted on the stone, and every single thing in her life was screaming at her to sit back down.

The amphitheater was a bowl of firelight and faces. Two hundred campers turned around on the stone tiers, and the ones in the back rows stood to see, and Clarisse's eyes went to them and then to the fire and then, traitorously, to Percy, who was sitting on the second step with Annabeth's blanket over his knees and his face turned up toward her with the exact expression of a person who has no idea what is coming.

He was smiling. He was always smiling at her. That was the trouble with him: he was always, always pleased to see her, and she had gone a long time without being able to afford that, and she could afford it now, and it was the most terrifying thing in the world.

Her hands shook. She took the paper out of her inside pocket and unfolded it, and the creases were deep, and the firelight came off the paper, and she read the title out loud in a voice that had the bottom drop out of it.

"Three Bunks," she said. "It's — it's short. Forty-seven lines. Shut up if you're going to."

Then she read.

She read it low and gruff, stumbling once on the second stanza, and then her voice found the level it had when she was giving orders and it stayed there. The poem went out across the amphitheater in short plain lines, and the fire popped, and the lake was behind her, and Clarisse La Rue did not look up once.

*I had one bunk and no witnesses.
My father gave me a second, then a third,
and now I know how everybody sounds
in the dark in this cabin.*

*The third one hums.*

Laughter went over the tiers — warm, surprised — and she kept going through it.

*He'll deny it. He's lying.
He hums when he's cleaning his sword,
off-key, under his breath,
like it might cut him if it hears.
He talks to the lake like it owes him money.
He sleeps with one arm across his eyes
like the ceiling has plans for him.
He cannot fold a towel. He never could.*

*He came home with a Cyclops for a brother
and was insufferable about it for a week,
and when the biggest one left on the ferry
Jackson waded into this lake
and held his ankle under the cold
till it went warm.*

The amphitheater went quiet. Not a polite quiet. The kind that happens when a thing that was funny stops being funny because it turns out to be true.

*Nobody told him to do that.
Nobody ever tells him to do that.
Nobody tells him to stay, either.
He just stays.
That's the thing about him.
Nobody tells him.*

*The sea is our father's temper.
All distance, all floor, no other side.
This is a lake you can see across.
It's fresh. It's cold in a way that has opinions.
It gives back what you give it:
a stone, a shout, a boy floating on his back,
a brother standing in it to his knees
holding an ankle.*

*I didn't order any of this.
I was the oldest and the loudest and the only one
who had to be right.
Now there are five of us at one fire
and somebody else is always wrong
and I have never been happier,
and if that gets around I will kill you.*

The whole amphitheater laughed. It was not a cruel laugh. It was two hundred people laughing with the whole of their chests at a girl who had just told them, in public, that she was happy, and who had known exactly how hard it would land, and who had done it anyway with a joke in the last line so it would not break anybody.

She kept her eyes down, and her voice went flat and plain and careful for the ending, and she let them laugh, and she finished.

*So. Plain words, since that's all I've got.
Jackson. Little brother. Third bunk.
If anybody ever laughs at you
they answer to me, and I hit harder
than I write.
You're ours. You're here.
Don't make it weird.*

She folded the paper along the creases once, hard, and put it back in her pocket, and looked up, and the silence that came at her was not the three-heartbeat kind. It lasted longer.

Then it broke.

It broke over her like a wave: the top row was cheering, the Apollo cabin was stamping, Silena was crying openly with her hand pressed over her mouth, and somebody on the other side of the fire started up a chant of Cabin Three that the whole amphitheater picked up and bellowed out across the lake, four syllables, no tune, filling the dark with it.

Percy had gone very red.

She could see it from there, the whole of it, the way his ears had gone the color of sunset and his throat was working and his eyes were bright and furious and wet, and he had stood up without any apparent intention of standing up, and he had his hands at his sides, and he looked exactly like somebody who had been hit by something enormous and was refusing to fall over.

He didn't say anything clever. He never did the things he planned.

Clarisse got out of the way of the front of the bowl before she could watch any more of it, and went down the tier toward her own seat, and Silena met her halfway and put both arms around her and held on, and the whole left side of the amphitheater saw daughter-of-Poseidon hold daughter-of-Poseidon and neither of them cared one bit.

"Nobody teases him," Silena said into her ear, fierce and low. "Silena's rule. Nobody teases him tonight."

"I was going to make it funny," Clarisse said. Her voice was wrecked. She did not recognize it. "I wanted it to be funny so it wouldn't — I didn't want to make it — "

"You made it true," Silena said. "That's better. You made it true and everybody heard it, and it's done, it's out, it's theirs and his and yours now, and you can never take it back, and that is exactly what you wanted."

Clarisse put her face into her sister's shoulder for about two seconds, and then she pulled back and wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist and sat down hard on the stone, breathing.

Down at the bowl, Percy was still standing. Annabeth had her hand on his arm, not pulling him down, just steadying him, the way she steadied things.

He looked across the fire and found Clarisse in the second row, and his face did something complicated and enormous, and he put one hand flat over his own chest, and then he turned it into a salute, and then, because he was Percy and could not help it, he turned the salute into a thumbs-up.

Clarisse laughed until she had to put her head on Silena's shoulder again.

---

Silena used the noise to stand up.

She stood while the amphitheater was still cheering for Clarisse, while the chant of Cabin Three was still rolling over the fire, because she had learned a thing about herself in sixteen years and it was this: if she waited for the quiet, she would never go. Her knees were water. Her hands were steady. She put the sign-up sheet in her pocket so nobody would see her doing it and stepped down two tiers toward the front of the bowl, where the firelight was strongest and the whole camp could see her face.

She had sung once in front of these people. January, in the cold, standing here in the same bowl with a blanket around her and Clarisse's arm around her waist, one song all the way through, where people could hear. She remembered every second of it. She remembered the shaking. She remembered the second line steadying. She remembered thinking, at the time, well, that's that, and it had not been that, it had been the beginning of something, and then the something had been hard, and she had stopped, and nine months had gone by, and now here she was again with the fire on her face and the whole valley listening.

"I'm Silena Beauregard," she said. Her voice did her the courtesy of holding. "Daughter of Poseidon. I feed you. I also sing, apparently, and I'm going to do it before I can change my mind, so nobody be encouraging, it's making it worse."

Laughter, and somebody shouted, "We love you, Silena!"

"That's the problem with this camp," she said, and smiled, and put both hands at her sides, and began.

It was a new song. She had written it in August, in the kitchen at six in the morning, on the back of a supply order, the way her mother had taught her to do everything — on the back of something official. It had a chorus you could wash dishes to, which was the whole point of a chorus you could wash dishes to. She sang the first verse quietly, the way she had been singing for nine months, under her breath, hums and half words, and then she looked up into the firelight and stopped being quiet.

"Set the table, light the fire," she sang. "Keep the place for who comes in. There's a chair for the one you're missing, and a plate for the ones coming home."

Her voice was clear. It had always been clear. It went out over the fire and across the meadow and it did not shake, not once, and Silena felt it happen — the thing that happened when the sound left her and belonged to the air instead — and she sang the second verse for the roomful of people who had been standing in her kitchen all summer eating bread she made at five in the morning.

"My mother said: flour on the hands is a prayer you can eat. You don't have to be holy to feed people. You only have to notice they're hungry. So I noticed. I keep noticing. That's my whole personality, apparently, ask anybody."

The amphitheater laughed with her and she laughed into the melody, and behind her, three rows up, the Apollo cabin began, softly, to harmonize on the chorus, and their three parts came in around her voice like the walls of a well, and Silena's throat closed and she sang the chorus anyway.

"Set the table, light the fire, keep the place for who comes in. There's a chair for the one you're missing, and a plate for the ones coming home."

And then she did the thing she had not done in nine months. She let the words run out and kept the tune, and went on humming with no words at all, the sailing song, the wordless one she trusted, the one she had used on patrol since she was twelve, and the whole amphitheater sat with it, and Clarisse's hand came round her waist from behind where she had come up the tiers without being noticed, and held on, and Silena leaned back into it and hummed.

She had been afraid of this for nine months. She had been afraid of it in January, too, and she had done it badly and beautifully and then had spent a whole summer being certain she could not do it again. She had been wrong. She had been wrong in exactly the way she needed to be wrong, out loud, in front of the people who had fed her too.

When the last of it went out of her, the amphitheater did not cheer for a second or two. It sat in the sound of the lake.

Then it went up like a bonfire.

Silena stood in the middle of it with her hand pressed over her mouth and her hips against the fire, and the Apollo cabin was still harmonizing, and somebody was stamping, and Mr. D had, against all predictions, stayed, and Silena Beauregard, sixteen and warm and perceptive and entirely, exactly herself, let herself be heard, and it was the best feeling that had ever happened to her, and it had cost nothing at all, and she was going to do it every single time for the rest of her life.

She bowed, which she never did, and then she sat down hard on the stone step beside Clarisse, and Clarisse put both arms around her, and neither of them said anything, which was correct, because some things in this family had become load-bearing.

---

The fire was on its way down to the good part — coals and low flames and the honest red glow that keeps even after the show is over — when Silena came down the tiers with a look Percy had learned, over the years, to be afraid of.

"You're last," she said. "You're last on the list, and I'm not telling you what's in your slot, and nobody is going to explain it, and the whole camp is about to chant."

"I'm not singing."

"Nobody said sing."

"Silena."

"I wrote you a slot in pencil, and I'm not telling you what's in it, and you're going to love it, and you're going to suffer." She had that warm, serene, immovable quality, and the amphitheater had already begun, to his complete horror, to chant. Not his name.

A hum. Two hundred people, humming. Badly. On purpose. At him.

He put both hands over his face. "Who told them?"

"I did," Silena called, entirely unrepentant, "so that the record is straight."

Annabeth had moved down one step below him and had her shoulder against his shoulder, and she said, quiet, for him only, "I hum when I'm thinking. I've never told you that. It seemed only fair to lose at the same time."

"I don't hum."

"Jackson." She was smiling; he could hear it in her voice. "Everyone knows. Thalia wrote a song about you in July, and the refrain was nine words long and every word was hum, and she made me sing it at a fire you were not at, and you were not missed."

He groaned. "Fine."

He went down the last two tiers into the firelight and stood where Clarisse had stood, and the amphitheater went quiet with the careful kindness of people who intended to make sure he survived, and he held up his hands.

"I only do it when I'm cleaning Riptide," he said. "That's it. That's the whole song. If it's even a song."

"It's hums."

"It's hums." He closed his eyes so he wouldn't have to see two hundred faces, and found the thing he found when his hands were polishing the sword and his mouth was doing whatever it wanted, and he let it out. Twelve seconds. Off-key. Small. The kind of sound a person makes when they believe nobody is listening.

The amphitheater heard all twelve seconds of it.

Then it lost its mind. Not politely, not kindly, but with the particular roar of two hundred people cheering for something they had all heard a hundred times and never mentioned, and the sound came back off the granite in the wrong key, and Annabeth was standing at the bottom of the tiers with her hand over her heart and tears running down her face, and Thalia had her head thrown back and was howling something that was possibly a compliment.

Then Annabeth said, "My turn."

"No," Thalia said.

"Yes." Annabeth stepped up onto the bottom tier beside him, and her hands went to her sides, and her face was doing the precise and terrified and calculating thing all at once. "You've all been waiting for this. I can't sing."

"Annabeth Grace," Thalia said. "Consort of the birds."

"I can't sing. I'm not being modest. I have a range of approximately no notes, and I intend to keep them private." She drew a breath. "But Silena says nobody leaves, so — one verse. Everybody. Thank you."

She sang.

She sang one verse, low, into the firelight, and her voice came out clear and true and carrying, the way bells are carrying, and the amphitheater went still all over again, and Percy's mouth actually opened, and the Apollo cabin's three harmonists turned to look at each other in frank professional outrage.

Annabeth stopped after one verse, red to the ears, and refused on principle to sing another.

"One verse," she announced to the fire. "I said one verse. Write it down, Silena, so I can never be asked to repeat it."

"You have a clear voice," Percy said, thick and wrecked and delighted.

"I have a verse," she said. "You have a hum. We're a band."

He put his arm around her and she put her arm around him, and the fire cracked and threw sparks up over both of them, and he turned his head and kissed her, slow and unhurried, in front of two hundred people who had already heard the poem and the hum and the verse and did not comment on any of it, because some things at this camp had become load-bearing. The fire was warm on their faces. Her hand had come to rest against his chest, over his heart.

"You're shaking," she said, quiet, when they surfaced.

"So are you."

"Good," she said. "Then we'll do it badly together. I think we just founded that tradition."

---

Thalia Grace stayed late on purpose, the way she always stayed late, because she liked the last of a fire better than the middle of it.

The amphitheater emptied the way theaters empty: slowly at first, and then all at once, the top rows going up into the dark, then the middle, names trailing behind them and marshmallow sticks going into the brazier to burn clean. Silena was at the bowl with the flat of the shovel, coaxing the fire down into the good red breathing it would keep until midnight, and she was humming, wordless and satisfied. Clarisse was folding blankets against the bottom tier with her jaw set and her hands quick, making them square because Silena liked them square, and Percy and Annabeth had not moved. They had one blanket over both their shoulders now, that being the only way to share, and her head was against his shoulder, and his chin was against her hair, and neither of them was talking.

Thalia watched them the way she watched things she had decided were hers to see, which was to say ferociously, and without being asked.

"I owe you a blanket for tomorrow," she said, arriving beside them.

Annabeth looked up, and her whole face did the thing it did for Thalia, which was nothing like what it did for anybody else. "You drew the star chart wrong. You said Delphinus was northeast."

"It's northeast."

"You said northeast and drew it southwest."

"Then I drew it southwest because it's got more room to be correct over there."

"It isn't southwest." Annabeth sat up, and the blanket slipped, and Percy rearranged it without commentary, and she leaned against Thalia's knee where she had dropped down onto the step below her. "You're wrong about the sky, big sister."

"Your sister," Thalia said, "is going to sleep next to you on a hard ridge tomorrow with too few blankets and no food and no regrets, and I want that on the record."

"On the record where?"

"In the book of Thalia," said Silena from the fire, without turning around, serene in the embers. "It's mostly star charts and apologies."

Thalia laughed, and put her hand flat on the top of Annabeth's head and let it rest there, and Annabeth tipped into the hand for one second, the way she used to when she was smaller and had not yet learned that she was allowed to be older, and then she stood, because Annabeth stood when a thing had been enough.

They went up the path together, the four of them, Thalia walking with her arm around Annabeth's shoulders and Percy on Annabeth's other side with his hands in his pockets, and the meadow smelled of burnt oak and cold freshwater and the last of the honey cake. Behind them the fire went down to its good red breathing. Below them the lake lay dark and silver with the stars on it, and the pines were quiet on both sides, and the whole camp stood behind them on the hill, warm, and a very long way from July.

There was a particular pleasure, Thalia thought, in being witnessed.

Not looked at. Not performed at. Witnessed — heard by the exact people who had already heard the worst of you and stayed anyway. Clarisse had read forty-seven plain lines about a boy who hummed, and the whole valley had heard her say she had never been happier, and she would carry that for the rest of her life. Silena had stopped being quiet about a thing she loved. Percy had hummed for twelve seconds with his whole heart in it, and nobody had let him be ashamed of it. And Annabeth, who had grown up with a sister who was always the loud one, had sung one verse in a clear voice, and the sky had kept it.

At the bottom of the cabin path, Thalia found Annabeth waiting with a blanket around her shoulders. Percy had gone ahead to put the borrowed cups away. That was thoughtful of him; it gave the sisters a few steps without anyone else listening.

"That second verse," Annabeth said. "You changed the words."

"I forgot them."

"You never forget lyrics."

"I forgot those ones." Thalia rubbed her hands together, trying to get the feeling back into her fingers. "Besides, nobody seemed to notice."

"I noticed."

Of course she had. Thalia had improvised after a line reminded her of an old argument with Beryl. It had been easier to make the words ridiculous than let the whole camp hear something she couldn't explain.

"I wasn't trying to ruin the song."

"I didn't say you ruined it."

"Then what are you saying?"

Annabeth stopped beside the path lantern. Its metal hood rattled in the wind. "I liked the version you made. But I could tell you weren't laughing for the same reason everyone else was."

Thalia looked at her. The younger sister who used to reach for Thalia's hand in crowded places was looking back without asking to be protected from the answer.

"I'll tell you another time," Thalia said.

Annabeth considered that, then nodded. "Okay."

A moment later she added, "You can sing the wrong words tomorrow too. I'm keeping the real ones."

"You're impossible."

"According to the best available witnesses, I'm very musical."

Thalia barked a laugh and tugged the blanket more securely around Annabeth's shoulders. They finished the walk with their boots crunching over the cold path. Tomorrow they would take the overnight kit up toward the ridge. Thalia would have to decide which story she wanted to tell at the fire, and she would decide it without letting Silena choose for her.

At the cabin door Annabeth took the folded program out of her pocket and gave Thalia the half she'd kept dry.

"For your trunk," she said.

Thalia accepted it, though her trunk was already full of things she claimed not to save.
