## Chapter 116: Cabin Three Rules

The Poseidon cabin offered three beds, two worktables, one weapons rack, and roughly eleven square feet unclaimed by something damp. Salt hung in the air, mixed with cedar and wet wool, and the floorboards gave faintly underfoot, tacky with tracked sand. Morning light slanted through the salt-fogged window and picked out fingerprints on the bronze lamp, a frayed blanket edge, the dark tide line where yesterday's boots had dried. A gull cried beyond the dunes, thin and far, and rigging clinked somewhere down at the shore.

At the smaller worktable, Percy tried to read a letter from Atlantis. Paper smelled faintly of brine and kelp, ink feathered by damp. Triton had folded it fish-shaped and crammed it with palace repairs plus bulletins about a hippocamp that had learned to open doors, each loop of script hurried and splashed. Percy had reached the part where it opened every door in the eastern wing, hooves clattering on pearl floors and guards shouting, when Clarisse's practice spear punched through the open window with a crack like dropped thunder. It struck the wall above his head and buried its point in a driftwood beam. Splinters ticked onto the tabletop.

Clarisse filled the doorway, breathing hard. Her gaze traveled from spear to brother.

“Meant for the target.”

“Was the target sitting at the table?”

“No.”

“Then our definitions of success differ.”

Silena peered in from the porch, dishcloth over one shoulder. “Everyone intact?”

“Target's fine,” Percy said. “Beam's got opinions.”

Retrieving her spear with a sharp tug, Clarisse tested the point. “Missed 'cause the practice patch is full of clutter.”

“Patch holds one rope,” Silena said. “Left across the path by someone.”

“Was going to move it.”

“When?”

“When finished.”

“Finished yesterday.”

“Finished with that drill.”

Planting the butt against the floorboards, Clarisse surveyed the cabin. “We need to talk about the patch. And the rack. And the table. And why I ask twice before gear gets put back.”

“Chores, too,” Silena said, arms folding. Peppermint soap clung to her sleeves. “Scrubbed the basin this morning. Yesterday as well.”

“Washed the cups,” Percy offered.

“Rinsed one cup and shelved it with soap still on.”

“Extra-clean soap.”

Eyebrows climbing, Silena waited.

“Okay,” Percy conceded. “Washed the cups badly.”

“Nobody owns sweeping the entry,” Silena went on. “So it defaults to whoever notices first.”

“Usually you,” Percy said.

“Exactly.”

No sharpness in her tone, which somehow drove it deeper. The dishcloth smell — lemon and hot water — rose off her sleeves as she folded her arms, and a damp curl stuck to her neck. Silena noticed work that vanished once done, then quietly stacked it onto her own list. Percy couldn't recall the last time she'd sat before everything found its place, boots tucked under the bench, shoulders finally dropping as the cabin settled around her like a held breath let go.

“Okay,” he said. “We rotate.”

“Also rotate the patch,” Clarisse agreed. “Yesterday we both tried to train there at once.”

“You threw knives at the post.”

“Practicing.”

“Practiced not getting hit.”

“Behind the post.”

“Highly effective technique.”

Rubbing her forehead, Silena sighed. “Let's talk before our sharing methods become a camp safety announcement.”

Something in Clarisse's face shifted — not softening, exactly. Clarisse never softened so much as lowered the shield she held it behind. “Then let's. Cabin agreement. We all live here. Same space, same gear. Loudest or oldest doesn't get first pick.”

Percy glanced over. “You're the oldest.”

“And I'm waiving first pick.”

“Decent of you.”

“Don't make it weird.”

“Was gonna make it a little weird.”

“Don't.”

Setting three mugs on the table with soft ceramic thunks, Silena said, “After breakfast, we write it out. Chores, training schedule, whatever else.”

“And quiet,” Percy said.

Both sisters looked at him.

His eyes dropped to Triton's letter. Its corners had curled where wayward spray still beaded atop the wood in a small, embarrassed puddle. He smoothed the fish-flat.

“Want an hour when the cabin isn't arena, chore station, or a conversation starting with my name — or all three simultaneously.”

Clarisse leaned a shoulder into the wall. “Want to be alone?”

“Sometimes.”

“We can clear out.”

“Know you can. Rather agree when, so I'm not asking everyone to freeze mid-task. And nobody assumes I'm mad.”

Silena nodded. “Fair.”

Clarisse's gaze drifted to the sand outside, where her practice rope still barred the path. “Fine. After breakfast, meeting. We'll build a schedule.”

“Sibling meeting?” Percy asked.

“Cabin meeting.”

“With siblings.”

“If you must phrase it that way.”

Percy grinned. “Must.”

Eye-roll from Clarisse, but no correction.

---

By breakfast's end, the cabin agreement had gained a title, a string-tethered pencil, and one visitor. Across packing paper, Silena had lettered “Cabin Three Rules” in sweeping blue. Tyson ducked through the doorway hauling a canvas sack, smile broad enough to alter weather. Their Cyclops half-brother, visiting from the undersea forges — not a fourth bunk, not a fourth claim on the chore wheel.

“Percy! Brought the things.”

“What things?”

The sack hit the floor gently. A small rain of oranges rolled out, bumping against table legs with soft wooden knocks, sweet citrus cutting through salt air and oiled leather. One spun to a stop against Percy's toe, its pebbled rind bright as a buoy. “Oranges. And sponges. And the big blue one.”

Snagging an orange before it vanished beneath Clarisse's bed, its weight cool and firm in his palm, Percy asked, “Big blue what?”

“Sponge.” From the sack Tyson produced it — bigger than a helmet, shaped like a star that had lost an argument with a wheel. Pores gaped; it squeaked faintly when squeezed. “Good for cleaning. Thought you might want it.”

“We do need cleaning,” Silena said, “though that's a generous quantity of sponge.”

“Not too much sponge,” Tyson declared solemnly. “One sponge.”

Clarisse lifted the paper. “Discussing a sharing agreement. You can stay, but the rules cover the three who live here.”

Tyson nodded gravely. “Visiting. Do not live here.”

“Right.”

“But can help with agreement.”

A beat of hesitation. “You can listen.”

“Good at listening.”

Atop the paper, Silena wrote *chore rota*.

Peering over her shoulder, Tyson asked, “What is a rota?”

“Rotation,” Silena explained. “We swap chores daily, so nobody gets stuck with the same ones.”

Tyson turned the phrase over. “It turns?”

“In a way.”

“Like a wheel?”

“Like a list,” Percy said. “Shifting list.”

“Has a wheel?”

“Not currently.”

Tyson eyed the paper as if it hid its best feature. “Could have a wheel.”

“List first,” Silena said, laughing.

“We need actual chores,” she added. “Not the ones we remember after a week's neglect.”

“Dishes,” Percy said. “Sweep the entry. Wipe the table. Empty the basin.”

“Stow weapons,” Clarisse said. “Patch the target net when it tears.”

“Chore?”

“Is if I keep doing it solo.”

Silena's pencil scratched. “Else?”

Tyson's hand shot up. “Feed the rota.”

Percy blinked. “The list?”

“If turning, may get hungry.”

“We'll monitor it,” Percy promised. “No starving lists on our watch.”

Clarisse's mouth twitched. “Fine. Next: training patch. Schedule giving all three of us time.”

“I'm out most mornings,” she continued. “Percy trains when he remembers he owns legs. Silena trains when we pry her off other duties first.”

“I train,” Silena protested. “Don't need it daily.”

“Shouldn't have to daily,” Percy said.

“Nobody's making me.”

“Know. Only don't want the schedule assuming we all want identical hours.”

Tapping the chair, Clarisse proposed, “We claim the slots we want. Anyone can sign first. Nobody hogs the week.”

“How long?” Silena asked.

“Hour?”

“Too long on forms-only days,” Percy said. “Too short when you've got a full drill mapped.”

“Reserve blocks, not mandates,” Clarisse said. “Finished early, you release the space.”

“Swaps?” Silena asked.

“Ask. Other person can decline.”

She laid the words down like a weapon placed hilt-first where everyone could see. The pencil ticked against wood as she spoke, and late-morning sun caught the dust above the table, making it glitter. Percy caught the meaning underneath: fairness wasn't demanding whatever she wanted and labeling it equal. She refused to let their cabin become another arena where everyone braced for her to strike first, shoulders high, jaws set, waiting for the blow that never needed to come home.

“Works,” Silena said, writing *ask before swapping*.

“Now quiet hour,” Percy said.

Tyson straightened. “Hour is quiet?”

“Name, mostly.”

“Must be quiet whole hour?”

“Only if you live here,” Clarisse said.

“But if visiting?”

“We'll tell you when it runs,” Percy said.

Slow nod from Tyson, then a hand cupped around his mouth in a stage whisper. “Will be quiet.”

“Not a test,” Percy said.

“Can do a test.”

“Know you can.” A hand on Tyson's forearm. “Want an hour to read or think. We'll talk after. Nobody needs to vanish or stand guard over me.”

Whisper dropping lower: “Can watch quietly.”

Percy sighed. “No watching.”

“Will not watch.”

Rising to inspect the paper, Clarisse asked, “What time?”

Percy had braced for objection. Instead she studied the blank space beneath the heading, head tilted, ponytail brushing the spear rack.

“After lunch? Two to three?”

“Usually hit the arena then,” Clarisse said. “Could keep doing that.”

“Don't reshape your day for me.”

“Not reshaping. Saying I'll be out.”

Setting the pencil down, Silena suggested, “Quiet hour needn't mean empty cabin. We can read or mend, skip loud work, table conversations till later.”

“And knock before entering?” Percy said. “Unless emergency.”

“Emergency, I'm not knocking,” Clarisse said.

“Good.”

Silena wrote it in. “Daily? Some days activities collide.”

“Keep it unless we all agree to shift for the day,” Percy said. “Shouldn't complicate everything.”

“So rule we can change,” Tyson said.

“Yes,” Silena said. “Rule we choose, not god-law.”

“Good,” Tyson said gravely. “God laws hard to change.”

Canvas flap rustling announced Annabeth, rolled sketch under one arm, pencil behind her ear, salt-dried curls escaping her braid. Sun had pinked her nose, and paper dust whitened her fingertips. Thalia trailed her, toting two apples polished on her shirt, their sweet crunch already scenting the doorway, and an expression advertising she'd come for snacks, not diplomacy. Leather and pine smoke clung to both, cut through with the outdoor smell of hot path dust.

“Interrupting?” Annabeth asked.

Percy glanced at the page. “We've got a rota, a quiet hour, and a shield-sized sponge. So yes, helpfully.”

“Meeting?” Thalia said. “Need to salute something?”

“Only the sponge,” Tyson said solemnly.

Thalia saluted it.

“Building a cabin agreement,” Silena explained. “Stay if you like, though rules bind residents mostly.”

Annabeth's eyes found Percy's. “Want outside input?”

“If you've got it.”

“Several,” Thalia said, biting nothing yet. “Like how annoying folks are when they rank their needs above everyone else's.”

“Useful,” Clarisse said.

“Also annoying when they pretend to have none.” Thalia claimed an apple with a crunch. “That one's personal.”

“What problem are you solving?” she asked, juice on her thumb. “Not what rule you want — what keeps happening that should feel different?”

Clarisse resettled. “Training time snarls. Patch and rack go to whoever arrives first. Want a fair schedule. And gear put back.”

Silena said, “Want chores shared without me playing reminder. Also want permission not to volunteer for every small thing.”

Hearing his own need echoed steadied Percy. “Want a quiet hour. And to say I'm tired without everyone assuming disaster.”

Thalia's gaze settled on him, level. “Don't make quiet hour do all the work. You need the hour, and you need the sentence: ‘Don't want to talk right now.’”

“Sounds obvious from you.”

“Useful things usually do.”

Annabeth nodded. “Schedule should ease asking, not become proof you're asking wrong.”

Clarisse frowned. “How would it do that?”

“If everyone gets a daily hour, someone might feel forced to use it — or owed a swap.” Annabeth tapped the page. “Signup beats quota.”

Glance at Percy. “That's what we said.”

“Then you're ahead of us.” Annabeth smiled at her.

Crunching apple, Thalia asked, “Forgot chore equals punishment, or reminder plus chance to fix?”

“Tell them,” Silena said.

“If I forget, tell me straight,” Percy said. “No clipboard patrol.”

Silena eyed him. “Rather not trip over the broom to spare your feelings.”

Examining the sponge, Tyson volunteered, “Could carry clipboard.”

“Visitor,” Clarisse said, gentler this time. “No cleaning or list-making required. You're here to see Percy.”

“Can see Percy while holding clipboard.”

“Can, but needn't,” Percy said.

Tyson mulled that. “Will try visiting without chores.”

“That's the idea,” Clarisse said.

“What do guests need?” Thalia asked.

“Ask before borrowing,” Clarisse said. “Don't wander into the patch mid-drill.”

“Ask before rearranging,” Silena said. “Percy once relocated every towel to a basket labeled ‘towels.’”

Percy looked over. “That was me.”

“Know.”

“Seemed efficient.”

“Efficient, but wrong address.”

“See the distinction now.”

“Friendship isn't a license to redecorate,” Annabeth said.

Hand up, Thalia protested, “For the record, never rearranged your cabin.”

“Moved the spear rack,” Clarisse said.

“Blocked the door.”

“Where I left it.”

“Then I moved it so we could evacuate in a hurry.”

Mouth opening, then closing. “Fair.”

Leaning toward Annabeth, Percy murmured, “Didn't bring the sketch to show me, did you?”

“Did.” Unrolling her sketch of the rear practice patch — walkway, rack, targets, tide line inked in crisp lines — she smoothed the curling edge. “Measuring the run-off channel.”

Bending over the paper, Clarisse squinted. “Not to scale.”

“Close enough to talk.”

“East target sits farther from the wall.”

“Can adjust.”

“Don't. I'll mark placements.” Tracing a line with a callused fingertip, she mapped it. “Shift this target and the rack wallside, footwork drills fit.”

“If we move the rack,” Thalia said, “must we ask each time?”

“No. We'll fix a home spot. Drill needs it shifted, you restore after.”

“Sounds like a rule,” Silena said, smiling.

“It is.”

Studying the sketch, Annabeth added, “Mark shared ground and the path. Then nobody leans on memory.”

“Help us draw a map?” Silena asked.

“Of course.”

Considering her, Clarisse nodded once. “But you're not on the rotation.”

“Wouldn't expect to be.” Annabeth smiled at Percy, chaste and easy. “Guest here.”

“Guest who maps is different,” Clarisse allowed.

“Accept those terms.”

Turning to Percy, Thalia said, “You can send me out if you need quiet. No need to wait for the official hour.”

“Mid-story?”

“Finish it outside.”

Beneath the table, Annabeth's hand found his. He squeezed back, grateful she could anchor without taking over.

“All right,” Silena said. “Let's record what we actually want, then fit the pieces.”

Claiming the pencil, Clarisse announced, “No voting on whether someone gets to need something.”

Three faces turned toward her.

“Rule,” she said firmly. “We negotiate logistics, but nobody proves they deserve rest or space or mat time.”

Silena's expression warmed. “Going on the board.”

“Board is still paper,” Tyson noted.

“We'll find a board,” Percy said.

“Rota could have one.”

“Noted,” Percy said.

---

Inside the storage box waited a sturdy cedar plank, already stamped with a small dent shaped like a lightning bolt. Sawdust puffed as they hauled it out, sharp and resinous in their noses, and the plank's underside felt rough with old splinters while its face had gone smooth from years of handling. Grit crusted Percy's palms as he gripped it, and the dent caught the light, pale against darkened grain.

While Silena sanded the edge smooth, Percy hunted paint and Clarisse fetched nails, the metallic clink ringing off the walls. Annabeth and Thalia slipped off to return borrowed apples. Tyson stayed porchside with the sponge, watching gulls tilt overhead.

“You can come in,” Percy told him. “Quiet hour hasn't started.”

“Practicing.”

“Practicing what?”

“Visiting without chores.”

“How's it going?”

Tyson regarded the sponge. “Doing well. Sponge is not.”

“Marks a great sponge.”

“Lazy sponge.”

Settling beside him, shoulders touching through sun-warmed cotton, Percy listened to canvas snap in the breeze and the far rhythmic knock of a hammer from the forge. Salt dried tight on his lips. A fly traced lazy loops over the sponge's blue pores. Sometimes family was easiest quiet together, with nothing to mend or carry, only the heat of the porch boards seeping through jeans and the gulls tilting white against blue.

“You don't have to earn visits,” Percy said.

“Know.” Tyson thumped the sponge's porous crown. “Wanted to bring it 'cause you have chores. Wanted to help.”

“Kind. Help if you want. Clarisse only meant you needn't.”

“And you are my brother,” Tyson said.

“Yeah.”

“So can visit without being useful.”

“Yeah.”

“Good.” Hands resting on the sponge. “Can also be useful because I want to.”

“Good too.”

From inside, Clarisse called, “Percy, want a visitor rule?”

“Ask before borrowing, don't crash training, and don't assume chores!”

“That last part earns the board,” Silena said.

“May need its own board,” Clarisse replied.

Back around the table, scarred wood cool under their forearms and smelling of lemon oil and iron, they built it. Clarisse sketched the patch in bold strokes, charcoal smudging her thumb, Silena ruled three chore columns with meticulous straight edges, Percy lettered the quiet hour in his slanting print. Tyson offered suggestions once Percy assured him the board required no feeding, his brow furrowed in solemn relief. Their signup let each reserve morning or afternoon blocks without obligation to fill them daily. Done early? Time returned to the pool. Swaps demanded asking; no stood complete.

Clarisse reread that final line. “Good.”

“Want mornings?” Silena asked.

“Most days. Not all.”

Percy claimed Tuesday and Friday afternoons; Silena took Wednesday and left Sunday breathing room.

“No stuffing every slot,” Clarisse said.

Grateful glance from Silena before she bent back to the page.

The chore roster stayed practical: dishes rotating daily, water spotted and steaming from the basin, the entry swept evenings with sand hissing across boards, the basin emptied every other day with a hollow glug, gear restored after training, leather and bronze clanking back onto pegs. Whoever spotted a repair flagged it instead of silently absorbing it, unless urgent — a frayed strap, a wobbling hinge, a crack threading the target frame.

“We handle our own laundry,” Silena said. “Shared towels, Saturdays. Rotate.” She didn't pencil herself first. Percy noticed — and acted.

“I'll take this Saturday,” he said.

Blink. “You needn't—”

“Know. Want to.”

Smiling, she inked his name beside Saturday.

Clarisse took the next, Silena the third, pencil scratching softly, letters neat as stitching. Ordinary on paper, smudged faintly where Percy's thumb had steadied the page. Percy had never considered chores load-bearing for family; usually they materialized after someone asked Silena, quietly, while everyone else kept talking.

“Quiet hour,” he said, tapping the heading where Silena's letters stood bold and blue. “Two to three daily, unless we unanimously shift for drills or quests. Cabin can stay occupied — bunks creaking, pages turning. No loud drills or arguments, no hammering. Need a conversation, ask if it waits. If it can't, say why, plainly, and we'll listen.”

“And no justifying the need for quiet,” Clarisse said.

“Right.”

Silena lettered that larger. “Signal?”

“Like what?”

“So you can skip answering the door.”

“Blue cloth strip on the hook. Displayed means quiet hour live.”

“Napping outside it?”

“Then I'll say so.”

“Not feeling talkative?”

Naming the small boundary aloud felt strange, yet Percy knew what he wanted. “‘Not now' covers it. Should suffice.”

Clarisse nodded once. “Does.”

For a few seconds advice paused. They let the truth sit between them, the way dust hangs in sun shafts, slow and visible. Somewhere outside, water slapped a piling and a gull mewled. Percy's thumb worried a splinter on the table edge, rough catching smooth, while Silena's breathing slowed and Clarisse's jaw unclenched by degrees.

Softly, Tyson offered, “Sometimes like quiet with someone. Not alone. Merely quiet.”

“Allowed,” Percy said.

“Want that too,” Silena said. “Company sometimes, without a task attached.”

Eyes on the signup sheet, Clarisse said, “Sometimes want to drill without becoming everyone's instructor. Like helping. Dislike being drafted as coach because I know which end of a spear to hold.”

Percy met her gaze. “You can tell me to quit angling for tips.”

“Don't mind asking. Mind when you ignore it 'cause you assume malice.” Her voice dropped, thumb rubbing a callus on her spear hand. “Spent years having every correction read as cruelty. Gets old.”

“Don't assume malice.”

“Did last week.”

“Called my stance ‘a tragic invitation to be stabbed.’”

“Was.”

“Read it as mean.”

“Accurate isn't mean.”

“Get that now.”

Study, then a single nod.

“Can signal when I need listening,” she said, “versus when I want you trying it different.”

“Helps.”

“I need this cabin to feel like home, not a waystation everyone else finishes using first,” Clarisse said, voice low and a little rough, fingers worrying a frayed cuff. Rope creaked outside in the wind; a hinge ticked as the door shifted. “Know I'm loud. Thunder in the hallway, boots too heavy on the boards. Can try not to startle you when I'm frustrated, can catch it sooner. But won't agree to stay muted around the clock because I come in loud — that's the shape I arrive in.”

“You won't,” Silena said. “We'll ask you to lower the volume when it's too much. Different from asking you to be someone else.”

“And I'll flag when I need a break,” Percy added, “without turning it into a vote on your sister credentials.”

Down at the page, Clarisse murmured, “Good.”

Twirling the pencil, its paint worn smooth where generations of campers had worried it, Silena said, “Need to sit before everything's finished. Love caring for people, but don't want sole custody of supplies and chores and shared space.” Her voice stayed soft, but her knuckles whitened briefly around the wood. The smell of sawdust and drying paint hung thick, catching the back of Percy's throat.

“We'll learn,” Percy said.

“And if I forget?”

“We'll say so.”

“And I won't bulldoze just 'cause faster,” Clarisse said.

Silena smiled, tired and genuine. “I'll try not to grab the broom before anyone else gets a turn.”

“Great,” Percy said, grinning despite himself. “Broom carries heavy expectations. Probably dreams of glory.”

“Wants to be useful, not taken for granted,” Silena said, stroking the worn handle where hands had polished it smooth and dark, bristles smelling faintly of straw and salt.

“Broom has a name?”

“Not yet.”

“Next week's agenda,” Clarisse said.

“No agenda next week,” Percy said.

“Will be if this board works.”

Thalia had proposed a Sunday check-in: rule stops working, they mend it. Board as tool, not judge.

Tyson wanted the board to promise guests could visit chore-free. Clarisse agreed, provided visitors knocked before wandering. Guests could use cups and eat what's offered, but ask before taking the last of anything.

Eyeing his oranges, the pile glowing bright against the weathered table, Tyson announced, “May have last orange,” holding one aloft like a small offering, rind dimpling under his thumb.

“Needn't offer,” Percy said.

“Want you to have it.”

Percy accepted. Citrus oil perfumed his thumb. “Thanks.”

“Now many oranges,” Tyson said, pleased. “No fighting last one.”

“Solved scarcity,” Clarisse said.

“Brought oranges.”

“Sometimes that's the mechanism,” Silena said.

They finished the board in late-afternoon gold that pooled across the floorboards and warmed the paint tins. Silena painted the heading deep blue, strokes smooth and unhurried, tongue caught between her teeth, turpentine sharp beneath the honeyed cedar smell. Clarisse added a small trident beside the title, claiming she merely tested dryness, paint tacky under her thumb. Percy drew three waves along the bottom, one per sibling, brush dripping slightly where he'd overloaded it. The waves resembled ill-behaved mustaches, wobbling at the edges where his hand shook with held-back laughter.

“Waves?” Silena asked.

“Waves with personality.”

“Arguing waves,” Clarisse said.

“Then accurate.”

Reaching for the brush, Tyson asked, “Can I add wheel?”

“Not on the board,” Clarisse said. “But a small circle beside ‘rota' on the paper.”

Carefully, Tyson drew a round wheel with three spokes, a tiny dot centered.

“What's the dot?” Percy asked.

“Rota's heart.”

“Of course.”

They nailed the board beside the door, cedar ringing under the hammer, each strike jolting up Percy's arm and scattering bright chips onto the porch. Clarisse held steady while Silena checked level, tongue between teeth, hair falling forward and stuck to her cheek with sweat. Percy's second nail drove crooked, head glinting sideways, but the board held — earnest, handmade, slightly absurd, paint still tacky where his thumb brushed it.

At the top, in Silena's lovely hand:

**CABIN THREE RULES**

Beneath, in Clarisse's angular script:

**ASK BEFORE YOU BORROW. PUT IT BACK.**

Silena had inked the chore rotation and towel Saturdays. Percy added quiet hour and, boxed, **NO ONE HAS TO PROVE THEY NEED REST, SPACE, OR TRAINING TIME.** The practice map slid into a sleeve beside Tyson's heart-centered rota wheel, ready for high seas.

Along the bottom edge, squeezed: **VISITORS ARE WELCOME. VISITORS DO NOT HAVE TO DO CHORES. VISITORS STILL HAVE TO ASK BEFORE MOVING THE SPEAR RACK.**

Clarisse stared. “Oddly specific.”

Thalia, returned with traded apples, raised a hand. “Seen.”

“Because you moved it,” Clarisse said.

“And because we wrote it,” Percy added.

Leaning against his shoulder, Annabeth said, “Board looks good.”

“Looks like it forecasts weather,” Percy said.

“Looks like weather wrote it,” she said.

Stepping before the board, Tyson read aloud. Reaching quiet hour, he dropped to near-inaudible. Percy bent closer to catch it.

“Tyson,” Percy said. “No need to whisper presently.”

“Practicing for two o'clock.”

“Nearly dinner.”

“Then will practice loudly.” Clearing his throat: “VISITORS DO NOT HAVE TO DO CHORES!”

Clarisse winced. “Not that loud.”

Softer, hands folded careful in his lap: “Visitors do not have to do chores.”

“Perfect,” Clarisse said, and warmth flickered through her sternness. Applause hovered soft and unspoken in the salt air.

The cabin settled, exhaling. Sea combed the shore in slow hushes, rope creaking faintly on the porch; the board creaked in the breeze, fresh paint sharp in the nostrils under the ever-present salt. Light went amber across the bunks, picking out folded blankets and the scuffed toes of sandals lined beneath. Silena and Annabeth compared sketches while Thalia needled Percy about the crooked nail, her voice warm with teasing. Tyson asked whether the sponge could live beside the basin, turning it in his big hands so its pores caught the light. Silena assured him it needed no permission to exist, and the big boy's relieved smile filled the small room like sun through water.

Practical things filled the board: chores, times, a map, irritant-avoidance, each letter still slightly tacky where fingers had lingered. It couldn't preempt every argument — voices would still rise, sand would still drift — but it offered a starting point when quarters felt tight or tempers frayed, a cedar-blue anchor to return to.

“We try quiet hour tomorrow,” he said, voice low in the settling quiet, floorboards ticking as they cooled. Salt air drifted through the doorway, lifting the paper rota's corner.

“We will,” Silena said, already stacking the paint tins, brush handles clinking.

“If it fails?” Clarisse asked.

“We adjust.”

Nod toward the board. “Good.”

Taking his hand, her fingers warm from paint water, Annabeth asked, “Walk before dinner? Air'll do us good.” Her thumb brushed his knuckle, hopeful.

Percy surveyed the cabin: siblings breathing softly in the lamplight, visitor turning an orange in his hands, damp floorboards tacky underfoot and smelling of salt and cedar, sponge bright by the basin, rules board with apparent opinions about weather, paint gleaming. He'd wanted an hour's quiet. He didn't want it now — wanted this, exactly this.

“Yeah,” he said. “But I'll be back.”

“We know,” Clarisse said, challenge absent.

“Needn't return with oranges,” Silena added.

“Wasn't planning theft.”

“Good. They're shelved.”

Lifting one, rind bright against his broad palm and smelling sharply of citrus oil, Tyson said, “Can carry one for walk.” Juice perfumed the air as his thumb dented the skin.

“Can,” Percy said. “Needn't.”

Tyson replaced it, then reclaimed it. “Want to.”

Out Percy walked, Annabeth's hand in his, her thumb tracing slow circles on his knuckles, Tyson cradling an orange like a small sun against his chest, its rind perfuming the air with every step. Gravel crunched under their sandals, warm from the day. Behind them, Clarisse and Silena studied their agreement like a chart to somewhere all three meant to reach, heads bent together in the doorway light, salt wind lifting the paper's corner.

Only names, times, small requests on cedar with a crooked nail, paint still sharp where sun warms it.

Still, the cabin felt a touch roomier, air moving easier between bunks, light falling kinder across the boards.
