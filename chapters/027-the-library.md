## Chapter 27: The Library

The Camp Half-Blood archives were not, strictly speaking, a library.

A library implied order. A library implied a catalog, and chairs that did not wobble, and shelves where the books stayed where you put them.

The room under the Big House was none of those things.

Annabeth stood in the doorway at nine in the morning on a drizzly Saturday, hands on her hips, staring at what Chiron cheerfully called "our historical collection" and what Mr. D had called "the mold dungeon, do not make me go down there."

It was a long, low room with stone walls and small, high windows that let in slanted bars of gray light. The air smelled like paper and cedar and old rain. There were shelves along every wall, shelves in the middle, boxes stacked on shelves, boxes stacked on boxes, and scrolls in clay jars with labels that had faded to nothing fifty years ago. A rolling ladder leaned at a dangerous angle. A stuffed minotaur head watched from the corner with one glass eye missing.

Somewhere in the dust, history was waiting.

"This is perfect," Annabeth breathed.

Behind her, Percy sneezed so hard he almost dropped the box he was carrying. "This is dusty."

"It's perfect," Annabeth repeated, stepping inside. Her sneakers left prints in the dust on the floorboards. "Ella, what do you think? Can we do it?"

Ella the harpy hopped in after them, feathers fluffed against the damp, red eyes darting everywhere at once. She had a canvas tote bag from the camp store slung around her neck that said I SURVIVED THE LABYRINTH (MOSTLY) and inside it were pencils, index cards, string, tape, and three different colors of sticky notes.

"Books," Ella said reverently. "So many books. Ella will organize. Ella loves to organize. 'The History of the Peloponnesian War, Volumes One through Eight, by Thucydides, son of Olorus.' Organize by author, by date, by subject, by size, by smell?"

"By subject first," Annabeth said. She'd been planning this for two weeks, ever since Chiron mentioned that no one had properly inventoried the archives since the 1970s. "Then by date inside each subject. And we make a master list. If we find anything damaged, we put it in the repair pile for Chiron."

Percy set his box down on the big oak table in the center of the room and looked around with the expression of a boy who had been promised pancakes and given paperwork. "You said research day. You made it sound fun."

"It will be fun," Annabeth said.

"It will be sneezing," Percy said, and sneezed again on cue.

"It can be both." Annabeth pulled her hair back into a ponytail and tied her camp bandana around her nose and mouth like a dust mask. "Come on. You wanted to learn more about Atlantis, right? Chiron said there are old copies of camp journals from demigods who went looking for it. If they're anywhere, they're down here."

That got his attention. Percy straightened up. "Seriously?"

"Seriously. Box twelve, according to his list. Somewhere in that pile." She pointed to a mountain of crates against the far wall.

Percy stared at the mountain. "Okay. Okay, that's actually cool."

"Right?" Annabeth grinned under her bandana. "You take the far wall. Ella and I will start with the photo boxes near the front. Grover's supposed to come help, too."

As if summoned, there was a thump on the stairs, and Grover clattered down sideways with a beanbag chair held over his head like a turtle shell.

"Sorry! Sorry, I'm late," he panted, dragging the beanbag into the room. "Juniper wanted to say goodbye, and then I had to find my allergy medicine, and then I—" He sneezed. "—forgot my allergy medicine."

"You brought a beanbag to an archive?" Percy asked.

"For morale," Grover said with dignity, dropping it in the sunniest corner by the windows. "And naps. I mean. Breaks. For morale breaks."

Annabeth laughed. "Fine. But you have to sort scrolls before any morale breaks."

"Deal." Grover sneezed again and sat down at the table.

Ella was already perched on a stool, opening the first photo box with talons so careful they barely whispered against the cardboard. "Photographs. Black and white. Color. Polaroid. 'Smile for the camera, Ella.' Ella is smiling."

Annabeth pulled up a stool beside her and tugged on cotton gloves. "Okay. Rule one: gloves for photos. Rule two: we label everything we can identify with pencil on the back. Rule three: if we don't know who it is, we put it in the mystery pile."

"Rule four: sneezing into elbows," Percy said, muffling another one.

"Rule four," Annabeth agreed.

The rain tapped softly against the high windows. Somewhere upstairs, someone was playing music, faint and far away. The lamp on the table buzzed warm yellow. It was, Annabeth thought, exactly the kind of morning she loved best — a puzzle you could touch.

She lifted the lid off the first box.

The top photo was a camp group shot, maybe from the 1980s, all feathered hair and orange shirts. The second was a blurry pegasus in flight. The third made her pause.

Three kids sat on the Big House porch steps, squinting into the sun. A tall girl with wild black hair and a leather jacket, maybe five or six, holding a tiny blond toddler on her lap. Next to them, cross-legged and scowling, a smaller girl with a buzz cut and a chocolate-smeared face, holding a wooden sword upside down.

Annabeth leaned closer. Her heart did a funny skip.

"Oh my gods," she whispered.

"What?" Percy came over, dripping dust. "Find Bigfoot?"

"Look." She turned the photo toward him. "That's — Percy, that's Thalia. That's got to be Thalia when she was little. And that baby —"

Percy's eyes widened. "Is that you?"

The toddler had curly blond hair in two lopsided puffs, chubby cheeks, and a fierce frown. She wore a too-big CAMP HALF-BLOOD shirt like a dress and was gripping Thalia's jacket with one fist and a cookie with the other.

Annabeth turned the photo over. In faded blue ink, in handwriting she recognized as Chiron's, it said: *Thalia, 6, with Annabeth, 2, and Clarisse, 3. Strawberry fields, summer.*

"Clarisse!" Percy barked a laugh. "With hair? No way."

"Shh!" Ella said, but she was leaning in too, eyes bright. "'Two little warriors and Annabeth's big sister.' Ella likes this story."

Annabeth stared at the photo. She didn't remember this day; it belonged to the childhood she was missing. She was two in the picture. But something in her chest ached in a good way, like a muscle stretching. Thalia at six, skinny elbows and scraped knees, grinning gap-toothed at the camera while baby Annabeth glared at the world like it had personally offended her.

"I look angry," Annabeth said.

"You look like you," Percy said fondly. "Tiny and mad about it."

"I was not mad. I was... focused. I was a focused baby."

"You were a mad baby. It's cute."

Annabeth felt her ears go warm. "Shut up and go find Atlantis."

Percy ruffled her ponytail on his way back to his mountain, and Annabeth carefully set the photo aside in a special pile. Not archive. Keep.

The morning settled into rhythm. Ella dictated descriptions while Annabeth wrote labels. Percy sneezed his way through crates, narrating everything he found in a running commentary.

"Okay, box labeled 'MISC 1962.' Got a... is this a hydra tooth? Why is there a hydra tooth in with tax receipts? Who did taxes with a hydra tooth as a paperweight?"

"Put it in the weird objects pile," Annabeth said without looking up.

"We have a weird objects pile?"

"We do now."

"Found a sock. One sock. Smells like cheese. Throwing it away."

"Good call."

"Found a journal from 1948! Guy named Michael says the lake monster is 'misunderstood and probably just lonely.' Aww."

"Put it in the creature pile."

Grover, to his credit, sorted three whole jars of scrolls before migrating to the beanbag "just to read labels more comfortably." Within ten minutes he was snoring softly, a scroll unrolled over his face like a newspaper, one goat hoof twitching in his sleep.

Ella watched him with fond exasperation. "'The satyr sleeps while the work waits.' Ella will wake him later. Ella lets him dream a little first."

Annabeth smiled and kept working. The next box was all Polaroids, the colors gone soft with age. Campfires. Sword practice. Someone's birthday with a cake shaped like a trireme. And then —

"Oh no," she said. "Oh, Thalia is going to kill me."

"What? What?" Percy abandoned his crate and came running, trailing dust.

It was a series of three old camp photos. Thalia looked about twelve, wearing a towel cape over her training clothes and holding a wooden sword at an off-camera enemy. In the second, the cape had slipped and she'd become tangled in it. In the third, Annabeth, about eight, was copying her with a washcloth tied at her shoulders while nine-year-old Clarisse pretended to fall backward from the attack. All three of them were laughing.

On the back, in Chiron's rounded handwriting: *Super Thalia and Super Annabeth save Olympus. Rain-day costume contest. Cabin prizes.*

Percy lost it. He laughed so hard he had to sit down on a crate. "Super Thalia! Oh man, she can never live this down."

"You cannot show her that," Annabeth said, but she was laughing too, laughing until her eyes watered. "She'll murder us."

"We have to show her that. It's the law. Sibling law."

"It's blackmail is what it is."

Ella tilted her head, feathers shaking with silent harpy laughter. "'Super Ella approves of capes.' Capes are aerodynamic. Capes are heroic."

The stairs creaked.

"Who approves of capes?" Silena Beauregard's voice floated down, bright and amused. A moment later she appeared with a wicker tray balanced on one hip, her pink cardigan dusted with flour. "I brought reinforcements. Hot tea, honey, and oatmeal cookies."

"You are a goddess," Percy said solemnly, clearing a spot on the table.

"I know." Silena set down the tray — a chipped enamel teapot, mismatched mugs, a plate of cookies still warm — and surveyed the room. She took in Grover snoring in the beanbag, Ella with sticky notes stuck to her feathers, Percy covered head to toe in gray dust, Annabeth with a bandana mask and gloves. "Oh, this is adorable. You're playing library."

"We're inventorying," Annabeth protested.

"Mm-hm." Silena poured tea. "And how is inventorying?"

Annabeth showed her the porch photo. Silena pressed both hands to her mouth. "Oh! Oh, look at Clarisse! Look at her little cheeks! She looks like a dumpling! Don't tell her I said that, she'd kill me."

"Everyone's getting killed today," Percy observed, accepting a mug.

"Shh, drink your tea." Silena tucked a blanket she must have brought around Grover's shoulders without waking him, then perched on the edge of the table. "What else have you found?"

Annabeth hesitated, then slid over the Super Thalia photos.

Silena stared for one second. Then she made a sound like a teakettle boiling over. "No. No way. Is that—"

"Thalia, age twelve, Terror of Laundry Day," Percy announced.

"We're dead," Annabeth said. "When she finds out we saw these, we're dead."

"When she finds out?" Silena was already grinning toward the stairs. "Honey, you should've hidden them faster."

Heavy boots on the wooden steps. A familiar voice, annoyed: "Why is there a trail of dust footprints leading down here? Who—"

Thalia Grace stopped halfway down the stairs, taking in the scene. Her black hair was damp from rain, her leather jacket beaded with it. She had a sword bag slung over her shoulder — she'd been at morning drills — and a smudge of mud on her cheek.

"What are you guys doing?" she asked.

"Nothing!" Annabeth, Percy, and Silena said at the same time.

Ella, who had never lied successfully in her life, said, "Ella is hiding embarrassing photographs of Thalia as a small child in a cape."

"Ella!" Percy hissed.

Thalia's electric-blue eyes narrowed. "What photographs?"

"Nothing!" Annabeth tried to sit on the Super Thalia photos. The stool wobbled.

Thalia crossed the room in four strides, plucked the photos from under Annabeth's elbow, and looked down.

Silence.

Then her ears turned bright red. All the way to the tips.

"Where did you get these," Thalia said flatly.

"Box seven," Ella offered helpfully. "'Bath time heroes. Date missing.'"

"Give me that." Thalia snatched the porch photo too, and stared at it. Her expression did something complicated — annoyance melting around the edges into something softer. "Gods. I remember this day."

"You do?" Annabeth asked quietly.

"Yeah." Thalia's thumb brushed the edge of the photo. "It rained for three days straight that summer. Mom had sent another letter she couldn't follow through on, and I was mad at everybody. You were eight. You wanted a costume contest because the games were canceled." She glanced at Clarisse in the picture. "Clarisse tried to steal your prize cookie, so you took her wooden sword and pretended to arrest her. Chiron took this right after. He said Annabeth and I looked like a patrol team and Clarisse looked like trouble."

Annabeth swallowed. "Even then?"

"Always," Thalia said gruffly. She cleared her throat. "Clarisse is gonna hate this photo."

"Clarisse with hair is breaking my brain," Percy said.

"She had the worst buzz cut," Thalia said, a grin breaking through. "Her dad gave it to her himself. She cried for a week, then decided she loved it because it made her look tough. She used to polish her wooden sword with her shirt."

Silena leaned against Thalia's shoulder. "You were a good big sister."

Thalia shrugged, but her red ears gave her away. "I tried. Someone had to keep the gremlins alive." She looked at the Super Thalia photos again and groaned. "Burn these."

"No way," Percy said. "These are historical documents."

"These are blackmail."

"These are adorable," Silena corrected, kissing Thalia's cheek. "My brave little hero. Did the washcloth cape help you fly?"

"I will end you," Thalia said without heat, but she was smiling now, tucking the porch photo into her jacket pocket. "I'm keeping this one. For the record. And the cape ones go—"

"Into the archive, properly labeled," Annabeth said firmly. "Grace sisters, ages twelve and eight, plus Clarisse, age nine. Rainy-day costume contest. And a note that says you were very brave and your cape was very red."

Thalia stared at her, then laughed — a real, startled laugh. "Fine. But if any copies show up on the dining pavilion wall, I'm blaming Percy."

"Why me?" Percy said.

"Because it'll be you."

Grover snorted in his sleep, startling himself awake. "Wha—? I'm awake! I was sorting! I sorted... in my dreams." He sat up, blanket sliding off, scroll stuck to his cheek. "Did someone say tea?"

Everyone laughed, and Silena handed him a mug. The rain pattered on. The lamp glowed. Ella carefully wrote on a fresh index card in neat pencil: *Box 7 — Thalia, Annabeth and Clarisse, rainy-day game, Chiron's photo.*

---

By late morning, Annabeth had stopped counting shelves and started counting mistakes.

The first was her own. She had told everyone to sort by subject, and they had done exactly that. The books about ancient wars had gone together, the maps into another pile, and the family photographs into a third. She'd forgotten that the original placement could tell them something about why the documents had been kept.

Ella discovered the problem when she opened a small wooden box whose lid said *winter supplies*. Inside were four photographs, a list of old medicine stocks and a letter from a camper asking his sister to send him boots.

"These belong together," Ella declared.

Annabeth looked at the mixed contents. "They don't have the same subject."

"Yes," Ella replied. "But they belong to the same person. The letter refers to the photograph, and the stock list is dated the day he left camp."

Annabeth read the notes. Ella was right. Sorting everything into abstract categories would make it easier to find individual objects and harder to understand what had connected them.

She went to get Chiron.

He arrived with a ledger from the upstairs office and listened while she explained the mistake. She expected him to tell her to put everything back. Instead he asked how many containers they had already moved.

"Seven full boxes and two jars."

"Did you record the original location of each?"

"Mostly. The labels were faded on some."

"Then stop moving anything else until you can document what has changed."

Annabeth felt her cheeks warm. The instruction was more serious than a comment about dusty shelves. She'd offered to help preserve the collection and might have made it less useful by cleaning too quickly.

"What should we do first?" she asked.

"Record what you've touched. Then establish a system that preserves both the original groups and the subjects you want people to search."

Ella brightened. "Two sets of references. By source and by subject."

"Exactly," Chiron said.

They put the sorted piles on separate tables. Annabeth assigned each original container a number and wrote down the objects that had been taken from it. Ella checked the description against what she remembered seeing. The system wasn't perfect, because they'd already lost the order of some loose photographs, but it would preserve what they still knew.

Percy returned from the far wall carrying a tin of old maps. He was annoyed to discover his pile could not be added to the new location without a note.

"Didn't we agree to organize?" he asked.

"We did. I missed a step."

"You?"

"Yes, Percy. I make mistakes."

"Good to know."

She gave him the ledger and made him record the tin's original shelf before opening it. He complied, then asked what they were going to do about the old boxes once the documents were cataloged.

"Keep them where they're stable," Ella replied. "The box can be evidence too. Writing on the lid, the marks on the wood, the order of the papers. Those things tell stories."

Grover looked up from his beanbag. "The old socks tell stories too?"

"Not unless someone wrote down why they were there," Annabeth said.

Ella considered. "A sock could have historical value."

"Then let's not make the decision while you are still half asleep," Thalia said from the stairs.

Chiron checked the shelving. One box had been stored too near a heater; he asked them to move it to a dry shelf with better airflow. The old rolling ladder was removed from use until the steward repaired its wheel. No one was to climb stacked crates to reach the high shelves.

The new procedure slowed everything down. Annabeth found herself irritated each time someone stopped to write another note. But after an hour, the labels began to tell a coherent story. They could search by subject without losing the relationship between objects that had arrived together.

Ella took the most damaged photographs to the supervised repair table, where a librarian would check the paper before they tried any cleaning. Annabeth wanted to help restore the colors. She wasn't qualified to do that, so she wrote a condition report instead.

"Can I call this progress?" Percy asked.

"Yes."

"Even though we've sorted fewer boxes than we thought we would?"

"Especially because we know what we're doing now."

Percy glanced at the map tin. He'd wanted to find anything about Atlantis and would have happily pulled the old charts out until he found a tower drawing. He had begun understanding why the order in which people kept things could matter. A map labeled *rumor* was very different evidence from a report written by someone who'd visited the palace.

They went back to work, this time carrying the original box with each document until its place in the catalog had been recorded. Annabeth had imagined a library where everything fit into neat columns. The actual room would take longer and remain full of uncertainties. It was becoming a better archive.



---

Percy had always thought libraries were places where fun went to be quiet.

But this — this dusty basement with his friends sneezing and laughing, with tea that tasted like honey and smoke, with Grover snoring and Ella muttering book titles under her breath like prayers — this was different.

After the photo excitement, Thalia stayed. She claimed it was to make sure they didn't find more blackmail, but she settled onto the floor with a box of old camp journals and started reading, her long legs stretched out, Annabeth's head eventually ending up leaning against her knee as Annabeth labeled photos. Silena curled up next to Grover's beanbag with her knitting. It was warm. The kind of warm that had nothing to do with the space heater humming in the corner.

Percy went back to his mountain. Box twelve. Atlantis.

He'd been thinking about Atlantis a lot lately. Not in a scary-prophecy way. Just... curious. It was where his dad was from, sort of. Where his family was from, if you went back far enough. He had seen it on his recent visit — white towers and blue light and music that sounded like whales singing. But he still depended on someone else to take him there. He wanted to understand the old surface routes, not just wait for another invitation.

Chiron's list said: *Box 12 — Journals, quest reports, maps. Unsorted. Includes A. (Atlantis?) material, 1950s-1980s. Handle with care.*

Box twelve was at the bottom of the stack, of course. Percy hauled crates off the top, sneezing, until he reached a wooden wine box with 12 written in marker. Inside, wrapped in an old orange T-shirt, were five notebooks and a tin tube.

He carried them to the table like treasure.

"Annabeth," he said. "I think I found it."

Annabeth looked up from her photos, eyes bright. She wiped her hands and came over, Ella hopping after her. Even Thalia glanced up.

Percy opened the first notebook. The handwriting was neat and slanted. *Property of Samuel Whitaker, son of Athena, 1962. Quest: mapping the western sea.*

"An Athena kid," Annabeth said approvingly. "Good notes, probably."

They flipped through together. Lists of currents. Sketches of islands. A pressed flower. And then, on page forty-three, a sketch that made Percy's breath catch.

A circle of towers under water, with lines radiating out like sun rays. Under it: *Sailor's story — city of orichalcum gates, bells that ring without hands. Sailor wept when telling it. Coordinates incomplete.*

"Orichalcum," Annabeth whispered. "That's Atlantean. The old metal. Ella, remember the book on metals?"

"'Orichalcum, second only to celestial bronze, shines like fire under water,'" Ella recited promptly. "'Mined only in Atlantis, lost with the city. Chapter Seven.' Ella remembers."

Percy turned the page. More notes. *Dreamed of towers again. Heard singing. Woke with salt on lips though miles from sea. Ask Chiron about Poseidon dreams?*

Percy felt a chill, good and strange. "He dreamed about it too."

The second notebook was from the seventies, a clear-sighted navigator named Maya. Her handwriting was loopy and fast, with doodles of fish in the margins. Percy felt oddly shy opening it, like reading someone's diary. But the first page said: *If you're reading this, hello! I hope you're a Poseidon kid. I hope you can ask the fish the questions I could never get them to answer.*

He grinned.

Maya's journal was less mapping, more stories. She'd collected every Atlantis mention she could find — quotes from Plato, from sailors, from old Nereids who liked to gossip. She'd drawn what she imagined the palace looked like: domes and bridges and gardens of coral. On one page she'd written in big letters: *IT'S REAL. I know it is. The water remembers even when we forget.*

Percy read that line twice.

"Water remembers," he said softly.

Annabeth nudged his shoulder. "You okay?"

"Yeah. It's just... nice. To know someone else thought about it like that."

The tin tube held a map, yellowed and cracked at the folds. When Percy carefully unrolled it and weighted the corners with books, they all gathered around — even Grover, awake now, even Silena.

It wasn't a normal map. It showed the Atlantic seaboard, but with extra islands drawn in pencil where no islands should be, and spirals marking currents, and in the middle of the ocean, a blank circle with the words: *here? music?*

Thalia whistled low. "Spooky."

"Cool spooky," Percy said. "Not bad spooky."

Ella leaned so close her beak almost touched the paper. "'Here be songs.' Ella likes this map. Ella would fly there if Ella could swim."

Annabeth was already copying details into her own notebook, her pencil flying. "We need to cross-reference this with the Plato texts upstairs. And the Nereid stories. If we plot the current spirals against modern charts—"

Percy watched her, fondness bubbling up like spring water. This was Annabeth at her happiest — puzzle pieces everywhere, making order out of mess. Dust on her nose. Tea gone cold beside her because she'd forgotten to drink it.

"You're doing the thing," he said.

"What thing?"

"The happy-planning thing. Your eyes go all starry."

"My eyes do not go starry."

"They do. It's cute."

"Percy Jackson, I am trying to do research."

"You're doing it cutely."

Thalia made a gagging sound. "Get a room. Preferably one without mold."

Silena laughed and threw a cookie at Thalia, who caught it in her mouth. Grover applauded. Ella requested a cookie toss as well and caught hers in her talons with a triumphant shriek that sent dust showering down from the shelves.

"Ghost!" Grover yelped, then sneezed. "Just dust. Just dust ghost."

Annabeth shook her head, but she was smiling so wide it hurt. She set her notebook down and leaned against Percy's side for just a second, shoulder to shoulder, looking at Maya's drawing of coral gardens.

"Do you think we could follow one of these old routes?" Percy asked quietly, so only she could hear. "To Atlantis, I mean. I want to show you the real thing."

Annabeth considered. She didn't give easy answers. That was one of the things Percy loved about her. "I think," she said slowly, "that if anyone could find the way, it'd be you. You listen to water better than anyone."

Percy felt his face go warm. "And you'll draw the map."

"And I'll draw the map," she agreed. "With proper labels. And no hydra teeth as paperweights."

"Deal."

They bumped fists, dusty gloves and all.

---

Thalia hadn't planned to spend her Saturday in a basement.

She'd planned to run drills, fix her shield strap, maybe nap on the porch roof. Big-sister stuff. Soldier stuff. Not... feelings stuff.

But then she'd heard laughter coming up the Big House stairs, and Silena's voice, and she'd followed it down like following music, and now she was sitting on the floor with a twelve-year-old's head on her knee and a cookie in her hand and an old photo burning a hole in her pocket.

Thalia Grace, border-patrol menace and scourge of monsters, was getting emotional over baby pictures. Disgusting.

Wonderful.

She watched them work. Annabeth labeling each photo with intense care, tongue poking out the corner of her mouth the way she had since she was two. Percy reading Maya's journal with his lips moving slightly, his finger tracing the words like they might disappear. Ella sorting and singing under her breath, a harpy lullaby about book glue and paper. Grover dozing again, tea mug clutched to his chest. Silena knitting something blue and humming.

Safe. They were all safe, and young, and dusty, and happy. No quests. No prophecies. Just rain and tea and old paper.

Thalia hadn't realized how much she'd needed a day like this until it was happening.

"You know," she said suddenly, "I have more of those. Photos. From when Annabeth was little. Mom kept a few, and I grabbed them when I ran. They're in my cabin. In a shoebox."

Annabeth looked up. "Really?"

"Yeah. There's one of you eating sand. Like, a whole fistful. At the beach. You were so mad when I took it away that you bit me."

"I did not bite you."

"You bit me. I have the scar. Look." Thalia held out her thumb. There was a tiny white mark.

Annabeth peered at it. "That could be anything."

"It's your teeth. I know your teeth."

Percy snorted tea through his nose. "Sand-eater."

"Shut up, fish boy," Annabeth said, but she was glowing. "Can I see them? The photos?"

Thalia hesitated. Those photos were... private. They were from before. From the apartment with the peeling paint and the sirens outside, from the days when it was just her and a little girl and an empty fridge and she'd been nine and terrified and pretending she wasn't. She'd kept them because they were proof she'd kept Annabeth alive. Proof she'd been enough, for a little while.

But looking at Annabeth now — twelve, strong, surrounded by friends who loved her, asking with wide gray eyes — Thalia thought maybe it was time to share them.

"Yeah," she said gruffly. "Yeah, okay. We'll do another one of these. In my cabin. I'll even provide snacks that aren't dusty."

"And no beanbag naps?" Grover asked hopefully from under his blanket.

"No promises," Thalia said.

Silena finished a row of knitting and held it up. It was a scarf, bright blue with silver stripes — Poseidon colors. "For you," she told Percy. "For when you go swimming and pretend you don't get cold."

"I don't get cold," Percy protested. "Cold gets... me? That didn't work. Thanks, Silena."

Silena draped it around his neck, and Percy beamed, immediately getting cookie crumbs on it. Annabeth rolled her eyes and brushed them off.

Ella held up a photo she'd just unwrapped. "Oh! Oh! New discovery! 'Baby Clarisse bites the counselor.' Ella repeats: baby Clarisse bites the counselor."

Everyone crowded around. It showed a furious toddler Clarisse, buzz cut growing out, latched onto the sleeve of a long-suffering teenage counselor, while three-year-old Annabeth watched with scholarly interest and Thalia laughed in the background.

"That tracks," Thalia said.

"We have to show Clarisse," Percy whispered.

"We will die," Annabeth whispered back.

"We will die laughing."

Thalia pocketed that one too. "For leverage," she said solemnly. "Friendship diplomacy. And big-sister privileges."

The afternoon light shifted, turning from gray to gold as the rain eased. Dust motes floated in the sunbeams like tiny stars. The tea went cold. The cookies disappeared. The mystery pile grew smaller. The labeled piles grew taller.

Annabeth stood back at one point, hands dusty, hair escaping its ponytail, and surveyed their work. Three shelves done. Boxes neatly stacked. Cards written. Photos sleeved.

"It's coming together," she said with quiet satisfaction.

Percy came up beside her, Maya's journal tucked under his arm like he'd already adopted it. Chiron might let him consult it again under the archive's rules; the journal still belonged in the collection. "Hey, Annabeth?"

"Yeah?"

"Thanks for making me come. This was... good."

Annabeth bumped his shoulder. "Told you. Fun."

"And sneezing."

"And sneezing."

They stood there a moment, watching Ella teach Grover how to shelve by size, watching Silena braid Thalia's hair while Thalia pretended to hate it, watching the sun find its way through the high windows.

No monsters. No battles. Just kids in a basement, piecing the past together one photo at a time.

---

Ella loved words. Words were feathers. Words were shiny things to collect and line up in rows.

But today Ella learned that photographs were words too.

Photographs said: *we were here. We were small. We were loved.*

Ella pasted labels and memorized faces. Ella learned that baby Annabeth frowned when thinking. Ella learned that young Thalia smiled with teeth. Ella learned that baby Clarisse bit. Ella learned that Samuel Whitaker drew very straight lines and Maya the navigator drew very curly fish.

Ella learned that Percy Jackson laughed like water over rocks when happy, and Annabeth Grace hummed when concentrating, three notes over and over, and Grover Underwood snored in E-flat, and Silena Beauregard said "oh, honey" when she loved something, and Thalia Grace went quiet when she loved something so much it hurt.

Ella collected all these things and put them in the nest of her memory, alongside book titles and quotes.

"'The History of Camp Half-Blood, Volume One: The Littlest Heroes,'" Ella murmured, writing on a fresh box label. "By all of us. Today."

Annabeth read it and smiled until her eyes shone. "That's perfect, Ella."

Percy found one more thing before they left — tucked in the back of Maya's journal, a folded piece of blue paper. Inside, in green ink: *Song for finding your way home when lost at sea. Sing it and think of light.*

Notes followed. Simple ones. A lullaby.

Percy showed it to Annabeth. Annabeth showed it to Grover. Grover played it on his reed pipes, softly, missing two notes. Ella sang the missing notes without thinking, high and clear. Silena harmonized. Thalia kept time on a book.

The music filled the dusty basement and rose up the stairs and out the high windows into the rain-washed air.

For a moment, Percy closed his eyes. He thought of white towers and blue light. He thought of bells ringing without hands. He thought of salt on lips and songs under water.

Home, he thought. Whatever home meant — a city under the sea, a camp in the woods, a basement full of friends — it was the people who remembered your name. Who labeled your photo and kept it safe.

He opened his eyes. Annabeth was watching him, smiling.

"What?" he asked.

"Nothing," she said. "Your eyes went starry."

"Liar."

"Fish boy."

"Sand-eater."

They grinned at each other, dusty and happy, while the music played on.

Later, when they trooped upstairs blinking into the evening light, Chiron met them on the porch.

"How was the archive?" he asked.

"Dusty," Percy said.

"Wonderful," Annabeth said.

"We found an old way to Atlantis," Ella said. "'Maybe.'"

"We found better," Thalia said, and showed him the porch photo. Chiron's eyes softened.

"Ah," he said. "I remember taking that. You three were covered in strawberry juice. Clarisse had swords in both hands. Annabeth had cookie in both hands. Thalia had her hands full."

"We still do," Thalia said, slinging an arm around Annabeth's shoulders while Percy leaned against them both, Grover tripping over the steps, Silena laughing, Ella fluttering overhead with her tote bag full of labels.

Chiron watched them go toward the dining pavilion, chattering about cape protocols and cookie distribution and whether hydra teeth should be allowed as paperweights (verdict: no).

He smiled into his tea.

Some days, history was not battles and prophecies. Some days, history was tea and dust and a song about going home.

And those were the days worth keeping.

At dinner, the story only grew. Grover reenacted his battle with the dust ghost, complete with sneezing sound effects that made the Apollo table applaud. Silena produced a second batch of oatmeal cookies from the kitchen and claimed the archive now smelled faintly of honey because she had declared it so. Ella recited the full catalog of Box 7 from memory to a delighted audience of younger campers, who demanded to see Super Thalia at once.

Thalia threatened to launch every single one of them into the lake. She said it with Annabeth tucked under one arm and Percy stealing cookies off her plate and Silena fixing her braid, and nobody believed her for a second.

Percy kept Maya's journal beside his plate and showed Annabeth the fish sketched in its margins. She compared one drawing to the rough map of Atlantis he'd borrowed from Chiron.

Across the table Thalia noticed a photograph propped beside Annabeth's cup.

"That one stays in the box," she warned.

"Of course," Annabeth said.

"Annie."

Annabeth put the photo in its paper sleeve, then tucked the sleeve into the folder labeled *Grace family, camp years*. Thalia watched her write the date properly. Only then did she go back to her meal.
