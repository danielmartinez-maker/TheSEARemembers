## Chapter 37: Sisters


Thalia Grace woke up to a blonde head on her stomach and an elbow in her ribs.

For half a second her whole body locked up — old reflex, hand already reaching for the hunting knife she kept under her pillow — and then she smelled strawberry shampoo and sun-warmed cabin wood and remembered where she was.

Zeus cabin. Cabin One. Her cabin. Not a motel floor in Richmond. Not the back seat of a car with the windows iced over.

Annabeth Grace was asleep on top of her, drooling a little on Thalia's sleep shirt, one sock on and one sock off, her notebook splayed open on the floor beside the bunk with a charcoal pencil leaking gray across the page.

Thalia stared at the ceiling. The cabin was too big for two people. That was the first thing everybody said when they walked in. Vaulted ceiling, white walls, marble floor that stayed cold through late winter, huge windows that made the whole place glow gold at sunrise. Two beds against opposite walls like bookends. Two trunks. Two lockers. A statue of Zeus in the corner that Thalia had draped with Mardi Gras beads last summer just to make him look less stern.

For years it had looked built for nobody, all that marble waiting for a king who rarely came. Thalia and Annabeth had made a home in it together. Jason's short visits from the Roman camp brought a spare bed and more noise; most mornings there were still only two sisters beneath the enormous ceiling.

Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus and Beryl. Twelve, and Thalia's little sister. The family names were simple enough; what unsettled Thalia was how little of their childhood Annabeth could remember now. Chiron had no neat answer for that.

"Annabeth," Thalia whispered. "You're crushing my lungs."

Annabeth mumbled something that sounded like "load-bearing pillar" and burrowed deeper.

Thalia grinned up at the ceiling. Outside she could hear the conch horn for breakfast still twenty minutes off, the distant clatter from the pavilion, a harpy shrieking at some poor satyr by the woods. Normal Camp Half-Blood Saturday morning sounds. No drills scheduled. No capture the flag. Luke had put a big hand-lettered sign on the Big House porch last night: NO LESSONS. NO QUEST BRIEFINGS. SLEEP IN OR ELSE. Even Mr. D had signed it, presumably under threat.

Annabeth shifted, blinked awake, and immediately looked guilty.

"Sorry! Sorry, did I — I came over because your bunk's warmer by the window and mine's freezing and then I guess I fell asleep?"

"You drooled on me."

"I did not!"

Thalia lifted her shirt hem to show the damp spot. Annabeth went bright red all the way to her ears.

"Okay. Maybe a little."

Thalia ruffled her hair, and Annabeth made a face but leaned into it anyway, the way she always did when she thought nobody was looking. That was the thing about Annabeth at twelve. Around Percy and Luke and the rest of the Hermes cabin she was all sharp elbows and sharper plans, lists and diagrams and *actually, if you'd let me finish*. Around Thalia, first thing in the morning before the armor went on, she was still soft.

"Happy Sisters Day," Annabeth said, suddenly brightening. She rolled off Thalia and landed hard on the marble floor with a thud, scrambling for her notebook. She flipped three pages, revealing yesterday's handwriting in dense blue ink, underlined twice.

Thalia sat up, rubbing her face. "What did you do."

"It's a schedule! I made us a schedule."

"Oh gods."

"No, listen, it's good! Rule: just the two of us. No Percy —" She held up a hand. "— and I love him, but he would eat all the snacks and narrate everything. No Luke, no Grover, no Chiron check-ins. Just sisters. I have it all planned."

She cleared her throat and read, very official:

"Phase One: Breakfast heist. Phase Two: Haircuts. Phase Three: Closet raid. Phase Four: Sparring. Phase Five: Photos. Phase Six: —" She hesitated.

"Phase Six what?" Thalia asked.

Annabeth looked up. Gray eyes, serious. "Talking. Just talking."

Thalia felt something tug low in her chest. She covered it by throwing a pillow at Annabeth's face.

"You're such a nerd."

"You're drooled on," Annabeth shot back, but she was beaming.

Thalia stood, stretched until her back cracked, punk bracelets jangling. Her black hair was a rat's nest, the blue streaks she re-dyed with river nymph dye standing straight up. She caught her reflection in the window and winced.

"Okay, shortstack. I'm in. But if Phase Two involves you coming at me with arts-and-crafts scissors, I'm invoking big-sister veto."

Annabeth already had the scissors in her hand. She'd hidden them behind her back. "These are real hair scissors! I borrowed them from the Aphrodite cabin! Silena said—"

"Oh, that makes me feel so much better."

---


Breakfast heist went perfectly, which is to say it went terribly and they got away with it anyway.

The plan had been simple: sneak into the pavilion kitchen before the harpies finished setting out platters, grab a picnic's worth of stuff, and vanish before Tantalus — no, Annabeth corrected herself, before Mr. D noticed. She'd drawn a little map with guard rotations and everything.

What actually happened was Thalia walked straight into the kitchen, said "Hey, beautiful, can I get two plates to go, my sister's starving," and the dryad on duty melted and gave them double portions plus extra bacon and two warm blueberry muffins wrapped in a napkin.

Annabeth stood in the doorway holding her useless map while Thalia winked at her over a stack of pancakes.

"That's not stealth!" Annabeth hissed once they were outside, clutching the warm bundle to her chest as they cut across the green toward the canoe lake. "That was just — flirting with the kitchen staff!"

"It was diplomacy," Thalia said loftily. She had a strip of bacon hanging out of her mouth. "I'm very diplomatic."

"You have bacon in your hair."

"I'm saving it for later."

They ate down by the lake, legs dangling off the dock, mist still curling off the water. Camp was waking up around them — the Apollo kids jogging the perimeter, Clarisse yelling at someone in the distance, the smell of woodsmoke and cut grass. A naiad popped up to stare at them, and Thalia tossed her a piece of muffin, which she caught and ate with great dignity before sinking back down.

Annabeth had packed her notebook out of habit, but she didn't open it. Thalia had brought a chipped blue radio, and she turned it low to an old rock station that crackled every time the wind hit the antenna. They ate in comfortable quiet for a while, swinging their feet in sync without meaning to.

"Okay," Annabeth said, licking syrup off her thumb. "Can I ask you something and you promise not to laugh?"

"No promises."

Annabeth kicked water at her. "When did you know you looked like — you? Like Thalia-style, not just whatever. The jacket and the boots and the —" She gestured at all of Thalia. The ripped black jeans, the Guns N' Roses shirt with the sleeves cut off, the silver bracelets all the way up both forearms, the black eyeliner smudged from yesterday, the spiky hair.

Thalia laughed, surprised. "This? This was an accident that got out of control. When I was eleven I cut my own bangs with a hunting knife because Beryl —" She stopped. Restarted. "Because I was mad. Looked awful. Then I met Luke on the road and he said I looked like a hedgehog that lost a fight, and I decided I liked that. The jacket was Luke's old army surplus one. He gave it to me when we were running from a hellhound outside Charleston and I was freezing. Said I looked tougher in it than he did. I just kept adding stuff."

She tugged at a bracelet. "This one's from a truck stop in Virginia. This one Annabeth — no, different Annabeth, waitress in Dahlonega — she gave it to me for luck. This one's just a hair tie I never took off."

Annabeth listened so hard she forgot to eat. She loved these stories the way other kids loved campfire myths. Thalia's life before camp was a road map of gas stations and Greyhounds and Luke and — for a while — a little boy with glasses and a Superman cape.

Thalia nudged her. "Why? You thinking of a rebrand? Princess curls not doing it for you anymore?"

Annabeth touched her own hair self-consciously. It was long, really long, past her shoulder blades, the same honey-blonde it had always been, curly in a way that tangled if you looked at it wrong. The Aphrodite girls were always trying to get her to let them style it. She always said no because satyr-trailing-through-the-woods was not compatible with hot rollers, but secretly she wondered.

"I dunno," she said. "I just — everybody knows you when they see you. I want —" She shrugged. "Maybe just a trim. Split ends."

Thalia studied her for a long moment, head tilted. The teasing dropped out of her voice.

"You'd tell me if it was more than split ends, right?"

Annabeth nodded quickly, throat tight for no reason. "Yeah. Just split ends. Phase Two."

Thalia stood and offered her hand, pulling Annabeth up with exaggerated effort. "Come on, Princess Split Ends. Let's go butcher your hair."

---


Haircuts in the Zeus cabin bathroom were a terrible idea, which is why they were perfect.

Thalia dragged a chair onto the white tile, laid out Silena's borrowed scissors, two combs, a spray bottle full of water, and a stack of towels she'd stolen from the Big House laundry. She taped a piece of notebook paper to the mirror that said SALON STORM in Annabeth's neat block letters, with a lightning bolt drawn underneath.

Annabeth sat in the chair in an old camp T-shirt, hair combed out damp, looking both excited and terrified.

"Okay, ground rules," Thalia said, wrapping a towel around Annabeth's shoulders like a cape. "I am not a professional. If I mess up, we tell Chiron a hellhound did it."

"Very reassuring."

"Shh. Salon Storm requires silence and trust. When did you last get it cut?"

"Frederick took me once. In the life I remember, before — before I came back." Annabeth's voice went a little flat. "He took me to a place with those sinks that lean back. The lady gave me a lollipop even though I was eleven. I was mad but I ate it."

Thalia's hands paused. Annabeth didn't talk about that other father much. Frederick Chase, professor of military history, a house full of books and model airplanes: Thalia knew him only from what Annabeth had told her. Beryl was their mother here. Still, Thalia understood loving a mortal parent who couldn't quite keep you safe from the other half of your blood.

"Okay," Thalia said softly. "Tell me if I pull, all right?"

She started slow. Comb, lift, snip. Comb, lift, snip. Blonde curls fell onto the tile in soft rings. Annabeth watched in the mirror, eyes wide.

"You have really good hair," Thalia said after a while, honestly surprised. "Like, stupid good. It's all thick and — gods, it's like spun gold or something. The poets weren't lying."

Annabeth snorted. "Don't get poetic on me, you'll drop the scissors."

"I'm serious! Mine just sticks straight up if I don't weigh it down. Yours does this princess thing without trying."

"Princess thing is annoying when you're trying to run from empousai."

"Still pretty."

Annabeth went pink and stared hard at her own lap. Thalia kept cutting, careful around the ears, trying to even out the layers the way she'd watched Silena do once.

"Can I —" Annabeth said after a while. "Can I do yours after? Just a trim? The back's all uneven."

Thalia's first instinct was to say no. Nobody touched her hair except her, and even then only when necessary. It was stupid, but hair was one of the few things that had always been hers on the road — no gods, no monsters, no adults telling her how a proper young lady should look.

But Annabeth was watching her in the mirror with that look, that hungry little-sister look that said *let me in*, and Thalia heard herself say, "Yeah. Okay. Just — be gentle, yeah?"

Annabeth's smile could have powered the whole cabin.

Thalia finished, stepped back. "Ta-da."

It wasn't perfect — one side was maybe a quarter inch longer than the other, and there was a curl that sprang up weird near Annabeth's temple — but Annabeth's hair fell clean and bright to just below her shoulders, bouncy, healthy, swinging when she turned her head. She shook it, eyes lighting up.

"Oh! Oh, I love it! I can feel air on my neck!" She twisted to see the back, grinning. "Thalia, you're good at this!"

"Don't sound so surprised," Thalia said, but she was grinning too, chest warm. She swept the blonde clippings into a little pile. "My turn. Don't make me bald."

They switched. Thalia sat, Annabeth standing on tiptoes behind her, tongue stuck out in concentration, spray bottle in one hand, scissors in the other. Thalia could see her in the mirror — brows furrowed, mouth moving silently as she measured.

"You okay back there, shortstack?"

"I'm not short, you're just mutated tall. Hold still."

Annabeth's hands were careful, almost reverent. Snip. Snip. Black hair with blue tips dusted Thalia's shoulders. The bathroom smelled like strawberry shampoo and metal.

"You know," Annabeth said quietly, combing through a section, "In that old life, I used to cut Frederick's hair. When I was little. He'd sit on the kitchen stool and I'd stand on a chair behind him with a comb. He said I was the only barber he trusted."

Thalia went very still. "Yeah?"

"Yeah. He has this cowlick in the back that sticks up no matter what. I always tried to flatten it." A small laugh. "Bobby and Matthew would put towels on their heads and pretend to be customers. stupid."

Bobby and Matthew. Her little half-brothers in the life she remembered. Mortal. Thalia had never met them; Annabeth never said their names lightly.

Thalia reached up without looking and squeezed Annabeth's wrist. "You're good at this too."

Annabeth finished, brushed the loose hair off Thalia's neck. Their eyes met in the mirror — gray on blue-green, same stormy shape around the eyes, Zeus's eyes, everyone said. Sisters.

"You look like you," Annabeth said. "Just — cleaner. Tougher."

Thalia turned, still caped in the towel, black and blue hair falling sharp around her jaw. She looked at Annabeth, blonde and bright and twelve, scissors still in her hand, and felt that tug again, stronger.

"So do you," she said.

---


The borrowed-clothes fight started the way all great wars start: over a jacket.

Thalia had stripped off the towel, shaken out her fresh hair, and gone to her locker to change out of the hair-covered T-shirt. Annabeth was sweeping up — because someone had to, and Thalia's idea of sweeping was kicking the pile under the sink — when she saw it hanging on the back of Thalia's locker door.

The jacket.

Black leather, silver studs on the cuffs, a little singed on the left sleeve from a drakon encounter Thalia refused to explain, pins all over the lapel: a tiny lightning bolt, a Ramones patch, a button that said MY OTHER CHARIOT IS A HELLHOUND. It smelled like leather and rain and Thalia's spearmint gum.

According to Thalia, Annabeth had wanted to wear it since she was nine. She could believe it; she wanted to now.

She waited until Thalia's back was turned, yanked it off the hook, and shoved her arms in. It was huge — sleeves past her fingertips, shoulders drooping, hem hitting mid-thigh. She felt like a kid playing dress-up. She felt awesome.

Thalia turned around in her sports bra, saw her, and stopped dead. "Absolutely not."

"What? Why?" Annabeth struck a pose. "I look cool!"

"You look like you're being eaten by a cow."

"I look like you!"

That stopped Thalia for a second. Something flickered across her face. Then she grinned, feral. "Oh, you wanna look like me? Fine. Give me the owl shirt."

Annabeth clutched at her own locker. Her gray Camp Half-Blood tee with the little owl over the heart was her favorite. It was soft from a hundred washes, and Percy had drawn a tiny trident on the sleeve in Sharpie last week and she'd drawn a lightning bolt next to it. "No!"

"Closet raid was Phase Three," Thalia said, advancing. "You wrote it. No take-backs."

"It's my shirt!"

"And that's my jacket!"

Annabeth bolted. Thalia lunged. They collided in the middle of the cabin in a tangle of too-long sleeves and bare feet on cold marble, shrieking with laughter. Annabeth tried to wriggle away, Thalia caught her around the waist, Annabeth elbowed her (accidentally, mostly), Thalia yelped and tickled her ribs in retaliation.

"Give — it — back —" Thalia gasped, trying to peel the jacket off while Annabeth squirmed.

"No! It's mine now! Finders keepers! Sister law!"

"There is no sister law!"

"I just made it! Section one: big sisters have to share!"

They crashed onto Thalia's bunk, Annabeth half inside the jacket like a turtle, Thalia trying to tug it off and laughing too hard to get a grip. Annabeth kicked a pillow off the bed. Thalia's bracelets jangled. The statue of Zeus looked down disapprovingly with Mardi Gras beads swinging.

"Okay, okay, truce!" Thalia finally wheezed, collapsing sideways, Annabeth sprawled across her legs. "Gods, you're vicious. Where'd you learn to fight like that?"

"Clarisse," Annabeth said proudly, pushing sweaty blonde hair out of her face. The jacket was twisted around her, one arm stuck. "She taught me that elbow thing."

"Remind me to thank her. And to never spar you for real."

Thalia sat up, still breathing hard, and instead of taking the jacket back, she adjusted it on Annabeth's shoulders. Folded the sleeves up carefully, three turns each, so Annabeth's hands came out. Straightened the collar. Her hands were gentle.

Annabeth went quiet. The laughter faded into something warmer, fizzing under her ribs.

"There," Thalia said softly. "Okay. Yeah. You do look cool."

"Really?"

"Really. Like a tiny, terrifying biker." Thalia tapped the lightning bolt pin. "You can borrow it. But if you get monster dust on it, you clean it."

Annabeth beamed and threw her arms around Thalia's neck, jacket sleeves flapping. Thalia hugged back hard, lifting her half off the bed.

"And I get the owl shirt," Thalia added into her hair.

"No!"

"Annabeth!"

"Fine! But only for today! And you have to tuck it in!"

Thalia pulled the gray shirt over her head — it was tight across her shoulders, the owl stretching — and tucked it into her ripped jeans with great ceremony. She posed like a statue. "How do I look?"

"Like a nerd," Annabeth said fondly.

"Your nerd," Thalia said, and stuck out her tongue.

Annabeth changed into Thalia's spare flannel — red and black, huge on her, sleeves rolled — over her camp shorts, keeping the leather jacket over it all. She looked ridiculous. Layers on layers, drowning in plaid and leather. She loved it.

Thalia looked at her, owl shirt and eyeliner and fresh haircut, and said, "Come on. Let's go show the arena how stupid we look."

---


The first person who saw them outside Cabin One was Percy.

He had been carrying a bucket of wet practice gear to the shed. When he looked up, the bucket hit the ground and sent a splash across his shoes.

Thalia put her hands on her hips. "What?"

Percy stared at her owl shirt, then at Annabeth's leather jacket, then back at Thalia.

"I'm trying to decide which one of you I should be more frightened of."

"Me," they said together.

Percy laughed. He retrieved the bucket and glanced at Annabeth's hair.

"You cut it."

"I did. Is that a problem?"

"No. It looks good."

He said it without sounding as though he'd prepared the answer. Annabeth was disproportionately pleased.

"Thank you," she said.

Thalia examined him. "You'd better not tell her it looks bad. She threatened to do mine next."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

"You sound frightened."

"I've had a lot of practice."

He went toward the shed, smiling to himself. Annabeth watched him for a moment, then realized Thalia was watching her watch him.

"Don't," she warned.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to."

"I was going to point out that your boyfriend looks like he forgot how to walk."

"He dropped a bucket."

"Exactly."

Annabeth began walking faster. Thalia fell into step without much effort.

Near the arts cabin, an Apollo camper called Thalia's name. He was holding two portrait sheets and a little graphite pencil, and he looked relieved to find the older girl out of training armor.

"We're doing a set of drawings for the cabin board," he explained. "Can you sit for ten minutes? The last picture we have of you is from the winter tournament."

Thalia looked at Annabeth. "She's the one with the new haircut."

"I already have her portrait from the architecture exhibit," the camper said.

"Then you can draw another."

Annabeth didn't know whether to be touched or irritated. Thalia had started trying to make sure everyone noticed her new hair within minutes of leaving the cabin. The Apollo boy wanted Thalia, and Annabeth had no intention of standing beside a chair while he politely found a way to refuse her sister's instruction.

"He asked you," she told Thalia. "Go sit."

"You're coming."

"To do what?"

"Make fun of the drawing when it looks nothing like me."

That was a decent invitation. Annabeth accepted.

The artist had set up a small sketch station beneath the eaves. Thalia sat with one knee over the other, trying to look nonchalant, which required more effort than posing like she was preparing for a fight. Annabeth settled on the floor with her notebook and watched the pencil move.

The boy asked Thalia to remove the owl shirt because the sleeves distorted the line of her shoulders. She refused.

"It's borrowed," she said. "I'm wearing it for a reason."

The boy tried to explain the practical problem. He wanted to draw the way the arm joined the torso, and the cloth was stretched so tightly he couldn't see it accurately.

Annabeth looked up. "He's not asking you to change because he dislikes my shirt. He's trying to draw the shape underneath."

Thalia considered. "Fine. Can I keep it on if I roll the sleeves?"

"That would help," the artist said.

He resumed drawing. Annabeth returned to her notebook and began sketching the chair, which she considered far more interesting than the portrait. The station had been assembled hastily from uneven boards, and one of the legs stood on a folded piece of cardboard to keep it level.

"If you keep putting the chair on the same spot," she told the artist, "we can make it stable with a proper shim."

He glanced down. "It does wobble."

"I know. I can help you fix it after your session."

Thalia turned her head to look.

"Stop moving," the artist complained.

"Sorry."

Annabeth grinned and worked on the drawing. She had felt invisible for years in the life she remembered whenever somebody treated a twelve-year-old girl's interest in architecture as a hobby she'd outgrow. Here the problem was different. People recognized her as Thalia's younger sister and a child of Zeus before they recognized the projects she'd built. Thalia usually tried to compensate by introducing her as the cleverest person in camp. Annabeth loved the intention and hated having to live up to it.

When the portrait was finished, the boy showed it to them. Thalia looked like herself, except the artist had made her chin more severe and drawn the borrowed owl shirt so faithfully that the emblem was visible over her heart.

"Well?" Thalia asked.

Annabeth examined it. "Good likeness. The shoulder's a little high."

The artist laughed. "I know. She moved."

"I was being artistic," Thalia protested.

"You were fidgeting."

Thalia snatched her own copy of the picture and held it beside her face. "Does it look like me?"

Annabeth nodded. The image caught an expression she knew from childhood, or rather from the stories her sister had told her about childhood: Thalia trying to appear dangerous while she was actually pleased to be noticed.

"It does," she said. "And my shirt looks better on you."

"That is a terrible lie."

"I'm very good at them."

The artist thanked them, and Annabeth went to find a proper wedge for the chair. Percy passed with his empty bucket on the way back from the shed. He glanced at Thalia's picture and said it was a good drawing.

"Except the shoulder," Annabeth said.

Thalia groaned. "She's going to say that every time."

"Probably," Percy replied.

Annabeth took the shim from the workshop's scrap box and tested it under the chair. It made the station level enough for the next portrait. The artist thanked her and marked her name beside the little repair note.

Thalia waited while she finished. She did not tell the boy that Annabeth could design better furniture than the entire camp, or that she ought to be asked to build a new station. She let her sister complete a small job and receive thanks for it.

As they left, Annabeth slipped her arm through Thalia's.

"That was fun."

"You mean the portrait or fixing a chair?"

"Both."

Thalia looked suspicious. "You only came because I made you."

"No. I came because you asked."

The distinction pleased them both, though neither said so. At the arena they parted to change into practice shoes, still wearing each other's clothes, with an hour left before lunch and no agreement yet about which one looked more ridiculous.

---

Sparring was Thalia's love language, even if she'd never say it out loud.

The arena was mostly empty by midday — a couple of Ares kids drilling in the corner, Lee Fletcher from Apollo picking arrows out of a target dummy, the son of Poseidon nowhere to be seen, thank the gods. Thalia had told Percy yesterday, very clearly, *if you show up tomorrow I will push you in the lake*, and he'd held up his hands and said, "Hey, sisters day is sacred, I get it," and then asked if they wanted him to pack snacks, which — okay, that had been sweet.

She and Annabeth took the far ring. Pale late-winter sun slanting through the open roof, dust motes dancing, the smell of straw and sweat and bronze.

"Helmets?" Annabeth asked, already pulling hers on.

"Helmets," Thalia confirmed. "And practice blades. No powers. No Aegis. Just —" She twirled her spear — no, practice javelin — "fundamentals."

Annabeth nodded, jaw set. She'd been drilling with Luke for weeks, and it showed. Her stance was clean, shield up, knees bent, eyes locked. Small but fast. Smart. Always thinking three moves ahead.

They circled. Thalia tapped her javelin against her shield. Annabeth feinted left, cut right. Thalia blocked easily, grinning.

"Good. Again."

They went through drills first — high, low, block, riposte — Thalia calling the rhythm, Annabeth answering, bronze ringing. Thalia corrected her grip twice, tapped her foot into place once. Annabeth corrected Thalia's tendency to drop her left shoulder, which Thalia pretended to be annoyed about but secretly loved, because who else at camp would dare tell Thalia Grace her form was sloppy?

Then they sparred for real.

Annabeth was good. Really good. She used her size, slipping under Thalia's reach, shield-bashing Thalia's knee (illegally, both of them knew, both of them ignored it), darting in to tap Thalia's ribs.

"Point!" Annabeth crowed.

"That was a love tap!"

"Point!"

Thalia laughed and came at her harder, spear a blur. Annabeth blocked, stumbled, caught herself, grinned wildly under her helmet. Sweat stuck blonde curls to her forehead. Thalia's heart swelled so big it hurt — look at her, look at her little sister holding her own.

Then Thalia swept Annabeth's legs — gently, she swore it was gently — and Annabeth went down in the straw with an *oof*, and Thalia dropped her practice spear and went to one knee beside her, hand out.

"You good?"

Annabeth grabbed her hand — and yanked.

Thalia face-planted into the straw next to her, helmet clanking. Annabeth shrieked with laughter.

"Oh, it's on," Thalia growled, rolling over and pinning Annabeth's shield arm. "You wanna wrestle, shortstack? Let's wrestle."

"I yield! I yield! Big sister strength, not fair — ah!"

Thalia had found the spot just under Annabeth's ribs that made her squeal. Annabeth thrashed, straw in her fresh haircut, flannel shirt riding up, leather jacket long abandoned on the sidelines.

"Thalia! Stop! I can't — breathe —"

"Say Thalia is the best big sister in the world."

"Never!"

Thalia tickled harder. Annabeth laughed so hard she snorted, which made Thalia laugh so hard she had to stop tickling and just flop over beside her, both of them gasping in the straw, helmets askew, staring up at the arena rafters where pigeons cooed.

Lee Fletcher walked by, looked at them, and said, "Should I come back later?"

"Yes," both girls said together, and he backed away with his hands up.

Annabeth rolled onto her side, cheek on her arm, straw stuck to her lip. She looked younger like this, flushed and happy, gray eyes bright. Thalia reached over and picked the straw out of her hair, one piece at a time.

"You okay?" Thalia asked. "For real? I didn't hurt you?"

Annabeth shook her head. "That was fun. I like when it's just us. No scores. No —" She waved a hand. "No destiny stuff. Just straw and stupid."

Thalia nodded, throat tight. She understood exactly what Annabeth meant. At camp, everything was training for something. Every spar was evaluated, every power measured, every dream potentially a prophecy. It was exhausting, especially for kids like them, children of the Big Three — no, child of Zeus and child of Poseidon, plus Thalia, another child of Zeus — with storms in their blood and oracles watching.

"Yeah," Thalia said. "Just stupid. My favorite."

Annabeth smiled, then yawned hugely. "Phase Five?"

"Phase Five," Thalia agreed. She stood, pulled Annabeth up, handed her the leather jacket. "Come on. I have something to show you."

---


Annabeth noticed the tear when they were leaving the arena.

It wasn't a big one. The seam under the arm of her owl shirt had opened where Thalia had stretched to catch a practice spear. But the pale fabric showed through just beneath the little emblem over the heart, and the sight made Annabeth stop walking.

"Thalia. Your shirt."

Thalia looked down, twisted her arm and saw the gap.

"Oh. Damn."

"That's my favorite."

"I know. Sorry. I'll fix it."

"You don't know how to sew."

"Then I'll learn."

Annabeth would usually have turned the answer into a joke. She was tired after sparring, and the shirt mattered more than she wanted it to. Percy had drawn the trident on the sleeve. She'd added the lightning bolt beside it. It was one of the first things they'd made together after waking in the altered camp, and sometimes she touched the ink when she was frightened that the memories of their older lives would grow too difficult to keep separate.

"I shouldn't have lent it to you," she said.

Thalia stopped. The sentence landed harder than the torn seam had.

"You asked for my jacket," she replied. "You said we should exchange things."

"I know."

"And it was an accident."

"I know that too."

"Then what's the problem?"

Annabeth didn't answer. She hated that she could be entirely correct about an accident and still feel angry with the person who caused it. She wanted her sister to understand the shirt wasn't just an old piece of clothing. She also knew that asking Thalia to wear something and then resenting her for using it wasn't fair.

They walked the rest of the way to Cabin One without speaking. Thalia went to her locker, changed out of the shirt and placed it carefully on the bed between them.

"I said I'd fix it," she repeated.

Annabeth touched the seam. "Silena can do it."

"I'm the one who tore it."

"That doesn't mean you should make it worse by trying to mend something you've never sewn before."

Thalia opened her mouth to argue. Then she shut it and sat on the floor.

"All right. I'll ask Silena to show me."

Annabeth considered whether that was enough. She realized she wanted her sister to care about the shirt, not necessarily to do every part of the repair with her own hands.

"Can I come?" she asked.

"Of course."

They found Silena in the common room of Cabin Three, sorting thread and small metal clasps. She looked at the torn seam and set aside her work.

"This isn't difficult," she said. "It's a straight stitch. The fabric's thin, though. If you pull it tight, it'll pucker."

Thalia took the needle with the wary expression she wore when somebody handed her a weapon she'd never trained with. Silena threaded it, then demonstrated the first two stitches on a scrap.

"Your turn."

Thalia tried. The needle went through the fabric, then through the edge of her thumb.

"Ow."

"That's why I told you to keep your hand out of the way."

"I heard you."

"You didn't listen."

Annabeth watched them. She had been expecting Thalia to give up and demand somebody else finish. Instead her sister tried again, more slowly. The first stitch was uneven. The second was better. After five minutes she could make a row of tiny dark threads across the torn seam.

Silena inspected the result. "That's good enough to hold, if you don't go back to sparring in it."

Thalia turned toward Annabeth. "I won't. Next time we're exchanging clothes, I want a contract."

"With what terms?"

"No sword drills in borrowed shirts. No taking my jacket to the forge. No magic practice with somebody else's sleeves."

Annabeth smiled. "Those are reasonable."

"Don't tell anyone I said something reasonable."

Silena gave them a small tin for storing the spare thread. Thalia asked if she could keep the needle too. Silena said yes, provided Thalia returned the scissors to the sewing box afterward.

They walked back to Cabin One with the repaired shirt folded between them. Annabeth had expected to feel relieved the moment the seam was closed. Instead she felt embarrassed by how sharply she'd spoken.

"I'm sorry I said I shouldn't have lent it to you," she said.

"You were upset."

"That wasn't an excuse."

Thalia looked at her. "I know you like that shirt. I forgot when I put it on. I thought it was funny because it was yours, not because it meant something different to you."

"Both are true."

"Then next time tell me about the second part before we start fighting over clothes."

Annabeth nodded. She had spent the morning trying to make a point about being old enough to choose what belonged to her. It felt appropriate, if irritating, to learn that sharing also meant letting somebody else handle a thing imperfectly.

Thalia put the shirt on Annabeth's pillow. The repair was visible if she looked closely, a little row of dark stitches beneath the owl. Annabeth ran her thumb across it.

"It's still mine," she said.

"Absolutely. And next time you want to borrow my jacket, ask before you steal it."

"You said I could."

"I said you could today."

"Sister law says today counts as precedent."

"Not in any court I respect."

They laughed. The conversation had not made either of them less protective of their favorite possessions. It had given them a way to ask what those possessions meant before using them as proof of how much they loved each other.

Thalia sat on her bed, looked at the old bronze trunk beneath it and hesitated. Then she reached for the key hanging around her neck.

"Come here," she said. "I want to show you something else."

---

Thalia's trunk was a museum.

Annabeth had seen it before, of course — in the weeks she could remember here — but she'd never been invited to really look. Thalia spoke of years sharing this cabin; Annabeth still had to learn its private corners again. Thalia kept it locked with a bronze padlock, key on a cord around her neck. She never said what was inside. Annabeth, who respected locked things on principle (and who had once picked the lock in thirty seconds when Thalia was in the shower, felt guilty, and never told), knew roughly: spare bracelets, a Swiss army knife, a mixtape Luke made her, a dented Polaroid camera with no film.

Now Thalia sat cross-legged on her bunk, trunk open between them, afternoon light pooling gold across the blankets. She'd changed back into her Guns N' Roses shirt, owl shirt folded neatly (neatly, for Thalia) on Annabeth's pillow to return later. Annabeth still wore the flannel and the leather jacket, sleeves rolled, feeling cozy and claimed.

"Okay," Thalia said. "Phase Five. Photos."

She lifted out a shoebox. Inside, loose prints, Polaroids with curling edges, a few newspaper clippings, a pressed flower.

Annabeth leaned in, careful not to touch until invited. "Are these —?"

"My road box," Thalia said. "Everything I kept from before. Luke has one too. His is neater. Mine's —" She gestured. "This."

First photo: a woman with big teased hair and bright lipstick holding a baby with a scowl. Beryl Grace, Thalia said, their mom. Actress, singer, could light up a room and burn it down in the same night. Thalia at maybe one, already frowning.

"She was — she tried," Thalia said quickly, the way she always did. "When she was good, she was really good. Pancakes shaped like Mickey Mouse. Singing in the car. Then Zeus —" She stopped. "Then other stuff. You know."

Annabeth nodded. She knew the broad strokes from Thalia's stories and Beryl's letters. Famous mortal mother, drinking, fear, monsters through the windows. There were good days in the photographs too; she could see them without remembering them.

Next: a gap-toothed Thalia holding a tiny bundle. A baby with wispy blond hair and huge blue eyes, wearing a Superman cape made from a pillowcase.

Annabeth's breath caught. "Jason?"

Thalia's whole face changed. Softened and cracked at once. "Yeah. Jason. He was — gods, he was such a weird little kid. He carried a spoon everywhere for like a year. Called it his sword. He thought thunder was Dad bowling."

Annabeth touched the edge of the photo lightly. "How old was he when —?"

"Two. Just turned two." Thalia stared at the photo. "I was eight. We were in this apartment in LA, and Mom was — not good. And this woman came. Juno, but I didn't know then. She said she'd take him somewhere safe. I tried to stop her. I bit her, I think." A short laugh. "I was eight and I bit a goddess. She just — looked sad. Said it was to protect him. To protect both of us. And then he was gone."

Silence. The cabin clock ticked. Outside, someone laughed by the volleyball net.

"Do you think he hates me?" Thalia asked quietly. "For not — for letting go?"

Annabeth's chest ached. She thought of Bobby and Matthew in the childhood she still remembered, and then of Jason, her brother here too. Thalia had carried the loss for years. Annabeth had photographs and what her sister could bear to tell her, and the guilt of not remembering the little boy for herself.

"No," Annabeth said fiercely. "He was two, Thalia. And you were eight. You didn't let go, you were — you survived. And if he's anything like you, he's strong and he's okay and one day you're gonna find him and he's gonna be so annoying you're gonna wish Juno kept him longer."

Thalia laughed wetly, wiping her eyes with the back of her hand. "Gods, I hope he's annoying. I hope he's the most annoying little brother on the planet."

"He's our brother," Annabeth said. "He has to be."

Thalia set the Jason photo aside, carefully, like it was glass. Underneath: Thalia and Luke, skinny and sunburnt, middle fingers up at the camera, and seven-year-old Annabeth Grace with tangled blonde hair and a bruised knee, glaring at the camera like it had personally offended her.

Annabeth gasped. "Oh my gods, is that me?"

"Yep. First week Luke started traveling with us. You tried to stab Luke with a steak knife because he ate your Pop-Tart."

"I did not!"

"You did. I have the scar to prove it." Thalia pointed to a faint white line on her thumb. "Okay, that scar's from a hellhound, but still."

Annabeth took the photo, holding it like treasure. It echoed the road she remembered — heat, fear, Luke's easy grin, Thalia's fierce eyes — but she couldn't claim this day as a memory. In this childhood Thalia had already been her sister, had already been keeping her safe. She studied the braid over the little girl's shoulder and wanted the story behind it.

"I looked so mad," Annabeth whispered.

"You were so mad," Thalia agreed fondly. "You were mad at the whole world. I loved you before you could even glare properly."

Annabeth looked up, eyes stinging. "Really?"

"Are you kidding? You were this tiny furious blonde thing with a backpack bigger than you, lecturing Luke on proper ambush tactics. I told Luke that night, that one's gonna live forever out of sheer spite."

They laughed, leaning shoulder to shoulder, going through the rest: Thalia's Polaroid of the pine tree on Half-Blood Hill on their first camp morning, Luke's terrible haircut phase, Annabeth asleep on a bus with her mouth open, Thalia asleep against a tree with Annabeth drooling on her even then.

At the bottom of the box, wrapped in a sock, was a framed photo Annabeth had never seen: Beryl holding a small Annabeth on her hip, both laughing, a model biplane in Annabeth's hands, Thalia standing close enough to steady her and Jason peeking from behind their mother's legs.

Annabeth stared.

"You kept this?" she whispered. "I don't remember it. Did I bring it to camp?"

"It fell out of your trunk during capture the flag setup," Thalia said. "I found it in the mud. I cleaned it. I was gonna give it back, but you looked so — I dunno. Like you didn't want to need it. So I kept it safe."

Annabeth held the frame tight. Their mother's smile, Jason's small face, her own missing front tooth. Home, complicated and mortal and hers, even with the day itself missing. Thalia had kept it safe long enough for her to learn what she was looking at.

"Thank you," she said, voice thick.

Thalia bumped her shoulder. "That's what sisters are for. Keeping the stuff you can't carry alone."

---


When Annabeth finally put the photograph down, she discovered that Thalia had stopped looking at it.

Her sister was sitting on the floor with the open trunk between her knees, pretending to sort old ticket stubs. The stub in her hand was from a movie theater in California. She had been turning it over for almost a minute.

"Do you want me to put the picture away?" Annabeth asked.

Thalia looked up. "Why?"

"You're not looking at it."

"I'm looking at the other things."

"Thalia."

Her sister sighed. "I don't want you to think I kept it because I wanted to convince you Mom was wonderful."

Annabeth held the frame against her knees. Beryl was smiling into the camera, and the little girl in her arms looked delighted by something outside the picture. Jason was half hidden by her skirt. Thalia stood close enough to hold Annabeth steady if their mother shifted her weight.

"I didn't think that," Annabeth said.

"You looked happy when you found it."

"I was happy. It has all of us in it."

"That's what worries me. There's a whole afternoon around that picture you can't see."

Annabeth waited. Thalia picked at the paper edge of the ticket stub until a small white strip came loose.

"Mom was drinking again by that evening," Thalia said. "I remember because she took us somewhere afterward and left the restaurant before we'd eaten. She said she had to make a phone call. I sat outside with you and Jason until a waiter brought us sandwiches."

Annabeth looked down at the picture. Beryl's smile had been there earlier that day. The little girl in her arms had been laughing. Both things could be true without telling her what happened later.

"I still like the picture," Annabeth said.

"I know. I just don't want you to decide I lied about the bad parts because there's one nice photograph."

"I don't think you're lying."

Thalia put the ticket stub back into the box. She had spent enough years telling herself she needed to remember every bad decision Beryl made that she sometimes feared the good memories would weaken her judgment. Annabeth had memories of a different mother, a woman who was dead in the altered life and alive in the one Percy remembered. No, that was Percy's complication. Annabeth's own complication was wanting to recognize Beryl without being able to.

"I know you want to write to her again," Thalia said.

"I do."

"And I know you've heard me say no to visits."

"Because Chiron hasn't agreed anything."

"Because I don't trust her to follow through."

Annabeth looked up. "Those aren't the same reason."

"They're both true."

"Then tell me both. Don't act as though the rules are the only thing you care about."

Thalia looked away. The younger sister who had once accepted a blanket and a bedtime story because there was nothing else to give her now wanted an answer with the uncomfortable parts left in.

"I want her to get better," Thalia said finally. "I want her to get so much better that if she offers us something, I don't have to spend the night wondering what she'll do when it goes wrong. But I can't make myself believe that because she's written a few good letters."

Annabeth nodded. She could understand wanting to believe and being unable to.

"Can I keep the picture on my desk?" she asked.

"Yes."

"And can you tell me if you don't like seeing it there?"

Thalia considered. "I can tell you. That doesn't mean you have to hide it."

"Good."

They sat for a while. From outside came the scrape of a practice sword against the arena fence and the sound of somebody calling for a missing sandal. Camp was continuing with the indifference of a place where people had their own work.

Annabeth picked up a small photograph of Jason, separate from the family frame. He was sitting on a floor, holding a spoon like a sword. She smiled despite herself.

"He looks like he was a lot of work."

"He was. He was also very polite. He'd hit you with the spoon and then apologize."

"That does sound like him."

"From his letters?"

"From the Iris-message. He kept apologizing for asking questions, remember?"

Thalia smiled. They had been given one conversation with their brother and were still trying to build a whole relationship out of the pieces it provided. It was hard not to imagine what he'd look like next year, whether his voice would change, whether he would know them when they met in person again.

"We should send him a picture of us," Annabeth said.

"A recent one?"

"Yes. With the haircuts. He can see what we look like now."

Thalia considered. "I'd like that."

"Can we borrow Silena's camera?"

"Ask her. And no posing me like I'm a recruit in a Roman inspection line."

"You stand like that on your own."

"I'm naturally dignified."

Annabeth laughed. She returned the older photographs to their sleeves, pausing over the family portrait. She didn't want it to stand for the whole childhood she'd lost. It could be a picture of one afternoon, good and bad things still happening beyond the frame. That was more useful than making it prove their mother had been one sort of person forever.

She put the frame on her desk beside the photograph of the shrine. Thalia glanced at it, then got up to help her straighten it.

"You're tilting it," Thalia said.

"Because the desk is uneven."

"Then fix the desk."

Annabeth looked at her. "Have you met me?"

"Unfortunately."

They wedged a scrap of cardboard under the shorter leg until the frame stood level. Then they closed the trunk, returned the key to the cord around Thalia's neck, and went to find food for the evening.

---

Dinner was picnic-style on Cabin One's west balcony.

Hal had inspected the railing, approved the folding bench Annabeth had helped design, and made it clear that the old trellis to the roof was still off-limits. Thalia had complained about the rules, then carried the snacks outside through the actual door: two apples, a bag of popcorn from the Apollo cabin stash, and lukewarm Cokes in glass bottles. Annabeth followed with the shoebox and blanket, taking exaggerated care to avoid scratching their newly finished bench.

They sat shoulder to shoulder, backs against the warm cabin wall, watching the sun go down over the Sound. Camp spread below them in golden light — cabins glowing, torches flickering on along the paths, the dining pavilion loud with laughter, the bronze dragon at the amphitheater catching the last sun. The pine tree on the hill stood dark against the sky. The ocean beyond glittered.

Popcorn between them. Annabeth's head on Thalia's shoulder, leather jacket draped over both of them like a blanket. Thalia's arm around her. Easy. Warm.

For a while they didn't talk. They pointed out constellations as they appeared — Orion, the hunter, the eagle of Zeus — and made up stupid stories for the ones they didn't know. That one's a shopping cart, Annabeth declared. That one's Luke's hair, Thalia said, and Annabeth laughed so hard she almost dropped her Coke.

Then quiet settled, soft as the evening breeze. Crickets. Water lapping. Distant singing from the amphitheater.

Annabeth said, very quietly, "Can we do Phase Six now?"

Thalia's stomach tightened. She'd known this was coming. Annabeth's schedules always ended with the real thing.

"Yeah," Thalia said. "Yeah, okay."

Annabeth sat up a little, hugging her knees, jacket slipping. She didn't look at Thalia. She looked at the horizon.

"I'm scared you're gonna leave," Annabeth blurted. Then winced. "No, wait, that's not — I mean —"

Thalia waited. She'd learned with Annabeth that interrupting made her organize her thoughts into sharper, smaller boxes, and what you wanted was the messy version.

Annabeth tried again. "You're sixteen. And sixteen for us — for Big Three kids, for Zeus kids — it's big. The prophecy. The — you know." She twisted the edge of the flannel. "And you're strong, Thalia. You're so strong everyone looks at you like you're already a hero. Luke says you're gonna get a quest any day. Chiron looks at you like he's waiting. And I'm —"

She stopped.

"You're what?" Thalia asked gently.

"I'm twelve," Annabeth said, frustrated. "I'm twelve and I'm smart and I work hard, but I'm not — I can't go with you. Nobody's gonna send a twelve-year-old on a quest with you. And quests change people. Luke's different since his last one. You will be too. You'll grow up and you'll — you'll leave. You'll be an adult hero and I'll still be here doing drills and braiding campers' hair and you'll —"

Her voice cracked.

"You'll forget what it's like to be us. Right now."

Thalia felt like she'd been punched. Not because it was unfair, but because it was exactly what she was afraid of, mirrored.

"Hey," Thalia said, pulling Annabeth back against her side. "Hey, look at me."

Annabeth looked, eyes bright with tears she refused to let fall. Stubborn, brilliant Annabeth.

"I'm not going anywhere tomorrow," Thalia said. "Okay? I'm right here. Roof. Popcorn. Stupid flannel that looks better on you than me."

"Thalia—"

"No, listen. I'm serious. I can't promise I won't get a quest. I can't promise I won't — grow up, I guess. That happens even if you don't want it to." She swallowed. "But I can promise I won't leave you behind on purpose. You're my sister. You hear me? My sister. Not my sidekick. Not my — kid to protect. My sister."

Annabeth stared. Thalia stared back, heart hammering louder than any hellhound.

Then Annabeth whispered, "Can I tell you what I'm really scared of?"

"Always."

"I'm scared you only see me as a kid." The words rushed out. "Like — Forever Nine. Little Annabeth with the steak knife who needs braids and bedtime stories. And I loved that, I did, you saved me, but I'm not nine anymore. I'm twelve. I can fight. I can plan. I have ideas about Daedalus and the Labyrinth and — and people listen to me now, sometimes. Percy listens. Luke listens. But you still — you still carry my bag when I'm tired and you still cut my meat when you think I'm not looking and —"

"I do not cut your meat," Thalia protested.

"You did last Tuesday!"

"That steak was tough!"

Annabeth laughed through tears, shoving her. Thalia caught her hand and held it.

"Sorry," Thalia said. "Go on."

Annabeth wiped her face with her sleeve, leaving a streak. "I just — I want you to be proud of me, not just — fond of me. Like I'm cute. I want to be someone you'd pick for your quest. Not someone you leave at camp to keep safe."

Thalia was quiet for a long moment. The sun dipped lower. A shooting star scratched across the darkening blue.

"You want to know what I'm scared of?" Thalia said finally.

Annabeth nodded, small.

"I'm scared you'll leave," Thalia said. "Not like — run away. Like grow. You're so smart, Annabeth. So much smarter than me. You see buildings in your head, whole cities, you talk about architecture like it's prayer. One day you're gonna build something huge, or you're gonna go on some quest that needs brains, not lightning, and you're gonna realize you don't need a big sister who breaks things and swears too much and can't even do math without crying."

Annabeth's mouth fell open. "Thalia—"

"No, let me finish. I'm sixteen and I still don't know what I'm good for except getting hit by lightning and surviving. You're twelve and you already know. You're gonna outgrow me so fast, and I'm gonna be stuck being the scary Zeus girl while you're off being amazing, and you'll — you'll love me, but you won't need me. And then what am I?"

Her voice broke on the last word. She hated it. She stared hard at the Sound so Annabeth wouldn't see.

Annabeth was quiet. Then she wormed her hand into Thalia's, fingers cold, grip fierce.

"You're my sister," Annabeth said simply. "That's what you are. Even if I'm ninety and building Olympus 2.0 and you're still stealing bacon with your mouth. You'll still be my sister."

Thalia laughed, wet and startled. "Olympus 2.0?"

"With better plumbing," Annabeth said solemnly. "And a library. And a balcony we can sit on without Chiron yelling."

"Chiron's gonna yell anyway."

"Let him."

They sat there, hands linked, shoulders pressed, jacket over their knees. No resolution. No promise that quests wouldn't come or growing wouldn't hurt or that Thalia would ever stop seeing seven-year-old Annabeth with a steak knife when she looked at twelve-year-old Annabeth with a bronze sword. No promise that Annabeth wouldn't one day walk into something Thalia couldn't follow.

Just closeness. Just this balcony, this sunset, this shared blood humming faintly like distant thunder.

Thalia tipped her head back against the chimney. Annabeth leaned her whole weight against Thalia's side, head tucked under Thalia's chin like when she was small. Thalia rested her cheek on top of Annabeth's fresh haircut, breathing in strawberry shampoo and straw and sunshine.

"Hey, Annabeth?" Thalia murmured after a while.

"Mm?"

"You'd be my first pick. For any quest. Brains over lightning, any day."

Annabeth went very still. Then, quietly, fiercely: "Really?"

"Really. I'd be dead in a ditch in two days without you. Who else is gonna tell me my shoulder form is sloppy?"

Annabeth laughed, watery. "Percy would. But he'd be nice about it."

"Percy would fall in the ditch with me."

"True."

More quiet. Stars pricking through. The camp bell for curfew, distant. Neither moved.

"Hey, Thalia?" Annabeth whispered.

"Mm?"

"You're not just the scary Zeus girl. You're — you're the person who braided my hair when I was seven and kept our family's photo safe and let me wear your jacket even though I'm sweaty. You're — home. You and Luke and Percy and camp. You're home."

Thalia closed her eyes. Her throat hurt. Her chest hurt in the good way, like after running, like after laughing too hard in the straw.

"Yeah?" she whispered.

"Yeah. So you don't have to be scared I'll outgrow you. You can't outgrow home. You just — carry it."

Thalia held her tighter. Annabeth held on back. Above them, the sky deepened to violet, then black, stars blazing cold and bright — Zeus's sky, their sky. Below, camp glowed warm.

They stayed until the popcorn was gone and the Cokes were empty and the air turned cool enough that Annabeth shivered and Thalia wrapped the leather jacket fully around her and then wrapped herself around the jacket.

"Come on, shortstack," Thalia said finally, standing and pulling Annabeth up. "Curfew. Hal approved the balcony, not sleeping out here."

Annabeth grinned, sleepy and happy, hair mussed, flannel wrinkled, Thalia's jacket huge on her shoulders. She held the shoebox carefully, the Jason photo on top.

They carried the shoebox and empty bottles through the balcony door. Thalia caught the corner of the blanket on the handle and nearly knocked the whole stack over, which made Annabeth laugh loud enough for someone down the hall to ask whether they were all right. "Perfectly," Thalia called back, rescuing the bottles before they hit the floor.

They brushed teeth side by side in the Salon Storm bathroom, blonde clippings still in the trash, foam on their chins. They changed into pajamas — Annabeth in the owl shirt Thalia gave back (folded, then unfolded, then thrown), Thalia in an old Ramones shirt — and collapsed into Thalia's bunk, which was bigger by two inches and therefore, by sister law, communal property.

Annabeth fell asleep first, head on Thalia's shoulder, one hand fisted in Thalia's shirt, breathing slow and even. Thalia stayed awake a little longer, watching moonlight move across the ceiling, listening to her little sister breathe, to the pine tree whispering on the hill, to thunder far out over the Sound that wasn't scary at all when you were inside with family.

She thought about Jason, somewhere out there, maybe looking at the same moon. She thought about what sixteen was supposed to demand of her. She thought about growing up, quests, leaving, staying.

She didn't solve any of it. She just tucked the blanket around Annabeth's shoulders, pressed a kiss to the top of her fresh haircut, and whispered into the dark:

"Love you, sis."

Annabeth, half-asleep, mumbled back: "Love you more. Sister law."

Thalia smiled, closed her eyes, and slept.
