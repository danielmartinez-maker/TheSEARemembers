## Chapter 189: After the Frost

The thaw came up from underneath.

That was the honest order of it. Nobody watched it arrive and nobody heard it announced, and on Monday night the cold had moved into Cabin Three and taken the foot of Percy's bunk as a lodging, and by Wednesday morning it was being carried out in instalments, quietly, by a weather that had made up its mind somewhere over Canada and had not troubled to tell the lake.

He woke at twenty past five to the sound of water doing what water does when it stops being ice, which is leaving.

Cabin Three's north gutter had frozen shut on Monday and had spent two days being a solid, insulting fact about the world. Now it had opinions again. The drip was not a rhythm yet. It was a negotiation: three drops close together, then a pause, then a long thin run that went on for four or five seconds and thought better of it, then nothing at all, then everything at once. Percy lay still and listened to it the way you listen to a person you are fond of talking in the next room, and worked out from the sound alone that the roof was letting go and that the day was going to be a wet one, and got up, because the other option was lying there doing arithmetic about mud.

The stove was a red heap under a grey skin. He put a split of birch on the embers and watched it take fire in three places at once, which birch always did, and shut the damper down to a crack, and breathed out.

Dressing took four acts, which was Wednesday beating Monday by a mile. Monday had taken nine, because Monday had been a taskmaster and had wanted it done properly. The wool shirt still smelled faintly of the dryers. The gloves with two fingers mended at the knuckle were where he had left them, on the shelf by the door, gone from stiff to merely damp, which was an improvement he had not expected to be capable of noticing and now found he was grateful for. The boots were the problem. The boots had stood in the frost on Monday and spent Tuesday drying out and had come through the whole business with the give of a loaf and the smell of a wet animal, and he worked his feet into them the way you get into a rented pair of skates, by main force and with the door shut behind him so nobody could offer advice.

The porch was wet to the elbow. That was the first real fact of Wednesday. He put his palm flat on the rail and it came away dark and cold, and the rail itself had gone from white and crunchy under the glove to black and willing, and the hook by the door stood empty, because the tenth lantern had come in out of the weather the night before and was sitting on the kitchen table where it belonged, still faintly warm, waiting to be filled again at dusk like everything else on the hill.

The hill had been replaced again.

On Monday somebody with a steady hand had drawn a field of white over all of it in the dark. On Wednesday somebody else, less patient, had taken a wet brush to half of it. In the shade, under the north wall of the pavilion and behind the boathouse and along the base of the slope where the sun had not got to yet, the white was still holding on, gone grey and woolly and tired of itself. Everywhere the light had touched, the ground had come back as brown-black mud and wet grass and wet gravel, and the tracks that had been pressed into the frost on Monday were standing full of water now, black sole-shapes filled to the brim, each one a small honest cup of what had happened two days ago.

Percy went down the west path. The boards squelched. That was the other sound of the day: not the drip but the squelch, and under it another sound, which was the whole hill quietly draining, the small tickle of runnels in the ditch, the gulp of the culvert at the bend taking more than it had taken in a fortnight.

The lamps were out. Somebody had relit nine of them for Tuesday, because Tuesday was Tempest Hall and rule five said whoever wanted to come came, and the second one of those had gone the way the first one had gone, with steam on the windows and fourteen on a slate and a boy who had brought his own towel, and by midnight the wind had taken four of the lamps and nobody had been out to relight them in the frost. Now the brackets stood empty along the hill with the wax beads dripping and the soot thumbprints running, and the path without its lamps was the path he would have known blindfolded in July, and he went down it at a walk with his hands in his pockets and his breath going up almost straight.

The lake had let go of its own edge somewhere around three.

He heard it from the third bend, and knew it even while he was still half asleep enough to think it was a dream, because it was a sound with a long tear in the middle of it: the ice shelf that had grown out from the shore all Monday night pulling free of the bank in one sheet, going out a yard, going out three, turning over on itself in the dark and grinding along the mud. Then the reeds started up. The reeds had each grown their own sleeve of ice in the frost, a clear tight crust standing out from every stem, and with the shelf moving under them again they began to knock, and the knocking went down the whole edge of the lake in a dry little rattle like beads shaken on a string, on and on, four or five seconds at a time, stopping, starting again, and it was the sound of the water coming back.

At the boathouse he stood a while and looked at it, because it was worth looking at.

The grey plates were still going out. Some of them were the size of a door and some the size of a hand and they went slowly, like things that had been asked to leave and were doing it with dignity, and where they had lain the water was open and black and moving, and the lake had stopped being a thing that absorbed light and had gone back to being a thing that held it, and the first weak light of the thaw morning came off the water and up the sand and hit the boathouse wall in a long wavering stripe that was not there on Monday and had not been there for a week.

Six canoes on the rack past the boathouse, upside down and roped, where they had been since the end of September. Monday's frost had grown on their rings and their ring-bolts and the little brass pull on each drain plug, a fine erect fuzz standing off the iron in every direction. The fuzz was gone. What was left was bare dark iron and green paint and a lot of water, and the ice that had been a hollow tube on his glove had come off in the night or was lying in crumbs along the sand under the hulls, and Percy hooked a finger through the nearest ring and found it cold in the honest way, wet and simple, the way metal is cold when it is only metal.

He got the shovel off the wall and cleared the ice chips out of the channel, which was the sort of job that is worth doing for its own sake on a morning when everything is letting go of everything else. There were more chips than he expected. The frost had made a shelf along the inside of the boathouse wall, thick at the bottom and thin at the top, and it had come away in the night in one piece and lay across the doorway in a slab the shape of the door, and he broke it with the shovel into four pieces and threw them out into the yard where they lay and steamed and were nothing, and by the time he was done his hands were wet to the wrist and his breath was going up thin and quick.

"You shovel first and ask questions never," said a voice from up the sand. "That's a documented condition too, isn't it."

Annabeth was standing on the path above the boathouse with the field book under her arm and mud to the ankle on one boot and the other boot dry, which meant she had been on the north path and had come back by the long way round, and she had her hair doing the thing it did when it had been rained on and then left to its own opinions, which was to go half curly at the ends and stick out at the back where the collar had been. She was not wearing a hat. She had not been wearing a hat for a week and a half and had taken the position that hats were for people who could not be trusted to notice weather.

"You've got mud on one boot," Percy said.

"I know which boot."

"How's the flow line."

Annabeth came down the last few steps and put the field book on the gunwale of the nearest canoe and opened it on the cover the way she always did, one-handed, without looking, the way other people flip a coin. She turned it round so it faced him. The page was the north spring page, the one with a hundred and four numbers on it and CORR in the margin and her initials beside the corrected readings, and along the bottom somebody had drawn a dotted line that was not a number and had never pretended to be.

"I walked it from the top," she said. "From the plate of granite. Down the terrace, through the pines, out at the wet corner of the north path where nobody knew it came out until Saturday." She put two fingers on the dotted line. "In October, before the frost, the ground was dry enough that I had to guess about forty feet of it. I pencilled it. I was honest about the pencil. And this morning, Percy, this morning the ground was wet enough that the whole line was standing water."

"That's the whole line."

"That's the whole line. Not guessed. Not pencilled. There was a stream of it going down the path with no bank on either side, just the water finding the low side of the gravel and running, for about forty feet, in the exact place the pencil said, and then it went into the ditch and I lost it at the culvert like a coward."

"You lost it at the culvert," Percy said, "like a person who had already got the thing."

"You can ink a line you've seen," Annabeth said. "You can't ink a line you liked the look of." She shut the book. She was glowing in the particular way she glowed when a piece of the world had agreed with her, which was not a smile and was not a performance, and was the thing he had spent a whole other life learning to look for, and he looked for it now, and found it, and put it in the pile.

"Chiron will want to see it."

"Chiron will want to see it and Chiron will be told about it at some point today, and I am not walking up to the Big House at half past six in the morning to have a conversation about drainage with a man who has been awake since four." She picked up the field book. "Where's Silena."

"Asleep. Clarisse asleep. Chloe asleep, which is a rare and beautiful thing, and four other people asleep, and I didn't ask who. I came out to hear the gutter."

"And?"

"And it's coming apart. The roof's letting go. It's going to be a wet one." He looked at the lake. The beads went on knocking down in the reeds, thinner now, further apart. "The shelf went at about three. I heard it from the third bend. It went out and turned over and made a noise like a bed being stripped."

Annabeth stood beside him and looked out at the water with her hands in her coat pockets and her chin down, and the light came up off it onto her face, and neither of them said anything for a bit, because there are things you say by standing next to somebody.

"The skiff's here," Percy said, after a while.

"I know."

"The heron's on it."

"I saw. It's been there since the flood."

He had not expected that to land the way it did. It was the sort of thing you could say about a bird, and she had said it about a bird, and it had not sounded like a bird thing at all, and he looked over at the skiff, and the heron was standing on the bow as though it had never been anything other than part of the boat, grey and unhurried and entirely unimpressed by the two of them, and it did not move when he walked a little nearer, and it did not move when he stopped being obvious about walking nearer, and he stopped being obvious about walking nearer.

"Right," Annabeth said, in a completely level voice, and turned to look up the sand. "Right. Clarisse is on the porch with a clipboard, and if we don't go up now she's going to come down here and add us to something."

She had got four steps before he caught her hand.

That was the thing he did without deciding to do. Her fingers were cold, and not cold in the ordinary October way, which was a way that warm water fixed in about a minute; they were cold the way melt-water leaves you, deep and in the joints, because she had been out in it since before the light with a pencil line in her head. He closed both hands around the one hand and then found he had both of hers, because she had let him, and she let him, and stood there on the path above the boathouse in the middle of a hill in the middle of a thaw with her hands inside his and her field book pinned under her arm, and did not take them away for a long moment.

"You're freezing," he said.

"You're warm," she said. "You're always warm. It's unfair and it's one of the main things I hold against you."

"I'm a Poseidon. I run hot."

"You run everything hot. It's a documented condition and I've filed it under facts."

"Under facts," Percy said, and she laughed, the real one, the short one that came out before she could tidy it, and he put her hands against his chest under the coat and held them there with both of his, and bent his head, and she tipped her face up at the same time, and they kissed in the middle of the path with the whole wet hill behind them and the reeds knocking in front of them and their breath going up into the same cold air.

It was not a long kiss. It was the other kind, the kind that had been getting longer since June: a first minute that was mostly breath and cold lips and the knowledge of exactly where the other person's hands were, and a second minute that was all warmth and arrived, and then she pulled back the width of a breath and looked at him with her eyes bright and her mouth still cold and said, "That's not the one," and he said, "No," and she said, "Good," and let him keep her hands a moment longer before she took them back.

"Go up," she said. "I'll be up in ten minutes. I've got the wet corner to think about."

"You've got the wet corner inked."

"I've got the wet corner inked," Annabeth said, "and I want ten minutes to stand there and be smug about it where nobody can see my face."

"That's a documented condition."

"Everything is a documented condition," Annabeth said, and shouldered the field book, and started up the sand, and got six steps, and stopped, and looked back at him with her chin over her shoulder in the grey light, and the expression on her face was the one he had been collecting since he was twelve years old and had not known he was collecting.

"Wednesday," she said.

"Wednesday," he said.

"Don't let Clarisse give you a chore," she said, and went on up the path, and the squelch of her boots in the wet gravel went away around the bend, and he stood at the boathouse with his hands still warm from her and watched the ice go out.

Annabeth stood at the wet corner of the north path for ten minutes and was smug about it, because she had earned the smugness and because there was nobody there to see her face.

It was worth ten minutes. It would have been worth an hour. The line that had been a pencil guess in early October was running in front of her in the gravel with no bank on either side, just the melt-water finding the low side and going down it in a flat brown sheet the width of a hand, and she had been wrong in her head about where it would go twice and right once, and the right once had been the one that mattered, and she stood there with her boots in the runoff and the field book shut under her arm and let herself have the whole ten minutes without apologising to anybody for it.

Then the ten minutes were up and she walked up the hill, because the thaw did not care about a girl's smugness and there were things the thaw was doing to the buildings.

The greenhouse bank was the first one. The bank stood above the greenhouse on the north side where the ground fell away, and on Monday it had been a white sculptured thing, beautiful and unhelpful. By Wednesday it was a wet brown slope with a scar of black across the middle of it where the frost had been holding the soil together and had now stopped holding it together, and a thin grey tongue of it was running down the scar and spreading at the bottom into a fan of silt, and Annabeth stood and looked at it the way an architect looks at a wall that has just told her something. The bank had not failed. The bank had told her it would like to be dealt with before it failed, and it had done so politely, and she wrote it on the inside cover of the field book in the small hand she used for things that were not numbers.

She went on up past the greenhouse itself. The glass was running with condensation on the inside, and every low pane held a bead of it in the corner, and where the sun had got onto the glass the beads were already gone and the panes were clear and the greens inside showed through as ordinary greens, which after Monday's white was a relief that surprised her. The frost-wilted things would be in the pots by the door, and somebody would be making soup out of them by the afternoon, and Annabeth knew this with the certainty of a person who had watched Camp Half-Blood do it every autumn of her life, twice.

The fallen pine came next. It had come down in a wind in September and nobody had got to it yet, and it lay across the upper clearing with its roots up in a plate of earth the size of a table, and the plate had filled with melt-water and become a shallow brown pond, and the pond was leaking out one corner in a thread that ran down the trunk and dripped off the butt. The wood was dark and soft and soaking, and Annabeth put her hand on it as she passed and found it cold the way a wet thing is cold and not the way Monday had been cold, and took her hand away again, and left the pine where it was, because the pine was on a list and the list was not a Wednesday list.

The torn awning was the last of the three. It hung over the north wall of the pavilion with one corner of its canvas ripped and tied up out of the way with a length of baler twine, and the tear had made a pocket in the canvas, and the pocket had filled with three days of rain and one night of melt, and it was now a grey sagging bag of water with a slow drip coming off the lowest point, and it dripped onto a stone below it that had been worn into a cup by doing exactly this every autumn for longer than Annabeth had been coming to camp. She looked at it for a while, and put it away, and went on, and did not tell anybody about the awning at all, because the awning was on a list too, and the list's owner would know.

She went past the Big House on the way up, and she did not go in, and she thought about the door at the back of Chiron's office with the flat shallow drawer built into the desk, and the lock that had been locked since a Thursday in September, and the thirty-nine marks she had counted twice herself and written into the front of the field book and then closed the book with the number inside it. Thirty-nine. The space for a fortieth had been there since the beginning, ruled and empty, the way a ruled line is empty, and she had not looked since the count, and she was not going to look today, and the not-looking had become its own small private ritual, the way a knot tied in a string becomes a thing you do with your fingers without meaning to.

She had got as far as deciding to go and see whether the pavilion had got a fire in it when the voice came down off the wall.

"You're standing in the drainage."

Thalia was sitting on the wall above her with her knees up and her elbows on them, in a coat that had seen better weeks, with a jar of something steaming in one hand and a second jar beside her on the stone, which meant she had been there long enough to make two, which meant she had come out to find her sister and had been waiting for her to come up the hill. Annabeth felt the warm thing start in her chest that always started when Thalia had been thinking about her, because it was not a thing she had grown up with and she had not grown out of it, and there was a part of her, not going anywhere, that counted it every time.

"You're sitting on the wet wall," Annabeth said.

"It's the only dry wall on the hill. That's why I'm on it." Thalia handed her the jar. It was tea, strong, with honey in it, and somebody's flask of it, and it was the best thing Annabeth had put in her hands all morning. "Clarisse's got the pavilion stove going. She's been in and out of there for an hour. She thinks she's being subtle."

"She is not subtle."

"She once told me I was 'in the way' by writing it on a slate and holding it up," Thalia said. "That was in front of eleven people. I have never recovered and I never intend to."

Annabeth climbed up onto the wall — it was wide enough, four courses of granite laid by somebody a hundred years ago who had not expected traffic, and Thalia had put her coat folded across the top of it — and sat down with the jar in both hands and her boots hanging, and the two of them sat there in the thaw with the steam coming off the tea and the whole wet hill laid out below them, and the drip off the pavilion roof going on behind them in its three-and-a-pause, and the grey afternoon that was not warm but had stopped being unkind.

"How's the north path," Thalia said.

"Wet. It's the whole wet. It's standing water for forty feet in the exact place the pencil said, and I'm going to ink it this afternoon, and you're going to say something nice about it."

"I'm going to say something extremely nice about it," Thalia said, "and then I'm going to say that you'll want to check it again after the next rain, because a line that runs once is a line that got lucky."

Annabeth looked at her sideways. "That's annoyingly good advice."

"I'm full of it. It's my best feature." Thalia drank her tea. She was sitting with her left leg drawn up under her and her weight very clearly on the right side, and she had been doing it, Annabeth realised, since August — sitting in positions that kept a boot off the ground, and Annabeth had put it down to long legs and habits and the general business of a girl who had taken a hill's worth of responsibility on at fifteen and had never once been thanked for it. "What about you. You've got a face on."

"I have not got a face on."

"You've got the face you get when you've decided something about your boyfriend and you haven't decided whether you're going to tell him."

Annabeth took a long drink of the tea so she would have something to do with her mouth. "I decided something about my boyfriend in the summer," she said, when she could. "It's been decided since June. It's a settled question. It's been settled for sixteen months and it's going to stay settled and I would like it entered on a slate."

"Entered on a slate," Thalia said. "Good. That's where it belongs, next to the awning." She tipped her head back and looked at the sky, and the thaw light came down onto her face, and she squinted into it, and her voice went off into the dry register she used for things that mattered, which was a register Annabeth had spent a long time learning to hear. "You know I'm going to be insufferable about him. You know that. It's in the contract."

"It's in the contract."

"I'm allowed to be insufferable and I'm allowed to be glad," Thalia said. "Those are two different clauses and I intend to use both." She shifted on the wall, and it was a small movement, a settling of the hips and a drawing up of the left knee that took the weight off a boot, and she did it the way you do a thing you have done ten thousand times, and Annabeth saw it and did not see it, and filed it under the cold, and the cold is where Thalia had been filing it since August.

"I put the note in the field book again," Annabeth said.

"You've got both of them, then."

"I've got his in the back cover. He's got mine in his coat pocket, which he tells people about, in case they were worried it wasn't true."

"Tell him it's going to give him a complex."

"He's got a complex. He's got a whole stack of them, they're numbered and cross-referenced, and I keep his in the front pocket of the field book, where I keep all of them." Annabeth turned the jar in her hands. The tea was going down and the jar was going warm, and she liked the weight of it, the ordinary weight of a warm thing in cold hands. "He's got a letter coming Thursday. He thinks I haven't noticed him waiting."

"Everybody's noticed him waiting. The whole hill has noticed him waiting. Chiron asked me whether the boy was always like this and I told him yes, and then I felt disloyal, and then I got over it."

"Did you feel disloyal or did you feel caught?"

"Both," Thalia said comfortably. "Both is what sisters are for. It's in the contract too." She drained the jar and set it on the stone, and knocked her boot against Annabeth's boot, once, which was the whole of the affection and was enough of it. "Soup tonight. I can smell it from here and it's not even on yet. That's how good it's going to be."

"Silena's doing the greens from the greenhouse," Annabeth said. "The frost ones are sweeter. She says it every year and she's right every year."

"She's right every year about everything except men," Thalia said, and then stopped, and the two of them looked at each other in the complete silence of two girls who had both thought of the same boy in the same second, and Thalia's mouth went into the shape of a person who has said something she did not intend to say, and Annabeth, who had never in her life been able to leave a thing alone, put her hand over her sister's hand on the stone between them and let it sit there, and did not say anything at all, which was the hardest thing she had done that week.

"He's got a bench at six," Thalia said, at last, to the sky.

"He's got a bench at six," Annabeth agreed.

"And he's shy, and she's shy, and they're both so shy it's a wonder anybody can find them." Thalia turned her hand over under Annabeth's, which was a thing Annabeth had not known Thalia did with anybody, and had done with her since she was small and frightened and had nothing else. "I'm not going to say anything about it. I just want it entered somewhere that I noticed and I'm not blind and I'm not making it a thing."

"Entered," Annabeth said. Her throat was doing something. "Entered on the slate, next to the awning."

"Next to the awning," Thalia said, and picked up the second jar, and found it had gone cold, and drank it anyway, because that was what Thalia did with things that were not perfect.

The stock pots went on at half past eleven, and the making of them was a thing Silena did with her whole body, the way other people sang.

The pavilion kitchen was the only real kitchen on the hill, and on a thaw day it became the warmest room in the world by two o'clock and stayed that way until midnight, and everybody knew it, and nobody ever wrote it down, and the writing of it down would have spoiled it. The stove was a big cast-iron thing on the north wall that Chiron's grandfather's predecessor had put in, and it had a range top with two lifted lids and a damper that stuck and a hook on the wall above it for hanging a pot, and the hook's bracket had been loose since the summer and nobody had fixed it, and Silena had been meaning to say something about it for six weeks.

She had the greens up to her elbows. The frost-wilted ones from the greenhouse, the kale and the chard and the tough outer leaves of the cabbage, all of them sweeter for having been through Monday's night and all of them lying in a heap of white-tipped water in the sink, and Silena was washing them in three changes, which was the rule, because a green out of a greenhouse in October carries sand and there is nothing worse than sand in a soup that took four hours.

"You're doing them one at a time," Clarisse said, from the doorway, with a crate of roots on her hip.

"I'm doing them properly."

"You're doing them one at a time like they're letters from somebody you like."

Silena did not look up. "Maybe I am. Maybe I like the greens. Have you got a problem with it."

"I've got a problem with the fact that the bar and I are ready and you've been at the sink since eleven." Clarisse set the crate down and looked into it with the satisfaction of a woman who had got a thing off a shelf. "Carrots from the sand bin, four parsnips, two leeks, the last of the onions. They're not pretty. Frost gets into the shoulders of them and they go soft and they taste better."

"They do," Silena said. "Frost gets into everything and makes it better. It's the whole argument, and I've made it at this sink four years running, and one year you'll stop arguing with me about it."

"I'm not arguing. I'm describing." Clarisse pulled a stool over with her boot and sat down on it in the way she always sat, which was like a person sitting on a fence, ready to leave. She had the clipboard under her arm out of habit, and the winter list on it, and the list had three lines on it that wanted a warm dry day and had now had one. "The bank's running."

"I know."

"Saw it on the way down. Grey tongue down the middle of it, and a fan at the bottom. It's fine today and it won't be fine forever."

"It's on the list."

"It's on the list," Clarisse said, and said it like a woman settling a debt. "Everything's on the list. That's what lists are for. Does this pot want more water."

"Yes. Two buckets. Not cold-cold. Cold-cold takes the fire down for ten minutes."

Clarisse went out to the pump and came back with two buckets and put them on the floor by the stove where they would take the heat off the iron, and did not say anything about it, and Silena, who had known this girl since she was nine years old and had been frightened of her for four of those years, felt the old warm thing move in her chest and moved on with the greens.

The soup was not a recipe. It was a method, and the method was: everything the frost had touched, everything the garden had finished with, everything in the sand bin that was going soft, and a bone if you had one, and time. Silena had a bone. It had come out of the Big House freezer in a cloth bag with a bit of twine round it and she had put it in cold water with an onion cut in half and a bay leaf and a teaspoon of peppercorns and had brought it up slow while the greenhouse got light, and by eleven the water had gone the colour of weak tea and the whole pavilion kitchen smelled of it, and that smell was the first hour of a thaw day and no other hour smelled like it.

She tipped the greens in in three lots, because if you put them all in at once the water stops and the pot sulks, and she pushed them down with a wooden spoon, and the water came back up, and the steam came off it and went onto the window, and the window went from clear to milk to running, and by the time the second lot was in there were two other people at the table with their boots off and their feet on the rail and nobody had noticed them come in.

Chloe got there first. She had been sleeping in Cabin Three since the storm, which was not her cabin and had never been her cabin, and she had attached herself to the stove the way a cat attaches itself to a warm brick, and she sat on the stool with her chin on her hands and watched the pot and made a running commentary that nobody had asked for and everybody liked.

"It's going to be green."

"Not green," Silena said. "Brown-green. Soup isn't green. Green is for salads and salads are a summer idea."

"Salads are a summer idea," Chloe repeated, in the tone of a person committing a scripture to memory.

"Summer is a summer idea," Silena said, and put the barley in, and stirred it, and looked out the window at the wet grey of the hill. "This is October. In October everything that grew gets put in a pot with something that swam, and we eat it out of bowls twice the size of our heads, and we do it again in November, and by December nobody complains about the weather any more because we've all stopped caring."

"That's a lie," Clarisse said. "I complain about the weather in December."

"You complain about the weather in July."

"I complain about the weather in July," Clarisse agreed, without any shame at all. "And in August. And today, because I've got three lines on a page that want a dry day, and a dry day isn't coming until Saturday, and Saturday's going to be taken up with windows."

"Windows are a Saturday job," Silena said.

"Windows are a Saturday job," Clarisse said. "I put them on a page on the second of October and they've been on the page ever since, and every week I move them down the page and every week they end up back at the top." She looked at the pot. "That's going to be the good one, isn't it."

"It's going to be the soup," Silena said. "It's never the good one. It's just the soup, and it's what's for Wednesday."

"It's the good one," Clarisse said, and got up off the stool, and picked up an empty bucket, and stopped in the doorway with her hand on the frame, and said, without turning round, in the voice of a girl who had had a whole family invented for her when she was twelve and had spent three years being suspicious of it and had spent the last year being unable to say so, "It smells like the day Grandma Rose used to make it. That's all. I'm not making a thing of it."

"You're making a thing of it," Silena said, gently.

"I know," Clarisse said. "It's Wednesday. I'm allowed." And she went out with the bucket, and through the window Silena watched her cross the yard to the pump in the rain with her collar up and her boots going squelch, squelch, all the way, and did not smile, because Clarisse would have felt it at that distance, and went back to the greens.

The thaw got into Cabin Three at half past three, and it got in through the door, and it came in the form of four children and six litres of mud.

Percy had had exactly eleven minutes to himself. He had come up the hill with his gloves stiffening on his hands and his boots making a noise at every step, and he had got the door open, and he had got one glove half off, and then the door had come open behind him and the hill had come in with it.

They were not Cabin Three kids. He would know them anywhere; he had been living with the same four since June and could tell their boots apart by the sound on the boards. These were the ones who had been out on the ice since first light, who had been out on the ice every day since Monday, who had been walking on the lake since it froze and had not been told twice, and who had come back up the hill with the thaw going out from under them and had arrived in the proportion of mud to child that a person arrives in when a lake edge lets go of its own crust at the moment you are standing on it.

"Boots," Clarisse said, from the table, without looking up from the clipboard. "Boots in the hall. Nobody takes one step."

"Nobody takes one step," four voices said back, in the tone of people who had been told before.

The hall was the four feet between the door and the stove, which on a good day was the warmest four feet on the hill and on a thaw day was a river bottom. The mud came off in sheets. It came off the soles and off the uppers and it came off in one solid piece from each boot like a sock, and it lay on the boards in four grey coils, and then the children stepped out of them and stood on the boards in their socks and left behind them six prints per boot that were going to be there until somebody got a bucket.

"That's a crate on the left," Silena said, coming through from the kitchen with her arms full of wet towel. "Percy, that's a crate on the left, and there's a plank behind the water butt, and the two of you can put them together before you tell me you can't."

"I didn't say I couldn't."

"You were going to say you couldn't. You were going to say it in a thoughtful way."

"I was going to say it thoughtfully," Percy admitted, and got the crate and the plank, and the four wet children stood in their socks and watched the two of them build a boot bench in the hall of Cabin Three, and the boot bench was: a crate, overturned; a plank, laid across it; and a towel over the plank, and it was the single greatest invention of the autumn and nobody had ever thought of it in June or July or August, because until Wednesday there had not been a month in living memory in which the same boot could get wet twice.

The boots went on the towel in a row. Two pairs were small and went at the left end, and one pair was enormous and went at the right end, and one pair was somebody's older sister's and were clearly borrowed and nobody said anything about it except Clarisse, who said, "Whose are those."

"They're mine," said the biggest of the four, in the voice of a boy who had been caught in a small lie and intended to stand by it.

"They're your sister's."

"They're mine now."

"They're hers on Saturday and they'll be hers on Saturday because I'll bring them up the hill myself in a sack." Clarisse wrote something on the board. "That's two pairs. Four pair. Six. Good. Six pairs of boots and I want six pairs of socks on the rail and nobody puts a wet sock back on a foot in this cabin. That's the rule, that's the law, and it's been the law since a Tuesday in the summer when somebody lost a toe to it."

"They never lost a toe," Silena said.

"They nearly lost a toe."

"A toe is a strong word for what nearly happened," Silena said, and took the first pair of socks off the smallest of the four while she was still talking, and peeled it off, and hung it on the stove rail where it steamed, and went on to the second without waiting to be thanked, and the second of the four said, "I can do it," and Silena said, "You can do it after I've done it," and did it, and hung it on the rail, and by then the rail had six pairs of wool socks on it and they were all steaming gently in the heat off the stove and the whole room smelled of wet wool going dry, which is a smell that gets into a person's clothes and stays there and is the smell of October more than any woodsmoke ever was.

Percy got his own socks off. He had not been asked, but the sock-rail was a public institution and there were rules, and he sat on the bottom step of the stairs with his elbows on his knees and peeled off two pairs, the inner pair and the outer pair, and hung them at the end of the rail with the other six pairs, and they were the heaviest thing there and they dripped onto the top of the stove and went *tss*, and the whole cabin laughed at him in a way that was entirely loving and entirely unbearable.

"Look at that," Clarisse said, to the room at large, with the clipboard under her arm. "That's a man who stood in a mountain stream for a hundred minutes in the frost. I'm not letting it go. I'm bringing it up at Christmas."

"You're bringing it up at Christmas," Percy agreed.

"With the people I don't like there."

"With the people you don't like there," Percy agreed, "and I'd like it noted that I did it for the wall."

"You did it for the girl," Silena said, from the kitchen doorway, and he caught the towel she threw him without looking at it, because a year of towels had made that a reflex, and she went back into the kitchen, and Clarisse said, "I did it for the wall" in a mocking sing-song voice at nobody at all, and wrote on the board, and Percy dried his face and covered for her by putting the smallest of the four children upside down.

The smallest one, whose name was Eddie and who was nine years old and had been sleeping in Cabin Three since the storm along with the rest of them, was found under a wet coat on a chair with his boots gone.

"They were right there," Eddie said, from inside the coat.

"They were on the bench. I put them on the bench. I put you on the bench." Percy lifted the coat off him. "Where are your boots, Edmund."

"They were right there."

"They were right there," Percy said, "and now they're in another dimension. That's the theory you're going with, and I'd like the record to show that I support it." He looked under the bench. He looked under the chairs. He looked behind the water butt, and found one boot standing upright by itself in a puddle with the top of it just visible, which was the sort of thing that happened in Cabin Three the way it happened in the sea. "One. Where's the other."

"Try the loft."

"The loft is four feet off the ground and you are three feet six inches tall."

"Try the loft," Eddie said, with the confidence of a boy who had put a boot in a loft.

Percy went up the loft ladder and looked, and it was in the loft, and it had not been thrown, and it had not been dropped, and it had gone in through the open hatch and landed in the middle of the floor with the sole up, which was not possible, and Percy had stopped saying that out loud about Cabin Three months ago. He brought it down and held it up by the ankle and looked at it, and the room applauded in the way that a room of wet children applauds a small miracle, and he put it on the bench next to its fellow, and it sat there steaming slightly in its own small way.

"Six pairs," Clarisse said, closing the board. "Plus mine. Plus yours."

"Plus mine."

"That's a documented number," Clarisse said. "It's been a documented number since the summer and it's going in the pile." She put the clipboard down and picked up a towel and threw it at his head with the flat, affectionate, entirely unerring aim of a sister, and it hit him in the middle of the face and hung there, and he stood with a wet towel on his head and four wet children laughing at him and the stove going behind the wall and the whole room going white at the window, and thought, in the middle of it, that this was the room in the world he would have described if somebody had asked him to describe home, and that he would have had to put in the mud.

"You've still got one boot on," Silena said, coming out with a basket.

"I've got one boot on because I'm about to go out again."

"Nobody's out again. The pump bucket's on the step and the awning's dripping into the cup in the stone and everything that can be fetched can be fetched by somebody with a boot on. Sit down." She put the basket down and took his remaining boot off him with the same efficiency she had taken the socks off the nine-year-old, and hung the inner sock on the rail and put the outer one next to it, and said, "Don't argue with me about the boot. You've got cold feet in the lake. You're fourteen. You can be cold in the lake again in the morning."

"I don't want to be cold in the morning," Percy said, "I want to be warm in the morning."

"Then take your boots off when somebody tells you," Silena said, "and stop standing in puddles in the hall. The hall is not a puddle." She went back into the kitchen, and he sat on the bottom step with a towel round his shoulders and the stove ticking behind the wall, and felt the day come back into him through his feet, which was a thing heat did slowly and in the right order: feet first, then knees, then the small of the back, then everything a person had been holding in their shoulders since Monday.

The pavilion was full by six, and it was the warmest room on the hill, and Annabeth stood in the doorway of it for a second before she went in because it looked like something.

It looked like a picture of a thing that did not happen any more. The windows had gone milk and were running in the corner, and the air was a physical thing you could see, a slow grey column going up to the ceiling and spreading and coming back down, and everybody in the room was in their shirtsleeves with their coats off, and the coats were not in a heap. They were on the backs of chairs and on the pegs by the door and over the tops of the hat rail and on the sill, and out of the corner of her eye Annabeth counted nineteen before she stopped counting, and they were all steaming.

The smell was the soup and it was the smell of the pot on the stove: the greens, dark and far down, and the barley cloudy, and something that had once been a bone, and a lot of pepper, and the sour-sweet of the vinegar somebody had put in at the end, and the steam carried all of it to the door where she stood and she breathed it in and felt her whole body stop hurrying.

There were four long tables and they were all four full, and the noise of a full pavilion on a thaw evening is a specific noise and it is not the noise of a dining room: it is the noise of a room where every window is running and every person inside has come in out of weather and does not intend to go back out, and so the voices are louder and slower and more content, and the benches scrape and cups go down, and nobody leaves.

Clarisse was at the stove with a long ladle and a look of a woman on a gallows. She had taken her jacket off, she had her sleeves pushed up, and she was serving in movements that were faster than she could talk, and the line went down the middle of the room and back again, and everybody got a bowl that was too big for them and everybody had been told to take bread.

Percy was at the end of the second table with his feet out in the aisle and a bowl in front of him the size of a small animal, and he looked up and saw her in the doorway and did not do anything about it except let his face do it, and Annabeth felt it go through her like a warm hand on the back of the neck.

"You're late," he said, when she got to him.

"I was at the sink. Silena's had the greens in three changes and I carried two."

"You carried two?"

"Two changes. Then I stood in the steam and let her do the third, which was the correct division of labour." She took the chair beside him, which was her chair now, which had been her chair since the second week of July, and she put her bowl down and found that her hand had been taken by the time it got there.

That was all it was. He had a hand on the table and she had put hers down near it and that had become holding, and neither of them had said anything about it, and this was the thing that none of the rest of the room would ever know and that the two of them would never think of mentioning: that they had built a language out of hands in a year and a half and were entirely fluent, and that right now, on a wet night in October, the sentence he was saying with three fingers on the inside of her wrist was something like *good*, and the sentence she was saying back with the flat of her hand against his was *good*.

The soup was the good one. Everybody had a second bowl, and the second bowl was a formality and everybody knew it, and Clarisse kept the ladle going with a face like a woman being paid for it, and Chiron rolled in from the Big House and sat with a cup and watched the room with the particular satisfaction of a man who had organised nothing and was prepared to accept the credit, and Mr. D stood in the doorway for ninety seconds with a cup of something that definitely was not soup and made several remarks about mud, and then went away again when nobody paid him for it.

Then the hook moved.

It was a small thing and it happened while Annabeth was eating. The iron hook on the wall above the stove, the one the big pot hung from when it was not on the range, had a bracket at the wall, and the bracket had been loose since the summer, and Silena had been meaning to say something about it for six weeks, and what happened was that the hook dipped about an inch while Clarisse was serving out of the pot that was standing on the range, and the pot was fine, and the hook held nothing, and the hook holding nothing was the only reason nobody noticed.

Except Annabeth noticed, because Annabeth noticed structural things the way other people noticed music, and she looked up from the soup with her eyebrows doing something, and had got as far as opening her mouth to say *the bracket*, when the door opened.

Charlie Beckendorf came in backwards with a tin under one arm.

He was wet. That was the first fact and it was the fact everybody in the room noticed, because he had the specific complete wetness of a boy who has been out in weather on purpose, with his shoulders dark and his hair plastered and his boots pasted at the ankle, and he came in and shut the door behind him with his hip and stood on the mat for exactly as long as it took to be a nuisance, and then took his boots off on the mat, one and then the other, and put his socks on the stove rail at the end of the row, and then went over to the wall by the stove with the tin.

Nobody at the tables stopped talking. In a room where four tables were full, a boy going quietly to a wall with a tin was nothing at all. But he did not go to a person and that was the telling thing: he went to the hook, and he looked up at the bracket, and he put the tin on the sill, and took a screwdriver out of it, and crouched, and got his shoulder against the wooden strip the stove stood on, and put the screwdriver up into the bracket and began to undo the top screw.

Clarisse kept ladling. She did not stop. She did not even look at the wall. What Clarisse did, Annabeth realised from where she sat at the second table, was keep ladling very hard in one direction, which had the effect of moving her body between the stove and the wall and making a modest amount of room in front of a bent boy with a screwdriver, and she said, to the room, "Nobody put a wet coat on the sill, the sill's for the drawing," and nobody knew what she meant, and she had no idea that she had said it.

Annabeth did not say anything. She had not said anything about two people walking up a hill separately on Saturday, and she was not going to say anything now, and she moved her bowl an inch and went on eating, and the only thing she did was reach for the bread, and that was not a comment.

She watched, obliquely, because she was Annabeth and could not help it, and she thought about the difference between a boy who is shy and a boy who is quiet, because the two get lumped together and they are not the same thing at all. A quiet boy is a boy with nothing in the room he wants to say. A shy boy is a boy with something in the room he wants badly to approach sideways. Charlie Beckendorf had come into a full pavilion on a thaw Wednesday and fixed a hook that nobody had mentioned out loud, and had done it the way a person does a job they have been given, and had not once looked toward the stove where Silena was standing with a ladle in her hand.

Silena was not looking at him either. Silena was cutting bread, with a care and precision that a bench of sawyers would have respected, into thick slices, into a basket, and she covered the basket with a cloth, and her face was entirely occupied with bread, and every thirty seconds or so she looked up at the room with the bright general attention of a hostess and never, not once, in the whole direction of the wall.

The bracket took four minutes. Annabeth timed it by the bowl. He tightened the top screw and then the bottom one, and then he pulled on the hook, and then he pulled on it again harder, and then he put the screwdriver away in the tin and stood up, and his shoulder found the low beam and he stood there for a second with his head down like a man who has finished the work and has not yet got out of the way of it.

"That's held," he said.

Silena looked up. "That's held," she said.

"It'll hold the pot and it'll hold the pot's pot. If it ever lets go again I want to hear about it."

"It's on a list," Silena said, and Clarisse, at the stove, said "It's on a list" at the same moment without turning round, and the two of them said it in the same words and stopped, and Clarisse kept ladling, and Annabeth put her face in her bowl because there are moments that require it.

Beckendorf took a bowl from the stack and filled it himself. Silena was carrying bread between the tables, but when she reached his end of the counter she stopped to check the bracket he'd repaired.

"That really is straight," she said.

"Straight enough for the pot."

"Straight enough for the pot's pot?"

"Please don't make that a real unit of measurement."

She smiled. He kept his eyes on the bracket while he finished checking that the bolt sat flush against the beam. He could have packed his tools and left without speaking to her again. Instead he set the little tin down on the counter.

"If you have another problem with it, come to the forge. Don't climb up there yourself to try tightening it."

"I hadn't planned to."

"You'd be surprised how often people do."

"I wouldn't," she said. "I have four siblings and a kitchen."

That got a laugh out of him. He washed his bowl, dried it, and put it on the stack. At the door he turned back.

"You said that soup's better the second day."

"It is."

"Is there any left for Thursday?"

Silena looked into the pot. There was more than enough for another meal, though she had planned to save some for the early patrol.

"If you come after the patrol gets fed, yes."

"Then I'll come after."

"Bring the bracket measurements," she said. "I want to make sure the second hook uses the same spacing."

He nodded as though that was the only reason he intended to return. When he had gone, Clarisse moved past with the empty ladle and glanced at the measuring tape Silena was putting in her apron pocket.

"Second hook?" she asked.

"We do need one."

Clarisse didn't argue. Silena went back to the bread counter, checking how much would be left for the patrol before she started another batch.
