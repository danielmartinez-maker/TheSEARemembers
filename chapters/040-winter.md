## Chapter 40: Winter


Annabeth Grace woke up cold.

Not the normal kind of cold, either. Not the draft-under-the-blanket, pull-your-socks-up kind. This was nose-cold. The tip of her nose was freezing, while the rest of her was sweating under three blankets and a quilted Zeus-blue comforter that Thalia had stolen from somewhere and declared communal property.

She blinked at the ceiling of Cabin One. Their cabin. The marble still held the cold, but Thalia's duffel bag was shoved under the second bunk and her jacket was slung over its footboard. Jason had left a tiny wooden eagle on the nightstand during his supervised visit from Camp Jupiter. Then Thalia had pinned up a photo of the three of them that some satyr had taken with a disposable camera. Now it felt less like an empty, echoing temple to Zeus and more like somewhere their family actually lived.

Somebody freezing.

"Thalia," Annabeth croaked. Her breath puffed. Actual visible breath. Inside. "Thalia. It's cold enough to see my breath."

From the bunk across the narrow aisle, a lump groaned. "Don't care."

"I'm serious. Look." Annabeth sat up, blankets pooling, and exhaled dramatically. A little cloud bloomed. "See?"

Thalia's black hair emerged from under her pile of blankets. Her nose was red. "That's disgusting. Stop breathing on me."

"It's February in New York. Why is it Arctic in here?"

"Because the gods hate us personally."

Annabeth swung her legs out of bed and immediately regretted it. The floorboards were ice. She yelped and hopped, grabbing socks from the floor — mismatched, one gray, one with lightning bolts — and shoving her feet into sneakers without tying them.

The window was frosted over at the edges. Real frost, lacy ferns spreading inward from the corners. Annabeth pressed her palm to the glass and wiped a circle clear.

Outside, Camp Half-Blood was white.

She stared.

It wasn't supposed to snow yet. Last week had been crisp autumn, leaves everywhere, the strawberry fields still stubbornly green. Now the whole valley was dusted in soft white. The Big House roof wore a cap of it. The cabins had white outlines. The lake was gray and choppy, but the docks were frosted. Half-bloods were already spilling out of cabins in pajamas and hastily thrown-on coats, shouting, pointing, slipping on the steps.

"Thalia! Snow!"

Thalia sat bolt upright. "What?"

"Snow! Real snow!"

Thalia squinted at the window, then flopped back down and pulled the blanket over her head. "Wake me when it's summer."

Annabeth was already pulling on her coat over her pajamas. She grabbed her camp necklace — three beads now, plus her father's college ring on the cord — and tucked it inside her shirt so the metal wouldn't freeze to her skin. She learned that lesson last winter in Virginia.

She yanked open the cabin door.

Cold air hit her face like a slap, clean and pine-sharp. The steps were dusted. She could see her breath rising in big clouds. Down the path, the Demeter kids were already rolling something that might become a snowman if they committed. Ares kids were pelting each other with snowballs and shouting. Somewhere, someone was laughing so hard they were wheezing.

Percy Jackson stood in the middle of the path in front of Cabin Three, wearing swim shorts, a hoodie, and one sock.

Annabeth stared. "Percy! What are you wearing?"

He looked down at himself, as if noticing for the first time. "I heard snow and I ran out. I didn't do a clothing check."

"You're going to lose toes!"

"I have excellent toes! They're hardy! Poseidon toes!"

Grover appeared in Cabin Three's doorway, wrapped in three scarves and a blanket like a sad burrito. "Percy, get back inside before I have to explain to your mom why you have frostbite in February!"

Percy scooped up a handful of snow and packed it. "But Grover! Snow!"

He threw it. It sailed in a perfect arc and hit the Ares cabin door with a splat.

Clarisse La Rue opened the door two seconds later, got hit in the face by a second snowball from a Demeter kid, and shouted, "OH IT IS ON!"

Annabeth leaned against her doorframe, grinning despite her chattering teeth. Her breath made clouds. Her cheeks stung. Her pajama pants were already damp at the cuffs.

Winter had come to Camp Half-Blood sideways and all at once, and nobody had been ready, and everybody was delighted.

Behind her, Thalia's voice, muffled by blankets: "If you let all the warm air out, I'm killing you!"

Annabeth slipped back inside and shut the door, but she was smiling. "You have to see it. It's beautiful."

Thalia peeked out from the blankets, one electric-blue eye visible. "Is it the pretty snow or the miserable slush snow?"

Annabeth wiped the window again and looked. The snow was glittering, too glittery, almost sparkling blue and silver in the morning light. It fell lightly still, flakes big and slow, but they shimmered oddly when they caught the sun.

She frowned. "It's... sparkly. Like, really sparkly."

"Magical sparkly or just snow sparkly?"

"I'm going to go with magical."

Thalia sighed theatrically and sat up, blankets around her shoulders like a queen's mantle. "Great. We're going to be frozen by a goddess with a snow globe. Love that for us."

Annabeth crawled back onto her own bunk and pulled the blanket up to her chin, scooting close to the wall so there was room. Thalia got the hint and flopped sideways across the aisle gap, half on her own bunk, half leaning against Annabeth's mattress, their shoulders bumping. They shared body heat and watched the window together as the strange glittering snow drifted down.

For a daughter of Zeus, Annabeth had always thought she would love storms best. Thunder, lightning, the crack and drama of it. But this — this silent white softness — felt like the sky doing something gentle for once.

"First snow," Thalia murmured. "Make a wish."

"That's for first stars."

"Make one anyway. The gods aren't checking the paperwork."

Annabeth closed her eyes. She didn't wish for anything big. Not for quests or glory or answers about prophecies. She wished the heat would stay on in the cabins and that Percy would put on real pants.

When she opened her eyes, Thalia was smirking at her.

"What did you wish for?" Thalia asked.

"That you'd get up and get dressed so we can go to breakfast before all the good muffins are gone."

"Liar."

"Come on." Annabeth shoved her playfully. "Cold snap means hot chocolate. And if there's hot chocolate, there's war."

The dining pavilion had never been this loud at breakfast.

Percy had managed pants — Grover had thrown jeans at his head and refused to leave until he put them on — and two socks that matched, which felt like a personal victory. He'd also found his blue hoodie, the one Amphitrite had sent from Atlantis with a note that said *Stay warm, my darling. Love, Mother.* He wore it over two shirts. The note was hers, though the blue reminded him of Sally and another childhood only he remembered. He still felt cold in a way he'd never felt at camp before, a damp chill that got into his elbows and knees.

But the pavilion was warm with bodies and noise and the smell of bacon and cinnamon oatmeal. And, most importantly, with gossip.

"Did you hear?" Katie Gardner slid onto the bench next to Grover, cheeks pink. "It's Khione."

Percy blinked. "Who?"

"Khione. Snow goddess. Daughter of Boreas." Katie lowered her voice, like saying it louder might summon her. "Travis heard from a naiad that she thought it would be funny to give camp an early winter. Like, as a joke."

Grover bleated softly. "A joke? It's twenty degrees!"

"She thinks it's hilarious," Connor Stoll confirmed, appearing upside down over the bench. He'd snuck up behind them. "She told the winds to do it overnight. Chiron's furious. He sent a strongly worded Iris-message."

Percy spooned oatmeal. "Can gods get detention?"

"If they could, Mr. D would have invented it," Annabeth said, sliding in across from him with Thalia right behind her. Annabeth's hair was damp from snowmelt, pulled back in a messy ponytail. Her gray eyes were bright. She had a smear of flour or snow or something white on her cheek.

Percy, without thinking, reached across and wiped it off with his thumb.

The whole table went quiet for half a second.

Annabeth froze. Percy froze. Thalia made a gagging noise and said, "Get a room, you two," which broke the tension, and everybody laughed, and Percy stared very hard at his oatmeal like it had personally offended him.

His ears were burning. Stupid ears.

"Anyway," Annabeth said quickly, a little pink herself. "Hot chocolate."

"What about it?" Percy asked, grateful for the subject change.

"The Stolls are claiming the Hephaestus cabin makes the best hot chocolate," Thalia said. "Beckendorf uses actual melted chocolate and chili. It's unfair."

"It's not unfair, it's chemistry," Beckendorf called from two tables over, not even looking up from his pancakes. He missed nothing.

"Demeter claims theirs is best because they use fresh mint and whipped cream from the cows," Katie said.

"Apollo claims theirs is best because they sing to it," Will Solace added mournfully, as if this embarrassed him, which it did.

Percy looked at Annabeth. Annabeth looked at Percy. In perfect unison, born of three months of being best friends and rivals and co-conspirators in every capture-the-flag game, they said:

"Oh, it's on."

And that was how the Hot Chocolate Wars began.

By lunch, there were four official factions, two unofficial ones, and a scoring whiteboard that Annabeth had nailed to the Big House porch with Chiron's bemused permission.

Rules, per Annabeth's neat block handwriting:

1. Each cabin (or alliance) produces one pot per evening tasting.
2. Secret ingredient allowed, but must be edible and non-poisonous (this rule added after the Stolls suggested Tabasco and toothpaste).
3. No bribing judges with dessert (this rule ignored immediately).
4. Judges rotate nightly. No voting for your own cabin.
5. Winner gets the Golden Mug (a spray-painted goblet from the arts-and-crafts room) displayed on their cabin mantle until next winter.

"This is the most serious thing you've ever organized," Thalia told Annabeth, watching her rule the whiteboard like a general. "Including the Pegasus riding schedule."

"This is important," Annabeth said loftily. "Morale is important. Warmth is important. Chocolate is important."

Percy, who had allied Cabin Three with Grover (representing the satyrs, wood nymphs, and general cloven-hoofed lobby) and Tyson — his Cyclops half-brother, visiting from the undersea forges and who worshipped Percy with enormous, single-eyed devotion — was taking this extremely seriously.

"Okay," he said that afternoon, huddled in Cabin Three with his sisters, Grover, visiting Tyson, and Ella. Ella was a small harpy with red feathers and a habit of repeating phrases she'd read, who had attached herself to Grover after he'd shared his lunch with her earlier that winter and who now lived mostly in the rafters of the Big House but visited Percy for storytime. "Ideas. Go."

"Sea salt!" Grover said immediately. "And — and — caramel! Like the candies! Sweet and salty!"

Tyson clapped his huge hands. "PEANUT BUTTER!"

Percy considered. "Peanut butter hot chocolate?"

Tyson nodded so hard his eye wobbled. "Tyson make peanut butter sandwiches good. Tyson make hot chocolate good."

Ella, perched on Percy's bunk with a blanket around her skinny shoulders, rocked back and forth and recited, "'Hot chocolate, steaming, warm as a dragon's breath in winter. Chapter seven.'" She blinked. "Ella likes cinnamon. Cinnamon is warm."

Percy grabbed a notebook — Annabeth had given him one and told him real strategists wrote things down, and he'd actually started using it — and scribbled: *sea salt + caramel + peanut butter + cinnamon? Too much?*

"Test batch," he declared. "Small pot. Grover, you're on stirring. Tyson, you're on tasting. Ella, you're on... supervisory sniffing."

Ella fluffed her feathers importantly. "Ella supervises."

Cabin Three's tiny kitchenette was not designed for serious brewing. It had a hot plate, a dented pot, and mugs that didn't match. Percy melted chocolate chips with milk while Grover stirred with intense focus, tongue sticking out. Tyson unwrapped caramels with surprising delicacy for someone who could bend a doorframe. Ella leaned over the pot and inhaled deeply every thirty seconds and announced, "Smells warmer. Smells warmer. Smells warmest."

The first taste was... interesting.

"Okay," Percy said, coughing. "Too much salt."

Second batch: better.

Third batch: Grover took a sip, closed his eyes, and sighed like he'd seen Elysium. "That's it. That's the one."

Tyson drank half a mug in one gulp and got a chocolate mustache and beamed. Ella dipped her beak daintily and said, "'Delicious beyond measure. Ella approves. Twice.'"

Percy marked the recipe with a star and wrote *CABIN THREE — SALTED CARAMEL PEANUT BUTTER CINNAMON (secret!)* and underlined it three times.

Outside, the snow kept falling, glittering blue. Someone shrieked with laughter. Someone else started a snowball fight that migrated past their window.

Percy looked around his tiny, too-cold cabin — at Grover in his blanket nest, Tyson humming as he washed spoons, Ella dozing with chocolate on her beak — and felt his chest go warm in a way that had nothing to do with cocoa.

"Best winter ever," he announced.

"Best winter ever," Grover agreed. "'Best winter ever,'" Ella echoed, and fell asleep.

By the time breakfast ended, Silena had a list of three problems the cold had caused, and none could be fixed with a snowball fight.

The water pipe behind the kitchen had frozen. A younger Apollo camper had arrived in a jacket too thin for the weather because he'd given his heavier one to his cousin. And the little heater in Cabin Eleven had stopped working, leaving so many children crowding around the common-room stove that nobody could pass through the doorway.

Silena took the list to Chiron rather than immediately trying to fix everything herself. The day was supposed to be a holiday surprise from a snow goddess, but she had learned during the eclipse that a celebration could become an emergency if nobody was willing to keep count.

Chiron was in the Big House study, looking at a weather report beside a map of camp.

"Do we know how long this is supposed to last?" she asked.

"Khione's messenger said a brief visit. The explanation was not specific enough for me to treat it as a forecast."

"Then I'd like permission to open the arts cabin as a warming room during the day. It has a proper fireplace and enough chairs for the younger campers. I'll ask the counselor to supervise."

Chiron looked at her notes. "That may work. What about food?"

"The kitchens can move the afternoon snacks there if the pipe is repaired first. If not, I'll arrange hot drinks from the Big House. The water should come from the heated storage tank, not the frozen line."

She expected him to approve the plan. Instead he pointed to the list of people who were supposed to help and asked who had agreed to each assignment.

Silena looked down. She had written Beckendorf beside the frozen pipe because he knew how to repair things; Clarisse beside the heater because she was strong; and Percy beside the drinks because he could produce water without a bucket. She had not asked any of them.

"I was going to tell them at lunch."

"Ask, rather than tell," Chiron said. "Beckendorf has another inspection scheduled, and Percy's ability to control water does not make him the camp plumbing service."

Silena felt an unreasonable flash of irritation. She knew the distinction. She was simply trying to move quickly.

"Then can we start by checking who's available?"

"Of course."

They did. Beckendorf had an hour before noon, but he wanted a second mechanic to help inspect the pipe before anyone attempted to warm it. Clarisse was assigned to supervise the arena doors, which were swelling in their frames as moisture froze. She could carry spare blankets to the arts room after that, not before. Percy offered to help with drinks but agreed to leave the frozen plumbing to the repair crew.

The plan became less elegant and more reliable.

At ten, Silena stood beside the open arts-cabin door with the first basket of blankets. The fireplace had been checked, the chimney draft worked, and a counselor from Apollo had volunteered to watch the younger children. Silena hung a small sign outside explaining that anybody who was cold could come in, but that the equipment room and paint storage were off-limits.

A Hermes child stepped through the door carrying a damp pillow.

"Can I stay here?" he asked.

"Yes. Is your cabin counselor aware you're coming?"

He nodded. "They're bringing the others."

"Good. Leave the pillow by the drying rack so it doesn't go straight onto the fire."

He obeyed, found a chair and immediately fell asleep.

Silena watched him for a moment. She'd spent the morning imagining an inviting holiday room with garlands and cocoa. It was also becoming a place where a cold child could sleep safely. She could do both, but the order mattered.

Grover arrived with Ella and a stack of books from the Big House. Ella was carrying one by its spine and looking offended by the suggestion that she should leave it near a fireplace.

"Heat damages paper," she informed Silena. "Heat and moisture. Two enemies. Three if you count mites."

"Then you can be in charge of keeping the books away from the hearth," Silena said.

Ella considered this an important appointment. She arranged them on a table far enough from the fire that nobody could reach from their chairs and accidentally knock a book into the flames.

Grover set down his own blanket. He'd brought it for a younger satyr who'd been shivering by the dining pavilion.

"Can we put an extra basket outside for people who don't want to come in?" he asked. "Some of the dryads won't leave their trees, even when they're cold."

Silena stopped. She had arranged everything for children who could walk through a door. The camp also sheltered creatures whose needs wouldn't fit the same solution.

"Ask the tree spirits what would help," she said. "We can bring the blankets out if they're useful, but I don't want to wrap a dryad's tree without permission."

Grover nodded and went to ask.

By midday, the arts cabin was noisy with small conversations, the smell of warm bread and the rustle of drying coats. Beckendorf reported that the pipe had not cracked; it needed to thaw gradually with the line isolated and the repair crew watching. The heater in Cabin Eleven was being assessed rather than pushed beyond its limits. Chiron had authorized temporary beds in the Big House for anyone whose room stayed too cold.

Silena read the updates and felt a small, reluctant sense of accomplishment. None of it was pretty yet. She hadn't cut a single paper snowflake. But when the youngest Apollo camper woke in his chair and asked whether there would be a story later, she could say yes without wondering whether he would spend the evening freezing.

She reached for the colored paper and scissors after the lunch dishes were cleared.

Now, at least, the decorating could begin.

Silena Beauregard believed, deeply and sincerely, that no emergency was so great it could not be improved by decorations.

Cold snap? Decorate.

Snow goddess prank? Decorate more.

Homesick winter campers trapped inside with too much energy and too little sunlight? Decorate aggressively, with garlands.

So by the second day of the cold, the Aphrodite cabin looked like a holiday market had exploded inside it. Paper snowflakes hung from every rafter — not the boring symmetrical kind, but elaborate cutouts with hearts and stars. Popcorn-and-cranberry strings looped along the bunk posts. Someone (Drew, surprisingly) had braided blue and silver ribbons into the window latches. The mirror was framed in fairy lights that Beckendorf had rigged to run off a tiny bronze automaton battery, because regular extension cords and Poseidon-weather did not mix.

It smelled like cinnamon and vanilla and the orange peels Silena simmered on the heater.

"It looks amazing," Annabeth said honestly when she ducked in that afternoon, shaking snow off her coat. She'd come to borrow glitter glue and stayed because it was warm. "How did you do all this in a day?"

"Collective trauma bonding and Lana's snowflake skills," Silena said, handing her a mug of apple cider. Lana, a younger Aphrodite girl, waved shyly from the floor where she was cutting out another flake. "Also, Clarisse helped."

Annabeth choked on her cider. "Clarisse helped decorate?"

"She carried the heavy box up the stairs and then pretended she wasn't enjoying herself," Silena said with a fond smile. "She stayed for cocoa. She's in the back."

Annabeth peeked around the room divider.

Clarisse La Rue, daughter of Poseidon, terror of the sword arena, was sitting on a pink rug with knitting needles in her hands and an expression of profound betrayal.

The knitting itself — if you could call it that — was a lumpy, holey, vaguely rectangular thing in blood-red yarn, with one edge significantly longer than the other and a dropped stitch big enough to poke a finger through.

"It's a scarf," Clarisse said defensively, without looking up. "Shut up."

"I didn't say anything!" Annabeth held up her hands.

"You were thinking loudly."

Silena settled onto the rug beside Clarisse with easy grace, tucking her skirt around her knees, and picked up her own knitting — a neat, even, pale-blue hat well on its way to completion. "She's doing great for day two."

"I'M TERRIBLE," Clarisse announced to the room. "This is the worst scarf in the history of scarves. Poseidon is going to disown me. My mother is going to see this and laugh."

"It's got character," offered Rosie, an Aphrodite younger kid, from her bunk.

"It's got holes," Clarisse said. "It's a scarf with windows."

Silena bumped her shoulder gently. "Keep going. The person you're making it for won't care about holes."

Clarisse's ears went red. She hunched lower over the needles. "Who said I'm making it for a person? Maybe I like suffering."

"Uh-huh." Silena didn't push. She'd guessed — everyone had guessed — that the scarf, red like a warning flag, sized for a neck much smaller than Clarisse's, was for her little brother back home, the mortal one she never talked about except once, late at night, when she'd admitted she missed him so much her teeth hurt. But Silena would never say it aloud and ruin it.

Instead, Silena did what she did best: she made space warm.

"Story time?" she called. "New chapter?"

A chorus of yeses. Blankets rustled. Girls (and Annabeth, and two Demeter kids who'd wandered in for the smell, and a sleepy Ella who'd fluttered in to escape the Hephaestus hammering) piled onto rugs and bunks, mugs in hands.

Silena picked up the book from the nightstand — *The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe*, battered paperback, pages soft. They'd started it earlier that winter as a joke — ha ha, another magical wardrobe, Narnia, how original — but somewhere around chapter four they'd all gotten hooked, and now story time was sacred.

She opened to the marked page, cleared her throat, and read.

Her voice was good for this — low and smooth, unhurried. She did voices without being cheesy about it. Mr. Beaver was gruff and kind. Lucy was earnest. Edmund was sulky in a way that made everybody laugh because he sounded exactly like Travis Stoll when denied dessert.

Outside, wind rattled the windows. Inside, the fairy lights glowed gold. Clarisse's needles clicked arrhythmically, then stopped as she got absorbed in the chapter and forgot to knit, scarf pooling in her lap. Lana leaned against Silena's side, half-asleep. Ella mouthed along with favorite lines she'd memorized: "'Always winter and never Christmas.' Oh. Oh, that's sad."

Annabeth, curled in the corner with cider going cold in her hands because she'd forgotten to drink it, felt her eyes grow heavy. It wasn't just the story. It was the warmth, the cinnamon air, the soft click of Silena's steady knitting anchoring the room while the storm muttered outside.

She thought about how the Aphrodite cabin had a reputation for being shallow — pretty girls, gossip, makeup. And here they were, weathering a magical snowstorm with homemade garlands and a shared book and a fearsome sea girl learning to knit a lopsided scarf for her little brother.

Silena finished the chapter on a cliffhanger — of course — and closed the book to a chorus of protests.

"Nooo!"

"One more!"

"Silena!"

"Nope." Silena smiled, unmoved. "Bedtime is bedtime. Cliffhangers build character. Also, Beckendorf promised to fix the heater if we let him have quiet after nine."

Groans. But nobody moved. They were too cozy.

Clarisse looked down at her scarf, held it up, squinted at the holes. "It's awful," she said again, but quieter this time. Almost fond.

"It's your first," Silena said. "My first hat looked like a saddlebag. Keep it. He'll love it because you made it."

Clarisse stared at her. "How did you—"

"I know things," Silena said lightly, and winked. "I have friends in Aphrodite. Knowing things about love is practically a group project."

Clarisse's face did something complicated — half scowl, half something softer. She shoved the knitting into her bag hastily, stood, and said gruffly, "Thanks for the cocoa," and left before anyone could make it weird.

But at the door she paused and said, over her shoulder, "Tomorrow. Same time? I want to hear what happens to Edmund, the little traitor."

"Tomorrow," Silena promised. "Bring your scarf."

After she left, Annabeth helped Silena stack mugs. "That was nice," she said quietly. "What you did."

Silena shrugged, but she looked pleased. "Everyone needs somewhere warm. Even Clarisse. Especially Clarisse."

Outside, the strange glittering snow fell on, but in here, with paper flakes and popcorn strings and a paused story waiting for tomorrow, winter felt manageable. Almost kind.

Clarisse La Rue was going to kill this scarf.

Not metaphorically. She was going to commit actual murder on red yarn with size-ten needles as her weapons.

She sat in the corner of the Ares cabin, visiting her training partners — which was freezing, because the Ares kids had left the windows open during a pillow fight and now the heater was losing — with her blanket over her head like a tent, squinting at the instructions Silena had written out for her in neat, loopy handwriting.

*Cast on 20. Knit 2, purl 2. Repeat until long enough. Cast off. Breathe.*

Clarisse could spear a dummy through the heart at twenty paces. She could disarm a camper twice her size. She could not, apparently, count to twenty consistently while knitting.

"Fourteen, fifteen, sixteen—" She counted the lumps on her needle. "Seventeen? Nineteen? Why are there nineteen? There were twenty a second ago!"

Sherman Yang looked over from his bunk, where he was trying to decorate a tiny plastic tree with miniature swords. "Did you drop one again?"

"I DID NOT DROP ANYTHING. The stitch escaped. It wanted freedom."

"Uh-huh."

"It's sabotage. The yarn hates me."

"It's yarn, Clarisse."

"You don't know! You don't know its heart!"

Across the cabin, her sparring partners were stringing lights with more enthusiasm than skill. Someone had hung a wreath made of old spear shafts on the door. Someone else had put a Santa hat on the stuffed boar's head above the mantle. The Ares cabin, for all its bravado, had gone full holiday in self-defense against the cold. Even the war maps on the wall had tinsel on them.

Clarisse glared at her scarf. It glared back, holey and uneven.

She thought about her brother, Mikey, seven years old, missing his two front teeth, who'd cried when she'd come back to camp the previous September and made her promise to write every week. She'd written. He'd written back in wobbly pencil: *Dear Clarisse, Mom says you are at special camp. Is it cold? I made you a drawing of a dragon. Love Mikey.*

She'd pinned the dragon above her bunk.

She wanted to send him something. Something she'd made. Something that said *I'm thinking about you even when I'm learning to stab things.* A scarf seemed right. Warm. Protective. Big-sisterly.

Except she sucked at it.

She stabbed the needle through a loop too aggressively and split the yarn. "Gods damn it!"

"Language," said Chris Rodriguez mildly from the doorway, stamping snow off his boots. He held two mugs. He offered one to Clarisse. "Hot chocolate test batch seven. Ares cabin entry. Drink and weep."

Clarisse took it suspiciously and sipped.

Her eyebrows shot up. "Whoa."

"Right?" Chris grinned. "Cayenne and cinnamon and dark chocolate. Sherman's abuela's recipe, apparently. Sherman's been holding out on us."

Sherman shrugged, modest. "Abuela knows."

"It's good," Clarisse admitted. "Like, actually good. Spicy. It punches you and then hugs you."

"That's the review we're putting on the whiteboard," Chris said, scribbling on a notepad. "'Punches you then hugs you — Clarisse.'"

Clarisse looked back at her scarf. An idea struck — terrible, wonderful. "Hey. If this scarf doesn't work out as a scarf... could it be a... hot chocolate cozy? Like, for the pot?"

Sherman and Chris stared at her lumpy red creation.

"...It's very... insulating," Chris said diplomatically. "Lots of ventilation."

"Shut up." But Clarisse was laughing despite herself, a short, surprised bark. The tension in her shoulders eased. "Fine. I'll keep going. But if Mikey gets a scarf with windows, that's his problem."

She knit another row. Slower this time. Breathe in, loop, breathe out, pull through. Silena had told her to treat it like sword drills — same motion, over and over, until muscle memory took over.

Knit. Breathe. Knit. Breathe.

It wasn't perfect. It was still lopsided. But the new rows were a little more even than the old ones. A little tighter. A little less holey.

"Huh," Clarisse said.

"What?" Sherman asked.

"Nothing." She kept knitting, hiding a small smile under her blanket tent. "Go hang your tiny swords."

Outside, someone shouted that the Hephaestus cabin was doing a test of their hot chocolate shotgun — whatever that meant — and half the Ares cabin thundered out to watch. Clarisse stayed put, warm in her blanket, cocoa steaming on the footlocker beside her, needles clicking.

For once, no one needed her to be tough. Just to be a sister, making something lumpy and red and full of love, one stubborn stitch at a time.

Annabeth had meant to spend the afternoon at the shrine.

She had finished it only recently, and the little stone left by the first visitor was still on her mind. She wanted to inspect the roof after the snow and check whether the path she'd repaired with Percy and Nico had frozen unevenly. But Chiron had closed the route through the strawberry fields while the wind was strong, and no amount of explaining that she knew the site better than anybody else changed his answer.

"It's not that I don't trust your judgment," he said.

"Then what's the issue?"

"The path is icy, the temperature is falling, and the work isn't urgent enough to justify sending a camper beyond the supervised area."

Annabeth looked at the weather map. "If a roof tile shifts, the whole mosaic could get wet."

"That's possible. It doesn't mean the roof has shifted."

She hated how calm he sounded. She also recognized the distinction. The shrine had been her project for weeks. Every problem had seemed solvable if she could reach it with a measuring tape and a willing pair of hands. Today, doing nothing was a form of judgment she had not practiced very often.

"Could I prepare the repair kit?" she asked.

"Certainly. You may also check whether the tools will be ready when the path reopens."

She went to the workshop, collected the inventory form and discovered that half the materials she wanted were in the wrong place. The small cedar shingles had been put beside the firewood, the cloth covers were drying in a different cabin, and somebody had borrowed her narrow trowel without signing it out.

Annabeth began reorganizing. It felt better than worrying about something she couldn't see.

After an hour, Thalia found her beside a pile of neatly labeled boxes.

"You missed the snowball fight."

"I know."

"Percy fell down trying to demonstrate a trick jump over a bench. It was impressive."

"Was he hurt?"

"Only in his dignity."

Annabeth continued sorting screws by size.

Thalia watched her. "You're avoiding the fun because you can't check the shrine."

"I'm preparing for when I can."

"You've been preparing since lunch."

"The inventory was a mess."

"Then you'll finish it faster if I help."

Annabeth looked up. Her sister was wearing gloves with two fingers cut off so she could feel the edge of her spear better. The exposed fingertips were red with cold.

"You're going to freeze those," Annabeth said.

"I came inside, didn't I?"

Thalia pulled up a stool and took the labeling pencil. She had no patience for small screws, but she could read a list and put the right boxes on a shelf. The two worked together until the table was clear enough that a repair crew could use it without asking where everything belonged.

When they finished, Annabeth examined the emergency sheet. There was still a blank beside the words *site inaccessible*.

"What am I supposed to put there?" she asked.

"Site inaccessible," Thalia said.

"That's not an action."

"Sometimes the action is waiting until it's safe to do the thing."

Annabeth didn't like that. Thalia knew because she didn't like it either.

"I spent all those years telling myself I could keep you safe if I never looked away," Thalia said. "I was good at noticing things, and terrible at admitting I couldn't stop every bad thing. The cold isn't an enemy you can defeat by getting there first."

Annabeth looked at her. "You still go on patrol whenever the weather turns strange."

"When Chiron authorizes it. And today he sent somebody older who knows the closed trails better than me."

"Did you argue?"

"Obviously. Then I listened."

They stood in the workshop with the wind rattling the window. Annabeth thought about the boy who'd left the pebble. He might have wanted to visit the shrine today. If the path was closed, he couldn't. A safe closure was still a service to him, even though she'd rather have the place open.

She wrote *inspection deferred until clearance* in the blank and signed the form.

Thalia looked pleased but didn't congratulate her for obeying a rule.

"Want to go see what Percy is doing with the snow fort?" she asked.

"Does the fort have any structural integrity?"

"None. That's why I think you should look."

They found the fort behind the Big House, already listing at an angle. Percy and Nico had been trying to build a wall high enough to hide from Clarisse, who could see their heads above it from the other side of the yard. The wall's base was too narrow, and half the blocks were made from soft powder rather than packed snow.

Annabeth examined it.

"Where are your supports?"

Percy pointed at the wall. "The wall is supporting itself."

"No, it isn't."

As if to demonstrate, one corner slumped into the trench.

Nico sighed. "I told him."

Annabeth knelt in the snow, set her notebook in a dry pocket and showed them how to widen the base using smaller blocks. She didn't need to take over the construction; she made Percy place the first supports himself, then let Nico test how much weight the new wall would bear before they added another layer.

Clarisse came around the corner while they were working.

"So," she said. "You've brought in a professional."

"Consultant," Annabeth corrected. "I don't accept responsibility for his original design."

Percy looked offended. "It had artistic merit."

"It had a very short life expectancy."

The others laughed. They rebuilt the fort into a lower, sturdier wall that could shelter a group from the wind without requiring anyone to climb onto it. Annabeth insisted on keeping the doorway wide enough for the smallest camper to leave without crawling.

The work did not fix the shrine. It kept her hands busy and gave the others a more usable place to play. When Chiron called everyone inside before the temperature dropped again, she was ready to leave.

Back in the workshop, she checked the emergency sheet once more, then closed the binder. Tomorrow might be the right day to inspect the shrine. This afternoon had belonged to the people standing in the snow with her.

---

By nightfall, the cold had teeth.

The temperature dropped as the sun went down behind the hills, turning the glittering snow violet and rose. The wind picked up, whistling through the cabin eaves. Chiron had announced at dinner that all evening activities were moved indoors, heaters were on full blast (which meant they'd be lukewarm at best), and no one was to go to the woods or the lake after dark. The cold was unnatural, he said gravely, and while Khione had assured him through intermediaries that it was all in good fun, he didn't trust fun that could cause hypothermia.

So campers battened down. Cabin doors shut. Blankets multiplied. The Hot Chocolate Wars tastings moved to the Big House living room, where a fire roared and judging was loud and chaotic and ended with the Demeter mint entry winning Night One by a single vote over Hephaestus chili, to much shouting.

Afterward, Annabeth and Thalia trudged back to Cabin One through snow that squeaked under their boots, carrying their mugs and the leftover cookies Silena had pressed on them.

Their cabin was freezing.

"Oh, come on!" Thalia kicked the sputtering heater. It rattled and produced a sigh of lukewarm air. "We worship the sky and we get the draftiest cabin?"

"High ceilings," Annabeth said through chattering teeth. "Heat rises. Physics."

"I hate physics."

"You're literally a daughter of Zeus. You are physics."

They stared at their two narrow bunks, separated by three feet of frigid air, and had the same thought at the same time.

"...Share?" Thalia said.

"Share," Annabeth agreed.

They pushed the mattresses together — or as together as bulky wooden frames allowed — and piled every blanket in the cabin on top: Thalia's heavy wool one, Annabeth's quilt, the spare from the empty third bunk, plus their coats spread over the top like an extra layer. They changed into the warmest pajamas they had (flannel, mismatched, with thick socks), brushed their teeth in record speed because the bathroom tiles were like ice, and dove under the covers together with twin gasps.

"Cold! Cold! Cold!"

"Your feet are ice!"

"Your hands are worse!"

They wrestled for blanket dominance for a minute, laughing, breath clouding, until they settled side by side on their backs, shoulders touching, staring up at the dark ceiling. Slowly, shared warmth built under the blankets, a small, precious pocket of heat.

"Better?" Thalia whispered.

"Much," Annabeth whispered back.

Quiet. The wind gusted outside. The cabin creaked. Somewhere, the Apollo cabin was playing guitar faintly through the walls.

Annabeth remembered sharing beds with friends on the road in her old life. Thalia remembered years of sharing with her little sister in this one. Now their shoulders touched and neither needed the other to explain what it felt like to have a godly parent who was more weather pattern than father, to feel eyes on you every time you entered a room because of who claimed you. That was rare and precious.

"Can I ask you something?" Annabeth said into the dark.

"Mm."

"Do you ever... get lonely? Being Zeus's daughter? Everyone expects you to be... strong. Leader-y. Not scared."

Thalia was quiet for a long moment. Then: "All the time. Especially at night. Jason's far away. Our mom... well. You know. It's just me and the sky, and the sky doesn't talk back unless it's thundering."

Annabeth turned on her side to face her. Thalia did the same. Under the blankets, they were nose to nose, sharing breath-warm air.

"I get scared," Annabeth admitted softly. "At night, mostly. That I'm not smart enough. That the prophecy — whatever it is — will come and I won't be ready. That I'll let people down. Percy, Grover, you. Luke..." Her voice caught on his name. Luke, who'd been like a big brother, who'd left, whose choices hung over camp like smoke. She didn't say more.

Thalia reached out under the blankets and found her hand and squeezed. "You know what I think? I think being scared is smart. It means you're paying attention. The dumb ones aren't scared. They're dead."

Annabeth huffed a laugh, tears pricking unexpectedly. "Thanks."

"Plus," Thalia added, "you're twelve and you've already survived more than most heroes twice your age. Give yourself a break, shortstack."

"You too, Grace."

They lay there, holding hands like little kids, listening to the wind.

"Tell me something happy," Thalia said. "Something you want. Not a quest. Just... a thing."

Annabeth thought. "I want to design a building that doesn't fall down," she said. "A real one. With my name on the cornerstone. I want to go to college. I want... I want a house with a library and big windows and no drafts. And I want Percy to stop leaving his socks everywhere."

Thalia snorted. "That last one's impossible. Even the gods can't do that."

"What do you want?" Annabeth asked.

Thalia stared at the ceiling. "I want to run. Like, really run, through the woods, under stars, and not be cold. I want Jason to be safe. I want..." She hesitated. "I want a family that stays. For more than a season."

Annabeth squeezed her hand harder. "We're staying."

"Promise?"

"Promise. Cabin One pact. No matter what — monsters, gods, Khione's stupid snow — we share blankets and tell the truth at night."

"Cabin One pact," Thalia repeated solemnly. Then, lighter: "Gods, that's cheesy."

"You're cheesy."

"You're cheesier."

They dissolved into stifled giggles, pulling the blankets over their heads to make a tent, flashlight on, like little kids again and not thinking about prophecies at all. Annabeth told Thalia about the architecture book she was reading, about flying buttresses. Thalia told Annabeth about the time she'd outrun a hellhound on a dare. They ranked hot chocolates, planned tomorrow's snow fort strategy, debated whether Ella could be taught to say "Cabin One rules."

Eventually, yawns won. Thalia fell asleep first, breathing slow and even, hair spilling across the pillow. Annabeth watched her for a minute, feeling fiercely protective and absurdly fond.

Outside, magical snow fell on a valley full of sleeping demigods. Inside Cabin One, two daughters of the sky kept each other warm, hands linked, blankets piled high.

It wasn't summer. It wasn't easy. But it was family.

Annabeth slept.

Percy couldn't sleep.

It wasn't just the cold, though Cabin Three was cold in a special, damp way — Poseidon cabin, always a little humid, now with frost on the inside of the porthole window and visiting Tyson snoring like a friendly avalanche in the guest bunk. It was his brain, buzzing with cocoa sugar and snow excitement and the lingering warmth of Annabeth's thumb on his cheek from that morning (he kept replaying it and then telling himself to stop replaying it because being twelve again made his girlfriend's teasing feel startlingly new).

He stared at the ceiling, where Amphitrite had helped him stick glow-in-the-dark stars during a visit from Atlantis. They glowed faintly green.

He gave up, pulled on three layers, his coat, his hat, gloves, two scarves (one blue, one borrowed from Grover that smelled like goats and apples), and his boots, and slipped outside with his blanket.

The night took his breath away.

The snow had stopped. The sky had cleared to black velvet, stars blazing in numbers he'd never seen at home in the city. The snow-covered ground reflected starlight, making everything glow soft blue. The camp was silent except for the distant hush of the sea and the occasional creak of a cabin settling. His breath rose in huge clouds.

"Percy?" A whisper from the shadows. Grover, also blanketed, horns poking through a knit hat with earflaps, appeared from behind Cabin Three. "Can't sleep either?"

"Too awake," Percy admitted.

"Same. Stomach's doing the thing." Grover patted his belly. "Too much chili chocolate. Worth it, though."

A flutter above, then Ella landed lightly on the porch rail, feathers puffed against the cold, a tiny blanket — actually a large napkin — tied around her shoulders like a cape. "'The stars are bright tonight. Bright as diamonds on velvet. Chapter twelve,'" she whispered. "Ella couldn't sleep. The Big House rafters are cold. And lonely."

Percy held open his blanket. "Come on. Blanket fort stargazing."

They settled on the porch steps, huddled shoulder to shoulder to shoulder — boy, satyr, harpy — sharing Percy's big blanket plus Grover's plus Ella's napkin-cape, which didn't help much but was noble in spirit. Percy pulled the camp's spare telescope (old, dented, Hephaestus-repaired) from beside the door where he'd left it that afternoon with grand plans.

"Okay," he whispered. "Lesson one, per Annabeth's bossy star charts she made me memorize: that's Cassiopeia. The W. She was a queen who bragged too much."

"'Pride goes before a fall. Ella knows that story,'" Ella murmured, leaning against his shoulder. Her feathers were soft and surprisingly warm.

"That's Orion," Grover said, pointing with a hoof. "My favorite. See the belt? Three stars. Hunters are good. Unless they're hunting satyrs. Then they're bad."

Percy laughed quietly, breath clouding. He found the North Star, the Big Dipper, the little smudge that Annabeth swore was Andromeda Galaxy and not a fingerprint on the lens.

They didn't talk much after that. Just watched, breathing together, blankets steaming faintly where their warmth met cold air.

Percy thought about his mom, asleep in their tiny apartment in the city, probably with the heat turned up too high and a cup of tea going cold on the nightstand while she graded papers. He thought about Tyson snoring inside, safe and warm. About Grover, whose leg bounced anxiously even when he was relaxed, who'd stayed by Percy's side through everything since kindergarten. About Ella, who memorized books because the world felt safer in quotes.

He thought about Annabeth, asleep in Cabin One, probably stealing all of Thalia's blankets. He hoped she was warm.

"Hey, Grover?" he said softly.

"Yeah?"

"Do you think... this is what it's supposed to feel like? Camp? Like... family? Even when it's freezing and a snow goddess is pranking us?"

Grover was quiet, then said, "I think family is whoever shares your blanket when it's cold without being asked."

Ella repeated sleepily, "'Shares your blanket... Ella likes that. Ella will remember.'"

She dozed off against Percy's shoulder, beak tucked into her feathers, tiny talons curled around the blanket edge.

Grover dozed too, head tipping onto Percy's other shoulder, hat sliding over one eye.

Percy stayed awake a little longer, keeping watch the way he'd learned to on quests — but this wasn't a quest watch, tense and fearful. This was a soft watch. A keeping-warm watch. He counted shooting stars (three) and made stupid wishes (extra blue pancakes, no monsters till spring, Annabeth beating Clarisse at capture-the-flag just once so she'd stop pouting about it).

The snow glittered blue-silver around them, magical and strange and — now that he was warm enough — beautiful. Somewhere out in the dark, the sea moved against the shore, his father's domain, restless even in winter.

"Thanks, Dad," Percy whispered to the night, not really expecting an answer. "For the water. And the... you know. The toes that don't freeze. Appreciate it."

The wind shifted, just slightly, smelling of salt.

Percy smiled, pulled the blanket tighter around his friends, and leaned back to watch the sky.

They woke to screaming.

Not monster screaming — delighted screaming. The kind that meant either someone had done something amazing or the Stolls had done something terrible. Sometimes both.

Percy stumbled out of Cabin Three — he'd eventually moved inside at 2 a.m. when Grover had started sleep-bleating and Ella had been carried inside to the warmest corner — to find half the camp gathered around the whiteboard on the Big House porch.

Overnight, in elegant, frosty script that steamed faintly in the cold air, someone had added a new line to Annabeth's Hot Chocolate Wars rules:

*5. Winner gets the Golden Mug. P.S. — YOU'RE WELCOME FOR THE SNOW. — K ❄*

Below it, in Chiron's neat, exasperated handwriting: *Campers: please do not challenge goddesses to rematches. Hot chocolate judging at 7 p.m. sharp. Wear coats.*

The camp erupted.

"She signed it!" Travis crowed. "With a snowflake!"

"She thinks she's so funny!" Katie said, hands on hips, but she was laughing.

"Are we... supposed to thank her?" Will asked. "Is that a trap?"

Chiron, trotting out in a heavy coat and a scarf that looked suspiciously like something Mrs. O'Leary— no, like something his students had knitted (lumpy, red, with windows), sighed the sigh of a centaur who had dealt with divine pranks for three thousand years.

"Khione sends her... regards," he said diplomatically. "She found your cocoa competition amusing. She has agreed not to freeze the lake solid. In exchange, she requests a mug of the winning hot chocolate left out overnight as an offering."

"She wants our cocoa?" Clarisse said, arms crossed, scarf-in-progress peeking from her coat pocket. "She pranks us and then demands tribute?"

"She's a goddess," Chiron said. "That's... fairly standard."

Annabeth, arriving with Thalia, both pink-cheeked and bright-eyed from a night of actual sleep (warm, shared-blanket sleep), read the frosty message and grinned.

"Fine," she announced. "We'll leave her a mug. But we're putting a note on it that says *Thanks for the snow, now please turn the heater up.*"

Laughter. Someone threw a snowball that hit Connor square in the back. He retaliated immediately. Within minutes, the porch dissolved into cheerful chaos.

That night's tasting was the biggest yet. The Big House living room overflowed — campers on couches, on the floor, on the stairs, mugs in hands, blankets everywhere. The fire crackled. Mr. D, who claimed to be above such things, judged with surprising seriousness and declared the Ares chili entry "adequately violent."

Cabin Three's salted caramel peanut butter cinnamon entry took second place, losing by two votes to Aphrodite's white chocolate raspberry, which Silena, who had helped her Aphrodite friends refine their recipe, applauded while their cabin screamed.

"It's rigged! Pretty privilege!" Travis shouted, but he was already getting seconds.

"Leave a mug for Khione?" Percy asked, holding the spare mug they'd poured.

Together, the winners carried it outside and set it on the porch rail. The snow glittered blue and silver under the moon. The stars were out again, impossibly bright. The air was sharp and clean.

Annabeth tucked her note under the mug: *Thanks for the snow. It was beautiful. — Camp Half-Blood (P.S. Please don't freeze our toes off. — A.C.)*

Thalia added, *Nice snow. Don't do it again. — T.G.*

Percy added, *Good snow for snowballs. 10/10. — P.J.*

Grover added a drawing of a goat giving a thumbs-up. Ella added a feather. Clarisse, after a moment's hesitation, added a tiny red yarn tassel — a practice bit from her scarf — and muttered, "For... festive purposes," and walked away quickly before anyone could comment.

Silena read aloud one more chapter by the fire that night, with half the camp sprawled and sleepy, Clarisse knitting in the corner (rows getting straighter), Percy and Annabeth sharing a blanket and arguing quietly over star charts, Thalia braiding Annabeth's hair without asking, Grover and Ella sharing popcorn, Tyson snoring gently against the couch.

When Silena closed the book — this time on a happy pause, Lucy safe, spring hinted at — nobody protested. They were too warm, too full of chocolate, too drowsy.

Outside, the offering mug steamed in the cold. By morning, it would be empty, with nothing but a faint scent of frost and a tiny, perfect snowflake etched into the porch rail where it had sat — Khione's version of a thank-you note.

But that night, winter was just winter. Not a battlefield, not a prophecy, not a test.

Just snow, and cocoa, and friends sharing blankets against the cold.

Just home.
