## Chapter 204: One Week, Then Ask

The splint made a small, dry sound when Thalia pulled on her boot.

She stopped with the laces between her fingers. The leather pressed against the wrap. Not sharply. Not enough to call pain, if she wanted to be irritating about the word. The heel was still tender, though. It had been tender when she woke. It had been tender when she went to sleep. A week of rest had changed some things and left others exactly where they were.

She loosened the laces and tried again.

The right boot went on normally. The left took another minute. She tied it the way Chiron had shown her, loose enough that the splint did not shift, snug enough that her foot did not slide.

Across the room, Annabeth shut her field book.

“You’re going to the Big House now?” she asked.

“After breakfast.”

“Chiron said to come before lunch.”

“He said to come today.”

“He also said the swelling would be easiest to compare in the morning.”

Thalia looked up. “You memorized that?”

“You were in the room.”

“I was talking to him.”

“And I was listening.”

It wasn’t an argument yet. Annabeth tucked the book beneath one arm and stood. Thalia watched her choose the repaired side of the floorboards, careful not to kick the stool leg that was still drying against the wall. The glue had gone cloudy at the joint. Annabeth had said it needed another day before anyone sat on it. Thalia had not tested it.

“You can come with me,” Thalia said. “If you want.”

“I do.”

“I don’t need you to take notes.”

“I know.”

“You don’t have to ask what he says.”

“I know that too.”

“Then what do you want to do?”

Annabeth considered. “Walk over there. Sit outside if you want me to. Go back to Cabin One afterward and work on the map. Maybe ask if he has more glue for the stool.”

“You can do all of that.”

“I know.”

Thalia tugged one lace, then let it go. She heard the sharpness in her voice after the words had already left. Annabeth did not pretend not to hear it.

“I’m not trying to supervise you,” Thalia said.

“You’ve said that before.”

“And?”

“And sometimes you mean it. Sometimes you mean you’re not supervising me unless I do something you think is dangerous.”

Thalia glanced at the splint. “This is dangerous.”

“Your heel is injured. That doesn’t make every decision I make about it your decision.”

The answer was calm. It still got under Thalia’s skin. Her first impulse was to point out that she was the one who had been in the office, the one who had walked home with a fresh splint, the one who knew what it felt like when the heel caught on a stair. Annabeth knew those things too. She had watched her sister try to conceal the limp.

She tied the lace into a bow. “I know.”

Annabeth picked up a clean scarf from the bed. “Do you want this?”

“It isn’t cold enough.”

“It was cold on the porch.”

“Then I’ll take it.”

“Good.”

Annabeth draped it over the chair beside her rather than wrapping it around Thalia’s neck. Thalia liked the offer better that way. She could put it on or leave it there; no one had to have a second argument.

The cabin smelled like yesterday’s woodsmoke and the last piece of toast cooling on the table. Morning light fell through the narrow window. The stool leaned in its corner with the repaired leg exposed, still waiting for the glue to set. A folded blanket lay over Thalia’s bed where she had put it before sleep. The splint’s edge showed beneath her trouser cuff.

She took the toast and stood.

Annabeth’s gaze went to her feet. “Do you want to sit while you eat?”

“I want to walk.”

“Okay.”

Thalia waited for a comment about the difference. Annabeth only returned to the table and picked up her own breakfast. She had learned not to turn each choice into a trial, or at least she was trying to. The effort was visible in her hands, the way she held the plate steady instead of reaching to carry both.

Thalia ate slowly. The toast had a tough edge where it had sat too long. She chewed it anyway.

“What changed this week?” Annabeth asked.

“About what?”

“The foot.”

“You’ve seen me walk.”

“I’m asking what it feels like to you.”

Thalia rested the plate on the table. “It doesn’t throb when I’m lying down anymore. The first few steps in the morning are stiff. Going downhill still pulls. If I turn fast, it hurts.”

“Any less than last week?”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

“Same place.”

Annabeth nodded and did not ask for a demonstration. “You can tell Chiron that.”

“I was going to.”

“I know.”

“Then why ask?”

“Because I wanted to know.”

That was fair, which did not mean Thalia liked how quickly she could say it.

Annabeth brushed a crumb from the table. “You don’t need to give me the whole report.”

“I know.”

“I’d still like to walk with you.”

“You said that.”

“I’m repeating it so you can hear the difference.”

Thalia stared at her sister. Annabeth looked back, steady but not challenging. She had their father’s gray eyes and her own expression, the one she used when she had made a decision and was willing to explain it once.

Thalia pulled on the scarf.

“Fine,” she said. “You can walk with me.”

“Thanks.”

“Don’t thank me for letting you walk.”

“I’m thanking you for asking.”

Thalia had no response that wouldn’t make it worse. She picked up the plate and carried it to the basin instead. Annabeth took hers. They washed the dishes with the window open a crack, the cold air coming in around the frame.

The first time Thalia lifted her heel to reach the upper shelf, the pain caught low and brief. She put her foot down again without making a face. Annabeth did not comment. She passed her the towel and dried the spoon she had been holding.

When they were done, Thalia checked the laces one more time. The left boot held. The wrap held. She put the empty plate in the rack and left the drying stool where it was.

“Ready?” Annabeth asked.

“Now I am.”

They stepped onto the porch. The loose rail waited at the corner, tagged in chalk where Beckendorf had marked the spot for a new screw. Thalia took the steps at her own speed. Annabeth walked beside her, not ahead and not behind.

The Big House sat across the clearing with its shutters open to the day. A couple of campers crossed the lawn carrying a bucket between them. Neither called out. Thalia liked the quiet. There was no audience waiting to turn her foot into an announcement.

At the bottom of the steps, Annabeth stopped.

“Do you want me beside you in the office?”

“If you want to sit there.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

Thalia breathed out. “I want you in the room. But I want to answer Chiron.”

“Then I’ll let you.”

“Don’t wait outside because you think I need space.”

“I won’t.”

“Don’t come in because you think I need help.”

“I’ll wait until you ask.”

It sounded like a rule they would both break at some point. Thalia recognized that and did not need to add a warning.

They crossed the clearing together.

---

Chiron had already put a folded towel on the low examination table.

He was by the window, sorting two narrow splints and a roll of clean wrap into a drawer. He glanced at Thalia’s boot, then at her face.

“Good morning.”

“Morning.”

“Do you want to start standing, or sit first?”

“I can stand.”

“Then stand.”

He did not say that she should prove it. He did not ask her to show how far she could walk. Thalia stood beside the table and held her shoulders straight while Chiron set the drawer shut. Annabeth chose the chair near the door and put her book on her lap. She did not open it.

Chiron turned back. “Tell me what changed.”

Thalia glanced at Annabeth. Her sister kept her gaze on her, but didn’t lean in.

“It’s better in the morning,” Thalia said. “Not good. Better. It doesn’t throb all night anymore. The first few steps are stiff. Downhill still hurts, and I can’t turn quickly without feeling it.”

“Has the pain moved?”

“No.”

“Has it become sharper?”

“No. It’s the same spot, just less often.”

“Have you been running?”

“No.”

“Hauling timber?”

“No.”

“Archery?”

“Not this week.”

Chiron nodded. “And the hall?”

“I went to the edge of the room Tuesday. I watched. I did not get in the water.”

“Did you want to?”

“I wanted to be there.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

Thalia looked at him. “I didn’t want to swim enough to risk it.”

“Good. You’re allowed to want two things at once.”

“I can do that without permission.”

“Yes.”

He moved the chair out from under the table. “Would you sit now?”

Thalia sat on the edge. Her boot loosened easily. The wrap beneath it had stayed in place. She undid the top laces and drew the boot off, careful not to catch the splint’s leather strap.

“Do you want me to remove the splint?” Chiron asked.

“I can.”

She lifted the strap, and he waited while she loosened the wrap. The heel was still tender before anyone touched it. That was the first thing she noticed and the thing she was least eager to say.

The swelling had gone down. She could see the difference where the skin no longer felt so tight under the edge of the wrap. The color had faded too. There was still a small puffiness around the back, but not as much as the first morning. She hated that she knew this because she’d checked every night.

Chiron knelt in front of the chair but kept his hands in his lap. “May I look?”

“Yes.”

He checked the skin, the remaining swelling, the wrap marks. “Does it hurt here?”

He pressed lightly at the first place.

“No.”

“Here?”

“Not there.”

He moved his thumb a little lower, to the spot she had named. Thalia flinched before she could decide not to.

“That’s the same point,” she said.

“Yes. Is it more or less tender than when I examined it last week?”

“Less if you don’t press it.”

“That is not a measure.”

“It is what I’ve got.”

“It helps. I’ll compare it with how you walked in and with what you tell me.” His hand retreated. “Can you stand with both feet even?”

She put the boot back on without tying it and stood. The heel accepted the floor, then sent a dull warning up through her leg. She made herself breathe through it.

“Even?” Chiron asked.

“Mostly.”

“Are you putting less weight on it?”

“Probably.”

“Not probably. Are you?”

She shifted her hip to center. “I am when it hurts.”

“That is a useful answer.”

“Is it?”

“It tells us you feel pain and adjust around it. It also means the heel isn’t ready for unrestricted activity.”

Thalia sat. “I didn’t expect unrestricted.”

“I want to say it clearly so neither of us starts guessing.”

“Fine.”

Chiron checked the wrap and splint again. “The swelling is lower. The tenderness is still there, but you say it is less frequent. That is improvement. It is not enough for running, patrols, hauling, or a full return to archery practice.”

“Can I use the line?”

“If you can take a short modified stance without pain. Stop if pain returns. You already know that from last week.”

“I know.”

“Knowing and following are separate skills.”

“So is saying the same thing five times.”

“You’re right. I’ll try to be more interesting.” He stood and crossed to the table, giving her room to put her boot back on. “I said I would bring Will in if it wasn’t improving. I’m not calling him today, because the swelling and pain are both better. If there is no further improvement over the next few days, or if it gets worse, I will ask him to examine it with me. You won’t have to convince me twice.”

Thalia liked that more than she wanted to. “And how long until I can go back?”

“I can’t tell you.”

“You said that last week.”

“It remains true.”

“So I just keep coming back until it doesn’t hurt?”

“We keep checking. We keep the activity small enough that you aren’t turning pain into a challenge. If it changes, we change the plan.”

She looked at her heel. The answer had no finish line. It did not tell her whether she would be back at the shooting line by the next campfire or still sitting on the sidelines when the first frost melted. There was no date for her to circle.

“How long for this splint?”

“Keep using it when you’re moving around camp until we review the tenderness again. You can loosen it while you’re sitting, as you did last week, if the wrap stays secure and there’s no pressure. Keep it dry. If it slips, come find me before you try to fix it with a belt.”

“I never used a belt.”

“I am remembering other people.”

“Then remember them at them.”

Chiron’s eyebrows lifted. “I will make a note.”

Annabeth touched the edge of her book. “Do you want me to write down what he said?”

Thalia looked at her. “I can remember.”

“I know.”

“Then don’t.”

Annabeth nodded and returned her hands to the book. She did not look offended, which was more generous than Thalia deserved after the way she had spoken that morning. Or perhaps Annabeth was simply letting her answer stand.

Chiron gave Thalia a fresh length of wrap, not a new splint. She secured it herself, and he watched only when she asked him to check whether it was too tight. He pressed the edge once.

“Good.”

“Good as in done?”

“Good as in secure.”

Thalia finished buckling the strap. “Can I go to Rhys’s lesson?”

“You can watch.”

“Can I shoot?”

“If the modified stance is comfortable and the first shot does not bring the pain back. Keep it brief. You don’t need to make a day out of it.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were thinking about how many arrows count as a day.”

“That’s not true.”

Chiron looked at her.

“I was thinking about it a little.”

“Then choose a small number before you get there.”

“Three.”

“Three is a number.”

“Thank you.”

“You’re welcome.”

She slid her boot fully into place. The pain stayed dull, but it was there. “If it hurts on the first one, I stop.”

“Yes.”

“If it hurts after two, I stop.”

“Yes.”

“If it doesn’t hurt, I stop after three.”

“That would be the number you chose.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

Thalia stood from the chair. Her heel pressed into the floor; she shifted once, then corrected. The splint supported her. It didn’t hide the ache or replace her decision. It did what a splint was supposed to do, which was both boring and irritating.

Chiron moved the chair back beneath the table. “Do you want a moment before you go?”

“No.”

“Then you’re finished.”

Thalia took her scarf from Annabeth, who had folded it neatly while waiting. “You ready?”

Annabeth got up. “Yes.”

They went out together.

---

Percy was on the Big House porch when Thalia and Annabeth came out.

He had an old camp map under his arm and a pencil behind his ear. He stood when he saw them, then seemed to think better of it and stayed where he was.

“How did it go?” he asked.

Thalia glanced at Chiron’s open door. “Better than it was.”

“Good.”

“I’m not cleared for anything big.”

“I didn’t ask.”

“No. You didn’t.”

She felt the words come out with more edge than she meant. Percy accepted them without stepping closer or trying to smooth the moment.

“Do you want me to say something?” he asked.

“No.”

“All right.”

He looked at the map in his hand. “Annabeth, I’ve marked the low path by the east garden. Do you want to look at it before I put it on the board?”

“Yes.” She took the map from him. “You used the old route.”

“It’s the one the kids know.”

“It’s the one that gets muddy when the rain backs up.”

“Which is why I marked it.”

“You marked the path as closed.”

“It’s slippery.”

“Sometimes.”

He tapped the pencil against his ear. “I thought it was better to be clear.”

“Clear is good. Accurate is better.” Annabeth rolled the paper toward herself. “We can mark the lower stretch as muddy and use the upper path as the temporary route. If you write ‘closed’ for the whole thing, people may think it’s unsafe even when it’s dry.”

“I wasn’t trying to say it was unsafe.”

“I know. But that’s what it reads like.”

Percy leaned over the map, careful not to crowd her hand. “Then I should change it.”

“If you want to.”

“I do.”

Thalia watched him take the pencil from behind his ear and erase the thick slash. A pale smudge remained. Annabeth held the page flat until he finished, then pointed to the place where the route bent around the garden wall.

“Put the note here,” she said. “Then it’s attached to the section that changes.”

“Here?”

“Two inches higher.”

He moved the pencil.

“Too far.”

“Can I get a measurement?”

“You’re holding the map.”

“That doesn’t tell me where your finger is.”

“My finger is on the spot.”

“It’s a moving spot.”

She put her finger down again, deliberately. “There.”

He made the mark.

“Good,” she said.

“Good as in done?”

“Good as in readable.”

“That’s the Chiron answer.”

“It’s the answer you needed.”

“I can live with that.”

Annabeth handed the map back. Their fingers touched along the fold. Percy did not let go right away; Annabeth looked at him, amused, and he released it.

“You kept the working edge from getting wet,” she said.

“I put it under my jacket.”

“Then you were useful.”

“I was hoping you’d notice.”

“I noticed the map. You were under it.”

“Exactly.”

Annabeth laughed. She tucked the map into her tube, then leaned her shoulder against his. It was quick and familiar, the kind of small contact that didn’t require either of them to explain it.

Thalia looked away before she could feel like she had been caught looking. She had watched them long enough to know how easily they could be themselves together. The argument about the map had not been a threat. Percy had changed the mark because Annabeth was right about what it would tell people. Annabeth had let him keep the note because he had noticed the muddy stretch. Neither had needed to turn the answer into a victory.

“You said you wanted to go back to Cabin One,” Percy told Annabeth.

“I do.”

“I’ll walk with you.”

“You have the east path board.”

“I can bring the map over after.”

“You’re going to rewrite the note now?”

“Probably.”

“Then stay and do it.”

He looked at the porch table, where Chiron had left a stack of blank cards. “I could do it later.”

“You could. I’m not asking you to come.”

“I know.”

Annabeth’s gaze stayed on him, waiting. He looked down at the map tube and then back at her.

“Do you want me to walk with you?” he asked.

“I want to finish checking the low path first. I’ll come back this way.”

“Then I’ll finish the note.”

“Okay.”

They touched hands once, and Annabeth turned toward the path. Percy watched her go only long enough to see that she had the tube tucked safely under her arm. Then he sat at the porch table and began copying the corrected wording onto one of Chiron’s cards.

Thalia took one of the chairs. “You’re not going after her?”

“I’m not.”

“She’s walking alone.”

“She said she was coming back this way.”

“And you believe her?”

“Yes.”

The answer was so simple that Thalia looked for a joke hidden inside it. Percy only sharpened the pencil.

“What?” he said.

“Nothing.”

“You made a face.”

“I have a face.”

“It looked skeptical.”

“It was.”

“Why?”

“You’re usually trying to walk everyone everywhere.”

“I’m trying to be polite.”

“You’re trying to make sure everybody gets where they’re going.”

“That’s not always bad.”

“No.”

“It’s not always bad?”

“It’s also not always what people want.”

He set the pencil down. “Annabeth says that too.”

“Maybe you should listen when both of us say it.”

“I did.”

“You’re sitting here.”

“Because Annabeth asked me to finish this.”

“You’re listening to her because she told you what she wanted.”

“That’s generally a good way to listen.”

Thalia glanced toward the path. Annabeth was already near the garden wall, still walking at her own pace. She did not turn around to see whether Percy followed.

“I’m not asking you to walk me,” Thalia said.

“I didn’t think you were.”

“Good.”

“I can keep the card at the table, though. If you want to write anything down.”

“I told Chiron what changed.”

“Right.”

“You don’t need to keep a record.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Percy.”

“I wasn’t.”

She watched his fingers move toward the pencil, stop, then rest flat on the table. He had heard the warning before she finished saying his name. It was a small thing. It mattered because it was small.

“Okay,” she said.

He looked up. “Did I pass?”

“You weren’t taking a test.”

“Then I’ll take the card as a compliment.”

“Take it as a card.”

He laughed. Thalia stood. The heel gave a quiet tug when she put her weight forward. She did not hide it, but she didn’t say anything either.

Percy’s eyes moved to the boot and then back to his pencil. He stayed seated.

She could have thanked him for that. She didn’t. She had not asked for the restraint as a favor.

At the porch steps she turned once. “Three arrows.”

“What?”

“I’m going to Rhys’s lesson. I can try three arrows if the first one doesn’t hurt.”

Percy’s mouth opened, then closed. “That sounds like a plan.”

“It’s not a promise.”

“I heard you.”

“Good.”

She went down the steps without waiting for his reply.

---

Rhys was setting the targets closer to the short line when Thalia reached the field.

He had four juniors with him, each holding a bow and waiting for the first instruction. One had a glove with the fingers folded back. Another kept stepping onto the chalk mark before Rhys finished measuring. No one had yet managed to aim at the wrong target, which seemed to disappoint them.

Rhys looked at Thalia’s boots and the splint strap, then at her face. “Do you want the bench or the line?”

“The bench first.”

“Good.”

“Don’t say it like you were waiting for that answer.”

“I was waiting for an answer.”

She lowered herself onto the bench. It was low enough that getting back up would be annoying, but not painful. The heel felt steady if she kept her foot flat.

Rhys set a folded blanket beside her. “For the splint.”

“I brought a scarf.”

“Then this can be for your hands.”

“It’s not that cold.”

“Then it can be for the bench.”

“That’s less insulting.”

He left it beside her and went back to the students.

Thalia watched him take the crooked glove from the smaller camper and turn it around. He showed him where the seam was folded beneath the palm. The kid tried it again, drew the string, and let go before he had aimed.

“Better,” Rhys said. “The bow isn’t going to escape.”

“It felt like it was.”

“It’s wood, not a horse.”

“It could be a horse.”

“Then you’d be the first camper to lose an archery match to a horse.”

The boy made a face. Rhys gave him the glove back.

Thalia rested her hands on her knees. The line looked the same as it had the week before, but she had not been there to stand on it. It felt odd to see the targets without being expected to take a place among them.

The camper with the folded glove tried again. This time he held the bow up until Rhys asked him to lower it. His arrow landed low on the target.

“Good,” Rhys said.

“It’s not on the colored part.”

“It’s in the target.”

“Barely.”

“Then next time, aim a little higher. You’re learning the bow and the distance. You don’t need to hit the center on your first shot.”

The child looked back toward the ring. “What if I want to?”

“Then you can want to. And you can still practice the part that gets you closer.”

Thalia looked away before Rhys could catch her listening. Her heel had settled into the bench, the ache quiet but not gone. She shifted one inch, testing the position. The splint stayed snug.

After the first round, Rhys came to sit on the end of the bench.

“Do you want to shoot?” he asked.

“I do.”

“Do you want to today?”

“That’s the part I’m deciding.”

“Take your time.”

Thalia rolled her shoulders. “Chiron said I can try a short modified stance if it doesn’t hurt. If the first one hurts, I stop.”

“Do you want me to hold the bow?”

“No.”

“Do you want me to move the stool?”

“There isn’t a stool.”

“I can find one.”

“I don’t want one.”

“All right.”

She stood, using the bench arm for balance. The heel didn’t protest, though she could feel it adjusting under the splint. She waited for the first step to become worse. It didn’t.

“I’m not carrying anything,” she said.

“I know.”

“I’m not staying for a whole practice.”

“I know.”

“I’m not asking you to watch me.”

“I can look at the targets.”

She gave him a warning look.

“Not at you,” he added.

“That’s worse.”

He shrugged and walked to the bow rack. “Pick one you like.”

She chose a familiar bow, light in the grip, and waited while Rhys handed her three arrows. He didn’t set the arrows in her hand like she needed help. She took them herself.

The younger campers had moved to the waiting mark. Rhys gave them a brief instruction about staying behind the line until everyone was done. Then he turned back to Thalia.

“Short stance,” she said.

“Your stance.”

“Modified. Chiron’s word.”

“I wasn’t going to give it a name.”

“Good.”

She set her uninjured foot forward, kept the injured heel back and supported, and made the smallest adjustment that still felt like archery. The position was strange. It took more concentration than drawing the bow. She was used to letting the body make small corrections without thinking. Now she could feel every shift.

The first arrow went low, then held in the outer ring.

She let the bow down.

“Pain?” Rhys asked.

“No.”

“Then that one counts.”

“I wasn't asking.”

“I was.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I know. I asked anyway.”

She checked the heel. It felt stable. There was a faint pressure beneath the wrap, not pain. She could have ended there. She had wanted to shoot three arrows, and the first had not hurt.

“One more,” she said.

Rhys nodded and stepped back.

The second arrow landed near the first. Thalia’s shoulder loosened, and for a second the old familiar rhythm came back. The line, the bow, the target. Her feet told her where to settle; her hands did the rest. Then she shifted her weight off the back foot, and the heel gave a short, sharp tug.

She lowered the bow immediately.

“Pain?” Rhys asked.

“Yes.”

“Do you want the bench?”

“Yes.”

He did not reach for the bow. She lowered it carefully, handed it to him when she was ready, and walked the two steps back. Rhys set the bow on the rack. He did not look at the target or count how many arrows she had fired.

The young camper with the crooked glove stared at her splint. “Did you hurt your foot?”

“Yes.”

“Does it hurt now?”

“A little.”

“Are you going to shoot again?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because it hurt.”

The boy looked disappointed, though not at her. “But you were good.”

“Still am.”

He thought about that. “Can I shoot again?”

“Ask Rhys.”

“I already did.”

“Then listen to Rhys.”

“He said one more.”

“Then take one more.”

The boy went back to the line.

Thalia sat down. The heel had not kept hurting. The sharper tug had faded, leaving the usual tenderness behind. She kept her foot flat and her hands around the empty scarf.

Rhys returned after the campers had taken their turn. “Do you want me to call Chiron?”

“No. It’s already settling.”

“Do you want to go back to the Big House?”

“No.”

“Do you want me to tell the class why you stopped?”

“No.”

“I won’t.”

“If they ask, I already told them.”

“Then I’ll let that be enough.”

She looked at the line. The two arrows were close together, not spectacular and not bad. She was glad she’d shot them. She was glad she had stopped. They didn’t cancel each other out.

The lesson continued. Rhys corrected the glove and showed one of the others how to stand without locking his elbow. Thalia offered no more advice from the bench. She watched the small things the children did between shots: the way they pulled a sleeve over their wrist, the way they tried not to look nervous, the quick glance toward Rhys when the arrows landed.

When the smaller camper shot into the target rim, he looked at Thalia again.

“Don’t ask me,” she said.

“I wasn’t.”

“You were thinking about it.”

“I wanted to know if I did it wrong.”

“You did something differently. Ask Rhys what.”

Rhys was already approaching. “What felt different?”

The boy pointed at his shoulder and showed how it had lifted. Rhys adjusted the bow’s angle, then asked him to try again. The arrow landed inside the outer ring.

The boy grinned. Rhys nodded, pleased but not surprised.

Thalia relaxed into the bench. She had been useful to the class without taking the bow from anyone. She hadn’t needed to turn the ache into a demonstration. That mattered more than she wanted it to.

By the end, all four campers had retrieved their arrows and put the bows away. Rhys carried the quiver to the rack. Thalia stayed seated until the path cleared.

“Do you want to walk back with me?” she asked.

“I do.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I know.”

She studied his face. “Did you want me to shoot?”

“I wanted you to want what you wanted.”

“That’s a stupid answer.”

“It’s true.”

“It can be both.”

“Probably.”

He offered his hand, palm open, and waited. She looked at it, then put her hand in his. He helped her rise without pulling, letting her find her balance before walking beside her.

At the path, she stopped. “I’m going to take the longer route.”

“Because it’s flatter?”

“Because I want to.”

“Then we’ll take the longer route.”

She squeezed his fingers once and let go. “You’re coming back to teach tomorrow.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“Good as in approved?”

“Good as in I’ll decide tomorrow if I want to watch.”

He nodded. “That makes sense.”

“Don’t sound surprised.”

“I’m not surprised.”

She believed him. They walked to the split in the path at an easy pace, without timing her steps or discussing the heel again.

---

The ache began on the walk back from the field.

It was not a sharp pain. Thalia could have ignored it if she wanted to. It was the sort of thing that lived in a line between the heel and the ankle, a small pull that reminded her the foot had not agreed to be ordinary again just because Chiron had said the swelling was better.

She stopped at the edge of the path.

Rhys looked over his shoulder. “What is it?”

“Nothing.”

He waited.

She watched a pair of campers carry a basket through the clearing. One of them was walking backward and talking with both hands, leaving the other to steer. Neither looked at her. She was grateful for that. The ache pressed again when she shifted her weight.

“Thalia?” Rhys asked.

“It tugged.”

“Do you want to sit?”

“I want to go back to Chiron.”

“Then go back to Chiron.”

He did not reach for her elbow. He did not offer to carry her. The absence of those offers felt almost like a kindness, though she knew better than to praise someone for not grabbing her.

“Go teach your lesson,” she said. “I can walk.”

“I can come with you.”

“I didn’t ask you to.”

“No. You didn’t.”

He took a half step, then stopped. The decision remained where she had left it.

Thalia exhaled. “I want you to come with me.”

“Okay.”

They went uphill at a pace that made her feel the ground under each step. The splint pressed against the back of her boot. It had been there all day and would be there tomorrow if she let it. She resented the strap for being useful.

At the Big House porch, Rhys stopped beside the rail. Chiron was inside, speaking to someone in the front room. Thalia could hear the other person’s voice but not the words. She shifted away from the step, where the boards were damp from the shade.

“Will you be all right?” Rhys asked.

“I’m not in a duel.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

“I know.”

He glanced toward the door. “Do you want me to wait?”

“No. You have four kids and a target line.”

“They can wait five minutes.”

“They’ve waited enough for me today.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yes.”

He pressed his thumb once against the edge of her sleeve, then let go. “I’ll be on the field until dinner.”

“I know where the field is.”

“I know you do.”

“Then go.”

His mouth tipped into a small, unoffended smile. “See you later.”

“Maybe.”

“Maybe is fine.”

He went down the porch steps without making a show of leaving her to cope. Thalia watched until he reached the path, then turned to the door.

Chiron came out before she could knock. He wore his reading glasses on the end of his nose and had an open book under one hand.

“Did something change?” he asked.

“The heel tugged after the second arrow.”

He closed the book with his finger marking the page. “Did you stop?”

“Yes.”

“Good. Sit down, please.”

The chair by the wall had a firm back and no arms. She chose the bench instead. It was easier to lower herself without having to balance on one leg. Chiron did not comment on the choice.

“Was it the same pain you had before the splint?” he asked.

“Not exactly. Smaller. Like a pull.”

“Did it continue while you were walking?”

“It came and went.”

“Is it happening now?”

“Not really.”

“Any sharp pain?”

“No.”

“Did you feel unsteady?”

“No.”

He set the book aside and washed his hands at the basin. When he turned back, he kept a few feet between them. “May I look at the heel?”

The question landed harder than it should have. Not because he had asked before; because he asked again, as if the first examination had not made her a permanent object under his hands.

“Okay.”

“Would you rather remove the boot yourself?”

“I can.”

“Take your time.”

She worked the laces loose, pulled the boot down, and untied the wrap around the splint. The familiar motions made her shoulders tighten. She tried to keep the heel raised while she adjusted her position. Chiron placed a folded towel beside her knee, not beneath the foot.

“Do you want it there?” he asked.

“Under my calf.”

He moved it. She settled her leg over the towel, and the pressure eased.

Chiron inspected the heel and pressed lightly along the edges of the swelling. He watched her face rather than asking whether it hurt after every touch. She appreciated the restraint, and hated that she had to appreciate it.

“Still tender here?”

“Yes.”

“Less than last time?”

“Yes.”

“Does the pull you felt at the line happen when I press?”

“No.”

He stopped. “I won’t call that clearance. Improvement is useful information, and the pain during the second arrow is useful too. It means your foot was finished for today.”

“I know.”

“You stopped.”

“I know that too.”

“Then we agree.”

She looked at the ceiling. “I don’t want every choice I make around it to become a lesson.”

“It doesn’t have to.”

“People keep saying that after they’ve already made it one.”

“Then tell them when you want the conversation to end.”

“And if they don’t listen?”

“Tell me.”

“That makes it a bigger conversation.”

“It might. I won’t pretend every answer is comfortable.”

Chiron set the wrap on the table. “You followed the instruction. You tried the modified stance, noticed pain, and stopped. The information changes what you do next. It doesn’t say anything about your character.”

Thalia stared at the strip of clean cloth. It was not a new idea. She had known it before she came in. Knowing was one thing; hearing it from someone who was not trying to turn the right choice into a medal was another.

“How long do I stop?” she asked.

“For today, no more shooting. Continue the rest, ice, wrap, and splint plan tonight. Tomorrow, don’t resume shooting until the tug has settled. If it returns when you try again, stop and come see me before strenuous training. We’ll keep watching the swelling and tenderness. If the improvement stalls or reverses, we bring Will in.”

“So I don’t have to decide now whether I’ll be on the line tomorrow.”

“No.”

“Or tomorrow’s hall.”

“That is a separate choice. The hall is an activity. You can visit and stay dry, leave early, or not go. You do not have to decide before you know what the heel is doing.”

“Everyone will know I’m deciding.”

“Everyone will survive.”

She almost smiled. “You say that like you’ve met them.”

“I have met all of them.”

Chiron handed her the wrap. She tied it at a comfortable pressure, not tight enough to turn it into a test. Then she fitted the splint around the heel and slid her foot back into the boot.

“I’ll write down what happened,” Chiron said. “The same way I wrote down the swelling today.”

“I don’t need the whole camp to read it.”

“Only the people who need to know for safety. And I’ll keep the entry private from the general camp board.”

“Thank you.”

“Would you like to read what I write?”

“Yeah.”

He brought the slate over and set it on the table, turning it so she could see. The entry was short: swelling improved; tenderness reduced; modified stance attempted; mild tug after second arrow; stopped; continue care plan; no clearance for strenuous training. He had not added that she had tried three arrows, that she was frustrated, or that she had looked at the sky instead of him while answering.

“That’s fine,” she said.

“Would you like anything changed?”

“No.”

He erased the slate and copied the note into the medical book. The scratch of the pencil was quiet.

Thalia laced her boot. “If I go to the hall tomorrow, I’m not getting in the water.”

“That sounds like a decision you can make tomorrow.”

“I know. I’m saying it now so you don’t think I’m going to sneak in.”

“I wasn’t going to think that.”

“You’re very committed to the idea that nobody has to prove anything.”

“I’m committed to letting people tell me what they intend.”

She stood, slow enough to test the heel without turning the motion into a dare. It gave one dull complaint, then quieted.

“I’m leaving.”

“All right.”

“You’re not going to tell me to be careful?”

“I was going to ask whether you wanted help with the door.”

“No.”

“Then I won’t.”

She pushed the door open herself. The air outside was cooling. The sun had started to sink toward the trees, the light thinning over the green.

At the bottom of the steps, she looked across the camp. She could see the archery field from there, but not the targets. She could hear Rhys counting someone’s draw and the answering thud of an arrow against packed straw.

For one breath she wanted to go back and take another shot. Not because she needed to know anything about her heel. Because stopping had left the day unfinished.

Then she let the want remain a want.

She walked toward Cabin One.



---

Dinner had started by the time Thalia reached the pavilion.

She took the path around the muddy stretch rather than testing it. It cost an extra minute, which was irritating, but not as irritating as the idea of letting a boot slide because she wanted to prove the path had not become dangerous while she was out of the room.

The pavilion was warm with soup and wet wool. Silena had set bowls along the long table, and Clarisse was arguing with Beckendorf about whether a repaired hinge counted as finished if it still squeaked. Percy sat across from Annabeth, one hand around his mug, the other turned palm-up on the table. Annabeth was showing him something on a small card. He listened while she pointed to a line, then took the card and read it again.

Nobody had moved a chair to reserve a place for Thalia. Nobody needed to. There was an open spot beside Silena, close to the end where she could leave without asking three people to stand.

She sat.

Silena set down a bowl of soup. “Do you want the bread?”

“Yes.”

“Do you want the thick piece or the toasted one?”

“Whichever one you were going to give me.”

“That is not an answer.”

“Then the thick one.”

Silena passed it over. Thalia broke it in half and dipped one piece into the broth.

“How was Rhys’s lesson?” Silena asked.

“Fine.”

Clarisse lifted her head. “That’s a suspiciously short review.”

“It was fine. The kids were loud. The targets were where they were supposed to be. One of the arrows landed in the grass.”

“Whose?”

“Not mine.”

“Then why did you notice?”

“It was near my boot.”

Clarisse smiled into her mug. “You’re very involved.”

“I was sitting down.”

“You can be involved sitting down.”

“That sounds like something Chiron would say.”

“Chiron stole it from me.”

“I don’t believe you.”

“You don’t have to.”

Across the table Percy set the card aside. He did not turn toward Thalia or ask about her heel. The effort not to ask was so visible she could almost hear it.

She swallowed her soup. “I tried two arrows.”

Percy looked at her, then at his cup. “And?”

“The second one tugged. I stopped.”

“Good.”

“Don’t say it like you’re grading me.”

“Right. Sorry.”

“You can ask if I want an opinion.”

“Do you?”

“Not now.”

“Okay.”

Annabeth lifted the card again. “This is the route note for the lower garden path. Percy wrote it, I checked it, and neither of us is allowed to rewrite it without showing the other.”

“Why?” Clarisse asked.

“Because the first version made it sound like the whole path was closed.”

“It is muddy.”

“Only the lower stretch.”

“Isn’t a muddy path a closed path?”

“No,” Annabeth said. “It’s a path with mud.”

Percy turned his mug a quarter turn under his palm. “I put the sign at the upper junction.”

“And the note tells people to use the dry route.”

“Do we need both?”

“We do if the rain comes back.”

Clarisse shrugged. “Put up a sign. If someone walks in mud, they can wash their boots.”

“That’s not the only problem,” Percy said. “The slope gets slick.”

“Then say that.”

“That's what the note says.”

Annabeth slid the card to the middle. “It says the ground is soft and the lower curve is slick. It gives the alternate route. It does not say the entire garden is unsafe.”

“Fine,” Clarisse said. “That’s a good sign.”

Annabeth looked at Percy. “That’s what I told him.”

“I’m not taking credit for your opinion,” he said.

“You were about to.”

“I was about to say the sign is good.”

“You can still say that.”

“The sign is good.”

Clarisse looked between them. “Does the sign need a chaperone too?”

“No,” Annabeth and Percy said together.

They glanced at one another, then both laughed, and Thalia saw again the uncomplicated shape of their trust. It could withstand a bad map mark, a correction, the small annoyance of being right at the wrong time. It did not ask her to decide which of them deserved her loyalty.

She finished her soup and tore the second half of the bread into smaller pieces.

“Silena,” she said, “is the hall still on tomorrow?”

“Yes. If you want to come.”

“I said I’d be there last week.”

“You were.”

“I went. I watched. I didn’t get in.”

Silena did not look surprised. “I was glad you came.”

Thalia stirred the soup once. “I haven’t decided about tomorrow. I know I’m not going in the water.”

“That’s your choice.”

“Will the floor be wet?”

“Some of it. I can keep the mat down by the door. The benches will be dry.”

“That’s not the same as not slipping.”

“No,” Silena said. “It isn’t. I’ll set up the dry side whether you come or not. That’s how the hall works.”

“Will the floor be wet?”

“Some of it. I can keep the mat down by the door. The benches will be dry.”

“That’s not the same as not slipping.”

“No,” Silena said. “It isn’t.”

Thalia liked that she did not pretend it was. “I’ll decide in the morning.”

“Okay.”

Clarisse stirred her soup. “If it rains, don’t go.”

“I know what rain is.”

“I’m not insulting your intelligence.”

“It sounded like you were telling me what to do.”

“I was telling you to stay out of a wet room with a bad heel.”

“I heard you.”

Clarisse held her gaze. “All right.”

The answer was not an apology. It was a stop. Thalia could accept a stop when she chose it.

She looked down the table. Leandros was not there. The empty place did not feel like a question; it felt like space left open for someone who might come or might not. She was glad nobody had made a speech about that either.

When the meal ended, people carried bowls to the wash station. Thalia stood after the table had cleared enough for her to move without needing to step over anyone’s boots. Percy was already at the basin with Annabeth. He rinsed a bowl, passed it to her, and she set it on the drying rack. Their hands met once at the rim. Neither of them flinched away or made the moment into a performance.

Thalia waited until they were done.

“Percy,” she said. “Can we talk after?”

His hands stopped in the dishwater. “Sure.”

“Not about the heel.”

“Okay.”

Annabeth looked from him to her. “Do you want me there?”

“No.”

“Okay.”

She dried her hands on the towel. “I’ll be inside if you need me.”

“I won’t.”

“I know.”

It was not a question, and not an invitation to persuade her. Annabeth set the towel back on its hook and went to collect the map tube. Percy watched her leave the pavilion, then looked at Thalia.

“Now?”

“When you’re done.”

“I’m done.”

“You can finish the bowl.”

He looked at the bowl in his hand, as though he had forgotten it existed. Then he put it on the rack.

They went out the side entrance, where the path was firmer. Thalia chose the route around the pavilion and toward the low stone wall, not the porch steps. Percy stayed beside her but did not offer his arm. His attention moved over the path once, then toward the darkening trees.

“You can walk next to me without watching the ground,” she said.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

“You looked at it three times.”

“I’m trying to be aware.”

“Of what?”

“Of what might make it harder.”

“Nothing has made it harder yet.”

He pulled his hands out of his pockets. “I can stop.”

She heard the honest uncertainty in the answer. Percy did not always know where care ended and taking over began. He had been trying to learn it for years, with mixed results. The fact that he tried did not make it her job to reassure him.

“Stop watching my foot,” she said. “You can keep walking.”

He looked ahead. “All right.”

They reached the low wall. The stones were cold and uneven. Thalia leaned against the part with the flattest top, leaving space for the heel. Percy stopped an arm’s length away.

“You said you wanted to talk,” he said.

“I did.”

He waited.

She had thought about what she would say on the walk over. In her mind, the words had been sharper and easier. Now they sat between them, not formed yet. The night had the smell of wet leaves and soup. In the pavilion, someone laughed at something Clarisse had said.

“Chapter 193,” she said. “At the shooting line.”

“I know.”

“You said my heel had been bad for three weeks. You were right.”

“I was.”

“You said it in front of everybody.”

“I did.”

“You were worried. You were also angry.”

“I was scared,” he said. “And I didn’t know how to get you to listen. That doesn’t make it okay.”

“Did you think I wasn’t listening?”

“I thought you were going to keep shooting until you proved you could.” He looked down at the wall, then back at her. “I thought if I said it loudly enough, I could make the day stop before you got hurt worse.”

“That is not how it works.”

“I know.”

“You didn’t know in the moment.”

“No.”

She had expected an explanation. He had given her the truth and stopped there. That left her with nothing easy to argue against.

“I let you say it,” she said. “I agreed to see Chiron. That didn’t mean you got to use the whole line as an audience.”

“You’re right.”

“I didn’t want an audience.”

“I’m sorry I made one.”

“You could have asked me to walk away with you.”

“I could have.”

“You could have said, ‘I’m worried, can I ask you something privately?’”

“I could have done that too.”

“Why didn’t you?”

“Because I saw you favoring it again and I thought if I waited another minute, you’d put another arrow in the target.” He rubbed his thumb across a shallow groove in the wall. “I made the fear into an order. I thought the fact that I was worried gave me a right to decide how the conversation happened.”

“It didn’t.”

“No.”

Thalia watched the dark between the trees. “I was mad at you.”

“I know.”

“I’m still a little mad at you.”

“I know that too.”

“You’re very good at knowing things after they happen.”

“I’m trying not to pretend I knew them before.”

She turned her head. “That was almost funny.”

“Almost.”

“I’m not telling you this so you’ll feel awful.”

“I don’t want you to have to take care of me about it.”

“Good.”

“I’m sorry. I should have asked you privately, and I should have let you tell me what you wanted to do next. I was wrong to make it public.”

Thalia drew one finger along a seam in the stone. The apology did not repair the day. It did not undo the silence on the line or the way every person there had watched her decide whether to obey. But he had not explained it away or added a request for reassurance at the end.

“I appreciate you saying it,” she said. “I still don’t want you deciding that the next time you’re worried, the answer is to say less and disappear.”

“I wouldn’t do that.”

“You might, if you get scared of doing it wrong.”

He took a breath. “Then I’ll ask. If you say you don’t want to talk, I’ll tell you why I’m worried and leave the choice with you.”

“That sounds like a plan.”

“It isn’t a promise?”

“Don’t make it one.”

“All right.”

She looked at him. “And if it’s about immediate danger?”

“Then I say what I see. I don’t make it about whether I’m right about everything else.”

“Better.”

“Can I ask if the heel’s better?”

“You can ask.”

“Is it?”

“It’s less swollen. It still hurts.”

“Thanks.”

“I didn’t say you get to do anything about that.”

“I know.”

“Then we’re done.”

“Okay.”

He did not reach for her. He did not wait to see if she would forgive him. The quiet after the apology felt different from the quiet that had followed the argument on the line. It was not full of an unfinished command. It had room for another day.

Thalia pushed away from the wall. “I’m going to Cabin One.”

“I’ll go the other way.”

“Good.”

They separated at the path fork. Percy headed toward the porch with his hands in his pockets. Thalia took the uphill route at the speed she chose. When she glanced back, he was still walking, not turning to check whether she had made it safely to the cabin.

She kept going.



---

Annabeth had laid the map tube on the table and taken the lid off the ink bottle.

Thalia came in without knocking. The room smelled of lamp oil, paper, and the cedar shavings Annabeth used to keep the drawers from sticking. A single sheet was spread across the table, weighted at the corners by two smooth stones.

“Did you finish the note?” Thalia asked.

“Percy did. I checked it before he put it on the board.”

“Was it right?”

“It said what he meant.”

“That isn’t the same as right.”

“No,” Annabeth said. “It isn’t.”

She dipped the pen and made a small correction in the margin. She did not ask where Thalia had been or what Percy had said. There was a kind of care in letting a conversation stay private.

Thalia settled on the edge of the bed. The heel sent up a short, dull ache, then stopped. She reached down to loosen the boot.

“Do you want help?” Annabeth asked.

“No.”

“All right.”

Thalia tugged the laces free. The splint had shifted a little inside the boot; she adjusted it, took the boot off, then flexed the foot once. She stopped before she tested it a second time.

“Can you hand me the cold pack?”

Annabeth put the pen down and went to the small cooler under the shelf. She wrapped the pack in a towel and brought it over.

“Do you want it under the heel or beside it?”

“Beside it first.”

Annabeth passed it to her. She did not try to place it herself.

Thalia set the pack against the side of the heel and leaned back on her hands. The cold came through the towel slowly. Her body wanted to turn it into proof. Cold meant healing. Pain meant failure. Rest meant weakness. She knew those were bad equations and still caught herself doing the arithmetic.

Annabeth returned to the map. “Do you want to tell me how the recheck went?”

“It’s better. Less swelling. Still tender.”

“Did Chiron change anything?”

“I tried two arrows after. The second tugged. He said no more today, and if it tugs again when I try later, I stop and see him before anything strenuous.”

“Are you going to try again tomorrow?”

“I don’t know.”

“Okay.”

“Would you ask me if you thought I should?”

“I’m not sure what I think.”

“That’s honest.”

“I think you did exactly what he asked. I also think I don’t know what your foot feels like.”

“You’ve been worried about it.”

“I have.”

“And you didn’t want me shooting.”

“I wanted you not to make it worse. I also wanted you to decide what that meant.”

“Did you want to write it down?”

“I wanted to keep track of what you told me. That’s different from writing it down.”

“Is it?”

“I can see how it might not feel different.”

Thalia shifted the cold pack a fraction. “I don’t want you collecting evidence.”

“I won’t.”

“I don’t want you and Percy comparing notes about what I did.”

“We won’t.”

“You both worry like you’re assigned a shift.”

Annabeth sat back. “I don’t want to be assigned a shift either.”

“Then why do it?”

“Because I love you. Because I have watched you decide pain is more convenient when other people can’t see it. Because I can be worried and still know that your body belongs to you.”

Thalia turned to look at her. “You don’t have to say that perfectly.”

“I’m not trying to.”

“I can hear when you’re making a speech.”

“I’m not making a speech. I’m trying to answer the question you asked.”

“It sounded like a speech.”

“Then I’ll say it another way. I’m worried. I don’t know what you should do tomorrow. I’m not going to decide for you.”

Thalia looked at the map, where Annabeth had circled the sharp bend in the low path and drawn a line toward the dry route. The circle was precise, but the line didn’t cover every option. It pointed without closing anything.

“Better,” she said.

“Good.”

“I might go to the hall.”

“You might.”

“I don’t know if I’ll go in the water.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I know.”

“I know that you know.”

“Do you?”

Annabeth set down the pen. “I do. I can still say it once.”

Thalia let the cold settle against the heel. She was tired enough to stop keeping track of how long she had held it there. Outside the cabin, footsteps crossed the path, then faded.

“If I go, will you come?”

“Do you want me to?”

“I’m asking.”

“I’d like to come. I can stay out of your way.”

“You don’t have to stay out of my way.”

“I know. You asked me not to manage you.”

“I asked you not to decide for me.”

“Those are related.”

“They’re not the same.”

Annabeth considered that. “You’re right.”

The answer came without defensiveness, quick as a door opening. Thalia felt the edge of her own annoyance soften. She did not want every disagreement to become a test of which of them was more reasonable. She wanted to be allowed to make a distinction without proving she deserved to.

“Come if you want,” she said. “If I go.”

“I do want to.”

“I’ll decide in the morning.”

“Okay.”

“And if I don’t go, I don’t want anyone to act like I failed a promise.”

Annabeth nodded. “I’ll tell Silena you haven’t decided.”

“Don’t tell her for me.”

“You’re right. I’ll wait for you to tell her.”

Thalia almost smiled. “You’re going to need a notebook.”

“I have several.”

“Maybe hide them.”

“I could.”

Annabeth capped the ink. “Do you want me to read while you ice that?”

“Not out loud.”

“I can read silently.”

“Then yes.”

Annabeth returned to her side of the room and picked up the book she had left open. She sat on the stool, one knee tucked up, and began where she had stopped. The cabin settled around them with small sounds: the paper turning, the lamp wick shifting, the table creaking as the evening cooled.

Thalia moved the cold pack when the skin underneath began to feel numb. She didn’t use the numbness as a reason to keep holding it there. She set the pack on the folded towel beside her.

“Do you want me to get the wrap?” Annabeth asked.

“I’ll get it.”

“Okay.”

Thalia reached toward the shelf. The wrap was just beyond her arm’s length, on the far side of the table. She could have leaned over and pulled it down. The movement would have put weight on the foot, and she was not sure whether that would make it hurt. The uncertainty irritated her more than the ache.

“Could you hand me the wrap?” she asked.

Annabeth closed the book around one finger and passed it to her.

“Thanks.”

“You’re welcome.”

No one marked the moment. No one called it progress. Thalia looped the cloth around her heel and tied the end where Chiron had shown her.

“Do you want the knot checked?” Annabeth asked.

“No.”

“Okay.”

She had asked. Thalia had answered. The evening continued.

She changed into loose socks and set the splint where she could reach it in the morning. The boot went under the bed, toe pointed toward the door. Nothing about the room had moved. The map stayed on the table. The low path remained muddy. The hall was still scheduled for Tuesday. None of those facts required her to decide what kind of person she was by sunrise.

Annabeth turned a page. “Should we leave the lamp on?”

“Low.”

“Okay.”

Thalia lay back on the bed and felt the heel settle into the folded blanket. It was not fixed. It did not have to be fixed tonight. She could hear Annabeth breathing across the room and the faint scrape of the pen when she made one more note in the margin.

“One week,” Thalia said, half to herself.

“What?”

“One week ago, I thought the hall was the thing I had to get better for.”

“And now?”

“Now I have to see what it feels like tomorrow.”

Annabeth lowered the book. “That sounds less convenient.”

“It is.”

“Do you want to decide now?”

“No.”

“Then you don’t have to.”

Thalia closed her eyes. The promise she had made the week before still mattered to her. So did the foot under the blanket, and the dull ache, and the part of herself that wanted a room full of people to see she had shown up. She could honor the promise by deciding again tomorrow. If she went, she would stay dry. If she didn’t, the choice would still be hers.

The lamp dimmed. Annabeth turned another page.

Thalia let the day end without finishing everything.
