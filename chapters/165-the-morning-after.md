## Chapter 165: The Morning After

Percy woke first, and the reason was the lake.

Not in words. The lake had stopped using words with him somewhere back in July and had graduated instead to the weather of his body — a pull at the base of his skull, a low green pressure behind his sternum, the tide saying: come down, come see, the water has done a thing overnight. On most mornings the suggestion was easily ignored. On this morning he had a reason to answer it with *not yet*. He was warm. There was nothing in the world he had to be anywhere for.

So he lay still and let the cabin have the morning before he took any of it.

Cabin Three kept a weather of its own. The pool at the end of the long room breathed low through its channels, a sound like somebody's grandmother sleeping — slow, warm, unhurried. Sunlight came through the high windows in pale shafts and lay across the mosaic hippocampus on the pool floor, so the water looked lit from underneath, green and gold and slow. The air smelled of lemon oil and pine pitch and, underneath both, woodsmoke, because everything they owned had been to a campfire last night and had come home wearing the smell of it. A quilt had fallen half off the couch sometime before dawn. Sand stood in a drift against the base of the door, tracked up the switchback by four pairs of shoes. Two empty bunks across from Silena's had been slept in by people who did not live here, and nobody had made a thing about it, and that was how Percy knew the summer had turned.

He turned his head on the pillow.

The end bunk by the window held Annabeth, and she had stayed.

Nobody had said it out loud. It had been past midnight when they came up the hill, the fire banked and the honey cake stone covered with the flat of the shovel, and Thalia had simply unhooked the visitor quilt from its post — the blue-striped one that had hung there all week with its corners squared, waiting for a purpose the way towels wait — and dropped it on the bunk across from Silena's. Annabeth had sat down on the edge of it with her shoes still on, looking at the long walk back up to Cabin One in the dark, and then she had lain down in her clothes. That was the whole of it. No debate, no note to Chiron, no announcement at breakfast that would never come. Camp was ending. Autumn was on its way. The cabins ran cold at night and Cabin Three had the pool, and sometimes the arithmetic of family did not require a pencil.

In sleep she had stopped planning. That was the rarest thing about her, and he was the only person allowed to see it. The precise set of her jaw had gone loose. Her mouth hung open a little. The braid she kept so faithfully wound had been shaken out somewhere in the night and lay across the pillow in a pale corona, and there was a smudge of graphite on her wrist where the pencil had rested all week, and her field book sat under her pillow, because even asleep she filed things.

Across the narrow aisle, Clarisse slept diagonally and took up the whole bunk and more of it, knees and elbows akimbo, her blanket mostly on the floor. She did not snore. She growled faintly on the exhale, which she would have denied with her last breath and then proven with her fists.

In the far corner, under the window that faced the lake, Thalia slept flat on her back with one arm flung out, the position of somebody the road had finally let go of. Four days of trees were still coming off her. Her flannel was folded on the post at the foot of the bunk and smelled faintly of granite.

Silena slept curled around a pillow with the expression of a cat that had found the one warm square of floor in the world, peaceful and total, the only one of them who had certainly slept eight full hours.

Percy lay in the warm and did the arithmetic nobody had asked him to do. Five present, counting himself. One absent, a white rock on a made-up bunk and a two-week promise. One far off in a colder, bigger camp — Jason, who would have eaten the bacon uncooked if he had been here and laughed about it after, and nobody said his name because his absence was a slow-settling thing. And one who came and went like weather, formal and brief, whose visitor towel still hung folded on its hook beside Tyson's, blue-striped, squared.

He slipped out from under the quilt. The tile was cool under his feet. He crossed to the pool and put his hand in out of long habit, wrist-deep, feeling the warm skin on top and the honest cold underneath where the channels came up from somewhere deep, and the lake said again, gently: *come down*.

Later, he told it. Be patient.

The kitchen corner of Cabin Three was really a counter, a cold box, a shelf of tins, and the two-burner plate Chiron pretended not to know about. Percy stood in front of it in the gray morning light with his feet bare and considered the contents of his life.

Flour. Eggs. Milk with two days at best. Butter. The good bread, sliced. A tin of bacon with four slices in it, five if fate was kind. Two peaches from Thursday's bake, fragrant enough to smell like the end of September. Honey from the apiary jar, wax seal cracked. And in the middle of the counter, weighted with a tin cup against the draft off the lake, a blank page he had been walking past since Tuesday.

Tyson's first letter came next week. Percy's answer was due.

He had been carrying the debt the way he carried most things, by not picking them up. There had been the survey week, and the canoes, and Thalia's homecoming, and Friday had been for the fire. But Saturday morning lay open with nothing in it, and Percy had learned, over five months of being somebody's brother in all the directions that counted, that the way you honored a blank page was to stop walking past it.

He got the bacon out of the cold box. Then the flour. Then he stood over the two burners with a skillet in one hand and realized, with a clarity he had earned honestly, that he had no idea how to make breakfast.

Cooking in this cabin belonged to Silena the way flight belongs to hawks, and she was asleep, and the morning was empty, and Percy Jackson had decided, somewhere in the dark around four without waking up enough to remember deciding it, that the least he could do was try to feed the people he loved.

So he made batter. The eggs went in first, then too much milk, then flour until it looked like something, and he beat it with a fork because he could not find the whisk, and the lumps gave up eventually, and it was, by any honest assessment, batter.

"Bacon first," said a voice from the bunk by the window.

Thalia had cracked one eye open. It was a skill she had, opening exactly one eye, the other staying shut like a door that had been painted. She had not moved otherwise. "The surveyor's rule. Hot food before ambition. You're holding a fork like a spear, Jackson. You'll hurt somebody."

"I'm cooking," Percy said.

"You're *plotting*. I can hear the plot. It crackles." Thalia sat up. Her hair was a catastrophe. She was wearing the shirt she had slept in, a camp shirt gone soft and gray, and she pushed the blankets off with the efficiency of a girl who had woken in four hundred woods and had preferences now. "Is there coffee, or do I have to file a grievance?"

"There's no coffee. There's leaves."

"Then I'm involved." She swung her legs off the bunk and stood, barefoot, eight inches taller than him and using it. "What's the plan. Don't say pancakes. Silena makes pancakes. You make smoke."

"I make pancakes."

"Wrong answer." She leaned past him, looked at the bowl, looked at the bacon, and made the sound of a woman assessing supply lines. "Fine. I'm the worst cook in this cabin, which makes me your second-in-command. Clearisse would just burn it for emphasis." She rolled her sleeves, which were already rolled, out of habit. "Give me the bacon. Bacon is not a breakfast. Bacon is a position."

She was not wrong about her competence. Thalia Grace's five months of camp cooking had produced exactly two edible things, both of them burned on the outside in ways she called "extra." But she handled the skillet with a kind of road confidence, and the bacon went in, and the smell of it filled the kitchen corner like a bell being rung, and something in the cabin began, very slowly, to wake up.

Percy took the first pancake at 6:40 in the morning. It stuck to the pan.

"Grease it," Thalia said, not looking up from the bacon, which she was turning with a fork because she had opinions about spatulas.

"It's greased."

"Grease it like you mean it. Like you're paying it." She flipped a bacon slice one-handed, caught it, ate it, and blocked his reach toward the pan with her forearm without ever quite looking at him. "Don't even think about it. There are four of us."

"There are five of us."

"There are four of us *awake*. And Silena swears by bacon in the morning, and she gets first serve, and Clarisse gets whatever she takes, and Annabeth —" Thalia grinned, sideways, dry, the surveyor assessing terrain "— Annabeth gets whatever's left, and you want there to be something left, so put the fork down."

"You're eating evidence."

"I'm protecting reserves. It's a skill. Ask Chiron."

The second pancake folded itself in half like a closing book. The third came out shaped, if you squinted, roughly like a whale. Percy looked at it for a while with a fork in his hand and felt the particular happiness of a fourteen-year-old boy who had no idea what he was doing in a kitchen full of sleeping people he would die for.

He tried for a trident on the fourth. He poured the batter slowly, drawing the three tines the way he would draw it on paper, and watched the edges set and the shape run, the way shapes do, until what came off the pan was a blob with aspirations.

"Surveyor's assessment," Thalia said, peering at it. "That's a squirrel."

"That's a trident."

"That's a squirrel that knows it's disappointing." She stole another slice of bacon and ate it standing over the pan, which was how she ate most things, guarding her flank. Then, softer, almost to herself: "This is disgusting. We're disgusting. It's good."

"It's good," Percy agreed.

"Don't let it go to your head. Silena will fix it. She always fixes it. That's the whole trick of her — she lets you do the thing and then she hands you the thing done." Thalia's face did the complicated thing it did when pride arrived without armor. "Four days I ate squirrel bread out of a satchel. I dreamed about this pan. Actual dreams. That's sad, Jackson."

"That's flattering," said a third voice, and Silena was sitting up in her bunk.

She had not opened her eyes yet. She sat against the wall with her hair in its night braid sliding loose and her chin tipped up, smelling the air the way she smelled everything, and then she opened her eyes and saw the flour on the counter, and the smoke that had begun to collect near the ceiling, and the whale-pancake on the plate, and she smiled the smile of a girl who had been waiting for this.

She said, "You're making pancakes."

"Disastrously," Thalia said.

"Then I'll feed you properly after." Silena pushed the quilt back. She was in her camp shirt tied at the waist and shorts, bare feet, hair pinned any-which-way. "Percy, don't let him use the fork for everything. Thalia, you're holding it wrong on purpose. Why is there flour on the floor already? It's been five minutes."

"It fell," Percy said.

"Nothing falls," Silena said, swinging her feet down, "until somebody pushes it. Which one pushed it?"

"Structural unsoundness," Thalia and Percy said together, and then both of them made the face of people who had stolen a phrase from the family attic and been caught wearing it.

Silena laughed, warm and unguarded, and went to the counter and surveyed the wreckage with her hands on her hips, and then, without taking anything away from either of them, she began adding grace.

She found the last two peaches and cut them into the good bread. She set the apiary jar within reach with the wax seal cracked further. She filled a pitcher with water and squeezed the last of the lemons over it and stirred in honey until it went cloudy and sweet, and set it on the counter with two glasses, and then she leaned against the counter with her arms folded and watched them cook, which was the only supervision either of them was going to accept.

"Clarisse," Silena called, without raising her voice at all. "Bacon's on."

Across the room, the blanket on the floor growled.

"She's awake," Thalia said.

"She's been awake since the bacon," Silena said. "She wakes for bacon and storms. Everything else is weather."

Clarisse sat up. Her braid was half out. She had her camp shirt on inside out and did not care, and she looked at the smoke, and the pan, and her brother holding a fork like a spear, and her older friend standing between him and the bacon with the posture of a border guard.

"You," she said to Thalia, "are eating my bacon."

"Your bacon," Thalia said.

"My cabin's bacon. Cabin Three strategic reserve. That plate is a military asset."

"I'm a guest."

"You're a mooch." Clarisse crossed the room barefoot, all elbows and bluntness, and stopped at Thalia's shoulder, and looked down at the plate. "How many."

"Some."

"Jackson. How many."

Percy, who had never in his life successfully lied to his sister, said, "Four."

Clarisse's jaw worked. Then she did the thing she always did when tenderness arrived without a permit — she made it logistics. "All right. Ration order. Two slices a head, five heads, ten slices, and we have four, so somebody goes without, and since the surveyor has already eaten four, the surveyor goes without."

"I walked four days!"

"You ate four days too. Rations are rations." She plucked two slices off the plate with her fingers, because she did not care about heat, and handed them to Silena, who took them with both hands like a gift, and then she looked at the pancakes, and at the whale, and at the squirrel-trident, and her face did something complicated and flat and fond all at once. "You made the shapes."

"I made shapes."

"You made shapes for the genius. Don't deny it. I know you." She shouldered him, not gently, on her way past. "Wake her up. She's pretending to sleep because she's precise about mornings and she wants to see if you'll offer."

"I thought she was asleep," Percy said.

"Annabeth Grace has never slept through bacon in her life." Clarisse settled onto the edge of the couch and took the far two slices like a queen collecting taxes. "Wake her, Jackson. Before I do, and I do it with water."

Percy crossed to the end bunk. He did not shake her. He had learned the safe methods: you stood close, and you let your shadow fall across her, and you waited for fourteen years of training and instinct to do the rest.

She came awake the way she did everything, all at once, gray eyes open and clear and already measuring the room. For a second she looked at the ceiling of a cabin she did not live in, and then she looked at him, and none of the surprise that ought to have been there arrived.

"You stayed," he said quietly.

"So did you." Her voice was rough with sleep, and she did not plan it, and that was how he knew she was still half under. She sat up. The field book went under the pillow. The braid came over her shoulder and she started, automatically, to refasten it, and then stopped, and let it lie. "Is that smoke?"

"It's pancakes."

"It's *smoke*."

"It's pancakes *and* smoke. That's the full truth of the situation."

She looked past him. Flour on the counter. Thalia guarding the plate. Clarisse on the couch with two slices and no shame. Silena leaning against the counter with her arms folded, delighted, already cutting peaches onto the good bread. The whale-pancake. The squirrel. The skillet with a film of something between batter and varnish smoking gently at its edges.

Annabeth looked at all of it, and her face did the thing where the planner surrendered to the evidence. "You're making breakfast."

"We are," Thalia said. "You're welcome to remain a guest. Guests get the pieces nobody trusts."

"I want a schedule."

"There's no schedule. There's smoke."

"I want a flip schedule." Annabeth was off the bunk by then, barefoot, in the shirt and shorts she had worn yesterday with her camp sweater pulled on over them against the morning chill, the sleeves past her knuckles. She took the pencil from behind her ear — it had been behind her ear, of course it had — and pulled the grocery slip from under the tin cup and wrote on the back of it in her fast square hand: "Griddle. Bacon. Peaches. Tea. Time." Then, smaller: "First batch 7:05." Then, in the margin, a tiny note-to-self that Percy could not read upside down but suspected was about the smoke.

"You can't schedule pancakes," he said.

"I can schedule anything. That's my genius." She took the fork out of his hand and replaced it with the spatula, which she had found, which had been on the counter the whole time. "You have the first burner. Thalia, you're on bacon defense, which you have already failed. Clarisse, stop eating the inventory. Silena, tell me if this is wrong."

"It's beautiful," Silena said.

"It's *chaos*."

"Yes," Silena agreed. "Warm chaos. Best kind. Go."

So they cooked. It took, in total, forty minutes and produced eleven pancakes, two of them acceptable, four of them edible, and five that entered the record as structural events. Annabeth kept the schedule. Percy flipped. Thalia stole bacon at approximately the rate a river steals a bank. Clarisse sat on the couch and delivered tactical commentary that was 30 percent criticism and 70 percent coaching, which was the most tenderness she was willing to put on record before coffee, or in this case before lemon water.

The second batch came out better. Percy managed a flip that did not fold, and the pancake went up and came down mostly flat, and Thalia applauded with the fork.

"He did a flip. A real one. Somebody log it."

"Logged," Silena said, though she had no book, and had to write it on her palm.

Third batch, he tried for the trident again and got a shape that Annabeth, briefly stopping her schedule to look, described as "a coastline. Unmapped."

"So it's a trident."

"It's a *coastline*." She looked at it for a second longer than cartography required, then handed him the plate, and did not smile, because Annabeth Grace did not smile at her own jokes. She looked like she wanted to. "Keep it. It's for the record. Tyson will want to see the food map."

"He gets the whale," Percy decided. "The whale's the best one. Even though it's a squirrel."

"The whale is the best one," Annabeth agreed. "Objective assessment. Recorded."

They ate on the floor by the pool, because nobody wanted the table. The tile was cool and the morning sun came across it in a long gold plank, and they sat in it with the plates on their knees and their backs against the couch and the cupboard, five of them in a crooked semicircle like a family portrait badly taken by somebody who loved everybody in it. The pancakes went fast. The bacon went faster, contested, rationed by decree, logged by palm. The peaches on the good bread were, by unanimous immediate vote, the best thing on the floor, and Silena accepted the news with the serenity of a woman who had known it when she sliced them.

Nobody talked about the campfire. That was the thing, Percy thought. They talked around it, the way water goes around a stone. Thalia retold the squirrel story with worse and worse structure until Clarisse's corrections were longer than the story. Clarisse described her volleyball week in the flat terms of a general's report, and Thalia declared it "boring," and Clarisse said "you're boring," and Silena said both of them were boring and handed them peaches. Annabeth ate two pancakes and kept adjusting the sleeve of the sweater, and Percy noticed, and did not say anything, because he had learned her.

Halfway through, she leaned over and folded a small square of paper into his shirt pocket.

He knew by then not to read it immediately, or rather, he read it immediately and said nothing, which was the same thing done right. He unfolded it under the pretext of getting more lemon water. Percy's ability to fold anything along a crease had been developed by exactly this ritual, over weeks of exchanging notes like a currency.

Her hand, precise as drafting. On one side, a small sketch of the north beach — the flat rock, the shallows, the line of the pines, drawn in four confident strokes. On the other side, words: WALK AT FOUR. THE ROCK'S WARM. P.S. — THE FLOAT'S UNTIED. YESTERDAY IT STAYED TIED. THAT'S A CHANGE, AND I LOG ALL CHANGES.

He folded it back along the creases and put it in his pocket over his heart, and reached sideways, found her hand where it rested on the tile, and laced his fingers through hers for a second, no more, a small held thing, and let go, and got more lemon water.

"You two," Thalia said, from behind a peach, "are disgusting."

"You're just jealous," Clarisse said. "Nobody writes you notes."

"I get notes," Thalia said. "I'm beloved. I've had a fan club."

"You had a fan club of one squirrel and it dropped a pinecone on you."

"It was a pinecone of *affection*."

"It was a pinecone of *disgust*."

"That remains unproven in a court of law."

Silena set down her plate. She looked at the five of them on the floor in the sun, at the flour on the counter and the smoke fading near the ceiling and the half-stripped peach on the bread board, and she did the thing she did when the arithmetic of a room was exactly what it needed to be. She smiled.

"What," Clarisse said, flat and suspicious.

"Nothing. It's just — Thalia's home, and the lake's still there, and the pancakes are almost food." Silena tucked her feet under her. "I was going to save the tower for Sunday. But I've decided: today is also a Sunday. Emotionally."

"The tower?" Annabeth said. "You made the tower?"

"I made the tower Thursday. Three layers, lemon syrup, whipped honey, the last peaches." Silena said it simply, the way she said things that had cost her a whole day. "It's in the cold box, judging itself. It wants admirers. I told it dinner."

"I'm emotionally a Sunday," Thalia said. "Somebody cut me a slice or I'll lie down on the tile and never get up."

"Emotionally a Sunday," Percy echoed. "That's going in the book."

"It's already in the book," Silena said. "The book of Thalia. It's mostly lunch times and excuses."

Percy looked around at all of them and felt the morning settle on him like a second blanket. There was nothing in the day. That was the gift of it. No survey to resume, no canoe to haul, no tally to keep, no panel to carry, no fire to build for a homecoming that had already happened. The summer was ending. The frost was a week or two out, and everybody knew it, and nobody was in a hurry. Somewhere out past the shallows the lake was saying, patiently, the way it always said it: *I have you. I have you. I have you.*
He put his hand flat on the warm tile and let it say it back.

Annabeth Grace discovered the truth about dishes at some point between the ninth pancake and the peach bread, and the truth was this: somebody always ends up doing them with somebody else, and that somebody else is the person you were going to spend the day with anyway.

She knelt at the wash-up corner by the pool with her sleeves pushed past her elbows and her hands in the warm rinse water, and Silena stood beside her with the towel over her shoulder, and between them the battered skillet and the pancake pan gave up their history with a minimum of fighting. The two-burner plate was cold. The flour had been conquered. Outside, the morning was warming into itself the way September mornings did at this camp, going from cool and gold to plain warm in the space of an hour, the season turning over in its sleep.

"Left side," Silena directed. "Stack them so they don't nest. If they nest they don't dry and then they taste like the lake."

"You have a whole philosophy of dish architecture."

"I have a whole philosophy of everything." Silena dried the skillet in three passes, moving the towel the way she moved everything, and her mouth did the warm, private thing it did when she was about to say something true and had decided it was allowed. "You're happy today. You're happy *flat*, not peak. You've been peak all week, and peak's tiring. Flat's home."

Annabeth considered that. She had been measured by a lot of people, but not often by somebody who measured the right things.

"I am flat," she admitted. "I didn't sleep much, and I woke up in a cabin that isn't mine, and Thalia's home, and the panel stayed where it was, and —" She stopped. Started again, precise, choosing the contour. "Thalia told me something at the fire last night. About not disappearing when she stops checking me. I've wanted that to be true for as long as I've known her. I didn't think I'd get to hear it."

"You got to hear it."

"I got to hear it." Annabeth set the plate down and reached for the pancake pan. "She stood on the granite shoulder and didn't know what to do with her hands. She said it out loud. To nobody, on a mountain. Do you know how hard it is to love somebody who teaches herself out loud?"

"Extremely," Silena said. "She's the loudest learner I've ever met. She learns by announcing."

"And now she's home, and she's going to sleep until noon, and the whole cabin feels like it leans the right direction."

Silena was quiet for a second. Then she said, "Percy's been waiting for Tuesday since Tuesday."

"Tyson's letter," Annabeth said. "He's been walking past the blank page."

"He'll write it. He writes to you." Silena's smile had a knowing edge to it, warm and merciless. "Notes every day last week. He thinks I don't know. I know."

"Thalia's going to know in about five minutes, too. She's perceptive when she's not being theatrical."

"Thalia's known since she got here. She watched him hold your hand under the quilt and made a face into her soup." Silena folded the last towel, slap, stack. "Chores. Clarisse needs help with the fire-circle detail after this — raking, the blankets, the wood. She asked for Percy specifically, which, for Clarisse, is a love letter. And Thalia's on the porch pretending to do field-book work. Go sit with her. She's got forty minutes in her before she falls asleep mid-sentence."

"You're assigning me."

"I'm assigning everyone. That's the job. The book says so." Silena untied her apron, which was just her shirt tied at the waist, and retied it, because there were more dishes in the world. "Also — the tower. Tonight, after dinner. You're all required. Clarisse will say she's not hungry. She's lying. Log it."

"Logged," Annabeth said, and dried her hands, and went.

Thalia was on the porch of Tempest Hall with her boots on the rail and her field book open on her knees, and she was not reading it. Annabeth knew the exact angle of Thalia's chin when she was pretending to work, and this was it — chin tipped just enough to look down at the page, eyes actually on the lake, where the water lay blue-green and placid and full of the morning.

Annabeth sat down on the rail beside her, not too close, letting the distance between them be whatever Thalia wanted it to be. That had been one of the week's lessons too. Not every closeness is a repair. Some of them are just... allowed.

"You're supposed to be asleep," Annabeth said.

"You're supposed to be in Cabin One."

"The bus doesn't come on Saturdays. And I stayed. Announcements were not made about it."

"Everything about you is an announcement." Thalia closed the field book on her finger. She was in yesterday's clothes still, the soft gray camp shirt and the cargo shorts with scuffed pockets, hair pulled back and already coming loose. The flannel was absent; she had given it to Annabeth last night at the fire, and Annabeth had not given it back, and Thalia had not asked. "You look better. Don't get smug. You look better because I can see you without standing in front of you. That's a thing I'm learning. Learning is humiliating. I wanted you to know."

"I do know. You told me. You were, and I quote, 'bad at it.'"

"I was *spectacularly* bad at it. It's my brand." Thalia shifted, elbows on knees, the book dangling from one hand. "So. Debrief. I've been in the woods, genius. What did I miss? And don't start with the map. I saw the map. Half of it's me."

"Do you want it in order?"

"I want it with the good parts left in and the bad parts made funny."

"That's not how reporting works."

"That's how *my* reporting works. Mine has narrative arc." Thalia pointed at her, boot-on-the-rail, eighteen and dusty even when clean. "The spear. Clarisse told me at the fire you throw like a cartographer. I want the full version."

"Clarisse taught me badly on purpose."

"Threw you a curveball. Yes. She said."

"Did she also tell you about the canoes? Percy scrubbed canoes all week. He volunteered, which he presented as chore, and Clarisse assigned it to him, which she presented as mercy, and they were both right and both pretending they weren't." Annabeth tucked her feet up under her, sitting sideways on the rail in a way that would have concerned any safety inspector. "The heron never left the cove. It stood on the skiff all week. Percy checked on it daily. He pretends it's a bird. It's a colleague."

"I had a hawk follow me on the west climb. Half a day. I thought it was my familiar. It turned out to be a turkey vulture with ambitions." Thalia's mouth did the sideways thing it did when a story was cheaper than the truth. "Did the panel behave?"

"And there it is. The panel." Annabeth said it lightly, because the light was the point. "It stayed in Chiron's office, it stayed thirty-nine, and I looked at the copy every morning like it owed me money. Somebody at the crafts table said this week it might take a fortieth before October. Somebody always says things. I told Olive I'll ink whatever the panel wants, when the panel wants it, and not one minute sooner."

"Good," Thalia said. "Order of operations. I like it."

"The water behaved," Annabeth added, because there was only one other thing in the file, and it could keep being background, the way it had been for weeks — a pressure in the ground, a cold thing under the granite, real and unbothered. "The seep. It stayed where you left it, under the slab, being winter-cold and smug about it. Next week Olive and I walk it with the pencils, once the air's cold enough that the paper doesn't sweat in my hands. No theories until I stand on it. That's the rule I decided."

"Bossy in your own report. I love it." Thalia's shoulder found hers, brief and hard, all collarbone and flannel and pine. "Tell me the rest. The float. The lake. Did anybody make trouble?"

"Nobody made trouble. That was the problem with the week. It was *quiet*. Quiteness with the edges singing."

"Tyson left Tuesday," Thalia said. "You all right after that? You did the whole goodbye thing, and then the whole rest-of-the-week thing, and Percy told me at the fire you stirred soup like it was a sacrament. Silena told me the same thing separately. You two have been co-narrating."

"Silena said it was a sacrament. I only stirred. Twice." Annabeth picked at the rolled sleeve of her sweater. "I was fine after Tuesday. I was fine because there were four of us left, and the four of us were fine, and being fine on purpose is different from being fine by accident. That's what Tuesday taught me."

Thalia looked at her a long moment. "You're going to write that down."

"Maybe."

"Put me in the margin."

"Every time."

They sat. The lake did its slow blue-green thing. Somewhere down in the trees, a voice was singing the sailing song with no words, unhurried, and then stopped, and then started again, and it was Silena, and it was a sound like a house being warm.

"Hey," Thalia said, quieter. "The last morning on the ridge. Thursday. Before I ran the downhill." She turned the field book over in her hands, looking at the leather cover like it might tell her how to start. "I stood on the granite shoulder where the old line dips. The seep was loud down below me, louder than it had any right to be at dawn. And I didn't check anything. Not the direction of camp, not the sun, not the iron boundary lines. I just stood there. And I thought — I thought, *downhill is where the people are, and I know exactly which way is downhill*, and I came down."

Annabeth waited. She had learned, over a lifetime of measuring, when to let a line finish.

"I didn't say that at the fire," Thalia said. "Some things you keep for the porch. That's all. I wanted you to have it on the record."

"I'm recording it," Annabeth said. Her voice had gone the thickness it went when something true arrived without armor. "I'm putting it in the file. Dated. Witnessed. You stood on a mountain and chose downhill with your whole chest. That's not a small measurement."

"That's the thing. It felt small." Thalia's grin was crooked and wet at the edges and did not embarrass her the way it used to. "It felt like nothing. That's what made me want to tell you. Finally something felt like nothing."

Annabeth leaned her head on her sister's shoulder. Just once, just briefly, the way they had stood at the fire and did not need to explain. Thalia's hand came up and settled on her braid, heavy and warm, and stayed there, and neither of them said anything about the frost that was coming, a week or two out, and the first cold morning that would find them all airing blankets on this same porch, and the fact that autumn would come whether the summer was finished or not.

"The blankets," Annabeth said finally, muffled. "They need airing before the first frost. I saw them on the couch."

"You saw them on the floor. Half of them were on the floor."

"They were *on* the floor. Now they're on the couch. Tonight they go up on the line by the cabin, and tomorrow they come down square, and we are not having a repeat of last winter when—"

"When when?"

"Nothing."

"When *when*, Annabeth?"

"Nothing!" Annabeth sat up, and her ears had gone pink, and Thalia made a delighted, vicious sound, because there was nothing in the world that Thalia Grace loved more than a withheld fact with structure in it. "There is no when. There was an incident with a quilt and a lake breeze and I am not discussing it before breakfast. We had *two* breakfasts."

"Two breakfasts! This is the best day."

From inside the cabin, Silena's voice, carrying without effort: "First frost means the tower! I've decided! Write it in the book!"

Thalia threw her head back and laughed, and the laugh went down the hill and out across the water and came back changed, and Annabeth, who had spent four days learning what it sounded like when a house held its breath, sat on the rail in the warm sun with her sister's hand in her hair and listened to it come back.

---

Clarisse assigned him the fire circle the way she assigned everything, by deciding it had already been his idea.

"You're on circle detail," she said, appearing in the kitchen doorway with her braid done over and her boots laced, all business, all bluntness. "Rake, ash to the garden beds, stones scrubbed, wood to the shed. The honey cake stone's sticky. It's *never* not sticky. I don't know how Silena does that. I think it's a calling."

"I didn't volunteer for that one," Percy said.

"You sure didn't. That's why it's fair." She jerked her head at his shirt. "And change that. You smell like a chimney. Spare shirt's on the hook. Blue one. I know you stole it in July, and I'm letting you keep stealing it, but if it comes back with holes you're buying me a new shirt and a new opinion of you."

The spare blue cutoff shirt was a Cabin Three institution: the third hook by the door, faded nearly to the color of the lake in shade, sleeves cut short in June by Clarisse with a knife she had not washed first. It had been stolen by, lent to, or reclaimed by every person who had ever slept here. Thalia had worn it once and been informed that it did not fit her and her frame was wrong. Percy pulled his smoke-smelling shirt off over his head and put the blue one on, soft as paper and two sizes generous, smelling like the lake and like Clarisse, which were close to the same smell.

"You stretch the neck," Clarisse said.

"I don't stretch the neck."

"You stretch the neck. You stretch everything you love. It's your whole personality." She picked up the rake. "Come on. Architecture before noon."

The fire circle sat behind Tempest Hall in the thin gold morning, ash-gray and tender, the stones still faintly warm from what they remembered of Friday. It took them forty minutes. Rake, lift, carry; ash to the garden beds where Silena said it went, because ash went where Silena said. Scrub the stones with the brush and the pail, get the honey off the cake stone with hot water and the flat of a knife. Stack the unburned oak in the wheelbarrow and trundle it around to the woodshed, where the split wood stood in walls like a library of next winter.

They worked, and the talking did not have to carry anything. Clarisse raked in long strokes without commenting on his raking, which, for Clarisse, was praise. She hauled the wheelbarrow over the roots without letting it tip and made him take the turns, and when he came back she said, "Better," which was the equivalent of a medal from anybody else, and moved on.

Halfway through the wood, with the sun warm on the back of his neck and the blue shirt going soft against his shoulders, Percy said, "She stayed."

"I know she stayed."

"How do—"

"Because you're standing straighter. You always stand straighter when she's here." Clarisse set a log on the stack. Adjusted it. Set the next one. "Don't get weird about it. We're sea people. We don't get weird about who sleeps where. We get weird about who *leaves*."

"I'm not—"

"I know you're not." She looked at him then, flat and direct, the look that had taken him five months to learn how to read, because it was not unkindness and it was not softness, it was Clarisse's particular alloy of both, forge-welded. "I'm saying. She stayed, and the world didn't tip. That's all. Table's level. The table was level yesterday. It's *still* level. Good."

"Good," Percy said.

"Stop saying good. You sound like Silena's out-loud thinking."

"Tell that to the out-loud thinker."

"I tell her daily." Clarisse threw the last logs against the stack with a thump that settled the woodpile into decision. She pulled the hair tie off her wrist and rebraided the tail of her hair with three economical motions, tucking it with a pin she kept in her pocket for exactly this. "That's the chore. Now come swim."

"That's—" Percy checked the sky out of habit, the surveyor's glance he had picked up from Thalia. "Wait, now?"

"Now. Work's done. The lake's right there. You've been putting your hand in the water all week like you're checking its temperature for a fever, and you haven't actually been in it since Friday." She was already pulling her camp shirt off over her head, revealing the dark swimsuit she wore under it, grabbing her own towel from the line where everything hung. "Move, Jackson."

They went down the north path barefoot, towels over shoulders, Clarisse in cutoff shorts and her swimsuit, Percy in trunks, the blue shirt abandoned on the rail. The north beach was theirs — the private stretch of white sand and flat rock and shallow green water that belonged to Cabin Three the way the cove belonged to the heron. In the shallows the Group Float sat tied to its stake, waxed and bright, waiting for the week's first use, and out past the sandbar the canoe hulls floated on their lines, scrubbed clean and gleaming.

Clarisse stopped at the water's edge and did the thing she did, which was to not ease in. She walked straight into the cold and went under to the shoulders and came up blowing, her braid plastered to her neck, and said, "There. It's honest."

"It's cold."

"It's supposed to be cold. It's *fresh*water. Not a bathtub." She splashed him, hard, a flat-handed wall of it that caught him in the chest. "Race. Paddle buoy and back. Loser scrubs the honey cake stone."

"You already made me scrububy the stone."

"Loser scrubs it *twice*. Go."

She went. Percy went after her, and the cold took him the way it always did, honest and enormous, the warm skin of the lake over the cold layer underneath, and he knew every temperature of her now and loved the hierarchy of it. Clarisse swam like she did everything, straight and furious and without flourish, and she beat him by a body length at the paddle buoy and knew it and said so with her whole face.

"You're getting slow," she said, treading water, streaming.

"You're getting fast."

"I'm staying. That's the trick. You get fast by staying in one lake." She turned on her back and floated, the way she always floated, hands folded on her chest, toes pointed, and let the water hold her up. For a while they just drifted, and the sun came down, and the lake went about its enormous quiet business.

Then Clarisse said, to the sky, "Two weeks Sunday."

"Tyson."

"Tyson. Silena's counting out loud in the kitchen. I'm counting quiet. Both kinds of counting." Her jaw worked. She did not open her eyes. "You're writing his letter tonight. I know because you've been walking past the blank page all week like it was a dog that bit you."

"I'm writing it tonight. Annabeth's—" He stopped. Started again. "Annabeth says he likes schedules. Concrete details. I want it to be— I've got a page with nothing on it, Clarisse. That's the problem. I've got a whole week of stuff and it all comes out as 'it was fine.' What do you even write?"

Clarisse was quiet a moment. She floated. The water moved her an inch north, then an inch south.

"You write the pancake," she said finally. "You write the whale."

"The whale."

"The whale-pancake, Jackson. You made a whale out of batter at seven in the morning. He'd love that. He'd love it more than anything else on the list." She cracked one eye. "And the cleat diagram. I drew it Thursday. It's on the counter. Tell him it's wrong."

"It's *not* wrong, it's—"

"It's wrong and I drew it anyway. Those are compatible facts." Clarisse rolled over and started for the shore, breaststroke, unhurried. "Also tell him his bunk's still made. Hospital corners. I checked Silena's work. Corners are true."

"I'll tell him you checked."

"Tell him I said the diagram's wrong. Emphasis on *I said*. He knows how to read me." She reached the shallows and stood, water sheeting off her, and looked back at him with her hands on her hips, blunt as a fence post. "You coming? We've got a beach to lie on till four."

"Four," Percy said. "Annabeth left a note. Walk at four."

"I know what time it is, Jackson. I don't need the Grace family scheduling me." But she was smiling, the flat fierce thing she did instead of grinning, and she threw herself down on the warm flat rock with her arms out and sand already sticking to her shoulders, and closed her eyes. "Wake me when the tide of affection hits. I'll pretend I'm not moved."

Percy swam out past the float, rolled on his back, and let the lake take his weight. The sky was enormous and going pale at the edges, the way it did when the season turned over, and down at the end of the shore, where the willows leaned into the water, the heron stood gray and still on the bow of the sunken skiff, one leg folded, watching them with the patience of a thing that had all the time in the world.

He watched it back. The lake said, I have you, and this time he almost said it out loud: I know. I know.

---

Annabeth found Percy on the rail where she had left him, in his own clothes again, hair still damp from the lake. He had changed for the occasion, which meant he had known exactly what she was asking without her saying it. Four o'clock was an appointment. She had drawn it on the grocery slip like a survey benchmark.

"Walk at four," she said.

"Walk at four," he agreed. "I checked the schedule. It said walk at four."

"It said warm rock, untied float, bring yourself."

"I brought myself. Twice, in case one got lost."

She pushed his shoulder, and he pushed back, and they went down the north path together with the sun on their right shoulders and the day going gold at its edges, the way the whole summer had been going gold at the edges for a week now, as if the season were drawing a slow closing line across everything and nobody was allowed to mind.

They walked the path partway toward the granite ridge. Not up. Nobody went up; there was no reason to go up today, and both of them knew it, and the knowing was its own small pleasure. The old line rose ahead of them in its gray shoulder, the trees thinning at its crown, the place where a cairn stood with a blue rag tied to it, waiting, unhurried, for next week and a cold morning and a girl with pencils. Annabeth looked at it for a while with the measuring tilt she got when she was getting a thing exactly right by eye, so that later, with paper, she would only be confirming what she had already loved.

"Next week," she said quietly.

"Next week," Percy agreed. "Olive, you, the pencils. I'll walk to the bottom of the slope with you and then I'll wait by the drain, because I'm bad at sitting still on a mountain and you're bad at waiting for me to be done noticing."

"That's a fair division of labor." She slipped her hand into his as she said it, because hand-holding by now required no announcement, and felt his fingers come up between hers and settle, warm and slightly rough from the rake handle, the calluses he had been collecting all summer on a boy who used to be a stranger to every camp.

He had a note for her. Of course he did. He had been carrying it all afternoon, folded small along the same creases she used, and he stopped on the path with the pines around them and put it in her hand with the seriousness of a man delivering a dispatch.

She opened it. One side: a sketch, quick and sure, of the whale-pancake — actually a whale this time, unmistakable, with a spout and a knowing eye. The other side, in his handwriting that leaned uphill: TRUE THING: I STAYED. SAME LAKE. SAME PEOPLE. SAME GIRL. THE WHALE IS OBJECTIVELY THE BEST PANCAKE AND HISTORY WILL VINDICATE ME.

"I'm filing this," Annabeth said.

"You filed it before you finished reading it. I watched your eyes."

"I regretting nothing."

He took her hand again, and they walked, and the ridge stood to the north of them and did not need anything from either of them, and the lake came into view through the trees below, blue-green and enormous and freshwater-honest, and then they were down onto the white sand of the private beach, and it was empty, and it was theirs.

The flat rock was warm. Annabeth had known it would be; she had spent a week learning the angle of the sun on this particular stone, the way she learned everything. But the float was the thing. She had untied it that afternoon after the dishes, walking out thigh-deep to pull the cord loose, and now it sat in the shallows waxed and waiting, and Percy looked at it and his whole face did the thing it did when something small got in under his ribs.

"You untied the float," he said.

"Logged the change. You keep it tied. It's been tied all week." She shrugged, adjusting her sleeve. "I thought — I didn't know if you'd want it untied. I thought untied was a change, and I thought maybe changes should be noted. So I noted it."

He didn't say anything. He pulled the float out by its rope until it floated free in the knee-deep water, and then he sat down on it, and then he looked at her, and she looked at him, and the whole day was quiet around them in the way that mattered.

She sat down beside him. The float dipped and stabilized and became, as it always became, a small raft of held-together-ness, and Percy put an arm over her shoulders and she leaned into the curve of it, half in and half out of the warm shallows, the water at their calves, the sun on their backs.

"Fourteen," Percy said, after a while.

"Fourteen," she agreed.

"People always say fourteen like it's a waiting room." He was looking out at the water, not at her, which she had learned meant the true things were coming. "Like you're parked. But this year — this year I got a cabin with a pool. I got sisters who count me. I got you." He turned his head then. "I got a girl who writes me notes and schedules my flips and unties the float when I'm too scared to untie it myself."

"You weren't scared of untying it. You were attached to the knot."

"I was attached to the knot," he allowed. His mouth did the wry turn. "Annabeth. Can I kiss you?"

"Yeah," she said, smiling despite herself. "That one's free. That one's always been free."

He kissed her then, slow and unhurried, the way they had learned to do it, hand-coming-up-to-cup-her-jaw, thumb-warm-against-her-cheek, careful as Tyson with a quilt, and she kissed him back with one hand fisted gently in the collar of his shirt, anchoring to the cloth the way she always anchored to the cloth, because there was so much else in the world she could have been anchoring to, and she had chosen him, and she kept choosing him, one small held thing at a time.

They stayed like that in the warm shallows with the float rocking under them and the sun going lower. When they broke apart it was only to breathe, and when they breathed it was the same cool air, and then they kissed again, slower, the kind of kiss that has nowhere to be and all afternoon to get there. Her hand found his, the way hands do when they have been having this conversation for months. His heartbeat was steady under her palm where she pressed it, and she counted it without thinking, the measurer, four beats, five, the arithmetic of a boy alive and happy and holding on.

"I wrote your name on a map once," she whispered, it tumbled out.

"Yeah?"

"In the margin. Weeks ago. Next to the water." Her forehead was against his. Their breath mixed in the cooling air. "I don't need the map to tell me where you are anymore. I just— I want you to know that you're printed on it. Under the contours. Where the ink is the realest."

Percy's voice, when it came, was thick and unashamed of it. "Permanent collection."

"Filed," she agreed, and kissed the corner of his mouth, and felt him smile against her, and then there was no more talking for a while, because the shallows were warm, and the rock was warm, and the float was untied, and there was nothing anywhere in the world that either of them had to be.

---

Dinner was soup, because Silena had decided that a day which had begun with a fire and ended with a lake did not require ceremony, and ceremony, in her experience, was what happened when a family did not trust itself to be ordinary on purpose.

They ate at the long table in the cabin's common room with the lamps lit and the windows open to the sound of the water, and then Silena brought out the tower.

It came out of the cold box in one piece, which was a small engineering miracle, three thin layers of olive-oil cake brushed with lemon syrup, stacked with whipped honey and the last of the peaches, crowned with figs and walnuts and a drizzle that ran down the sides on purpose, exactly as designed. She set it on the table and stepped back and put her hands behind her, and the whole cabin went quiet in the way of people who had known her long enough to know what the quiet was for.

"Practice tower," Silena said. "First frost means the real one. When the frost actually comes. Whole cabin required, no exceptions, Chloe — no—" She caught herself, smiling. "No exceptions. Tonight is just because today is emotionally a Sunday."

"It's structurally unsound," Clarisse announced, and cut herself the largest slice on the table, and ate three slices standing up over the sink.

"That's a compliment in her language," Thalia said, mouth full.

"It's an observation."

"It's a love letter. We all know."

"Shut up, Sky."

Percy ate two slices at the table with his elbow next to Annabeth's, and then he pushed his chair back, and he looked at the counter, and the table watched him without watching him, which was a thing this cabin knew how to do.

He sat down at the counter with the page. Annabeth came and sat on the stool beside him with her feet hooked on the rung and her chin on her hand, and did not say anything for a while, and the lamps hummed, and the page stayed blank, and then she reached over and turned it over so a clean side was up.

"Concrete details," she said. "Tyson likes schedules. He likes knowing when things happened. Write it like a report. Your kind of report. Not the ink report, the other one."

"The one with whales in it," Percy said.

"The one with whales in it."

So he wrote. It took a long time, and it was easy, and it was nothing like surveying, and it was the truest thing he had done all day. He wrote that Tyson had left Tuesday and the cabin had stayed level. That the canoes were scrubbed, all of them, hull and bench, and stored and gleaming. That he, Percy, had made pancakes Saturday morning with Thalia, who had stolen four pieces of bacon and called it protection, and that he had made a whale out of the batter, and that the whale was objectively the best pancake and history would vindicate him, and he signed that part *Percy* with the y looped.

He wrote that the bunk was still made. Hospital corners. That Clarisse had checked the corners herself, twice, and found them true. That Silena had made a tower. That Thalia was home, and the hill felt different now, and she snored like a ridge in a storm.

And at the end, because Annabeth told him that letters needed a true thing at the end, he wrote: *Two weeks, Tyson. Silena counts out loud. I count quiet. Both kinds of counting. See you Sunday.*

Clarisse, passing with the last of the tower on a plate, dropped a folded square onto the counter without stopping. "The cleat diagram," she said. "It's wrong. Tell him I said it's wrong."

"It's not wrong—"

"It's wrong and I drew it anyway. Tell him those are both true." She was gone around the corner before he could answer, which was her exit style, and there was no need to look at her face to know what it looked like.

Thalia added a postscript in her blocky hurried hand, the hand that wrote march bearings: *T. — The ridge is fine. The squirrel lost. I'm still telling it I won.* And under that, smaller: *Tell him I walked the whole line. He'll want to know somebody else walked it too.*

Silena wrapped a honey stick in wax paper and set it on the counter. "For the road," she said. "Not a bribe. A tasting note. For later comparison."

They put the letter together as an assembly line, the way this family did everything. Annabeth folded it along the creases and wrote TYSON on the front in her block title letters. Percy drew a trident on the corner, lopsided and earnest, the one nobody ever commented on because everybody had decided it was load-bearing. The cleat diagram went inside. The honey stick went in a twist of its own, so it would not stain the paper.

Then Annabeth said, "Sunday," and Percy said, "Sunday," and it was decided, the way the important things were always decided, instantly and by both of them at once.

Chiron would send the letter with the hippocamp mail from the cove dock on Sunday morning; Brine had carried Tyson once and would carry his letters now, and the dock was where mail lived. And that meant a walk, and Percy, who had been promised nothing and everything, closed his hand around the letter and said, "Then Sunday. Dock at nine. Bring the schedule."

"I don't need the schedule for that."

"You bring the schedule for everything."

"I bring the schedule for *you*," Annabeth said, "because you'd walk into the lake with your shoes on."

Percy laughed, and folded the letter in half — the double of the notes, one for a brother across the water and one already in his pocket for a girl beside him — and set it on the shelf by the door, where the outgoing things waited for outgoing days.

The evening came down soft over the cabin. Thalia fell asleep on the couch with the flannel over her and the field book on her chest, roadless at last, and nobody woke her, and nobody drew on her face, and this restraint was logged in the invisible book as the family's finest hour. Silena banked the lamps down one by one, warm at the edges, and went around checking that the outgoing letter was squared, that the tower plate was covered, that the visitor towels still hung on their hooks, blue-striped.

Percy sat with Annabeth on the back step of the cabin with a quilt around their shoulders, one quilt, and looked down at the lake. The willows were going dark in the cove. The heron stood on the bow of the sunken skiff in the last gray light, witness to the whole warm thing, and did not move, and did not mind them, and did not need them to be anything.

"Nothing today," Annabeth said quietly. "We did nothing today."

"We ate. We raked. We swam. We untied a float."

"That's what I mean. We did nothing. And it was the best day in a week." Her head found his shoulder. Her hand found his under the quilt, cold and warm, lacing. "The summer's ending, Percy. I keep waiting to feel scared about it. And I don't. I just feel like — like we're getting ready for something. Not a quest. Something quieter."

"Frost," Percy said.

"Frost," she agreed. "And the spring on the ridge. And the letter on Sunday."

"And the tower," Percy said, "for when the frost actually comes. Whole cabin required."

"Structural unsoundness," Annabeth said, in a perfect flat impression of Clarisse that she had never in her life admitted to practicing, and Percy laughed until his eyes watered and the sound went out across the water and came back changed, and the lake took it the way it took everything, without comment, the way it had taken his father's promises and his mother's warnings and a summer's worth of a boy learning to stay.

Annabeth drew her knees under the quilt and glanced back toward the path. It was getting dark enough that the turn by the reeds had disappeared. They had promised to be back before the lamps were needed.

"Silena expects that letter tomorrow," she said.

Percy looked at her. "You're going to remind me right now?"

"You asked me to remind you if you forgot."

"I haven't forgotten."

"You forgot the paper."

He put a hand in his pocket, feeling for the note he'd meant to answer. The folded sheet was still there. He had carried it through breakfast, the shoreline walk, and the whole afternoon without writing a word.

"I didn't forget the paper," he said, taking it out. "I forgot the part where I write on it."

"That is generally considered the letter."

Annabeth reached for her satchel and passed him her spare pencil. He balanced the note against his knee. For a moment the pencil hovered. Then he wrote three lines, crossed out one of them, and wrote something simpler in its place. He didn't let her read it. She didn't try.

"Tomorrow," he said, folding the paper, "I'll ask Hermes cabin when the next mail leaves."

"That sounds almost like a plan."

"Don't tell Clarisse. She'll put it on the board."

He tucked the note away and helped Annabeth fold the quilt. The skiff was still below the bank, half in the dark shallows, but the walk up was a real distance and the last stretch had no lantern. She checked the trail before they set off. Percy took her hand on the uneven ground, where doing so was useful as well as affectionate.

At the bend, he glanced back. The water was barely visible through the pines. He could miss a brother and still have a letter to write. Both things would be there tomorrow. He followed Annabeth uphill while the first lamps came on above the cabins.
