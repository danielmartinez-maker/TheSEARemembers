## Chapter 5: Clarisse

A few weeks after Percy arrived at camp, Clarisse took him on border patrol and made him carry the supply bag.

"Why do I get everything heavy?"

"Because I carried it last time."

"I wasn't on patrol last time."

"Exactly. You've got catching up to do."

The trail climbed past the strawberry fields into the pines. The ground was stiff with cold, and at the first observation post Clarisse made him repeat the horn signals until he stopped confusing movement with contact. She checked the western approach, listened for an answering call, and sat on a flat rock.

Then she took out a block of wood and a folding knife.

"What's that?" Percy asked.

"A piece of wood."

"You've got a gift for explanation."

"Thank you."

He watched her shape one pointed ear, then another. What he had taken for a lump began to resemble a crouching animal.

"A cat?"

"Panther."

"With wings?"

"You got a problem with that?"

"No. Flying cats are underrepresented in mythology."

Clarisse glanced up. "You're not as funny as you think."

"You should hear the jokes I don't tell."

"Please don't make me."

She turned the wood and cut toward herself. The blade took off a fine curl. Percy sat at the other end of the rock, leaving space between them.

"Silena said you carve."

"Silena talks."

"She said I should learn something from you that doesn't involve getting hit with a spear."

"Can't think of anything."

"Teach me that."

She looked at the knife and then at him.

"Ever used one?"

"Yes."

"For carving, Jackson. Not fighting."

"Then no."

Clarisse gave him a smaller piece of wood and showed him how to cut with the grain. His first attempt gouged a chunk out of the side. She clicked her tongue and took his wrist, turning it so that the edge pointed safely away from his other hand.

"Less force. You're not stabbing the tree."

"This is why I use swords."

"This is why you need practice."

For ten minutes they worked without saying much. Percy kept one eye on the trail and made several terrible cuts. Clarisse's panther acquired a second wing. Hers looked as though it might fly off the rock; his resembled a potato that had survived an accident.

"Who taught you?" he asked.

"An older camper."

"Not Poseidon?"

Clarisse stopped carving.

Percy heard how the question sounded. He had become used to people telling him that Poseidon had taught him to swim, fight and read old currents in Atlantis. He knew those things secondhand, from a childhood he couldn't remember. His sisters had not grown up in that household.

"No," she said. "Not Dad."

He waited. She turned the panther over, shaving a rough spot on its back.

"He came to a tournament once," she said. "I got to the final round. I'd been practicing for weeks."

"Did he see it?"

"The last few minutes. Had to leave before the prizes." She flicked a shaving off her thumb. "I won."

"Did he congratulate you?"

"Chiron did."

Percy stared at his wooden potato.

"That's rough."

"It was a tournament. I got a medal."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know."

A horn sounded once from the west. Both of them stood. Clarisse shut the knife and took position behind the rock. Percy checked the tree line and kept the signal horn ready. A few seconds later he saw three deer moving between the trunks.

"Animals, west of the lower trail," he said.

"Good. Don't signal back unless they cross our post."

They watched until the deer disappeared. Clarisse didn't relax until the path was clear.

"Silena writes him letters," she said, returning to the rock. "Sometimes she sends plans for things she's designed. He asks if Chiron approved them, as though that's all she wants to hear."

Percy remembered Silena crouched beside the cabin drain, labeling a thread from the damaged filter cloth and planning how to repair it properly. He could imagine how much time she'd spent on a drawing before putting it in an envelope.

"Has she told him she wants more than that?"

"She's fourteen. He ought to ask."

Percy almost defended Poseidon. There were so many ways he could have tried: a god had duties, a palace to govern, a hundred children scattered across the world. None of those explanations accounted for why a father could learn his son's favorite meal and overlook his daughter's best work.

"You're right," Percy said.

Clarisse looked at him as though she'd expected a fight.

"Obviously."

They sat without talking for a while. Percy got his knife stuck in the wood again. Clarisse tugged it free, made a clean cut and handed it back.

"Don't go telling him you heard any of this."

"I won't."

"And don't start feeling sorry for me."

"I don't."

"You look like it."

"I'm trying to understand."

"Then keep showing up. You promised you'd come to drills, remember?"

"I remember."

"Don't turn it into one of those big promises people make because the conversation got uncomfortable. Just be there."

Percy put the little block back in his pocket. He could do that.

"Same time tomorrow," he said.

"You're still going to be bad at it."

"Then you'll have something to complain about."

"I already do."

She rose, brushing sawdust from her trousers, and held out the supply bag. He took it before she could ask twice.

They started down the trail. A little way from the post, Clarisse passed him the patrol horn as well.

"Why do I get that too?"

"Because you knew when not to blow it."

He couldn't tell whether that was praise. With Clarisse, it probably was.
---

At breakfast the next morning, Clarisse acted much as she always did. She claimed the largest portion of eggs, told Percy his spear was in the wrong rack and corrected his grip twice during morning drills.

But she had left a place for him at the end of the bench.

He sat down before she could change her mind. Silena looked from one of them to the other and wisely said nothing.

One conversation hadn't fixed years of disappointment. Percy hadn't expected it to. Clarisse still made him repeat the last drill after everyone else had finished, and he showed up for the extra round instead of looking for an excuse.

When they were done she tossed him the supply bag.

"Same time tomorrow."

"I'll bring my wooden potato."

"Keep that thing away from my good knife."

He carried the bag back to Cabin Three.
---

Clarisse's extra drill was supposed to take twenty minutes.

Percy knew this because she'd written twenty minutes on the practice schedule, underlined it, and told him he would not be allowed to complain if he arrived late. After an hour, they were still in the arena, and she had stopped keeping track of him entirely.

Instead she was watching a small Hermes camper try to buckle a practice shield onto her arm.

"Wrong side," Clarisse said.

The girl changed hands. The shield dropped to the sand.

"That's not what I meant."

"You said wrong side."

"I meant turn the strap over."

The girl glared at the shield. She could not have been much more than ten. Her helmet had a dent over one ear and kept sliding over her eyebrows.

Percy looked at Clarisse. "You could just show her."

"I've shown her twice."

"Then show her a different way."

Clarisse's eyes narrowed.

"Is this where you tell me all about teaching?"

"No. This is where I volunteer to be the idiot in the demonstration."

The girl looked up with interest.

Clarisse considered him for a long moment, then tossed him another shield.

"Fine. Strap it on wrong."

"Won't be difficult."

He put the loop around his wrist so the shield hung below his elbow. The leather bit awkwardly into the skin. He raised the shield and tried to block a slow thrust from Clarisse's blunt spear. The shield swung sideways, leaving his chest exposed. Clarisse tapped him in the ribs.

"Dead," she said.

"Lovely morning."

She walked over and turned the strap.

"Your forearm should carry the load. Your hand keeps the edge steady. When it's hanging from your wrist, the whole thing becomes a door with a broken hinge."

Percy tried again. This time the shield stayed in line, although Clarisse's strike was hard enough to rattle his arm.

"Better?"

"Better than being dead."

Clarisse turned to the girl. "Your turn."

The girl managed the buckle, lifted the shield and held it so high she could barely see over the rim.

"Don't hide behind it," Percy said. "Watch the spear."

Clarisse sent him a warning look, then stepped back to give the girl more room.

"Again," she said.

They practiced until the girl's arms trembled. She started making the same mistake every third attempt, lifting the shield too early and losing her view of the spear. Percy could see her concentration fraying. Clarisse could see it too, but she kept asking for another repetition.

"She's tired," Percy said.

"That doesn't stop a monster."

"It does stop her learning the next bit."

The girl scowled. "I can do another."

"Great," Percy said. "But can you do a better one?"

That gave her pause.

Clarisse lowered her spear. The arena was almost empty now. The sun had crossed the roof of the forge and was making the sand bright enough to hurt their eyes.

"Water," Clarisse said.

The girl sat down so fast her shield knocked against her helmet. Percy fetched a cup from the barrel. When he came back, Clarisse was examining the practice shield for a worn fastening.

"What's your name?" Percy asked the girl.

"Bea."

"Is this your first year?"

"Second. First year they wouldn't let me train with the older kids."

"That explains why your helmet thinks you're smaller."

"It's borrowed."

"From a person with a much smaller head."

Bea smiled despite herself. Clarisse pulled the helmet off and tightened the inner strap by two holes.

"There," she said. "Now it'll stay put."

Bea put it back on and gave a short, experimental nod.

"It doesn't hit my nose anymore."

"Fantastic," Percy said. "We've saved your nose."

Clarisse handed back the shield and pointed to the far end of the arena.

"Two minutes. Then you do one clean block and go get lunch."

"One?"

"One correct one."

Bea drank, stood and positioned herself opposite Clarisse. The first thrust was slow. The shield turned at the wrong angle, and Clarisse stopped before making contact.

"Again."

Bea reset. She watched the tip of the spear, raised the shield only when it moved, and caught the blow on the bronze rim. Her feet stayed planted.

Clarisse gave a curt nod.

"That's it. Go eat."

Bea lingered, uncertain whether a single successful block could count as a real lesson.

"Did I pass?"

"You did what I asked. Tomorrow we do two."

Bea grinned, then ran toward the pavilion so quickly she almost lost the helmet again.

Percy watched her go.

"You could've told her she did well."

"I did."

"You said she did what you asked."

"Same thing."

"Not always."

Clarisse turned to him, irritated.

"Is that what you think you've figured out today? That I'm mean?"

"No. I think you want her to be able to defend herself. You kept her out here because you're worried she won't."

"She's a Hermes kid. They get cornered on patrol too."

"Exactly." He started stacking the spare shields. "You're teaching her as if she's facing a monster this afternoon. She needed a chance to learn without being scared of you."

Clarisse picked up the next shield, inspected the strap and set it in the repair pile.

"She's scared of the spear."

"She can be scared of both."

"Then she'd better learn to deal with both."

Percy did not answer immediately. In another life, he'd been pushed through dangerous lessons because the world refused to wait for him to grow older. Some of that training had saved him. Some had made him think he deserved every injury that followed.

He didn't want to use those memories to lecture his sister. He wasn't sure how much of them belonged in this conversation.

"You made a better teacher when you fixed her helmet," he said.

Clarisse stopped.

"That's because her helmet was broken."

"Her confidence was a little broken too."

"You sound like Chiron."

"Sorry."

"Don't apologize. It's annoying."

She threw him the last shield. He caught it and checked the straps before adding it to the rack.

For a few minutes they cleaned up together without speaking. The arena was empty, and the late morning sun had begun to warm the wooden practice dummies.

"I used to hate it when instructors yelled at me," Clarisse said suddenly.

Percy looked up.

"Then why do you do it?"

"Because I hate it when people don't listen." She rubbed the worn leather of a shield strap between two fingers. "And sometimes they don't listen until I raise my voice."

"Sometimes," Percy agreed.

"I didn't say always."

"No."

Clarisse glanced toward the pavilion, where Bea was probably telling her friends how she'd passed the shield test.

"I'll ask her tomorrow whether she remembers the footwork."

"She will."

"You don't know that."

"No. But I think she wants to."

Clarisse hung the final shield in its place.

"Now your turn."

Percy stared. "We just finished."

"Bea did."

"That's cruel."

"That's training. Pick up your sword."

He groaned, but he picked it up.

She made him practice the same block three times, each against a different angle of attack. He got the first wrong, the second almost right and the third well enough that she didn't correct him.

"Good," she said.

Percy lowered his sword.

"You could've said that earlier."

"You hadn't done it earlier."

He couldn't argue with that. It was a good lesson, even if Clarisse would never admit they'd both had something to learn.
---

Percy found Silena in the library that afternoon with an atlas spread across three tables and a needle between her teeth.

He had been looking for Clarisse, but Silena noticed him trying to read the spines on the agriculture shelf and beckoned him over. She took the needle from her mouth before speaking.

"You've passed this shelf twice. Either you've developed a sudden interest in crop rotation, or you're avoiding somebody."

"I was hoping Clarisse might be here. She said she'd help me with a shield strap after lunch."

"She's at the arena. Matt asked her to look at some damaged practice gear." Silena held the book cover flat and examined the torn fabric along its spine. "You can hold this while I stitch, if you aren't in a hurry."

Percy put his bag on the floor and took the weight of the atlas so she could work. The cover was made from stiff board wrapped in cloth. Several pages near the front had come loose and threatened to slide out every time he shifted his hand.

"How old is this thing?"

"Older than the library's current shelving, which isn't saying much. Chiron says it was donated by an explorer who believed the Mediterranean would be much easier to navigate if every monster had the decency to live where it was drawn on the map."

"Didn't work, apparently."

"Not for the explorer. His second edition is considerably shorter."

Percy laughed. Silena eased the needle through the frayed fabric, pulling the thread slowly until the tear closed another inch.

"How long have you been doing that?"

"Repairing the atlas? Since lunch. Repairing books? Since I discovered the library has a budget for new weapons manuals but apparently not for replacing volumes people have spilled soup on."

"I meant when did you learn?"

"One of the Athena kids showed me. After that I kept practicing. It's mostly patience and the willingness to undo a bad stitch before it becomes six bad stitches."

Percy watched the needle emerge on the wrong side of the cover. Silena noticed it immediately, sighed and unpicked the last loop.

"Like that," she added.

He moved his hand so the spine lay straighter. He wanted to ask about Clarisse, but Silena had already given him a job, and for once he resisted interrupting somebody else's work simply because his question felt important.

Silena glanced at him over the atlas.

"You can ask. Your face is doing something exhausting."

"Clarisse told me about a tournament. One where Poseidon came to watch her, but he left before the prizes."

Silena's fingers slowed. She didn't look surprised.

"She doesn't usually tell people that story."

"I know. I'm not trying to make her regret it."

"Then don't spend the afternoon asking everybody else what it meant to her."

"I wasn't going to. I just keep thinking about how different things were for us." Percy adjusted his grip as a page slipped loose. "She had to wonder whether he'd turn up. I grew up in his house. I don't remember most of that childhood, but everybody acts as though I should. It's hard to work out what I ought to say to either of you."

Silena set the needle down. She studied his expression before answering.

"Start by not calling one childhood fair and the other unfair as though we can balance them on a scale. Clarisse and I have had a good life at camp, even when we're furious with Dad. You didn't decide where he raised you. We didn't decide where he left us. None of us can repair that with a clever argument."

"I know I can't fix it."

"You keep looking as though you're planning to try." She picked up a loose sheet, checked its page number and tucked it into place. "I don't want you apologizing for every nice thing he ever did for you. I'd rather you asked us about things we actually care about now."

"Like the irrigation plan."

"Yes. Or Clarisse's carving. Or whether we want to spend our Saturday pulling weeds out of the strawberry beds. Which, incidentally, we do not, but Chiron has informed me that wanting things is not the same as getting them."

Percy smiled. "I did help with the drainage cut."

"And then you made it worse before making it better, if Clarisse's account is to be believed."

"She told you about that?"

"Percy, your shoes were full of mud and a Hermes camper came through the dining pavilion carrying half a cart wheel. It wasn't difficult to put together."

He let go of the atlas to gesture in his defense. Three loose pages slid across the table. Silena caught two and stared at the third as it drifted to the floor.

"That," she said, "is why you're helping me."

"Sorry."

"Pick up the page. Carefully. The ink is older than both of us, and if you tear it I'm going to tell Clarisse you broke something else."

He retrieved it by the edges and passed it back. Silena didn't make him feel foolish for long. She handed him a clean cloth, showed him where to support the spine, and let him try a single stitch.

His first attempt pulled too tightly and puckered the fabric.

She caught his wrist before he could make the next.

"Don't fight the thread. It's not trying to win."

"I have very little experience with things that aren't."

"Then consider this an education."

She unpicked his stitch, laid the material smooth and guided him through the next one without holding his hand. The needle went where it was supposed to. Percy looked absurdly pleased with himself.

"You're good at teaching," he said.

"I'm good at keeping people from destroying my books. There is a difference."

For a while they worked in companionable quiet. Silena asked him which part of camp felt most like home so far, and he described the hollow water channels under Cabin Three and Clarisse's habit of scolding him for leaving things in the wrong place. When he mentioned the rooms in Atlantis he'd learned about from Amphitrite, Silena listened without asking why his descriptions sometimes sounded as though he'd been told them rather than remembering them.

Finally Percy said, "Does it bother you when I talk about him?"

"Our father?"

"Yes."

"Sometimes," she admitted. "It depends what you're saying. I don't mind hearing that he's kind to you. I mind when somebody tells me that means I've misunderstood all the years he wasn't there for us."

"I won't say that."

"I believe you." She passed him another page to hold steady. "And one of these days I'd like to visit Atlantis without spending the entire trip feeling like I have to prove I belong. That isn't something you can promise for him, either."

"No. But if you ever come while I'm there, I'll show you around. Or I'll ask somebody who actually remembers how the palace works to show us both."

"That sounds more realistic." She smiled at him. "I'd like that."

They finished the binding and set the atlas between two heavy dictionaries while the glue dried. Silena wrote the date and the repair method on a card for the library records. Then she took the cloth from Percy's hands and found a strip of pale thread caught on his sleeve.

"You've got half a book following you home."

"Is that a problem?"

"It is when I'm trying to return all of it to the shelf."

She pulled the thread away, packed her repair kit and pointed him toward the door.

"Clarisse should still be at the arena. If she's in a foul mood, remind her she promised to help with your shield strap before she agreed to repair every damaged weapon in camp."

Percy picked up his bag.

"And if she asks what we talked about?"

"Tell her we fixed a book. That's what we did."

He headed out with sore fingers, a lesson in binding he was unlikely to master quickly, and no expectation that one conversation could settle what he and his sisters had inherited from Poseidon.
---

Percy found Clarisse at the lake just before dinner, sitting at the far end of a low dock with her boots beside her.

He'd been sent to retrieve a coil of rope left by the canoe crew. He wasn't looking for his sister. The coil, however, was at her feet.

"You planning to steal that?" he asked.

"Depends. Is it any good?"

"It's ours."

"Then it's probably terrible."

She nudged it toward him with one bare foot. Percy picked it up and checked for cuts. Most of the length was sound, but the last yard had begun to fray.

"I'll mark it for repair," he said.

"Look at you. Learning the rules."

"I've had an excellent teacher."

"Chiron?"

"A terrifying woman with a knife."

Clarisse snorted and drew something out of her jacket pocket.

It was the panther she'd been working on at the border post. Finished now, or nearly so. The wings were carved in overlapping rows of feathers, and the paws were too large for the body. The face had a crooked expression that made it look permanently annoyed.

She held it out.

"Here."

Percy took it before she decided not to offer.

"What's this for?"

"Your birthday. Late. Or early. Whichever makes fewer questions."

"You don't even know when my birthday is."

"Exactly. Efficient system."

He turned it in his fingers. The wing feathers were rough along the undersides, but the front of the animal had been polished smooth. A great deal of patient work had gone into something Clarisse was pretending not to care about.

"Thanks," he said.

"Don't make a thing of it."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were looking at it like you were about to make a thing of it."

"I was looking at its face. It looks like you."

"Give it back."

He tucked it into his shirt. "Too late."

She reached for it. He stepped aside. The dock creaked, and she caught his sleeve before he could stumble off the edge.

"Careful, idiot."

"I'm standing on a dock."

"That's the problem with docks. There's water beside them."

"I'm a son of Poseidon."

"That won't stop you cracking your head on a piling."

She let go. Percy sat a few planks away and put the panther on his knee.

"Silena says you make these when you're thinking."

"Silena also says we should sort the supply cupboard by color. She has opinions."

"Her system works."

"It works because I carry the heavy things."

"She'd say it works because she knows where the heavy things go."

Clarisse gave him a sideways look.

"She's been making you do chores again?"

"I volunteered."

"Then you've been at camp long enough to make bad decisions."

They watched a pair of naiads argue with a camper who was trying to push a canoe off its cradle without checking the rope. Clarisse called out the correct knot before the boy could get himself tangled. He waved in thanks and tried again.

Percy glanced at her.

"You teach everybody like that?"

"Only the ones who might break something."

"That's everybody."

"Yes."

She tucked her hands into her jacket sleeves. The breeze off the lake had turned colder.

"I'm still angry with Dad," she said.

Percy hadn't asked. The admission caught him off guard.

"Okay."

"And I don't want you deciding it would be better if we all sat together and sorted out our feelings."

"I wasn't planning a family meeting."

"Good. It would be awful."

He thought about the wooden panther on his knee. Clarisse had been working on it while they discussed Poseidon at the border, and she'd finished it without saying another word about what she'd told him.

"I can't make him better at being your father," Percy said.

"I know."

"But I can be here."

"You're here now."

"Yeah."

"That's a start."

Percy leaned back on his palms. For a while they listened to the ropes clatter against the canoe racks.

"Have you shown Silena that panther?" she asked.

"Not yet."

"Don't."

"Why?"

"She'll ask where hers is."

He looked at the winged creature.

"Does she have one?"

"Three." Clarisse considered that. "Four, if she's kept the one I made when I was nine."

"Then this is an exclusive edition."

"Don't let it go to your head."

The dinner horn sounded. Clarisse put on her boots. Percy tucked the rope under one arm and the panther safely into a pocket where its wings wouldn't catch.

As they started back, Clarisse noticed he'd left his jacket unzipped.

"You're going to freeze."

"Yes, Mother."

She shoved him, not hard enough to knock him off the path.

"Don't call me that."

"Yes, terrifying woman with a knife."

"Better."

They reached the cabin before the second horn. Percy set the damaged rope beside the supply chest and found a spot for the panther on the shelf over his bunk.

Clarisse pretended not to notice where he'd put it.
---

Annabeth found Percy at the lake after dinner. He'd gone back to return the repaired section of rope and ended up sitting on the dock longer than he meant to.

"Thalia is looking for you," she said.

"That's never good."

"She wants to know whether you moved the practice shields."

"Oh. Then it's fine."

"Percy."

"I put them where Clarisse told me. I can show her tomorrow."

Annabeth sat beside him, pulling her jacket tight. She'd brought a notebook but didn't open it.

"You've been spending a lot of time with Clarisse."

"She's teaching me how not to get killed by routine camp maintenance."

"Useful skill."

"Underrated."

Annabeth smiled, but it didn't last. Percy waited. He had learned that she sometimes needed a minute to decide whether a thing was worth saying, and that trying to hurry her usually meant they argued about the wrong part of it.

"Thalia thinks you're getting into something with the Hall records again," she said.

"She's not entirely wrong."

"I know. That's what bothers me."

Percy turned toward her.

"You want to stop?"

"No. I want to stop acting like we have to figure everything out before breakfast. Nico has a ledger. Zoe knows the repair system. Silena knows people who worked on the water channels. We're not alone in it anymore."

"You're right."

"I wish you'd stop saying that when I haven't finished."

"Sorry."

She rested the notebook on her knees.

"I spent some time with Thalia today. She told me our father visited last week."

Percy took a moment to understand the pronoun. Zeus. Of course.

"Did you see him?"

"No. I must have been at training. She said he looked completely different from his last visit. New face, new voice, different hair. Like meeting someone and being told they're the same person you spoke to a month earlier."

"That sounds like him."

"To you, maybe. I don't remember growing up with him. Thalia does. And now she has to describe how he behaved as though I should remember it too."

Percy looked at the water.

"What did he say?"

"That he'd try to come again soon." Annabeth picked at the edge of her notebook. "Thalia doesn't believe him."

"Do you?"

"I don't know him well enough in this life to have an opinion. That's the problem."

He had no answer. There was a version of Poseidon in his memory who had sometimes been too distant to reach, and there was the father whose household he'd woken up in. Neither made Zeus easier to understand.

"I miss Athena," Annabeth said.

It wasn't a grand declaration. She said it quietly, looking at her closed notebook.

"Even when we were fighting, I knew what she expected from me. Here I can't tell whether Zeus cares how I train or whether he'd recognize me if I walked past him."

Percy nudged her knee with his.

"I think he'd recognize you."

"That isn't based on any evidence."

"No. You're right."

She gave him a look.

"Don't start."

He smiled, then grew serious.

"I miss Sally, too. Every time I write home and Amphitrite asks something about when I was little, I remember a different kitchen. I don't know what to tell her without making her worry."

"Are you going to tell her?"

"No. Not about the other life."

Annabeth nodded. They'd agreed on that, and it still felt like a difficult choice.

Percy drew the wooden panther from his pocket and showed her.

"Clarisse made this."

Annabeth took it gently.

"The wings are good."

"She said it was a birthday present."

"When's your birthday?"

"Not according to her system, apparently."

Annabeth ran her thumb over the uneven paws.

"She must have worked on this for hours."

"She did." Percy watched her expression. "What?"

"Nothing."

"That is a very specific nothing."

"Thalia used to make things for me. She tells me about them, and I can't remember getting them." Annabeth handed back the panther. "It's strange being jealous of a little carved animal. At least you can remember the day Clarisse gave it to you."

Percy slipped his hand into hers.

"You can still make new things with Thalia."

"We're fixing the portrait frame."

"How's that going?"

"Slowly. Which is apparently better than my plan to finish it in one night."

He laughed.

They stayed a few more minutes, but not until curfew. Annabeth stood first, and he followed. The camp lights shone between the trees. Percy could hear Clarisse complaining to someone near Cabin Three.

At the fork, Annabeth turned toward Cabin One.

"Tell Thalia about the shields tomorrow," she said.

"I will."

"And don't start another secret investigation before lunch."

"I make no promises about lunch."

"Percy."

"After lunch, then."

She shook her head, laughing despite herself, and walked back toward her sister. Percy headed for Cabin Three with the carved panther in his pocket, thinking that having people to come home to did not solve the question of what had happened to their past.

It did give him a reason not to rush the answer.
---

Clarisse intercepted Percy before breakfast the next morning, one greave unfastened and her sword hanging loose at her side.

"Come on," she said.

"Where?"

"Hall. Nico found something. Chiron's there."

They ran uphill. A few campers were already gathered outside the Hall of the Gods. A post had been knocked out of the border fence, and a streak of dark residue ran from the broken earth to the Hall's eastern entrance. Two columns were scored along their lower edges. The roof stood. Through the doors Percy could see workers setting canvas over a damaged section of mosaic.

Chiron was questioning a pair of guards.

"A drakon crossed the boundary," he said when he saw them. "Not the one we fought last week. Smaller, but fast. We drove it away. It reached the colonnade before it vanished into the trees."

"What was it after?" Annabeth asked.

"We don't know," Chiron said. "The west store room was forced open, and some building records were scattered. Until we've counted them, I won't say anything is missing."

Nico knelt beside a bronze fixture wrenched from the wall. He had been comparing the screws with a drawing in his ledger.

"This was loosened before the creature hit it," he said. "Look at the threading."

Clarisse crouched. "Maybe the workers were repairing it."

"Maybe," Nico agreed. "But it was supposed to hold a ward. If it was already loose, the drakon didn't have to break much to get inside."

Percy thought of the old prophecy pages and the signed note. He wanted the scattered papers to amount to a single answer. Nico was being more careful than that.

"Whoever altered the Hall's rules had authority to do it," Nico said. "I've got a name on a purchase order."

"Whose?" Silena asked.

"Zeus's. Or rather, his steward's. He authorized the new fixtures. I can't tell whether the repair happened afterward with his knowledge."

Thalia stood a few steps away, spear in hand. "Say what you mean."

Nico looked up. "I mean we shouldn't pretend a signature on an order proves someone sent a monster. It tells us who paid for the work. That's all."

"Good," Thalia said. "Then we won't be inventing accusations before breakfast."

She was angry anyway. Percy could see it in the way she held her spear, but he couldn't tell whether the anger was at the implications or at being forced to hear them in front of an audience.

Chiron ordered the Hall closed until the wards could be checked. The younger campers were sent back to the pavilion. Clarisse volunteered to help remove broken stones; Silena offered to catalogue damaged records. Percy and Annabeth stayed long enough to copy the serial number from the fixture and the name on the repair sheet.

By the time they were allowed to leave, the Hall still stood, and they had a handful of questions, no proof of who had altered their history, and work assignments before lunch.

Thalia passed them without speaking. Annabeth watched her go, then followed her toward Cabin One. Percy let her. Some conversations weren't improved by having him there.
