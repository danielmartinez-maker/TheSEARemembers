## Chapter 5: Clarisse

Three weeks after arriving at Camp Half-Blood, Percy went on border patrol with Clarisse and discovered what she did with her hands when she wasn't holding a sword.

Not the surface truth — the warrior's mask she wore for the world, all sharp edges and refusal to flinch. Something deeper. Something she handed him on border patrol, perched on a sun-warmed rock and shaving curls off a wooden panther while October light slid across the sky.

The patrol trail smelled of pine pitch and crushed thyme. Below them Camp murmured — distant bronze on bronze from the arena, a conch call from the beach, smoke from the forges threading sideways in the breeze. Percy had walked this route a dozen times, but that afternoon the light lingered longer on the ridgeline, gilding the strawberry fields, and Clarisse's knife made a steady shhk-shhk against the grain.

Clarisse was carving the panther for Silena, although she wouldn't admit it until Percy guessed.

Not of monsters. Not of dying. Of being forgotten.

"I was seven," she said, eyes fixed on the carving. Wood shavings dusted her boots. "That's when it hit me. Two years at camp, and Poseidon hadn't come by once. Not once. And the other kids — the ones with parents who bothered to show up — they got letters. Visits. Little proofs somebody out there was keeping score."

She lifted the panther. The wings stood half-formed, rough along the edges. She rotated it slowly between her palms.

"Me? Nothing. No letters. No visits. And the other Big Three kids — they had families. Parents who fought with them and bragged on them and actually turned up to things. Even when it was messy, even when it drove them crazy, at least they *had* somebody." Her jaw worked. "I had Camp, and Silena, and myself. I kept telling myself that ought to be enough."

Her gaze flicked to Percy. Guarded. Unreadable.

"Seven. That's when I started training like my life depended on it. Because if I got strong enough to survive, maybe I'd get strong enough for *them* to look twice. To actually *want* me. Stupid, I know — I figured if I turned into the best fighter at camp, the one who kept everybody breathing, then maybe—"

Her voice cut out. Cicadas filled the gap. A pine needle spiraled down onto the rock between them.

"Maybe what?" Percy asked softly.

"Maybe they'd say my name." The crack in her voice was hairline-thin. "Maybe one day they'd point and say: *that's my daughter. That's Clarisse. She's worth something.*"

Percy's throat closed. Salt and sawdust hung in the air, and the knife had gone still in her hands. He pictured Sally — blue cookies cooling on the counter, that cramped apartment with its chipped paint, the way she had gone to war for him every single day without fanfare. Then he tried to picture growing up in that same apartment and still wondering whether his parents remembered his birthday, whether his name carried any weight beyond the roll call at breakfast.

"That isn't why you fight now," he said.

"And you'd know that how?"

"Because I've been watching. On the field, in the mess line, everywhere. Getting noticed stopped being the point a long time ago. Now it's about keeping your people alive."

A long quiet settled. Wind moved through the pines. Somewhere downhill, a naiad laughed.

"Annabeth," Clarisse said at last.

"Yeah."

"I fight for her. Always have. She's the only person who's ever looked at me and seen *me* — not a useful blade, not a weapon to point, just me. Like existing is sufficient."

Beside the trail, a beetle worked its way over bark. Percy tracked it while he thought of Clarisse checking Silena's bunk at night. The panthers she whittled in secret and named for her sister. The solitary load she hauled because she'd concluded that solitude kept Silena safest.

"Then we're the same," he said. "She's my person too. Different reasons. Same gravity."

Clarisse studied him — that frank, searching stare she used when deciding whether someone was feeding her a line.

"You love her," she said.

"Yeah."

"Not only protect. Love."

"Yeah."

"And she seems different lately. Quieter. You notice it too, right? Because I don't know what to do with it."

"I know. She and I have been talking. But I'm not going to put words in her mouth."

Another stretched silence. Then: "That's stupid."

"Probably."

"You'll get hurt."

"Also probably."

"And you'll do it anyway."

"That's the stupid part."

Laughter burst out of her — genuine, startled free. "You're an idiot, Jackson."

"Heard that before."

Still, she kept smiling, and the smile remade her face. Through a crack in the warrior's mask Percy glimpsed the girl beneath. Scared. Lonely. Hungry to be seen. Pine sap and sawdust scented the air between them.

Thirteen, maybe — young enough that she shouldn't have been responsible for keeping an entire cabin safe. Too young for the load, too practiced at burying the soft parts under competence, because the alternative hurt too much to face head-on.

"Clarisse," he said. "You're not invisible. I see you. Annabeth sees you. Whatever's bearing down on us — you have people now. People who won't forget."

She glanced toward the treeline, blinking fast. "Don't pledge what you can't deliver, Jackson."

"Then I won't promise forever," he said. "I'll promise I'll be here for drills tomorrow. If I don't show up, you can come drag me out of bed."

Clarisse turned the panther over in her hand. "That's not much of a promise."

"It's one I can keep."

Quiet again, pine needles ticking against rock. Then she rose, slid the unfinished panther into her belt, and extended her hand.

"Partners, then," she said. "In this. Whatever this turns out to be."

Percy clasped her forearm. "Partners." Her grip was warm and firm as a sword oath, sawdust gritty between their palms.

"Don't make me regret it. I don't hand out second chances — ask anyone at this camp."

"Wouldn't dream of it, partner. Not ever, I swear."

---

By next morning, something in Clarisse had eased.

Nothing overt — at breakfast she still ate alone, still drilled harder than anyone on the field, still snapped at anyone who crowded her. Yet beneath the steel ran a new current. Her shoulders rode lower. The corners of her eyes had softened.

The same Clarisse. Perhaps slightly more willing to let him stand beside her.

Percy caught it because he looked for it. In his original life he had witnessed enough fractured people mending to recognize the early signs.

Neither mentioned the patrol. No need. A shift had occurred, acknowledged on both sides without words, and that sufficed. At sword practice Percy watched her correct a younger camper's grip instead of barking, her palm light on the kid's wrist, and the gesture — small, unremarkable to anyone else — landed like a signal flare.

---

That afternoon, Percy found Silena in the library repairing the binding of an atlas.

He had been looking for Clarisse. Silena had noticed him pass the same shelf twice and finally waved him over.

"Either sit down or stop pretending you're interested in agricultural tariffs," she said.

"I was reading the spine."

"It's upside down."

Percy sat. Silena threaded a needle through the torn cover and pulled it taut. Her hands were deft, and she kept working while she talked.

"Clarisse has been in a better mood," Percy said.

"She's been arguing with everybody. So, yes."

"What does she do when she's upset?"

"Why are you asking me instead of her?"

"Because she'll pretend she isn't upset and challenge me to a fight."

"Then you'll have your answer."

Percy watched her tuck the thread through the binding. "I'm trying to understand why she never talks about our father."

Silena stopped pulling. A small piece of the cover cracked beneath her thumb.

"What about him?" she asked.

"He raised me. He doesn't seem to have been around for either of you. I didn't know that when I got here."

"How could you?" She turned the atlas over and examined the damaged corner. "He visits sometimes. Not often. He asks about our training, whether we're behaving, whether Chiron is keeping us safe. He has a talent for asking questions that sound like an inspection."

"Does Clarisse talk to him?"

"Yes. That's the worst part. She prepares things to show him for weeks. Half the time he arrives in the middle of a crisis and doesn't have a minute to look."

Percy felt a flash of anger he didn't have an obvious place to put.

"You could've told me."

"We didn't know you before last month." Silena looked directly at him. "And we weren't planning to greet our little brother by giving him a list of grievances about our father."

"Fair."

"Also, don't make this into something you can fix with a speech. Clarisse is still angry even when she laughs with you. So am I."

He had been halfway toward offering precisely the wrong promise.

"What should I do, then?"

"Turn up. When you say you'll train with her, do it. Don't make her work to prove she's worth your time."

"I can do that."

"You can try. We're all good at making promises when there's no cost yet."

Silena returned to the atlas. Percy noticed she had a repair kit full of scraps and mismatched thread, none of it fancy. She worked with what she could get.

"Why are you fixing that yourself?" he asked.

"Because the library won't buy another. And because I like fixing books."

"I thought you liked designing things."

"I do. I also like having a page stay attached when I turn it." She looked at him over the atlas. "You can help. Hold this flat."

He did. For the next ten minutes they worked together. Once he pulled the thread too tightly and tore the paper, and Silena took the needle out of his hand until he'd stopped apologizing.

"You are spectacularly bad at this," she said.

"So I've been told."

"Good. Then you won't be offended when I ask Clarisse to teach you something you're better at."

They finished the binding and set the atlas beneath two heavy dictionaries to dry. Percy walked out of the library with a job to do the next morning and no illusion that he'd solved anything.

---

That evening Percy spotted Clarisse at the lake — not their cove, Annabeth's, though near enough to make out her silhouette on the dock. Feet trailing in the water, hands busy. Whittling, by the rhythm of her shoulders.

He held off. Settling onto a nearby boulder, granite still radiating daylight, he watched the sun bleed out and granted her privacy, reflecting on how trust accrues. Gradually. In increments. By returning, again and again, until the other person credits the intent.

Eventually Clarisse rose. Crossed over. Dropped onto the boulder beside him uninvited.

"Thought you were seeing your girlfriend," she said.

"Already am. She's right over there."

Clarisse snorted. "Naturally." She brandished her handiwork — the earlier panther, now complete. Wings unfurled. Eyes keen. Beautiful. "Crafted this for you. Assign no significance."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

"Good." She pressed it into his palm, wood still warm from her grip. "Even exchange. You listened. I manufactured an object."

He rotated the panther. Wing feathers etched in fine lines snagged the final sunlight. The expression managed ferocity without cruelty. Knowing eyes.

"Clarisse, this is exceptional."

"Obviously." Pleasure leaked through the flat delivery. "Silena insists I occupy my hands beyond training. *Art*, she labels it. As though carving qualifies as art rather than — I don't know — a thinking aid."

"It qualifies."

"Perhaps." A beat, water lapping the pilings. "Percy. May I pose something?"

"Always."

"Do you ever doubt the reality? Like, fundamentally? Whether we're awake or dreaming? Or snared in something? Occasionally it plays like a narrative. As though some author scripted us and we're merely hitting marks."

Percy weighed the odd question. Then again, odd defined his circumstances.

"At times," he admitted, thumb tracing the panther's wing. "At times I sense the role. As though someone assigned my identity and I'm simply... complying."

"Precisely." Her stare stayed on the darkening water, where the first stars pricked reflection. "Yet the inverse strikes too. Occasionally I'm so *present* the rest evaporates. Fear evaporates. Only being remains."

"That's the worthwhile passage."

"Yeah." A small, private smile. "It is."

Side by side they remained while orange bled to violet to black, while constellations pricked through, while the world simplified for a little while into water and stone and breath. Midges stitched the air above the shallows. A fish rose with a soft plop, rings widening across reflected Cassiopeia, and Clarisse's shoulder rested a fraction closer than camaraderie strictly required — the unconscious lean of someone relearning proximity.

---

Later that night Annabeth tracked Percy to the lakeshore, her sandals whispering through damp grass.

"You've been orbiting Clarisse a lot," she observed, folding down beside him. Reeds whispered. Mud sucked softly at the bank.

"Problem?"

"Uncertain. Perhaps." Knees drawn up, chin resting there. "She's your sister, Percy. And sharing you doesn't come naturally."

A bullfrog called. Percy let the quiet gather before answering. "Annabeth, she possesses you. Always will. I'm not displacing that."

"Awareness doesn't dissolve complication. Everything tangles."

"Biography of our lives."

That earned a laugh. "Yeah."

They lingered, and after a while Annabeth tipped against his shoulder. His arm settled around her. Overhead the stars multiplied.

"I unearthed something today," Annabeth said. "Concerning Thalia. Concerning... our father."

"Regarding?"

"He visited. Last week. First appearance in months." A hesitation. "He presented differently. Altered from prior. Altered visage, altered hair, altered entirety. Like encountering a stranger."

"Zeus never repeats a face," Percy said. "Standard for him."

"Understood. But how does one anchor a daughter to a father who arrives wearing a new countenance each visit?"

Peculiar, Percy mused — envying a steadiness he himself had never owned in his original life: Poseidon here, consistent, recognizable.

"Mine doesn't shape-shift," he said. "Poseidon. Identical each arrival. Lately I wonder whether consistency guarantees superiority. At least yours materializes. Changed or not. At least he comes."

Annabeth fell silent. Lake water licked stone.

"You drew a decent father," she murmured.

"One father, two histories," Percy said. "Poseidon here knows how I take my tea. In the life I remember, I waited years just to meet him. I can't decide whether being closer makes his mistakes hurt more."

Annabeth knew the waiting he meant. She heard the gravel in his tone and left the question room to stand.

"You miss her," she said. "Sally."

"Daily."

"I miss my mother too. Not Beryl — Athena." A pause, breath misting faintly. "In my original timeline, she embodied everything. She credited me. Perceived me, genuinely perceived me, before I comprehended myself. Then she withdrew, and I lost her, and ever since I've labored to grow into the person she envisioned."

"Heavy inheritance."

"Identity, I think." She angled to face him. "Does the missing ever lighten?"

"No. You merely strengthen the muscles that bear it."

They remained in the dark, two children mourning parents mislaid across timelines, and the hush between them approached closer to peace than anything since their arrival. Crickets sawed in the reeds. The dock ropes creaked. Annabeth's hair smelled faintly of woodsmoke from the pavilion fire, and Percy memorized the moment the way he memorized charts — shoreline, star positions, the exact pressure of her shoulder — in case darker days required it.

---

Clarisse intercepted Percy before breakfast the next morning, one greave unfastened and her sword hanging loose at her side.

"Come on," she said.

"Where?"

"Hall. Nico found something. Chiron's there."

They ran uphill. A few campers were already gathered outside the Hall of the Gods. A post had been knocked out of the border fence, and a streak of dark residue ran from the broken earth to the Hall's eastern entrance. Two columns were scored along their lower edges. The roof stood. Through the doors Percy could see workers setting canvas over a damaged section of mosaic.

Chiron was questioning a pair of guards.

"A drakon crossed the boundary," he said when he saw them. "Not the one we fought last week. Smaller, but fast. We drove it away. It reached the colonnade before it vanished into the trees."

"What was it after?" Annabeth asked.

"We don't know," Chiron said. "The west store room was forced open, and some building records were scattered. Until we've counted them, I won't say anything is missing."

Nico knelt beside a bronze fixture wrenched from the wall. He had been comparing the screws with a drawing in his ledger.

"This was loosened before the creature hit it," he said. "Look at the threading."

Clarisse crouched. "Maybe the workers were repairing it."

"Maybe," Nico agreed. "But it was supposed to hold a ward. If it was already loose, the drakon didn't have to break much to get inside."

Percy thought of the old prophecy pages and the signed note. He wanted the scattered papers to amount to a single answer. Nico was being more careful than that.

"Whoever altered the Hall's rules had authority to do it," Nico said. "I've got a name on a purchase order."

"Whose?" Silena asked.

"Zeus's. Or rather, his steward's. He authorized the new fixtures. I can't tell whether the repair happened afterward with his knowledge."

Thalia stood a few steps away, spear in hand. "Say what you mean."

Nico looked up. "I mean we shouldn't pretend a signature on an order proves someone sent a monster. It tells us who paid for the work. That's all."

"Good," Thalia said. "Then we won't be inventing accusations before breakfast."

She was angry anyway. Percy could see it in the way she held her spear, but he couldn't tell whether the anger was at the implications or at being forced to hear them in front of an audience.

Chiron ordered the Hall closed until the wards could be checked. The younger campers were sent back to the pavilion. Clarisse volunteered to help remove broken stones; Silena offered to catalogue damaged records. Percy and Annabeth stayed long enough to copy the serial number from the fixture and the name on the repair sheet.

By the time they were allowed to leave, the Hall still stood, and they had a handful of questions, no proof of who had altered their history, and work assignments before lunch.

Thalia passed them without speaking. Annabeth watched her go, then followed her toward Cabin One. Percy let her. Some conversations weren't improved by having him there.
