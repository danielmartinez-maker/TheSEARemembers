## Chapter 5: Clarisse

A few weeks after Percy arrived at camp, Clarisse took him on the eastern border patrol and handed him a supply bag that appeared to have been packed by someone preparing for an expedition across an entire continent. It contained rope, a first-aid kit, spare bowstring, two rolled rain capes, and three signal horns, although Clarisse already had one hanging from her belt. Something heavy shifted against his hip whenever he changed the strap from one shoulder to the other, and he began to suspect that at least one of the horns had been included to discover whether he would complain. The morning had the sharp, clean smell that came after a cold night, when the first sunlight warmed the needles of the pines while frost still lay unbroken in their shade. A few weeks was not enough time to know a place, but it had been long enough for him to discover that camp was larger in its habits than its boundaries suggested. Every path had a purpose, every repair somebody responsible for it, and every stretch of quiet ground concealed a story that older campers believed everybody already knew.

Clarisse moved ahead with the easy confidence of someone who had walked the route so often she no longer needed to watch the familiar parts. She still examined the ground at the places where it mattered. At the first marker she crouched beside the carved boundary post, checked a line of scratches near its base, and ran her glove over the wood to see whether any of the marks had been made since the last watch. Percy watched her work instead of trying to identify the post from his old memories of camp. In that other life Clarisse had often looked like anger made flesh, a rival who struck first and discovered the reasons afterward, but the girl beside him now was older than he was, patient in small practical matters, and reluctant to announce what she knew until the work required it. The difference made him feel ignorant in a way that had nothing to do with swordsmanship.

“You could've warned me that the bag weighs as much as a small horse,” Percy said when they reached the marker. He shifted the strap once more and felt the brass rim of a horn knock against his ribs. “I thought I was volunteering for a walk, not carrying everything the camp owns.”

Clarisse kept her eyes on the scratches. “You didn't volunteer, Jackson. You got assigned because you spent ten minutes hunting for the gloves you left in the wrong trunk, and somebody decided you could make up the time by being useful. If you'd put them where Silena told you, we'd have been here before the sun reached the treeline.”

“I found them eventually,” Percy replied, dragging the gloves from his pocket and pulling them on. One finger caught in a crooked seam that Silena had mended for him yesterday. “It turns out that owning a bag full of emergency equipment doesn't automatically make me organized.”

“I'm delighted you're learning. Now use the gloves if you're going to touch the markers, because the wood's old and you don't need a splinter on the hand you're supposed to hold a sword with.” She straightened and gave the post a final look, satisfied enough to move on. “And don't take my joking about the bag as permission to leave anything behind. We carry more than we expect to need because the one time we don't, somebody ends up needing it.”

The path curved through scrub oak and pine, climbing gradually toward a shoulder of higher ground where the eastern fence met the natural ridge. The country beyond camp had its own order, less obvious than the numbered trails and carefully kept shelters inside the boundary. Long pale roots broke through the slope and vanished beneath stones covered with lichen, while rain had cut a narrow channel beside the track where runoff travelled after storms. In a low hollow Percy could see the remnant of an older wall, its rocks settled into the earth and its upper courses dismantled for later repairs. He wondered who had placed it there, and whether that person had ever imagined a Greek camp existing among these American trees. He had visited lands where the past lay visible under every new foundation. Here the past hid beneath moss and ordinary labor, but that did not make it any less real.

At the second observation point Clarisse stopped where two routes met beneath a crooked pine. A narrow view opened toward the lower hillside, and another toward a line of old fencing that disappeared among the trees. She took one of the horns from the bag and explained that the patrol signals were not designed to sound impressive. They had to be short enough for a frightened camper to remember and different enough that nobody would mistake a warning for an instruction to move toward danger. Percy knew versions of those signals from an older war, yet the ones used here belonged to different patrol routes and a different chain of command. He took the horn and repeated the sequence she demonstrated, then hesitated on the distinction between a call announcing contact and one ordering retreat.

“Try that again,” Clarisse said, without the gloating he had expected when he got it wrong. “Three short blasts mean you need everybody moving away from the threat before you think about winning a fight. If you've got younger campers with you, you take them toward the safest open route and send somebody for help. You're not there to become the heroic idiot in the songs. You're there to make sure people come back.”

Percy considered the distant fence and tried the signal again. The horn's note travelled across the hollow and returned faintly from the trees. “So the point isn't to tell everyone I found a monster,” he said. “It's to make sure they know what I need them to do about it. I suppose that's easier than shouting an explanation while somebody's trying to eat me.”

“It's considerably easier, and I recommend you stick to it.” Clarisse held out her hand for the horn, but she did not take it until he had demonstrated the other signal correctly. “Chiron can train a good fighter in a year or two if the camper listens. Training somebody to stay sensible when they see a friend in danger takes longer, because the urge to help makes people reckless. That's not an excuse to stay out of trouble. It just means you learn the difference between being brave and being useful.”

They stood watch for a quarter hour, listening to the trees and the occasional call from another station. Percy learned that the valley carried certain sounds much farther than others: a loose stone sliding on the dry slope sounded close even when it fell beyond the next turn, while voices from the lower camp became indistinct almost as soon as the wind changed. He could feel water running beneath the ground in places the path did not cross any visible stream, small springs moving between layers of stone and joining the lake farther downhill. He considered mentioning what he sensed, then decided to wait until Clarisse asked. The knowledge was useful, but it was not automatically more important than what she saw with her own eyes.

When their turn at the observation point was nearly over, Clarisse sat on a flat rock with her spear laid within reach and drew a folding knife from inside her jacket. She produced a short piece of wood, already worked into the shape of an animal whose head was beginning to emerge from the grain. Percy had seen her mend a spear shaft and tighten the fittings on a shield, but he had not imagined she would spend a quiet watch carving something small enough to fit in her palm. The creature had a broad feline body and a pair of wings sketched along its back, though one ear stood taller than the other and its face still showed the rough marks of the first cuts.

“Is that for practice, or are we expecting the camp's panthers to grow wings?” Percy asked, crouching beside the rock. He was careful not to come so close that he would crowd her hands. “Because I don't think I packed a horn signal for flying cats.”

“It's supposed to be a panther,” Clarisse said, examining the uneven ear with an expression of professional disappointment. “The wings are Silena's idea. She said it looked too serious without them, which is a strange complaint from someone who wants it sitting beside her paints.”

“So you're making it for her.”

“I started it because she said our shelf looked as though it belonged in a storage room instead of somebody's home. Then she saw the sketch and told me the shelf would be more useful with a drawer. She's right about the drawer, but the panther's already half carved, so I'm finishing it before I build anything.” Clarisse worked the knife along the grain, raising a narrow curl of wood that fell across the toe of her boot. “Don't make that face. People can give each other presents without putting on a ceremony.”

“I wasn't going to put on a ceremony. I was thinking you have a pretty good eye for the shape of it.” Percy looked at the creature's broad back, where the first wing followed the natural line of the wood. “It reminds me of the decorations on some of the old halls under the sea. They're all enormous and carved in stone, though, so the people who made them probably had more room to hide mistakes.”

Clarisse gave him a sidelong look. “You saying the ear's a mistake?”

“I'm saying if I tried to make an ear that small, the whole head would come off.”

“That's more sensible.” She put the panther beside her and offered him a smaller block, one that still held a knot near the end. “Here. Try taking a shaving from the side. Hold it against your knee so it doesn't roll, and keep the blade moving away from your fingers. If you take one off, Chiron will expect me to explain why we returned from patrol with less of you than we left with.”

Percy accepted the wood and turned it over in his hands. Its surface held the faint roughness of dry bark, and the knot offered resistance at precisely the place where a novice would be tempted to force the knife. He had learned sword forms from people who could cut a moving target in the dark, but a carving blade demanded an entirely different sort of control. His first stroke bit too deeply. A wedge came away from the side, leaving an ugly gouge where he had hoped for a smooth curve. The embarrassment was so immediate that he almost tossed the block into the grass, then remembered that Clarisse was watching him with more interest than amusement.

“You tried to stab it,” she observed. “That's probably a useful skill in the arena, but timber generally responds better if you persuade it instead.”

“Last week I was trying to drown a drainage ditch. At least this time I've chosen an object that won't flood the paths if I get it wrong.” Percy held the block out for inspection and raised his eyebrows. “Is there any chance this becomes a panther, or should I aim for a potato while I still can?”

“A potato would be ambitious.” Clarisse repositioned his hand, turning the block so the grain ran with the cut. Her touch was brief and practical, the touch of someone demonstrating a movement rather than taking the work away from him. “Watch where the knife wants to go. If you push against the grain, it splits. If you take a thinner strip and let the blade follow it, you get something you can shape.”

He tried again. A curled shaving fell across his glove, almost translucent at the edge, and for a moment he felt the particular satisfaction that came from learning a thing with his hands instead of being told he ought to remember already knowing it. The shore of Atlantis, the halls beneath the sea, even the training yard were full of skills this new body apparently possessed without offering him the memories of acquiring them. Here, on an unremarkable hillside, he had made a small cut correctly because his sister had shown him how. It was an achievement nobody would record, which made it unexpectedly valuable.

“Who taught you?” he asked, looking at the neat wing beginning to emerge beneath Clarisse's knife. “You make it look easy, and I can't imagine Chiron includes carving in the patrol manual.”

“An older camper who used to make little wooden birds for kids who were homesick,” Clarisse said. Her expression softened around the memory before she seemed to notice what she was doing. “I broke one by accident when I was smaller. She told me I could either stand around apologizing every time I saw her or learn to make a replacement. Mine looked like a badly injured potato for a while, but she helped me get the beak right. I kept at it after that.”

“What happened to the bird?” Percy asked.

“I gave it to the kid she'd been making it for. He didn't mind that one wing was crooked, which was good, because I couldn't get it straight no matter what I did.” She brushed a shaving from the panther and considered the line of its back. “I suppose most things we make for other people turn out a little different from what we meant. Sometimes they like them anyway.”

Percy looked at the block in his hand and tried to imagine it becoming something that anybody might want on a shelf. In Atlantis he had been surrounded by beautiful things made by craftsmen whose names he did not know, and in his first remembered life he had taken far too long to understand how much care could be contained in an object someone had repaired rather than replaced. Clarisse's animal was neither grand nor perfect. It carried the time she had chosen to give Silena, and he understood the gift better for having spent a few minutes trying to copy the work. He should have left the thought there. Instead, remembering Triton leaning over a practice sword in the palace and Amphitrite reading in the garden pool, he asked the question that had been troubling him since he learned how differently his sisters knew their father.

“Was Poseidon ever interested in it?”

The knife paused. Percy wished he could take the question back. He had been thinking of Triton teaching him sword drills in Atlantis and Amphitrite reading near the garden pool; for a moment, he had forgotten Clarisse's experience of their father was entirely different.

"He didn't teach me, if that's what you're asking," Clarisse said.

"I wasn't trying to—"

"I know you're curious." She put the closed knife on her knee. "That's one of your less irritating habits, most days. But you should understand that asking about him doesn't always get you a good story."

Percy sat down on the rock opposite her.

"I know you don't owe me an explanation. I don't want to keep pretending we're all talking about the same man when we aren't."

Clarisse's eyes narrowed, not in anger exactly, but as though she was deciding whether to trust what he'd said.

"He came to a tournament when I was younger," she told him after a while. "I got through to the final round. Chiron sent word, and for once Dad actually turned up. I thought he might stay long enough to see the medals."

"Did he?"

"He watched the last fight, then somebody from the sea came to fetch him. Crisis somewhere. By the time I got off the field he was gone." She picked up the panther and resumed cutting. "I won, by the way. Took me three matches to get there and nearly cost me two teeth."

Percy looked at the rough grain of the wood in his hands. "I wish he'd stayed."

"That's nice of you. Wishing doesn't do much." Her voice wasn't unkind, but it made him look up. "Silena sent him drawings last summer. Detailed ones. Water channels and planting beds, all labeled. He sent back a letter asking whether Chiron had approved the project. She said it was thoughtful of him to write."

"Was it?"

"It was better than nothing, and that's what she told herself." Clarisse scraped a stubborn piece of bark from the panther's wing. "Look, I don't need you to go to Atlantis and demand he apologize. Silena doesn't either. You'd get caught in the middle, and we'd still be where we started."

"I wasn't planning to make him apologize."

"You were thinking about something. I could see your face."

He couldn't deny it. He'd been looking for a way to make the problem smaller. Perhaps if Poseidon understood what he'd missed, perhaps if he wrote more regularly or attended a tournament, the years before Percy's arrival would stop hurting his sisters. That was an unreasonable amount of faith to put in one conversation.

"I'm used to trying to fix things before they get worse," Percy said.

"You're twelve, Jackson. You could try learning where we keep the clean practice shields before you take responsibility for our entire family."

He laughed, though the comment struck closer than she knew.

"Fine. I'll start with the shields."

"Excellent. They're in the shed you walked past twice this morning."

A horn sounded from the western post, one long note. Clarisse closed her knife immediately and stood. Percy reached for his own horn, but she stopped him with a hand against his wrist.

"Wait. That's movement, not contact. Check the lower trail and tell me what you can see before you answer."

They spread out a few paces. Through the brush Percy caught the movement of three animals moving slowly between the trees. Their ears were up; the first deer stopped to sniff the ground, then followed the others down the slope.

"Deer," he said. "At least three. They're heading away from us."

"Good. We don't sound a second warning for deer unless something's chasing them. Keep watching until they're out of view."

When the path was clear, Clarisse returned to her rock and picked up the block of wood. She shaved a thin line between the panther's wings, concentrating.

Percy worked on his own piece. He discovered that a gentler grip made the knife easier to control. Clarisse glanced at his hands once and didn't correct him.

"Silena told me you were helping with the lake map," she said eventually. "She likes having someone take an interest in the work instead of just asking whether the water's ready when they want it."

"I liked it. Turns out the lake has more ordinary plumbing than mysterious divine secrets."

"Don't sound disappointed. Ordinary plumbing keeps the strawberries alive."

He leaned forward to inspect the wing she was carving.

"Is there anything you actually want from me, apart from helping with patrols and staying out of the way?"

Clarisse thought about it.

"Come when you say you're coming. Don't tell me I'm brave when I've had a bad day, and don't ask Silena about me every time I look annoyed. If I want you to know something, I'll tell you."

"That's a reasonable list."

"It's a short one. I could make it longer."

He nodded toward the panther. "And maybe teach me how to make one of those without accidentally removing its head."

"That might take years."

"I've got time."

Clarisse looked at him for a few seconds. Her expression didn't soften dramatically. She simply turned the wood in her palm and said, "Then show up tomorrow. Same hour. Bring something less terrible to carve."

They started back to camp when the watch changed. Percy carried the extra horns and the first-aid kit, and Clarisse carried her panther tucked inside her jacket. Halfway down the trail, she corrected the way he'd hung the supply bag so its weight wouldn't pull on one shoulder.

He let her fix it. She didn't make a point of being helpful, and he didn't make a point of thanking her twice.

It was enough to finish the patrol together.
---

At breakfast the next morning, Clarisse acted much as she always did. She claimed the largest portion of eggs, told Percy his spear was in the wrong rack and corrected his grip twice during morning drills.

But she had left a place for him at the end of the bench.

He sat down before she could change her mind. Silena looked from one of them to the other and wisely said nothing.

One conversation hadn't fixed years of disappointment. Percy hadn't expected it to. Clarisse still made him repeat the last drill after everyone else had finished, and he showed up for the extra round instead of looking for an excuse.

When they were done she tossed him the supply bag.

"Same time tomorrow."

"I'll bring my wooden potato."

"Keep that thing away from my good knife."

He carried the bag back to Cabin Three.
---

Clarisse's extra drill was supposed to take twenty minutes.

Percy knew this because she'd written twenty minutes on the practice schedule, underlined it, and told him he would not be allowed to complain if he arrived late. After an hour, they were still in the arena, and she had stopped keeping track of him entirely.

Instead she was watching a small Hermes camper try to buckle a practice shield onto her arm.

Clarisse pointed at the strap. "Turn this over."

The girl tried, and the shield dropped to the sand. She glared at it; her helmet, dented above one ear, kept slipping over her eyebrows. "You said wrong side."

"I meant the strap, not the whole shield."

Percy looked at Clarisse. "Would it help if you showed her a different way?"

"I've shown her twice."

"Then let me be the bad example."

Clarisse's eyes narrowed. "Is this where you tell me all about teaching?"

"No. This is where I volunteer to be the idiot in the demonstration."



Clarisse considered him for a long moment, then tossed him another shield.

"Fine. Strap it on wrong."

"Won't be difficult."

He put the loop around his wrist so the shield hung below his elbow. The leather bit awkwardly into the skin. He raised the shield and tried to block a slow thrust from Clarisse's blunt spear. The shield swung sideways, leaving his chest exposed. Clarisse tapped him in the ribs.

"Dead," she said.

"Lovely morning."

She walked over and turned the strap.

"Your forearm should carry the load. Your hand keeps the edge steady. When it's hanging from your wrist, the whole thing becomes a door with a broken hinge."

Percy tried again. This time the shield stayed in line, although Clarisse's strike was hard enough to rattle his arm.

"Better?"

"Better than being dead."

Clarisse turned to the girl. "Your turn."

The girl managed the buckle, lifted the shield and held it so high she could barely see over the rim.

"Don't hide behind it," Percy said. "Watch the spear."

Clarisse sent him a warning look, then stepped back to give the girl more room.

"Again," she said.

They practiced until the girl's arms trembled. She started making the same mistake every third attempt, lifting the shield too early and losing her view of the spear. Percy could see her concentration fraying. Clarisse could see it too, but she kept asking for another repetition.

"She's tired," Percy said.

"That doesn't stop a monster."

"No, but it makes it harder for her to learn the next part."

The girl scowled. "I can do another."

"Can you do a better one?" Percy asked.

That gave her pause.

Clarisse lowered her spear. The arena was almost empty now. The sun had crossed the roof of the forge and was making the sand bright enough to hurt their eyes.

"Water," Clarisse said.

The girl sat down so fast her shield knocked against her helmet. Percy fetched a cup from the barrel. When he came back, Clarisse was examining the practice shield for a worn fastening.

"What's your name?" Percy asked the girl.

"Bea."

"Is this your first year?"

"Second. First year they wouldn't let me train with the older kids."

"That explains why your helmet thinks you're smaller."

"It's borrowed."

"From a person with a much smaller head."

Bea smiled despite herself. Clarisse pulled the helmet off and tightened the inner strap by two holes.

"There," she said. "Now it'll stay put."

Bea put it back on and gave a short, experimental nod.

"It doesn't hit my nose anymore."

"Fantastic," Percy said. "We've saved your nose."

Clarisse handed back the shield and pointed to the far end of the arena.

"Two minutes. Then you do one clean block and go get lunch."

"One?"

"One correct one."

Bea drank, stood and positioned herself opposite Clarisse. The first thrust was slow. The shield turned at the wrong angle, and Clarisse stopped before making contact.

"Again."

Bea reset. She watched the tip of the spear, raised the shield only when it moved, and caught the blow on the bronze rim. Her feet stayed planted.

Clarisse gave a curt nod.

"That's it. Go eat."

Bea lingered, uncertain whether a single successful block could count as a real lesson.

"Did I pass?"

"You did what I asked. Tomorrow we do two."

Bea grinned, then ran toward the pavilion so quickly she almost lost the helmet again.

Percy watched her go.

"You could've told her she did well," Percy said after Bea had gone. "She spent the entire lesson expecting to fail, and the first time she blocked you properly, you sounded as though you were checking a box."

Clarisse inspected the buckle of a practice shield. "I told her she'd earned the right to stop. She knows what that means in this arena."

"Maybe she does. But it might be worth telling her directly once in a while. She's not going to get worse at fighting because somebody noticed she improved."

Clarisse turned to him, irritated.

"Is that what you think you've figured out today? That I'm mean?"

"No. I think you want her to be able to defend herself. You kept her out here because you're worried she won't."

"She's a Hermes kid. They get cornered on patrol too."

"Exactly." He started stacking the spare shields. "You're teaching her as if she's facing a monster this afternoon. She needed a chance to learn without being scared of you."

Clarisse picked up the next shield, inspected the strap and set it in the repair pile.

"She's scared of the spear."

"She can be scared of both."

"Then she'd better learn to deal with both."

Percy did not answer immediately. In another life, he'd been pushed through dangerous lessons because the world refused to wait for him to grow older. Some of that training had saved him. Some had made him think he deserved every injury that followed.

He didn't want to use those memories to lecture his sister. He wasn't sure how much of them belonged in this conversation.

"You made a better teacher when you fixed her helmet," he said.

Clarisse stopped.

"Her helmet was broken," Clarisse said, a little defensively. "Of course I fixed it. I'm not putting somebody on the training field with equipment that doesn't fit."

"I know. I'm saying she looked more confident after you did. You made it easier for her to concentrate on the spear instead of her helmet falling into her eyes."

Clarisse considered that, then handed Percy a shield to inspect. "You sound like Chiron when he wants me to change how I teach. He might be onto something occasionally. Don't tell him I said that."

She threw him the last shield. He caught it and checked the straps before adding it to the rack.

For a few minutes they cleaned up together without speaking. The arena was empty, and the late morning sun had begun to warm the wooden practice dummies.

"I used to hate it when instructors yelled at me," Clarisse said suddenly.

Percy looked up.

"Then why do you do it?"

"Because I hate it when people don't listen." She rubbed the worn leather of a shield strap between two fingers. "And sometimes they don't listen until I raise my voice."

"Sometimes," Percy agreed.

"I didn't say always."

"No."

Clarisse glanced toward the pavilion, where Bea was probably telling her friends how she'd passed the shield test.

"I'll ask Bea tomorrow whether she remembers the footwork," Clarisse said. "If she does, we can move on to the next set."

"She was trying hard enough that I think she will," Percy said, returning a shield to its hook. "And if she doesn't, we know which part to practice again."

Clarisse hung the final shield in its place.

"Now your turn."

Percy stared. "We just finished."

"Bea did."

"That's cruel."

"That's training. Pick up your sword."

He groaned, but he picked it up.

She made him practice the same block three times, each against a different angle of attack. He got the first wrong, the second almost right and the third well enough that she didn't correct him.

"Good," she said.

Percy lowered his sword.

"You could've said that earlier."

"You hadn't done it earlier."

He couldn't argue with that. It was a good lesson, even if Clarisse would never admit they'd both had something to learn.
---

Percy found Silena in the library that afternoon with an atlas spread across three tables and a needle between her teeth.

He had been looking for Clarisse, but Silena noticed him trying to read the spines on the agriculture shelf and beckoned him over. She took the needle from her mouth before speaking.

"You've passed this shelf twice. Either you've developed a sudden interest in crop rotation, or you're avoiding somebody."

"I was hoping Clarisse might be here. She said she'd help me with a shield strap after lunch."

"She's at the arena. Matt asked her to look at some damaged practice gear." Silena held the book cover flat and examined the torn fabric along its spine. "You can hold this while I stitch, if you aren't in a hurry."

Percy put his bag on the floor and took the weight of the atlas so she could work. The cover was made from stiff board wrapped in cloth. Several pages near the front had come loose and threatened to slide out every time he shifted his hand.

"How old is this thing?"

"Older than the library's current shelving, which isn't saying much. Chiron says it was donated by an explorer who believed the Mediterranean would be much easier to navigate if every monster had the decency to live where it was drawn on the map."

"Didn't work, apparently."

"Not for the explorer. His second edition is considerably shorter."

Percy laughed. Silena eased the needle through the frayed fabric, pulling the thread slowly until the tear closed another inch.

"How long have you been doing that?"

"Repairing the atlas? Since lunch. Repairing books? Since I discovered the library has a budget for new weapons manuals but apparently not for replacing volumes people have spilled soup on."

"I meant when did you learn?"

"One of the Athena kids showed me. After that I kept practicing. It's mostly patience and the willingness to undo a bad stitch before it becomes six bad stitches."

Percy watched the needle emerge on the wrong side of the cover. Silena noticed it immediately, sighed and unpicked the last loop.

"Like that," she added.

He moved his hand so the spine lay straighter. He wanted to ask about Clarisse, but Silena had already given him a job, and for once he resisted interrupting somebody else's work simply because his question felt important.

Silena glanced at him over the atlas.

"You can ask. Your face is doing something exhausting."

"Clarisse told me about a tournament. One where Poseidon came to watch her, but he left before the prizes."

Silena's fingers slowed. She didn't look surprised.

"She doesn't usually tell people that story."

"I know. I'm not trying to make her regret it."

"Then don't spend the afternoon asking everybody else what it meant to her."

"I wasn't going to. I just keep thinking about how different things were for us." Percy adjusted his grip as a page slipped loose. "She had to wonder whether he'd turn up. I grew up in his house. I don't remember most of that childhood, but everybody acts as though I should. It's hard to work out what I ought to say to either of you."

Silena set the needle down. She studied his expression before answering.

"Start by not calling one childhood fair and the other unfair as though we can balance them on a scale. Clarisse and I have had a good life at camp, even when we're furious with Dad. You didn't decide where he raised you. We didn't decide where he left us. None of us can repair that with a clever argument."

"I know I can't fix it."

"You keep looking as though you're planning to try." She picked up a loose sheet, checked its page number and tucked it into place. "I don't want you apologizing for every nice thing he ever did for you. I'd rather you asked us about things we actually care about now."

"Like the irrigation plan."

"Yes. Or Clarisse's carving. Or whether we want to spend our Saturday pulling weeds out of the strawberry beds. Which, incidentally, we do not, but Chiron has informed me that wanting things is not the same as getting them."

Percy smiled. "I did help with the drainage cut."

"And then you made it worse before making it better, if Clarisse's account is to be believed."

"She told you about that?"

"Percy, your shoes were full of mud and a Hermes camper came through the dining pavilion carrying half a cart wheel. It wasn't difficult to put together."

He let go of the atlas to gesture in his defense. Three loose pages slid across the table. Silena caught two and stared at the third as it drifted to the floor.

"That," she said, "is why you're helping me."

"Sorry."

"Pick up the page. Carefully. The ink is older than both of us, and if you tear it I'm going to tell Clarisse you broke something else."

He retrieved it by the edges and passed it back. Silena didn't make him feel foolish for long. She handed him a clean cloth, showed him where to support the spine, and let him try a single stitch.

His first attempt pulled too tightly and puckered the fabric.

She caught his wrist before he could make the next.

"Don't fight the thread. It's not trying to win."

"I have very little experience with things that aren't."

"Then consider this an education."

She unpicked his stitch, laid the material smooth and guided him through the next one without holding his hand. The needle went where it was supposed to. Percy looked absurdly pleased with himself.

"You're good at teaching," he said.

"I'm good at keeping people from destroying my books. There is a difference."

For a while they worked in companionable quiet. Silena asked him which part of camp felt most like home so far, and he described the hollow water channels under Cabin Three and Clarisse's habit of scolding him for leaving things in the wrong place. When he mentioned the rooms in Atlantis he'd learned about from Amphitrite, Silena listened without asking why his descriptions sometimes sounded as though he'd been told them rather than remembering them.

Finally Percy said, "Does it bother you when I talk about him?"

"Our father?"

"Yes."

"Sometimes," she admitted. "It depends what you're saying. I don't mind hearing that he's kind to you. I mind when somebody tells me that means I've misunderstood all the years he wasn't there for us."

"I won't say that."

"I believe you." She passed him another page to hold steady. "And one of these days I'd like to visit Atlantis without spending the entire trip feeling like I have to prove I belong. That isn't something you can promise for him, either."

"No. But if you ever come while I'm there, I'll show you around. Or I'll ask somebody who actually remembers how the palace works to show us both."

"That sounds more realistic." She smiled at him. "I'd like that."

They finished the binding and set the atlas between two heavy dictionaries while the glue dried. Silena wrote the date and the repair method on a card for the library records. Then she took the cloth from Percy's hands and found a strip of pale thread caught on his sleeve.

"You've got half a book following you home."

"Is that a problem?"

"It is when I'm trying to return all of it to the shelf."

She pulled the thread away, packed her repair kit and pointed him toward the door.

"Clarisse should still be at the arena. If she's in a foul mood, remind her she promised to help with your shield strap before she agreed to repair every damaged weapon in camp."

Percy picked up his bag.

"And if she asks what we talked about?"

"Tell her we fixed a book. That's what we did."

He headed out with sore fingers, a lesson in binding he was unlikely to master quickly, and no expectation that one conversation could settle what he and his sisters had inherited from Poseidon.

Silena's afternoon project involved a shovel, a broken measuring stick and three people who disagreed about where the water was supposed to go.

Percy learned this when she intercepted him outside the library and asked whether he wanted to see the irrigation system working properly. By then he'd already made a mess of the atlas, and he thought the least he could do was carry something that couldn't be torn with one careless hand.

The strawberry beds lay in neat rows below the ridge, protected by low windbreaks. Between the rows ran narrow channels lined with gravel. The stream feeding them was meant to pass through a screened box before dividing toward the plots. Instead, most of the water was finding its way into a muddy depression beside the fourth row.

Silena crouched next to the screen, testing the wooden frame. A daughter of Demeter named Mara stood nearby with her arms folded, watching a smaller camper try to clear the accumulated water with a bucket.

"I don't want to take another set of measurements if we're going to ignore them," Mara said. "The western plants are already too wet. You can't fix that by digging one more ditch."

"I'm not asking you to dig another ditch," Silena replied. She lifted the frame out and set it on a cloth. "I'm asking you to wait until I understand why water is escaping from the box. If the joints are open, changing the slope won't help."

Mara glanced at Percy, apparently wondering whether his arrival was meant to settle the argument.

"Is he here because he's Poseidon's son?"

"He's here because he offered to carry tools," Silena said. "He's also the only person who knows where I put the spare bracket, because I told him before breakfast. Percy, would you get it from the bag?"

He took the bag off his shoulder and found the bracket beneath a folded diagram. The metal was cold and unremarkable, the sort of thing he would have ignored before spending several mornings learning what went wrong when someone treated a water system like an extension of their own power.

Silena held the bracket against the inside of the frame. It didn't fit. She changed the angle, inspected the old screw holes and tried again.

"This frame was replaced last week. The person who cut it used the old measurements, but the channel has shifted since summer." She turned it over and pointed to a strip of cloth sewn across the bottom. "And the filter material's been stretched until the stitching started to tear."

Percy recognized the pale green thread. "That's the same stuff we found in the cabin drain."

"Probably from the same roll. I was hoping it was just one badly made section."

Mara knelt to inspect the fabric, irritation giving way to interest. "Could we use the old screen until we get a new piece?"

"If it's still in the stores. I thought Chiron had sent it for repairs." Silena checked the drawing. "This system doesn't need a new design. It needs the right parts."

The smaller camper, who had been carrying buckets from the flooded row, put his bucket down.

"Can I stop now? I've moved the water into the ditch twice and it keeps coming back."

"Yes," Silena said. "You should've stopped when it stopped helping. Thank you for trying. Go get dry socks before dinner."

The boy left with an expression of enormous relief. Percy watched him go, then moved the bucket out of the walking path.

Mara's attention had returned to the plants. She ran a finger beneath a leaf and showed Silena a row of tiny brown marks.

"These are already showing stress. If the roots sit in water all night, we could lose the row."

"Then we'll block this branch until morning and let the excess drain away," Silena said. "But first I want to see whether the leak is happening at the screen or downstream. Otherwise we could be cutting off water to the healthy beds for no reason."

She handed Percy the measuring stick. Half the painted markings had worn off.

"Can you hold this upright in the channel while I check the flow?"

He placed it against the gravel, bracing his foot away from the edge. Water moved around the wood, barely reaching his ankle. The channel had enough current to make him aware of it even without looking. He could have shifted the stream with a thought, but Silena had asked him to hold a stick, and he suspected that was because she actually needed a reliable reference point.

After a few minutes Mara called out a reading from the other end of the bed. Silena wrote it down, compared it against her old notebook and frowned.

"The flow is slower here than it was last month, but the water level is higher at the entrance. Something's obstructing the first bend."

"Should I check it?" Percy asked.

"With your eyes and the rake. Not by making a wave."

He gave her a wounded look. "One drainage accident and nobody trusts me around water anymore."

"Three, if you count the laundry cart and the time you flooded the basin during cabin inspection."

"Those were related incidents."

"They all involved you touching water before you knew where it was going. That's the common feature."

Mara laughed. She was beginning to look less annoyed, which Percy took as a sign that the work had found a direction.

He followed the channel upstream. Beneath a flat stone near the edge, a clump of roots had trapped a bundle of straw and leaves. He pulled the debris free with the rake and watched the water drop slightly. Silena checked the measurement again and nodded.

"That helps. It's not the whole problem, but at least we've identified something we can fix without rebuilding the system."

By the time they finished taking readings, the sun had moved low enough that long shadows crossed the fields. The mud on Percy's shoes had dried in a crust. Silena had three pages of notes and one new plan that Mara seemed willing to support: block the damaged branch for the night, check the surviving filter cloth in the stores and reopen it after the repair team had tested the flow.

Mara gathered her notebook and tools. Before leaving, she looked at Silena.

"I'm sorry I said you'd ignored the measurements. I didn't realize the frame had been replaced. I just didn't want to lose the plants."

"I know. Next time, show me the leaves first. I might still disagree about the cause, but I need that information."

Mara nodded, then headed toward the greenhouse with a much lighter step.

Percy stayed behind to help stack the old fittings. Silena was cleaning the edge of the screen, removing mud from a joint with the corner of her cloth. She worked carefully even though the broken frame was going to be replaced.

"You seem to spend a lot of time repairing other people's mistakes," he said.

"Somebody repaired mine last summer. I set one of the channels too steep and washed a week's worth of soil into the low beds. Mara helped me rebuild it." Silena wrung out the cloth. "I was furious with her for noticing, because I was already embarrassed. Then she spent an entire afternoon moving gravel with me."

"She didn't hold it over your head?"

"She teased me about it for a month, which was deserved. Then we had a new channel that worked. That's generally how good work goes. Somebody catches the mistake and somebody fixes it."

Percy considered the little screen in her hands. He'd spent years in the life he remembered trying to keep people alive, with immediate consequences when something went wrong. Here the stakes were a row of strawberries and the patience of people who had to use the system afterward. It seemed less dramatic. It also meant Silena could be proud of a solution that lasted beyond one heroic afternoon.

"Do you like doing this more than training?" he asked.

"Usually. I like training well enough, but I don't want my entire life to be measured by how many people I can beat in a fight." She set the cleaned frame on its side. "Dad knows Clarisse is good with a spear. I don't think he knows what I do with the rest of my day, and I got tired of waiting for him to ask."

"Could you send him the drawings from today?"

"I could. I might, eventually. But I'm not going to spend tonight trying to make the work look impressive enough for someone who wasn't here to see it."

She said it without much anger. That was almost harder for Percy to hear than if she'd raised her voice.

"Would you mind showing me how to finish the measurements tomorrow?" he asked.

Silena looked up, evidently trying to decide whether he was asking because he meant it.

"You already spent an afternoon holding a stick in a ditch. You're under no obligation to come back."

"I know. I'd like to understand the part you drew before anyone started digging. The maps make more sense when I can see what they're for."

That seemed to satisfy her. She rolled the diagram around a cardboard tube and tucked it into her bag.

"Then come before lunch. We'll go through the north channel while there's enough light to read the old markings. Bring shoes you don't care about."

"My shoes have given up caring."

"I noticed."

As they left the field, Silena gave the closed branch one final inspection. Percy carried the screen and a bag of wet cloth back toward the stores. He felt tired in a pleasant, ordinary way. He had done nothing spectacular with his powers all afternoon, and nobody had needed him to.

The camp bell rang as they reached the path. Silena headed toward the pavilion to speak with the kitchens about the water schedule. Percy volunteered to return the remaining tools, which was how he ended up walking toward the canoe dock with a coil of rope that had no business being left outside.

He could see Clarisse sitting at the far end before he reached the shore.

Percy found Clarisse at the lake just before dinner, sitting at the far end of a low dock with her boots beside her.

He'd been sent to retrieve a coil of rope left by the canoe crew. He wasn't looking for his sister. The coil, however, was at her feet.

"You planning to take that rope home?" Percy asked, nodding at the coil by Clarisse's feet. "The canoe crew's been looking for it."

Clarisse nudged the coil toward him. "They can have it. The last section's fraying, so tell them not to use it for anything that needs to hold weight. I was about to put it in the repair pile."

Clarisse nudged the rope toward him with one bare foot. Percy picked it up and checked for cuts. Most of the length was sound, but the last yard had begun to fray.

"I'll mark it for repair when I get back," Percy said, separating the damaged end from the rest.

"You're finally learning how this place runs. It took you long enough to stop assuming a rope would announce when it was worn out."

"I learned from somebody who carries a knife everywhere and treats broken equipment as a personal insult. You may have met her."

Clarisse rolled her eyes, but she was smiling when she turned back to the water.

Clarisse drew the panther from her jacket pocket, turning it over to check the wings. She'd started it for Silena's shelf, but Silena preferred storage she could actually use. Clarisse had finished carving it anyway, and now she looked almost annoyed at herself for bringing it down to the lake. The face had been polished smooth where her thumb would have rested as she worked, but the paw joints and undersides of the wings still showed the separate knife cuts. The paws were too large for the body. Its crooked expression looked like one Clarisse would have recognized across a dining table.

She held it out.

"Here."

Percy took it before she decided not to offer.

"What's this for?" Percy asked, turning the little animal carefully so its wings didn't catch on his sleeves.

Clarisse kept looking across the lake. "Your birthday, if that makes it less awkward. I don't know when it is yet, and you don't have to make a speech. Silena wanted a drawer, not a wooden cat. I figured you'd keep this somewhere it won't get stepped on."

"I do," Percy said. "And for the record, the birthday system needs work, but the panther doesn't."

He turned it in his fingers. The wing feathers were rough along the undersides, but the front of the animal had been polished smooth. A great deal of patient work had gone into something Clarisse was pretending not to care about.

"Thanks," he said. He could remember her teaching him to cut away from his fingers, and now the gift gave that lesson a shape he could hold. He turned it once more, then tucked it where the wings wouldn't catch on his shirt.

"Don't make a thing of it."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were looking at it like you were about to make a thing of it."

"I was looking at its face. It looks like you."

"Give it back."

He tucked it into his shirt. "Too late."

She reached for it. He stepped aside. The dock creaked, and she caught his sleeve before he could stumble off the edge.

"Watch your feet," Clarisse said, catching his sleeve as the dock shifted beneath him. "Being Poseidon's son won't stop you cracking your head on the edge of a piling. I'd rather not spend dinner explaining to Chiron why you fell in while admiring a wooden cat."

Percy recovered his balance and looked at her. "I thought we agreed it was a panther. It's even got wings."

"Then try to keep it out of the lake."

She let go. Percy sat a few planks away and put the panther on his knee.

"Silena says you make these when you're thinking."

"Silena also says we should sort the supply cupboard by color. She has opinions."

"Her system works."

"It works because I carry the heavy things."

"She'd say it works because she knows where the heavy things go."

Clarisse gave him a sideways look.

"She's been making you do chores again?"

"I volunteered."

"Then you've been at camp long enough to make bad decisions."

They watched a pair of naiads argue with a camper who was trying to push a canoe off its cradle without checking the rope. Clarisse called out the correct knot before the boy could get himself tangled. He waved in thanks and tried again.

Percy glanced at her.

"You teach everybody like that?"

"Only the ones who might break something."

"That's everybody."

"Yes."

She tucked her hands into her jacket sleeves. The breeze off the lake had turned colder.

"I'm still angry with Dad," she said.

Percy hadn't asked. The admission caught him off guard.

"Okay."

"And I don't want you deciding it would be better if we all sat together and sorted out our feelings."

"I wasn't planning a family meeting."

"Good. It would be awful."

He thought about the wooden panther on his knee. Clarisse had been working on it while they discussed Poseidon at the border, and she'd finished it without saying another word about what she'd told him.

"I can't make him better at being your father, and I know I can't fix what already happened," Percy said. He settled the carved panther on his knee. "I just want you and Silena to know I'm not going to vanish the moment camp stops being new to me. I like having you both around, even when you make me carry every heavy thing in the cabin."

Clarisse examined him from beneath the loose hair at her forehead. "Then keep doing what you've been doing. Show up for drills, put your gear away, don't make me chase you down every time there's a job. If you manage that for a while, I'll probably believe you."

Percy leaned back on his palms. For a while they listened to the ropes clatter against the canoe racks.

"Have you shown Silena that panther?" she asked.

"Not yet."

"Don't."

"Why?"

"She'll ask where hers is."

He looked at the winged creature.

"Does she have one?"

"Three." Clarisse considered that. "Four, if she's kept the one I made when I was nine."

"Then this is an exclusive edition."

"Don't let it go to your head."

The dinner horn sounded. Clarisse put on her boots. Percy tucked the rope under one arm and the panther safely into a pocket where its wings wouldn't catch.

As they started back, Clarisse noticed he'd left his jacket unzipped.

"You're going to freeze."

"Yes, Mother."

She shoved him, not hard enough to knock him off the path.

"Don't call me that."

"Yes, terrifying woman with a knife."

"Better."

They reached the cabin before the second horn. Percy set the damaged rope beside the supply chest and found a spot for the panther on the shelf over his bunk.

Clarisse pretended not to notice where he'd put it.

Annabeth found Percy sitting at the end of the canoe dock after dinner, with the repaired section of rope beside his boots and the small wooden panther Clarisse had given him balanced on his knee. The lake had lost the day's warmth. Its surface was dark where the pines overhung the bank and silver toward the middle, where the last of the sky showed between banks of cloud. Every few minutes a breeze came down the slope from the training grounds and stirred the ropes against their cleats, making a soft percussion among the creak of boards and the slap of water against the pilings. Percy was looking out over the lake with the expression Annabeth knew from the aftermath of difficult conversations, when he wanted to understand what had happened before deciding whether he was allowed to feel relieved.

“You know Thalia's looking for you,” she said as she stepped onto the dock. “Before you start imagining that she's discovered our latest collection of problems, it's about the practice shields. She thinks someone took them from the rack she used this morning, and apparently your name was the last one written beside the equipment list.”

Percy looked up, blinking as though he had been thinking of something much farther away. “Oh. Clarisse had me move them after drills because the rack near the door was getting damp. They're on the upper shelf in the armory, and I forgot to change the location on the list. I should've told Thalia before dinner.”

“That would have saved me a fairly educational conversation about how people who borrow equipment should be made to carry it home on their backs.” Annabeth sat beside him, drawing her jacket around her knees before she looked at the little carving. “You could still tell her in the morning, preferably before she decides it's evidence that Poseidon's cabin has declared war on organized storage.”

“She'd enjoy that war too much. I'd lose before breakfast.” Percy picked the panther up by its body rather than its wings, turning it so she could see the deliberately uneven face. “Clarisse gave me this earlier. She says it's for my birthday, whenever she works out when that is. I think she wanted to finish a project she'd started for Silena, but she wouldn't let me thank her long enough to find out.”

Annabeth held out her hand. When Percy passed the carving over, she examined it with the care she gave to models and tools, following the cuts along the wings and the small places where the grain had forced Clarisse to change the shape. It was imperfect in a way she liked. Someone had known where to stop carving rather than remove every mark left by the work. “This took her a while,” she said. “Look at the underside of the wings. She had to turn the knife against the grain to get them to sit like that. Did she teach you how?”

“She tried. I made something that could be a potato if you were generous about the anatomy.” Percy smiled when Annabeth raised an eyebrow. “It turns out I'm less talented at carving wood than I am at annoying Clarisse. She's been very patient about one of those things.”

“It's nice that she's letting you see this part of her,” Annabeth said. She turned the panther again, then gave it back. “You used to tell me how impossible she was, and now you spend half your time following her around the training grounds. I know this isn't the Clarisse you remember, but I like that you're getting to know the person who's here.”

Percy placed the carved creature on the boards between them, carefully away from the water. “She doesn't want me to solve anything between her and Poseidon. I keep catching myself wanting to do it anyway. In Atlantis, Dad was there for breakfast and training and a dozen little things I didn't even know I'd missed in the other life. Then Clarisse tells me about him leaving halfway through a tournament, and I can't make those two versions of him fit together.”

Annabeth watched a narrow canoe returning toward the storage shed. The camper in its stern had to work harder on one side because the wind pushed at the bow, and she found herself thinking of all the corrections a person made while moving toward a place that never stopped shifting. “Maybe they aren't supposed to fit,” she said. “You can believe he loves you and still believe he failed her. If you make either of them less real, it's only going to make your conversations with Clarisse harder.”

“I know. She told me to show up for drills and chores rather than make speeches about family.” Percy rubbed at the faint cut on one finger, earned while Clarisse had been teaching him to shape the wood. “I think I can manage that. It's the trying not to do more that's difficult.”

“She knows,” Annabeth said, and nudged his knee gently with hers. “I suspect she can see you thinking about it from across the pavilion. You don't have a particularly mysterious face when somebody you care about is upset.”

He turned to her. “That's a cruel thing to say to a person who spent two wars perfecting an expression of heroic indifference.”

“You spent two wars getting caught whenever you tried to hide something from me.” She let the teasing soften as she looked toward the illuminated windows of Cabin One on the hill. “Thalia's been talking about Zeus again. Not because she wants to, I think, but because somebody asked which face he wore on his last visit, and I couldn't answer. She said he had a different voice and hair from the time before, that even the way he stood had changed. She spoke as though I ought to be able to put the descriptions together and recognize him.”

Percy listened without interrupting. He knew the gods could choose their appearances, and the shifting faces of Zeus were not a mystery in themselves. What mattered was the way Annabeth had described her sister's voice, as if a remembered father could become almost impossible to hold in one's mind when the face never stayed the same. He waited for Annabeth to continue rather than turn the conversation into another investigation.

“I asked Thalia whether she believed he'd come back soon,” Annabeth said. “She looked at me like I'd asked whether winter was going to be warm. Apparently he said he would, and apparently he's said it before. I wanted to ask whether he ever remembers the things he promised us when he comes back wearing somebody else's face, but I could tell she was tired of being the one who knows what he usually does.”

“Do you want to ask him yourself?” Percy said. “Not right this minute, obviously. I doubt we'd get a useful answer by hunting for him through the clouds.”

“I don't know whether I do. Part of me wants him to recognize me without being told who I am, which is ridiculous because I don't even know what he looks like from one visit to the next.” She drew her knees closer. “And part of me misses Athena so much that I feel guilty whenever Thalia tells a story about us being Zeus's daughters. With Athena I knew why we disagreed, even when it hurt. Here I don't know what he expects, or whether he thinks about me when he's somewhere else.”

Percy looked down at his hands. In the fading light they seemed younger than they had in the old world, and he remembered the hundreds of practical things Sally had done without turning them into lessons. “I miss my mom too. I keep catching myself remembering her kitchen when Amphitrite asks what I used to eat when I was little, and the answers don't match anything I can say to her. I love Amphitrite, and I can see how much she cares about me. But Sally's still in every memory I have of growing up. It's not something I can put down because somebody else has been kind.”

Annabeth knew how carefully he was choosing his words. Sally had lived through the childhood they remembered, while this altered history held a death he had not experienced and a household he could not recall. Amphitrite was not responsible for the loss. Neither was Percy. Annabeth found herself thinking of Thalia keeping a bedside lamp on when she had nightmares, and of Athena showing pride in her work only after making her struggle to earn it. The feelings were not substitutes for one another. They occupied the same life and sometimes made that life harder to explain.

“When Thalia describes things we did together,” she said, “I can see the pictures she means. I can work out which year she was talking about and what I must have been like from the way she says it. But there's no memory behind the pictures, and I think that hurts her more than she wants to admit. I wish I could tell her exactly what happened to us, just once, without making her question everything else she knows.”

Percy moved the panther away from a wet place on the boards and rested his hand palm-up beside hers. “I wish we could too. I don't think we can do it safely yet, and I don't want us telling half the truth just because the whole one is frightening. But you don't have to pretend the stories mean nothing. They're things Thalia lived, even if you didn't remember living them. Maybe she can tell you what she wants you to know without making it a test.”

Annabeth took his hand. The advice was imperfect, which made it easier to accept. He had not offered a method for recovering lost years or a way to make her feel differently about Zeus. He had reminded her that listening was something she could do without pretending to possess memories she did not. “We're repairing a frame together,” she told him. “The portrait in Cabin One has been leaning against the wall for weeks. I wanted to finish it all in one evening, and Thalia made me admit that the glue needed time to dry.”

“I'm shocked that you, of all people, were in a hurry to finish a construction project.”

“It's a repair, Seaweed Brain. That makes it worse. I can see what the finished thing should look like, so waiting for the materials is infuriating.” She grinned and squeezed his fingers. “Thalia's not bad at the work, though. She's better at holding a piece steady than I expected. She can sit with me for an hour without deciding the whole thing needs a battle plan, as long as I don't make it sound as though she has to.”

Percy picked up the panther and set it in her palm again for a moment. “Then she can teach me to hold the frame while you glue it. I'll have years of practice by the time you let me do anything complicated.”

“No, because you'd immediately tell us it looked fine with three nails and a prayer.” Annabeth passed the panther back and stood, brushing dust from the hem of her jacket. “Come on. Curfew's coming, and you still owe Thalia a proper explanation about the shields. Tell her where you put them before she decides to search every cabin herself.”

Percy got to his feet and gathered the coil of rope. The dock creaked beneath them as their combined weight shifted toward shore, and they walked slowly enough to speak about unimportant things: how cold the water had become, whether the younger campers would ever stop borrowing each other's coats, and what Silena might say about the panther once she discovered Clarisse had given it away. At the fork beneath Cabin One, Annabeth gave him a brief kiss and took the uphill path, while Percy continued toward Cabin Three with the little carving safe inside his jacket. The people waiting in their separate cabins could not restore a past neither of them fully understood. They could, however, make room for another ordinary evening, and Percy was beginning to recognize how much trust had to be built from evenings like this one.

When Percy returned to Cabin Three that night, there was a hand-drawn schedule pinned to the inside of the door.

It had five columns, more rows than anybody could reasonably need, and several corrections in two different kinds of ink. Silena's handwriting was neat enough to belong in a textbook. Clarisse's additions were larger and pressed so firmly into the paper that Percy could feel the grooves through the back.

He read the headings: patrol, repairs, meals, training, stores.

His name appeared in three places at once.

"Am I supposed to be in the arena and on the eastern fence at the same time?" he asked.

Clarisse was kneeling beside her trunk, checking the straps on her greaves. Silena had a bundle of folded work cloths on her bunk. Neither looked pleased to see the schedule.

"That's what I've been explaining," Silena said. "I can't send him to inspect the channel at noon if you've promised Matt he'll cover the afternoon patrol. And he can't do two jobs while Chiron has him in lessons."

"The patrol needs a third person," Clarisse answered without looking up. "The regular guard is out sick, and Matt doesn't want anybody walking the eastern approach alone until the ward inspection's finished."

"Then write the request down and wait for Chiron to assign somebody. You don't get to put Percy's name wherever there's a hole."

Percy set his bag down. "Can I see which one of you is winning before you make me part of this?"

Silena pointed at the schedule.

"Neither of us, apparently. That's the problem. I thought we agreed you'd help me check the repaired filter tomorrow, and Clarisse volunteered you for a patrol at the same hour without asking."

"I thought the filter was fixed," Clarisse said.

"The leak is controlled. That's not the same as knowing the replacement will work. Mara spent half an afternoon moving seedlings to keep them out of the flooded row, and I don't want her doing it again because we didn't test the channel."

Clarisse began to wind a strap around her hand. Percy had seen her do that when she was angry enough to need a task. He also knew Silena wasn't going to drop the subject simply because her sister was becoming difficult.

"Why can't you take somebody else?" Clarisse asked. "There are a dozen campers who know the water beds better than Percy."

"Because he was there today. He helped us take the measurements, and I said I'd show him how we check the slope before opening the branch. If I change the arrangement every time somebody else wants a helper, I might as well stop making one."

Percy looked from one sister to the other.

"I should probably mention I only have two arms and one head. I could be wrong, but I'm fairly sure Chiron expects the head to turn up for lessons in the morning."

"Your head is usually the least useful part of you during sword practice," Clarisse muttered.

Silena didn't laugh.

"This isn't about whether Percy can take a joke. It's about you offering up his afternoon without checking with him or me. You've done it before."

Clarisse pulled the strap too tight. The leather creaked.

"Fine. I should've asked. But the wards aren't working properly, and a monster got past our defenses last week. I'd rather have him on patrol than digging holes when something else comes through."

A few days ago Percy might have offered to handle both tasks, reasoning that an older version of himself had done far more difficult things with much less sleep. He had already discovered that a twelve-year-old body was not impressed by his memories. Even if he could stay awake, dividing his attention between two jobs that mattered to other people would not make either job better.

"Who's running the patrol?" he asked.

"Matt and me. If Chiron approves the extra rotation. I said I could bring you because you've been learning the horn signals."

"Could I go later, after the water check?"

"Not unless the schedule changes."

"Then that's the decision we need to ask Chiron to make. I told Silena I'd help, and she's already planned the job around that. I'm not going to pretend I can cancel whenever something more exciting turns up."

Clarisse looked at him. He couldn't tell whether she was pleased with the answer or offended by it.

"The patrol isn't exciting," she said.

"I know. I've carried the bag."

"Then you know why somebody has to go."

"I do. But you said showing up when I promise is what matters. I promised Silena first."

Clarisse put the greave aside. She didn't apologize. She got up, went to the schedule and crossed his name out of the patrol column so hard that the pencil point broke.

"There. If Matt complains, I'll tell him your sister has you measuring puddles."

Silena's head snapped up.

"You can be angry with me without insulting the work."

"That's not what I meant."

"Then say what you meant. I don't expect you to love the fields, but every camper eats from them, and you know how much it takes to keep them growing."

Clarisse looked at the broken pencil point on the floor. Percy recognized the moment when anger stopped being useful and someone had to decide whether to keep going anyway.

"I know," she said. "I helped dig the channels, remember?"

"I remember. It's one of the reasons I'm surprised you keep talking as though fixing them is a waste of time."

Clarisse leaned back against the doorframe. The argument had taken some of the fight out of her, though Percy doubted she would admit it.

"I get nervous when people aren't on the border," she said. "I know the patrols won't catch everything. But after the drakon, Chiron asked us to watch the eastern path again, and I want enough people there that nobody has to go looking for help alone."

Silena folded the last cloth, smoothing the edges.

"I understand. I want the patrol covered too. I just don't want all the other jobs to become optional because somebody calls them less dangerous."

Percy was beginning to see that neither sister wanted to lose the argument because losing felt like admitting the other one's work mattered more. The truth was less convenient. Both of them had been keeping this cabin and several parts of camp working long before he arrived.

He stepped toward the schedule, careful not to take the pencil from Clarisse's hand.

"What if we check with Chiron after breakfast? I can ask whether somebody with more patrol experience is available. If he needs me on the border, I'll go. But I want him to know I'm supposed to help test the channel first, so he can decide what actually needs covering."

Silena considered the suggestion. "I can move the test to the morning if Mara can come. We need decent light to read the markings anyway. I'd rather not have to ask everybody to change plans twice."

Clarisse glanced at her.

"I can ask Matt to wait until Chiron confirms the rota. I shouldn't have put a name on it before he did."

It was about as much agreement as Percy expected to get. The schedule still looked like a battlefield, but its next version might at least be one the three of them could follow.

Silena took it down from the door and laid it flat on her desk. She retrieved a fresh piece of paper and began copying the assignments she knew were correct, leaving the disputed patrol slot blank.

"Since we're already rearranging the week," she said, "the cabin inspection is still Friday. Which means the pile of training gear at the foot of Percy's bunk needs to find a home before it develops one of its own."

Percy looked toward his bunk. The pile was smaller than yesterday. He was fairly certain that counted as progress.

"I was going to clean it."

"You said that this morning," Clarisse observed.

"I was going to clean it twice."

"Then you're doing very well at the going-to part."

He went to fetch his equipment. They worked around one another in the small cabin, Clarisse checking straps, Silena redrawing the rota and Percy sorting wet leather from dry. No one announced that the argument had been settled. Every so often one of them asked for a hook, a pencil or a place to put something. It was easier than discussing the disagreement again.

When Percy reached his practice shield, he found the outer strap had come loose where Clarisse had warned him it would.

"Could somebody tell me whether this is fixable?" he asked.

Clarisse came over and examined the fastening. "It's fixable. The buckle isn't damaged, but the leather has stretched. If you keep tightening it the way you have been, the next thing that'll break is your wrist."

She showed him how to remove the old knot and rethread the strap. He held the shield steady while she worked. A few days earlier she would have taken it away and repaired it herself. Tonight she made him complete the final loop, then tested it by pulling hard against the buckle.

"Better. You can use it tomorrow, provided you don't do anything stupid."

"No promises about the stupid part. It's one of my stronger skills."

"At least you're aware of it."

Silena looked up from the desk.

"If you're both finished performing, I'd like someone to check whether we still have enough bandages for the patrol kit. Chiron used half the last box after the drakon."

Clarisse opened the supply cupboard. Percy crouched beside her to count the rolls, trying not to let the loose panther carving in his pocket knock against the shelves.

He set it on the desk for safekeeping.

Silena noticed immediately.

"Where did you get that?"

"Clarisse made it."

"She did?" Silena picked it up and examined the wings. "It's beautiful. You've finally figured out how to carve the feathers without splitting the grain."

Clarisse's ears turned red.

"It took me three tries."

"I know. You left the first two failures under your bunk."

"They weren't failures. They were practice pieces."

"Of course they were." Silena placed the panther carefully beside Percy's notebook. "Are you making one for me?"

Clarisse looked as though she had been waiting for this question since the day she started carving.

"You've got four."

"Three and a half. The old one lost a wing when the shelf fell."

"That was because you put too many books on it."

"The books were perfectly reasonable. Your shelf was poorly attached."

Percy watched them with interest. They disagreed about shelves and carvings in exactly the same way they disagreed about patrols and irrigation. The argument was familiar to both of them, which meant neither needed to explain all of it to him.

"You could ask her to make you a new shelf," he suggested.

Silena turned to him with an expression of appalled delight.

"I was going to ask you, actually. You offered to help with the stores, remember?"

Percy stared at the ceiling.

"That's what I get for trying to help."

Clarisse had already returned to the cupboard, but he could see her smiling.

They finished counting bandages before lights-out. When the final lamp was turned down, the new schedule was back on the door with Percy's morning water check written in one column and an empty slot beside the afternoon patrol.

It wasn't a perfect plan. It would do until they spoke to Chiron.

Percy got into bed with aching hands and sawdust still caught in the cuff of his shirt. Across the room Silena put away her ink, and Clarisse set her newly repaired greave beside her trunk where she wouldn't trip over it in the dark.

He remembered her instruction from their patrol: show up when you say you will.

Tomorrow he would. The sisters had already given him more than enough chances to practice.
---

Clarisse intercepted Percy before breakfast the next morning, one greave unfastened and her sword hanging loose at her side.

"Come on," she said.

"Where?"

"Hall. Nico found something. Chiron's there."

They ran uphill. A few campers were already gathered outside the Hall of the Gods. A post had been knocked out of the border fence, and a streak of dark residue ran from the broken earth to the Hall's eastern entrance. Two columns were scored along their lower edges. The roof stood. Through the doors Percy could see workers setting canvas over a damaged section of mosaic.

Chiron was questioning a pair of guards.

"A drakon crossed the boundary," he said when he saw them. "Not the one we fought last week. Smaller, but fast. We drove it away. It reached the colonnade before it vanished into the trees."

"What was it after?" Annabeth asked.

"We don't know," Chiron said. "The west store room was forced open, and some building records were scattered. Until we've counted them, I won't say anything is missing."

Nico knelt beside a bronze fixture wrenched from the wall. He had been comparing the screws with a drawing in his ledger.

"This was loosened before the creature hit it," he said. "Look at the threading."

Clarisse crouched. "Maybe the workers were repairing it."

"Maybe," Nico agreed. "But it was supposed to hold a ward. If it was already loose, the drakon didn't have to break much to get inside."

Percy thought of the old prophecy pages and the signed note. He wanted the scattered papers to amount to a single answer. Nico was being more careful than that.

"Whoever altered the Hall's rules had authority to do it," Nico said. "I've got a name on a purchase order."

"Whose?" Silena asked.

"Zeus's. Or rather, his steward's. He authorized the new fixtures. I can't tell whether the repair happened afterward with his knowledge."

Thalia stood a few steps away, spear in hand. "Say what you mean."

Nico looked up. "I mean we shouldn't pretend a signature on an order proves someone sent a monster. It tells us who paid for the work. That's all."

"Good," Thalia said. "Then we won't be inventing accusations before breakfast."

She was angry anyway. Percy could see it in the way she held her spear, but he couldn't tell whether the anger was at the implications or at being forced to hear them in front of an audience.

Chiron ordered the Hall closed until the wards could be checked. The younger campers were sent back to the pavilion. Clarisse volunteered to help remove broken stones; Silena offered to catalogue damaged records. Percy and Annabeth stayed long enough to copy the serial number from the fixture and the name on the repair sheet.

By the time they were allowed to leave, the Hall still stood, and they had a handful of questions, no proof of who had altered their history, and work assignments before lunch.

Thalia passed them without speaking. Annabeth watched her go, then followed her toward Cabin One. Percy let her. Some conversations weren't improved by having him there.
