## Chapter 103: The Things We Remember

Annabeth began the letter before Percy found it because she had run out of reasons to put it off.

The first attempt was on the back of a supply list. It lasted until the word *always*, which she crossed out so hard the paper tore. The second was in her notebook, between a sketch of the Hades shrine and a set of measurements she had already checked twice. That one sounded like a report. She wrote a third on a clean page and got as far as *I know what I remember* before she stopped.

The problem wasn't that she didn't know what to say. She knew too much. She could list the first time Percy made her laugh, and the first time he made her furious, and the many years between those two facts. There were memories she wanted to keep close and memories she could not bear to hand over in neat sentences. She could write that he had held her in a cave while the world tried to swallow them. She could write that she knew how he sounded when he was pretending to be brave. She could write that she had loved him long enough for the word to feel less like a decision than a fact.

But none of that was new. Percy remembered it too. A letter that only proved they shared a past would not answer the question that had begun to bother her: what did she choose when she wasn't leaning on the past?

She shut the notebook. The library window rattled once as a gust struck the shutters. Someone had left a candle burning on the far table, and the flame bent sideways before steadying. Annabeth watched it until she was sure it would not catch the papers nearby. She had practiced that kind of attention since her powers had started answering too quickly. Notice the first change. Stop before the second. Don't mistake control for certainty.

The letter did not need to prove anything. It could be small and still be true.

She started again on a clean sheet.

The library door opened behind her. She covered the page with her hand before looking up. Thalia stood in the doorway with a rolled camp map tucked beneath her arm.

“You hiding from the construction crew?” Thalia asked.

“I'm working.”

“That explains the books. I thought you might be planning their deaths.”

“The books are innocent.”

“And the crew?”

“They keep measuring the shrine from the wrong wall.”

“So you're considering it.”

Annabeth gave her a look. Thalia moved farther into the room, stopped when she saw the paper beneath Annabeth's palm, and turned her attention to the map instead.

“I need to check whether the west path is still clear,” she said. “Chiron wants the older campers to stop leaving boards in the grass.”

“You can take the path by the forge. They've moved most of it.”

“Most?”

“One stack is still blocking the turn.”

“I'll tell them.” Thalia glanced at the covered page, not at Annabeth's face. “You don't have to show me.”

Annabeth had not realized she was bracing for the question until it did not come. “I wasn't going to.”

“I know.”

There was no hurt in it. That made Annabeth look up.

Thalia tapped the map against her leg. “You're allowed to write Percy a letter without making it a family meeting.”

“I know that too.”

“Good. I was trying to say something supportive, but that sounded like a lecture.”

“It did.”

“I'll keep practicing.”

Thalia started to leave, then stopped at the door. “Do you need the library to yourself?”

Annabeth looked at the blank space on the page. “No. I just need time.”

“All right.” Thalia did not ask how much. She went back outside and let the door close behind her.

Annabeth uncovered the page. The draft had one new mark on it: a faint crease where her fingers had pressed against the paper. She smoothed it with the side of her hand, then began again.

She wrote about the boy who had brought her a pencil without asking her to explain why she needed one. She wrote about how he listened when she talked about buildings, and how he looked at her when a gust moved the leaves as if the wind had not made the choice for her. She wrote that she liked the way he asked questions and waited for answers.

When she got to the remembered life, she stopped. There were things she could tell him that no one else could understand, but she did not need to put all of them on the page. She didn't want the letter to become a list of proof. She wanted to say one thing that was hers now.

*I want to keep choosing you.*

The line made her stomach tighten. She held the pen still and let the feeling pass without crossing anything out.

The next morning, she copied the letter onto a fresh page. She folded it twice and pressed the crease flat with her thumbnail. A little blue wax from the shrine supplies was soft from the sun on the windowsill. She did not need to seal it, but she liked making the letter feel finished. The stamp left a crooked trident in the wax, its middle point longer than the other two.

She carried it to the library and waited until Percy left his book open on the table. The Greek temple diagrams were spread in front of him; he was comparing two different roof plans and pretending he understood why the second one had four more columns than the first. She tucked the letter between the pages he had marked, careful not to cover his notes.

Then she left before he came back.

She had not told him to read it alone. She hoped he would choose to.

---

The next morning, Percy noticed the folded letter where Annabeth had left it.

He recognized the handwriting before he saw the name. Annabeth had written it at the library the day before, then tucked it into his book when he wasn't looking. It rested between chapters on Greek temple roofs. He carried it around for most of the morning without opening it.

Not because he was afraid of what it said. Because he liked knowing she had written something just for him.

He finally read it after training, alone beneath the cottonwood tree by the canoe lake.

*Percy,*

*I keep thinking about how strange it is to remember loving you in one life and choosing you again in this one. I know I loved you. I remember being that girl. And I also know the boy who brought me an extra pencil because mine broke, and who pretended not to notice when I was scared of the lightning drill. Both are real. Maybe that is enough.*

*I like you now. I like how you listen when I talk about buildings, even when I get carried away. I like that you don't treat my powers like they make me scary. I like that you ask before you assume. And I like your stupid jokes, even though I refuse to laugh at most of them.*

*I remember what we were before. I know what I want now. I want to keep choosing you. That's all I can promise, and I think it's enough.*

*Your favorite person,*

*Annabeth*

Percy read it once, then again, then a third time. He folded it along the same creases and put it carefully in his pocket.

He didn't need to answer right away. He wanted to answer right. That was different.

He kept the book open on his knee for another minute, though the diagrams had stopped making sense. Around him the lake moved against the shore in quiet, uneven taps. A canoe knocked its rope against the dock. Someone was calling for a missing sandal on the path behind the trees. The camp had not paused for the letter; Percy was glad. If everyone stopped to look at him, he might have started acting as if he had been handed a prophecy.

Annabeth's words were not a prophecy. They were an offer. He could take it seriously without treating it as a guarantee that nothing would ever change.

He pressed his thumb against the fold. It was important that the page belonged to her, not to the version of them who had survived everything. That old version mattered. He would not lie about it. He also did not want to answer her by turning their years together into a claim on her future.

He thought of the library that morning. Annabeth had leaned over a diagram and pointed to a roofline, explaining how a mistake in the angle would send rain straight into the entrance. Percy had been tired and had asked if the builders could just add a really big umbrella. She had told him the umbrella would have to be structurally sound. He had said he could probably find one. She had shaken her head, but she had stayed beside him and kept explaining.

The memory was ordinary, which was why he trusted it. There had been no danger to push them together, no desperate promise, no terrifying choice. He had liked her there, at a table with bad diagrams, because she was Annabeth: impatient with sloppy work, generous with what she knew, and willing to start over if the first explanation didn't land.

He folded the letter and slid it into the inside pocket of his jacket. Then he closed the book and stood.

He had a training block before lunch. He was supposed to help carry practice spears back to the rack. He could do those things before he wrote anything.

That was not avoiding the letter. At least, he thought it wasn't.

---

Silena found him at the lake, still holding the folded page.

"That from Annabeth?" she asked.

"Maybe."

"You're smiling at it. That's not subtle."

Percy folded the letter smaller and tucked it away. "How do you know what to write back?"

Silena sat beside him. "You write what's true. You don't try to make it sound like a poem. That's how you end up with a poem nobody believes."

"I was going to write a poem."

"Then don't." She nudged his shoulder. "Write a letter. Tell her what you like about her now. Not what you remember."

That was what Percy wanted to do. It was also what frightened him. Percy had loved Annabeth for years, in the life they both remembered. He was thirteen and learning how to love her in a world where she had a different childhood and a different power and a sister who watched him like a hawk.

"What if I get it wrong?"

Silena's smile softened. "Then you tell her you got it wrong. That's what letters are for. You can try again."

Percy nodded. He could do that.

“You say that like you're deciding to jump off a roof,” Silena said.

“I'm deciding to write a letter.”

“Which is much more dangerous. The roof has rules.”

“I thought you were the one who said to be honest.”

“I did. I didn't say it would be easy.” Silena leaned back on her hands and looked across the lake. “You want to tell her something she doesn't already know.”

“I want her to know I mean it.”

“She probably knows that.”

“Probably isn't the same as saying it.”

“No. It isn't.” Silena picked up a dry leaf and turned it over between her fingers. “But if you tell her you like who she's becoming, make sure you mean Annabeth as she is. Don't make her carry the job of being proof that the old life mattered.”

Percy watched the leaf turn in Silena's hands. “I don't think I do that.”

“You might not. I'm saying it because it's easy to do without noticing.”

He took that seriously. There were things he wanted to remember exactly. There were other things he didn't want to force into every conversation. He could miss the person they had been without asking this Annabeth to stand in for her.

“I like her now,” he said. “I don't want to make it sound like she's on trial.”

“Then don't.” Silena flicked the leaf toward the water. It caught on the grass instead. She stood to retrieve it. “And don't let me write your letter for you.”

“You gave me advice.”

“Advice isn't a draft.”

“What if I want a draft?”

“Then you can write it yourself and blame the bad parts on me.”

They walked back toward the cabins. The path was busy with campers coming from the arena, carrying shields and water jugs. A boy from the Hephaestus cabin rolled a wheelbarrow full of short planks past them. One board slipped, bounced once in the dust, and skidded toward the path. Percy caught it with his shoe before it hit Silena's ankle.

“Thanks,” the boy called.

Percy handed it back. The board was heavier than it looked, and its rough edge snagged his palm. He rubbed the spot once, then dropped his hand. Silena had already turned toward the pavilion, where two younger campers were trying to balance a tray between them.

“You can head back,” she said. “I told Clarisse I'd help count the practice gear before dinner.”

“Want me to come?”

“No. She needs me to check the wraps and make a list. She'll complain if you try to help and then tell you to leave.”

“She complains when I don't help too.”

“That's because she's your sister. It doesn't mean every complaint is an invitation.”

Percy considered that. Silena gave him a small wave and went toward the armory instead of the pavilion. He was glad she had somewhere she wanted to be. He did not need the advice to turn into a whole afternoon together.

He returned to Cabin Three long enough to wash the dirt off his palm. He took out the letter and read the last paragraph again, then put it away. The camp jacket was on its hook, and the inside pocket was deep enough that the folded page didn't press against his ribs when he moved. He decided that was a good place for it until he had an answer.

At lunch he sat with the others and answered questions about the empty shrine shelves. Annabeth asked whether the boards for the second shelf had arrived. Percy told her he'd seen a cart near the forge but hadn't checked what was on it. She made a note on the margin of her sketch, then passed him the bowl of peaches.

“That was the last one,” she said.

He looked down at the fruit. “You can have it.”

“I have one.”

“Then you can keep yours and I'll eat this one.”

“That's not how offering works.”

“It's how fruit works.”

She rolled her eyes and took the peach back. Her fingertips brushed his. She did not look at his jacket pocket. Neither did he. They talked about the shrine's dimensions and whether the mosaic could be laid over the uneven stone at the entrance. He asked if a border would make the pattern easier to repair. She drew a quick line, measured it against the scale, and said she'd have to check.

He liked the way she said that. She didn't pretend to know what she hadn't tested. She didn't mind changing a plan when the evidence changed. He had admired that in her for years, but this was a new example and a new afternoon. It belonged here.

After lunch, they split to their duties. Percy took the spears to the rack. Annabeth went to check the boards. Neither of them made the letter into a secret that everyone had to guard. It was private because she had written it to him, and he would decide when to answer.

---

He found a quiet spot by the forge and started writing.

He had borrowed a small table near the outer wall, where the forge's heat did not reach as strongly. Someone had left a tin of nails under the bench. A length of bronze wire curled beside it, and the air carried the smell of coal and warm iron. He chose that place because he could hear the hammering without having to talk over it. The sound gave him somewhere to put his attention when the words stopped.

The first page stayed blank for a long time.

He tried writing the first sentence in the way he remembered doing it in the older life: too fast, convinced that if he didn't put down every thought at once, one of them would disappear. That method had worked badly then too. The ink blotted beneath his hand. He blotted it again with a scrap of cloth and left a dark smear across the corner.

He wrote *I remember you* and stared at it.

That was true. It was also not enough. He remembered her in a world where she had grown up beneath a different cabin roof, with different people, with a history that belonged to both of them. This Annabeth had grown up with Thalia. She had learned to measure rooms in the Grace cabin. She had let Thalia help her build the parts of a life she could not remember. He could not honestly say he knew all of her. Loving her did not make him an expert on the child she had been.

He crossed out the line.

The second attempt began, *There are things I love about you that have nothing to do with before.* That was close, but the phrase made the past sound like a burden he wanted to throw away. He didn't. They were allowed to keep the old things. The question was what else they could add.

He scratched through *nothing* and wrote *more than* above it. Then he crossed out the whole sentence.

From inside the forge came a sharp metallic clank, followed by a curse and a laugh. Percy looked toward the open doorway. A camper was trying to lift a bent hinge into position while another held it with tongs. The hinge slipped. They both backed away before it hit the floor.

“That was supposed to be the easy part,” one of them said.

“It is the easy part.”

“Then I'm in trouble.”

The older camper set the hinge aside and checked it for cracks. Nobody made a speech about the mistake. They found another piece and started again.

Percy looked at the paper. Maybe that was what he needed: not a sentence proving he understood the whole situation, just a sentence he could stand behind without adding a footnote.

He wrote about the map, the breakfast table, and the way Annabeth's questions had changed. In the old life, she already knew what he would leave unsaid. Here, she asked because she wanted to know. He liked that she did not pretend they could skip the awkward parts just because they had a history. He liked that she could remember loving him and still tell him when he was being annoying.

That last thought sounded more like her, and he kept it.

Then he reached the line that frightened him. *I want to keep choosing you.*

He could write it back. It was what he felt. But she had called it her promise, and returning the same words felt like copying homework. He wanted to answer her, not echo her.

He set down the pen. The pages had begun to pile up, and he kept every one. The crossed-out drafts weren't mistakes he needed to hide from her. They were proof that he had tried to say the truth without making it grander than it was. Still, he did not want to hand her a stack of his uncertainty and ask her to sort it out for him.

He selected one clean sheet. He could hear the practice field beyond the forge, where the afternoon session was ending. Armor clattered into bins. Someone called for a healer. A conch sounded the end of the block.

He would write later, when he had finished the work he had promised to do.

He did finish it. The next morning, he went to the shrine with Annabeth to check the shelf boards. He carried the shorter pieces, and she checked their lengths against the drawing. One was too warped to use. Percy tried to set it aside where it wouldn't roll; Annabeth caught the other end before it hit the ground.

“That's not going to straighten,” she said.

“Could we brace it?”

“We could. Then we'd have to brace the brace.”

“A brace for the brace. Sounds sturdy.”

“It sounds like a lot of work to avoid admitting the board is bad.”

He let go. She placed the warped board on the discard stack and made a note of the measurement. They marked the edges that would need sanding, then carried the usable pieces toward the workbench.

At the end of the task, she wiped sawdust from her fingers on a rag. There was a faint red mark where the pencil had rested against her thumb. Percy wanted to ask whether it hurt. He knew that was not a serious injury; she had barely noticed it herself. So he held out the rag instead.

“Thanks.” She took it and wiped her hands. “You're good at carrying things without taking over.”

“I have a lot of practice carrying things.”

“That isn't what I meant.”

“I know.”

She looked at him, and the pause held something he could not quite name. He wanted to touch her hand. He did not. There was no urgency. They had the rest of the morning to work side by side, and she had not asked him to turn the moment into something else.

“We can do the mosaic test after lunch,” she said.

“I'll be there.”

“You don't have to come if you have something else to do.”

“I want to.”

“Okay.” She returned to the plan. “Then bring the chalk. I keep losing mine.”

It was a small request, but it was new. Percy made sure to remember it.

He found chalk in the supply box and wrote her name on the paper wrapper, then hesitated. That would make the chalk seem like a gift. She had not asked for a gift; she had asked him to bring a tool. He crossed out her name and wrote *shrine test* instead.

The distinction amused him. He left the wrapper on the bench and went to lunch.

He could have given her the letter then. It was inside his jacket, folded once more along the edge. He felt its presence every time he reached for anything. At lunch, Annabeth sat across from him and asked Silena if the dining pavilion had ordered more bowls. Silena said she would check after sorting the pantry list. Clarisse was in a hurry to get to the arena and reached over Percy's plate for the last piece of bread. He moved it away. She took it anyway.

“You could ask,” he said.

“I could.” She bit into the bread. “I didn't.”

“That's not how manners work.”

“Manners are for people who want the last piece.”

“You clearly wanted it.”

“Exactly.”

Silena nudged the breadbasket toward Percy. “There are more in the kitchen.”

“I'll get them,” he said.

“You don't have to.”

“I know. I want to.”

He stood before Clarisse could claim the basket too. In the kitchen, the smell of toasted grain was stronger. He asked the cook for a tray and carried it out carefully. On the way, he passed a bench where Noah and a younger camper were looking at the training schedule.

“Do you know if the lower field is open after dinner?” Noah asked.

“I think so. Clarisse said she'd be there, but I don't know how long.”

“Okay. Thanks.” Noah glanced down at the list. “I'll ask her.”

Percy kept walking. He did not feel the old pinch of attention when Noah said Annabeth's name a moment later. He heard the boy ask about the shrine and how the shelf was coming. Annabeth described the boards and the bad measurements with the same focus she had given Percy. Noah listened. Percy put the bread on the table and sat beside her.

“The chalk wrapper says shrine test,” he said.

Annabeth looked at it. “Good. I don't want to lose it again.”

“No one is going to mistake it for a personal inscription.”

“I wasn't worried about that.”

“I know.”

Her mouth moved like she almost smiled. He passed her the breadbasket. She took a piece and continued explaining the shelf plan to Noah. Percy listened and added what he could. There was no need to be the only person she talked to. He had been trying to learn that, and today it felt less like a rule and more like a thing that was true.

After lunch they tested the chalk line on the shrine floor. It did not show well on the dark stone, so Annabeth changed the plan and used a piece of white string. They spent an hour trying different placements for the mosaic's center. Percy held one end while she moved the other. When she needed both hands to measure, he pinned the string with his shoe.

“A little left,” she said.

He shifted.

“Your other left.”

“I have two?”

“Apparently.”

They both stepped back. The line crossed the floor at an angle that made the pattern look wrong.

“The wall isn't square,” Annabeth said.

“Is that fixable?”

“The wall? Probably not without making it worse. The pattern, yes.” She rubbed the chalk dust from her palm. “We can anchor it to the entrance instead. It'll be slightly off-center, but it will feel deliberate.”

“Like a crooked trident.”

“Exactly like a crooked trident.”

She looked at him. “Do you still have the letter?”

He went still. She had not asked about it before.

“Yes,” he said. “I haven't answered yet.”

“You don't have to.”

“I want to.”

“Then you can.” She crouched to gather the string. “You don't need to rush because I sent it.”

“I know.”

“I don't want you to write something just because you think you owe me one.”

“I don't.”

She looped the string around her fingers, careful not to tangle it. “Okay.”

They cleaned up the chalk and string. Annabeth carried the sketchbook back to Cabin One. Percy put the tools away, then walked to the forge with the jacket pocket pressing against his side.

He had the words. Now he had to decide how to give them to her.

The first draft was terrible. It was full of grand statements about fate and memory and the Fates themselves. He crossed it out before he finished the second paragraph.

The second draft was worse. It rhymed. Percy didn't know why he kept trying to make it rhyme. Maybe because poetry felt like something you were supposed to give someone you loved. He tore the page in half.

The third draft began: *Dear Annabeth, I am writing this because Silena says I should write what is true.*

That seemed honest enough.

He wrote about the time she'd corrected his map of camp, then apologized when she realized he'd drawn it from memory rather than from the orientation map. He wrote about how she made room for him at the breakfast table even when the Poseidon table had empty seats. He wrote about the way she asked him questions about Atlantis as if his memories mattered, even when they didn't match the stories people expected to hear.

He wrote that he liked who she was becoming, not only who she'd been.

He stopped there. The sentence felt important. He read it over, thought about changing it, then decided not to.

At the bottom, he added: *I choose you too. Even if we have to learn each other twice.*

He folded the letter, sealed it with wax he'd borrowed from the forge, and wrote her name on the outside.

It took him two more days to deliver it.

The letter was short enough to fold into three uneven panels. He had copied it onto clean paper after the ink on the first draft bled through the back. The final version did not explain every thought he had while writing. It said that he liked the way she listened to a different answer than she expected. It said he liked how she showed him a sketch and asked what he thought, even when she could already see what needed changing. It said he liked her stubbornness when it was aimed at a problem and her patience when it was aimed at him.

He wrote that they could remember the older life without asking it to decide what came next. He wrote that he did not want her to feel she had to be the same girl in order to be loved by him. He stopped before listing all the things he remembered. Some were his to carry quietly; some were theirs to talk about together when they wanted. The page did not need to hold everything.

At the bottom, he wrote: *I choose you too. Even if we have to learn each other twice.*

He read it once. Then he folded the paper and pressed the crease with the blunt edge of the pen. He did not seal it with the wax stamp. Annabeth had used the blue wax because she wanted to; he did not need to match it. He borrowed a small envelope from the Big House office and wrote her name in the center.

For two days he carried it in his jacket. Every time he saw Annabeth, he thought of giving it to her. Every time he did, something ordinary got in the way: a toolbox she needed carried, a question from Clarisse, a task Chiron had asked him to finish, or simply the fact that she was talking to someone else and he didn't want to interrupt. He told himself he was waiting for the right moment. He also knew he was afraid of watching her read it.

On the second evening, Silena caught him checking the pocket before he went to dinner.

“You still have it.”

“I do.”

“Do you want me to tell you to stop carrying it?”

“Would that help?”

“Probably not.”

“Then don't.”

“Okay.” She tied an apron around her waist and glanced toward the dining pavilion. “I need to finish the pantry count before the cooks decide I promised to do it tomorrow.”

“You can leave it. I'll finish the last shelf.”

“I don't want you to.”

Percy blinked. She tugged the apron strings into a knot. “You have enough to do. I asked because I wanted to know if you'd seen the extra jars, not because I need you to take over.”

“Right. Sorry.”

“It's fine.” She pushed a basket toward him, then took it back. “Actually, no. I can carry it.”

He let go. Silena had a way of making kindness feel like something you chose, not something you owed. He had forgotten how often he tried to convert a person's trust into a job for himself.

“I'll see you at dinner,” he said.

“If you don't bring that letter to her, I'll see you at breakfast too.”

“That's not a threat.”

“It's a schedule.”

He left her to the pantry. At dinner he watched Annabeth laugh at something Thalia said, then stopped himself from counting how long she looked at him afterward. They had already talked about being together. Noah had asked; they had answered. Nobody needed to repeat the declaration every time Annabeth smiled at someone else.

When the meal ended, Percy waited while the pavilion emptied. Annabeth stayed to help stack cups. He could have gone to her side and passed the letter across the table. Instead, he helped carry the last tray to the wash station. She stood at the basin with her sleeves rolled up, rinsing a cup before handing it to the dishwasher.

“You don't have to stay,” she said.

“I know. I want to.”

“You keep saying that today.”

“It's been a very true day.”

She set the cup on the drying rack. “That sounds like something you would write.”

“Maybe.”

“Maybe?”

“I haven't decided.”

She looked at him, waiting. He took the envelope from his pocket. “I have an answer for you.”

Annabeth's hands paused over the basin. “You finished it.”

“I did.”

“Do you want me to read it now?”

He looked around the kitchen. The dishwasher was stacking plates on a cart; one of the nymphs had returned to collect a pitcher. There was no crowd, exactly, but it was not private. He did not want to make her answer in front of anyone. He also didn't want to take the choice away.

“You can read it whenever you want,” he said. “Or not right now.”

“I'll take it.” She dried her hands on a towel and held them out. “I want to read it by myself first.”

“Okay.”

He gave it to her. She slid the envelope into the pocket of her sketchbook case. Neither of them tried to make the handoff into a ceremony. Then she picked up the next cup and turned back to the sink.

“You can go,” she said.

“You sure?”

“Yes. I want a little time with it.”

He nodded. “I'll see you tomorrow.”

“You will.”

He left the kitchen without asking whether she liked the letter. She had not read it yet, and he did not need to know her reaction before she was ready to give it.

At the path, he nearly turned back. He had left his jacket on the bench beside the wash station. For a second, he considered using it as an excuse to check whether Annabeth had opened the envelope. Then he saw the jacket was still over his arm. He had been carrying it the whole time.

He could have walked back anyway. He kept going.

The late light stretched across the grass. A pair of campers were practicing throws with a knotted rope, arguing over whose turn it was to retrieve it. Percy joined the next round when they asked. He missed the first catch and got the second. By the time the game ended, he had stopped wondering whether Annabeth was reading the letter yet. He knew she would come find him if she wanted to talk. If she needed more time, that was hers too.

He expected to feel lighter once the letter was out of his pocket. Instead, the space there felt conspicuous. He kept reaching for the folded page before remembering that he had handed it over. He went to the lake and sat at the end of the dock, where the boards were warm from the day's sun. A canoe drifted on its line. The little waves nudged it toward the post and let it go again.

He tried to picture what Annabeth might read in his words. That was a mistake. He knew the letter; she would get to have her own reaction to it. He couldn't edit the feeling from here.

The water beneath the dock was clear enough to show pebbles at the edge. He dipped his fingers in and moved them slowly. A ripple spread out, hit the canoe, and sent it knocking softly against its line.

“You can stop asking the lake to answer,” Clarisse said from the path.

Percy pulled his hand out. “I wasn't.”

“You were looking at it like it owed you money.”

She dropped onto the dock beside him, leaving a careful space between them. Her hair was tied back from her face, and there was a chalk smear on the side of her shorts. She smelled faintly of leather and the metal oil used on the practice shields.

“How was training?” he asked.

“Fine.”

“That's it?”

“Noah improved his turn. His foot keeps sliding, but he noticed before I had to say anything.”

“That's good.”

“It is. He asked me whether he could keep practicing after dinner. I told him not tonight.”

“Why?”

“Because he was tired and the field was getting dark. He made a face about it.”

“Did you make one back?”

“Maybe.”

She leaned her elbows on her knees. She had been making room for Noah in the small ways she'd been taught: showing him the field, correcting his stance, telling him when to stop. She did not have to like every new camper. She had decided to teach him anyway.

“You're thinking about Annabeth,” she said.

“I gave her the letter.”

Clarisse made a sound that might have been approval. “Good.”

“That's all?”

“What do you want, a medal?”

“No.”

“Then that's all.”

He smiled despite himself. “You can say something nice.”

“I can. I don't have to.”

“You and Silena gave me advice.”

“Silena gave you advice. I told you to stop bending forks.”

“That was advice.”

“Then you already got some.”

Clarisse pushed herself up from the dock. She brushed dust from her hands and headed back toward the path. After a few steps she turned around.

“Don't spend all night guessing what she thinks,” she said. “If she wants you to know, she'll tell you.”

“Okay.”

“And if she says the letter was bad, write a better one.”

“That's a lot of confidence in my writing.”

“I have confidence in your ability to try again.”

Then she was gone. Percy watched the canoe bump its post. His sisters did not always know what to say. They did know when he was trying to make a simple thing impossible.

He walked back to Cabin Three. The jacket pocket was empty, and he let it stay that way.

Annabeth didn't say anything at breakfast. She didn't say anything during training. She didn't say anything when Percy helped her move books from the Athena cabin's old storage room to the shrine's new shelves.

She had read his letter alone, sitting on the steps below Cabin One while Thalia was inside looking for her camp jacket. She did not read it quickly. She read the first paragraph, stopped, and looked out over the path. She came back to the paper and read the next one. Then she folded it, held it against her knee, and waited until she could read it without trying to decide whether the words were enough.

They were not a contract. That was a relief. Percy did not tell her that the years they remembered made her choices inevitable. He did not ask her to prove she still loved him. He had written about the way she asked questions, and the way she changed her mind when a plan did not work. He had noticed the life she was building around him, instead of treating it as an extension of the one they remembered.

The phrase about learning each other twice caught at her. It was close to what she believed, but it did not erase the difficulty. There were days when the two lives sat beside each other like two different maps. One had roads she could follow blindfolded. The other had familiar people marked in a geography she had not lived. She did not want to choose one and throw the other away.

She had never expected Percy to tell her to. Still, it was good to see that he understood.

She tucked the letter between the pages of her sketchbook. Later, when she went to bring him the chalk wrapper, she took the letter out again and read the last line once more. She didn't need to answer it immediately. She wanted to answer with something more than another line that said she felt the same.

The shrine sketch was unfinished. She had drawn the outer wall and marked three possible patterns along the floor. She wanted to show him what she meant by a future they could make without pretending it was already planned. That was not the only reason she drew it. She liked drawing the shrine. She liked thinking about how a person might walk into it and feel the noise of camp fall away. She liked asking Percy what he saw, because his answers surprised her even when they were not useful.

So she kept sketching through the afternoon. She tried a border of tiny waves, then decided it was too much. She tried stars and made them too even. She filled the gap with vines instead, following the line of the stone as if the plants had begun growing there before anyone thought to ask them.

She left open space at the entrance. The floor would not be a picture of their old life. It would be an invitation for whoever came next to bring their own meaning with them.

When she tied the page with a strand of blue thread, the knot came out lopsided. She could have fixed it. She let it stay.

At breakfast she had the sketch in her case and Percy's letter inside the sketchbook. He had passed her the honey; she had reminded Thalia to check the rain gutter. No one had asked about the envelopes. Thalia sat across from her, pouring tea from a chipped pot.

“Did you sleep?” Thalia asked.

“Some.”

“That isn't a number.”

“It's the one I have.”

Thalia nodded as if she could accept that for now. “Chiron said the gutter might need another bracket.”

“It needs two.”

“You already checked?”

“Yesterday.”

“I thought you were fixing the shrine shelves.”

“I can do more than one thing.”

“I know.” Thalia set down the pot. “I wasn't telling you not to.”

Annabeth heard the apology in the adjustment, not the words. “I know.”

They spent the rest of breakfast discussing whether to ask someone from the Hephaestus cabin for help with the bracket. Annabeth wrote down the measurements and slid the note to Thalia. At the end of the meal, she gathered her sketchbook and went to find Percy.

Then, at dusk, she handed him a tiny rolled-up paper tied with blue thread.

He opened it. Inside was a sketch of the shrine — not the finished one, but an imagined version with vines growing up its sides and a mosaic floor patterned after the night sky.

At the bottom she'd written: *I want to see what we build next.*

Percy looked at her.

"I liked your letter," she said. "It was better than a poem."

"That's good. The poem was terrible."

"I know. I found it in the fire pit."

"You read it?"

"I did. I have questions about the rhyme scheme."

Percy groaned. Annabeth laughed, and then she reached up and touched his cheek with the backs of her fingers. The gesture was so gentle he forgot what he was about to say.

"I like who you're becoming too," she said.

Her thumb brushed his cheek once. Percy leaned into the touch, then stopped himself. He wanted to kiss her. He wanted to ask if she wanted that, but he was learning that not every quiet moment had to be turned into a question or a test.

Annabeth noticed anyway.

"You can ask," she said.

"Can I kiss you?"

"Yes."

He kissed her. It was a little longer than the one by the shrine, but still gentle. Annabeth's hand rested on his shoulder, steady and warm. When they parted, she stayed close, her forehead against his.

"That was a good question," she murmured.

"I can ask again next time."

"Please do."

Thalia found Percy's letter before anyone told her what it said.

She had gone into the Athena cabin to look for the compass Chiron had asked her to use on the west path. The compass was old, brass at the rim, and stubborn about settling if anyone stood too close to it. Annabeth usually kept it on the upper shelf with the shrine plans. Thalia remembered that because she had helped Annabeth sort the plans into piles. She had not asked what any of them meant.

The storage room smelled of cedar and dust. Someone had left a bundle of canvas against the wall. It blocked the lower drawers, and Thalia had to turn sideways to get past. She found a pair of broken tongs, three rolls of twine, and a stack of paper that was supposed to be blank. The compass was not there.

She moved the canvas. The fabric dragged along her forearm, catching on the seam of her sleeve. A folded note slipped from between two books and landed on the floor. She picked it up before it could slide beneath the shelf.

The handwriting was Percy's. Thalia knew it from the labels he put on the tool drawers, where he wrote too low on the line and sometimes left the last word leaning upward. The paper was folded in half, not sealed. One line showed where the crease had shifted: *I like who you're becoming.*

Thalia stopped reading there.

The page had been tucked between two books as a marker. It might have been a note about the shrine. It might have been a letter. Either way, it was private. She had already decided not to read the rest. This time she had to choose it again.

Thalia put the page back and checked the compass in the drawer. The needle was steady. She took it outside, then changed her mind and went to the arena. There were two practice shields left in the grass. She stacked them on the rack, checked the straps, and carried the one with a cracked rim to the equipment shed.

There was no reason she needed to keep busy. She knew that. Knowing did not make standing still feel natural.

She opened the shed and set the shield on the workbench. A loose rivet clicked against the metal edge. She tested the fastenings with her thumb, found the one that had shifted, and marked it with a piece of blue chalk. It would need a new rivet, not another turn of the screwdriver. She wrote that on the repair slate and hung the shield where nobody would use it by accident.

The work was small enough to finish and too small to matter. That helped. She could complete one thing without making it a promise to repair everything.

When she returned to Cabin One, Annabeth was still at the shrine. Thalia heard her and Percy arguing about the string's angle before she could see them.

“It is centered from the entrance,” Annabeth said.

“It looks like it's leaning.”

“Because the wall leans.”

“The wall has a strong opinion.”

“The wall is stone. It doesn't have opinions.”

“Then who made it crooked?”

“Someone who didn't have a level.”

“I could find you a level.”

“I have a level.”

Thalia walked around the wall and looked at the mark. The line was even, but it did look wrong at first glance. “What if you make the border wider at the entrance and let it narrow toward the back?”

Annabeth turned. “That's what I was thinking.”

“You didn't say.”

“I was testing it.”

“I know. I was trying to help.”

“You did.” Annabeth set the string down. “Sorry. I heard the suggestion like you were correcting me.”

Thalia could have told her that was fine. Instead, she said, “I was correcting the line. That's the whole point of looking at it together.”

Annabeth let out a breath. “Right.”

“You don't have to get it perfect on the first try.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Annabeth gave her a flat look. Thalia raised both hands.

“I know. That sounded like I was checking whether you knew.”

“It did.”

“I meant the line.”

“I know.”

They both looked at the wall. Percy had taken the chalk and was measuring the wider edge. “I can mark a second one,” he said. “Then you can decide which looks less like it's falling over.”

“Do that,” Annabeth said.

He marked the second line. Annabeth crouched to compare them, then stood and moved several feet away. “The second. It gives the entrance more room.”

Thalia nodded. “That's better.”

“It is.” Annabeth leaned over the drawing. “We should test the pattern with paper before we put anything down.”

“I'll cut the paper,” Thalia said.

“I can cut it.”

“I know. I can still help.”

Annabeth looked at her for a beat, then handed her the scissors. “All right. I'll mark the pieces.”

They worked at opposite ends of the table. Annabeth called out the measurements; Thalia cut rectangles from the scrap paper. Percy sorted them into piles. When he put one in the wrong stack, Annabeth corrected him. When he put a second one wrong, Thalia tapped the pile with her scissors.

“I'm helping,” Percy said.

“You are,” Annabeth told him. “That's why I'm correcting you.”

“I thought those were separate.”

“Not today.”

The paper pattern took shape on the floor. The wider border left enough room by the entrance and made the skewed wall seem intentional. Annabeth moved two pieces, then stopped and asked Thalia what she thought. Thalia said the corner should be less sharp. Annabeth adjusted it. Percy lay on the floor to see the pattern from the height of someone entering the shrine. He said the open center made the room feel larger.

“That's useful,” Annabeth said.

“I have useful thoughts sometimes.”

“I didn't say you didn't.”

Thalia caught the look they exchanged. They were comfortable with each other in the small ways people got comfortable: in the assumption that the next sentence would come, and the ease of disagreeing without turning every correction into a warning.

It pinched somewhere beneath her ribs. She let it. The feeling could be there while she kept cutting paper.

When they were done, Annabeth gathered the sketchbook and put it back in its case. Percy swept the floor. Thalia stacked the paper pieces and set them aside for another test. She did not mention the letters. Annabeth did not ask what she had been doing in the storage room.

She could have unfolded the page. Her fingers were already under the edge. No one would have known she had read it; she knew Annabeth's handwriting better than most people knew their own. The temptation was ordinary and ugly. It came from the same place as checking the door twice after Annabeth had gone to sleep, or watching the path when she was late coming back. Thalia had spent so long collecting details because details helped her keep her sister safe. Sometimes that habit kept going after the reason was gone.

She refolded the page on its existing crease. She set it between the books again, exactly where she had found it.

The compass turned up in the drawer below. Thalia closed the door and stood in the small storage room for a moment, listening to the cabin around her. A bunk creaked. Someone was rummaging in a trunk. The usual activity went on without needing her to oversee it.

She carried the compass outside and set it on the porch rail. The needle spun twice before stopping. The west path ran behind Cabin Nine and around the back of the forge. Chiron had asked her to check it because she knew where the old drainage line cut through the earth. She could do that. She could also stop turning every errand into a chance to inspect what Annabeth was doing.

She did not feel proud of herself for leaving the letter alone. It was a small decision, and she had made it too late to pretend she had never wanted to look. She did feel relieved. The line she'd seen was enough to tell her the letter was kind. It did not tell her whether Annabeth felt excited, scared, certain, or some mixture of all three. Those answers were Annabeth's to give.

Thalia took the map and walked the west path. The compass led her past the stacked boards, where the new campers had left a hammer on the grass. She picked it up and carried it to the tool box. At the drainage cut, she checked the ground for soft spots. Two stones had shifted from the edge. She pushed one back into place, then found that her palm still ached beneath the wrap from the earlier scrape.

She flexed her fingers. No sharp pain. The movement tugged at the bandage, and she stopped. The old instinct was to test it harder, prove it was fine, and be done with the question. She heard Will's voice in her head telling her that a clean bandage did not mean she could ignore it. She left it alone.

The compass needle drifted toward the forge. She had finished the path check and could go to the infirmary, or she could sit somewhere for five minutes without being useful. She chose the steps outside Cabin One. There was a patch of sun on the upper stair. She sat there with the map folded across her knees and the compass beside her boot.

The camp moved around her. A group of younger campers carried shields from the arena. Someone called for Noah to bring a water jug. A pair of Aphrodite campers were arguing over a ribbon that had tangled around a tree branch. Thalia heard each thing, recognized where she might be needed, and stayed on the step.

She thought about the line. *I like who you're becoming.*

The words sounded like permission and praise. She knew they were meant for Percy, not her, and that was fine. Annabeth had always wanted to be taken seriously. Even when Thalia was doing her best, she could slip into treating her sister's independence as a risk to manage instead of a choice to respect.

Thalia was happy Percy had noticed what Annabeth was becoming. She was less sure what she was becoming herself if nobody needed her to keep watch. The thought made her shift on the step. The map's edge pressed into her thumb.

She could say she wanted Annabeth to have a future. She did want that. But the future had started to look like a room where Thalia might not be asked to come in. She could not blame Annabeth for the feeling. It belonged to Thalia, and she would have to decide what to do with it.

The cabin door opened. A younger camper came out carrying a pair of boots and a bundle of damp socks.

“Do you know if the laundry line is full?” the girl asked.

Thalia looked toward the trees. “The south one has space. The north one snapped yesterday.”

“Thanks.”

The girl walked off. Thalia almost called after her to bring the socks back inside if they started to rain. She closed her mouth. The sky was clear. The girl knew how a clothesline worked.

Annabeth crossed the path with her sketchbook tucked under her arm. She had not seen Thalia yet. Her face was turned toward the shrine, where Percy was carrying a box of tools. They exchanged a few words, and Annabeth pointed out something on the drawing. Percy leaned over to look. He did not touch her or interrupt. He followed the line she indicated.

Thalia watched only long enough to see Annabeth explain and Percy listen. Then she looked down at the compass.

It was not a threat. It was not a crisis. It was two people working on a building.

She wanted to be able to see that and let it remain simple.

Annabeth came up the steps a few minutes later. She had a smudge of chalk on her sleeve and a piece of string hanging from the sketchbook.

“Did you find the compass?”

“I did. It's temperamental.”

“It always was.”

“I checked the west path too. The stones at the drainage cut need another look tomorrow.”

“I'll go with you.”

Thalia looked at her. “You don't have to.”

“I know. I want to.”

The answer was familiar and still new. Annabeth could want to come because she was interested in the drainage cut, or because she wanted time with Thalia, or because she did not want Thalia to think she was leaving. Thalia did not need to make her clarify.

“All right,” she said. “Bring a pair of gloves. The stone edge is rough.”

“I will.”

Annabeth glanced at the map. “Did you see the new sketch?”

“Not yet.”

“I can show you later.”

“I'd like that.”

She set the sketchbook on the rail but did not open it. “I was going to ask if you wanted to come see what Percy and I found for the mosaic. It's crooked.”

“The floor?”

“The wall. The pattern can still work, but not the way I drew it.”

“Do you want help fixing it?”

“I want your opinion. You don't have to fix it.”

“I can do that.”

“Good.” Annabeth took the map from Thalia's knees and tapped the west path with one finger. “This should be clear after we move the boards.”

“Probably.”

“Probably isn't a number.”

Thalia gave her a look. Annabeth's mouth tilted at one corner. She had borrowed Thalia's line from breakfast, or at least the shape of it. Thalia let herself smile.

They went to look at the shrine. Annabeth showed her the altered plan: the entrance would anchor the mosaic rather than the center of the room. The shift would make one side wider than the other. Thalia walked the floor and imagined where a visitor would stand. She pointed out that a wider border near the door would give people somewhere to stop without blocking the path.

Annabeth marked it down. “That's better.”

“It was your idea.”

“It was my problem. You found the useful part.”

Percy brought the box of tools over and set it down. “Can I see?”

Annabeth showed him the new line. He moved to the opposite wall and looked back at the entrance. “It feels like you walk into the picture instead of looking at it from the door.”

Annabeth glanced at Thalia. “That's what I wanted.”

“Then we should keep this one,” Thalia said.

For a little while they worked out the measurements together. Thalia held the string, Percy marked the edge, and Annabeth adjusted the pattern. No one tried to turn it into a lesson about who knew her best. When they finished, Annabeth said she wanted to add some vines and a sky pattern. She did not explain what either meant. Thalia did not ask.

They packed the tools away and walked back to Cabin One. Annabeth carried her sketchbook. Thalia kept the compass. They talked about the rain gutter and the best time to check the path the next day. The letter remained folded in the storage room, private and unread.

She was pleased for Annabeth. She was. Percy was kind to her, listened to her, and never acted as if her powers or her complicated memories made her difficult to love. He was also a good kid with a complicated family and an old sadness he did not always talk about.

Thalia trusted him more than she had expected to.

The difficult part was watching Annabeth choose a future Thalia couldn't protect her from. A relationship was a kind of growing up. It meant Annabeth was making a life that didn't revolve around her sister. Thalia knew that was healthy. She knew it was supposed to happen.

Knowing didn't mean it never hurt.

Annabeth came out of the cabin carrying her sketchbook. "You all right?"

"I'm fine."

Annabeth raised an eyebrow.

Thalia sighed. "I'm happy for you. And I'm getting used to that being complicated."

"You don't have to be happy every second."

"I know. I can be happy and a little sad at the same time. Apparently that's legal."

Annabeth sat beside her. "I don't want you to think I'm leaving you behind."

"I don't." Thalia bumped her shoulder. "You're allowed to have other people. You're allowed to like them in whatever way makes sense to you. Just keep talking to me."

"I will."

"And if Percy starts getting smug, tell me. I have a slingshot and a lot of spare acorns."

Annabeth laughed. "I'll keep that in mind."

Thalia's smile became real. She was learning that loving her sister didn't mean holding on so tightly Annabeth couldn't move. It meant walking beside her when she did.

---

Percy kept Annabeth's letter in the inside pocket of his camp jacket. It was folded along its original creases, soft at the corners from being reread.

Sometimes he took it out when he was homesick for Atlantis. Sometimes when he missed Sally. Sometimes when the memories of the older life made him feel as if he were standing in two places at once.

The letter didn't fix anything. It didn't need to.

It reminded him that Annabeth was here now. That she was choosing him in this life, with the memories she had and the person she'd become.

That was enough to make the future feel less like a memory and more like a place they might reach together.
