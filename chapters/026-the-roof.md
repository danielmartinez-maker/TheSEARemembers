## Chapter 26: The Roof


Annabeth Grace knew seven ways out of the Zeus cabin after curfew without creaking a single bunk.

She'd counted them on her first night in Cabin One, back when the hunters' moon was still high and Thalia was snoring in the bunk across from hers with one arm thrown over her eyes and sparks crawling faintly along her bracelet. Annabeth had lain awake listening to the unfamiliar thunder-hum that lived in the walls now — different from the low owl-quiet of the Athena cabin she still dreamed about — and she'd mapped exits the way other kids counted sheep.

Window latch. Back corner where the wall boards didn't quite meet the foundation. Front door if you lifted before you pulled. She'd tested all of them by the end of the first week. Not to break rules. Just to know.

Tonight she used the window.

Percy was waiting behind the tool shed where she'd told him to meet her, exactly where she'd drawn an X on the little map she'd slipped into his copy of *The Odyssey* at dinner. He was wearing his camp hoodie over pajamas, his hair sticking up in the back, his sneakers untied. When he saw her, his whole face did that brightening thing it did, like someone had turned a lamp on inside him.

"You came," Annabeth said, unnecessarily, and immediately felt silly.

"Of course I came," Percy said. "Your note said 'secret. Bring socks. Trust me.' How am I supposed to say no to that?"

"I said bring *warm* socks," Annabeth corrected, but she took his hand anyway. His fingers were cold. She laced hers through them without thinking about it, because years of being his girlfriend lived inside the weeks they'd spent here — strawberry harvests and amphitheater nights and falling asleep on his shoulder during lunch — and holding his hand was a familiar habit, like checking her pencil behind her ear.

"This way," she whispered. "Quiet."

The camp at night was a different place. During the day Camp Half-Blood was all noise and motion — conch horns and sword practice and Clarisse yelling and the pegasi whinnying and the strawberry fields smelling like sugar. At night it went still and silver. The moon washed the paths pale. The cabins stood in their uneven horseshoe around the green, windows dark, doors shut, the bronze dragon on top of the Hephaestus cabin glinting. Far down the hill, the ocean made a low steady sound, in and out, in and out. The lake was glass. The pine trees along the border stood black against the stars.

Percy followed her across the green on tiptoe, both of them freezing every time a pebble crunched too loud.

"Where are we going?" he breathed.

"You'll see," Annabeth said.

She led him back to Cabin One, though the old instinct still tugged toward Cabin Six. She knew which roof stood here. Missing the other one didn't make her lose her way.

The Zeus cabin was big and bright even at night, white marble with tall columns and bronze double doors that gleamed faintly under the moon. During the day it looked proud and a little lonely, standing at the head of the green. At night it looked softer. The thunderheads carved over the door didn't look angry. They just looked like clouds.

Annabeth didn't go to the door. She went around the side, to where a trellis covered in honeysuckle climbed the back wall, half-wild, the vines thick as her wrist. Someone — years ago, some Zeus kid with more nerve than sense — had nailed small wooden slats between the trellis beams, making a sort of ladder. From the top of the trellis you could swing onto the narrow ledge that ran under the eaves, and from there, if you weren't scared of heights and you knew exactly where to put your feet, you could pull yourself up onto the flat part of the roof behind the pediment.

Percy stared up. "Annabeth. That's — that's climbing a building."

"It's climbing *my* building," Annabeth said. "Come on. I'm right here. I'll show you where to step."

"Have you done this before?"

"Lots of times," Annabeth said. Which was true and not true in a way that made her stomach twist. "Just — not with this body. Not up this wall."

Percy looked at her. He didn't ask what she meant. He was learning to hear the double-meaning in her sentences, the way she was learning to hear it in his. Retained memories, unfamiliar present. Two lives pressed together like pages stuck with rain.

He squeezed her hand. "Okay. You first. I'll catch you if you slip."

"You can't catch me from below, Seaweed Brain."

"Sure I can. I'm very catchy."

Annabeth snorted, and then she was climbing, because if she stood there thinking about it she would think about the other roof too much, and she wanted to be here, tonight, with him.

The honeysuckle was cool and a little damp under her palms. The scent rose around her as she moved — sweet and green and night-heavy. She found each slat by feel. Left foot, right hand, breathe. The ledge was exactly where she'd measured it, and her feet trusted the old climbing practice even on new stone. Familiar movements from somewhere else. The same careful balance. Herself.

She swung a leg over the eaves, got a knee on the marble, and hauled herself up onto the flat roof behind the triangular pediment. The marble was cold through her pajama pants. The sky opened above her, huge and star-crowded.

She lay there for a second, breathing, grinning up at Aquila — the eagle, her dad's constellation in this life — and then she leaned over the edge.

"Okay! There's a good handhold to your left — yes, that one — now your foot goes — no, the other — yeah! Like that!"

Percy climbed like he swam: strong and not particularly graceful, committing completely. Halfway up a vine slipped under his sneaker and he yelped. Annabeth reached down without thinking and grabbed his wrist.

"I've got you," she said.

"I know," Percy said, breathing hard, and smiled up at her, and climbed the rest of the way while she held on. He rolled over the edge beside her, landing hard on his shoulder, laughing under his breath.

"Ow. Worth it." He sat up and looked around. "Whoa."

From the roof, Camp Half-Blood spread out below them in moonlight. The green, the cabins with their glowing numbers, the Big House with its warm porch light still on, the amphitheater pale as a shell in the hillside, the strawberry fields dark and rippling when the wind moved through, the woods a black wall to the north, and beyond everything, the sea — a flat silver plate to the horizon, stitched to the sky.

"You can see everything," Percy whispered.

"That's why I come up here," Annabeth said.

She crawled to the back corner behind the pediment, where the roof formed a shallow nook sheltered from the wind, and pried up a loose marble tile she'd found on her second night. Underneath was her stash: a folded wool blanket that smelled faintly of cedar, a second thinner blanket, a flashlight with dying batteries, a notebook with a rubber band around it, and a stub of pencil.

Percy watched her lay it all out. "You've been holding out on me, Grace."

"I needed somewhere that was just mine for a minute," Annabeth said. She spread the wool blanket and sat down, patting the space beside her. "Before I shared it."

Percy sat close enough that their shoulders touched. The marble was hard underneath, but the blanket helped. The wind moved over the pediment and broke around them, leaving their little corner almost still.

For a while they just sat. The stars turned slowly. An owl called from the woods. Somewhere in the Aphrodite cabin someone giggled in their sleep. The flag on top of the Big House snapped once.

"This is nice," Percy said quietly. "Thank you for bringing me."

Annabeth leaned her head against his shoulder. "You're welcome."

She'd been up here a dozen times alone since she came to this version of camp. She'd never brought anyone. Not Thalia, not even Grover. She'd told herself it was because she didn't want to get anyone in trouble. The truth was simpler and harder to say: some places you have to make sure are yours before you can stand to share them.

Tonight, with Percy's shoulder warm under her cheek and his cold fingers finally warming up where they rested against her knee, she was glad she'd waited, and glad the waiting was over.

Percy had been on roofs before. In his remembered childhood there was Sally's apartment building in Manhattan, where she hung laundry and grew basil in tin cans and told him stories about his dad while the traffic roared below. There was Yancy Academy's gym, once, on a dare, for about forty seconds before a teacher caught him. Recently there was the reef-dome of the palace, which wasn't a roof exactly but felt like one — coral arching overhead while light filtered down blue and green.

None of them felt like this. High and hidden and quiet, with Annabeth's head on his shoulder and the whole camp asleep below.

"So," Percy said after a while, "is this an Athena cabin tradition? Roof-sitting?"

Annabeth was quiet for a beat too long.

"Yeah," she said finally. "It was. Is." She pulled the thin blanket over their legs. "In — before. In the other memory. The Athena cabin has — had — this perfect flat roof. You could get to it from the library window if you were skinny enough and didn't mind splinters. I found it when I was seven. First week at camp. I couldn't sleep because everything smelled wrong — like cedar and old books instead of home — and I climbed out the window and just... sat up there. I could see the whole camp. It made it smaller. Easier to understand. Like a model."

Percy wrapped an arm around her shoulders, careful, giving her room to move away if she wanted. She didn't. She settled closer.

"I used to take books up there," Annabeth went on, her voice low. "And my sketchbook. I'd draw the cabins from above. Redesign them. I had this whole plan for a better Athena cabin — bigger windows, a reading loft, a dumbwaiter from the kitchen for midnight snacks. Malcolm said it was impractical. He was probably right. I still wanted it."

She laughed a little, but it sounded wet around the edges.

"What?" Percy said softly.

"It's stupid. It's just — that roof was mine for years. Years of sneaking up there when I was sad or mad or thinking too loud. And now —" She gestured at the marble under them, the columns, the bronze. "Now I'm in a different cabin with a different dad and a different roof, and I love Thalia, I do, and I love — there's so much that's good here. But sometimes I go to turn down the Athena cabin path without thinking. And then I remember. And I feel like I lost something I can't even explain to anyone, because how do you miss a life you also still remember?"

Percy thought about that. He had the same doubled feeling, like looking at two transparencies laid on top of each other. Camp Half-Blood with Annabeth as daughter of Athena, smart and prickly and always three steps ahead, meeting him on the hill when he was twelve and just arrived. Camp Half-Blood with Annabeth as daughter of Zeus, bright and bossy and still three steps ahead, saving him the good syrup at breakfast. One remembered, one here around him. Both mattered.

"I get it," he said. "I dream about Sally's apartment and then I wake up here, and for a second I can still smell it. Blue pancakes. Laundry soap. Rain on the fire escape. Atlantis is here, real, but all the childhood rooms they show me are new to me. I know they raised me. I just don't remember being raised there. The palace or the apartment — I don't want to stop calling either one home."

Annabeth lifted her head to look at him. "Tell me about them. Both. Like you told the Atlantis story, but the real part."

Percy stared out at the sea. The moon made a road on the water, straight and shining. He'd always loved that — the moon-road. In his dreams he could swim up it.

"Atlantis," he said slowly, "isn't like in books. When I went back recently it was... loud. Not noisy-loud. Alive-loud. Water moving everywhere — canals instead of streets, tide gardens like I told that night. But also voices. Hundreds of them. Singing, arguing, laughing. The markets smell like salt and citrus and hot stone. The guards at the palace gates know my name. There's this garden behind the palace where the coral grows in these arches, pink and gold, and a bench Amphitrite says Dad used to sit on with me. I don't remember those mornings. But he sat beside me on the visit, and put his hand on my head, and it covered my whole skull. I felt huge and tiny at the same time. Like I belonged to something massive that was trying hard not to drop me."

He swallowed. His throat felt tight.

"And then there's what I remember," he said. "Sally making pancakes in our tiny kitchen, the radio playing something old, her singing off-key, the whole apartment smelling like butter and blueberries, and Gabe snoring on the couch —" He stopped. Even saying Gabe's name felt strange up here, under the clean stars. "And it's small. So small compared with Atlantis. Two rooms and a fire escape. But Mom looks up when I come in, and smiles like I'm the best thing she's ever seen, and says 'Morning, baby,' and gives me the first pancake even if it's lopsided. That was home too. That's still home in my head. I know she died here when I was two. I don't know how to make the years I remember feel farther away just because everyone says they never happened."

Annabeth was watching him with those grey eyes that missed nothing. She reached over and took his hand, lacing their fingers tight.

"Maybe home isn't a place," she said. "Maybe it's people who save you the first pancake."

Percy laughed, a little shaky. "Sally Jackson philosophy, as translated by Annabeth Grace."

"I'm serious." She bumped her shoulder against his. "Your mom sounds like... like she made a whole world in that apartment. Blue food and stories and — and safety. That's architecture too, you know. Building a home out of nothing. That's harder than marble."

Percy felt his eyes sting. He blinked hard at the stars. "I miss her. Even though everyone here lost her years ago. I miss her like I'm homesick for two places at once."

"Me too," Annabeth whispered. "I'm homesick for two places at once. That's exactly it."

They sat with that for a while. The owl called again. The wind lifted Annabeth's ponytail — she'd let it down before climbing, and now her curls blew across Percy's cheek, smelling like sunscreen and honeysuckle.

"Can I tell you something weird?" Annabeth said.

"Always."

"I dream about New Rome." She said it like a confession. "Not just camp. The city we visited. Marble and gold, on hills by a river. Temples and a forum and a pomerium line you couldn't cross with weapons. Kids in purple shirts instead of orange. People drawing aqueducts, talking about exams, laughing like there wasn't always a quest waiting. I remember those streets, and then I dream about how we'd planned to live there. The shortcut to the bakery and which fountain had the coldest water. I haven't seen it in this life. But I miss it."

Percy's skin prickled. "I dream about that too."

"You do?"

"Yeah. Not every night. But sometimes. The Tiber, the hills, the baths where the water's always warm, eagles everywhere. I remember the tour. And then in the dream we're seventeen again and I'm wearing purple, because I finally got those recommendation letters, and there's a girl next to me with blonde hair in a ponytail, drawing a bridge on a wax tablet while everyone else is arguing about class schedules." He glanced at her. "Guess who."

Annabeth's mouth fell open. "Percy —"

"I know. It's nuts. I was trying to get us there, before this happened. Those recommendation letters felt harder than a quest some days. And now Chiron gets all cloudy when I ask about Rome. He says 'There are things the Mist hides for a reason, my boy,' and then changes the subject to Latin declensions. I know Jason's there now. I don't know how much of the city we'd recognize, or whether the future we wanted still has room for us."

Annabeth laughed despite herself. "That sounds like Chiron."

"Do you think —" Percy hesitated. "Do you think we'll get to live there? Here? Like we planned? Or —"

"I think," Annabeth said carefully, "we remember the city we visited, and we dream about what we wanted. That doesn't make the dream a promise from the gods. But it doesn't mean we have to throw the plan away." She pulled her notebook out from under the blanket — the one from her stash — and showed him the rubber band. "I draw it sometimes. New Rome. The streets I remember, and what I'd build if we ever got there. So I don't lose the shape of it."

She opened to a page: a street curving up a hill, colonnades, a domed temple, tiny figures in togas, notes in the margins in her cramped handwriting — *bakery?? 2nd left? fountain—cold! pomerium—no weapons!*

Percy stared. "That's — Annabeth, that's exactly — the bakery on the left, with the blue awning —"

"You've seen it?"

"From the tour. The bread smelled like rosemary." Percy shook his head, half-laughing, half-unsettled. "Gods. We're haunted by a city."

"Haunted by a city," Annabeth repeated, and wrote it in the margin. "That's good. Can I use that?"

"It's yours."

She wrote it down, then closed the notebook and set it aside. She leaned back against him, her head finding the curve of his shoulder like it was designed for it.

"Do you think we'll ever go back there?" she asked quietly. "To stay?"

"I hope so," Percy said. "If it's got you drawing bridges and me in purple, it can't be all bad."

"It feels important," Annabeth murmured. "Like a promise we made and still remember."

Percy pressed his cheek to the top of her head. "Then we'll keep it. Someday."

Above them the stars wheeled, indifferent and bright. Below them the camp slept. The sea kept breathing. For a little while, with the blanket over their legs and the notebook full of a city they had visited in the life they remembered, the doubled feeling eased — not gone, just quieter, like two songs played in harmony instead of at odds.

Then a voice from below said, very clearly: "If you two lovebirds throw a blanket down right now, I might forgive you for not inviting me."

Thalia Grace had known Annabeth was sneaking out approximately ninety seconds after Annabeth started sneaking out.

It wasn't hard. Thalia was a light sleeper on a good night, and a daughter of Zeus during storm season was never a good sleeper. The air got staticky before thunder. Her teeth buzzed. She woke up for every shift in pressure. So when Annabeth's bunk creaked and the window latch clicked and small sneakers whispered across the marble, Thalia cracked one eye open and smiled into her pillow.

*Have fun, little sis,* she'd thought, and rolled over.

She'd given Annabeth twenty minutes. Then she'd gotten up, pulled on her jacket over her pajamas, shoved her feet into boots without tying them, and gone to the kitchen.

This was the part Annabeth didn't know: Thalia had her own secret stash. Not on a roof. In the Big House kitchen, third cabinet from the sink, behind the decaf nobody drank — three packets of hot chocolate mix, liberated from the winter stores, plus a thermos. She'd been saving them for a bad night. A storm night. A night when the buzzing got too loud and the worry wouldn't settle and she needed something warm to hold.

Tonight wasn't a bad night. But she'd seen the way Annabeth had been quiet at dinner, pushing peas around her plate while Percy told Grover about canoe tips, that faraway look in her grey eyes that meant she was remembering too much at once. And she'd seen Percy squeeze Annabeth's hand under the table like he'd learned to do — quick and casual, like checking a knot.

Thalia knew that look from her sister's face now. She didn't have Annabeth's memories, but she recognized a bad night when she saw one.

So she'd filled the thermos with hot water from the always-warm tap — thank you, Hestia's hearth — whisked in two packets (one for each of them, and she'd drink from the thermos lid herself, she wasn't an animal), grabbed the spare wool blanket from the foot of her bunk, and followed the faint honeysuckle-sweet scent of her sister's trail to the back of the Zeus cabin.

She'd stood in the shadows for a minute, listening to the low murmur of voices from the roof. She couldn't make out words, just tones — Percy's low and steady, Annabeth's quicker, rising and falling. No crying. Good. Just talking. That was allowed.

She'd almost left them to it. Almost. But then Annabeth laughed — that real laugh she only did around Percy, bright and unguarded — and Thalia thought about her cold bunk and the buzzing in her teeth and the worry she'd been carrying about whether her sister needed company, and she decided she was tired of being noble and lonely.

Hence: shouting up at them like a goblin.

Now two faces appeared over the edge of the roof, moonlit and guilty and delighted all at once.

"Thalia!" Annabeth hissed. "How — how long have you —"

"Long enough to know you owe me cocoa tax for spying," Thalia said. She held up the thermos and the folded blanket like offerings. "You gonna throw down that blanket or do I have to climb up there and show you how it's done, children?"

Percy disappeared. A second later a corner of wool flopped over the edge. Thalia grabbed it, tested the knot he'd tied to the trellis — solid, good, Kelp Head had learned something from all those sailing knots — and climbed.

It was an easy climb for her. She'd been climbing trees since she was six, water towers since she was eight, everything since forever. She was over the eaves and sitting in their little nook before Percy had even finished saying "Careful, the third slat —"

"Third slat's loose," Thalia finished, settling down on the other side of Annabeth, spreading her blanket over all three of them. "I know. I checked on the way up." She looked around, nodding approvingly. "Nice spot, sis. Good sight lines. Defensible. Romantic. Ten out of ten. I'm furious you didn't tell me sooner."

Annabeth went pink to the tips of her ears. "I was going to — I just —"

"Relax." Thalia bumped her shoulder, then unscrewed the thermos. Steam curled up, smelling like chocolate and warmth. She poured into the two plastic cups she'd brought — Annabeth got the one with the chipped rim, Percy got the tall one, she kept the lid for herself — and handed them over. "I come bearing peace offerings. Drink. It's the good kind. With the little marshmallows that turn into foam."

Percy took his cup with both hands like it was treasure. "Thalia, you're a goddess."

"I know," Thalia said modestly. "A daughter of Zeus, to be precise. It's a curse."

They all drank. The cocoa was almost too hot, sweet and rich, the kind that warmed your hands through the cup and your chest from the inside. The wind moved over the pediment. The stars burned.

Thalia sighed and leaned back on her elbows, boots sticking out from under the shared blanket. "So. Secret roof club. I like it. We need a name. The Roof Rats? The Sky Sneakers? The —"

"The Night Architects?" Annabeth suggested.

"The Cocoa Conspirators?" Percy said.

"The Kids Who Are Grounded If Chiron Looks Up?" Thalia said, and they all snorted into their cups.

Quiet settled again, but different now — warmer, fuller, three heartbeats instead of two. Thalia stared up at the sky and felt the static in her jaw ease a little. There was something about being high up that helped. Less air between her and the clouds. Less buzzing.

After a while Annabeth said, very softly, "Thalia? Were you ever mad? That I — that I can't remember —" She gestured vaguely at the cabin under them, the lightning bolts, their shared roof. "All the years you had with me? That I talk about Athena when you've always known me as your sister?"

Thalia turned her head. Annabeth was watching her anxiously, chewing her lip. Percy was very deliberately looking at the stars, giving them space while staying close enough that Annabeth could feel him there.

Thalia thought about lying. *No, of course not, it's great, nothing hurts, it's awesome.* That would be easy. That would be kind.

She didn't lie. Thalia didn't do that to family.

"At first?" she said honestly. "Yeah. A little. Not at you. At — whoever rearranged the furniture in your head without asking." She sipped her cocoa. "When you told me about your other life — the one where I was a Hunter and you were Athena's kid — I kept trying to put myself in it. Finding you and Luke on the road. Carrying you when your legs gave out. Standing in front of you when monsters came. Some of that sounds close to the road I remember. But Half-Blood Hill —" Her voice caught. She cleared her throat. "The tree story. I can't remember it. I hate that you can."

Percy made a small sound. Annabeth's hand found Thalia's under the blanket and held on hard.

"Your Thalia missed years," she said quietly. "You told me she was a tree while you grew up. While you met Percy. While you became this amazing, terrifying, brilliant person without her there to see it. And now I'm here, and you remember a whole future I never saw." She stared at the stars. "My childhood has you in it. Our mom, our brother before he was taken, all the bad apartments, Luke, getting here together. Sparring and stealing cookies. I got to be your big sister from the start. And suddenly some mornings you look at the bunk like you aren't sure it belongs to me."

She laughed, short and rough. "I wanted you safe. I still want you safe. And you're here beside me, and I love that, and sometimes I get mad because I tell you a story I thought we'd laugh over and you look sorry instead. Then I feel guilty for getting mad at something you can't help. How messed up is that? I don't want to make you pretend you remember just so I don't have to miss being remembered."

Annabeth's eyes were wet. "Thalia —"

"Let me finish. I'm on a roll. Cocoa makes me philosophical." Thalia grinned to show she was mostly okay, then went serious again. "I remember the girl I raised. You remember being older, fighting wars, doing things I don't even know how to picture. Sometimes I expect you to need help with your braid and you start telling me how to fix a roof support. Sometimes you wake up scared and I don't know which nightmare it was. I can ask. I can listen. I can't make your old life mine, or make you forget it because mine would be easier to talk about. And then there's Luke —" She stopped.

The name hung in the cold air.

Percy sat up a little. "Luke? Luke Castellan? Hermes kid?"

Thalia nodded, staring into her cocoa lid. "Yeah. He's my best friend. My brother by choice. The three of us, on the road. You were seven, I was eleven, and Luke was the bigger kid trying to keep us fed. We survived because we had each other." Her voice went flat. "Then you told me about the Luke you remember. Kronos. What he did. How he died saving you at the end. Gods. And I look at him now —" She glanced toward the Hermes cabin below, dark and quiet. "He's still here. He's organizing capture-the-flag and cheating at cards and ruffling your hair. I love him, and I'm scared of what you told me at the same time. I can't remember that betrayal. I can't prove the same thing will happen here. I just don't know what to do with being warned."

Nobody spoke. The wind moved. Far below, the ocean kept its rhythm.

Annabeth shifted until she was leaning against Thalia's side, as Thalia said she had when she was seven and her sister was eleven and the world was monsters and roadside diners. Percy leaned in from the other side, his shoulder pressing to Thalia's, solid and warm.

"We remember him," Annabeth whispered. "Both ways. The good Luke and the — the other. I dream about him holding the sky. And about him dealing poker in the Hermes cabin. Both."

"I dream about him calling me Perce," Percy said quietly. "And about fighting him on a cruise ship. And when I see him at breakfast, I don't know whether to punch him or hug him. I know this life has changed. I still feel it."

"Ugh. Even hearing the story is hard." Thalia laughed wetly. "Breakfast with Luke is the weirdest. He steals my bacon and I'm like 'I love you, don't betray us and die, please pass the salt.'"

That broke the tension. Annabeth giggled through tears. Percy snorted cocoa out his nose and had to wipe it with his sleeve while the girls howled.

"Nice, Jackson," Thalia said. "Real smooth. The ladies love a guy who snorts."

"Annabeth loves me," Percy said loftily, and Annabeth, still laughing, leaned over and kissed his cheek — quick and sweet and unembarrassed, because Thalia was family and family didn't tease about that kind of love, they just made room for it.

"Ew. Cute," Thalia said, but she was smiling. She put an arm around each of them, pulling the blanket tighter over all three. "Okay. My turn to ask. What were you two talking about before I rudely invited myself? The deep stuff. I could hear the tones."

Annabeth and Percy glanced at each other.

"Home," Annabeth said. "What it means. Missing childhoods. New Rome."

"Oh, New Rome." Thalia nodded slowly. "Yeah. Jason told me about it too."

"He told you?" Percy said.

"Sure. Camp Jupiter. Twelfth Legion. Eagles and purple and serious faces." Thalia's voice took on a wistful edge. "I've never stood on that hill, but Jason describes it in his messages. The training yard and the city he wants to draw. And you two have told me about the older people you knew there. Reyna, Hazel, Frank. I try to picture it. Jason's there now, ten and trying so hard to get everything right. That visit meant a lot. He's still my little brother. Yours too, Annabeth. But eight years apart is a long time, and I can't pretend one visit or one rainbow lets me know all the things he learned without us."

Annabeth tightened her grip on Thalia's hand. Percy pressed closer.

"I keep thinking," Thalia went on, quieter, "New Rome is real. It's out there with real kids in it, and Jason going to lessons, and somebody deciding what he gets for breakfast. Not just a place in the stories you tell me. I can write to him. I can ask for another visit. I can learn what he likes now instead of getting stuck wishing I still knew everything." She laughed a little. "Mortal radio. The station's faint, but you keep tuning until you hear the voice. You don't get to fill in whatever you wanted it to say."

"That actually makes sense," Annabeth said slowly. "He wants drawings of here, and I keep drawing what I remember. I can draw where we are now too. The climbing wall, the crooked strawberry paths, you stealing cocoa. Give him somewhere he can picture us."

"What about a picture of Thalia stealing my juice boxes?" Percy asked.

Thalia shrugged. "Heroic tax. Older-sister privilege. When have I ever needed a good reason?" She looked at each of them. "And maybe add the muffins. If we ask the gods for another visit, there should be evidence that we feed people well. They're too proud to admit a bunch of kids can manage a perfectly good family afternoon with no lightning speeches."

Percy considered this. "I like that theory. The gods are too proud to arrange a picnic without turning it into a council meeting. Very on-brand."

"Right?" Thalia clinked her cocoa lid against their cups. "To divine incompetence."

"To divine incompetence," Annabeth and Percy echoed, and they all drank.

Annabeth had always thought of understanding as a blueprint thing — lines meeting at right angles, load-bearing walls, everything labeled.

This wasn't like that. This was warmer and messier. Three kids under two and a half blankets, sharing cocoa that was going lukewarm, talking in circles without arriving anywhere, and somehow feeling less alone inside the mismatch.

She told them about Athena versus Zeus.

It was the hardest one to say out loud, harder than the roof, harder than New Rome. Because how did you explain missing a mother who'd never been your mother in this life without insulting the father who was?

"In the Athena memory," Annabeth said slowly, staring at the chipped rim of her cup, "my mom was — is — Athena. Wisdom. Crafts. Weaving. She loved me in this cool, distant way, like the sun through glass. She was proud when I was smart. She gave me a cap that made me invisible when I was little and said 'You are my daughter, no one will ever look down on you.' And I spent my whole childhood trying to be worthy of that. Trying to be smart enough, brave enough, *useful* enough that she'd look at me directly. And when she did — gods, when she did, it was like — like solving a proof. Everything clicking into place."

She picked at a loose thread on the blanket.

"And my dad — Frederick — in that life he — he tried. But he had a new wife, new kids, and I was this weird reminder that he'd fallen in love with a goddess once. He loved me, but he didn't know what to do with me. So I ran. I was seven. Luke and Thalia found me. Camp became home because home didn't have room."

Percy made a soft sound. Thalia's arm tightened around her.

"In this life," Annabeth went on, "my dad is Zeus. King of the gods. Lightning. Storms. He — he's *a lot*. Thalia told me he argued with our mom through the clouds. She told me about claiming light bright enough to make the whole camp cover their ears, and an eagle dropping a wind-up owl on my bunk. A wind-up owl. That hoots." She laughed. "I don't remember receiving it. I have the owl. I have the lightning now. He's embarrassing and loud when he turns up, and then suddenly he's gone, and I can't tell which part of him to expect next. But when he does look at me —"

She stopped, tears spilling over before she could stop them. She wiped them angrily.

"It's nice," she finished in a small voice. "To be claimed loudly. To feel, for a moment, like I don't have to earn being looked at. Thalia says Dad brags about us to the other gods at solstices. *Brags.* Athena never bragged. She annotated. There's a difference."

Thalia snorted. "Dad definitely brags. Last solstice he got into a shouting match with Poseidon about whose kid swam faster versus flew — well, threw lightning — better. Poseidon said Percy could out-swim an eagle. Dad said Thalia could out-fry a squid. It almost came to blows. Artemis left early."

Percy perked up. "My dad bragged about me?"

"Oh yeah," Thalia said. "Poseidon talks about you constantly, according to Dad's complaints. 'My son this, my son that, tide gardens, blah blah.' Dad's very annoyed. It's great."

Percy went pink with pleasure and tried to hide it by drinking cocoa. Annabeth reached over and squeezed his knee.

"The thing is," Annabeth said, wiping her eyes, "I feel disloyal. When I'm happy that Zeus is my dad here — sometimes loud and proud and embarrassing — I feel like I'm betraying her. Athena. Like I'm saying she wasn't enough. But she *was*. She was everything. She taught me to think. To build. To see the world as something I could fix." She looked up at the sky. "And then I feel disloyal the other way. When I miss her, when I draw owls in the margins and reach for books on weaving, I feel like I'm telling Dad he's just a replacement. But he's not. He's my dad. Both of them are — were — real."

Thalia was quiet. She picked at the paper cup's rim. “You can miss her. You don't have to apologize to me for drawing owls.” She glanced at the wind-up owl Annabeth had described. “I wasn't there for the life you remember, and I can't give those years back. But you don't have to pretend Dad's easy either. He embarrasses me at solstice too.” She nudged Annabeth's knee. “You're my sister. I don't need you to erase Athena to prove it.”

Percy blinked. "Wait, how did I get in this philosophy?"

"Because you're the pancake bridge," Thalia said solemnly. "You connect all homes."

"I am the pancake bridge," Percy repeated. "Put that on my tombstone."

Annabeth laughed until she cried, leaning into both of them, cocoa sloshing. "Gods, I love you both. Don't ever tell anyone I said that. I have a reputation for emotional repression."

"Noted," Percy said. "Archived. Will use as blackmail."

"Use it and die, Jackson."

"Love you too, Grace."

Thalia stood up suddenly, shedding blankets, and stretched, her silhouette black against the stars. "Okay. Philosophizing is done. New activity. Everyone up. Socks off."

"What?" Percy said.

"Socks off, Kelp Head. Trust me." Thalia was already tugging off her boots and socks, revealing pale feet. She stepped onto the bare marble in the moonlight. "It's freezing and it feels *amazing*. Grounds you. Literally. My mom — my mortal mom, Beryl — she used to say lightning needs a ground wire or it fries everything. This is our ground wire."

Annabeth and Percy looked at each other, then toed off their socks too. The marble was ice-cold, shocking after the blankets. Annabeth gasped. Percy yelped.

"Oh gods that's cold —" Percy danced in place.

"Shh! You'll wake the whole cabin!" Annabeth hissed, but she was giggling, hopping from foot to foot, her curls flying.

Thalia grabbed their hands — Annabeth on one side, Percy on the other — and they stood in a line on the freezing roof, in pajamas, under the entire wheeling sky, holding on to each other for balance and warmth.

"Okay," Thalia said quietly. "Say one thing you're sure of. Even with the memories you carry and the ones you're missing. One true thing. I'll go first." She stared up. "I'm sure that I love you two idiots more than anyone in any timeline. That's load-bearing. You can't demolish that."

Annabeth felt her throat close. "Thalia —"

"Your turn, sis."

Annabeth looked at Percy, at Thalia, at the camp below and the sea beyond and the stars above. She thought about Athena's owls and Zeus's eagles, about flat roofs and marble pediments, about New Rome with its bakery and its cold fountain, about blue pancakes and tide gardens.

"I'm sure," Annabeth said, "that home is wherever I can see the sky with you. Roof, tree, ship, city — doesn't matter. If you're there, I can breathe."

Percy squeezed her hand. His feet were numb. His nose was cold. His heart felt like a hearth.

"I'm sure," Percy said, "that the sea remembers and the sky forgets and we — we do both. We remember *and* we keep going. And right now, right here, I'm happy. Even with everything doubled and confusing. I'm happy."

They stood there until their feet went numb and they had to sit down laughing, piling blankets back over their legs, finishing the last sips of cocoa, feet tucked under each other for warmth.

Thalia repoured the dregs, splitting them with exaggerated fairness. "Last drops. Make a wish."

They each drank. Annabeth wished for more nights like this — no monsters, no prophecies, just roofs and cocoa. Percy wished his mom could see the moon-road right now. Thalia wished for her little brother at Camp Jupiter to be warm.

None of them said their wishes aloud. Some things you keep under the tile with the notebook.

They stayed until the sky paled in the east, until the stars thinned and the sea turned from silver to grey-blue and the first birds started in the woods.

Thalia checked the horizon with the expertise of someone who'd been caught after curfew before. "Okay, Roof Rats. Time to scatter. Chiron does his dawn walk in twenty minutes and he *always* looks up. I don't know why. Centaur instinct."

They packed up quickly and quietly — blankets folded, cups rinsed with the last of the thermos water, tile replaced over Annabeth's stash. Thalia went down first, then Annabeth, then Percy, with Thalia talking him through every foothold from below while Annabeth hovered halfway, one hand on his ankle like she could anchor him by will.

They landed in the damp grass behind the Zeus cabin as the sky turned pink. Their socks were in their pockets. Their hair was wild. They smelled like honeysuckle and chocolate.

For a moment none of them moved. The spell of the roof still clung — that high, clear feeling of being above the world and understanding it for a minute.

Thalia broke it first, pulling both of them into a fierce hug that smelled like ozone and cocoa.

"Same time next week?" she mumbled into Annabeth's hair.

"You're not invited," Annabeth said, hugging back hard.

"Rude. I'm bringing more cocoa."

"You're definitely invited," Percy said, nearly squashed between them. "Pancake bridge says so."

"Pancake bridge has spoken," Thalia agreed solemnly. She ruffled Percy's hair, kissed Annabeth's forehead, and jogged toward the cabin window, boots in one hand, blanket under her arm. At the corner she turned back, her electric-blue eyes bright even in the dawn. "Hey. Whatever you remember — whatever's coming, Rome or Atlantis or whatever the gods throw at us — we've got this. Yeah?"

Annabeth took Percy's hand. Percy squeezed back.

"Yeah," they said together.

Thalia saluted and vanished through the window.

Annabeth walked Percy halfway to the Poseidon cabin, both of them yawning, shoulders bumping in the grey light. At the fork by the green they stopped. The camp was waking — a light on in the Big House, a pegasus stirring, the conch horn about to sound.

"Thank you," Annabeth said quietly, "for coming up. For listening. For — the pancake bridge thing."

"Anytime." Percy hesitated, then leaned in and kissed her forehead, quick and warm, the way Thalia had done to Annabeth. It felt right — family warmth and the love they'd already chosen tangled up, age-twelve and years of memories at once. "Best secret ever, Grace."

"Best secret ever," Annabeth agreed. She smiled, sleepy and soft, and jogged backward toward her cabin. "Sweet dreams, Seaweed Brain. See you at breakfast."

"Sweet dreams, Wise Girl."

Percy watched her climb through the window — Thalia's hand reaching out to haul her in at the last second — then turned toward Cabin Three with Annabeth's spare blanket tucked under one arm. Before he reached the path, he remembered the ladder they'd hidden beside Cabin One and doubled back across the green to check it. Dawn silvered the dew on the grass. The camp still seemed asleep, though the Big House light had brightened and a pegasus was stirring. Thalia had promised to put the ladder away before anyone noticed, though she hadn't said which of them would explain the scratched paint. For another night like this, Annabeth would need a safer route than the ladder they had hidden from the counselors.

“Breakfast later?” Annabeth asked from the doorway.

“If you're awake.”

“I’ll be awake.”

He headed for Cabin Three again, blanket still beneath his arm.

The cabin steward had been trying to explain the difference between an observation platform and a roof to the camp's residents for years.

Annabeth found this out when she came to the workshop with a sketch of the balcony she wanted to improve. The steward was a gray-haired satyr named Hal, whose spectacles had been repaired with a piece of wire and whose shelves held more unlabeled spare screws than anyone ought to trust.

"Can you tell me what this was originally built for?" she asked, pointing to the trellis on her drawing.

"Decoration," he replied.

"And the wooden slats?"

"Somebody added those when they wanted to climb to the roof. Nobody made them to support people. I'm surprised half of them are still attached."

Annabeth looked at the cross-section she'd sketched. The old trellis had a sound frame under the vines, but the thin slats had been nailed through places that were never designed to bear weight. She had used them as a ladder because they'd looked like a ladder. That was exactly the kind of assumption she normally criticized when another student made it.

"Could we replace the slats with a proper access ladder?" she asked.

"For maintenance, perhaps. But it would need a lock and the work crew would have to approve it. We don't need more children climbing the wall at night."

She considered that. She had come expecting him to agree to a solution because she'd drawn it well. His response wasn't a rejection of the design. It was a question about its use.

"What about the balcony?" she asked.

"That's meant for people. Why isn't it good enough?"

"It faces the practice yard. I'd like a place with a view of the Sound."

Hal unfolded the cabin's original plans. The western balcony was shallow because the architect had wanted a tall uninterrupted wall facing the hill. The load-bearing posts could support a bench and a small table, but the railing had been designed for a person standing rather than leaning their weight against it for hours.

"If you want to sit out there, put the bench against the inside wall," he said. "And don't build anything until we check the rail. Chiron will want you to use it only at approved times."

Annabeth made a note. The constraint was disappointing and therefore worth taking seriously.

"Could we test the seating arrangement with a mock-up?"

"Yes, if you keep it on the ground. Bring the measurements tomorrow. I'll have the carpenter look at them."

She thanked him, then hesitated at the door.

"Would you mind if I helped take down the loose slats?"

"Under supervision. We don't pull things off buildings because we're embarrassed they failed."

Annabeth felt her cheeks warm. "I wasn't going to."

Hal looked over his spectacles. "You were thinking about it."

"Possibly."

"Then I'm glad you asked."

She returned to Cabin One with a list of decisions still awaiting approval. It was the opposite of the satisfying surprise she'd wanted to arrange for Percy. She could not make it magical by hiding the work. She could make it useful by giving the balcony a place where people could sit without worrying whether their weight was on the wrong board.

Thalia was polishing a shield when Annabeth entered. She looked at the notebook and raised an eyebrow.

"You've made another plan."

"A bench. For the balcony."

"Didn't we already have a balcony?"

"We have a narrow space with one uncomfortable chair. That's not the same thing."

Thalia put down the shield. "Then tell me the problem."

Annabeth explained the original drawing, the proposed bench and Hal's concern about the railing. Thalia listened, then asked whether the bench would leave enough room for someone carrying an injured camper through the balcony door.

Annabeth stopped. She hadn't considered that the balcony might be used for emergency access when the front of the cabin was blocked.

"You're right," she said. "We should make sure the passage stays open."

Thalia looked pleased, then tried to hide it. She was learning that Annabeth didn't need praise for every sensible observation, particularly when the observation came from someone else.

"Can I help you measure?" she asked.

"Yes. But don't move the whole chair before I mark where it stands."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were."

Thalia laughed and went to fetch the tape measure.

They measured the width of the doorway, the clearance around the corner and the place where the bench could sit without blocking movement. Annabeth drew three versions and discarded two. The last was smaller than she'd wanted, but it left enough space for a person with a crutch to pass. It could be moved if the steward needed access to the gutter.

Thalia examined the drawing. "There's room for three."

"That was the intention."

"Percy?"

"Maybe."

"Fine. Then we'll make sure the bench is strong enough for you and your fish boy and one extremely patient older sister."

Annabeth smiled. She had been expecting a joke about romance or being too young to stay out late. Thalia's offer was practical and included herself without insisting she be present every time.

"You're allowed to come," Annabeth said.

"I'm allowed to ask first," Thalia corrected.

She looked surprised at her sister's answer. Thalia seemed to mean it.

They spent the next hour making a full-size cardboard template on the ground near the arts shed. Beckendorf passed by and offered two offcuts that would work for the supports if Hal approved the design. Annabeth refused to take them until she knew the correct width. He gave her a look of affectionate exasperation.

"Most campers ask me for wood and worry about the measurements afterward."

"That's why so many benches wobble."

"I won't argue."

By the time the template was finished, Percy arrived from water training. He was carrying a towel and the expression he wore after a lesson had gone well but left him too tired to make a big announcement about it.

"What are you building?" he asked.

"Somewhere you can sit without falling off a roof," Annabeth said.

He examined the outline. "That's an improvement."

"I thought you'd approve."

"I approve of most things that end with fewer broken limbs."

He crouched beside the cardboard and asked whether there was a way to fold the backrest flat when the bench wasn't in use. Annabeth thought about it. A folding back would introduce another hinge, which could fail or pinch fingers. She rejected the idea but wrote down the constraint, because he had been right to ask.

Percy didn't seem offended. He helped them move the template against the wall, where the three of them sat on the low practice blocks to test the sight line. The view toward the Sound was partly blocked by trees. It wasn't the broad expanse Annabeth had imagined from the roof. But she could see the water in the distance and the lights of the cabins below.

"Not bad," Percy said.

"Not perfect," Annabeth replied.

"That may have to be good enough."

Thalia looked at her sister. "You don't have to love the first version. You can build something better once we have permission."

Annabeth considered the cardboard in front of her. It was the first time she'd worked on a private space without assuming she needed to claim it by going somewhere nobody else was allowed to go. The design had become less secret and more achievable.

She folded the template and put it in the workshop for the next day's review. Nothing had been built yet. But she had asked the people responsible, checked the limits and included the people who would actually use it.

The next afternoon, Thalia reported the loose third slat to the cabin steward.

She had climbed for years and recognized how easily familiarity could make a dangerous structure feel harmless. The night before she'd brought hot chocolate to Annabeth and Percy on the roof. She'd tested the trellis as she climbed, spotted the loose plank and told herself she knew what she was doing.

In daylight, with the steward examining the cracked fitting, she wasn't proud of her judgment.

"You knew this was loose?" he asked.

"I noticed last night."

"And you climbed anyway?"

"Yes."

He wrote something on the inspection sheet. Thalia had expected a lecture. His silence was worse.

"I wasn't trying to put anyone in danger," she said.

"Nobody ever is. The question is whether you changed your plan after noticing the problem."

She hadn't. She'd spread blankets over a narrow section of roof and let two younger children drink cocoa beside her because she wanted them to feel safe. The memory was warm and embarrassing at once.

"Can you fix it?" she asked.

"Yes. The platform's closed until we do. I'll make sure Chiron posts a notice."

Thalia offered to help. He accepted, but made it clear she would stay on the ground while the repair crew checked the trellis.

When she returned to Cabin One, Annabeth was at the desk with her notebook.

"Something wrong?"

"The roof slat. I told the steward. He's closing the platform until it's repaired."

Annabeth frowned. "You checked it when you climbed."

"I did. That didn't make it safe. We should've used the balcony."

"You were the one who came up after us."

"I know." Thalia sat beside her. "I made the same choice and acted like mine was better because I was older."

Annabeth considered that. She had spent years being told by older campers that experience justified risks they wouldn't let younger people take. It felt strange to hear her sister admit she'd done exactly that.

"Would you still come up with cocoa if the roof were safe?" she asked.

"Maybe. But you'd get to tell me you wanted time alone first."

"I'd probably let you stay."

"Probably?"

"You brought the good marshmallows."

Thalia laughed. Then she suggested working on a safer place to sit out at night. Annabeth drew a small bench for the balcony, leaving space for a shelf where they could put the cups without balancing them on their knees. Thalia asked whether the steward had approved the load. Annabeth gave her an exaggerated look.

"You're becoming Chiron."

"That's what happens when I make a bad decision and listen to what somebody says about it."

Annabeth copied the measurements from the last balcony inspection before proposing any construction. She was pleased that her sister let her handle the drawing rather than taking over.

Percy stopped by after afternoon practice. He examined the bench plan and offered to help carry the wood, provided the steward approved it. Neither sister asked him to return to the roof. He was glad, though he didn't say so.

Chiron received Thalia's written account of the rooftop access before dinner. She signed the log, accepted a short repair-site chore and asked whether the approved balcony could be used after curfew with permission. He said they could submit a request for specific evenings.

That night, Thalia and Annabeth sat on the cabin steps. Percy brought three paper cups of cocoa from the pavilion. It wasn't as private or dramatic as the previous night, but they could still hear the Sound and see the last light on the cabins. Thalia didn't need a dangerous height to recognize how much she liked their company.

The following morning, Hal examined Annabeth's bench plan and asked three questions that sent her back to the drawing table. The first was about how the bench would be fixed so it couldn't slide during high winds. The second was whether the wood would rot if rain blew in through the balcony opening. The third was where they intended to store the bench when the cabin needed cleaning.

Annabeth had answers to the first two. She hadn't thought about the third.

"We could make it light enough to carry," she said.

"Light enough isn't a storage location."

Thalia, who had come to watch the review, pointed to the empty recess beside the storage cupboard. The space was narrow, but a folding seat could fit there if the legs were designed to lock properly.

"That's better," Hal said. "Now we need to check whether the locking mechanism will hold."

Annabeth drew a second joint on the margin of the plan. She had wanted to finish the bench quickly. Every additional requirement seemed to move the project another day away. But she remembered the loose slat above the trellis and recognized the same temptation to accept a drawing because it looked plausible.

"Can I build a small prototype first?"

"Yes. Bring it here before you make the full-sized version."

Thalia helped her choose offcuts that matched the intended wood. They spent an hour cutting a basic joint and testing it with a weight supplied by the workshop. It held when the load was centered. It shifted when they put the weight near one edge.

"Then we need another brace," Annabeth said.

"Or a different hinge," Thalia suggested.

They tried both on the prototype. The second hinge moved smoothly but left the seat with an awkward gap. The additional brace was less elegant and more stable. Annabeth chose it.

When Percy arrived after lunch, he found them sitting on the ground beside an unfinished wooden rectangle.

"Is that the bench?"

"That's the part that keeps the bench from falling apart," Annabeth replied.

"Good part to start with."

He examined the brace and asked whether they needed help carrying the larger boards from the storehouse. Hal said they could have them once the plan was approved. Percy accepted the delay without suggesting he could move everything himself.

Annabeth marked the final change on the drawing. She couldn't claim she'd built the balcony she'd imagined the night before. She had something better for the moment: a prototype that failed visibly, a correction she could test, and two people who were interested in the project because they wanted to use it with her.

Chiron signed the work order later that afternoon. The balcony could be used once the railing inspection was complete, and the bench could be installed after Hal approved the full-size joints. Annabeth wrote both conditions at the top of the plan before taking it back to Cabin One.

Thalia read them and smiled.

"You're going to put that paper next to your bed, aren't you?"

"Probably."

"Good. Then you won't be able to pretend you forgot."

Annabeth put the plan on the desk where she would see it in the morning. She still wanted nights with Percy under the stars. She had stopped assuming the only way to have them was to choose the highest place nobody had given her permission to use.
