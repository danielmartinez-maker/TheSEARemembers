## Chapter 164: The Fire Kept Her Place

Annabeth Grace knew the exact minute the boundary path gave Thalia back, because the Big House porch boards told her first.

She had been standing at the map wall since just after midday, with a pencil behind her ear and graphite on her left cuff and her camp shirt sticking to her back where the September sun came through the screens. Faded orange cotton, sleeves rolled to the elbow, sturdy olive shorts with the pockets full of tools, hair pulled back in a ponytail that had gone loose and been redone twice. Practical clothes for a working day, chosen for ladders and paper, and she had told herself she was only up here to fix the north path shading while the light was good.

The map covered most of the parlor wall now, pinned in overlapping sheets, edges curling where the tape had given up. The canoe lake sat in the middle of it in blue-green wash, every contour verified by wading, the north path drawn in with its wet margin and the small careful words in the margin in Percy's hand, water, unseen, with its little arrow pointing uphill toward the granite ridge. Below the ridge line the paper was still blank. That blank had been bothering her for three days in the particular way a missing tooth bothers a tongue.

Percy had left her a note that morning on the drafting table, folded along the same creases he always used, his handwriting leaning uphill. Walked the north path at dawn. Still wet. Still coming from uphill. Saved you the bench by the window. — P, with the y looped the way he thought nobody noticed. And underneath, smaller, a trident, lopsided and earnest. She had put it in her pocket with the others. Four days of notes now, one for each morning Thalia had been gone, each one shorter than the last, as if he were rationing his certainty.

Monday's had said she would be back Friday. Tuesday's had said Tyson would have liked the fog. Wednesday's had said Clarisse snores when she naps, do not tell her I told you. Thursday's had said only the bench line and the trident, as if by Thursday he had run out of jokes and decided honesty was enough.

She heard the porch boards first because she was listening for them. A boot rhythm she knew the way she knew her own pencil marks. Quick, impatient, taking the steps like an argument, pausing at the top the way Thalia always paused to kick the dirt off her heels.

Annabeth's pencil stopped mid-shade.

The screen door banged. Boots crossed the hall. And then Thalia Grace was in the parlor doorway with four days of road on her, dusty to the knees, hair black and wild and full of pine needles, her gray flannel tied around her waist by the sleeves over a camp shirt gone dark with sweat at the collar and cargo shorts scuffed at the pockets, boots unlaced and caked with red mud to the ankle. She had a canvas survey satchel slung across her chest, a rolled map tube on her back, and a smear of granite dust across one cheek like a badge. She looked thinner. She looked taller. She looked exactly like herself, only more so, as if the trees had sharpened her.

In her hand, wrapped in oilcloth the way Annabeth had wrapped it Monday morning, was the panel copy.

Annabeth did not remember crossing the room.

She remembered the oilcloth pressing into her ribs. She remembered Thalia's arms coming around her hard enough to lift her half off the floor, the survey satchel knocking against her elbow, the smell of pine sap and sweat and cold granite and four nights of woodsmoke, the static in Thalia's shirt crackling faintly against her cheek. She remembered making a sound that was not a word and not quite a laugh, and Thalia laughing into her hair, rough and wet at the edges, saying, "Careful, genius, I'm filthy," and Annabeth saying, "I don't care," which was not a sentence she said often and which therefore counted double.

"You grew," Thalia said, setting her down but not letting go, holding her at arm's length to look at her. "Four days and you grew half an inch. It's the cartography. Standing all day makes you tall."

"I did not grow. You shrank. You walked yourself short." Annabeth's voice came out thick. She steadied it with effort, precise even here, planning even in joy. "You're early. You said sundown. It's barely past noon."

"Finished the last ridge at dawn and ran the downhill." Thalia shrugged the map tube higher on her shoulder. "Thought I'd beat the heat. Thought —" She stopped. Her grin went crooked. "Thought I might miss lunch. Silena threatened in writing to stop feeding me if I missed another Friday."

Annabeth laughed, and the laugh shook loose the tightness that had been sitting behind her ribs since Monday. "She fed us enough for six all week. She said somebody had to eat your share or the table would tilt."

"Then the table held." Thalia's hands tightened once on her shoulders and let go. She looked past Annabeth at the wall, at the lake in blue-green wash, at the blank under the ridge line, and her expression did the complicated thing it did when pride and guilt arrived together. "You kept the place."

"I kept the place," Annabeth said. It was what Thalia had told her to do about Tyson, weeks ago in the loft, and Annabeth had filed it like a sounding and used it all week for this instead. "Come and look. Then you have to tell Chiron first. Then me. That's the order. I decided."

"Bossy in your own parlor. I love it." Thalia stepped to the map, dusty boots loud on the boards, and stood in front of the blank with her hands on her hips. "You left it empty."

"I left it empty on purpose. Olive wanted to pencil in a guess. I said no guesses on the wall." Annabeth came to stand beside her, shoulder almost touching, the way they stood when they were measuring together. "Is it water? Percy says it's water. He stood in the mud Monday before you left and said it was coming from somewhere uphill that isn't on the map."

Thalia was quiet a moment. Then she unwrapped the oilcloth with the care of somebody handling a borrowed instrument, and laid the panel copy on the table under the window. Thirty-nine tally marks in Annabeth's hand, counted once from the panel in Chiron's office and written into her field book, neat as ledger lines. Thalia smoothed the oilcloth flat with one dusty palm.

"It's water," Thalia said. "Cold enough to ache. Up under the granite on the north ridge, where the old line dips. There's a slab the size of a cabin door shifted maybe a foot since June, and underneath it a seep coming out clear as glass. I could hear it before I could see it. Put my hand in and went numb to the wrist." She grinned, dry and teasing, the protective edge tucked underneath. "Marked it with a cairn and a blue rag. Told it to stay put till my genius sister gets there with her pencils."

Annabeth's breath went out slowly. "A spring. An unmapped spring under granite. That explains the French drain. That explains Olive's silt." She was already reaching for a pencil, then stopped herself, hand hovering. "No. Chiron first. You report. Then we draw. That's the order. I decided that too."

"Noted. Filed. Obeyed." Thalia rewrapped the panel copy, tucked it back into the satchel, and bumped Annabeth's shoulder with her own. "Hey. You okay? Really. Four days with just the sea kids and no lightning to supervise. That must have been restful."

Annabeth considered the question the way she considered a contour, wanting the true line. "It was quiet. Percy left notes. Clarisse taught me how to throw a spear badly. Silena let me stir soup, which is apparently a sacrament." She looked up, gray eyes clear and frustrated and fond all at once. "I missed you. I didn't put it on the map. But I missed you."

Thalia's throat worked. She put her arm around Annabeth's shoulders, brief and hard, all collarbone and jacket dust and pine, the Grace-sister hug that had no softness in it and therefore held everything. "Missed you worse. The trees don't argue. It's boring. I had to argue with a squirrel Wednesday. Lost."

Annabeth leaned into her a second, allowed it, then stepped back and straightened, planner resurfacing. "Go. Chiron's in the office. He waited lunch for you. Then wash. You smell like a campfire that lost a fight."

"Love you too, baby sister." Thalia headed for the hall, boots loud, then paused in the doorway and looked back at the map wall, at the small figure by the window with graphite on her cuff and a folded note in her pocket. "Annabeth. Thank you. For the copy. For keeping it. I walked straighter knowing exactly where I was supposed to be."

"You're welcome," Annabeth said. "Now go report so I can draw your water."

Thalia went, and the porch boards creaked, and Annabeth stood alone in the parlor with the sun coming through the screens and the lake on the wall and the blank under the ridge no longer blank in her head, and she put her hand flat over the pocket where Percy's Thursday note sat, and smiled at nothing, and waited for the house to give her sister back a second time.

---

Thalia found Chiron in his office with the afternoon sun laid across the desk in one long plank and the smell of lemon oil and old paper thick around him, and for a second she just stood in the doorway with her boots dirty and her satchel heavy and let the relief of being inside walls do whatever it wanted to her face.

He looked up from a stack of supply lists, and his eyes went soft in the way that made her feel eighteen and seven at once.

"Well," he said. "The ridge gives her back."

"Mostly intact." Thalia crossed the room, set the survey satchel on the desk, and stood at parade rest without deciding to, boots together, shoulders square. Road habit. Four days of it. "Boundary survey, sir. Full line walked. Monday to Friday. All old markers checked."

Chiron wheeled closer, and Thalia unrolled the field sheets from the map tube and laid them out, granite dust shaking off onto his blotter. Her handwriting, blocky and hurried, march bearings and marker numbers and, in the margins, small sketches of things she had not been able to leave out. A pine with a split crown. A hawk circling a thermal. A cairn with a blue rag tied to it.

She gave it straight, the way he had taught her. The east markers had held. The south line had drifted where the creek cut new bank, two stones tipped, nothing structural. The west climb was overgrown but sound. And the north ridge, where the old line dipped under the granite shoulder, the slab the size of a cabin door shifted a foot since June, cold seep coming out from underneath clear as glass, audible before visible, enough flow to explain the silt Olive had drawn and the French drain by the big oak.

She kept it light. That was the order she had decided on the downhill that morning. One or two concrete details, no revelations, no theories about where the water went under the rock or what had moved the slab. The architect of the old line went unnamed in her report because he was unnamed in her head. She had walked his stones for four days and admired them and resented them and thanked them, and that was enough for Friday.

"The seep is marked," she finished. "Cairn and blue rag, north side of the slab. Annabeth will want to walk it with her pencils. I told her to wait for your word."

Chiron studied the sheets a long moment, tapping one finger against the cairn sketch. "And the markers otherwise?"

"Holding. Two need resetting on the south creek. I set temporary cairns. Wrote the bearings." She hesitated. "Sir. It was — it was good walking. I didn't know how much I wanted it until I was out there."

"I know," he said quietly. "Your sister knew before you did. She argued for it like a lawyer." He reached into the desk drawer and drew out a small folded paper, creased and recreased, and held it up. "She left this for you Monday. Said to give it to you on return if you came in dusty and tried to report before eating. Her words."

Thalia took it. Annabeth's hand, precise as drafting. WELCOME BACK. EAT FIRST. REPORT SECOND. DRAW THIRD. — A. Underneath, in smaller letters, as if added later, I kept your place.

Thalia read it twice. Folded it along the same creases. Put it in the breast pocket of her camp shirt, over her heart, patting it flat.

"She's running the camp now," Thalia said, thickly. "I'm just the survey crew."

"You are the survey crew who came home," Chiron said. "That is the harder job." He gathered the sheets, tapped them square, and set the panel copy aside with care. "This goes back in the office tonight. Thank you for carrying it safely. I know what it cost her to lend it."

Thalia nodded. She had carried the oilcloth-wrapped copy for four days in the top of her satchel, checking it every night the way she used to check Annabeth's breathing, and every night it had said the same thing. Thirty-nine marks. No fortieth. On the third night, camped under the granite shoulder with the seep murmuring below her, she had counted them again by firelight just to be sure, and found the same number, and felt something loosen and tighten at once. She did not say this to Chiron. She said only, "I'll get it back to her tonight. She can file it."

"Eat first," Chiron said, handing her an apple from the bowl on his desk. September-sweet, cool and red. "That is an order. Your sisters have been cooking since ten."

Thalia took the apple, polished it on her dusty shirt, and bit into it standing in his office like a kid. Juice ran down her wrist. "Sir," she said around the bite, "permission to be reclaimed?"

"Granted," Chiron said, smiling. "Go be reclaimed, Thalia."

She went out through the parlor, where Annabeth was already shading the ridge blank with the faintest pencil ghost of a slab, and stole a look over her shoulder, and Annabeth, without looking up, said, "Eat first," and Thalia laughed all the way down the porch steps with the apple in her hand and the field dust on her boots and four days of trees coming off her shoulders like weather breaking.

---

Percy Jackson heard Thalia coming from the canoe beach, because the whole lake side of camp heard her coming.

He was waist-deep in freshwater off the flat rock at the north beach with a bucket in one hand and a scrub brush in the other, doing the Friday chore he had volunteered for Monday without telling anybody why. The canoe hulls needed scrubbing before they went up for the cold. The water came up to his thighs, cool and enormous and green-gold in the early afternoon sun, honest cold underneath its shine, and under his soles the white sand was firm and then soft and then firm again. He wore his faded orange camp shirt with the sleeves cut short back in June, swim trunks underneath and his jeans rolled at the cuffs on the rock behind him, feet bare, hair salt-stiff at the temples despite four days of lake. Functional clothes for a working afternoon, chosen for wading, and he had been in them since breakfast.

Behind him on the warm stone, in the shade of the pines, sat the welcome-home spread his sisters had been building since ten. He had not built it. He had been assigned scrubbing because Clarisse said his hands needed occupying and Silena said his heart needed occupying and both of them were right. But he had watched it accumulate, load by load, up the switchback path. A canvas sack of the good bread, baked that morning, the crust still crackling. A tin of honey. Apples, biggest to smallest, lined on a flat stone. A jug of iced tea with lemon slices floating, sweating in the sun. A folded quilt for later, towels stacked square so they would not hurt anyone.

Clarisse had carried the heavy things. Silena had arranged them. Annabeth had come down at noon with graphite on her cuff and added a small folded note to the bread sack without explanation, and Percy had not asked, because some notes were not for him, and that was fine.

Now the pines above the path were full of noise. Thalia's laugh, rough with road dust. Silena's warm scold. Clarisse's blunt bark. Annabeth's precise protest, overridden, laughing anyway. Percy set the bucket on the hull and stood in the shallows with water streaming off his arms and watched the switchback give them back.

Thalia came out of the trees first, dusty and grinning, apple core in one hand, survey satchel still across her chest, and stopped dead at the edge of the sand at the sight of the spread.

"Oh no," she said. "No. This is a trap. This is how you fatten surveyors for sacrifice."

"Get down here," Clarisse said behind her, carrying the last load, a pot of soup wrapped in towels, braid swinging forward over one shoulder, cutoff shirt riding loose over her swimsuit, feet bare and sandy. "You're filthy and you're late and the bread's getting cold."

"I'm early. I ran the downhill." Thalia dropped her satchel on the sand, kicked her muddy boots off beside it, and stood in her bare feet with granite dust on her shins and looked at the lake, at the rock, at the boy in the shallows. "Jackson. You're scrubbing canoes. That's devotional. That's disgusting."

"I'm scrubbing one canoe. The others are scared." Percy waded out, water sheeting off him, and stopped in front of her with his hands dripping. He looked at her dusty face, her wild hair, the smear of granite on her cheek, and felt the week tilt and settle. "Hey, Sky. You brought the weather back with you."

"Brought water too. Cold enough to numb your wrist. Ask your girlfriend, she'll draw it." Thalia grabbed him by the back of the neck, the way she grabbed people she liked, and pulled him into a hard one-armed hug that got lake water all down her dusty shirt. "You held the fort?"

"Held the fort. Scrubbed the fort. Ate your share." Percy hugged her back, brief and fierce, observant and wry even here, noticing the way her hand shook once with tiredness and then steadied. "You look like a mountain chewed you up."

"Tasted bad. Spit me out." She released him, stepped back, and looked at the spread properly. "Silena. This is — this is too much."

Silena stood by the quilt with her hair coming down in the heat, sleeves pushed to the elbow, flour on one forearm, sixteen and warm and perceptive, the girl who had kept this cabin's arithmetic for five years. She wore her camp shirt over her swimsuit, shorts with the pockets full of hair ties, feet bare in the sand. She looked at Thalia the way she looked at bread coming out of the oven, checking the rise.

"It's exactly enough," Silena said. "Sit. Eat before you report. Chiron's orders and mine. Annabeth wrote it down so it's law."

Thalia sat. She sat in the sand in her dusty shorts with her bare feet tucked under her and let Silena hand her bread and honey, let Clarisse pour her tea, let Annabeth, arriving last down the path with her bag over her shoulder, sit beside her shoulder to shoulder without a word and steal half her bread without asking. Percy watched from the shallows a second longer, the boy who noticed things, and then waded out and sat with them in the circle on the warm stone, water drying on his arms, sand sticking to his knees, and felt the family close around the absent place like water closing over a dive.

Thalia ate like a wolf and talked like a storyteller, which is to say she told only the stories she chose. The squirrel she argued with and lost. The hawk that followed her for half a day on the west climb. The night the wind got under her tarp and she woke with her boots full of pine needles. The temporary cairns on the south creek, two stones tipped, bearings written. The slab and the seep, one concrete detail, the blue rag fluttering, the water so cold it ached. She did not tell the night she counted the tally marks by firelight. She did not tell the morning she stood on the granite shoulder and said out loud to nobody, I have one thing that's mine, and felt the ridge say back, keep walking. Those were hers. Chiron said to be known, and she was learning, but slowly, the way you learn a stroke, one length at a time.

"You walked the whole thing," Clarisse said flatly. She sat with her knees up, arms around them, blunt and grudging-tender, watching Thalia eat. "Four days. No patrol. No harpy. Just trees."

"Just trees," Thalia agreed. "Trees and stones and one squirrel with opinions."

"And you came back." Clarisse said it like a measurement. "Most people leave and get vague. You came back louder."

"I'm always loud. It's my best feature and my worst habit." Thalia bumped Annabeth's shoulder. "Ask the genius. She measured me."

"Confirmed loud," Annabeth said, precise and planning even in affection, stealing more bread. "Also dusty. Also four minutes late for lunch, which Silena logged."

"Logged where?" Thalia demanded.

"In the book," Silena said serenely, pouring more tea. "The book of Thalia. It's mostly lunch times and excuses."

Percy laughed, and the laugh went out across the freshwater and came back changed, and the heron on the bow of the sunken skiff in the willow cove turned its head to look at them, gray and still among the reeds, witness to the whole ordinary precious thing. He reached for bread, and his hand found Annabeth's hand on the sack at the same moment, and they both stopped, and then he laced his fingers through hers, cold and warm, lake and sun, and held on while he ate one-handed, and nobody commented, because some things in this family had become load-bearing and therefore invisible.

Thalia saw it. She saw it and filed it and grinned into her tea.

"So," she said. "Who's going to swim with the surveyor? Because I have four days of dirt on me and the lake is right there and I am not waiting till evening."

"Race you," Percy said instantly.

"You'll lose," Clarisse said. "She swims like a storm."

"Then I'll lose fast." Percy stood, pulled his camp shirt over his head in one motion, draped it on the rock, and stood in his trunks with the sun on his shoulders, lake water still beading on his arms. Annabeth looked away with the practiced ease of a girl who had learned exactly where to look and when, which was anywhere else, and handed him her hair tie without being asked. He tied his hair back, grinning. "Come on, Sky. Show me what the ridge taught you."

Thalia stood, peeled her dusty shirt off over her head, revealing the dark sports top underneath she wore for hiking, shook the pine needles out of her hair, and ran for the water barefoot across the hot sand. Percy ran after her. They hit the shallows in a spray of white sand and green water, and the cold took them both at once, and Thalia went under with a shout and came up gasping and laughing, and Percy dove clean over the sandbar and surfaced twenty feet out, shaking water out of his eyes, and the afternoon arranged itself around that sound, the way water arranges itself around a stone.

Clarisse La Rue had a theory about homecomings, and the theory was this: you fed them, you beat them, and then you let them sleep.

She did not say this out loud. She said, instead, standing ankle-deep in freshwater with soup on her mind and sand on her feet, "Thalia. You're clean enough. Get out before you wrinkle."

Thalia floated on her back twenty feet out with her hair spread around her head like spilled ink, arms out, eyes closed against the sun. Fifteen feet beyond her Percy floated in the same attitude, hands folded on his chest, toes pointed, doing a poor imitation of a corpse. The lake held them both, green-gold and patient, the cold layer three feet down rising around their backs, the warm skin on top from the day's sun lapping at their chins. The boundary rock sat mid-lake with its white X just visible, the willow cove thick and green to the left where the heron stood gray and still on the bow of the half-sunk skiff, one leg folded, watching.

"I am communing," Thalia said without opening her eyes. "Four days of trees and I forgot what floating was. Leave me."

"You've communed for ten minutes. You're done." Clarisse waded in to her knees, braid swinging forward over one shoulder, cutoff shirt tied up now over her swimsuit against the heat, cargo shorts abandoned on the rock. Fifteen and blunt and sun-browned, the girl who hit things for a living and fed people as a second job. "Also Jackson's turning blue. He does that when he shows off."

"I am not blue," Percy said, also without opening his eyes. "I'm Mediterranean. I'm supposed to be pink."

"You're purple. Get out." Clarisse reached out with one bare foot and splashed water at both of them, hard enough to soak their faces. "Soup's on. Silena says now."

Thalia came up sputtering, pushing hair out of her face. "Soup. At the beach. She carried soup down the switchback."

"Wrapped in towels. Still hot." Clarisse turned for shore, because Clarisse did not linger in water when food was involved. "Come on. Annabeth saved you the flat rock. She says it's got the best sun."

Percy rolled over and swam the slow lazy crawl back to the shallows, and Thalia fell in beside him, and the two of them came out of the freshwater streaming and shaking, and found the circle rearranged. Silena had set the pot in the middle of the quilt, towels folded back, steam rising into the pine smell. Annabeth sat on the flat rock with her olive shorts rolled once at the hem against the damp, Clarisse's spare shirt gone now, back in her own dry camp shirt, hair rebraided tight for wind, graphite still on her cuff. She had her field book open on her knee and a pencil in her hand, and she was drawing the cairn from Thalia's description, small square stones with a blue rag, asking questions without looking up.

"How high?" she said as Thalia toweled off. "The cairn. How high, exactly?"

"Two feet. Maybe twenty-six inches." Thalia sat beside her, dripping, towel over her shoulders, and peered at the sketch. "Higher on the downhill side. I built it against the slab so it wouldn't roll."

"And the rag? Which way was the wind?"

"Southwest. It was streaming northeast. Why does that matter?"

"It matters for the drawing." Annabeth shaded the rag a fraction darker, blew the graphite dust away, checked the line against the light. Precise and planning, even in joy. That was how she loved people, by getting the details right. "There. Temporary until I walk it. Don't let Olive see. She'll ink it."

"I won't let Olive see." Thalia bumped her with her damp shoulder. "Hey. You stirred soup this week? Silena told me. She said it was a sacrament."

"She let me stir twice. I didn't burn it." Annabeth closed the field book on her pencil. "Clarisse taught me to throw a spear. Badly. On purpose, I think, so I'd feel better about the map."

"I taught you to throw badly because you throw badly," Clarisse said, ladling soup into tin cups, blunt as ever. "It wasn't therapy. It was physics." But she handed Annabeth the first cup, guests first, sisters first, Annabeth first, the rule so old nobody remembered making it, and when Annabeth took it their fingers brushed, and Clarisse held on half a second longer than necessary, and neither of them said anything about it, which was how gifts worked when Clarisse was the giver.

They ate soup sitting in the sun with their feet in the sand, and the bread went fast, and the apples went biggest to smallest by Tyson's old system even though Tyson had departed Tuesday and was not there to enforce it. Nobody said his name. Somebody did not need to. The empty place at the circle where the biggest boy would have sat was folded neatly, like a quilt that lived in a heap but had been folded square so it would not hurt anyone. Clarisse's fear-of-leaving sat in the middle of her chest like a stone in a shoe, touched and not resolved, present and not spoken, and she managed it the way she managed everything. She ate. She stayed. She counted the heads, one, two, three, four, five with Thalia back, and found the number held.

Percy sat beside Annabeth on the flat rock with his knees touching hers, tin cup in one hand, her hand in the other, and told Thalia the week's news the way he told everything. Wry and observant, noticing the small things and leaving the big ones for later. The French drain holding. Olive's silt map. Marcus telling Annabeth her contour interval was aspirational. The heron waiting all week. The love notes both ways, his and hers, four mornings running, though he did not say that part out loud, only squeezed Annabeth's fingers once, and she squeezed back, and Thalia saw and grinned into her soup.

"You two are disgusting," Thalia announced. "Four days and you're worse. You're holding hands while eating soup. That's a skill issue."

"We're multitalented," Percy said solemnly. "Silena taught us. She can decide the future with flour on her arm."

"I can," Silena agreed, serenely, ladling more soup. She sat with her back against the rock, hair down now, drying in the sun, camp shirt sticking to her shoulders where the lake had kissed them. Sixteen and warm and perceptive, watching the way she always watched. Who needed seconds. Who was quiet. Who was performing fullness to hide emptiness. "Thalia. Tell the one about the tarp. Annabeth wants to hear it properly."

"I want to hear all of them properly," Annabeth said. "I want the bearings and the squirrel."

So Thalia told it, and the afternoon went gold at the edges, and the shallows went rose-gold, and a fish jumped once out past the sandbar with a flat silver smack and was gone, and the soup pot emptied, and the bread sack crumpled, and the iced tea jug sweated itself empty, and nobody moved to go up, because the siblings had reclaimed her and were not finished reclaiming her yet.

It was Silena who finally stood, shaking sand off her shorts, and said, "Campfire tonight. Welcome-home. All five. I'll get the honey cakes. Clarisse, you get the wood. Percy, you get the quilt. Annabeth, you bring the map story. Thalia —" She looked at the road-worn girl on the flat rock with soup on her chin and lake in her hair. "You just bring yourself. Washed. Chiron's orders and mine."

"Yes, ma'am," Thalia said meekly, which made everybody laugh, because Thalia was never meek, and the laugh went out across the freshwater and came back changed, and the heron smoothed one feather and looked away, and the pines went by on both sides as they climbed the switchback together.

Silena Beauregard had learned, over five Septembers of feeding people, that a welcome-home needed three things, and none of them was food.

It needed a fire laid before dark. It needed a place kept. And it needed somebody to remember what the absent person liked best, so the return felt like a sentence finishing instead of a new one starting.

She moved around the campfire circle behind Tempest Hall in the blue hour with the ease of long practice, sleeves pushed to the elbow, hair pinned up again, flour already on one forearm though she had only just started. She wore her evening clothes now, the day's lake damp exchanged for dry things. Soft gray sweater over her camp shirt, long skirt over leggings for the evening chill, feet in worn sneakers instead of bare. Functional clothes for a night outdoors, chosen for warmth, and she had changed behind the towel screen while Clarisse stood guard and complained about the line for the showers.

The fire lay was hers. She built it the way her mother had taught her before camp, log-cabin style, kindling in the middle like a secret. Clarisse arrived with an armload of split oak, braid rebraided for night, dry camp shirt and her old green jacket over it against the chill, jeans with the knees blown out, boots on. She dumped the wood without ceremony and stood back with her hands in her jacket pockets, watching Silena work.

"Thalia likes birch bark," Clarisse said. "For starting. She says it catches like gossip."

"I know." Silena tucked a curl of bark into the kindling. "I saved some from Tuesday. Tyson split it. He said to keep the thin pieces for Friday. He didn't know why Friday. He just said Friday would need it."

Clarisse was quiet a second. The name sat between them, folded neatly, touched and not resolved. Tyson departed Tuesday, promised return, letters pending. The corner bunk made up. The visitor towel washed and folded separately. The fear-of-leaving stone in the shoe, present for both of them, managed by staying.

"He was right," Clarisse said at last, blunt and low. "Friday needed it." She crouched, struck the match on the stone, held it to the bark. The flame took, small and gold, and Silena fed it pine needles, then twigs, and the fire stood up, crackling, and the circle filled with the smell of it. Warm dust, resin, honey from the cakes waiting on the flat stone.

Percy arrived with the quilt over one shoulder and two blankets over the other, jeans with the cuffs unrolled now, dry camp shirt, hair still damp and curling at the temples, lake pebble in his pocket. He laid the quilt on the log bench, blankets folded beside it, and stood back to admire the fire.

"Professional," he said. "Ten minutes per occurrence, free for fire builders."

"Flattery gets you honey cake," Silena said. "Sit. You're pink."

"I'm Mediterranean."

"You're dehydrated. Drink." Clarisse shoved the canteen at him, hydration enforcement, retroactive and free for Poseidon cabin, and he drank, made a face, handed it back, and bumped his shoulder against hers, and she let him, which was as close to tenderness as Clarisse got before full dark.

Annabeth came down the path with her bag over her shoulder and Thalia beside her, both of them changed for evening. Annabeth in her dry camp shirt with the sleeves down now against the chill, olive shorts exchanged for long pants with the pockets still full of tools, hair rebraided, a folded paper in her pocket that had not been there that morning. Thalia washed at last, hair damp and combed, granite dust gone, in clean cargo pants and her camp shirt with the gray flannel over it now, sleeves rolled, open like a jacket, boots clean, the survey satchel gone, the panel copy gone back to Chiron's office as ordered. Road-worn still around the eyes, but softer. Reclaimed.

Silena looked at the five places around the fire, counted without seeming to count, and felt the arithmetic hold. Five. The number that had been four all week. The table would not tilt tonight.

"Places," she said, clapping flour off her hands. "Thalia in the middle. It's your fire. Everybody else where you land."

Thalia stopped at the edge of the circle. The firelight moved on her face, gold and restless. For a second she looked eighteen and seven at once, the girl who had stood on a hill and been told to be known, the girl who had walked four days of trees to find one thing that was hers.

"You kept my place," she said quietly.

"We always keep it," Silena said. "That's the job. Sit before you fall."

Thalia sat. She sat on the log bench in the middle with the quilt behind her, and Annabeth sat beside her shoulder to shoulder, and Percy sat on Annabeth's other side close enough that their knees touched, and Clarisse sat on Thalia's other side, and Silena took the last place with the honey cakes in her lap, and the fire stood up between them and the stars began to come out one by one over the far pines, and the lake flashed dark-silver through the trees to the left, freshwater and moonlit, honest and cold underneath its night shine.

Silena handed out the cakes. Honey and warmth and September. Thalia took one, bit into it, closed her eyes.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, that's the thing. That's the thing I missed. The trees don't bake."

"The trees don't argue either," Annabeth said, precise and dry, the planner allowing herself joy. "You said it was boring."

"It was boring. It was so boring I started talking to water." Thalia opened her eyes, grinning, dry and teasing-protective, the older sister fully back in her chair. "Speaking of water. Ask the genius about the slab. She drew it three times before dinner. I watched her throw two away."

"I threw one away," Annabeth corrected. "The second is filed. The third is on the wall." She looked around the circle, gray eyes clear in the firelight. "It's a spring. That's all we know. Cold seep under granite. Thalia marked it. I'll walk it next week with Olive. No theories till then."

"Good," Clarisse said. "Theories are how quests start. We're full up on quests." She said it flatly, and everybody laughed, because everybody knew it was true and nobody wanted it to be a lesson.

Percy leaned forward, elbows on knees, quilt slipping off his shoulder, and poked the fire with a stick. Sparks went up, gold against blue. "Tell the squirrel story properly," he said. "Silena says you lost."

"I did lose." Thalia settled back, cake in one hand, tea in the other, firelight on her face. "Wednesday. West climb. I'm eating lunch on a fallen oak, minding my own business, and this squirrel comes down the trunk upside down and stares at my bread. I tell it, no, this is surveyor bread, get your own. It stares harder. I break off a crumb, toss it. It catches the crumb, eats it, stares harder. I say, that's all, I'm on rations. It chitters at me. Actual chitters. Judgmental." She paused for effect. "I gave it half my lunch. It took the half, went up the tree, dropped a pinecone on my head. That's the whole story. I lost a negotiation to a rodent."

The circle erupted. Clarisse laughed the short bark she used when she had been surprised into it. Silena laughed with her hand over her mouth, flour on her cheek. Annabeth laughed the undefended laugh she used when something had been frightening and was over, though nothing was frightening now, only warm. Percy laughed until he had to set his cake down, and when he leaned sideways his shoulder found Annabeth's shoulder, and he stayed there, and she stayed there, and the fire kept the whole ordinary precious thing the way the lake kept everything, without comment.

"Your turn," Thalia said, pointing at Clarisse with her cake. "What did I miss? And don't say nothing. Something happened every day. I can feel it."

Clarisse looked into the fire. The flame moved in her dark eyes. Blunt and grudging, terrified of middles, brave about everything else. "Tyson left Tuesday," she said. "Clean. No crying at the ferry. Silena cried after. I counted the days. Still counting." She said it flatly, touched and not resolved, the stone in the shoe acknowledged without being removed. "Percy held his ankle in the lake Monday. Silena made bread Thursday because she decided moods were optional. Annabeth threw a spear like a cartographer. I taught volleyball like a general." She shrugged. "Same shirt. Different fold."

Thalia reached over and put her hand flat on Clarisse's knee, brief and hard. No words. Clarisse covered it with her own for half a second, then let go, and picked up her tea, and stared into the fire, and blinked hard, and said, "Dish towel," very quietly, to nobody, which made no sense and perfect sense, and Silena smiled into her cake, and the circle held.

"Letters," Silena said, warm and perceptive, moving the evening forward the way she moved everything forward, by feeding it. "Tyson's first letter comes next week. Percy, you're answering. Clarisse, you're drawing the diagram of the cleat. Annabeth, you're writing the map key. I'm writing the rest. That's decided."

"Decided where?" Percy asked.

"In the book," Silena said serenely. "The book of Tyson. It's mostly laundry times and excuses."

More laughter. The fire crackled. Somebody's guitar started up at the pavilion, far off, and somebody else joined in, off-key and earnest. The wind brought pine and dishwater and honey. The stars thickened. The heron, far down in the willow cove, stood gray and still on the bow of the sunken skiff, witness to the whole warm thing, waiting for whatever it waited for, and said nothing, because that was its tradition too.

---

Annabeth had been quiet a long time, and Thalia noticed, because noticing was the habit she was trying to break and had not broken yet.

They sat shoulder to shoulder on the log bench with the fire between them and the night, the quilt pulled over both their laps now because the September evening had turned thin and cold once the sun dropped. Annabeth wore her long pants and dry camp shirt with the sleeves down, Thalia's old borrowed flannel draped over her shoulders now against the chill, sleeves hanging past her wrists. Thalia wore her own flannel open over her camp shirt, the twin to the loan, smelling of woodsmoke and pine. Sisters in layers, the way they had been since Annabeth was small enough to disappear inside Thalia's jacket.

Around them the campfire had settled into its middle hour. The guitar at the pavilion had given up. The honey cakes were gone. Silena was telling Percy the story of the first time Clarisse tried to bake, with Clarisse interrupting every third sentence to correct the record, blunt and furious and secretly pleased. Percy heckled both of them, observant and wry, his shoulder still touching Annabeth's shoulder, his hand finding hers under the quilt without looking. The fire popped. Sparks went up. The stars came out thicker, until the sky over the pines was more light than dark.

Thalia looked at her sister's profile in the firelight. Fourteen, with graphite still on her cuff though she had washed, with a folded note in her pocket she kept touching through the cloth. Thalia had watched her all afternoon steal glances at Percy the way you check a bearing, quick and certain, and watched Percy steal them back, and felt something in her chest she finally had a name for. Not jealousy. Not loss. The particular warmth of a job shared instead of carried alone.

"Hey," Thalia said quietly, under the teasing. "Walk with me? Just to the edge. Just us."

Annabeth looked up. Then nodded, and slipped out from under the quilt, and Percy let her hand go without a word, the boy who had learned when to hold and when to release, and Silena kept talking to cover them, warm and perceptive, raising her voice just enough, and Clarisse threw a log on the fire with unnecessary force to cover the quiet, blunt and loving, and the two Grace sisters stepped out of the circle into the blue dark beyond the firelight.

They stopped at the edge of the pines where the lake flashed through the trees, dark-silver and moonlit. The air smelled of resin and cold stone and woodsmoke. The static that lived in Thalia's clothes ticked faintly. Above them the sky was huge and clear, the kind of September clear that made every constellation sharp enough to cut.

For a while neither said anything. Then Annabeth said, "Thirty-one," very quietly.

Thalia closed her eyes. "You counted again."

"I stopped counting Tuesday. It was twenty that day. Wednesday it was twelve. Yesterday it was nine." Annabeth's voice was steady, precise even here. "You checked me nine times yesterday from four days away. I felt every one. Silena told me you asked about me on the trail. Percy said you dreamed about the Slate. Chiron said you sent a message by harpy asking if I'd eaten lunch." She looked up, gray eyes bright in the dark. "You're learning. You're bad at it. But you're learning."

Thalia laughed, wet and rough. "I'm terrible at it. I stood on the granite shoulder Wednesday night and didn't know what to do with my hands because there was nobody to stand in front of." She pushed her hair back with both hands. "Annabeth. I have to tell you something true. I walked four days and the truest thing I found wasn't water."

"Tell me."

"I don't disappear when I stop checking you." Thalia said it slowly, like reading a bearing. "I thought I would. I thought Thalia was a checkpoint and without the checkpoint there'd be nothing left. But out there — the squirrel, the hawk, the cairn, the seep so cold it numbed my wrist — I was still me. I was still somebody. I liked her. She argued with squirrels and lost. She built a cairn straight. She missed her sister without checking her." Her voice cracked. "You were right. You were right and I hate it. Don't tell Percy I said that."

"I won't." Annabeth stepped closer, and the flannel slipped off one shoulder, and Thalia fixed it, the way she had fixed it a hundred times, hands gentle, asking without words. Annabeth leaned into the fixing a second, allowed it, then said, "Can I tell you something true back?"

"Always."

"I kept your place because I needed it kept, not because you needed keeping." Annabeth's voice went soft in the way it did when she was being careful. "The map wall looked wrong without your water. The table tilted. Percy left notes but they weren't you. Clarisse taught me the spear but she threw it wrong on purpose and we both knew." She swallowed. "You're my sister. Not my guard. My sister. I need you the way I need the north on a compass. Not to stand in front of me. To know which way I'm facing."

Thalia's throat closed. She opened her arms, asking, and Annabeth went into them, and Thalia held her hard, the Grace-sister hug, all collarbone and flannel and pine, and Annabeth held back just as hard, fourteen and fierce, and neither of them cried, though both came close, because some tenderness was too complete for tears.

"North," Thalia repeated thickly. "I can do north. North is easy. North just stands there."

"North just stands there," Annabeth agreed, muffled in her shoulder. "And I can find everything else from it."

They stood like that while the fire crackled behind them and the lake flashed and the stars burned. Then Annabeth stepped back, wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist, straightened the flannel with planner's precision, and said, "We should go back. Silena's saving us cake and Clarisse is lying about my spear form."

"She's not lying. You throw like a cartographer." Thalia put her arm around her shoulders, brief and hard, and walked her back into the circle, into the warmth, into the kept place. "Come on, North. Let's go be known."

Percy knew, the moment they came back into the firelight, that something had shifted and settled.

He sat on the log bench with the quilt over his knees, tin cup in his hands, honey on his wrist, and watched the Grace sisters return shoulder to shoulder, Thalia's arm around Annabeth's shoulders, Annabeth's head tipped just enough to lean without seeming to lean. He had spent five months learning to read Annabeth the way he read water, by surface and undertow, and he could see the undertow had eased. Her jaw was unclenched. Her hands were still. The folded note in her pocket stayed in her pocket instead of being checked.

Thalia sat in the middle again, and Annabeth sat beside her, and Percy shifted to make room, and Annabeth settled against his shoulder like a boat finding its mooring, and he put his arm around her without thinking, the way he had learned to do that summer, careful and certain at once. Fourteen and fourteen. Boyfriend and girlfriend, the words still new enough to feel like a map freshly inked, old enough by Friday to feel like a kept place.

Clarisse watched them over the fire, blunt and grudging-tender, and said, "Toe."

Thalia groaned. "No. Absolutely not. I'm home four minutes and you want the Toe."

"I want the Toe," Clarisse said. "Silena wants the Toe. Jackson wants the Toe. Genius, tell her we want the Toe."

Annabeth leaned forward, firelight on her face, precise and conspiratorial. "We want the Toe."

"The Toe" was the constellation game, and it belonged to the north beach private spot the way the heron belonged to the cove. Someone picked a constellation, someone else had to find its smallest star, the toe, the fingertip, the faintest point, and name something true about it. Percy had invented it in July to teach Annabeth the sky without lectures. Annabeth had perfected it by August. Clarisse played to win. Silena played to keep score. Thalia played to argue.

Silena dealt honey cake like cards. "Rules. One star each. True thing attached. No repeats. Thalia goes first because she's the guest of honor and she needs handicapping."

"I don't need handicapping. I need a constellation." Thalia tipped her head back, black hair falling, eyes tracing the vault. "Lyra. Toe's the little double by Vega. Epsilon. True thing —" She paused, dry and teasing, then soft underneath. "True thing is I missed this sky. The ridge sky is bigger but it's lonely. This sky has people in it."

"Mm," Silena said, logging it in the book of Thalia. "Clarisse."

"Cygnus." Clarisse pointed without looking up. "Albireo. The beak. Gold and blue. True thing is I taught volleyball like a general and liked it." Blunt, daring anyone to smile. Everybody smiled. She scowled into her tea, secretly pleased.

"Silena," Percy said.

"Cassiopeia." Silena found it instantly, warm and certain. "The bottom point. True thing is I baked Thursday because waiting for a mood felt like waiting for permission, and I decided this family doesn't need permission to be sweet." She looked around the circle. "Also Tyson split the birch. That's logged too."

Percy went next. He found his constellation slowly, the boy who noticed things, tracing the dark between stars instead of the stars themselves. "Delphinus," he said at last. "The little dolphin. Toe's the tail. True thing is —" He stopped. Looked at the fire. Looked at Annabeth's hand in his under the quilt. "True thing is I left a palace once and thought I'd never stay anywhere. This week I stayed. Four days. Same rock. Same lake. Same people. I didn't count the hours once." His voice went thick. He let it be thick. "That's mine. Filed."

Silena reached over and squeezed his knee. Clarisse stared into the fire and blinked hard. Thalia nodded once, slowly, the surveyor recognizing a true bearing.

Annabeth went last. She tipped her head back, gray eyes tracing the vault with the bird-dog tilt she got when something was being measured, and found her star without hesitation. "Aquila," she said. "The eagle. Toe's Alshain. Faint one by Altair." She was quiet a moment. Then, precise and planning even in tenderness, "True thing is I kept the panel copy's place on the wall all week without drawing it. Thirty-nine marks." She paused. "Someone at the crafts table said the panel might take a fortieth before October. I don't know if that's true. I haven't looked. I'm leaving it open."

The circle went quiet. The 40th-tally possibility sat in the middle like a stone, mentioned once as an open thread, not resolved, not chased. Thalia nodded slowly. Clarisse poked the fire. Silena logged nothing, which was itself a kind of logging. Percy squeezed Annabeth's hand once, and she squeezed back, and the thread stayed open the way the ridge blank had stayed open, a promise to somebody not there yet.

"Good," Thalia said quietly. "Leave it open. Some things you don't survey till they're ready."

The fire popped. The stars burned. The lake flashed dark-silver through the trees, freshwater and moonlit, and far down in the willow cove the heron shifted on the bow of the sunken skiff and settled, witness to the whole warm thing.

---

Percy knew the evening was ending the way he knew the tide, by the small withdrawals.

Silena banked the fire first, the way she always did, coaxing it down to coals with the flat of the shovel, covering the honey cake stone, stacking the tin cups to carry up. Clarisse gathered the blankets, shaking sand off though there was no sand, folding with the raccoon precision Percy teased her about and she pretended not to hear. Thalia stood and stretched, road-tired at last now that the fire had stopped holding her up, and Silena put a hand on her back and steered her toward the path, saying, "Bed. Real bed. No arguing. The book of Thalia says lights out," and Thalia went meekly, which made everybody laugh one last time, because Thalia was never meek, and the laugh went up with the sparks and settled over the circle like a quilt.

Annabeth stayed beside him on the log bench. She did not stand with the others. She pulled the quilt up around both their shoulders, one quilt, the only way to share, and leaned her head against his shoulder, and Percy put his arm around her and held on, and the family filed up the path without them, giving them the last of the fire without being asked.

Clarisse paused at the edge of the pines. She looked back, braid dark against her jacket, firelight on her face, blunt and grudging and entirely tender. "Ten minutes," she said. "Then the fire's out and the harpies patrol. Don't make me come back."

"We won't," Percy said.

"See that you don't." She went, boots soft on needles, and Silena went after her singing softly with no words, the sailing song with no words she trusted, and Thalia went last, turning once to look back at the two small figures on the bench under one quilt, her sister and her brother-in-everything-that-counted, and lifted one hand, and Annabeth lifted hers back, and then the pines closed and the footsteps faded and the fire settled to coals and it was just the two of them.

Fourteen and fourteen. The coals breathing gold. The stars thick overhead. The lake air cool on their faces, pine and woodsmoke and honey lingering, the quilt warm around their shoulders, knees touching, hands finding each other under the wool without looking, fingers lacing cold and warm.

For a while neither spoke. Then Annabeth said, very quietly, "I have something for you."

She drew the folded paper from her pocket. Not one of his notes. Hers. Her hand, precise as drafting, four lines. She unfolded it along the creases with lake-cold fingers, smoothed it on her knee, handed it to him.

Percy read it by firelight. WELCOME BACK, THALIA, it said across the top, in the block letters she used for titles. Underneath, smaller, in her working hand, For walking straight and coming back louder. For the water you found and the stories you kept. We kept your place. — A. And at the bottom, in the smallest letters, as if added later, P.S. Percy helped. He held the fort. He held my hand. He counts double.

Percy read it twice. His throat closed the way it did when something true went past without armor.

"She counts double too," he said thickly. "The girl who writes postscripts."

"She's learning to share the map." Annabeth took the paper back, refolded it along the same creases, tucked it into his shirt pocket, over his heart, patting it flat the way Thalia had patted her own note that morning. "For the book. The book of Thalia. Filed."

He covered her hand with his where it rested on his chest, holding it there a second, heartbeat steady under her palm, breath visible in the cold air between them. Sensory warmth only, sustained and tender. Lake air. Sun gone to ember. The wool of the quilt. Her hair against his cheek, pine and graphite and woodsmoke.

"Annabeth," he said. "Can I kiss you?"

She looked up, gray eyes clear in the coal-glow, the planner allowing herself exactly what she wanted. "Yeah," she said. "That's the one you're allowed to do forever. That one's free."

He kissed her then, slow and lingering, unhurried, the kiss of two kids who had all evening folded into ten minutes and no urgency anywhere in it. Lake on their lips and honey between them, his hand coming up to cup her jaw the way he always did, thumb warm against her cheek, careful as Tyson with a quilt. She kissed him back with her free hand fisted gently in his shirt, holding the cloth instead of him, anchoring to the cotton so she would not have to anchor to anything else. They broke apart, foreheads touching, breathing the same cool air, hands still laced under the quilt.

"Jealous?" she asked lightly, echoing a question from another night, another dock, another middle. "Of the week? Of her?"

He considered, observant and wry even here. "A little. Monday I was. Monday you were hers all day and I got replaced by a rock." He bumped his forehead against hers. "But Friday the rock gave her back. And you saved me a postscript. And Clarisse gave me extra cake when she thought nobody was looking. So I'm learning."

"You're Cabin Three," Annabeth said solemnly. "Ten minutes per occurrence, retroactive, free for boyfriends who scrub canoes."

"Tell Clarisse that. She'll kill me."

"She'll allow it." Annabeth smiled, and the smile went out across the coals and came back changed, and the lake kept it. "She counted five tonight and the number held. I watched her do it. That's basically a love note."

Percy laughed, wet-eyed, and pulled the quilt tighter around them both, and they sat under it with the coals breathing gold and the stars coming out thicker over the far pines, and she showed him the last thing in her pocket. His Thursday note, carried all day. NORTH BEACH HAS GOOD ROCK, with the small lopsided trident. Saved you the bench by the window.

"Keep it?" he asked, echoing another night.

"Keeping it," she said. "Filed. Permanent collection." She tucked it back, patted the pocket. "Tools checked. Boyfriend checked."

"Boyfriend held twice," Percy said, remembering Tyson's words from another lake afternoon, holding her hand a little tighter under the quilt. "By water and by you."

They sat until the coals went rose and the wind brought pine and cold freshwater off the lake, until Clarisse's ten minutes ran out and her boots sounded on the path, unhurried, giving them the last thirty seconds without saying so. Then they stood, folded the quilt square so it would not hurt anyone, collected the cups for Silena, checked the fire with their feet out of new habit, steady and silent, and walked up the hill hand in hand through the blue dark, sneakers soft on needles, pockets full of tools and pebbles and love notes, the stars burning overhead and the heron waiting below and the cabin light showing warm through the trees ahead.

At the fork above the fire ring, Thalia was waiting with one boot on a fallen log, tightening a lace that had been loose since the afternoon. Annabeth stopped short. She had expected her sister to go ahead with Clarisse, not to stay behind.

"Thought you were gone," Annabeth said.

"I was going to be. Then I remembered you left your field book by the stove."

Annabeth looked down at her empty hand. "I thought Percy had it."

Percy shook his head. Thalia produced the book from inside her jacket and handed it across. The cover was warm from the fire, and a corner of the newest sketch had been smudged where Annabeth had closed it too quickly.

"You could've left it at Cabin One," Annabeth said.

"Could've."

Thalia tested the repaired lace. It held. "When I was on the trail, I kept noticing places you'd have stopped to measure. Couldn't remember half the names you give things. I did remember that you hate losing your book."

Annabeth opened her mouth to argue about the difference between naming and measuring, then let it go. She looked at the smudged page instead. Thalia had been away long enough for Annabeth to worry that the days apart might have changed things she couldn't put back. Here her sister was, taking entirely too much responsibility for a book that Annabeth could have collected herself.

"Next time," Annabeth said, "tell me where you're going before you vanish."

"Next time, stop reading the map when I'm trying to tell you."

They looked at each other. Neither apologized, which was perhaps the right outcome for an argument that had only just begun to be spoken honestly.

"Breakfast?" Thalia asked.

"At eight," Annabeth replied.

"Try to be there by eight."

Thalia walked on ahead. Percy waited until Annabeth tucked the field book under her arm, then took her free hand. She followed the familiar sound of her sister's boots up the path, thinking about the way paper could keep a measurement and still fail to record the exact moment someone came home.
