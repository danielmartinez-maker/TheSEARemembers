## Chapter 144: Rain on the Lake

Percy Jackson knew the lake was going to misbehave before the sky did anything about it.

It was the kind of late-summer afternoon that made the whole of Camp Half-Blood go soft and golden at the edges. Heat sat low over the canoe lake in a bright haze, cicadas screaming in the pines, dragonflies stitching blue needles over the water. The lake itself was glass-green and sleepy, smelling of warm mud and water lilies and sunscreen washed off a hundred ankles. Every paddle stroke left a white wrinkle that smoothed itself out in seconds.

Percy sat in the bow of the second canoe with his sneakers off and his feet in the water. He wore his orange CAMP HALF-BLOOD shirt with the sleeves cut ragged at the shoulder, old swim trunks faded to the color of sea glass, and his camp necklace with three beads clicking against his collarbone when he leaned. His hair was damp with sweat and lake spray, curling black against his forehead. The sun was hot on his arms, salt drying tight on his skin from the morning swim in the Sound.

Behind him, Clarisse La Rue was supposed to be steering and was mostly using her paddle to splash him.

"Stop," Percy said without turning around, as a cold sheet hit the back of his neck.

"No," Clarisse said cheerfully.

She sat in the stern in her own orange camp shirt knotted at the waist over a black sports top, khaki shorts frayed at the hem, her dark hair pulled up in a high, severe ponytail that had gone fuzzy in the humidity. A thin white scar showed on her right calf where a hellhound had grazed her last winter. Her arms were brown from the sun, her paddle moving with short, powerful strokes that kept rocking the canoe on purpose.

"You're supposed to be practicing J-strokes," Percy said. "Chiron said."

"I'm practicing annoying little brother strokes," Clarisse said, and splashed him again.

In the canoe beside them, Silena Beauregard laughed, bright and clear across the water. She wore a faded blue tank top with a little embroidered shell at the neck, white shorts with a frayed lace edge, and her seaweed ribbon tied around her wrist instead of her hair because of the heat. Her dark curls were piled up in a loose bun with two strands escaping to stick to her damp neck. She paddled with easy grace, her canoe gliding straight while theirs wobbled.

"Children," Silena called, in her best eldest-sister voice. "Behave. You're making waves."

"We are waves," Clarisse said. "We're Poseidon."

"You're ponds," said Annabeth Grace from Silena's bow, without looking up from her sketchbook.

Annabeth sat cross-legged in the bow with her notebook balanced on her knees, pencil tucked behind her ear, another pencil in her hand moving fast. She wore her orange camp shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbow, the hem damp where lake water had splashed it, and denim cutoff shorts with a frayed pocket where she kept spare erasers. Her blond ponytail was coming loose, flyaways haloed gold in the sun, sunscreen shining faintly on her nose. Her water shoes, bright blue with white soles, were tucked neatly under the thwart because she liked to feel the hull with her bare feet when she drew. She glanced up, gray eyes squinting against glare, and grinned at Percy across the six feet of glittering water between them.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi," Percy said, and immediately forgot to be annoyed at Clarisse.

It was stupid how that still worked. Fourteen now, same number at last, and still one look from Annabeth could reset his whole afternoon. The canoe rocked. Clarisse made a gagging noise behind him.

"Get a room," Clarisse said. "Or, you know, paddle."

"We're on break," Percy said. "It's rest hour."

"It's lake hour," Thalia Grace corrected from the dock, cupping her hands around her mouth.

Thalia stood at the end of the canoe-lake dock in her black tank top with the skull stencil cracked from a hundred washes, her orange camp overshirt tied around her waist by the sleeves, and cutoffs with a safety pin holding the left pocket closed. Her leather jacket was slung over the dock post — too hot to wear, too Thalia to leave behind. Her choppy black hair was tied back with a strip of black cloth, electric-blue eyes narrowed against the sun, combat boots unlaced and baking on the planks beside her bare feet. She had a coiled rope over one shoulder and a lantern in one hand, and she looked like she was about to be extremely in charge.

"Break's over in five!" Thalia shouted. "Chiron says one more relay, then you can go back to being useless!"

Jason Grace, still on his approved visit from Camp Jupiter and helping his older sisters with Chiron's lake session, waved from beside her on the dock with a clipboard. He wore a pale gray camp shirt, jeans rolled to the knee, and his old sneakers soaked from helping the naiads reset the buoys. His blond hair was sun-white at the tips, his scarred lip pulled in a grin.

"Will says no relay in this heat!" Jason called, reading the message on his clipboard. "Just bring 'em in! Water check!"

"Water check!" Thalia echoed, and blew the conch.

The sound rolled low over the lake. From the far reeds, two younger Hermes kids in a wobbly canoe waved and started paddling in. A naiad surfaced near the lily pads, hair streaming green-brown, and waved at Percy before sinking again with a flick that smelled faintly of mint.

Percy lifted his feet, water sheeting off his ankles, and reached for his sneakers. The air had changed while he wasn't paying attention. He felt it first in his teeth, that faint metallic lift, then on his forearms where the fine hairs rose. The cicadas had gone quiet all at once. The pines on the far shore stood very still, as if listening.

He looked west.

Over the strawberry fields, past the Big House roof, the sky had stacked itself into a tall, bruised tower. White at the top where the sun still hit it, slate-purple at the base, moving fast. Too fast for the summer heat. The breeze that touched his sweaty neck was suddenly cool, smelling of ozone and wet stone and distant rain on hot dust.

"Uh," Percy said.

Annabeth had felt it too. She had stopped drawing, pencil frozen mid-line, head tipped. Her eyes went from cloud to water to Thalia on the dock, fast and calculating. She capped her pencil with practiced fingers and slid her notebook into the waterproof sleeve Silena tossed her without being asked.

"Storm," Annabeth said. "Summer pop-up. Big."

Silena was already turning her canoe, hair coming loose from its bun in the first gust. "How long?"

Percy tasted the air. Salt and electricity. Storm and Sea, even in fresh water. The lake under them had gone flat and dark, green to gray, dragonflies gone. A cold finger ran up his spine.

"Twenty minutes, maybe less," he said. "It's moving fast. Wind's picking up from the west."

As if to prove him right, the wind hit. It came across the lake in a visible line, ruffling gray water to white, lifting Silena's loose strands straight out, rocking both canoes sideways. The temperature dropped five degrees in a breath. Thunder cleared its throat far off, low and rolling.

"Okay!" Thalia shouted from the dock, all business now, rope already in her hands. "Everybody in! Now! This one's going to break!"

What followed was the particular chaos Percy secretly loved, the camp moving like a single animal. The Hermes kids paddled frantically, splashing. Silena's canoe shot forward, clean and straight, Annabeth in the bow calling cadence. "Left! Left! Right together!" Clarisse dug her paddle deep, muscle bunching in her shoulders, and their canoe surged so fast Percy's stomach dropped.

"Lean!" Clarisse barked.

"I'm leaning!" Percy barked back, grabbing the gunwales as spray slapped his face, cold after heat. His shirt stuck to his back. His trunks dripped. The lake slopped over the side and pooled around his feet, smelling of mud.

The dock rushed toward them. Jason was waist-deep at the end, catching bows. Thalia hauled rope, boots back on and laced in three seconds flat, shouting numbers. "Four! Three! Two! Beach it if you have to!"

They beached it. Both canoes scraped up onto the sand with a crunch as the first fat drops hit, splatting dark on the hot dock planks. Percy jumped out, sneakers in one hand, and hauled with Clarisse until his shoulders burned. Rain hissed on the sun-baked wood. The air smelled suddenly of pine sap and wet earth and hot metal cooling.

"Boathouse!" Thalia yelled, pointing with the lantern as lightning flickered white inside the western tower and thunder answered, closer. "Go! Go! Leave the boats, Grover's team will flip 'em!"

Percy grabbed Annabeth's hand without thinking, her fingers warm and calloused from pencils and rope, and ran.

---

The canoe-lake boathouse was not Tempest Hall by the sea — that gray weathered temple to salt and storms with its bronze trident going green. This was smaller, older, a long low shed of silvered wood tucked into the pines at the north end of the lake, smelling of varnish and rope and old canvas. Its double doors stood open, dark inside. A bronze plaque over the lintel read LAKESIDE, 1978, with a tiny trident and lightning bolt crossed underneath, green with age. A rack of overturned canoes lined one wall outside, paddles stacked like ribs. Inside, racks for life jackets, coiled lines, a workbench scarred with initials, and a stone fireplace at the far end that nobody used in summer but which suddenly looked very friendly.

They piled in just as the sky opened.

Rain hit the shingled roof all at once, a roar that drowned Thalia's count. Wind slammed the doors. Jason caught one, Percy caught the other, and they shoved them shut against a wall of gray-green water. The lanterns inside swung on their hooks. Dust rose and settled. For a second everyone just stood there, dripping, breathing hard, blinking in the gloom.

Then the teasing started, because of course it did.

"Nice steering, Pond," Annabeth said to Clarisse, wringing out her ponytail onto the floorboards. A puddle spread around her bare feet. Her denim shorts were dark with wet, her orange shirt clinging to her shoulders, her sketchbook sleeve dripping. "Very straight. Very dry."

Clarisse shook her head like a dog, spraying everyone. Droplets flew off her ponytail, off her nose, off her camp shirt. "You're welcome, Sky Brat. I saved your sketchbook with my excellent waves."

"You splashed my erasers," Annabeth said, but she was laughing, cheeks pink, gray eyes bright. She pushed her wet bangs back. "They're soggy now. Soggy erasers smear."

"Then draw soggy things," Clarisse said. "Draw me drowning Percy."

"I already have three of those," Annabeth said.

Percy laughed and shook out his own hair, salt and lake water flying. His cut-sleeve shirt hung heavy, cold against his skin after the heat. His swim trunks squelched. He set his sneakers by the door where they immediately formed a puddle smelling of rubber and lake mud.

Silena came in last, carrying Thalia's leather jacket over her head like a tent, her blue tank top soaked to a darker blue, white shorts translucent at the hem, her bun collapsed entirely into wet curls streaming down her back. Water ran off her elbows. Her ribbon, still tied around her wrist, had bled a faint green stripe onto her skin.

"My beautiful hair," Silena announced tragically to the room, striking a pose in the doorway as thunder boomed and made the windows rattle. "Ruined. Drowned. A tragedy in three acts."

"It's just wet," Clarisse said.

"It's ruined wet," Silena said. "There's a difference and you know it, Clarisse La Rue."

Thalia slammed the bolt home on the doors, turned, and grinned, rain beading on her black tank, her overshirt tied around her waist now soaked, her bootlaces dripping. She set the lantern on the workbench and shook water from her hands. Static snapped faintly off her fingertips, blue-white, and she frowned at it.

"Okay, roll call," Thalia said. "Poseidon pond scum?"

"Here and damp," Percy said.

"Here and perfect," Silena said.

"Here and violent," Clarisse said.

"Sky team?" Thalia said.

"Here and soggy," Annabeth said, lifting her hand. "Present."

"Here with the clipboard," Jason said, wringing out his gray shirt. He'd lost his clipboard somewhere between dock and door. "Is everybody okay? Anybody twisted anything?"

A chorus of nos. Outside, rain hammered, wind moaned in the eaves, lake water slapped the dock pilings with a hollow knock-knock-knock. Through the one small square window high on the west wall, the world was white-gray and streaming. Lightning flickered, closer now, and thunder followed in five seconds. The boathouse smelled of wet wood and rope oil and damp canvas and six people steaming faintly in the cool air.

Jason checked his watch, then the sky through the window. "Cell's right over us. Chiron just Iris-messaged —" He held up his drachma, rain-speckled. "Everyone to shelter, stay put, no swimming, no hill work till it passes. Probably forty minutes. Maybe an hour."

"An hour in a shed?" Clarisse said, but without real complaint. She was already pulling life jackets down to make seats. "We gonna just sit and drip?"

"No," said Silena, brightening. She reached into the dry-box by the door — every boathouse had one — and pulled out, with a flourish, a battered tin of cards, a stack of dry towels that smelled of cedar, and, miraculously, a half-bag of ambrosia squares in wax paper. "We're going to be civilized."

Percy felt his whole body relax. Trapped by rain with his sisters and his girlfriend — best friend and more and always — with dry towels and cards and nothing to fight. His shoulders dropped an inch. He caught Annabeth's eye and she smiled, small and private, and mouthed trapped.

He mouthed good trapped, and her ears went pink.

Thalia saw it and made a loud gagging noise that echoed off the rafters. "Okay! Towels! Everybody strip — I mean, dry off! Don't be gross! Ponds, stop dripping on my boots!"

They toweled off in the dim light, laughing, elbows bumping. The towels were rough and warm from the box, smelling of cedar and sun. Silena wrapped one around her shoulders like a shawl over her wet tank, her curls dripping onto it. Clarisse toweled her ponytail hard, then her face, then tossed the towel at Percy's head. It landed with a damp thump.

"Ow," Percy said from under it.

"You're welcome," Clarisse said. She'd pulled her knotted camp shirt loose to wring it, revealing the black sports top underneath, her khaki shorts dark and heavy, her feet bare and muddy. She sat cross-legged on a life jacket, tough and comfortable as always.

Percy dried his hair until it stood up in every direction, then pulled his camp shirt away from his skin with a grimace. It was cold and clinging. Annabeth, beside him on an overturned canoe, had toed off her water shoes — she'd put them back on to run — and was drying her feet with intense concentration, her denim shorts sticking to her thighs, her rolled sleeves dripping. Her sketchbook sleeve lay across her knees, beading water.

"You okay?" Percy murmured, low enough that only she heard under the rain roar.

Annabeth glanced up, rainwater still on her lashes, gray eyes soft. "Yeah. Just wet. You?"

"Better now we're inside," Percy said. "Your notebook survive?"

She patted the sleeve. "Silena saved it. She's the best honorary sister."

"Hey," Thalia said, offended, while Clarisse and Percy protested their own rescue skills, and Annabeth laughed.

"You're my best sister," Annabeth told Thalia solemnly. "Silena's my best dry-box friend. Ponds can share the honors."

"Accepted," Silena said, tossing Annabeth a dry sock for her pencil. "Use this. Don't let your pages cockle."

Thalia had claimed the workbench, sitting on it with her long legs dangling, her cutoffs dripping onto the wood, her tank top sticking to her shoulders. She'd hung her leather jacket on a peg — too wet to wear — and her black overshirt tied around her waist was soaked. She ran her fingers through her wet hair, making it spike, and lit the big storm lantern with a flick of her lighter. Warm gold light bloomed, smelling faintly of oil, pushing back the gray gloom. Outside, the storm growled.

"Okay," Thalia said, holding up the lantern. Light caught the sharp planes of her face, the silver bracelet on her wrist, the lightning scar that ran up her arm faint in the glow. "Power's going to flicker. Storm's right overhead. No sky stuff till it passes — Chiron's orders, which Jason brought us, and my orders. We're just people in a shed."

"Just people?" Jason said, sitting on a coil of rope in his damp gray shirt and rolled jeans, his feet bare now, his sneakers steaming by the cold fireplace. "Speak for yourself. I'm a responsible clipboard assistant."

"You lost your clipboard," Thalia, Annabeth, Clarisse, Silena, and Percy said together.

Jason pressed a hand to his heart. "Betrayed by family. All of you. Especially you, bossy big sisters."

"We learned from you," Annabeth said sweetly, and leaned her shoulder briefly against Percy's as she settled. Her wet sleeve was cold through his wet sleeve, but the pressure was warm.

Percy tried very hard not to grin like an idiot and failed.

Silena cracked the card tin. Inside were two decks, one with mermaids on the back gone soft at the corners, one with pegasi. She shuffled with practiced flourish, cards whispering, her wrist ribbon flashing green.

"Spite and Malice?" Silena continued, dealing the backstory while lightning flashed white through the high window. "Beryl Grace rules?"

Thalia groaned, but fondly. "Mom's cutthroat motel rules? You're on. Loser has to wring out everybody's socks."

"Deal," Clarisse said, cracking her knuckles. "I'm going to destroy all of you. Pond style."

They made a circle on the floorboards with life jackets for cushions and the dry-box lid for a table. The lantern sat in the middle, gold light pooling on scarred wood, rain roaring overhead like the whole lake had been picked up and poured on the roof. The boathouse smelled of wet wool and cedar and warm oil, of Silena's strawberry shampoo going damp and Clarisse's leather wrist guard and Annabeth's pencil shavings.

Percy sat with his back against the overturned canoe, knees up, his cut-sleeve shirt finally starting to dry at the shoulders where the lantern heat touched it. His swim trunks were still damp, his feet bare and cold on the wood, his camp necklace sticking to his throat. Annabeth settled beside him, close enough that their knees touched, her denim shorts rough against his leg, her orange shirt sleeve brushing his arm. She smelled like rain and sunscreen and graphite. She laid her sketchbook aside, pages safe, and pulled her damp ponytail over one shoulder.

Silena dealt with quick, precise flicks, her wet curls dripping onto the cards. She wore the towel still around her shoulders, blue tank dark at the collar, white shorts leaving damp patches on the life jacket. Her feet were bare, toenails painted pale coral, chipped at the big toe from dock work. "Okay, motel rules, as taught by Beryl Grace herself via Thalia, via long winter nights. Stack in the middle, play fast, no crying, gloating encouraged."

"Beryl Grace rules," Thalia said, shuffling her hand with thumbs that still sparked faintly when she got excited. She'd pulled her overshirt from her waist and draped it over her knees like a blanket, black tank damp but drying, cutoffs frayed white at the seams, her bare feet tucked under her. Her hair dripped onto her cheeks. "Mom learned this on tour. Rainy days stuck in motels with no money for movies. She and her drummer would play for hours. Loser did dishes. Mom always lost on purpose when I was little so I'd feel good, then hustled me when I got cocky."

Annabeth looked up, surprised and soft. "You never told me that part."

"There's a lot I haven't told you, baby," Thalia said, bumping Annabeth's foot with her own under the table. "You were three. You ate the queens."

"I did not eat the queens," Annabeth said with dignity, arranging her cards. Her gray eyes flicked over her hand, already counting. She'd kicked off her water shoes again, her bare soles dusty from the floorboards, her rolled sleeves pushed higher. A pencil smudge marked her left thumb. "Color sorting is a perfectly respectable explanation. Maybe I was helping."

"You put them in your mouth to sort them," Thalia said. "Mom fished a queen of hearts out of your mouth and said, well, at least she likes hearts."

Everyone laughed, and Annabeth blushed to the tips of her ears, which Percy found deeply unfair in the middle of a card game. She nudged Thalia's ankle hard.

"Play," Annabeth ordered. "And no mercy because I'm soggy."

"No mercy," Clarisse agreed, slapping a card down with enough force to rock the lid. "Take that, Sky Brat."

The game went fast and loud, the way all their games went. Clarisse played like she fought — aggressive, grinning, narrating every move. "Boom. Blocked. Cry about it, Seaweed Brain." Silena played like she danced — smooth, smiling, quietly ruthless, slipping cards in while everyone argued. "Oh, honey, did you need that pile? I'm so sorry. I'm taking it." Jason played like a Roman trainee — fair, steady, explaining rules to no one who needed them, losing gracefully. "Good play, Annabeth. Good — ow, Clarisse, that was my stack."

Thalia played like weather, all instinct and nerve, talking trash in a stage whisper. "Watch and learn, ponds. Grace girls don't lose in sheds."

Annabeth played like herself — quiet, counting, three moves ahead, gray eyes flicking from hand to pile to Percy's hand and back. She sat with her shoulder pressed to Percy's upper arm now, damp cotton to damp cotton, her ponytail dripping onto his shoulder every time she leaned. Her denim shorts rode up when she crossed her legs, and she tugged them down without looking, still counting cards. Her concentration smelled almost visible, graphite and rain.

Percy played like water — adaptive, patient, then sudden. He held his cards in damp fingers, his cut-sleeve shirt sticking to his back, his swim trunks squeaking on the life jacket. He let Clarisse think he was losing, then swept two piles in a row while she was gloating.

"Hey!" Clarisse said. "That's illegal! That's pond cheating!"

"That's called paying attention," Percy said, fanning his cards. "You should try it, sis."

"Don't call me sis when you steal my pile," Clarisse said, but she was grinning, her ponytail frizzed huge in the humidity, her knotted shirt damp at the waist, her khaki shorts smelling of lake. She threw a damp sock at him. It hit Annabeth instead.

"Hey!" Annabeth said, laughing, sock on her head. Her hair was coming completely loose now, blond strands sticking to her wet cheeks, her orange shirt collar stretched from toweling. She pulled the sock off and threw it back, hitting Jason.

"War!" Jason shouted, and for a minute the game dissolved into sock warfare. Thunder boomed directly overhead, shaking dust from the rafters.

Outside, rain sheeted sideways past the high window, white-gray and endless. Lake water had risen to kiss the bottom of the doors, seeping in a thin cold line that smelled of mud. Wind howled in the pines, branches scraping the roof. Inside, it was warm and close, breath fogging faintly, skin going pink at the cheeks.

They played three rounds. Silena won the first with a quiet sweep that made Thalia throw her cards up like confetti. Clarisse won the second by sheer intimidation.

Annabeth won the third by counting, laying her final card with a small, satisfied tap, gray eyes gleaming. "Math supremacy," she said, and stuck her tongue out at Clarisse.

Percy lost all three and didn't care, because Annabeth kept leaning a little harder against his shoulder every time she played a good hand, and because the boathouse smelled like his whole family drying out together.

After the cards, stories.

It happened naturally, the way it always did when rain trapped them. Jason banked the cold fireplace with driftwood someone had left and lit it with Thalia's lighter, just for light and cheer, not heat. Flames took with a soft whoomph, smelling of pine resin and old smoke. The fire threw dancing orange over silvered walls, over the racks of orange life jackets gone gold, over the coiled ropes and the workbench scarred with thirty years of initials — AP + GT, JL, SB hearts, TK with a trident. Thalia turned the lantern low to save oil, leaving firelight to do the work. Shadows stretched long.

Silena sighed and stretched out on her stomach on a spread towel, chin in her hands, her blue tank riding up at the back, her white shorts damp and sandy at the cuffs, her long wet curls spread over her shoulders in a dark, dripping fan. Water pooled under her elbows. Her coral toenails glowed in firelight.

"Somebody do something about this," she said, lifting a heavy hank of hair and letting it slap wetly against her back. "It's cold and it's heavy and it's going to tangle into a drowned rat and Chiron's going to make me cut it."

Clarisse, sitting behind her on an overturned bucket, snorted. "Come here, drama queen. I'll braid it before you cry into the lake."

"You? Braid?" Silena said, but she was already scooting back between Clarisse's knees, trusting completely.

"I'm one of three Cabin Three siblings," Clarisse said, with exaggerated patience. "Of course I can braid. Even Kelp Head's hair when he was six and cried about tangles."

"I demand evidence," Percy said.

"You cried," Clarisse and Silena said together, while Thalia laughed at the testimony.

"Then I must have got soap in my eyes," Percy said to Annabeth, low and injured. "It was a chemical issue."

Annabeth patted his knee, solemn, her fingers cold and wet and wonderful. "I believe you. Mostly."

Clarisse's big hands, scarred at the knuckles and gentle in a way she never was with swords, gathered Silena's heavy wet hair. She worked with concentration, tongue caught between her teeth, her knotted camp shirt sleeves pushed up, her black sports top strap showing, her khaki shorts stretched over her crossed legs. Her ponytail had come loose and hung in a damp tail over one shoulder, dripping onto Silena's back. She finger-combed first, easing tangles with surprising patience, her touch light at the scalp.

"Hold still," Clarisse murmured. "God, Sel, there's like ten pounds of lake in here."

"Squeeze it out, don't pull," Silena instructed, eyes closed, blissful already. Her blue tank slipped off one shoulder, her towel pooled around her waist, her bare feet flexing. "Yeah, like that. You're good at this."

"I'm good at everything," Clarisse said, but softly, without edge. Firelight caught the sharp line of her jaw gone tender, the sunburn peeling faintly on her nose, the camp necklace with its beads knocking against her collarbone as she leaned. She divided the hair into three thick ropes, water running down her forearms, and began a tight Dutch braid close to the scalp, fingers moving fast and sure.

Percy watched, chest aching in a good way. Clarisse the terror of the arena, Clarisse who'd once threatened to feed Connor Stoll a spoon, braiding her sister's wet hair in a boathouse while rain tried to drown the world. Her hands were careful at Silena's temples, smoothing flyaways with lake-wet palms.

"Tell me if it's too tight," Clarisse said.

"It's perfect," Silena murmured, half-asleep already, her voice honey-thick. "You always do it tighter on the left. I like that. It lasts."

"I know," Clarisse said. "I remember."

Annabeth watched too, head tipped against Percy's shoulder now without pretense, her damp ponytail spilling over his arm, her denim shorts rough, her orange shirt warm from firelight. Her gray eyes were soft. "You told me you did mine like that when I was seven and threw up on the carousel," she said to Thalia, quiet. "Rain day. You braided it back so Mom wouldn't see I'd been crying. I wish I could remember your hands."

Thalia, sprawled on the workbench with her boots off and her bare feet dirty, her black tank drying to gray at the edges, her cutoffs frayed and damp, went still. Firelight caught the silver in her bracelet, the wet spikes of her hair, the sudden shine in her electric-blue eyes that she blinked away fast.

"Yeah," Thalia said, voice rougher than usual. "I remember."

"Tell it," Annabeth said. "The rain story. The real one. Not the cleaned-up one."

Jason looked up from poking the fire, his gray shirt steaming, his rolled jeans damp to the knee, his blond hair dripping. "Which one? The motel story you sent me?"

"The blue-tarp one," Annabeth said. "Please."

Thalia groaned and flopped back, arms over her face, her tank riding up to show a strip of pale stomach, her safety-pinned pocket glinting. "Since I told you that one, you always want it when it rains."

"Because it's the best one," Annabeth said, nudging Percy with her elbow to make room, settling deeper into his side. Her knee knocked his. Her fingers found the hem of his cut-sleeve shirt and held it, unconsciously, the way she did when she was listening hard. "Tell it. We're trapped. It's tradition."

"Fine," Thalia said, sitting up, hugging her knees. Her bare feet curled on the bench, her cutoffs damp under her thighs, her overshirt still over her knees like a blanket. Rain roared, fire crackled, Clarisse's fingers kept braiding, steady rhythmic pulls. "But you have to let me tell it right. No interrupting with questions, baby."

"No promises," Annabeth said, and Thalia threw a dry rope coil at her, which Percy caught one-handed.

Thalia took a breath, and her voice changed. Less lieutenant, less big-sister teasing. Younger. Remembering.

"Okay. So. Mom was between gigs. I was nine, Annabeth was five. Skinny little thing with big gray eyes and a red cape made out of Mom's scarf. We were in this rental outside Asbury, one of those houses that leans. Screen door that never shut right. Mom was working doubles at a bar down the shore, coming home smelling like beer and perfume and rain."

Annabeth nodded against Percy's shoulder, her ponytail tickling his neck. Her orange shirt had gone soft with wet and heat, her denim shorts smelled of lake mud, her bare feet tucked under her. She held Percy's shirt hem a little tighter.

"It rained for three days straight," Thalia went on. Firelight moved over her face, over the lightning scar, over the chipped black polish on her fingernails. Her black tank clung at the collar, her cutoffs dripped steadily onto the bench. "Not like this. Worse. Sideways. The roof leaked over our bunk. Mom put a pot under it and it went ting-ting-ting all night and Annabeth couldn't sleep because she kept counting the tings."

"Forty-seven, you told me," Annabeth murmured. "Then it overflowed and I had to start over."

"You cried because you lost count," Thalia said, smiling crooked. "Anyway, third day, the power went out. No TV, no lights, nothing. Mom came home early because the bar flooded, soaked to the bone in her leather jacket — my jacket now — hair plastered, cursing like a sailor. She looked at us sitting on the bunk in the dark with the pot going ting and she said, oh hell no, my girls are not sitting in the dark listening to drips."

Thalia's voice cracked just slightly. She covered it by clearing her throat. Jason watched her steadily, his gray eyes kind, his solemn game face off and his brother face on. Clarisse's hands never stopped, braid growing thick and neat down Silena's back, water squeezed out with each cross. Silena listened with eyes closed, smiling.

"Mom dragged the blue tarp off the porch — you know, those cheap blue ones that smell like plastic and mildew — and she strung it over the bunk like a tent. With clothespins and broom handles and pure spite. Then she crawled in with us, still in her wet jacket, and she said, camping. We're camping. No miserable girls in my tent."

Percy felt Annabeth's breath catch, small. He shifted just enough to let her lean more fully, his cut-sleeve shirt stretching where she held it, his swim trunks cold under him, his bare feet cold. He rested his chin very lightly, very briefly, against the top of her damp head, then lifted it, heart thudding. Chaste and careful. Fourteen meant knowing exactly where the line was and staying sweetly behind it.

Thalia saw it and didn't tease, for once. Her eyes went soft.

"She told stories," Thalia said. "Bad ones. Beryl Grace and the Diamond Heist, Beryl Grace and the Flooded Bar. She did all the voices. Annabeth sat in her lap with her wet socks on and her cape around her neck and laughed so hard she hiccuped. The rain hammered the tarp and leaked through in one corner and Mom stuck her coffee mug under it and said, see? Indoor plumbing. Luxury."

Annabeth laughed wetly against Percy's shoulder. "You said the mug had a chip. I can almost picture it. I always imagine the tarp smelling like the beach."

"It smelled like mold," Thalia said. "But yeah. Beach mold. Anyway, we fell asleep like that, all three in the bunk under the tarp, rain going crazy overhead. I woke up at like two a.m. and Mom was still awake, just sitting there with her jacket over us both, humming off-key so we wouldn't hear the drips. She looked tired. Older than she was. But she winked at me and whispered, don't worry, Storm Cloud. I got you. Rain can't get you if I hear it first."

Silence, except rain and fire. Clarisse finished the braid with a small blue hair tie from her wrist, tying it off tight. She smoothed Silena's temples with her thumbs, lingering a second, sister-rough and sister-tender.

"There," Clarisse said quietly. "Done. Rat saved."

Silena reached back and squeezed Clarisse's knee hard, eyes shining. "Thank you."

Thalia rubbed her face hard with both hands, her spikes sticking up worse, her tank damp at the armpits, her cutoffs cold. "Anyway. That's it. Stupid story. Mom wasn't — she wasn't always good. You all know that. She drank, she forgot, she chose gigs over us. But that night she was good. She heard the rain first so we didn't have to be scared of it."

Annabeth sat up, eyes bright and wet, her loose ponytail falling over her shoulder, her orange shirt twisted where she'd clutched Percy. She crossed to Thalia in two steps, bare feet slapping wet wood, denim shorts damp, and climbed up onto the workbench beside her sister, shoulder to shoulder, head to shoulder. Thalia's arm came around her automatically, strong and sure, leather bracelet cold against Annabeth's cheek.

"Thank you for hearing it first," Annabeth whispered, too low for anyone but Thalia and Percy — but Percy heard, because he was listening with his whole body. "Then. And now."

Thalia kissed the top of her head hard, fierce. "Always, baby. Eldest Grace job. I hear it first, you figure out what to do about it."

"And I smooth the edges," Annabeth said, smiling through tears. "And ponds carry it away."

"Damn right," Clarisse said, gruff, tying off Silena's braid with an extra knot. Her eyes were suspiciously bright in firelight. Her knotted shirt had come undone, hanging loose over her black top, her khaki shorts sandy. "Somebody write that down before Grace makes it corny."

"Already wrote it," Annabeth said, tapping her temple. "Since you taught me here. In pen."

Laughter broke the ache, warm. Jason fed the fire another stick, his gray shirt nearly dry at the shoulders now, his rolled jeans steaming. Thunder rolled, farther this time — six seconds after lightning. The storm was moving, breathing.

Percy felt the shift in pressure and glanced at the high window. Rain still sheeted, but lighter, silver instead of white. The wind had dropped. The lake knock against the pilings had slowed. His power, which had sat quiet and coiled all afternoon, lifted its head and sniffed.

After stories, shadows.

It was Jason's idea, because visiting little brothers get restless. He took the storm lantern and turned it up until the flame burned tall and gold, then hung it from the central beam where it swung faintly in the draft, throwing huge shadows on the silvered wall. Outside, lightning flickered paler now, thunder answering in eight, nine seconds.

"Shadow puppets," Jason announced. "Boathouse championship. Loser wrings socks with Thalia."

"You're on, little brother," Thalia said, instantly competitive, sliding off the bench. Her bare feet hit the floorboards with a slap, her black tank loose, her cutoffs riding high, her hair wild. "I was unbeaten at the Richmond motel at age ten."

"By your own story, you were unbeaten because Annabeth let you win," Jason said.

"Then I was being generous," Annabeth said, settling back against Percy's side with a happy sigh, her denim shorts squeaking, her orange shirt warm now, her ponytail finally undone completely, blond hair falling in damp waves to her shoulders. She smelled of rain and fire. "You told me she did a dog that looked like a shoe and I clapped like she'd invented dogs."

"Betrayed by my own blood," Thalia declared, stepping into the light. Her shadow leapt giant across the wall, black tank and spiky hair huge. She held up her hands, fingers twisted — and a perfect, snarling dog appeared, jaw opening and closing as she flexed her thumbs.

Silena applauded, her new braid thick and gleaming down her back, still damp but neat, blue tank drying to pale, white shorts sandy, towel around her shoulders like a queen's cape. "Oh, good! Do the bird!"

Thalia did the bird, then a crab that made Clarisse snort so hard she clapped both hands over her nose. Clarisse tried a horse and made something that looked, in Annabeth's words, like a haunted jelly donut.

"That's not a horse," Percy said, wheezing. "That's a potato with legs."

"It's a war horse," Clarisse said with dignity, her big hands casting a lopsided shadow, her knotted shirt slipping off one shoulder, her khaki shorts dusty, her braid-loosened ponytail dripping. "It carries warriors. Shut up, Kelp Head. You try."

Percy stepped up, heart thumping in the good performance way. His cut-sleeve shirt hung loose, his swim trunks damp and cold, his bare feet sticking faintly to the resinous floor. Firelight warmed his face, lantern gold from above. He held his hands in the beam, angled just so — and a heron unfolded on the wall, long neck, sharp beak opening.

Annabeth gasped. Silena clapped. Even Thalia nodded, impressed.

"Okay, pond," Thalia said. "Okay."

Percy made the heron drink, then fly, his wrists turning smoothly. Then, grinning, he added a second hand — a little fish darting, the heron missing, the fish doing a victory wiggle that made Annabeth laugh so hard she slid sideways off the canoe and had to catch herself on Percy's leg. Her fingers dug into his calf, cold and strong, her damp hair falling over her face, her orange shirt riding up at the back.

"Do the hippocampus!" Silena begged, her braid swinging as she leaned forward, blue tank slipping, white shorts dusty. "Please! With the tail!"

Percy tried. The hippocampus looked drunk. Everyone howled, thunder covering them, rain easing to a steady patter instead of a roar. The boathouse no longer shook. Dust drifted gold in lantern light. The fire burned low and sweet, smelling of pine.

While they played, Annabeth drew.

She'd retrieved her sketchbook from its sleeve, pages dry and safe, and sat cross-legged in firelight with her pencil moving in quick, sure strokes. Her hair fell forward in damp curtains, her orange shirt sleeves pushed to the shoulder, her denim shorts tucked under her thighs, her bare feet dusty. Pencil smudge grew on her thumb, graphite on her wrist. She drew without looking up, glancing from high window to page and back.

Percy saw over her shoulder when he sat back down, breathless from puppets: rain on water. Not the lake as it was now, gray and hidden, but as she'd memorized it that afternoon — circles overlapping circles, each drop a crown, each crown a ring, dragonfly wings for scale, a canoe bow cutting through. In the corner, quick studies: Clarisse's hands mid-braid, Silena's closed eyes, Thalia's shadow dog, Jason's lantern. And in the margin, tiny and precise: his own hand holding cards, her shoulder leaning on his arm, labeled in block letters that made his throat tighten — ANCHOR POINT.

"You draw us like we're important," Percy said quietly, so only she heard.

Annabeth glanced up, gray eyes catching firelight and lantern gold, cheeks pink, pencil paused. Her damp hair stuck to her neck, her sunscreen long washed away, freckles showing across her nose. "You are important," she said simply. "You're my anchor point. All of you. I just — I want to remember exactly how the rain sounded with us dry inside."

Percy swallowed. His cut-sleeve shirt suddenly felt too tight at the throat, his camp necklace too warm. "Can I keep that one? When it's done?"

She tore it out before he could blink — the rain study, not the people — and pressed it into his damp palm, graphite smudging. Her fingers lingered a second, cold then warm. "For your wall. So you remember we were here when the storm was loud and we were louder."

He folded it carefully and tucked it into his trunks pocket, where it would get damp anyway, and didn't care. "Best souvenir."

She leaned her head against his shoulder again, heavier this time, tired and content, her hair damp against his neck, her orange shirt soft. He let his cheek rest very lightly against her hair for three seconds, then straightened, grinning like an idiot at Clarisse's knowing smirk.

"What?" Percy mouthed.

Clarisse mouthed back, "Pond sap," and threw a card at him.

The lights flickered.

It happened the way summer storms do it — no warning except the air itself. One breath the lantern burned steady gold and the fire crackled orange and everything smelled of pine smoke and wet wool. The next, the fine hairs on Percy's arms stood straight up, the air went metallic and sharp, ozone flooding the boathouse like someone had cracked a battery open. The lantern flame jumped, blue at the edges. Static crawled over Thalia's spiked hair with an audible snap-crackle.

Percy felt it in his back teeth first, then in the lake-water still pooled around the doors — a tug, a hum, charge looking for somewhere to go.

"Oh," Annabeth said, very quietly, sitting bolt upright. Her sketchbook slid off her knees. Her damp hair lifted faintly off her shoulders. "Oh — sky's pooling."

Thalia was already moving, lieutenant-fast, bare feet slapping. "Talk to me, baby. Where?"

Annabeth's gray eyes went distant in the way Percy knew — reading weather the way other kids read comics. Her fingers tingled visibly, tiny blue sparks popping at the nails. She held her right hand up, two fingers extended, left hand tucked behind her back in perfect safety form without thinking. Drilled in. Winter buckets, cove drill, reservoir rules. Her orange shirt sleeves crackled, her denim shorts clung, her bare soles pressed flat to the wood.

"Roof line," Annabeth said, fast and calm. "West corner. Wire hook where the lantern chain meets the beam — it's acting like a rod. Charge building. Small. Practice-sized. But if it arcs to the wet wall —"

"It'll ride the damp down to the door pool," Jason finished, already kicking the thin sheet of lake water away from the doors with his bare feet, his rolled jeans splashing, his gray shirt snapping in static. "And we're all barefoot in a puddle. Okay. Okay, standard grounding?"

"Standard grounding," Thalia confirmed, stepping to Annabeth's side, shoulder to shoulder. Her black tank snapped with static, her cutoffs clung, her bare feet planted wide. She tucked her own left hand behind her back, mirroring her sister, and raised her right hand. Sparks danced between her knuckles, hot and white-gold, familiar. "I take point, you smooth? Like drill?"

Annabeth hesitated — one heartbeat, old habit flashing across her face. Let big sister take the risk. Stay in command. Stay safe. Percy saw it and held his breath, his cut-sleeve shirt suddenly cold, his swim trunks damp, his feet tingling where they touched wet wood. Last fall she would have stepped back. Last spring she would have called timing and let Thalia touch.

Now, at fourteen, after sixty-eight seconds in the infirmary and thirty-one seconds live in the cove, Annabeth Grace shook her head.

"No," she said, steady. "I take point. Small draw, my initiation. You spot. Like we practiced. I'm rested, I'm dry enough, Will cleared me for small work yesterday."

Thalia stared, then grinned wide and proud and a little wet-eyed. "Hell yes. Do it. I've got you."

"Reservoir rules?" Percy said quietly from the floor, meeting Annabeth's eyes. His hands curled on his knees, water-sense open and humming. He could feel the charge pooling over them like a held breath, taste copper on his tongue.

Annabeth met his gaze and held it, gray to green, calm. "Reservoir rules," she said. "You hold. I aim. Nobody plays hero."

"Pinky?" Percy said, holding up his own pinky across the two feet between them, solemn as an oath on the Styx.

Annabeth laughed, breathless, and linked air with him, finger to finger without touching. "Pinky. Tell me if it tingles wrong?"

"I promise," Percy said. "You promise you'll stop if you go dizzy?"

"I promise," Annabeth said. "Eighty seconds max. Probably ten."

Clarisse and Silena moved without being told, pond-team perfect. Clarisse grabbed the dry towels and threw them down over the door puddle, breaking the water path, her knotted shirt flying, her khaki shorts splashed, her braid-loosened hair crackling. Silena dragged the life-jacket cushions back from the wet zone, her thick braid swinging, blue tank pale in lantern light, white shorts dusty, her wrist ribbon flashing. Jason killed the lantern and kicked sand over the low fire until it smoked, smelling of snuffed pine, leaving only gray storm light through the high window and the blue-white flicker of gathering charge.

The boathouse went dim and humming. Rain pattered, gentler now. Thunder breathed far off, ten seconds out. The air tasted of metal and rain and cedar.

"Okay," Annabeth said, voice clear in the hush. "On three. One. Two —"

She touched the beam hook with two fingertips.

Light ran, small and clean.

Percy felt it arrive the way he always felt Annabeth in current — not Thalia's hot crack, not wild sky splitting, but a steady hum, a held note, soda bubbles up his spine. Blue-white-gold thread running from roof hook through her fingers, through her smoothing field, down through Thalia's spotting hand hovering a breath behind her wrist, into the grounding line they'd made with towels and rope and intent. The hook glowed faintly. The air sang. The hair on everyone's arms lifted, then settled.

Annabeth stood with left hand behind her back, right hand steady, damp hair lifting, orange shirt crackling, denim shorts damp, bare feet grounded on dry wood Silena had shoved under her just in time. Her face was calm, color high, gray eyes narrowed in focus. No pale thing. No wobble. A tiny spark popped on her nail and died, then another, steady as a metronome.

"Five seconds," Jason counted softly, his practiced counting voice low. "Six. Seven. Steady. Breathe, Annabeth."

"Eight. Nine," Thalia murmured, her own hand glowing faint where she smoothed the edges, white-gold braided with blue-white, sisters side by side in storm light. Her black tank glowed at the seams, her cutoffs damp, her bare feet steady. Pride all over her face. "Beautiful, baby. Clean edges. You're humming perfect."

Percy held the water still with pressure and intent, whispering to the thin lake sheet by the doors. Stay. Don't move. Don't give it somewhere else to go. The sea in him, even in fresh water, listened. The puddle stilled. The tug eased.

At twelve seconds Annabeth lifted her fingers away, clean. The glow faded. The hum died. The air went soft and normal, smelling of rain and snuffed fire and hot metal cooling.

For a second no one breathed. Then Thalia whooped so loud dust fell from the rafters, grabbed Annabeth around the waist, and spun her in a circle while Annabeth shrieked and laughed, damp hair flying, orange shirt twisting, denim shorts flashing.

"You did it!" Thalia shouted. "Initiated, held, released, live roof, no cove, no pool, no Will with honey tea! That's my sister! Sixty-eight seconds my ass — that was real!"

"I did it," Annabeth gasped, dizzy and grinning, color high, eyes wide. She stared at her fingertips, tiny red marks fading already. "Did it tingle wrong? Percy, tell me true."

Percy exhaled all at once, hands shaking faintly with leftover hum, his cut-sleeve shirt sticking to his back, his swim trunks cold. He met her eyes and grinned so wide his cheeks hurt. "Truth? Soda bubbles. Nice. Yours. Steady. Perfect."

Annabeth absorbed this like data and treasure at once, nodding slowly, the way she had in the cove drill. "Good. That means smoothing held through initiation even without water channel. That's what we've been training for."

With Thalia beside him, Jason checked her pulse with two fingers to her wrist, using the field first aid he had learned at Camp Jupiter, his gray shirt damp, his rolled jeans sandy. "Pulse fast but strong. Color good. Breathing good. Rest, hydrate, no second attempts. Chiron's orders still count."

"Yes, sir," Annabeth said meekly, accepting the water bottle Silena tossed her. She drank, wiped her mouth, and beamed at Percy over the bottle. He beamed back, still sitting on the floor with his knees up, his bare feet cold, his heart doing something complicated and tender and proud.

Clarisse clapped Annabeth on the shoulder hard enough to stagger her, grinning so hard it ruined her tough face. Her knotted shirt hung loose, her black top showed, her khaki shorts smelled of lake and smoke. "Okay, Sky Brat. That was — that was pretty good. Don't let it go to your head."

"It was amazing," Silena corrected, hugging Annabeth sideways, her thick braid thumping, blue tank soft, white shorts dusty, towel still around her shoulders. She smelled of strawberries and rain. "Our Mind. Our Sky. Our brave girl."

"No hero stuff," Annabeth said, leaning into both of them, her damp waves sticking to Silena's cheek, her orange shirt wrinkled. "Just grounding. Just practice. Thalia spotted the whole time. Percy held the water. You broke the path. It was all of us."

"Storm and Sea," Percy said, holding out his pinky. It trembled faintly with tiredness and pride.

"Storm and Sea," Clarisse repeated, linking hers through his immediately, strong and sure, her knuckles scraped, her hand warm.

Silena added hers, smiling sleepily, her wrist ribbon damp. "And Mind."

Thalia extended her pinky without getting up from where she'd flopped back onto the workbench, breathless, her black tank riding up, her cutoffs damp, her bare feet dirty. "And Sky, you idiots. Don't leave out Sky."

Annabeth leaned her head against Percy's shoulder, just for a second, just to reach, and linked her pinky through the knot of theirs. Her hand was warm from work, fingertips faintly red but relaxed. "Mind's here. Always."

They sat like that for a moment, five pinkies tangled over a cold lantern, while rain ticked softly on the roof and the last static faded off the beam.

After the surge, quiet.

Jason relit the lantern low, gold light blooming again over silvered walls, over the braid gleaming down Silena's back, over Clarisse's frizzed ponytail, over Thalia's spiked hair gone soft with damp, over Annabeth's loose waves. He left the fire snuffed — too warm now, air gone close and sweet. The boathouse smelled of rain easing, of wet rope drying, of cedar and smoke and six people breathing in sync.

Percy realized he was cold. Really cold, now that adrenaline faded. His cut-sleeve orange shirt was damp and clinging, his swim trunks clammy, his bare feet icy on the floorboards, his camp necklace cold against his throat. Goosebumps ran up his arms. Annabeth, beside him, shivered once, hard, her orange shirt dark at the shoulders, her denim shorts cold, her bare feet tucked under her thighs for warmth, her damp hair dripping onto her neck.

Without a word, Percy reached for the dry bundle by the door — his lake jacket, the lightweight navy windbreaker with the white Poseidon stripe he'd left in the dry-box that morning. It smelled of sun and canvas, still warm from the box. He shook it out and held it open.

"Here," he said quietly. "You're shivering."

Annabeth looked at him, gray eyes soft in lantern gold, rainwater still on her lashes, cheeks pink from work and cold. "You'll be cold."

"I'm Poseidon," Percy said, with exaggerated dignity that made Clarisse snort. "I'm waterproof. Mostly. Take it."

She took it. She slid her arms into the sleeves, too long for her, navy nylon swallowing her orange shirt, white stripe bright over her shoulders. It smelled like him — salt and lake and sun — and she pulled it tight around herself, tucking her damp hair inside the collar, her denim shorts hidden under the hem, her bare feet peeking out. She looked small and warm and impossibly dear.

"Thank you," she whispered, fingers clutching the zipper. Her ears went pink. "It smells like you."

"Good," Percy said stupidly, heart thudding. His cut-sleeve shirt stuck to his shoulders, his swim trunks dripped, but he felt warm all through. "I mean — good. Stay warm."

She leaned against him then, fully, without pretense or counting, her jacketed shoulder to his damp cotton shoulder, her head tipped to rest in the curve of his neck. Her damp waves tickled his throat, her jacket crinkled, her cold fingers found the hem of his shirt again and held it. He went very still, then very carefully let his own shoulder drop an inch so she had a better pillow, and did not move a fraction even as his arm started to fall asleep. Chaste and certain at fourteen, just a lean, just warmth shared while rain talked on the roof.

Thalia saw it and covered her mouth with both hands to stifle a squeal, failing completely. Silena saw it and pressed her hands to her heart, her braid over one shoulder, blue tank nearly dry, white shorts sandy. Clarisse saw it and pretended to gag, but she was grinning so hard it ruined the effect, her big hands resting on Silena's shoulders where she'd left them after braiding. Jason saw it and smiled solemnly, and turned the lantern down a touch to give them shadow.

"Young love," Jason sighed happily. "Plus excellent grounding technique."

"Shut up, little brother," Annabeth murmured without lifting her head, but she was smiling, her fingers tightening on Percy's shirt. Percy squeezed her jacketed shoulder back, once, lightly.

Outside, the storm was leaving.

Percy felt it first, the way he always did — pressure lifting off his teeth, tug easing in the door puddle, lake water settling from slap to lap. Rain thinned from roar to patter to tick, then to nothing but drips off the eaves going plink-plink-plink onto the dock planks. Thunder breathed once more, far east over the Sound, twelve seconds after lightning too pale to see through the window. Wind died in the pines. Cicadas started again, tentative, then full-throated.

Thalia unbolted the double doors and shoved them open.

Light poured in, silver-gold and wet. The world outside was washed clean and steaming faintly. The canoe lake lay flat and silver under a white sky, mist rising off it in slow white ribbons that smelled of warm mud and lilies and rain. Every leaf dripped. The dock planks shone dark gold. The overturned canoes outside glowed. A rainbow, faint and perfect, arched over the far reeds where a naiad surfaced to stare. The air was cool and sweet, pine sap and wet earth and sun coming back.

"Oh," Silena breathed, stepping out in her blue tank now drying pale, white shorts glowing, thick braid gleaming wet-black down her back, towel around her shoulders fluttering. Her coral toenails flashed on wet wood. "Oh, it's beautiful."

They filed out, blinking, stretching, steaming faintly in returning sun. Clarisse shook out her ponytail, her knotted shirt hanging loose, black top underneath, khaki shorts dripping, bare feet slapping warm puddles. Thalia pulled her leather jacket back on over her damp black tank, despite heat, because it was armor, her cutoffs frayed, her bare feet dirty, her hair spiking in steam. Jason shouldered a coil of rope, his gray shirt sticking, rolled jeans soaked, feet bare and muddy. Annabeth stepped out in Percy's navy jacket swallowing her, orange shirt underneath, denim shorts hidden, bare feet careful on wet boards, her sketchbook sleeve tucked under her arm.

Percy came last, in his damp cut-sleeve shirt and swim trunks, feet bare, hair standing up, camp necklace glinting. His trunks pocket held Annabeth's rain study, damp and safe.

They stood on the dock while mist curled off the lake and wrapped their ankles, cool and soft. No one spoke for a long time. There was nothing to say the lake didn't say better — lap-lap against the pilings, hollow wooden knock, dragonflies returning in blue flashes.

Finally Silena lifted her face to the sun breaking through, eyes closed, braid dripping. "Say it," she mumbled.

"Say what?" Percy asked, though he knew, smiling at Annabeth in his jacket leaning on his shoulder.

"The thing. The promise thing. From the reservoir. And the beacon. And the cove drill."

Clarisse snorted into her hands, but her eyes were soft, sunburn peeling on her nose. "She means the corny thing."

"It's not corny," Silena protested weakly. "It's important. Especially after practice. We have to say it after practice too, so we remember."

Percy held out his pinky. It was steady now, warm from sun. "Storm and Sea," he said.

Clarisse linked hers through his immediately, strong and sure. "Storm and Sea," she repeated.

Silena added hers, smiling sleepily, her wrist ribbon green in sun. "And Mind."

From the dock post Thalia extended her pinky without getting up from leaning. "And Sky, you idiots. Don't leave out Sky."

Annabeth leaned her jacketed shoulder harder into Percy's side, just for a second, just to reach, and linked her pinky through the knot of theirs. Her hand was warm from his jacket, fingertips faintly red from work but relaxed. Navy sleeve swallowed her wrist, white stripe bright.

"Mind's here," she murmured. "Always."

They stood like that for a moment, five pinkies tangled over misty water, while the last rain dripped off the eaves and the lake steamed white and gold.

Then Annabeth tugged Percy's sleeve, low, for him alone under the others' laughter. "Hi," she whispered, gray eyes catching sun and mist, cheeks pink, his jacket huge on her.

"Hi," Percy whispered back, bumping his shoulder against hers, holding her steady while mist curled around their bare feet. "Good trapped?"

Annabeth smiled, and leaned back into his shoulder, and watched mist burn off the lake with him. "Best trapped."

Behind them the boathouse doors swung faintly in the breeze, lantern still burning low inside, cards scattered on the dry-box lid, towels steaming on the floorboards — waiting dry for the next storm.

And over the canoe lake, rain cleared to mist, and mist cleared to gold.
