## Chapter 202: The Measure of a Gift

On Tuesday morning, Silena set a second sheet of paper beside the unfinished three-wave copy.

The first had the pencil lines Percy drew after their tide-pool walk and the washes she had chosen since. The second was only a rectangle divided into narrow bands. She wanted to test how the same blue changed with different amounts of water, then with light from the pavilion windows. It was a plain problem. Plain problems could still be hard.

Clarisse put a plate beside the rectangle. “You’re doing scales now?”

“Color tests.”

“What do you call the thing with lines?”

“A grid.”

“That sounds like a scale.”

“It’s not.”

“Then what is a scale?”

Silena dipped a spoon into the pot. “Something you weigh fish on.”

Clarisse considered that. “That’s a scale too.”

“Exactly.”

“Then this is a color scale.”

Silena gave up. “Fine.”

Clarisse moved the bread basket within reach. She didn’t look at the watercolor. “The pavilion table is open after breakfast. Chiron said the council maps aren’t using it.”

“Did he say that because he wants me there?”

“He said it because I asked whether the table was free.”

Silena looked at her sister.

Clarisse took the knife and began cutting bread. “I didn’t tell him what you were painting.”

“Thanks.”

“I know what a private thing looks like.”

Silena heard the roughness under it. Clarisse had been trying lately to separate care from control. She did not always manage it on the first attempt, but she was trying.

“I’m not keeping the whole thing secret,” Silena said. “I just want to work before people start having opinions.”

“That makes sense.”

Clarisse set the knife down. “I can tell them not to come by.”

“You don’t have to manage that either.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were about to.”

Clarisse’s mouth twitched. “I was about to ask if you wanted me to.”

Silena picked up the plate. “No. But you can eat with me.”

Clarisse sat. They divided the bread, the way they had done before either of them had a reason to think about it. Clarisse took the end piece. Silena kept the middle, which was softer. The pot steamed between them, warm and slow.

Percy arrived with Annabeth a few minutes later. Annabeth had the camp map rolled under one arm, and Percy had a ruler tucked into his belt.

“Did you steal that from Annabeth?” Silena asked.

“She said I could bring it.”

“I said he could carry it,” Annabeth corrected. “That’s not the same as borrowing.”

Percy lifted the ruler. “I am carrying it.”

“You’re also walking toward the pavilion.”

“I’m carrying it in that direction.”

Annabeth gave him a look. He grinned and held out the ruler to her. She took it, then looped her fingers through his for a moment before she set the map down. The exchange was as easy as breathing. They could disagree over a word and still find one another at the end of the sentence.

“Are you using the pavilion table?” Annabeth asked Silena.

“After breakfast.”

“I can leave the map at the Big House.”

“The table’s open. You can use the other end.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yes. I’m not taking the whole pavilion.”

Annabeth nodded. “Then I’ll keep the map away from your paint.”

“Please.”

Percy looked at the two watercolor sheets. “Is this part of the picture for the letter?”

“No.”

“Okay.”

He did not ask what it was for. Silena appreciated the restraint. She also noticed how much effort it took him not to ask a second question.

“Do you still want to make a copy of the three waves?” he said.

“I do.”

“Do you know when?”

“No.”

“Okay.”

He leaned a hip against the table. “I want to send it to Amphitrite eventually. But I don’t want you to feel like it has to be ready because I said that.”

Silena watched his face. He had not said it carelessly. He had already considered whether wanting the picture would make it harder for her to choose.

“I know,” she said. “I’m still deciding what it needs.”

“Then take the time.”

Annabeth put the map on the table and pressed the loose end flat. “He’s been trying not to make other people’s plans for them.”

Percy looked at her. “I can answer for myself.”

“I know.”

“Then why did you say it?”

“Because I wanted you to hear that I noticed.”

His expression changed. Not much. Enough.

“Thanks,” he said.

Annabeth slid the ruler beneath the map to keep it from curling. Percy bent and kissed her temple, quick and familiar. Then he took the seat beside her and helped anchor the corners. They began discussing whether the damp path by the forge would stay firm after another day without rain. Neither one took the map from the other. The conversation belonged to them.

“Do you want to walk after lunch?” Percy asked.

Annabeth checked the pencil note. “I want to finish the map first.”

“That’s fine.”

“I know it is.”

He nudged the ruler toward her. “I wasn’t asking you to stop.”

“I know.”

Silena caught the softness in Annabeth’s voice. She and Percy had been together long enough to hear the question beneath the words and still ask the words out loud. Percy did not move the map or tell her the route could wait. Annabeth did not treat his invitation as a demand.

“After lunch,” she said. “If the path dries.”

“If it doesn’t?”

“We’ll decide then.”

“Okay.”

He leaned close and rested his forehead against hers for a brief second. “I’ll see you later.”

“You will.”

Neither of them tried to turn the plan into a promise bigger than the afternoon. Percy went to fetch another cup. Annabeth kept marking the route. The quiet between them stayed comfortable.

Silena gathered her paints and the two sheets. “I’ll see you at lunch.”

“We’ll be around,” Percy said.

“Not at my table.”

He glanced at Annabeth. “Apparently we have a table now.”

“Only until lunch,” Silena said.

Annabeth smiled. “We’ll remember.”

The pavilion had a clear place by the windows and a bowl of clean water at the far end. Silena set up her tin, jar, two brushes, scrap cloth, and portfolio. She placed the three-wave copy face down, then weighed the new grid with a smooth stone. She took enough room for her elbows and left the rest of the table empty.

For a while, she tested the blue.

The first strip spread too far. The second dried lighter than she expected. She wrote down the ratio beside it, then put a small cross where the paper had buckled. The next one held a dark seam at the edge. She marked that too.

The exercise had nothing to do with the picture being made for Percy. It had nothing to do with Amphitrite. It did not need to become a gift.

Silena liked that.

She turned the sheet and began another row. When the pigment reached the paper’s edge, the blue gathered in a thin line. She lifted the brush and let it settle. There was a measure of water that made the color run, and another that let it stay. She wrote neither down as a rule.

Around noon, Silena’s brush stopped gliding.

She rinsed it, dried the handle, and tried again. The bristles had begun to split at the tip. She could mend them with a thread wrap later, but for now she changed to the second brush. The test strip had dried more evenly than the first. She wrote *thin layer, wait before second* beside it.

The pavilion doors opened. Silena looked up, expecting Chiron with the stack of folded cloth he kept near the serving table. Amphitrite stepped inside instead, carrying no bundle and wearing the same dark shawl from her last visit.

Chiron had told Silena that Amphitrite planned to be in camp for several days to spend time with Percy and Clarisse. He had left Silena free to choose whether she joined that time.

She stopped just inside the doorway. “Is this a bad time?”

Silena glanced at the paper. “I’m working.”

“I can leave.”

Silena considered the page. The next row could wait. “You can sit at the other end. I’m not ready to show you anything.”

“I won’t ask.”

Amphitrite took the seat across from her, leaving the paint and the empty chair between them. She put her hands on the table and waited.

Silena thought of something to say, then let the thought go. She mixed more blue and tested the new brush. The line came out broad and uneven.

“Too much water?” Amphitrite asked.

“Too much pressure.”

“Can you fix it?”

“It’s a test. It can be bad.”

Amphitrite nodded. “Then it can stay bad.”

Silena looked up. The answer had not tried to turn the mark into something useful. She liked that.

“Were you coming to see Percy?” she asked.

“I wanted to speak with him. He’s with Annabeth now.”

“Do you want me to tell him?”

“No. I’ll wait until he’s finished with the map.”

“You can sit there until then.”

“Thank you.”

They sat without talking for a few minutes. Outside, someone dragged a chair across the porch. The sound stopped, then started again. Silena kept the brush moving.

“Do you want water?” Amphitrite asked.

“The tin cup is clean.”

“I meant to drink.”

“I have the jar.”

“That’s paint water.”

Silena looked at the cups, then laughed. “You’re right.”

Amphitrite stood and went to the side table. She filled a second cup from the pitcher and brought it back. She did not touch the jar or the paper. Silena drank, and the water was cool.

“Thank you.”

“You’re welcome.”

The silence came back. It was not the same silence as before. Amphitrite had done a small thing that did not require Silena to accept a lesson, a present, or an invitation. Silena could say thank you and continue what she was doing.

After a while, she put the brush down. “I’ve written to my mother before.”

Amphitrite did not lean closer. “Do you want to talk about that?”

“Not what I wrote.”

“All right.”

“I don’t know if the last letter got there.”

“That can be hard.”

Silena watched her face. Amphitrite had said *can be*, not *must be*. She had left a place for Silena’s own answer.

“Sometimes it’s easier when I don’t know,” Silena said. “If I don’t know, I don’t have to decide what it means.”

Amphitrite nodded once. “I won’t tell you what it means.”

“I know.”

“And I won’t ask what was in it.”

“Thank you.”

Silena moved the wet paper a few inches away from the cup. “I don’t want you to think you’re supposed to replace anything.”

“I don’t.”

“I know that’s not what you’re doing.”

“Still,” Amphitrite said. “You don’t owe me a place because I’m someone’s mother.”

Silena’s fingers settled on the edge of the portfolio. The folded letter was inside. She had put it there because it belonged among her own papers, not because it was a secret she was ready to hand over.

“You don’t have to say that,” Silena said.

“I wanted to.”

“Why?”

“Because I have spent a long time being told what a family should look like. I don’t want to make you fit one of my ideas.”

Silena turned the brush between her fingers. “I don’t know what I want it to look like.”

“That’s allowed.”

“Does it bother you?”

“It can be difficult not to know where I stand.”

“That sounds like it bothers you.”

“It does sometimes.” Amphitrite looked down at her own hands. “That doesn’t make it yours to fix.”

Silena sat with that for a moment. There was a truth in it that felt different from a promise. Amphitrite was not pretending the uncertainty was easy. She was also not asking Silena to make it easier.

“I don’t want you to be a person who only visits when someone needs something,” Silena said.

“I don’t want that either.”

“What do you want?”

“To be someone you can ask to sit with you, if you want company.”

“And if I don’t?”

“Then I’ll find something else to do.”

Silena looked at her. “You wouldn’t be angry?”

“I might be disappointed. I would still leave.”

The answer was plain. No shine, no oath. Silena nodded and returned to the test strip.

“Would you like to hold that cup while I move the board?” she asked.

Amphitrite lifted it from the table. “Yes.”

Silena slid the painted paper toward the window, away from the draft. Neither of them said the exchange meant more than it did.

Percy came through the pavilion door after the midday meeting, with Annabeth beside him and the camp map tucked beneath his arm. He stopped when he saw Amphitrite.

“Hi, Mom,” he said.

“Hello, Percy.”

He glanced at Silena’s paper and then away. “Are you using the table?”

“Yes,” Silena said. “You can have the other end.”

“Thanks.”

Annabeth unrolled the map. Percy kept a hand on the corner while she set down the ruler. The two of them had learned how to share a table without negotiating every inch of it; they still asked when the answer mattered.

“Did you see the muddy mark?” he asked.

“I did.”

“I thought it was farther uphill.”

“It was yesterday. It moved after the rain.”

“Then we should mark both.”

Annabeth looked at him. “That makes the route look worse.”

“It is worse.”

“Until it dries.”

“People walk there before it dries.”

She traced the alternate path with the ruler. “We can show the dry route and note the temporary one.”

“Okay.”

He let her make the mark. The map shifted under his hand, and he held it steady. When she finished, she looked up at him. Percy’s expression had been tense; it eased when she met his eyes.

“Thanks,” he said.

“For holding paper?”

“For not making me prove I’m right before we fix it.”

Annabeth tucked the pencil behind her ear. “You don’t have to be right to tell me what you see.”

He smiled. “That’s good, because I’m wrong a lot.”

“Not about everything.”

He leaned closer, and she kissed his cheek. It was brief, ordinary, and theirs. They went back to the map.

Silena returned to the swatches. She could hear their low conversation but not every word. She did not need to. They were not trying to include her in the exchange. That was its own courtesy.

Amphitrite waited until they finished with the map before standing. “I should go.”

“You can stay,” Silena said.

“I was waiting for Percy.”

“I know.”

Percy looked up. “I’m done.”

“Then I’ll walk you to the gate,” Amphitrite said.

He glanced at Annabeth. “Are you coming?”

“I’ll finish the route note and meet you at the cabins.”

“I can wait.”

“You can. I’d rather finish it.”

He studied her face, then nodded. “I’ll see you there.”

Annabeth tapped the map with the end of the pencil. “Don’t take the ruler.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You have it in your hand.”

Percy looked down at the ruler. “I was holding it.”

“Keep holding it until you put it down.”

He laughed, set it beside her, and left with Amphitrite. She did not turn back to Silena before they went out. Silena wondered if she had thought about it, then reminded herself that a person could leave a room without sending a message.

When Annabeth finished the note, she rolled the map and came over to the paint table.

“Do you want us to clear the other end?” she asked.

“No. You can leave it.”

“Then I’ll come back later for the map.”

“Take your time.”

Annabeth smiled. “I will.”

She found Percy at the gate after the bend. Silena watched them through the open doors. They walked together toward the cabins, Percy carrying the map under one arm and Annabeth holding the ruler. Their shoulders bumped once. Neither moved away.

Silena turned back to the grid. She had three blue bands she liked and four she did not. That was enough information for today.

The next afternoon, she asked Chiron whether the pavilion table would be free after the meal.

“It should be,” he said. “Do you need it?”

“I want to use it for a while.”

“Then I’ll tell the kitchen crew to leave the window end clear.”

“You don’t have to save it.”

“I’m not saving it. I’m telling you what is already open.”

“Okay.”

He waited. “Would you like Amphitrite to know you’ll be there?”

Silena considered the question. “If she’s coming to camp anyway, you can tell her I’ll be painting.”

“Would you like me to invite her?”

“No. I’d like you to tell her where I’ll be. She can decide if she wants to come.”

“Understood.”

Silena took the grid home. The paper was dry enough to tuck into the portfolio, but she left the unfinished wave copy in place. Its blue had gone lighter at the edge. The line looked different now that she knew what it would do.

Clarisse was mending a strap by the fire. “You got the table?”

“I asked for it.”

“Good.”

“I didn’t ask her to come.”

Clarisse pulled the thread through the canvas. “That’s good too.”

Silena watched her tie off the end. “You don’t like the idea of her coming?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“You can say that.”

“I just did.”

Silena took the other chair. “I want to know her.”

Clarisse kept her eyes on the strap. “I know.”

“I don’t want that to mean I’m ready to call her anything.”

“I didn’t say you were.”

“You were going to ask.”

“I was going to ask how you felt.”

“That’s different.”

“Is it?”

“It’s a little different.”

Clarisse set the strap aside and looked at her. “Then how do you feel?”

“Interested. Nervous. A little stupid for being nervous.”

“You don’t look stupid.”

“Good.”

Clarisse pushed the needle and thread toward the middle of the table. “Want to fix this side?”

Silena examined the loose seam. “I can.”

“Thanks.”

They worked in quiet for a while, each on one end of the strap. Clarisse's stitch stayed even and tight. Silena’s first pass wandered, then came straighter after she loosened the thread. Clarisse did not correct her hand. She waited until Silena asked.

“Is this going to hold?” Silena said.

“It’ll hold until the next repair.”

“That’s not very reassuring.”

“It isn’t supposed to be forever.”

Silena smiled. “I think it’s fine.”

Clarisse nodded and went back to her side.

On Wednesday afternoon, the pavilion table was open.

Silena arrived with the portfolio beneath her arm and a small jar of clean water in her hand. She had brought no lunch, only a piece of bread wrapped in cloth. At the window, the light moved across the wooden table in a wide yellow stripe.

The grid from yesterday stayed in the portfolio. She took out a fresh sheet and drew three rectangles. The first would hold the blue she had already tested. The second would hold a gray wash. The third she left blank.

She had just mixed the gray when Amphitrite came in.

“I was told you might be here,” she said. “Would you like company?”

Silena glanced at the open chair. “If you don’t mind being quiet.”

“I can do that.”

Amphitrite sat opposite her, taking care not to put her sleeves near the water. She had brought no box, no food, no folded cloth. Her hands were empty.

Silena began to paint. The gray ran thinly through the first rectangle, and she stopped before it reached the border. She added one more drop of pigment and waited.

Amphitrite watched the paper. She did not lean over it. Silena had given permission for company, not for a critique.

“Would you like to try a strip?” Silena asked.

“I have never painted with watercolors.”

“That’s all right.”

“I might do it badly.”

“Everyone does, when they start.”

“That is reassuring.”

“It isn’t supposed to be.”

Amphitrite smiled. “Then I’ll try.”

Silena tore a blank strip from the end of her practice paper and set it between them. She put a clean brush beside it. “Wet the brush first. Not too much. Then let the color pull from the edge.”

Amphitrite dipped the brush into the water, then into the blue. She pressed it to the paper. The pigment spread quickly, leaving a dark pool in the center and a pale run at the edge.

She lifted the brush. “That was too much.”

“Too much water?”

“Too much of everything.”

Silena laughed. “You can leave it.”

“Can I?”

“It’s only a test.”

Amphitrite looked at the shape, then nodded. “I thought I should correct it.”

“You can, if you want.”

“I think I want to see what it does.”

The answer pleased Silena. She turned back to her own page. They worked in parallel for a while. The water dried faster near the window. Amphitrite’s blue held a dark center; Silena’s gray gathered along the edge and left the middle pale. Neither one tried to make the other sheet match.

“Do you like that?” Silena asked.

Amphitrite studied the blue pool. “I like the part I didn’t plan.”

“You like the mistake?”

“I like that it happened.” She tilted her head. “That isn’t the same as wanting it in the finished piece.”

“Exactly.”

“I thought you would say that.”

“Why?”

“Because you’ve said no to me before.”

Silena set her brush down. “That doesn’t mean I’m going to say no every time.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Amphitrite took a moment. “I’m learning to ask the question without building a preferred answer into it.”

Silena looked at the page again. “That might take practice.”

“It has.”

Amphitrite rinsed the brush. A streak of blue remained near the ferrule. “I should have asked before I suggested you show me the larger picture last time.”

“You did ask.”

“I did. But I was already hoping you would.”

“That’s allowed.”

“I know. I am trying to make sure the hope doesn’t become an expectation.”

Silena understood the difference. It was not that wanting something made it unfair. It was what a person did with the want after hearing no.

“You can hope,” Silena said. “Just don’t tell me what the answer is supposed to be.”

“I can do that.”

Silena did not answer immediately. She turned to the three rectangles and measured the thin gray wash. “The first one needs another layer.”

“Can I ask why?”

“Yes.”

“The color looks even.”

“Even isn’t the same as finished.”

“Is that true of the painting too?”

Silena saw the unfinished three-wave copy in the portfolio. “Sometimes.”

Amphitrite nodded without asking to see it.

They worked until the window stripe moved off the table. The blue pool dried with a dark ring around it. The gray line stayed light. Silena wrote the drying time on the back of her sheet, then handed Amphitrite a pencil.

“Would you record yours?”

“Why?”

“So we know how long it took.”

Amphitrite wrote *blue, too much water, dry by the second bell*. Her letters were smaller than Silena’s, neat and close together.

Silena read it. “You don't need to call it too much.”

“It was too much.”

“It was the amount you used.”

“That sounds less judgmental.”

“It is.”

Amphitrite crossed out *too much* and wrote *deep pool*. Silena watched her do it. The words were not more accurate, exactly, but they left room for the mark to be what it was.

The dark ring did not vanish. It became part of the sample.

On Thursday morning, Silena put the dried color tests into her portfolio. She left Amphitrite’s strip in the middle of the table, where its dark ring caught the light.

The mark was uneven. That was what she liked about it.

Percy came down first. He stopped when he saw the new sample.

“Is this the one she painted?”

“Mm-hm.”

“She did that?”

“After I showed her where to start.”

He leaned closer, then remembered himself and stepped back. “Can I look?”

“You can look from there.”

He did. “It’s darker in the middle.”

“That happens when the water gathers.”

“Is that bad?”

“Not if you want it.”

He nodded. “I like the edge.”

Silena looked up at him. “You don’t have to like it.”

“I know. I do.”

“Okay.”

He pulled a chair out and sat on the far side of the table. Annabeth arrived with two folded sheets of paper and a line of graphite on her thumb. She glanced at the sample.

“Is it yours?” she asked Silena.

“Amphitrite’s.”

“Did she leave it?”

“I asked her to try it.”

Annabeth looked at the blue, then at Silena. “Do you want to keep it?”

“I haven’t decided.”

“Then you can wait.”

Percy watched Annabeth. “That’s what I said.”

“You asked if it was bad.”

“I wanted to know.”

“Then you asked.”

Silena found herself smiling. Percy reached across the table and took Annabeth’s hand. He turned it palm-up to see the graphite mark.

“You’ve been drawing on yourself again,” he said.

“It’s pencil.”

“It doesn’t come off.”

“It does if you wash your hands.”

“Do you want me to get water?”

“I can get it.”

“Okay.”

He did not stand. Annabeth looked at him, then leaned her shoulder into his. “You can come with me.”

“Now?”

“When I’m done.”

He nodded and returned to his bread. The map he’d brought stayed rolled beneath his chair. They did not make every offer into an obligation. Silena thought of how often she had heard adults say a family should be able to read each other’s minds, as if needing to ask was proof of distance. Percy and Annabeth kept asking anyway.

Clarisse came in carrying a small envelope.

Silena’s stomach tightened before she recognized her own handwriting on the front.

“Where did you find that?”

“Under your portfolio. It was sticking out.”

“You didn’t open it?”

Clarisse’s eyebrows rose. “No.”

Silena took the envelope. She had written it to Maria months ago. The seal was intact. The letter had not been sent; she had left it in her portfolio instead of deciding what to do with it.

“I didn’t know it was still there,” she said.

“I can put it back.”

“No. I’ll do it.”

Clarisse held out her hand. “Do you want me to leave?”

“You can stay.”

“Do you want me to ask about it?”

“No.”

“Okay.”

Clarisse returned to the stove. The conversation ended there. Silena put the envelope on the table beside Amphitrite’s test. Two pieces of paper, neither one ready to travel.

Percy followed her gaze. “Is that the letter you wrote to your mom?”

“Yes.”

“Do you want to talk about it?”

“Not yet.”

“All right.”

He did not ask if it was about Amphitrite or if she planned to send it. Annabeth watched him for a beat, then slipped her hand into his again. This time he took it without looking down.

Silena picked up the envelope and returned it to the portfolio. The letter stayed sealed. She could read it later or leave it there. The decision did not have to be made before breakfast.

“Are you going to the pavilion?” Clarisse asked.

“After I finish the page.”

“Do you want to take that sample?”

“I don’t know.”

Clarisse nodded toward it. “Then don’t.”

“I might want it later.”

“Then take it later.”

Silena laughed. “You make everything sound simple.”

“I make the question simple. The answer can be whatever it is.”

That was the sort of thing Clarisse said when she was tired of being the person who had to have an answer for everyone else. Silena could hear the effort behind it.

“I’m going to bring it,” she said. “If I want to leave it with her, I’ll decide there.”

“Okay.”

“Is that a bad idea?”

“It’s your paper.”

“Not mine. Hers.”

“Then ask her.”

Clarisse sat across from her. “You don’t have to hand it over because she made it, either.”

“I know.”

“I’m not saying you don’t.”

Silena touched the portfolio clasp. “I know that too.”

Clarisse took the plate from the table. “Good.”

After breakfast, Percy and Annabeth went to wash their hands. They walked out together, Percy holding the door while Annabeth tried to balance the folded pages and the ruler. She laughed when the ruler slid under her arm. He caught it before it hit the floor.

“Smooth,” she said.

“Very.”

They turned back toward each other at the path and kissed, quick and familiar, then headed to the Big House side by side.

Silena carried her portfolio toward the pavilion. The sample went into the outer pocket, where she could reach it without opening the letter.

In the pavilion, the window end was clear. Silena set down her portfolio and took out the test sheet. She placed Amphitrite’s sample next to the new page.

She did not know yet whether she wanted to keep it or give it back.

Amphitrite came in a little while later. She carried a cup from the Big House but nothing else.

“Would you mind if I sit?” she asked.

“You can.”

“Would you like me to bring the sample?”

“I brought it.”

Amphitrite set her cup down. “I thought you might.”

“Why?”

“I wasn’t sure. I hoped you would.”

Silena put the paper between them. “I wanted to see if it changed after a day.”

“It looks lighter.”

“It did.”

“Does that make the first one wrong?”

“No. It means the first one was still wet.”

Amphitrite leaned forward, but did not touch it. “Do you want to paint another?”

“I’m not sure.”

“Would you like me to?”

Silena glanced at her. “You want to paint?”

“I would like to try again. I’d also be happy to sit here without making a mark.”

Silena slid the clean brush toward her. “Then make one.”

Amphitrite took it. This time she wiped it along the rim of the water cup before touching the pigment. The line began pale, then deepened where the paper dipped.

“Better?” she asked.

“Different.”

“Is that your way of saying it’s good?”

“It’s my way of saying it’s different.”

Amphitrite looked at the brush in her hand. “You don’t praise people just to keep them trying.”

“I can say something is good when it is.”

“And this?”

“This is a line.”

Amphitrite laughed. “Fair.”

The laugh loosened the afternoon. Silena painted her own band of gray and let it dry beside the blue. The two colors met at the edge, but neither ran into the other.

“How do you know when to stop?” Amphitrite asked.

“When I don’t want to change it anymore.”

“That sounds like the answer.”

“It’s not always. Sometimes I want to change it and I know I’ll make it worse.”

“What do you do then?”

“Stop anyway.”

Amphitrite sat back. “I’ve made decisions because I thought a little more effort would make them better.”

“Did it?”

“Sometimes.” She smiled, but it faded quickly. “Sometimes I only made them harder to undo.”

Silena waited. She did not ask what decisions. Amphitrite had offered a sentence, not opened a door.

“You can leave it for a while,” Silena said. “See what it looks like when it dries.”

“I think I can do that.”

They worked quietly. Silena could hear the dining pavilion in use, chairs scraping and someone laughing near the kitchen. The table did not feel private, but it felt separate enough. Nobody came over to see what she was painting. Nobody asked if she would finish before dinner.

When the second blue line had dried, Silena picked up Amphitrite’s first sample. The dark pool had softened to a ring. A lighter center showed through.

“Would you like to keep it?” she asked.

Amphitrite looked at the strip. “Are you giving it to me?”

“If you want it.”

“I do.”

“Then you can have it.”

“Thank you.”

Silena waited for a question about what it meant. It did not come.

“Do you want me to keep the second one too?” Amphitrite asked.

“You can decide after it dries.”

“That sounds fair.”

“Does it?”

“I think so.”

Silena took the first strip and wrote the date on its back. She did not add a title. Amphitrite folded the paper once, then stopped.

“Don’t fold it,” Silena said. “The pigment will crack.”

“Right. I’m sorry.”

“Just keep it flat.”

Amphitrite laid it between two clean pages. “I’ll take care of it.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I know.” She looked at the paper. “I want to.”

Silena closed the portfolio. The strip belonged to Amphitrite now, but the blue remained only blue. It did not explain who Silena was, or what their relationship would become. She liked that it could travel without carrying those answers.

---

Clarisse found Silena at the pavilion after dinner. The table had been cleared, and the paint was packed away. Silena was writing the drying notes on the back of the grid.

“Are you coming back to the cabin?” Clarisse asked.

“In a minute.”

“Do you want company?”

Silena looked up. Clarisse had her jacket on and a coil of new cord looped over one shoulder. “You don’t have to wait for me.”

“I know.”

“Do you want to?”

“I do.”

Silena finished the line and put the pencil down. “Okay.”

Clarisse leaned against the table. “I saw Amphitrite this afternoon.”

“How was that?”

“She asked whether I wanted to walk with her.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

“Did you tell her?”

“I said I had somewhere to be.”

Silena looked at the coil of cord. “Did you?”

“I do now.”

“Where?”

“Back at the cabin.”

Silena smiled. “That counts.”

Clarisse rolled the cord into a smaller coil. “I don’t know what she wants from me.”

“You could ask.”

“I don’t want to.”

“That’s allowed.”

“I know.”

Silena picked up the portfolio. “Do you want me to say something to her?”

“No.”

“Okay.”

Clarisse shifted her weight. “I’m not trying to stop you from spending time with her.”

“I know.”

“I don’t want you thinking that me saying no means you have to say no too.”

“I don’t.”

“Good.”

Silena watched her. Clarisse did not often explain herself twice. She did now because the subject mattered enough to make the words awkward.

“I like her,” Silena said. “I don’t know her.”

“That makes sense.”

“I liked the afternoon.”

Clarisse nodded, then looked away. “That’s good.”

“Does it bother you?”

“It makes me nervous.”

“That’s different.”

“Not always.”

Silena closed the portfolio. “I’m not going to make you do anything.”

“I know.”

“I don’t need you to decide first whether she’s safe for me.”

Clarisse’s eyes returned to hers. “I have a hard time knowing when to stop doing that.”

“You can ask.”

“I did.”

“You asked if I wanted company.”

Clarisse glanced at the door. “I meant it.”

Silena touched her arm. “Then stay.”

They walked back to Cabin Three together. The camp path had cooled after sunset, and the wind pushed leaves along the ground in small circles. Clarisse carried the cord. Silena carried her portfolio. Neither of them asked the other to trade.

At the cabin, Percy had the window blanket pulled loose again. He was trying to tie its upper corner to a nail while Annabeth held the chair steady.

“You’re going to need a shorter cord,” Clarisse said.

“I have one,” Percy replied.

“Where?”

He looked at Annabeth.

“It’s in your pocket,” she said.

Percy found it in his pocket. “Right.”

Annabeth laughed under her breath. She kept a hand on the chair until he had climbed down, then touched his elbow. He leaned into the touch before they both turned to look at the blanket.

“Here,” Clarisse said, offering her coil.

Percy took the short length he needed, then handed the rest back. “Thanks.”

“Don’t use all of it.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

Clarisse gave him a look.

Annabeth took the cord and threaded it through the blanket loop. Percy held the fabric taut while she tied the knot. It took them two tries to get the tension even. On the second, Annabeth pulled the knot too tight and Percy said, “That’s going to tear the cloth.”

“I know.” She loosened it by a fraction.

“Now?”

“Now it holds.”

He checked the blanket with two fingers. It stayed in place. Annabeth bumped his shoulder, and he tapped his forehead against hers before stepping away.

“Good enough until the right pin arrives,” Clarisse said.

“That’s what Silena said about the strap.”

“Did she?”

“Earlier.”

Silena folded her portfolio beneath her bunk. “The paper is more patient than the blanket.”

“Everything is more patient than the blanket,” Percy said.

Annabeth turned off the lantern by the window. “We can fix it tomorrow.”

“We?” Percy asked.

“If you want to help.”

“I do.”

“Then yes.”

Their voices faded as they headed toward the door, still talking about the map and the weather. They walked out together, Percy carrying Annabeth’s ruler this time without pretending that he was only holding it.

Clarisse set the new cord by the window. “The old knot slipped.”

“It did.”

“Do you want me to fix it now?”

“No. It can wait.”

Clarisse shrugged. “Okay.”

She did not stand there to make sure Silena went to bed. Silena appreciated that more than the new cord.

Before she climbed into her bunk, she checked that the letter to Maria was still in the portfolio. The envelope remained sealed. She put the portfolio back in its place and turned off the lamp.

She did not know whether she would send the letter. She did not know what it would mean if her mother answered. The uncertainty stayed in the dark with her, not solved by Amphitrite’s visit or Clarisse’s worry.

That was all right for one night.

---

On Friday morning, Silena wrote a note and left it with Chiron.

She did not write *I need something*. She did not ask Amphitrite to come see a painting. She wrote: *If you are still at camp this afternoon, would you like to walk with me? You can say no.*

Chiron read it once. “Do you want me to send this?”

“Yes.”

“I’ll give it to her.”

Silena folded another sheet of paper while he carried the note away. She had no answer yet. That was the point of asking.

At lunch, Clarisse noticed the blank space beside her plate. “Are you waiting for her?”

“No.”

“Are you hoping she comes?”

Silena set her fork down. “A little.”

Clarisse nodded. “That’s different.”

“Is it?”

“It is if you want it to be.”

Silena ate the rest of her meal. She did not spend the afternoon checking every sound at the door. She helped move the empty cups to the kitchen, then went outside to collect the folded towels that had dried on the rail. By the time she returned, the pavilion was mostly empty.

Amphitrite was standing by the window with a small paper envelope in her hand. It was the sheet they had placed around the blue sample.

“I got your note,” she said.

“Do you want to walk?”

“I do.”

“Then we can.”

They left the pavilion without taking anything with them. Silena did not carry the portfolio. Amphitrite did not carry the sample.

The path looped between the cabins and the practice field. A group of younger campers were arguing over a borrowed rake. One had put it down across the path; the other wanted it returned to the shed before dinner. They stopped to let the two campers pass, neither offering to decide who was right.

“Do you ever miss being able to settle things with one word?” Silena asked.

“Sometimes.”

“Does it work?”

“Not as often as I think it will.”

Silena laughed. “That sounds familiar.”

Amphitrite smiled. “I hear the same thing from my sons.”

“Which one?”

“More than one.”

Silena could have asked which stories. She didn’t. Amphitrite did not name them.

They walked the next stretch without trying to fill the quiet. Near the archery field, Silena saw Thalia sitting on the porch with the splint. She lifted a hand in greeting. Thalia lifted hers back. No one spoke about the heel.

“Are you tired?” Amphitrite asked.

“Not yet.”

“Would you like to turn back?”

“No.”

“Good.”

The answer made Silena glance at her. “Good?”

“I like walking with you.”

“You could’ve said that.”

“I did.”

“You said good.”

“Both can be true.”

Silena smiled. “I guess.”

They passed the path to the Big House. A breeze brought the smell of wood smoke from the kitchens. Silena asked Amphitrite what they were cooking.

“I don’t know.”

“You haven’t been inside?”

“Not yet.”

“Do you want to guess?”

“Soup.”

“Everything becomes soup if you wait long enough.”

“I’ve learned that.”

Silena looked at her. “You don’t have to make conversation.”

“I know.”

“Sometimes I go quiet when I don’t know what to say.”

“I can sit quietly.”

“Would you?”

“Yes.”

Silena slowed near a flat stone beside the path. “Then let’s sit for a minute.”

Amphitrite sat at the other end of the stone. She did not reach across the space between them. Silena could hear the practice field in the distance, the clack of arrows against straw and the call for a pause. She could hear the trees moving overhead.

“Did you bring the sample?” Silena asked.

“I left it in the Big House. I didn’t want to fold it.”

“Good.”

“I hope you don’t mind that I kept it.”

“I gave it to you.”

“I know. I still wanted to make sure.”

Silena shifted her shoes on the gravel. “You can keep it.”

“Thank you.”

“You don’t have to bring it back next time.”

“I won’t.”

They sat for another few minutes. Silena did not know what made the walk different from the first time Amphitrite had visited. No gift had arrived. No promise had been made. She had asked to see her, and Amphitrite had come because she wanted to.

When they stood, neither reached for the other’s hand. They walked back to the pavilion at the same pace.

“Would you like to eat with us?” Amphitrite asked.

“Who is us?”

“Clarisse, Percy, and Annabeth. Chiron said there may be enough soup for everyone.”

“I can come.”

“Only if you want.”

“I know.”

Silena understood that answer now as a small thing they were learning how to say to each other.

At the Big House, the soup was lentil with carrots. Silena had guessed wrong.

Chiron set the pot in the middle of the table. “There’s bread too.”

“Is it from the basket?” Percy asked.

“It’s from the kitchen.”

“Is that a different basket?”

“Yes.”

Percy accepted the answer and reached for the bowl. Annabeth put a hand over the ladle.

“Wait until everyone is seated.”

“I was reaching for the bowl.”

“You were reaching for the ladle.”

“I could have been reaching for the bowl.”

“You weren’t.”

Silena watched them. Percy let his hand drop. Annabeth handed the ladle to him instead of serving him. He filled his bowl, then passed it back.

“Thank you,” he said.

“You’re welcome.”

He kissed her shoulder before she sat down. Annabeth leaned into him, then took her place by Silena. They were easy with each other in a way that did not erase the little negotiations. Silena liked watching them keep choosing the next small thing.

Amphitrite sat beside Chiron. She did not take the empty chair next to Silena until Silena gestured toward it.

“Here is fine,” Silena said.

“Thank you.”

Clarisse sat across the table. She nodded to Amphitrite, then reached for the bread.

“Did you walk?” she asked Silena.

“We did.”

“Was it cold?”

“A little.”

“Did you want a coat?”

“I had one.”

Clarisse glanced at the chair where Silena had left her jacket. “Good.”

Amphitrite took a spoonful of soup. “It’s good.”

Chiron looked pleased. “I’ll accept that.”

“It needs more salt,” Clarisse said.

“Then add some,” Chiron replied.

Clarisse passed the salt without a complaint. Silena tasted the soup. It was warm and soft, the carrots still holding their shape. She added a pinch of salt.

Amphitrite noticed the amount. “You take less than Clarisse.”

“I like the lentils.”

“So does she,” Clarisse said. “She just wants the whole bowl to taste like salt.”

“I don’t.”

“You do.”

“Not the whole bowl.”

Percy reached for the salt. “What about me?”

“You put too much on everything,” Annabeth said.

“I don’t.”

“You put it on bread.”

“It’s good on bread.”

“Not with soup.”

“It is if you dip the bread.”

Annabeth dipped hers and shook her head. “Still too much.”

He took it from her. “You’re impossible.”

“You’ve said that before.”

“I’ll say it again.”

She smiled at him. “I know.”

The table stayed loud around them. Nobody turned to Amphitrite and asked her to explain how she had learned to be a mother. Nobody asked Silena whether she had decided what the visit meant. The soup came and went. Percy asked for more bread. Clarisse told him to take the end piece. Annabeth asked Chiron whether the map would be needed before breakfast. Chiron said it would keep until the morning. No one made a ceremony out of the fact that Amphitrite was there.

When the bowls were nearly empty, Amphitrite reached for the water jug. Silena’s cup was empty too. Amphitrite filled it, then set the jug down without asking whether Silena wanted a refill.

Silena looked at the cup. “Thank you.”

“Of course.”

It was small. She was glad it could stay small.

After dinner, Chiron began stacking bowls. Silena rose to help. Amphitrite stood too, then paused.

“Would you like me to carry those?” she asked.

“I can take the bread basket.”

“All right.”

Silena carried the basket toward the kitchen. Amphitrite took the empty cups. They met at the counter and set them down side by side. Neither one took over the other's task.

“Do you want to come back tomorrow?” Silena asked.

Amphitrite turned from the cups. “Would you?”

“I think so.”

“Then I would like to.”

“Maybe we could walk again. Or eat. We don’t have to paint.”

“I’d like that.”

“Good.”

Silena rinsed the basket’s crumbs into the bin. She did not set a time. She did not ask Amphitrite to promise she would be available. They could find each other tomorrow if they both wanted to.

In the main room, Percy held the door for Annabeth while she carried the map. She stopped beside him and fixed the fold that had worked loose. He waited with his hand on the latch. When she finished, she kissed his cheek and went out first.

Percy looked over at Silena. “You coming?”

“In a minute.”

He nodded. “I’ll wait outside.”

Annabeth called back from the path, “Don’t let him lose the ruler.”

“I’m carrying it,” he said.

“Carefully,” she said.

He walked out after her, shaking his head. Their voices blended with the evening sounds near the cabins.

Silena stood by the counter with Amphitrite. She could feel the sealed letter in her portfolio across the room, but she did not need to retrieve it. It remained hers. The new sample was with Amphitrite. That was hers too.

Two pieces of paper, two different choices. Neither one needed to explain the other.

Clarisse walked back toward Cabin Three with Silena.

At the path turn, she stopped and looked toward the Big House. The lights were on in the study. Through the window, Silena could see Chiron moving papers across the desk.

“Do you want to go back in?” Silena asked.

“No.”

“Do you want me to stay with you?”

Clarisse looked at her. “Are you asking because you want to?”

“Yes.”

“Then yes.”

They sat on the low wall by the path. The stone had cooled during dinner. Clarisse put her hands beneath her arms and tucked her chin into the collar of her jacket.

“I thought you’d have more to say about her,” Silena said.

“Why?”

“You knew her before I did.”

“I knew her as a queen. I knew her as Percy’s mother. I don’t know her as someone who wants to be close to me.”

“Does it feel like she wants that?”

“I don’t know.”

Silena leaned her elbows on her knees. “You can take your time.”

“I know.”

“I mean it.”

“I know that too.”

Clarisse rubbed a thumb over the hem of her sleeve. “I don’t want you to think I’m waiting for you to decide whether she’s good enough.”

“I don’t.”

“I don’t want you to tell me she’s good enough.”

“I wouldn’t.”

“Or tell me I should give her a chance because she’s trying.”

“I wouldn’t do that either.”

Clarisse glanced over. “You could.”

“I could do a lot of things.”

“That’s true.”

“I’m not in charge of what you feel about her.”

“I’m not in charge of what you feel, either.”

Silena smiled. “We’re learning.”

“Don’t say it like we’re in a lesson.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You were.”

“I was thinking it.”

Clarisse rolled her eyes, but the tension eased from her shoulders. “I do like that she leaves when I say no.”

“That matters.”

“It does.”

“Does it make you want her to come back?”

“Not every time.”

“Okay.”

“I might change my mind later.”

“You might not.”

“Right.”

They sat with the answer. Silena knew Clarisse wanted certainty from time to time, wanted to know whether a person would stay and what it would cost if they did. Silena could not give her that. She could keep her company while the answer stayed open.

Clarisse nudged Silena’s boot with hers. “Do you think she likes the paper?”

“She said she did.”

“Do you believe her?”

“I think she likes it.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No.”

Clarisse let the thought settle. “You gave it to her.”

“I did.”

“Do you regret it?”

Silena took a moment. “No.”

“Good.”

“I don’t know if I’ll give her anything else.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I know.”

Clarisse looked toward the study again. “I may tell her I don’t want to walk next time.”

“You can.”

“Even if you do?”

“Even if I do.”

“Good.”

Silena bumped her shoulder into Clarisse’s. “You don’t have to give me an answer about how you feel just because I asked.”

“I’m not.”

“You are.”

Clarisse leaned her shoulder into hers. “I’m tired.”

“That’s an answer.”

“It’s the one I have.”

“Then it counts.”

They stayed until the wind made the wall uncomfortable. When they got up, Clarisse held out a hand to help Silena down even though the drop was small. Silena took it. Clarisse’s grip was firm and quick; she let go as soon as Silena was on the path.

Inside, Clarisse checked the window blanket once and then stopped herself from checking it again. Silena saw her notice the impulse. Neither of them said anything.

The next morning, Silena left her painting things in the portfolio.

She went to the pavilion with two cups and a plate of sliced pears. Amphitrite was already there, sitting at the window end.

“I didn’t bring a project,” Silena said.

“That’s all right.”

“Do you want pear?”

“Yes.”

Silena set the plate between them and sat down. They ate without talking for a while. The pears were cold from the pantry and a little too ripe at the center. Silena picked out the soft pieces and put them near the edge of the plate.

“You don’t have to do that,” Amphitrite said.

“I like the firmer ones.”

“Then I’ll take these.”

She took the soft pieces, one at a time, without turning them into a compliment or a statement. Silena ate the firm slices. Their cups were mismatched. One had a chip in the rim; the other had a painted green line around it. Neither one belonged to anyone in particular.

“Do you come here often?” Silena asked.

“To camp?”

“Yes.”

“Less than Percy would like. More than I can always manage.”

“Does he tell you that?”

“Sometimes.”

“What do you say?”

“That I hear him.”

“Does that help?”

“Sometimes. Sometimes it only tells him I heard the sentence.”

Silena smiled. “That happens.”

“What do you say when it does?”

“I tell him the sentence was not enough.”

“Does he listen?”

“He tries.”

Amphitrite nodded. “That is something.”

“Sometimes.”

“Yes.”

Silena ran her thumb along the chipped edge of the cup. “I don't know how often I want you to come.”

“You don’t have to know.”

“I might know differently next time.”

“That’s all right.”

“Would you still come?”

“If you ask me to.”

“And if I don’t?”

“Then I won’t.”

Silena looked at her. “That’s simple.”

“It isn’t always easy.”

“Do you find it easy to wait?”

“No.” Amphitrite took a sip. “I’m not always patient. Sometimes I want to fill the silence with an offer, because I think it will make the other person feel cared for.”

“Does it?”

“Not if the person needed the silence.”

Silena turned the cup in her hands. “I don’t always know what I need until I have it.”

“Neither do I.”

That answer surprised her. “You don’t?”

“I’m a person, Silena.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

The question was gentle, but it made her stop. Amphitrite did not mean the title, the sea, or the old things she could command. She meant herself. Silena had been thinking of her as a parent, a queen, and a person who might choose to care for her. Those were not the same as knowing her.

“I don’t know you yet,” Silena said.

“I know.”

“I like talking to you.”

“I like talking to you too.”

“I don’t want it to be a promise about anything else.”

“It isn’t.”

Silena set the cup down. The words had been difficult to say, but they had not broken the conversation.

“Do you want to know me?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because I like what I’ve seen of you. Because I want to know more, if you want to let me. Those are the reasons I have today.”

“Will they change?”

“Probably. I’ll learn more.”

Silena considered the honesty of that. It did not promise that nothing would hurt. It did not ask her to become a finished picture of someone Amphitrite already loved.

“Okay,” she said.

Amphitrite looked down at her cup. “That is enough for today.”

Silena smiled. “You sound like Chiron.”

“I have learned a few things from him.”

“He says that too.”

They stayed until the pears were gone. Silena told her the camp’s kitchens kept the soft fruit near the back so no one would take it first. Amphitrite told her the pears she liked were the ones that bruised too easily to travel. Neither story made the other one more important.

When they stood, Silena carried the empty plate to the kitchen. Amphitrite followed with the cups.

“You can leave those,” Silena said.

“I can carry two cups.”

“I know.”

“Then let me.”

Silena handed her the chipped one. They put both cups in the wash basin.

“I might use the table again next week,” Silena said.

“Would you like me to come?”

“Maybe. I’ll ask if I do.”

“I’ll be glad if you do.”

“And if I don’t?”

“I’ll still be glad you had the table.”

Silena nodded. That answer gave her something she could carry without having to turn it into a decision.

At the door, Amphitrite paused. “Would you like a goodbye?”

Silena blinked. “What?”

“Some people like a hug. Some don’t.”

Silena knew the word she wanted, but it was not a title or a promise. “A hug is okay.”

Amphitrite waited until Silena stepped closer, then wrapped her arms around her lightly. The embrace was brief. It did not make Silena forget her mother, or Clarisse and Percy, or the still-sealed letter in her portfolio. When they let go, the afternoon did not require an explanation.

“See you,” Silena said.

“If you want to.”

“I do.”

Silena turned back to the table. The work sheets were still in her portfolio. She had not painted that morning, and nothing was lost.

When Silena returned to Cabin Three, Percy was sitting on the floor beside the map, sorting its corners into a neat stack. Annabeth was on the bunk with her feet tucked under her, reading the notes he had made.

“Did you finish the blue tests?” Percy asked.

“Some of them.”

“Are you going to finish the three-wave copy?”

“Not today.”

“Okay.”

He did not look disappointed. He checked the edge of the map instead. “Do you want this copy for the letter?”

“I still haven’t decided.”

“Then I’ll wait.”

Annabeth lowered the page. “You don’t have to wait for the picture to write.”

“I know.”

“Do you want to write?”

“Not yet.”

Silena hung her coat beside her bunk. “That’s allowed.”

Percy smiled at her. “I’ve heard.”

Annabeth slid off the bunk and came to stand beside him. Percy showed her a crooked line on the map. She said it could be the path, but only if they marked where it was uncertain. He added a small question mark. She leaned down to kiss the top of his head, and he caught her hand before she straightened. She let him keep it while he finished the mark.

Silena took her portfolio from beneath the bunk. The letter to Maria remained sealed inside. The three-wave copy lay behind it, still unfinished. She moved the copy to the front and checked the color at the edge. The pigment had dried pale, exactly as she had expected. She could add a second wash later. She could also leave the white space.

Silena took out a scrap and tested the gray beside the blue. The colors touched, then spread into a dull green-gray she hadn’t expected. It was not the color she wanted for the wave. It was not useless either. She set the strip beside the open portfolio and let it dry.

The blue Amphitrite had painted was no longer there to compare with. Silena had given it away. She could still remember how the dark ring had gathered at the center, but memory was not the same as having the paper in front of her. She didn’t need the exact sample back. She needed to know what she had learned from it.

She wrote *dark center, pale edge* on the strip, then paused. The words were simple and accurate. She left them there.

The unfinished picture stayed where it was. The new strip belonged to no one yet. Silena closed her palette, washed the brush, and put the water jar away before the pigment settled at the bottom.

Clarisse came in with a folded blanket over her arm. “The temporary cord held.”

“Good.”

“Do you want to help me check the lower corner?”

“After I put this away.”

“Take your time.”

Clarisse set the blanket on her bunk and began sorting the thread. She did not ask what Silena had done with the sample or how she felt about it. If she wanted to talk, she could.

Silena closed the portfolio and set it on the shelf. Percy unrolled the map again. Annabeth sat beside him, pointing out a line that needed a note. Their quiet conversation filled the room without asking Silena to join.

She was glad she had spent the afternoon with Amphitrite. She was glad that Clarisse had not needed to make the same choice. She could be glad about both and leave each relationship where it was.

After Clarisse tied off the blanket cord, she passed Silena the needle. Silena took it and began repairing a loose thread on her own sleeve.

The work was small and not a gift. It would keep the seam from opening farther. That was enough for tonight.

She tied the sleeve thread off and checked the seam from both sides. One stitch leaned. The next sat straight. It would hold until the cloth needed another repair.

Clarisse looked over. “Did you finish?”

“The sleeve?”

“Yes.”

“Enough.”

Clarisse studied the seam. “It’s uneven.”

“I know.”

“It’ll hold.”

“I know that too.”

Clarisse went back to the window blanket. Silena folded the sleeve over her wrist and tested the pull. The thread held.

She set the portfolio back beneath the bunk. The letter to Maria stayed sealed. The three-wave copy stayed unfinished. The new color strip would dry overnight. The sample she had given Amphitrite was somewhere in the Big House, flat between two pages.

Silena did not need to decide which paper mattered most. They belonged to different parts of her life, and she could let them remain different. She turned off the lamp and climbed into bed with the scent of paint still faint on her fingers.
