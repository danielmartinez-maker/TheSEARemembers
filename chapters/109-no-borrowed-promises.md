## Chapter 109: No Borrowed Promises

Annabeth found the photograph in the bottom of Percy's bag.

It was old, softened at the corners, and bent once down the middle. The image showed Percy and Annabeth as teenagers, older than they were now, standing in front of a stone building with a banner that read NEW ROME UNIVERSITY. They were smiling at whoever held the camera. Percy had his arm around her shoulders. Annabeth's hand rested over his heart.

She stared at it for a long time.

The fold ran through the space between them, not quite dividing either face. The paper had softened along the crease as if it had been opened and closed too many times. There were small pale scratches on the glossy surface, a thumbprint clouding the corner, and a nick beside the banner. Annabeth tilted it toward the light. Nothing in the image moved. The stone building stayed behind the two people who had been her and Percy, and the older version of herself kept her hand over his heart.

She could remember the day pictured. In memory, the stone building had looked paler in the sunlight, and the air carried the smells of the city around it. The banner's lettering had caught Percy's attention. He had made a joke about it being misspelled, and she had laughed because he had said it with the certainty of someone prepared to argue with the sign.

The remembered moment did not unfold into a full account of how the photograph had been taken. There was only the pause in front of the entrance, the weight of Percy's arm around her shoulders, her own hand resting over his heart, and the awareness of an unseen camera. She remembered being happy in that instant. The image contained that much and no explanation for everything beyond it.

The details arrived with the familiarity of something lived and the distance of something she had not lived in this body. There was no feeling of waking up inside the photograph. She knew the girl because she remembered being her. The present Annabeth was also a Grace, Thalia's sister and a daughter of Zeus; she had no firsthand memories of the altered childhood that had made those facts real to everyone else. She knew the life she was building now, one present interaction at a time.

Annabeth turned the photograph carefully by its edges. The back was blank. No date, no handwriting, no location beyond the banner in the image. She did not write on it. She could not make a note on the back that would explain why its presence made the room feel suddenly smaller.

Percy's bag lay open beside the table. He had been looking for a pencil, judging by the scattered paper and the ruler resting across the top. Annabeth recognized the uneven way he folded things, a habit that had been his in the remembered life and was his now. The photograph did not tell her how it had come to be here. It was simply in the bag, and Percy had said he did not know it was there until they got back from the quest.

She set it down on the table, image-side up. The gesture felt less like putting it away than making room to see it without holding it. Then she moved the ruler so its metal edge would not touch the bent paper.

The image belonged to a life she remembered and a future she could no longer take for granted. The girl in the photo was her, but not exactly the Annabeth who now stood in a Grace family and carried lightning in her body. The distinction did not make the older girl a stranger. It made the photograph a poor instruction manual.

She picked the photograph up again as Percy came into the Poseidon cabin, holding it by its edges.

"I was looking for my pencil," he said. Then he saw her face. "Oh."

"You brought this with you?"

"It was in my jacket pocket. I didn't even know it was there until we got back from the quest."

"Do you know who took it?"

"No."

"When?"

"I don't know."

She could feel the questions assembling themselves, each one looking for a path from the paper in front of her to a larger explanation. When had the image been printed? Where had it been kept? Had they planned to preserve it? None of those questions had answers in the photograph, and Percy had told her only what he knew: it had been in his jacket pocket, and he had not noticed it until they returned from the quest.

"I don't want you to guess," she said.

"I won't."

"Even if you think a guess would make me feel better."

"Especially then."

Annabeth nodded. She did not need a theory about how the photograph got here. She needed to understand what the two of them wanted to do with it now that they had found it. The unknowns could stay unknown without becoming a test of how much either of them remembered.

"And I don't want it passed around," she said. "It's ours to decide about."

"It won't leave the cabin unless you say so."

"I don't want to keep it from Thalia because I think she would do something wrong. I just don't want to explain the picture today."

"That's enough."

He didn't make the promise sound grand. He also didn't ask her to justify it. Annabeth noticed how much easier it was to choose privacy when she did not have to defend the choice against an imagined accusation. She had spent the first moments with the photograph feeling that its existence demanded a response. She could instead set a boundary around who saw it and take the rest one question at a time.

He stayed on the other side of the table. His gaze flicked from the photograph to her hands and back, not reaching for it. That space mattered. She could have handed it to him, or turned it facedown, or asked him to put it away. Instead she kept both hands at the table's edge.

"Do you want it back?" she asked.

"If you want me to take it."

"I asked what you want."

Percy considered the question. "I want to keep it. I don't want to use it to tell you what to do."

"Those are different things."

"I know."

Annabeth looked at the photograph again. She wanted to put it somewhere safe, but she did not want its safety to become a reason she had to look at it. She wanted to keep it, but not as a blueprint. She wanted Percy to understand that she could care about the girl in the image and still make choices the girl had never been asked to make.

"Then let's leave it here while we talk," she said. "I don't want you to hide it, and I don't want to be the one who has to decide where it lives."

"Okay."

He moved the ruler farther away, giving the picture more space. It was such a small courtesy that Annabeth nearly smiled. She noticed that he had not corrected her use of “we” or tried to guess what she wanted. The photograph stayed where she had put it, neither reclaimed nor discarded.

Percy glanced at the open bag. "I still need the pencil."

Annabeth followed his gaze. A loose stack of papers sat beneath the strap, and one corner had folded under the bag's lining. The photograph was on the table now. She didn't want them to knock it onto the floor while searching.

"I'll hold the papers," she said.

He nodded and waited while she lifted the stack by its edges. There were folded notes and a blank sheet, but no pencil. Percy checked the outer pocket, then the narrow seam where the strap met the bag. His fingers stopped.

"Found it?" she asked.

"No. I found the cap."

He held up a small wooden pencil cap. It was the wrong end of the search, but Annabeth laughed. She steadied the papers while he turned the bag over enough to see beneath its lining. The pencil rolled into view and tapped against the floor.

Percy picked it up. "I was looking right at it."

"You were looking at a photograph."

"I had a different goal when I walked in."

"Goals change."

He looked at her, then at the pencil. "Apparently."

She handed back the papers. He placed them in the bag without mixing them with the photograph. The little search had no meaning beyond finding what he had come for, but it gave the room a way to return to ordinary life after the image had made everything feel like a question.

"You look happy."

"We were."

"I know." She set it on the table. "That's the problem. I remember being happy with you. I remember loving you. And then I look at this life, and I worry we're trying to keep every old plan because we're afraid of losing anything else."

Percy didn't move closer. He didn't reach for her hand. He waited.

"I need you to tell me if you want to slow down," he said. "Or stop. You don't owe me anything because of this picture. Or because of what you remember."

"I know that."

"Do you?"

The question wasn't accusing. It was gentle, and that made it harder.

Annabeth folded her arms. "Sometimes I feel like I'm borrowing our older promises. I still love you. But if I need a different plan now, I feel like I'm betraying everything we worked for. Like choosing you means following every instruction we left ourselves."

She glanced at the photograph. "And this isn't the only part of my life that has two versions. The girl in the picture didn't grow up with Thalia. The Annabeth who is standing here is her sister, but I don't remember the childhood that made that true for everyone else. I know the fact. I know what has happened between us since we came back. I can't fill in the years from somebody else's account and pretend I was there in my own memory."

Percy's expression shifted, but he did not interrupt.

"I don't want to use the photograph to decide which version of me is real," Annabeth continued. "And I don't want you to tell me that the old version is the only one who counts because she knew you longer."

"I won't," he said. "You don't have to prove that you remember something in order to choose what matters to you now."

"I want to be Thalia's sister. I want to be with you. I also need space to learn what those things mean in this life, not just follow a story I remember."

He nodded. "Then we can let the present keep adding to it."

That was not an answer about how every piece would fit. It was permission for the pieces to remain uneven while she learned them. Annabeth let her arms uncross. The photograph stayed on the table, and the family fact stayed true, but neither had to settle the rest of the day.

Percy nodded slowly. "I don't want you to borrow any promises."

"Then what do you want?"

"You. As you are now. Not a copy of the person I remember. Not a promise from somebody else." He paused. "I want to be with you if you want to be with me. And if you need room to decide, I'll give you room."

Annabeth looked down at the photograph. "The older me would have kissed you right now."

"Maybe."

"I think I want to. But I need us to be here together, not trying to repeat a photograph."

"Then take your time."

She studied the picture again. The older girl had one hand against Percy's chest. The pose looked natural, unplanned. The kind of thing you did when you trusted someone enough to stand close without thinking about it.

Annabeth put the photograph down.

"I want to be with you," she said. "I want the hand-holding. I want the letters. I want to kiss you. But I need us to keep asking each other. I need to know I can change my mind without losing you."

"You can."

"And if I do change my mind?"

"I'll be sad. But I'll still care about you."

She watched him, waiting for a catch. There wasn't one.

"Okay," she said.

Percy smiled a little. "Okay?"

"Okay, I'm choosing you. Today. Not forever because a picture says so. Today, because I want you here."

"Today is good."

She stepped closer and took his hand. "And I want to kiss you."

"Then you can."

Annabeth leaned in. She kissed him, slow and sure, then drew back before the moment could become too much. Percy didn't chase her. He only kept holding her hand.

"Thank you," she said.

"For what?"

"For letting our plans be new, even when we aren't."

He pressed his forehead to hers. "We're both new at this. This time."

---

They spent the afternoon on the hill, sorting through their memories.

Not all of them. Just the easy ones. The first time they met. The first time Percy made Annabeth laugh. The first time Annabeth trusted him with a plan. They laid the memories out between them like cards, noticing which still made them laugh and which hurt more now that the future around them had changed.

Annabeth had brought a small stack of blank cards from her cabin. She wrote one phrase on each, no details: **first meeting**, **blue candy**, **first plan**. The cards were only labels, not a record of everything they could remember. She set them on the grass with space between them.

"I don't want to make a list of our whole life," she said. "That would turn it into homework."

"We can stop whenever you want."

"I know. I want to do this much."

She picked up the first card and turned it over. The blank side gave them room to add a question, but she wasn't sure she wanted one. She had remembered meeting him in a life that had now been erased. The first look, the first argument, the way he had become someone she could trust—all of it belonged to a version of their history that no one else could confirm. She could remember it clearly, yet that clarity did not mean she had to make it the authority on everything that came next.

"What would you write on the back?" Percy asked.

"Nothing yet. Maybe, 'what do I still choose?' But that's too broad."

"You don't have to answer it today."

"I don't want every memory to become a question about whether I still love you. Some of them can just be memories."

"Good," he said. "I don't want to be a test you have to pass."

Annabeth placed the card down with the written side visible. The act of labeling it had made the memory feel less like a corridor she had to walk all the way down. She could hold one piece of it without following every door it opened.

They tried a simple sorting rule: a card could go in the middle if she wanted to talk about it, near her if she wanted to keep it private for now, or back in the stack if she did not want to look at it today. No one had to explain the placement. Percy agreed to follow it too. When he chose not to turn over a card, Annabeth did not ask what was written there.

He took one of the blank cards and wrote **not today** on its back. He kept it face down beside his knee. Annabeth didn't ask which remembered moment he had been thinking about, or whether he would ever want to discuss it. The rule was for both of them. If a card could remain private without an explanation, then neither of them had to perform openness to prove that the other was trusted.

Percy rested a hand near the card, not covering it, and looked at the three Annabeth had labeled. "You choose which one we start with."

"I already chose these."

"I meant the order."

She shifted the first-meeting card aside and kept the blue-candy one in front. It was the easiest place to begin, not because it answered the most, but because she could remember laughing without needing to settle what their first meeting meant.

The three cards stayed in the middle. That did not mean every part of those memories was easy. It meant they had chosen those topics for this afternoon, and they could leave the rest untouched.

"This one feels like mine," Annabeth said. "The first time you brought me blue candy from the dining pavilion because you heard I liked it."

"I thought we agreed never to speak of that."

"You got the wrong kind, though."

"That sounds like me."

"You got the wrong kind three times."

"That's less like me."

"You were trying very hard."

"That's more like me."

She held the blue-candy card between them. "I want to keep this one, but I don't want it to count as proof that the older plan was right."

"It can count as proof that I tried to bring you candy," Percy said. "I don't think it has to prove anything else."

"And that I laughed, even though you got the wrong kind three times."

"I was persistent."

"You were stubborn."

"Those are related."

The memory opened for her in a small, bright piece. Percy had brought her candy because he had heard she liked it. The first kind had been wrong. He noticed before she could decide whether to tell him and tried again later. The second kind had been wrong too. By the third, she had started to laugh at the seriousness with which he inspected each wrapper, as if a precise answer could be found by concentrating harder.

She remembered how careful he had been not to make the mistake her problem. He had never asked her to pretend he had gotten it right. He had only kept trying, and she had been allowed to laugh without the laughter becoming a verdict on him. The memory was small enough to hold in her mind without calling up every other thing that happened after it.

There was no single moment in it that could serve as a promise. There was a boy who listened, got the detail wrong, and tried again; there was a girl who let him know the difference and laughed with him. The part she wanted to keep was not that he had always known what she liked. He hadn't. It was that the mistake had not ended the kindness.

The remembered scene settled back into the afternoon. Percy was still beside her, waiting while she chose what to write. He did not try to improve the memory or add a line of his own.

Annabeth smiled and wrote on the back of the card: **A small kindness can stay small.** She paused, then added: **I remember laughing.** The words did not describe the whole day or explain what the memory meant. They named two pieces she wanted to carry without turning them into a promise.

She placed the card in the center. Percy did not move it closer to himself. He let the choice remain hers.

They laughed. Annabeth found that the memories didn't vanish when they laughed at them. They didn't take over either. They simply sat there, part of the person she was becoming.

The third card was harder. **First plan** had no image attached to it, only the knowledge that she had once trusted Percy with a plan. She could remember the feeling of handing it over and waiting to see what he would do with it. The memory did not make the present version of him automatically trustworthy about every decision. It did remind her that they had learned how to work together before either of them knew the shape of the future.

She remembered the plan less as a sequence of steps than as the pause before she let someone else see it. In that life, Percy had been someone she could place a piece of unfinished thinking in front of without surrendering ownership of it. The card could hold that fact without becoming a record of what she had asked him to do.

"I remember trusting you," she said. "I don't remember that as the same thing as agreeing with every suggestion you made."

"You didn't have to."

"Right. I could still change the plan."

Percy glanced at the blank back of the card. "You can do that now too."

"I know. But I don't want to use the old version of me as proof that I always should."

"I don't want to tell you what the plan was," she said. "I want to keep the trust, but the details feel tangled up with everything that changed."

"Then we don't need the details."

"I also don't want to pretend I came to trust you only because of the erased future. I have reasons from this life too."

"You don't have to sort out which reason came first."

She watched him carefully. "I don't want you to treat the memory as a contract."

"I won't."

"You can still remind me of what I chose, if I ask. But you can't use it to tell me what I owe you now."

Percy considered that, then nodded. "That's fair. If you ask, I'll tell you what I remember. I won't bring it up to win an argument."

Annabeth wrote on the back of **first plan**: **Trust can be remembered; the plan can stay unfinished.** She did not add what the plan had been. The card stayed in the middle, but she turned it face down. She wanted its presence without the details pressing into the afternoon.

The first meeting card remained blank on its back. Neither of them tried to make her explain it. She remembered the beginning of their relationship in the old future, but that did not require her to reproduce its steps in the present. One card could remain a label with no lesson attached.

She looked at the words **first meeting** and felt the memory behind them without trying to reconstruct the room or the conversation. It was a remembered beginning, but not the only beginning that mattered. There had been years after it, and then the separate beginning of the present life they were living now. She could not use one first moment to make the rest inevitable.

"I know what the card means," she said. "I just don't want to write a summary that makes it sound like everything was decided then."

"Then we leave it blank," Percy said.

She turned the card face down and put it back at the edge of the stack. The memory remained available to her, but she did not have to pull it into the center just to prove she had not forgotten it. The act of setting it aside was not rejection. It was a decision about how much of the past she wanted to use that afternoon.

Percy showed her the photograph again. He held it by the edges and kept it level between them.

Annabeth focused on the building. She could make out the line of the stonework, the broad entrance, and the banner stretched above the doors. The image gave her evidence of the facade, not of what the university had meant to her or what she might want from a school now. She asked Percy for her pencil and used the back of one blank card to sketch the proportions. It was a quick elevation, more geometry than art.

"You can tell the front is symmetrical," she said. "The wings are close to the same width. The entrance is set back."

"Does that help?"

"It helps me look at it as a building. I was seeing only us."

She drew the roofline, stopped, then measured the banner against the entrance with the pencil. The letters were readable, but the picture could not tell her whether she would have liked studying there. It could not say whether the future version of her had chosen the place freely, or whether it had seemed like the obvious next step for reasons she did not know. Those answers were not hidden in the facade.

The first sketch squeezed the entrance too close to the edge of the card. Annabeth rubbed it out and shifted the central line inward. Percy held the photograph still, tilting it only when the glare hid the stonework. She made another pass, this time keeping the entrance centered and leaving room for both wings.

"I can't get a scale from this," she said. "The camera angle changes the proportions."

"You can still draw what you see."

"Yes. But I need to mark it as a study, not a plan."

She wrote **not to scale** in the corner. The words stopped the drawing from pretending to be a measured elevation. She did not know whether the photographer had stood directly in front of the building or a little to one side, and the image gave her no reliable way to recover the distance. She could trace the impression and keep the uncertainty attached to it.

Percy looked from the photograph to her card. "You could draw the two of us too."

"I already have the photograph for that."

"Right."

"I want the building on this card. I want to remember that I noticed it."

She kept the sketch small and left the edges unfinished. The act of drawing did not make the university part of her present plan. It did let her keep one specific interest that had drawn her to the image, without confusing that interest with a decision she had not made.

"I don't want to make this into a list of things I have to recover," she said. "I want to know what I actually want to learn."

"You want to learn about buildings."

"Yes. I do. That part doesn't belong to the photograph."

She had remembered New Rome as part of their future plans, but the photograph could not tell her how they had arrived at that choice or whether she had wanted it for the same reasons she might want something now. Annabeth didn't ask Percy to fill that gap. She knew he remembered the plan too, but she did not want the memory to turn into a recommendation.

"I know we talked about New Rome," she said. "I don't know whether I would choose it now."

"You don't have to decide now."

"I want to remember that I cared about architecture before the picture. I also want to be allowed to find out what I care about next."

Percy nodded toward the drawing. "Then the building can stay a building."

"And the university can stay part of the old plan until I decide what I want."

She drew a light line under the notes on the card, separating the visible structure from the blank space where she had not written a memory or a decision. She did not cross out the university's name from the photograph. She did not add it to a list of things she was supposed to pursue. The page could hold both facts without forcing them into the same category.

She turned the sketch card around and wrote **visible in the image** at the top. Beneath it she listed the entrance, facade, and banner. Then on the other side she wrote **remembered by me** and left it blank. She was not going to treat the photograph as proof of an entire future, but she also did not have to pretend she knew nothing about the life it showed.

Percy watched her separate the two sides. "You don't have to choose only one."

"I know. The point is that I choose what to do with each."

He held the photograph out. She took it and laid it on the grass beside the cards, away from her pencil. The image remained unchanged. Her notes did not touch it.

"I like her," Annabeth said.

"The older you?"

"She looks like she knows what she wants. And like she worked hard to get it." Annabeth picked up the picture and handed it back. "I don't have to become exactly her. But I don't want to pretend she wasn't me either."

"You don't."

"And you don't have to pretend all those years disappeared because we're younger now. We remember them, even when different parts hurt. You get to be sad about that."

Percy looked toward the Sound. "Sometimes I worry that the older me is going to crowd you out. That I keep expecting us to fit plans we made before any of this happened."

"Then tell me when you notice. And I'll tell you when I feel like I'm being compared."

"Deal."

They shook on it, solemnly, and Annabeth laughed at how serious the handshake was.

The wind lifted one of the cards before Annabeth could stack it. Percy caught it against his knee. He did not look at the blank side or turn it over to see whether she had written anything else. He waited for her to take it.

"You don't have to treat every card like it contains a secret," she said.

"I know. I just don't want to read something you didn't choose to show me."

She took the card and added it to the others. "That is the difference. I can choose what I show you."

They returned the pencil and the empty cards to her pocket. Annabeth asked Percy to bring the photograph along when they walked back toward the cabins. She wanted to look at it once more before she decided where she wanted it kept. She chose to carry it herself, holding the bent edges carefully. The hill had been their work space for the afternoon; the paper, memory cards, and sketch were all separate pieces, and she did not need to combine them into one record.

By the time they reached the path, she had decided the photograph could stay with Percy. That was not because he had more right to it than she did. It was because it had been in his bag, he wanted to keep it, and she did not want it in her own pocket as if she had to carry it everywhere. She could ask to see it again. For now, the choice was enough.

She handed it to him. Percy slid it back into the inner pocket without smoothing the fold. Neither of them tried to make the paper more permanent than it was.

They continued down the path, and Annabeth remembered the drainage sketch she had folded into the same bag. The two pieces of paper had nothing to do with each other, but the afternoon had made her more careful about how a page could look like a conclusion when it only contained a piece of evidence.

"I want to look at the shrine sketch before dinner," she said. "Not measure again. I want to make sure I haven't drawn a question as if it were an answer."

Percy waited while she took the folded sheet out. They stopped beside the path, where a flat stone provided a place to hold the page. Annabeth unfolded it without setting the photograph beside it. She could look at one record at a time.

The drawing showed the drainage channel as it had appeared during the previous inspection. The endpoint measurements did not agree. Leaves and a twig had been at the grate; a separate leaf had rested on an upper stone. The line under the edge stones was only a possibility. She had left that mark dashed, but in one margin note she had written that water might be passing underneath as if it were already the most likely explanation.

Annabeth placed the page so Percy could read it without touching the pencil marks. "Tell me what you think happened here. Just from the drawing."

He leaned closer. "The leaf on the upper stone washed down toward the grate?"

"I don't know that it did."

"The way it's drawn makes it look like one path."

Annabeth followed his eye. He was right. The separate mark had its own label, but the two strokes that showed the channel's direction ran close enough to it that the page suggested movement between them. She had been careful to keep the facts separate in her notes, then made them look connected in the diagram.

"That's exactly what I don't want to do," she said.

She erased the short connecting stroke. It left a pale shadow. Then she drew the upper stone farther from the channel edge, not as a measured distance but as a clearer spatial separation, and added **not traced** beside it. Percy shifted the page and looked again.

"Now?"

"Now I can tell that it's a different observation."

He pointed to the two endpoints. "And these are the measurements that disagreed?"

"Yes. I should say what each one started and ended at. The values alone make them look like competing answers, when they might be measurements of different spans."

She annotated both endpoints in words, drawing a short bracket from each measured start to its stop. She did not choose between them. That distinction would matter when someone checked the foundation; a clean line could not substitute for knowing where a measurement began.

"This one is still open," Percy said, indicating the dashed mark beneath the edge stones.

"Yes. It stays open until we see the water move under there or the crew tells us what they found."

"And this is the grate."

"The grate as it looked when we saw it. Not where every leaf came from."

Percy looked again at the marks. "I can read that now."

The page had passed a modest test: someone who had been present, but who was not responsible for drawing it, could separate the observations from the unverified explanation. She chose not to add an arrow showing a flow direction because no one had tested it. She did not write a repair instruction. The page now told a reader what to preserve until the proper check.

Annabeth circled the word *might* and underlined it once.

"I don't know that it's happening," she said. "I know it's one question."

"The maintenance crew can check it."

"Yes. But I keep wanting to find a reason before we have the test. If I can name the cause, I can draw a fix."

"And if the cause is different?"

"Then I build the wrong thing." She took a second pencil from her bag and wrote **unverified** beside the dashed mark. "I don't want the sketch to hide that."

She added a key to the lower corner: solid lines for visible edges, a small circle for something observed but not yet explained, and a broken line for a possibility requiring a test. She kept the two measurement endpoints as separate marks instead of drawing one line through both. When she began to erase the first set of notes, she stopped and put the eraser down.

"I shouldn't remove the earlier reading," she said. "We need to know that the endpoints didn't match."

"Could you move the numbers to the margin?"

"Yes. Then the sketch still shows where they came from, and the page doesn't pretend they measured the same thing."

She moved the numbers into a small note, leaving both marks on the channel itself. The new legend made the drawing less elegant and more legible. A person who had not been there would be able to tell which lines were observation and which ones still needed a test. That was more important than making the page look finished.

Percy held one corner so the breeze would not fold the page back. He did not use the water to demonstrate what might be flowing beneath the stones. The channel remained exactly as they had left it. Annabeth drew a small box in the margin for the eventual test result, but did not fill it. Beneath it she wrote: **Record what the crew sees before deciding what to move.**

The note was not a new question for Chiron or a schedule for the crew. It was a reminder to herself about the order of the work. First observe, then test, then decide. She had asked those questions yesterday; today she could make their status clearer on the page.

The page still felt unfinished in a way she could not solve with another label. She had made the old measurements legible, but the drawing itself was only a narrow piece of the shrine: a channel, a grate, a few stones. The photograph had given her a broad facade with two people placed in front of it. The channel page gave her a handful of marks that did not agree. Neither showed the structure as she could see it now.

Annabeth looked up from the paper. From where they had stopped, the shrine stood beyond the path, its entrance facing the open ground. The afternoon sun reached one side of the stonework and left the other in shade. A few of the small mosaic tiles caught the light, but the pattern broke where a newer tile met older stone. She could see the narrow drainage line along the foundation without needing to go back to it.

She took a clean card from the stack. “I want to draw the front.”

Percy glanced at the sketch in her other hand. “The channel?”

“The shrine. From here. Just what I can see.”

He stepped out of her line of sight. “Do you want me to move?”

“Not yet. I want to decide what belongs in the drawing before I decide what to leave out.”

She set the drainage page on the flat stone and weighted one corner with her pencil case. The new card was stiff enough to hold without a board, though the wind lifted its edge whenever she lowered her hand. Percy offered to hold it; she asked him to wait until she had chosen a view. She wanted to know which parts she could keep steady on her own.

From the path, the shrine looked almost square. She put a light horizontal mark near the top of the card, then a vertical line for the entry. Her first pass placed the doorway in the center and divided the front into equal halves. It was the sort of order she reached for automatically: a center, two sides, a shape that would balance if she made each side match.

She stopped before darkening the lines. The right edge of the doorway did not line up with the mosaic border. The left side had a thicker stone column. She had put both columns the same width because the first vertical stroke divided the card evenly, not because she had checked the building.

“What are you looking at?” Percy asked.

“Whether the entrance is centered.”

She held the pencil in front of the building, aligning its shaft with the doorway and then the outer edges. It was not a formal measurement. It only gave her a comparison: the opening sat a little closer to the wider column than she had drawn. The roofline also dropped slightly on the shaded side. The unevenness could be perspective, construction, or a small change in the ground beneath the paving. The view did not tell her which.

She erased the equal halves. The gray marks remained, faintly visible beneath the new lines. Rather than cover them, she left them as a record of the first assumption and moved the doorway a little to the left. The roofline followed the angle she could actually see instead of a level she had supplied from memory.

The card was too small to show every stone. She decided to draw the outside corners, the doorway, the two columns, and the mosaic band. The channel would be a separate inset, not a dark line running along the whole base. She had already seen that the line could suggest a continuous path where she had only observed separate details. On the new drawing she left a gap where weeds and the angle of the foundation blocked her view.

Percy shifted his weight. His shoulder crossed the lower edge of the shrine from where she stood.

“I’m in the way,” he said.

“A little.”

He moved farther along the path. The changed angle made the doorway seem wider and the left column nearly disappear behind the wall. Annabeth lowered the card. A sketch could be accurate to one view and misleading about the building as a whole. She wrote a small arrow in the margin to show where she had been standing and labeled the card **from path, facing entrance**. She did not draw an imaginary view of the hidden side.

“That’s a lot of writing for a sketch,” Percy said.

“It’s less than someone would need to ask where I stood.”

He nodded and remained out of her sightline. Annabeth tried the same outline from the new position. The doorway shifted relative to the columns, so she stopped after the first few strokes. She did not want the second view to look like a correction to the first; it was a different angle. She took another blank card from her stack, marked it **from path, left of center**, and drew only the doorway and the near column, the pieces that changed most clearly.

The comparison gave her something she could not get from the photograph. There was no banner to name the building, no camera frame containing its intended center, and no posed figures to fix the scale. The shrine in front of her kept changing as she moved. The subject was not a stable rectangle waiting to be copied. It was stone seen from particular places, with some edges open to view and others concealed.

She returned to her first position and held the two cards at arm’s length. The outlines did not match. That was not a flaw to erase. One showed the doorway near the wider column; the other made the near column dominate. Both could describe the shrine, provided she kept the viewpoint attached. Neither explained when the tiles had been placed, why the stones varied, or how the water moved beneath the foundation.

“Which one is right?” Percy asked.

“Both, for the views they show.” She tapped the first card, then the second. “Neither is the whole building.”

She considered adding a third sketch from straight on. The thought felt satisfying because it promised to settle the question of symmetry. But reaching a straight-on view would mean stepping into the marked work area and standing close to the channel. She had said she would not measure again, and the crew's inspection had not happened. The drawing did not need a third angle today.

Instead she took the first card and darkened only the edges she could see clearly: the doorway, the broader left column, the tilt of the roofline, the uneven band of tile. She kept the opposite edge faint where the wall disappeared behind the column. In the margin she wrote **visible edge** and **hidden behind column**. The second label looked clumsy. She left it anyway.

The pencil work was not difficult in the way a puzzle was difficult. Nothing had to be solved. She needed to keep noticing when her hand tried to make the structure easier than it was. Each time she replaced a jagged or unequal line with a clean one, she risked turning an observation into a design. She could imagine repairing the mosaic, regularizing the columns, directing the water away from the foundation. But those were possible plans, not features she had been asked to alter.

She drew a small outline of the mosaic border and marked where its pattern broke. Then she paused over the newer-looking tile. It might have been a repair. It might simply have come from a different batch. The visible difference was enough to draw; its history was not hers to supply. She put a question mark in the margin and wrote **material change; date unknown**.

Percy came closer, stopping beside the flat stone rather than looking over her shoulder. “Can I see?”

She turned the card toward him. “Tell me what you can identify. Not what you think it means.”

He leaned over it without touching the paper. “Doorway. Two columns. The left one's wider. The roof slopes down a little. This line is the channel?”

“The visible section, from this angle.”

“And the blank part is where you couldn't see?”

“Yes.”

He traced the air above the card, stopping before his finger reached it. “I can tell where you stood. I can't tell what happened to the tile.”

“Good.”

“Is that the whole test?”

“For this drawing.” She took the card back. “If a person who wasn't standing here can separate the observed parts from my question, then I haven't hidden the question in the lines.”

The test was modest, and it did not make Percy an expert on the shrine or the water. He had seen the building before, but he had not drawn it. Annabeth had asked him to read the marks as marks, not confirm a repair theory. His answer showed her where the sketch communicated and where it still needed a note.

She added a small key: a solid line for an edge she could see, a broken line for an edge partly obscured, and a question mark for a feature whose explanation remained unknown. The key took up more space than she wanted. She considered removing it, then looked again at the first clean rectangle she had almost drawn. The key belonged there precisely because her mind preferred a cleaner building.

The card had room for one more note, but not for a scale bar. She had not measured the wall, and the doorway's apparent width changed with her angle. A scale would have made the sketch look more authoritative than the work behind it. She could still show relative placement: the entrance between the columns, the mosaic above it, the channel below the foundation. She drew those relationships with light guide marks, then added **relative positions only** beside the key.

She checked the composition by covering the notes with her hand. Without the labels, the solid doorway and the pale broken edge looked like parts of one finished outline. The distinction she had made for herself disappeared when she tried to read the card quickly. She erased the hidden edge and redrew it with gaps large enough to remain visible at a glance. Then she set the page down and looked at the shrine instead of the drawing, counting the separate places where the foundation showed between grass and stone.

The channel appeared in three short lengths from this spot, interrupted by the raised base and a patch of shadow. She had joined them into one line on the first version of the drainage sketch because she understood them as one channel. That could be true as a description of the intended structure and still exceed what the page had shown. She added no new line to the channel drawing. On the architecture card, she kept each visible length separate and marked the interruption as **not visible from here**, not as a gap in the stonework.

Percy looked from the card to the building. “If you were showing this to someone, would you give them both views?”

“Only if they needed to know how the entrance shifts from different places. If they just need the shape, one might be enough.”

“And if they need the channel?”

“They need the close drawing and the inspection. This card doesn't answer that.”

She considered adding an arrow to show the path between the two viewpoints. Instead, she drew two small squares at the bottom of the card, one for each standing place, and labeled them **A** and **B**. A short note said which sketch matched each square. It was plain enough that Percy could follow it, and plain enough that she could remember where the view came from without relying on how she felt while drawing.

On the reverse, she made a list of things that belonged to the present view: the entrance, the asymmetrical columns, the broken tile band, the channel visible from the path, the way the roofline changed with the angle. Beneath that she wrote **not established here** and listed the tile's date, the hidden foundation, the direction of flow, and whether anything needed repair. She used the card's edge to separate the two lists.

At the bottom she wrote, **present-view study; maintenance check pending**.

She looked at the cards side by side. In the photograph, the university facade had been flattened into one image, the angle fixed by a camera whose position she could not recover. The shrine had offered more than one view, but each view remained partial. Neither paper gave her permission to invent what lay outside its edges. The difference was not that one image was true and the other false. It was that she could test some of the present drawing by moving her feet and looking again.

Annabeth walked three steps to the left, keeping outside the work area. From there, the doorway lined up with the wider column exactly as it had in her second card. She walked back to her original place. The first view returned. She did not redraw either one. The change in position explained why the outlines differed without making either observation disappear.

When she returned to the flat stone, she saw that a smear from the eraser had dulled the margin note. She rewrote the viewpoint in smaller letters and put the card beneath the corner of the channel page to keep it from lifting. Then she compared the new sketch with the channel diagram.

She turned the cards so their labels faced up: the drainage page showed what was measured and what remained unverified; the architecture card showed her viewpoints and visible edges. The maintenance check would need its own inspection.

“I don't need to take this to anyone,” she said. “I just wanted to draw what is here.”

Percy glanced toward the shrine. “You got it on the card.”

“Part of it.”

“Part is still something.”

She did not answer with a promise or a judgment. She turned the card over once more and checked that the labels were legible, then set it beside the channel drawing. The shrine remained where it was: uneven, partly hidden, and still waiting for a proper check of the work at its base.

"I want the drawing to get better," she said, "without pretending I know more than I do."

"That sounds like the kind of plan that can wait for real information."

"It can."

She folded the sketch along its existing creases. There was no reason to return to the channel or move the debris. They left the drainage site alone, with the foundation check, flow test, and any repair still pending. Annabeth put the drawing back in the outer pocket, separate from the inner pocket where Percy kept the photograph.

She slid the two small architecture views into the back of her own card stack and returned them to her pocket. She would keep those studies with her notes, not in the same pocket as the photograph.

The small difference in where each page went was deliberate. The photograph belonged to their shared erased future; the drainage sketch belonged to a task they could still complete in this life. Neither had to answer a question meant for the other.

At dinner, Percy sat beside her at the Zeus table. Thalia watched them for a few seconds, then passed Annabeth the bread basket without comment.

Clarisse leaned across from the Poseidon table. "You two having a council meeting over there?"

"We're negotiating terms," Annabeth said.

"About what?"

"Memory, choice, and whether Percy is allowed to steal the last roll."

"He is not," Clarisse said. "That's Poseidon cabin law."

"It's not an actual law," Percy said.

"It is now."

Clarisse said it with the certainty of someone who expected nobody to waste time asking what the penalty was. Percy looked toward Silena for an appeal. Silena was already lifting the basket, but she did not pass it to him. She set it beside Annabeth instead.

"The law is about who gets to choose," Silena said. "The person holding the basket decides where it goes."

"That seems reasonable," Annabeth said.

"I think it is a conspiracy," Percy replied.

Clarisse leaned back in her chair. "You can challenge the law at the next meeting."

"There is no next meeting."

"Then you have time to prepare your argument."

Annabeth took the basket, broke the last roll in half, and put one piece on Percy's plate. She kept the other half. "Now the law has a compromise clause."

"That's not how laws work," Percy said.

"It is how this one works."

Clarisse looked at the two halves. "Fine. But if someone takes the whole roll next time, I call a hearing."

Silena passed the butter. "You can be the judge."

"I was going to be the judge."

The table laughed, and the moment passed without drawing attention to the cards in Annabeth's pocket or the photograph in Percy's bag. She liked that she could set a boundary around what mattered and still join an ordinary argument. Nothing about the private conversation required the rest of the evening to become solemn.

Thalia caught Annabeth's eye. "You okay?"

Annabeth nodded. She could give the shortest answer and leave the rest with herself. "We talked. I'm all right, and I don't want to go into it tonight."

"Good."

Thalia didn't ask what they'd talked about. She didn't demand to see the photograph or tell Annabeth what she should feel. She simply stayed beside her and returned to eating dinner.

Annabeth reached under the table and took Percy's hand. It was a small choice, made in the middle of ordinary noise, and it felt more like hers than any memory ever could.

After dinner, Annabeth folded the blank-card stack into her pocket and asked Percy if he would walk with her to the lake. He came without asking whether she wanted to talk more. The evening air had cooled; a breeze crossed the water and shifted the loose strand of hair from her cheek. She took out the card with the quick facade sketch and turned it over to a clean side.

"I want to draw something from today," she said. "Not the photo."

Percy looked at the card. "Do you want me to sit?"

"If you can stay still for longer than a minute."

"I can try."

He sat on the low bank where the grass met the sand, knees drawn up, his hands loose between them. Annabeth set herself at an angle so she could see him and the water behind him. She sketched the line of his shoulder first, then the slope of his back and the turn of his head as he looked toward the lake. He was not posed like the person in the photograph. No arm around her, no hand over a heart, no building behind them that promised a future.

The pencil moved faster than the first attempt at the facade. She had to pause twice to correct the proportions. Percy shifted when a pebble pressed into his heel, and the change altered the angle of his shoulder.

"Don't move," she said.

"I have a rock attacking me."

"Then tell the rock to wait."

He laughed, which made her look up. She had drawn his mouth as a line, but the laugh changed his face. She rubbed the mark away and tried again. The correction left a gray smear that she softened with her thumb. She liked that the sketch showed the correction. It was a record of looking at the person in front of her, not a tracing of somebody else's picture.

"You can look at me," she said. "I don't need you to hold a pose."

Percy turned his head toward her. "That might make it harder."

"Probably."

He stayed as he was, and she drew the outline of his profile. She put the lake behind him in two strokes, then added the edge where the shore curved. The paper was too small for detail, but the limits helped. She did not have to draw their entire future or even the whole view. She only needed enough to know what she had seen.

"Do you want to be in it?" he asked.

"You're already in it."

"I mean, do you want to draw me beside you?"

Annabeth looked at the space she had left open next to him. "I think so."

She drew the second figure smaller, blond hair suggested with a few short lines, shoulder turned toward him. She did not give either of them a dramatic expression. The two figures sat beside the lake, close enough that their shoulders touched. She added one uneven line under their feet to show where the bank ended. The rest she left blank.

Her first outline made the second figure lean too far forward, as if she were trying to reach the one beside her. Annabeth erased that line and moved the shoulder back. She wanted the two bodies to touch because she had watched them choose to sit together, not because one needed to be pulled toward the other. The correction left the space between their backs loose and unforced.

"You changed it," Percy said.

"The angle was wrong."

"I thought it looked fine."

"You weren't drawing it."

He nodded, accepting the correction as an answer rather than an invitation to persuade her. Annabeth sharpened the profile of the dark-haired figure's chin, then stopped before adding a face. The card was too small to hold a portrait; the posture and the lake were enough to show what she wanted to keep.

"It doesn't look like the picture," Percy said.

"Good."

"I meant it looks different."

"It is different."

She could see the old photograph in her mind without needing to put it in front of them. Here, the drawing showed the day she had just lived: the discussion, the cards, the way Percy had waited while she chose what to say, and the dinner where she had taken his hand. The sketch did not carry a caption, and she did not write one.

Percy did not ask whether she planned to keep it. He waited until she turned the card toward herself and checked the lines.

"I'm going to keep it," she said. "I want to remember this view."

"Okay."

She put the pencil away. When the breeze lifted the card's edge, Percy held it down while she checked the line of the shore. She tucked it safely into her pocket.

---

That night, Annabeth returned the photograph to Percy's bag. She tucked it in the inner pocket, smoothing the fold but not trying to erase it.

Beside it, she slipped a new sketch: two kids at the lake, one dark-haired and one blond, with their shoulders touching. No caption. No prophecy. No promise of what they would become.

Just what they were that day.

When Percy found it the next morning, he didn't ask whether she was sure. He only found her at breakfast, sat beside her, and offered his hand.

He had unfolded the sketch carefully enough to see the two figures and the shoreline, but he hadn't written on it or tucked it into the photograph's crease. When Annabeth came to the table, he placed the card beside her plate, leaving the photograph in the inner pocket of his bag.

"It fits there," he said, touching the edge of the sketch rather than the photo.

Annabeth looked at the two pieces. The old picture was a thing they remembered. The drawing was something she had made the night before. They could occupy the same pocket without becoming the same promise.

"Keep them both there," she said. "But don't fold the sketch into the photograph."

"I won't."

She took the card and set it beside her breakfast. The corner bent slightly under the weight of her cup, so she moved the cup before it could crease. Percy watched her rescue the pencil drawing from breakfast, then looked away before she had to explain why she cared about the paper.

Then she took his hand.
