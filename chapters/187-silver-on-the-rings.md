## Chapter 187: Silver on the Rings

The cold had moved into Cabin Three during the night and taken up residence at the foot of Percy's bunk.

He knew the difference between a draught and a tenant. A draught came down the boards from somewhere and went somewhere else, and if you moved an inch it went with you and then it was somebody else's problem. This one had settled in. It lay across his ankles and the tops of his feet with the patience of a thing that had been there since two in the morning, and when he pushed the blanket down to look at it, his breath went up towards the ceiling in a rope thick enough to see lamplight move inside, and the lamp was not even lit.

He lay still and did the arithmetic. The stove was not down to embers; the stove was out, he could tell by the quality of the silence coming off the iron, which is a thing only people who have slept within ten feet of a stove for five months know how to hear. The window was shut. The door was shut. And still the room had gone to the temperature of a root cellar, which meant the night had not been cold in the ordinary, reasonable, middle-of-October way. It had been cold the way a person is rude: completely, and on purpose, and to everybody at once.

So he got up, because the only sane reply to a cold that had come into your bed uninvited was to go out and meet it in the open, where at least it was showing off.

Getting dressed took nine acts. Socks first, two pairs, one drawn inside the other, a trick he had learned from a man who had spent his childhood on a trawler and had never once explained how it worked. Then trousers, the heavy wool shirt that still smelled faintly of the dryers after four washes, his field jacket, the gloves with two fingers mended at the knuckle, and his boots, which had been left standing to dry by the stove the night before and had come out of the night as stiff as two loaves.

He was at the door before he had to go back for the second glove, which had ended up under Silena's blanket where things he dropped always ended up, and he got it and got out, and did not slam the door, which was the only courtesy he had time for at ten to six.

"I put it under my blanket," Silena said, without opening her eyes, "because you leave things where they fall, and if you wake Chloe you will be sleeping in the canoe shed for the rest of the season." She rolled over, pulled the blanket up over her shoulder, and was asleep again in about four seconds, which was a talent he had never managed and had stopped asking about lessons in.

Outside, the hill had been replaced.

That was the first thought and it stayed the first thought for some time. The grass he had walked over a hundred times since June, knee-high in the patches and flat in the worn places, brown at the tips and green at the base the way grass goes in October, was not there. In its place stood a field of white that had been drawn on, blade by blade, in the dark, by somebody with a very steady hand and a great many hours to spare. Every blade carried its own line of it, on one side only, so the whole slope leaned a little the way the wind had come during the night, and in the hollows and behind the sheds, where the wind had not reached, the white lay down flat and uninterrupted, a clean page with no handwriting in it anywhere.

His boots went in and came up dark. The prints stayed behind: black sole-shapes pressed into the white, clear as a stamp, each one showing the tread pattern he had worn off his left heel since August. He stood at the top of the steps and looked back along the line he had just made and thought that it was the most honest mark anybody had left on that hill in a week, because everything else anybody had done in the last week had been got at by weather or by time, and a footprint in a frost does not lie about where a person has been.

He went down past the pavilion. The lake, at the bottom of the slope, had stopped being a thing that reflected overnight and become a thing that absorbed. Grey, with no shine anywhere on it, beaded in strips with a mist that sat on the water and did not rise, did not move, did not do anything at all except lie there being the colour of a chalkboard left out in the rain.

Six canoes were on their rack past the boathouse where they had been since the end of September, upside down and roped, and Percy went down to them because he had promised himself on the night they went up on the rack that he would look at them once a week whether or not there was any reason to.

There was a reason.

Frost had grown on the iron in the night, and it had grown the way rust grows, out of the metal, in the dark, without asking anybody, except that it was white and had managed six hours instead of six years. The rings along each gunwale, the four ring-bolts holding the tie-downs, the little brass pull on the drain plug: all of them had put on a pile of it, a fine erect fuzz standing off the iron in every direction and holding the grey that was coming into the east the way a wire holds a spark. Where a ring lay flat against a hull, the frost had made a perfect white washer, a clean untouched circle with half an inch of green paint in the middle of it, and that half inch of green was the only colour on the whole beach.

Percy hooked a finger through the nearest ring and lifted.

The frost came off the iron in one crumbly sleeve and hung on his glove for a second, a hollow tube still holding the shape of the ring inside it, and then it went to water and ran into the seam of the cuff, and the iron underneath was bare and dark and colder than the frost had been, because frost is an insulator, which was a fact he had read somewhere and for which he now had wet evidence.

"You will have that in your glove for the rest of the day and you will deserve it."

Clarisse was at the far end of the rack with a clipboard, coat collar up, hair done badly, which meant she had been out here before the light. She had been round the whole thing. He could see the route she had walked: one set of prints coming down the west side, along the rack, around the back, out towards the boathouse, all of it stamped black into the white without a deviation and without a place where she had stopped twice at anything.

"What time did you get up?"

"Four." She wrote something on the board. "The list got cold. The list and I had a disagreement about who was going to move first, and the list won."

"Does the list know it's a holiday?"

"It's Monday."

"It's the coldest night of the year so far and I've declared it a holiday, and I've got the authority."

"You've got nothing," Clarisse said. She was not looking at him when she said it. She was looking along the rack at six canoes wearing white on their rings, and she let her eyes go down the row the way a person counts a thing they are personally responsible for, and then she put the clipboard under her arm and said, "Breath's visible at breakfast. Silena's got the stove going and she's already lost one argument with it. Go and eat before she puts the whole loaf in a basket with your name on it, because she will, and then it's a chore."

Breakfast in Cabin Three happened in two stages, and everybody in the family knew the shape of it and nobody had ever written it down.

Stage one was the stove. The room when he came in was the temperature of the yard, and Silena was standing at the range with her hands out and her shoulders up around her ears and the kettle not yet singing, and the four kids already at the table were in their coats with their collars up, eating fast and saying very little, because talking costs breath and breath was visible. You could watch a sentence leave a person's mouth that morning: it came out of them as a small white cloud with words inside it, went about a foot and a half, and stopped existing. Chloe got as far as "the pump's" before the sentence dissolved over the bread plate and she gave up on it and shrugged instead.

Stage two was the stove catching. When it did, it did it all at once, with a knock and a rush of heat that came across the floor like a wave, and everybody moved their chair in at the same time without anybody saying anything, and coats came off, and within four minutes there were six people at that table talking over each other in the ordinary way, and the room fogged from the breathing and the cooking both, so that the window went from clear to milk in the time it takes to eat an egg.

Percy took the heel of the loaf.

"That's the heel," Clarisse said.

"It's called the heel, which makes it legally the youngest person's. It's in the code."

"There is no code."

"There's a code," Silena said, from the stove, not turning round. "I'll write one. It's going in the book tonight, right under rule five, and it's going to say the heel goes to whoever is willing to fight about it at seven in the morning, which is a badly written rule and I don't care."

"The heel goes to whoever's willing to fight about it," Percy repeated, working the crust free with both hands. "That's the best law this cabin has ever had."

"It's the only law this cabin has ever had," Clarisse said, "and you've just proved it's a stupid one, because you're the one who wanted it." But she pushed the butter across the table to him with two fingers, which was the part of the argument she actually cared about, and then she put the winter list face up beside her plate, because Clarisse read the winter list the way other people read the weather.

It had three lines on it still. She did not read them out. She did not have to; Percy had learned them by heart in the last fortnight, the way you learn a song somebody keeps playing: the greenhouse banked, the pavilion's west awning mended along the torn seam, the pine down in the cedar brake cut and stacked before it fell on something that mattered. Three jobs that needed a warm day, and there were no warm days coming, and the list knew it, and that was the disagreement it had won at four in the morning.

"Here's the day," Silena said, and set the pot down between them, and put her hands on her hips the way she did when she had organised something and wanted it noticed before she explained it. "Cider's on. It's going to stay on. At nine I'm taking the basket down to the hut and putting every sock and every scarf in this cabin through the dryers, and whatever comes out of those dryers does not go back in a drawer, it goes on feet, immediately, while it's still got the heat in it. Training's in the arena because the sand has gone to iron and I am not watching anybody crack a wrist on frozen ground for the sake of a schedule."

"Chiron said the arena."

"Chiron said the arena and I'm saying the arena. Same place. I'm just louder." She sat down and took the pot back and poured for herself last, which was how she always did it, and looked at Percy with the particular flatness of a sister about to deliver a finding. "You've got frost down your cuff."

"I've got frost down my cuff."

"You went down to the canoes before breakfast."

"I went down to the canoes before breakfast."

"And?"

Percy looked at his sisters across a table of steaming plates in a room with a fogged window and a stove that had just decided to be generous, and he held up his bare hand with the wet glove still on it, so that the two of them could see the dark stripe running from his wrist to his second finger where the ring had melted.

"Silver on all six rings," he said. "Every bolt, every plug. It's the best thing I've seen since October started, and I'm putting it in the day, and if anybody wants to come down and look at it after the dryer run, the offer stands."

Clarisse looked at the wet stripe for a second.

"I'll bring the board," she said.

Silena Beauregard had been awake since five and had spent the first hour of her Monday doing absolutely nothing about it, which took more discipline than anything else she did all week.

The thing she was not thinking about was Sunday. That was all it was. It was a Sunday that had happened, that was over, that was in the past tense and filed, and she had not thought about it once while she was asleep and she was not going to think about it now while she was lying in her bunk listening to her brother lose an argument with a glove. It had been a conversation. It had been a conversation at the bottom of a beach under a quilt with a fish sewn into it, and it had lasted until the lamps went out, and there had been a part of it where they had both stopped talking at exactly the same moment and neither of them had started again for what she estimated, afterwards, in bed, with her face in her pillow, at somewhere around eleven seconds.

Eleven seconds, and she had counted them twice in the dark, and that small sum was the entire crime, every bit of the damage a Sunday could do, and she was going to carry it around all week like a coin she would not spend.

She got up, she dressed, she took the mending from under the mattress where she had put it Friday, and she looked at it for as long as it took to fold it three ways and put it back, and then she took the note out of the pocket of her apron to check that the note was still in the pocket of her apron, which it was, which it had been every time she had checked it since Friday, which was now five times, and she put the apron on and went to light the second burner.

Her brothers came in the way they always came in: Percy first, at ten to six, with frost on his cuff and a story about a canoe, and Clarisse a minute behind him with a clipboard and a ruling she had been carrying around since the dark. She fed them both. She watched the two of them do the thing they had been doing for five months, which was that Clarisse said a thing in a voice like a gate closing and Percy agreed with the half of it he liked and invented a law about the other half, and neither of them noticed that it was affection, and both of them would have denied it in writing.

When the dryer run was announced and Percy went out for more wood, Clarisse stayed where she was with the list and did not look up, and said, in the voice she used for the bottom line of an inventory, "You're on the second check."

"I haven't done the first check."

"I know. I've done the first check." She turned a page she was not reading. "So have I. Twice. Since four."

Silena put both hands on the table, because the table was the only thing in the room that was not moving.

"He walked me up as far as the west path," she said.

"I know."

"I know you know. I'm telling you anyway. He walked me up as far as the west path and he asked me three questions and I answered two of them badly, and one of them I answered so badly that I have thought about it eleven times since, and every time I think about it I think of a better answer, and none of the better answers are any good either, because the actual answer was fine and I just said it in the wrong order."

"What were the three questions?"

"The first one was about the eyelets."

"There were sixteen of them."

"There were sixteen of them, and he remembered that there were sixteen of them, Clarisse, he remembered the number, he didn't say 'the eyelets', he said 'the sixteen', and I had to stand there and be a person who was not going to fall over in front of a fire." She took a breath and let it out slowly, watching it go. "The second one was whether the mending had come back all right, which it had, and which it came back with a note, which he already knew, because he had written the note, and he asked me anyway, because that is a thing he does."

Silena stopped. The kitchen had gone very warm, and she realized she was smiling with her whole shoulders, so she made herself look at the stove.

"And the third question," Clarisse said.

"The third question I answered badly. In the wrong order. And he didn't correct me, he just stood there and let me get to the right end of it on my own, and when I got there he said—" She stopped again, and this time she stopped on purpose, and took the spoon and stirred a pot that did not need stirring. "He said good. Just good. One word. And then the lamps went out and we came up the hill."

Clarisse wrote something on the winter list that was not on the winter list.

"That's the report," she said.

"That's the report."

"Filed." Clarisse turned the list around and slid it across the table so Silena could see what she had written, and what she had written, in the block hand, under the three undone jobs, was: SUNDAY - DONE. And under that, in the same hand: ASK HER ABOUT THE THIRD ONE AGAIN LATER, WHEN SHE'S NOT HOLDING A SPOON.

"You are the worst person in this cabin."

"I'm the third worst and you know the ranking," Clarisse said, and took her list back, and did not smile, and was entirely pleased with herself, and Silena went to get the basket, because the dryers were the warmest room in camp and she had a whole family to put back together before noon.

Annabeth Grace woke at six with her sister's boots already gone.

The morning's first fact, and it was a bad one. Thalia's boots were not the kind of boots that went missing; Thalia's boots were the kind of boots that were always exactly where they had been dropped, which was usually in the middle of the floor, at an angle, in the way, with a laces-knot in them that nobody but Thalia could have tied. And now there was a Thalia-shaped absence at the side of her bunk, and a rectangle of cold coming in under the door, and no boots.

Annabeth got up and dressed in the grey dark of Cabin One, which was a cabin built for two people who had never once agreed on where the window should be and had ended up with two windows, one of which let in the weather and the other of which let in the light, and this morning the weather window was doing the work. She put her hand flat on the sill to reach past it and took it back off again, the way you take a hand off a cold pan.

Down at the bottom of the west path, the nine lanterns had gone out for the morning, one after another along the switchbacks, and each one had left its little hour's worth of business behind it: a bead of wax on the hook, a smear of soot on the bracket, and, on the grass below the third one, a perfect white ring where the heat had kept the frost off and the frost had come right up to the edge of the warmth and stopped.

She found her sister at the trough.

The trough was the camp's oldest piece of plumbing, a granite basin fed off the hill spring, and this morning it had a skin on it, a grey unbroken sheet with one corner pushed up where Thalia had broken it with the edge of a bucket. Thalia was in her shirtsleeves. She had her sleeves pushed to the elbow and her forearms under the water and she was doing the thing Annabeth had watched her do a hundred times and had never once been able to do herself, which was to stand still in cold water and win.

"Morning," Annabeth said.

"Morning." Thalia did not turn round. She let the bucket fill and then she took it out and set it on the stones, and when she straightened up she put her right hand on her left shoulder and rolled it once, a small hard roll, the kind a person does when they think they are alone, and then she took the hand off again and left it at her side as if it had merely been passing.

"You've been up since four," Annabeth said. "You've been to the range. You've been to the range twice since Sunday and it's Monday."

"I've been up since five and I've been to the trough, as evidenced by the trough." Thalia picked up the bucket with both hands and turned and looked at her sister over the top of it, and her hair was unbrushed, and her face had the specific bright cheerfulness of a woman who has been caught and intends to be cheerful about it. "Two weeks of shooting, she says. She's counting my sessions. I have a stalker and she wears my sister's face."

"Two weeks of shooting in the cold," Annabeth said, "which is a different thing, and which you are doing because somebody corrected your elbow in September and you have not been able to leave it alone since."

"Somebody corrected my elbow and my elbow has been grateful ever since. It's a professional relationship." Thalia went past her up the path with the bucket, and Annabeth walked beside her, and did not say anything else for eleven steps, and then Thalia said, in a completely different voice, a flat one, from somewhere down near her boots: "The ground's iron. Everything's iron. The whole hill's gone to iron overnight and I could not tell you how much I don't want to talk about it."

"You don't have to talk about it."

"I know I don't have to talk about it. That's the best thing about this family, nobody makes you talk about anything." She set the bucket down at the bottom of the Cabin One steps and put her boot on the first one and stopped there, with her hand on her knee, for exactly as long as it took Annabeth to decide, on purpose, to look at the lake instead of at her sister, because Thalia Grace had spent her whole life being the person who carried things, and had learned early that some things get said faster when nobody is watching them get said. "Right," Thalia said, a second later, in her normal voice, entirely reconstructed. "Cocoa. I'm making cocoa. I'm making cocoa because I'm eighteen and this is my house and there is no law."

"There's a law," Annabeth said, going up past her with the bucket, because somebody had to carry it and it was not going to be her sister, not twice, not on a morning like this one. "I'll write it. It goes under the heel rule."

"There is no heel rule."

"There's a rule about everything that gets put on a list at four in the morning, and it's always the same rule, and it's always that somebody else carries the bucket."

Cabin One had a stove that had been lit by a person who was not going to admit to having lit it, and behind the stove on the rail, a pair of gloves, damp at the fingers, turned inside out, still cold at the cuffs. Annabeth hung her coat and said nothing about the gloves at all, and put the water on, and got the tin down, and the two of them stood at the counter with their shoulders not touching and their breath going up into a cabin that had not yet decided to be warm, and it was, Annabeth thought, exactly like every other morning they had ever had, except that one of them had been shooting for two weeks and had not said a true thing about her shoulder.

So she made the cocoa too thick, the way Thalia liked it and would never ask for, and she put the second cup in her sister's hands before she sat down, and Thalia took it and held it a moment in both palms without drinking, cupped like a bird she was not going to frighten, and said, without looking up: "You noticed."

"I noticed."

"You're not going to say what you noticed."

"No."

"Good." Thalia drank. "Because I noticed you noticed, and now neither of us has said it, and it's the most civil conversation we've had since Tuesday." She put her elbow on the table and her chin on her fist and her eyes on the window, where the frost was doing its slow work on the outside of the glass, drawing a white fern across the corner where the warm air from the stove had not reached yet. "He's coming Friday," she said. "For the range. He said he'd bring the good arrows and I said there were no good arrows, and I have been thinking about that sentence for four days."

"You've been thinking about the sentence."

"I've been thinking about the sentence. I've been thinking about the sentence while standing in a trough at half past five in the morning, Annabeth. That's not a compliment to him, that's a medical fact, and if you tell him I will deny it in front of Chiron."

"I'm not going to tell him anything. I don't know him."

"You know him better than I do and it's been going on for a month." Thalia grinned into her mug, which was the grin Annabeth had grown up on, and then she said, in the voice of a woman changing the subject with a crane: "Your boy went down to the canoe rack before light. Clarisse told me at the trough. Out of nowhere, with a bucket, and then she walked away, and I have been trying to work out whether that counts as conversation."

"It counts as reporting," Annabeth said. "She reports things sideways, usually at a person's head, and then walks off. It's the only kind she does."

"It's the best kind there is." Thalia put her mug down and stretched both arms above her head, slowly, and did the shoulder again on the far side, where she thought the angle was safer, and Annabeth watched the window instead of her sister and let the second one go, because the first one had shown her all it was going to show, and because there was a long cold day in front of both of them and it was not going to get any warmer because two girls said something about it.

The arena at eight in the morning in a hard frost was a different building from the arena in September, and Percy knew it before he got through the gate, because he could hear it.

In September the sand took the sound of a footfall and gave it back soft. This morning the ground under the gate gave back nothing at all. It had gone to board overnight, a single unbroken surface with a pale hard shine on it, and when he stepped onto it the print came out black and shallow and stayed there the way a print stays in set concrete, and there was a noise with it, a small dry crack, like a biscuit breaking under a boot.

"Sand's out," said the kid holding the gate, who was eleven and had been standing there with his hands in his sleeves for ten minutes trying to decide whether to go home. "And the dummies have got fur on them."

He was right about the dummies. They stood along the east wall with a white crust on their straw shoulders and their canvas heads, and where the frost had caught the hemp it had grown the same erect pile as the iron rings down on the beach.

The training that morning happened under the west arcade, which was the four-bay stone gallery along the arena's uphill side, roofed, open at the front, with the weapon racks set back under it and a brazier that Clarisse had got going at ten to eight and that she had got going, from the look of it, entirely by being rude to it.

"Two weeks," she said, as he came in. "You don't get a coat. Coats catch. Coats are a thing that happens to a person's sword arm at the exact moment it matters."

"It's nine degrees."

"It's cold and you're going to be cold, and cold people keep moving, and moving people are warm, and that is the entire lesson for Monday, so if anybody wants to go home and tell Chiron I said so, tell him I said so with my whole chest." She had the practice swords laid out along the rack in a row, blunt things with basket hilts and wintered grips, and she had put the good ones at the near end and the bad ones, the ones with the worn wraps, at the far end, which was a Clarisse arrangement: the person who gets there last gets the thing that still works.

They worked up and down the arcade in pairs, and the arcade was only thirty feet long, so it was not really sparring, it was a line of people doing footwork in a queue and getting told about it. Percy got through two partners and a telling-off and then found himself opposite his sister, who had clearly lost the coin she would never admit to having flipped.

"Feet," she said.

"My feet are fine."

"Your feet are the reason you got beat eleven times in September and you know it and you've done nothing about it since, because you got told once and you wrote it down and then you went and stood in a stream." She put her practice sword up and did not take her eyes off him. "Again. Heel under hip. Toe where you're going. You fight the way a person reads a menu, Jackson, all over the place and hungry."

"And yet."

"And yet nothing. You've got a good wrist and a bad map. Do it again."

So he did it again, and again after that, up and down the arcade with his breath going in front of him and his fingers going from cold to clumsy to gone and back again whenever he got near the brazier, and somewhere in the fourth or fifth pass his feet started putting themselves where his feet had always known to go and had been waiting, patiently, for him to catch up with, and Clarisse saw it happen because Clarisse saw everything that happened in a room and said only: "There. Now you're a person. Don't stand on it."

At ten past nine there was a noise from the gate that was not a footstep, and Silena came in across the frozen sand with a lidded basket on her hip and her coat done to the throat and her breath going in front of her like a small white flag of truce.

"Dryer run," she said, and set the basket down on the bench and opened it, and out of it came a heat, a real one, a soft warm breath of machine-hot air with the smell of the hut in it, of steam and lavender and warm cotton, and eleven people stopped being soldiers and became children again in about a second and a half.

"Feet," Silena said. "Now. While it's still got the heat in it. I'm not carrying it back up the hill because somebody wanted to finish a drill."

It was, Percy thought, standing there with a pair of his own socks from the bottom of the pile still hot against his palms, the most effective order his family had given anybody in a month, and it had been given by a girl with a basket and a braid, in a voice with no threat in it whatsoever, and every single person in that arcade did exactly what she said, including Clarisse, who took the last pair, looked at them, looked at Silena, and said, "These are mine, and you put them on top, which means you went down twice."

"I went down twice."

"Good." Clarisse sat down on the bench in front of the whole camp and put them on, one at a time, with her boots across her knees, and did not say another word about it, and that, in the language of Cabin Three, was a public commendation with a medal attached.

They broke at eleven on account of a wind that came over the arcade wall and took the brazier down two sizes, and Percy went out through the gate with his new socks in his boots and his old socks in his pocket and his hands slowly getting their minds back, and went round the west side of the arena instead of up the hill, because the climbing wall stood fifty yards past the arena's west corner and the ground behind it, in the shadow of the whole sixty feet of it, had been in the dark since Saturday and had never once seen the sun.

The lee of the climbing wall was the warmest place on the hill and the coldest, and it was both at once, and it was Annabeth's on a morning like this one. She had the rope bag and a coil of new line and a folding rule, and she was sitting on the flat stone at the base with her back against the wall's plinth and her boots on the frost and her field book open on her knees, and she was not doing anything about the cold at all, because she had found the one place where the cold could not get at a person from any direction except straight down, and had simply moved in.

"You're in my shadow," she said, without looking up.

"I'm in your shadow."

"It's a good shadow. It's the only one that isn't wet." She turned a page. "Your sister sent me hot socks and I've declined them on the grounds that my feet are already a separate country. How was the arcade?"

"Cold, and then instructive, and then cold again."

"That's her whole personality in three words."

"She says my feet fight like a person reading a menu."

Annabeth looked up from the field book then, properly, and gave him the smile she kept for things that were true and funny and slightly unfair, and said, "She said that in September and you wrote it down. You've still got it on the inside cover, in your handwriting, underlined twice."

"I underlined it three times."

"You underlined it twice and the third one is a coffee ring." She went back to the page, and he stood there a moment in her shadow with the frost all around him and the sun on the far side of the wall doing nothing at all for anybody, and then he sat down on the stone beside her, because the stone was warm where the sun had reached it around the plinth, and because he had run out of anywhere else he needed to be.

Annabeth Grace had the whole of a Monday stretched out in front of her with nothing much in it, and she spent the first ten minutes of it being aware of that, which was her entire problem in a sentence and which she had stopped trying to fix around the time she was eleven.

Percy sat down on the warm part of the stone with his back against the plinth and did not say anything for a while, and she did not say anything for a while, and the climbing wall went up sixty feet on their left with its painted holds running green and blue and red into a grey sky, and the frost on the ground in front of them had never seen the sun and had no plans to, and it was, she thought, turning a page she had already read, a very good place to be invisible in.

"What are you doing at four o'clock," he said.

"I'm doing this."

"You're doing this until four."

"Then I'm doing this until four, and then I'm doing whatever's at four." She put the pencil behind her ear. "Why."

"Because at four the light starts going and I'd like to be somewhere I can watch it go. That's the whole of it. It's not a plan, it's a preference. I have preferences now. I'm a person with preferences."

"You've had preferences since August and you've been hiding them under a queue of chores." She closed the field book. "Four o'clock. Where."

"Four o'clock, the porch, with cider, and if the porch is taken by your sister and my sister, which it will be, then the steps."

"The steps it is."

So they stayed where they were until the light started to think about going, which gave them the best part of five hours, and they spent them the way they spent most of October afternoons: working, arguing softly about a contour, and neither of them going anywhere at all.

They went up the hill at a quarter to four with the field book and the rope bag and nothing else, and the frost went ahead of them the whole way, because the sun had come round at noon and taken the white off the open ground, and then thought better of it and gone behind the ridge at ten past three, and the temperature had come down with it fast, the way it does on a hillside in October, so that the path they had walked at lunchtime in soft mud had set into a hard dark ribbon by the time their boots were on it, and their two sets of prints went along it side by side all the way up, black on brown, on a hill that had been white that morning and was going to be white again by nine.

Cabin Three, from the outside, at four in the afternoon, had a fogged window.

Not fogged the way a window fogs when a room is damp. Fogged the way a window fogs when a room is warm and full of people: milk-white from corner to corner, with two clear half-moons where somebody's elbow had been and a thin stripe along the sill where the cold from outside had won a hand's width of it back, and, through the milk, blurred and enormous, the orange of the stove door.

"It's four degrees colder in this cabin when the door's been opened than when it hasn't," Clarisse said from the big chair, without looking up from the list, "and it's going to stay that way if the two of you stand in the doorway doing arithmetic about your boots. Step on it. Put something on your feet. You've walked up a hill and you'll cool in ten minutes, and I am not starting a Monday night with a shivering brother."

"You've got the list out on a Monday."

"The list is out every night. The list is out on holidays. The list was out the night of the storm, in the dark, by a lamp, and it will be out tomorrow, and the day after that, and the day after that." She turned a page. "Dryer basket's by the stove. Socks are on top."

The socks were on top, and there were four pairs, and this was the part of a hard frost that Annabeth had not known she was waiting for since the summer closed: the part where the whole camp quietly rearranged itself around a temperature. The pools shut, the arena opened, and a girl with a basket went down at nine to a stone hut behind the cabins and came back up with a heap of machine-hot cotton and handed it out to a family one pair at a time.

Percy took a pair and held them a moment in both hands, the way a person holds a small animal, and then he looked down at her boots, and she looked down at her boots, and she said, "No."

"Your left one's damp. You've been out on frost since eight. I can hear it."

"You can hear my boot."

"I can hear your boot and it's got things to say." He held out the pair. "Trade. You take the hot ones and give me the damp ones, and then neither of us is a person walking around in a damp sock on the coldest night of the autumn out of pride."

"I don't have pride about socks."

"You have pride about socks. You have pride about everything. It's the most attractive thing about you and it's about to cost you a sock."

Annabeth Grace took off her left boot in the middle of her boyfriend's cabin, in front of his sister, and traded him a damp sock for a warm one, and did it with the flat efficiency of a woman signing a contract, and put her boot back on, and said, "The other one's fine."

"The other one's a separate country. You said so yourself at the wall this morning."

"It's still fine. It's fine the way a boot is fine." She stood up and stamped once, experimentally, and the warmth went from her foot into somewhere in the region of her entire spine, and she let out a breath she had been holding since the ridge went into shade, and said, in a tone of pure discovery: "Oh. That's dishonest. That is a dishonest quantity of warm."

Percy looked enormously pleased with himself and did not say one word about it, which was either growth or a tactic, and Annabeth decided not to investigate.

They carried the cider out onto the porch at five past four, two mugs going out and a third handed back through the door to Clarisse, who had refused to come out on the grounds that the list would get cold, and the two of them sat down on the second step, which was theirs now, in the way that certain steps at a camp become somebody's, not by law but by the accumulated weight of however many evenings have been spent sitting on them.

The sun went down behind the ridge at ten past five and did not linger. It took the colour off the grass first, and then the colour off the trees, and then, all at once, the whole hill, so that by the time the light left the lake the two of them were sitting in the blue-grey that comes after, with the frost coming back into the open ground a foot at a time in front of them, and their breath going up together into the space between their faces.

Annabeth put her mug down on the step beside her and turned her hand palm-up on her knee, which was not a gesture anybody had ever taught either of them. It had arrived one evening in June, in a canoe, and it had stayed, and it was the only sentence in the language that had never once needed translating.

Percy took it. His fingers were cold and his palm was not, and he laced his through hers and turned her hand over and looked at it for a second, at the pencil callus on the middle finger and the small white scar on the heel of it. The sight brought back the memory he carried from the future that had been erased: a blade slipping in a boathouse, in a month this life had not reached. Then he put his mouth against her knuckles, lightly, the way a person tests a doorknob in winter, and stayed there, and Annabeth's whole attention went down into that one square inch of skin and stayed there with him.

"You're thinking out loud," he said, against her hand.

"I'm thinking quietly."

"You're thinking at a volume I can hear and it's about the survey."

"It was about the survey four hours ago. It isn't about the survey now." She turned her hand over and put her thumb across his wrist, where the pulse ran, and felt it go on doing its work under the cold skin, steady and unhurried, and said, "It's about the fact that you got frost off a ring this morning with this hand, and it's the same hand you're holding mine with now, and somewhere in this family we've decided that's ordinary."

"It is ordinary. It's the most ordinary thing I've got." He lifted his head and looked at her, and the light was going out of the sky behind her, and the first of the west-path lamps had come on two hundred yards down among the trees, a small amber point in the dark, and his face was the only thing in the world at that hour with any warmth in it, and Annabeth decided, with the part of her that made decisions quickly and kept them for years, that she was not going inside for a while.

She got her other hand up and put it flat against the side of his neck, under the collar, where the cold had got in, and Percy made a noise like a man who had been ambushed by a piece of ice and did not move a single inch away from it, and she laughed, and the laugh broke out of her like steam off a kettle, and hung a heartbeat in the cold, and was gone.

"That," he said, "is the most unfair thing anybody in this family has ever done to me."

"It's the most effective thing anybody in this family has ever done to you."

"Both of those are true and I want you to know that I'm letting you." He put his own hand up and covered hers where it lay against his neck, and held it there, and that was how they sat, with the frost coming up the hill towards them and the lamps coming on down the path one after another, and neither of them in any hurry, and the cider going cold on the step beside them, and neither of them mentioning it, because the only warm things on that porch were the two of them and they had both decided, silently and without discussion, not to be the one who said so out loud first.

Then Annabeth leaned across the eight inches between them, which had been a formality for about an hour, and put her mouth on his.

It was not a quick one. Nothing they did in October was quick any more, and this one in particular had the shape of a thing that had been waiting since a Saturday night in September to be done properly on a porch in the cold. So she took it slowly, and he let her, and the first of it was just the cold of it, two people chilled to the bone and neither of them willing to be the one who warmed first, and after that stopped mattering, and the second of it went on for a long time: long enough that her hand slid from his neck into his hair, long enough that his hand came up and settled where her jacket met the back of her neck, long enough that the hill behind them went from blue-grey to black and the tenth lamp came on below the cedar brake and neither of them moved at all.

They broke for breath, which is the part that people who have not done this do not understand, because the breaking is not the ending of it. They broke and stayed where they were, an inch apart, breathing the same cold air, her forehead against his jaw, his thumb moving once slowly along the line of her shoulder, and the breath they shared kept forming and dissolving between them in a small bright cloud, and Percy said, quietly, into her hair: "We should go in."

"We should go in."

"Neither of us is going in."

"No." She did not lift her head. "We're not going in. We're going to sit here until one of our sisters comes out and tells us to, and then we're going to argue about it, and then we're going to go in."

"That's the plan?"

"That's the plan. I've had worse plans. I've had plans with survey rods in them."

He laughed, and it went through her where she was pressed against him, and she kept her eyes closed and let it, and then he put his arm properly around her and pulled her in, and she went, because she always went, because that had been the arrangement since the beginning, and the blanket from the porch hook came over both of them, and they got arranged, which took a while and involved a great deal of negotiation about whose elbow was where and which of them was leaning on the post, and by the time they were arranged the light had gone entirely, and the lake at the bottom of the hill had turned into nothing at all, and the two of them sat in the dark on the second step of Cabin Three under a blanket too small for both of them, shoulder to shoulder down the whole length of their bodies.

An hour later Annabeth was asleep.

Not properly asleep. Properly asleep on a porch in October with a hundred and twenty campers on the hill behind her was a state she had never once achieved and was not going to achieve now; what she did instead was something in the country next door to it. A slow leaning. A slowing down. Her breath evening out until it matched the breathing of the person she was leaning on, and her head going by degrees, a quarter of an inch at a time, until it was against his shoulder and then against his neck, and her hand, which had been holding his sleeve, sliding down until it found his, and staying there with her fingers hooked loosely through his, in the grip of a person who has stopped paying attention to holding on and is being held instead.

Percy did not move. Percy did the thing he had learned to do across two lifetimes and one long hill, which was to be the place where somebody was resting and to make no fuss about it at all. He sat with his back against the porch post and his girl asleep on his shoulder under a blanket that was too small for both of them, and he watched the frost come back up the hill, and he felt her breathing slow until it was slower than his, and he put his cheek against her hair and closed his eyes and did not sleep, because somebody had to keep watch on a porch in the cold, and there was nobody else there, and he had never once minded that particular job.

The door opened at ten past seven and Clarisse stood in the rectangle of stove-light with her list and her mug and looked down at the two of them for a moment, and said, in the flat voice she used for counting heads on a beach at midnight: "Bed."

"In a minute," Annabeth said, from somewhere near his collarbone, with her eyes shut.

"It's been a minute for about an hour." Clarisse put her mug down on the rail, picked up the cider from the step, looked at it, considered it, drank it, and set it back. "Cider's cold. Both of you are cold. The list says bed, and the list isn't going to argue about it, and neither am I." She went back to the door and then, without turning round, in the voice her family used for saying important things to the backs of heads: "Good day, by the way. Best frost I've seen in three years, and the socks worked, and there's a mug of hot things on the table for whichever of you comes in first, and if nobody comes in, I'm having that too."

The door shut and the rectangle of light went with it, and Annabeth sat up slowly, with her hair on one side and her glasses a little bent, and looked at the dark hill in front of her, and said: "She drank my cider."

"She drank both our ciders. She'd have drunk the whole porch if we'd given her another minute." He stood up and held out both hands, and she took them, and he pulled her up, and she stood there in the dark with the blanket around her shoulders and her head still at the angle of a person who has spent forty minutes asleep on somebody's shoulder, and said: "I wasn't asleep."

"I know."

"I was resting my eyes in a strategic fashion."

"I know that too."

"Percy."

"Annabeth." He put the blanket back on the hook and opened the door for her, and the stove-light came out across the two of them, and the porch was the coldest place on the hill, and neither of them mentioned it once, and they went in.

Thalia Grace went back down to the range at six, which was a thing nobody at that camp did, and which she did three nights a week, and which she had told nobody about, and which was the entire reason her right shoulder had been singing at her since Sunday.

The range at six was not a range. It was a fence, a line of straw butts gone grey in the dark, and a hundred and thirty yards of cold air with nothing in it, and she stood at the peg with her bow across her arm and her quiver on the post and did not nock anything for a while, because the light had gone and there was no point, and because the first three times she raised the bow that evening her arm told her, in a flat administrative voice, exactly what it thought of two weeks of shooting in weather like this one.

So she lowered it, and stood there, and said, out loud, to the fence and the dark and nobody: "Right."

"Right what?"

Rhys Calder was coming up the path from the Hephaestus side with a lantern in one hand and a bowyer's file in the other, in a coat too big for him, with his hair doing the thing it did when he had been indoors at a bench all day and had come out without checking. He stopped at the edge of the light and did not come any closer, which she had noticed in a month of archery: he stopped at the edge of things and waited, the way a person waits when the last three feet of a conversation are not his to take.

"Right nothing," Thalia said. "Right, it's dark, and I'm going home."

"Good." He put the file in his pocket. "It's nine degrees and the range is closed and the frost has already taken the first peg, so you're not going to get a straight answer out of any arrow you loose tonight, and you know it, and you came down anyway."

"I came down to collect."

"You came down to collect and you haven't loosed one, and you've been standing there long enough for me to walk up from the forge and stop being polite about it." He held the lantern out a little, the way you hold a light so somebody can see without being seen, and said, "Shoulders go in the cold. Everybody's do. You've got two weeks of a hard draw in it and it's telling you things and you're going to pretend it isn't, and I'm not going to make you say it, because it's not my business and I like my fingers."

"Smart."

"I'm a very smart man with cold hands." He set the lantern down on the post between them. "I'll take the quiver as far as the path and you can take it from there and neither of us has to talk about it. That's the offer. It's the only one I've got tonight."

Thalia Grace looked at the lantern, and the man, and the hundred and thirty yards of dark that she was not going to shoot into, and she picked up the quiver and put it in his hand, and said: "If you tell my sister I'll deny it."

"I'm not telling anybody. I'm carrying a bag of arrows uphill." He turned and they went up the path side by side, not touching, with the lantern swinging between them, and after a while he said, in the tone of a man reading a receipt out loud: "You shot eleven tens on Sunday."

"I shot twelve."

"You shot eleven and one that you called a ten because it was close, and I let it stand because arguing about a tenth of an inch in the cold is how a person gets a reputation." He lifted the lantern at the bend to show the frost, which had come back onto the path in the twenty minutes since it was last walked, and then he set it down on the third post, exactly halfway up, and said, "That's my three feet. Good night, Grace."

"Good night, Calder."

She watched him go on up towards the cabins with his hands in his pockets and no lantern, and she stood in the dark for four seconds, which was the budget, and then she picked the lantern up off the third post and carried it the rest of the way, because he had put it there for her to carry, and because neither of them had had to say so, and that, in her experience of a life spent being the one who carried things, was the strangest and best thing anybody had done for her in about eleven years.

Percy Jackson ate the mug of hot things at ten past seven, in the cabin, standing up, and gave the rest of it to Thalia, who came in off the path with a lantern that was not hers, and neither of them mentioned the lantern.

The camp outside was doing what it always did on the night before a hall night, which was to get slowly and visibly excited. You could hear it in the pavilion, and in two kids at the west-path bend having a completely unnecessary argument about whether rule five covered their cousin from the Hermes cabin, which it did, and had since October.

Clarisse had the winter list face down on the table, which was the rule she had invented and enforced herself, and the hall went on the bottom of it every Tuesday and Thursday in the same block hand and was never once crossed off, because it was not that kind of job.

"Hall's tomorrow at seven," she said, to the room. "Bathhouse first, then the hall, and if anybody turns up in clothes they've been training in, I'm putting them in the deep end with their boots on." She looked up. "That's you, Jackson."

"I haven't decided whether I'm coming."

"You've decided twice since breakfast and both times it was yes, and you said it to the canoe rack." She put the pencil down. "Rule five. Read it and then go to bed."

---

Annabeth Grace was at the Cabin One table at eleven with the field book open and the lamp turned low, and she wrote the day down the way she wrote every day down, in small even figures, because a day that is not written down is a day that has not been finished with.

The temperature at the north spring seep, four o'clock: the same as Saturday. The temperature at the lake margin, five past five, with her hand flat on the water: cold enough to take the hand back, and she wrote that one in words instead of numbers, which she did only when a number would mean nothing in three years. The frost line on the west path at seven: back to the second switchback, half an hour after it had been walked. The wind, none. The sky, clear and getting clearer, which was the part that mattered, because a clear October sky on a hill is not weather, it is an instruction: every degree of heat the ground collected during the day goes back up between midnight and dawn, and the frost that had silvered six canoe rings that morning was going to have a brother by Tuesday breakfast, and a bigger one.

She wrote: TUESDAY. HEAVIER. WATCH THE GUTTERS.

And under that, because it was hers and because nobody else read the margin notes: *Quilt on the poles. Heron on the bow. Tyson's letter due Thursday. Everything on the hill is either iron or asleep.*

Annabeth closed the field book, then opened it again long enough to underline the warning about the gutters. She had drawn the frost line carefully and made a note about the wind, but hadn't written down who would check the drains in the morning. A measurement without a useful consequence was only half a job.

She added: ASK CHIRON BEFORE BREAKFAST. Then she left the pencil on the table, where it couldn't go missing under her pillow.

Through the wall came the usual sounds of Thalia settling down, boots dropping one at a time, then the scrape of the chair against the floor. Annabeth heard the pause before her sister spoke.

"Go to bed, Annie."

"I'm going."

"You said that five minutes ago."

"I was writing."

"I know. You only sound that guilty when you're writing."

Annabeth turned the lamp lower. The sketch on the page was still open to the frost-ring measurement. She ran a finger along the penciled line and decided not to redraw it. The line was accurate enough.

"Thalia?"

"What?"

"You don't have to come with me in the morning. It's a gutter check."

"Wonderful. A thrilling outing."

"That's why I don't expect a crowd."

Her sister made an amused noise through the wall, then rolled over. Annabeth closed the cover properly this time. She would speak to Chiron when there was light enough to see the roof and safe enough to climb. Outside the clear sky promised a harder frost than the night before. The promise wasn't good or bad. It was a reason to leave her boots beside the door, ready for the morning.
