## Chapter 115: The Old Photograph

Annabeth had learned a shelf could turn dangerous without bothering to be tall. Even dust had opinions in the Zeus cabin, and that morning they were all hers to manage.

The one in the Zeus cabin stood only shoulder-high, yet it combined a loose backboard, a stubborn hinge, and a collection of objects arranged by the sisters and their occasional visiting brother, with mutually exclusive theories of storage. Dust furred the upper ledge, grey and soft under Annabeth's fingertip, and every touch shook loose the faint smell of old pine and sun-warmed varnish, with a metallic tang from the helmet perched on top. Light slanted through the narrow window and laid a bright bar across the warped wood, exposing scratches, a water ring, and a pencil mark where someone had once measured something and given up.

Thalia called it “the history shelf.”

Annabeth called it “a structural hazard with a mythology problem.”

“If you say that again,” Thalia warned, bracing the shelf one-handed, “your books go up there.”

“Then it'd be a mythology problem. You've got at least one lightning book upside down.”

“It bends the rules.”

Setting the screwdriver down, Annabeth gave the shelf a careful push with her palm. Wood creaked. It wobbled once, then settled, the backboard holding fast. “There. Nobody dies from a falling helmet today.”

“That's the spirit. Aim low, exceed expectations.”

Beyond the open door, morning camp moved at full tilt: campers crossing the green with shields and breakfast plates, somebody hauling a suspicious bundle of laundry, a naiad arguing with a canoe about where it had been parked. The canoe's defense sounded thin. Somewhere, bacon fat popped and a conch coughed half a note. The air carried cut grass and woodsmoke, damp canvas and warm stone, and through it all the bright clatter of dishes from the pavilion, spoons ringing against bowls, sandals slapping the paths.

Thalia tested the shelf with her shoulder, listening for the creak that meant the screws had bitten. The wood groaned, then held, and the faint tremor traveled up through her arm into her jaw. Annabeth watched the way morning light caught the dust they'd disturbed, gold motes hanging and turning slowly above the tools.

Crouching for the last screws, Annabeth's fingers brushed something pale wedged behind the backboard. A slip of white against the shadowed wall. For half a second it passed for a note — then the white border caught the light, glossy and unmistakable.

“Thalia?”

“If that's another screw, it's yours now.”

By the corners, Annabeth lifted the picture free. It had been folded once, the crease gone soft with age. The colors had thinned to ghosts: a patch of red, a washed blue sky, the greenish gray of sidewalk after rain. The paper felt cool and slightly slick, its edge feathery where the fold had worn.

Three children filled the frame.

Thalia stood at the back, maybe eight or nine, hair shorter than Annabeth had ever seen in photographs and sticking up in tufts around her head. One arm curled around Annabeth's middle. The other hitched a toddler onto her hip. Jason's round face turned toward the camera, solemn, one fist clamped around the strap of Thalia's jacket.

Small enough that her feet barely reached the step beneath her, Annabeth sat in front in a yellow raincoat, hair escaping its braid. A paper boat rested in both her hands, cradled with the solemn care of a sailor guarding salvage from a wreck.

The nose was hers. So was the set of the mouth. Recognition arrived sideways — less a retrieved moment than a room entered after someone else rearranged the furniture: unfamiliar in arrangement, familiar in shape.

Her thumb hovered short of the paper boat.

Underneath the raincoat and sidewalk rose something else: the blue Athena cabin, bronze and lemon polish and old paper, older girls arguing over a map while gray eyes — her mother's eyes — corrected the angle of a miniature temple roof. No date came stapled to the memory. Only the stubborn conviction of having belonged there.

The picture belonged to this sky. The cabin memory belonged to another. Both arrived uninvited.

Turning the photograph over, Annabeth found faded blue ink in round, slanted handwriting: *The whole crew. Safe harbor, at last.* Beryl's, perhaps. Or Thalia's, younger.

“You found that?” Thalia asked.

She'd rounded the shelf, teasing gone quiet. Her hand lifted toward the picture, then stopped midair. “Thought that was in the box.”

“Wedged behind the board.”

“Tracks.” Thalia's gaze moved between the photo and Annabeth's face. “Want to keep it out?”

“Not sure yet.”

“Okay.” Thalia let her hand drop. “No need to decide before breakfast.”

Small kindness, that. It landed anyway. The tightness behind Annabeth's eyes eased a fraction, and she became aware of the cabin around her again — the cool packed-earth floor under her knees, the smell of Thalia's leather wristband and the faint ozone that always clung to Zeus cabin bedding after a storm, the steady tick of the loose hinge cooling in the morning air.

Sliding the photograph into the front pocket of her notebook, Annabeth pressed it flat. Paper whispered against paper. “I'll bring it along.”

“Sure.” Thalia reclaimed the screwdriver, twirling it once. “And I'll pretend I wasn't about to ask what you remember.”

“You can ask later.”

“Deal.”

They finished securing the shelf together. Thalia set the helmet back on top, then — at one look from Annabeth — relocated it to the lower ledge. A limited victory. Annabeth had learned to collect those.

At the door, Thalia bumped her shoulder. “Come eat. Skip breakfast and Silena sends a search party. Clarisse brings a spear.”

“Sounds less like a search party, more like a threat.”

“Why not both?”

Sunshine spilled across the threshold, warm with cut grass and woodsmoke from the kitchens. Stepping into it, Annabeth kept one hand over the notebook pocket, as if the paper might slip free and take flight.

---

Percy had saved a place at the end of the Zeus table, between himself and a basket of toast.

A napkin folded into a small, determined tent guarded the bread. His handiwork, clearly. He brightened when Annabeth sat. The pavilion hummed around them — milk sloshing in jugs, Ares kids banging benches, the warm yeasty breath of the toast basket rising into her face, butter melting into soft yellow pools.

“Toast protection detail,” he said. “Dangerous pavilion.”

“Protecting it from whom?”

“The Stolls. Possibly Clarisse. Threat hierarchy pending.”

Fishing a slice from beneath the napkin, Annabeth raised an eyebrow. “Security breach.”

“Controlled test. You're part of the test.”

She smiled, though her free hand drifted to the notebook pocket. Percy clocked the gesture. Instead of reaching across or prying, he buttered an apple slice, set fruit on her plate, and waited a beat before asking, “What's on your mind?”

Easier to answer for the asking — and harder to wave away.

“Found a photograph in the cabin. Me, Thalia, and Jason. From before camp.”

Attention sharpened his expression, without alarm. “Can I see?”

Between their plates, Annabeth laid the picture down. Leaning in, he kept his syrupy fingers scrupulously clear of the gloss.

“You look ready to lecture the photographer on the boat's historical significance,” he said.

“Could've been significant.”

“Fair. Age seven, I once informed a lifeguard a plastic shark was historically significant.”

“And was it?”

“Nope. Excellent shark, though.”

The laugh loosened something behind her ribs. Not entirely. Enough to breathe around.

“Don't remember it being taken,” she said. “I can picture rain, Thalia's jacket, Jason yanking my hair. But that's looking at it and filling in stories I've heard, not remembering the day.”

“No need to prove which bits are yours.”

“I know. Not asking you to quiz me.”

“I know.” Though the pavilion roared around them, he lowered his voice. “Only saying it 'cause I'm curious, not 'cause you owe me footnotes.”

Another look at the faded colors. “It's dragging the other life up with it. Athena's cabin. My old bunk. Books, maps, that absolute certainty about who I was supposed to become.”

“And now?”

“Now I stare at this and hear: you should recognize every second. Fail the girl in the photo if you don't. Then the Athena memories barge in, and I feel like I'm betraying Thalia by missing a childhood that wasn't hers.”

Percy studied the photo, then her. “Two separate loads.”

“All tangled.”

“Believe it.” He turned his cup slowly between his palms. “Want help untangling, or should I sit here and run toast defense against the Stolls?”

“Both, if you've got the range.”

“Overachiever, right here.”

“You left one sock out in a snowstorm.”

“Overachiever in selected fields.”

Aloud, Annabeth tried to sort it. Fact: three kids on a wet step. Fact: handwriting, author unknown. Possibly rain. The Athena cabin, the older life — vivid to her, even where it didn't belong to the picture.

“Maybe the picture doesn't have to rule on the memories,” Percy said. “It can stay three kids on a step. And the Athena stuff can stay memories you carry — not orders about what you want now.”

“Say it like it's easy.”

“It's not.” His thumb traced the rim of his cup. “I get flashes from a life I can't move back into. Doesn't make them fake. Doesn't mean I've got to rebuild that life brick for brick, either.”

She knew how he loved Atlantis as home and still grieved Sally, the mother he'd loved in the older life. One love hadn't canceled the other.

“So what do you do with it?” she asked.

“Some days I tell the story. Some days I miss somebody. Some days I swim and don't argue with canoes.”

“Has the canoe apologized?”

“Being difficult.”

“Rough.”

“Coping.”

Her fingers eased around the picture's edge. “Don't want to throw the Athena memories out. Don't want them driving, either.”

“Then they don't get the wheel.”

“Not that simple.”

“No. But you could start with one thing.” He nodded at her notebook. “Decide what happens to this picture. That needn't settle everything else.”

Choosing the picture's fate without letting the past dictate or pretending the present came easy — that felt possible.

“Want Thalia's story,” she said. “First.”

“Good next step.”

“And I want to keep it.”

“Then keep it.”

“It's Thalia's too.”

“Then ask what she wants.”

Nodding, Annabeth took his offered hand across the table and squeezed once. His palm held the cup's warmth, a little damp with condensation, thumb rough with a healing blister from the rig. They stayed linked for a breath while camp clattered on — a plate ringing bright against stone, a satyr laughing with his mouth full, Clarisse informing someone a serving spoon was not a spear, her voice carrying clean across the clamor.

Percy glanced toward the noise. “Intervene?”

“No. Clarisse has a point.”

“That's the worrying part.”

Shoulder to his, she leaned in. “Thanks for not trying to fix it.”

“Fixing the toast. The rest, I'm hearing out.”

Quick and familiar, she kissed his cheek. “Good.”

“Also, I can help build a frame.”

“No carving.”

“Holding nails. I'm excellent at holding.”

“Approved.”

He grinned. “Learning.”

---

After breakfast, Annabeth carried the picture to the work table behind the Big House.

Wind combed the maples overhead; the yard smelled of cut grass, metal filings, and coffee drifting from an open window. A file rasped somewhere in the forge, rhythmic and distant, and shavings curled warm and fragrant under Annabeth's sandals. She studied the photo while measuring the weather vane's loose bracket, thumb smudged with graphite, and fresh details surfaced without any magic — Thalia's jacket strap wound around Jason's fist, a crayon drawing half-hidden under a boot, a strand of hair pasted to a cheek by damp. The bracket's cool edge pressed into her palm as she steadied it, ticking faintly in the breeze.

Thalia had cared for the girl in the picture before that girl had owned the word *sister*. The Athena memories held a different family: half-siblings, camp rules, a mother composed of wisdom and distance. Pride at being claimed; loneliness from trying to shine before she'd learned how to tire.

Neither truth shrank the photograph, nor made her current life a mistake.

A camera caught what it caught. It couldn't supply the missing day, or separate imagined details from Thalia's retellings, or decide which childhood she ought to miss. Pencil in hand, Annabeth drew a line on the work sheet, then paused.

In the sketchbook margin she wrote four short headings.

*What I can see.*

*What I imagine.*

*What I've been told.*

*What I choose now.*

Beneath the first: *Thalia's arm around us. Jason glaring at the weather like he's filing a complaint. Me with a paper boat.*

Beneath the second: *Rain smell. A hand on my shoulder. Details I can imagine, not recover.*

The third stayed blank. Thalia hadn't told this story yet.

Beneath the fourth: *Ask her. Keep the picture if she wants me to.*

Edges, at least, if not untangled string.

“You know,” came a voice behind her, “most folks write down the bracket dimensions.”

Turning, Annabeth found Percy with wood stacked in his arms, twine hooked around one wrist, a damp curl stuck to his forehead. Sawdust clung to his sleeves.

“Did write those down.”

“You drafted an emotional blueprint.”

“Compact blueprint.”

He thumped the wood onto the table. “Chiron said the scrap pile's fair game if I helped Beckendorf haul it. Figured — frame?”

“Carried lumber clear across camp?”

“Not far.”

“From the forge?”

“Define far.”

“Thought so.”

He flipped a board, checking for the straightest grain. “Make one? You measure, I'll hold still.”

“Specialty?”

“Strong stillness record.”

“Got thrown off a dock by your own wave once.”

“Rude wave.”

Laughing, Annabeth tucked the photo safe and sketched a frame pattern. Cedar, marked corners, one botched cut that sent them back to start. Sawdust sweetened the air, catching in her throat with its piney bite; the pencil rasped softly against grain, and the cedar felt silky where Percy had already sanded, rough where he hadn't. A fly droned past and settled on the warm wood. Sawdust clung to Percy's forearms in pale streaks, and he blew it off with a quick puff that scattered gold into sunlight.

Percy held each strip steady while she measured, advising only when asked. He sanded an edge with care and tested it with his thumb before passing it back.

“Good at this,” she said.

“Holding?”

“Paying attention.”

Openly, he met her eyes. “Paying attention to you, yeah.”

Heat touched her cheeks. “Rehearsed.”

“Natural talent.”

“Menace.”

“Still allowed to help?”

“Yes.”

Shaped but unglued — Annabeth wanted Thalia's say before committing to display — the frame waited. She wrapped the photo in clean paper and slipped it into her notebook.

They headed back toward the cabins together, Percy toting the unfinished frame under one arm. Hands brushed once, then found each other. His fingers curled around hers without tugging. Quiet settled, even with the old cabin, the old photograph, and the paper boat crowding her head.

“Keep thinking the Athena memories should run cleaner,” she said after a while. Gravel crunched underfoot; pine sap sharpened the breeze. “They don't. They're all mixed with homesickness and temper and trying to earn attention I already had.”

“Memories don't come edited for clarity.”

“Yours do?”

“Every one heroic. Perfect lighting.”

“I've watched you eat with syrup down your chin.”

“Heroically.”

“Naturally.”

She squeezed his hand. “Glad I can give you the messy version.”

“Want the messy version.”

His steadiness — near without crowding, offering help instead of seizing it — made belief easy. She was choosing Percy now: not because some older memory had paired them, but because she wanted him alongside her on a sunlit path, carrying a crooked frame.

“Want to tell Thalia today,” she said.

“Then we'll find her.”

“No need to both go.”

“I know.”

“But I want you there.”

“Okay.”

---

Thalia occupied the Zeus cabin porch after lunch, sharpening a practice knife with more focus than the edge required. Steel hissed faintly against stone, a thin rhythmic shiver. Sun beat on the porch boards and drew out the smell of hot wood and dry grass; cicadas stitched the afternoon with their high electric hum. A bead of sweat traced Thalia's temple and she wiped it absently on her sleeve without breaking rhythm.

Annabeth settled on the opposite step. Percy set the unfinished frame at the porch edge, then hovered until her glance sent him to the post — near enough to matter, far enough to leave the sisters air.

“Found this behind the shelf,” Annabeth said.

Thalia's gaze dropped to the photograph. The knife stilled. “Wondered where that went.”

“What's the story?”

“The true one, or the one I invented at eight when you asked why we were soaked?”

“There were multiple versions?”

“At least three. Artistic license.”

“True one, please.”

Thalia weighed her words, thumb worrying a frayed seam on her jeans. “Laundromat on the way to the coast. Mom ran laundry. Curb flooded, so we waited out the rain before catching the bus.”

“Rain — I can almost picture it.”

“I've told you bits. You were four. Asked if the dryers were ship portholes.”

Glancing down, Annabeth almost smiled. “Sounds like me.”

“Was you to the life. Floor was ocean, baskets were lifeboats. Jason was too small to play along. Wanted to chew the handles.”

“In the picture too?”

“On everything. I kept prying his fingers off the dryer door. You were captain, so you drafted me as lookout and ordered a rescue if seas turned rough.”

“Did you?”

“Fished it from a puddle outside. You were deeply impressed.”

Vision blurring at the faded edges, Annabeth blinked hard, irritated at her eyes. “Who took it?”

“Mom's disposable camera. Right after you declared me the bravest lookout in the world.” Thalia laughed, quick and fond. “I informed you I was the *only* lookout in the world.”

“Technicality.”

“You promised to check the charts.”

Annabeth turned to the back: *The whole crew. Safe harbor, at last.* “Her writing?”

“Yeah.” Thalia studied the looping ink. “She wrote down the bright spots. Used to think she was trying to prove we were okay.”

“And now?”

“Think she wanted proof a good afternoon could survive a bad week.” Thalia rubbed denim under her thumb. “Different thing.”

Holding the photo where both could see, Annabeth asked, “Kept it for her?”

“First, yeah. Crammed everything portable into that box. Terrified we'd lose the pictures.” A pause. “Then kept this one 'cause I like it.”

“Why this one?”

“You looked in charge.”

“Was four.”

“Bossy at four. Gifted.”

“Been taking notes, I see.”

Thalia nudged her knee with her own. “Serious about that boat. Found Jason a job even while he tried to eat the basket. Kept handing out posts till the whole operation made sense.”

Paper edge pressed into Annabeth's fingertip. “I can't remember that day.”

“Don't have to.”

“But I like hearing it.”

“Then I'll keep telling it.” Thalia met her eyes. “And if you understand it differently, that's fine too. Being there doesn't mean I get to tell you what to feel about it.”

The sentence loosened a knot Annabeth had mistaken for law. Thalia had raised her; Thalia was her sister — her older sister, daughter of Zeus like her. Neither fact obliged Annabeth to borrow Thalia's certainty in order to belong.

“Sometimes I miss Athena,” Annabeth said. “Not only being claimed. The cabin, the way life made sense around her. Then I look at this and feel I've got to pick which childhood counts.”

A glance toward Percy — examining the porch post with great dedication — drew his small nod. Listening, not intruding.

“Annie,” Thalia said, “love being your sister. Don't need you to erase anyone else to prove it.”

Thalia's arm settled loose and warm behind her shoulders; Annabeth leaned in, shoulder to shoulder, denim rasping softly, sunscreen and leather rising off Thalia's skin. The porch boards pressed hard lines into the backs of her legs, grounding her. The photograph and Thalia's stories could sit beside her own memories without replacing them: a child whose bus ride Thalia could describe, the daughter of Athena who built plans from fear amid lamplight and chalk dust, the girl here now, breathing pine and sun-baked wood. She could make room for the child she couldn't remember being.

Lips pressed together, Annabeth managed, “You were the one there.”

“Was, and loved you. Still do. Your Athena memories are yours too. Missing her isn't a contest.”

“What if a version of me from then feels more familiar?”

Thalia's face tightened, then eased. “Tell me anyway. I'll have feelings — person here, not cabin mascot — and I'll handle them. Don't edit yourself to keep me comfortable.”

That bluntness was so purely Thalia that laughter bubbled up. “You'd make a lousy mascot.”

“Excellent mascot. Statue gets a spear.”

“Statue scowling at campers tracking mud inside?”

“Sacred duty.”

Leaning harder into her sister, Annabeth said, “Want to keep it. If you're good with that.”

“Am.”

“Want it up. Next to Jason's visit photo.”

Thalia's brows climbed. “Where he saluted and capsized the lemonade?”

“That exact one.”

“Fair. Before and after he learned to stand still.”

“He never learned.”

“Neither did you.”

“Can too stand still.”

From the post, Percy piped up. “You're both doing it right now.”

“Different,” Annabeth said.

“Advanced stillness,” Thalia added solemnly.

Hands raised, Percy surrendered. “Wouldn't dream of questioning the experts.”

Laughter broke free, and Thalia's arm tightened around her shoulders. Small. Enough.

---

By late afternoon, the picture owned a frame.

Nothing elaborate. Annabeth had cut narrow cedar strips and sanded each edge till the dust sweetened the air; Percy had held every piece steady while she joined the corners; Thalia had unearthed a small square of glass in Big House storage and declared it “probably safe, unless cursed — separate inspection.”

One corner sat a fraction high. Annabeth marked the flaw in pencil, then rubbed the mark away.

“Sure?” Percy asked.

“Out of square.”

“So's the cabin.”

“Building.”

“And this is a frame. Both have corners.”

“Not a structural argument.”

“Best I've got.”

Thalia lifted the frame to eye level, tilting it against the light. “Like the corner. Looks handmade.”

“It *is* handmade.”

“Then the corner's doing its job.”

Inside went the photograph, paper boat centered and visible, clean backing slipped behind. Annabeth's fingertips lingered on the glass, cool and flawless except where a smudge bloomed and she polished it away on her sleeve. Cedar dust still sweetened her cuffs, and the frame smelled faintly of oil from her hands. Beside it would hang the newer shot — Jason, Thalia, and Annabeth from his Camp Jupiter visit, years after the relay, Jason clutching the drawing they'd mailed him, its corners soft from travel.

They belonged together regardless.

“Both up?” Annabeth asked Thalia.

“They're yours too.”

“Know. Still asking.”

Thalia's smile warmed. “Yes. Hang them.”

Measuring the repaired shelf, Annabeth set the frames side by side: three small siblings then, reunited years later. Neither picture mended the distance, yet both testified the bond had held.

“Looks good,” Percy said.

“Looks level,” Annabeth corrected.

“Meant that.”

Leaning against the shelf, Thalia drawled, “Tomorrow we add Percy falling off the canoe.”

“Doesn't exist,” Percy said.

“Lives in my memory.”

“Inadmissible.”

“Scared the frame'll need to be bigger.”

“Might. Hair takes up space.”

Turning to him, Annabeth deadpanned, “First accurate statement today.”

Hand to chest, he sighed. “Learning so much from you both.”

The dinner bell rang. Silena's call rolled across camp — latecomers earned dish duty — followed by Clarisse shouting her respect for the threat. Thalia offered her hand.

“Come on, Captain.”

The nickname from the laundromat story settled alongside the others: Beff, owl, wise girl, sister. Hers to answer.

“Lead on, lookout.”

“Best lookout in the world.”

“*Only* lookout in the world.”

“Technicality.”

Laughing into the amber evening, they started out, gravel crunching underfoot and pine resin sharp in the cooling air. Where the path widened, its edges soft with trampled clover, Percy fell in beside Annabeth and laced his fingers through hers. Her thumb traced the calluses on his palm — rope, water, sword-hilt — familiar as her own pencil callus. She leaned briefly against him, shoulder fitting to shoulder, then ducked a low branch heavy with pine scent, needles brushing her hair.

Inside the pavilion, friends squabbled over the strawberries. Annabeth dove in. Percy nudged the basket with a curl of water and collected three complaints plus a harpy warning.

The photograph stayed on the shelf.

Tomorrow she would know the rain through Thalia's voice, even if the day itself remained missing. The Athena cabin would persist in her mind — books, shields, the mother she'd loved. This cabin would persist too, crooked frames and her sister's jackets and cedar shavings underfoot.

The memories could share the room without agreeing on everything.

Taking a strawberry, Annabeth passed half to Percy, fingers brushing. Across the table, Thalia caught her eye and lifted her cup. Annabeth lifted hers in answer.

For now, the picture had somewhere to stay. Dust drifted through the last light over the shelf, cedar and old paper mingling in the warm air. So did she.
