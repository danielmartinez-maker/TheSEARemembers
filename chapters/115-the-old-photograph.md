## Chapter 115: The Old Photograph

Annabeth had learned a shelf could turn dangerous without bothering to be tall. Even dust had opinions in the Zeus cabin, and that morning they were all hers to manage.

The one in the Zeus cabin stood only shoulder-high, yet it combined a loose backboard, a stubborn hinge, and a collection of objects arranged by the sisters and their occasional visiting brother, with mutually exclusive theories of storage. Dust furred the upper ledge, grey and soft under Annabeth's fingertip, and every touch shook loose the faint smell of old pine and sun-warmed varnish, with a metallic tang from the helmet perched on top. Light slanted through the narrow window and laid a bright bar across the warped wood, exposing scratches, a water ring, and a pencil mark where someone had once measured something and given up.

Thalia called it “the history shelf.”

Annabeth called it “a structural hazard with a mythology problem.”

“If you say that again,” Thalia warned, bracing the shelf one-handed, “your books go up there.”

“Then it'd be a mythology problem. You've got at least one lightning book upside down.”

“It bends the rules.”

Setting the screwdriver down, Annabeth gave the shelf a careful push with her palm. Wood creaked. It wobbled once, then settled, the backboard holding fast. “There. Nobody dies from a falling helmet today.”

“That's the spirit. Aim low, exceed expectations.”

Beyond the open door, morning camp moved at full tilt: campers crossing the green with shields and breakfast plates, somebody hauling a suspicious bundle of laundry, a naiad arguing with a canoe about where it had been parked. The canoe's defense sounded thin. Somewhere, bacon fat popped and a conch coughed half a note. The air carried cut grass and woodsmoke, damp canvas and warm stone, and through it all the bright clatter of dishes from the pavilion, spoons ringing against bowls, sandals slapping the paths.

Thalia tested the shelf with her shoulder. Wood groaned, then held. She stepped back, gave Annabeth a quick nod, and the loose backboard stopped trembling. Annabeth watched the way morning light caught the dust they'd disturbed, gold motes hanging and turning slowly above the tools.

Crouching for the last screws, Annabeth's fingers brushed something pale wedged behind the backboard. A slip of white against the shadowed wall. For half a second it passed for a note — then the white border caught the light, glossy and unmistakable.

“Thalia?”

“If that's another screw, it's yours now.”

By the corners, Annabeth lifted the picture free. It had been folded once, the crease gone soft with age. The colors had thinned to ghosts: a patch of red, a washed blue sky, the greenish gray of sidewalk after rain. The paper felt cool and slightly slick, its edge feathery where the fold had worn.

Three children filled the frame.

Thalia stood at the back, maybe eight or nine, hair shorter than Annabeth had ever seen in photographs and sticking up in tufts around her head. One arm curled around Annabeth's middle. The other hitched a toddler onto her hip. Jason's round face turned toward the camera, solemn, one fist clamped around the strap of Thalia's jacket.

Small enough that her feet barely reached the step beneath her, Annabeth sat in front in a yellow raincoat, hair escaping its braid. A paper boat rested in both her hands, cradled with the solemn care of a sailor guarding salvage from a wreck.

The nose was hers. So was the set of the mouth. Recognition arrived sideways — less a retrieved moment than a room entered after someone else rearranged the furniture: unfamiliar in arrangement, familiar in shape.

Her thumb hovered short of the paper boat.

Underneath the raincoat and sidewalk rose something else: the blue Athena cabin, bronze and lemon polish and old paper, older girls arguing over a map while gray eyes — her mother's eyes — corrected the angle of a miniature temple roof. No date came stapled to the memory. Only the stubborn conviction of having belonged there.

The picture belonged to this sky. The cabin memory belonged to another. Both arrived uninvited.

Turning the photograph over, Annabeth found faded blue ink in round, slanted handwriting: *The whole crew. Safe harbor, at last.* Beryl's, perhaps. Or Thalia's, younger.

“You found that?” Thalia asked.

She'd rounded the shelf, teasing gone quiet. Her hand lifted toward the picture, then stopped midair. “Thought that was in the box.”

“Wedged behind the board.”

“Tracks.” Thalia's gaze moved between the photo and Annabeth's face. “Want to keep it out?”

“Not sure yet.”

“Okay.” Thalia let her hand drop. “No need to decide before breakfast.”

Small kindness, that. It landed anyway. The tightness behind Annabeth's eyes eased a fraction, and she became aware of the cabin around her again — the cool packed-earth floor under her knees, the smell of Thalia's leather wristband and the faint ozone that always clung to Zeus cabin bedding after a storm, the steady tick of the loose hinge cooling in the morning air.

Sliding the photograph into the front pocket of her notebook, Annabeth pressed it flat. Paper whispered against paper. “I'll bring it along.”

“Sure.” Thalia reclaimed the screwdriver, twirling it once. “And I'll pretend I wasn't about to ask what you remember.”

“You can ask later.”

“Deal.”

They finished securing the shelf together. Thalia set the helmet back on top, then — at one look from Annabeth — relocated it to the lower ledge. A limited victory. Annabeth had learned to collect those.

At the door, Thalia bumped her shoulder. “Come eat. Skip breakfast and Silena sends a search party. Clarisse brings a spear.”

“Sounds less like a search party, more like a threat.”

“Why not both?”

Sunshine spilled across the threshold, warm with cut grass and woodsmoke from the kitchens. Stepping into it, Annabeth kept one hand over the notebook pocket, as if the paper might slip free and take flight.

---

Percy had saved a place at the end of the Zeus table, between himself and a basket of toast.

A napkin folded into a small, determined tent guarded the bread. His handiwork, clearly. He brightened when Annabeth sat. The pavilion hummed around them — milk sloshing in jugs, Ares kids banging benches, the warm yeasty breath of the toast basket rising into her face, butter melting into soft yellow pools.

“Toast protection detail,” he said. “Dangerous pavilion.”

“Protecting it from whom?”

“The Stolls. Possibly Clarisse. Threat hierarchy pending.”

Fishing a slice from beneath the napkin, Annabeth raised an eyebrow. “Security breach.”

“Controlled test. You're part of the test.”

She smiled, though her free hand drifted to the notebook pocket. Percy clocked the gesture. Instead of reaching across or prying, he buttered an apple slice, set fruit on her plate, and waited a beat before asking, “What's on your mind?”

Easier to answer for the asking — and harder to wave away.

“Found a photograph in the cabin. Me, Thalia, and Jason. From before camp.”

Attention sharpened his expression, without alarm. “Can I see?”

Between their plates, Annabeth laid the picture down. Leaning in, he kept his syrupy fingers scrupulously clear of the gloss.

The end of the table was clear of other campers, and the serving line's clatter rolled across the pavilion. Percy bent closer and kept his voice low; Annabeth had to lean in to hear him.

“You look ready to lecture the photographer on the boat's historical significance,” he said.

“Could've been significant.”

“Fair. Age seven, I once informed a lifeguard a plastic shark was historically significant.”

“And was it?”

“Nope. Excellent shark, though.”

The laugh loosened something behind her ribs. Not entirely. Enough to breathe around.

“Don't remember it being taken,” she said. “I can picture rain, Thalia's jacket, Jason yanking my hair. But that's looking at it and filling in stories I've heard, not remembering the day.”

“No need to prove which bits are yours.”

“I know. Not asking you to quiz me.”

“I know.” Though the pavilion roared around them, he lowered his voice. “Only saying it 'cause I'm curious, not 'cause you owe me footnotes.”

Another look at the faded colors. “It's dragging the other life up with it. Athena's cabin. My old bunk. Books, maps, that absolute certainty about who I was supposed to become.”

“And now?”

“Now I stare at this and hear: you should recognize every second. Fail the girl in the photo if you don't. Then the Athena memories barge in, and I feel like I'm betraying Thalia by missing a childhood that wasn't hers.”

Percy studied the photo, then her. “Two separate loads.”

“All tangled.”

“Believe it.” He turned his cup slowly between his palms. “Want help untangling, or should I sit here and run toast defense against the Stolls?”

“Both, if you've got the range.”

“Overachiever, right here.”

“You left one sock out in a snowstorm.”

“Overachiever in selected fields.”

Aloud, Annabeth tried to sort it. Fact: three kids on a wet step. Fact: handwriting, author unknown. Possibly rain. The Athena cabin, the older life — vivid to her, even where it didn't belong to the picture.

“Maybe the picture doesn't have to rule on the memories,” Percy said. “It can stay three kids on a step. And the Athena stuff can stay memories you carry — not orders about what you want now.”

“Say it like it's easy.”

“It's not.” His thumb traced the rim of his cup. “I get flashes from a life I can't move back into. Doesn't make them fake. Doesn't mean I've got to rebuild that life brick for brick, either.”

She knew how he loved Atlantis as home and still grieved Sally, the mother he'd loved in the older life. One love hadn't canceled the other.

“So what do you do with it?” she asked.

“Some days I tell the story. Some days I miss somebody. Some days I swim and don't argue with canoes.”

“Has the canoe apologized?”

“Being difficult.”

“Rough.”

“Coping.”

Her fingers eased around the picture's edge. “Don't want to throw the Athena memories out. Don't want them driving, either.”

“Then they don't get the wheel.”

“Not that simple.”

“No. But you could start with one thing.” He nodded at her notebook. “Decide what happens to this picture. That needn't settle everything else.”

Choosing the picture's fate without letting the past dictate or pretending the present came easy — that felt possible.

“Want Thalia's story,” she said. “First.”

“Good next step.”

“And I want to keep it.”

“Then keep it.”

“It's Thalia's too.”

“Then ask what she wants.”

Nodding, Annabeth took his offered hand across the table and squeezed once. His palm held the cup's warmth, a little damp with condensation, thumb rough with a healing blister from the rig. They stayed linked for a breath while camp clattered on — a plate ringing bright against stone, a satyr laughing with his mouth full, Clarisse informing someone a serving spoon was not a spear, her voice carrying clean across the clamor.

Percy glanced toward the noise. “Intervene?”

“No. Clarisse has a point.”

“That's the worrying part.”

Shoulder to his, she leaned in. “Thanks for not trying to fix it.”

“Fixing the toast. The rest, I'm hearing out.”

Quick and familiar, she kissed his cheek. “Good.”

“Also, I can help build a frame.”

“No carving.”

“Holding nails. I'm excellent at holding.”

“Approved.”

He grinned. “Learning.”

---

After breakfast, Annabeth carried the picture to the work table behind the Big House.

Wind combed the maples overhead; the yard smelled of cut grass, metal filings, and coffee drifting from an open window. A file rasped somewhere in the forge, rhythmic and distant, and shavings curled warm and fragrant under Annabeth's sandals. She studied the photo while measuring the weather vane's loose bracket, thumb smudged with graphite, and fresh details surfaced without any magic — Thalia's jacket strap wound around Jason's fist, a crayon drawing half-hidden under a boot, a strand of hair pasted to a cheek by damp. The bracket's cool edge pressed into her palm as she steadied it, ticking faintly in the breeze.

Thalia had cared for the girl in the picture before that girl had owned the word *sister*. The Athena memories held a different family: half-siblings, camp rules, a mother composed of wisdom and distance. Pride at being claimed; loneliness from trying to shine before she'd learned how to tire.

Neither truth shrank the photograph, nor made her current life a mistake.

A camera caught what it caught. It couldn't supply the missing day, or separate imagined details from Thalia's retellings, or decide which childhood she ought to miss. Pencil in hand, Annabeth drew a line on the work sheet, then paused.

In the sketchbook margin she wrote four short headings.

*What I can see.*

*What I imagine.*

*What I've been told.*

*What I choose now.*

Beneath the first: *Thalia's arm around us. Jason glaring at the weather like he's filing a complaint. Me with a paper boat.*

Beneath the second: *Rain smell. A hand on my shoulder. Details I can imagine, not recover.*

The third stayed blank. Thalia hadn't told this story yet.

Beneath the fourth: *Ask her. Keep the picture if she wants me to.*

Edges, at least, if not untangled string.

The weather vane on the work table had been waiting for her that morning. Its bracket had loosened at one corner, and she wanted to measure it before deciding whether the existing screws could be reused. She could have left it there. The photograph had made the morning feel like a room with too many doors, and a repair was a thing with a clear beginning: find the problem, test the joint, decide what the material could carry.

Annabeth returned to the bracket and set the picture face down on a clean square of cloth. Not hidden, just protected from a stray file or a drop of oil. The vane's narrow arrow shifted under the breeze from the open yard. Its pointer had caught on the stop, and the metal gave a small click every time the wind nudged it. She steadied the base with one hand and loosened the nut with the other.

The first measurement suggested the hole had widened. She checked it again, then compared it with the unbroken side. The difference was smaller than she thought. Not enough to justify a new bracket, but enough that another turn of the screw would only pull the wood harder. She had almost written a replacement into the margin before looking closely. The mistake was familiar: decide what the object needed, then gather evidence to support the decision.

She rubbed the graphite mark off the paper and started over. The vane had not failed; it had drifted. A thin washer and a better angle might keep the arrow moving without letting the screw chew into the post. She marked the dimensions, set the nut in a small dish, and made a note to ask Chiron whether the spare washers from the forge were the right size. Nothing in the job required her to invent a history for the bracket. It only needed to point where the wind was going.

The photograph stayed on its cloth while she worked. When she finished measuring, Annabeth returned it to the notebook pocket and folded the cloth around the pencil shavings. The paper boat had not moved. Neither had the line of ink on the back. Whatever the picture could tell her, it would still be there after she had finished the repair.

“You know,” came a voice behind her, “most folks write down the bracket dimensions.”

Turning, Annabeth found Percy with wood stacked in his arms, twine hooked around one wrist, a damp curl stuck to his forehead. Sawdust clung to his sleeves.

“Did write those down.”

“You drafted an emotional blueprint.”

“Compact blueprint.”

He thumped the wood onto the table. “Chiron said the scrap pile's fair game if I helped Beckendorf haul it. Figured — frame?”

“Carried lumber clear across camp?”

“Not far.”

“From the forge?”

“Define far.”

“Thought so.”

He flipped a board, checking for the straightest grain. “Make one? You measure, I'll hold still.”

“Specialty?”

“Strong stillness record.”

“Got thrown off a dock by your own wave once.”

“Rude wave.”

Laughing, Annabeth tucked the photo safe and sketched a frame pattern. Cedar, marked corners, one botched cut that sent them back to start. Sawdust sweetened the air, catching in her throat with its piney bite; the pencil rasped softly against grain, and the cedar felt silky where Percy had already sanded, rough where he hadn't. A fly droned past and settled on the warm wood. Sawdust clung to Percy's forearms in pale streaks, and he blew it off with a quick puff that scattered gold into sunlight.

Percy held each strip steady while she measured, advising only when asked. He sanded an edge with care and tested it with his thumb before passing it back.

The first pair of lengths matched. The second did not. Annabeth had measured from the inside corner on one strip and the outside edge on the other, a difference small enough to hide until she laid them together. The cedar sat at an angle, one end lifting off the table.

“I can recut it,” Percy said.

“I can recut it.”

He pulled his hands away from the board. “Your project.”

“I know.” She checked the pencil marks again. “I don't want to start over because I rushed.”

“Then don't rush.”

“That sounds suspiciously like advice.”

“It was meant to be a statement of support.”

“You can support me by keeping that end from sliding.”

He set his palm on the far corner. “Like this?”

“A little farther in. The cut line is here.” She showed him the mark. “If you hold beyond it, the board can flex.”

“Got it.” He moved his hand. “No heroic lumber rescue.”

“The lumber is not in danger.”

“I was trying to make it feel included.”

She put the square against the end and found the mistake by lining up the grain. The board had a slight bow, almost invisible until the edges met. She could rotate it and keep the bow on the inside, where the backing would hold it; she could cut a new length and waste the clean strip. Both choices were sound. The question was how much the frame needed to look as if it had never been made by hand.

“If I turn it, the curve will face the photograph,” she said. “The glass should keep it from lifting.”

“Would that bother you?”

“It bothers me that I made the cut wrong.”

“That's not the same question.”

Annabeth checked his face for teasing. Percy watched the board, leaving her the space to decide. He hadn't reached for the saw or told her which option would be easier. That was the difference between helping and taking over, even when his hands could finish the work faster.

“It doesn't have to be perfect,” she said. “It has to keep the picture in place.”

“Then the bow can do a job.”

“You're very generous to the wood.”

“It has had a rough morning.”

She rotated the strip and set the square again. This time the mark lined up. Percy kept it still while she drew the saw, slow at the beginning, then with a steady pull. The teeth caught once. Her shoulders tensed, and she eased the angle rather than forcing it. A little curl of cedar dropped to the table.

“Better,” he said.

“The cut?”

“The decision.”

She slid the pieces together. There was still a narrow gap at one corner, but the strips sat flat. She measured the diagonals and found them unequal by less than the width of her pencil line. The frame would be handmade. It would not be an excuse to be careless, and it didn't need to pretend the first plan had worked exactly.

Percy fetched a scrap to test the glue before they used it on the frame. The first bead was too wide and squeezed onto his thumb. He stared at it.

“Strong stillness record,” Annabeth reminded him.

“Stillness isn't the same as glue resistance.”

“I'll add that to your resume.”

They wiped the test piece clean and tried again, this time using a narrow line. The glue held after a minute, though Percy kept his fingers on the joint until she told him it was safe to let go. They didn't attach the picture or close the frame. Annabeth wanted to see whether the four pieces stayed square when she moved them to the porch, and she wanted Thalia's answer before she made the old photograph into something permanent on the shelf.

“You can say no to the frame,” Percy said as they gathered the scraps.

“I know.”

“I meant Thalia.”

“I know that too.” She wrapped the strips in cloth. “I want to ask what she thinks before we glue anything. I don't want to ask her to approve a decision I've already made.”

“Then we have four unglued pieces.”

“And a very successful test joint.”

“I was going to say that.”

“You can say it now.”

“Very successful.”

“There. You contributed.”

“Good at this,” she said.

“Holding?”

“Paying attention.”

Openly, he met her eyes. “Paying attention to you, yeah.”

Heat touched her cheeks. “Rehearsed.”

“Natural talent.”

“Menace.”

“Still allowed to help?”

“Yes.”

Shaped but unglued — Annabeth wanted Thalia's say before committing to display — the frame waited. She wrapped the photo in clean paper and slipped it into her notebook.

They headed back toward the cabins together, Percy toting the unfinished frame under one arm. Hands brushed once, then found each other. His fingers curled around hers without tugging. Quiet settled, even with the old cabin, the old photograph, and the paper boat crowding her head.

“Keep thinking the Athena memories should run cleaner,” she said after a while. Gravel crunched underfoot; pine sap sharpened the breeze. “They don't. They're all mixed with homesickness and temper and trying to earn attention I already had.”

“Memories don't come edited for clarity.”

“Yours do?”

“Every one heroic. Perfect lighting.”

“I've watched you eat with syrup down your chin.”

“Heroically.”

“Naturally.”

She squeezed his hand. “Glad I can give you the messy version.”

“Want the messy version.”

His steadiness — near without crowding, offering help instead of seizing it — made belief easy. She was choosing Percy now: not because some older memory had paired them, but because she wanted him alongside her on a sunlit path, carrying a crooked frame.

“Want to tell Thalia today,” she said.

“Then we'll find her.”

“No need to both go.”

“I know.”

“But I want you there.”

“Okay.”

Thalia occupied the Zeus cabin porch after lunch, sharpening a practice knife with more focus than the edge required. Steel hissed faintly against stone, a thin rhythmic shiver. Sun beat on the porch boards and drew out the smell of hot wood and dry grass; cicadas stitched the afternoon with their high electric hum. A bead of sweat traced Thalia's temple and she wiped it absently on her sleeve without breaking rhythm.

Annabeth settled on the opposite step. Percy set the unfinished frame at the porch edge, then hovered until her glance sent him to the post — near enough to matter, far enough to leave the sisters air.

“Found this behind the shelf,” Annabeth said.

Thalia's gaze dropped to the photograph. The knife stilled. “Wondered where that went.”

“What's the story?”

“The true one, or the one I invented at eight when you asked why we were soaked?”

“There were multiple versions?”

“At least three. Artistic license.”

“True one, please.”

Thalia weighed her words, thumb worrying a frayed seam on her jeans. “Laundromat on the way to the coast. Mom ran laundry. Curb flooded, so we waited out the rain before catching the bus.”

“Rain — I can almost picture it.”

“I've told you bits. You were four. Asked if the dryers were ship portholes.”

Glancing down, Annabeth almost smiled. “Sounds like me.”

“Was you to the life. Floor was ocean, baskets were lifeboats. Jason was too small to play along. Wanted to chew the handles.”

“In the picture too?”

“On everything. I kept prying his fingers off the dryer door. You were captain, so you drafted me as lookout and ordered a rescue if seas turned rough.”

“Did you?”

“Fished it from a puddle outside. You were deeply impressed.”

Vision blurring at the faded edges, Annabeth blinked hard, irritated at her eyes. “Who took it?”

“Mom's disposable camera. Right after you declared me the bravest lookout in the world.” Thalia laughed, quick and fond. “I informed you I was the *only* lookout in the world.”

“Technicality.”

“You promised to check the charts.”

Annabeth turned to the back: *The whole crew. Safe harbor, at last.* “Her writing?”

“Yeah.” Thalia studied the looping ink. “She wrote down the bright spots. Used to think she was trying to prove we were okay.”

“And now?”

“Think she wanted proof a good afternoon could survive a bad week.” Thalia rubbed denim under her thumb. “Different thing.”

Holding the photo where both could see, Annabeth asked, “Kept it for her?”

“First, yeah. Crammed everything portable into that box. Terrified we'd lose the pictures.” A pause. “Then kept this one 'cause I like it.”

“Why this one?”

“You looked in charge.”

“Was four.”

“Bossy at four. Gifted.”

“Been taking notes, I see.”

Thalia nudged her knee with her own. “Serious about that boat. Found Jason a job even while he tried to eat the basket. Kept handing out posts till the whole operation made sense.”

Paper edge pressed into Annabeth's fingertip. “I can't remember that day.”

“Don't have to.”

“But I like hearing it.”

“Then I'll keep telling it.” Thalia met her eyes. “And if you understand it differently, that's fine too. Being there doesn't mean I get to tell you what to feel about it.”

The sentence loosened a knot Annabeth had mistaken for law. Thalia had raised her; Thalia was her sister — her older sister, daughter of Zeus like her. Neither fact obliged Annabeth to borrow Thalia's certainty in order to belong.

“Sometimes I miss Athena,” Annabeth said. “Not only being claimed. The cabin, the way life made sense around her. Then I look at this and feel I've got to pick which childhood counts.”

A glance toward Percy — examining the porch post with great dedication — drew his small nod. Listening, not intruding.

“Annie,” Thalia said, “love being your sister. Don't need you to erase anyone else to prove it.”

Thalia's arm settled loose and warm behind her shoulders; Annabeth leaned in, shoulder to shoulder, denim rasping softly, sunscreen and leather rising off Thalia's skin. The porch boards pressed hard lines into the backs of her legs, grounding her. The photograph and Thalia's stories could sit beside her own memories without replacing them: a child whose bus ride Thalia could describe, the daughter of Athena who built plans from fear amid lamplight and chalk dust, the girl here now, breathing pine and sun-baked wood. She could make room for the child she couldn't remember being.

Lips pressed together, Annabeth managed, “You were the one there.”

“Was, and loved you. Still do. Your Athena memories are yours too. Missing her isn't a contest.”

“What if a version of me from then feels more familiar?”

Thalia's face tightened, then eased. “Tell me anyway. I'll have feelings — person here, not cabin mascot — and I'll handle them. Don't edit yourself to keep me comfortable.”

That bluntness was so purely Thalia that laughter bubbled up. “You'd make a lousy mascot.”

“Excellent mascot. Statue gets a spear.”

“Statue scowling at campers tracking mud inside?”

“Sacred duty.”

Leaning harder into her sister, Annabeth said, “Want to keep it. If you're good with that.”

“Am.”

“Want it up. Next to Jason's visit photo.”

Thalia's brows climbed. “Where he saluted and capsized the lemonade?”

“That exact one.”

“Fair. Before and after he learned to stand still.”

“He never learned.”

“Neither did you.”

“Can too stand still.”

From the post, Percy piped up. “You're both doing it right now.”

“Different,” Annabeth said.

“Advanced stillness,” Thalia added solemnly.

Hands raised, Percy surrendered. “Wouldn't dream of questioning the experts.”

Laughter broke free, and Thalia's arm tightened around her shoulders. Small. Enough.

---

Annabeth found Thalia in the Big House corridor after the conversation, carrying the photograph flat between the pages of her notebook. The porch conversation had given the picture a story, but the backing had begun to curl at the fold. She didn't want to press it under a book and discover later that she had flattened one edge by crushing another.

“Do we have anything that could protect it?” she asked. “A sleeve, maybe. Or glass that's clean enough to use.”

Thalia looked at the notebook. “You want the picture out?”

“I want to keep it from getting worse. I still want to put it up. I haven't changed my mind.”

“Then storage room. Chiron keeps the spare panes there for the frames he hasn't fixed.”

The corridor held the cool smell of stone and old paper. A window at the far end threw a rectangle of light across the floor, and dust showed in it whenever someone passed. Thalia walked ahead, taking the turn toward the back rooms without hurrying. Annabeth followed with her hand against the notebook pocket. At the storeroom door, Thalia nudged it open with her shoulder. The hinges gave a low complaint.

Shelves ran along the walls, crowded with canvas, rolled maps, a chipped lamp, and boxes labeled in Chiron's careful hand. A stack of wooden frames leaned beside a tin of bent nails. Annabeth stayed at the threshold until Thalia shifted a crate away from the narrow aisle.

“Mind the corner,” Thalia said. “There are some nails under that rag.”

Annabeth moved around the crate, then paused in front of a pane propped against the back wall. It had a cloudy patch in one corner and a clean line down the center where someone had wiped it. The edges were taped. She didn't touch it.

“Could be enough,” Thalia said. “If we cut it to fit.”

“We shouldn't cut it here.”

“Wasn't planning to.”

“And I don't want to put pressure on the photograph.”

“Then don't.” Thalia bent to inspect the other panes. “Could use the backing board and leave the front open.”

“Dust would get in.”

“Then the glass.”

“I want to know if the cloudy patch is on the glass or in it.”

“You can ask Chiron.”

Annabeth took a breath. She had asked for material, and now she was building an inspection plan around it. “I can check the edge without touching the surface.”

“You can. Or we can take it back to him and ask.”

“You're right. I don't need to figure out the whole problem before I have the glass.”

“I wasn't saying you couldn't.” Thalia lifted a thin wood strip, then set it down again. “I was saying the rest of the work can wait until we know what we're working with.”

“That's fair.”

Thalia found a smaller pane in a shallow box, no bigger than the open frame. A paper label on its corner had no words left, only a brown mark where ink had bled through. She held it at the edges and angled it toward the window. Dust traced a pale line across the surface; no cracks showed. The pane was slightly wider than the sketch Annabeth had made.

“This could work,” Thalia said. “If the frame is a little bigger.”

“The photograph is already smaller than the frame.”

“Then it has room.”

“It would rattle.”

“A strip of felt.”

“We don't have felt in the workshop.”

“We can find cloth.”

Annabeth considered the two options. The glass would protect the photo, but only if the frame held the pane securely and the backing did not press too hard. She could make the fit exact and risk the wood shifting after the first humid day. Or she could leave a small margin and use a soft strip along the edge. It wasn't a difficult problem, but it was not only a matter of making the frame look right.

“I want a spacer,” she said. “Something soft enough not to rub the print, but firm enough to keep the glass from moving.”

Thalia nodded. “We can ask Chiron for something that won't shed.”

“Would you come with me?”

“Sure.”

They carried the pane between them, Thalia gripping one end and Annabeth supporting the other. In the Big House office, Chiron listened while Annabeth explained the size and the cloudy patch. He examined the edge, not the face, then held it toward the daylight.

“The clouding is on the surface,” he said. “It may clean off, but the edge tape should stay in place. If you use it, leave the photograph room to move. Old paper changes with damp weather.”

“Could it sit under the glass without being attached?” Annabeth asked.

“That would be safer than putting adhesive on it. A narrow paper mat might keep it centered.”

Thalia pointed at the small stack of pale sheets on the desk. “Do we have those?”

“We have plain paper. I cannot promise it is archival.” Chiron handed them a clean sheet. “For a camp display, this will keep the print from touching the wood. If the photograph is especially valuable, it should not live in direct sunlight.”

Annabeth looked down at the photograph's faded colors through the open notebook. “It already spent years in a box.”

“Then a little more shade may help it last.”

That changed the design. The shelf had a narrow top ledge beneath the window, where afternoon light fell strongest. Annabeth had pictured the two photos side by side there. The lower ledge would be safer, but it held the helmet and a stack of books. They would have to shift the display or choose another place.

“We can set it on the lower ledge,” Thalia said. “The helmet can go somewhere else.”

“I moved it once today.”

“It survived.”

“The shelf still has a loose backboard.”

“We can brace the back. We don't have to hang anything.”

Annabeth looked at her. “I was planning to hang them.”

“You said you wanted them up. A shelf is up.”

“I know. I meant on the wall.”

“Then let's not decide until we see what the frame weighs.” Thalia lifted the glass toward the window again, careful not to catch the light in Annabeth's eyes. “We can put the photo on the shelf for now. If the light is wrong, we move it.”

Annabeth considered the simpler plan. A display could be temporary without being careless. It could be visible without making a claim about how long it belonged there. “All right. We can test it before dinner.”

Chiron lent them a folded piece of clean cloth and a paper sheet. They returned the unused pane to its box, then carried the smaller one back to the forge table. Annabeth wrote down the size she needed to leave around the photograph. The work had become more complicated, but no one had asked her to settle the meaning of the picture before she could protect it.

“Still want to make the frame?” Thalia asked.

“Yes.”

“Good. I want to see it.”

“You get to help choose where it goes.”

“I know.” Thalia adjusted her grip on the cloth. “I also want to help build it, if you'll let me.”

“You can hold the square.”

“Very important work.”

“The most important.”

“I knew I raised you right.”

The words came easily, then caught between them. Thalia glanced down at the cloth as if she had found a loose thread. Annabeth did not correct her or pretend the sentence had brought back a memory. It described the person Thalia had been in this childhood, and the work they were doing now did not make that childhood hers. She could hear the phrase, understand what Thalia meant, and answer from the present.

“You taught me to use a square?” Annabeth asked.

“I taught you to steal my pencils.”

“That sounds less useful.”

“You've turned out all right.”

“Debatable.”

Thalia nudged her shoulder with the cloth bundle. “Come on. You have a frame to make.”

By late afternoon, the picture owned a frame.

Nothing elaborate. Annabeth had cut narrow cedar strips and sanded each edge till the dust sweetened the air; Percy had held every piece steady while she joined the corners; Thalia had unearthed a small square of glass in Big House storage and declared it “probably safe, unless cursed — separate inspection.”

One corner sat a fraction high. Annabeth marked the flaw in pencil, then rubbed the mark away.

“Sure?” Percy asked.

“Out of square.”

“So's the cabin.”

“Building.”

“And this is a frame. Both have corners.”

“Not a structural argument.”

“Best I've got.”

Thalia lifted the frame to eye level, tilting it against the light. “Like the corner. Looks handmade.”

“It *is* handmade.”

“Then the corner's doing its job.”

Annabeth put the frame on the table again and checked the joints from underneath. The glue had set, but the wood was still slightly damp where it had been wiped. One corner sat a fraction high. She could sand it level, but the cedar was already thinner there, and another pass might make the glass sit unevenly.

“Should we clamp it?” Thalia asked.

“Not yet. I want to check the diagonals first.”

Thalia held the frame by its outside edges while Annabeth measured from corner to corner. The two lengths did not match. She marked the longer diagonal in pencil, turned the frame, and checked it again. The difference came from the pressure she had used while gluing the last joint. The wood had shifted a little under her hand.

“Can we correct it?” Thalia asked.

“Maybe. If we pull it too far, the joint will split.”

Percy looked over from the other side of the table. “Do you want me to hold it?”

“I want you to hold the square against the top edge. Thalia, keep the bottom from sliding. Don't push yet.”

They each took a position. Annabeth loosened the temporary tie around the frame, then pressed the long corner a little inward. The wood resisted, then gave by a tiny amount. She checked the diagonals. Closer, but not even.

“Enough?” Percy asked.

“Almost.”

“You always say that when you're about to make us move one more millimeter.”

“One millimeter matters.”

“To this frame.”

“To the glass. If the pane rocks, it'll tap the edge every time someone bumps the shelf.”

“Then we don't bump the shelf.”

Thalia glanced at him. “Have you met this cabin?”

“Fair point.”

They shifted the corner once more. This time the diagonals matched closely enough that Annabeth stopped measuring. It was still possible to see that one edge was not perfectly square if she looked for it. She did. The line of the cedar was clear in the late light, the grain bending slightly near the knot.

“I can sand the corner,” she said.

“You can,” Thalia replied. “Do you want to?”

Annabeth held the frame up to the light. The uneven corner would not make the photo tilt. The joint was secure. She could reduce the ridge, but she could also make the thin side fragile and keep sanding until the original shape disappeared.

“No,” she said. “It's stable.”

Thalia released the lower edge. “Then that's done.”

“Not until we test the glass.”

“You said the joint was done.”

“The joint is done. The frame isn't.”

Percy put the square down. “I have been informed.”

Annabeth laid the pane on the cloth. It was clean enough to use, but the cloudy patch needed more than a sleeve. She found a clean section of rag and tried the smallest amount of water on the corner, using the cloth rather than her finger. The mark loosened. She dried it with another piece of linen and inspected the surface from both sides.

“Chiron said the tape stays on the edge,” Thalia said.

“It does. I'm only cleaning the face.”

“All right.”

“I'm not trying to make it new. I just need to see through it.”

“No one thinks you're trying to make it new.”

Annabeth set the glass aside and prepared a narrow paper mat. She used the pencil marks to measure the open space, then cut the paper with a small knife. The first cut wandered off the line. She put that strip aside and made a second. The edge came out straight, though it was too wide by a few millimeters.

“Want me to hold the ruler?” Percy asked.

“I have it.”

“I know. I'm offering.”

She eased the ruler across the paper. “Hold this end, then. Don't let it move.”

Percy leaned in and set his thumb on the ruler. His hand was warm against the cool metal. She drew the blade along the edge in one slow stroke. The paper separated cleanly. When she lifted the ruler, the strip sat at the correct width.

Thalia inspected it. “That'll keep the photo centered.”

“It should.”

“You can say that like it isn't a promise.”

Annabeth glanced at her. “I'm trying to be exact.”

“You can be exact without being certain about the next ten years.”

The comment was small, almost casual, but it caught Annabeth's attention. She looked down at the photograph, still protected in clean paper. The frame would preserve what the camera caught, not every detail of the day or the people in it. It might last for years. It might need repair next season. Neither possibility was a reason to leave it loose behind a shelf.

“Then it should work for now,” she said.

“For now is enough for a frame,” Percy said.

“For a lot of things,” Thalia added.

They set the paper mat, then the photograph, then the glass. The pane settled without rocking. Annabeth checked each side, ran the pads of her fingers along the wood, and stopped before she could press the old paper flat. The picture stayed centered. The frame held without a nail or a drop of glue touching the photograph.

“That's it,” she said.

“Are we allowed to look?” Percy asked.

“You have been looking this whole time.”

“I was looking at the tools. Now I'm looking at the frame.”

Thalia picked it up by the sides. “It's light.”

“The glass isn't heavy enough to pull the joint apart,” Annabeth said. “But we should still set it on the shelf and see.”

“We will.” Thalia turned it toward herself, then toward Annabeth. “You can decide where it goes.”

“We can decide where it goes.”

Thalia nodded, satisfied with the correction. Percy reached for the frame, then stopped. Annabeth handed it to him by the bottom rail. He carried it level, without being asked to slow down.

Inside went the photograph, paper boat centered and visible, clean backing slipped behind. Annabeth's fingertips lingered on the glass, cool and flawless except where a smudge bloomed and she polished it away on her sleeve. Cedar dust still sweetened her cuffs, and the frame smelled faintly of oil from her hands. Beside it would hang the later Camp Jupiter visit photo: Jason, Thalia, and Annabeth together, Jason clutching a drawing, its corners soft from travel.

They belonged together regardless.

The square of glass did not sit flush the first time. It rocked in the frame by a fraction, enough to make a faint tick when Annabeth lifted one side. She set it down and checked the four corners. The paper mat had shifted toward the bottom, making the boat appear to drift off-center. She could have trimmed the mat or moved the image; instead, she lifted the glass and tried the backing again, keeping the fold of the old photograph away from the pressure point.

“Do you want me to hold it?” Thalia asked.

“Yes. Just the edge.”

Thalia braced the pane with two fingers while Annabeth slid the mat into place. They were both quiet until the photo sat squarely in the opening. Thalia's hands were steady; Annabeth had to remind herself not to take over just because the angles were precise. The paper boat returned to the middle. The frame closed without pinching the corner.

“That looks right,” Thalia said.

Annabeth inspected it from the side, then from the front. The glass changed the picture. It flattened the crease and caught the window's reflection, putting a pale bar across the children's faces when she angled it toward the light.

“It looks right from here,” she said. “But the light's wrong.”

“We can move it.”

“I don't want to move it every afternoon.”

Thalia turned the frame until the glare disappeared. “Then we use the lower shelf.”

Annabeth looked toward the cabin. The top ledge had seemed like the obvious place because it was clear and close to eye level. The lower shelf held the helmet and a row of books that had not been opened in weeks. A paper map was tucked behind them, one corner curled toward the wall. It was not an empty place waiting for her decision. It belonged to a set of things that would have to move.

“The helmet won't fit beside the books,” she said.

“It can go under the table.”

“That makes it easier to kick.”

“It's already been kicked twice this month.”

“By you?”

“Not relevant.”

Annabeth set the frame aside and went to test the shelf. The upper boards were stable enough to hold the frames, but the backboard still flexed when she pressed near the left screw. She put her hand under the shelf and pushed gently. The brace they'd added that morning held. The wood shifted less than before, though the load would be distributed differently if they placed both photographs together.

“We need to see how heavy they are,” she said.

“They're pictures, not shields.”

“The wood has limits either way.”

Thalia lifted the newer frame and held it out. “This one has a glass front too.”

Annabeth took it by the sides. It weighed more than the new one, its corners joined with cleaner cuts and an old backing board. She placed it on the shelf, not at the edge but close to the support. Then she put the new frame beside it. The left board dipped slightly.

“That's too much for the brace where it is,” Annabeth said.

“We can move the support.”

“Or we can put one frame on the other side.”

“There isn't room.”

“If we move the books down and set the helmet on the floor—”

“The floor is where boots go.”

“Under the table, then.”

“That was my idea.”

“It was.” Annabeth glanced at her. “I don't have a better one.”

Thalia tapped the shelf's right side. “Could shift the brace under this point and put the heavier frame nearest the wall. Your new one is lighter. If the backboard holds, the weight goes into the side support.”

“We'd have to check that the lower screw is seated.”

“We'll check it.”

“And the books can't sit directly under the frame. If it slips—”

“Then we move the books before we put the pictures up.”

It was ordinary carpentry, but Annabeth found herself watching how Thalia waited for her answer. The shelf had been Thalia's before Annabeth entered the room with a screwdriver. The objects had accumulated under both of them, and some belonged to Jason. The new photograph could be hers without becoming the only thing that mattered.

“Let's move the books first,” Annabeth said. “Then we'll brace the side and test the load.”

They carried the books to the table and sorted them by size so the heavier ones would sit flat. Annabeth found two with loose covers and set them apart to repair later. Thalia lifted the helmet from the upper ledge and carried it beneath the table, turning it so no one would catch a boot in its rim. She tucked a folded cloth inside to keep dust from settling in it.

“You don't have to do that,” Annabeth said.

“I know.” Thalia smoothed the cloth once. “I don't want to take it back out every time someone moves a chair.”

“All right.”

The two women tested the shelf with the books first. The board dipped only a little, the brace held, and the screw stayed flush. They moved the heavier frame into place, then set Annabeth's photograph beside it. Their frames did not match. The new cedar was light; the older wood had darkened at the corners. The photograph from the past had a crease and faded ink. The newer one held a drawing with soft, handled corners.

Annabeth took a step back. “The smaller one should go closer to the wall.”

“Because it's lighter?”

“Because the two frames are different depths. The glass on the new one catches the light if it sits in front.”

Thalia shifted it back a finger's width. The reflection moved off the paper boat.

“Better?”

“Better.”

“Looks like they belong together.”

Annabeth looked at the pictures again. The older photo did not become the beginning of a story that the newer one completed. One showed a child in a yellow raincoat and two smaller siblings; the other showed three older siblings meeting again after time apart. The photos gave her two shapes to look at. They did not tell her what every year between them had felt like.

“They can sit next to each other,” she said. “They don't have to explain each other.”

Thalia rubbed a thumb along the shelf edge. “I wasn't going to make them.”

“I know.”

“You can say that without apologizing.”

Annabeth considered whether she had been about to apologize. “I wasn't apologizing.”

“Good.”

“I was checking.”

“You can check.”

“And you can tell me when I'm making a whole system out of a shelf.”

“I can tell you when I want to.” Thalia's smile appeared briefly. “Sometimes I might let you make the system. If the shelf needs one.”

Annabeth laughed under her breath. “Then it's settled.”

“Both up?” Annabeth asked Thalia.

“They're yours too.”

“Know. Still asking.”

Thalia's smile warmed. “Yes. Hang them.”

Measuring the repaired shelf, Annabeth set the frames side by side: three small siblings then, reunited years later. Neither picture mended the distance, yet both testified the bond had held.

“Looks good,” Percy said.

“Looks level,” Annabeth corrected.

“Meant that.”

Leaning against the shelf, Thalia drawled, “Tomorrow we add Percy falling off the canoe.”

“Doesn't exist,” Percy said.

“Lives in my memory.”

“Inadmissible.”

“Scared the frame'll need to be bigger.”

“Might. Hair takes up space.”

Turning to him, Annabeth deadpanned, “First accurate statement today.”

Hand to chest, he sighed. “Learning so much from you both.”

The dinner bell rang. Silena's call rolled across camp — latecomers earned dish duty — followed by Clarisse shouting her respect for the threat. Thalia offered her hand.

“Come on, Captain.”

The nickname from the laundromat story settled alongside the others: Beff, owl, wise girl, sister. Hers to answer.

“Lead on, lookout.”

“Best lookout in the world.”

“*Only* lookout in the world.”

“Technicality.”

Laughing into the amber evening, they started out, gravel crunching underfoot and pine resin sharp in the cooling air. Where the path widened, its edges soft with trampled clover, Percy fell in beside Annabeth and laced his fingers through hers. Her thumb traced the calluses on his palm — rope, water, sword-hilt — familiar as her own pencil callus. She leaned briefly against him, shoulder fitting to shoulder, then ducked a low branch heavy with pine scent, needles brushing her hair.

Inside the pavilion, friends squabbled over the strawberries. Annabeth dove in. Percy nudged the basket with a curl of water and collected three complaints plus a harpy warning.

The basket skidded toward him, then overshot the center of the table. One strawberry bounced out, struck the rim of a cup, and rolled toward the floor. Annabeth caught it against her palm. Across from her, a camper held up a plate while another tried to rescue the napkins from the watery track.

“I thought you were nudging it,” Clarisse said.

“I was.” Percy looked at the thin curl of water still moving around the basket. “The table's more slippery than it looked.”

“That's why we use hands,” Silena said, reaching for the cloth. She blotted the water before it reached the bread. “Or ask someone to pass it.”

“I was trying to keep it from getting stuck between the pitchers.”

“It was not stuck,” Annabeth said. She set the rescued strawberry back in the basket. “It was sitting there.”

“Sitting there dramatically.”

“The berry?”

“The basket.”

Clarisse slid it back to the middle with both hands. “Now it's sitting there safely.”

“I appreciate your expertise.”

“You can appreciate it while you wipe the table.”

Percy took the cloth from Silena. He could have dried the spill with another curl of water, but the juice had mixed with the wet patch, making it harder to pull cleanly without sending it toward someone's plate. He wiped from the outer edge inward, then turned the cloth to a dry side. The table no longer shone, though one sticky spot remained where the berry had struck the cup.

“Missed a bit,” Annabeth said.

He looked. “I did.”

She pointed to it with the stem of her fork. Percy wiped the spot a second time. Clarisse had already claimed a strawberry, but she hadn't taken another. Silena started to stand to refill the pitcher.

“I'll get it,” Percy said.

“You don't have to—”

“I caused the water situation.” He got up with the pitcher. “I can solve the water situation.”

“The pitcher is heavy,” Clarisse said.

“It's a pitcher.”

“And you're carrying it badly.”

Percy adjusted his grip. “Is there a correct way?”

“The one where you don't spill it.”

“I'll try that.”

He carried it to the barrel and filled it from the tap. When he came back, the water inside shifted toward the spout, and he slowed his steps until the surface stopped rocking. Clarisse watched him set it down without spilling a drop.

“Acceptable,” she said.

“High praise.”

“Don't let it change you.”

Silena passed the cloth back to him. “The table's clean. Keep this one for the basin.”

“You were going to wash it anyway.”

“I was going to eat first.”

He looked at her. She was not angry, but she had set the herb-label samples aside and taken a strawberry before he left. Her cup remained full. She had not moved it while she cleaned. The thing he had meant as help had put another task in her hands.

“Right,” Percy said. “Sorry.”

“Thank you.” She picked up her fork. “Now sit down before Clarisse decides you need a lesson in carrying cups too.”

“I know how to carry cups.”

“You once carried six at once and lost four.”

“That was a different table.”

“The cups were the same.”

Annabeth passed him half a strawberry. “You can have the one you saved.”

“I thought you saved it.”

“I did. I'm sharing.”

He accepted it, their fingers brushing for an instant. “Then I'm honored.”

“Don't make it ceremonial.”

“Too late. I should have asked for a speech.”

“You can have the speech after you wash the cloth.”

Thalia leaned across the table and pointed at the basket. “Are we done moving these?”

“Yes,” Percy and Annabeth said together.

Clarisse took another strawberry. “Good. I was about to put a shield over them.”

“That would have been excessive,” Silena said.

“It would have worked.”

“So would putting the basket in the middle.”

“We did that.”

“After the shield threat.”

“That's how plans improve.”

The conversation drifted away from the table's wet patch. Around them, campers called for the last bread, complained about the dish duty list, and tried to trade slices of melon for a second serving of strawberries. The basket stayed in the center. Percy passed it by hand when someone asked, and no one mentioned the water again.

Annabeth listened to the ordinary argument over food and the scrape of plates. Her sister's voice joined Silena's, then fell quiet while Thalia gave Percy a look for reaching across a bowl. The old photograph had not made the current family disappear, and the current family had not made the child in the picture less real. Both were part of the shelf now, along with the books they'd moved and the helmet tucked safely under the table.

The photograph stayed on the shelf.

After dinner, Annabeth and Thalia returned to the cabin to put the cups away. The lamp on the worktable was still lit, and when Thalia opened the door the glass caught its reflection. The paper boat disappeared behind a narrow white flare.

“We should turn the lamp,” Annabeth said.

Thalia lifted it by the base and moved it toward the far side of the table. The light shifted across the room. In the frame, the children came back into view.

“There,” Thalia said.

Annabeth stood beside her, not directly in front of the picture. From that angle, the three faces were clear, but the older photograph beside it picked up the light instead. She moved the smaller frame back a little and turned it toward the wall. The glare slid away from the glass.

“Better,” Thalia said.

“It doesn't have to be perfect from every place in the room.”

“Good. There are too many places in this room.”

Annabeth rested the lamp on the table and checked that its cord was clear of the books. The shelf held. Neither frame had shifted. The folded paper mat stayed under the photograph without touching it, and the old crease remained visible, a line the glass couldn't erase.

She stepped back to the doorway. The new frame looked almost too pale beside the older one, the cedar still holding its fresh color. Thalia moved to the opposite wall and looked from there too. The lamp made the newer photograph easier to see; the old picture stayed legible in the reflected light, though its faded blue had gone gray.

“It looks different from here,” Thalia said.

“The frame or the picture?”

“Both, I think.” She came closer and adjusted the edge so it sat parallel to the shelf. “The boat's brighter when the light comes from the side.”

Annabeth looked again. The paper boat did stand out now, its folded edges catching the small lamp's glow. The raincoat was no longer a single yellow shape; she could see the creases in the fabric and the shadow beneath one sleeve. The image had not changed. The angle had.

“I'll leave it like that,” she said.

“And if you change your mind?”

“I'll move it.”

“Good.” Thalia let her hand fall. “You don't have to make the first choice permanent.”

Annabeth looked at the two frames, then at the shelf that held them. “Neither do you.”

“I know.”

They left the lamp burning while they finished putting away the cups. The photographs stayed where they were, visible from the doorway and protected from the strongest strip of window light. It was a practical answer that could survive the night without deciding anything beyond where the frames belonged for now.

“I'll check it again tomorrow,” she said.

“You can.”

“If it moves, we can change the support.”

“If it moves, we change the support.” Thalia adjusted the lamp's shade. “If it doesn't, we leave it.”

Annabeth nodded. That was a plan with room for what the shelf actually did. She glanced at the photographs one more time, then left the lamp where it was.

Tomorrow she would know the rain through Thalia's voice, even if the day itself remained missing. The Athena cabin would persist in her mind — books, shields, the mother she'd loved. This cabin would persist too, crooked frames and her sister's jackets and cedar shavings underfoot.

The memories could share the room without agreeing on everything.

Annabeth went back to the shelf and stood beside Thalia. The new frame's cedar caught the lamp's warmth; beyond the glass, the paper boat held its place in the rain. She checked the support once, without touching it. The shelf held both photographs. Tomorrow she could check again. Tonight, both pictures could stay beside one another, intact.
