## Chapter 105: The Place at the Table

Clarisse did not announce that she was doing something nice.

She simply appeared at the Poseidon cabin door after dinner and said, "We're going to the beach. Bring a jacket."

Percy looked up from the card game he was losing to Silena. "Why?"

"Because I said so."

"That's not a reason."

"It's the reason you're getting." She turned to Annabeth, who was sitting on the floor by the bunk with a stack of drawings. "You too, Grace."

Annabeth blinked. "I have work."

"Your work will still be there in an hour."

"That's not how deadlines work."

"Tonight it is." Clarisse held the door open. "Come on. Silena's coming. Percy can bring his favorite person."

Percy stood. Annabeth's expression warmed at the phrase, then she gathered her sketches and put them away.

Silena won the last hand, collected the cards, and joined them at the door. "Clarisse has been planning this all day. Don't ruin the surprise by asking what it is."

"You just told them it was a surprise," Clarisse said.

"I said you were planning something. I didn't say what."

"You said too much."

"I said one sentence."

"It was one sentence too many."

They walked down to the beach under a sky full of clouds. The wind was cold enough to make Percy glad he'd brought his jacket. Silena carried a lantern. Clarisse carried a paper bag. Annabeth walked beside Percy, their hands brushing until their fingers linked.

The beach was empty. Clarisse had chosen the stretch below the western bluff, where the campfire light couldn't reach and the surf came in slow and even.

They didn’t go straight to the water. Clarisse walked the stretch first, looking at the sand and the dark edge beneath the bluff. She tested a patch of ground with her boot, then another. Silena set the lantern where the sand was firm. Annabeth stayed back from the wet line and watched the tide come in.

“You’ve checked this place already,” Percy said.

“I’m checking it now.”

“What changed?”

“The tide.”

“That’s fair.”

“Don’t sound surprised.”

“I’m not.”

“You are.”

“I’m impressed.”

“That’s worse.”

Silena laughed and put the paper bag down above the damp sand. The bottom had begun to soften from the walk. She lifted it again and set it on a piece of driftwood.

“We should keep the figures out of the wet,” she said.

“They’re wrapped,” Clarisse replied.

“The bag’s getting damp.”

“Then don’t leave it on the sand.”

“I just moved it.”

“Good.”

Clarisse’s attention stayed on the ground. She was not looking for a threat; she was making sure the place she had chosen would work. Percy recognized the habit. Before a drill, Clarisse checked footing. Before a meal, Silena checked whether everybody had somewhere to sit. They each cared for a room in their own way, and neither needed the other’s method to be wrong.

Annabeth moved nearer the log. “Is this where you wanted to set everything?”

“Maybe.”

“We can put the lantern farther from the water.”

“I know.”

“I’m asking whether you want to.”

Clarisse glanced back, then pointed to a hollow in the sand. “There.”

Annabeth carried the lantern there and lowered it carefully. The light showed a scatter of small stones and a narrow channel the tide had cut. She picked out the stones before setting the lantern down.

“That better?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“You don’t have to wait for me to say yes before doing everything.”

“I was waiting because it’s your plan.”

Clarisse looked at her for a moment. “It’s our evening.”

“That isn’t exactly the same thing.”

“No.”

“So where do you want me to help?”

Clarisse gestured at the driftwood. “Keep the bag from blowing away.”

Annabeth set one hand on the paper. “Done.”

Percy sat on the log and checked the inside pocket of his jacket. The clam postcard was still inside its paper sleeve, its corner tucked behind the fold. The cartoon clam's sunglasses looked ridiculous in the lantern light.

“Is that the clam?” Silena asked.

"The postcard," Percy said. "The clam's on it."

"You brought it to camp?"

"Annabeth said I could have the clam."

"I said you could carry the card," she corrected.

"I carried it."

"That doesn't mean you get to fold it in half."

"It was already in a sleeve."

"Then keep it in the sleeve."

Clarisse glanced at the card. "If you're going to keep it, keep it flat."

"I was."

"You were bending the corner."

"I was checking."

"That's not what checking looks like," Clarisse said.

Percy slid the card back into its sleeve. "It's a postcard."

"Which means it will end up in a drawer with all the other postcards," Annabeth said.

"I don't have a postcard drawer."

"You will."

"That sounds like a threat."

"It's a prediction."

Clarisse shrugged. "If it matters to you, put it somewhere safe."

Percy looked at the ridiculous clam in its sunglasses, his name penciled on the back. It had come from his date with Annabeth. She had let him carry it, and he had chosen to keep it with him tonight. That was enough.

Clarisse put the paper bag on the log. “Now we can do the rest.”

“The rest of what?” Percy asked.

“The reason we’re here.”

“I thought you said not to ask.”

“I said not to ask what the surprise was.”

“And now?”

“Now you’ll see.”

She opened the bag.

She set the paper bag down and took out four small wooden figures. A panther with blunt wings. A sea horse. A little ship with a crooked mast. An owl, its head turned sharply to one side.

Percy recognized the panther from Clarisse's carving. It was the same one she'd given him months ago, only this version was smoother, the wings finished, the eyes picked out in dark stain.

"I made these," she said, as if daring them to comment. "For the cabin. Not some big ceremony. Just something to mark that we're..."

She faltered. Silena rescued her.

"That we're family," she said. "The three of us."

Percy looked at the figures. "Clarisse, these are incredible."

"They're wood. Don't make it weird."

"I wasn't—"

"You were about to get emotional. I could see it."

Annabeth took the owl carefully. "You made this for me?"

Clarisse shrugged. "It looks like you. Always watching. Probably judging the rest of us."

"Accurate," Silena said.

Annabeth smiled and traced the owl's little carved feathers. "Thank you."

Clarisse looked away toward the water. "You don't have to thank me."

"I want to."

For a few moments the four of them stood together, wind tugging at their clothes, waves sliding over the sand. Percy couldn't tell what Clarisse meant by the figures beyond what she had managed to say, and he didn't ask her to explain.

Silena placed the lantern in the sand and lit it. "We should say something."

Clarisse groaned. "No speeches."

"Not a speech. A toast." Silena lifted the lantern. "To the cabin. To being annoying together. To nobody having to earn their place."

Clarisse looked at her sister. "That's a speech."

"It's a short toast."

Percy lifted the little ship. Annabeth held the owl against her chest. Clarisse reluctantly picked up the panther.

"To the cabin," Percy said. "And to the people in it."

"And the people who keep showing up at the door," Annabeth added.

Clarisse glanced at her, then at Percy. "Fine. To that too."

They didn't clink glasses. They touched the little carved figures together instead. The sound was soft, almost lost under the surf.

The figures stayed on the log after the toast. Clarisse shifted them until each had a little room around it. The panther’s wing nearly touched the ship. She moved the ship. Then she moved the sea horse so the curled tail wouldn’t catch on a splinter. Finally she turned the owl a fraction toward the others.

“You’re arranging them again,” Percy said.

“I’m making room.”

“For what?”

“For them.”

“That’s the same thing.”

“It isn’t.”

Silena leaned closer. “The owl is looking toward the panther now.”

“It was before.”

“Not from where I was sitting.”

“Then you were sitting in the wrong place.”

“There are four of us.”

“So there are four places.”

“Not all of us can sit at the same angle.”

Clarisse looked at the figures. “I know.”

The answer made Percy think of the table in Cabin Three. There were always more things on it than there was room for: cups, folded notes, maps, cards, projects that somebody had promised to finish. They still found space to eat. It was not a perfect arrangement. It was one they kept making again.

“The figures don’t have to face us all at once,” Annabeth said.

“I want them together.”

“They are together.”

“Not if the owl looks away.”

“It doesn’t look away. It looks to the side.”

“That’s not what I mean.”

“I know. I’m saying it can be with the others without staring at them.”

Clarisse frowned. “It’s a carving.”

“You made it to look like me.”

“I said it looked like you.”

“That means you saw something in it.”

“It has an owl’s head.”

“And it’s watching.”

“Owls watch.”

“So do I.”

Clarisse didn’t answer. She turned the owl so it faced the water. The carved eyes seemed to follow the lantern’s reflection along the surf.

“That’s better,” Annabeth said.

“You just said it should face the others.”

“I said it didn’t have to.”

“You said it was looking away.”

“It was.”

“Now it’s looking at the water.”

“That’s where it wants to look.”

“It doesn’t want anything.”

“It’s your carving. You made the choice.”

“I made a shape.”

“You made it point somewhere.”

Clarisse studied the owl again. “It’s not supposed to be about what I want.”

“Then what is it about?” Percy asked.

“The cabin.”

“What about the cabin?”

“That we’re there.”

“All of us?”

“All of us.”

“Even if we’re not in the cabin right now?”

“That’s what markers are for.”

Percy rolled his cup between his palms. “I like having something that says where we are.”

“The cabin’s where we sleep,” Clarisse said.

“It’s where we sleep. That’s not the only thing.”

“It’s where we keep our stuff.”

“And where we fight over cards.”

“You lose at cards.”

“I’m improving.”

“You’re not.”

“That’s not what you said last week.”

“I said you were improving at shuffling.”

“I thought you meant the game.”

“That’s because you weren’t listening.”

“I was listening. I misunderstood.”

“That’s not the same.”

“You’re right.”

Clarisse tilted her head. “I don’t like that.”

“What?”

“You saying that every time I tell you something.”

“I’m trying to hear you.”

“Then hear me without turning it into a whole thing.”

“I can do that.”

“You said you were improving at cards. That wasn’t what I said.”

“I know. I thought it was.”

“So you heard the wrong thing.”

“Yeah.”

“Then just say that.”

“I heard the wrong thing.”

“Good.”

Silena lifted her cup. “To better listening.”

“No more toasts,” Clarisse said.

Percy started to ask whether she meant the figures or the cabin, then caught himself and let the question go. He took a sip from his cup instead. The drink had cooled enough not to burn; he didn't ask Silena how she had made it.

The wind flattened the grass above the beach. It tugged at Percy’s jacket and lifted the corner of the paper bag. Silena set her cup down and pinned the bag with two fingers.

“We should put the figures away,” she said.

“They’re not going anywhere,” Clarisse replied.

“The bag is.”

“Then put a stone on it.”

“The stones are holding the lantern.”

“There are more stones.”

“Those are wet.”

“They can dry.”

“I don’t want to put a wet stone on the bag.”

Clarisse stood and looked around the beach. The nearest rocks were dark with water. She walked up the slope and came back with a flat piece of driftwood, no longer than her hand.

“This work?”

Silena tested the weight. “Yes.”

“Then use it.”

Silena placed it on the folded bag. The paper stopped moving.

“There.”

“Thanks,” Silena said.

“It was just a piece of wood.”

“You found it.”

“It was right there.”

“I still appreciate it.”

Clarisse sat again. She didn’t dismiss the thanks. Percy noticed that too.

For a while they listened to the waves. Annabeth rested her shoulder against his. He could feel the edge of her sketchbook through the fabric of her bag where she had tucked it before leaving the cabin. She had not brought it out. She was not trying to work. The night did not ask her to produce anything.

“Did you like the harbor?” Silena asked.

Annabeth looked up. “Yes. The boats were smaller than I expected.”

“You expected bigger ones?”

“I thought there’d be more sailboats.”

“There were a few.”

“There were more motorboats.”

“Did you like those?”

“I liked the way the ropes were arranged. Some of them had different knots.”

Clarisse raised an eyebrow. “You went on a date and studied knots?”

“I can do more than one thing.”

“Did Percy notice the knots?”

“No,” Percy said.

"He was busy with the clam postcard," Annabeth added.

“I noticed the harbor too.”

“You noticed the gull.”

“That was at the food stand.”

“You watched it steal a fry.”

“It was a good theft.”

“You’re complimenting the gull now?”

“It had a plan.”

“It had wings.”

“That was part of the plan.”

Silena shook her head. “I’m learning more about this date than I expected.”

“You can ask whatever you want,” Annabeth said.

“I know. I’m asking.”

“Then we’ll tell you.”

“You don’t have to tell everything.”

“We won’t.”

Clarisse watched them. “Do you want to hear about every part?”

Silena considered. “I want to hear what they want to tell. I don’t want them to give me a report because they think I’m owed one.”

“Good,” Clarisse said.

“And I like hearing about the funny parts.”

“The gull was funny,” Percy said.

“You said it was a good theft.”

“It can be both.”

Annabeth’s hand found his under the edge of his jacket. She squeezed once, then let her fingers rest between his. The warmth of her palm was clear against the evening air.

“Did you want to go?” Clarisse asked Annabeth.

“Yes.”

“Did he ask you?”

“I asked him.”

Clarisse nodded. “Good.”

“You keep saying that.”

“Because I mean it.”

“I know.”

“Don’t start.”

“I’m not.”

Clarisse turned toward Percy. “Did you want to?”

“Yes.”

“Even if you were tired?”

“I would’ve said something.”

“Would you?”

“I think so.”

“That’s not the same as knowing.”

Percy felt the trap in the phrase, but it wasn’t meant as a trap. Clarisse was asking whether he could disagree with Annabeth and remain himself. It was a fair question. He was glad she asked it plainly.

“I’m still learning how to say what I want before I try to guess what everybody else needs,” he said. “But I wanted to go today.”

“And if you hadn’t?”

“I would’ve told her.”

Annabeth’s thumb moved across his knuckle. “I’d have listened.”

“You don’t have to promise that for the next time,” Percy said.

“I’m telling you what I would do.”

“You can decide then.”

“I can.”

Clarisse picked a grain of sand from her sleeve. “It’s good when people can say no.”

“It’s good when people can hear it,” Annabeth replied.

“Yeah.”

“And when a yes belongs to the person saying it.”

“You’ve got a lot of rules for a date.”

“They’re not rules. They’re choices.”

“That sounds like rules.”

“You can choose not to follow them.”

“I can choose not to go on a date.”

“Exactly.”

Clarisse glanced at Percy. “You’re both very serious.”

“We can be serious and still have fun.”

“Were you having fun?”

“Yes,” Annabeth said.

“Even when you were cold?”

“The cold part wasn’t fun.”

“Then why stay?”

“We didn’t stay. We went back toward the main beach.”

“You could have gone to the van.”

“We could have.”

“Why didn’t you?”

“The weather was changing, but it wasn’t dangerous. We moved to the public path and kept the van in view.”

“You decided that?”

“Together.”

Clarisse looked at Percy. “And you agreed?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because she was right.”

“You don’t have to say that.”

“I’m not saying it because she’s Annabeth. I’m saying it because we both looked at the sky and decided to stop swimming.”

“You could have gone back in.”

“I could have. I didn’t want to.”

“Because of the weather?”

“Because we’d had enough water. We wanted to see the harbor.”

“And you did.”

“We did.”

Clarisse leaned back against the log. “All right.”

The answer had no hidden accusation. Percy felt his shoulders ease. He had been ready to defend the date as though it had to pass a test, but Clarisse had not asked for proof that he could keep Annabeth safe or that they knew one another perfectly. She wanted to know whether they had chosen the day for themselves.

They had.

“The best part was the float lesson,” Annabeth said.

“Not the fries?” Percy asked.

“The fries were good.”

“Not the postcards?”

“I liked the postcards.”

“Not the clam?”

"The clam was a cartoon."

“A shell can be the best part.”

“It wasn’t.”

“What made the lesson the best part?” Silena asked.

Annabeth looked down at their joined hands. “I was afraid I’d look foolish. Then I did, and it didn’t matter.”

“Did Percy laugh?”

“No.”

“I might have smiled.”

“You did.”

“Because you were laughing.”

“I was laughing at myself.”

“I liked seeing you laugh.”

“That sounds like something you’d say.”

“I mean it.”

“I know.”

“You can say you know without sounding annoyed.”

“I wasn’t annoyed.”

“I know.”

“You’re both doing it now,” Clarisse said.

“Doing what?”

“Talking like the other person has to prove they heard you.”

Annabeth looked at Percy. “Do I do that?”

“Sometimes.”

“Do I?”

“Sometimes.”

“What should I do instead?”

“I don’t know. Maybe ask if I heard you.”

“Did you hear me?”

“Yes.”

“What did I say?”

“You were afraid you’d look foolish in the water. You did, and then it didn’t matter because you were laughing at yourself.”

“That’s what I said.”

“Then I heard you.”

Annabeth’s mouth curved. “Yes.”

“No, you were supposed to ask me.”

“I was confirming.”

“That’s different.”

“It is.”

Clarisse turned to Silena. “Do I do that?”

“Sometimes.”

“What do you want me to do instead?”

“Ask me if I’ve heard you.”

“Have you heard me?”

“Yes.”

“What did I say?”

“You said sometimes.”

“That’s not what I said.”

“It’s exactly what you said.”

“You missed the rest.”

“There was no rest.”

“There was.”

Silena laughed. “I was joking.”

“That was a bad joke.”

“It worked.”

“It didn’t.”

“You answered.”

“I answered because you asked.”

“Then the joke worked.”

“No.”

Silena leaned against the log, pleased with herself. Clarisse looked like she might argue longer, then stopped. “Fine.”

“You heard me,” Silena said.

“I heard you.”

“What did I say?”

“That sometimes I do it.”

“And?”

“And you’d rather I ask if you heard me.”

“Yes.”

“I can ask.”

“Thanks.”

“You’re welcome.”

The exchange didn’t fix every sharp edge. It gave each of them something to try the next time the same small misunderstanding came around. Percy could picture them at breakfast, Clarisse asking if Silena had heard her instead of repeating the whole sentence louder, Silena answering yes or asking her to say it again. The change would be small enough that nobody else might notice. It would still belong to them.

The clouds passed over the moon. The lantern seemed brighter after the moonlight disappeared. Annabeth shifted closer to Percy, and he moved his shoulder so she could rest comfortably. She watched the figures for a while.

“I like the way the owl’s head turns,” she said.

“It took a long time to get right,” Clarisse said.

“You changed it?”

“A few times.”

“Why?”

“It kept looking angry.”

“It still looks a little angry.”

“That’s because it’s an owl.”

“I thought you said it looks like me.”

“You can be angry too.”

“I can.”

“Then it looks like you.”

“That makes sense.”

“You’re not offended?”

“No. I like that you noticed.”

Clarisse turned the owl toward her. “It looks like you thinking.”

“That’s different from looking angry.”

“You think angrily.”

“I don’t think there’s a way to do that.”

“There is if you’re thinking about why someone moved your map.”

“That’s because they moved it.”

“Without asking.”

“Exactly.”

Clarisse’s mouth twitched. “Then it’s accurate.”

“I’ll accept that.”

The compliment had come with an edge, but the edge did not cut. Percy hoped Annabeth had heard the care he thought Clarisse meant to put beneath it. He felt glad the owl gave Clarisse a way to show what she noticed about Annabeth without making her announce it to the whole beach.

“Do you want to keep the owl?” Clarisse asked.

“In the cabin?”

“Yes.”

“I’d like it there.”

“You can take it to your cabin if you want.”

“I’m in the Zeus cabin.”

“I know.”

“Then I’d rather it stay here.”

“Okay.”

“If you want it to.”

“I made it for the cabin.”

“And for me?”

Clarisse paused. “Yes.”

Annabeth nodded. “Then I’ll remember that.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I want to.”

“Fine.”

“Thank you.”

Clarisse looked back at the water. “You already said that.”

“I know.”

“You’re all impossible.”

“You invited us,” Percy reminded her.

“I did.”

“You could have asked us to leave.”

“I could have.”

“You didn’t.”

“No.”

There was no need to ask why. The figures were there. The cocoa was warm. The people Clarisse had invited were still sitting with her. The space around the lantern held the difference between them without asking anyone to bridge it all at once.

“I’m glad you asked,” Percy said.

“Don’t make it weird.”

“I’m not.”

“You are.”

“I’m just glad.”

“Then be glad.”

“I am.”

“Good.”

Silena lifted the thermos, shook it, and heard only a small slosh. “There’s enough for one more cup.”

“Who wants it?” Clarisse asked.

No one answered immediately. Silena held out the cup to Annabeth, who shook her head. Percy had finished his. Clarisse looked at the thermos.

“You can have it,” Silena said.

“I don’t want it.”

“Then I’ll take it.”

“You don’t have to ask.”

“I wanted to.”

“Then take it.”

Silena poured the last little bit into her cup. It was barely enough to cover the bottom. She drank it anyway.

“That was not a full cup,” Clarisse said.

“It was enough.”

“That was two sips,” Clarisse said.

“That was what I wanted.”

“You could say when you aren't sure.”

Silena shook her head. “I didn't know until I saw what was left. I wanted that much, not more.”

Clarisse glanced at the empty thermos. “All right. I was checking, not saying you couldn't have it.”

“I know. You can ask me.”

“I did.”

“You did. We're good.”

Clarisse let out a breath. “Fine.”

The moment passed without a hug or a promise. Silena had said what she wanted. Clarisse had asked; she had also accepted the answer. Percy thought of how easy it was to decide someone needed saving when they hesitated. It was harder to wait long enough to find out whether they wanted help.

 He looked at Annabeth. “You want to head back soon?”

“In a minute.”

“Do you want my jacket?”

“No. I’m warm enough.”

“You’re sure?”

“Yes.”

“Okay.”

“You can stop checking.”

“I’m not checking. I’m offering.”

“You can offer once.”

“I did.”

“Good.”

“Are you cold?” she asked.

“A little.”

“Do you want the jacket?”

“It’s mine.”

“I know.”

“You can borrow it if you want.”

“I’m warm enough.”

“Then I’ll keep it.”

She squeezed his hand. “Good.”

They sat for another moment. The moon came out again. The carved figures threw small shadows along the log.

“We should pack them before the lantern burns out,” Silena said.

“It has plenty of oil,” Clarisse replied.

“We don’t know how much.”

“It was full.”

“How long ago?”

“Before dinner.”

“That’s enough time to burn a lot.”

“It’s a lantern. It can burn for hours.”

“Then it’ll be fine.”

“You’re the one who brought it.”

“You’re the one who asked us to come outside.”

“I didn’t ask you to bring a lantern.”

“I wanted us to see.”

“We can see.”

“We can see now.”

“That’s what I said.”

“All right.”

Clarisse glanced at the dark beyond the lantern. “We can stay until it gets low.”

“Or until we get cold,” Annabeth said.

“That too.”

“You want to stay?” Percy asked.

“Yes.”

“Then we’ll stay.”

“You don’t have to stay just because I do.”

“I want to.”

“You can tell me if that changes.”

“I will.”

“Good.”

The small decisions accumulated. The lantern could stay lit. The figures could stay where they were. Silena could finish the last of the cocoa. Annabeth could rest against Percy. Clarisse could decide when they left. Nobody needed to pretend that agreement meant everyone felt exactly the same.

“What are you going to do with the owl?” Percy asked.

“We said it’s for the cabin.”

“I meant after tonight.”

“I’ll finish the base.”

“Then it goes on the shelf?”

“If it fits.”

“And if it doesn’t?”

“I’ll find another place.”

“You could keep it on your bunk.”

“I could.”

“Would you?”

“I don’t know.”

“You don’t have to decide.”

“I know.”

“You’re allowed to like a thing and not know where it goes.”

“I know.”

“Okay.”

Clarisse turned to him. “You’re not going to tell me what the figures mean?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because you made them.”

“You can have an opinion.”

“I like them. I think they look like they belong together.”

“That’s an opinion.”

“I know.”

“You just said you wouldn’t tell me what they mean.”

“I won’t tell you what you meant.”

Clarisse looked at the figures again. “Good.”

“I can tell you what I see.”

“What do you see?”

“Four people who don’t look alike, but who can be in the same place.”

“That’s not what I made.”

“It’s what I see.”

She didn’t answer. Percy wondered whether he had said too much. He had not meant to claim the figures as proof of anything. He meant only that they reminded him of their cabin, which held people with different parents, different memories, and different ways of being loved.

“You don’t have to turn them into a symbol,” Clarisse said.

“I’m not.”

“You are.”

“I’m saying what they look like to me.”

“That’s a symbol.”

“Then I guess I am.”

“It doesn’t have to mean anything.”

“It doesn’t.”

“You can just like the carving.”

“I do.”

“Then stop adding stuff.”

“Okay.”

He stopped. Clarisse watched him, checking whether he would keep explaining. He didn’t. The beach held their silence without making it a problem.

After a while, Clarisse spoke. “I made them because I wanted to.”

“I know.”

Her eyes narrowed.

“Sorry. I mean, I heard you.”

“That’s better.”

“You don’t have to explain more.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“Good.”

She reached for the panther and turned it around. The new angle showed the inside of one wing where the stain had not reached. She rubbed at it with a thumb, then stopped when she saw the dark mark it left on her skin.

“The stain’s still wet?” Silena asked.

“No.”

“Then why did it rub off?”

“It didn’t.”

“There’s a mark.”

“That’s from earlier.”

“We can get oil from the kitchen.”

“Tomorrow.”

“Okay.”

“I’ll wash it.”

“You can.”

“I know.”

Silena looked at her but let the subject go.

“I like that the panther’s wing doesn’t match,” Percy said.

“It’s supposed to.”

“It looks like it’s moving.”

“It isn’t.”

“I mean it looks like it’s about to.”

“That’s because the wing is raised.”

“I like it.”

“You said that.”

“I like it again.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I want to.”

Clarisse gave him a look. “You sound like Silena.”

“I’ve been around her too long.”

“That’s not a bad thing.”

“I didn’t say it was.”

“Good.”

Clarisse put the panther down. She did not finish the sentence she seemed to be considering. She didn’t need to. Percy could stay where he was without asking her to translate every look. If she wanted to say more, she could.

“Do you want to hear about the postcards?” Annabeth asked Silena.

“Yes. What did you write on the one for yourselves?”

“Montauk, with you.”

“That's sweet.”

“It was Percy's idea. I chose to write it.”

Clarisse nodded. “You can keep that card private. I'm not asking to see it.”

“Thanks. I can tell you about the public harbor postcard instead.”

Silena leaned closer. “What's on it?”

Annabeth described the harbor view. It showed a line of boats, the curve of the breakwater, and a lighthouse in the distance. She said the printed colors were too bright, but the photograph had a good sense of distance. She had bought a second card with a clam illustration because Percy had insisted the cartoon clam deserved a portrait.

“Did you write on that one?” Silena asked.

“No. It’s still blank.”

“Are you going to send it to someone?”

“Maybe. I might keep it.”

“That’s allowed.”

Silena smiled. “I’m learning.”

“What are you learning?”

“That you can know something and still want to say it.”

Annabeth considered. “That’s true.”

“And that I can decide later.”

“Yes.”

“And that I don’t have to choose the clam card because Percy thinks it’s funny.”

“You don’t.”

“I might anyway.”

“You can.”

“I’ll see.”

The postcard remained with Annabeth. The message was already written on the one she planned to keep. She had not decided whether to send the blank one. Percy did not know what she would choose, and he did not need to know before she did.

“What about your knife?” Clarisse asked him.

Percy felt his hand go to his pocket. “I still have it.”

“I know.”

“I’ll give it back tonight.”

“Good.”

“I didn’t use it.”

“I know that too.”

“I didn’t mean to forget.”

“You forgot.”

“Yeah.”

“That’s why I asked.”

“I’ll return it when we get back.”

“All right.”

The matter was not serious enough for Percy to keep apologizing, but Clarisse did not pretend the loan had been meaningless. He had borrowed something. He would return it. The small accountability felt like part of the place at the table, too: people could remind each other without turning every mistake into a judgment about whether they belonged.

“We should put the figures somewhere dry,” Silena said.

“The bag is dry,” Clarisse replied.

“The bottom’s damp.”

“Then we’ll fold it.”

“We can use my jacket.”

“It’ll get sand in it.”

“I can wash it.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I know.”

Clarisse studied the paper bag. “We can wrap each one separately and put them in the bag.”

“With what?” Percy asked.

“The napkins.”

Silena produced four from her pocket. “I brought these for the cocoa.”

“You brought everything,” Clarisse said.

“Not everything. I didn’t bring a blanket.”

“You were supposed to bring a blanket?”

“No. I’m saying I didn’t.”

“Why would you bring a blanket?”

“I wouldn’t. It’s cold.”

“We have jackets.”

“I know.”

Clarisse took one napkin. “Wrap the ship with this.”

“You do it,” Silena said.

“I am.”

“I meant you can show me how you want it done.”

Clarisse slowed. “Just wrap it around the mast first. Then fold the ends underneath.”

Silena did. The napkin covered the mast and protected the carved hull. “Like that?”

“Yeah.”

“Good?”

“Good.”

Silena wrapped the other three figures. Clarisse checked each one but did not undo the folds. The owl’s beak stuck out of the napkin. Annabeth tucked the corner over it.

“That’s better,” Clarisse said.

“Thanks.”

“I was checking.”

“I know.”

Clarisse frowned, but then laughed. “Now you’re doing it.”

“I’m learning.”

“Don’t learn that.”

“Too late.”

---

Later, while Silena and Annabeth hunted for flat stones to stack around the lantern, Clarisse walked with Percy along the waterline.

"You're doing okay with the dating thing," she said.

Percy looked at her. "That's what this is about?"

"No. But I figured I'd mention it while we're walking. You're not hovering. You're not trying to make decisions for her. That's good."

"I don't want to treat her like the person I remember instead of the person she is now."

Clarisse nodded. "Good. She's not a memory."

"I know."

"And she isn't mine to guard either." Clarisse kicked a pebble into the surf. "I have to keep reminding myself. She’s become like a sister to me by choice. I didn’t raise her; I’m her friend. She doesn’t need me to be between her and every bad thing that might happen."

"It's hard to stop protecting someone when that's what you've always done."

"Yeah." She glanced at him. "You get it."

"I think so."

Clarisse didn’t answer. Percy waited for her to make fun of the hesitation, but she kept her eyes on the water. A wave ran up the beach and pulled a string of foam around her boots.

“You think so?” she asked.

“I’m thinking.”

“You already said that.”

“I’m still doing it.”

“About what?”

“About knowing.”

She glanced at him. “You either know or you don’t.”

“That’s not always how it works.”

“It’s how it works when I ask you a question.”

Percy kicked a pebble. It rolled along the wet sand, stopped at the line of foam, and disappeared under the next wave. “I wanted to go. I knew that. I don’t know if I’d have told her right away if I didn’t.”

“Why not?”

“Sometimes I try to figure out what people need before I tell them what I need.”

“That sounds stupid.”

“It can be.”

“Why do you do it?”

“I don’t know.”

“You really don’t know?”

He considered making a joke. It would have been easy to tell her he had a limited supply of good ideas and was trying to ration them. Clarisse would have called that a bad answer, and she would have been right.

“I’m used to trying to protect people,” he said. “And sometimes I get it mixed up with deciding what they should do.”

“You think you’d do that to Annabeth?”

“I don’t want to.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“I don’t know if I have.”

“Then ask her.”

“I have.”

“What did she say?”

“That she wants me to trust her choices.”

“Do you?”

“Yes.”

“Then there you go.”

“It’s not that simple.”

“It is simple. It’s not easy.”

Percy looked at her. “That sounds like something Silena would say.”

“It sounds like something I said.”

“You’re right.”

Clarisse gave him a look.

“I’m just agreeing.”

“Then do it without the speech.”

“Okay.”

“You’re not going to decide what she can handle?”

“I’ll try not to.”

“What if you think she can’t?”

“I can tell her what I’m worried about and let her choose.”

“And if she disagrees?”

“Then she disagrees.”

“You’ll still let her?”

“Yes.”

Clarisse nodded. “Good.”

The answer did not make Percy feel absolved. It gave him something concrete to do. He could tell Annabeth what he felt, then listen. He did not need to anticipate her answer or turn worry into a rule.

“You make it sound like you’ve had to learn that too,” he said.

“I have.”

“With Annabeth?”

“With everyone.”

“What changed?”

“Nothing changed. I’m trying to.”

“That’s different.”

“It is.”

They walked beyond the wet sand. Clarisse kept close to the water but did not go in. She had taken off her shoes at the start of the walk and carried them by their laces. Percy’s socks were damp at the toes.

“You can take your shoes off,” she said.

“I know.”

“Then do it.”

“It’s cold.”

“The sand’s cold too.”

“I’m trying not to get sand in my shoes.”

“You already have sand in your shoes.”

“That’s a good point.”

“You can take them off now.”

“I’d have to carry them.”

“You can carry them.”

"Then I have the shoes and postcard."

“You’ll survive.”

“Probably.”

Clarisse waited while he sat on the log and took off his shoes. A handful of sand spilled from one. He brushed it out and shook the shoe twice. His socks were damp, but not soaked.

“There,” she said.

“I didn’t say you were right.”

“You didn’t have to.”

“You really don’t like being told that.”

“I don’t like people acting like I need them to explain everything.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You were.”

“I thought you were giving me advice.”

“I was telling you to take off your shoes.”

“That’s advice.”

“It’s an order.”

“You said I could.”

“I said you can. Then you did.”

“I made my own choice.”

“Yes.”

“So it wasn’t an order.”

“It was a suggestion.”

“You said order.”

“I changed my mind.”

Percy laughed. “You can do that?”

“I can do whatever I want.”

“That seems unfair.”

“It is.”

“Should we make a rule about it?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because I don’t want a rule.”

“Okay.”

“You’re trying to turn everything into a rule now.”

“I’m trying to understand.”

“You can understand without a rule.”

He put his shoes together and tucked them under his arm. “That might be harder.”

“Then practice.”

They walked again. The sand was cold, but softer under his bare feet. The tide reached his toes and retreated. Percy curled them against the chill.

“You don’t have to do that,” Clarisse said.

“Do what?”

“Make yourself uncomfortable because you think that’s what the moment requires.”

“I’m not.”

“You’re freezing.”

“My feet are cold.”

“Put your shoes back on.”

“You told me to take them off.”

“I suggested it.”

“Then I can choose to put them back on.”

“Yes.”

“I’ll wait until we turn around.”

“Fine.”

“You don’t have to agree.”

“I do agree.”

“All right.”

“You’re doing the same thing again.”

“What?”

“Looking for permission to make your own choice.”

Percy slowed. “I wasn’t.”

“You asked if I agree.”

“I was talking.”

“Then talk.”

“I want to wait until we turn around.”

“Okay.”

The answer was simple. Clarisse did not ask him to prove he could keep his shoes off. She did not tell him the cold was good for him. Percy took another few steps. The sand still chilled his feet. He decided to keep walking.

“Does this happen to you?” he asked.

“What?”

“Do you decide something because you think people expect it?”

“Sometimes.”

“What do you do?”

“I get mad.”

“Then what?”

“Then I figure out whether I was mad at them or at myself.”

“Does that work?”

“Sometimes.”

“And if you’re mad at them?”

“I tell them.”

“Even if it’s me?”

“Especially if it’s you.”

“Why especially me?”

“Because you’ll listen.”

Percy did not know what to say. The tide made its slow way in and out. He could hear Silena and Annabeth behind them, talking about the rocks. Clarisse had not said she trusted him with everything. She had said she thought he would listen. That was a smaller thing, and it felt earned.

“I’ll try to,” he said.

“You don’t have to make a promise.”

“It’s not a promise. It’s what I want to do.”

“Okay.”

They reached a stretch where the sand turned to pebbles. Clarisse stopped. The water was deeper there, dark and quick. She pointed to a line of stones set into the bluff.

“I was going to have us sit there.”

“It’s too close to the water,” Percy said.

“The tide isn’t up that high.”

“It could shift.”

“The tide’s coming in.”

“Then it could reach the stones.”

Clarisse looked at the water, then the stones. “We can sit higher.”

“The driftwood is better.”

“It’s farther away.”

“We can hear the surf.”

“You can hear it from anywhere.”

“Not as well.”

“You want to sit by the water?”

“I want to hear it.”

“Then sit on the driftwood.”

Percy understood that she was letting him choose. He also understood she had her own reason for choosing the stones. “You wanted to sit here?”

“I thought it would be nice.”

“We can sit here for a minute and see.”

“You think it’s unsafe.”

“I think the water could reach it.”

“How soon?”

“I don’t know.”

Clarisse studied the edge of the water. “We can stay above the second stone. If the water reaches it, we move.”

“That works.”

“You’re sure?”

“We can leave whenever we want.”

“Good.”

They sat on the dry stones, keeping their shoes and the carvings above them. The water brushed the lowest rock, then drew back. Percy watched the next wave approach. It came higher, stopped short of the second stone, and pulled away.

“How long do we stay?” Clarisse asked.

“Until you’re ready to move.”

“That’s not a time.”

“We can count waves.”

“How many?”

“Five.”

“Why five?”

“It’s enough to see if the water keeps coming higher.”

“It doesn’t come in the same every time.”

“Exactly.”

“That’s not a good reason.”

“It’s a test.”

“You and Annabeth have turned into Chiron.”

“He’s very wise.”

“Don’t tell him I said that.”

“I won’t.”

They counted. The third wave came no higher than the first. The fourth reached the lower stone and drained away. The fifth curled around the rock and hissed at their feet.

Clarisse stood. “We’re moving.”

“Because it reached the second stone?”

“Because it reached the first.”

“It reached the first before.”

“And now we’re leaving.”

“Okay.”

She climbed onto the firmer sand. Percy followed, putting his shoes back on. The second wave soaked the toes of his socks.

“You should have put them on sooner,” Clarisse said.

“I wanted to see.”

“Now you saw.”

“I did.”

“Next time, keep your shoes on.”

“Maybe.”

“You like that word.”

“You used it about the shelf.”

“That’s different.”

“How?”

“I made the figures.”

“I have the shoes.”

“You don’t have to make it the same.”

“I’m not. I’m saying we can both decide.”

“Fine.”

“That wasn’t a yes.”

“It was a fine.”

“Which means?”

“That we’re walking back.”

“Okay.”

Clarisse glanced at him. “You’re trying too hard.”

“Maybe.”

“There it is again.”

“It works.”

“It works until it doesn’t.”

“Then I’ll stop.”

“You can stop now.”

“I’ll try.”

She shook her head. “You’re impossible.”

“You invited me.”

“I know.”

“That means you chose this.”

“I chose the beach.”

“With us.”

“Yes.”

He decided not to ask why. Clarisse had said enough for the evening. She had invited them, made figures, brought them to the water, and checked the ground before setting anything down. He could remember those actions without deciding they stood for a larger promise.

“Do you think Annabeth will come back to the beach with us?” Clarisse asked.

“You should ask her.”

“I did.”

“She said yes.”

“She said she’d like that.”

“That’s a yes.”

“It’s a maybe.”

“It’s a possibility.”

“You’re doing it again.”

“Doing what?”

“Making her answer into what you want it to mean.”

Percy stopped. “I’m not.”

“You said it was a yes.”

“She said she’d like to.”

“That’s not the same as promising.”

“I know.”

“Then don’t say it like one.”

“I didn’t.”

“You did.”

Percy felt a familiar defensiveness rise, then settle. Clarisse had not told him Annabeth would refuse. She was pointing at the exact difference between interest and a plan. “You’re right. She said she’d like to. We haven’t planned anything.”

“Good.”

“You can ask her again sometime.”

“I can.”

“And she can decide then.”

“Yes.”

Clarisse’s shoulders eased. “That’s all I meant.”

“I know.”

She glanced at him.

“I heard you,” he corrected.

“Better.”

They turned back toward the lantern. Annabeth and Silena were still by the driftwood, collecting stones for the ring around its base. They had not heard the conversation. The conversation belonged to Percy and Clarisse, and he intended to keep it there unless she chose to share it.

“What did you say to her?” Clarisse asked.

“Nothing.”

“You said something.”

“I said we can ask her again another time.”

“That’s not nothing.”

“I know.”

“You don’t have to say that.”

“I heard you.”

“Good.”

“I haven’t told her we planned anything.”

“I didn’t think you did.”

“I won’t.”

“You don’t have to promise.”

“It’s not a promise. I’m telling you what I’m doing.”

Clarisse seemed to accept that. They walked the rest of the way without speaking. The water followed them at a distance, quiet enough that Percy could hear Silena laughing at something Annabeth said.

They walked another few paces. The water curled around their ankles and withdrew.

"I like her," Clarisse said. "Not just because she's your favorite person. She's sharp. She doesn't let me get away with being a jerk. And she sees things about me I don't always want seen."

"She does that."

"If she hurts you, I'll still be on her side."

"I would expect nothing less."

Clarisse stopped and gave him a sideways look. "You know what I mean."

"I do. And if I hurt her, I expect you to tell me."

"Oh, I won't tell you." She resumed walking. "I'll make you run laps until you figure it out."

Percy laughed. "That seems fair."

"It is."

They walked back toward the lantern at an easy pace. Clarisse kept to the firmer strip of sand, Percy beside her. He had put his shoes back on, though the damp socks made each step feel slightly wrong. Clarisse glanced at his feet once and said nothing.

“You don’t have to pretend not to notice,” he said.

“I’m not pretending.”

“You looked.”

“I have eyes.”

“You noticed the socks are wet.”

“They’re your socks.”

“You could tell me to change them.”

“You can change them when you get back.”

“That sounds like telling me.”

“It’s information.”

“I’m learning the difference.”

“Good.”

“You don’t have to say good every time.”

“I’ll say what I want.”

“Fair.”

The lantern’s glow moved over the beach as Silena adjusted its position. Annabeth stood near the driftwood with one hand on the paper bag. The figures had been placed in a rough circle. Their shadows crossed but did not obscure one another.

“We’re back,” Clarisse called.

“We can see you,” Silena replied.

“You moved the lantern.”

“The wind changed.”

“It was fine where it was.”

“It started to rock.”

“How much?”

“Enough that I moved it.”

Clarisse approached and tested the lantern’s base. She pressed one finger against the metal. It stayed upright.

“It’s steady now,” she said.

“It is.”

“The stones are too close to the flame.”

“They’re outside the glass.”

“The paper bag isn’t.”

Silena moved the bag farther away. “Better?”

“Yes.”

“You can tell me what you notice without making it sound like I did something wrong.”

“I didn’t say you did.”

“I know.”

“I was checking the lantern.”

“I know.”

“Then we’re fine.”

“We are.”

Silena gave the bag a final tug, making sure it wouldn’t slide. The lantern held steady. Annabeth placed a flat stone along the landward side of the circle, leaving the water side open.

“Why leave a gap?” Percy asked.

“So the light can get out,” she said.

“It can get out over the stones.”

“Not as much.”

“Do we need it to?”

“I like being able to see the water.”

“Then the gap makes sense.”

Clarisse crouched beside the ring. “The stones can hold the lantern if the wind picks up.”

“They can also trap heat,” Annabeth said. “We shouldn’t put anything against the glass.”

“Nothing is against it.”

“The nearest stone is close.”

Clarisse moved it farther out. “There.”

“That works.”

“You can stop checking.”

“I was checking the clearance.”

“It’s clear.”

“Now it is.”

Clarisse looked at the adjusted ring. “We should check it again if the wind changes.”

“We can,” Annabeth said.

“I’ll check.”

“All right.”

Silena crouched and found a second flat stone. “The landward side is still low.”

“We need one more here,” Annabeth said.

“It has to be flat enough not to roll,” Percy said.

He searched near the driftwood and found a stone, palm-sized, with one smooth side and one jagged edge. He brought it back and tested it on the sand. It tipped once. He turned it over. It settled.

“This one?” he asked.

“Try it beside the other,” Annabeth said.

He placed it. The ring became more even, though not perfectly round. The lantern light passed through the gap toward the water.

“That’s good,” Clarisse said.

“Do we need another?” Percy asked.

“No.”

“How do we know?”

“Because it’s steady.”

“We tested it?”

“I did.”

“Then that’s enough.”

The lantern gave off a warm circle of light. Annabeth sat down beside Percy. Silena arranged the cups in the sand where they wouldn’t tip. Clarisse set the bag behind the driftwood and checked the figures one more time. The sea horse and ship had not moved. The panther’s unfinished wing faced the path. The owl remained at the center.

“That’s a better arrangement,” Percy said.

“It’s crooked,” Clarisse replied.

“It’s stable.”

“Both can be true,” Annabeth said.

Clarisse shrugged. “It’ll do.”

She lowered herself onto the log. The others settled around her, leaving enough space that no one had to sit in the damp sand. The lantern was not a ceremonial centerpiece. It was there so they could see one another and keep the figures safe while the wind moved over the beach.

The figures rested between them, close enough to be seen by everyone. No one had claimed them as a promise. They were Clarisse’s work, shared with the cabin because she had made them for the cabin. They could stay together for the evening and be moved later if the table or shelf required it.

For tonight, the circle held.

"It looks like a very small council meeting," Annabeth said.

"A council of animals," Silena replied. "They've reached a decision. The sand is cold, and everyone should have cocoa."

She produced a thermos from the bag. Clarisse stared. "You brought cocoa?"

"Of course."

"Why didn't you say so?"

"Because you would've drunk it before the ceremony."

"There was no ceremony."

"Exactly."

They sat in a loose circle around the lantern and passed the thermos. Annabeth tucked her shoulder under Percy's arm; he rested his cheek briefly against her hair. She smelled like pencil shavings and salt air.

"This is nice," she murmured.

"You sound surprised."

"I thought Clarisse was going to make us spar in the dark."

"I considered it," Clarisse said. "But the sand's cold, and Silena brought cocoa."

"A compelling argument," Annabeth said.

---

They stayed until the lantern burned low. Silena capped the thermos and started gathering the cups. Clarisse lifted the figures from the stone ring and wrapped them in the paper bag, with the panther on top so its wings would not catch. Annabeth held the lantern while the others checked that the flame was out.

“We’ll bring them back to the cabin,” Clarisse said.

“All four?” Percy asked.

“All four.”

“Together?”

“Yes.”

“Okay.”

Clarisse glanced at him. “You don’t have to check every decision.”

“I wanted to make sure.”

“You can just come with us.”

“I will.”

Clarisse and Silena started up the path ahead, still arguing about whether the carved ship's mast was crooked on purpose. Percy and Annabeth followed at an easier pace. They carried the bag, thermos and lantern; the paper rustled when Clarisse shifted it. Percy tucked the clam postcard safely into its paper sleeve and slid it back inside his jacket. At the cabin, Clarisse put the figures on the table, where they could stay overnight without catching sand or wind. The shelf above the window was narrow; she said she would decide where they belonged after looking at it in daylight. No one moved them there.

“The panther’s still yours,” Clarisse told Percy.

“I know.”

“You don’t have to take it out tonight.”

“I won’t.”

“It stays with the others until I finish the wings.”

“Okay.”

The panther was the one Clarisse had given him months ago, finished further and brought out with the rest. Now it stood beside the sea horse, ship and owl, part of the cabin set for the evening. Percy could decide where to keep it after they returned.

“You can put it back on your shelf when we’re done,” Clarisse said.

“If you want it with the others tomorrow?”

“I’ll tell you.”

“Okay.”

“It’s still yours.”

“I know.”

“You can keep it wherever you want.”

“I’ll put it back on my shelf tonight.”

“Good.”

The decision was small, and Percy liked having it. He could carry the carving to the beach, bring it back with the others, and return it to the place he had chosen for it. The gesture did not cancel the time Clarisse had spent finishing it or the place she wanted it to mark. It let both things be true.

---

"I like your family," Annabeth said.

Percy turned to her. “You mean the carvings or the people?”

“The people. The carvings are nice too.”

“Clarisse is a lot.”

“She is.”

“Silena is easier.”

“She’s different.”

“That wasn’t a criticism.”

“I know.”

They walked beneath the pine trees. The lantern’s light fell away behind them. Ahead, the cabin lights made small squares through the branches.

“Sometimes I don’t know where I fit with them,” Annabeth said. “Clarisse says I’m her friend. Silena says I’m welcome. They’re both kind to me, but I don’t have the years they have together.”

“You don’t have to pretend you do.”

“I know.”

“And you can be close to them without becoming another Poseidon sibling.”

“I don’t want to be one.”

“I know.”

“There you go again.”

“I heard you.”

“I’m not upset.”

“I know.”

She nudged his shoulder. “You really are working on it.”

“I’m trying.”

“I don’t need you to solve where I fit.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were thinking about it.”

“I can think about things without solving them.”

“That’s new.”

“It’s a work in progress.”

“I like that.”

Annabeth gave him a look, but her hand found his again. “I like that Clarisse asked me if I wanted the owl. She didn’t assume I’d want it because she made it to look like me.”

“She wanted you to have it.”

“Yes. But she let me answer.”

“She’s learning too.”

“We all are.”

“You don’t have to be grateful for being asked.”

“I’m not grateful she asked. I’m glad she did.”

“That’s different.”

“It is.”

He squeezed her hand. “I’m glad you were there.”

“I wanted to be.”

“I know.”

“Percy.”

“I heard you.”

“Better.”

They reached the path where the cabins divided. Annabeth glanced toward Cabin One, then back at him. She was not hurrying. The night did not require a conversation to end neatly before they parted.

“I want to tell Thalia about the owl,” she said. “Not everything we talked about.”

“You can tell her whatever you want.”

“I know. I want to tell her because it was a good thing.”

“Then tell her that.”

“I will.”

“You don’t have to mention the rest.”

“I won’t.”

“And you don’t have to give her the postcard tonight.”

“I know. I’ll decide tomorrow.”

“Okay.”

“You can keep your letter private too.”

“I will.”

The agreement did not turn the evening into a secret. They had spent it with Clarisse and Silena. They had told them ordinary things about the date. But the parts that belonged only to Percy and Annabeth remained theirs unless they chose otherwise.

“I’m glad we went,” Annabeth said.

“Me too.”

“Even though Clarisse was planning it all day?”

“Especially because she was.”

“You like being surprised?”

“Sometimes.”

“You knew there was a surprise.”

“I knew she had a paper bag.”

“That’s not the same.”

“It was enough.”

Annabeth smiled. “You’re getting better at not needing to know everything first.”

“I have a lot to learn.”

“You don’t need to make a project out of that either.”

“I’ll try not to.”

“Good.”

He looked down at their joined hands. "I was about to promise I would stop. That sounds like another project."

"Then don't promise. Just notice."

"I can try."

"That I believe."

They walked a few more steps. "If I start treating you like the person I remember, tell me."

"I will. But I want you to meet the person who's here too."

"I do. I'm glad you're here."

"I'm glad I'm here too."

He knew she meant the path, the camp, the night with the carvings, and more than those. She did not have to unpack all of it for him. He could hold the sentence as it was.

She squeezed his hand once. “Come on. I’ll be cold if we stand here.”

“You said you were warm enough.”

“I was. Now I’m not.”

“That’s fair.”

“You can walk me to the cabin.”

“I was going to.”

“You don’t have to.”

“I want to.”

“Okay.”

They crossed the last stretch together.

"Clarisse wants you at the table as Annabeth. You don't have to be one of Poseidon's kids for that."

"I know. I want to be there as your friend."

Percy stopped beneath the pine trees, away from the cabin lights. "Can I ask you something?"

"Always."

"Are you happy with us? With this?"

Annabeth thought about it before answering. He loved that about her. She didn't hand out comfort just because it was requested.

"Yes," she said. "I'm happy. Sometimes I'm confused. Sometimes I feel like I'm standing in two lives at once. But when I'm with you, I don't feel like I have to choose one memory over the other."

"That's good."

"And you?"

"I'm happy too. I miss the older version of us sometimes, but I don't want to skip this part. I want to know you here."

Annabeth's eyes softened. "Then we keep doing that."

"Yeah."

She leaned up and kissed him. It was warm and unhurried, and when they parted she stayed close enough that he could feel her smile.

"Goodnight, favorite person," she said.

"Goodnight."

She went to the Zeus cabin. Percy watched her until the door shut, then turned toward Cabin Three. Back at the common table, he lifted the panther from the set and carried it to his bunk. The clam postcard rested flat inside its sleeve in his jacket pocket.

He carried both to his shelf.

The jacket was still over his arm. Percy checked the inner pocket and found Clarisse's knife where he had put it before the date. The wrapping was dry, and the clasp had not caught on anything. He took it back to the common room instead of leaving it for morning.

Clarisse was stacking the cups beside the thermos. She glanced at the wrapped knife in his hand. “You remembered.”

“I said I would.” He held it out by the wrapped handle. “I didn't use it, and it stayed dry.”

She tested the tie with her thumb, then tucked the knife into the small drawer where she kept her practice gear. “Good. I don't want to find it under a pile of wet towels tomorrow.”

“You'd know it was mine.”

“I'd know it was borrowed. That's why I wanted it back.” She shut the drawer and turned to the cups. “Can you take these to the wash basin?”

Percy gathered them. “Sure.”

There was no ceremony to the exchange. Clarisse had lent him something useful, reminded him to return it, and waited until he did. He had been late getting back from Montauk and had forgotten the knife during dinner; neither fact meant he could not be trusted with it now. The small correction sat alongside the evening's other things: the figures safe on the table, Silena's cocoa packed away, and Annabeth's postcard still hers to decide about.

At the basin, Percy rinsed the cups while Clarisse folded the damp paper bag around the lantern. Silena checked that the thermos lid was tight. They worked without needing to turn the night into another discussion about what anyone had chosen or what the choice proved. When Percy returned, Clarisse had already set the knife drawer closed and gone to wash the sand from her hands.

He went to his bunk with the postcard and the panther, leaving the borrowed knife where its owner kept it.
