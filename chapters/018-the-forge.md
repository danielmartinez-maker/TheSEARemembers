## Chapter 18: The Forge

Percy knew better than to trust Annabeth when she showed up before breakfast with a rolled-up piece of graph paper under her arm and that particular look on her face.

It wasn't the I'm-going-to-push-you-in-the-lake look. It wasn't even the I'm-about-to-argue-with-a-god look. It was worse. It was the I've-had-an-idea-at-three-in-the-morning-and-drew-schematics look.

"No," he said immediately, mouth full of toast.

"You haven't even heard what it is yet," Annabeth said, dropping into the seat across from him at the Poseidon table. Which she wasn't supposed to do, technically, since she was Zeus's kid now and supposed to sit at the Zeus table with Thalia, but nobody at Camp Half-Blood enforced breakfast seating that hard on a Tuesday, and Thalia was still asleep anyway.

"No," Percy repeated. "Last time you had schematics, I ended up carrying lumber up Half-Blood Hill for three hours."

"That was a good wall," Annabeth said. "That wall held."

"That wall gave me splinters in places I didn't know you could get splinters."

Annabeth unrolled the graph paper over his plate. Percy moved his eggs just in time. On the paper, in crisp pencil lines with measurements written in the margins in her tiny, perfect handwriting, was a temple.

Not a big one. A model, maybe two feet long. A classical Greek temple with six columns across the front, a pediment, a cella inside. Notes crowded the edges: *bronze frame — 1:50 scale? — test joinery — votive for Athena? No. For - ?*

"You want to build a temple," Percy said.

"A model temple," Annabeth corrected. "Small. Bronze and wood. I've been thinking about it since —" She stopped. Since she remembered being seventeen, she meant. Since she remembered a lifetime of sketching temples she would never get to build in this life because her godly parent had changed from the goddess of wisdom and craft to the king of the sky. She didn't say it out loud. At breakfast, with the Apollo table singing over oatmeal three tables away, you didn't say stuff like that. "I've just been thinking about it. I want to make something with my hands that isn't a weapon or a trap. Something that's just... beautiful. And precise."

Percy looked at the drawing, then at her. Her hair was pulled back in its usual ponytail, but a few curls had escaped and stuck to her neck in the warming morning air. Her gray eyes — still gray, even though she was Zeus's daughter now, storm-gray instead of Athena-silver-gray, though Percy was probably the only person who noticed the difference — were bright in a way they only got when she talked about buildings.

"Okay," he said.

Annabeth blinked. "Okay?"

"Yeah. Okay, I'll help. Where do we build a bronze temple?"

"The forge, obviously." Annabeth rolled the paper back up and tapped him on the forehead with it. "Finish your toast, Seaweed Brain. We need to get there before the Hephaestus cabin gets busy with armor repairs."

Percy groaned, but he finished his toast. He'd follow Annabeth into a forge on a hot morning. He'd follow her pretty much anywhere. The remembering-everything-from-another-life thing had only made that more certain, if he was honest. His younger body still blushed over every grin from her; behind that blush was the love they had already chosen and the memory of everything they had survived together, deeper and stranger than anyone watching breakfast could guess.

He grabbed a second piece of toast for the road.

---

The Hephaestus forge sat in a hollow on the western edge of camp, half sunk into the hillside so the heat wouldn't set the forest on fire. Even from twenty yards away, Percy could feel it rolling out the open double doors like a wave. Inside, it was all orange glow and clang and the smell of coal and hot metal and oil.

Percy loved the ocean. He loved lakes and rivers and rain and even swimming pools. He did not love standing inside what felt like a giant pizza oven.

Annabeth, on the other hand, walked in like she was coming home.

"Oh, it's hot," she said happily, breathing in. "Perfect. Beckendorf? You here?"

A tall boy with dark skin and hands the size of dinner plates looked up from an anvil near the back. Charles Beckendorf, fifteen, head counselor of the Hephaestus cabin, a horseshoe pinched in tongs in one hand and a hammer in the other. He grinned when he saw them.

"If it isn't my favorite theoretical architect and her very reluctant assistant," he said. "What do you need?"

"Forge time," Annabeth said. "And advice. I want to build a frame for a model temple. Bronze. Small stock, maybe quarter-inch square? I need to do a lot of tiny right-angle joins and some decorative work on the pediment."

Beckendorf raised an eyebrow. "You know how to braze?"

"I know the theory," Annabeth said, which in Annabeth-speak meant she'd read three books about it and watched once from a distance. "I was hoping you'd supervise."

"Supervise, sure. You know the rules — no one works alone, and if you don't know a tool, you ask." He glanced at Percy. "You ever done metalwork, Jackson?"

Percy held up his hands. "I once bent a fork trying to look cool. Does that count?"

"It does not count," Beckendorf said solemnly. "You can be on quench duty. You're a Poseidon kid, right? You can do the water thing?"

"I can do the water thing," Percy said, relieved. Water he understood.

"Good. Quench tank's over there. Keep it full and keep it clean. And don't —" Beckendorf pointed a thick finger at him. "— don't touch anything glowing. Everything in here is hotter than it looks."

"Got it. Don't touch the glowy death things."

Annabeth was already pulling out her sketchbook — a different one from the graph paper, a battered notebook full of notes — and spreading it on a workbench that Beckendorf cleared for her with one sweep of his arm. She started copying measurements, biting her lip, pencil moving fast.

Percy watched her for a second. She did this thing when she drew where her whole body went still except her hand, like the rest of the world went quiet. He remembered her doing it at seventeen, hunched over maps in the Athena cabin (which wasn't her cabin anymore), and at twelve, hunched over the same kind of notebook on the Big House porch. Same girl. Different life. Same hand.

"You're staring," said a familiar voice behind him.

Percy jumped. Clarisse La Rue stood in the doorway, spear over her shoulder, eyebrow raised. At thirteen, Clarisse was already taller than Percy by an inch, with her father's dark hair cut short and her eyes narrowed in permanent challenge. She wore her CAMP HALF-BLOOD shirt with the sleeves ripped off and a leather bracer on her right forearm.

"I'm hydrating," Percy said, lifting his water bottle. "With my eyes. Shut up."

Clarisse snorted and walked in, heat not bothering her at all. Of course not. She was Poseidon's daughter too, but she'd grown up coming to the forge to get her weapons fixed. "Beckendorf! My spearhead's chipped again. That stupid Ares kid — Sherman — keeps aiming for the shaft instead of blocking like he's supposed to."

"Let me see," Beckendorf said, taking the spear. He examined the leaf-shaped head, running his thumb near the edge. "Yeah, that's a roll, not a chip. I can grind it, but honestly you're due for a new head. This one's getting thin."

Clarisse's face lit up in a way it never did for anything except fighting and, Percy suspected, secretly, her siblings. "Can I make it? Myself? I've watched you like six times."

Beckendorf considered. "You know the safety stuff?"

"Gloves, glasses, apron, tongs, quench, don't breathe the weird smoke," Clarisse recited.

"Okay. I'll set you up at forge three. Small stock, we'll do a simple leaf. It'll take you most of the morning."

"Yes!" Clarisse pumped her fist. She caught Percy looking and scowled. "What?"

"Nothing. Just never seen you excited about arts and crafts."

"It's not arts and crafts, it's a spearhead, Prissy. There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Yes. One stabs people."

Behind them, Annabeth laughed without looking up from her sketch. Clarisse flipped Percy off with the hand not holding her spear shaft, but she was smiling.

The doors banged open again, letting in a blast of relatively cool morning air. Silena Beauregard slipped in, fourteen and effortlessly graceful even in denim shorts and a tied-off camp shirt, her dark hair in a long braid over one shoulder. She carried a small velvet pouch like it held something precious.

"Please tell me someone can fix a clasp without melting the whole necklace," she announced to the forge at large. "I begged Aphrodite-cabin-style. With eyelashes."

Jake Mason, a lanky Hephaestus kid with soot on his nose, looked up from a grindstone. "What'd you break?"

Silena opened the pouch and tipped out a delicate silver necklace with a small blue bead — sea glass, Percy realized — and a clasp that hung crooked. "It was my mom's. Well, a mortal gift-shop version of something my mom would wear, which makes it even more important, actually. The loop pulled open. I tried to close it with pliers and made it worse."

Jake held out his hand. Silena dropped the necklace into it. He squinted. "Tiny silver work. Yeah, I can solder that. Ten minutes, if you don't mind waiting while I finish this axe?"

"I don't mind," Silena said, fanning herself. "Though how you all stand this heat, I will never know. My mascara is going to migrate south for the winter."

"You get used to it," Beckendorf said. "Or you pass out and someone drags you outside. Either way you learn."

"I prefer option one," Silena said, settling onto a stool near Annabeth's bench. "What are you making, Annabeth?"

Annabeth turned her sketchbook so Silena could see. "Model temple. Bronze frame, wood infill. Like a practice piece. I want to test if I can get the proportions right at small scale."

Silena leaned in, eyes wide. "That's gorgeous. You drew that?"

"This morning. It's rough. The column spacing is off on the north side — I need to recalculate the corner contraction." Annabeth's pencil was already moving, fixing a line. "Doric temples cheat the corner columns closer together so the triglyphs line up. If I don't get that right at scale, it'll look wrong even if people can't say why."

Silena blinked. "You lost me at Doric, but I love watching you do this. You get this little crease right here." She tapped her own forehead between her eyebrows. "It's cute."

"It's concentration," Annabeth said, but she smiled.

Percy, filling the quench tank with a hose and then smoothing the water with his hand — coaxing it cooler, cleaner, the way he could coax the sea — watched the two girls with their heads bent over the sketch. Silena was Poseidon too, his half-sister, and she'd adopted Annabeth the way she adopted everyone, with total warmth and zero hesitation. It did something tight and grateful in Percy's chest to see it. Annabeth had lost so much when she went from Athena to Zeus — her cabin, her claimed siblings, her neat category. But she'd gained Thalia, and she'd kept this: Silena braiding her hair before capture the flag, Clarisse saving her a seat at the campfire even while insulting her.

Family reshuffled. Not lost.

"Quench tank's ready," Percy announced. "Pristine, cool, delightful. Come quench your things in my delightful water."

"No one is quenching anything in your delightful water yet," Beckendorf said. "Clarisse, apron on. Annabeth, let me find you some stock. Percy — stay by the tank and look useful."

"I can look useful," Percy said. "I'm excellent at looking useful."

---

Annabeth loved the forge the way some people loved libraries.

Which sounded insane, she knew, because it was loud and hot and smelled like sulfur and sweat, and the floor was gritty with scale and the air shimmered over the forges. But it was also a place where things were made on purpose. Where you started with a drawing and ended with an object that hadn't existed before. Where measurements mattered and patience mattered and if you did it right, the thing held.

It reminded her of the best parts of building in the life she remembered, from twelve and Athena's to seventeen and still drawing. Those years were hers, even in this younger Zeus-born body.

Beckendorf set her up at a side bench with a small vise, a jeweler's saw, files, a butane torch for brazing, and a pile of quarter-inch bronze square stock cut into rough lengths. He showed her how to light the torch, how to adjust for a neutral flame, how to flux a joint and feed the brazing rod.

"You're not welding," he explained. "Bronze this thin, you'd blow through. You're brazing — you're melting a filler metal into the joint, not the base metal. Think of it like hot glue, except it's metal and it's six hundred degrees."

Annabeth nodded, taking mental notes in triplicate. Hot glue, but metal. Capillary action draws the filler in. Clean the joint first. Heat the metal, not the rod.

She clamped two pieces in the vise at a right angle, fluxed them, lit the torch with a striker. Her hands shook a little at first — fire was fire, and she was Zeus's daughter now, lightning in her blood, not hearth-fire — but then muscle memory kicked in. Not her twelve-year-old muscle memory. Her seventeen-year-old memory. Long nights in Bunker 9 with Leo, holding a flashlight while he soldered wires, handing him the right fitting before he asked. Watching him braze a bronze plate and explaining, once, that Athena kids knew structures, and structures were structures whether they were stone or circuitry.

*Structures are structures.* She breathed in, breathed out, and touched the flame to the joint.

The bronze heated, dulled, then glowed faintly. She touched the rod to the edge. It flowed — actually flowed, silver-bright, sucked into the seam like water into a sponge. She pulled the torch back, heart hammering.

"Ha!" she said out loud.

Beckendorf leaned over. "Let it cool. Then try to break it."

Annabeth waited ten seconds, then grabbed the L-shape with pliers and tugged. It held.

"It held," she breathed.

"Good joint," Beckendorf said, approving. "You've got steady hands. Most first-timers glob it on. You used just enough. Do twenty more of those and you'll have your base frame."

Twenty more. Annabeth looked at her drawing — the stylobate frame, the rectangle that would support six by thirteen columns at scale — and felt the familiar click in her chest when a plan became real work. She pulled her stool up, adjusted her safety glasses, and started measuring.

Cut. File. Flux. Clamp. Heat. Feed. Cool. Test.

Cut. File. Flux. Clamp. Heat. Feed. Cool. Test.

The rhythm settled over her like a song. The forge noise faded to a background roar. Someone — Jake — was grinding something, throwing sparks in a bright fan. Clarisse was pumping the bellows at forge three, cursing cheerfully when she overdid it. Silena was telling a story about the Aphrodite cabin's latest drama, all hand gestures. Percy was by the quench tank, trailing his fingers in the water and making little swirls rise up like tiny waterspouts to entertain a bored-looking Hephaestus kid.

Annabeth smiled to herself and kept working.

The third joint was crooked. The fifth joint had too much filler and looked lumpy. The seventh was perfect. By the tenth, her shoulders ached and sweat ran down her spine, but she had a neat pile of brass-colored L's and T's cooling on a firebrick, and the base rectangle was starting to take shape in the vise.

She stopped to sketch the pediment detail — how to suggest triglyphs and metopes at 1:50 scale without the whole thing turning to mush. Tiny strips? Engraving? She drew three options, chewing her pencil.

"Whatcha doing?"

Annabeth looked up. Thalia stood in the doorway, arms crossed, nose wrinkled at the heat. At sixteen, Thalia Grace was all long limbs and black hair spiked with — today — a blue streak, a silver bracelet with little lightning bolts on her wrist, and an expression that said she'd rather be anywhere else. Behind her, through the open doors, blue sky and green trees looked impossibly cool and clean.

"Thalia!" Annabeth said, surprised and pleased. "What are you doing here?"

"Looking for you. Zeus table missed you at breakfast. Again." Thalia stepped inside, then immediately stepped back toward the draft. "Gods, it's like Hades' armpit in here. How are you all not dead?"

"We're thriving," Clarisse said from forge three, face smudged. "Come stir my charcoal, Grace. It'll put hair on your chest."

"I have enough hair on my chest, thanks," Thalia said dryly. She came over to Annabeth's bench, peering at the sketchbook. "What's all this?"

"Model temple," Annabeth said. "Bronze frame. I'm doing the stylobate now."

Thalia picked up one of the cooled L-joints, turning it over. "You made this?"

"Just now. Beckendorf showed me how to braze."

"Huh." Thalia set it down carefully. "It's good. Clean."

Annabeth felt warmth that had nothing to do with the forge. Praise from Thalia was rare and therefore valuable. Thalia was her full sister here — same father, Zeus, same mother, Beryl, same electric buzz under the skin when storms gathered — but Annabeth was still learning the childhood her sister knew. Thalia had spent years raising her, then found herself beside a twelve-year-old who remembered being seventeen in another life. It was awkward sometimes. Thalia covered awkward with punk-rock standoffishness.

"Thanks," Annabeth said. "You want to try? I have extra stock."

Thalia held up both hands. "Nope. I hate forges. Hate heat, hate hammers, hate getting burned. I'm a night-air-and-open-sky kind of demigod. You know that."

"You love campfires," Silena pointed out sweetly from her stool.

"Campfires are different. Campfires have marshmallows and songs and no one expects me to make anything."

"Just hold this while I flux it?" Annabeth wheedled, holding up two pieces. "Please? I need a third hand and Percy will just make finger puppets with the water again."

"Hey!" Percy said from the quench tank. "My water puppets are excellent. Tell her, Jake."

"They're okay," Jake said.

Thalia sighed the long-suffering sigh of older sisters everywhere, pulled up a stool, and held the pieces. "Fine. But I'm not putting on an apron. I have a reputation."

"You have soot on your nose already," Annabeth told her.

"That's punk," Thalia said. "It's intentional."

Annabeth fluxed the joint, clamped it, lit the torch. Thalia held steady, watching the flame with narrowed eyes. Annabeth noticed her sister's hand didn't shake at all, even that close to heat.

"You're good at this," Annabeth said.

"I'm good at holding still and looking annoyed," Thalia said. "It's a gift."

"No, really. Steady. Like —" Annabeth paused, torch in hand. Like a builder, she wanted to say. Like someone who could make things if she decided to. But Thalia had spent so long defining herself as the runaway, the fighter, the storm, not the maker. Annabeth understood. She'd spent a whole lifetime defining herself as Athena's, the wise girl, the architect, and then woken up at twelve as Zeus's, all static and sky and power with nowhere to put it.

She touched the flame to the joint, fed the rod, watched it flow. Perfect.

"See?" she said, lifting the torch. "We make a good team."

Thalia examined the joint, then Annabeth's face, then the sketchbook full of temples. Something soft crossed her expression, there and gone. "Yeah. Okay. Give me another one."

---

Clarisse had been looking forward to hitting Sherman all week, but making a spearhead turned out to be almost as good.

Almost. Nothing was as good as hitting Sherman.

Beckendorf set her up with a chunk of steel stock, tongs, gloves that went up to her elbows, and a lecture about not rushing. Clarisse listened with her arms crossed and her jaw set, which was how she listened to everything, but inside she was buzzing. Her old spearhead had been made by someone else — good work, Hephaestus-cabin standard, but not hers. This one would be hers. From a bar of dumb metal to a point that could punch through a drakon's hide.

"Heat it to cherry red," Beckendorf said, "not orange, not yellow. Cherry. Then bring it to the anvil and draw out the point. Small hits. Let the hammer do the work."

Clarisse thrust the steel into the coke forge, working the bellows the way he'd shown her. The metal went dull gray, then faint red, then brighter. She watched like a hawk. Cherry red. That was — there. She yanked it out with tongs, carried it to the anvil, and started hammering.

Clang. Clang. Clang.

It was loud and it jarred her arms and it was fantastic.

"Smaller hits!" Beckendorf called over the noise. "You're trying to persuade it, not murder it!"

"I can do both!" Clarisse shouted back, but she lightened up. Tap-tap-tap, turning the stock a quarter-turn each time, drawing the end into a taper. The steel moved under the hammer like clay, which was wild if you thought about it. Hard thing becomes soft when it's hot. Lesson there, probably. Silena would know what it meant.

She reheated, hammered, reheated, hammered. Sweat poured down her face. Her arms burned. A blister rose on her thumb inside the glove. She didn't care. Each time the shape got closer — a diamond cross-section, a leaf widening then narrowing to a point. Beckendorf came over to check, nodded, showed her how to use the edge of the anvil to define the shoulders.

"Good," he said. "Now normalize it — heat it up and let it air-cool, twice, to relieve stress — then we'll harden and temper."

Clarisse did what he said, though waiting for metal to cool when you wanted to keep hitting it tested every bit of patience she owned. While the blade air-cooled, she watched the rest of the forge.

Annabeth and Thalia bent over the little bronze frame, heads almost touching, arguing cheerfully about column spacing. Silena had moved to Percy's quench tank, dangling her fingers in the water and sighing at how cool it was. Percy was making a tiny whirlpool for her, showing off, and Silena was laughing. Jake finished the axe and took Silena's necklace to a tiny bench with a magnifier, soldering the clasp with a pencil torch while Silena hovered and gave instructions about not scratching the bead.

It was — nice. Clarisse didn't use words like cozy or domestic, because she was Clarisse, but the forge on a warm morning with her siblings and the Hephaestus kids and the clang and the heat, everyone making something — it settled in her chest like a good meal.

She was Poseidon's daughter. Percy had told her she had been Ares's daughter in the other life he and Annabeth remembered: different spear, different cabin, a father who praised rage. Clarisse had no flashes of that history, no other childhood waiting behind this one. She knew her own father, and she liked the sea's lesson better than the one in Percy's account. The sea didn't ask her to be angry all the time. The sea could be calm, too. The sea could hold you up.

"Clarisse! Quench!" Beckendorf said.

She grabbed the hardened blade — heated to critical, non-magnetic, Beckendorf had tested it with a magnet on a stick like a magic trick — and plunged it into Percy's tank.

Hssssssss. Steam billowed. Percy swirled the water around the steel with his hand, eyes focused, coaxing an even cool. Clarisse watched the water move — not random, deliberate, hugging the blade.

"Even quench," Beckendorf said, impressed. "Nice work, Percy. No soft spots that way."

Percy grinned. "Water's my thing. Metal's scary. Water's friendly."

"Water can drown you," Clarisse said.

"Not me," Percy said. "I'm special."

"You're special, all right," Clarisse said, but she bumped his shoulder with hers. Her blade came out dark and hard, and Beckendorf showed her how to temper it in the oven for an hour, then grind the edge.

Clarisse held the blackened, rough spearhead in her tongs and thought it was the most beautiful thing she'd ever seen. It wasn't finished, but it was hers.

"Good?" Percy asked.

"Good," Clarisse said. She didn't say thank you for the quench, because she was Clarisse, but she nodded at him, and he nodded back, and that was the same thing.

---

Silena had no business being in a forge.

She knew this. The forge knew this. Her hair knew this — it kept trying to escape its braid and stick to her neck. Her skin knew this — it glistened in a way that was cute on romance-novel covers and less cute when soot stuck to it. Her entire carefully maintained aesthetic screamed for sea breezes and shade.

But Silena was also Poseidon's daughter in this life, which meant she was stubborn about staying where the people she loved were, even if the location was actively trying to broil her.

And she loved watching people make things. She loved Annabeth's furrowed brow, Clarisse's hammering joy, Percy's quiet pride in his quench tank, Jake's careful hands on her necklace. She loved Thalia pretending not to care while holding Annabeth's pieces perfectly steady.

Jake Mason peered through his magnifier, tiny torch hissing. "Almost — got it. Hold still, pretty thing."

"Are you talking to me or the necklace?" Silena asked.

"Necklace," Jake said without looking up. "No offense."

"None taken. The necklace is very pretty."

He killed the torch, quenched the jump ring in a tiny cup of water, then held the necklace up. The clasp closed with a clean click. The silver loop was smooth, the blue sea-glass bead glowing in the forge light.

"Oh!" Silena breathed, taking it. She fastened it around her neck, fingers finding the motion easily now. The bead rested cool against her collarbone. "It's perfect. Thank you!"

Jake shrugged, ears pink. "Easy fix. You owe the cabin a favor. Come sweep sometime."

"I will absolutely not sweep," Silena said cheerfully. "But I will bring cookies. Oatmeal chocolate chip. My mom's recipe."

"Deal," Jake said. "Cookies beat sweeping."

Silena turned to show Annabeth. "Look! Fixed!"

Annabeth glanced up, smiled. "Pretty. Is that sea glass?"

"Yeah. Percy found it for me." Silena touched the bead. "Last month at Montauk — well, camp beach, but same idea. He dove down and got it because I said the color matched my eyes. He came up with seaweed on his head and acted like it was a crown."

Percy, overhearing, bowed. "King of Seaweed. At your service."

"You kept it for a week," Clarisse said. "It started to smell."

"It was a good crown," Percy said with dignity.

Silena laughed and fanned herself with a piece of scrap cardboard. Her gaze drifted to Annabeth's work. The base frame was done — a neat bronze rectangle with cross-supports, all joints clean. Now Annabeth was starting the columns: tiny bronze rods, each maybe three inches tall, with little folded collars top and bottom to suggest capitals and bases. Thalia was cutting rod lengths with a tubing cutter while Annabeth filed the ends square.

"You know," Silena said thoughtfully, "I never thought about it before, but temples are like jewelry. For gods. Big jewelry you can walk inside."

Annabeth paused, file mid-stroke. "Huh. Yeah. Actually — that's not wrong. Same idea. Proportion, symmetry, ornament where it means something. A necklace has a focal bead, a temple has a cult statue. Both frame something precious."

"See? I'm architectural." Silena struck a pose. "Hire me."

"You're hired," Annabeth said. "As design consultant. Tell me if this looks right."

She set six tiny columns upright in a row on the base, holding them with bits of clay to mock up the facade. Even unfinished, held with clay and hope, it looked like something. A promise of a temple.

Silena tilted her head. "It's lovely. But —" She pointed. "That one in the middle looks lonely? Or the spacing feels wide? I don't know the words, I just know what my eyes like."

Annabeth's eyes lit up. "No, that's exactly right! The spacing is too wide — I did regular spacing, but Doric needs the corners pulled in, which makes the middle look wide by comparison if you don't adjust the whole rhythm. Hold on." She grabbed her pencil, recalculated, marked new positions. "Silena, you're a genius."

"I am a genius," Silena agreed modestly. "A hot, sweaty genius who needs lemonade."

"I'll get you lemonade," Percy said immediately. "Quench-master also does beverage runs. It's in the job description."

"Bring the whole pitcher," Thalia said, not looking up from the tubing cutter. "And cups. Paper, not bronze. I don't trust you with hot metal cups."

Percy saluted and jogged out into the blinding sunlight, happy to escape the heat for a minute. Silena watched him go, fondness swelling. Her little brother — half-brother, technically, but little brother in every way that counted. Twelve years old and already trying to take care of everyone with water and jokes.

She turned back. Thalia had cut all the columns and was now, despite her claimed hatred of forges, filing the ends with surprising care, blowing off the filings, checking each against a steel rule.

"You like it," Silena observed quietly, sitting beside her.

Thalia shrugged. "It's okay. Better than sword-sharpening. At least this is for something pretty."

"You'd be good at making things," Silena said. "If you wanted."

Thalia was quiet for a second, file whispering. "My dad's the sky. Everything I do is — fast. Lightning. Wind. Blow in, blow up, blow out. Making things is slow. I don't know if I'm built for slow."

"You're sitting in a forge filing bronze to within a sixty-fourth of an inch," Silena pointed out. "I'd say you're built for whatever you decide."

Thalia glanced at her, then smiled crookedly. "When did you get wise, Beauregard?"

"I'm always wise. I just hide it under excellent hair."

---

By noon, the forge was an inferno and Annabeth was in heaven.

The base frame was brazed solid. Twelve columns stood in clay mock-up, soon to be fixed permanently. She'd cut the architrave strips — the beams that would sit on top of the columns — and was fitting them one by one. Thalia held, Annabeth heated, the filler flowed. They'd developed a rhythm: flux, clamp, heat, feed, cool, check. Flux, clamp, heat, feed, cool, check.

Percy returned with lemonade and stayed as general assistant — fetching, holding, quenching each small piece in his tank with a tenderness that made Beckendorf nod approvingly. Even tiny bronze pieces could warp if quenched unevenly, and Percy's water never wobbled.

"How do you do that?" Annabeth asked him once, watching him swirl water around a red-hot bracket without touching it.

Percy shrugged. "I just — ask it to be even. Like, hey water, be cool all over. And it listens. It's easier than big stuff. Big waves have opinions. This is just a bucket."

"Everything has opinions if you listen," Annabeth said, half-joking, half-serious. She remembered Percy holding back the Hudson, raising hurricanes, talking to horses. Of course he could talk a bucket into behaving.

She fitted the last architrave strip, brazed it, set the whole colonnade aside to cool. Then she pulled out the pediment sketch — the triangular gable that would crown the front. This was the decorative part, the part she'd been saving. She wanted to suggest sculpture inside the triangle: not full figures, too small, but shapes. An owl, maybe. Athena's owl. Or —

She paused, pencil hovering.

Athena's owl. But she wasn't Athena's daughter. Not anymore. She was Zeus's. Did she still get to make owls? Did she still get to love marble temples and olive trees and weaving patterns? Or was she supposed to love eagles and oak and lightning now?

Thalia, watching her hesitate, nudged her. "What?"

"Nothing. Just — deciding on the pediment. I was going to do an owl."

"So do an owl."

"But —" Annabeth gestured vaguely. "Zeus. Shouldn't it be an eagle or a thunderbolt or something? I feel like I'm — clinging. To Athena stuff. When I'm not hers."

Thalia was quiet for a moment, turning a bronze offcut over in her fingers. The forge roared around them. Someone laughed — Clarisse, at something Jake said. Water hissed.

"Annabeth," Thalia said at last. "You remember being seventeen, right? You told me about it. You remember who you were."

"Yeah."

"Who was your mom then?"

"Athena." Annabeth's throat tightened. "She was — she was my mom. She gave me the cap and the laptop and the — she argued with me and pushed me and I spent my whole life trying to make her proud."

"And who's your dad now?"

"Zeus." Annabeth glanced at Thalia, her sister, her proof. "Him."

"Okay. So you had a mom who taught you to build, and you have a dad who — what? Throws lightning and broods on a throne?"

Annabeth laughed wetly. "Pretty much."

"So build something that can survive lightning." Thalia tapped the little temple frame. "That's what you do, right? You're the builder. It doesn't matter which god gave you the brains. You still have them. You still love this." She gestured at the sketchbook, the bronze, the whole sunlit-hot mess. "Dad gave you power. You brought the craft you learned in that other life. I don't know who changed our history, but they didn't take that knowledge from you. Use both. Make an owl that could sit through a thunderstorm."

Annabeth stared at her. Then she grabbed her pencil and drew — fast, sure — an owl with wings spread, not perched and sleepy but mantling, defiant, feathers etched as bold lines, eyes wide. Under it she wrote: *bronze plate, pierced work — saw + files — owl in storm.*

"An owl in a thunderstorm," she whispered. "Yeah."

She found a thin bronze plate in the scrap bin — Beckendorf waved her permission — and transferred the owl sketch with a scribe. Then she drilled a starter hole, threaded a jeweler's saw blade through, and started sawing.

It was slow work. Painstaking. The blade broke twice. Her eyes watered from concentration. Thalia held the plate steady without being asked, turning it when Annabeth needed. Percy brought her cold water for her wrists. Silena leaned over her shoulder, murmuring encouragement. Clarisse, waiting for her temper cycle, came over to watch and admitted, grudgingly, that it was "pretty cool for non-stabby art."

Annabeth sawed, filed, smoothed. The owl emerged: round body, fierce eyes (two tiny punched dots), wings spread wide, each feather a filed notch. She domed it slightly with a dapping punch, giving it dimension, then brazed it to a backing plate that would sit in the pediment triangle.

When she held it up, bronze still warm, the forge light caught the feather notches and made them glow.

"Oh," Silena said softly. "Oh, that's lovely."

Thalia whistled low. "Okay, architect. That's really good."

Percy leaned in close, gray-green eyes reflecting orange. "It's you," he said quietly, so only she heard. "Builder and storm. Both."

Annabeth's chest went tight and bright at once. She remembered seventeen, standing on Olympus while gods argued over a map, trying to make a plan they would actually listen to. Athena's craft had been hers then. Zeus's charge was in her blood here. That didn't make him a second parent in the old life, or take the builder out of this one. When she brazed, the heat and the patient join belonged to the same work. She could put an owl on her own temple. She could make room for a hidden lightning bolt too.

"Help me put it together?" she asked, voice rough.

They all helped. Beckendorf supervised the final brazing — architraves to columns, pediment frames to architraves, owl plate set into the front triangle like a jewel. Percy quenched each subassembly with infinite care, water sighing into steam. Silena polished the cooled bronze with a soft cloth and paste until it shone. Clarisse, of all people, used a tiny punch to add egg-and-dart suggestion to the base trim — "My mom — my mortal mom — likes patterns," she muttered, ears red. "Shut up." Thalia wired a tiny lightning bolt — copper wire, twisted — into the back pediment, hidden, "for structural luck."

Late afternoon sun slanted through the forge doors when they finished. Annabeth set the model temple on the workbench: two feet long, eight inches tall, bronze gleaming, twelve columns straight, owl fierce in the gable.

It wasn't perfect. One corner joint was a little lumpy. A column leaned a fraction. The owl's left eye was marginally bigger than the right. Annabeth catalogued every flaw instantly.

It was perfect.

The forge crew gathered around. Beckendorf nodded slowly. "Good proportions. Solid joints. Nice decorative work. You should be proud."

Jake said, "Can I put a tiny anvil inside the cella? For scale?"

"No," Annabeth and Beckendorf said together.

Silena draped an arm over Annabeth's shoulders, both of them sweaty and soot-streaked. "It's beautiful, sweetie. Truly. Where will you put it?"

Annabeth thought. A votive. An offering. To whom? To Athena, who taught her? To Zeus, who claimed her? To both? To the idea that a girl could love buildings more than battles and still belong?

"My cabin," she said at last. "Zeus cabin. On the windowsill. So morning light hits the owl."

Thalia smiled — real, unguarded. "Good. Dad'll like it. He likes shiny things that survive storms."

Percy picked up the temple with both hands, careful, and held it to the light. Bronze glowed honey-gold. The owl threw a tiny shadow. "It survived us," he said. "That's basically a thunderstorm."

"Hey!" Clarisse said. "I'm delicate."

"You hammered steel for three hours," Silena said.

"I hammered delicately."

They laughed, tired and happy and heat-drunk. Beckendorf shooed them out — "Go eat dinner, you all smell like coal" — but not before pressing a small bronze owl offcut into Annabeth's hand. "Practice piece. You filed that notch clean. Keep it."

Annabeth closed her fingers around the warm metal. Outside, the evening air felt cool as spring water after the forge. Crickets sang. The camp smelled like pines and dinner from the pavilion — pizza, tonight, praise the gods.

They walked up the hill together, five abreast where the path allowed: Clarisse swinging her new spear shaft (head tempering in the oven, to be ground tomorrow), Silena touching her fixed necklace, Thalia with soot on her nose and bronze dust on her hands, Percy carrying the temple like a holy relic, Annabeth beside him with sketches and sweat and satisfaction humming under her skin.

"Next time," Percy said, "we build something with water. A fountain. A nice, cool, non-fire fountain."

"Next time," Annabeth agreed, bumping his shoulder, "you can braze and I'll do the quenching."

"You'd flood the forge."

"I would not flood the forge. I'd mist it. Strategically."

"Strategic flooding," Thalia said. "Very Zeus. I'm proud."

Annabeth laughed, tilted her face to the evening sky — blue fading to gold, first stars pricking through, clouds piled like marble blocks on the horizon — and felt the two halves of herself settle, builder and storm, craft and power, memory and present, into something that held.

Like a good joint. Heated, joined, cooled. Stronger together than apart.

Behind them, the forge glowed on, orange and steady, making and mending for the whole camp. Ahead, dinner waited. The little bronze owl rode in Percy's hands, wings spread, ready for any weather.
