## Chapter 150: What Keeps

Percy woke up because Clarisse had made her bed.

Not the kind of made bed that happened when a harpy with a grudge came through on inspection day — blankets hauled up, pillow flung on top, the whole arrangement held together by spite and the knowledge that nobody would look too closely. This was a made bed in the way that meant someone had stood over it and made a decision. Corners squared. Blanket smoothed flat enough to bounce a coin. Pillow fluffed and set down at a right angle to the wall.

Clarisse La Rue did not make decisions about pillows. Clarisse made decisions about people, and about where people sat, and about how long they were allowed to stay there.

Percy lay in his bunk and looked at it.

The Poseidon cabin was doing its before-dawn thing. Greenish light came through the window in long slants that had not yet cleared the trees, and the shells on the sill — a hundred of them, a decade of them, ocean shells in a lake cabin, because that was the joke and also because it wasn't a joke — threw their small shadows across the floor. The air smelled the way the cabin always smelled: wet wool, green growing things, the clean mineral smell of a lake that had sat in the dark all night.

Six bunks. Three people. His, Silena's — empty, blankets kicked down to her knees, a paperback splayed face-down on the mattress — and Clarisse's, made.

He got up, the floor cold enough that he stood on the balls of his feet, and dressed out of habit in the dark: the faded orange camp shirt with the frayed collar that had been his since his first week and had shrunk in the wash and now stopped above his wrist; the shorts with the rust-colored stain on the thigh from the west shore wall repair; the socks, one of which had a hole in the heel he had been meaning to fix for three weeks. He stuffed his pockets out of the other habit: a length of twine, a bent nail he had pulled from the wall for reasons he had never been able to explain, a smooth grey stone the lake had given him in April.

The camp at this hour was the kind of empty that made you check your own pulse. Not silent — the crickets were still going, the lake was still moving, something was still thrashing around in the underbrush with the commitment of a creature that did not own a clock. But the paths were empty, the pavilion was dark with its benches up on the tables, and the cabins were shut and sleeping, except for one thin curl of smoke from the Hephaestus forge where somebody had stayed up late being clever and was going to be unbearable about it by noon.

Percy walked down to the lake. The water was going from black to grey to the faintest possible blue, still holding the stars like it had not yet decided whether to give them back. The dock stretched out in front of him, planks silvered with dew, and out past the willow cove the surface lay flat as a held breath.

He sat down at the wrong end.

That was the first thing he noticed about the new arrangement. He had walked the whole dock without thinking about it and sat at the end nobody used — the end closest to the reeds, where the fishing tackle was tied off in a snarl nobody had ever dared to separate — and it had not felt like anything. It had felt like sitting. For most of the summer that walk had been a decision and the sitting had been a position, and both of those things had been load-bearing. Now they were just a walk and a sit.

He put his heels on the plank beside him and let the cold get into them. Then he looked at the cove.

The willows were doing their thing — the trailing branches touching the water, the shade that sat deeper in August than it had any right to, the small green tunnel you had to duck under to reach the skiff. The skiff was still there. Sunken, or half-sunken, or resting, depending on how honest a person wanted to be: a wooden hull gone soft and grey, tilted on its side. And on the bow, in the exact place it had been for weeks, stood the heron.

Grey as the skiff. Still as the skiff. One leg drawn up under its body, the other planted on the wood like it had been appointed.

"You still here?" Percy said.

The heron looked at him with the flat yellow patience of a bird that has never once in its life been in a hurry.

"Yeah," Percy said. "Me too."

He had been sitting for maybe ten minutes when he heard the gravel — fast steps, then a performed pause, then fast steps again, and then a voice off the path saying "ow" to nobody in particular.

Then Silena Beauregard arrived at the bottom of the dock carrying a wooden crate in both arms, and in the crate, bedded in a damp cloth, were strawberries. A great many strawberries.

Percy stood up. "Where did you get those?"

Silena was wearing her hair in the lopsided knot she always slept in, and an oversized purple hoodie — Aphrodite cabin, borrowed from somebody two sizes bigger — that had swallowed her arms to the middle. One sleeve was pushed up to the elbow; the other was soaked to the shoulder and smelled like dish soap. She set the crate down with the care of someone delivering an organ.

"The Demeter garden," she said.

"Whose Demeter garden?"

"Nobody uses the north beds. They planted three rows in May and then discovered they don't like picking them, because picking strawberries means kneeling in dirt for an hour, and the north beds are the wet dirt. The drainage thing."

"The drainage problem," Percy said.

"Which Annabeth drew, which Olive found, and which is now a whole thing with a culvert." Silena waved a hand toward the north path, which was not visible from the dock. "So the strawberries are sitting in the damp getting old, and getting old means rotting, and I am not going to stand here and watch food rot."

"You stole them."

"I redistributed them. Which is different. Redistribution has a policy."

"It has a hat."

"I used my hat." She said this with total seriousness, which was the thing about Silena — she could deliver the most deranged sentence in a completely level voice and it took you four seconds to catch up. "The brim holds about forty. The rest I carried in my shirt, which I do not recommend, and which is why my sleeve is wet, and which I will be explaining to nobody, because nobody ever asks."

Percy took one. It was cold from the morning and it tasted like dirt and sun and the particular sugar that only exists in a fruit for about two weeks in August.

"These are good," he said.

"They're excellent, and they are rotting on the vine, and every single one you eat is a small act of justice." She sat down on the edge of the dock, hooking her knees up, and looked at him with the particular warmth that meant she was about to say something true. "You're alone."

"I'm not alone. You're here."

"Before that."

Percy munched. "Before that I was alone."

"And it was fine."

Silena dug in the crate and turned three strawberries over before finding the one she considered acceptable — a quality-control thing Percy had watched her perform with grapes, crackers, and once, memorably, an entire pie.

"They're not coming," Percy said.

"Nobody said a time. That's the part I keep not being able to get over. There's no time."

Silena chewed and looked out at the lake. From down here the water was going silver in the middle, the way it did when the sun came up behind the pines on the far shore and laid a road of light across it.

"I keep waiting to feel relieved," she said. "That's the embarrassing part. I thought this morning I'd wake up and feel — I don't know. Fixed. All week I thought, if we just say the thing out loud, then the thing is said, and then it's repaired, and then I'll know where I sit." She pulled her knees tighter. "And then I woke up and the bed was a bed and the dock was a dock and I still didn't know where I sat. I just knew I didn't have to."

Percy sat down next to her. The crate of stolen strawberries sat between them like a small confession.

"Where did you sit?" he asked. "This morning. Before you came down here."

"Here." She pointed at the plank she was on. "Not my plank. Not the one I've had since May. This one. The one next to the crate." She looked at it. "It's a worse plank. It's got a splinter."

Percy looked. There was, in fact, a splinter.

"You sat on it anyway," he said.

"I sat on it anyway."

They let that sit. Somewhere up the hill a door banged and a voice shouted something incomprehensible at a goat.

"Clarisse made her bed," Percy said.

Silena's eyebrows went up. "Made it?"

"Made it. Squares. Corners. Pillow fluffed."

"That's either very good or very ominous."

"It's both."

"She's coming down," Silena said. "She'll take the long way up behind us on the gravel path so she can hear what we're saying first. She always does the long way. She just pretends it's the direct route."

Percy looked up the hill. The gravel path was empty, the sun had not cleared the pines, and the whole north slope was still in blue shadow with the mist sitting in it.

"Ten minutes," he said. "Her rule. From before. Ten minutes per occurrence, retroactive. She's going to come down here and bill us."

Silena laughed — a real one, sudden and surprised, the kind that came out of her before she had decided to let it. "Oh, she's going to try."

"She's going to try," Percy agreed, and he could not remember the last time he had been excited about Clarisse coming down a path.

She took the long way. Of course she took the long way.

---

Clarisse had been awake since four, and she had a lot of feelings about it.

Four in the morning was not a civilized hour. It was for people who had somewhere to be and no imagination about how to get there. She lay in her bunk listening to the cabin and made a list of the things she had done wrong in the last twelve hours. It was a long list. She got through item nine before she realized she had made her bed.

That was another item.

She gave up on sleep at five, pulled on the Ares cabin tee that had been a hand-me-down from a girl two years older and had never once fit right in the shoulders, and walked out into the blue dark. She hit the dummies until her shoulders ached and threw the spear until her arm shook, and then she threw it one more time and it stuck in the straw dummy with the flat nutritious sound of a good throw, dead center, thirty feet, no wobble.

"Good," she told the dummy. "Nobody saw it."

The argument was over. That was the thing she kept circling. She had said the things, Thalia had said the things, Percy had said the thing that had turned the whole night sideways, and then they had all gone to bed. And now it was morning, and she had to walk down a hill.

At six she went back to the cabin to wash the sweat off, and did her hair badly — a braid down one side, tight at the top and loose at the bottom, the kind of lopsided job that made people ask if she had been in a fight. She pulled on her camp shorts, grey and sun-faded, with the drawstring that had never once held, and considered the boots and put them on anyway. The left one had worn through at the heel in June and had been letting in lake water ever since, which she had decided not to mention to anyone, because a camp boot was a camp boot, and you wore it until it fell apart, and then you wore the pieces.

Then she went the long way, the way she always did: north, up the back of the hill, down through the strawberry beds behind the Demeter cabin, and along the north path — the worst-drained quarter-mile in camp since before she got here, and now, she had to stop and look at it, a properly cut drainage channel with a stone-lined culvert and a plank walkway laid across the wet part. Good work. Annoyingly good work.

Clarisse kicked a pebble into the culvert and kept walking, and told herself that the lump in her throat was allergies.

She came out onto the planks.

"Clarisse!" Silena said, in the same tone she used for a dog she had not seen for an hour.

"You took the long way," Percy said.

"I took the direct route."

"You took the long way."

"It was a direct route with a detour."

Clarisse sat down at the far end of the dock — the end closest to the water where the planks were oldest and darkest — and put her boots in the exact spot she always put them, which was a mistake, because that was her spot, and putting her boots in her spot on purpose was the opposite of not having a spot.

"You made your bed," Percy said.

"It's a bed. That's what they're for."

"You fluffed the pillow."

"I fluffed all the pillows."

"Clarisse." Percy put one hand over his heart. "I'm so proud of you. Look at her, Silena. Look at what she's become."

"I'm not becoming anything. I'm sitting here." She nodded at the crate. "What's that?"

"Stolen goods," Silena said.

"Excellent." Clarisse took a strawberry, bit into it, and allowed herself a half-second of pleasure before remembering that she was annoyed. "These are good."

"They're excellent, and they're rotting on the vine."

"Where'd you get the crate?"

"I didn't get a crate. I got the strawberries and then I got the crate from the kitchens and then I brought the crate back and then I brought the strawberries here." Silena said all of this at the same speed she said everything, which was fast, with her hands moving, one of them still damp. "It's the crate's day off."

Clarisse chewed. She looked at Percy, who was looking at her with the insufferable expression of a person who had been right about something and was managing, with visible effort, not to mention it.

"Say it," she said.

"I'm not saying anything." He took another strawberry and turned it over in his fingers. "I'm just sitting here. On a plank. In a crate-adjacent position. I'm not saying one word about how right I was."

"You were right."

"I was."

"I hate that," Clarisse said, "more than I have ever hated anything."

"You're going to hate it more later. Thalia hasn't gotten here yet."

Clarisse put her boots on the plank and leaned back on her hands. The sun was properly on her face now and there was a loon somewhere out past the buoys doing its laugh, and she had not slept, and she had a left boot with a hole in it, and she had never in her life been more comfortable on a piece of wood.

She reached into her shorts pocket and pulled out a small waterproof notebook — a field notebook, the kind she had stolen from the Athena supply closet in her first week and kept ever since for tallies of spear scores and arguments won and lost. Since late June it had held the ledger.

She had been building the ledger since the night the argument about the dock had become a thing — not the fight, the arrangement. Who sat where. How long. What it had cost. It had started as a joke and then it had become a rule and then it had become a piece of paper she carried around so that she would never be caught not knowing where she stood.

She flipped it open now. The page was filled in her small blunt handwriting — dates, initials, arrows, a column of numbers that meant minutes. Ten minutes per occurrence. Retroactive. Double for Zeus-cabin accompaniment. She had written the rule at the top of the page in June and underlined it twice because it had been funny then.

She flipped to a clean page. She held the pen over it for a while.

Then she turned back to the ledger page and ripped it out.

Silena, who was watching her face rather than her hands — a Silena specialty — said nothing. Percy said nothing either. The paper made a small dry sound in the quiet. Clarisse folded the torn sheet once, twice, three times, creasing it hard with her thumbnail, and turned it into a boat. A bad one. The corners were wrong and one side was longer than the other and it listed badly, but it was a boat, and she held it out over the water.

"You going to drown it," Percy said.

"I'm going to sail it."

"It's a folded ledger."

"It's a boat now." She set it on the surface.

The lake took it. That was the thing about the canoe lake — it did not argue. It took the boat, and the paper darkened, and the boat turned slowly, badly, listing, drifting a few feet out from the dock. Then it stopped. Then the wind pushed it back. Then it was against the piling, bumping gently, not sinking at all.

"Yeah," she said.

"Not sinking," Percy said. "Lakes give things back. It's different out there."

"I know what it's like out there." But she reached out and picked the wet folded page off the piling and put it in her pocket, and she did not dry it off, and she did not look at either of them while she did it.

Silena handed her another strawberry. She took it.

Thalia did not wake up gradually. Thalia woke up the way weather happens.

One moment the Zeus cabin was dark and quiet and full of eleven sleeping girls, and the next moment Thalia was sitting bolt upright in her bunk with her heart going, the sheets wrapped around her legs, and she could not have said what had woken her, only that something had.

She had learned, over eighteen years, that the only cure for waking up wrong was to put her feet on the ground and move. So she dressed — camp shorts, a Zeus cabin tee with the neck stretched out, the blue hoodie she had stolen from her bunkmate Chris and never returned — and went out into the cold.

The camp at four-thirty was hers. That was one of the things she had not expected when she gave up immortality to come back here. She had expected to be a stranger and possibly a guest, not the owner of an empty camp at four in the morning.

She ran the boundary twice, past the new stone of the west shore wall and the white X on the boundary rock, and by the second lap she had stopped thinking, and by the third she had stopped being a person and was just a heartbeat going like a drum. At the lake she took her shoes off and put her feet in up to the ankles, because the water was cold and she needed the cold, and because she had not stopped being a person yet and was not sure she wanted to. Then she went swimming — a lap, out to the buoys and back, the whole world narrowing down to the sound of her own breathing. When she came out she lay on her back on the dock and let the sun work on her, and she fell asleep like that, which was mortifying, and which she did not notice until a shadow crossed her face.

"Thalia."

She opened one eye. Percy was standing over her.

"How long have you been there?"

"A while. You've been snoring."

"I've been told I sleep attractively." She sat up. Her hair was wet and her hoodie was damp down one side where she had been lying on it. "Are they all here?"

"All four."

"Clarisse?"

"Clarisse. She made her bed."

"That's either good or —"

"Both."

Thalia pulled her knees up and put her arms around them, and then, because she noticed herself doing it, made herself stop. "What's she like?"

"Tired."

She got up. Her knees ached from the run. The hoodie bagged on her and she pulled the drawstring without noticing, which did nothing, because Chris had tied the string in a knot in March and Thalia had never untied it. "Where's Annabeth?"

Percy pointed down the dock, toward the far end, where a girl in a soaked-sleeved hoodie and a boy at the reed end were sitting with a crate of strawberries between them and a pile of wet folded paper on the plank.

"She's not down here yet," Percy said. "She's not down here first today."

Thalia looked at him. Percy looked back and did the thing he did where he waited, and Thalia understood that he was not going to explain, and that the not-explaining was a kind of gift, and that she was supposed to go find out.

She walked down the dock, the planks cold under her bare feet. Silena was on one side with her knees up. Clarisse was on the end, boots on the plank, a waterproof notebook open on her lap, and she did not look up when Thalia arrived, which was either a challenge or a truce, and Thalia had never been able to tell which.

"Hey," Thalia said.

"Hey," Clarisse said, to the notebook.

"Nice braid."

"It's not a braid. It's a hair situation."

"It's a braid that's losing."

Clarisse looked up. Her face did the thing it did when she was deciding between hitting someone and loving them, and Thalia watched the decision happen in real time, watched the shoulders come down a quarter inch.

"You're wet," Clarisse said.

"I went swimming."

"It's cold."

"You're wearing a hoodie in August."

"It's my hoodie." Clarisse closed the notebook. "From Chris."

"Stolen, you mean."

"Borrowed indefinitely."

Thalia sat down next to her — not close, not far, in the particular distance that meant neither of them was conceding anything — and put her cold wet hand on the back of Clarisse's neck.

Clarisse shrieked. It was not a controlled sound. It came out of her at a volume that should not have been possible from a person who had been reading a notebook thirty seconds earlier, and it sent a loon up off the water and made Silena drop a strawberry.

"Don't," Clarisse said, when she could speak.

"You're freezing."

"You're a monster."

"You're warm, that's the problem." Thalia kept her hand there for exactly as long as it took for Clarisse to decide not to remove it, and then removed it herself. "There. Now we're even for the argument."

"We're not even. You lost the argument."

"I conceded the argument. That's a victory."

"That's the same thing."

"It's not the same thing."

"Thalia." Silena had recovered her strawberry. "She's been up since four. She made her bed and she ran the wall and she's in a mood. Don't start."

"I'm not starting. I'm commenting."

"You're starting."

Thalia held up both hands in surrender, then leaned back on them and looked at the sky, which was going from pale to the hard blue of a camp morning with nothing scheduled in it.

"Where's your sister?" she said to Clarisse.

"Which one?"

"Any of them. You've got two."

"Silena's right here and you're sitting next to her, so."

"I mean the other one. The annoying one."

Clarisse's mouth did something. "Annabeth's in the Big House. Fixing the map."

"She's always fixing the map."

"She's the Cartographer," Silena said. "Capital letters. Chiron wrote it on a piece of paper. There's a certificate and everything."

"Of course there's a certificate." Thalia sat up, hugging her knees again and this time not correcting herself for it. "She's got a certificate and I've got a hoodie with a knotted string."

"Is she okay?" Thalia said, quieter.

"Annabeth?"

"Yeah."

Silena and Clarisse exchanged a look — the whole exchange took a quarter of a second and contained a full conversation. Thalia watched it happen and felt something in her chest go tight and then let go, because that was what the look was. That was what it had always been, since March, and she had not known she was allowed to see it.

"She's fine," Clarisse said. "She's Annabeth-fine."

"That's not a real category."

"It's the most real category I've got. She's been here since five in the morning with a girl from her cabin following her around with a notebook." Clarisse paused. "Olive. Who's about nine and takes notes on everything and asked me yesterday how many craters a spear makes."

Thalia grinned. "What'd you tell her?"

"I told her it depends on the spear."

"It doesn't depend on the spear."

"It absolutely depends on the spear. You throw a bad spear it doesn't make a crater at all, it makes a dent, and a dent is worse, because a dent means you tried." Clarisse was warming up now, the way she did, the way she would about anyone's little sister, and Thalia filed that away somewhere she could get at it later. "Annabeth was drawing the whole camp when she was nine. That's because Annabeth is insufferable."

"Yeah," Thalia said. "She is."

It should have been sharp. It came out soft, and both of them heard it come out soft, and neither of them said anything about it, and that was the whole difference, and Thalia thought: oh. This is what it's like when it isn't a thing you have to win.

Annabeth made schedules for mornings.

She had been doing it since she was small — a discipline she had developed the way other children developed a fear of the dark. If the morning had a shape, the morning could not surprise her. She had, at that moment, a list in her own small square handwriting on a fresh page, and it said:

NORTH PATH — 0500, with O.
RETURN SATCHEL — 0600
LAKE — 0630, DOCK, ALL FIVE
BREAKFAST — 0730
DRAWING — 0900–1200

She had written ALL FIVE in capital letters and drawn a box around it, and the box was the problem.

It was 0620. She had missed the 0500 with Olive by fifteen minutes, which had been fine, because Olive had not noticed, and had, in fact, been sitting in the mud at the top of the north path with a lantern and a thermos and an expression of such total commitment that Annabeth had felt something complicated in her throat and had changed the subject to culvert grades.

She was walking down the hill in the wrong clothes, her satchel — the canvas one Chiron had given her three days ago, with the waxed flap and the outside pockets, one for pencils and one for the folding rule — heavy on her shoulder, and she knew exactly why. A camp shirt with the sleeves cut off — her own work, done with a knife and no plan, ragged at the edges, and the reason her shoulders were now the color of the inside of a seashell. Over it, a gray hooded sweatshirt from the lost-and-found bin, three sizes too big, the cuffs frayed and the left one soaked from where she had knelt in the mud to check the culvert invert. Khaki shorts, sun-bleached, covered in graphite. Boots half-laced. In her pockets: a stub of pencil, a rubber band, a ten-inch length of twine, a bent paperclip she had been using as a makeshift divider, and a small brass compass that had belonged to her father.

The custody of the morning had been the arrangement. That was the thing. Every morning for four months, the dock had a shape, and she had fit into the shape, and the shape had told her where to be, and she had never once had to decide.

She stood at the top of the gravel path and looked down at the lake. The sun had the hill in a firm grip. From here she could see the whole camp waking up the way it did on a day with nothing scheduled — kids drifting toward the pavilion in ones and twos, the flag going up on the Big House porch, Chiron's voice somewhere doing the administrative thing with somebody who had broken a curfew. The lake was a flat sheet of hammered tin, and at the end of the dock, four shapes.

Four. Not five, and none of them in the right place. Clarisse at the water end, Thalia next to her with their shoulders nearly touching, Silena in the middle with a crate, and Percy at the far end — the reed end, the end that had never been anyone's. Annabeth counted it twice to be sure.

She went down. The dock planks were warm and dry toward the land end and damp and cool toward the water end, and the damp came up through her bootsoles. She walked the whole length of it, which she always did, and she did not decide where she was going to sit until she was standing over the space between Silena and Percy and Silena had, without looking, moved over three inches.

So she sat there.

"You're wet," Thalia said.

"One cuff. It's a system."

"Where were you?" Percy said.

"North path. The culvert's holding, but the invert's wrong — it's pitched two degrees too flat, so the silt will build at the mouth. I told Olive we'd need to re-pitch it before the first frost." Annabeth heard herself say this in her fastest voice, the one that filled space, and made herself stop. "It's fine. It's a summer problem."

"Everything's a system with you," Clarisse said.

"Everything is a system, Clarisse. That's not me. That's the world."

Percy passed her the crate. It had been sitting between him and Silena for an hour and had been picked over with the discrimination of professionals. Annabeth looked at the strawberries, selected the second-largest one, which was a long-standing rule of hers — the largest was always overrated and usually hollow — and took it.

"These are stolen," she said.

"Redistributed," Silena said.

"From the north beds. That I just spent two hours on."

"You spent two hours on the drainage."

"I spent two hours on the drainage of the district in which these strawberries were rotting."

Thalia put her hand on the back of Annabeth's neck, which she had been doing for years, and which Annabeth had spent years learning not to flinch from, and said, "You're tense."

"I'm on a schedule."

"What schedule?"

Annabeth opened her notebook. Percy leaned over to look at it, which she allowed, because he had learned the rules and one of the rules was that he could look at the schedule page and not at the map pages.

"North path at five," Annabeth said. "Satchel at six. Lake at six-thirty. Box around it. All five."

"And what time is it?"

"It's six twenty-two."

"So you're early."

"I'm not early. I'm late. I missed the five o'clock." Annabeth closed the notebook and looked at it in her lap. "I was supposed to be here at six-thirty and all of you were supposed to be here at six-thirty, and instead you were here at five and five-fifteen and I don't know when, and I was on the hill."

"Being the Cartographer," Clarisse said. It should have sounded like a jab. It came out like something else. Annabeth looked at her, and Clarisse was examining her thumbnail with what might have been intensity, and added, "Capital letters. On a certificate. Chiron's having it framed."

"There's a certificate?"

"There's a certificate. It's happening tomorrow."

"Oh," Annabeth said, in a much smaller voice than she intended.

Thalia's hand was still on her neck. Her thumb moved once, a small absent circle, the way it had on the night of the argument when Annabeth had sat down next to her by the campfire and refused to leave.

"You don't have to be on the schedule," Thalia said.

"I know."

"You say I know the way other people say 'I surrender.'"

"I'm aware." Annabeth looked at the box she had drawn around ALL FIVE. "I keep waiting for it to feel like a rule. I took the box off it and I still drew the box. I wrote ALL FIVE in capitals and I put it on a list with a time, and I came down here at six twenty-two because six twenty-two feels safe."

Percy, who usually answered fast and adjusted later, was quiet for a second. Then he said, "What would you have done if we weren't here?"

Annabeth opened her mouth. Closed it.

"I'd have sat down," she said. "At the end. Where you are."

"You came over here and sat next to me, which is not the end, so."

"I sat next to Silena."

"You sat next to Silena, who moved over, which means Silena let you, which means nobody picked a spot." Percy took a strawberry. "You wrote a schedule so that nobody would have to choose. And then nobody chose, and it's six twenty-two, and here we are, and it's fine."

"It's not fine," Annabeth said. "It's better."

"Yeah," Percy said. "It's better."

---

They went to the lake.

It was not a decision, and nobody suggested it. Clarisse stood up first, which was a Clarisse move, and pulled her shirt over her head, and Thalia said "Wait for me" and started pulling at the knot in her hoodie string, and Silena said "I'm not wearing a bra to swim in the lake, so if anyone tells anyone" and nobody answered because nobody had to, and Percy was already out of his shirt.

The lake was cold.

That was the first thing, every time, and Annabeth had been swimming in this lake since March and had never once gotten used to it. It came up over her boots and it was a cold that made her whole body contract, and then — this was the trick of it — the cold found its level. It came up to her waist and it stopped being cold and started being the temperature of the water, and then the sun did the rest.

She waded out with her clothes on, because Annabeth did not swim and then change and then swim. The lake took her cut-off-sleeve shirt and the gray hoodie and the khaki shorts and turned them into heavy awkward underwater things that pulled at her, and she stood there with her hair coming down and her cuffs darkening up her arms and the water holding her at the chest, and she let it.

It was not the ocean.

She had never seen the ocean — born in a city, raised by a father who was afraid of it, and come to this camp at nine with salt still in her family's history but never in her own bones. The lake was old and it was wide and deep enough to hide things in, but it had edges. You could see the other side, and the bottom if the light was right. It smelled of lily pads and wet stone and the green smell of things growing in the dark, and when it went into her mouth it tasted like rain and not like salt. Percy told her once that the ocean took things and this lake gave them back, and she had decided long ago that it was the truest thing anyone had ever said to her.

They were out to the buoys. Clarisse had gone ahead of everyone the way she did everything — decisively, boots off and shirt off, walking in like she was claiming it, gone, coming up thirty feet out with her hair slicked flat to her head. Thalia had gone in with a shriek and a splash, and was now floating on her back with her face to the sun. Silena had waded in to her knees and sat down in the shallow with her arms around her knees, her wet hoodie floating around her like a purple lily. Annabeth floated, arms out, ears under, and she thought about nothing at all for eleven seconds, which was a personal record.

Percy went under and did not come up.

She knew where he was. He came up thirty feet to her left, shaking the water out of his eyes, grinning.

"You don't have to do that," she said.

"Do what?"

"Disappear."

"I didn't disappear. I went under. There's a whole world down there. You want to see the skiff?"

He put his head back down. Annabeth tucked and followed him.

Under the surface the lake was a different country. The sound of everything above stopped, replaced by the long low hum the lake made when nobody was asking it for anything. The light came down in columns, green-gold, and lit a world of weed and sand and old stone.

And past the willow curtain, where the shade sat deep and the water ran a degree cooler, the skiff sat on the bottom.

It had not sunk so much as it had been accepted. The hull had come to rest at an angle in maybe fourteen feet of water, the keel in the mud, the prow up where the light could reach it. It was soft with age and furred with algae and the color of wet newsprint, and the shape of it was still a shape. You could see where the seats had been, and where a rope had once been tied.

And standing on the bow, its feet on the wood and its body rising out of the water into the green light, was the heron.

Upright. Unbothered. Its feathers moving very slightly in the water the way hair moves. One leg planted. One leg drawn up. The yellow eye turned and found Annabeth through four feet of lake water and did not blink.

Annabeth's lungs told her it was time to go up. She went up.

"She's real," she said, when she broke the surface.

"Who?" Thalia, floating nearby, only her face above water.

"The heron. On the skiff. She's standing on the bow. Under about fourteen feet of water, and she wasn't scared of me at all."

Clarisse came up beside her, blowing water out of her nose. "There's a heron."

"There's a heron on the skiff."

Clarisse looked at Percy, who was treading water and looking pleased with himself in the way of a person who had brought you to a thing and was not going to demand credit for it. "Is it magic?"

"It's a bird. I think it's the lake's bird."

"Everything's the lake's bird with you," Thalia said, and she kicked water at him, and he let her.

Annabeth swam to the dock, because she needed to write down the depth, and the compass bearing from the dock, and the fact that the bird had held her ground, which was apparently the kind of detail that went on a map that showed changes. She pulled herself up onto the planks.

The dock was a wreck. It was their wreck. Clarisse's boots stood at the water end with the socks stuffed into the tops of them, the left one a dark wet shape where the water had come in through the hole in the heel. Thalia's shirt and shorts were spread over the railing to dry, next to a pair of running shoes with no socks anywhere near them. Silena's hoodie was draped over a piling in a purple wreath. Two towels, one borrowed. A waterproof notebook with a pen clipped to the cover. A crate of strawberries with the cloth gone damp. A bra, folded, on the far end plank, which nobody was going to mention and which Annabeth was going to remember forever and never mention.

She sat on the plank with her legs hanging off the edge and her soaked sleeves darkening to the elbows and her hair dripping onto the wood between her feet. She wrote for a while. The pencil was damp and took the line badly and she wrote around it.

It was 0715 when Silena climbed up out of the water and sat down next to her, and 0720 when Thalia and Clarisse came up the ladder arguing about whether it was cold, and 0725 when Percy came up with a handful of things from the bottom: a fishing lure, a bottle cap, and a smooth grey stone the exact color of the sky.

He put the stone in Annabeth's hand and closed her fingers around it.

"Map it," he said.

"I'm not mapping a stone."

"It's a stone from the bottom of the lake. Map the stone."

"You're mapping the heron."

"I'm recording a sighting." She turned the stone over. It was warm from her hand. "And you're going to hold still for thirty seconds so I can get the angles right."

He held still. He actually held still, which he did for nobody, his arms out, his face solemn, and Thalia made a sound from the railing that might have been a sob or might have been laughter, and Clarisse said "He's very brave" in a flat voice, and Annabeth drew him.

The lake at eight in the morning, four feet below her, was the color of a thing that had nothing to hide.

---

Clarisse got the fire going before anyone had asked her to.

It was not a big fire. It was four logs laid in a square on the gravel above the beach with kindling under them and a match from a waterproof book, built the way she built everything — fast, ugly, functional. Nobody was going to tell Clarisse La Rue not to build a fire next to four wet campers at eight in the morning, and drying on was a legitimate use of a fire.

They laid it all out. The shirts and the shorts and the hoodie and the wet towels and the socks, arranged on the railing and the flat stones around the fire with the looseness of people who had done this a dozen times and were never going to make a system of it. Annabeth's gray sweatshirt dripped from the top of a post. Percy's orange shirt hung from the nail by the canoe rack, arms out, like it had given up. The steam came off the dock planks where the sun was hitting them and off the towels where it wasn't.

Percy sat with his back against the piling and watched his sister climb up out of the lake.

Annabeth came up the ladder wet and stopped, dripping, and put her hands flat against her own face, and then she looked at him.

"What?" she said.

"You've got lake on you."

"I got in the lake."

"You got in the lake and now you're on the dock and now there's lake on you, and I'm just — I'm reporting it."

"Very observant for fourteen."

"Very observant for someone who's dating someone who gets in lakes." He shifted over on the plank, which had enough room for two if neither of them had long legs, which neither of them did. "Come here."

She came. She sat down next to him, shoulder to shoulder, both of them dripping, and there was a leaf in her hair, a small one, that she had not noticed, and that he reached over and took out.

"You had a leaf," he said.

"I have a leaf system." She let her head tip sideways until it was resting against his shoulder, which was a thing she did now, and had not done in April, and had done with increasing frequency since June in a way that Percy kept quiet about because saying it out loud made her stop doing it. "Percy."

"Yeah."

"Are you going to do it again?"

He knew what she meant. He had been waiting for someone to ask him, and he had decided it was going to be her, because it was always going to be her. "Fix something?"

"Fix us. You did it yesterday. On the dock. You said the thing." Her voice was very small and very level. "And then you said the other thing, and then Clarisse and Thalia cried, and then it was fixed, and then we all went to bed. And I've been thinking since five in the morning about what happens next time."

"I'm not going to do it again," Percy said.

"Okay."

"I'm not going to be the one who says the thing every time. That's not — that's not what I signed up for. I don't want to be the camp's designated mediator."

"Good."

"Good?"

"Good." She lifted her head and looked at him. "I didn't want a fixer. I wanted a boyfriend who happens to be somebody's brother, and who couldn't stand watching his sisters destroy each other. Those are different jobs and only one of them is yours."

He looked at her. "Only one of them is mine."

"You get to be fourteen." She said it firmly, and there was a thing she did with her jaw when she was being serious about something that made her look older than fourteen, which was a lie, and also not. "You get to be fourteen, and you get to be bad at it, and you get to be mad at them and not know what to say. And if you don't know what to say, you can just sit there. That's allowed."

Percy felt something in his chest do the thing it did when she was right about him, which was: it folded. "I didn't know you were going to say that."

"I say things."

"You say things at six twenty-two with a schedule."

"I say things on schedules, yes, that's the whole problem, I'm working on it." She put her hand on the plank between them, palm up, and looked at it, and did not look at him, and this was the part where Percy understood that she had been rehearsing this too, and that she had chosen to do it anyway.

He put his hand in hers.

Her fingers were cold, the way they always were after the lake, the way they had been since the day they met. He closed his fingers around them and brought her hand into his lap and held it there with both of his, and the sun was warm on his back, and out in the cove the heron did not move.

She turned her hand over so that her palm was against his, so that their fingers laced badly, the way they always laced. "I used to think the arrangement was the only thing holding us together. That if we took it apart we'd all fall down."

"What do you believe now?"

She looked at the lake. "I believe I'm sitting on a plank next to you and my hand is freezing and I'm not cold, and that's the whole test, and it's passing."

Percy squeezed her hand, and she squeezed back, and then she let go of his hand and put hers on the side of his face, which was a newer thing, and her other hand came up to hold his jaw, and she leaned in.

She kissed him.

It was a short kiss. It was a real kiss. Her mouth was cold and tasted like the lake, and her hair was wet and fell forward against his cheek, and her hands were trembling very slightly, and she pulled back an inch, and then came back and did it again, longer, and then put her forehead against his and stayed there.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi."

"I'm better at this than I was in March."

"You were terrified in March."

"You were good at it and you were terrified in March. That's allowed too." He kissed her, this time, which he had been wanting to do since she came up the ladder, and it was a better kiss than either of the other two because it had three kisses of practice behind it, and then it stopped, and they sat there with their foreheads together, and the lake went on being the lake.

From the other end of the dock, without looking up, Clarisse said: "Ten minutes."

Percy lifted his head. "Ten minutes for what?"

"For that. Retroactive." She was examining her boots with the deepest concentration of anyone in living memory. "Double for —" She stopped. She started again. "No. Not double."

"Not double?"

"I'm not enforcing double."

"For Zeus-cabin accompaniment," Percy said.

"Thalia's over there." Clarisse nodded at the railing, where Thalia was lying on her stomach in the sun, pointedly, ostentatiously not listening. "She's accompaniment. If she were over here. Which she isn't."

"She's right there."

"She's over there." Clarisse picked up her left boot, looked at the worn-through heel, and put it back down. "I'm reassessing my policy."

Silena, who had been sitting cross-legged on a towel with her hair a wet dark rope over one shoulder, said, "She's been reassessing her policy since five in the morning. She ripped the ledger out."

"I didn't rip the ledger out. I —" Clarisse stopped. "Fine. I ripped the ledger out."

"You sank the ledger in a boat," Percy said.

"I sailed it in a boat."

"It didn't sink."

"Lakes give things back," Clarisse said, in a flat voice, and then, because she was Clarisse, "That's not a thing I believe. That's a lake thing. I don't believe lake things."

Thalia rolled over onto her back. "I believe lake things."

"Nobody asked you. You engage with lake things. It's a whole problem." Thalia flopped onto the plank and lay there with her arms out and her hair hanging off the edge. "I'm cold and I'm dry and I'm happy. That's a lie and I'm happy."

Annabeth picked up her notebook. She did not write anything. She looked at the page she had written at 0725 — the depth of the skiff, the bearing, the stone — and then she crossed out the box around ALL FIVE, slowly, so that everyone could see her do it.

"There," she said. "No time. No box." She closed the notebook. "I'm going to have to draw that, though. The not-having-a-box. That's going to be the hardest part of the map."

Clarisse sat up. "Then draw it like a lake."

"I don't know how to draw a thing like a lake."

"Me neither," Clarisse said. "That's why I hit things."

---

Nobody wanted lunch, and nobody wanted dinner, and the whole day went past the dock the way a summer day goes past a thing that is not paying attention to it.

Annabeth noticed it in the early afternoon, when the fire had burned down to a low orange bed and the last of the clothes had gone from wet to stiff with sun, and when she realized she had not checked her schedule in four hours. She took the notebook out of her satchel and looked at the schedule page. The box was crossed out. Below it, in the small clear writing she used for things that mattered, she had written: DOCK. NO TIME. It was the most accurate thing she had written all summer.

She was on the dock with all of them. That was the other thing. She had spent four months believing that the five of them were a thing with edges, a unit that arrived together and left together and sat in a fixed formation in the presence of witnesses. She had organized half her mental map around it. And now, on the warm plank with the sun on her shoulders, she watched it happen differently.

Clarisse left first.

She got up at around three, which was a Clarisse time of day, and picked up her boots, and stood holding them with her socks hanging out of the tops, and did not say anything.

"Going?" Silena said.

"Arena."

"Everything's hot."

"I'm aware of the temperature. I have skin." Clarisse put one boot on. It went on badly and she did not fix it. "I'm just going to go hit things. It's not a thing."

"It's not a thing," Annabeth said.

"Don't make it a thing."

"We're not making it a thing."

Clarisse put the other boot on. She looked at Percy, who was lying on his back with one arm over his eyes, and at Annabeth, and at Silena, and at Thalia, who was on the railing doing a handstand she was definitely going to fall out of. Then she picked up her spear from the rack where it had been standing since morning, and said, to the lake, in a voice that was not for any of them, "I'm glad you're here. All of you. Don't make it a thing."

And she walked up the dock and up the path, in her boots with no socks, with her bad braid and her ripped ledger in her pocket and her spear on her shoulder, and she did not look back, and she did not have to, because nobody made it a thing.

Thalia swung down off the railing.

"I'm going with her," she announced.

"To the arena?" Silena said.

"To the arena. I'm going to stand there and watch her be competent. It's a whole evening." Thalia was already on her feet, pulling her shirt down over her wet hair. "You can come if you want."

"I'm not dressed for the arena," Annabeth said.

"Neither am I. It's the arena. Nobody's dressed." Thalia looked at Percy. "Percy?"

Percy moved his arm off his eyes. "It's not my thing. It's their thing." He sat up. "It's a you two thing."

Thalia looked down the dock in the direction Clarisse had gone. The path was empty. Then she looked back at Percy, and her face did something very small and very quick, and Annabeth saw it happen and filed it and did not say a word.

"Yeah," Thalia said quietly. "It's a you two thing."

She went up the dock at a run, and the gravel crunched, and then it didn't.

"That was nice," Silena said.

"That was nice," Annabeth agreed.

Silena was sitting on a towel with her legs folded under her in the way that looked uncomfortable and apparently wasn't, her hair a dark rope over one shoulder, drying. She had been quiet most of the afternoon, which for Silena meant doing the invisible work of being there — moving the crate out of the sun, refilling a water bottle, re-sorting the drying towels into an order she considered logical. Annabeth had known her long enough to know that this was Silena in the middle of a big feeling, and that the best thing to do was wait.

"Can I say something?" Silena said.

"I invite it."

Silena turned the water bottle around in her hands. "When I was nine I had a friend who had a chart, a real chart, with names on it, of who she was allowed to be friends with, and she made me promise not to be friends with anybody who wasn't on it. And it worked, for a while, because it told her where the edges were, and she had a mom who moved a lot, and the edges were the part she could count on." She put the bottle down. "I've been thinking about that all morning. About Clarisse's chart. Which wasn't a chart. Which was a dock. But it was the same shape."

"The edges," Annabeth said.

"And I don't think the edges were the thing. I think we've been treating the shape like the thing because the shape is what we can see." Silena reached over and put her hand over Annabeth's, the way she did with everyone, easily, often. "And what we can see isn't what keeps us."

"What keeps us?"

Silena looked out at the lake. The sun had moved off the middle of it and the water was the flat late-afternoon blue, the heron out in the cove where it had been all day.

"I don't know. That's what I wanted to say." Silena shook her head. "I've been trying to figure it out since March and I thought it was going to be the dock, and then I thought it was going to be Percy, and now I think it might just be that we keep choosing it. Every morning. Without the chart."

Annabeth sat with that. Out past the willow cove, on the bottom of the lake, the heron had been standing on the bow of a skiff for six weeks without once coming ashore.

"We should go up," she said eventually. "I have to put something on the map."

Silena stood and stretched, and grabbed her hoodie off the railing. "Do you want company?"

"I want Percy."

Silena looked at her. Then at Percy, who was sitting on the end of the dock with his feet in the water and his chin in his hands, being fourteen.

"I think you can have both," Silena said, and went up the path alone, humming, and left them on the dock.

Percy came back from the water when Annabeth stood up. He stood, and picked up his shirt from the nail, and did not put it on, and fell in beside her, and they walked up the dock together, and it was the most ordinary thing that had happened all week, and it took all of her effort to keep her face straight about it.

The Big House was quiet in the late afternoon. The map was on the wall of the lobby, lit by the low sun through the door, held up by four bronze thumbtacks and a strip of tape at the bottom corner that Annabeth had repaired with the patience of a surgeon. It read:

CAMP HALF-BLOOD — AS OF THIS MONTH — DRAWN BY ANNABETH GRACE

She opened her satchel and took out a small square of paper. On it she had written, in her clear map hand:

THE DOCK — SUBJECT TO CHANGE. DO NOT RELY ON ITS SHAPE.

She pinned it to the map, at the point where the dock would be, and stepped back, and felt something leave her shoulders that she had been carrying since March.

"Chiron's going to love that," Percy said.

"Chiron is going to hate it."

"Chiron is going to have it laminated."

"Chiron is going to motion to remove it from the record and then secretly be delighted." She looked at the map. Every change she had drawn — the moved buoys, the repaired wall, the new culvert on the north path, the new planks on the dock, the willows grown deeper over the cove, the arrows and the dates and the notes. And now, in the middle of the lake, in her own small writing: A HERON STANDS ON THE SUNKEN SKIFF. IT HAS BEEN THERE SIX WEEKS. IT DOES NOT LEAVE.

"How long was that heron standing there before you drew it?" Percy said.

"I don't know. All summer. I guessed at six weeks. I'll draw the date properly when I know it."

They went back down to the lake without deciding to, and took the ridge path behind the Hermes cabin, and came out above the willows, where the whole lake was laid out under the last of the sun, and the dock was empty, and the fire was a bed of coals sending up a thin thread of smoke, and there was only water.

They sat on the ridge for a while and did not talk. Percy put his shirt on. Annabeth put her hands in her pockets and felt the twine and the bent paperclip and the small brass compass, and did not take any of them out.

When the sun was off the water they went down and sat on the dock, and Clarisse came back from the arena with her hair stuck to her face and a split knuckle, and Thalia came back an hour after that with her hair standing straight up from the static and a smile she was not even pretending to hide, and Silena came back down with a wet towel and a clean shirt and no explanation of where she had been.

They sat on the dock until the stars came out.

They did not sit in the arrangement. Nobody chose a plank and nobody kept one. Thalia lay down and put her head in Clarisse's lap, which had never happened in the history of the arrangement, and Clarisse looked down at her like she was deciding whether to permit it, and then permitted it, and kept very still. Silena sat with her back against the piling and her eyes closed. Percy sat at the water end, at the reed end, in the spot that had never been anyone's, and Annabeth sat next to him with her shoulder against his arm.

"She's still there," Percy said, after a while, nodding toward the cove.

"I know."

"You going to draw that?"

"Tomorrow."

"With a date?"

"With a date."

Clarisse looked down at Thalia's hair and said, in the flat voice she used for things she meant, "Draw it with an arrow."

"An arrow to where?"

"To now. Draw the change. Isn't that the whole thing you do?" She cleared her throat. "The map of changes. Draw this one too."

Annabeth wrote it on her hand, because there was no light and no notebook and no time, and it looked like a small black smudge on her knuckles, and she did not wash it off until Tuesday.

The lake moved under the dock. The stars came out. The heron stood on the bow of a sunken skiff in the willow cove, and the skiff stayed where it was, and the heron did not leave, and the dock held five people who had not been arranged.
