## Chapter 173: The Long Way Up the Hill

Percy Jackson woke to the smell of dead fire and the discovery that a flat rock is only flat until a person has slept on it.

He lay still a while with his eyes shut, cataloguing the damage the way a sailor checked a hull after weather. Sore right shoulder, from being the pillow. Sore hip, from the stone he had sworn was sand. Sand in the creases of both elbows, which was new. The wool blanket lay half across his chest and half across Annabeth, who was a warm parenthesis against his side and had one cold foot wedged against his shin in a way he had long since stopped arguing with, because his shin had given up and joined her side weeks ago.

Above him the sky was doing the thing the sky did in early October: holding a gray it had no intention of explaining. Not the blue of the week before and not yet the low iron gray of deep autumn, just a wide pale nothing, the color of a page before you draw on it. Out past the sandbar the mist stood on the lake in one long sheet, thin enough that the boundary rock showed through it, pale with its white X, and the far shore was a suggestion with trees on it.

Somewhere near his left foot, somebody's teeth were chattering.

"You're doing that on purpose," Thalia said, from under her flannel.

"I'm not doing anything," Clarisse said. "I'm asleep."

"You can't be asleep. The whole beach heard the fire go out."

"The fire didn't go out. I banked it."

"Into what? A question?"

Percy opened his eyes. This was, he had learned over one summer, the correct way to greet a Cabin Three morning: horizontally, so nobody could accuse you of being awake for the interesting part.

The north beach looked like a place where a small, cheerful disaster had spent the night and then gone home. The fire ring held a low gray ring of ash with two black stubs of oak in it and one stubborn coal that had been holding on out of pure spite since four in the morning. The quilts were a wreck of corners. The canvas satchel sat where Clarisse had dropped it, upright, disciplined, the only object on the whole beach that looked like it had somewhere to be. The check-in slate lay across the flat rock with the hours initialed up its left side in five different hands, which was the most accurate portrait of his family Percy had ever seen: one neat, two reckless, one in the blocky capitals of a girl who wrote like she was hammering stakes, and one small and round and tucked in between the others as if it had been snuck in around a corner.

The folding camp chair, which weighed the same as a small horse and which Clarisse had carried down the hill out of pure spite, had been folded at some point in the night and used as a pillow by nobody, which meant Clarisse had folded it in the dark while everyone thought she was asleep, which meant Clarisse had not slept, which everyone knew and nobody was going to say.

"Where's the fourth blanket," Clarisse said.

"Good morning to you too."

"Third and fourth. There are two more blankets down here than there are people. I counted going down."

"You always count going down," Percy said.

"I always count going down," she agreed, unbothered. "Two extra. Both wool. Both gray. Neither one mine."

Percy pushed himself up on one elbow. Which was a mistake, because his elbow was sand, and the elbow slid, and he went back down flat, and Annabeth, who had not opened her eyes, said, "Graceful," into his shoulder in the voice of a girl conducting a survey of his dignity.

The blankets in question were at the edge of the nest, outside the ring of their bodies, folded with a care that none of them had displayed for anything in the last twelve hours.

"Those are mine," Silena said. She was sitting up on the flat rock with her hair down and her knees drawn up and the dented harpy thermos in both hands, watching the mist the way other people watched television. Everything she wore had been slept in and she had somehow still come out of it looking composed, which was a trick she had been performing since June. "One is mine. I don't know about the other one."

Percy looked at the second blanket. Gray wool, one corner stained dark with something old, a stitched edge that had been mended twice in two different colors of thread, so that the seam looked like a coastline.

"That's a boathouse blanket," he said.

"Boathouse blankets are green," Clarisse said.

"That one's gray."

"That's because it isn't a boathouse blanket, Jackson. Boathouse blankets are green. There's a rack of them in the tackle room, they're all green, and they're all exactly like each other, which is a thing I know because I counted them in June when O'Fee accused the cabin of losing one."

"O'Fee accuses us of losing one every June."

"O'Fee is often right."

Thalia sat up out of her flannel like a bear coming out of a den, wild-haired and entirely unrepentant. "Somebody brought it," she said. "Somebody brought a blanket down in the dark and now nobody remembers. It's a mystery. Put it in the book. Second mystery of the season." She squinted at the blanket. "It's got a burn mark on the corner. It came off the fire. It's been here all night and it has lived more than I have."

"It's a blanket," Annabeth said, finally opening her eyes, gray and clear and full of the flat morning light. "It's a blanket whose owner forgot where they left it. It is not a mystery. Mysteries have places to be."

"Everything in this family is a mystery if you wait long enough and refuse to ask," Thalia said.

"We'll take it up," Clarisse said, standing, brushing sand off her green jacket with two brisk hands. "Whatever it is. Up with us. We do not leave a blanket on the ground for the harpy to find and report, because I am not doing another inventory with O'Fee, and because the last time I did an inventory with O'Fee I learned things about my own cabin that I do not care to know."

"Like the spoons."

"Like the spoons," Clarisse agreed, and the two of them looked at each other with the mutual gravity of people who had survived a war together, which they had, and it had been about spoons.

They packed the beach.

That was the thing about the morning after. Nobody was funny yet. Percy could feel it in all of them, a shared tiredness with the joy still sitting under it like coals under ash. Nobody had their wits all the way up. Clarisse packed like a quartermaster in a hurry: quilts shaken twice and folded, towels stacked by owner whether or not the owners wanted them stacked by owner, the skillet scrubbed out with sand and a handful of lake water because the skillet had fried eggs twice and had clearly been through enough. Silena poured the last of the harpy soup into the tin cup, and nobody asked where the soup had come from, which was the correct etiquette, and which Percy appreciated. Thalia went off along the shore for a minute by herself and came back with her hands full of kindling, which she dropped by the ring without comment, because even after four days of ridge survey and one night on the ground, Thalia Grace could not walk past a dead fire without leaving it wood.

Annabeth folded the schedule.

Percy watched her do it. She had been carrying the paper all night, in her pocket, sat on and creased soft, and now she took it out and folded it along the old creases, and then, standing there on the cold beach with sand in the cuffs of her shorts, she did something he had never seen her do, which was to fold it the wrong way: running the new crease across the old ones, blurring all her careful lines into a soft cross-hatch, turning the timeline into a thing that had been used instead of a thing that had been followed.

Then she put it back in her pocket.

"You're keeping it," Percy said.

"It's evidence," Annabeth said. "Somebody has to keep the evidence that last night happened."

"That's what the sand in your hair is for."

"The sand in my hair is a temporary condition." She turned to look up at him, and the morning light was flat and unkind and made everybody look like the inside of a fog bank, and it did absolutely nothing to her, and Percy, who had been awake for four minutes, understood with the clarity of four minutes that this was the entire problem with his life and he intended to keep it.

"You have a burn on your cheek," she said.

"I do not."

"From the coals. You slept with your face toward the fire because you always turn toward the fire, which Clarisse has told you is a bad habit in a person who swims in cold water twice a week." She reached up and put her thumb on his jaw, right where it stung, and left it there. "There. Now I've logged it. You're a documented hazard."

"I've been trying to tell people," he said, and kissed her.

It was not a big kiss. There was nowhere on this beach to have a big kiss: four other people within twenty feet, all of them smelling of woodsmoke, one of them actively folding towels. It was a slow, sandy, good-morning kiss, unhurried in the way you can only be unhurried when you are tired in your bones and warm in your chest at the same time, and she came up onto the balls of her feet into it without any particular plan, and her hand flattened against his shirt front and stayed there, and his hand found the back of her head and stayed there, and the lake went on pushing its small cold waves up the sand like an audience being patient.

Somebody whistled. It was not Thalia.

"Ten minutes," Clarisse said, to the fire ring. "Per occurrence. I'm not counting the first one because it's before breakfast and nobody has the right to my rules before breakfast."

"You said that about last night."

"Last night I was asleep."

"You were never asleep once."

"No comment," Clarisse said, which was, in the language of Cabin Three, an admission wrapped in a load-bearing wall, and Percy let her have it, because she had spent the whole night keeping five people warm and alive on a beach in the first frost of October and had told nobody, and because he was going to need her to hear the words "thank you" eventually, and today was not going to be the day, and both of them knew it.

The walk up was a disaster in the best way.

They took the long path, because the short cut ran down a slope of wet leaves and pine needles that had gone slick in the frost, and because Clarisse, having carried a chair down a hill out of spite, was not going to concede a single further point to the terrain. So they went the long way, around the bottom of the ridge and up the switchback, strung out along the path in a tired, arguing line: Clarisse in the front with the satchel and the seat of the folding chair at her hip like a shield, Thalia behind her with the skillet because somebody had to, Silena in the middle carrying nothing and looking like she was carrying everything, Annabeth holding the slate so nothing could rub off the initials, and Percy in the back with the two mystery blankets, which was the least anyone could ask of the only person who had spent the night with his feet against a rock.

The sky came up over the pines slowly, the way it does when it is preoccupied. Mist lay in the dips of the path like milk in the bowls of a table. Somewhere below them a loon called once and did not call again, which was the correct number of times for a loon to call on an October morning, and Percy found himself listening for the second call anyway, the way you listen for a stair that isn't in the house.

Halfway up, Thalia fell into step beside him.

"You're carrying two blankets," she said.

"I'm carrying two blankets."

"We have no idea where one of them came from."

"We have no idea where one of them came from," Percy agreed. "It's mine now. I've decided. I'm going to put it on my bunk and when somebody comes looking for it I'll deny everything, and if nobody comes looking for it, then it was always mine and the universe just took a while to catch up."

Thalia looked at him sideways for a moment. She looked wrecked and pleased and eleven years old, which was not a combination that appeared on Thalia Grace very often and which she would have denied in court.

"This is the first frost," she said. Not a question.

"Reeds," Percy said. "Last night. Frost on the reed heads out past the cove, going all the way down the margin. I touched one. It came off on my fingers."

"And Chiron's rule was that if the frost came, the water's closed."

"That's what he said."

"Then he's going to close the water on the same morning you all slept in it," Thalia said, and grinned with her whole face, wicked and delighted. "Do you understand what that means, Jackson? You got the last one. You got the actual last warm night, on a technicality, argued for by me, at eleven o'clock at night, on Chiron's porch, which I will be denying in public for the rest of my natural life. You and my sister and Clarisse and Silena got the last one, and the paperwork is going to be formalized about forty minutes from now at breakfast by a centaur with a clipboard, and there is not a single thing anyone in this camp can do about it."

"You're taking this personally."

"I'm taking it the correct amount personally," Thalia said. She bumped his shoulder with hers, hard, the Grace-sister version of a handshake, and then she didn't say anything else for twenty steps, which for Thalia was nearly a speech, and Percy, who had learned to read her the way he read water, by surface and undertow, let it sit.

The last rise came out of the pines into the open grass above the cabins, and the smell of the pavilion came down to meet them: wood smoke and oatmeal and, under it, the specific bright smell of a place that had been cleaned before dawn by people who had no idea what a hard night their friends were having.

"Breakfast," Silena said, in the voice of a prophet.

"Bed," said Clarisse, in the voice of a heretic.

"Both," Annabeth said, "in that order, and I've written it down, so it's happening," and she held up the slate with its five columns of initials as if that settled anything, and it did, because it was the family's strangest and most reliable law: whatever Annabeth wrote on a slate, they all showed up for.

The Big House at seven on a Sunday morning in October was the quietest building at camp, which Annabeth had known since her first week and had never once explained to anybody, because explaining it would have used it up.

The porch smelled of cold paint and lamp oil and the ghost of last night's cocoa. The parlor door stood open. Inside, the lamp was lit, and Chiron was at the long table with his reading glasses on and two papers in front of him and a cup of tea going cold at his elbow, and he did not look up when she came in, which meant he had heard them all the way up the path and had decided to let her have the first word, which was one of the things she liked about him.

"Slate," Annabeth said, and laid it on the table corner, out of the way of the papers, exactly the way she had been taught to lay out drawings so nothing got knocked. Five columns. Five hands. Every hour from dusk to first light, initialed by whoever had stayed awake for it. "Nobody lost anybody. Ms. O'Fee came at midnight and wrote us down in her little book. She also left soup, which I am not explaining."

"The soup," Chiron said, "is explained on this side of the desk." He set down his pen and looked at the paper in front of him with the expression of a man who had been reading the same sentence for an hour in the hope that it would improve. "At one-forty this morning I received a written complaint, by wing, regarding the unsuitable nature of my beach as a site of death by pneumonia, signed, witnessed, and dated, with a decorative border. It is the fourth one this year, and the first one with a border. I have read it eleven times."

"Is it a good complaint?"

"It is an excellent complaint." Chiron took his glasses off, folded them, and looked at her, and his eyes were warm and tired in the way they were always tired, the way of somebody who had been awake for a thousand years and had stopped expecting to catch up on it. "You had the first frost," he said.

"We had the first frost," Annabeth said. "On the reeds, past the cove. Percy touched one. It came off on his fingers."

"The almanac said the second of October. It said the second for eleven years, and it has been wrong for eleven years, and it is wrong again by one day, and I have decided to stop forgiving it." He picked up the second paper, which Annabeth could now see was the almanac page itself, marked in his square hand. "The water closes. That was the condition, and a condition I state is a condition I keep, which is the entire reason the camp listens when I state one."

"It closes today."

"It closes today. Canoes stowed and covered before the end of the month, buoys in by the middle of it, the swim dock pulled to the shore side on the first of November. If you and your cabin want to be in the water after this morning, you will be in the water in Tempest Hall, on the tile, where the temperature is a decision instead of an event."

Annabeth had a thought then. She had it the way she had her best thoughts, which was not in a flash of lightning but as a small tidy item placed on a shelf by her own hand, where she could see it and come back to it. The lake was closing. The lake had been, for a whole summer, the place where every one of them had gone when they did not know what to do with a feeling. Take the lake out of the week and something had to go in.

"Can I ask for something," she said.

"You always can. You rarely do, which is why I have stopped being worried by it. Ask."

"The spring under the ridge. The slab and the seep that Thalia found. It feeds the French drain and it feeds the silt in Olive's clay and it is not on my map, because nobody has drawn it, because nobody has been up there with a rod and a level and a set of hands." She heard herself going fast and made herself slow down, because a request made fast sounded like a wish, and a request made slow sounded like a plan. "I want to survey it. Properly. Not a walk, not a look, an actual survey: run the line, take the fall, wade the seep, draw it true the first time. Thalia knows the ground, Olive knows the clay, Marcus can hold a rod and not talk. And it's the last water we've got that isn't closed, so it seems like the right month to go and find out where our water comes from."

Chiron was quiet for a moment. Then he did the thing he did that had made her trust him in the first place, which was to treat the request as though it had already been granted and simply move on to the terms.

"Not this week," he said. "The ridge has had four days of a Grace on it and a frost on top of that, and I have spent the morning being complained at by a harpy about one night on a beach. You will take a third person, you will file the route the night before with whoever is on the porch, and you will come back down by dark, which at this time of year is not a suggestion."

"Next Saturday."

"Next Saturday." He wrote it on the corner of the almanac page, a small square hand, and Annabeth watched her own plan become a line on somebody else's paper, which was a feeling she had no intention of describing to anyone. Then he said, without looking up, "And take the boy."

"Which boy."

"The one who found the water coming from uphill and stood in the mud on a Monday telling you about it," Chiron said. "He will carry the rod. He will also make the walk enjoyable, which is worth more than the rod, and if you tell him I said that I will deny it in writing and win."

Annabeth did not smile. She was aware of her face, though, doing something near it.

"Filed," she said. "Under facts."

"Under facts," Chiron agreed, gravely, and went back to his papers, and she was at the door when he said one more thing, in the flat voice he used for the flat facts. "Annabeth. Your sister walked four days of our northern boundary and made a note on every spring, seam, and slide from here to the ridge, and she did it alone, in weather, carrying a copy of a page that matters, and it is the best piece of survey work that has come off this camp in nine years. She will tell you she was a mule. She was not a mule."

"I know what she was," Annabeth said.

"I know you do," said Chiron. "That is why I am telling the person who will remember it when the two of you are old."

She stopped in the hall on the way out, which she had not planned to do, and which she did every time.

Chiron's office door had a flat shallow drawer built into the desk, and the drawer had a lock, and the lock had been locked since the Thursday in September when five people had stood around a table and learned how to feel sick in the same minute. Thirty-nine marks. She had counted them twice herself and written the count into her field book and then closed the book with the number inside it, and she had left the fortieth space open on purpose, and she had not looked at the panel since, because looking was something you did to a thing that was finished.

She stood in the hall and did not open anything and did not look, and then she went out, because that was the whole of the ritual and the ritual took about eleven seconds, and eleven seconds was all she was willing to give it on the first morning of October.

Thalia was on the porch steps of the Big House with her boots off and her socks rolled down and her heels inspected, one at a time, with the grim attention of a woman checking an axle, which was what a Grace sister looked like on the morning after a night on the ground. She had walked up from Cabin One in her socks, because it was Sunday, and Sunday had rules of its own.

"You're bleeding," Annabeth said.

"I'm not bleeding, I'm complaining, it's different." Thalia put the boot back on and did not lace it. "Did he count us?"

"He counted us. He also read a complaint about pneumonia with a decorative border."

"The border was mine," Thalia said. "I drew it while she was writing. Interlocking bolts. She liked it, she said it gave the letter gravity." She squinted at the lake, which was still holding its sheet of morning mist down in the bowl of the camp, still as a saucer. "So. Closed?"

"Closed as of today. Canoes stowed by the end of the month. Buoys in by the middle."

"Good," Thalia said, and it came out flat enough that Annabeth looked at her. "Not good like I'm glad. Good like I said it would and then it did. I sat on a ridge for four days and watched the season get ready, and I came home and reported it, and this morning a centaur with a clipboard wrote it on a piece of paper, and now the whole camp has to live in the thing I saw first." She pressed the heel of her hand against the step, considering. "That's the part of the job I didn't know about. Not finding it. Everybody can find a slab. Making it official. Making forty kids not swim because you said what the ridge said."

"You didn't make them. The frost did."

"The frost made the frost. I made the ruling." Thalia grinned sideways, tired and sharp. "Anyway. It's down and I don't have to carry it anymore, that's the main thing. Chiron's got it on his paper, so it's his problem now, and my problem is a nap and a hot cloth for this heel and absolutely nothing else until Thursday."

"About Thursday," Annabeth said, and Thalia's eyes came around like a hawk's, because she had been waiting for it since dawn and had decided, on the steps, to make her sister say it out loud.

"Say it properly."

"Chiron's made the spring official. Next Saturday, with a rod and a level and the two of you and Marcus and—" she paused, and found that the pause was going to do the work for her whether she wanted it to or not, "—and Percy. To carry things. And to enjoy it, which is apparently worth more than the rod."

Thalia's face did the complicated thing it did when joy and something else arrived holding hands. "You asked for it."

"I asked for it on the first morning of the first frost because the season was taking my lake and I wanted to be standing somewhere when it did."

"That's a terrible reason to plan a survey."

"It's the only reason anyone plans a survey," Annabeth said. "You think I drew the whole camp because I ran out of paper? I drew it because in June everything was new and I could feel it starting to be ordinary and I wanted a record of the part before."

Thalia leaned her head back against the porch rail and looked at her sister for a long moment. Annabeth let her. They had never once been the kind of sisters who needed to fill a silence; the Grace house had been full of silences with edges on them, and they had both learned young that the only silences worth keeping were the ones you chose.

"Draw it so it looks like somebody was up there," Thalia said at last. "The slab, the cairn, the rag going northeast. When I'm fifty I want to walk up to the wall and see that somebody stood on that ridge and saw the thing I saw and put it down where it would stay."

"It will."

"I know it will. That's not the part I'm worried about." Thalia stood up, one boot off, one boot on, and offered Annabeth a hand down the steps for no reason except that she had one to offer. "Come on, genius. You wrote 'breakfast, then bed' on a slate in front of witnesses. Even the furniture in this camp knows you don't break a slate."

Silena Beauregard had a theory about the morning after, and the theory was this: nobody in the world is cool at nine in the morning on one night of sleep, and the only dignified response is to feed everybody until they stop trying.

They did not go to the pavilion. The pavilion was for campers with a schedule and a shirt that had been washed in the last three days, and none of the five of them qualified, so they took the long table in the Cabin Three kitchen, which had been built for a cabin of twelve and had never once in this timeline held twelve, and which Silena had quietly made hers the way she made everything hers: by being the only one who knew where the good pan was.

The state of them was a study.

Thalia had eaten her oatmeal standing up and then laid her forehead on the table and stayed there, one boot still unlaced, and announced that she was participating. Percy had brought the mystery blanket, which he had carried up the whole hill, and folded it on the floor by the stove and lain down on it immediately, because he had decided that a blanket that had no owner had no obligations and could therefore be a floor. Clarisse had taken one chair at the end of the table and was doing the thing she did when she was tired, which was to refuse to be tired, visibly: she sat very straight and drank her water with a grimness that suggested the water had wronged her.

Annabeth sat on the bench with her field book open and a plate she was not eating from and one hand resting in Percy's hair, which was on the bench beside her because Percy had dragged his head up from the floor the moment the bacon smell started and had not gone back down.

Hungry, Silena noted, was a thing that reorganized a family. She broke six eggs into the pan because six was what she could hold in one line of sight, put yesterday's bread in the oven to warm because yesterday's bread was still better than today's absence of bread, and cut the last of the apples the orchard had given at the end of September into a wooden bowl, thumb-sized, because everybody could be trusted to eat a thing if it came already in bites.

"You cooked on a beach," Clarisse said. "You've been cooking since Thursday. Sit down and let me burn something."

"You burn toast."

"Toast is a food."

"Toast is a rumor of a food," Silena said, and then, because Clarisse was already standing, and because a tired Clarisse with her hands on a task was a Clarisse who would survive the week, she handed her the spatula. "Two minutes a side. If it goes black, we eat it and lie about it."

"I don't lie."

"Then we eat it and I lie about it," Silena said. "I'm better at it."

Clarisse fried the eggs with total focus and a certain amount of smoke, and the eggs came out exactly right, which she pretended not to notice. Thalia, forehead still down, sniffed the air and said, into the wood, "You people are so loud," and then ate the entire plate that was put in front of her without lifting her head more than four inches off the table, which the rest of them watched with genuine respect.

That was the morning, in the end: honey on everything, plates going around, the stove ticking as it cooled, somebody's feet on somebody's knee under the table, the low unhurried argument about nothing that a family has when it has stopped performing. Percy told the story of the mystery blanket at least three times as if it were new, and got the same reaction each time, which was Clarisse telling him that it was a boathouse blanket and him saying that it was gray, and both of them enjoying it more than either would admit. Annabeth ate her eggs with one hand and drew the frost on the back page of her field book with the other, and the drawing, when she held it up, was the lake in the silver, mist on the sheet, the boundary rock pale, and Thalia took one look at it and said, "Huh. That's ours," which was the highest compliment available and was received as one.

Under the table, Percy's hand found Annabeth's, the way it did, without announcement and without hurry. She turned her hand over and let his fingers close in hers on top of her knee, and went on explaining a drainage theory on Olive's behalf, and did not once look down at it, and did not once let go.

Silena watched them do it, from the stove, with the pan in her hand and nothing on her face at all. It was the small one, the under-the-table one, that got her every time. Her little brother could kiss his girlfriend in front of a whole beach and Silena could deal with that, she had recovered from that at ten o'clock last night and again at dawn, but the hand under the table, the thing that had no audience and no reason, the thing that was just where a hand wanted to be, which happened to be in another hand — she had no defense at all against that one, and the only thing she could do about it was put more honey in the tea, which she then had to drink.

Around ten, the house caught up with them.

Thalia went first, without ceremony, boots still on, straight down onto the bench with her flannel over her face, exactly as she had slept, as if she had been holding her spot. Clarisse held out another twenty minutes out of pure spite and then sat down in the big chair by the window and was asleep before her head settled, jaw set, arms crossed, absolutely unassailable. Percy, who had been upright on the bench exactly long enough to eat two more pieces of bread, folded slowly sideways until his shoulder was against Annabeth's and his head was in the exact nook of her neck that had his name on it, and Annabeth, without stopping what she was writing, lifted her arm, let him in, and went on writing with her notebook propped on his shoulder, which was a system nobody had approved and everybody defended.

Silena put the last of the dishes in the sink and stood a moment in the middle of her kitchen with her hands in a towel and looked at the room: three of them down, one of them leaning on a girl, the whole cabin gone still and warm and smelling of honey and woodsmoke, four blankets on four people, and the fifth person in the family standing alone in the middle of it with a towel.

That was the thing about being the one who fed everyone. You did the dishes.

She hung the towel on its hook, and pulled on her sweater, and went out quietly, because the skillet had to go back to the forge.

It had been borrowed on Thursday, in the middle of the first cold snap, when Silena had wanted to bake and Cabin Three's good pan had developed a theory about warping. She had walked it up to Hephaestus in the afternoon and left it on the bench by the door with a note, and now here it was, scoured clean, the note still pinned under the handle, and she needed to walk it up because leaving a thing with a note on it was a transaction, and now the transaction was done and the pan had to go home.

That was her excuse, and it was, in the way of most of her excuses, honest and also not true.

The path up to the forge ran behind the climbing wall and along the top of the hill where the pines thinned and the ground went to rock, and in October it was one of the best walks at camp, which Silena had noticed in the way she noticed everything, by walking it and saying nothing about it to anybody.

The camp was doing its Sunday morning underneath her as she went, all of it slow and gold, the smoke going straight up off the pavilion because there was no wind, the archery range empty, two Hermes kids carrying a canoe up from the water toward the racks with the look of people who had been told to and were not over it yet. The lake lay below and to the left, going silver, a whole summer's worth of water closing itself down without any help from anybody.

Halfway up, she stopped.

At the top of the rise, where the path came out onto the rock, Charlie Beckendorf was carrying a canvas of clay.

The clay was one of the big ones, the half-barrel sacks that came up from the river every October and weighed more than a person should be allowed to lift, and Olive Nakamura's whole map wall project lived on them, and Olive was ten and would have tried to carry it herself, and instead there was Beckendorf, one sack under one arm and the other balanced against his hip, big and quiet and slow, walking it down the hill toward the Hephaestus cabin like the sack weighed what a sweater weighed. Nobody was with him. Nobody had asked him. He had not been told about it by a harpy, or a centaur, or a note. He just happened to know that the clay came down on Sundays and that the person who used it could not lift it, and so here he was, in the gray of a first-frost morning, doing the thing, and doing it in the middle distance where he did not have to be thanked.

Silena stopped walking entirely.

It happened in her body before it happened in her head: the pause, mid-step, the way her breath did something at the top of the inhale and then gave up on finishing. Her hands were around the skillet handle, one on the towel and one on the iron, and she stood on the path in her sweater with her hair down and watched a boy carry a sack of mud down a hill because a little girl needed it, and she had absolutely no joke available. She looked for one. She reached into the place where she kept a line for everything, and came back empty-handed, with her face doing something she could feel and could not stop and would have paid money to interrupt.

Then he turned, because he had heard her boots, and there was a second where he was just a large tired person with a canvas sack, and then he saw her, and the whole frame changed.

"Beauregard," he said. "You left the pan."

"I brought the pan," she said. Her voice came out one note too bright, and she heard it happen, and hated it, and did it anyway. "I came to bring the pan. To give it back. It's the pan. It's from Thursday."

"I know what it's from." He set one sack down and took the skillet from her with the hand that was free, which he did lightly, in the ordinary way of a person who took heavy things from people by reflex and no longer thought about it. "Strap's riveted. Last week I put two rivets in the handle end because you had it wrapped with string, and string in a kitchen with a fire in it is a message you're sending to somebody."

Silena looked at the handle. Two small brass rivets, neat as punctuation, in exactly the place a person's thumb went when they tipped a full pan, and she had wrapped that handle with string in June and had been meaning to fix it since August and had said so, out loud, in the pavilion, once, to a table of people, in a sentence she had not remembered until right now.

"Oh," she said.

"It's nothing."

"It's not nothing, it's the handle."

"It's two rivets." He said it mildly, already picking the sack back up, already three sentences into the next thing, which was Olive's wall and how the clay wanted more sand in it and how the kiln would be cold until Wednesday. He did not look at her the way people looked at her. He was not performing, and he was not unkind, and he was not, as far as she could tell, thinking about her at all beyond the fact that she was a person on a path with a pan. He was just there, in the frost-smoke, carrying the whole morning on one shoulder, and he had put two rivets in her pan because she had said something six weeks ago and he had remembered it, and his hands were enormous, and he blushed, faintly, at the throat, when he said it was nothing — which meant he knew it was not, which meant he had thought about it, which was much worse.

"Anyway," Beckendorf said. "Tell your cabin the pump in the boathouse is frozen and I've got it. They don't need to come and fix it. Last time they came and fixed a thing they fixed it differently."

"That was Clarisse."

"That was definitely Clarisse," he agreed, and went on down the hill with the clay, and did not look back, and the frost broke under his boots the whole way, and Silena stood at the top of the path for four full seconds after he was out of sight, which was three seconds longer than she had ever stood anywhere in her life.

Then she went back down to the cabin to put more honey in the tea.

She caught herself doing it — hand on the honey jar, tea already sweet — and put the jar down, and stood in her kitchen with three people asleep around her and one person asleep on another person, and did the honest arithmetic, which was this. She had spent her whole life watching other people fall in love. She had been the audience for it, the best audience, the one who said *good for you* and meant it and made the cake. She knew the whole grammar of it from the outside, from every angle, from other people's weddings and other people's breakups and the way Annabeth went quiet when Percy walked into a room. She had simply never been on the inside of a sentence, and she had no idea what to do with her hands, and it had happened over a sack of clay on a Sunday, with absolutely no warning, and she had said the words *it's the pan* four times out loud.

"You're standing in the kitchen with a towel," Thalia said, from under her flannel, without moving.

"I'm thinking."

"You don't think. You stir." A pause. "You made the tea too sweet. It's on the stove. I can smell it from here."

"I made it the correct amount of sweet."

"You made it Silena sweet, which is sweet like an apology." Thalia did not move. "Whoever it is, I don't want to know. I've decided to be ignorant. It's a gift."

Silena drank the tea. It was, in fact, too sweet, and she drank all of it standing up in her own kitchen and told nobody anything, and that was the whole of her Sunday.

Percy woke up on the warm flat stone above the north beach at half past four in the afternoon with the sun on his face and the sound of somebody dragging a canoe.

He had not meant to come back down here. He had meant to nap in the cabin, in a bed, like a person, and he had gotten as far as the porch with the mystery blanket over his shoulder and then done the thing he always did, which was to end up following the lake down the hill again, because the lake was the only place at camp that never once asked him what he did with his evenings.

So he had lain down on the flat stone at the top of the beach, out of the shade, on the one rock that held heat through a frost like a banked oven, and slept for an hour and a half, and now he was awake, and the sun was gold and low, and Clarisse was thirty feet away dragging a canoe up the sand by herself.

"You're doing that wrong," he said, without moving.

"I'm doing it with my arms, which is the way it's done."

"You're doing it alone, which is the way it's done by people with a grudge."

"I don't have a grudge. I have a schedule. Canoes stowed before the end of the month, and the month started today, and I am not spending the last week of October dragging boats in the rain."

Percy rolled onto his back and looked at the sky, which had cleared while he slept and was now doing the particular October thing of being very blue and completely unhelpful about it, the way a sky is when it knows it has taken something away from you and intends to be beautiful while you notice. Then he got up, because it was Clarisse, and because a person did not lie on a warm rock while a person dragged a canoe, and went down the sand and got his hands under the other end.

The canoe came up the beach like a complaint.

"You're supposed to lift," Clarisse said.

"I am lifting."

"You're lifting with your face."

"It's my strongest feature."

They got it up onto the rack behind the boathouse and turned it over, and the hull, when it came up into the light, was streaked with the fine brown line of the high-water mark and one long scratch from the June storm that nobody had ever gotten around to sanding out. Percy stood with his hand flat on the overturned bottom of it for a second, and looked at the lake going silver down the slope, and had a thought he was not going to say out loud, which was that this was the last time this particular canoe would be upside down and dry until April, and that he had been in it in July with Annabeth in front and Clarisse shouting at him from the shore, and that the whole of that July was now the bottom of a pile.

"What," Clarisse said.

"Nothing."

"You've got a face."

"It's my strongest feature."

"It was a comment, not a compliment." She wiped her hands on her shorts and stood there a moment, arms crossed, looking at the rack of canoes the way she looked at a ledger. Four up. Two to go. Then she said, without looking at him, "You went out to the rock last night."

"I did."

"In the dark. Roped."

"You held the rope."

"I'm aware that I held the rope. That's the part I'm getting at." She squinted out at the boundary rock, small and white-Xed in the middle of the silver. "For a week I've been the one telling everybody to be careful about this lake, and last night I stood on the sand and held the other end of a line on a boy who was swimming out to a rock in October, and I didn't say a word about it. Not one. You know why?"

"Because you'd already banked the fire and you didn't want to get up."

"Because I looked at the water and I knew it was fine." She said it flat, the way she said the true things, with her face pointed away. "That's new. That's the new part. Not you swimming. Me knowing before it happened instead of after." She picked up the end of the next canoe and waited. "Come on. Two left. Then I'm putting the chair on the porch and never carrying it again."

They did the last two, and it hurt, and neither of them mentioned the hurting even once, which by the standard of Cabin Three was a conversation of considerable intimacy, and when it was done Clarisse went up the hill to strike the first four canoes off Chiron's winter list and did not say goodbye, and Percy stayed down on the sand a minute with his hands in his pockets, and then went up to the top of the beach and lay back down on the warm stone, because the sun had about an hour left in it and he had learned, over one summer, to spend an hour like that on purpose.

Annabeth found him there.

She came down the path with her field book under her arm and her hair still in the mess from the morning and a pencil behind her ear, and she saw him on the stone and stopped, and looked at the lake, and the whole of her went still the way it did when she had found a corner of something that fit.

"You're on the warm rock," she said.

"It's the last warm rock of the year. Somebody has to be on it."

"Somebody has to be on it," she agreed, and came and lay down beside him on the stone, on her side, facing him, with her field book pillowed under her arm, and neither of them said anything for a while, which was the correct usage of an hour.

The stone held the heat like a thing with a memory. The lake went on being silver. Down in the cove, past the willows, the heron stood on the bow of the half-sunk skiff with one leg folded and the whole of its patience intact, and the reeds along the margin, which had been silver with frost this morning, were dry and brown and clattering faintly in a wind too small to reach them.

"Silena's in the cabin with her hands in a towel," Annabeth said, after a while. "She's been doing something with honey in the tea and not saying anything about it. I've decided not to investigate."

"That's unlike you."

"It's exactly like me. I investigate the things I can draw."

He turned his head on the stone to look at her. The low gold light had gotten into her face and made her freckles come out over the bridge of her nose, and her eyes were closed, and there was graphite on her cheek, and she was not doing anything at all, which he had learned was the rarest thing about her and the thing he liked best.

"Annabeth," he said.

"Mm."

"You know the season's over."

"I know. I was at the desk with the clipboard. I'm aware of the paperwork."

"Does it bother you?"

She thought about it, which was one of the things he loved: that she never gave him the answer that was supposed to be given. "It bothers me in a way I don't have a name for," she said at last. "It isn't sad. The lake's still there. It's going to be there in February, under ice, doing whatever it does. But it's going to be a thing I'm not allowed in, and I've never had a thing in my life that was mine to be in, and now I do, and now it's closed, and I'm fourteen, and I want to argue with the calendar, and the calendar doesn't take questions."

"The calendar's a coward," Percy said.

"The calendar is a coward," she agreed. "Write it down. I'll file it under facts."

He kissed her then, slow, on the warm stone, in the last of the light.

There was nobody on the north beach but them and a heron, and the wind had gone somewhere else, and the stone was warm under his shoulder, and Annabeth's hand came up and found his jaw and stayed there, thumb against his cheekbone, and she tasted of the honey tea she had been complaining about, and she kissed him back the way she did everything, with her whole attention, unhurried, as if the measurement of it mattered. He felt the little dry edge of graphite on her fingertips against his face. He felt her breath change and even out. Somewhere a long way off the pavilion bell rang twice for something nobody needed, and neither of them moved for it, and the heron out in the cove shifted its weight from one leg to the other and settled, and the light went on doing what it was doing, which was leaving, slowly, without anybody's permission.

When they stopped they stayed close, foreheads on each other, breathing the same cold inch of air.

"We should go back up," she said, without moving.

"We should absolutely go back up."

They did not go back up. They lay on the warm rock with the wool blanket — the real one, Clarisse's, the one that had come down with them the first time — pulled over both of them, and watched the light go out of the far shore one parcel at a time, and she traced the seam of his sleeve with one finger for no reason he could find, and he held onto her hand and did not count a single thing.

The map wall had a smell that belonged to the last hour of a Sunday, which was the smell of lamp oil, cold screens, and a building deciding whether to light the rest of itself.

Annabeth worked by the one lamp with her sleeves rolled and her pencil moving, and the parlor was quiet behind her the way it was quiet on the first night of anything. The camp had come back to itself after a day of lying around like a house after a party: the pavilion was clearing the last of its tables, the archery range had closed, the forge had gone cold a while ago, and somebody down by the cabins was playing a guitar badly and with real commitment. Out past the screens the lake had gone from silver to slate, and the sky above it was doing the October thing it did at dusk, holding light on the water after the water deserved it.

She was drawing the frost.

Not the lake. The frost: the white line that had come down the reed margin in the dark, and the thin silvering along the north path, and the low ground by the boathouse where the first cold had sat and left a mark. It went onto the wall as a light pencil wash over the existing sheets, over the blue-green of the lake and the drawn contours of the shore, and when she stepped back, it made the whole map look like a page that had been out in the cold, which was exactly the point.

Because a map was, in the end, a record of what had happened to a place. Not what was there. What had happened.

She worked the north edge last, and the blank under the ridge line looked back at her the way it had looked at her all week: an empty mouth of paper, a place the drawn country ran out. She stood in front of it with the pencil in her hand and did the arithmetic she had been putting off since the porch.

Chiron had said Saturday. Chiron had written it in his small square hand on the corner of an almanac page, which made it real, which made it a thing that would happen. And between now and Saturday there were five days of an ordinary week, and after Saturday there would be a slab and a seep and a set of measurements instead of a blank.

She did not draw the spring. Drawing it now would be a guess, and there were no guesses on the wall. What she did instead was smaller and, to her, enormous: at the top of the blank, in her small neat record hand, she pencilled a single line.

NORTH SPRING — SURVEY PENDING.

Then, underneath, in brackets, tiny: (THALIA — SLAB. SEEP. CAIRN, BLUE RAG.)

She looked at it for a minute, the way she looked at anything she had just made, checking it for the flaw. There wasn't one. It was a heading on a page that had been blank since August, and it had her sister's name in it, and it did not pretend to know a single thing it did not know.

Behind her, the screen door banged.

"Beauregard says the blanket's from the arena," Percy said, coming in with the mystery blanket over his arm and sand still on the hem of it. "She says the Hermes cabin lost one in September and put a sign on the board about it, and she says the sign is still up and there is a reward of one granola bar, and that the blanket is gray and has a burn on the corner and is therefore the exact blanket, and she says — " he drew himself up into his best imitation of Silena, which was not good — " *Percy, you cannot steal a blanket that has a sign.* "

"The sign is a granola bar," Annabeth said. "You'd be trading a blanket for a granola bar."

"A granola bar I don't want."

"Then keep the blanket."

"I've already given it back," Percy said, and dropped it on the table, and she looked at him, and he shrugged. "I gave it back. It's a Hermes blanket. Somebody slept under it and got cold somewhere and thought about it later. That's a real blanket. You don't get to keep those." He came and stood beside her at the wall, close, and put his hands in his pockets, and looked at what she had drawn, and read the small pencil line twice. "Survey pending."

"Survey pending."

"You put her name on the wall."

"I put her name on the drawing of the thing she found," Annabeth said. "It's her slab."

Percy was quiet a second. Then he said, "She's going to come in here and see her own name on the wall and pretend it's nothing, and then she's going to go outside and tell somebody who doesn't care."

"Probably Silena."

"Definitely Silena," he agreed. "Silena knows everything anyway. She knew about the blanket before we did." He took his hand out of his pocket and put something on the drafting table: a folded paper, folded along the old creases, with the corner worn soft from a day in a fleece. "That's for tomorrow morning. Don't read it tonight."

"Why not?"

"Because it's a morning note and you'll read it at night and be up all night improving it."

Annabeth picked it up, and did not open it, and put it in her pocket with the others, and the paper went in warm, the only warm thing in a room that had gone cold an hour ago.

"Annabeth."

"Mm."

"Today was the first day of the cold," Percy said, looking at the map, at the lake and the frost and the small pencilled heading where the blank had been. "I'm not being atmospheric. I just want it noted. We got up off a beach in the frost, and everybody lived, and you put a heading on your wall, and Clarisse put the chair on the porch and never carried it again, and Silena did something with honey she won't talk about, and Thalia slept on a bench with her boots on. That's the whole day."

"That's the whole day," Annabeth said. She looked at the wall — the whole summer drawn out in overlapping sheets, the lake in blue-green wash, the north path, the frost coming down over the reed margin in a light hand — and then she reached over, without ceremony, and switched off the lamp.

Annabeth turned the lamp down until the map's edges were barely visible. Percy stayed where he was, looking at the page she'd added after the overnight. There was an empty rectangle beside the line showing the frost, and he put a finger just above it.

"What's that for?"

"Measurements we didn't take."

"That's inconveniently honest."

"It's better than making up a number." She shut the field book and slid it under the wall map, leaving the rectangle blank. "I want to know how far the frost came down the bank. We can go back when it's light."

Percy looked toward the window. The night had already taken the path beyond the porch, and Chiron had posted a notice about slick ground along the lower steps.

"Not now."

"I wasn't proposing now."

"You had the face."

"I have a face all the time, Percy."

"A suspiciously useful one."

She reached for the chalk beside the slate and added two words beneath tomorrow's date: FROST LINE. Then she looked at the rest of the list, where Silena had written the bread count and Clarisse had squeezed a tool inspection into the smallest available space.

"That's a lot of work for a day we keep calling ordinary," Annabeth said.

"Then maybe the ordinary days are the ones where people do their jobs."

"That almost sounded like something responsible."

"Don't spread it around."

She replaced the chalk and slipped her hand into his. As they went out, Percy pulled the parlor door shut carefully enough that the latch didn't wake anyone upstairs. The first cold evening of October had made the wood swell. He tried the latch once more to be certain it caught.

"You're going to put that on Clarisse's list," Annabeth said.

"Obviously."

She laughed, and they took the safer upper path to the cabins. The frost measurement could wait until daylight; the door was secure for tonight.
