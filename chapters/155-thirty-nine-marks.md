## Chapter 155: Thirty-Nine Marks

Percy woke before the conch with lake water in his head.

Not a dream of it, exactly. More the leftover hum from the night before, low and even, like a note held too long on a string. He lay in the Cabin Three bunk with the wool blanket kicked down to his knees and one arm flung over his eyes, listening to the ordinary sounds sort themselves out: Clarisse breathing in the bunk against the wall, slow and unbothered; the screen door ticking as it cooled; somewhere out past the eaves a jay working itself up to an opinion.

Fourteen and tired in a way sleep hadn't fixed. His shoulders ached. His fingertips still felt pruned. On the floor by his bunk his camp shirt from last night hung half off the laundry hook, stiff with dried freshwater, the hem still smelling faintly of willow bark and mud. He'd thrown it there at one in the morning when they'd finally come up the hill. He hadn't bothered with the sneakers. They sat under the bunk with lake grit in the treads.

He didn't get up right away. He stared at the bunk above him and ran through the inventory the way Clarisse had taught him.

Panel: out of the hull, counted, thirty-nine. Location: under Annabeth's bed in Cabin One, wrapped in her folded gray shirt. Heron: last seen leaving over the willows at dusk. Lake: ringing, cold seam, outward push, fish gone from the cove. Told: nobody yet. Owed: Clarisse tonight, which was now this morning. Chiron in the morning, which was now later this morning. Thalia: problem unsolved.

He swung his legs out, fished his spare shirt from the cubby — sun-bleached orange, the neck gone soft from a hundred washes, sleeves cut short last summer when Clarisse had taken the shears to all three of their shirts and declared that tan lines were a small price for not rowing in wet cuffs — and pulled it on. His jeans from yesterday were still damp at the cuffs. He rolled them one more turn and left them. Bare feet on the cabin floorboards, cold. September was doing that thing where the days still believed in summer but the mornings had started filing dissent.

Clarisse didn't wake when he crossed the room. She slept the way she did everything else: committed, elbows out, one hand curled around the edge of her blanket like somebody might try to take it. Her clipboard hung from the bedpost on its usual nail, the top sheet blank. Since she'd ripped out the ledger page two weeks ago she hadn't replaced it with anything. The nail looked strangely bare without the old rule glaring down at them.

Percy slipped out before he could wake her. The air outside hit his face clean and pine-sharp. The grass between the cabins was wet enough to darken his feet. Mist hung low over the canoe lake in long torn bands, the kind that burned off by eight. The Big House weathervane stood dead still. No wind. Good. He wanted to see what the water did without any excuses.

He took the boat ramp path at a jog-trot, sneakers unlaced in his hand, and came out on the dock boards just as the east went pale gold behind the far pines.

The lake lay flat and doubled. Mist sat on it in patches. The swimming buoys rode their chains without swinging. Out past them the water had that early-morning skin on it, the cool top layer that would warm by ten and go glassy by noon.

And on the second piling down, feet tucked, neck folded, stood the heron.

Percy stopped with one sneaker half on.

It was the same bird. He would have sworn it in any court his father kept. Same gray, same black eyebrow stripe, same patient, unhurried stillness. It stood facing the cove end of the lake, not the open water, not the fish-rich shallows by the marsh. It watched the willow cove the way a lifeguard watches a lane.

"You're up early," Percy said.

The heron turned its head half a degree. One yellow eye considered him. It did not fly.

Percy sat down on the dock boards, legs dangling, and looked where the bird looked. From here the cove was a darker green notch, willow crowns still black against the lightening sky. He couldn't see the seam from this distance. He could feel it, though, the way he felt everything else about this lake — a faint wrongness at the base of his skull, like a toothache in a tooth he didn't have. The outward push. The cold that didn't mix.

He put his fingertips to the dock wood, then to the water between the pilings. Morning water, cold on top, colder underneath. Normal here. Out there, past the boundary rock with its fresh white X, it wouldn't be normal.

"You stayed all night, huh?" he said to the heron. "On the sunken skiff? Holding the boat's place?"

The heron shifted one foot, resettled. A dab of mud clung to its left leg. Cove mud.

Percy felt something loosen and tighten at the same time. Company was company. But company that kept vigil meant there was something to keep vigil over.

"Yeah," he said. "Me too."

He didn't go out to the cove. That had been the rule they'd made on the path last night without quite making it: not alone, not without daylight and a second pair of eyes and a plan. Annabeth would kill him — precisely, with citations — if he went poking at a cut hole in a hull before breakfast. Clarisse would kill him more generally, with whatever was nearest.

So he sat with the heron and watched the mist tear and mend, and after a while he heard the path gravel crunch behind him and knew the step before he turned.

Annabeth came down the ramp with her hands in the pockets of her camp hoodie — the faded gray one with the frayed cuffs she wore on cold mornings over her shirt — and her hair still in last night's braid, gone fuzzy at the edges. Her eyes had the puffy, determined look of somebody who had slept three hours and decided that was sufficient.

She stopped when she saw the heron. Then she came and sat beside him without a word and leaned her shoulder into his, hard, the way tired people lean when they don't have the budget for grace.

"Morning," Percy said.

"Morning." She looked at the bird. "Still here."

"Still here. Cove mud on its leg."

"I know." She tipped her head against his shoulder for exactly four seconds, then sat up straight again, because Annabeth Chase at dawn was still Annabeth Chase. "We need to talk about who we tell. Before breakfast. Before anyone's awake enough to make it weird."

"Agreed," Percy said. "But first — did you sleep?"

"Some." She held up thumb and forefinger a half inch apart. "You?"

"Some." He matched her measurement, then widened it slightly, conceding. "Okay, more than you. Clarisse snores like a bilge pump and I still slept."

That got the smallest smile. The heron ignored them both with magnificent professionalism.

Percy took her cold hand in his warm one and held it on the dock boards between them, and they sat like that while the light came up gold along the piling tops and the lake kept its counsel underneath.

---

Annabeth had not intended to take the panel out from under the bed before dawn.

She had intended to sleep. She had laid the board — wrapped in her gray camp shirt with the real pockets, notched side in — on the floorboards under her bunk, pushed it back against the wall where her spare blanket would hide it, and told herself, firmly, in the voice she used on Olive Nakamura when Olive wanted to re-survey at midnight: morning. It keeps till morning.

Then she'd lain awake for an hour listening to the Cabin One rafters tick and thinking about her hands.

Her hands knew the shape of the cove. That was the part from last night that wouldn't settle. Not the panel, not the thirty-nine, not the seam. Her hands. On the flat rock, on the gunwale, on the panel's edge — they'd moved like they'd been there before, finding holds before her eyes found them. Eight feet, she'd said, without being told. Freshwater, she'd said, the first week, before anyone told her. It sat wrong, like a map with a fold in it.

At five-forty, with the sky going gray at the shutters, she'd given up, rolled out of bed, and pulled the bundle out by one corner.

Now she sat cross-legged on her bunk with the morning coming sideways through the shutters in thin bars, the panel across her knees, and a pencil behind her ear she didn't remember putting there. She wore the camp hoodie over her tank top and yesterday's shorts, feet bare against the cool floorboards, braid coming undone down her back. The cabin smelled of cedar and paper and the lemon oil she used on her drafting tools. Outside, somebody's shower ran. Ordinary morning.

The panel was not ordinary in the morning.

In daylight the wood showed its age more honestly: tea-colored on the outside face, pale on the notched underside where it had been hidden against the cut. The edges where she'd pried it free were clean. Tool-cut, no splintering. Whoever had fitted it had measured twice. Annabeth ran her thumb along the starboard edge and felt the faint warmth again — not sun-warmth, the cabin was still cool — but the low, held warmth she'd felt last night, strongest under the notches, as if the wood remembered being touched there.

Thirty-nine notches. She counted again, because counting twice was the rule. Nine groups of four with a slash through each, plus three loose at the end. The cuts were narrow, V-shaped, made with a small straight blade. A marking knife, maybe. Or a sharpened awl. Consistent pressure, consistent angle. Someone patient. Someone who came back.

She was halfway through the third recount when her thumb caught.

She stopped. Went back. Ran the pad of her finger over the twenty-third mark, then the twenty-second, then the twenty-third again.

The twenty-third notch was different.

It was cut in the opposite direction — the V leaned left where all the others leaned right — and twice as deep, the groove dark at the bottom where the blade had bitten twice. And at its base, almost too small to see without tipping the board to the light, a tiny cross-nick, a second cut no longer than an eyelash, crossing the main groove at the bottom like a footnote.

Annabeth sat very still with the bars of light moving across her knees.

Everything else about the panel said repetition. Same hand, same tool, same count, thirty-nine times. This one mark said interruption. A different day. A different pressure. A hand that had hesitated, or hurried, or needed to remember that this one wasn't like the others.

She got her notebook — the blue-covered one with the torn first page — and turned to a fresh sheet. She drew the panel in plan, underside up, and marked each group in her small square hand. At the twenty-third position she didn't just draw the notch. She drew it twice, larger, in section, with the cross-nick exaggerated and labeled: 23 — OPP. DIR., 2X DEEP, X-NICK @ BASE. DIFFERENT TOOL? DIFFERENT DAY? She wrote the time, the light, the temperature of the wood against her wrist. She wrote WARMEST UNDER NOTCHES — RE-READ? and underlined it.

Then she sat back and looked at what she'd written, and felt the familiar click-and-drop of a map settling into a new shape. Not a solution. A dimension. Whoever had counted thirty-nine somethings had needed to mark that one count as other. Which meant the counts weren't identical. Which meant whatever was being counted could vary.

She wrapped the panel again in the gray shirt, tied it with the spare bootlace she kept for bundling surveys, and sat with it on her knees while the cabin filled with light.

One mark different. Small. Concrete. Not a monster, not a message, not a name. Just a hand, one day out of thirty-nine, pressing harder and cutting back the other way, as if to say: this one. Remember this one.

She pressed her palm flat to the wrapped bundle, feeling the warmth through the cloth, and made the decision she'd been circling since midnight.

Clarisse first. As promised. Then the other three together. Then Chiron. Thalia — Thalia needed a separate plan, because Thalia leaving for four days with a secret under her sister's bed was a different shape of problem than Thalia staying with one.

She laced her boots, slung the bundle under her arm inside a canvas tote so it just looked like survey gear, and went down to the lake to find Percy before anyone else could find them.

---

Percy heard her coming before he saw her, because the dock boards telegraphed every step and because Annabeth walked like she mapped — heel first, even pace, no wasted motion.

He didn't turn. He kept his eyes on the cove notch and felt her sit down beside him again, this time with something canvas and rectangular bumping his hip.

"You brought it," he said.

"I brought it. Don't look at it here." Annabeth settled the tote across her knees and leaned into him again, shoulder to shoulder, the way they'd sat last night on the sunken skiff's bench. Her hoodie sleeve was soft against his arm. She smelled of cedar shavings and sleep. "I found something. This morning. In the light."

Percy glanced sideways. Her face had the particular stillness it got when she'd found a thing she didn't yet want to be excited about. "One new thing?"

"One." She kept her voice low, though the only audience was the heron, which had tucked its head and appeared to be asleep standing up. "The twenty-third mark. It's cut backwards. Deeper. There's a little cross at the bottom. Different pressure, maybe different blade. Everything else is repetition. That one is — annotated."

Percy let that sit. The mist thinned over the buoys. A fish rose out past the swim line with a soft ploop and was gone.

"So whoever counted," he said slowly, "counted something that wasn't always the same."

"Yes." She nodded once, sharp. "Thirty-nine readings. Thirty-eight the same. One different enough to mark."

"That's not nothing."

"No. It's not nothing and it's not enough." She rubbed her thumb along the tote strap, back and forth, a small worried motion she would have hated anyone else noticing. "Percy. I don't want to carry this alone for another day. I meant what I said last night — Clarisse tonight, Chiron in the morning. It's morning."

Percy picked at a splinter on the dock board. His hand found hers without looking, fingers slotting between hers, lake-cold against sleep-warm. They sat like that while the sun cleared the pines and laid a warm line across their knees.

"Clarisse first," he said. "We promised. Her shore, her boathouse, her call about the skiffs. She'll be at the boathouse by seven doing hull checks."

"And then?" Annabeth tipped her head to look at him. The braid fuzz made a halo in the backlight. She looked fourteen and forty at once, tired and exact. "Chiron before lunch? Thalia — she's packing for the survey. Chiron said four days. If she leaves this morning we lose the window."

Percy thought about Thalia at the archery range until nine, about the set of her shoulders when she got protective, about the way she'd stood between Annabeth and a storm drain last month like a lightning rod with opinions. Telling Thalia a thing like this right before she walked out for four days was either necessary or cruel, and he couldn't tell which.

"We tell Clarisse alone," he said. "Then we figure out Thalia before we do the five-together thing. I don't want to blindside her in front of everybody."

Annabeth was quiet. Then she turned her hand over in his and laced their fingers properly, palm to palm, and rested their joined hands on top of the tote, over the hidden panel, like a seal.

"Okay," she said. "Clarisse first. Together."

"After breakfast? Boathouse?"

"Before breakfast. Boathouse now." She stood, pulling him up with her, and for a moment they were just two tired fourteen-year-olds on a dock at sunrise, holding hands, with a heron pretending not to watch. Percy reached up and tucked a loose strand of her hair back behind her ear, and she let him, and leaned the smallest fraction into his palm before stepping back.

"Come on," she said, softer. "If we wait till we're rested we'll wait forever."

The heron opened one eye as they left. Percy could have sworn it watched them up the ramp, toward the boathouse, toward the first telling.

---

Clarisse woke to an empty cabin and knew, instantly, that she was being managed.

It was the quiet that gave it away. No Percy clattering the water bucket. No wet sneakers thumping. Just the screen door easing shut somewhere down the hill and the jay still running its mouth outside. She sat up with her hair flattened on one side and her spare blue cutoff-sleeve shirt twisted around her shoulders — the one she kept for rowing days, sleeves cut wide so the seams didn't chafe — and stared at Percy's stripped bunk.

"Gone to the lake," she muttered to the empty room. "Of course. Boy goes to the lake when he's got something to not say."

She didn't hurry. That was the thing about being fifteen and the eldest in a cabin of three who acted like seven: hurrying told them they'd won. She pulled her jeans off the hook — stiff, salt-faded at the knees from a summer of beaching skiffs, cuffs still dark from yesterday's inventory — and yanked them on. She checked the clipboard on its nail out of habit, found the top sheet still blank, and left it there. No ten-minutes-per-occurrence. Not anymore. She'd ripped that page out and the lake had refused to sink it and she'd been reassessing her policy ever since, which mostly meant standing in the boathouse with her hands on her hips feeling like she'd lost a fence and gained a field.

She found them where she knew she'd find them: boathouse doorway, trying to look casual and failing at it in two different styles.

Percy leaned against the doorframe with his arms crossed, bare feet now in dry socks and the unlaced sneakers finally tied, orange shirt hanging loose over damp-cuffed jeans. Annabeth stood beside him with a canvas tote held in both hands like it contained either a baby or a bomb, hoodie sleeves shoved to the elbows, boots laced double. Both of them had the pale, bright-eyed look of kids who'd stayed up too late and gotten up too early on purpose.

"You're both terrible at sneaking," Clarisse said by way of greeting, unlocking the boathouse and shouldering past them. "If you're going to ambush me, bring food."

"We brought something better," Percy said.

"Nothing's better than food. Spit it out."

Annabeth glanced at Percy. Percy nodded. Annabeth set the tote on the workbench, moved aside a coil of spare painter line and the tin of copper tacks, and unwrapped the gray shirt to show the board.

Clarisse looked at it for a long time without touching it.

It was a plain rectangle of wood, tea-dark on one side, pale and notched on the other. It lay on Annabeth's folded shirt on the scarred workbench between the oar rack and the hull-good clipboard, and in the dusty boathouse light it looked profoundly unremarkable — like a piece of firewood somebody had gotten precious about.

"That's the thing from the cove," Clarisse said flatly. "The panel. You pulled it."

"Last night," Annabeth said. "From the starboard hull of the sunken skiff. It was fitted over a cut hole. Thirty-nine tally marks on the underside. We counted twice."

Clarisse picked it up. She turned it over once, hefted it, ran her thumb down the notches the way she'd run a thumb down a rope to check for wear. Her hands were broader than Annabeth's, knuckles scabbed from hauling skiffs, nails cut short. The board looked small in them.

"It's warm," she said.

"Yes," Percy said. "Warmest under the notches. Like it's been read. A lot."

Clarisse grunted. She carried the board to the doorway light, tilted it, squinted. Her mouth flattened.

"Twenty-three's backwards," she said.

Annabeth blinked. "You saw that — already?"

"I'm not blind. I do inventory." Clarisse set the board down with more care than she'd picked it up with. "Okay. Talk. From the top. No cartographer interpretive dance, no fish-boy feelings-first. What, where, when."

So they told her. Percy did most of it — the rings without wind, the seam that didn't mix, the outward current against the calm, the skiff twelve feet off her HOLD PLACE mark, the hole cut with a tool, the heron standing on the bow and then walking the gunwale toward the arrow, the pacing shadow in the reeds, the fish massed past the boundary rock, the lake saying not yet at the white X. Annabeth filled in measurements, times, the eight-feet admission she still couldn't source, the ozone smell like a storm that didn't happen. They didn't embroider. Clarisse hated embroidery more than she hated wet rope.

Clarisse listened the way she listened to damage reports: arms crossed, jaw working, eyes going from the board to their faces and back. When they finished she didn't speak for a full ten seconds. The boathouse creaked. Water lapped the ramp. Somewhere up the hill the breakfast bell hadn't yet rung.

"You're sure about the current," she said finally. "Cold, outward, against the wind."

"Yes," Percy said.

"And the seam. Doesn't mix."

"Yes," Annabeth said.

"And the bird's still here. At dawn. On our dock."

"Second piling," Percy said. "Cove mud on its leg."

Clarisse exhaled through her nose. She picked up the board again, turned it notched-side up, and laid her palm flat over the marks without touching them, just feeling the warmth rise.

"You're idiots for pulling it without telling me," she said. "You're idiots for going in at dusk without a third. You're idiots for sitting on a flat rock in the dark watching a shadow pace." She looked up. Her expression had gone from flat to something younger and more annoyed. "And you're my idiots, so now it's my problem too. Good job. I hope you're happy."

Percy felt something in his shoulders drop three inches. "So you believe us."

"I believe the board's warm and twenty-three's backwards and you both look like warmed-over death," Clarisse said bluntly. "I believe you found a cut hole in my lake and somebody counted thirty-nine of something. I don't believe anything else yet because there's nothing else to believe." She tapped the board once with a blunt finger. "This stays out of the firewood pile. This goes to Chiron today. And nobody goes back in that cove without me or a plan. Preferably both."

"Agreed," Annabeth said quickly. "That's what we wanted — Chiron before lunch. But Thalia—"

"Thalia's leaving." Clarisse cut her off, not unkindly, just finally. "Boundary survey. Four days. She's packing at Cabin One right now, probably trying to fit four days of socks into a two-day pack." She looked at Annabeth, and her voice dropped half a register, grudging in the way that meant she was saying something she didn't want to bill for. "You haven't told her."

"No," Annabeth admitted. "Last night we said — Clarisse tonight, Chiron tomorrow. Thalia — we didn't — she's protective. If we tell her right before she walks out—"

"If you don't tell her and she hears it from Chiron on the trail, she'll come back and kill you both," Clarisse said. "Worse. She'll be hurt." She turned the board over again, tea-side up, as if giving them a minute. "Look. Thalia's my sister too, in the way that counts. All five of us. You tell the protective one before she leaves or you don't tell her at all until she's back, and if you pick door two you better be ready to live with it."

Percy looked at his sister — fifteen, blunt, standing in the dusty light with lake grit on her boots and a warm board in her hands like it might bite — and felt the old fear of hers sitting right under the surface: that he would leave, that the lake would keep him or the world would take him, that staying was a thing other people did. He'd told her once he didn't know how to stay anywhere. She'd never let him forget he'd said it, because Clarisse kept everything, just like the lake.

"We're not leaving," he said quietly. "We're telling. That's staying."

Clarisse held his gaze, then jerked her chin once, accepting it the way she accepted an oar shipped correctly — without ceremony, but noted.

"Fine," she said. "Here's the policy while I'm reassessing my policy: board goes in the boathouse locker till after breakfast. Locked. You two eat. Then we do Thalia. All five if we can get Silena. No more pairs sneaking. I'm sick of pairs sneaking."

Annabeth opened her mouth, closed it, nodded. "Okay."

"And Percy." Clarisse pointed at his cuffs, still dark and damp. "Roll those again before Silena sees. She'll have opinions."

That was as close to absolution as Clarisse gave before breakfast. Percy took it.

---

Thalia had her pack open on her bunk and her temper half-packed with it.

The Cabin One shutters stood wide to the morning, letting in gold across the floorboards. She knelt beside her duffel in her worn boots — the brown pair with the left lace replaced by orange paracord — and her archery bracer still strapped to her forearm from dawn practice. Her dark hair was pulled back in a low knot, practical, two strands sticking to her neck.

Four days. Boundary survey. Chiron's assignment, handed over two nights ago with a map case and a list of markers to check from the north ridge to the old property line. She'd done it before. She liked it, mostly — walking the edge of camp with a purpose, nobody needing her to be careful with her spark, nobody watching Annabeth out of the corner of their eye to see if little sister was keeping up.

Only this morning the packing wasn't going right. The socks wouldn't fit. The map case kept sliding. And her sister hadn't come home until one in the morning smelling of lake mud, and had left again before dawn with a tote bag and that look.

Thalia zipped the duffel with more force than necessary just as the door opened.

Annabeth came in first, Percy behind her, Clarisse behind him like a tugboat escorting two wayward skiffs. Thalia looked up, took in the formation, and leaned back on her heels.

"Oh good," she said dryly. "A delegation. I love delegations before coffee. They always mean somebody did something."

"Hey to you too," Percy said.

"Hey, fish boy. You're in my cabin before breakfast, which means you're either hungry or guilty." Thalia's eyes flicked to Annabeth's tote, to Clarisse's folded arms, back to Annabeth's face. Her teasing edge stayed, but something protective moved in underneath it, the way it always did when Annabeth looked tired. "You look like hell, Beth. Both of you look like hell. Clarisse looks annoyingly rested, which means she slept and you didn't tell her till morning."

Clarisse snorted. "Give the girl a prize."

"We told her this morning," Annabeth said. She sat down on the edge of Thalia's bunk, which put her shoulder to shoulder with her sister's pack, and set the tote on her knees. "Thalia. You're leaving today. Four days. We have something — we found something last night in the cove. We told Clarisse first because it's her shore and we promised. We need to tell you before you go, but we need to do it right because — because if we tell you wrong you'll cancel the survey and stay."

Thalia went very still. The dry amusement drained out of her face and left the older-sister attention underneath, sharp and absolute.

"Try me," she said.

So Annabeth told her — shorter this time, practiced now, precise and planning even when tired: the rings, the seam, the current, the fitted panel, thirty-nine, the heron, the pacing shadow, the fish gone past the rock. Percy added the lake parts in his wry, observant shorthand — the hum under the gunwale, the not-yet at the boundary rock, the cold that didn't mix. Clarisse stood with her back to the door and added the only part that was hers: "Board's warm. Twenty-three's backwards. Locker in the boathouse till after breakfast."

Thalia listened without interrupting, which was how Annabeth knew she was worried. Thalia-at-ease interrupted constantly. Thalia-worried went quiet and kept her hands busy — in this case, lacing and unlacing the paracord bootlace, over and over.

When they finished, Thalia looked at the tote. "Show me."

Annabeth unwrapped the gray shirt and laid the panel across Thalia's pack. Thalia picked it up, turned it notched-side up, ran her thumb over the marks without touching quite, the way Clarisse had. She found the twenty-third mark on her own in under ten seconds. She tipped it to the light to see the cross-nick.

"Huh," she said.

That was all for a moment. Then she set the board down carefully on the bunk blanket, stood up, and walked to the shutters. She stood there with her back to them, looking out toward the lake end of camp, where the willow crowns showed green over the cabin roofs. Her shoulders had gone up around her ears.

"You're fourteen," she said to the shutters. "You're both fourteen. You went into a cove at dusk with a current that shouldn't exist and a hole cut in a hull and a thing pacing in the reeds, and you pulled a board off it and brought it home in a tote bag."

"We had each other," Annabeth said quietly. "And we had a plan. Record first, then touch. And we didn't go past the flat rock alone—"

"You went at all." Thalia turned. Her eyes were bright, angry in the way that was really fear with better posture. Static lifted the loose strands of her hair for half a second before she forced it down. "Do you have any idea what it sounds like from over here? My little sister and her boyfriend in eight feet of weird water with something counting?"

Percy felt Annabeth flinch beside him — not at boyfriend, at little sister — and he put his hand on the bunk blanket near hers, not quite holding, just there.

"Thalia," he said. "We did the careful version. We came back. We're telling."

"I know you're telling." Thalia dragged a hand down her face. "Gods, I know. That's the only reason I'm not — okay. Okay. Let me think." She paced two steps, turned, paced back. The flannel overshirt swung. "If I stay, Chiron's survey doesn't get done and the north markers don't get checked and somebody else has to do it in the rain next week. If I go, I'm four days out with you two sitting on a counted door in my lake—"

"Our lake," Annabeth said, small but firm. "My map. My cove."

"Our lake," Thalia corrected herself immediately, and some of the tension went out of her jaw. "Sorry. Our lake. Your map." She came back to the bunk and sat down hard beside Annabeth, pack and panel and all, and slung an arm around her sister's shoulders. Annabeth leaned into it at once, the way Grace sisters leaned — all at once and entirely, like they'd rehearsed falling. "Beth. You did right telling me. Even right-before-I-leave. Don't — don't do the thing where you decide I'm too protective to tell. I'm protective because I'm your sister. That's the job. You don't get to fire me from it to make it easier."

Annabeth's eyes went shiny. She blinked hard, precise even about that. "I wasn't going to not tell you."

"You thought about it," Thalia said, dry again, teasing again, but gentler. "I can see the thought all over Fish Boy's face. He's got guilt like a hat."

"I wear many hats," Percy said. "Guilt is the orange one."

That got a snort out of Clarisse and a reluctant smile out of Thalia. Thalia ruffled Percy's hair hard enough to hurt, the way she did when she liked him and hated that she liked him.

"Okay," Thalia said. "Here's what's happening. I'm still leaving — Chiron's counting on the survey and I'm not flaking because the lake got weird, the lake's always weird, that's why we live here. But I'm not leaving for four days with a secret under your bed. So we do the five-together thing before I pack the food. Silena, boathouse or north beach, everybody sees the board, everybody hears the same story once, no telephone game. Then we take it to Chiron together and he tells us not to be idiots, and then I go and you four stay out of the cove till I'm back or till Chiron says otherwise. Deal?"

Annabeth looked at Percy. Percy looked at Clarisse. Clarisse shrugged — deal.

"Deal," Annabeth said.

Thalia squeezed her shoulder once, hard, and stood. "Good. Now get out of my cabin so I can fit socks in a bag. And Percy — roll your cuffs. You look like you waded here."

"I did wade here," Percy said. "Emotionally."

"Out," Thalia said, but she was smiling.

Silena knew something was happening before anyone told her, because the bread told her.

She stood in the Cabin Three doorway with a flour-dusted tea towel over one arm and a warm loaf wrapped in a second towel against her chest, and watched the four of them come up the path in a formation that had argument written all over it.

Silena was sixteen, and she wore her morning practically, but with attention. Her camp shirt was the soft yellow one gone pale at the seams, flour on the front where she'd leaned into the kneading, sleeves pushed to the elbows. Her hair was pinned up with two pencils, a few strands coming down in the warming air.

Jasmine, the Cabin Three cat, wove around her ankles. The morning smelled of pine warming and the good yeasty smell coming off the loaf.

"Oh good," Silena called. "My siblings look conspiratorial. I baked for conspiracies."

Percy broke formation first. He came up the steps and leaned his forehead briefly against her shoulder, the way Poseidon siblings greeted when words were too much work. He smelled of lake and sleep. His orange shirt had gone crooked at the collar. Silena bumped him with her hip.

"You look terrible, little brother," she said warmly. "Sit. Bread will fix some of it."

"Only some?" Percy said.

"Bread has limits. Even mine." She looked past him to the others. "Thalia, you were supposed to be packing food, not collecting sisters. Annabeth, you didn't sleep. Clarisse, you're carrying something like it's going to explode."

"It's wood," Clarisse said. "It won't explode."

"Everything you carry looks like it's going to explode, love. It's your walk." Silena unwrapped the loaf a corner and tore the heel off. She handed it to Thalia, who took it automatically. "Eat. Then talk. Then we decide."

Thalia ate. That was how Silena knew it was serious — Thalia Grace eating bread standing up without arguing about the crust.

They ended up at the north beach, because the north beach was theirs. The Poseidon cabin's private picnic spot, pine-needled and quiet, with the flat stones for sitting and the little fire ring nobody used in September and the water lapping clean and freshwater-sweet against the pebbles. The morning had warmed into the kind of early-September blue-and-gold that made the lake look poured. No mist now. Just light coming down in sheets and the far shore sharp at the edges. The French drain up on the north path ticked faintly as it drained the night's dew — Olive's problem, solved, holding.

Silena spread the tea towel on the widest stone and set the loaf down. She kept the second towel for the panel when Annabeth unwrapped it, because Silena was perceptive that way — she'd seen the shape through the canvas and understood, before anyone spoke, that this thing needed a clean place to lie.

All five of them sat in a loose ring. Thalia with her boots dug into the pebbles, pack at her feet. Annabeth cross-legged with her notebook already open, hoodie shed in the warmth to show her tank top and the gray shirt underneath tied at the waist. Percy with his damp cuffs finally re-rolled, bare feet tucked under him. Clarisse with her blue cutoff sleeves catching the sun, arms crossed, tote now empty beside her.

And the panel on the towel between them, notched side up, in full daylight with bread crumbs around it.

Silena looked at it for a long moment. Then she looked at each of their faces in turn — Thalia's protective worry, Annabeth's exhausted precision, Percy's wry attention stretched thin, Clarisse's grudging guard — and she understood the shape of the morning.

"Okay," she said. "This is the reveal, then. All five together."

"All five," Annabeth confirmed. "We told Clarisse alone because we promised. We told Thalia alone because she's leaving. But we wanted — we needed one telling where everybody hears the same thing. No versions."

Silena nodded. She broke the loaf and handed pieces around — crusty, still steaming, the crumb open and fragrant — and while they ate she let them tell it once through, straight, the way Clarisse had demanded: rings, seam, current, twelve feet off, cut hole, thirty-nine, twenty-three backwards with its little cross, heron at dawn with cove mud, fish gone, lake saying not yet.

Nobody interrupted. Even Thalia, who'd heard it, sat and ate bread and let Annabeth say it whole for Silena. That was a Grace-sister courtesy Silena had seen before: you let the teller tell, even twice, because the second telling is for the new listener.

When Annabeth finished, Silena picked up the panel. She held it the way she held warm bread — supporting the middle, not pinching the edges — and ran her fingertip just above the notches, feeling the warmth without touching, her brow furrowed.

"It's been held a lot," she said quietly. "Whoever cut these didn't just cut and leave. They came back and ran their thumb down them. Like counting rosary. Like checking."

Annabeth stared. "That's what I thought. Warmest under the notches."

"And this one." Silena tipped the board so the twenty-third mark caught the sun. "This one they pressed harder. They were afraid they'd forget which one was different, so they made sure they couldn't." She set the board down gently. "That's not a monster thing. That's a person thing. Scared to forget."

Percy looked at his oldest sister with something like relief. "You get it."

"I get that you're all carrying something heavy and trying to carry it so the others don't have to," Silena said. She tore another piece of bread and pressed it into Clarisse's hand, because Clarisse when worried forgot to eat. "Which is sweet and stupid. We're five. We carry together or it gets heavier."

Clarisse took the bread. "I said boathouse locker till Chiron. I stand by that."

"And I said I'm still leaving but not with secrets," Thalia put in. "I stand by that."

"And I," Silena said, "say we take it to Chiron after breakfast, all five, so he hears it once and nobody has to re-tell and re-worry." She looked at Annabeth, then Percy. "And I say the cost is this: once all five know, we all worry. Thalia carries it on the trail. Clarisse carries it on the shore. I carry it in the kitchen. You two carry it on the map and the water. That's what telling costs. It's heavier for everyone and lighter for no one — except it's shared, and shared is the only way we do things."

Annabeth's eyes filled. She blinked, precise, and leaned into Thalia on one side and Percy on the other, and both of them leaned back. Clarisse reached over and yanked Percy's collar straight with a blunt tug that was really an embrace with plausible deniability.

"Also," Silena added, lighter, breaking the moment before it got too heavy for morning, "the cost is that Percy does dishes for a week for going into a weird cove at dusk without telling his sisters."

"What—" Percy started.

"Shared carrying," Silena said sweetly. "Plus you look like you need something to do with your hands."

"I have things to do with my hands," Percy protested. "Boathouse inventory. Canoe for Tyson — he visits in two weeks and wants it fixed, and he's not moving into our cabin no matter what he told Triton on the phone."

"Good clarification," Thalia said dryly. "No more boys in Cabin Three. We have enough."

"Hey," Percy said.

"You said it, not me." Thalia threw a pebble at him. He caught it one-handed. Annabeth stole it from him and put it in her pocket for no reason except that it was his catch.

Silena watched them tease — PCS bickering about dishes and cuffs, Grace sisters shoulder to shoulder with crumbs on their knees — and felt the morning settle. The panel lay on the towel between the bread and the notebook, warm in the sun, thirty-nine marks up. Revealed. All five knew. The consequence was already moving: Thalia's pack repacked in her head with worry folded in, Clarisse's shore now a watch, Silena's kitchen now listening. No going back to two knowing and three not.

She was glad and sorry at once, the way she always was when bread ran out — glad it had fed, sorry it was gone.

"Eat," she said, pushing the loaf to the middle. "Then Chiron. Then Thalia leaves and the rest of us hold the shore."

Percy knew the lake was showing off for Clarisse, because the lake liked Clarisse.

They took the good skiff out late in the morning, after the bread and before Chiron, with the sun high enough to burn the last mist off. Percy rowed. Clarisse sat in the bow with her clipboard balanced on her knees and a spare coil of line at her feet, eyes on the water like it owed her an explanation.

Annabeth hadn't come. That had been the deal: Annabeth stayed to copy her notes clean for Chiron, and Percy took Clarisse to see the seam and the hole so the report would have two witnesses. Daylight, a plan, a check-in time — in Clarisse's new language, "acceptable risk with documentation."

The freshwater smell came up as they left the dock — clean, mineral, faintly green, nothing salt in it. He loved it differently than the old sea of his other life: the hull sounded higher and softer on it.

"Stop narrating in your head and row straight," Clarisse said without looking up. "You're doing the drift thing."

"I'm rowing straight. The current's pushing."

"There's no current in this lake."

"Tell that to the lake."

They passed the swimming buoys, the rings gone in the daylight, the water flat and guileless. They passed the boundary rock with its white X still tacky at the edges. Percy shipped the oars for a moment and trailed his fingers. Normal here. Warm top, cool underneath. Ordinary.

Then they crossed into the green.

The willow cove took them the way it always took boats — shade first, then cool, then quiet. The air dropped five degrees under the crowns. Willow leaves lay on the surface in scattered gold-green, turning slowly. The smell was mud and bark and the sweet rot of a shoreline eating itself politely. Light came through in broken coins and lay on Clarisse's shoulders and the gunwales.

And the seam was there.

Percy felt it before he saw it — the temperature change against his wrist where his hand hung over the side, the faint resistance as the bow crossed it. On the lake side, sun-warmed surface water. On the cove side, cold-shadow cold, glassy, holding the willow light in pieces. Between them a line, soft as weather, where the two didn't mix. In daylight it showed as a difference in texture more than color: open water rippled faintly with the noon breeze, cove water stayed glass.

Clarisse put her hand in without being asked. She held it there, frowning.

"Cold," she said. "That's — that's not shade cold. That's February cold."

"Outward push, too," Percy said. He let the skiff drift. It swung two degrees and began moving backward, out toward the lake, though no wind blew into the cove. "Feel it under the hull?"

Clarisse braced her palms on the gunwales, feeling rather than seeing. Her jaw tightened. "Yeah. I feel it." She looked up the cove toward the sunken skiff at its head, half in, half out, tea-colored and freckled with rot. "Take us in. Slow."

He rowed them in with shallow strokes, no wake. The reeds stood thick at the arrow end, green-gold in the sun. The flat rock sat dry-topped in the middle, sunlit, ordinary. No heron now — the bird had left the dock by mid-morning, presumably back here, but the bow of the sunken skiff stood empty.

Clarisse stepped over to the sunken hull with the easy balance of a girl who'd spent three summers beaching boats, and caught the rotted gunwale. The wood squelched under her palm. She made a face.

"Ugh. That's — okay. That's disgusting." She peered down into the hull where the panel had been. The cut hole showed dark against the inner boards, edges smooth, water moving in it with a slow in-out like breathing. "That's cut. That's not rot. Rot doesn't do rectangles."

"No," Percy said. "It doesn't."

Clarisse sat back on her heels in their skiff, clipboard forgotten, and looked at the hole for a long time. Percy watched his sister — fifteen, blunt, grudging with praise and generous with labor — do the thing she did when inventory became responsibility. Her hands had gone still. Her eyes kept moving: hole to reeds to flat rock to the mouth seam and back.

"The arrow's off," she said finally. "Annabeth said twelve feet. I believe her. I set that skiff's position with her in July and it's walked."

"You remember?"

"I remember everything on my shore." Clarisse said it without pride, just fact. She picked up the clipboard, flipped to a blank sheet, and wrote in her blocky hand: COVE — SEAM COLD / OUT PUSH / HOLE CUT RECT / SKIFF MOVED. Then she hesitated, pencil hovering, and added underneath, smaller: NO SOLO. PLAN + PAIR.

Percy leaned on his oars, watching. "That's new policy?"

"That's reassessing." Clarisse tore her eyes off the hole with visible effort. "You know what I keep thinking? Whoever cut that hole stood where I'm standing. In this cove. In our lake. With a saw and a plan and thirty-nine mornings. And nobody saw them." She looked at Percy, and for a second the blunt guard dropped and the younger fear showed — the one about him leaving, about people she kept walking out. "You said you don't know how to stay anywhere. This — this is staying, you know. Coming out here in daylight with your sister to look at a scary hole so we can tell Chiron straight. That's the staying kind of thing."

Percy felt his throat go tight. He bumped her boot with his oar, gently, the PCS version of a hug when hands were full.

"I'm here," he said. "I'm in the boat. Where else would I be?"

"On a quest. In Atlantis. Dead." Clarisse ticked them off, grudging affection returning. "Pick one."

"Boat," Percy said. "I pick boat."

"Good choice." She stowed the clipboard and reached for the oar. "My turn to row back. You trail your fingers and do the fish-boy listening thing and tell me if the seam moves while we cross it. And roll your left cuff — it's come undone and it's driving me crazy."

He rolled the cuff. She rowed them out past the flat rock, past the seam — cold to warm, a soft give like pushing through a curtain — past the boundary rock toward home, steady and strong, and Percy listened with his hand in the freshwater the whole way, feeling the cove push out behind them, patient as a clock, counting without them.

---

Annabeth had prepared for Chiron the way she prepared for everything: by making the mess legible.

She sat on the Big House porch steps with her notebook open across her knees and Olive Nakamura beside her, cross-checking. The midday heat had come up warm — true early-September warm, cicadas going in the pines, the porch boards hot through her shorts — and her hoodie lay folded on the step behind her. She wore the gray shirt with the real pockets now, sleeves neat to the elbow, braid re-done tight for the meeting. Her boots were dusty from the north beach. The canvas tote with the panel sat at her feet, retied with the bootlace.

Olive, twelve and earnest with a pencil perpetually behind her ear, peered at the cove sketch. "The arrow moved but the flat rock didn't?"

"Flat rock's bedrock. It doesn't walk. The hull did." Annabeth tapped the note: 23 — OPP. DIR., 2X DEEP, X-NICK @ BASE. "Draw it exactly. Don't tidy it."

Olive copied it carefully. "Good luck with Chiron."

Inside, the Big House office smelled of lemon oil and old paper and the faint horse-sweet smell of Chiron in his wheelchair form. The five of them crowded in — Thalia with her pack still on one shoulder, Clarisse with the tote, Silena with crumbs still on her yellow shirt, Percy with his cuffs finally dry, Annabeth with her notebook — and laid the panel on Chiron's desk on the folded tea towel.

Chiron listened the way he always listened: fully, without reaching for the object until the telling was done. Annabeth gave the report straight, Percy added the water parts, Clarisse added the boathouse verification with times, Thalia added the survey conflict plainly, Silena added the shared-carrying part that none of the others would have said. No embroidery. No theories about architects. No names for who might have built a door under a lake.

When they finished, Chiron picked up the panel. He turned it over once, held his palm above the notches to feel the warmth, found the twenty-third mark with eyes that had read a thousand student maps, and set it down.

"Thirty-nine readings," he said. "A fitted panel. A moved hull. A seam that does not mix in a freshwater lake. And a heron keeping watch." He looked at each of them in turn. "You did rightly to tell Clarisse first, and rightly to tell together. Secrets kept in pairs in this camp have a way of becoming secrets kept from sisters, and that is a cost I will not have you pay."

Thalia exhaled. Annabeth felt Percy's hand find hers behind Clarisse's back.

"Here is my decision," Chiron said. "The panel stays with me, in this office, locked. Annabeth, your drawings and notes are the working copy — continue them. The cove is not forbidden, but it is not solitary. No one goes past the boundary rock alone until we understand the current. Pairs at minimum, with a check-in time left at the boathouse. Clarisse, your policy stands." He glanced at her clipboard. "Write it plainly. No retroactive billing."

Clarisse flushed to the ears. "Retired," she muttered. "Already ripped out."

"I know," Chiron said kindly. "The lake told me. It declined to sink the page, I hear."

That broke the tension. Even Thalia laughed.

"Thalia," Chiron went on, "your survey stands. Four days. The camp needs its boundaries walked, and I need one daughter on the ridge who can read weather before it reads her. You will carry a full account with you — Annabeth's copy — and you will not hurry home. The lake has been keeping things a very long time. It will keep four more days."

Thalia nodded slowly, the protective line in her shoulders easing half an inch. "And if it counts forty while I'm gone?"

"Then we will record forty," Chiron said. "That is what cartographers and sons of the sea do. We record, we watch, we do not chase doors before we have mapped their frames." He wrapped the panel again in the gray shirt with surprising gentleness. "No quest. No expedition. School continues. Boathouse continues. Bread continues." A nod to Silena. "Go and eat lunch. And Thalia — come back with all your socks."

They filed out blinking into the noon glare, five abreast on the porch steps, and Annabeth felt the morning's weight shift. Decided. Panel with Chiron. All five knowing. Thalia leaving with a copy instead of a secret. The cost Silena had named — everyone worrying a little — settled over them lighter than she'd feared, because it was even.

Percy squeezed her hand once, hard. Obviously, his eyes said. Obviously, hers answered.

---

Chiron had said go and eat lunch, and Silena took it as a direct order from the highest available authority.

She led them down to the north beach at a forced march, vetoing the pavilion with the flat statement that what they needed was "pebbles, not benches," and produced from various pockets and folds a lunch that had no business existing: half a loaf of the good bread, a waxed packet of cheese gone soft at the edges, three peaches, a knot of treaty honey in cloth, and — triumphantly — a full second packet of smoked fish she claimed she'd "planned for exactly this."

"You planned for finding a mysterious tally panel and having to report it to Chiron?" Thalia asked, collapsing onto the widest flat stone.

"I planned for feeding five people at noon," Silena said with dignity, tearing the bread. "The circumstances were decorative."

They ate sprawled on the sun-warmed stones with their shoes off and the lake lapping a yard from their toes. Clarisse claimed the peach with the best color and then, watched by everyone, cut it into five uneven pieces with her belt knife and dealt them out like cards — keeping the smallest for herself without comment. Thalia saw it. Thalia said nothing, but her boot knocked once against Clarisse's ankle, deliberate, and Clarisse's ears went pink.

"Policy," Annabeth said around bread, "is written. NO SOLO PAST ROCK. PLAN plus PAIR. CHECK-IN. Clarisse, read it back."

Clarisse patted her clipboard where it lay facedown on the pebbles. "It's written. It's plain. There is no billing schedule and there never was."

"There was a billing schedule," Percy said. "Ten minutes per occurrence, retroactive, double for Zeus-cabin accompaniment. I was there. I paid."

"You paid in leaves," Clarisse said. "A willow leaf. I still have it. It's pressed in the ledger next to the ripped page."

"You kept the leaf?"

"I keep evidence." She bit into her peach piece. "It's policy."

Thalia stretched full-length on the hot stone with her pack for a pillow, three hours from her trail departure, and announced to the sky that this was the correct use of a crisis morning. "Panic, report, bread. In that order. The Romans had it backwards."

"The Romans had bread first," Annabeth said. "Panem et circenses. Bread and then everything else."

"The Romans," Silena said, "never had my bread, so their priorities were unsound." She broke the last of the loaf and pressed half into Thalia's hand. "Trail piece. For the ridge. Eat it tomorrow when the weather reads you first."

Thalia took it, and something in her face went unguarded for a second — eighteen and leaving and being packed for by a sixteen-year-old with honey on her cuffs. "Thanks, bread girl."

"Anytime, weather girl."

Percy lay back with his apple core balanced on his chest and watched a jay work the pines, shoulder to shoulder with Annabeth, ankle to ankle with Clarisse, and thought that Chiron's orders were the best orders he'd ever been given. Nobody mentioned tallies. Nobody mentioned seams. Somebody — Silena — hummed through a mouthful of peach, and somebody else — Clarisse — stole the hum and turned it into a marching cadence, and Thalia threw a pebble at both of them, and the lake kept lapping, freshwater-sweet, keeping the crumbs.

Percy found her at the north beach at dusk, the way he'd known he would.

The day had burned itself warm and gold and then let go, evening cooling the way early September evenings did — pine shadows lengthening across the pebbles, the lake going glassy and pink at the edges, the first frogs starting up behind the boathouse. The private picnic spot lay quiet, fire ring empty, flat stones still holding the day's heat. Silena's tea towel still lay folded on the widest stone where she'd left it at noon, forgotten in the Chiron rush.

Annabeth sat on that stone with her knees drawn up, hoodie back on against the cooling air, notebook closed beside her. Her braid had come half undone again. The gray shirt with the real pockets was back in the tote, empty now — panel gone to Chiron's locker — and she wore instead her faded camp tank under the hoodie, sleeves pushed up. She looked fourteen and tired and entirely herself.

Percy came down the path with two apples from the pavilion and his orange shirt gone damp again at the hem from skipping stones with Olive while he waited for the light to change. He sat beside her without a word and handed her an apple. She took it, bumped his shoulder with hers, and they ate looking at the water.

For a while neither spoke. The lake lapped freshwater-sweet against the pebbles. A loon called far off. The willow cove end lay dark green and quiet — no rings visible from here, no seam, just the ordinary evening water with the sky doubled in it.

"Thalia got off?" Percy asked finally.

"Three o'clock. North ridge trail." Annabeth polished her apple on her hoodie cuff. "She hugged me so hard my ribs creaked. Then she hugged you in absentia and told me to tell you to stay in the boat."

"I stayed in the boat today."

"I told her. She said good, keep it up for four days." Annabeth leaned her head against his shoulder, apple forgotten in her lap. Her hair smelled of sun and pencil shavings. "Clarisse wrote the new policy on the clipboard. NO SOLO PAST ROCK. PLAN + PAIR. CHECK-IN. In block letters. No minutes."

"Progress," Percy said. He set his apple core aside and put his arm around her shoulders, and she settled into him the way they settled — practiced, easy, no performance to it. His hand found hers on the stone between them, fingers lacing, lake-rough against paper-rough.

"Are you sad?" Annabeth asked quietly. "That we gave it to Chiron?"

Percy thought about it, watching the light go gold to rose on the water. "No. Relieved, mostly. It's — it's like we carried something cold all night and finally set it down on a table where someone else can watch it too." He turned his hand over in hers, thumb tracing the pencil callus on her middle finger. "Are you?"

"A little. It's my cove. My map." She sighed, the tired sound from the dock that morning. "But Silena was right. Telling costs, but not telling costs more. Thalia on the trail with a copy is better than Thalia on the trail with a feeling that we're hiding something. And Clarisse — did you see her face when Chiron said her policy stands?"

"She stood three inches taller."

"She needed to hear it from someone that isn't us." Annabeth tipped her face up to look at him. The evening light lay warm across her cheeks, the tired brightness still in her eyes. "Percy. Last night you said you don't know how to stay anywhere. Did you mean it?"

He held her gaze. This was the conversation under all the other conversations, the standing thread neither ledger nor panel could cut.

"I meant I never learned," he said honestly, wryness set aside. "Growing up — going where Mom could keep us safe, then Atlantis, then here — staying always felt like something other people got to do. Like a trick I missed." He squeezed her hand. "But I'm learning. Boathouse inventory. Rolling cuffs when I'm told. Taking my sister to see a scary hole in daylight. Giving the board to Chiron instead of sitting up all night with it." He smiled, small. "Choosing the same life two days in a row. That's what you said, kind of. Choosing till it joins you."

Annabeth studied him for a long moment. Then she set her apple down, took his face in both her hands — cool palms, warm fingers — and kissed him.

It was a dusk-on-the-pebbles kind of kiss: unhurried, deliberate, tired and tender at once, the kind where you go slowly because there's no bell and no watch and no one on the north beach but the frogs. He tasted of apple and lake air. She had one hand at his temple, thumb at his cheekbone, and he had his hand flat on her back, holding steady, and the lake kept its small sounds against the stones and did not interrupt.

When they broke apart they didn't break apart. They stayed forehead to forehead, breathing the same cooling air, her hands still framing his face, his arm still around her.

"That's for staying," Annabeth said softly.

"I'm staying," Percy said.

"I know. I'm mapping it." She rested her head back on his shoulder, and he rested his cheek against her hair, and they sat like that while the crescent came up over the pavilion and the lake doubled it underneath, patient, keeping everything — footsteps and tallies and kisses and bread crumbs — a very long time.

After a while Percy picked up a flat pebble, skipped it twice across the glassy edge, and Annabeth stole the next one to throw herself. Hers plopped. His went three times. She demanded a rematch.

And the cove lay green and quiet at the end of the lake, holding its seam and its count, waiting for morning like a measuring line about to be drawn.
