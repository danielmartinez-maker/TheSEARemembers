## Chapter 33: Architecture

Annabeth Grace woke up with a building in her head.

She had done this for years in the life she remembered. Here, in Cabin One, she still woke with unfinished plans turning over in her head before she was entirely awake, staircases that went nowhere and then, suddenly, somewhere. Usually they faded by breakfast. This one didn't.

She sat up in her bunk in Cabin One — Zeus's cabin, all high windows and storm-gray blankets and that faint smell of ozone that never quite left, even on sunny days — and grabbed her sketchbook off the nightstand before the image could dissolve.

It was small. That was the first thing she drew. Not a temple. Not a monument. Camp Half-Blood already had plenty of big marble things that made you feel tiny when you stood in front of them. This was something else. Low walls. Open to the sky in the middle. Three sides, curving in like hands around a fire.

She drew a circle. Then she divided it, not evenly. Three arcs meeting in the center.

"You're drawing before teeth-brushing," said a voice from above. "That's new. Even for you."

Thalia leaned over the edge of the top bunk, hair a black tangle, eyeliner smudged from yesterday. She'd come in late from border patrol and still had pine needles caught in her blanket. The top bunk in Cabin One was hers, and she defended its height advantage against Annabeth's complaints with ruthless efficiency. Daughter of Zeus. Sixteen. Annabeth's actual big sister, and her sparring partner whenever breakfast could be delayed long enough for a challenge.

"Teeth can wait," Annabeth said, shading a wall. "Buildings can't."

Thalia swung down, landed barefoot on the cold wood floor. "Let me see."

Annabeth tilted the book. The sketch was rough — pencil lines, erased and redrawn, notes in the margins. *open center? rain pool?* *bench height for small kids?* *mosaic?*

"What is it?" Thalia asked.

"A shrine," Annabeth said. "For us. For Big Three kids."

Thalia went quiet in a way that made Annabeth look up.

"What do you mean, for us?" Thalia asked carefully.

Annabeth tapped her pencil against the page. "There's the gods' shrines by the dining pavilion, right? All twelve Olympians, plus a bunch of little ones everyone's added over the years. But there's nothing that's just... ours. Nothing that says, hey, if you're a kid of Zeus or Poseidon or Hades and you feel weird about that, here's a place to sit. I keep thinking about it. Nico eats alone half the time because the Hades table makes everyone nervous. Percy gets stared at when he does anything with water. I get stared at when there's a thunderstorm and everyone looks at me like I called it personally."

"You kind of love thunderstorms, though," Thalia said.

"That's not the point!" Annabeth said, but she was grinning. "The point is we don't have a place. The cabins are for sleeping. The arena is for fighting. The Big House is for meetings where Chiron tells us not to die. I want to make somewhere that's just for being."

Thalia studied the sketch for a long moment. Then she smiled, slow and real.

"Okay, architect," she said. "I'm in. Where are you going to put it?"

Annabeth flipped the page. She'd drawn a little map of camp yesterday during free period, just doodling while Percy tried to teach her how to skip stones at the lake and failed because she kept analyzing the angle instead of just throwing. There was a spot she'd been thinking about — on the low rise between the lake and the woods, where the path curved and there was a flat patch of grass with three old olive trees forming a triangle. Not too close to anything, not too far. You could see the sky from there. You could hear the water if the wind was right.

"There," she said, pointing.

Thalia nodded. "Good sight lines. High ground, so it won't flood. And those trees will give you natural shade without blocking the sky. Nice pick."

Annabeth felt a warm rush in her chest. Praise from Thalia about tactical placement hit different than praise from Chiron about essays.

"I haven't asked Chiron yet," Annabeth admitted.

"So let's go ask him," Thalia said, already pulling on her jacket. "Before breakfast. Catch him before the day gets crazy."

---

Chiron was on the porch of the Big House with his morning coffee — which, for a centaur, meant a mug the size of a soup bowl — reading a stack of supply lists and looking like he wished the supply lists would read themselves.

"Girls," he said warmly as they climbed the steps. "You're up early."

"Annabeth has a building in her head," Thalia announced, pushing Annabeth forward gently.

Chiron raised an eyebrow. "Does she? Well. Better out than in. Tell me."

So Annabeth did. She talked fast, the way she always did when she was excited and trying not to show it, flipping her sketchbook open and showing him the circle-divided-in-three, the low walls, the open center. She explained about the shrines and how there wasn't one for the Big Three kids, how she wanted something small and open where anyone could sit, with space for each of them — sea and sky and underworld — but together, not separated.

Chiron listened the way he always listened, fully, without interrupting, his eyes moving between her face and the sketch.

When she finished, he was quiet for a moment, stroking his beard.

"May I ask why now?" he said gently. "You've been at camp for four years, Annabeth. You've drawn the dining pavilion redesign twice and the climbing wall drainage plan once — which, I might add, saved us a great deal of trouble last spring. Why this project, right now?"

Annabeth looked down at her sketch. She'd been asking herself the same thing since she'd woken up.

"I think..." she said slowly. "Things feel different. Percy's here to stay now. Nico's here with Bianca. We keep working together, all of us, and then going back to our separate cabins. Thalia's told me about Percy's old visits, but I know him here now. I know Clarisse and Silena. I want a place where we can bring all that together without having to borrow a table or wait for a training session. It feels like we should mark that somehow. Like, we exist. We're not just stories counselors tell new kids to scare them."

Chiron's expression softened.

"And," Annabeth added, quieter, "my dad... Zeus... he's not exactly the kind of dad you can just visit for the weekend. I don't want to build him a temple. I want to build something for us. The kids. Does that make sense? Is that disrespectful?"

"No," Chiron said firmly. "It makes perfect sense, and it is not disrespectful. The gods have their thrones on Olympus. The children need somewhere on earth. I think it's a wonderful idea."

Annabeth's whole face lit up.

"Now," Chiron went on, holding up a finger, "there are conditions. It must be small, as you say. It must be safe — no deep pits, no sharp edges, no enchantments you cannot fully control. It must be built with permission from the dryads in those olive trees; you will ask them, politely, and if they say no, you will find another spot. And you must do it as a community project. No staying up all night alone with a chisel. You eat, you sleep, you train. Understood?"

"Understood!" Annabeth said.

"And Annabeth?" Chiron smiled. "You will need to present me with a proper plan. Measurements. Materials. A list of helpers. An architect does not simply stack stones and hope."

"Yes! I mean — yes, sir. I can do that. I already started—"

"I can see that," Chiron said, nodding at the sketchbook, where she'd already started writing *MEASUREMENTS???* in large letters and underlining it three times. "Come back to me in three days with a proposal. If it is sound, you may build."

Thalia whooped and grabbed Annabeth around the shoulders as they ran down the steps.

"Three days!" Thalia said. "You can totally do that. You're Annabeth Grace. You probably dream in graph paper."

"I do, actually," Annabeth said. "Last week I dreamed my teeth were little Ionic columns."

"That's deeply concerning," Thalia said. "Also deeply on brand. Come on, let's get pancakes before Percy eats them all."

---

The thing about designing a building for other people, Annabeth discovered over the next two days, was that you actually had to talk to the other people.

This sounded obvious. It was not obvious at seven in the morning with a pencil behind her ear and seventeen different ideas fighting in her head.

She started, sensibly, with research. She spent all of morning lessons in the library in the Big House, pulling out books on Greek shrines and hero shrines and wayside chapels. She learned that the smallest shrines were often the most loved — a niche in a wall with a lamp, a spring with a cup chained to it, a tree with ribbons. People didn't need grandeur. They needed a place to leave something and a place to sit while they thought about what they'd left.

She filled four pages with notes. *Must have: seating. Must have: water? Must have: shade + sun choice. Must have: place for offerings that won't blow away / get eaten by harpies (lids!).*

At lunch she sat with her notebook open and interviewed people, which made her feel very grown-up and a little bit like a reporter for the school paper she'd never been on.

"Okay," she said to Percy, who was sitting across from her trying to build a mashed-potato volcano and mostly succeeding. "If you had a little shrine just for, you know, us — Big Three kids — what would you want in it?"

Percy blinked. Sea-green eyes, hair still damp from morning canoeing, a smear of butter on his chin. He was twelve, same as Annabeth, but sometimes he looked younger when he was concentrating, tongue stuck out a little.

"Like, for praying and stuff?" he asked.

"Not just praying. For hanging out. Thinking. Being... you know. A Poseidon kid without everyone watching."

Percy thought about it, stirring his potatoes. "Water," he said finally. "Has to have water. Not like a fountain that's all fancy. Just... somewhere to put my hands in. When I'm stressed, being near water helps. Even just hearing it. In the life I remember, at Sally's apartment, I used to run the faucet when I couldn't sleep. It made me feel better. So yeah. Water."

Annabeth wrote *WATER — quiet basin? channel?* and underlined it.

"What else?"

"Something blue," Percy said. "And somewhere to sit where you can see the lake. And..." He hesitated. "Is this going to sound dumb? Something Sally would have liked. She can't come here, you know? I wish I could show her a picture and have her think it was pretty. She liked pretty things. In our old life, she made these blue cookies—"

"I know about the blue cookies, Percy, you've told me forty times," Annabeth said fondly.

"They're really good cookies," Percy said defensively. "Anyway. Something pretty. Not just rocks."

"Noted: pretty, not just rocks," Annabeth said, writing it down. "Percy-approved pretty."

"Hey!" Percy leaned over to look at her sketch. "That's actually good. The three-curves thing. Like waves and clouds?"

"Like arms," Annabeth said. "Or hands. Holding something in the middle."

"What goes in the middle?"

"I don't know yet," Annabeth admitted. "Fire? That's traditional. A central hearth. But with three of us... maybe water in the middle? Or light?"

Percy tapped the center circle she'd drawn. "What if it's empty? Like, just open to the sky, and rain can fall in, and you can see stars at night? And whatever anyone needs, they bring it?"

Annabeth stared at him.

"Percy Jackson," she said, "that was genuinely insightful."

"I have my moments," Percy said modestly, then ruined it by eating a spoonful of mashed-potato volcano in one bite. "Can I help build it? I'm strong. I can carry stuff."

"You are strong," Annabeth agreed. "You once carried Grover and our entire luggage up Half-Blood Hill because you said the donkey looked tired."

"The donkey DID look tired."

"I'm putting you down for heavy lifting," Annabeth said, writing *PERCY — stones, water feature digging, general muscle.* "Report tomorrow after sword class with work clothes."

"Aye aye, chief architect," Percy said, saluting with his spoon.

---

Thalia was next, and Thalia had opinions.

They walked the site together that afternoon, pacing it out with a measuring tape Annabeth had borrowed from the arts-and-crafts cabin. The grass was soft underfoot, dotted with clover. The three olive trees stood exactly where Annabeth remembered, gnarled and silver-green, leaves hissing softly in the breeze. Bees moved lazily between wildflowers. From the top of the rise you could see the lake glittering to the east and the forest dark to the west, and directly overhead, a huge bowl of sky.

"It's perfect," Thalia said, arms spread, turning in a circle. "You can feel it. The air's different here. Clean."

"The dryads said it was okay," Annabeth said. "I brought them honey and asked very politely and promised not to hurt the roots. They said the middle tree likes poetry, so I have to read to her once a week."

"You negotiated with tree spirits for land rights," Thalia said. "You're basically a real estate developer now."

"I'm an architect," Annabeth corrected. "There's a difference. Developers care about money. Architects care about —" she gestured vaguely, "— light. And flow. And whether a bench makes you want to sit on it."

"Okay, Frank Lloyd Wright," Thalia grinned. "So what do you need from me?"

"Lightning protection," Annabeth said promptly. "You're the expert. If I build low stone walls and put in a metal rod or a hearth, and we're on a hill, and I'm a daughter of Zeus — I'm basically building a lightning target. I need to make it safe. And I want to... I don't know. Honor the sky without making it scary. A lot of kids are scared of storms because of us."

Thalia nodded slowly, her usual joking manner dropping away. She understood the fear of storms — she'd spent enough nights keeping Annabeth away from the windows when their own static made the glass sing. Storms followed her too.

"Yeah," she said. "Yeah, I get that. Okay. So here's what we do. We put in a proper lightning rod — copper, tall but not too tall, set off to the side, grounded deep with a copper cable. I'll show you how. My dad —" she glanced up,habit, "— he taught me a little about it, in dreams sometimes. How to ground the charge. And we make it beautiful. Not just a spike. We can coil it like a vine, or shape the top like a pinecone or a star. And we connect it to the design, so kids can see it and understand: lightning goes HERE, not into you. It's controlled. It's safe."

Annabeth scribbled furiously. *LIGHTNING ROD — copper, vine coil, side placement, Thalia to supervise grounding.*

"Also," Thalia added, kicking at a tuft of grass, "can we have wind chimes? I know that sounds cheesy, but... I've heard the visiting Hunters' little tent bells. When the wind moves, you hear everyone else's bells, and you know you're not alone, even in the dark. I'd like that. Something that makes sound when the sky moves."

"Wind chimes," Annabeth wrote, and then, because Thalia looked a little embarrassed, she added, "That's a great idea. Sky music."

Thalia bumped her shoulder. "You're good at this, you know. Asking people. Most brainiacs just tell people what they're getting."

"I'm trying," Annabeth said. "Chiron said community project. And also — I want it to actually feel like ours. Not just mine."

They measured the triangle between the trees: roughly twenty-four feet on each side. Annabeth marked the center with a stick and a piece of blue yarn. They used the tape to lay out a circle eighteen feet across for the shrine itself, leaving room for the trees' roots and a path.

"This is going to be real," Thalia said quietly, standing in the middle of the yarn circle, looking up at the sky through the olive leaves. "We're going to have a place."

"Yeah," Annabeth said, throat suddenly tight. "We are."

---

Clarisse and Silena were, unexpectedly, the hardest interview and the best one.

Annabeth found them by the arts-and-crafts cabin, sorting through boxes of tiles for a mosaic project the Demeter kids were doing. Clarisse — big, bristling, daughter of Poseidon, usually more interested in spears than seashells — was holding up a piece of blue glass to the light with surprising gentleness. Silena — daughter of Poseidon, all smiles and charm bracelets — was organizing shells by color in a muffin tin.

"You're doing mosaics?" Annabeth asked, surprised.

Clarisse scowled on reflex, then shrugged. "Silena roped me in. Said I had good color sense. Which I do. Poseidon kids know blue. All the blues. Sea blue, storm blue, deep-water blue—"

"She's very talented," Silena said, looping an arm around Clarisse's shoulders. Clarisse pretended to hate it and clearly didn't. "We're helping with the border. What do you need, Annabeth?"

Annabeth explained the shrine. As she talked, Silena's eyes got wider and wider, and Clarisse's scowl slowly faded into something like interest.

"So you need decoration?" Silena asked. "Oh, please let us help. Please. Poseidon cabin lives for this stuff, and everyone always thinks we're just about fighting, but we know mosaics, we know textiles—"

"I want seashell mosaics," Annabeth said. "Percy said pretty, not just rocks. And I was thinking — the Poseidon side could have a wave mosaic, with shells and blue glass. Would you...?"

"We would LOVE to," Silena said. "Clarisse, tell her about the shell idea."

Clarisse crossed her arms. "It's not a big deal. I just — the Ares shrine where we leave offerings after sparring is all weapons and boar heads and stuff. It's cool, but it's not... me, exactly. Dad's sea shrine feels different, and I want something of that here. I like the beach. When I was little, before camp, my mom used to take me to the Jersey shore and we'd collect shells. I still have a bucket in my cabin. I was thinking, if you're doing a sea wall, you could press shells right into the mortar while it's wet. Real shells, not just tiles. So it smells like salt and you can touch it."

Annabeth stared. "Clarisse, that's perfect."

Clarisse shrugged, ears pink. "Whatever. It's just an idea."

"It's a great idea," Annabeth said firmly, writing *SEA WALL MOSAIC — shells pressed in mortar + blue glass wave, Clarisse + Silena lead.* "Will you both lead that part? I'll give you the dimensions and you can design it however you want."

Silena clapped her hands. "We'll make it gorgeous. Swirls and waves and maybe some starfish shapes for the sky part? To tie it together?"

"And what about you, Silena?" Annabeth asked. "What would you want in a place like that, if it was for everyone?"

Silena thought, tapping a shell against her chin. "Somewhere to leave notes," she said. "Like, little cubbies or cracks where you can tuck a prayer or a wish or a name. Aphrodite's shrine has ribbons, and people write on them. It's nice to know other people were there and felt things too. Even if you never meet them."

Annabeth wrote that down, underlining it twice: *LEAVING PLACE — niches for notes/offerings.*

Clarisse was quiet for a moment. Then she said, gruffly, "Benches with backs. So old veterans with bad backs can sit. And new kids who're scared can lean. My brother —" she stopped. "Just. Benches with backs. Low ones. So your feet touch the ground."

Annabeth wrote it down without comment, but she reached over and squeezed Clarisse's wrist briefly. Clarisse squeezed back, hard, then let go and pretended to be very interested in a piece of red tile.

---

Grover found Annabeth, as Grover always did, when she was sitting alone thinking too hard.

She was at the site at golden hour, sketchbook in her lap, trying to work out the water feature. Percy wanted water. But how? A fountain needed pipes. Pipes needed plumbing. Plumbing needed Hephaestus kids and money. A basin would go stagnant. A channel from the lake would be a massive dig.

She was chewing her pencil eraser flat when she heard the familiar *clip-clop* on the path and the rustle of leaves that always seemed to follow Grover around like he was his own personal forest.

"Hey," Grover said, settling beside her, knees cracking. Satyr ages were complicated; somehow he seemed both younger and older than all of them — big eyes, scruffy chin, reed pipes tucked in his vest pocket. "Whatcha drawing?"

Annabeth showed him. He studied it seriously, nose wrinkled.

"You need plants," he declared.

"I need water," Annabeth sighed. "Plants are easy. Water is hard."

"Plants ARE water," Grover said wisely. "Sort of. Listen. You don't need pipes. You need a rain bowl."

"A what?"

"A shallow stone bowl in the center, slightly tilted, that catches rain. No pipes, no pumps. When it rains, it fills. When it's dry, it's a sundial — you can mark the rim with hours. And around it, you plant things that like both wet and dry. Sedum. Thyme. Little mountain flowers. They'll drink the splash. And the bees will come. And it'll smell amazing when you step on the thyme."

Annabeth stared at him, pencil frozen. Then she started drawing fast — a shallow bowl, wide and low, with a notched rim.

"Grover, that's brilliant."

"I know," Grover said modestly. "I'm a nature spirit. It's literally my job." He leaned closer. "Can I do the planting? I know what the dryads like. And I can ask the naiads at the lake for some smooth stones for the bottom of the bowl, so when there's water you can see the stones through it. And... can we plant rosemary by the entrance? For remembrance. So nobody who comes here feels forgotten."

Annabeth's eyes stung suddenly. She blinked hard and pretended a gnat had flown into them.

"Rosemary by the entrance," she repeated, writing it down. "Grover in charge of all green things."

"And one more thing," Grover said, pulling grass out from between his toes. "Leave one corner wild. Don't pave it, don't plant it in rows. Just let whatever wants to grow, grow. The wild needs a place too. Pan —" his voice wobbled a little on the name of his lost god, "— Pan would have liked that."

Annabeth put her hand on his shoulder. "One wild corner. Promise."

Grover smiled, and the wind moved through the olive trees, and for a moment the whole hill smelled like thyme and sun and growing things, even though nothing was planted yet.

---

Nico was last, and Annabeth was nervous about Nico.

Nico di Angelo was ten, small for his age, all black clothes and white skin and dark eyes that seemed too big for his face. Son of Hades. He and Bianca had arrived at camp after losing decades in the Lotus Hotel, and Nico had... not taken it well. Bianca could try lessons and talk to strangers while he still struggled to believe the calendar. He'd spent long stretches in the Hades cabin, barely eating, barely talking, summoning skeletons when he had nightmares and then apologizing to them in the morning.

Lately he'd been coming out more. He played Mythomagic with Percy on rainy afternoons. He let Silena braid colored strings into his wrist. He'd even laughed once at dinner when Grover got a bread roll stuck on one of his horns. But he still vanished when groups got too big, still sat at the edge of things like he was waiting to be told to leave.

Annabeth found him behind the Hades cabin, sitting in the narrow strip of shadow between the cabin wall and the fence, reading a Mythomagic card guide with a flashlight even though it was daytime. The shadows were thick there, cool and blue.

"Hey," Annabeth said softly, crouching down so she wasn't looming. "Can I sit?"

Nico shrugged, scooting over a little. That was a yes, in Nico language.

Annabeth sat in the dust, not minding her jeans. She didn't open her sketchbook right away. She just sat with him for a minute, watching ants carry crumbs.

"I'm building something," she said finally. "A little shrine. For me and Thalia, Percy and his sisters, you and Bianca. For Big Three kids. So we have a place."

Nico didn't look up. "The gods don't want Hades stuff near their stuff."

"It's not near their stuff," Annabeth said. "It's ours. Separate. Equal. Three sides. One for sea, one for sky, one for..." She hesitated. "For shadows. For the underworld. For you."

Nico's fingers tightened on his cards.

"I don't know what you'd like," Annabeth said honestly. "And I don't want to guess wrong and make it all skulls and spooky stuff if that's not what you want. So I'm asking. What would make you feel... safe? Or welcome? Or just... like you could breathe?"

Nico was quiet for so long Annabeth thought he wasn't going to answer. Then, in a very small voice, he said, "Dark."

"Dark?" Annabeth repeated gently.

"Everyone always makes everything bright," Nico mumbled, staring at his shoes. "The dining pavilion has all those torches. The cabins have windows. Even Artemis's shrine is all silver and shiny. But bright hurts my eyes sometimes. And... my dad's realm isn't all fire. A lot of it is just... quiet. And dark. And cool. Like a cave with water dripping. Or like under a blanket. I like shadows. They don't stare."

Annabeth nodded slowly, understanding blooming. "A shadows corner," she said. "Not scary dark. Soft dark. Cool dark."

"Yeah," Nico whispered. "And... could it have one of those stones that glows a little? Like... dead people's coins? No, that's weird—"

"It's not weird," Annabeth said quickly. "Like moonstone? Or those glow-in-the-dark pebbles? So it's never completely black. Just dim. Like stars underground."

Nico looked up then, eyes shining. "Yeah. Like stars underground."

Annabeth opened her sketchbook and drew as he watched — a low niche in the third wall, deep enough to sit in, roofed with a slab to make real shade, with a smooth back stone where flecks of mica or glow-pebble could be set like constellations.

"Here," she said, showing him. "North side, so it stays shady most of the day. Big enough for two people, in case you want company, but with a high back so you feel hidden. And we'll put moonstone chips in the mortar. And you can pick the constellation. Any one you want."

Nico studied the drawing intently. Then he pointed. "Can it have... Bianca's name? Just... small. In the corner. So she knows it's her place too."

Annabeth's throat closed. In their old life she had not been able to give Bianca this. Here she could make room for both living siblings. She had to swallow twice before she could speak.

"Yes," she said. "Absolutely. We'll carve it. Small and careful. She'll be part of the foundation."

Nico nodded, blinking fast, and went back to staring at his cards. But his shoulder leaned, just slightly, against Annabeth's arm. And he stayed there, in the shadow with her, until the conch horn blew for dinner.

Annabeth wrote in her book, in her neatest letters: *SHADOW NICHE — north wall, roofed, moonstone stars, BIANCA.*

Before Annabeth wrote the proposal, she had to learn what the ground beneath the hollow could hold.

It was the first question Beckendorf asked when she showed him the drawing. She had drawn a shallow foundation for the low walls, a central floor of reclaimed tile and a roof light enough to leave the middle open. He put the drawing on his workbench and pointed to the three olive trees.

"How close is the nearest footing to that root?"

Annabeth checked her notes. "Two feet."

"From the trunk or from the root itself?"

"From the trunk."

"Then you haven't measured the root."

She looked at him. The difference had seemed trivial while she was drawing, because the trees looked stable and the foundation was small. Now she imagined a branch extending beneath the earth exactly where she intended to dig.

"So we need a site survey."

"At least a shallow one. And you have to ask Grover whether the trees can tolerate work that close to them. I'm not putting a building up that makes the next storm more likely to bring an olive tree down."

They went to the site with a measuring rod, several wooden stakes and a folded piece of tracing paper. Grover came because he knew the dryads who worked the grove. One of them met them at the edge of the shade and listened to their plan without being particularly impressed by the drawings.

"People always think roots end where they can no longer see them," she said.

Annabeth bristled. "I know they extend beyond the crown."

"Then why have you drawn a wall through the ground they occupy?"

It was a fair question, and she disliked it on principle. She looked at the plan, then asked where the largest roots traveled. The dryad showed them by touching the earth and making a slight movement ripple through the surface where the old roots lay close to the top. It wasn't a complete map. The smaller roots remained unseen.

"We'll keep the foundations outside this area," Annabeth said.

"And what about the rain?" the dryad asked.

Annabeth looked at the slope. Water came down from the higher meadow, passed between two trees and drained toward the strawberry fields. Her open central floor would catch some of it. The low wall on the west side might obstruct the rest.

Beckendorf gave her the measuring rod. "Walk it."

She did, following the faint channel where last week's rain had left darker soil. It passed almost through the center of her plan. She had placed the water feature there because it looked beautiful in the morning light. She hadn't asked where the water already wanted to go.

Percy arrived halfway through the survey, carrying a basket of tools from the forge.

"You found something?" he asked.

"The ground is doing exactly what it's always done," Annabeth said. "I drew a building across it."

Percy set down the basket and crouched beside the channel. He could feel the moisture moving beneath the soil, but not without concentrating. He didn't offer to redirect it. He asked where the flow went after it reached the lower path.

"Toward the fields," Grover said. "The plants need some of it. The rest goes into the drainage trench."

"Then we shouldn't block it."

"Yes," Annabeth replied. "That's what we've established."

He looked at her, surprised by her sharpness. She knew he wasn't the source of the problem, and that made her irritation worse.

"Sorry," she said. "I thought the design was almost done. Every time I ask someone to check it, I find another thing I missed."

"That means they're checking it properly," Beckendorf said.

Annabeth almost argued. Then she remembered the climbing wall's stop cord, which she had insisted be tested before anyone used it. Nobody had accused her of ruining the competition by finding a problem. She had accepted the delay because the correction made everyone safer.

"All right," she said. "I need another sheet of paper."

She set the tracing sheet over the drawing and marked the known root zones and water channel in different pencil strokes. The result left a clear crescent of ground between the trees, smaller than her original footprint.

"Could the roof fit here?" she asked Beckendorf.

"If the supports are independent of the roots and we test the soil."

"Then the walls have to move inward."

"Or you build fewer of them."

She examined the possibility. A lower structure would leave more of the hollow open and require fewer materials. She had been planning eight pillars because eight friends had helped make the idea possible. There was no reason the support system had to number eight if four pillars could hold the roof. The friends could still be represented in the mosaic, which didn't need deep excavation.

"Four pillars," she said. "A lighter roof. No fountain pipes. The water can pass outside the floor along the existing channel."

Grover looked toward the dryad. She nodded.

"That would leave the roots alone," she said. "I still want to see where the workers plan to store the timber. Don't pile it against the trunks."

Annabeth wrote the condition down. She knew the meeting had become longer than anyone expected. She also felt the small pleasure of a design growing more honest as it changed.

When they finished, she asked Percy to carry the stakes back to the workbench. He did, then waited while she folded the tracing paper.

"Are you upset I asked about the water?" he said.

"No. I was embarrassed that I hadn't."

"You notice things I miss all the time."

"That doesn't mean I like missing things."

"I know."

She smiled reluctantly. They had spent years in another life learning how to divide a problem into the things she could calculate and the things he could feel. Here the task was quieter, but the skill mattered just as much.

She rolled the revised plan. It wasn't yet ready for Chiron. It was finally ready for somebody to judge on its merits rather than on what she'd imagined the site looked like.

---

The proposal to Chiron was seven pages long.

Annabeth stayed up late two nights in a row — not ALL night, because Chiron had said to sleep, but until the cabin lights flickered — drafting it at the little desk by the window in Cabin One while Thalia braided her hair and quizzed her on measurements.

Page one: site map, with the three olive trees, the yarn circle, the path, compass rose pointing north.

Page two: plan view — an eighteen-foot circle divided into three arcs, each six feet of curved wall, three feet high, with gaps for entrances. Central rain bowl, four feet across. Benches with backs along each wall. Offering niches. Shadow niche on the north arc. Lightning rod placement to the southeast, ten feet clear.

Page three: elevations — what each wall would look like from inside. Sea wall with shell mosaic and wave pattern. Sky wall with wind chimes and a carved eagle feather motif (Thalia's suggestion: "not Zeus's face, that's creepy, just a feather"). Shadow wall with moonstone stars and Bianca's name.

Page four: materials list — fieldstone from the creek bed (with naiad permission), mortar mix from the Hephaestus stores, copper rod and cable (borrowed from the Big House lightning stores), blue glass and tiles (donated by arts-and-crafts), shells (Clarisse's bucket plus a collection trip), thyme and sedum and rosemary starts (Grover's contacts), lumber for benches (camp stores, cutoffs).

Page five: helpers — Percy (hauling, digging), Thalia (lightning rod, blessing), Clarisse + Silena (mosaic design and execution), Grover (planting, dryad liaison), Nico (shadow niche design, star placement), Beckendorf from Hephaestus (mortar advice), Lee Fletcher from Apollo (wind chimes tuning), plus any volunteers for carrying.

Page six: timeline — three weekends. Weekend one: clear and level, lay foundation. Weekend two: build walls, set bowl. Weekend three: mosaics, planting, finishing.

Page seven: a watercolor sketch of what it would look like finished, with sun on the stones and rosemary by the path and three kids sitting on the walls, tiny figures with no faces, just shapes. In the center, the rain bowl catching the sky.

Chiron read every page. He adjusted his glasses. He asked questions about drainage (Annabeth had a detail drawing ready) and mortar curing time (Beckendorf had briefed her) and whether the benches would be anchored (yes, with stone footings).

Then he signed the last page with a flourish.

"Approved," he said. "You may begin Saturday. And Annabeth — this is excellent work. Truly professional. Daedalus himself would be impressed by your thoroughness."

Annabeth floated out of the Big House like she'd been filled with helium. Thalia, waiting on the steps, caught her and spun her around.

"He signed it! He signed it!"

"I saw! I was there!"

They ran all the way to the dining pavilion shouting, bursting in to tell Percy, who stood up on the Poseidon table and yelled "SHRINE! SHRINE! SHRINE!" until Mr. D threatened to turn him into a shrub, which only made everyone chant louder.

---

The week before construction began, Silena asked Annabeth a question that wasn't on any of her architectural forms.

"Who gets to say no when the work becomes too much?"

Annabeth was sitting at the arts table, drawing the revised plan with the root boundaries marked on tracing paper. She'd spent the afternoon assigning material quantities. Stone, timber, mortar and reclaimed tile had each been recorded in a column. She knew which tools would be needed and which camper could operate them under supervision.

"What do you mean?" she asked.

"Your volunteers. What if Clarisse agrees to carry stones on Saturday and then has to go to her own training? What if Nico gets tired? What if the younger kids don't want to work once they see how many buckets there are?"

"Then someone else takes over."

"Who?"

Annabeth looked at her work schedule. She had written names in neat rows, but most of the slots didn't identify a replacement. In the plan, volunteer labor looked like a collection of reliable quantities. In reality it belonged to people who had made promises in good faith and had other obligations.

"I'll ask them," she said.

"Before you put their names down, please."

She felt herself flush. Silena had been helping from the beginning, and Annabeth trusted her. It was uncomfortable to discover she'd been treating even her closest friends as pieces of a system that had to work precisely as intended.

They went to the dining pavilion together. Clarisse was eating a late lunch with Nico, who had spilled something red on the corner of his Mythomagic cards and was trying to clean them with a napkin.

"Can you help us review the schedule?" Annabeth asked.

Clarisse looked suspicious. "Does this mean more work?"

"It might mean less."

"Interesting. Go on."

Annabeth explained that she needed to know which shifts each volunteer could actually manage. Clarisse had offered to move the largest stones, but she also led a weapons group Saturday morning. Nico was willing to help carry small tools and sort tiles, but he didn't want to miss his game practice. The older campers who'd offered to dig a shallow footing would need to stop when the weather became too hot.

"We could work in two blocks," Clarisse suggested. "One early, one after lunch. Nobody has to stay the whole day."

"That would slow construction."

"Then construction takes longer."

Annabeth started to object, then looked at the site notes. Chiron had already approved a staged build. She didn't have an external deadline. The pressure to finish in a single weekend was hers.

Nico raised his hand as though they were in class.

"I don't want to be assigned to just carrying things because I'm small."

"All right," Annabeth said. "What would you like to do?"

"I can help sort the dark tiles. And I want to make the little part by the shaded corner. You said there'd be a place for people to sit out of the sun."

"There will."

"Then I want to help measure it."

She wrote his name beside the tile and seating station rather than the transport line. Clarisse grinned at him.

"Look at you, negotiating."

"You're supposed to negotiate when somebody gives you a bad deal."

"Who taught you that?"

"You."

Clarisse stopped smiling. "I did not."

"You did yesterday when we were trading cards."

Silena laughed. Annabeth copied the new arrangement onto the schedule. It had fewer guaranteed workers than before, but she knew what each person had agreed to do.

That evening she brought the plan to Chiron. He looked at the volunteer list and asked what happened if somebody was injured.

"The relevant station stops. We don't transfer an untrained camper to the tools just to keep to the schedule. Someone checks the injury with the healer and we reschedule the work."

"And if the weather changes?"

"We cover the materials, clear the paths and stop until we can inspect the site."

"Good. Who is responsible for making that decision?"

Annabeth hesitated. She wanted to say she was. The entire project had begun in her head. She had organized the interviews, made the models and drawn the roof. It felt natural to assume she should decide when the work was safe.

"The supervising instructor," she said. "I can make recommendations. I shouldn't overrule someone responsible for the tools or the site."

Chiron nodded. "That distinction will matter."

He signed the volunteer schedule with a note to the workshop staff. Annabeth returned to the arts cabin and found Percy trying to calculate how many buckets of gravel he could carry without making two trips.

"How many do you want me to move?" he asked.

"Whatever you can safely carry after sword class. We don't have to do everything in one afternoon."

He studied her. "You're getting sensible."

"Please don't tell anybody. It would ruin my reputation."

They worked on the final checklist together. Percy drew a little fish beside the section of the plan that described the drainage channel. Annabeth erased the fish because it covered the measurement, then redrew a smaller one in the margin when she saw his expression.

They finished just before curfew. The schedule was less ambitious, the drawing was more precise and nobody had been promised they could finish the entire shrine in a weekend. Annabeth felt nervous about how much remained uncertain. She also trusted the plan more.

On Friday the first group collected its tools. Nico arrived early, carrying his own ruler. Clarisse came after training and asked where the large stones were stacked. Silena brought water and a first-aid kit. Percy appeared with a notebook and a borrowed wheelbarrow whose wheel squeaked on every turn.

Annabeth looked at the people standing around her. They had not been conscripted into her dream. They had chosen what they wanted to help build. She gave them the revised schedule and began with a safety briefing, not a speech about what the shrine would mean when it was finished.

---

Saturday dawned clear and gold.

Annabeth woke before the horn, dressed in her oldest t-shirt and shorts, laced her sneakers double-tight, and tucked her hair back with a bandana Silena had lent her (pink, with white stars — Silena insisted architects needed signature style). She rolled up her proposal and stuck it in her backpack with pencils, tape measure, string, stakes, work gloves, and the peanut butter sandwiches Thalia had made at midnight because "founders need snacks."

The site was misty when she arrived, dew on the clover, olive leaves silver in the early light. For a moment she just stood on the path, looking at the empty circle of grass where the yarn had been, and felt a flutter of nerves. What if she'd measured wrong? What if the stones were too heavy? What if nobody came?

Then she heard voices on the path — Percy, half-running with a wheelbarrow, Grover trotting behind with a bag of tools, Thalia carrying a copper rod like a staff, Clarisse and Silena with buckets clanking, Nico trailing with his hands in his pockets but definitely there, Beckendorf with a mortar hoe over his shoulder, Lee Fletcher with a bag of chimes, and behind them, a whole crowd of curious campers in work clothes.

They came. They all came.

Annabeth's nerves melted into something warm and fizzing, like sunlight in apple juice.

"Okay!" she called, clapping her hands, voice squeaking a little. She cleared her throat and tried again, channeling Chiron. "Okay! Thank you all for coming! Here's the plan—"

She unrolled her drawings and pinned them to a board she'd propped between two stakes. She explained the circle, the three walls, the jobs. She assigned teams. She handed out gloves. She felt like a general and a teacher and a kid playing pretend, all at once.

And then they worked.

Percy was, as promised, muscle. He hauled fieldstones from the creek in the wheelbarrow, trip after trip, arms straining, grinning every time Annabeth told him "that one's perfect" and directed where to stack them by size. He carried the biggest ones with Beckendorf, counting "one, two, THREE, lift!" and staggering dramatically. When Annabeth's string lines kept slipping, he held the stakes with his feet while she tied them, patient as a fence post.

"You're good at this," he told her during a water break, both of them dusty and sweaty. "Bossing people around."

"I'm not bossing," Annabeth protested. "I'm coordinating."

"You're bossing beautifully," Percy said solemnly, and she threw a clod of dirt at him, and he threw one back, and Thalia yelled "NO DIRT FIGHTS ON THE WORK SITE" while laughing so hard she had to sit down.

Thalia's lightning rod took all morning. She and Annabeth dug a deep hole to the southeast, Thalia showing Annabeth how to coil the copper cable, how to pack the grounding plate with charcoal and salt for conductivity, how to seal the connections. Thalia worked with reverent care, murmuring almost like a prayer as she set the rod upright — a slender copper spike that Annabeth had helped shape in the forge with a twisted vine pattern climbing it, topped with a small copper star.

"It's not about controlling the sky," Thalia said quietly as they tamped the earth around it. "It's about giving it a path. Respecting it. My dad... he doesn't do gentle. But we can make a place where the power has somewhere safe to go. That's the best we can do, sometimes."

Annabeth nodded, understanding. They tied blue and yellow ribbons to the rod — wind markers — and watched them stream.

Clarisse and Silena took charge of sorting. Clarisse dumped her bucket of shells onto a tarp — cockles and mussels and periwinkles and bits of sea glass, all carefully washed — and Silena arranged them in gradients while explaining her wave design to anyone who'd listen. They'd drawn the wave on paper: a great curling breaker that flowed across the sea wall, with foam made of white quartz chips and water made of blue glass and shells.

"Kids can add to it later," Silena said, eyes bright. "We'll leave a little mortar patch unfinished, with a sign: ADD YOUR SHELL. So it's never done. It keeps growing."

Clarisse, to everyone's amazement, turned out to be a natural teacher. She showed the younger kids how to press a shell in without squishing the mortar out, how to angle it so it caught the light. "Like this," she rumbled, guiding a tiny Demeter kid's hand. "Gentle. You're not stabbing it. You're tucking it in."

Grover moved around the edges, on his knees in the dirt, marking planting spots with sticks and twine. He'd brought flats of thyme and sedum from the greenhouse, and a big rosemary bush in a pot for the entrance, and a bag of wildflower seeds for the wild corner. He talked to the olive dryads constantly — "Excuse me, ma'am, we'll stay clear of that root, yes, thank you" — and made everyone step carefully.

"Feel that?" he said to Annabeth, pressing her hand to the soil where they'd leveled the circle. "It's happy. It likes being part of this."

Annabeth wasn't sure she felt anything except dirt, but she nodded anyway, because Grover's joy was infectious.

Nico hovered at first, watching from the shade of the middle olive tree. Annabeth didn't push. She just kept working, laying out the north arc with string, and after a while Nico drifted closer, toeing the line she'd marked.

"Is this where...?" he asked quietly.

"Yeah," Annabeth said. "Shadow wall goes here. Want to help me set the footing stones? They have to be really level, so the roof slab sits right."

Nico crouched beside her. Together they placed the first flat stones, checking with a level, tapping them with mallets. Nico was meticulous, adjusting pebbles underneath by millimeters. When the first stone was perfectly level, he looked up at Annabeth with the faintest smile.

"Good?" he asked.

"Perfect," Annabeth said. "You're hired as my precision specialist."

Nico's smile grew a tiny bit. He stayed for the rest of the morning, setting stones in the shade, humming tunelessly under his breath.

By noon, the sun was high and everyone was filthy. The foundation trench — a shallow, circular trench eighteen feet across, six inches deep, filled with packed gravel and the first course of level fieldstones — was nearly complete. Annabeth walked the circle, checking each stone with her level, making notes, adjusting.

It was real. Low and rough and only one stone high, but unmistakably a circle. A place.

Chiron arrived as they were breaking for lunch — sandwiches and lemonade hauled up by the dryads' good grace and a few naiads who'd come to see what the noise was. He dismounted from his wheelchair form into his full centaur shape to walk the site, nodding gravely at the trench, the rod, the sorted shells, the marked plantings.

"Excellent," he pronounced. "True craftsmanship begins with a true foundation. You have laid yours well."

Annabeth stood in the center of the circle, pizza in one hand, pencil in the other, surrounded by her friends — Percy with his arm around Grover, Thalia tuning a wind chime, Clarisse showing Silena a perfect shell, Nico sitting in the future shadow corner with his sandwich, swinging his feet.

The olive leaves rustled. The ribbons on the lightning rod fluttered. The lake glittered through the trees. The rosemary by the path released its sharp, clean scent when someone brushed it.

Annabeth looked down at her dusty sneakers, planted on level stone in the middle of her own design, and felt something settle in her chest, solid and warm as a foundation stone.

They still had two weekends of work — walls to raise, mortar to mix, mosaics to set, bowl to carve, chimes to hang, plants to tuck in, Bianca's name to carve small and careful. But the hardest part was done. The idea was out of her head and into the earth. People had touched it. People had added to it. It was already bigger than her.

Percy caught her eye and raised his lemonade cup. "To architecture!" he declared.

"To architecture!" everyone echoed, laughing, raising cups and shells and trowels.

Annabeth raised her pencil high.

"To us," she said quietly, but they heard her, and they cheered, and the sound rang across the hill and into the sky, where clouds gathered white and harmless, and the sun shone down on three olive trees and a circle of stones and a group of kids who, for once, had somewhere that was exactly theirs.

The following Saturday, before anybody began laying the mosaic, the first real mistake appeared.

Beckendorf arrived from the forge with a stack of cut cedar lengths for the roof supports. They had been measured from the approved sketch and prepared the day before. He laid them out on the grass and asked Annabeth to check the labels against the work order.

She looked at the first piece, then the second. The lengths didn't match the revised plan.

"These are too long," she said.

He frowned. "They match the order."

"The previous order."

They laid the drawings beside each other. Annabeth had moved the pillars inward after the root survey and reduced the span of the roof. She had updated the drawing, but she'd forgotten to send the revision to the forge before the timber was cut.

"I'm sorry," she said.

Beckendorf examined the ends of the wood. The lengths weren't ruined, but trimming them would require additional time and a new set of checks. He had other work that afternoon and could not simply abandon it because the architect had sent the wrong measurements.

"Can you reuse them?" Percy asked.

"Possibly," Beckendorf said. "But not without checking the bearing surfaces again. The roof has to hold up in bad weather, not only on a nice Saturday."

Annabeth felt herself becoming defensive. She wanted to point out that her revision had improved the design and that the old measurements were an understandable mistake. Neither statement changed the fact that somebody had spent an hour cutting wood she couldn't yet use.

"I should have given you the new drawing," she said. "I'll help correct the order."

"Good. Then I'll come back after lunch and we'll decide whether we can trim these or save them for another project."

Clarisse was standing nearby, holding a basket of tools. She looked disappointed. The roof supports were supposed to be the next task, and her shift ended before lunch.

"Can we work on something else?" she asked.

Annabeth checked the schedule. The mosaic tiles had already been sorted, but the floor needed a dry fitting before mortar could be mixed. That work did not depend on the roof.

"We can test the central pattern," she said. "No adhesive yet. Just lay the tiles out and make sure the curves fit."

Clarisse accepted the change with a shrug. Nico and Silena joined them on the ground, setting the small pieces on a cloth over the prepared bed. Annabeth showed them where the owl and wave patterns would meet, then stepped back and let them experiment with the arrangement.

The first attempt produced a gap along the outer ring large enough to trap a shoe heel. Clarisse pointed it out.

"That can't stay."

"I know."

"Then why don't we make the outer pieces wider?"

"Because they're reclaimed tile. We don't have wider ones in the same color."

Silena suggested a narrow line of darker pieces between the patterns. It would change the design slightly but allow the outside edge to remain smooth. Annabeth looked at the drawing, then the actual tiles. On paper the mosaic had been perfect. On the ground it needed to accommodate the material they had.

"Try it," she said.

Nico selected the darkest pieces and fitted them together, leaving enough space for mortar. The pattern became more interesting. The owl and waves no longer collided at a single point; they were separated by a thin band of shadow.

"That's better," he said.

Annabeth agreed. She'd been worried that every compromise would dilute her vision. Some were improving it in ways she hadn't anticipated.

At lunch, Beckendorf returned with the revised timber measurements. He confirmed that the longer lengths could be shortened safely, but some of the offcuts would be too narrow for load-bearing work. He proposed using them later as trim pieces or for a storage shelf.

"That means we won't finish the roof today," Annabeth said.

"No. We can prepare the joints and make sure everything fits before we raise it."

She looked at the half-completed structure. The unfinished appearance embarrassed her even though the project had never been scheduled to be complete in a day. Friends were working in small groups, talking quietly as they carried stones and swept soil away from the paths. Nobody was waiting for her to declare the building perfect before they enjoyed helping.

"Then we'll prepare the joints," she said.

The afternoon work went slowly. Beckendorf showed Percy and Clarisse how to mark the cuts, then left the actual saw work to the trained staff. Annabeth checked each measurement twice. Nico finished the dry fitting and took a photograph so the pattern could be recreated after the tiles were lifted to prepare the mortar bed. Grover inspected the tree roots and said the stored timber had been moved far enough away to satisfy the dryad.

At three, the clouds thickened. The supervising instructor stopped work while there was still time to move the tools under cover.

"We could finish the last two joints," Annabeth offered.

"Not if the rain starts before we can protect the timber."

"There's only about twenty minutes of work left."

"Then there will be twenty minutes of work tomorrow."

She swallowed the argument. Two weeks earlier she'd written a schedule with no room for delays. Now she knew exactly which job could wait, where the materials belonged and how to leave the site safe for other campers to pass.

They covered the timber, marked the incomplete section with a rope barrier and carried the tools to the shed. Annabeth checked the list to make sure nobody had left a bucket in the path. When the first drops fell, the workers were already on the way to the pavilion.

Percy caught up with her.

"Are you disappointed?"

"Very."

"Why?"

"Because I wanted to see it standing."

"You will."

"That's not much comfort."

He considered. "Would you rather it stood for an hour and then fell down?"

"No."

"Then you're doing the slower thing for a reason."

She glanced at him. He had spent most of the day carrying materials and asking whether he could help with the parts he understood. He wasn't trying to tell her disappointment was silly. He was reminding her that the purpose of building something wasn't the moment they could call it finished.

"I'm sorry about the wood," she said.

"You already told Beckendorf."

"I wanted to tell you too. You brought the old order to the forge for me."

"I did, because it was the one you gave me. We both missed the revision number."

Annabeth looked at him. "You don't have to share the mistake."

"I know. But next time I can check which drawing I'm carrying."

They walked under the shelter of the dining pavilion as the rain began to fall in earnest. Clarisse was already there, telling Silena that the mosaic would have looked better if they'd made the shadow band twice as thick. Nico argued that it would cover the owl's wing. They had been working on Annabeth's project all day, and they were still arguing about the design because it mattered to them too.

Annabeth spread the revised drawing on a dry table, carefully weighting its corners with empty cups.

"All right," she said. "Let's look at the border one more time before tomorrow."

They gathered around her. Outside, rain pattered against the roof. The half-built shrine was covered and safe. Their next day's work had a plan that accounted for what had actually happened, not what she'd expected that morning. The dryad would inspect the root boundary again before the first footing was completed. Beckendorf would sign the corrected timber list. Silena would keep a separate ledger of who had volunteered for each shift. Annabeth wrote those three tasks beside the date, folded the sketch and finally sat down to eat the dinner she'd nearly forgotten.

---

Annabeth went around the half-finished site after the others had gone to wash up. She marked a crooked stone for resetting and moved three tools beneath the tarpaulin so the rain wouldn't rust them. This was the second work Saturday; the original plan reserved one final weekend for walls, mosaics, planting and finishing. She would keep that date only if the footing passed inspection and the corrected supports fit.

Thalia appeared with the work schedule under one arm.

"You're coming to dinner," she said.

"I need five more minutes."

"You said that twenty minutes ago."

Annabeth looked at the chalk lines. There was much more to build. She put down the pencil and followed her sister down the hill, making Thalia promise to help carry the measuring rods back in the morning.
