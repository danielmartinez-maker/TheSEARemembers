## Chapter 47: Snow That Asks Permission

The snow at Camp Half-Blood never fell quite right.

Percy had noticed it with the first snow since his arrival, and he noticed it again now, standing on the frost-hard porch of Cabin Three with a chipped mug of hot chocolate going lukewarm in his hands. The flakes came down too slow over the strawberry fields, hanging in the air like they were deciding whether they were allowed to land. In the valley beyond Half-Blood Hill, the mortal world got blizzards. Here, the weather had to ask permission from the barrier, and from Mr. D, and from whatever mood the gods were in that week.

And this week, the mood was bad.

He breathed in. Cold stung his nose. Behind him, through the cabin door, he could hear Clarisse snoring loud enough to rattle the conch shells on the wall, and Silena humming while she brushed her hair, half-asleep and off-key.

It was early. Gray light pressed against the curtains. The sea was a dull iron line far beyond the hill, invisible but present, tugging at the back of his teeth the way it always did in winter when he stayed too long on land.

Percy was twelve. He knew that in the way you knew your own name. He also remembered being seventeen. He remembered the doors of Olympus opening and the immortality they had offered him there, a future he had refused. He remembered Annabeth at seventeen, tall and sun-browned and terrifyingly smart, laughing with her whole face when she finally let herself laugh. He remembered too much to fit comfortably inside a twelve-year-old body, and some mornings it made his chest ache in a way that had nothing to do with growing.

This morning it ached for a different reason. He hadn't slept well. Dreams had chased him — a dark throne room, lightning flickering where lightning shouldn't be able to flicker, a voice like stone grinding on stone saying theft, theft, theft.

He took another sip and made a face. Cold chocolate. Great.

The door creaked. Silena stuck her head out, hair in a loose braid over one shoulder, a thick blue sweater swallowing her hands. At fourteen she carried herself like she was older, except in the mornings before she was fully awake, when she looked exactly her age.

"You're brooding on the porch again," she said. "You're going to freeze."

"I'm a son of Poseidon," Percy said. "We don't freeze. We dramatically endure."

Silena snorted and stole his mug, drank, made the same face he had. "Cold. Dramatically undrinkable."

"Give it back, traitor."

She handed it back and leaned against the doorframe beside him, shoulder to shoulder. From here they could see the whole commons: the Big House with smoke curling from its chimney, the pavilion with its braziers already lit, the cabins with snow piled on their roofs. Cabin One gleamed white and sharp even in gray light, its marble columns dusted with snow. Someone — probably Thalia — had hung a string of blue Christmas lights around the door anyway, in open defiance of classical architecture.

"Have you seen the sky?" Silena asked quietly.

Percy looked up.

The sky over camp was usually a watercolor wash in winter, pale blue and soft. Today it was streaked with high, thin clouds moving too fast, and underneath them a bruised yellow-gray that reminded him of a storm that couldn't decide what it wanted to be.

"Yeah," he said. "It's weird."

"It's been weird for three days," Silena said. "Clarisse says I'm being paranoid."

"Clarisse says everyone's being paranoid except Clarisse."

"True." Silena nudged him. "Did you dream?"

Percy hesitated. In Cabin Three they told each other things. That was the rule they made themselves. Three half-siblings under one roof. They looked out for each other.

"Something about Olympus," he admitted. "Cold. Angry."

Silena was quiet for a second. Then she said, "Me too. I heard bells. Like alarm bells, but far away."

Before Percy could answer, a snowball hit the porch railing three inches from his elbow and exploded.

"LOVE BIRDS!" Clarisse bellowed from the middle of the path, already packing another one. She was thirteen, broad-shouldered and fierce even in flannel pajama pants and a parka thrown over them, her dark hair in a wild ponytail. "Get down here before I tell Annabeth you're cuddling your sister!"

"We're not cuddling!" Percy yelled. "We're standing!"

"Emotionally cuddling!" Clarisse launched the second snowball. It went wide and nailed the Cabin Three mailbox.

Silena laughed, bright and sudden, and the tight feeling in Percy's chest eased a little. This was camp. This was his family. Snow and stupid jokes and cold hot chocolate. Whatever the gods were doing up there, they couldn't have this.

He set the mug down and scooped up snow from the porch rail. "You're dead, Clarisse!"

"Bring it, little brother!"

---

Breakfast was loud.

The dining pavilion had no walls, which in late winter was either brave or insane, and the wind kept sneaking in around the bronze braziers and making the flames shiver. Everyone sat in their cabin groups, hunched in coats and scarves, breath fogging. The harpies in hairnets — disgruntled winter help from the kitchens — swooped back and forth with platters, screeching at anyone who tried to take food without offering to the gods first.

Percy sat with his sisters, scraping half his pancakes into the fire for his dad. The flames flared blue-green for a second, salt and sea air, and he felt that distant, familiar attention brush past him. Tired. Distracted.

That worried him more than anger would have.

Across the pavilion, at the Zeus table, Annabeth caught his eye and smiled.

She was twelve, small for her age, her blond hair pulled back in a thick braid. She wore a Camp Half-Blood sweatshirt three sizes too big — Thalia's — with the sleeves pushed up, and her gray eyes were bright even from far away. When she saw him looking, she lifted her mug in a silent toast.

Percy grinned so fast his face hurt.

Chiron had sat them both down weeks ago with lemonade and a talk so awkward Percy still cringed thinking about it. Thalia had insisted on being present. It had been mortifying, though she had eventually stopped looking as if handholding needed an armed escort. They were a couple. Everyone at camp knew.

It meant, right now, Annabeth mouthing are you okay across the pavilion while Thalia pretended not to notice.

Percy nodded, then shrugged, then mouthed later.

Annabeth narrowed her eyes. That was her thinking face. Even at twelve with pancake syrup on her chin, she had that same look she'd had at seventeen bent over blueprints in the Athena cabin, like she was already three steps ahead.

Thalia, sixteen and tall and punk in a way that made the younger Ares kids both terrified and obsessed with her, leaned over and said something to Annabeth that made her laugh.

On Percy's left, Clarisse elbowed him hard enough to slosh his milk.

"Stop making goo-eyes at Grace," she said. "You're embarrassing the family."

"I'm not making goo-eyes."

"You're making extra goo-eyes. The gooiest."

Silena, on his right, was braiding a strand of her hair with red ribbon and watching the Zeus table with a thoughtful look. "Thalia looks tired," she said quietly. "Annabeth too. Do you think they dreamed?"

Percy followed her gaze. Now that she mentioned it, Annabeth did have shadows under her eyes. Thalia kept rolling her shoulders like her back hurt, and every time thunder rumbled far away — too far to be natural — her hand twitched toward her spear.

"Maybe," he said.

The pavilion doors banged. Grover trotted in late and slid into place at the satyr table. Luke Castellan stood to offer for Hermes cabin, caught Percy's eye and nodded.

Then Chiron walked in, and the room went quiet in a wave.

---

Chiron didn't have to do anything to get attention. He just had to look like he had something to say. Today, in his wheelchair with a thick blanket over his knees, his beard neatly trimmed and his eyes grave, he had that look so strongly that even the harpies stopped screeching.

"May I have your attention," he said, and his voice carried without shouting. "A quick announcement before activities. As many of you know, the gods held council on Olympus at the winter solstice. Matters from that meeting remain unsettled."

A ripple went through the pavilion. Everyone knew about the winter council. It was like hearing your parents were still arguing about a big meeting concerning you — technically distant, practically terrifying.

Chiron continued, calm and steady. "Rumors travel fast, even to camp. I want you to hear truth from me, not whispers from the woods. There has been — a disturbance on Olympus. An item of great power has gone missing."

Percy felt Annabeth go still across the room without even looking at her. He knew. He knew in his bones, in the seventeen-year-old memory that suddenly sat up inside his twelve-year-old chest and said oh no. Oh no, it's starting.

Chiron chose his words carefully. "I will not name it. I will not speculate on who is responsible. The gods are discussing it privately. While they do, camp will continue as normal. Extra sword lessons. Extra capture the flag. Stay within the borders. No one is in trouble. No one is accused."

It was the wrong thing to say. Percy could feel it land wrong. No one is accused made everyone immediately think accused of what, who, why.

Mr. D, sitting at the head table with a Diet Coke and a magazine he wasn't reading, snorted. "Yes, yes. Don't steal godly weapons, don't start wars, blah blah blah. Can we eat?"

A few people laughed, thin and nervous.

Chiron smiled, but it didn't reach his eyes. "Eat. And remember — we are a family here. Whatever happens on Olympus, we take care of our own."

He wheeled back. Conversation restarted, louder and faster than before, all hiss and rumor.

Percy stared at his plate. His pancakes had gone cold.

Missing. An item of great power. He didn't need Chiron to name it. Lightning. A bolt that was not a metaphor but a real thing, humming with power that made your teeth ache. Zeus's master bolt. Gone.

Annabeth was staring at him now, openly. Her face had gone pale.

She remembered too.

Clarisse kicked him under the table. "Hey," she muttered, low. "Hey. Breathe. You're doing the Atlantis prince face."

"What Atlantis prince face?" Percy whispered back.

"The one where you look seventeen and sad and like you're about to do something noble and stupid."

Silena leaned in. "Do you know what it is?" she whispered. "The thing that's missing?"

Percy looked at his sisters. His throat felt dry.

"I think so," he said. "And if I'm right, we're about to have a really bad winter."


---

Percy caught Chiron before the centaur could leave the pavilion.

He had to move quickly. The breakfast crowd was already splitting into little groups, each group talking faster than the last, and two Hermes kids had set off toward the cabins as though somebody had announced a race. Percy saw Grover trying to get their attention and failing.

"Chiron?" he called.

Annabeth was right behind him, with Thalia on her other side. None of the three had agreed to come together. Percy suspected they had all simply arrived at the same thought.

Chiron paused by the pavilion steps. He looked at the three of them, then over their heads at the campers streaming out into the snow.

"If you're asking whether I can tell you the missing object's name, the answer is no."

"We haven't asked that yet," Annabeth said.

"You were about to."

"I was going to ask how you plan to stop the entire camp from accusing whoever they don't like."

Chiron looked at her for a moment. Thalia turned toward Annabeth with visible surprise, then gave a small, approving nod.

"That is a fair question," he said. "For now, nobody is to be accused publicly. The council has given us no evidence of wrongdoing by any camper. I will make that clear at evening announcements."

"By evening," Thalia said, "the Stolls will have named three thieves and a getaway chariot."

"Then I will make it clear before afternoon activities as well."

Percy watched Chiron's hands. One held the edge of his blanket and the other rested on the wheel of his chair. He wasn't fidgeting, exactly, but the fabric had gathered so tightly in his fingers that the knuckles stood out.

"Do you believe somebody here stole something?" Percy asked.

"I have not said that."

"That's not an answer."

"No, Percy," Chiron said, more sharply than Percy expected. He glanced toward the cabins and lowered his voice. "I do not have enough information to form an honest answer. If I begin guessing, I become another source of rumors. The difference is that campers tend to believe my guesses."

Percy wanted to argue. He knew the old shape of this story well enough to be frightened by what might follow, but the people and relationships around him were different now. If he told Chiron that Zeus's bolt was missing because he remembered a quest from another life, he would have to explain why this memory should be trusted in a world already proving it could change.

Annabeth stepped closer. "Can we say that? The part about evidence?"

"You may say you spoke to me, and that I have seen no evidence naming anyone at camp."

"Not that no evidence exists," Annabeth said.

"Correct. Those are two different claims."

Thalia folded her arms. "And what are we supposed to do when someone says being a child of Poseidon is evidence?"

Chiron's eyes moved to Percy. For a second he looked terribly old.

"Bring me the words, the time and the person who said them. I will handle what can be handled through camp discipline."

"That's an administrative answer," Thalia said.

"It is the answer I can act on."

Percy knew Thalia wanted more. He could feel it in the way the cold air around her seemed to tighten. If Zeus had brought this trouble to her sister's home, she would prefer to be able to find him, stand in front of him and demand that he stop. But Chiron couldn't arrange a conversation with Zeus the way he arranged sword practice.

"What happens to the people who get blamed while you write down names?" Percy asked.

Chiron was quiet for a moment.

"I hope their friends stand beside them," he said. "And I hope their friends know the difference between defending someone and starting another fight."

A snowflake landed on Thalia's hair and vanished. She looked away.

Percy thought about Clarisse, who would take the second option before breakfast if somebody gave her half an excuse.

"Okay," he said. "No fights."

Thalia snorted.

"I said no fights," Percy repeated, looking at her. "I didn't say I wasn't angry."

"Good distinction," Annabeth said.

Chiron reached the bottom step. "You three have training in half an hour. Attend it. Routine is not a cure for uncertainty, but abandoning every ordinary obligation will not improve matters."

He rolled toward the Big House. Percy stayed on the steps, watching campers cross the field in the snow. Annabeth was already writing on the back of her breakfast schedule.

"What are you doing?" he asked.

"Dates. Things people say. If Chiron wants facts, I'd rather have them before somebody decides a rumor counts as one."

Thalia read over her shoulder. "You realize that makes you the most dangerous person in camp with a pencil."

"Only to people who don't check their sources."

Percy wished the remark could have made him laugh. It almost did.

---

By lunch, the rumors had grown teeth.

Percy had always been amazed at how fast gossip moved at camp, faster than Iris messages, faster than wind nymphs. By the time sword practice ended — a sloppy, distracted session where even Clarisse kept missing parries — the story had already split into four versions.

Version one, from the Apollo cabin: Someone stole Zeus's master bolt right out of his hands on Olympus. A thief snuck in during the night. The gods woke up and it was just gone.

Version two, from the Ares table, now the Ares cabin where Clarisse often trained and argued with her sparring partners: Poseidon stole it to make a super trident-bolt combo and overthrow Zeus. Duh.

Version three, from a wide-eyed Hermes kid who had heard it from a wood nymph who had heard it from a satyr: It wasn't stolen, it was lost. Zeus misplaced it. Which was somehow scarier, because what did it mean if the king of the gods could lose his weapon?

Version four, whispered by the Hecate kids who liked to be mysterious: It was a demigod. Had to be. Gods couldn't steal from gods like that. Someone half-blood had climbed Olympus and taken it.

Percy heard all four before noon and hated all four.

He found Annabeth at the forge, which was where she went when she needed to think and couldn't stand the library. She wasn't forging. She was sitting on an upturned bucket outside, watching Beckendorf hammer something inside, her notebook open on her knees but her pencil still.

She looked up when she heard him, and her whole face changed.

"Hey," she said softly.

"Hey." He sat beside her on the cold ground, not caring about snow seeping through his jeans. "You dreamed too."

It wasn't a question. She nodded anyway.

"Thunder," she said. "And my dad — Zeus — standing on a balcony looking down at the sea. He looked... I don't have a word. Not angry yet. Just cold. Like he'd already decided."

Percy's stomach twisted. "I dreamed about a throne room. And the word theft."

Annabeth closed her notebook. Her hands were shaking a little, so she tucked them into her sleeves. "Percy. It's the bolt. It has to be. Master bolt, missing after the winter solstice council. That's — that's how it started. Before."

Before meant before, in the other life, the first life, when she was Athena's daughter and he was still just Poseidon's son and not a prince of Atlantis, when they were seventeen and the world had already tried to end twice. They didn't talk about Before much. It felt too big for their mouths now.

"I know," he said. "I was hoping I was wrong."

"You weren't." She glanced toward the forge door, then back at him. Her voice dropped. "I remember who took it before. Luke stole the bolt and the helm. Ares carried them, and the backpack put the bolt on us. But there's no reason to assume the same route now. We're here earlier. My family is different. I know what Luke did then —" She stopped.

Percy went cold. "Luke?"

Annabeth shook her head quickly. "I don't know what he's done here. Don't — don't repeat that as an accusation. He's been nice. He's been good this year."

Percy looked past her, toward the cabins. Luke was at the volleyball court with the Hermes kids, laughing, showing a new kid how to serve. Normal. Friendly.

But Annabeth's uncertainty scared him more than certainty would have. Knowing the old theft did not give them evidence for this one. A wrong accusation could warn the thief instead of stopping him.

"We should tell Chiron," Annabeth whispered. "What happened before, and what we can actually confirm here. That Zeus is going to blame your dad."

Percy picked at the snow. "Would he believe us? Or would it just make it worse? Hi, Chiron, we're twelve but actually seventeen, and by the way the king of the gods is about to accuse my dad of theft and start a countdown to war. Merry Christmas."

Annabeth huffed a reluctant laugh. "When you put it like that."

"We need to be careful," Percy said. He hated how old his voice sounded to himself. "If Zeus thinks Poseidon did it, he's going to be watching Cabin Three. And if he's watching you —"

"He already is," Annabeth said quietly. "Thalia and me. At breakfast, after Chiron spoke, Katie from Demeter told me we must feel so honored to be Zeus's daughters right now. Like we're supposed to prove something."

Percy frowned. "Prove what?"

"That we're loyal. That we're not — I don't know. Spies for the sea." She tried to smile and failed. "Thalia told her to shove it. Very big-sister. Then Thalia went pale because she realized she'd just snapped at a twelve-year-old in front of everyone."

Percy reached over and took her hand. Her fingers were freezing. He wrapped both his hands around hers to warm them, chafing gently. It was something he'd done a hundred times, in this life and the last. Chaste. Simple. Their normal.

Annabeth leaned her shoulder against his. "I'm scared," she admitted, so quietly the forge hammer almost covered it.

"Me too," he said.

They sat like that while snow settled on their hair. Inside, metal rang on metal. Outside, the sky churned yellow-gray.

---

The watching started that afternoon.

It wasn't obvious at first. It was the kind of thing you felt before you saw — a prickle between the shoulder blades during archery, a conversation that stopped when you walked past the lava wall.

Percy noticed it because Amphitrite's recent letters had warned him about it. Watch the eyes, she had written. People look, then look away too fast.

At dinner, people looked, then looked away too fast.

Cabin Three walked in together, as always — Percy in the middle, Clarisse cracking her knuckles, Silena with her chin up — and the pavilion got just a fraction quieter. Not silent. Just... aware.

"Don't stare back," Silena murmured, smiling like she wasn't saying anything important. "Smile. Be normal."

"I am normal," Clarisse muttered. "I'm the most normal."

"Clarisse, you once wrestled a hellhound for fun."

"It was a dare!"

Percy tried to smile. He offered to the gods, ate his stew, listened to Silena chatter. But he could feel it. The Hermes table whispering. A younger Apollo kid asking loudly why Poseidon would need lightning, and his older sister shushing him.

Across the room, the Zeus table was getting the opposite treatment — too much attention, too bright. People kept going over to Thalia and Annabeth with encouragement that sounded like pressure. Thalia handled it with clipped nods. Annabeth handled it by going very still and polite.

After dinner, Thalia caught Percy by the arm near the braziers. Her grip was strong, her eyes stormy.

"Walk with me," she said. It wasn't a request.

They walked to the edge of the pavilion, out of earshot. Snow hissed in the flames.

"Is it true?" Thalia asked without preamble. "What they're saying. That Zeus thinks your dad stole his bolt."

Percy stared at her. "How do you know that part? Chiron didn't say —"

"Chiron didn't have to." Thalia's laugh was short. "I got an Iris message an hour ago. From my dad's attendant. Very formal. Very proud. Reminding me that daughters of Zeus stand for loyalty and justice in trying times and that the king of the gods is certain the sea grows restless and ambitious." She spat the last words. "Restless and ambitious. That's my dad talking about your dad."

Percy's hands curled. "My dad wouldn't steal. He wouldn't — he doesn't want war."

"I know that." Thalia rubbed her face. She looked sixteen going on forty. "You think I don't know that? I've eaten dinner with your family. I've seen your dad look at you like you hung the moon. But my dad —" She glanced toward Annabeth, who was waiting a few feet away pretending not to listen. "My dad is proud and he's scared, and when he's scared he hits first. And right now he's telling his daughters to prove we're not divided."

Annabeth stepped closer. "Thalia —"

"It's fine," Thalia said, but her voice cracked. "It's fine, Annie. We'll be fine. We just have to be perfect. No mistakes. No fights. No reason for anyone to say the Zeus girls are soft on Poseidon."

Percy felt sick. "We're not soft. We're friends. We're family."

"I know." Thalia met his eyes, and for a second she was just a tired older sister, not a daughter of Zeus or anything but herself. "But family is what's going to get us all in trouble if the gods start choosing sides."

She squeezed his shoulder and walked away before he could answer. Annabeth lingered.

"She got the message and I didn't," Annabeth said quietly. "I'm younger. Less important. Or maybe — maybe he doesn't trust me because I'm always with you."

Percy took her hand quickly, squeezed, let go before anyone could comment. "He trusts you. You're Annabeth. You're the most trustworthy person I know."

She smiled crookedly. "Biased."

"Accurate."

Behind them, someone coughed loudly. A group of Ares kids passing by, staring. Percy stared back until they moved on.

Annabeth sighed. "Camp is going to be unbearable."

"Worse than the time the toilets backed up with hellhound fur?"

"Much worse."

---

That night, Cabin Three stayed up late.

They did that sometimes, when the wind howled and none of them wanted to be alone with dreams. Silena dragged all their blankets onto the floor between the bunks, Clarisse built a nest of pillows like a fort commander, Percy made hot chocolate that actually stayed hot this time. They turned the conch-shell lamp low so the walls glowed pink and blue.

Clarisse lay on her stomach, chin in her hands. "Okay. Facts. Someone stole Zeus's big lightning stick. Zeus thinks Dad did it. Dad didn't do it. Right?"

"Right," Percy and Silena said together.

"Why does he think Dad did it?" Clarisse asked.

Silena twisted her braid. "Because they're brothers and they fight. Because the sea has been getting stronger. Atlantis has been — you know. Rebuilding. Amphitrite says the surface world keeps polluting and Dad keeps having to clean it and Zeus keeps taking credit for rain."

Percy nodded. Amphitrite had said as much in her letters. The sea is restless, she'd written. Your father works day and night to keep the old treaties.

"Also," Percy said slowly, "because Poseidon is the only one powerful enough to steal from Zeus and live. That's what people think. If it was a demigod, no one wants to say it, because then they'd have to look here."

Clarisse sat up. "Here? You think someone at camp took it?"

"I think someone wants us to think about who took it," Percy said carefully. He couldn't say what he remembered about Luke, about betrayal and a beach and a scorpion. Not without proof. Not about someone who'd been kind to him. "I think we're being played."

Silena pulled her blanket tighter. "Amphitrite would say courts love a scapegoat. Give them a villain and they stop looking for the truth."

"Smart," Clarisse said. "Annoying, but smart."

They were quiet. Outside, wind rattled the shutters. Somewhere, an owl called, and something else called back that was not an owl.

"Are we in danger?" Silena asked at last, small. Not the fourteen-year-old designer who could stare down Ares kids. Just his sister.

Percy looked at her, at Clarisse trying to look brave. He thought about Atlantis, about crowns and thrones, about being twelve and being told you might be the reason for a war.

"Not tonight," he said firmly. "Tonight we're just us. No gods. No watching. Just bad hot chocolate and Clarisse's snoring."

"Hey!"

Silena giggled and threw a pillow at him. Clarisse threw one back. Soon feathers were flying and they were laughing too hard to be scared.

Later, when they'd settled, Silena fell asleep with her head on Clarisse's arm, and Clarisse fell asleep mid-sentence. Percy stayed awake a little longer, watching the shell lamp flicker.

Not tonight. But tomorrow? Tomorrow the watching would still be there.

---

The next two days were fluff and dread braided together so tightly Percy couldn't tell them apart.

On the surface, camp tried to be normal. Snowball capture the flag — Clarisse led the blue team to victory by hiding the flag inside a snowman. Arts and crafts — Silena taught younger campers to knit scarves, and Annabeth made one with uneven edges that Percy wore anyway. Evening campfire — Thalia told a hydra story that made even the Demeter kids forget to be nervous for an hour.

Percy held onto those moments. He memorized them the way Annabeth memorized maps.

Annabeth and Percy met every afternoon by the lake, where the water had frozen at the edges but stayed liquid in the middle, refusing to ice over for Poseidon's son. They skipped stones. They did homework. They talked in low voices about what they remembered.

"I remember the Oracle," Annabeth said one afternoon, breath fogging. "Green smoke. A prophecy. Three would go — you, me, and Grover. To the Underworld. Hades thought we stole his helm. The bolt appeared in Ares's backpack. They were pitting everyone against each other."

Percy turned a smooth stone in his fingers. "War. Zeus and Poseidon and Hades all accusing each other. And Kronos —" He stopped. The name felt too big, too cold. "Someone working for him. Using the theft."

Annabeth nodded grimly. "That was the old theft. The Titan. He was waking up. He used us. Used Luke —" She cut herself off again, eyes wide.

They looked at each other.

"Do you think it's happening again?" Percy asked. "Exactly the same?"

"I think the gods don't change," Annabeth said. "And I think whoever took the bolt knows that. If you want a war, you steal from Zeus and let him blame Poseidon. It's easy."

Percy skipped his stone. It bounced three times before sinking. "Then we have to stop it. Before the summer solstice."

Annabeth was quiet. Then she said, "We're twelve, Percy."

"I know."

"We're not supposed to go on quests until we're older. Chiron would never allow it."

"I know."

She leaned against his shoulder. He leaned back. Their scarves tangled.

"I hate this," she whispered. "Remembering being older and still being stuck. I keep thinking — if I were seventeen, I'd know what to do. I'd have a plan. Now I just have — feelings. And a knit scarf that looks like a snake."

Percy laughed despite himself. "It's a good snake."

"It's a terrible snake and you love it because I made it."

"True."

They sat until their noses were red. It was fluff. It was perfect. Underneath it, dread hummed like a plucked wire.

Because the watching hadn't stopped. It had gotten worse.

---

By the third day, it was open.

Percy walked into sword practice and found two new kids staring at him. Not subtle. Just staring.

"That's him," one whispered. "The Poseidon prince. You think he knows where it is?"

"Shut up," Percy said, louder than he meant to. Clarisse put a hand on his arm.

"Ignore them," she muttered. "They're babies. They don't know."

But it wasn't just babies. At lunch, an older Hermes kid asked Silena, all smiles, if Atlantis had any new weapons lately. At archery, someone asked Thalia if it was hard living so close to traitors, meaning Cabin Three, and Thalia nocked an arrow so fast the kid walked away.

And the Zeus girls were getting it from the other side.

Annabeth told Percy about it in hurried whispers between classes. How a counselor from Apollo had pulled Thalia aside and suggested she make a public offering to Zeus to show where her loyalties lie. How an older camper had told Annabeth she should spend less time with Poseidon kids right now, for optics. Optics. Like they were politicians instead of twelve-year-olds.

"Thalia told him to mind his own business," Annabeth said, fierce and miserable. "But then she cried after. She never cries. She said she feels like she's being split in half. Like if she defends you, she's betraying Dad, and if she defends Dad, she's betraying us."

Percy felt that like a punch. "She's not betraying anyone."

"I know. But Zeus keeps sending messages. Little ones. Reminders. Loyalty. Duty. He hasn't even messaged me directly, and that feels worse, like I'm being tested to see if I'll come begging."

Percy wanted to march up to Olympus and yell at the king of the gods. He wanted to be seventeen again, with Riptide and a track record, someone people listened to. Instead he was twelve with a dull practice sword and a reputation he hadn't earned.

That night at dinner, Chiron watched everything with sad, old eyes. Percy could see him noting who sat where, who whispered, who stared. Mr. D drank Diet Coke and pretended not to care, but his fingers tapped restlessly on the table. Even the gods' CC — the camp directors — felt it.

After dinner, a note arrived for Percy, Thalia, Annabeth, Clarisse, and Silena. Same neat handwriting. Same words.

Come to the Big House at eight. We need to talk. — C.

---

The Big House living room was warm and smelled like tea and old books. A fire crackled. Chiron sat in his wheelchair by the hearth, his blanket over his knees, his expression kind and tired. Mr. D was conspicuously absent, which told Percy how serious this was.

The five of them filed in — Thalia first, chin high, Annabeth close behind her, then Percy with his sisters. They sat on the couches. No one spoke.

Chiron let the silence sit for a moment. Then he said, "Thank you for coming. I know the last few days have been difficult."

Clarisse snorted. "That's one word for it."

"People are scared," Chiron said. "When people are scared, they look for someone to blame. It is human. It is also godly. I want you to know that I do not blame any of you. Neither does this camp."

Thalia's hands were fists in her lap. "But Zeus does."

Chiron didn't flinch. "Zeus is angry and frightened. His symbol of power has been taken. He believes —" He paused. "He believes Poseidon has reason to take it. Old grievances. Growing power in the sea. He has said as much in council."

Silena made a small sound. Percy felt cold all over.

"Did Dad do it?" Clarisse asked bluntly. "Just say it. Did he?"

"No," Chiron said firmly. "I have spoken with Poseidon. He denies it. I believe him. The sea has no interest in war. But belief is not proof, and Zeus demands proof."

Annabeth spoke for the first time, her voice thin but steady. "What about us? Are we accused?"

Chiron looked at her with deep sorrow. "No. Never. But you are — visible. As children of the Big Three, you carry your parents' power and their politics. People will watch you to see which way the wind blows. That is unfair. It is also true."

Thalia stared into the fire. "He wants us to choose."

"He wants to feel secure," Chiron corrected gently. "Which, for Zeus, often looks like demanding loyalty."

Percy felt Annabeth's hand creep across the cushion toward him. He took it without looking, lacing their fingers under the edge of the blanket Silena had draped over their laps. Her hand was cold. His was sweaty. They held on anyway.

Chiron kept talking, about staying together, about not rising to taunts, about reporting bullying, about the solstice. Percy tried to listen, but most of his attention was on Annabeth's hand in his, small and cold. She was scared. He could feel it in the tremor she tried to hide. He was scared too. The old, seventeen-year-old fear — of war, of quests, of losing people — sitting inside a twelve-year-old body that wanted to cry.

He squeezed her hand. She squeezed back. Twice. Their signal. I'm here. I'm here.

Under the table, hidden, safe. Together.

Clarisse was saying something brave about how Cabin Three could handle staring. Silena was asking quietly if Atlantis was safe. Thalia was asking what would happen if the bolt wasn't found by the summer solstice.

Chiron's answer was careful. "Then the council will have to decide on action. Zeus has set a deadline. The summer solstice. If it is not returned —" He didn't finish.

He didn't have to.

Percy remembered. Twenty-one days? No — that was later. This was different. This was the beginning, when no one knew how bad it would get. A deadline. A threat. The sky against the sea.

Annabeth's fingers tightened. Percy could feel her thinking, racing ahead the way she always did. He could almost hear her seventeen-year-old voice saying we have to go, we have to find it, we have to stop the war.

But they were twelve. And Chiron was watching them with eyes that had seen three thousand years of brave children die.

"For now," Chiron said, "you do nothing except be children. Train. Learn. Take care of each other. Promise me you will not go looking for trouble."

They all nodded. Even Thalia. Even Clarisse.

Percy nodded too, while holding Annabeth's hand, while thinking about lightning and thrones and a thief no one could name.

He didn't feel like a promise-keeper. He felt like a prince with a secret and a girlfriend with nightmares and two cabins full of siblings caught between sea and sky.

---

They walked back together through the snow.

It was full dark, the path lit by torches that hissed. Thalia walked with Clarisse a little ahead, talking in low voices. Silena walked with Annabeth, their heads bent together.

Percy trailed a step behind, watching them all. His family. His strange, patched-together, god-touched family.

Annabeth dropped back to walk beside him. Without a word, she slipped her hand into his. Glove to glove. Cold and woolly and perfect.

"Chiron knows more than he's saying," she murmured.

"Yeah."

"You think he'll let us help?"

Percy looked at her. Snowflakes caught on her eyelashes. Her gray eyes reflected torchlight.

"I think he's going to try to protect us," he said. "And I think it's not going to work."

Annabeth nodded slowly. "The quest. It's going to happen. Us. Grover. It's going to be us."

Percy swallowed. "Are you ready?"

"No." She smiled, small and brave. "Are you?"

"No."

They walked the rest of the way hand in hand. Ahead, Thalia glanced back, saw them, and deliberately turned forward again, giving them privacy. Clarisse saw too and made a gagging face, but softly, without malice. Silena just smiled.

At the fork where the path split toward Cabin One and Cabin Three, they stopped. They had to separate. Curfew. Rules. Optics.

Annabeth stood on tiptoe — she had to, even though they were the same age — and kissed Percy's cheek quickly, chaste and sweet. "Goodnight, Seaweed Brain."

"Goodnight, Wise Girl."

Thalia cleared her throat loudly. "Bed. Now. Before I become the worst chaperone in history."

"Love you!" Annabeth called to Percy as Thalia steered her away.

"Love you — uh, as a friend! Chastely!" Percy called back, and Clarisse nearly shoved him into a snowbank.

"Gods, little brother. Smooth."

Silena laughed, linking arms with both of them as they headed for Cabin Three. "Very princely. Amphitrite would be proud."

Percy grinned, but as they reached their porch, he looked up.

The sky had cleared enough to show stars. Somewhere up there, Olympus glowed. Somewhere up there, a king sat on a throne without his weapon, growing angrier by the hour.

The bolt was gone. Camp was scared, and his sisters were brave, and his girlfriend was brave, and he was twelve with memories of seventeen pressing on his ribs like shrapnel.

He held the door for his sisters. Inside was warmth and blankets and the smell of the sea.

Percy took one last look at Cabin One, where a light still burned in the window where Annabeth slept. He thought about her hand in his under the blanket, trembling and holding on. Scared together was better than scared alone. He knew that now. He'd known it at seventeen, and he knew it at twelve.

Tomorrow there would be more whispers. More watching. More pressure to prove loyalty to fathers who threw lightning and stirred hurricanes.

Tonight, at least, they were all under the same sky, under the same barrier, alive.

He went inside and bolted the door.
