## Chapter 77: Beach Day (Atlantis Style)

The bright April morning at Camp Half-Blood started with the conch.

Not the war conch from the Big House, all brass and urgency. This was deeper, rounder, a sound that rolled up from the Sound and through the strawberry fields and into every open cabin window like someone pouring warm water into a bowl.

Percy Jackson woke up knowing who it was before he even opened his eyes.

Tyson, back for a short weekend visit approved by Chiron and the undersea forges, was already sitting up in the guest bunk, his one big eye wide and shining.

"Brother!" Tyson whispered, delighted and trying - and failing - to whisper quietly enough not to wake Percy, Clarisse, and Silena in their bunks. "The sea mother calls!"

Percy scrubbed a hand through his hair and grinned, sleep still clinging to him. "Yeah, big guy. I heard."

Through the cabin's shell-framed window, the Long Island Sound was glittering that impossible morning blue, flat and bright as hammered glass. And out near the second buoy, where the water dropped off into camp-protected green, something white was arching out of the water and diving back down again.

A hippocampus. And behind it, towed on a net of glowing seaweed, a cluster of bobbing shapes that even from half a mile away Percy could tell were not normal driftwood.

He sat up fast. The Sea Remembers, after all. It always started like this — a tug behind his navel, a familiar pressure in his ears, like the ocean clearing its throat to say his name.

By the time he and Tyson got down to the beach in swim shorts and camp tees, flip-flops slapping, half the camp was already gathering.

Annabeth was there first, of course, her blonde hair pulled back in a high ponytail, a notebook already tucked under her arm out of sheer habit, her gray eyes narrowed at the approaching hippocampus like she was calculating speed and wake displacement. She wore an orange Camp Half-Blood T-shirt over her swimsuit and a pair of battered denim shorts, her camp bead necklace bright against her sun-browned skin.

She glanced at Percy and her whole face softened, that small private smile she only ever gave him.

"Morning, Seaweed Brain," she called.

"Morning, Wise Girl," Percy said, his heart doing the stupid little lift it always did. Years of memories. Two wars survived in the life they remembered. And she still did that to him.

Grover trotted down behind them, his crutches set aside for the beach day, his goat legs kicking up sand, a straw hat jammed over his curly hair. "Does anybody else feel blessedly unprepared for divine intervention before breakfast?" he asked. "Because I do."

"Shut up and help me with the rope," Clarisse La Rue barked, already ankle-deep in the surf in her Ares-red one-piece and board shorts, her spear planted point-down in the wet sand behind her. She looked like she had been awake for an hour doing push-ups. She probably had.

Silena Beauregard came drifting down the dune path like the morning was staged for her, which for Silena it kind of was. Percy's sister didn't walk to the beach, she arrived. Her dark hair was in two loose braids threaded with blue string, a white cover-up fluttering around her pink swimsuit, a woven tote over her shoulder already labeled SHELLS in puffy paint letters. Charlie Beckendorf walked beside her, huge and quiet, carrying about four beach chairs, two umbrellas, and a cooler like it was nothing.

And then, to everyone's surprise, Thalia Grace.

She came down the steps from the forest trail alone, her black hair streaked with electric blue at the tips from some prank of the Stoll brothers that she had apparently decided to keep, her silver-gray jacket tied around her waist over a black tank and shorts. Her electric-blue eyes were ringed faintly with tiredness — Thalia never slept well before a full moon — but her shoulders were down. Relaxed. For Thalia, that was basically a spa commercial.

"Don't stare," she told Percy flatly as she passed him. "I can do beach. I have done beach before."

"You hate sand," Percy reminded her.

"I hate a lot of things," Thalia said. "I'm working on it."

The hippocampus — Rainbow, Percy realized with a jolt of fondness — gave a happy whinny as he neared the shallows, tossing his white mane, his forelegs pawing the water. Behind him, the net settled gently onto the sandbar as the tide pushed in.

It was not driftwood.

It was a gift. Very obviously a queen's gift.

Three huge conch-shell baskets, each as big as a bathtub, woven through with living kelp that kept them cool and damp. Inside, packed in layers of cool mist and sea-silk cloth, was an entire party.

Tyson waded straight out without hesitation and hugged Rainbow around the neck, nearly getting knocked over by affection. "Good Rainbow! Good messenger!"

Rainbow nickered and nudged a sealed scallop-shell letter toward Percy with his nose.

Percy cracked it open. Inside, written in glowing blue ink on mother-of-pearl that didn't get wet or smudge, in elegant, looping handwriting:

*My dearest stepson and his brave companions —*

*The sea remembers sunny days, too. Not only storms.*

*Poseidon tells me you children train too hard and rest too little. He is right about little, so listen to me for once.*

*I send you a tide-pool party, Atlantis style. Everything is safe. Everything is charmed to return to the sea by sunset. Do not worry about messes. The ocean loves a mess, if it is a happy one.*

*Miss Annabeth — I have included the architect's set you admired in the palace courtyard. Build something that lasts past the tide. I dare you.*

*Miss Clarisse — the waves today will be strong enough to be interesting, if you dare them back.*

*Miss Silena — the shells I send are only starters. The best ones choose their finders.*

*Miss Thalia — rest, child of the sky. Even storms sun themselves sometimes. No one will think less of you for lying still for an hour.*

*Eat. Swim. Laugh loudly enough that my husband complains he can hear you in his throne room.*

*With affection,*
*Amphitrite, Queen*

Grover read over Percy's shoulder and let out a long, low whistle. "Okay. That's the coolest stepmom letter ever. My mom just sends me oat bars."

"Open the baskets! Open the baskets!" The Stoll brothers had materialized, as they always did when gifts were involved.

What spilled out onto the sand in the next twenty minutes was pure Atlantis.

The first basket held food: clusters of sweet blue grapes that tasted like saltwater taffy and fresh rain, honey-cakes stamped with tridents, chilled berry-juice bottles that stayed cold no matter how long they sat in the sun, and a dozen glass jars of what Amphitrite's note called *sunset dip* that changed color from gold to pink as you ate it. There were also sandwiches — real, mortal-world turkey and cheese and PB&J — because, as a second smaller note said, *I know growing heroes need mortal food, too. Poseidon insisted.*

The second basket held the party kit: Atlantean silk canopies anchored by living coral weights, self-cooling towels, reef-safe sunscreen that smelled like vanilla, shell snorkels that whispered fish names, and a set of living tide-pool bowls.

That last one made Annabeth gasp out loud.

When Tyson set them above the tideline and poured in seawater, the rims unfurled like flowers into perfect miniature tide pools, complete with tiny anemones, hermit crabs, and orange and purple starfish. The creatures were charmed, Amphitrite's note explained — safe, happy, and returned by sunset.

"You hear that, Stolls?" Clarisse said immediately. "No eating the crabs."

"We make no promises," Travis said solemnly.

The third basket was Annabeth's architect set, and Percy knew from the way her breath caught that Amphitrite had absolutely nailed it.

Miniature Atlantean building tools: rust-proof trowels, a pearl level that always found true, jars of living sand that stuck when you wanted it to, tiny shell columns and arches, and a parchment labeled *For Foundations That Outlast Tides.*

Annabeth held the parchment like it was a holy text. "Percy," she breathed. "This is — this is actual Atlantean engineering. Pre-sinking. Do you know what this means?"

"That you're about to disappear into a sand castle for six hours?" Percy guessed, fond.

"That I'm about to build the greatest sand castle in the history of Long Island," Annabeth corrected, already rolling up the sleeves of her camp shirt. "Help me carry it up to the flat part."

How the whole camp ended up on the beach by ten o'clock, Percy couldn't have reconstructed later if you'd paid him. Word traveled fast when free food was involved.

The Apollo kids staked out volleyball. The Demeter kids claimed a canopy. Beckendorf eyed the coral weights like he wanted to reverse-engineer them. Nico appeared with a book, pretended he wasn't enjoying himself, and stayed all day.

Chiron rolled down, read the note, and said, "No training today. Beach day. Mr. Stoll, if that water balloon hits my tail, you will be mucking stalls for a month."

"Love you too, Chiron!" Connor called, lowering his arm very slowly.

Mr. D was conspicuously absent, which everyone agreed improved the weather by at least ten degrees.

Percy almost joined the rush to unpack the baskets when he noticed Bea watching Rainbow from the high, dry sand. Her stuffed seal was tucked beneath one arm. In her free hand she held the folded paper boat she'd made with Silena earlier that week.

"Are the little animals going home?" she asked him.

Percy looked toward the living tide-pool bowls. "That’s what the letter says."

"Can they stay if somebody wants one?"

The easy thing would be to promise her another animal. He didn't have that right. Amphitrite had sent them to visit, not to become somebody's souvenirs.

"Let's ask exactly how it works," he said.

They went to the shallows together. Tyson was hugging Rainbow's neck and telling him he was a magnificent messenger. Percy waited until the hippocampus had finished nuzzling his brother, then asked about the creatures and their return.

Rainbow sent him an image of water moving through sheltered coral, little animals passing between the rocks, and a line of white foam closing over a nursery pool. Percy couldn't tell whether it was Rainbow's own memory or a picture included in the delivery instructions. He checked the letter again.

On the back, in much smaller script, was a line he had missed: *The bowls are for observation, not keeping. Their inhabitants return to protected saltwater nurseries at sunset. Set no bowl in Camp Half-Blood's freshwater lake.*

He read that part to Bea.

"Because lake water isn't ocean water?" she asked.

"Exactly."

"I wouldn't like somebody putting my seal in a different house without telling me."

Percy smiled. "I don't think he'd like it either."

Bea considered the nearest bowl. "Then I'm glad they go home."

She asked whether she could watch the enchantment end later. Percy said yes, if her counselor agreed and she stayed in the supervised part of the beach. She nodded and went to find Silena, leaving Percy with the shell letter.

He carried it to Annabeth, who was still studying Amphitrite's architect kit rather than opening it. The pearl level rested in a molded case, and the parchment plan had tiny notes at its margins that Annabeth was reading with the concentration she usually reserved for a structural fault.

"She remembered the thing you asked her about," Percy said.

Annabeth looked up. "I was looking at this level for thirty seconds when we visited the courtyard. She remembered the exact model."

"She's good at that."

"I know." Annabeth turned the parchment over. "Sometimes I still forget she knows me."

Percy sat beside her. "She's met you. She's written to you. She knows about your projects."

"Yes. What I mean is that she knows this version of me. I sometimes catch myself thinking she must have been there for the other one too."

Percy understood. Amphitrite had raised the child who'd grown up in Atlantis after Sally died in this history. He remembered none of that childhood from the inside. But he knew how she'd checked his departure bag, how she'd spoken to Annabeth, and how she'd hugged him when she visited camp. Those were encounters he could honestly call his own.

"I wrote her about the anniversary," he said. "And Tyson's visit. She asked whether Clarisse and Silena were still fighting over the laundry rack."

"And were they?"

"They'd moved on to who got to control the windows."

Annabeth laughed.

"I didn't tell her why some old memories are hard for me," he continued. "She doesn't know about the rewrite. I told her the things I've actually been doing. That feels easier now."

Annabeth studied him a moment, then took his hand. "You can love her without borrowing memories of childhood you don't have."

"I know." He watched Tyson and Rainbow in the water. "And I can miss Sally without making Amphitrite pay for it."

They stayed beside the baskets while camp came down to the beach. Silena had already marked a place above the high-water line for bags and shoes. Percy noticed Lucy helping her. The girl's light-up sneakers had nearly got wet on her first camp beach visit; now she was moving bags away from the incoming tide before anyone asked her.

Grover arrived with a clipboard and three younger campers.

"Chiron wants us to inventory the things that have to be sent home," he said. "He says a magical gift doesn't excuse us from keeping track of animals and property."

"I can help," Percy said.

Milo, who had been carrying spare towels, raised his hand. "Can I write the list?"

Grover gave him the pencil. "Write what we actually know. If we can't identify something, put 'unknown.' Don't make a guess sound official."

Milo wrote slowly and carefully. Percy watched him label a silver tool *unknown shell instrument* rather than inventing a name. The boy looked more comfortable with a task than he had on his first day at camp, though he still checked the path to the Big House whenever someone mentioned families.

"You talked to Chiron about the letter?" Percy asked quietly.

"Yeah."

"Do you need any help?"

"No. We made a plan."

Percy nodded. He didn't ask to hear it. Milo had trusted him enough to ask for help reaching his brother, not to give him access to every private conversation afterward.

Together they counted bowls, canopies, and equipment. Annabeth helped identify the architect tools. Tyson made sure the sea-silk ties were not wound around Rainbow's legs, and Grover posted one of the older campers near the living pools to keep curious hands from grabbing animals without permission.

By the time the list was finished, the beach looked ready. Bea had found a place beside Ella where she could see the bowls without touching them. Lucy was telling another newcomer where the tide mark was. Milo was still holding the inventory, asking Grover whether the final signature should be his or Chiron's.

Percy folded Amphitrite's letter and slipped it into his pocket. It had been a gift from his mother, written to people she recognized and cared about. He didn't have to pretend she knew the secret she didn't know to accept it.

Rainbow whinnied one last time, nudged Tyson affectionately, and turned toward deeper water. Percy watched him disappear beyond the sandbar. Some presents, he thought, were meant to stay. Others were meant to be enjoyed and returned.

---

By eleven, the beach had sorted itself into happy chaos.

Percy kicked off his flip-flops and waded in to his knees, letting the cold Atlantic close over his feet, and felt his whole body exhale. Every wave told him things: *mackerel fifty yards out, tide turning in two hours, sandbar shifted.*

He dove under the first big breaker and came up laughing, shaking water out of his hair.

"Show-off!" Annabeth shouted from shore, where she was kneeling in the damp flat sand with her architect's set spread around her like a general's maps. She had already sketched three different plans in her notebook and discarded two. "Some of us are doing structural engineering here!"

"Some of us are doing cannonballs!" Percy shouted back, and did one to prove it, to Tyson's thunderous applause.

The tide pools became instant headquarters. Silena set up beside them with her tote, a magnifying glass, and a waterproof field guide.

"Okay, rule," Silena announced to the cluster of Aphrodite and Hermes and Demeter kids gathered around. "Amphitrite said the best shells choose their finders, so we have to walk slow and look for the ones that shine. No grabbing. Let them catch your eye."

She demonstrated, walking the wrack line with her head bent, her braids swinging. Within minutes she found a perfect rose-tinted scallop, then a spiraled whelk with a pearly lip, then a tiny sand dollar intact on both sides, which made all the little kids gasp.

"You have the eye," Beckendorf said quietly, coming up behind her with a bottle of water so she wouldn't get dehydrated. He tucked a stray piece of hair behind her ear, his big calloused hand impossibly gentle.

Silena beamed up at him. "Stay and help me? You always spot the Scotch bonnets."

"For you, I'll spot anything," Beckendorf said, and meant it, and the Aphrodite girls sighed collectively.

A few yards away, Clarisse was running her wave-jump contest.

She had planted her spear as the start line and pointed at the sets.

"All right, losers!" she bellowed. "Rules are simple! You stand here! Wave comes! You jump! Highest jump without getting knocked on your butt wins! No powers! Jackson, that means you! No talking to the water!"

"Wouldn't dream of it," Percy said piously, which was a lie, because the water was already gossiping to him about which wave was biggest.

The line formed fast: Sherman Yang and the other Ares kids, a couple of Apollo kids who thought jumping was basically flying, Grover (who immediately regretted joining), and, to everyone's shock, Thalia.

"Oh, you're in?" Clarisse grinned, feral and delighted. "Grace! Didn't know sky girls could jump."

"Sky girls invented jumping, La Rue," Thalia shot back. "We fall from the sky for fun. Try to keep up."

The first few waves were waist-high. Grover got spun and came up with seaweed on his head, which made Tyson laugh so hard he fell over backward.

Then the sets got bigger.

Clarisse timed a chest-high breaker perfectly, landing steady with her arms up. "Beat that!"

Thalia watched the next green glassy wave, ran three steps, and launched herself impossibly high, landing light. The beach erupted.

"Tie!" Travis Stoll declared, acting as self-appointed judge. "Sudden death! Bigger wave!"

Percy, bobbing beyond the breakers, felt the next big one coming and nodded toward the horizon. *Get ready.*

They jumped together, side by side, shouting with joy as the water tossed them and set them back down.

"Okay!" Clarisse panted, shoving wet hair out of her face. "Okay! Draw! Grace isn't terrible!"

"High praise from La Rue!" Thalia laughed, splashing her. "Don't get soft on me!"

"I'll still kill you at capture the flag!"

"Looking forward to it!"

Grover, watching from the shallows with seaweed still in his hair, leaned toward Percy and said quietly, "Have you ever seen those two not want to murder each other?"

"Nope," Percy said. "Amphitrite's a miracle worker."

---

Annabeth's sand castle rose the way she did everything: fast, precise, and slightly terrifying to watch.

By noon it had walls.

She had chosen the perfect flat spot just above the high-tide line,-packed the foundation with the living gold sand mixed with regular damp sand, and used Amphitrite's pearl-level to get everything true. Tyson, who was the best assistant any architect could dream of — strong, patient, and delighted to follow exact instructions — helped her carry buckets of water and pat down towers.

Percy drifted over, dripping, and crouched beside her to watch.

It wasn't just a castle. Concentric rings, bridges arched over little moats that actually held water, shell columns holding a central dome, staircases that spiraled like real Atlantean palaces. Annabeth worked with her lip caught between her teeth, placing each arch.

"You need a flag," Percy said.

"I need structural integrity on the east curtain wall," Annabeth said without looking up, "but I'll accept a flag."

Percy found a straight piece of driftwood and a strip of blue sea-silk from the baskets and planted it on the highest tower. It fluttered in the sea breeze.

Annabeth sat back and looked up at him, sand-smudged, ponytail loose. "What do you think?"

"I think Amphitrite dared the wrong girl," Percy said honestly. "This thing's going to survive the tide. It's going to survive the apocalypse."

Annabeth laughed, that real laugh that crinkled her eyes, and bumped her sandy shoulder against his wet one. "Come here often to flatter architects?"

"Only my favorite one."

Around them, younger kids built lopsided tributary castles. Annabeth leaned over and showed a Demeter girl how to pack a tower mold. "Tap, then lift straight up. Perfect."

The little girl beamed like she'd been blessed by Athena herself. Annabeth remembered how much an encouraging word could mean.

Beckendorf wandered over with Silena to admire the castle, Silena's tote now heavy and clinking. "That's straight out of the palace courtyard," he said, recognizing the dome style. "You nailed the buttresses."

"Amphitrite sent the plans," Annabeth said, tapping the parchment. "Some of this is two thousand years old and still better than anything we build on the surface."

"Classic," Beckendorf grinned. "Gods hoard the good engineering."

Silena knelt gracefully and set a perfect pink scallop shell into the castle's front gate like an offering. "For beauty," she declared. "Every strong place needs something beautiful or people won't want to defend it."

Annabeth looked at her for a long moment, then nodded seriously. "You're right. Leave it there."

She was still studying the pink scallop when the east wall moved.

It wasn't dramatic. One of the narrow sand buttresses sank a fraction of an inch and took the little bridge with it. A row of shells tilted, their edges suddenly no longer level. Annabeth put her hand beneath the bridge before it could collapse altogether.

"Nobody touch that side," she said.

Percy stopped in the middle of planting another flag. Tyson froze with a bucket of water in his hand.

"Was it me?" Percy asked.

"No. I don't know yet."

She hated saying that in front of an audience. The castle was supposed to be the proof that she could use Atlantean tools properly, the answer to Amphitrite's challenge. She had measured the towers three times. Now one wall was falling in a direction it shouldn't.

Beckendorf crouched beside her. He didn't touch the structure until she nodded.

"The soil under the foundation is wetter than the surface," he said.

"It's supposed to be."

"Not that wet."

Annabeth pushed a finger into the sand beside the wall. Water came up around it, clear and cold. She had chosen ground above the visible high-water line, but the wet layer beneath it had been moving as the tide turned. The foundation wasn't resting on the firm surface she thought she'd prepared.

"That's not on the drawing," she said.

"The drawing doesn't know this beach," Beckendorf replied.

She felt the flare of annoyance before she could stop it. It wasn't his fault, and he hadn't said anything unfair. She'd spent years treating drawings as a way to keep unpredictable things where they belonged. Here the ocean had decided to ignore a line she'd drawn on paper.

"Percy, can you feel how the water's moving under here?" she asked.

He put his palm against the damp ground, away from the castle. For several seconds he didn't speak.

"The tide's pushing through the sand from the side," he said. "It's not dangerous to us. It's going to keep getting wetter around the east wall."

"Can you stop it?"

"I could push the water back for a while."

Annabeth looked at the castle, then at the tide. He could solve the immediate problem with his powers, and everyone would applaud. The next time the structure met an ordinary wave it would fail for exactly the same reason.

"Don't," she said. "I need it to stand without you holding the beach in place."

Percy nodded. He didn't look offended. She was grateful for that.

A girl from the Demeter cabin had been watching Annabeth build since morning. She approached carrying a bucket of shells.

"Do you need the sand I collected?" she asked.

"Maybe. What's your name?"

"Mara."

"Okay, Mara. Could you help us test something?"

Mara nodded eagerly. Annabeth borrowed the bucket and made a small mound of sand a few feet away from the castle. She pressed it flat, then asked Tyson to add water slowly along one edge while she watched what happened inside. The mound sagged and split. She added a shallow channel on the next attempt; the water flowed out along it instead of collecting beneath the center.

"See?" she told Mara. "The sand needs somewhere for water to go. If we trap it beneath the wall, it pushes the wall up or washes the grains out from underneath."

Mara considered the two mounds. "Like when you make a path between garden beds?"

"Yes. Exactly like that."

"We put stones at the bottom of ours."

Annabeth glanced toward the castle. "That's not a bad idea."

She asked Beckendorf for a small handful of beach pebbles, not the decorative shells Silena had already chosen. They were going to have to rebuild the east wall with a drainage channel and a foundation less dependent on the charmed sand sticking in the right places.

Mara sat beside her with a handful of pebbles. "Am I allowed to make part of it?"

Annabeth hesitated. It would be faster to do the work herself. It would also mean the girl went back to her own crooked castle having spent most of the morning watching an older camper make everything look effortless.

"You can make the channel," she said. "I'll show you the slope. Then you tell me if the water flows where we want it."

Mara looked astonished. "Me?"

"Yes. But it has to work. I won't tell you it's perfect if it isn't."

Mara grinned. "That's okay."

They worked side by side. Tyson brought water in smaller amounts and waited until Mara said she was ready. Beckendorf tested the rebuilt footing with a piece of flat driftwood. Percy provided information about how the tide was turning but did not move it.

The first channel was too shallow. The second ran directly beneath a tower and made Annabeth swear. The third carried water out past the base without washing the supporting sand away.

"That one," Mara announced, proud and a little breathless.

"That one," Annabeth agreed.

Lucy arrived while they were rebuilding. She had a notebook under her arm and her light-up sneakers tied by their laces to the strap of her bag. She watched Mara placing pebbles, then pointed at the miniature steps leading into the castle's inner courtyard.

"How do people in little chairs get inside?" she asked.

Annabeth looked up. "What?"

"If someone can't walk up stairs. Where do they go?"

"It's a sand castle, Lucy."

"I know. But you're building it like a real place."

The question made Annabeth look at her design more carefully. The courtyard had two elegant staircases and one steep bridge. She had copied the plan from an old Atlantean drawing without asking what kind of people were supposed to use the spaces.

"There could be a ramp on the west side," she said.

"Would it fit?"

"Not if we keep the second staircase."

Lucy crouched and measured the space with two fingers. "I think the ramp is better."

"So do I."

Annabeth lifted the staircase away. It came off more easily than she wanted to admit. Tyson helped her shape a long, shallow slope, and Lucy pressed little lines across the surface to make it look less slippery. They didn't call it finished until a smooth shell could be pushed from the outer gate to the courtyard without falling over.

Silena came to see the changes. Her pink scallop was still at the front gate, but the wall around it had been reshaped and the courtyard was open to the west.

"You changed the design," she said.

"Two major changes."

"Why?"

Annabeth showed her the drainage channel, and Mara demonstrated it with a cup of seawater. Silena watched the water leave the foundation without undermining the wall.

"And the ramp?" Silena asked.

"Lucy's idea."

Silena smiled at Lucy. "I'd use that entrance even if I could use the stairs."

"Why?" Lucy asked.

"So I could carry all the shells I collected without tripping." Silena lifted her heavy tote to demonstrate.

Lucy laughed. Annabeth looked at the modified courtyard. It was less symmetrical than the original palace plan. She liked it more.

When Percy brought back the last of the water, Annabeth checked the level one final time. The rebuilt wall was straight. The channel worked. Her castle had become something the old drawing hadn't shown her how to make.

"Do you think Amphitrite would still approve?" Percy asked.

"I think she'd ask why I hadn't thought of the drain earlier."

"Sounds like her."

"And then she'd want to see the ramp."

Percy leaned closer to inspect it. "Mara and Lucy both get their names on the plans."

Annabeth took out her notebook and wrote the names beside the revisions. She included Beckendorf, Tyson and Percy under the parts they had actually helped with, because being responsible for a design didn't mean pretending to have made every part of it alone.

Mara read the notes over her shoulder. "Can I show my cabin?"

"Yes. You can tell them which channel you made."

The girl hurried away. Lucy stayed long enough to test the shell on the ramp once more, then went to join Bea at the tide pools. Annabeth sat back on her heels, sandy and tired.

"Still think it's going to survive the apocalypse?" she asked Percy.

"No," he said.

She made a face.

"I think it'll survive the next tide," he continued. "That's actually more impressive."

Annabeth looked at the east wall. She could not promise the sand would last forever, whatever Amphitrite's dare said. She could promise she'd learned enough to make this version better before the water returned.

She brushed her hands clean, picked up the pearl level and tucked it safely back in its case.

Thalia actually Relaxing — capital R, as Travis Stoll later described it — became the afternoon's low-key miracle.

After the contest, Thalia toweled off, used her jacket as a pillow on a cooling towel under a canopy, put on sunglasses, and stayed there.

She didn't patrol. She just lay listening to surf and Grover's pipes, hands folded, face tipped to the sun.

Percy noticed because he was attuned to Thalia like family. She was Annabeth's sister and his friend; caring about her had become easy. He flopped nearby with a bottle of berry juice.

"Don't say 'I told you so,'" Thalia said without opening her eyes, "or I'll zap you."

"I wasn't going to say anything," Percy said innocently.

"You were thinking it loudly."

"I'm just glad you're chilling. You haven't since —" He stopped. Since the recent alarms. Since the weight of patrols and worrying about Annabeth and everything. He didn't need to say it.

Thalia lifted her glasses. "Amphitrite said no one will think less of me for lying still. No one's attacked. Sky hasn't fallen."

"See? Queens know things."

"Don't let Annabeth hear you say that about another queen," Thalia smirked, settling her glasses back down. "Go pester your girlfriend. I'm perfecting doing nothing."

An hour later she was still there, half-asleep, wearing a Demeter kid's flower crown and Silena's spare cover-up. When Silena asked for the cover-up back, Thalia returned it without complaining and kept the flower crown.

Percy wandered over with two bottles of cold juice. He set one where Thalia could reach it and sat on the sand beside the canopy.

"You're staring," she said without opening her eyes.

"I'm admiring the flower arrangement."

"Do that somewhere else."

"There's a daisy growing out of your hair."

"That's what flowers do."

Percy laughed. He looked toward the beach where Clarisse was supervising the last of her wave-jump contest. The surf had grown more powerful, and she was calling the younger kids back above the breaking line. She saw him watching and gave a sharp thumbs-up to show the group was accounted for.

"You really took the afternoon off," he said.

"Chiron approved it."

"From everything?"

"From patrol. From equipment inspection. Not from having a brain."

Percy looked at the flower crown again. He could still hear the half-serious insult in her voice, but she wasn't sitting bolt upright or watching every shadow move across the dunes.

"What did you have to do to get the afternoon off?" he asked.

"Ask."

"That's all?"

"Mostly. I asked Chiron if someone else could cover my route. He showed me the schedule, told me who was already on duty, and gave me a day. It was absurdly simple. I almost made it complicated."

"Why?"

She lifted her sunglasses. Her electric-blue eyes looked tired and bright at once.

"Because for a while I thought being the oldest meant being the person who never stopped. If Annabeth was sick I stayed awake. If Jason wrote from Jupiter I answered before I did anything else. If a counselor said the border felt strange, I volunteered for an extra watch. It seemed easier than admitting I couldn't do it all."

"You've always been like that."

"I know. It wasn't always good for anybody."

Percy turned a pebble beneath his thumb. The family promise they'd made at the memorial yesterday had been about showing up when asked, not controlling one another's lives. He could see Thalia putting the distinction to work here, though she probably wouldn't thank him for saying so.

"Annabeth worries about you," he said.

"Annabeth worries about the structural integrity of breakfast."

"That's not a denial."

Thalia sighed and pushed her glasses back into place. "I worry about her too."

"You don't have to prove it by staying on guard every night."

"No kidding. She told me that after the play."

Percy raised an eyebrow. "When?"

"Private conversation."

He hesitated. "Okay."

Thalia turned her head enough to look at him. "You're getting better at that."

"Not instantly asking for details?"

"Yeah."

"My sisters are training me. It's terrifying."

"Good." Thalia stared toward the sea for a while. "I told Zoe I would walk with her at the new moon. I still have to check the patrol schedule. I don't want to promise a time I can't keep."

"Could ask Chiron to swap."

"Maybe. But she said she only wants company to the last marked lantern. Not a guard posted over her ritual."

"You can do that."

"I think so."

Percy drank from his bottle. It was cool enough that condensation ran over his fingers. A year earlier, he might have told Thalia that her plan was easy and she should stop worrying. Now he knew how much work it could take her to let someone go down a path alone after agreeing to help them get there.

Bea came walking past the canopy with her counselor, the stuffed seal held firmly against her chest. She stopped when she saw Thalia's crown.

"Did you make that?"

"A Demeter kid did."

"Can you make another one?"

"Probably not a good one."

Bea considered. "Can I try?"

Thalia sat up. "Sure. Ask before pulling flowers. Some of these belong to the beds the Demeter cabin planted, and they will absolutely prosecute."

The counselor smiled. "We can gather fallen flowers by the dunes."

Bea went off with the counselor, checking the ground for anything already loose. Thalia watched her go without following. Percy noticed, because on the new kids' first day she'd been ready to carry the girl anywhere she wanted.

"You didn't get up," he said.

"I saw."

"Very mature."

"I am a pillar of mature decision-making."

"You're wearing a crown made of daisies."

"That means I'm royalty. Bow."

Percy gave the least convincing bow in history. Thalia tapped his shoulder with a plastic drinking bottle as though knighting him.

A few minutes later Bea returned with a handful of fallen petals and three small stems. She asked Thalia to show her how to tie them. Thalia tried, got the first knot wrong, and let Bea demonstrate a different way. The finished little circle was crooked, but Bea put it around the stuffed seal's head with evident satisfaction.

"Better than mine," Thalia said.

Bea looked at her critically. "Yours is bigger."

"Then mine is more powerful."

Bea smiled and walked back toward the tide pools.

Thalia lay down again and adjusted her flower crown so it didn't press against the towel. Percy found himself looking toward the ocean, where Rainbow had vanished earlier that morning.

"Doesn't it feel weird?" he asked. "A whole day without somebody trying to kill us?"

"Very," Thalia said. "But don't invent a monster to make yourself comfortable."

"Wasn't planning on it."

"You were thinking it loudly."

He smiled, finished his juice and stood. Thalia didn't ask him to stay and keep her company. He didn't ask her to come supervise the next activity. For once the quiet wasn't something either of them had to defend.

---

The tide-pool party kit kept giving all afternoon.

The living tide pools became a marine seminar run by Grover, who knew each creature's mood.

"That anemone's happy," Grover told a nervous Hermes boy. "Touch one tentacle gently. Like this."

The boy did, gasped, then narrated everything to Tyson, who listened seriously.

"Brother says the starfish is sleepy," Tyson told Percy, cradling it gently. "I will sing it a lullaby."

He did, off-key, while kids sat around listening. Percy felt his chest go warm.

Clarisse sat with Tyson a while and let him show her the hermit crabs, gentle with them.

"What?" she snapped at Percy's stare. "I like small angry things. Relatable."

Percy held up his hands. "Didn't say a word."

"You were thinking it."

"I'm thinking Amphitrite should send beach kits every week."

Clarisse snorted, but she didn't disagree. She set the crab back gently and watched it scuttle. "My mom would never," she muttered, almost too quiet to hear. Then, louder: "Pass me a honey-cake, Jackson, before I change my mind about liking you today."

It was, from Clarisse, basically a love poem.

Lunch was sprawled and sandy: sandwiches, sunset dip turned pink, grapes, cold juice. Chiron told an Atlantis story, and even Nico smiled at Poseidon wiping out into his own throne room.

Silena spread her finds: scallops, whelks, sea glass, and one rare perfect blue piece she pressed into Annabeth's hand. "For the highest window. So she can always see the ocean."

Beckendorf built her a rinse station and labeled her best finds, because she liked things documented beautifully.

Grover fell asleep in the sun with his hat over his face and woke up with a crab sitting on his chest, which he declared was a sign of great favor from the wild.

Everything was golden and salt-sweet and easy in a way demigod life almost never was.

Which was why, around four o'clock, when the light went honey-colored and the tide was low and flat, Percy found himself looking at Annabeth and jerking his chin down the beach, toward the empty curve of shoreline past the second dune.

"Walk?" he asked.

Annabeth looked at her castle — complete, domed, flagged, with Silena's scallop at the gate and blue sea glass in the tower — looked at the happy chaos around it, and stood, brushing sand off her knees.

"Walk," she agreed.

---

They walked in comfortable silence at first, the way only people who have survived things together can.

The sand was cool and packed near the water, perfect for walking. Small waves lapped in, foaming around their ankles and retreating, leaving lace. Gulls cried far overhead. Behind them, the camp beach sounds faded to a happy murmur: Clarisse shouting, Tyson laughing, Grover's pipes starting up again.

Percy carried his shoes in one hand. Annabeth had left her notebook behind — a mark of trust, for her — and let her hair down from its ponytail so the sea breeze lifted it.

For a while they just looked for shells without really looking, the way Silena had taught everyone that morning. Percy found a white slipper shell and handed it to Annabeth without thinking. She turned it over, smiled, and slipped it into her shorts pocket.

"The Sea Remembers," Percy said quietly, kicking at a line of foam. "You know, when Amphitrite wrote that? About sunny days too? I think I needed to hear that."

Annabeth glanced at him sideways. "You've been waiting for the other shoe to drop all day."

"Yeah," Percy admitted. "It's stupid. Perfect day, everyone's happy, no monsters, and part of my brain is still on patrol. Like — when's the tide going to turn? Literally and... you know."

"I know," Annabeth said softly. She stopped walking and faced the water, her arms wrapped around herself against the breeze. "I do the same thing with blueprints. I build something and immediately start calculating how it could fail. Earthquake, siege, erosion. Athena would have called it wisdom in our old life. Sometimes I think it's just fear with better math."

Percy stepped a little closer, his shoulder brushing hers. "Do you think we'll ever get to just... have days like this? Without the countdown?"

Annabeth was quiet for a long moment, watching a pelican dive far out. Then she said, "I think about the future a lot. More than you do, probably."

"Probably," Percy agreed. "You think about everything a lot. It's hot."

She elbowed him, but she was smiling. "Seaweed Brain. I'm serious."

"I know. So am I. Keep going."

She took a breath, the breeze tugging strands of hair across her face. Percy resisted the urge to tuck them back, barely.

"Okay. So. Next school year. High school." She said it like she was testing a bridge's weight limit. "Chiron says we could look into a school arrangement outside camp when it's time. Not just worksheets between patrols. Regular classes, mortal grades, a bed that isn't next to Thalia's boots. Beryl wants to be part of those conversations, too, though we haven't agreed to anything. And part of me wants it. Normal. Lockers and homework and nobody trying to kill me during gym."

Percy felt something cold slip down his spine that had nothing to do with the water around his ankles. "Yeah?"

"Yeah. But then — Camp." Annabeth turned to look back up the beach, toward the distant bright canopies, the tiny figures, the sand castle glinting gold. "How do I leave this? How do I leave... everyone? Tyson knows there's a place for him when he visits Cabin Three. Grover's got his searcher's license and his nature work. Thalia's — gods, did you see her today? She actually slept. Clarisse laughed without threatening anyone. Silena..." She shook her head. "I architected a sand castle with living sand from Atlantis, Percy. Where else am I going to do that?"

Percy swallowed. "So what are you saying? You have to choose?"

"Don't you feel it?" Annabeth asked, her gray eyes meeting his, stormy and vulnerable in a way she only ever let him see. "We're growing up. We remember a war with Kronos, but that doesn't prove it will unfold the same way again. And if another war comes, what happens after? What does a demigod do at seventeen? Eighteen? We don't get to go to college and get apartments and have normal lives. Well. Some do. Rarely. Mostly we... we either stay here and teach and patrol, or we try out there and hope the monsters don't find us."

Her voice wobbled just slightly on the last part. Percy hated that wobble more than any monster roar.

He didn't think. He just reached out and took her hand.

It was sandy and a little cold from the breeze, her fingers rough with castle-building. His was warm and wet and salty. Their palms fit together like they'd been designed by the same architect who did Atlantis — which, considering Amphitrite was probably watching and taking credit, maybe they had been.

Annabeth looked down at their joined hands, then up at him. Her expression softened. She squeezed once, the familiar answer to his familiar offer of steadiness, and let out the breath she'd been holding.

Percy felt his ears go hot. "Still here, Wise Girl. Even when the map gets complicated."

"I know," Annabeth said, lacing her fingers through his and holding on. Warm and sandy and steady. The hand-hold they had shared through quests and ordinary afternoons, saying *I'm here* without needing a new promise. "It's good. Keep holding on."

They started walking again, slower now, hand in hand, letting the waves wash over their feet. Neither of them needed to explain the hold. Talking about a future they wanted could still make an ordinary touch matter more.

"For the record," Percy said after a while, his thumb brushing lightly over her knuckles, "I think about the future too. Just... not in blueprints. More in... feelings. And my feeling is I don't want to do high school without you making fun of my essays. And I don't want to do Camp without you bossing me around about fortifications."

Annabeth laughed wetly. "That's your big future plan? Me insulting your grammar?"

"It's a solid foundation," Percy said solemnly. "Ask any architect."

She swung their joined hands gently as they walked. "Percy? Can I tell you something dumb?"

"Always."

"Sometimes when I picture the future — like, really let myself picture it — it's not San Francisco or New York or Camp. It's just... a house near the water. Small. Nothing fancy. With a porch where I can draw and you can come in smelling like the ocean and complain about your day. And it's quiet. No prophecies. No countdowns. Just... us. And our family visiting. Tyson crashing on the couch. Thalia stealing food from the fridge. Grover eating all the plants in the garden and apologizing to them."

Percy felt his throat close up. "That's not dumb," he managed. "That's... that's the best thing I've ever heard."

"You think we'll get it?" Annabeth whispered. "Even a piece of it?"

Percy stopped walking. Turned to face her fully, still holding her hand, the sunset light turning the water behind her to hammered gold. He thought about the Sea Remembers — how it held every storm and every calm, how Amphitrite had said the sea remembers sunny days too. How maybe growing up didn't have to mean choosing one shore and abandoning the other. How maybe you could build something that lasted past the tide if you built it right.

"I think," he said slowly, carefully, because this mattered, "that we don't have to decide everything now. High school, Camp — maybe it's not either-or. Maybe we find a school with Chiron's help. Maybe I flunk freshman English in New York and you tutor me over IM. Maybe summers are here, always. Maybe we build that house later, after. I think as long as we're... us... we'll figure out the map as we go. You're the architect, remember? You can redraw."

Annabeth stared at him. Then, to his utter shock, her eyes filled with tears — not sad tears, bright ones — and she laughed.

"When did you get wise, Jackson?" she asked.

"I've always been wise," Percy said. "You just never noticed because you're smarter."

She squeezed his hand hard. "Don't let go yet, okay?"

"Not planning on it."

They stood there a while, watching the sun sink lower, their shadows stretching long and joined down the wet sand. A dolphin arced far out, and Percy felt it wave — literally felt its happy *hello, sea-friend!* ripple through the water — and he waved back with his free hand, grinning.

Annabeth leaned her head briefly against his shoulder. Just for a second. Just enough. Warm and familiar and perfect. The sea breeze wrapped around them, smelling of salt and vanilla sunscreen and honey-cakes from far up the beach.

"I love days like this," Annabeth murmured. "I want to remember this one. Exactly like this."

"The Sea Remembers," Percy said softly. "So will we."

---

When they walked back, hand in hand, the beach came into view and their private conversation gave way to the familiar camp noise. Annabeth swung their joined hands once, and Percy squeezed back. The party had shifted into golden-hour mode.

Someone — probably Apollo kids — had strung little glowing pearls from the canopy edges. They cast blue-white light as the sun dipped. The tide-pool bowls glowed faintly from within. Beckendorf had started a small driftwood bonfire in a ring of stones (safely, with three buckets of water nearby and Chiron's explicit permission), and people were gathered around toasting marshmallows that the Hermes kids swore they hadn't stolen.

Clarisse was sitting by the fire, her hair damp and curly, her arm around a younger Ares girl who was showing her a shell. She saw Percy and Annabeth coming and smirked, but didn't say anything. Just raised an eyebrow at their sandy joined-then-unjoined hands and mouthed *saps* when Annabeth wasn't looking. Percy turned scarlet.

Silena ran up, her tote now overflowing, her cheeks pink with sun. "You guys missed it! Thalia found a conch! A real one! She said the ocean gave it to her because she sat still long enough to listen!"

Sure enough, Thalia was holding a large, rosy queen conch to her ear by the fire, her expression wondering and unguarded. "You can hear the storm in it," she told the little kids crowded around. "But far away. Like it's sleeping." She caught Percy's eye and shrugged, a little embarrassed, a little pleased. "Don't make it weird, Jackson."

"Wouldn't dream of it," Percy said.

Tyson came barreling down the sand, his arms full of towels and buckets, his face alight. "Brother! Annabeth! We saved you food! And we did not let the Stolls eat the castle! Although Travis tried to charge admission!"

Annabeth hurried to her castle — still standing, proud and gold in the sunset, its flag fluttering, its moats reflecting the sky. A small crowd of younger kids stood guard around it with driftwood swords. "Admission is free!" she declared, kneeling to fix a tiny wall where a wave had licked a little too high. "But tours require telling me one thing you love about the ocean."

"I love that it tickles!" one kid shouted.

"Accepted."

"I love that Percy talks to fish!"

"Accepted, but he's still a Seaweed Brain."

"Hey!" Percy protested, sitting down beside her in the sand, close enough that their knees touched. He took her hand for a moment, then let her get back to the little wall she'd been fixing. His shoulder rested against hers, and she leaned back just slightly, and that was its own kind of holding on.

Silena settled beside Beckendorf across the fire, sorting shells into his big palms for safekeeping, her head on his shoulder. Grover tuned his pipes. Thalia closed her eyes and listened, the conch in her lap. Clarisse heckled the Stolls' attempt at a sea shanty and then joined in on the chorus, loud and off-key. Nico, from under his umbrella, kept time with one foot when he thought no one was looking. Chiron watched it all with ancient, kind eyes.

Milo came running from the waterline with the inventory clutched in one hand.

"There's a bowl missing."

Percy looked up. "What?"

"Grover counted six. I can only find five."

The music continued behind them, but several campers near the pools turned. Tyson stopped carrying towels and came to stand beside Milo, who kept pointing between the list and the scattered items on the beach.

"Did we count the one Ella was watching?" Percy asked.

"Yes. I wrote it down. Six."

"All right. Nobody's in trouble. We look."

Grover came over from the fire and read the sheet. He didn't dismiss Milo's count or assume the boy had made an error. They checked the baskets, then the shaded spaces behind the canopies. The sixth bowl wasn't there.

Bea had been sitting with Lucy near the shell display. She approached with her stuffed seal beneath one arm.

"I saw someone carry a little pool away," she said.

"Who?" Percy asked.

She shook her head. "I don't know. A big person."

"Which way did they go?"

"Up toward the path with the signs. I thought they were putting it somewhere safe."

Percy looked at Grover. If a bowl containing saltwater animals ended up beside the freshwater canoe lake, its enchantment might return the creatures before anybody realized what had happened. Amphitrite's instructions had been precise. They needed to find the object, not blame whichever camper misunderstood the rules.

"You did the right thing telling us," Percy said to Bea. "You don't have to help look unless you want to."

"I want to. But I want Lucy to come."

Lucy nodded and took her hand. They stayed beside Grover while Percy and Tyson went toward the upper path.

At the turning by the storage shed they found an Apollo camper kneeling beside a bowl. Inside, a tiny orange starfish was moving slowly along the rim. The boy had been trying to show it to an injured friend who couldn't walk down the dune.

He looked up in alarm when he saw them.

"I was bringing it back," he said. "I only wanted Callie to see it."

Percy crouched, keeping his voice level. "I believe you. But the bowls have to stay in their designated area until they return home."

"She's in the infirmary annex. She can't come down here."

"Then we can figure out a different way to include her."

Tyson knelt and inspected the rim. "Water still good. Charm still present."

Percy showed the camper the line in Amphitrite's letter about the protected nursery pools. The boy read it twice. He hadn't seen the full instructions because he'd joined the party after the inventory had been posted.

"Nobody told me," he said.

"Then we needed a better sign," Percy answered.

They carried the bowl back, with the camper walking beside them. Percy asked whether Callie liked shells or drawings, and the boy said she collected pictures of sea creatures. Lucy overheard and offered to draw the little starfish before the return charm activated, provided the animal could stay safely in the bowl while she worked.

Annabeth brought her spare paper. Bea suggested the starfish should have a name but accepted Grover's reminder that the creature didn't belong to them. She decided a drawing could have a name even if its subject didn't know it.

"Orange Captain," she proposed.

Lucy drew a small starfish with a very large captain's hat. The Apollo camper laughed for the first time since they found him.

"Callie will love it."

He helped carry the drawing to the annex with a counselor. The bowl stayed under Grover's supervision and was returned to the line before sunset.

Percy went back to Milo, who was still guarding the clipboard like a military document.

"You were right," he said.

Milo looked relieved. "Was the bowl okay?"

"Yes. We found it before the charm ended."

"Good." He crossed out the note he'd made beside number six, then wrote *returned to station* in a new line. "I thought I'd counted wrong."

"Sometimes you do count wrong," Percy said. "The point is to check rather than pretend."

Milo smiled. He had been trusted with a task that mattered, and when something went wrong, the adults and older campers hadn't treated his fear as the problem.

Percy watched the boy hand the clipboard to Grover for the final signature. The rescued bowl glowed gently beside the others, with six accounted for. For once a crisis had ended without a weapon being drawn or a prophecy being fulfilled. They had listened to a child, followed a trail and returned something that wasn't theirs.

As the sun touched the water, the tide-pool bowls and the baskets and the silk canopies began to glow softly at the edges — Amphitrite's charm winding down. One by one, the living tide pools sealed themselves gently, the little creatures inside curling up sleepy and content. Tyson carried each bowl reverently down to the waterline with Percy's help, and together the brothers checked that every lid was properly sealed. The bowls lifted from the sand, their rims closing as the nursery charm carried the inhabitants back to their sheltered Atlantean pools. Neither boy emptied foreign creatures into Long Island Sound.

"Goodbye! Be safe!" Tyson called, waving huge. "Thank the Queen for us!"

Rainbow whinnied from beyond the breakers, where he had waited all day, and dipped his head in acknowledgment.

The baskets folded themselves flat. The canopies loosened their coral weights. The leftover food packed itself neatly for the Big House refrigerator. Nothing was left behind but damp sand, footprints, and Annabeth's castle.

And even that, as the tide crept higher, lapping at its outer ring, held.

The first wave kissed the foundation and retreated. The walls stood.

The second wave filled the moats, making them shine. The bridges held. The dome held. The flag waved.

"Knew it," Percy whispered, nudging Annabeth. "Apocalypse-proof."

Annabeth's eyes were shining brighter than the pearl-lights. "Amphitrite dared me to build something that lasts past the tide," she said. "Look."

The whole beach watched as the third wave came in and foamed around the outer rings. The living sand lost its magical shine as Amphitrite's return charm reached sunset. The water took one corner tower but passed through Mara's channel without lifting the main foundation, leaving the dome and Silena's scallop intact.

There was a collective gasp, then cheering. Tyson lifted Annabeth clean off her feet in a hug. Silena cried happy tears. Clarisse whistled through her fingers. Thalia stood and clapped slowly, smiling a real, unguarded smile.

Mara came racing along the waterline, followed by Lucy with her shoes slung over one shoulder.

"The channel worked!" Mara shouted.

"It did," Annabeth said.

"Does that mean the castle stays forever?"

Annabeth looked at the missing turret. "No. The next tide might be stronger. The foundations are still sand. But we improved the design, and tonight it stood without me asking Percy to hold back the ocean."

Mara took that in. She looked disappointed for a moment, then nodded.

"Could we make it again tomorrow?"

"Not exactly the same," Annabeth said. "We wouldn't have the Atlantean tools. And some parts would need to be different. But we have the drawings. You could make your own version."

Lucy looked at the surviving ramp. "I'd keep that."

"So would I."

Annabeth had copied the relevant measurements into her ordinary notebook before the enchanted tools went back with the baskets. The pearl level had returned to its case, and she had let it go without trying to hide it among her belongings. Percy watched her show Mara the sketch of the drainage channel, with both girls' names written beside the parts they'd proposed.

"Amphitrite said to make something that lasts past the tide," Percy reminded her.

"Maybe she wanted me to notice that 'past the tide' isn't the same as 'forever.'"

He smiled. "That sounds like something she'd say."

Mara and Lucy went off to tell the other children how the castle had held. Annabeth stood with the notebook under one arm, looking pleased and a little sad in the way she did when a finished project was ready to be left alone.

Percy just looked at Annabeth, sandy and sun-pink and brilliant, and felt the Sea in him settle, calm and certain as a harbor at dawn.

Later, much later, after the bonfire burned low and the pearls dimmed and Chiron shooed everyone up the hill with promises of hot chocolate in the mess hall, Percy and Annabeth lingered for just a moment at the top of the dune.

Below them, the beach was empty except for the castle. Its borrowed enchantment had ended, and one turret was gone. The rest held in the twilight through ordinary sand, careful joints and a drainage channel, a small Atlantis keeping watch over the Sound.

Annabeth slipped her hand into his again, sure and familiar. She gave him a little tug toward the path, then paused beside him to remember the castle exactly like this.

"Best beach day ever?" Percy asked quietly.

Annabeth squeezed his fingers, steady and full of future.

"Best beach day so far," she corrected. "We're going to have more. I'm architecting it that way."

The sea murmured agreement behind them, remembering.

And hand in hand, they walked back to Camp together.
