## Chapter 20: Cake and Lightning

In the life he remembered, Percy Jackson had survived a Minotaur, a Fury on a bus, and Clarisse La Rue attempting to give him a swirly on his first day at Camp Half-Blood.

None of that had prepared him for Silena Beauregard looking at him like he was her last hope.

"No," Percy said immediately.

They were behind the mess hall after breakfast, tucked between the dish-return window and the stack of empty nectar crates where nobody was supposed to hang out, which was exactly why Silena had chosen it. Grover was there too, nervously chewing on a napkin. Ella the harpy was perched on top of the crates, feathers fluffed, clutching a sparkly ribbon like it was a sacred artifact.

"Please," Silena said. She clasped her hands. Her hair was perfect even at eight in the morning, which Percy found personally offensive. "Percy, please. You're the only one she won't suspect."

"Suspect what?"

Grover spat out a piece of napkin. "Thalia's birthday party. Sixteen. We're doing it in three days."

Percy blinked. "Thalia's already sixteen. We're doing the party now?"

"See?" Silena gestured at him. "This is why we need a meeting. She never had a proper sixteenth party. Annabeth told me last night and made me swear on the Styx not to tell, and then I immediately told Grover, and Grover told Ella, and Ella told three dryads and a naiad, so now we have to move fast before it stops being a secret."

Ella flapped her wings importantly. "Ella remembers. Birthdays are for cake and songs. Ella knows seventeen songs. Ella will sing all seventeen songs."

"That's... a lot of songs, Ella," Grover said gently.

"Ella will narrow it down to nine," Ella conceded.

Percy leaned against the crates. "Okay, so we throw her a surprise party. That sounds... actually fun. Why did you think I'd say no?"

Silena and Grover exchanged a look.

"Because," Silena said delicately, "the plan requires baking. And decorations. And keeping a secret from Annabeth's sister while living in the same cabin cluster as Annabeth's sister. And Clarisse is involved."

Percy felt a chill despite the morning heat. "Clarisse is involved? Voluntarily?"

"She offered," Grover said, like he still couldn't believe it himself.

"Clarisse La Rue offered to help throw a nice surprise birthday party for Thalia Grace?" Percy repeated slowly. "Is this a prank? Am I about to get shoved in a canoe full of shaving cream?"

"No prank," said a voice behind him.

Percy jumped so hard he nearly knocked over the crates. Clarisse was standing there, arms crossed, spear slung across her back like she never took it off even for breakfast. Which, for all Percy knew, she didn't.

"Gods, don't DO that," Percy said, hand over his heart.

Clarisse ignored him and looked at Silena. "You told him?"

"I told him the soft version," Silena said.

"There's a hard version?" Percy asked.

Clarisse ticked off on her fingers. "One, Thalia saved my patrol on the north woods run last winter when a hellhound got past the boundary. I owe her. Two, if anyone finds out I cried at a birthday party I will deny it and then I will kill them. Three, if you ruin the cake, Jackson, I will kill you first."

Percy looked at Grover. "See? This is why I said no."

"You didn't say no," Silena pointed out. "You asked questions. That's basically yes."

Ella dropped the ribbon on Percy's head. "Percy says yes. Ella heard him. Ella is witness."

"Fine!" Percy threw up his hands, ribbon fluttering off. "Fine. I'm in. What's the plan?"

Silena's whole face lit up, and suddenly Percy understood why people followed Aphrodite kids into battle even when it was a terrible idea. She pulled a folded piece of parchment out of her pocket, covered in pink ink and little stars.

"Operation: Cake and Lightning," she announced. "We have seventy-two hours."

---

Clarisse La Rue did not do cute.

This was a known fact, like the sky is blue and Ares kids win capture the flag when they cheat properly. Clarisse did spears. Clarisse did war chariots and shield walls and screaming loud enough to make satyrs drop their pipes.

Clarisse did not do streamers.

And yet there she was, two days before Thalia's belated party, lying on her stomach in the Ares cabin's borrowed craft corner with Silena Beauregard, surrounded by enough blue and silver tissue paper to stuff a pegasus.

"No," Clarisse said flatly, holding up Silena's sketch. "Absolutely not."

"What's wrong with it?" Silena asked.

"You drew a lightning bolt with a smile on it. Lightning doesn't smile. It destroys."

"It's cute!"

"It's a weather phenomenon that kills people."

Silena snatched the sketch back. "Thalia is going to love it. She's punk-rock lightning. She'll appreciate whimsy."

Clarisse groaned and flopped face-first into a pile of crepe paper. It crinkled loudly. "Why am I doing this?"

"Because Thalia dragged you out of that flooded creek during war games when your armor was too heavy and you were too stubborn to ditch it," Silena said without looking up from her scissors. "Your words. At dinner. In front of everybody."

"I was concussed," Clarisse muttered into the paper. "I didn't mean it emotionally."

"You cried a little."

"I HAD CREEK WATER IN MY EYES."

Silena patted her shoulder. "Sure you did, sweetie."

Clarisse sat up, ripping a piece of blue paper in half out of sheer stress. "Okay, fine. But if we're doing this, we're doing it right. None of that flimsy Aphrodite stuff that falls down if someone sneezes. We're building decorations that can survive a cyclops sneezing on them."

"Ooh, good point," Silena said, making a note in pink ink. "Cyclops-proof streamers. Thalia-proof, too — she throws electricity when she's startled."

"Exactly. We need duct tape. And wire. And —" Clarisse paused. "How do you feel about painting a giant banner that says HAPPY BIRTHDAY STORM GIRL?"

Silena stared at her. "That's... actually sweet?"

"It's intimidating. It's perfect. Pine trees are intimidating. They survive winter."

"Clarisse, that's the nicest thing you've ever said about a plant."

"Don't tell anyone."

They worked in silence for a while, cutting and folding. Outside, the Ares cabin sounded like it always did — someone sparring, someone shouting about whose spear was whose, Sherman Yang yelling about push-ups. Inside, it smelled like glue and marker fumes.

Clarisse glanced at Silena, who was humming while she braided silver ribbon into lightning shapes.

"You ever... have someone show up for you like this?" Clarisse asked, before she could stop herself. She immediately wanted to shove the words back in her mouth. "Forget it. Stupid question."

Silena didn't tease her. That was the annoying thing about Silena — she knew exactly when to be serious. "My siblings do. Cabin Three, we're... we're a lot, but we show up. My friend Drew from Aphrodite once stayed up all night to re-sew my chiton before the solstice procession because I'd ripped it. She complained the whole time, but she did it."

Clarisse nodded, focusing very hard on a paper lightning bolt. "The Ares training group doesn't really do birthdays. Our dad... Poseidon isn't big on cake. He does tides, not frosting. He's big on 'congratulations, you survived another year, now learn the next current.'"

"Well," Silena said, tying off a ribbon with a sharp little bow, "you're doing birthdays now."

"Guess so." Clarisse held up her lightning bolt. It was crooked and aggressive and the edges were jagged where she'd cut too fast. It looked nothing like Silena's neat ones. "Mine looks like it's going to mug someone."

Silena laughed so hard she snorted. "It's perfect. Thalia will love that one most. Put it in the center."

Clarisse felt her ears go hot. "Shut up."

"Make me a second one, tough guy."

Clarisse made a second one.

---

The Camp Half-Blood kitchens were ruled by a rotating staff of harpies, dryads, and deeply tired satyrs, and they operated under one absolute law: campers did not bake unsupervised.

This law existed for excellent reasons, most of which were about to be demonstrated.

"It can't be that hard," Percy said, staring at the recipe Silena had copied from the camp cookbook. "Flour, sugar, eggs, blue stuff."

"That's not what it says," Grover said, peering over his shoulder. Grover had flour on his nose already and they hadn't even opened anything yet. "It says 'fold in the blueberries gently.' What's folding? Like laundry?"

Ella, perched on the flour bin, flipped through a tattered cookbook with her talons. "Ella knows baking. Ella has read forty-seven cookbooks. Ella has eaten eleven cookbooks. The eating is not recommended. The reading is helpful."

"Great, Ella's our expert," Percy said.

"First step!" Ella announced. "'Preheat oven to three hundred and fifty degrees.' Ella loves preheating. It sounds like pre-heating, which is heating before heating. Very efficient."

Silena tied an apron around her waist — pink, of course, with KISS THE COOK in glitter — and surveyed their supplies. Someone — probably Chiron, who knew everything and said nothing — had left them a corner of the kitchen after dinner, with a note that said *Good luck. Mr. D has been warned about the smoke alarms.* Which was encouraging and terrifying at the same time.

"Okay," Silena said, clapping her hands. "Percy, you crack the eggs. Grover, you measure flour. Ella, you supervise and don't eat the raw batter."

"Ella makes no promises," Ella said solemnly. "Batter is delicious and Ella is weak."

Percy picked up an egg. "How hard can this be?"

Famous last words.

Percy cracked the egg with the confidence of someone who had never cracked an egg with these smaller hands. Shell went everywhere — in the bowl, on the counter, one heroic fragment launched itself all the way to Grover's ear.

"Okay, that was a warm-up," Percy said.

"That was a crime," Grover said, picking shell out of his fur.

Second egg: better. Third egg: Percy got cocky and tried to do it one-handed like he'd seen on TV. Yolk slid down his wrist and onto the floor.

Ella leaned over. "Ella rates that attempt a four out of ten. Points for drama. Deductions for waste."

Meanwhile, Grover was measuring flour. The recipe called for three cups. Grover poured with great concentration, tongue sticking out, while Silena sifted sugar.

"Grover," Silena said carefully, "that's... that's a lot of flour."

Grover looked down. The measuring cup was overflowing. The counter was white. His hooves were white. His eyebrows were white.

"Maybe the cup is small," Grover suggested.

"Maybe you're supposed to level it off," Percy said.

"Level it off with what?"

"A knife?"

Grover picked up a butter knife and attempted to level the flour. He sneezed — a huge, goat-y *BAA-CHOO* — and a cloud of flour erupted into the air like Mount Vesuvius.

When the dust settled, all four of them were ghost-white. Ella shook out her feathers, sneezing tiny harpy sneezes.

"Ella is now a ghost," Ella announced. "Boo."

Percy started laughing so hard he had to sit down on the kitchen tiles. Grover was laughing too, wiping flour out of his eyes. Even Silena, who had flour in her perfect hair, was giggling.

"Okay," Silena said, catching her breath. "New plan. Grover, you are banned from dry ingredients. You do the wet ingredients. Percy, you do dry, but slowly. Like, glacially slowly."

"What do I do?" Ella asked.

"You read the next step very carefully."

Ella squinted at the book. "'Add two sticks of butter, softened.' What is softened? Is the butter emotional?"

"It means room temperature," Silena said. "Not melted, just soft."

Grover held up the butter. "This butter is very hard. It has no emotions at all."

"Microwave it for ten seconds," Silena said.

Grover put the butter in the microwave. Percy measured flour with the focus of a bomb defusal expert. Silena creamed sugar and — after fishing out three eggshells — eggs.

Then there was a smell.

"Do you smell that?" Percy asked.

"Smell what?" Grover asked.

"That... hot... buttery..."

Ella gasped. "THE BUTTER. ELLA FORGOT TO WATCH THE TIME."

Grover yanked open the microwave. The butter had not softened. The butter had achieved plasma state. It was bubbling and popping and half the wrapper had melted to the plate.

"How long did you put it in for?" Silena asked faintly.

Grover looked at the microwave display. "Ten... minutes?"

"GROVER!"

"I thought you said ten and then more numbers!"

Percy was laughing so hard his stomach hurt. "We haven't even gotten to the blue part and we've already committed arson."

Silena fanned the smoke away from the alarm with a dish towel. "Okay. Okay. It's fine. We have more butter. Grover, step away from the microwave. Percy, you handle butter. Ten SECONDS."

"Ten seconds," Grover repeated mournfully, sitting in the corner with Ella patting his head with a wing. "Ella understands. Numbers are hard. Ella once tried to count to one hundred and ended up at 'banana.'"

"Ella is wise," Grover said.

They regrouped. They melted butter correctly. They mixed batter. It actually started to look like batter — thick, pale gold, promising.

"Now the blue part!" Percy said.

This was important. Percy had been trying to explain why he wanted blue food without explaining memories that did not belong to the twelve years everyone else knew. In his remembered original childhood she had started it when he was little — blue pancakes, blue waffles, blue birthday cake — because Gabe had once said blue food didn't exist and Sally had taken that personally. Here, where Sally had died when he was two, he couldn't honestly claim to have grown up eating that way. He could make blue food now, and keep the more complicated explanation between himself and Annabeth. It was a mom thing he could carry across the missing years. And Thalia had rarely had a birthday their own mother could make easy. He wanted this one to be easy.

So the cake had to be blue. Bluer than the Aegean. Blue like Sally would make.

Silena held up a tiny vial of food coloring, plus a bowl of mashed blueberries. "Ready?"

Percy poured. He stirred.

The batter turned... gray.

"That's... gray," Grover said.

"That's battleship gray," Percy said. "That's the color of disappointment."

"Add more!" Ella cried.

Percy added more. Stirred.

Now it was a sort of greenish-teal, like pond scum.

"That's swamp," Grover said. "That's not birthday. That's swamp."

"More blueberries!" Silena said. "And a drop of red? Color theory, red plus blue makes — no wait, that's purple. Don't add red."

"TOO LATE," Percy said, having already added red.

Now it was... purple-brown. It looked like something a monster would cough up.

Ella peered into the bowl. "Ella loves Thalia. Ella will be honest. That looks like poison."

The kitchen doors swung open. A harpy cook stuck her head in, took one look at the bowl, and said, "Absolutely not," and left.

Silena stared at the bowl. Percy stared at the bowl. Grover dipped a finger in, tasted it, and said, "Tastes fine, actually."

"But it looks like mud," Percy said.

Silena squared her shoulders. "Attempt one is a learning experience. We pour this into the practice pan, we bake it to learn timing, and tomorrow we try again with double blue and NO red. Percy, do not touch the red."

"I got excited!"

"Channel that excitement into stirring."

They poured the mud-batter into a pan and shoved it in the oven. While it baked, they cleaned flour off every surface, including themselves and, mysteriously, the ceiling.

When the timer dinged, they pulled out a lopsided, gray-purple cake that leaned distinctly to the left.

Percy looked at it. "It's... leaning."

"It's got character," Grover said loyally.

"It's leaning like the Tower of Pisa," Percy said. "Should we... taste it?"

They each took a tiny bite.

"...chewy," Ella said.

"...eggshell," Grover said, picking something out of his teeth.

Silena set down her fork. "Okay. Tomorrow. Fresh start. Nobody tells Thalia. Nobody tells Annabeth — wait, Annabeth knows?"

"Annabeth is making the gift," Percy said. "She's in on it, but she's pretending she's not in on it so Thalia doesn't get suspicious that Annabeth's acting weird."

"Annabeth acting normal is acting weird," Grover said. "She's always planning something."

"True," Silena said. "Okay. Dish duty. And if anyone asks why we smell like smoke, we were attacked by a flour monster."

"A flour monster," Percy repeated. "That's the cover story?"

"It's the truth, emotionally," Silena said.

Annabeth Grace knew how to build things.

She knew how to measure a wall and square a corner and lash a spar so it would hold in a storm. In her remembered life, Athena kids learned early: the world made sense if you drew it first. Plans were safety. Blueprints were promises.

Gifts were harder. Gifts didn't have load-bearing walls.

She sat in the back of the Zeus cabin — her cabin, shared with Thalia throughout the childhood she knew only through her sister's stories — with a block of pine wood, a carving knife, and a knot in her stomach the size of a minotaur.

Thalia was finally going to get her sixteenth celebration.

Annabeth's last birthday had been her twelfth. Thalia told her she had braided silver thread into her hair that morning, taught her a new knife trick, and pretended she hadn't stayed up late to finish mending Annabeth's favorite jacket. Annabeth couldn't remember that birthday, but she had the jacket. Thalia did stuff like that — acted like caring was annoying while doing the most caring things imaginable.

Now Annabeth wanted to give her something back, and nothing in the camp store was right. Nothing shiny or new fit Thalia, who wore the same black eyeliner until it smudged and the same leather jacket even on the warmest afternoons.

So Annabeth was making it herself. Two things, actually.

The first: a pine tree, carved from pine wood she'd found after the last storm broke a branch off the big pine on Half-Blood Hill. In Annabeth's original life that tree had been Thalia herself. She remembered the hill and the sacrifice clearly, and sometimes woke from the memory crying because she knew exactly why. Her living sister here had never become a tree. This gift was for the girl who patrolled the boundary now, loud and alive, stealing Annabeth's fries and ruffling her hair until Annabeth yelled. A symbol of keeping a home safe, not a history forced onto someone who hadn't lived it.

She wanted Thalia to know: *you held the line. You hold it still. You're rooted, even when you run.*

The carving was slow. Annabeth wasn't great at whittling — her hands were better at straight lines and right angles — but she'd been working for a week in secret, during free periods, hiding the wood under her mattress. The tree was emerging: a sturdy trunk, layered branches. Lopsided, but sincere.

The second gift was harder: a lightning pendant.

She'd traded three weeks of dessert to the Hephaestus cabin for a small silver disk and a length of leather cord, and she'd spent two evenings with Beckendorf while he showed her how to stamp a design without cracking the metal.

"Easy," Beckendorf had said in his deep, patient voice, guiding her hands. "Tap, don't smash. Let the tool do the work."

"Like this?" Annabeth had asked, tapping too hard and denting the edge.

"Like... gentler. Pretend it's your little brother's head. You want to get his attention, not concuss him."

"My little brother's across the country."

"Pretend it's Percy."

Annabeth had laughed and gotten it right on the next try: a clean, bold lightning bolt down the center of the disk. On the back, in tiny careful letters, she'd scratched: *FOR THE SKY — A.*

Because Thalia was sky. Annabeth was sky too, in this life — daughter of Zeus, not Athena — and that had been strange at first. She'd grown up thinking she was supposed to be the smart one, the planner, and then someone had shuffled the deck and she'd landed in Zeus's cabin with electricity crackling at her fingertips and a hunger for storms. But Thalia had shown her what sky could be: not just power, but protection. Lightning that struck *for* people, not *at* them.

Annabeth set the pendant aside and went back to the tree, shaving a curl of wood so thin it was translucent.

The cabin door banged open.

Annabeth shoved the carving under her pillow in one motion and grabbed the nearest book, opening it upside down.

"Hey, wise girl."

Thalia stood in the doorway, hair spiked with static, cheeks flushed from sword practice, a new rip in her jeans. She flopped onto her bunk — the one that had always been permanently messy with Thalia's jackets and knives and one sock Annabeth was convinced reproduced asexually.

"What are you reading?" Thalia asked.

Annabeth looked down at her book. It was upside down. It was also in Ancient Greek, which she could read, but the point stood.

"A book," Annabeth said with great dignity. "About... reading."

Thalia squinted at her. "You're weird today."

"I'm always weird."

"Weirder than usual. You've been weird all week. You keep disappearing. You keep smiling at nothing." Thalia sat up, suddenly suspicious. "Are you pranking me? Is this about my birthday?"

Annabeth's heart hammered. "What birthday?"

"My birthday party. In two days. The one everyone thinks I don't know they planned."

"I don't know what you're talking about," Annabeth said, which was such a bad lie that Thalia started laughing.

"Oh my gods, you're terrible at this," Thalia said, throwing her pillow at Annabeth's head. Annabeth caught it and threw it back. "You have the worst poker face in the history of poker."

"I have an excellent poker face!"

"You just tried to read a book upside down to cover up... whatever's under your pillow."

Annabeth instinctively sat on her pillow. "There's nothing under my pillow."

"There's definitely something under your pillow."

"It's... a spare pillow. For... pillow emergencies."

Thalia flopped back, grinning at the ceiling. "Sure, Annie. Pillow emergencies." Her voice softened. "You don't have to make a big deal, you know. Birthdays were never really... we didn't do them. Mom..."

Annabeth knew. She knew about Beryl Grace, the way you know about a storm you've only heard in stories — the damage was still visible in the landscape. Thalia talked about it rarely, but Annabeth had seen the way her sister went quiet over Beryl's letters and when other campers got easier news from home.

"Well," Annabeth said carefully, picking at a thread on her blanket, "maybe we do them now."

Thalia looked at her for a long moment. Something flickered across her face — surprise, and something older, something that looked almost like fear of hoping.

"Yeah?" Thalia said quietly.

"Yeah," Annabeth said. "Now get out so I can handle my pillow emergency in private."

Thalia laughed, loud and bright, and ruffled Annabeth's hair on her way out. "Love you, brat."

"Love you too, pinecone," Annabeth said.

When the door closed, Annabeth pulled the half-carved tree back out and held it tight.

*I'm going to get this right,* she promised it. *For her.*

Thalia Grace knew about the surprise party.

Of course she knew. She was sixteen, observant, and very much alive. She'd noticed Percy whispering to Grover and then both of them shutting up the second she walked into the mess hall. She'd noticed Clarisse carrying what was very obviously a roll of blue streamers under her breastplate and claiming it was "tactical camouflage." She'd noticed Silena measuring the bonfire area with a ribbon while pretending to sunbathe. She'd noticed Ella following her around singing what Ella claimed were "definitely not birthday songs, no, Ella doesn't know what birthday you mean."

Subtle, these people were not.

Thalia played along because... well, because it felt good to be the kind of person people threw secret parties for.

Which was weird. And a little scary. And she was absolutely not going to think about that too hard.

Instead, on the afternoon before her birthday party, she let herself be kidnapped by the training-cabin prank war, which was either part of the distraction plan or just campers being feral. Honestly, with this group, it could be both.

It started when Percy challenged her to spar.

"Come on," Percy said, bouncing on his toes in the arena, Riptide uncapped and gleaming. "One round. Winner gets the last blue pancake tomorrow."

"You're on, Kelp Head," Thalia said, cracking her knuckles, electricity sparking faintly. "Prepare to eat dirt."

They circled. Percy was getting better — fast feet, clever wrist, that infuriating Poseidon instinct for balance. Thalia was stronger and meaner and had four years on him, but Percy fought like water: always moving, finding cracks.

Thalia lunged. Percy sidestepped and flicked water from his blade — where had he even gotten water? — straight into her face.

"HEY!" Thalia sputtered.

The Ares kids on the sidelines howled. Clarisse shouted, "FOUL! DISQUALIFY HIM! ...actually, good move, keep going!"

Thalia wiped water from her eyes, grinning despite herself. "Oh, it's like that?"

She raised her hand and called a tiny spark — just enough to make Percy's hair stand straight up like a sea urchin.

Percy patted his head. "Do I look cool?"

"You look like you stuck a fork in a socket."

"I look AMAZING."

Grover, who was supposed to be refereeing, was too busy laughing to call points. Annabeth, keeping score on a wax tablet, wrote *Percy 1 - Thalia 1 - Dignity 0* and held it up.

Then Silena — sweet, innocent Silena — "tripped" while carrying a bucket of soapy water for "cleaning shields" and somehow managed to dump half of it directly onto the arena floor between Percy and Thalia.

Both of them slipped. Percy went down windmilling. Thalia grabbed for him, caught his wrist, and they both went sprawling into the suds, sliding three feet and crashing into a pile of practice dummies.

The sidelines erupted. Ella, from the rafters, shouted, "ELLA GIVES THAT FALL A TEN! ELLA HAS JUDGED MANY FALLS!"

Thalia lay on her back in soapy water, Percy next to her wheezing with laughter, a practice dummy's straw head resting on her stomach like a weird hat.

"This," Thalia gasped, "is the stupidest sparring match in history."

"Best one, though," Percy said, grinning sideways at her. His hair was full of soap bubbles. One floated off his head and popped on Thalia's nose.

Thalia started laughing — real laughing, the kind that hurt her ribs. Percy joined in. Annabeth abandoned the score tablet and flopped down next to them, getting her own jeans soaked. Grover piled on, bleating. Even Clarisse came over, stood above them with her arms crossed, looked at the four of them lying in a puddle like idiots, and said, "You're all disgraces to combat," and then sat down right in the soap with them.

Silena took a picture with a magical instant camera borrowed from the Aphrodite cabin — *click-whirr* — and the photo developed showing all five of them, soaked and grinning, Thalia in the middle with soap in her spiked hair like a foam mohawk.

"That one's for the cabin wall," Silena said softly, and Thalia felt something warm and strange bloom in her chest.

Later, while they were toweling off, Annabeth tugged Thalia's sleeve.

"Hey," Annabeth said quietly. "Tomorrow. After dinner. Bonfire. Be there? Like, no excuses, no patrol swaps."

Thalia pretended to think hard. "Hmm. I dunno. I might have to wash my hair."

"Thalia."

"I might have to count the pine cones. Very important. Very urgent pine cone census."

"THALIA."

Thalia bumped her shoulder against her little sister's. "I'll be there, Annie. Wouldn't miss it."

Annabeth smiled — relieved and mischievous and guilty all at once — and Thalia had to look away before her own face gave her away.

*They think I don't know,* Thalia thought, wringing water out of her jacket. *Idiots. I love them so much it makes my teeth hurt.*

That night, alone in the Zeus cabin after Annabeth had fallen asleep with a book on her face, Thalia sat on her bunk and stared at the ceiling.

Sixteen.

Reaching sixteen had felt like a cliff edge. Prophecies made birthdays into threats for children of the Big Three. Annabeth had told her the old-life story, the tree and the hill, and Thalia hated how much fear her sister still carried from it. She had once joked that the gods would probably never let her reach seventeen. Annabeth had gone pale and thrown a pillow at her and said *don't say that.* Thalia had stopped making that joke.

Now her belated party was tomorrow. She was here. She had a sister who braided her hair and a Seaweed Brain who put soap in it and friends who built terrible decorations and a satyr who'd cried when she'd sprained her ankle last month. Sixteen didn't have to mean waiting alone for the next disaster.

People who showed up.

Thalia rolled over and pretended to sleep before she did something embarrassing like cry.

"Today," Silena declared on the morning of Thalia's birthday party, flour already on her cheek like war paint, "we make it BLUE."

Percy, Grover, and Ella stood at attention in the kitchen corner. The practice cake — the gray-purple leaning tower — sat on the counter as a warning to future generations. Someone had stuck a little flag in it that said *NEVER AGAIN.*

"Lessons learned," Silena said, pointing at the flag. "One: Grover does not touch the microwave. Two: Percy does not touch the red dye. Three: we measure flour like scientists, not like... Grover."

"Hey!" Grover said, then, "Fair."

"Ella's role?" Ella asked, feathers freshly washed, eyes bright.

"Ella, you are on blueberry duty. Mash, but don't eat all of them."

"Ella will eat some of them," Ella negotiated. "For quality control."

"Reasonable," Silena allowed.

They began. Carefully this time. Reverently. Percy cracked eggs over a separate bowl so shell could be fished out easily — a trick he remembered Sally using, backed up by Amphitrite's advice during his Iris-call home last week, when he'd tried to casually ask about cake without saying *birthday surprise.*

Amphitrite had guessed it was for a party. Percy let her believe that was all he was worrying about. Nothing he'd said to her hinted at his lost years or the life with Sally he remembered.

*Blue cake, little tide?* Amphitrite had said, laughing with a warmth Percy was learning to trust. *For Thalia? That's sweet. Remember — a little dye goes a long way, and add the blueberries last so they don't turn it gray. And Percy? Tell her I said happy birthday. Anyone who looks out for my boy gets blue cake for life.*

Percy had hung up with his throat tight and his resolve tripled.

Now he measured flour with a spoon, leveling with a knife like a normal person. Grover poured milk slowly, counting out loud. Silena creamed butter and sugar until it was fluffy. Ella mashed blueberries with intense focus, blue juice up to her elbows, occasionally popping one in her beak and humming.

"Okay," Silena whispered. "The blue moment."

Percy held the vial of blue dye like it was ambrosia. "Everyone quiet. This requires concentration."

He added three drops. Stirred.

Pale blue. Like a clear sky just before noon.

"More," Grover breathed.

Two more drops. Stirred.

Cornflower. Camp T-shirt blue.

"More," Ella whispered. "Bluer. Like the sea. Like Sally."

Percy added the mashed blueberries, folding gently — he'd finally learned what folding meant, thanks to Ella demonstrating with a towel — and stirred.

The batter bloomed into color: deep, rich, ocean blue, flecked with darker blueberry stars. It looked like the Aegean at sunset. It looked like Sally's kitchen in the life he remembered. It looked like love.

Silence in the kitchen corner.

"It's blue," Grover said reverently.

"It's REALLY blue," Percy said, eyes stinging a little. "Like, aggressively blue. Like, Smurf blue."

"It's perfect," Silena said, wiping her eyes and pretending it was flour. "Sally would be proud."

"Ella cries," Ella announced, tearing up. "Ella is not ashamed. Blue is emotional."

They poured it into the prepared pans — two round layers, carefully greased — and slid them into the oven. Then they sat on the floor and stared at the oven window like it was an oracle.

"How long?" Percy asked.

"Thirty-five minutes," Silena said. "And we do NOT open the door early. Cakes can smell fear."

They waited. They played tic-tac-toe on a floury counter. Grover ate a blueberry and turned his tongue blue. Ella sang three of her nine birthday songs as rehearsal. Percy tried not to chew his nails.

When the timer dinged, Silena pulled out two perfect, level, deeply blue layers. They were springy. They smelled like vanilla and summer.

Percy stared. "We... we did it? We actually baked?"

"We baked!" Grover shouted, picking Percy up in a hug and spinning him. "WE'RE BAKERS!"

Ella flapped around the kitchen, showering blueberry leaves everywhere. "CAKE! CAKE! ELLA LOVES CAKE!"

Silena was already whipping frosting — white frosting, so the blue would pop when cut. "Quick, frost while it's cool. Grover, hold the layers steady. Percy, spread. Ella, sprinkles."

They frosted with more enthusiasm than skill. The frosting was a little lumpy. One side had a visible fingerprint. The sprinkles — silver lightning bolts Silena had special-ordered from the Aphrodite cabin's craft stash — went on slightly crooked.

It was the most beautiful cake Percy had ever seen.

They stood back, arms around each other, blue up to their elbows, flour in their hair.

"Happy birthday, Thalia," Percy whispered to the cake.

The cake, being a cake, said nothing. But it gleamed.

---

Thalia spent the entire day of her belated sixteenth party pretending she didn't know what was happening.

This was harder than it sounded, because everyone was terrible at secrets.

At breakfast, Annabeth was vibrating with suppressed excitement and kept kicking Thalia under the table every time Thalia said the word "birthday."

At sword practice, Clarisse — CLARISSE — smiled at her. It was terrifying. "Have a good day, Grace," Clarisse said, clapping her on the shoulder hard enough to bruise. "See you tonight. At the... regular... bonfire. That is normal."

"Totally normal bonfire," Thalia agreed solemnly. "Nothing special."

Clarisse nodded too many times and walked into a door.

At lunch, Grover gave her an extra bread roll "because you look hungry, no reason," and Ella flew overhead trailing a banner that very clearly said HAPPY BIRTHDAY THA— before Ella realized, squeaked, and hastily rolled it up.

By dinner, Thalia's cheeks hurt from trying not to smile.

After dinner, Annabeth grabbed her wrist. "Come on. Bonfire. Now."

"I'm eating," Thalia protested, mouth full of bread.

"No you're not, you're done." Annabeth dragged her up. "Let's go."

"You've got crumbs on —" Thalia started, but Annabeth was already pulling her toward the bonfire area, chattering a mile a minute about shield formations to cover her nerves.

They rounded the path — and the bonfire area exploded with light.

Well, not exploded. Clarisse had rigged blue and silver lanterns in the trees — cyclops-proofed with wire, just as planned — and they'd all lit at once when Grover hit the tripwire. Streamers hung everywhere: neat silver ones from Silena, crooked aggressive ones from Clarisse that did indeed look like they might mug someone. A giant banner read HAPPY BIRTHDAY STORM GIRL in red paint that dripped convincingly like blood. Someone — probably the Stolls — had added a second banner underneath that said WE DIDN'T EAT ALL THE CAKE (YET).

And there were people. So many people.

Thalia and Annabeth — the entire Zeus cabin — were there. Percy and Grover and Silena and Clarisse and Ella and Beckendorf and the Stolls and Katie Gardner and even Mr. D, who was holding a Diet Coke and looking vaguely annoyed to be there but there anyway. Chiron stood at the back, smiling like he'd known all along — because he had.

"SURPRISE!" everyone shouted. Ella shouted it nine times in a row, in different harmonies.

Thalia stopped dead. Annabeth's hand was still on her wrist, trembling a little.

Thalia looked at the lights. At the crooked lightning bolts. At the cake on the table — a gloriously blue cake with white frosting and silver sprinkles, slightly lopsided, with a fingerprint on one side and sixteen candles (fifteen straight, one leaning) burning bright.

She looked at Percy, who had blue frosting in his hair and was grinning so wide his face might split. At Grover, bleating happily. At Silena, hands clasped, eyes shining. At Clarisse, arms crossed, trying to look tough and failing because she was smiling. At Ella, already starting song number one.

Thalia opened her mouth. Closed it. Tried again.

"You guys are the worst at secrets," she said, voice cracking. "Like, historically bad. Clarisse called me 'birthday girl' this morning and then tried to pretend she'd said 'battle girl.'"

Clarisse groaned. "I knew I messed that up."

"I knew since Tuesday," Thalia said. "Ella's banner said my name."

"Ella regrets nothing!" Ella cried. "HAPPY BIRTHDAY THALIA!"

Everyone laughed. Then everyone was hugging her at once — Percy first, nearly knocking her over, then Grover, then Silena, then a bone-crushing one from Clarisse that lifted her off her feet.

"Happy birthday, Grace," Clarisse muttered. "Don't make it weird."

"Too late," Thalia whispered back, hugging her harder. "Thank you."

Then Annabeth was there, hanging back a little, nervous in a way Thalia rarely saw. Annabeth, who sparred with hellhounds and back-talked gods, twisting her hands in her shirt.

"Hey," Thalia said softly. "Hey, Annie. Come here."

Annabeth stepped forward and Thalia pulled her into the tightest hug of the night, lifting her clean off the ground while Annabeth shrieked and laughed.

"Put me down, you storm cloud!" Annabeth giggled.

"Never," Thalia said, but she did, settling Annabeth back down and keeping an arm around her shoulders. "You did all this?"

"We all did," Annabeth said. "But I — I made you something. Not party something. Just... come here when there's cake?"

"There's always cake with you people."

They sang. Oh gods, they sang — Ella leading all nine songs, Grover harmonizing on the reed pipes, Percy singing loudly and off-key, the Stolls adding entirely new verses about Thalia wrestling a bear (which had happened) and Thalia eating an entire pizza in one sitting (which had also happened). Thalia laughed until she cried, leaning against a log with her sister's head on her shoulder.

Then Percy and Grover carried the cake over with great ceremony, Silena hovering with a fire extinguisher "just in case," Clarisse clearing a path like a bodyguard.

"Make a wish!" Percy said.

Thalia looked at the blue cake. Really looked at it. Blue like the sea. Blue like the deeper water beyond the shore. Blue like someone's mom had taught someone's friend that birthdays should be colorful.

"Who made this?" she asked quietly.

Percy scuffed his shoe. "Uh. Me. Mostly. With help. It was gray yesterday. Today it's blue. Long story involving Grover and a microwave."

"Amphitrite—" Percy started, then stopped, shy. "She says happy birthday. She says anyone who looks out for me gets blue cake for life."

Thalia stared at him. Then she did something that shocked everyone, including herself: she burst into tears.

Not pretty crying. Full, punk-rock, ugly crying, right there in front of the bonfire.

"Oh no," Percy said, panicking. "Oh no, is it the cake? Is it too blue? Grover, is it too blue?"

"It's perfect," Thalia sobbed, laughing at the same time. "It's the most perfect cake I've ever — nobody ever — my mom never —"

Annabeth's arm tightened around her. Grover handed her a napkin. Silena pretended to fuss with the lanterns so Thalia could wipe her face. Clarisse loudly announced, "SHE'S ALLERGIC TO FEELINGS, GIVE HER SPACE," which made Thalia laugh through tears.

Thalia closed her eyes and made a wish.

*Let me stay. Let them stay. Let this be real.*

She blew out the candles — all sixteen, in one breath, plus the leaning one relit and she had to blow it again while everyone cheered — and Percy cut the cake with the focus of a surgeon. The inside was even bluer than the outside, studded with blueberries like constellations.

"Blue!" Ella shrieked with delight. "ELLA WAS RIGHT! BLUE IS EMOTIONAL AND DELICIOUS!"

It was delicious. Moist, vanilla-sweet, bursting with berries. Thalia ate two slices and got blue frosting on her nose. Percy got it in his hair again. Grover turned entirely blue around the mouth and looked like a Smurf-goat. Mr. D took one bite, said, "Acceptable. The blue is... acceptable," which from him was practically a sonnet.

Then presents. Silena gave her a braided silver-and-blue bracelet. Grover gave her a set of reed pipes ("so you can call me if you get lost, just... don't, actually, I'm bad at directions"). Clarisse gave her a whetstone in a little box with HAPPY BIRTHDAY, DON'T DIE engraved on it — Thalia nearly cried again. Ella gave her a mixtape of harpy songs titled THALIA'S BATTLE HYMNS (VOLUME ONE OF SEVENTEEN). Beckendorf gave her a newly tuned shield boss. The Stolls gave her a coupon for "one free prank, no retaliation," which Thalia immediately promised to use on them.

Then Annabeth stood up, holding something behind her back.

"Okay," Annabeth said, suddenly shy again. "Mine's stupid. It's handmade. Don't laugh."

"I would never laugh at you," Thalia said, and meant it.

Annabeth held out her hands. In one palm: a wooden pine tree, carved from pale pine wood, sanded smooth, each branch carefully shaped. A little lopsided. Completely sincere. In the other: a silver disk on a leather cord, stamped with a bold lightning bolt. On the back, tiny letters: *FOR THE SKY — A.*

The bonfire crackled. Someone's laugh faded. Thalia stared.

"Annie..." Thalia breathed. "Did you make these?"

Annabeth nodded, ears red. "The tree is... you know. The pine. Half-Blood Hill. You patrol that hill. You help keep it safe. You're rooted. Even when you run, you're rooted. And the lightning — that's us. Sky. You and me. Zeus kids. I thought... both sides. The part that holds, and the part that strikes."

Thalia couldn't speak. She turned the little tree over in her hands — she could see the knife marks, the places where Annabeth's hand had slipped and kept going. She turned the pendant over, traced the letters with her thumb.

"You made this for here?" Thalia whispered. "Not only for the other story you told me?"

Annabeth nodded, eyes bright. "I remember the hill. The storm. You standing there in my old life. But that isn't your memory, and it isn't all this means. I see you keeping watch here. I see you staying. I'm glad — I'm so glad you get to just be my sister. Who steals my fries."

Thalia laughed wetly. "I steal your fries because you let me."

"Never."

Thalia fastened the pendant around her neck — Annabeth had to help because Thalia's hands were shaking — and held the little tree to her chest.

"Annie, this is —" Thalia's voice broke. "This is the best gift anyone's ever — how did you —"

Annabeth shrugged, blinking fast. "I pay attention."

Thalia pulled her sister into her lap like she had when Annabeth was seven and scared of thunderstorms — which was ironic, for Zeus kids, but storms had been scary when Mom was yelling — and held her tight while Annabeth pretended not to cry into her jacket.

Around them, the party went on. Percy was trying to teach Ella to roast marshmallows without setting them on fire (failing). Grover and Silena were slow-dancing badly to pipe music. Clarisse was arm-wrestling Beckendorf and winning. Chiron was telling Mr. D a story that Mr. D was pretending not to enjoy.

But in that little circle by the fire, there were just two sisters: one sixteen, one twelve, holding on.

"I thought nobody would show up," Thalia whispered, so quiet only Annabeth could hear. "For years, I thought... people don't stick. Mom didn't. Dad... gods are busy. I thought if I kept moving, it wouldn't hurt when people left."

Annabeth pulled back just enough to look her in the eye, fierce and bright and twelve years old and absolutely certain. "We're not leaving. You're stuck with us. You're stuck with me. Even when you're annoying and you use all the hot water and you sing in the shower."

"I do not sing in the shower."

"You sing *incredibly* badly in the shower."

Thalia laughed, real and free, and hugged her again. The pendant pressed warm between them. The little wooden tree sat safe in her jacket pocket, over her heart.

People showed up. They'd shown up with crooked streamers and lopsided blue cake and handmade gifts and nine songs. They'd shown up, and they'd stay.

Thalia Grace, sixteen and finally celebrated, daughter of Zeus, sister of Annabeth, friend of idiots, sat by the bonfire with blue frosting on her nose and lightning around her neck and roots in her pocket, and felt — for the first time in longer than she could remember — entirely, completely home.

---

Long after the lanterns burned low, after Mr. D had left and Chiron had banked the fire, after Ella had sung her ninth and final song to thunderous applause and Grover had fallen asleep with his head on a log, Annabeth and Thalia and Percy walked back to the cabins together.

Percy carried the leftover cake like it was treasure. Thalia wore her pendant. Annabeth carried the photo Silena had taken — the five of them in soapy water, grinning — now signed by everyone in silver marker.

"Best birthday?" Annabeth asked, bumping Thalia's hip.

Thalia pretended to consider. "Hmm. The cake was blue. The banner threatened me. Clarisse smiled. Ella sang nine songs. Percy got soap in my hair yesterday and frosting in it today. I'd say..."

"Say it," Percy said.

"Best birthday ever," Thalia admitted. "Don't tell the cake we had on the actual day. It'll get jealous."

"The gas station cake you told me about? Because we missed the bus?" Annabeth asked.

"And it was great," Thalia said. "But this... this was better."

They reached the Zeus cabin. Percy handed Thalia the cake box with a bow. "For breakfast. Don't let Annabeth eat it all."

"I make no promises," Annabeth said.

"Goodnight, birthday girl," Percy said, backing toward the Poseidon cabin. "Happy belated sixteen, Storm Girl."

"Goodnight, Kelp Head," Thalia said softly.

Percy left. The sisters went inside. Thalia set the little wooden tree on the shelf above her bunk, next to the photo Silena had given her, where she could see it first thing every morning. She hung the bracelet from Silena on her wrist. She set Clarisse's whetstone on her trunk.

Then she climbed into bed, still wearing the lightning pendant.

Annabeth, from her own bunk, whispered, "Thalia?"

"Yeah, Annie?"

"Are you happy?"

A pause. Crickets outside. The pine on the hill rustling in the night wind.

"Yeah," Thalia said. "Yeah, I really am."

"Good," Annabeth whispered. "That's all I wanted."

"Love you, brat."

"Love you too, pinecone."

And under a sky full of stars, with cake and lightning and people who showed up, two sisters fell asleep — safe, celebrated, home.
