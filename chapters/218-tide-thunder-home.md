## Chapter 218: Tide, Thunder, Home

The meal began with three different lists.

Annabeth had made the first one: seven names, one table, and a space beside each for a task the person had chosen. Percy had made the second: foods he thought everyone might eat, including two kinds of bread and a stew he had not asked Chiron how to prepare. Ianthe had made the third, which was a schedule with serving times, seating order, and a small column marked “responsible person.”

Annabeth set the papers side by side on the Big House table.

“Which one are we using?” Percy asked.

“None of them yet.”

“Then why are they here?”

“To see where they disagree.”

“They disagree about everything.”

“Not everything.”

Percy leaned over the pages. “Your list says ‘meal.’ Mine says stew. Ianthe’s says ‘warm first course at six.’ That is not a disagreement. That’s the same thing written three ways.”

“Three ways become three jobs if no one checks.”

“You’ve been talking to Chiron too much.”

“I’ve been watching you make a list and start cooking before you finish reading it.”

“I read while I cook.”

“You read the first line.”

“That’s the important one.”

Annabeth lowered her pencil. “We don’t know if it’s the important one.”

He looked at her. “You’re right.”

The quick agreement surprised her. Percy nudged the paper toward her and let his hand rest beside her wrist. She touched two fingers to his knuckles before she returned to the list.

After Poseidon’s visit, Percy had said the seven of them might share a seasonal meal before the kitchen changed over to winter stores. He had spoken as though the logistics were the only difficult part. Annabeth had asked whether he wanted to invite everyone himself.

“No,” he had said. “I don’t want to tell them what it means.”

“What does it mean to you?”

He had taken a moment. “That we can eat together.”

She had agreed. They had not decided whether there would be a second meal.

Now she picked up her pencil and drew a line through Ianthe’s seating order.

Ianthe’s hand reached across the table for the page. Annabeth moved the pencil before it caught the edge.

“I have not finished the schedule,” Ianthe said.

“I know.”

“Why did you cross that out?”

“Because the seating order is deciding who sits next to whom before we’ve asked whether anyone cares.”

“I was trying to prevent an awkward arrangement.”

“By arranging people.”

“That is how a table works.”

“A table can work without telling people where to sit.”

“Then we will have to keep moving chairs.”

“We can.”

Ianthe’s expression tightened. She had the same weather notebook tucked beneath her arm that she had been carrying for weeks. The folded hill drawing from the portrait game was no longer tucked in the back; Annabeth did not know where she kept it now. She did not ask.

“I want the meal to happen on time,” Ianthe said. “If everyone arrives at a different hour and takes tasks at random, the food will be cold.”

“Then we can set a time.”

“And a task list.”

“If people choose a task.”

“People don’t always choose.”

“Then they can eat without doing one.”

Ianthe looked down at the sheet. “That wastes the opportunity to make it easier.”

“For who?”

“For the group.”

“Which group?”

“The seven of us.”

“Then ask the seven of us.”

Ianthe’s jaw shifted. She looked toward the door, where the sound of someone carrying wood passed and faded.

“Fine,” she said. “I will ask.”

Annabeth put her pencil down. “I’m not telling you to stop making plans. I’m asking you to leave space for other people to answer.”

“I know.” Ianthe pulled the page closer. “I can ask if they want a schedule.”

“That’s a start.”

“I didn’t ask for a grade.”

“You didn’t get one.”

Percy turned his list sideways. “What if we just ask everyone whether they want to come, and tell them the meal is happening at six?”

“Then we know who is coming,” Annabeth said. “The place still needs to be reserved.”

“I’ll ask Chiron.”

“I can ask him,” Ianthe said.

“You can ask him about the room,” Annabeth replied. “We don’t need to make one person collect every answer.”

Ianthe looked at the line she had written under “responsible person.” She drew a line through that heading. “All right.”

“Do you want to send the invitations?” Percy asked her.

“Why me?”

“You wrote the schedule.”

“That is not an answer.”

“No. I was asking.”

“I don’t want to.”

“Then don’t.”

She sat back. “I can ask one person.”

“Who?” Annabeth asked.

Ianthe glanced toward the window. “Thalia.”

“You can ask her,” Annabeth said. “You don’t have to ask on behalf of the group.”

“I know.”

“Do you want to ask her?”

“I think so.”

“You think so, or you do?”

Ianthe pulled her notebook out and placed it on top of the schedule. “I’ll decide.”

The answer was not the same as an agreement, but Annabeth did not need it to be.

She looked down at the list of names. Clarisse. Silena. Leandros. Thalia. Ianthe. Percy. Annabeth. Seven people who shared a parent in different combinations, or had chosen to love one of them, or had lived enough years beside the others to be treated like a sibling without being able to say exactly when it had started.

There was no line for parent. Nobody had put one on the page.

Percy tapped the paper. “We should ask Clarisse first. She’ll say if the room is too crowded.”

“Do you want to ask her?”

“I can.”

“Then ask her. Don’t tell her why she should come.”

“I won’t.”

Annabeth took the page with the seven names and folded it once. “I’ll ask Silena and Leandros. Ianthe can ask Thalia if she decides she wants to.”

“And I’ll ask Chiron about the room,” Percy said.

“What room?”

“The side room off the pavilion. It has two tables and a door that closes.”

“Is that where you want the meal?”

“I don’t know. It’s warm. It’s not the Big House dining room, and it’s not our cabin.”

“Do you want a place that feels like nobody’s territory?”

“Maybe.”

“That’s a good reason to ask about it.”

He smiled. “You can put it on the list.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“It’s your reason.”

“You can write it down.”

“I don’t need to.”

“Then I’ll remember it.”

“Will you?”

He pressed a hand to his chest. “I remember most things.”

“Not where the measuring tape is.”

“It is in the drawer.”

“It was in the drawer. Then you used it to mark the pantry shelf.”

“That was a useful place for it.”

“You left it on the shelf.”

“I was going to put it away.”

“You were going to do a lot of things.”

“I’ll put it away now.”

He did. Annabeth watched him return with the tape and place it in the correct drawer, not the one beside the stove where the scissors were kept. It was such a small correction that she almost didn’t mention it.

“Thank you,” she said.

Percy caught the edge of her sleeve. “You’re welcome.”

She looked up. He was smiling at her in a way that did not ask the room to notice. She brushed her thumb over the inside of his wrist. His hand stayed near hers until the first knock came.

Clarisse stepped into the Big House room with a coil of leather cord over one shoulder. Her eyes moved over the three lists.

“What are those?”

“An invitation,” Percy said.

“That looks like a battle plan.”

“It started smaller.”

“Who’s coming?”

“We don’t know. Would you like to?”

Clarisse took a few seconds before she answered. “Where?”

“We’re asking about the side room.”

“Why?”

“It’s warm, separate from the cabins, and has enough space,” Annabeth said. “You can say no.”

“I know I can say no.”

“I know you do.”

Clarisse glanced at Percy. “Are you trying to get everyone to talk about the gods?”

“No.”

“Are you sure?”

“No,” Percy said. “I’m trying to have a meal.”

“That’s better.”

“Do you want to come?”

Clarisse put a finger under the cord. “I can help set the table. I’m not organizing anyone’s conversation.”

“Okay,” Annabeth said.

“And I’m not going to stay for dessert just because someone thinks we should.”

“Okay.”

“Then I’ll come.”

No one wrote her name under a task. Annabeth made a small mark beside “yes” and left the blank beside it untouched.

“Do you want to ask Silena?” Clarisse asked.

“I’ll ask her.”

“Don’t ask whether she can help with food. Ask if she wants to come.”

“I will.”

Clarisse nodded and left the cord on the chair. “This is for tying the paper lanterns. I can use it later.”

“Do you want us to keep it here?” Percy asked.

“I put it here.”

“Right.”

She turned toward the door again. “If the table needs moving, ask before you take it apart.”

“Of course,” Annabeth said.

Clarisse went out. Percy looked at the three lists.

“One yes,” he said.

“One answer,” Annabeth corrected.

“Same thing.”

“No. It isn’t.”

“Fair.”

They folded the schedule and put it under the other papers. The room was still only a possibility. The meal was still an invitation. No one had been assigned to make it feel like home.

---

Silena was in the greenhouse when Annabeth found her. She had a list clipped to the board, and a small pot rested on the ledge beside the door. The herbs inside were trimmed back for the colder days; some leaves had already gone dull at the tips.

“Do you have a minute?” Annabeth asked.

“I do. I’m not moving the pots until I finish this list.”

“I’m not asking you to.”

Silena looked over the paper. “What is it?”

“Percy wants the seven of us to have a meal together. We’re asking to use the side room off the pavilion. You can come and do nothing, or choose to help with something. You don’t have to make the food or manage the table.”

“That’s very specific.”

“We’ve learned a few things.”

Silena set her pencil down. “I want to come.”

“Okay.”

“I want to bring bread.”

“If you want to.”

“I want to choose which bread.”

“Of course.”

“And I don’t want anyone to call it the thing that made everyone feel better.”

Annabeth nodded. “I won’t.”

“Do you think anyone else will?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then I’ll tell them if they do.”

“That works.”

Silena glanced at her list. “I have rosemary left from the last batch. I can make the flat loaf with the oil on top. It keeps well.”

“Will it be enough for everyone?”

“Yes.”

“Do you want me to help?”

“Not yet. I have to finish this list first.”

“All right.”

“You can stay while I finish it, if you want.”

Annabeth looked at the pot on the ledge. “Can I look at the leaves?”

“You can look.”

“Can I touch them?”

Silena gave her a small smile. “No.”

“Okay.”

Silena returned to her list. Annabeth stood beside the door, watching the soft green leaves without leaning close. The meal had one more yes, and the bread belonged to Silena to make.

Leandros was at the marked path, where Beckendorf had left a stack of clean boards to dry. He had his notebook open on one knee. The wind moved the paper against his palm.

“Do you have time?” Annabeth asked.

“For what?”

“Percy’s arranging a meal for the seven siblings. We’re asking to use the side room off the pavilion. You can come without bringing anything or choose a task.”

Leandros closed the notebook. “What kind of meal?”

“Seasonal. The kitchen is changing over the stores.”

“Who’s deciding what to cook?”

“People can decide what they want to bring.”

“That doesn’t answer the question.”

“There isn’t one person deciding.”

“Then we should plan the quantities. If it’s seven people, we need enough for seven.”

“And anyone who’s helping in the room?”

“We can count them.”

“We don’t have a final count.”

“Then you use the higher number.”

“Which higher number?”

“The one that assumes someone will arrive late or want seconds.”

“That’s not a quantity.”

“It is a way to calculate one.”

Annabeth folded her arms. “Do you want to come?”

Leandros looked at the boards. “Yes.”

“Do you want to help?”

“I can carry the serving bowls.”

“Would you like to?”

“I can.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

He looked at her, then at the notebook. “I want to help carry them. It’s a job that needs doing.”

“Okay.”

“I can bring a water jug too.”

“Chiron can decide where the water goes.”

“I wasn’t deciding. I was saying I can carry it.”

“Then I’ll write that down.”

She didn’t have a list. He had already noticed. She took out the folded page with the seven names and wrote his offer in the margin.

“Don’t write it like I’m responsible for the water,” he said.

“I’ll write ‘offers to carry bowls or jug.’”

“Good.”

“Do you want to pick one now?”

“No. I’ll see which one needs carrying.”

“That’s fine.”

He opened his notebook again. “The room has a door that closes?”

“It does.”

“Could we leave it open?”

“Do you want it open?”

“I don’t know. If it’s shut, people might think they have to talk.”

Annabeth looked at him. “Would an open door make it easier?”

“Maybe.”

“We can ask everyone when we get there.”

He nodded. His eyes moved to the lower path sign. “The side room has a current window?”

“No.”

“It should.”

“Why?”

“So it can be ventilated.”

“It has a regular window.”

“That isn’t the same.”

“No. It isn’t.”

He smiled as if she had agreed with him. Annabeth did not smile back.

“Do you want to come for the meal or inspect the room?” she asked.

“I can do both.”

“You can come for the meal.”

“I will.”

He tucked the notebook under his arm. “I’ll ask Clarisse if I should carry the bowls.”

“Ask the person who is moving them.”

“She’ll know.”

“She might not be the person who needs to answer.”

Leandros’s mouth tightened. He did not argue. “I’ll ask Percy.”

“You can ask whoever has the bowls.”

“That could be Percy.”

“It could.”

He left the notebook in his hand and went back to the board stack. Annabeth walked toward the hill to find Ianthe.

She was at the Big House porch, sorting the weather sheets into one folder and a small stack. The folder was labeled by date and location. The smaller stack had been marked “questions.” Ianthe kept one finger on the top page.

“I want to ask Thalia about the meal,” she said before Annabeth could speak.

“Do you want me to come with you?”

“No.”

“All right.”

Ianthe’s eyes moved to the folded list in Annabeth’s hand. “Is everyone coming?”

“Clarisse and Silena said yes. Leandros did too.”

“Did they choose tasks?”

“They chose what they might bring or help with.”

“Is there a schedule?”

“Not yet.”

“Do people know the time?”

“Six.”

“Will there be enough bowls?”

“We’ll count them before the meal.”

“You could count now.”

“We could. Do you want to?”

Ianthe drew her hand off the weather sheet. “No. I’ll ask Thalia if she wants to come.”

“Good.”

She stood, taking the folder but leaving the smaller stack on the table. “I don’t want to make her feel like she has to.”

“Then tell her that.”

“I will.”

Annabeth watched her cross the porch. She did not follow.

Thalia was on the shaded side of the path with a book open across her lap. Her cane leaned against the bench. She had one finger marking a page while she looked up at Ianthe.

“Can I ask you something?” Ianthe said.

“You can.”

“Percy wants to have a meal with the seven of us. At six, in the side room off the pavilion. You can come without taking a task. You can leave whenever you want.”

Thalia looked at the book. “Who’s planning the conversation?”

“No one.”

“Who’s asking me to make it normal?”

“No one.”

“Who’s checking whether I’m upset if I sit quietly?”

Ianthe paused. “No one. I hope.”

“That’s not the same as a promise.”

“No. I can ask before I check.”

Thalia shut the book over her finger. “You can.”

“Do you want to come?”

“I think so.”

“You don’t have to decide now.”

“I can decide now.”

“Do you want to bring anything?”

“No.”

“All right.”

Thalia lifted one eyebrow. “You sound disappointed.”

“I was trying to make sure you had a choice.”

“That is a choice.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I’m trying to.”

Thalia glanced toward the porch, where Annabeth was still standing. “Are we bringing up Zeus?”

“Not unless you want to.”

“Do you want to?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then we don’t need to plan it into dinner.”

“I can agree to that.”

“Good. I’ll come. I may leave after we eat.”

“Okay.”

“Do you want me to bring a book?”

“If you want.”

“I might.”

Ianthe turned back toward the porch. “Thank you.”

Thalia reopened her book. “You asked. I answered.”

There was no promise in the exchange, and neither of them tried to make one.

When Annabeth caught up with Ianthe, she said, “That seemed clear.”

“It was.”

“Are you glad she’s coming?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“You don’t have to know yet.”

“I know.”

Annabeth let the answer pass without correction. They went back to the Big House and found Percy waiting with the room key. He had a paper in his other hand.

“Chiron said we can have the side room,” he said. “He’ll have the winter stew in the kitchen, but he’s not going to sit with us unless we ask.”

“Did he say that?” Annabeth asked.

“He said he had other things to do.”

“Did you ask if he wanted to eat?”

“No.”

“Then we don’t know.”

Percy looked at the key in his hand. “Do you want him there?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“We can ask him.”

“We can.”

Ianthe glanced from one to the other. “Should we reserve him a place?”

“No,” Annabeth said. “If we ask and he wants to join, we can add a chair.”

“Then the seating is still open.”

“Yes.”

“I don’t like that.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I know you like to know where everyone will sit.”

“It prevents confusion.”

“It can also decide for them.”

“I heard you.”

“Good.”

Percy touched his hand to Annabeth’s shoulder and let it fall. “I’ll ask Chiron whether he’s coming. I won’t put his name on the list until he answers.”

“That works,” Annabeth said.

Ianthe picked up the paper where she had crossed out the seating order. “Can I make a count of bowls and spoons?”

“Yes,” Annabeth said. “Just the count.”

“And if there aren’t enough?”

“Ask the kitchen what we can borrow.”

“Should I ask Chiron?”

“You can.”

Ianthe looked at her. “I’m asking you whether I should.”

“Then yes. Ask Chiron.”

“Thank you.”

She picked up the pencil and crossed off the heading “responsible person” a second time. The paper was becoming soft at the crease. She folded it carefully and set it aside.

---

The day of the meal, Annabeth met Percy outside the side room with a folded tablecloth under one arm and a list of supplies in her pocket. The room smelled like wood smoke from the kitchen. A long table stood against the wall. The spare table had been pushed near the door, where the floorboards dipped slightly.

Percy checked the room key against the lock. “You said seven chairs.”

“I said we need seven chairs.”

“There are eight.”

“One can stay by the wall.”

“Why?”

“In case someone needs it.”

“Who?”

“Someone who wants to sit down.”

“That’s a good answer.”

He unlocked the door. “I wasn’t expecting another person.”

“Then why ask?”

“I was trying to find out whether you had one in mind.”

“I don’t.”

“Good.”

He leaned his shoulder against the door while Annabeth went inside. The table was long enough for four on each side, with one end nearly touching the wall. The chair by the wall had a cracked back. Annabeth tested it and set it aside.

“This one shouldn’t be used,” she said.

“I can fix it.”

“You can ask Beckendorf whether it’s repairable.”

“I can ask. I can also look at the joint.”

“After we finish setting the room.”

He smiled. “You’re very good at giving me the order I was about to give myself.”

“I’m not giving you an order.”

“I know.”

She set the tablecloth down. “Do you?”

He came over and took one corner. “I know you’re trying to keep the room simple.”

“Is that what you think I’m doing?”

“I think you like knowing where everything goes.”

“That’s not the same as needing it all to happen my way.”

“No.” He smoothed the cloth over the table. “It isn’t.”

Annabeth pressed the opposite corner flat, and Percy let her set the edge before he moved the centerpiece. It was a plain bowl with a handful of pinecones and late red leaves. No one had put names or symbols on them.

The door opened behind them. Ianthe entered with a stack of counted bowls balanced against her hip.

“I asked Chiron,” she said. “There are exactly seven bowls in the kitchen cupboard.”

“Good,” Percy said. “We have seven people.”

“Unless someone wants seconds.”

“Seconds don’t need another bowl,” Annabeth said.

“I know. I brought two extra.”

Ianthe lifted the stack onto the table. One bowl rattled against the next. She caught the upper one before it slipped.

“We can put those near the serving table,” she said. “Then people won’t have to wait.”

“You can place them where you want,” Annabeth said. “We’re not assigning seats yet.”

Ianthe’s expression shifted. “I asked where the extra bowls should go.”

“Then put them near the serving table.”

“I was telling you the reason.”

“Okay.”

Ianthe placed the stack on the sideboard. She measured the distance between them and the wall with the edge of her hand.

Percy carried the cracked chair back to the hallway. “This one stays out.”

“Did Beckendorf say that?” Ianthe asked.

“No, I did.”

“Why?”

“Because the back moves.”

“Then we should have him inspect it.”

“We can ask after dinner.”

“If it’s broken, we should not put it in the room.”

“It’s already out of the room.”

“Then it is not a problem.”

“It could be for someone else.”

Ianthe looked at the chair. “I can put a note on it.”

“You can,” Percy said. “Do you want to?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then ask Beckendorf later.”

She frowned. “You’re all very fond of leaving things until later.”

“Only when they can wait.”

“Some things get forgotten.”

“Then write a note.”

“I have a note.”

“Good.”

She glanced at Annabeth. “You said there would be no seating order.”

“There won’t be.”

“Then we don’t know who gets the places near the wall.”

“Anyone can choose.”

“What if someone chooses the same place?”

“They can ask.”

“That seems inefficient.”

“It’s dinner.”

Ianthe picked up one of the bowls and set it on the table. “I don’t think disorganized people are stupid.”

Annabeth looked at her. “I didn’t say you did.”

“You thought it.”

“I thought the seating order might make people feel managed.”

“That is different.”

“Yes.”

“I don’t want people to think I assume they can’t choose a chair.”

“Then leave the chairs open.”

“I have.”

Ianthe pushed the bowl into place. “I’m not trying to control every part of this.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I know you’re trying not to.”

The answer seemed to bother Ianthe. “That is not the same as believing me.”

“No,” Annabeth said. “It isn’t.”

Ianthe glanced down at the bowl. “I don’t like that.”

“I know.”

Percy moved the spare chair next to the wall. “This one can be used if Beckendorf says it’s safe.”

“I thought it was broken,” Ianthe said.

“It might not be. I’m asking.”

“Then leave it in the hall until he answers.”

“I’ll leave it in the hall.”

“Thank you.”

Leandros arrived with two wide serving bowls and a small water pitcher. He carried the heavier bowl in one hand and the pitcher in the other.

“Where do you want these?” he asked.

“Serving table,” Ianthe said.

“Which one?”

“The sideboard.”

“I was going to put the bowls on the table.”

“Then they’d be in the way.”

“They’re for serving.”

“People need room to set their own dishes down.”

“The sideboard is too narrow.”

Annabeth looked at the room. The sideboard had enough space for two bowls and the pitcher if the cups were moved. She said so.

Leandros looked at her. “I already carried them in.”

“You can set them there.”

Ianthe reached for the pitcher. “I can move the cups.”

“I’ve got it,” Leandros said.

“You don’t need to carry everything because it’s heavy.”

“I didn’t say I did.”

“You picked the biggest bowl.”

“It was the first one I saw.”

“You could have asked which one we needed.”

“I asked where they went.”

“After you brought them.”

He drew back, the pitcher still in his hand. “Do you want me to put it down?”

“Yes,” Ianthe said.

“Then say that.”

“I did.”

“After you told me what I shouldn’t carry.”

Annabeth saw Ianthe’s chin rise. “I was trying to keep the table clear.”

“I was trying to help.”

“You were helping in the wrong direction.”

“Then show me the right one.”

Ianthe looked as though she wanted to answer with the whole arrangement, not the task. She took a breath. “Put the pitcher on the sideboard. I’ll move two cups.”

Leandros set it down. “All right.”

“You can still carry the serving bowl.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

He gave her an annoyed look and moved the bowl. Ianthe moved the cups. The sideboard worked.

“See?” she said.

“I saw.”

“Then there was no reason to argue.”

“You told me I was carrying the wrong thing.”

“You were.”

“I asked what needed doing.”

“After you had decided.”

“I didn’t decide. I picked up a bowl.”

“That is deciding.”

“It is picking up a bowl.”

Their voices were carrying into the hall. Annabeth put the linen napkins beside the bowls. “Both of you can stop.”

They did not stop at once.

Leandros set the bowl down a little too hard. Ianthe moved one of the napkins away from the edge.

Annabeth kept her hands on the table. “We can arrange dinner without making every choice a test of who gets to lead.”

“I wasn’t leading,” Leandros said.

“You were,” Ianthe replied.

“You were too.”

“Don’t answer for her,” Annabeth said to him.

He looked at Annabeth. “I wasn’t.”

“You were explaining what she was doing instead of asking.”

Ianthe’s shoulders lowered a fraction. “I was trying to keep the room from becoming chaotic.”

“Why?” Percy asked.

“Because then people don’t know what to do.”

“Then they can ask.”

“Not everyone asks.”

“That doesn’t mean you should answer for them.”

“I know.”

The sentence was quick, but it was not enough to erase the earlier tone. Ianthe rearranged one cup, then stopped herself from straightening the next.

“Where do you want these?” she asked Annabeth.

“Leave them near the pitcher. People can pick them up.”

“All right.”

Leandros turned toward the corridor. “I’ll get the bread basket.”

“Do you want to?” Ianthe asked.

“I said I would.”

“Okay.”

He left, and Ianthe watched him go.

“Do you want to ask him to wait until we know where it goes?” Annabeth asked.

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because he’s bringing bread.”

“You can still ask.”

“I don’t want to.”

“That’s fine.”

Ianthe leaned a hand on the sideboard. “I know when I’m being difficult.”

“You know some of the time.”

“That was blunt.”

“You asked.”

“I didn’t ask for bluntness.”

“Then I should have asked first.”

Ianthe made a small sound that might have been a laugh. “That’s not necessary.”

“It could be.”

“No. You can tell me when I’m being difficult.”

“I can.”

“Just not in front of him.”

Annabeth nodded. “I can ask next time.”

“Good.”

The table stood ready for bowls, cups, and seven chairs. The door to the side room remained open. Nobody had put a chair near it for an absent parent.

Leandros returned with the bread basket tucked under his arm. He placed it on the sideboard and looked around the room.

“Is this all of us?” he asked.

“Seven,” Percy said.

“Does that mean no one else can come?”

“It means we asked seven people.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No,” Percy said. “It isn’t.”

Leandros took the water pitcher and moved it two inches away from the bowls. “If Poseidon wanted to come, would we make room?”

Clarisse answered from the doorway. “He hasn’t been invited.”

“And if he were?”

“This meal is for the seven of us.”

“You get to decide that?”

“We all do.”

Ianthe set the folded schedule on the counter. “Should Zeus be asked too?”

Thalia entered behind Clarisse. “Did you want him here?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then we can ask ourselves before we ask him.”

“I don’t want him to think I’m rejecting him.”

“Are you?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then you don’t have to decide what it means tonight.”

Leandros looked between them. “If we’re having a family meal, why not have the family?”

“Because there are seven of us who have agreed to this meal,” Clarisse said. “That’s enough.”

“It might be strange to leave them out.”

“They aren’t being punished,” Thalia said. “We’re not arranging a meeting with them.”

“I wasn’t trying to arrange one,” Ianthe replied.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Thalia set her book on the spare chair. “I’m not asking you to prove why you want him there. I’m saying we can have one meal without deciding what the next one has to be.”

Ianthe looked at the table. Seven bowls sat in a row; seven chairs had been set around it. The eighth chair remained in the hall.

“Fine,” she said. “We don’t invite them tonight.”

“No one is going to send them a message about it,” Percy added. “No one is a messenger.”

Leandros looked at Percy. “I wasn’t asking you to message him.”

“I know. I’m saying it because I’m not doing it.”

“All right.”

Clarisse dragged one chair away from the end and set it on the left side. “This spot is better.”

“Why?” Ianthe asked.

“Because the door swings wide.”

“That’s true.”

“Do you want to move the bowls?” Annabeth asked.

“I can,” Clarisse said.

“No,” Ianthe replied. “I’ll move them.”

Clarisse looked at her. “Do you want to?”

“Yes.”

“Then do it.”

Ianthe moved the bowls to the center of the sideboard, leaving enough room for the pitcher and bread. It was a useful adjustment. No one praised her for it. She did not ask them to.

---

Chiron sent the stew over with two campers from the kitchen. The larger pot had a cloth wrapped around its handles. One camper set it on the table and asked whether they needed bread.

“We have bread,” Silena said.

“Chiron said there’s a tray if you do.”

“Can we decide after we eat?”

“Sure.”

The campers left the room with the empty tray. Silena carried her basket to the table and set it near the middle, where anyone could reach it.

“I brought the rosemary flat loaf,” she said. “It’s for the meal. I’m not serving it.”

“Got it,” Percy said.

“Take a piece if you want one.”

She sat beside Clarisse. Clarisse chose the chair nearest the door. Thalia picked a seat on the other side of her and set her book under the table. Ianthe chose the place with her back to the wall, then looked at the open doorway.

“Do you want to sit somewhere else?” Annabeth asked.

“Why?”

“You look like you’re still deciding.”

“I am.”

“Then you can.”

Ianthe stood and moved one chair closer to the table’s end. She left the doorway clear.

Leandros carried the pot to the center and started to set it down. Percy took one handle.

“Which side?” Leandros asked.

“Here’s fine.”

“Wait. If it’s there, the ladle has to cross over the bread.”

“Then I’ll move the bread basket,” Percy said.

“Silena put it there.”

“I know.”

“Ask her.”

Percy looked at Silena. “Do you want the bread basket moved?”

“No.”

“All right.”

Leandros shifted the pot six inches in the other direction. “This should work.”

“It will,” Annabeth said.

The serving spoon rested across the rim. Percy started to reach for it, then stopped.

“Do you want to serve yourself first?” he asked Annabeth.

“Yes.”

He handed it to her. She filled her bowl, set the spoon down and passed it to Thalia. No one kept track of who had been served before whom.

The stew was warm and heavy with winter vegetables. Chiron had added a little pepper, but not enough to make it hot. The bread had a shallow gold crust and an uneven edge where the dough had risen more on one side than the other.

Silena tore it into pieces and left the basket in the middle. No one asked her to slice it for them.

Ianthe looked at the stew. “Is there a list of what’s in it?”

“No,” Percy said.

“Does Chiron know?”

“He made it.”

“I know that. I mean, did he write down the ingredients?”

“Why?”

“Because people have allergies.”

“Everyone here knows what they can eat,” Clarisse said.

“I don’t know what Leandros can eat.”

“I can eat this,” Leandros said.

“Do you have any allergies?”

“Not that I know of.”

“Then we don’t need a list.”

“It would still be helpful.”

“Maybe next time,” Annabeth said. “Tonight we can ask Chiron if anyone needs to know.”

Ianthe glanced at her. “That’s not very efficient.”

“It is the answer we have.”

She opened her mouth, closed it, and took a spoonful. “The carrots are soft.”

“Is that a complaint?” Thalia asked.

“It’s an observation.”

“Is it useful?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“Then maybe save it for the next list.”

Ianthe frowned, but there was a quick flicker in her face that might have been amusement. “You’re both very comfortable not knowing.”

“Not always,” Thalia said.

“I know.”

They ate for a few minutes without speaking. The meal did not become easier because the room was quiet. It became easier because nobody pointed to the quiet and asked who was supposed to fill it.

Percy reached for the pepper. Annabeth caught the shaker first and handed it to him.

“Do you want some?” she asked.

“I do now.”

“You can decide before it reaches the bowl.”

“I’m deciding.”

“You usually decide after you shake it.”

“That is still deciding.”

She tipped the shaker into his open palm. He considered the amount and added less than usual.

“See?” he said.

“I saw.”

“Was that a compliment?”

“No.”

“Good.”

She leaned her shoulder against his. He leaned back, just enough to touch her. She did not move away.

At the other end, Leandros was studying the spoon. “The handle is bent.”

“Then don’t use it to lever open the bread basket,” Clarisse said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

“You were looking at it.”

“I can look at a spoon without breaking it.”

“Good.”

“Why do you keep saying good?”

“Because I’m glad you’re not breaking it.”

“I don’t break everything I touch.”

“No one said you did.”

He put the spoon down. “You say things like that as if you expect me to prove I’m safe to have around.”

Clarisse looked at him for a moment. “I expect you not to break things.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No.”

He took another spoonful. “I didn’t ask for this meal so you could all watch me.”

“Nobody said you did.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“Not always.”

The admission did not end the conversation. Leandros passed the pepper toward Silena. She took it, shook a little into her bowl, and gave it to Ianthe. The shaker went around the table.

“What do you want to do when you get the letter?” Annabeth asked him.

Leandros looked up. “What letter?”

“The one Poseidon said he might send.”

“He didn’t say he might send one. He said he would ask Chiron where to send it.”

“That’s what I mean.”

“I don’t know yet.”

“Do you want him to write about your readings?”

“I want him to write because he wants to.”

“Could he ask about the readings too?”

“Maybe.”

“What if he doesn’t?”

Leandros stopped with his bowl halfway to his mouth. “Then I’ll answer whatever he wrote.”

“Would you ask him about the compass?”

“I know how it works.”

“I meant about the times he visited you.”

Leandros set down the bowl. “I remember some of it.”

“I didn’t mean to ask you for the details.”

“I know.”

Annabeth nodded. She had not asked him to share them. She had asked what he wanted. Leandros looked toward the tablecloth and then back up.

“I might ask whether he remembers what my mother told him,” he said. “I don’t know if I want to know the answer.”

“You don’t have to decide now,” Percy said.

“I wasn’t asking you to decide.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Percy put his spoon down. “No. I was trying to say you don’t owe us an answer.”

“I know.”

“Okay.”

“Could we talk about something else?”

“Sure,” Percy said.

Leandros pressed his fingers against the rim of the bowl. “What’s the winter meal called?”

“Nothing,” Clarisse said.

“That’s not true. Everything has a name.”

“It’s dinner.”

“It’s the seasonal meal.”

“Chiron called it dinner.”

“Chiron calls everything dinner.”

“I don’t.”

The answer came from Thalia, who had so far spoken little. She turned her page in the book under the table. “The kitchen calls it the first winter stew.”

Ianthe looked toward the door. “Is it the first winter stew?”

“It’s the first stew with the winter storage roots.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“Then we can call it that.”

“I don’t care what it’s called.”

“You asked.”

“I was curious.”

“Then your curiosity has an answer.”

Ianthe considered the phrasing. “That sounded like Annabeth.”

“I’m sitting right here,” Annabeth said.

“I know.”

The meal loosened around the joke. It was not all laughter. It did not need to be.

Silena reached for another piece of bread. “The rosemary came from the greenhouse. The leaves were smaller than the last batch.”

“Did it change the flavor?” Percy asked.

“A little. I used less because they were more concentrated.”

“Can you tell?”

“I can.”

“Can you teach me?”

“I can, if I decide I want to.”

“Would you?”

“Maybe another day.”

“Okay.”

Percy passed the basket to Clarisse. She took a piece and held it for a second before she bit into it.

“It’s good,” she said.

Silena’s eyebrows rose. “That’s all?”

“That’s the review.”

“You could add that you like it.”

“I did.”

“It was implied.”

“I don’t imply things.”

“Sometimes.”

“I said it was good.”

Silena smiled. “I heard you.”

Clarisse set the bread down beside her bowl. There was no need to make the comment warmer or turn it into a statement about the sisters’ bond. The bread was hers to bake and share, and Clarisse was free to take a second piece or not.

Leandros picked up the basket. “Should I pass this to the other end?”

“Ask,” Ianthe said.

He paused. “Would anyone like more bread?”

Thalia reached for the basket. “Yes.”

Ianthe shook her head. Annabeth took a small piece. Percy declined and then accepted half of Annabeth’s when she broke it.

Leandros held the basket out to Clarisse.

“I’m fine,” she said.

He passed it to Silena without comment. The basket made its way around the table and returned to the middle.

The seven bowls were not empty yet. The meal had not reached the part where anyone could stand up without asking whether everyone was finished. The room stayed warm. Outside the window, the dark gathered around the pavilion.

No one had set a place for either god. No one had made their absence the subject of the meal.

Annabeth looked down at the table. There were crumbs near the bread basket, a little stew spilled beside Ianthe’s bowl, and the pepper shaker had made a pale mark on the cloth.

“Do you want to save that cloth?” Percy asked.

“Yes.”

“Then I’ll move the bowls.”

“After we’re done.”

“Okay.”

Ianthe looked at the spill. “It should be wiped before it dries.”

“Do you want to wipe it?” Clarisse asked.

“I can.”

“Do you want to?”

Ianthe studied the spot. “I don’t know.”

“Then leave it.”

“But it will stain.”

“Probably.”

“That seems unnecessary.”

“It’s one spot.”

“I can wipe it.”

“You can.”

Ianthe took the napkin beside her bowl and dabbed the edge of the spill, then stopped when the cloth began to pull color from the tablecloth.

“That’s enough,” Annabeth said.

“I know.”

She put the napkin down. The stain remained, smaller but visible. Ianthe’s mouth tightened.

“Do you want to finish after the meal?” Annabeth asked.

“I don’t know if it will come out.”

“We can ask Chiron.”

“Fine.”

She picked up her bowl and continued eating. No one told her she had made it worse. No one praised her for trying. The spot stayed on the cloth while the meal went on.

The stew had cooled enough that the steam no longer clouded the windows. Annabeth could see the winter field through the glass, a pale strip beyond the pavilion, where the grass bent toward the shore in the wind. Inside, spoons tapped bowls at uneven intervals. Nobody had found a rhythm, and the room did not need one.

Percy broke a piece from the heel of the loaf. He left the rest near Silena, who had set it beside her own bowl.

“Is there another one?” Leandros asked.

“There’s half a loaf,” Silena said.

“I thought there were two.”

“One has the stew.”

“Oh.” Leandros looked at the piece in his hand. “I can give it back.”

“You can eat it.”

He glanced at her, then at the loaf. “Okay.”

Ianthe examined her bowl. “It would help if the serving amounts were decided before everyone sat down.”

“Why?” Clarisse asked.

“So no one gets less because they waited.”

“No one waited.”

“You did.”

“I was talking.”

“That is what I mean.”

Clarisse leaned one elbow on the table. “You think I missed dinner because I was talking?”

“I think people shouldn’t have to ask for what ought to be set out.”

“Then they can ask.”

“That makes the person asking responsible for noticing the shortage.”

“Someone has to notice.”

“Someone can notice before we begin.”

Ianthe spoke with the certainty she used when she had counted a stack and found a number that came out even. Annabeth understood the impulse. A plan could keep the room from tipping into confusion. But there were seven bowls, a pot of stew, bread, and people who could ask.

Percy dipped the last of his bread into his bowl. “There’s enough for seconds.”

“That is not the same as making sure everyone gets one,” Ianthe said.

“You want me to serve everyone in order?” he asked.

“I want the food shared fairly.”

“Those aren’t the same.”

“No,” Annabeth said. “We could pass the pot.”

Ianthe looked at her. “That is what I suggested.”

“You said you wanted the servings decided.”

“Which is what passing the pot does.”

Clarisse pushed her bowl away from the damp patch. “I don’t need you to measure mine.”

“I didn’t say I would measure yours.”

“You said you needed a system.”

“For the table.”

“Then make one for yourself.”

Ianthe’s hand stopped near the pitcher. She moved it back to her lap. “You’re treating this like a challenge.”

“I’m eating.”

The rebuttal Annabeth had prepared dissolved. She had started to make a plan for their argument before either of them had asked her to.

“I can pass the pot,” Percy said, already reaching for it. “Anyone who wants more can take more.”

Clarisse looked at Ianthe. “That’s fine with me.”

Ianthe’s mouth tightened. “Fine.”

Percy set the pot in the middle. No one complimented the arrangement or called the problem solved. Annabeth passed the bread. It made one full circle and returned with a small piece left.

Leandros asked whether the stew had barley. Chiron had left an ingredient card beside the pot, and Percy read it aloud: barley, leek, beans, cider.

“Cider makes it sweet,” Leandros said.

“Not much,” Percy replied.

“I can taste it.”

“You can taste everything.”

“That’s not a bad thing.”

“It is when you treat every ingredient like evidence.”

“Food is evidence of what someone thought would work.”

“Or of what they had in the kitchen,” Clarisse said.

“Both can be true.”

Ianthe turned the card around. “There is pepper, too. That’s why it smells different from the pavilion stew.”

“The pavilion uses more salt,” Clarisse said.

“Is that a rule?”

“No. It’s a kitchen.”

“I thought recipes were written down.”

“Some are.”

“Then how do you know what changes?”

“You taste it.”

“That seems unreliable.”

Silena dipped another piece of bread into her bowl. “If someone measures my loaf, I’m going to make a second one with too much rosemary just to see what happens.”

“You could make it with less salt,” Leandros said.

“I could.”

“Would you?”

“No.”

He shook his head. “You’re difficult to advise.”

“I’m not asking.”

Ianthe picked up the pencil beside the ingredient card and started a list on the blank side.

“What are you writing?” Clarisse asked.

“Things people like.”

“Did we agree you could keep a list?”

“It’s only a list.”

“Lists get passed around.”

“I wasn’t going to show it to anyone.”

“You might.”

Ianthe set down the pencil. “Fine.” She did not tear up the card or hide it. The three words remained there, a half-started record with no name at the top.

The wind gave the window a light shake. Thalia’s attention shifted toward the glass, but she stayed in her chair. Her cane leaned against the wall nearby, within reach. No one asked about her heel.

“Does it always get louder before rain?” Ianthe asked.

“Sometimes,” Leandros said.

“Sometimes isn’t useful.”

“It’s accurate.”

Ianthe gave him a look. “You make guesses all the time.”

“I say when I’m guessing.”

“You didn’t yesterday.”

“I did.”

“I’m not saying the rack notes are verified,” Ianthe said. “I know they still need checking.”

“Then don’t talk as if the same answer applies to the whole weather.”

“I didn’t. I said I want to know before it rains.”

“That isn’t a reading.”

“It’s a preference.”

Leandros leaned back. “All right.”

“I can want a result without saying I have one.”

“You can.”

They had not agreed about yesterday’s estimate, but the distinction stayed between them without becoming another argument.

Leandros looked from Ianthe to Thalia. “You’ve been looking this way.”

“I was looking at the window,” Ianthe said.

“From that angle?” Clarisse asked.

“I can ask without everyone making it sound like a trick.”

“Then ask,” Thalia said.

Ianthe turned. “Would you talk to me about Zeus sometime?”

Thalia considered her. Annabeth stayed quiet; she would not answer for her sister.

“I might,” Thalia said. “It depends what you want to ask.”

“I don’t know yet.”

“Then you don’t have to ask now.”

“I’m not asking you to tell me everything.”

“I didn’t think you were. You can ask me directly. Don’t make Annabeth carry the question.”

Ianthe glanced at Annabeth. “I wouldn’t.”

“I know you can ask me directly,” Thalia said. “I’m telling you I want it that way.”

“I can do that.”

Thalia nodded once and returned to her bowl. She did not open a story about Zeus, and Ianthe did not press.

Annabeth leaned closer to Percy. “You have pepper on your sleeve.”

“I know.”

“Did you notice before I told you?”

“No.”

She brushed it away with her thumb. His hand found the back of hers on the table and stayed there, light enough that she could move whenever she wanted. She turned her palm under his. The warmth was familiar without being a performance for the room.

“Your bowl’s empty,” he murmured.

“So is yours.”

“Want more?”

“Half a ladle.”

He waited while she passed the pot, then added less than she’d asked and caught her look. “Half a ladle. I’m learning.”

“Slowly.”

“Very slowly.”

She pressed her shoulder against his for a second. Their closeness needed no comment. The conversation at the other end of the table continued.

Clarisse pushed her bowl back. “I’m done.”

“More bread?” Silena asked.

“No.”

Silena took the loaf without insisting. She wrapped what remained in the towel she had brought from the kitchen. It was not serving; it was keeping the bread from drying out.

Leandros picked up his bowl. “Chiron said the kitchen needs it back. I can carry the pot, too.”

“It’s full,” Clarisse said.

“I know.”

“Then don’t lift it by one handle.”

“I know how to carry a pot.”

“I didn’t say you didn’t.”

“You said it like you did.”

Clarisse set her bowl down. “If you want me to stop giving you directions, say that.”

“I want you to ask before you decide I need them.”

“All right.”

He carried his bowl to the tray and set both hands beneath the pot’s handles.

“Chiron asked for the bowls,” Percy said. “The pot can wait for him.”

“I offered.”

“I heard you.”

“Then why answer?”

“Because you keep acting like each offer has to prove something.”

Leandros stared at him. “You don’t know what I’m proving.”

“No,” Percy said. “I don’t.”

He lifted his own bowl onto the tray. The pot remained in the middle of the table, still warm, while the meal continued around it.

---

When the last bowls were scraped clean, the paper lanterns were still folded in their basket by the door.

Clarisse stood and stretched her fingers. “I can tie these.”

“Do you want to?” Annabeth asked.

“Yes. I brought the cord.”

“Then you choose how many.”

“Six.”

“There are seven of us,” Ianthe said.

“Six is enough.”

“It would be one each.”

“Did anyone say they wanted one?”

Ianthe glanced around the table. No one answered. She picked up a paper form. “Can I make one?”

“Sure,” Clarisse said.

“Do you want a specific color?” Annabeth asked.

“Blue.”

“There are two blues.”

“The darker one.”

“Take it.”

Ianthe set the paper down and examined the diagram on the basket. “The numbering is wrong.”

“Which part?” Percy asked.

“Step three is shown before step two.”

“It might be a printing mistake.”

“I can fix it.”

Clarisse reached for a folded sheet. “We can follow the creases.”

“That isn’t the point.”

“What is?”

“People shouldn’t have to figure out the instructions are wrong.”

“Maybe the person who made them thought they’d help.”

“If they’re wrong, they don’t.”

Annabeth watched Ianthe press a crease into the diagram with her thumbnail. The correction was small; the force was not.

“Not everything wrong needs to be fixed by you,” Clarisse said.

“I didn’t say it did.”

“You picked it up.”

“Because I thought it would be useful.”

“You always think that.”

“I do not.”

Leandros stacked the empty bowls on the tray. “You do.”

Ianthe turned toward him. “Why are you answering for her?”

“I’m not. I’ve heard you tell people their notes are useless because they weren’t formatted your way.”

“They were wrong.”

“Some were unfinished.”

“Unfinished work can be worse than wrong work.”

“Maybe if someone is using it.”

“People use bad notes.”

“People also use things you haven’t checked.”

Ianthe smoothed the diagram against the table. “You think I should leave mistakes alone.”

“No. I think you should stop acting like every mistake is an emergency.”

“That’s easy for you to say.”

“Why?”

“Because nobody expects you to have the answer.”

Leandros’s face changed. Annabeth saw the blow land before he looked away.

Clarisse straightened. “That was a cheap shot.”

“I didn’t mean—”

“You meant enough to say it.”

“I was talking about expectations.”

“You were talking about him.”

Leandros placed the last bowl on the tray. “It’s fine.”

“No,” Ianthe said. “It isn’t. I was trying to say I’m the one people expect to be organized.”

“Then say that.”

“You don’t know what it’s like to have people decide you’re difficult before you finish a sentence.”

Clarisse’s expression flattened. “You think that hasn’t happened to me?”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You meant I get to do whatever I want because I can make people back up.”

“I didn’t say that either.”

“What did you say?”

“I said people expect me to have the answer.”

“Then I heard you. You also said nobody expects Leandros to have one.”

Ianthe looked at him. “I was wrong to say that.”

Leandros kept his eyes on the tray. “Okay.”

“I’m sorry.”

“I heard you.”

It was not forgiveness. Annabeth did not try to turn it into more. Ianthe had named the sentence, and Leandros had accepted that it had been said. Whatever happened next would depend on what they did.

“Are we still making lanterns?” Percy asked.

Clarisse looked at the basket. “If people want to.”

“I want to make one,” Percy said.

“Then make one.”

Ianthe lifted the diagram from the table. “I’m still going to correct this.”

“Ask first,” Clarisse said.

“It’s the craft sheet. Who am I asking?”

“Whoever owns it.”

“It’s in the basket.”

“That doesn’t mean you own it.”

“It doesn’t have a name.”

“Then it’s Chiron’s.”

Ianthe looked at Annabeth. “Is it?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then we should ask him.”

“We can,” Percy said. “Or we can make the lanterns from the creases and tell him we noticed the diagram.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No,” Annabeth said. “It isn’t. Do you want to wait for him?”

Ianthe glanced toward the door. “No.”

“Then you can leave it as it is.”

She returned the diagram to the basket, placing it on top rather than under the forms. She picked up the dark blue paper. “I’ll make one.”

Clarisse pulled the basket closer. “We can fold around the table.”

“I can hand out the strings,” Ianthe said.

“Do you want to?” Clarisse asked.

Ianthe paused. “I want to.”

“Then go ahead.”

She set the spool beside the forms and cut seven pieces before anyone could stop her.

“I asked for six,” Clarisse said.

“I thought Percy was making one.”

“He is,” Percy said. “I’ll use the seventh.”

Clarisse did not answer. She took her cord and tied a loop on the first lantern.

Leandros chose pale green paper and studied the diagram. “This step is backwards.”

“Is it?” Annabeth asked.

“You open the bottom before you fold the sides. Otherwise it spreads.”

“Do you want to try it that way?”

“I can try both.”

Ianthe glanced at him. “Mark the fold on scrap first.”

He looked up. “I can make a mistake.”

“I didn’t say you couldn’t.”

“You told me not to draw on the paper.”

“Because you were holding the finished sheet.”

“I was going to use scrap.”

“Then use scrap.”

He held the paper without moving. Ianthe’s mouth tightened, then she looked back at her own folds. “I was trying to help.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I heard you.”

He took a scrap piece from the basket. They did not resolve whether the warning had been necessary. He tested the fold once and then returned to the green sheet. The bottom spread as he predicted, but the lantern held its shape when he tucked the edge. He left the crease slightly crooked.

“It works,” Annabeth said.

“It’s crooked.”

“Do you want to redo it?”

“No.”

“Then it can stay.”

He nodded and went on to the next fold. Ianthe did not correct the angle.

The work became quieter, though no one had agreed to quiet. Silena wrapped the leftover loaf and put it in her bag. She did not take the bowls or assign anyone else a job. Clarisse tied six lanterns, one at a time. She did not decide who got which. The finished shapes rested in a row, some blue, one green, one yellow, two white and Percy’s uneven one.

Thalia stayed seated near the end of the table, folding a yellow lantern without rushing. Her cane leaned against the wall within reach. When Ianthe reached across to move the spool closer, she paused.

“Can I put this here?” Ianthe asked.

“Where?”

“Next to you.”

“Sure.”

Ianthe set it down. Thalia took a length of string when she was ready.

“Want help with the knot?” Ianthe asked.

“I can do it.”

“All right.”

Thalia made the loop, threaded the end through, and pulled. The first knot slipped open. She tried again, slower, then held up the lantern. It stayed. Ianthe returned to her paper without asking again.

Annabeth finished her folds and found the top opening had collapsed. She turned the lantern over. “I did this backwards.”

“You can start over,” Ianthe said.

“I know.”

“Do you want another sheet?”

“I might.”

Ianthe looked through the stack. “The pale one is free. I don’t want that color.”

“Then I’ll take it.”

Annabeth started again. Percy finished his lantern and set it beside hers. His was lopsided, with one edge sharply folded and the other soft. He turned it so the crease faced the wall.

“Why are you hiding the good side?” she asked.

“I’m protecting the audience.”

“The audience is us.”

“Exactly.”

She nudged his elbow. He caught her hand briefly, thumb brushing over her knuckle, then let her return to the paper.

Ianthe’s dark blue lantern was the most exact. Its seams met cleanly and the bottom held level. She set it at the end of the row, then moved it to make more room.

“Not a line?” she asked.

“No,” Clarisse said. “Just together.”

Ianthe left it where it was.

The last knot on her lantern tightened unevenly. She pulled once, then stopped herself from pulling again.

“Do you want help?” Leandros asked.

“No.”

“All right.”

She loosened the knot with her thumbnail, cut a new piece of string, and tied it more loosely. The paper kept a shallow crease at the corner. She examined it, then placed the lantern with the others.

The row was not sorted by color or size or who had made each one. Clarisse had set them where there was space. Ianthe considered the arrangement.

“That works,” she said.

“It does,” Clarisse answered.

No one called it a beginning or made a plan for another meal. The lanterns would go up in the pavilion if Chiron approved them, or stay in the basket if he did not. The corrected diagram remained on top of the stack. The bowls waited on the tray for the kitchen. The stain on the cloth was still there, a rust-colored mark that had spread no farther.

Percy lifted the tray. “I’ll take these.”

“Both handles,” Clarisse said.

“Yes.”

Leandros started to reach for the pot, then drew his hands back. “I’ll leave it for Chiron.”

“Okay,” Clarisse said.

Silena stood with the wrapped loaf in her bag. “I’m going back to the cabin.”

“Want company?” Annabeth asked.

“No, thanks.”

“Okay.”

Silena left without anyone asking her to stay or carry a message. Thalia waited until the others moved away from the chairs, then reached for her cane. She used it for the shallow step out of the room and went at her own pace. Ianthe watched but did not follow. Clarisse went after her a minute later, carrying no lanterns and saying nothing about where she was headed.

Leandros remained near the tray. “I’m still going to wait for the letter.”

“Okay,” Annabeth said.

“I know it might not come.”

“Okay.”

“I don’t want everyone asking whether I’m disappointed.”

“I can tell them that.”

He looked at her. “I didn’t ask you to.”

“You’re right.”

Some of the tension left his face, but not all. “I’ll tell them if I want them to know.”

“Good.”

Ianthe had gathered the unused paper sheets. “You said I could tell you what I meant.”

“I did.”

“You didn’t ask what I wanted before you answered.”

“I didn’t. I’m sorry.”

“I wanted someone to say the instructions were wrong.”

“They were.”

“You could have said that.”

“I thought Percy had.”

“Percy said it might be a printing mistake.”

“It might be.”

“Those aren’t the same.”

“No,” Annabeth said. “They aren’t.”

Ianthe ran a thumb along the paper stack. “I’ll ask Chiron what he wants done with it.”

“Okay.”

“Not tonight.”

“Fine.”

She put the papers beside the lanterns. The room looked less like a dining room now that the bowls were gone. The chairs sat at different angles, and the pale mark from the spill had darkened at its center.

“Do you want to stay?” Annabeth asked.

“I don’t know.”

“You can leave when you want.”

“I know.”

Annabeth waited. She would not turn the pause into another question.

Ianthe tucked the diagram under the basket lid. “I’m going to the Big House.”

“Want me to walk with you?”

“No.”

“All right.”

Ianthe carried the diagram with her as she left. Percy set the tray down inside the doorway so Chiron could take it when he returned, then came back to Annabeth and pressed his shoulder to hers.

“You did okay,” he said.

“So did you.”

“I almost told Leandros what he was feeling.”

“I know.”

“I wanted to.”

“I know.”

He rested his forehead against hers for a moment. She closed her eyes. Their relationship had its own history and ordinary present; it did not need to solve the other siblings' choices. When he pulled back, she smiled.

“We should go,” she said.

“Do you want to take one?”

“Clarisse tied six.”

“You can make seven and still tie six.”

Annabeth looked at the row. “Let’s leave them in the basket until Chiron approves them.”

“Okay.”

They walked out together. Behind them, the chairs remained where their owners had left them: some tucked close to the table, some turned at an angle, one far enough back to leave space. The room did not hold an answer about what any of them would say next time. It held the bowls, the stained cloth, and seven paper lanterns that leaned at different heights.

Annabeth let Percy take her hand as they crossed the pavilion. He squeezed once, then loosened his grip. She kept her fingers linked with his.

The season was changing. The meal had happened. Those facts could stand without promising anything else.
