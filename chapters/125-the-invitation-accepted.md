## Chapter 125: The Invitation Accepted

On Friday morning, Percy packed as if the sea might ask to see his homework. He folded his spare clothes twice, checked the list Annabeth had made, and made sure he had not packed three knives.

“That was one time,” he said.

“It was three knives,” Annabeth replied from the doorway.

“Exactly. One time.”

Her own satchel over one shoulder, Annabeth came in. A small green charm rested at her throat, the sea-glass one Amphitrite had sent before their first visit. “Did you pack the map we made?”

“Silena has it.”

“Did you pack a shirt that isn’t the one you’re wearing?”

“Yes.”

“A second one?”

“I’m going underwater, not moving to another planet.”

“Atlantis is a city.” She waited until he added another shirt, then nodded as if a critical flaw had been corrected.

Outside Cabin Three, Silena guarded the camp’s gift: a sealed jar of strawberry jam and a hand-drawn map. The pavilion and garden were green and gold, the lake a blue crescent, the cabins clustered beneath the pines. Percy had drawn the channels beneath the lake; Clarisse’s arena was labeled *training ground (watch your toes)*.

“The laundry line is missing,” Percy said.

“It didn’t fit,” Silena replied. “I put a small hazard symbol by the path instead.”

Down the cabin steps came Clarisse with her spear strapped across her back and a sword at her hip, salt and oil sharp on the leather. A dark-blue tournament ribbon was tied around the handle of her travel bag. She had packed one knife where it belonged and another where it did not, then agreed to tell Triton about the second one before they reached the palace’s protected rooms.

“You’re carrying the jam?” she asked.

“I’m carrying the gift,” Silena said.

“The jam is the gift.”

“It’s the part you can eat.”

“That’s what makes it a good gift.”

Chiron waited near the camp gate. He had checked the route with Triton at dawn but was staying behind to welcome a group of younger campers.

Thalia had taken the weekend watch in his place. “If the palace gives you a ceremonial job, remember you can resign.”

“What kind of job?” Silena asked.

“Harbor inspector. Keeper of the spoons. Official person who tells the biting chair it’s being rude.”

“I’d take that last one,” Clarisse said.

Annabeth adjusted the strap of her satchel. Thalia’s expression softened. “I wish I could come for the whole weekend, but I’ve promised to cover the watch. Camp needs that more than you need me looking over your shoulder.”

“You mean you’ll survive a weekend without looking over mine,” Annabeth said.

“I can do both.” Thalia touched Annabeth’s elbow. “Send word when you arrive. And when you leave. The middle part can be all yours.”

“I’ll send word.”

Thalia gave Percy a nod, trusting him without making him responsible for every breath they took. It helped more than he wanted to admit.

Checking the buckle on her bag, then checking it again, Clarisse let her gaze move over the path, the lake, the trees, and the group gathered around them. Like a soldier reviewing a route she stood — and to Percy, sometimes the review was for the soldier, too, pine pitch and lake water filling the pause between them.

“Ready?” he asked.

“I’ve been ready since breakfast.”

“You haven’t eaten breakfast.”

“I was ready to eat it.”

Silena lifted the basket. “The jam is ready. That’s the important thing.”

“The jam can’t travel alone,” Annabeth said.

“It can if it has enough confidence,” Silena replied.

Chiron opened the gate. “The invitation is for the weekend. You have the return route and the means to call for it if you choose to leave early. I’ll be here when you come back.”

“We know,” Percy said.

“Knowing and hearing are different comforts.”

Clarisse settled her bag more firmly on her shoulder. “We’ll be back Sunday.”

“I shall expect you,” Chiron said.

No one asked Clarisse whether she was nervous. Naming the feeling might only make it feel watched.

---

Triton was waiting at the lake’s edge, where the water widened toward the coast. He had arrived without raising the whole lake this time. The water only lifted around his boots, as if it were keeping a secret for him.

“You brought the jam,” he said.

Silena held up the basket. “A gift from camp.”

“Mother will like that.”

Triton’s gaze moved over their bags, then settled on Clarisse’s spear. “Your weapons can travel with you. They’ll need to be peace-bound at the inner hall, as we discussed. The palace guard will keep them in the visible rack and return them when you leave.”

“I’m keeping the knife in my boot,” Clarisse said.

“You told me about it. That makes it concealed from the guard but not from me.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“No. It’s more honest.”

A moment studying him, then a nod. “Fine.”

Triton brought out three small charms on silver cords. Annabeth already wore her green glass charm. The new ones were a pearl disk for Silena and a dark-red shell for Clarisse, each etched with thin curling lines.

“Breathing charms,” he explained. “They’ll maintain a small air-current around your face during the passage. You can breathe underwater without them, but the current carries you more comfortably if everyone shares the same layer.”

“I don’t need a bubble,” Clarisse said.

“You don’t need it to breathe,” Triton said. “The bubble also keeps the water from pulling at your clothes and stops the descent from feeling like a long dive.”

“I know how to dive.”

“I know. This is different.”

“Then I’ll decide if I like it.” Clarisse took the charm and tied it around her wrist rather than her neck.

Silena turned the pearl disk in her fingers. “Does it glow?”

“When it’s working,” Triton said.

“That’s crystal clear.”

“I learned from Percy.”

Annabeth lifted her own charm. “It glows. And it doesn’t make you a clam.”

“Yet,” Percy said.

Triton touched the lake with the end of his trident. The water folded open in a long, sloping corridor, blue and transparent, the surface curving overhead like a second sky. Through it Percy could see the far coast, then the darker water beyond. A current rose from the opening, clear as glass, and wrapped around their legs.

Chiron and Thalia stayed at the bank. A few campers had come to the hill to watch. One waved; a younger boy held up a straw hat like a flag.

“Send word,” Thalia called.

“We will,” Annabeth answered.

Silena stepped into the current first. Her charm flashed soft pearl-white. The water rose around her and held her upright. She let out a delighted breath that turned into a string of bubbles in the air-pocket.

“I can breathe,” she announced.

“That is what it’s for,” Clarisse said, stepping in behind her.

“It’s still nice to know.”

Clarisse’s charm lit red. She tightened its cord, then moved to the edge of the current. Her shoulders sat too square; the hand on her bag strap had gone hard around the leather. The signs were plain, but he didn’t call attention to them. Instead he stepped into the water beside her, close enough for his sleeve to brush hers.

“The route follows the shelf until the palace inlet,” Percy said. “Triton can hold us in place if you want to stop.”

“I heard him explain it. I’m not stopping, and don’t tell Silena to stop looking unless she swims into something.”

“I’ll tell her not to swim into it.”

Annabeth came close enough to take Percy’s hand. Her green charm pulsed once. He turned his palm beneath hers, and she fitted her fingers through his. “Ready?”

“Ready.”

The current lifted them. The lake’s surface closed overhead, and daylight broke into long ribbons that traveled along the water. The air around their faces had the faint sweetness of salt and shell. It carried their voices with a soft, clear edge, so Silena’s laugh sounded as if it had been placed in a glass bowl.

The corridor bent toward open water. The lake gave way to the sea, and the last green light faded behind them. Fish flashed silver and blue beside the current. Silena leaned toward them until the water gently turned her back toward the path.

“The water is steering me,” she said.

“It’s keeping you on the route,” Triton answered.

“It has impeccable manners.”

“It waits until you’re looking away before it moves you,” Percy said.

As the water beneath them deepened, Clarisse drifted closer, salt-sweet current tugging at their clothes. She did not grab his arm. Her fingers stayed around the strap of her bag, but her eyes searched the dark below and found the pale outline of the shelf ahead. When she saw it, a fraction of the tension left her neck.

“There,” Percy said. “The route follows that ledge.”

“I can see it. Stop narrating the sea.”

“I thought you liked knowing where it was.”

“I like knowing. I don’t need a tour every time it changes color.”

“It’s a distinct blue, almost its own language.”

“Let the blue speak for itself,” Annabeth said.

The journey grew quieter beneath a shelf of coral. Sunlight did not reach this far, but the water held its own lights: lamps along the ridge, jellyfish pulsing like stars, and bright windows on distant buildings. Percy recognized the southern approach by the current around the stone towers. His body knew the way before he could name it. The others were traveling beside him, on their own terms.

Silena pointed at anemones that opened as they passed. “They’re waving.”

“They’re reacting to the current,” Annabeth said.

“Both can be true.”

Clarisse studied a line of carved markers along the shelf. “Are those for navigation?”

“They mark the old boundary of the city,” Triton said. “The new one extends farther east.”

“How do you know which way to go without them?”

“The current will tell you.”

“I don’t trust a current to give directions.”

“You trusted this one.”

“I’m still reviewing its performance.”

The passage bent once more, and Atlantis opened around them.

It rose from the seafloor in terraces of pale stone, green glass, and living coral. Bridges arched between towers. Gardens grew along the balconies, and silver fish moved through the streets like loose ribbons of light. Below it, windows and lanterns gathered the light until the whole place held its own morning, bright as citrus against the dark.

Silena forgot to speak again.

“That’s it?” Clarisse asked, as if checking whether the city was real.

“That’s Atlantis.”

“It’s bigger than your stories.”

“I might have left out a few streets.”

Annabeth squeezed his hand once. She did not ask him to explain what it felt like. Already measuring their spans, she studied the bridges. Wonder lit her face, questions gathering behind it. The charm at her throat lit green against the blue.

---

Triton guided them through the southern inlet, past a row of coral columns and a plaza where the water carried the sound of a bronze bell. The palace stood above the rest of the city on a rise of white stone. Its high windows were blue-green, its outer walls traced with delicate lines of shell and silver. The structure was not a single block but a cluster of wings joined by open galleries and gardens.

The place lived in his bones. His body recognized turns his mind still had to learn, so the first glimpse felt like a song whose tune came before its words, salt and warm stone familiar without a remembered day attached.

He also knew that the western tower had acquired a new balcony since his last visit, and the gate’s carved sea-horses had been cleaned. The palace had gone on changing without waiting for him. That was a comfort, though he could not have explained why.

The current carried them into a sheltered courtyard. A row of attendants waited along the wall, but there was no herald, no line of guards blocking the path. Amphitrite stood beneath the nearest archway in a robe the color of pale jade. Her silver circlet shone softly in the water.

Poseidon stood beside her.

He wore sea-green robes without a crown, which made him look less like a king and more like the father who had once carried a basket of camp bread as if it were a battle standard. He had his hands folded behind his back. When he saw Percy, his smile came easily. When his gaze moved to Clarisse and Silena, he seemed to remember he had been planning what to say and promptly forgot it.

“Welcome to Atlantis,” Amphitrite said. Her voice traveled through the water with the same warmth Percy remembered from the garden. “I am glad you came.”

Silena’s whole face brightened. “Thank you for having us, Your Majesty.”

“You may call me Amphitrite,” she said. “If that feels comfortable. If not, Your Majesty is perfectly welcome.”

“I think I’ll start with Your Majesty,” Silena said. “I might get used to the other one.”

“Then we’ll let the name wait.” Amphitrite turned to Clarisse. “I am glad you are here.”

Clarisse put her hand over her heart, palm out, and gave the small practiced nod Triton had taught them. “Thank you for inviting us.”

“You are welcome.” Amphitrite did not reach for her, or say anything that asked Clarisse to feel a certain way. She looked at the red coral token on Clarisse’s wrist and smiled. “You brought the garden with you.”

Clarisse’s thumb moved once over the rough polished edge. “It fit in my bag.”

“I’m glad.”

Amphitrite greeted Annabeth next, then hugged Percy, resting one hand briefly on the back of his head before letting him go. The old familiarity felt different with all of them around him.

Poseidon stepped forward after her. “Percy.”

“Dad.”

His father’s smile lifted. He glanced toward the girls. “Silena. Clarisse.”

Silena answered, “Hello, Father.”

Clarisse gave him the same measured nod she had used at camp. “Father.”

“I’m glad you came,” Poseidon said. Then he paused, as if he had meant to say more but had not trusted the rest of it. “I’ve been looking forward to this.”

Silena smiled. “We’ve been looking forward to it, too.”

“Some of us,” Clarisse said.

Poseidon nodded. “Some of us. That’s all right.”

He lifted one hand as if he might reach for her shoulder, then stopped before touching her. Clarisse noticed the halt. So did Percy, chest tight.

“Would you like a hug?” Poseidon asked.

Clarisse looked at his hand, then at his face. “Not right now.”

“All right.” He lowered it. “I’m glad you told me.”

Clarisse’s chin tipped upward. Something in her stance settled. Poseidon did not try again. He turned to Annabeth.

“Annabeth Grace,” he said. “We’re honored you accepted the invitation.”

“Thank you, Lord Poseidon.” She gave the formal greeting. “I’m glad to be here.”

“I’m glad you’re here, too.”

“That’s a good start,” Triton said. “No succession disputes yet.”

“Yet?” Annabeth asked.

“We are trying to have a quiet weekend,” Amphitrite said. “Let us not challenge the grammar before dinner.”

“The grammar started it,” Annabeth said.

At his laugh, low and relieved, the attendants loosened, and the palace began to feel less like a stage set and more like a place where people lived, bread-warm and brine-sharp.

Triton introduced the household attendant and showed them where to leave their bags. Clarisse asked whether the weapon rack would be visible from the guest corridor.

“It will be in the entry room,” Triton said. “Your weapons will remain in your sight when they are not with you.”

“Good.”

“The palace has a security watch on every outer gate,” Poseidon said. “No one will take them without telling you. We can arrange an escort if you want to carry one outside the family rooms.”

Clarisse studied him. “I’ll decide if I need one.”

“Of course.”

They crossed the courtyard. A faint chiming drifted through the palace channels. Triton told them that, as a child, Percy had pretended it was a sea-spirit learning music. Triton had called it the central current. Percy decided he preferred the boy’s version.

The entrance hall smelled of salt, warm stone, and flower oil. A mosaic of pale blue and cream shells covered the floor beneath a ceiling curved like the underside of a wave. Sunlight drifted across it in shifting strips.

Silena studied the mosaic. “Are those real shells?”

“Some,” Amphitrite said. “The floor was repaired after the eastern channel shifted.”

“The water moved a wall,” Poseidon explained.

“Was anyone hurt?” Clarisse asked.

“No. A chair floated into the kitchens and refused to leave,” Triton said. “It’s the biting chair.”

“The biting chair is in the kitchen?” Annabeth asked.

“Not anymore,” Triton said. “It is in a gallery, where it can reflect on its behavior.”

Clarisse gave him a long look. “You said it was an antique.”

“It is.”

“You can still discipline an antique.”

“Mother agrees with you.”

Amphitrite’s smile was bright. “I did not say discipline. I said a chair should not bite a guest.”

“That sounds like discipline,” Annabeth said.

---

Their rooms were in the quiet family wing, which opened onto a garden pool. Triton showed them the route to the main hall, the nearest exit, and the small shell bell they could ring for help.

“You’ll be close to us,” he said. “But each room can be closed for privacy. The connecting doors open from either side. No one will enter without knocking.”

“Even the king and queen?” Clarisse asked.

“Especially the king and queen,” Amphitrite answered. “This is where we sleep, not where we hold court.”

Clarisse looked at the door, testing its latch with one hand. “Good.”

“You can choose whether the doors between the rooms stay open,” Triton said. “They don’t decide that for you.”

Silena chose the room with the wide window over the floating garden. “Do you want this one?” she asked Clarisse.

“You do. Take it.”

Silena stepped inside, smiling. “I do.”

Clarisse chose the room across the corridor, where she could see the hallway and courtyard. She checked the latch, door, and closet. Percy let her count the exits.

“Do you want me to show you the guard post?” Triton asked.

“Yes.”

“I’ll show you now.”

Poseidon took the room at the corridor’s end. Amphitrite had her own private room, she explained, and would not be staying with the guests. Clarisse noticed, then turned back to her room.

Annabeth’s room was beside Percy’s. She studied the desk, lamp, window, and shelves. “The desk is too far from the light.”

“You can move it,” Triton said.

“It’s built in.”

“Then I can ask someone to shift the lamp.”

“That’s good.” She set down her satchel. “I don’t want to be difficult.”

“You’re looking at a room,” Percy said. “It can be badly arranged.”

“This one has a window seat,” Triton said. “It was designed for reading.”

Annabeth crossed to the window and sat on the broad ledge. “This part is good.”

Over the city the window looked. Gone quiet, Annabeth mapped the building in her mind while the lamps threw thin gold across her hands. Percy held back his explanations until she looked at him, letting her have the first reading.

“Can you show me the outside?”

“All weekend,” he said.

“You can unpack or see the nearby rooms before dinner,” Triton said.

“Can we do both?” Silena asked, setting down the jam basket. “I want to see the flower garden.”

Clarisse returned from the guard post. “The rack is where he said. Two guards at the entry, one in the courtyard. I know their names and the way back.”

“Good,” Percy said.

“It’s an inventory. Stop looking pleased.”

Triton led them along the garden corridor. The flowers floated in shallow pools, their pale petals turning toward the current. Some were green at the edges and silver in the center; others changed color as they passed, taking on hints of Silena’s dark curls, Clarisse’s dark armor, and Annabeth’s gray-blue eyes.

Silena leaned close to a cluster of yellow blooms. “They’re copying us.”

“They’re responding to light and movement,” Triton said.

“Still copying.”

Clarisse stared at a bloom that had turned the color of her sleeve. “If it copies my face, I’m leaving.”

“It can learn,” Percy said.

“Please don’t train the flowers,” Annabeth replied.

They passed the little dining room, and Triton told them how Percy had once tried to ride a chair down the east hall during a lesson. He had caught Percy before the stairs.

“You were six,” Triton said.

“I knew where the chair was going,” Percy said.

“You didn’t know there was a stair.”

“That was the problem,” Annabeth said.

“You are both impossible,” Percy said.

“It’s one of the reasons we get along,” Triton replied.

The corridor widened before the main hall. Silena greeted two attendants by name. They smiled back, and Clarisse watched them move through the palace like it was an ordinary home.

“Does everyone know we’re here?” she asked.

“The household staff do,” Triton said. “The city will know by morning. There is no announcement tonight.”

“Good.”

“Tomorrow is yours to plan—the garden, the harbor, the archive, or nothing.”

“I’d like to reserve a little of nothing,” Silena said.

“You may.”

Clarisse glanced toward the dining hall. “And dinner?”

“Dinner is dinner,” Triton said. “The king and queen will be there. We’ll eat in the family room, not the court hall. No herald, no formal speeches.”

“How many spoons?” Clarisse asked.

“As many as the meal requires.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It is a better answer than Percy’s.”

“The small silver spoon is for pearlfruit,” Percy said.

“And the jam?” Silena asked.

“Not part of dinner,” Triton said.

“Then we brought the wrong gift.”

“It’s a gift, not a course,” Clarisse said.

---

When the hour arrived, Triton met them at the family-room entrance. The weapons had been secured in the rack. Clarisse had watched the guard place her spear there, checked that the lock was visible, and declined to let the other knife go anywhere but her own boot. The guard had made no objection after Triton confirmed she had told them about it.

“You remember the greeting,” Triton said.

“I remember,” Clarisse replied.

“There is no need for a formal introduction here. If you want to use it, you can. If you don’t, that is also fine.”

“I’m not bowing to the door.”

“The door is not expecting it.”

Silena adjusted the basket handle, then set it down on a side table. The camp map lay folded inside. “Maybe we should wait to give the gift until after we sit.”

“That’s sensible,” Annabeth said.

“It’s also because I don’t want to drop the jam in front of the king.”

“That is also sensible.”

The family room opened onto a broad balcony overlooking the city. It was smaller than the formal hall, with a low ceiling and warm light coming from pearl lamps set into the walls. The table was long enough for all of them but not so large they had to shout across it. A blue bowl sat in the center, holding shell fruit and a few pale flowers. The place settings were simple, though there were still more utensils than Percy remembered needing.

Amphitrite waited near the head of the table. Poseidon stood beside her, again with his hands clasped behind his back. He seemed to have been trying to decide where to stand so nobody would think he was making a speech. Triton had chosen the chair at the side, not the one at the far end.

“Come in,” Amphitrite said. “You don’t need to wait for anyone to announce you.”

Silena touched her hand to her heart and turned her palm outward. Clarisse did the same, crisp and sure. Annabeth followed with the familiar careful nod. Percy started to make the gesture, then remembered he had already greeted his parents in the courtyard.

He lowered his hand.

Poseidon watched him. “You can greet me again if you like.”

“I’m good.”

“I’m conserving my formal gestures. I have a limited supply,” Percy said.

“That isn’t how gestures work,” Annabeth said.

“You can use your name,” Amphitrite said. “You are not required to perform for your own family.”

Clarisse glanced at her. “You can say that in the family room?”

“Especially here.”

Poseidon reached to pull out Annabeth’s chair, then stopped as she reached for it herself. “Would you like me to—”

“I’ve got it,” Annabeth said.

He stepped back. “Of course.”

The small exchange passed without awkwardness only because Poseidon let it. Percy noticed how careful his father was being, and how hard the care seemed to be. At camp, he had once carried too much food at once because he wanted to help. Here, he was trying to leave space for everyone to decide what help they wanted.

“Where should we sit?” Silena asked.

“Anywhere. You can sit together,” Amphitrite said.

Silena chose a seat near Clarisse. Annabeth sat to Percy’s left. Clarisse took the place on his right, leaving an empty chair between her and Poseidon. She looked at the space once, measuring it, then pulled her chair in. Percy did not see her relax, but she stayed.

Poseidon chose the chair across from her. He did not comment on the distance.

The seven settled around the table, the room’s current lifting the tablecloth’s edge while the city lights trembled beyond the balcony.

“I brought you something from camp,” Silena said. She stood to retrieve the basket, then placed it between Amphitrite and the center bowl. “It’s strawberry jam. We made it ourselves. The map is from all of us.”

Amphitrite opened the map carefully. “This is beautiful. I can see where you spend your days.”

“The scale is accurate,” Annabeth said. “The laundry line may not be.”

“The laundry line is a threat,” Clarisse said.

“Noted,” Amphitrite said, her smile widening. “I will remember to keep an eye on it.”

Poseidon leaned across to see the drawing. “You put the lake in the center.”

“It is in the center of camp,” Percy said.

“And the sea beneath it,” Silena added. “Percy drew that part.”

Poseidon traced the route without touching the page. “You made a map of where you live and brought it here.”

“We wanted you to have it,” Silena said.

For a moment, Poseidon did not answer. Then he nodded. “Thank you. I’ll keep it.”

“You can put it somewhere people will see it,” Silena said. “Not only in a drawer.”

“I will.”

Clarisse loosened the blue tournament ribbon from her bag and set it beside the map. “This is from camp. It’s not a prize. I kept it because the color didn’t look terrible with my gear.”

“That is high praise,” Percy said.

“It’s a ribbon,” Clarisse replied.

Amphitrite touched its edge. “Then I will keep it with the map.”

Poseidon leaned back. “We should have asked what you wanted to see before I planned anything.”

“You planned things?” Clarisse asked.

He looked briefly at Triton, who offered no rescue. “I made a list.”

“Of what?”

“The harbor, gardens, archive, training pool, and market.”

“That sounds like a full schedule,” Annabeth said.

“It was a list,” Poseidon said. “Not an order.”

“Then it can wait,” Clarisse told him. “We’re here for the weekend. We can decide tomorrow.”

“Yes,” Poseidon said. “You can.”

“Good.”

Amphitrite took her place at the table. The attendants brought in the first course: chilled sea greens, bright slices of pearlfruit, and a small dish of warm bread. A silver spoon rested beside each place setting, but no one had yet explained why there were two more beside Percy’s plate.

Silena leaned close. “Which one is for the pearlfruit?”

“The small one,” Percy said.

“There are two small ones.”

Clarisse reached for the broadest spoon. “I’ll use this.”

“That one is for the soup,” Triton said.

“Then I’ll have soup.”

Poseidon cleared his throat. “The first course is sea greens.”

Clarisse glanced at the spoon nearest her hand. “This one?”

“Yes.”

“Good. I knew that.”

Annabeth examined the pair beside her plate. “And these two?”

“One is for the warm sauce,” Poseidon said. “The other is for the shellfish.”

“Which is which?”

He paused. “I should have checked.”

“That’s all right,” Annabeth said. “We’ll discover the truth together.”

“Both work,” Triton said. “One scoops, one pours.”

“I’ll use the one that doesn’t pour dinner into my mouth,” Clarisse said, setting the large spoon down.

Poseidon sat with his hands on the table, visibly trying not to look relieved that the utensils had become the subject. Amphitrite began the meal by lifting her cup. There was no demand for a toast, no required speech. She looked at every person around the table before she drank.

“To the weekend,” she said. “And to the people who crossed the water to share it with us.”

Silena raised her cup. “To the jam.”

“To the map,” Clarisse said.

“To everyone finding the right spoon,” Annabeth added.

“That may be beyond us,” Percy said.

Poseidon’s laugh was a little late, as if he had been waiting to make sure the joke was meant for him. But it was real. “Then to asking when we don’t know.”

The cups touched. Outside, the city shimmered in the dark water, and the dinner table gathered all their reflections into one wavering circle.

The first course waited between them. No one had to know the whole weekend yet. They were here, around the table, and the invitation had become something they could take one bite at a time.
