## Chapter 126: The Palace Below

Until he went through the door, the palace did not look like a place Percy had once lived. From the terrace, coral balconies and pale towers rose through the blue, brine and flower-oil on the current; banners turned slowly above the entrance. Clarisse studied the arch while Silena fingered the shell token from Amphitrite’s garden. Annabeth had her sketchbook ready.

“It’s bigger than I imagined,” Silena said.

“Your drawing was smaller,” Clarisse replied. “Show us the nursery.”

That morning Thalia had finished her watch and come for a few hours through Chiron’s approved passage, wearing the breathing charm Triton had sent. Tyson had joined them from the undersea forges. Amphitrite, in a simple blue robe, welcomed them to wander without a schedule. Poseidon stood beside her, and Triton had cleared the family corridor. Tyson smiled at Percy. “Show us the places where you were small.”

“Some of them,” Percy said. “I wasn’t small everywhere.”

“You still can’t reach the upper shelves,” Clarisse said.

Annabeth opened her sketchbook. “I’ll draw what I notice. Tell me if there’s anything you’d prefer to keep private.”

“Don’t let the biting chair look dignified,” Clarisse said.

“It bit a footman,” Percy explained.

“The footman wore a fish-shaped slipper,” Triton said. “The chair mistook it for lunch.”

Annabeth marked that in her sketchbook and followed Percy through the west doors.

The hallway was cool and pale, lit by blue glass lamps. Percy knew the little dip in the floor from a storm long ago and the shell-shaped mark near the third column; his feet remembered the route.

“The nursery’s past the family rooms,” he said, taking the turn without looking.

The nursery doors were a pair of blue-green panels, each carved with small fish. Percy put his hand on one. The door opened at his touch with a soft, familiar click.

The room beyond was round and bright. Its ceiling curved like the inside of a shell, with small lights scattered through it like a night sky. A low bed stood beside a window whose view looked into a garden of coral. There were shelves for toys and books, a painted wooden whale on a table, and a broad mural that circled the walls from floor to ceiling.

Whales swam through the painted blue. Some were enormous, some no bigger than Percy’s hand. Between them moved tiny silver fish, a green turtle, and a red crab carrying what looked like a pearl nearly as large as itself.

Silena’s face softened. “It’s beautiful.”

“Amphitrite painted most of it,” Percy said. “I helped.”

Clarisse looked at the wall. “Which parts?”

Percy pointed out his crooked fish and the crab he had drawn carrying a pearl. Silena touched her finger to the turtle’s painted crown and declared it queen of the nursery. Tyson had painted a giant, one-eyed fish beside the whales, though Triton insisted he had originally been asked to draw a whale. “The whale needed a friend,” Tyson said. Poseidon laughed. “It seemed pleased.”

Taking in the nursery, his sisters stood side by side. Easier to show them the mural than the parts of himself it held. Amphitrite told them how everyone had come and gone here: her voice while she read, Triton’s footsteps in the hall, Poseidon carrying Percy in from the garden when he fell asleep on a bench. She pointed to the whales he had asked to see after bad dreams. Percy studied them, wishing he could remember the comfort she described.

Clarisse stood near the bed. The mattress had been replaced many times, but the carved frame remained. A small line of marks ran up one side of the bedpost, each with a date and a name in careful Atlantean script.

“What are those?” she asked.

“Height marks,” Percy said. “Triton started them.”

Clarisse traced the space beside the marks without touching the wood. There was a blank stretch between two dates. “Why did you stop measuring?”

“He insisted the post had grown,” Triton said. “He was five.”

“It had standards to meet,” Percy said.

Silena laughed. Clarisse did not. She stayed by the bed, looking at the old marks, the hand-painted whales, the toys kept clean on the shelf. She had not been a stranger to care. Percy knew that. Their mortal mothers had loved them. But the room had a particular ease to it, the evidence of a child expected to wake up safe and return to the same bed.

“You had all this,” Clarisse said.

Her voice was quiet, but it did not sound like an accusation. That made it harder to answer casually.

“I did,” Percy said.

Clarisse’s eyes stayed on the bedpost. “I’m glad you did.”

He could hear the second thought she was not saying. He did not want her to have to pretend it was not there.

“And?” he asked gently.

She drew a breath through her nose. “And I wish I’d had it too.”

Nobody rushed to fill the quiet. Amphitrite stood with her hands loosely clasped, offering no explanation that could make the truth less true. Poseidon’s expression had gone still. Silena shifted closer to Clarisse, her sleeve brushing her sister’s arm.

“That makes sense,” Percy said.

Clarisse turned toward him. “I don’t want you to feel bad for having a home.”

“I don’t.”

“I don’t want you to think I hate this place.”

“I don’t.”

“I do want it to have been different for me.”

“You can want that.”

Her mouth tightened, then relaxed. “Good.”

Silena took her hand for a moment. Clarisse let her. Annabeth stayed near the mural, pencil still, letting the room hold the silence without turning it into an emergency.

“Do you want to see the garden next?” Percy asked after a little while.

Clarisse looked once more at the bedpost. “Yes.”

“All right.”

Before they left, Annabeth sketched the whale mural, including the enormous one-eyed fish. Tyson watched the drawing take shape with a pleased smile.

“Make the eye bigger,” he suggested.

“It’s already bigger than its head,” Annabeth said.

“It can be bigger.”

“I’ll consider it.”

“That means yes,” Tyson said, and followed them out.

---

The family garden was a courtyard open to the slow current overhead. Coral made shady groves; gold sea lilies lined the path, and blue flowers clung to the ledges. A narrow channel wound around an island of sand.

The water ran cool over stone in the channel Amphitrite said he had played in until supper. Triton pointed out the coral tree where Percy had hidden from him, then the broad leaf under which he had fallen asleep while tiny fish sheltered in his hair. Percy looked from one place to the next, fitting their stories to the garden.

“This is where you hid?” Silena asked, pointing at the coral tree.

“One of the places,” Percy said. Triton remembered that Percy had always left a foot sticking out. Percy maintained that it was a decoy.

Annabeth moved slowly around the courtyard, sketching the layout and the way the current bent between the coral trunks. “The path isn’t symmetrical.”

“It follows the roots,” Amphitrite said. “The garden was here before the newer hall was built. We kept what was already growing.”

“A straight path would have cut through the largest grove,” Annabeth said.

“That’s why we kept it,” Poseidon said. “I was the architect, and I agreed with myself.”

Clarisse moved ahead, glancing between the pathways and the open water above them. Her shoulders had lowered since the nursery. She stopped beside a broad patch of sea grass, where a narrow stone bench sat under the coral tree.

“This was your favorite place?” Clarisse asked.

“Sometimes. It was quiet here. Not empty — hushed, the way deep water holds sound.”

“Amphitrite says she’d sit nearby when I was upset and tell me about the garden. If I wanted to talk, I could. If not, she’d wait. She still does that.”

Clarisse looked at the bench, then at Amphitrite. “That sounds nice.”

“It was,” Percy said.

“It doesn’t mean you had to like every day,” Amphitrite added. “No childhood is made perfect by a lovely room or a patient garden.”

Clarisse nodded. She seemed grateful not to be asked to make the memory simpler than it was.

Silena stopped beside a bed of blue flowers. As she circled it, the petals turned to follow her. She laughed, and Annabeth sketched their bright faces. Tyson suggested she add a label for “the sandal fish,” earning a quiet groan from Clarisse.

They continued through the grove. Percy showed them the pool where Triton had taught him to float and the place where Poseidon had taught him to listen to currents by closing his eyes and telling one from another.

“He was curious about how the garden moved,” Poseidon said. “He listened for nearly an hour, then fell asleep.”

“The current was soothing,” Percy said.

At the far side of the garden, the path narrowed between two coral hedges. A small mosaic was set in the wall beyond them: a blue whale beneath a yellow sun, with a single human-shaped figure waving from its back. Percy stopped before he could decide whether he had meant to.

“That one’s different,” Silena said.

“It’s from the surface,” Percy said. “I made it on a visit after coming here, telling Amphitrite about Sally’s kitchen in the life I remember. I drew the whale and a sun, and then I added…”

“The little blue house,” Amphitrite said. “You said it needed a door big enough for everyone.”

Percy had almost forgotten leaving the mosaic here. Making it had let him show Amphitrite a kitchen he remembered from the life in which Sally had raised him, a place absent from the childhood she knew. The blue house was only a crooked square; the whale was much better.

Silena looked at the mosaic, then at him. “Can I ask about her?”

Percy nodded.

“Your mortal mother. Sally.” Silena hesitated, choosing her words with care. “I know you’ve talked about her before. I don’t want to make you tell us anything you don’t want to. I wondered what she was like. And whether she knew you’d come here.”

Percy took a moment. The Sally he remembered had raised him in Manhattan. Here she had died when he was two. He could answer honestly without pretending those histories matched.

“She knew me. She knew who my father was, and that I was loved by more than one place, even when it hurt.”

“Did she know this place?” Silena asked.

“In the life I remember, she knew about the sea. Here, she died before I came to Atlantis. I don’t know what she hoped for it. She talked about beautiful things surviving by staying hidden until they were safe to be seen.”

“That sounds like her,” Poseidon said quietly.

Percy looked at him. His father had been watching the mosaic, not Percy. “After she died, you brought me here.”

Poseidon nodded. The current slipped through the garden, stirring the flowers and their sweet green scent. “You were two. I came to the surface for you. I carried you down myself.”

“You held on to a blue blanket and kept asking when you would see her again,” Poseidon said. “I told you that you would not see her in the way you wanted, but that she loved you. You asked me to say it again.”

Percy pictured a small child with damp hair, fists curled in a blanket, the ocean too large to understand. The story did not take anything from the memories he carried. It gave them a shore.

“I’m glad you told me,” he said.

Poseidon’s hand settled gently at the back of his shoulder. “I should have said it more often. I thought bringing you somewhere safe was the same as helping you through what had happened. It wasn’t. Amphitrite knew better.”

“We learned together,” Amphitrite said. “You missed Sally, and no one asked you to stop. I could love you without asking to be loved in her place.”

Silena glanced at the mosaic. “Did you know Sally?”

“A little,” Amphitrite said. “We wrote to one another before she died. She sent me a letter about Percy’s insistence that every meal should have something blue in it. She said she had stopped arguing with him about the color of food because the argument was more trouble than the dye.”

Percy smiled. “That sounds like her.”

“She hoped you would always have people to care for you, even when she couldn’t be with you.” Amphitrite’s gaze rested on Percy. “I kept that letter for a long time.”

Silena’s face had gone thoughtful. “She sounds like she knew how to make room for people.”

“She was practical and funny,” Amphitrite said. “She never pretended the gods were easy to deal with, and she could be kind without being impressed by power. Once she wrote that you wanted your blue blanket beside her chair at breakfast. She said the blanket apparently needed company as much as you did.”

Silena’s smile warmed. Clarisse listened with her chin lifted and her arms loosely folded.

“I’m glad she wanted you to have this care,” Silena said. “Not because this fixes anything. Because someone kept trying to make you safe.”

“Yeah,” Percy said.

Clarisse looked at Poseidon, then back at the mosaic. “I’m trying not to be jealous of a little kid you carried here.”

“You can be jealous of what he had,” Amphitrite said. “It doesn’t mean you want him to have had less.”

Clarisse’s mouth twitched. “That’s exactly the problem.”

“It can be both,” Percy said. “I can be glad I had them and sorry you didn’t get the same things.”

“That’s not your fault.”

“It’s still true.”

Clarisse looked at him for a long second, then bumped her shoulder against his. “I know.”

Poseidon did not try to place a hand on her or tell her the past could be repaired. “You deserved care, Clarisse. So did Silena. I cannot make our histories match. I can only be here now and listen when you tell me what it was like for you.”

Silena took Clarisse’s hand. “We had our moms. They did everything they could.”

“I’m glad they were there for you,” Poseidon said.

“I still wonder what it would have been like if you had been there too,” Silena said.

“You have the right to wonder.”

The answer did not mend the distance between them, but it let the distance be named without asking either girl to soften it for him.

After a moment, Annabeth touched the edge of her page. “Would you like me to draw this mosaic?”

Percy looked at the little blue house and whale. “If you want to.”

“I do.”

She drew the mosaic, leaving the edges unfinished and the blue house more room than the wall allowed. Thalia stood beside her, shoulder touching hers.

“The training pool is next,” Percy said.

“Good,” Clarisse said. “I want to know whether this palace has anything that can actually fight back.”

---

The training pool was built into a broad chamber near the eastern side of the palace. Its water was a deep green, fed by the sea through bronze grates. A narrow gallery ran above it, with practice spears and wooden shields on racks. The pool floor was a mosaic of a hippocamp, its mane flowing around the tiled curves.

He knew the room by its bright echoes — Triton’s calls from the gallery, splashes from the shallow end, salt-sharp and sun-warmed. Triton described floating first in the nursery pool, then swimming here, and the long effort of teaching Percy not to steer with his hands.

Clarisse had already picked up a practice spear. “Can I?”

“Of course,” Triton said. “Keep the tip below the gallery.”

“I know how to use a spear.”

“I know. This is a different kind.”

She tested the weight, then gave it a measured swing. The water around the shaft parted in a clean line. Her expression brightened, briefly, before she brought it under control.

“The balance is good,” she said.

“The wood is grown from a pressure-resistant tree,” Triton replied. “It can flex without splintering.”

“Better than most weapons at camp.”

“You may keep one for the weekend if you want.”

Clarisse glanced toward him. “I might.”

Silena dipped her hand into the pool, and the water curled around her fingers. Mornings here had mostly been his: determined laps and refusals to stop when tired, as Triton remembered it; long lessons that sometimes ended with him asleep in the current, as Triton went on to describe it. Tyson confirmed that hippocampi could sneeze underwater, a subject Percy had once used to keep Triton talking.

Clarisse shifted the practice spear from hand to hand. “Show me the current.”

Percy stepped onto the submerged platform. “The pool has three lanes. This one carries you clockwise. The other two run against it or cross under the middle. If you use the water instead of fighting it, you can change direction without losing speed.”

“Show me.”

Percy pushed off. He let the current take him around the outer lane, then angled across the pool. His shoulder cut through the water, his hand turned, and the current carried him through the center. He resurfaced by Clarisse’s platform.

Clarisse followed, spear held close. Her first pass was direct, her strokes strong and sure. She tried to cut across the current and lost speed. On the second attempt she waited, watched the water, then shifted her weight a fraction earlier. The current caught her and carried her through.

“Better,” Percy said.

“I know,” Clarisse replied, though her smile gave her away.

Silena laughed from the edge. “You both sound like you’re having fun.”

“We are,” Clarisse said, before she could make it sound like a complaint.

Annabeth sketched the crossing currents from the gallery. Thalia leaned beside her, pleased to see her sister so absorbed in the room’s design.

Triton tossed a small wooden disk into the pool. It spun over the water, caught in the cross-current, and shot toward Percy. Percy tapped it aside with his palm. It curved toward Clarisse. She lunged, caught it between her fingers, and sent it back at Triton hard enough to make him duck.

“You said it was a lesson,” she called.

“It is.” Triton retrieved the disk. “You have learned to watch the current.”

“I already knew that.”

“You learned to watch this one.”

Without arguing she took it. The difference showed plainly: she was not pretending to know the pool already, and Triton was not asking her to prove that she belonged there. Together they were learning its rhythm, breath by breath.

They stayed until the light shifted. Tyson splashed with Percy in the shallow end; Silena and Annabeth sat at the edge while Clarisse struck the center of a floating target. Climbing out, Percy heard her say she was glad he’d had something that was his, though she still wished she’d had it too. He agreed that both things could be true. She asked to borrow a practice spear for the weekend.

“I’ll have it ready,” Triton said.

---

The stables stood behind coral pillars where the palace currents widened into quiet channels. Each open-water pen was bounded by pale coral rails; brushes and reins hung in orderly rows. “Triton maintains this place,” Percy said. “And names the brushes,” Triton added. “It helps to know which one you need.”

Silena watched a pale-gold hippocamp with a silver mane drift toward them, its finned tail sweeping slowly behind its horse-like forelegs.

“Can we meet them?” she asked.

“Offer your hand and let them decide,” Triton said.

The hippocamp reached Silena and touched her palm with its nose. She smiled, then waited until it nudged her fingers again before rubbing its cheek.

Clarisse stood a little back, eyeing the row of pens. Percy could tell she was assessing the animals’ strength and the space around them, the way she had done with the palace doors. A dark, blue-maned hippocamp came to the rail and bumped its head against the coral.

“That’s Wavecut,” Triton said. “She’s impatient, but kind.”

Wavecut gave a sharp snort. Clarisse held out her hand. The hippocamp sniffed her knuckles, then pressed her muzzle against Clarisse’s shoulder hard enough to rock her back a step.

Clarisse rubbed the animal between the eyes. Wavecut’s eyelids drooped.

“She’s soft as velvet,” Silena said, fingers lingering in the coarse-sweet mane-smell. Clarisse gave her a warning look but kept petting the hippocamp.

Poseidon rested a hand on the rail. “Percy’s first hippocamp was a cream-colored gelding named Tidepool. He once steered him toward the coral.”

“Tidepool chose that direction,” Percy said.

Triton told them how he held the reins while Percy learned to balance, let go when asked, then was asked to hold on again. “I was allowed to change my mind,” Percy said.

In Triton’s account he had been small enough to trust his brother’s hand on the reins without thinking. Clarisse watched him with sadness, curiosity, and a little amusement at the child who had asked his brother to hold on and let go in the same breath.

“Can we ride?” she asked.

“Not out beyond the stable channel today,” Triton said. “But we can take them around the inner loop if everyone is comfortable.”

“I’m comfortable,” Clarisse said.

“I’d like to try,” Silena said. “If they’re willing.”

Annabeth paused to sketch the stable’s rail design while Thalia stood beside her and Tyson inspected the named brushes.

The stable keeper brought out two smaller hippocampi, mottled-gray Current and reddish-brown Gull, who followed Percy’s pocket in search of fruit. Triton showed the sisters how to hold the reins lightly and guide without pulling. Silena asked whether a hippocamp could tell nervousness from quiet. “They feel the current around you,” Triton said. “They know how you move before you do.”

Clarisse mounted Wavecut from the low platform. The hippocamp shifted once, then settled. Clarisse adjusted her balance with a small movement of her hips, the way she did on horseback at camp, and Wavecut started forward at an easy pace.

Silena followed on Current, shoulders tense at first, then relaxing as the hippocamp glided around the posts. Triton corrected her hand before she could steer toward the brush rack; Silena insisted the detour had been intentional.

Percy mounted Gull, who bumped his elbow and nosed at his pocket. “He’s supposed to be dignified,” Percy said. Tyson pointed out that Gull was hungry.

They rode the inner channel in a loose circle. Triton swam alongside, near Wavecut’s bridle but not holding it. Clarisse shook her head, and he let his hand fall away.

Wavecut turned under Clarisse’s direction. Her sister’s posture loosened as the ride continued. She leaned down to whisper something to the hippocamp, and the animal answered with a soft snort. Percy couldn’t hear the words, but he recognized the look that followed: Clarisse had found something she could enjoy without having to earn it.

When they dismounted, Silena rubbed Current’s neck and asked to ride again tomorrow. Clarisse said she would return too, choosing the inner loop today and the outer channel when she knew Wavecut better. Poseidon nodded. Amphitrite called it wise; Clarisse called it practical, without arguing further.

---

By late afternoon, the family gathered in the small dining room overlooking the garden. A bowl of water at the table’s center reflected the sea above. Roasted shellfish, kelp rolls, citrus, and warm bread passed from hand to hand.

Clarisse chose a spot with a view of the doors. Silena sat beside her. Annabeth and Thalia settled across from them. Tyson sat next to Percy, who had to move his chair twice to leave enough space for Tyson’s shoulders.

“The nursery was less formal than I pictured,” Silena said.

Triton told them how Percy used to hide from his tutor behind the western fountain, claiming a dolphin had summoned him.

Silena laughed, then looked at Clarisse. “Which place did you like best?”

Clarisse picked a piece of bread from the bowl. “The stable.”

“Not the garden?”

“The garden was good,” Clarisse said. “The stable had an animal who wasn’t impressed by the palace.”

Clarisse gave him a look, then tore the bread in half. She passed the larger piece to Silena without comment.

Annabeth laid her sketchbook between the bowls. Its pages held the mural, the blue-house mosaic, the pool currents, and the stables, drawn with gaps where the water moved. Tyson approved of the one-eyed fish, though he still wanted its eye bigger. Poseidon said the palace looked different in someone else’s drawing. “I drew what I noticed,” Annabeth said. Thalia liked the garden path that circled back without returning to the same place.

Clarisse studied the drawing of the nursery. “You made the bed look smaller.”

“It was small,” Percy said.

“It looks small in the drawing.”

“You want it bigger?” Annabeth asked.

Clarisse considered. “No. It’s right.”

Clarisse traced the air above the drawn height marks. “I don’t want to be jealous of it forever.”

“You don’t have to decide forever today,” Silena said, leaning against her. “We can make new memories without changing what we missed.”

“I want to. I think.”

Poseidon set down his cup. “The palace can be somewhere you visit, somewhere you like, or both. If you want to leave, we’ll help. If you want to return, we’ll be glad.”

Clarisse nodded and reached for more bread.

Silena turned to Amphitrite. “I liked hearing about Sally. I didn’t know you knew her.”

“I knew her only a little, but I was grateful for what she shared with me,” Amphitrite said. “She never spoke as if love were a contest. She wanted Percy to have the care he needed, wherever it came from.”

The words settled gently. Annabeth’s sketch showed the crooked blue door wide enough for everyone.

“She would have liked the garden,” he said.

“I think she would have,” Amphitrite replied. “I wish I could have shown it to her.”

No one tried to turn that wish into more. The meal moved on to bread sculptures: Poseidon shaped a hippocamp that looked like a determined eel, Silena made a shell, and Clarisse made a spear. Percy ate his before Tyson could claim it as a fish.

After the meal, the blue flowers glowed in the cooling current. Clarisse and Silena sat shoulder to shoulder; Annabeth showed Thalia a sketch while Poseidon and Triton debated whether Gull had stolen an apple. Tyson crowned the bread eel with herbs.

No one had to be in the same place in the family to sit at the same table.

“There’s one more place,” Percy said.

Clarisse looked up. “The biting chair?”

“No. The coral arch by the eastern channel.”

“What’s there?” Silena asked.

“A passage out to the sheltered lagoon. It’s where I used to swim when I’d finished training. Triton says the current is quiet there at this hour.”

“It is,” Triton said. “As long as you stay within the reef markers.”

Clarisse stood. “Then show us.”

---

The coral arch beyond the stables opened onto a shallow lagoon. Tiny shells gleamed in its pale branches; lanternfish lit the channel beyond. On recent visits Percy had swum through it, racing Triton and following Tyson through the kelp. From inside, it framed the open water like a doorway into a painting.

Annabeth sketched the arch and its lanternfish, adding a small figure with a sketchbook near the edge. Thalia leaned against her shoulder as she finished, then Annabeth tucked the book away. “Ready?”

Silena stepped toward the water. “Ready.”

Clarisse looked toward Poseidon, then at the blue channel beyond the arch. Her coral token glinted at her wrist. She did not ask whether she was allowed. She looked at the way the current moved and the space between the reef markers, taking its measure.

“It’s safe,” Poseidon said. “But you don’t have to go through.”

“I know.” She looked back at him. “I want to.”

“Then go.”

Triton waded into the lagoon and offered his hand to Silena. She took it, then laughed when the water rose around her shoulders. Annabeth followed, the sea-glass charm at her throat shining green. Thalia went beside her, keeping close without steering her. Tyson ducked under the surface with an enormous splash that sent the lanternfish scattering.

“Tyson!” Triton called.

“The water was in the way!” Tyson replied, already grinning.

Clarisse stepped in after them. The current curled around her, familiar now, not tame but readable. Percy watched her glance to either side, locate her sister and friends, then look at him.

“You coming?” Clarisse asked.

“I’m right behind you.”

“Don’t get lost in your own palace.”

Silena laughed and swam beneath the arch. Annabeth went after her, one hand brushing the water as if testing its shape. Thalia followed close, then Tyson, who turned sideways to fit his shoulders through the coral opening. Triton went next, easy and sure. Clarisse looked back once at Poseidon and Amphitrite. They stood together on the ledge, not calling her home or urging her onward, only watching with open faces.

Clarisse lifted one hand in a small wave and passed beneath the arch.

Annabeth brushed Percy’s hand as she swam past. He caught her fingers for a moment; she smiled and slipped ahead after her sister.

Percy followed her. He heard his father’s low laugh behind him and Amphitrite’s voice calling that the lagoon would hold them until the evening bell. The blue channel widened ahead. The others were already swimming through it, leaving a scatter of bubbles and ripples in their wake.

For one bright moment, the palace stood behind them, the open sea before them, and all the children of the family moved together through the coral arch.
