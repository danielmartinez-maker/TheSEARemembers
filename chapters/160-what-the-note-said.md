## Chapter 160: What the Note Said

Percy Jackson woke before the conch on Monday with his hand already in his pocket.

Not the pocket of the jeans he had worn yesterday, which lay over the foot of his bunk with the cuffs still damp and the knees still sandy. The other pocket. The pocket of the gray sweatpants he had slept in, soft and thin at the knees, because sometime in the night he had woken half enough to transfer three things from one pair of pants to the other without fully waking at all: the lake pebble, smooth and white as a tooth; Silena's spare hair tie, green thread frayed; and the small folded square of notebook paper that had been there since Saturday in the boathouse, when Annabeth had stopped him at the last reed-clump with two fingers on his cuff and slipped it in quick as a filing correction and said, It's just a note, Don't make a face.

He had not made a face. He had put his hand over it and nodded like an oath. And then the whole rest of Saturday had been paint and varnish and Olive's red notebook, and Sunday had been laundry and volleyball and the float and the dock, and the note had ridden in his pocket through all of it, getting softer at the corners, waiting. He had not read it because there had never been a moment private enough for it. That was what he told himself. The truer thing, the one he only admitted in the blue dark before morning, was that he had been a little afraid to read it. Not afraid of what it said. Afraid of how much he would feel when he read it.

Now it was Monday. The last full day. Tyson's ferry left tomorrow morning, Tuesday, early enough that Chiron had said to pack tonight and leave the dawn free for goodbyes. Percy had done the subtraction the moment he woke: today was all they had, whole and uncut. Today had to be inhabited all the way through. He lay in his bunk in Tempest Hall with the high window going pale above him and the pool moving water under the floor with its low salt-rain breath, and he felt the square of paper through the sweatpants cloth with his thumb, and he thought, now. Before anyone else is awake. Before the day starts spending itself.

He sat up carefully. The cabin was still in its night shape. Across the room Tyson breathed in the corner bunk, deep and even, one great arm flung over the blanket, the round white rock from the forges centered on his pillow even in sleep because he had put it there and then fallen asleep around it. Forge-smoke and hot bronze hung faint in the air, under the lemon oil and wet stone. Clarisse was a still mound in her bunk, braid trailing over the edge, whistle cord looped around the bedpost. Silena was turned toward the wall, hair in a loose braid for sleep, one hand hanging off the bunk with a needle still pinched between her fingers from last night's mending, the way she always fell asleep mid-stitch when she was tired and happy at once.

Percy slid his feet onto the cool tile and stood without letting the floor creak. He wore the faded orange camp shirt he had slept in, sleeves cut short back in June, hem washed almost white at the shoulder where the trident had been, and the gray sweatpants. His sneakers waited by the door, toes pointed out, exactly even, because Tyson had lined them up yesterday and nobody had disturbed them since. He left the sneakers. He took the quilt from the foot of his bunk, the spare one Tyson had folded into a square that would not hurt anyone, and he tucked the note deeper into his pocket and went barefoot out the pool door to the stone deck behind the cabin, where morning came first.

The deck was cold under his feet. Mid-September cold, the kind that lived in stone even when the afternoon promised warmth. The sky over the pines was the color of wet shell, going gold at the eastern edge. The lake showed through the trees, flat and gray-blue, freshwater green underneath, mist standing on it in a thin sheet. Pine resin and cold water and lemon oil from the door behind him. Somewhere up the hill a harpy turned over in its sleep and muttered about dishes.

Percy wrapped the quilt around his shoulders and sat on the second step with his back against the warm wall where the pool heat leaked through. He took the note out.

It was smaller than he remembered. A square of Athena cabin notebook paper, folded twice, creased soft from two days in pockets. His name on the outside in Annabeth's precise hand, the letters level as ruled lines: PERCY. Under it, smaller, in pencil gone faint from handling: read when it's quiet.

His throat closed. He unfolded it with fingers that were suddenly clumsy, the way they got when he was trying to tie a knot too carefully.

Inside, in the same precise hand, with one word pressed harder where the pencil had paused:

*Percy —*

*If I mapped this week the way I map the lake, I would put you down as a fixed point. Not because you stay still. You don't. You move like water moves. I mean because everything else gets truer when I measure from you.*

*Saturday you checked your pockets like Dad's armorers taught you and then you let Olive paint over your eyebrows. Sunday you got hit by a serve on purpose so the little kids would laugh, and then you held Tyson's ankle like it was your job and you didn't let go even when your fingers went white. You think nobody notices the second part. I notice the second part. That's my job. I'm the one who notices.*

*I keep trying to draw around the leaving. Thalia says I can't. She's right and it's annoying. So I'm not drawing around it. I'm drawing inside it. Here: north beach, flat rock, third cleat (fixed, initialed T.C.). Here: the bench where you sat with Silena's mending on your knees and pretended you knew how to sew. Here: this step, where you're probably reading this, because you always go where it's warm and private when something matters.*

*You are my favorite place to measure from.*

*Don't answer this fast. Just keep it. Check it with your tools tonight and see if it's still true.*

*— Annabeth*

*P.S. I ate the peach. Thalia was right. It was love. It was disgusting. I liked it.*

Percy read it twice. Then he read it a third time, slower, because the letters went blurry in the middle and he had to wait for them to come back.

The reaction came the way water came up through sand: not all at once, but everywhere. Warmth first, up the back of his neck and into his ears, the particular heat of being known exactly and liked anyway. Then the breath thing, the tightness under the ribs that was not quite ache and not quite lift. Then the ridiculous, unstoppable grin that pulled at his mouth even while his eyes were wet, so that he sat there on the cold step in a quilt with a tear tracking down one cheek and a smile he could not have suppressed to save his life, fourteen years old and sun-browned and lake-haired, holding a square of paper like it was a compass.

She had seen the second part. That was the line that undid him. He thought about Sunday: flopping theatrically when the serve hit his shoulder, the eleven-year-olds howling, Tyson standing over him asking if he was broken and whether he needed carrying. He had played it for laughs. He had also meant it. Letting Tyson haul him upright by the back of his shirt, feeling the huge hand steady and careful, had been the safest he had felt all week. And Annabeth had seen both at once, the performance and the anchor under it, and written it down without making it smaller.

You are my favorite place to measure from.

He read that line again, and then a fourth time, and each time it settled somewhere else: the back of his neck, the tight place under his ribs, the secret corner of himself that was still, no matter what the calendar said, seventeen years old and amazed to be allowed. He had spent that older life nearly losing her to distance and a war, and this one carrying the reputation of the one who left — the palace, the quests, everywhere he had not stayed. And here was the girl who remembered every version of him, saying in pencil that he was the coordinate she measured from. The place she stayed.

His thumb went to the one word pressed harder than the rest, where her pencil had paused. He traced the dent the graphite had left in the fibers. Somebody had hesitated there, and chosen the word anyway.

He pressed the heel of his hand to his eyes and laughed once, quietly, because if Clarisse woke up and found him crying on the back step she would enforce hydration and never let him hear the end of it. He folded the note along its old creases, exactly, tenderly, the way Tyson handled quilts and birds with hurt wings, and slipped it back into his pocket and put his hand over it through the cloth. Tools checked, he thought, remembering last night on the dock when she had shown him his own note — NORTH BEACH HAS GOOD ROCK, trident lopsided underneath — and patted her pocket and said, Filed, permanent collection. He understood now why she had patted it. It was not paper anymore. It was a sounding. It told you where the deep water was.

The sky had gone gold while he read. The mist on the lake through the trees was thinning. Behind him the cabin breathed: a bunk spring going ping as Tyson turned over, Clarisse muttering something about whistles in her sleep, Silena's needle ticking faintly against the tile where it slipped from her fingers. Monday morning, last full day, ferry tomorrow. The ending coming the way weather came, visible at the edge of the sky. Percy sat in the quilt with Annabeth's words warm inside his pocket like a stone carried in a fist, and felt the old urge to count — hours left, meals left, swims left — rise up and then, deliberately, set it down. In it, no counting. He had said it on the dock last night and meant it. He meant it again now, with the note read and the day unspent in front of him like calm water.

He stayed until his feet went numb on the stone. Then he stood, folded the quilt into its harmless square, and went back inside to start the day with his tools checked and his favorite place measured and his heart beating a little too fast in a way that felt exactly like lake air going down cold and clean.

Silena Beauregard woke to the smell of forge-smoke and the sound of somebody trying not to be happy out loud.

She knew both smells and both sounds the way she knew her own sewing kit: forge-smoke was Tyson, huge and warm in the corner bunk, sitting on the edge of it with his boots already on and his hands folded, waiting for permission to start the day. The trying-not-to-be-happy sound was Percy, back from wherever he had gone barefoot at dawn — the back step, by the cold on his feet and the quilt square under his arm — with his hair sticking up and his eyes bright and his hand going to his sweatpants pocket every few seconds like checking a wall was still there.

Monday. Last full day. Ferry tomorrow. Silena did the arithmetic lying still with her eyes half open, the way she had done it every morning since Tyson arrived, and felt the hollow shape of Tuesday press briefly against her ribs and then recede, because today was here and required feeding.

She sat up. She wore her long camp shirt for sleep, faded orange washed pale, sleeves to the elbow, over soft dark shorts, hair in a loose sleep braid she would redo before breakfast. The cabin around her was in its morning order: bunks made or half-made, pool breathing under the floor, lemon oil sharp from the tin Percy had knocked open with his elbow, towels on hooks. On the shelf above the spear rack, wrapped in oilcloth and saying nothing, lay Annabeth's tally copy — thirty-nine marks in her neat hand, counted once from the panel that now sat locked in the flat drawer in Chiron's office where the old surveys lived. Background, settled, not for today. Today was for hands and food.

Clarisse was already up, perched on the edge of her bunk lacing her boots. She wore her practice clothes for the morning: her own cutoff shirt, sleeves jagged where she had cut them herself two summers ago, faded blue and smelling faintly of lemon oil, over a plain dark swimsuit with sturdy straps made for laps, and worn khaki shorts with frayed hems and pockets that actually held things. Her braid was fresh for the day, tight and wet at the end from the pool. Tyson wore his forge shirt, dark gray with the soot washed thin, sleeves strained at the shoulders, over cargo shorts with every pocket full — bolt, charcoal stub wrapped in a leaf, round white rock transferred from pillow to pocket — and his big feet bare because his sandals had gone to a Hermes kid yesterday and never come back. Percy, she noted, had swapped the sweatpants for yesterday's jeans with the damp cuffs, cuffs rolled higher now, and his day shirt, the orange one with the trident washed white, sneakers lined up by the door waiting to be disturbed.

“Morning,” Silena said, and the room turned to her the way it always did, not because she was loudest but because she was the one who decided what morning meant.

“Morning,” Tyson said, with enormous relief, like she had rung a bell. “Silena. Today is the last full day. Tomorrow is the ferry. I counted on my fingers twice to be sure.”

“I know, sweetheart,” Silena said. Warm and perceptive, the voice that had kept this cabin's arithmetic for five years, the one that could say the true thing without making it heavier. “Today is Monday. We have all of it.”

“I have a plan,” Tyson said. He unfolded his hands. “Yesterday you asked what I wanted for the last day and I said I needed to think. I thought while I hung the laundry and while I listened with my feet on the dock. I thought inside.” He tapped his chest, where he kept the important things. “I want three things. A meal I help make. A chore I do well. And a swim where everybody floats. That is all. Ordinary things. I like ordinary things best because they happen every day, so there are more of them to remember.”

Percy made the small sound he made when Tyson reframed arithmetic without knowing it. Clarisse stopped lacing.

“A meal, a chore, and a swim,” Silena repeated. She swung her legs over the side, feet finding the cool tile, and went to Tyson and put both hands on his huge forearm where it rested on his knee, the way you would greet a wall you loved. “That's a perfect plan. What's the meal?”

“Peanut butter sandwiches,” Tyson said promptly. “With apples cut thin. Like at the forges when the work is long. Beckendorf's brother cuts the apples thin so they do not slide. I can cut thin. I have steady hands.” He held them up, scarred knuckles, charcoal faint on the back. “And lemonade with lemons, because lemons are your whole personality.”

Silena laughed until her eyes were wet. “Who told you that?”

“Percy told me,” Tyson said. “In July. I remembered inside.”

“Of course you did.” She squeezed his arm. “And the chore?”

“The cleats,” Tyson said. “Every cleat on the dock. Not just the third one. I felt them all with my feet last night. Two are loose. One is tired. I have bolts.” He patted the cargo pocket, which clanked faintly. “Percy can hold the light. Clarisse can tell me if it is straight. You can bring the oil so they do not squeak. Annabeth can write it on the map so nobody forgets. Everybody helps a little. Then it is done fast and it holds.”

Clarisse looked up, blunt and grudging-tender even half-awake. “That's a good chore. That's a real chore. No decorative oar-arms needed.”

“My arms are fine for holding,” Percy said automatically, hand going to his pocket again, and Silena saw the corner of folded paper there, soft with handling, and understood all at once where he had been at dawn and what he had been reading and why his eyes were bright. She said nothing. Some tools you did not inventory out loud.

“And the swim?” she asked Tyson.

“The north beach,” Tyson said. “Our rock. The float where you hold my ankle even though the water holds me. I want to say the true thing again. I had another one. It came out like laundry water this morning while I was waiting.” He looked from face to face, one great brown eye earnest. “Is it all right to have two true things? Or is it one per visit?”

Silena felt her throat close the way it had at lunch yesterday when he talked about shirts and drying. She kept her hands on his arm and let it be thick. “It's one per float, sweetheart. You can float as many times as the water holds you.”

“Good,” Tyson said, satisfied. “Then today I will float once. With everybody. And I will remember it inside, where there is a lot of room.”

Clarisse stood, boots laced, whistle cord around her neck. “All right. Meal, chore, swim. Committee approves.” But her voice lacked its usual velocity, and she reached out as she passed and ruffled the back of Tyson's hair with one quick, rough, unmistakably tender swipe, the way you would touch something hot to check it was real. “Eat fast. Pavilion in fifteen. If Jackson's not dressed, leave him.”

“I am dressed,” Percy protested.

“You're pocket-checking again,” Clarisse said. “You're doing the thing where you touch it every ten seconds. Whatever it is, file it and move. We have a last day to inhabit.”

Percy went pink to the ears. Tyson watched the exchange with deep approval.

“He checks his tools,” Tyson announced. “A worker checks his tools. A brother checks his pockets.”

“See?” Percy said. “Tyson understands me.”

“He understands everybody,” Silena said. She stood and tied her hair up for the kitchen, sleeves pushed higher, flour already on her mind. “Come on. Let's go feed the ordinary things.”

Clarisse La Rue had exactly eleven minutes to get her brother to the pavilion before the oatmeal went gluey, and Tyson was spending them lining up boots.

Not his own boots. Everybody's. He had carried the north laundry bin down the hill with the towels for the day, set it by the pavilion steps, and then stopped cold at the sight of the shoe pile by the door — three days of campers kicking off sneakers and sandals in a hurry — and gone to his knees in the dirt to fix it. Big hands, gentle as gates closing, setting each pair side by side, toes out, even as ruled paper. The Hermes kid whose sandals Tyson had been wearing all weekend watched with his mouth open, holding a bowl of grapes.

“Hallway's not a barracks, big guy,” Clarisse said, but without force. She stood over him with her hands in her khaki shorts pockets, braid over one shoulder, whistle cord swinging. Morning sun slatted through the pines. The pavilion smelled of toast and hot dishwater and basil from the window boxes. “Chiron doesn't inspect shoes.”

“I know,” Tyson said, not looking up. He straightened a tiny Apollo sandal with total seriousness. “But shoes that point the same way do not get lost. Shoes that point different ways run away from each other. Then somebody cries before breakfast and Chiron has paperwork.” He set the last pair down, rocked back on his heels to admire the row, and nodded, satisfied. “There. Now nobody will cry.”

The Hermes kid whispered, “Thank you,” and took his sandals and ran.

Percy, behind Clarisse with the laundry bin now on his hip because Tyson had handed it off like a baton, muttered, “He's a menace to chaos. A menace.”

“He's right about the paperwork,” Clarisse said. “Move. Oatmeal.”

Breakfast on a Monday in mid-September was the least performed meal of the week. No visitors to impress except the one they already loved, no feast, no send-off yet. Just the long tables under the awnings moving in the wind, the clatter of trays, the lake flashing beyond the pines, freshwater blue and honest. Silena went straight behind the counter to help with the lemon water, sleeves up, flour already on one forearm from stealing a roll to test. Percy slid onto the Poseidon bench with his tray — oatmeal with brown sugar, toast, an apple he saved whole — and Tyson settled beside him with oatmeal and toast and three carrots he had brought from his cargo pocket because, he explained, carrots helped you see in the dark and the dock boards had dark underneath.

Annabeth arrived two minutes later with her bag over her shoulder and graphite on her left cuff, hair braided tight for the day. She wore her gray camp shirt with real pockets, sleeves shoved past the elbows, over the same sturdy olive shorts she had worn all week, white sneakers double-knotted, pencil behind her ear. She took the seat across from Percy without asking, the way girlfriends who had earned it did, and stole his toast crust before he could protest.

“Hey,” Percy said, and the whole morning rearranged around the word. His hand went to his pocket — the note, filed — and then to her hand under the table, quick and sure. Her fingers were cold from the walk down from Cabin One. His were warm from oatmeal and sun.

“Hey,” Annabeth said. Precise and planning even in affection, but her ears went pink. “You read it.” It was not a question.

Percy nodded, throat suddenly tight over oatmeal. “Yeah. On the back step. This morning. When it was quiet.”

“Good,” she said, and squeezed his fingers twice under the table — the second one he felt all the way up his arm — and let go to doctor her oatmeal with the seriousness of a cartographer, and neither of them said anything else about it because the table was full and the day was full and some tools you checked silently. But Clarisse, watching from the end of the bench with her own bowl, saw the way Percy's shoulders dropped half an inch, the way Annabeth's pencil stopped tapping, and filed it under things that were working.

Tyson, oblivious and completely perceptive at once, pushed his toast toward Annabeth. “You can have my crusts. Percy likes the big carrots and you like the crusts. I remembered inside.”

Annabeth stared. “You noticed that?”

“I notice with my eye,” Tyson said simply. “It is big. It sees a lot.” He turned to Clarisse. “After breakfast we do the cleats. I have bolts. Three bolts. One for the loose one, one for the tired one, one extra in case the dock is hungrier than I think.”

Clarisse ate her oatmeal standing up because she ate everything standing up when there was work. “Define tired.”

“The fifth cleat spins a little when you pull,” Tyson said. “Not loose. Tired. Like it held too many boats and needs a rest. I will give it oil and a new bolt and tell it it did well.”

A Demeter girl two seats down giggled. Clarisse did not giggle. Clarisse felt something in her chest do the thing it did when Tyson talked about objects like they were people — the grudging-tender thing she hated and could not stop. Fifteen, blunt as a spear butt, terrified of middles and ends alike, standing in a pavilion with oatmeal going gluey and a brother who apologized to volleyballs.

“Fine,” she said. “After breakfast. Dock. Bring the oil.”

The dock at ten on the last full day was gold and green and smelling of warm wood and clean freshwater. The lake lay flat to the far shore, the boundary rock mid-lake with its white X chalked fresh, the willow cove to the left with the heron standing gray and still on the bow of the half-sunk skiff. Cicadas sawed in the pines. The boards were warm underfoot, the water underneath slapping softly at the pilings, cold and green and honest when you looked down through the gaps.

Tyson worked the way he did everything: totally, without irony, without armor. He lay on his belly on the dock with his head over the edge to reach the cleat bolts underneath, huge shoulders blocking the sun, tongue caught between his teeth, while Percy held the toolbox open and handed him things and Clarisse crouched beside with the oil can and the rag, calling straightness like a range officer.

“Left a hair,” Clarisse said. “Tap it. No, tap, don't hit. That's a cleat, not a discus.”

Tyson tapped it with the hammer, gentle as a heartbeat. “Is it straight?”

Clarisse sighted down the metal. “Straight. Oil it.”

Silena oiled it, sleeve tied back, hair coming down in the heat. Annabeth sat cross-legged at the end with her notebook open, updating the canoe lake inset in pencil: third cleat tight, fifth cleat re-bolted, initialed T.C. in the margin because credit mattered. Her tally copy was back on the Cabin Three shelf in oilcloth, the panel locked in Chiron's flat drawer. No mystery today. Just maintenance, which was its own kind of love.

Percy held the light even though it was daylight, because Tyson had asked him to hold the light and holding was what his arms were for. He lay on his stomach with his chin on the boards, jeans rolled to the knee, sneakers off and lined up toes-out behind him by new habit, watching his big brother's hands work a bolt the way other people watched music.

“You're good at this,” Percy said quietly.

“I am good at holding heavy things still,” Tyson said, not looking up. “The forges taught me. You hold it still and the fire does the work. Here you hold it still and the lake does the work. It is almost the same.” He tightened the last turn, tested it with his foot — listening with his feet, faster — and nodded. “This one will not wobble. Now nobody needs paperwork.”

Clarisse sat back on her heels and wiped sweat off her forehead with her wrist. “Two down. One tired to go.”

They moved down the dock in a ragged line, tools clanking, boards warm and steady under them because Tyson's morning work already held. At the fifth cleat Tyson stopped and put his big hand flat on the metal for a second before he touched it with tools, the way you would greet someone before asking for something.

“You did well,” he told the cleat, solemnly. “You held many boats. Now rest a little. I will hold you.”

Percy looked away fast, because his eyes were wet and Clarisse would enforce hydration. Silena did not look away. Silena watched with her oil can in both hands and her mouth doing the soft thing, sixteen and warm and completely undone by kindness to hardware.

Clarisse cleared her throat. “All right. Bolt it before I start feeling things at ten in the morning.”

Tyson bolted it. Percy held the light. Silena oiled. Annabeth wrote T.C. in the margin. The cleat went tight and silent, the way true things went when they were finally seated.

Tyson stood, dusted his hands, and looked down the length of the dock — every cleat straight, every board steady, the tin cup and the coil of rope coiled right — and smiled the small complete smile of a boy who had done a chore well on the last day and found it fair.

“Done,” he announced. “The dock will hold. Even when I am at the forges. It will hold without me.”

Clarisse stared at him. The words landed the way his volleyball serves landed: too much over, straight into the pines. The dock will hold without me. She had been counting all morning — hours left, meals left, tonight's packing, tomorrow's ferry — and here was Tyson saying the fear out loud again, over a cleat, like laundry water squeezed from a cloth. Blunt, sincere, unarmored. The fear-of-leaving thread, touched, not resolved. She felt Silena's shoulder bump hers, the PCS language, shoulder to shoulder, and she bumped back without looking.

“Yeah,” Clarisse said, voice a little too level. “That's the point of fixing things right. They hold when you're gone.” She stood and tossed him the rag. “Good work. Best lines all month, best cleats all summer. You're hired. Permanently. Remote.”

Tyson caught the rag and folded it, pleased. “Remote,” he repeated, trying the word. “That means I work here even when I am there. Like remembering inside.”

“Exactly like that,” Silena said thickly, and handed him an apple, because apples helped and Percy had told him so in July and he had remembered.

Percy bumped Tyson's shoulder with his own, hard, the wry observant boy checking the wall was still there. “Thanks for letting me hold the light in daylight.”

“You hold well,” Tyson said kindly. “Even with short arms.”

“My arms are normal length.”

“For a human,” Tyson said, and the whole dock laughed, and the lake kept it.

Tyson was happy because his hands smelled like oil and his feet felt the dock holding steady underneath, and because now it was time for the meal.

He had waited for the meal since morning. Not breakfast — breakfast was pavilion food, good food, toast and oatmeal that other people made. The meal was his. Peanut butter sandwiches with apples cut thin, and lemonade with lemons, made by his own hands on the flat rock at the north beach, for his brother and his sisters and Annabeth. At the forges, when the work was long, Beckendorf's brother cut the apples thin so they did not slide, and everybody ate sitting on the warm stone outside with their feet in the dust, and nobody talked much because chewing was busy work. Tyson liked that. He liked food that needed both hands. He liked sitting close. He liked ordinary things best because there were more of them to remember.

Silena let him carry everything. That was good. He was strong. He carried the big satchel with the bread and the peanut butter jar and the apples and the lemons and the tin cup and the knife wrapped in a cloth, all in one arm, and the quilt in the other arm, and Percy carried the water jug because Percy wanted to help and Tyson let him help a little so Percy would not feel useless. Percy was small but he tried hard. Tyson loved him.

The north beach was their place. Cabin Three's place. Half-moon of sand and warm flat stone, pines running right down into the water, glassy shallows over white sand, the boundary rock sitting mid-lake with its white X. Freshwater smell, green like cucumbers and stones. The heron was at the willow cove on the bow of the half-sunk skiff, gray and still, waiting the way it always waited. Tyson waved to it. The heron looked at him and smoothed one feather and looked away. That was hello in heron language. Tyson knew.

“Here,” Tyson said, and set the satchel down on the flat rock with great care. “We eat here. The rock is warm. Warm rock makes the peanut butter soft.”

Clarisse sat on the sand with her boots off, feet in the shallows. Silena sat on the rock with her mending tote at last abandoned. Percy sat cross-legged with his jeans rolled to the knee and his sneakers lined up toes-out. Annabeth sat beside Percy with her notebook closed, pencil down, which meant she was off duty. Five of them. Good number. Tyson counted on his fingers to be sure. Five.

He laid the food out biggest to smallest, because that was the right order. Bread first. Then peanut butter jar. Then apples, four of them, red and cool. Then lemons, three, yellow and bumpy. Silena had brought the sugar and the water. Percy had brought the appetite, which was also important.

“I will cut,” Tyson said. “I have steady hands. Watch.”

He cut the apples thin. Very thin. So thin the light came through. Beckendorf's brother would be proud. He laid the thin slices on the bread without sliding, peanut butter thick underneath so they stuck. He cut the lemons in half and squeezed them into the water with sugar, stirring with the long spoon Silena gave him, tasting, adding more sugar, tasting again. Lemons were Silena's whole personality. He wanted it to taste like her: bright and warm at once.

Percy watched with his chin on his knees. “You're a chef now?”

“I am a brother with a knife,” Tyson said. “It is almost the same. But safer if you are careful. I am careful.” He handed the first sandwich to Silena, because sisters first. The second to Clarisse, because she was also a sister and she was hungry even when she said she was not. The third to Annabeth, because she was Percy's girlfriend and girlfriends were almost sisters. The fourth to Percy, biggest, because Percy liked the big ones and Tyson had noticed on day one and filed it inside where there was a lot of room. The fifth to himself. Even. Fair.

They ate sitting close with their feet in the freshwater, cool and clean, sun high and ordinary, pine resin and peanut butter and lemon in the air. Nobody performed. Nobody told stories too loud. Middle-day lunch told the truth, Silena always said, and last-day lunch was almost grief, but this was not grief. This was hunger. People reached past each other. People complained about the seeds. People were themselves.

Tyson chewed and listened. He liked listening. Listening with ears was good. Listening with feet was faster, but listening with ears heard more words.

Clarisse said the bread was good and the apples were thin enough and the lemonade was too sweet, then drank three cups. That meant she liked it. Clarisse liked things by complaining precisely. Tyson knew.

Silena said the peanut butter was soft from the warm rock and asked where he learned to stir without splashing, and he said the forges taught him, you hold it still and the fire does the work. She laughed with her eyes wet and said, that's the whole deal, sweetheart. Tyson did not understand the whole deal but he was glad she had it.

Annabeth said the sandwiches did not slide and asked if she could write THIN LIKE FORGE LIGHT in the margin of her map, and he said yes, and she wrote it with graphite on her cuff. That meant it was true.

Percy said nothing for a while. He just ate fast, talking faster between bites, bumping Tyson's shoulder with his own every few minutes like checking a wall was still there. Bump. Chew. Bump. Chew. Tyson absorbed each bump with pleasure. He was a wall that loved being checked.

Then Percy went quiet. He held his sandwich in both hands and looked out at the lake, at the boundary rock with its white X, at the far shore going hazy with heat, and his face did the thing it did when he was in it all the way, no counting. Tyson saw. His eye was big. It saw a lot.

“Percy?” Tyson asked. “Are you sad? You are chewing slow.”

Percy looked at him. Fourteen and sun-browned, honey wrapper in his pocket, note in his pocket, lake in his hair. “Nah. I'm good. I'm just — this is good. You're good at this.”

“I am good at ordinary,” Tyson said. “Ordinary happens every day. So there are more to remember. When I am at the forges tomorrow night, I will remember this rock. Warm. Peanut butter soft. Everybody chewing. I will keep it inside.” He tapped his chest. “There is a lot of room inside.”

Silena made the laugh-sob sound and went to rinse her hands to hide it. Clarisse stared at the lake and blinked hard and said, “Dish towel,” very quietly, to nobody. Annabeth reached under the rock shadow where Percy's free hand lay in the warm sand and held it, her fingertips cool from the lemonade tin, his warm from the stone. She did not say anything. She did not have to. Here, kept, said the pressure of her hand, and Percy, still learning the verbs, held back.

Percy bumped Tyson's shoulder again, harder. His eyes were wet. “Thanks for the meal,” he said quietly. “Best one all month.”

Tyson nodded, satisfied. He lined the apple cores up biggest to smallest for compost. He poured the last lemonade, even shares, no favorites. He wiped the knife and wrapped it in its cloth. Chore done well. Meal made well. One thing left: the swim.

He looked at the water. Freshwater. Green. Cold underneath, warm on top from the day's sun. The biggest water he could see the other side of too. Percy had told him that weeks ago. He had remembered inside.

“After we digest,” Tyson announced, “we float.”

Annabeth Grace had mapped the north beach six times, and the water still surprised her every time she went in.

She stood on the flat rock with the sun on her shoulders and her notebook safe on the quilt, pencils capped, boots lined up toes-out beside Percy's sneakers by new habit. She wore her camp shirt, sleeves rolled past the elbows, over her swimsuit — plain dark blue, sturdy straps, made for real swimming — and her olive shorts folded neatly on the rock because Silena had taught her never to swim in pockets. Her hair was rebraided tight for water, graphite still on her left cuff no matter how many times she washed, feet bare and sandy. Below her the shallows ran glassy over white sand, then dropped to green, then to the dark gold of afternoon. Freshwater, every contour verified by wading. Cold three feet down, warm on top where the day had laid its hand. Pine resin and sun-warmed stone and clean green lake. The heron gone from the skiff at last, off waiting somewhere else.

Five of them on the rock. Last full day, ferry tomorrow. Thalia had come back Friday after three days on the boundary, the sealed note in her pack and trail mix in a sock. The panel locked in Chiron's flat drawer. The tally copy wrapped in oilcloth on the Cabin Three shelf. Background, settled. Today was for floating.

Silena explained the rules for Tyson even though he knew them, because explaining was part of it: one at a time, somebody holds your ankle so the current doesn't take you, when your ears go under you say one true thing, just one, you don't plan it, and when you come up nobody asks. If you explain it, the float is over and you're on dishes for a week. That's most of it. The floating's the point. The true thing's just the ticket price.

Tyson listened with total concentration, hands folded, BE KIND long washed off but the memory of it still on his knuckles. “One true thing,” he repeated. “Unexplained. Like a bolt.”

“Like a bolt,” Silena agreed, eyes bright. Sixteen, warm and perceptive, hair coming down in the heat, spare blue shirt already set aside for whoever shivered first.

Clarisse went first without testing, the way she did everything that mattered: quick, no drama, braid trailing. She wore her cutoff shirt loose over her swimsuit still, khaki shorts folded on the sand with her boots. She lay back, went under, came up shining, fingers tightening once on Silena's ankle and letting go. No explanation. Rule held.

Annabeth watched her future sister-in-law — not by blood, by choice, which counted more — and thought about maps. A map was a promise you made to somebody who was not there yet. She had told Percy that once. Thalia had told her yesterday in the loft: you can't cartograph grief, baby sister. Keep the place. She had drawn the visitor's bunk into the Cabin Three inset, small square in the corner labeled T, rock on the pillow. Any honest map had to admit who was sleeping where while they slept there. Tomorrow the bunk would be empty and the towel would stay on its hook and the arithmetic would close like water over a dive. She knew it. She hated knowing and not being able to draw around it. So she was practicing drawing inside it: here, warm rock, peanut butter soft. Here, fifth cleat tight, initialed T.C. Here, her boyfriend holding a giant's ankle like it was his job.

Silena went second. Hair spreading in the green light, true thing said with ears full of lake, up with no explanation, because that was the rule. Then Percy. Annabeth watched her boyfriend — fourteen, precise in his own wry way, orange shirt abandoned on the rock, swim shorts that had been his in April and were mostly string by September, lake-pebble and note safe on the quilt — fill his lungs and sink with the neatness he had learned all summer, mouth moving once under water, surfacing with his hair streaming and his face doing the thing it did when he had gotten a line exactly right. He did not explain it. She did not ask. Her hand found his under water anyway, cold and warm, graphite and lake, and held.

Then Tyson. The lake went quiet. Even the cicadas seemed to pause. He took a huge breath and went under, great brow smooth, eye closed, mouth moving once, small and unhurried. He came up streaming, smiled the small complete smile, said nothing. Paid his ticket and found it fair.

They floated in a line after that, five heads in a row, ankles held, water holding them twice. The sun slid lower. The shallows went rose-gold. A fish jumped once past the sandbar with a flat silver smack. Annabeth's fingers ached pleasantly from holding Clarisse's ankle. Percy's free hand stayed laced with hers under water, and she thought about yesterday on the dock when she had told him her true thing was about staying from the other side, about keeping him on her map exactly where he was: here, wet cuffs, honey wrapper, holding. Today he was here again, holding again, and the map held.

Tyson spoke — not the true thing, just the ordinary thing after, in his simple literal voice: “The water holds me. Even though I am heavy. It does not drop me. Thank you for holding my ankle anyway. Even though the water holds me. It is good to be held twice.”

Annabeth felt her throat close the way it did when something true went past without armor. She had spent all week making ferry plans, forge schedules, letters, like logistics would save her. Thalia was right. Keep the place. Let him go. Keep the bunk on the map. She tightened her grip on Percy's hand under water and felt him tighten back, and that was the whole answer, unplanned and exact.

Then, because five siblings and almost-siblings in a lake at golden hour could not stay solemn, Percy splashed Clarisse. Clarisse dunked him. Silena shrieked and went under to hide her laugh. Tyson lifted the nearest sister — Silena, shrieking louder — clean off her feet in one arm and set her back down like a laundry bin, apologizing to the lake for the waves. Annabeth floated on her back and laughed until water got in her mouth and she had to spit freshwater and laugh more, fourteen and precise and completely unmeasured for once, hair coming loose, graphite finally washing off her cuff into the green.

They crawled out dripping and sat with feet in the water and the quilt over shoulders, all five under one quilt, impossible and therefore exactly what they did. The wind had turned, thin edge under the gold. Clarisse's spare shirt — clean, dry, smelling of lemon oil — went to Tyson this time without comment, draped over his massive shoulders like a flag, because he was shivering in his wet forge shirt and Clarisse saw it first. He wore it proudly, sleeves to the elbow, utterly content.

Tyson sat in the middle with the quilt over his knees and lined the rescued apple cores up again biggest to smallest. “I said the true thing,” he reported to Annabeth, quietly, like confirming a delivery. “It was a good one. About staying. But from the other side. Like yours.”

Annabeth stared. “You heard yesterday? On the dock?”

“I listen with my feet,” Tyson said. “And with my ears when I am awake. The dock carries sound. You said you keep making plans. You said you will keep the place. That is good. Keeping is good. Forgetting is bad. The sky forgets sometimes.” He frowned, working it. “Thalia is sky. She goes far. But she keeps too. She keeps in her jacket pocket. I saw.”

Annabeth felt something shift in her chest, the way a sheet shifts on a line when the wind changes: not fixed, not answered, just touched. “Yeah,” she managed. “Yeah, she does.”

Tyson nodded, satisfied, and handed her the thinnest apple slice he had saved in a leaf. “For the map lady. Thin like forge light. So you remember.”

Annabeth took it. She held it with both hands. She did not eat it yet because she wanted to keep holding it a minute. Precise and planning, even in affection: that was how she loved people, by getting the details right. “Filed,” she said thickly. “Permanent collection.”

Percy bumped her shoulder, wry and observant, lake-wet and grinning. “You're Cabin Three adjacent now. No take-backs.”

“No take-backs,” Annabeth agreed, and leaned her head against his shoulder for exactly three seconds in front of everybody — cold-wet hair at his neck, her weight real and chosen and terrifying — because fourteen and girlfriend and brave enough to be seen, and the lake kept that too. Percy sat very still, the way you sit when something you were afraid to want happens on purpose. Silena smiled into the quilt and let them have the three seconds, and then a fourth, because nobody stopped her.

Percy knew the walk back would be the part he remembered longest, so he walked it slow on purpose.

Late afternoon on the switchback from the north beach, pine needles soft underfoot, lake flashing through the trees to the left going rose at the edges. Five of them in a ragged line, damp and sandy and sun-warmed, carrying the day's evidence: quilt wrung out and folded square so it would not hurt anyone, towels in a neat stack, tin cup and apple cores for compost, Annabeth's notebook with THIN LIKE FORGE LIGHT in the margin, Tyson's empty satchel now full of wet things. The air had turned thin, October edge under the gold, pine resin and dishwater and somebody's distant singing up from the pavilion. Percy's jeans were rolled to the knee, cuffs damp, sneakers slapping, orange shirt dry at last and smelling of sun and peanut butter. His hand went to his pocket every few minutes — note, pebble, hair tie, honey wrapper — tools checked, brother checked, boyfriend checked.

Tyson led, huge and happy, Clarisse's spare shirt still draped over his shoulders like a flag, cargo shorts dripping, bare feet silent on the needles. He stopped at the bend where the willows closed over the cove and looked down to the half-sunk skiff. The heron was back, gray and still on the bow, one leg folded.

“She waited,” Tyson announced, pleased. “She left and she came back. That is good. Waiting is better when you come back.”

Clarisse, behind him with the quilt over one shoulder, snorted. “Profound. Write that on your hand.”

Tyson considered. “I have no charcoal. I will remember inside.” He tapped his chest and kept walking, ducking the low branch out of long habit even though he cleared it by habit now, not by need.

Silena fell into step beside Percy, bumped him, the PCS language. “You okay?” she asked quietly, warm and perceptive, the voice that had kept his arithmetic since June.

Percy thought about it. Morning on the back step with Annabeth's words warm in his pocket. Mid-morning on the dock holding the light in daylight. Midday on the warm rock with peanut butter soft and everybody chewing. Afternoon in the line, holding Tyson's ankle while the giant said his true thing and the water held them twice. The ending coming tomorrow with the ferry, visible as weather. Thalia back from three days on the boundary. The panel locked, the copy shelved, background. None of it resolved. All of it inhabited.

“Yeah,” he said. “I'm in it. I'm not counting. Well. I'm counting a little. But mostly I'm in it.”

“Good,” Silena said. “That's the entire arrangement. You're allowed to be in it even when you can see the end.” She squeezed his arm once and let go to catch up with Clarisse, singing softly with no words, the sailing song with no words she trusted, and Clarisse fell into step beside her, and Percy felt his throat close at the ordinary precious sight of his sisters walking uphill in moon-coming light, lanterns not yet lit, cabin light showing warm through the trees ahead.

Tempest Hall at dusk was its best self: pool breathing small and steady under the floor, four bunks made up, four towels on four hooks, forge-smoke and lemon oil in the air. Tyson went straight to the corner bunk and stood over his packing with total seriousness — forge shirt folded, cargo shorts for tomorrow laid out, round white rock centered on the pillow until morning, bronze bolt for luck in the pocket, sandals borrowed back from the Hermes kid and lined up toes-out. He packed the way he did everything: totally, without hurry, without armor.

Percy sat on his own bunk and watched, quilt square on his knees, and felt the thing he had not known how to say at lunch yesterday when Tyson said he did not want to leave. He tried now, wry and observant, the boy who noticed things and sometimes said them.

“You know you're coming back, right?” Percy said. “Not just — I mean, the bunk stays. The towel stays. The rock spot stays. Silena keeps the map with the T on it. Annabeth keeps it. It's not — you're not getting erased. You're just folded somewhere else for a while.”

Tyson looked up, one great eye bright. “Like the laundry. Edges hold the shape. Shirts still get dry.”

“Yeah. Like that. You taught us that.” Percy picked at the quilt edge. “I left a palace once. I know how leaving feels like disappearing. It isn't. Not here. Here you leave footprints with your feet that listen. The dock remembers. The third cleat remembers. I remember.”

Tyson came over and sat on the edge of Percy's bunk, which creaked dramatically and held, and put one huge hand on Percy's head, completely covering it, gentle as a gate closing. “You remember inside,” he said. “Where there is a lot of room. I remember you too. You steer. I bring peanut butter. That is the promise from July. Promises are edges. They hold.”

Percy laughed until his eyes were wet, head still under his brother's hand. “Yeah. You steer — no, I steer. You bring peanut butter.”

“I bring peanut butter,” Tyson agreed, and ruffled Percy's hair once, rough and tender, and went back to his packing, and Percy sat with the quilt on his knees and Annabeth's note warm in his pocket and felt held twice, by water and by ankle, by lake and by brother, and did not bother wiping his eyes because Clarisse was pretending not to see and Silena was mending something that did not need mending to give him privacy, which was how love worked in Cabin Three.

At the door, with the dusk going blue, Silena pressed two apples and the tin cup of iced tea into his hands. “Thalia's covering patrol,” she murmured. “Don't tell Clarisse. Take the quilt. Answer it slow. No rush.”

Percy stared. “How did you —”

“Sweetheart, you touch that pocket every ten seconds and you look at Annabeth like she's the fixed point.” Silena tucked the hair tie into his pocket for tomorrow, kissed his forehead, pushed him gently toward the path. “Go. We'll keep the packing warm.”

He went, quilt over one shoulder, apples and tea in hand, sneakers slapping, heart doing the fast-and-steady thing it did when a day was ending and beginning at the very same time.

Percy found Annabeth already at the end of the dock with her feet in the water and his note in her hands.

Evening had come fast once the sun dropped — mid-September evenings did that, gold going blue at the edges, wind thin off the pines, pavilion lanterns ticking on one by one up the hill. The lake lay evening-dark under its last rose shine, freshwater cold and clean around the pilings, the boundary rock a dark shape mid-lake with its white X gone pale. A loon called once. The boards were still warm from the day under his sneakers, cooling at the edges. Tyson's cleats held the dock steady without a sound.

Annabeth wore Clarisse's borrowed blue shirt from yesterday, washed and returned and borrowed again without comment, sleeves flopping past her wrists, hem to mid-thigh over her own dry camp shirt and olive shorts, smelling of lemon oil and lake and somebody else's sister. Her hair was down now, rebraided loose for evening, graphite finally gone from her cuff, sneakers off and lined up toes-out beside his when he kicked his off. She held his note — NORTH BEACH HAS GOOD ROCK, trident lopsided underneath — folded soft from a day in with her pencils. Filed, permanent collection. She had told him so last night. She was still carrying it.

Percy sat beside her with the quilt and the tea and the apples, shoulder to shoulder, knees touching, hands finding each other on the boards without looking, fingers lacing cold and warm. He poured the iced tea into the tin cup and handed it to her first, the way Clarisse had taught him: guests first, sisters first, Annabeth first. She drank, handed it back, lemon and cold and sweet. He drank. They shared the apple, passing it back and forth, bites alternating, juice on their wrists. Two fourteen-year-olds who had chosen each other, doing everything the way they did everything: without hurry, the whole evening in front of them, no audience but the lake.

“You answered slow,” Annabeth said. Precise, planning, but soft. “You kept it a whole day. I told you not to answer fast and you actually listened. That's growth.”

“I was scared to read it,” Percy admitted. Observant and wry, the boy who noticed things, looking out at the water instead of at her because the truth came easier sideways. “Not of what it said. Of how much I'd feel. Then I read it on the back step this morning and I cried on the cold stone like an idiot and grinned at the same time, and Tyson's bunk spring went ping, and I thought, okay. That's the size of it. That's how much.”

Annabeth turned his hand over, traced the lifeline with one cold fingertip, precise as drafting — the graphite callus on her middle finger rough against his palm, the touch of a girl who read the world by its edges. “How much?”

“Favorite-place-to-measure-from much.” His voice went thick. He let it be thick. “You see the second part, Annabeth. The part where I mean it under the joke. Nobody else — well, Silena sees, Clarisse sees, Tyson sees everything. But you write it down. You make it true on paper. When you wrote that I held his ankle like it was my job and didn't let go even when my fingers went white — I didn't know anyone saw my fingers go white. I thought I hid it.”

“I notice the second part,” she said simply. “That's my job. I'm the one who notices.” She leaned her head against his shoulder. His shirt smelled like sun and peanut butter and warm rock. “Was it still true tonight? Did you check with your tools?”

Percy reached into his pocket with his free hand and pulled out two things: her note, refolded along its old creases, soft at the corners; and a new square, his own handwriting, folded twice, a little crooked where his fingers had hurried.

Her breath caught. “You wrote back.”

“Slow. Like you said. All day.” He held it out. His hand was not quite steady. “Read when it's quiet. It's quiet now.”

Annabeth took it with both hands, the way you take something precious, and unfolded it in the blue light. Inside, in his earnest, slightly slanted hand, with one word pressed harder where he had paused:

*Annabeth —*

*I read yours three times on the back step and the sky went gold while I read and I didn't notice until after. That's how good it was.*

*You said I'm a fixed point. I'm not fixed. I move like water moves, you said it right. But I want to be the kind of moving you can measure from. Like the lake. The lake moves all the time and you still map it. You just map it honest, while it's moving. I'll try to be mappable. I'll check my pockets and steer straight and hold ankles and not count when I'm in it. That's my promise. It's not edges. It's just me.*

*Today Tyson made sandwiches thin like forge light and fixed the tired cleat and told it it did well. I held the light in daylight because he asked me to. You wrote T.C. in the margin. Silena oiled. Clarisse said straight. Everybody helped a little. Then it was done fast and it held. I want us to be like that. Everybody helps a little. Then it holds, even when somebody's at the forges.*

*You are my favorite person to be un-fixed with.*

*Keep this? File it? Permanent collection?*

*— Percy*

*P.S. You were right about the step. I always go where it's warm and private when something matters. You notice everything. It's annoying. I love it.*

Annabeth read it twice. Then she pressed it flat against her knees to smooth it, refolded it along his crooked creases exactly, tenderly, and slipped it into her pocket with her pencils, patted the pocket. Tools checked. Her eyes were bright in the lantern light from up the hill. Her ears were pink. She did not wipe her eyes because he did not wipe his, and that was the agreement.

“Filed,” she whispered. “Permanent collection. Mappable. All of it.”

Percy laughed, wet-eyed, and bumped his forehead against hers, and she bumped back, and the dock rocked gently and settled, Tyson's bolts holding. They sat under the quilt — one quilt, the only way to share — with their feet in the freshwater and the stars coming out one by one over the far shore, shoulders touching, hands laced on the boards, breathing the same cool air that smelled of pine and lake and lemon and sun gone thin.

He kissed her then. Slow, lingering, unhurried, the kiss of two kids who had all evening and no urgency. Lake on their lips and apple between them. His hand came up to cup her jaw the way he always did, thumb cool against her cheek, careful as Tyson with a quilt. Her hand came up to his shoulder, fingers curling in his shirt, steadying, the cartographer holding her fixed point while both of them moved.

It did not hurry. Neither of them was keeping time, which was the whole point. The boards stayed warm under their feet from the day. The water slapped soft and regular at the pilings. The first stars came out over the far shore like lanterns being lit along a shore. He could feel her pulse where his thumb rested, quick, then slower, and knew she could feel his. Warmth, heartbeat, breath. The world went soft at the edges like mist on morning water, came back sharper and kinder, went soft again.

They broke apart by degrees — forehead first, then breath, then the last inch — eyes closed, smiling with wet faces in the blue dark.

“Hi,” he said.

“Hi.” She bumped his forehead with hers, gentle as drafting. “You're shaking.”

“So are you.”

“Good. Then it's mutual and documented.”

“Jealous?” he asked lightly, remembering last night. “Of the float? Of the ease?”

Annabeth considered, precise even here, forehead still against his. “Less. Today Tyson told me my true thing back to me. About keeping the place. He listens with his feet. The dock carries sound.” She huffed a small laugh. “I keep trying to draw around the leaving. I'm practicing drawing inside it. Here: this dock, third cleat fixed, fifth cleat re-bolted. Here: you, with tea and apples and a note answered slow. Here: me, kept.”

“Kept,” Percy repeated. He pulled the quilt tighter over both their shoulders, tucked it around her hands where they lay laced with his, the shared warmth building under the cloth. “You're kept. You're Cabin Three adjacent. No take-backs. Ten minutes per occurrence, free for girlfriends who file things.”

“Tell Clarisse that. She'll kill you.”

“She'll allow it. She gave Tyson her shirt. That's basically adoption papers.”

Annabeth laughed, and the laugh went out across the water and came back changed, and the lake kept it. She cuddled closer under the quilt, head on his shoulder, his cheek resting lightly against her hair, both of them looking out at the dark water where the heron's reflection would be if the heron hunted at night. No urgency. Just warmth, heartbeat, breath, lake air, sun's memory still in the boards under their palms, the feel of hand-holding, exact and steady.

The lanterns blurred. The loon called again. The wind brought dishwater and Silena's wordless sailing song up from the cabin path, and neither of them got up. Her breathing went slow and even against his shoulder — not asleep so much as floating, the way she floated in the lake. His own eyelids had gone heavy. He pulled the quilt up over her shoulder and kept watch over the dark water, and for a while neither of them moved.

She sat up at last, blinking, and yawned into her fist, and did not apologize, which was its own kind of trust. “We should go up,” she said.

“Yeah.” He did not let go of her hand. Neither did she.

At last they stood, folded the quilt into a square so it would not hurt anyone, collected the tin cup and the apple cores for compost, checked the cleats with their feet out of new habit — steady, silent, Tyson's work holding — and walked up the hill hand in hand through the blue dusk, sneakers slapping, borrowed shirt flapping, pockets full of tools and pebbles and love notes answered both ways.
