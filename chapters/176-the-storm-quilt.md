## Chapter 176: The Storm Quilt

The storm came in from the north-west all afternoon and did not arrive until dark, which Chiron said afterward was the insulting part.

It had been on the almanac since Sunday, in the same square hand that had been one day wrong about the frost, and this time it was one day right. Percy spent Wednesday morning watching the barometer in the Big House parlor while Annabeth copied its numbers into her field book with the enthusiasm of a girl who had been given a new instrument, and by two o'clock the pines along the ridge had started to move in a way that had nothing to do with any wind he could feel, which meant the real thing was still a long way off and already reaching for them.

"Front's coming," he said, on the porch.

"Front's been coming since breakfast," Clarisse said. She had the winter list out and had already crossed three things off it and written two new things underneath, which meant she had been thinking about the storm since breakfast, which meant she had been thinking about it since Monday. "Chiron wants the greenhouse banked, the hay in, the shutters on the west cabins latched rather than propped, and everything loose on the docks either in the boathouse or tied to the boathouse. That's a two-hour job and we've got three hours, so we're going to be smug about it."

"We're going to be smug about a two-hour job."

"We're going to be smug about a job we finished early, Jackson, because the alternative is being serious about a job we finished late, and I've decided which kind of family we are."

They did it in an hour and a half. Marcus and two of the Apollo kids took the docks. Katie took the greenhouse with six of her small ones and a wheelbarrow full of straw and a level of joy that Percy found slightly suspicious until he realized that for a Demeter kid, banking a greenhouse for a storm was the single best thing that could happen in October. Clarisse did the shutters herself because the west cabins were where the youngest kids slept and she had opinions about how a shutter should sit that had to be demonstrated rather than described.

Percy got the boathouse, which meant the boathouse, the canoe racks, the mule, and the two dock lamps, and then, because he was there and because nobody had asked him to, he went around the outside of the building and checked every tie-down he could find and replaced two of them out of the coil in the tackle room, and stood for a minute in the wind with his hands in his pockets looking at the sky.

The lake had gone the color of pewter. The wind was pulling long flat sheets of it into the reeds, and the reed margin was all one movement, all the way down the north shore, like a thing breathing; the boundary rock was white and clear, and the willows had turned their backs to it, and down in the cove the half-sunk skiff was rolling on its mooring with the heron nowhere in sight, which was the only piece of news in the whole scene, because the heron was always in sight, and the heron had gone, and the heron, Percy understood, was smarter than all of them.

"Sixty knots by midnight," Chiron said, from the path behind him.

Percy turned. The centaur had come down the hill with his coat buttoned to the neck and no clipboard, which was a rare and slightly unnerving state for him.

"That's a lot," Percy said.

"It is a great deal. It is not the worst this hill has seen and it is not the worst this hill will see, and I mention that so that nobody stays up all night being brave about it." Chiron looked out at the lake the way he looked at papers, reading it. "The cabins are sound. The hall is sound. The Big House is a great deal too sentimental, and I will be sitting in it with a lamp and a cup of tea through the worst of it, because that is what the roof is for. You will be in the cabin, and the cabin is the best-built thing at this camp because three generations of Poseidon children have argued with it."

"The cabin's got a leak over the east window."

"Then you will learn something about the east window." Chiron turned his chair back up the hill and then stopped. "Percy. The small ones will come. Wednesday night, a storm, the lights will go, and every child under twelve at this camp will decide that the correct place to be is wherever the crowd is. Let them in. All of them. There is no version of tonight where the hall stays a cabin for three."

"How many is all of them?"

"More than the towels," Chiron said, which was the most alarming thing anybody had said to him all week, and went up the hill.

The rain arrived at ten past six, all at once, the way it does when the front finally gets there: not a buildup but a wall, one enormous gray hand laid flat across the whole camp, and then the drum of it, and then the long rolling down the pines. Inside a minute the paths were streams, and inside four minutes the gutters on Cabin Three were doing something Percy had never heard them do, which was to shout.

The lights went at twenty past.

Percy was in the commons with a towel on his head when the hum that nobody had noticed all summer stopped, and the whole cabin went dark and the whole camp went dark beyond the window, the pavilion and the forge and the paths and everything, all of it at once, so fast that for a second he could see the lightning better than he had been able to with the lights on.

The cabin did not panic. That was the first surprise of the evening and it was Clarisse's, mostly: two oil lamps and a box of candles were already on the long table before anybody needed them, because they had been on the long table since three o'clock, and the fire in the stove had been built up to coals and banked twice, and there was a bucket of drinking water and a stack of the flat camp candles in tin holders going around the room like a collection plate.

"Nobody walks anywhere with a candle," Clarisse said, in the dark, in her ordinary voice. "Somebody made that rule in this cabin before I was born and they were right. If you have business in the passage, take the lamp or take a person."

"He means take a buddy," said Olive, from the middle of the room, where she had arrived eleven minutes earlier with a bag and no apparent intention of leaving.

"I mean take the lamp."

"You mean take a buddy."

"I mean take the lamp and the buddy," Clarisse said, and Olive said "Ha," and that was the end of Wednesday until eight o'clock, at which point Cabin Three stopped being a cabin for three and started being a cabin for whatever the hill sent down to it.

---

Silena Beauregard had one rule about storms, and the rule was: a storm is a kitchen, and a kitchen is a storm, and the two of them want the same thing, which is everybody in one room eating something warm.

So when the hill started sending children down to the cabin, she had already had the big pot on the coals for an hour.

It went like this. At five past eight the door banged and it was Katie Gardner with four of hers and a wet cardboard box that turned out to be, inexplicably, two cabbages and a shoe. At ten past, Olive Nakamura arrived with her own bag and a stated intention of staying the night, which she had cleared with Chiron personally, which meant she had asked Chiron and Chiron, who had no idea how to say no to a ten-year-old with a plan, had said yes with conditions. At quarter past, three of the Apollo cabin came in soaked to the shoulders with a story about their window, and by twenty past, the Apollo window had been added to Clarisse's list. At half past, Neve came up from the forge with both hands blistered from a day of wet metal and got sat down by the stove and not allowed to move. By nine there were eleven of them in the commons, and three of them were asleep, and one of them was eating soup with a shoe next to his bowl.

And in the middle of it, since there was nothing else to do with the weather, the mending started.

It started the way everything started in that family, which was with Silena quietly doing a job until somebody asked her how. She had the tote open on the long table with the needles in their paper roll and the thread in its four colours and the button jar and the flat tin of pins, and she had started on a sock, because a sock was what a person started with, and by the time Percy came in from the passage with the lamp, there were four kids at the table watching her bring a heel back to life with a wooden darning egg and a needle, which is, if you have never seen it, close to a magic trick.

"Do mine," said Rosie, holding up a sock with a hole in it the size of a fist.

"That sock's finished."

"It's not finished, I'm still wearing it."

"This sock has been retired. It has served." Silena took it anyway and put it in the pile. "Where's the other one?"

"Ares cabin."

"Then we'll do the first one and hang it on the wall as a warning."

Clarisse had the winter list spread out on the table's far end, working through it with a candle at her elbow and the lamp, and after a while she looked up and did the thing that Silena had been waiting all evening to see her do, which was to reach into the canvas satchel and take out, from the very bottom, a bundle of cloth wrapped in a paper band.

"The men's rack in the boathouse," she said, by way of explanation. "Twelve shirts and eight pairs of trousers, all torn, none of them ours, all of them going to be every kid's problem in November." She dropped it on the table with a thump. "It's a two-evening job. I've decided we're doing it tonight."

"Tonight," Silena said.

"Tonight. We're going to be a lot of hands in one room and nothing to do with them."

Which is how the storm quilt actually started, although nobody called it that until much later.

What happened first was that Neve — who was eleven and burned and bored and had been told to sit still — asked whether it was true that the north beach used to have a quilt that belonged to the fire ring. And Silena said yes, there had been a quilt, and it had been the wool one they took down all summer, and it had gone into the lake twice and come out twice and by September the batting had given up, so it had been retired up to Cabin Three where it lived on the back of the big chair. And Neve said, "So there isn't one."

And Katie said, from the far end of the table, "So we make one."

The next twenty minutes were a negotiation of enormous seriousness. It was to be a beach quilt: heavy, ugly, washable, warm, big enough for five people to sit under at a fire. It was to be made of the good parts of things nobody wore any more, which Clarisse produced from the satchel: four soft shirts past repair, a blanket with a burn, two pairs of flannel trousers with one leg each. It was to have a square from everybody who had ever sat at that fire ring, each one stitched by the person themselves, no exceptions, no help, no matter how bad it was.

That last condition was Percy's, and he was roundly argued with, and it stood anyway, because Olive Nakamura said in front of eleven people, "That way in twenty years it still says who made it," and there was no coming back from that.

So the table filled up with square panels. Clarisse cut them, because Clarisse cut things straight, and handed them out like ration cards, and the room got very quiet in the particular way a room gets when eleven people are trying to be good at something they are bad at.

Olive drew the boathouse roof, with the word UNVERIFIED stitched under it in a running stitch so small you had to hold it up to the lamp.

Rosie sewed a fish. It was a very good fish. It had an eye and everything, and the fish was the only thing on the panel, and Rosie would not say why, and it did not need a why.

The Hermes boy who had eaten the soup with the shoe beside his bowl sewed a shoe, which was the single funniest thing that had happened at this camp in a month, and which he then refused to explain to a single person, and which everybody in the room agreed on the spot had to be in the quilt.

Marcus did an axe, carefully, in grey thread, and did not talk the whole time, which was Marcus.

Neve did the forge: a hammer crossed with a pair of tongs, which in her drawing looked like the actual tools and in her stitching looked like two spoons fighting, and she looked at it a while and then said "Fine," and put it in the pile, which was the correct attitude and rare at eleven.

Katie did a leaf. Thalia, who had come down at nine from Cabin One with her sister and one boot on and the other foot in a sock, sat down and sewed a lightning bolt into her square in copper thread, crooked and unmistakable, and when Percy asked what it was for she said "Structural luck," and when he asked why the thread was copper she said, "Because copper's what I had," and that was Thalia all over.

Percy did a trident. It came out lopsided, which he had expected, and smaller than the square, which he had not, so he stitched his own initial in the corner to fill the space, and showed it to Annabeth, and she looked at it for a long moment and said, "You've put the P on the wrong side," and he said, "The P's fine," and she said, "The P is on the wrong side of the trident," and he said, "The trident's fine," and they went on like that for four minutes, and everybody at the table enjoyed it, and Rosie fell asleep across two chairs.

Annabeth did a corner of a map: three contour lines and a small triangle and the letter N with an arrow, forty-four stitches, not one of them out of place, and she did it in the time it took Percy to do the trident, and when it was done she held it at arm's length and then put it down without saying anything, which was Annabeth's way of being pleased.

The door opened at half past nine and the storm came in sideways and so did Charlie Beckendorf.

He was soaked. Not rained-on: soaked, filled, water running off the ends of his hair and out of the sleeves of his coat, boots full, tool roll held up against his chest with both arms like something he had saved from a river. He stood in the doorway dripping onto Silena's towel line and looked around the room at eleven kids and two lamps and a table full of sewing, and said, with the flat mild amazement of a large person who has been out in the weather for two hours, "What's happening in here."

"Sit down," Silena said.

"I'm wet."

"You are catastrophically wet, which is why you're going to sit down on the chair by the stove and not on my floor." She was already up and moving, which she was aware of in the way you are aware of anything your body does without asking you, and she had a towel off the line and the dry socks out of the tin drawer before she had finished the sentence. "The stove's hot. The chair's clear. Give me the coat."

He gave her the coat, which weighed about nine pounds, and sat down on the chair by the stove, and the whole commons rearranged itself around him, which is a thing that happens around a large quiet person in a room full of children: the small ones drew in, and the noise went up, and Rosie woke up and stared at him with her mouth open because he was the biggest man she had ever been in a room with.

"Lanterns," he said, to the room in general. "Six of nine up. The three at the pavilion went in this morning and the curve one I did in the rain at seven, and then the line by the big house went over and I spent an hour on the ladder with the wire in my teeth, and the wind won, and I've got two frames left and no lamp glass in them and I'm not going back up a ladder at midnight for a frame." He rubbed his face with a towel. "So I've got a bag of glass in the top of the tool roll and nine frames' worth of nothing to do with it, and if anybody in this room is bored I've got a job."

Seven children looked at him.

"You can't be serious," Clarisse said.

"I'm always serious. It's a bench job, it's frame and rivet, it doesn't need heat, and it's boring, and bored is what I've got in here."

So the other half of the long table became a lantern works, and that was the evening: eleven kids, two lamps, a stove, a mending pile, and a boy in dry socks showing a ten-year-old how to set a rivet without cracking the glass, and the rain going at the roof like something with a grudge, and the whole room warm, and the whole camp dark outside it.

Silena sat down at the end of the table with his coat over her knees and her needle, and found, in the left cuff, the tear.

It was a long one, six inches, along the seam where a coat sleeve bends, which was where every coat on that hill gave out. His work glove was in the pocket, and the glove had split at the thumb, and there was a burned hole in the other palm from a bench and two stitches already in it in somebody else's thread.

She did the coat first, because the coat was easy and she could do it without thinking, and while she did it she listened to him talk to Olive about glass.

"Amber or plain," Olive said.

"Amber. All nine."

"Why?"

"Because a person walking up a hill at night ought to see a light and think somebody's home." He said it without any weight at all, in the same voice he used for rivets, and then went on about the frame. And Silena, bent over his sleeve in the candlelight with her needle in her hand, went completely still for two seconds, and then finished the seam, which she did in the long even stitches she used for shells.

"Glove," she said, when she was done.

"It's not worth doing."

"It's a glove. Give it here."

He looked at her then. It was the first time all evening, and it was not a glance; it was a look, plain and steady, at a girl with a needle in her hand and his coat across her knees, in a room full of lamp light and other people. Silena made herself hold it, which took about everything she had, and did not say anything clever, because nothing clever existed.

"Right," Beckendorf said, and handed over the glove.

His fingers touched hers when he did it — not a grab, not a fumble, just the ordinary unavoidable contact of two people passing an object across a narrow table — and it lasted about a second, and Silena felt the whole of it from the wrist down, and did the arithmetic on how many times in her life she had passed an object to somebody without noticing, and then deliberately stopped doing the arithmetic, and threaded her needle, and sewed the thumb, and undid his stitches and put proper ones, and it took eleven minutes and she did not look up once.

At eleven o'clock the rain went from a drum to a roar and back to a drum, the wind took a shutter somewhere up the hill and nobody went to look, and Clarisse laid the finished lantern frames in a row on the floor by the wall like a military formation and said it was a good night's work and meant it, and Beckendorf, who had been told to sit down and had stayed sat down for two hours, discovered that there was now a small Apollo kid asleep on his shoulder and made no attempt whatsoever to move.

The quilt pile went into the tote. The socks went into the basket. The soup pot came off the coals and emptied and went in the sink. The candle stumps were collected and relit in the tin holders, and one of them was put on the windowsill so that anybody out in the dark on the hill could see that there was a lit room at the bottom of the path.

Silena found him at the door, at half past eleven, pulling the wet coat back on over dry socks.

"You don't have to go up," she said.

"Frames are on your floor, glass is in the bag, and I need the bench at six or the day's gone." He got the coat on and looked at the room behind her: eleven kids in a commons, three asleep, and the table half a sewing shop and half a lantern works, and the fire banked, and the lamp on the sill. "You had a kitchen and an emergency room and a factory in here tonight and nobody got hurt and everybody ate. I've been in this camp nine years. That was a good one."

"Come back tomorrow," Silena said, "and I'll have your glove."

"You've already done the glove."

"Then come back tomorrow for the other one," she said, which was the only true thing she could think of, and it came out much too fast, and she heard it happen, and did not take it back.

Beckendorf considered her for a second, in the doorway, with the rain on the step behind him.

"Right," he said again, and went out into the weather and pulled the door to behind him, and Silena stood in the passage a moment with her hand still flat on the latch, listening to him go up the path through the water, and then went back to the table and sewed, by herself, in a room full of other people's breathing, until the candles went out one by one and there was nothing left to do but put the last lamp on the sill and go to bed.

Annabeth Grace had a theory about storms, and the theory was that a storm was the only weather that told the truth about a building.

You could learn everything about a house in six hours of wind. Where the frame gave and where it held. Which window wanted a wedge and which one only wanted the shutter latched. Where the sound was a drum and where it was a song. Half the camp's cabins, she had decided after two hours of listening, were built by people who had never heard their own building in a storm, and one of them was going to be a problem by February, and she had a list, and she was not going to tell anybody which cabin was on it until she had been up in its roof.

She sat at the end of the long table in Cabin Three with the survey papers out and the lamp between her and her sister, and did the arithmetic of Saturday.

"Front's through by tomorrow night," Thalia said, chewing the end of a pencil, which she had denied doing for four days and did constantly. "Friday's clear. Friday night's frost. Saturday's — " she looked at Annabeth's field book, where the wind arrow and the pressure line were copied in small even numbers, " — Saturday's dry, with a north wind, and cold, and the ground like a board."

"That's the problem."

"Dry ground's a problem?"

"Dry ground on a ridge is fine. The problem is the seep." Annabeth turned the copy of Thalia's margin notes around and tapped the entry. "The spring comes out from under a slab that's shifted a foot since June. It rained six inches between Sunday and tonight. If the seep is fed by the ridge and not by a deep line, then Saturday it's either a trickle, because the ridge holds its water like a stone and gives it out slowly, or it's a river, because six inches went in and it has to come out somewhere. Both of those are good days. Both of them tell me something. What I can't have is the seep being the same as it was on the fourteenth, because then I've walked up a mountain to learn nothing."

"So we want the river."

"We want the river. A river tells you where it comes from by how loud it is." Annabeth drew the arrow on the fair sheet and put the pencil down. "If it's a trickle we survey the seam and the slab and we come back in the spring to see the difference. If it's a river we can rod the bed and take the fall and find out where it enters the rock, which is the whole question, and we'll be wet past the knees, and you'll be pleased and not say so."

Thalia was quiet, watching the lamp. Around them the commons was doing the thing commonses do at eleven o'clock in a storm: one kid asleep with her chair tipped back, two playing a card game on the floor with the cards held close because of the drafts, Rosie in the middle of the room under two blankets and a coat, insensible, sleeping the sleep of the just. Clarisse was in the big chair with the list on her knee and her eyes shut and every intention of being awake.

"Is he back Friday," Thalia said, eventually, in a perfectly flat voice.

"He's back Friday."

"Mail day."

"Mail day." Annabeth looked at her sister. "You're going to the pavilion at breakfast and you're going to be reading something when he walks in."

"Am I."

"Yes. Because you've already decided to, and because you told me on Tuesday that you don't rehearse, which was a lie you told yourself so you'd stop having to think about it, and because — " Annabeth turned a page, mildly, — " reading is the one thing you can do with your hands that makes you look like you've got important business."

Thalia looked at her for a long moment across the lamp. Then she laughed, once, dry and caught. "I hate that you're the smartest person in my whole life."

"You raised me."

"I raised you *badly*," Thalia said. "I taught you to read a ridge and argue with a grown-up and nothing else, and then you went and did that."

Annabeth was still smiling at the page when the lamp moved, because somebody had come and sat down next to her, which she had known was coming because the coat had a particular smell when it had been rained on and dried by a stove.

"Percy."

"Survey meeting." He put his chin on his hand and looked at the papers, which were upside down to him and which he read anyway, because he had spent a summer reading her work upside down and had gotten good at it. "Saturday."

"Saturday."

"Chiron says if the wind comes north at sixty overnight it'll take the top foot off the ridge, and the ridge up there is mostly stone so it'll just make it colder, and I said I'd ask you whether that matters and he said you'd already know."

"It doesn't matter, except that we bring two more layers and the rope goes in the bag instead of on the harness." Annabeth folded the fair sheet and put it under the field book and weighted them with the lamp, and then, because the whole evening had been fine but long and the whole camp was asleep and the storm was doing the roof a favor, she said, in a different voice, "Come and sit on the floor under the table quilt before I do one more line of work tonight. I'll be unbearable tomorrow."

So they sat on the floor at the end of the room, behind the table, with their backs against the wall and the retired wool quilt — the one that had been to the lake and back — over both their shoulders, and the storm going over the roof in long sheets, and the two of them in a warm corner of a crowded room with nobody looking at them at all, which was, Annabeth had discovered over one summer, the best possible conditions for the thing she liked most in the world.

She put her head on his shoulder. It was not a thing she would have written down or admitted, and it took about four seconds to stop being a statement and start being a fact, and then she did not think about it again for twenty minutes.

"Today I learned," she said, into the flannel, "that the wind comes off the ridge and hits the west cabins at an angle, and that if the shutters are propped on the latch side they work, and if they're propped on the hinge side they tear, and that Clarisse has known that for four years and has been doing it correctly and has never once explained it to anybody."

"That's Clarisse."

"That's Clarisse. Also that Rosie can't be removed from a sofa by any known force, and that Neve stitches like a person cutting metal, and that the whole camp turned up in your cabin tonight inside twenty minutes and nobody had to be asked." She went quiet a moment. "I've been drawing this camp since June. I know where every building is and how many steps down every path and where the ground goes. I did not know until tonight what it looks like when a hill decides where it wants to be in a storm."

"And where's that."

"Here," Annabeth said. "Obviously here. That's why the map's been wrong."

Percy did not say anything for a while, which was one of the things she liked: that he could let a sentence stand up on its own without leaning on it. Out in the passage the wind put one long hand along the wall and the whole cabin creaked, and the kids on the floor went on with their cards, and the lamp on the sill guttered and stood back up.

"Note," Percy said.

"What?"

"There's a note. In the field book. I put it in at six and then everything happened and I forgot, and now it's in there and I have to tell you, because if you find it tomorrow in front of people you'll do your face."

Annabeth sat up. "You put a note in my field book."

"Inside the back cover. Flat. Not loose."

She got the field book off the table and opened the back cover and there it was: a folded square of paper, folded along the same creases he always used, slid between the cover and the flap where it would not fall out and would not be seen. She looked at him over the top of the book with an expression she could not entirely file, and did not open it, and did not put it away either.

"In front of people I do a face," she said.

"You do a whole face. It's the best thing about you."

"It is not the best thing about me." She put the field book down and leaned back against the wall and did not open the note, which was a decision she made on purpose, because there were children on the floor four feet away and a lamp to be put out and one of them had to count the heads in this cabin before anybody slept, and because the note was going to be a private thing and a private thing needed a private minute and the minute was not now. "Tell me what's in it."

"No."

"One word."

"No."

"Percy."

"No," Percy said, and then, because she had turned and was looking at him with her chin up and her hair coming out of its plait and the quilt on her shoulders and the storm in the roof, he kissed her, and she let him, which was a decision, and it was slow and quiet and behind the table where nobody could see, and the storm took the sound of it out of the room the way it took everything else that night, and when it was over she stayed where she was with her forehead against his jaw and her hand flat on his chest and said, very quietly, "One word."

"Four seconds," Percy said.

"That's two."

"It's four. Two for you and two for me, and I'm counting up."

Annabeth breathed out something that was almost a laugh and did not move, and the lamp went on burning on the sill for anybody out in the dark on the hill who might be looking, and the storm went over the camp all night, and the cabin held.

---

Percy woke at midnight and did not know why until he heard it, which was the iron door.

It was a heavy door and it sat in a heavy frame, and in a sixty-knot wind the passage pulled it — one long hollow knock and then quiet, and then the knock again, patient, the way a thing knocks when it knows it cannot be ignored forever. Anywhere else in the camp that sound would have been the wind. Here it was the hall, and the hall full of warm salt water in the dark, and every time the door moved a hand of steam went down the passage and into the commons and made the room smell, for a second, like somewhere else.

Percy got up. He was not the only one, which he discovered at the passage door, because Clarisse was already standing in it with a lamp in one hand and her jacket on over her pajamas and her feet bare on the cold tile.

"I heard it," he said.

"I've heard it four times."

"Then why are we still standing here?"

"Because the twelfth time is when I go down, and this is the fifth." She handed him the lamp without being asked, which was her way of saying *come on*, and they went down the passage together in the dark, past the sleeping commons, into the small tiled lobby in front of the iron door.

It was warm. It was astonishingly warm: the hall had been running all evening and the tile had taken the heat and given it back, and the two of them stood in their pajamas in a warm salt-smelling room at midnight in a storm and looked at a door that was moving by a quarter inch against its latch.

"It's not the wind," Clarisse said.

"It's the wind."

"It's the wind in the shaft. There's a vent over the water and the wind comes down it and it pushes the air in the hall around and the air in the hall pushes the door, so the door's not fighting the storm, it's fighting a room." She listened to it a while, head cocked, the way she listened to a wall in a house she was deciding about. "Two of us can latch it from inside if somebody holds the lamp. I'm not doing it in the dark in bare feet."

So they did it in the light, in bare feet, and it took four minutes, and when it was latched the knocking stopped and the whole passage went quiet, and Clarisse stood with both hands flat on the iron for a moment and her ear against it, checking, the way she checked everything, and then said, "Good," and turned around and sat down right there on the dry tile with her back against the wall.

Percy sat down next to her, because the tile was warm and because there was nowhere to be at midnight in a storm.

They talked for a while about nothing, which is a thing that Percy had learned to value in the last five months and had not been able to do with anybody when he arrived. She complained about the winter list. He complained about the boats. She told him the story of the west-cabin shutters — four years old, and how she had learned it, which was that an eleven-year-old kid had been sleeping in the wrong place for eleven weeks and had come back with a cough and a cold that went into his chest, and Clarisse La Rue, who was fifteen now and had been thirteen then, had spent a week of her own summer in the rafters of three cabins with a spirit level, working out which shutters worked, and had never told anybody, and had never been thanked, and did not want to be.

"You know the thing you said in September," Percy said. "About the staying part."

"I know the thing I said yesterday about the staying part."

"Both." He had his hands around his knees and his bare feet on the warm tile. "You said you stay because you show up on the boring day. Tonight's a boring day. Tomorrow's going to be a boring day. There's going to be a week of boring days."

"There's going to be five months of them."

"I know." The wind put a hand on the roof and moved it and let it go. "I want to ask you something and then I'm not going to talk about it again, because I know how you are about talking."

"Say it."

"Am I doing it right?"

Clarisse was quiet for a while. When she answered she answered the question he had asked and not the one she could have made fun of, which was a thing she did about twice a month and which was the whole reason to be in a room with her.

"You're doing it better than anything," she said. "You came in on the boat without being told. You ate what was put in front of you. You went and fixed the tie-downs nobody asked you about, and you let the small ones hang off you at the pool, and tonight you gave a seven-year-old a swimming lesson in the middle of a storm because she asked, and the entire hill moved into our kitchen at eight o'clock and you didn't once ask how long anybody was staying." She picked at the tile with a fingernail. "That's the job. That's the whole job. There's no other part of it. I've been doing it since I was thirteen and I only learned it out loud last week, from myself, in front of you, because you made me say it."

"So I'm doing it right."

"You're doing it right, Jackson, and if you ever say I said so I'll put you in the lake."

"The lake's closed."

"Then I'll wait," Clarisse said, and then, at about ten past one, a shutter on the west cabins went — they heard it over the whole camp, one flat crack and then a long loose bang, bang, bang — and Clarisse was up off the tile and through the commons and into her boots before Percy had finished standing.

"That's the little kids' cabin," she said, at the door, coat over pajamas, lamp in hand, and Percy already had his coat and his boots half on, and neither of them said anything about the other one coming, because it was not a question that had a version where the answer was no.

The camp in the dark in a storm was the best thing Percy had seen since the first frost.

He could not see it, which was the point. There was no shape to anything; there was just a black moving world with the lamp making one orange room around the two of them, and the world going past on both sides, and the water out there somewhere in the dark, and the wind doing everything at once to everything. The paths were streams. The puddles were lakes. Somewhere above them the pines were playing an instrument. They went up the hill with the lamp between them and their heads down and did not say a word the whole way, and it took twice as long as it should have, and by the time they got to the west cabins Clarisse had water in both boots and Percy had it running off the end of his nose and neither of them had stopped once.

The shutter had torn off the middle hinge and was hanging on the bottom one, banging. The kids inside were awake — he could hear them through the wall, the specific noise of eight small children in the dark deciding whether to be frightened — and Clarisse did not stop to knock. She hooked the lamp onto the porch post and got both hands on the shutter and hefted it up off the ground and held it flat against the frame while Percy got the wire out of his pocket and ran it around the latch side and twisted it off, twice, hard, the way he had learned to do on a boat, and it was not a repair, it was a mooring, and it held.

"That's not a fix," Clarisse said. "That's a rap."

"It'll hold till Monday."

"It'll hold till Monday." She wiped her face with the back of her wrist. "Good enough. In."

They went in to check. Eight kids, four bunks, one of them crying and the rest of them pretending not to be, and Clarisse La Rue stood in a room of nine-year-olds with water running off her coat and said, in the flat ordinary voice she used for the winter list, "The shutter's off and the wind's got it and I've put a rap on it, so it's going to bang a bit and it's not going anywhere, and nobody's getting up, and in the morning somebody's going to be very smug about this." Then she looked around the room once, at eight faces, and said, "Anybody need the lamp?"

Nobody needed the lamp. Which meant: they were all right. Which meant, in the language she had invented for herself and used on a hill of children for four years, that the number held.

At the door, with the rain coming in sideways, the smallest one said, in a small voice, "Clarisse."

"What."

"Thanks for the shutter."

"It's not a shutter, it's wire." She pulled the door shut. "Go to sleep."

They went back down the hill with the lamp, in their ruined boots, at half past one in the morning, in the middle of the worst weather the hill had seen in two years, and Percy became aware somewhere on the path that he was grinning like an idiot, and when he looked over at Clarisse she was doing the same thing, and neither of them said anything about it, and that was the best conversation he had with anybody all week.

---

The morning after a storm was Clarisse La Rue's favorite thing in the world, and she had never once admitted it to anybody, because the whole pleasure of it was private.

Everything was better after a storm. The air was cleaner and colder and went into your chest like a knife that was on your side. The puddles in the low ground had frozen into flat sheets of glass with leaves in them, and every leaf looked deliberate. The camp was quieter and all the edges were sharper, and the whole hill had a washed blue look, like a drawing that somebody had gone over with the eraser and then redrawn properly.

And there was damage, which was the best part, because damage had a list.

She was up at six and around most of the camp by eight with a pencil and the winter list, and by the time the pavilion bell went for breakfast she had it: two pines down by the forge and one of them leaning in the cedar brake, the pavilion's west awning torn along a seam, the rope ladder at the climbing wall thrown clear of its pegs and lying in the grass, one shutter hinge torn at the west cabins with a wire rap holding the shutter, four lamps at the boathouse wet and two out, and the hall — checked at dawn with a lamp and her boots still wet from the night — the hall dry, warm, and unhurt.

Twelve towels. That was the other number, and it was on the list, and she had written it herself at half past six in her blocky hand: TWELVE. Because twelve people had slept in the Commons on Wednesday night and eleven of them had been sent up the hill by eight with bread in one hand and their socks in the other, and one of them, who was ten years old and had cleared it with Chiron personally, was still in Cabin Three eating toast and taking up a chair.

"Frozen," Olive announced, from the table, holding up one of the flat ice discs out of the low path. "It's two inches and it's got a leaf in it."

"Put that outside before it melts on the floor."

"It's *evidence*. It's the first ice of the year."

"It's water on my boards, Nakamura. Out."

Olive put it out on the porch rail, where it lasted most of the morning, and where Silena came and looked at it twice and then put a saucer under it so it would not drip on the rail, which was the entire difference between Silena and Clarisse in one gesture.

The eleven had gone up the hill at eight, and Clarisse had run the handoff from the porch like a shipping clerk, because that was the job nobody had assigned her and the one she had taken anyway.

"One at a time. Coats on before you're on the step. Boots laced, both of them, I'm checking."

"They're laced."

"They are not laced, they are *on*. Laced is a knot. You can't walk down a hill with a boot full of water in October, ask me how I know." She got the last two laced herself, kneeling on the boards in her coat with her own boots still wet from the night, and then stood at the door with a loaf cut into eleven pieces and a bowl of dry socks, and handed each kid a piece of bread and a pair of somebody else's socks and a sentence, which is the standard equipment for a trip up a hill in October.

Rosie, who was seven and had a fish on a quilt panel and a new skill in her face, stopped on the second step and came back and hugged her around the middle, hard, without warning, the way a seven-year-old does.

Clarisse stood still for it, arms out, holding bread in one hand and socks in the other.

"Don't make it a thing," she said, to the top of Rosie's head.

"I'm not making it a thing."

"You're making it a whole thing."

"I can swim now," Rosie said, into her coat, and then went up the hill without looking back, and Clarisse La Rue watched her go and said nothing at all for about four seconds, which in her private ledger was a considerable sum of money.

Silena had been up since five. She had, in that two-hour window, baked a batch of bread, made a pot of porridge big enough for a cabin of twelve, dried the towels that had been used by eleven borrowed kids, and finished the first full row of the storm quilt, which was now folded on the back of the big chair with three of its panels sewn together and seven pinned and waiting.

"That's a good row," Clarisse said, at the chair, with a mug in her hand and her coat still on.

"The row's crooked."

"The row's crooked because Rosie's fish is an inch bigger than everybody else's square, and the fish stays."

"The fish stays," Silena agreed, and went back to the stove, and that was the whole meeting.

Clarisse took her mug to the end of the table where her own square was still sitting under the lamp, untouched, and looked at it for a while.

She had cut it and handed out every other square in the room the night before, and had then spent two hours going around helping people thread needles, and had never sewn her own, because she did not know what to put on it and had decided that not knowing was a reason to do it later, which was a lie she had told herself in plain sight and which had worked at the time.

She sat down and threaded the needle, which took one attempt because her hands were good, and thought about what a person put on a quilt panel.

Olive put her building. Rosie put a fish. Marcus put an axe, because he was Marcus. Neve put her trade. Katie put her mother's plant. Thalia put a lightning bolt for structural luck, which was the single most Thalia panel that could possibly exist. Percy put a trident and his own initial in the corner, and Annabeth put a piece of a map of a place that had not been surveyed yet, which was the single most Annabeth panel that could possibly exist.

Clarisse looked at her own square of soft grey flannel and thought about the fire ring at the north beach, and the five of them in the frost, and the count.

Then she took the needle and put it in at the corner and pulled it through, and sewed, in plain cotton thread, four short upright strokes and one across, which is the old way of writing *five* when you have no pen and only a needle, and then she sat there a while with it in her lap and did not put it in the pile until Silena came round the table with the bread and saw it and did not say a word about it, which was the way this family said things.

The door opened at ten past eight and Charlie Beckendorf came in with the ninth lantern frame under his arm and a canvas bag in the other hand, and two pairs of dry socks rolled together on top of the bag.

"Socks," he said, and put them on the end of the table. "They're yours. Washed them at four. Frame's finished, it needs glass and I've got the glass at the bench, and the three at the pavilion are on their hooks and lit, so the west path is done end to end."

"End to end," Clarisse repeated, from her chair, without getting up, which was her highest compliment and was understood as such.

"End to end." He looked around the commons, at the bread and the porridge and the twelve towels on the line and the quilt on the chair, and his face did the thing it did when he was cataloguing material, which was that it went completely still. "Did you sew the row?"

"Silena sewed the row."

"Who sewed the fish?"

"A seven-year-old from the Demeter cabin. Which is why the row's an inch crooked at the end, and the fish stays."

"The fish stays," Beckendorf said, and meant it the way Clarisse had meant it, and it was the first time in five months that she had found herself agreeing with a Hephaestus kid in front of witnesses, and she decided not to examine the feeling.

He found Silena in the kitchen corner with the porridge, and Clarisse, who was in the big chair with a mug and the winter list and a clear view of the whole room, watched the two of them do the thing they had been doing for a week, which was: stand two feet apart, talk about nothing with great energy, and not look at each other for more than one second at a time.

"Glove," Silena said, and handed it over.

He looked at it: the thumb stitched, the burn hole darned over, the two bad stitches of his replaced with four good ones, and the palm patched across the wear line with a piece of the same grey flannel as the quilt squares.

"That's a better glove than the other one now," he said.

"Then bring me the other one."

"That's what I was going to say."

"Then say it."

"Bring me the other one," Beckendorf said, and Silena laughed out loud in her own kitchen before she could stop herself, and the sound went around the room, and Clarisse, from the chair, with her list on her knee, made a note on the corner of the page which said nothing at all and looked exactly like every other note on the list, and which she then drew a box around, because a box made it a task rather than a person's private business, and because Clarisse La Rue had a rule about other people's business and had never once broken it where anybody could see.

Annabeth read the note on Thursday morning, at the end of the bench on the Cabin One porch, in the cold, with a mug of tea going cold beside her and her boots on and her survey bag already packed by the door.

She had waited for it on purpose. That was the whole thing about the note, and about the week, and about the way she had learned to be in this family: it had gone into her field book on Wednesday night and she had left it there through eleven hours of storm and sleep and one very loud breakfast, because a note was not a fact and did not have to be dealt with at the speed of one.

Percy's handwriting leaned uphill and gave up on the ends of words. There was a trident in the corner, lopsided and earnest, the way there always was.

*For the back cover. Flat. Not loose.*

*Wednesday. Nobody tells you this so I'm telling you. The best nights on this hill are not the big ones. The campfire, the north beach, the last warm night — those were the big ones and I'd take them again tomorrow. But last night was a Wednesday, and there was a storm, and the whole hill came and sat in our kitchen, and eleven kids slept on our floor, and you fell asleep against a wall with a quilt on your shoulders and a pencil in your hair. That's the one I'd keep if I only got to keep one. I don't know why yet. I'm working on it.*

*Second thing. Saturday. I know you've got the pressure numbers and the wind and the whole arithmetic. I just want to say that whatever the seep does, the day is going to be good, because you'll be up on that ridge with your sister and a rod and a book, and you've been wanting it since August, and I've watched you want things before now, and this is the first one I've seen you let yourself have.*

*Third thing, and then I'm done, because three things is a letter and four is a speech.*

*Four seconds. Two for you and two for me. I'm going to bring it up in February and you already know it.*

*-- P.*

Annabeth read it twice, which was her standard, and then a third time, which was not, and then sat with it in the cold on the Cabin One porch and did the thing that a note is for, which is to take a person somewhere and set them down.

She could hear the camp waking up around the hill: the pavilion bell on its hook, the smoke going up straight because the north wind had blown everything else out of the sky, the pines dripping. The frozen puddle on the low path had gone to water at the edge and stayed ice in the middle, and the leaves in it looked like something in a display case.

Saturday. Thursday was mail day, and the pouch was coming up the hill this afternoon; Friday was Calder back; Friday was Thalia pretending to read something at breakfast and doing a bad job of it. Saturday was going to be the survey: a slab the size of a cabin door, a seep under the granite, a cairn with a blue rag on it going northeast in a southwest wind, and a sheet of paper that had been blank since August with a heading on it that she had written herself.

Annabeth got out her pencil, which was the only pencil, and opened the field book flat on her knee, and on the back page, under the note, in her small upright record hand, she wrote her reply.

*Kept. Both.*

*Saturday: two layers, the rope in the bag, and no guessing. If the seep's running, we rod it and thank the rain. If it's not, we draw the seam and come back in the spring. Either way I'm standing on it, and you're carrying things, and she's bringing the rag.*

Then, because four lines were four lines and it was Thursday and the frost was coming and she had learned that the people in this family answered letters, she added one more, in the same small hand, without any flourishes, the way she added a measurement.

*Also you should know that I only got one of the two seconds and I have it.*

She put the pencil behind her ear and closed the book and held it for a second against her knees, and then she got up and went inside past the windowsill where the granite stone sat with its words on it, where it had lived since the week her sister came home, and she put the field book on the table by the door so she would not forget it, and went to find a second layer, because it was Thursday, because it was going to be cold, and because she intended to be standing on that ridge on Saturday, in the wind, with her family, writing down what was actually there.
