## Chapter 121: The Cabin Three Pact

Percy had learned to recognize the sound of Clarisse leaving.

Not the door. The door to Cabin Three never shut quietly. It swelled in damp weather, scraped the threshold, and complained all the way into its frame. The sound was more specific: the little pause after the scrape, while everyone inside decided whether to call after her.

This morning, Percy heard the pause.

He opened one eye. The cabin was still blue with early light. Silena’s bunk was empty, but the kettle on the kitchenette counter was steaming. Clarisse stood at the door in boots and a battered jacket, spear balanced against one shoulder. She had made it as far as the handle.

“Going somewhere?” Percy asked.

Clarisse glanced back. “The arena.”

“To fight?”

“To practice.”

“With the spear?”

“That’s generally how it works.”

“You could tell us before you reach the door.”

Clarisse’s gaze sharpened. Percy sat up, suddenly aware that he had managed to make a normal question sound like a challenge. The spear tip caught a narrow stripe of dawn.

“I’m allowed to walk to the arena without filing a report,” she said.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Around the silence, the cabin settled. Water ticked in the pipes, and beyond the window, gulls had begun arguing over the day’s first breakfast.

“I wasn’t asking for permission,” Percy said. “I wanted to know where you were going.”

“Now you do.”

“I do.”

Clarisse waited, as if she expected a second question disguised as the first. Percy didn’t have one. He knew the difference between wanting to know and wanting to control. He also knew the uncomfortable little drop in his stomach when someone he loved disappeared around a corner without warning.

“I’ll be back before breakfast,” she said at last. “If I lose a fight with a training dummy, nobody needs to know.”

“I’m telling Silena.”

“Then I’ll fight you instead.”

The door scraped shut behind her.

Percy lay back against the cold pillow, wool and sea-salt in his nose, but sleep had already left. The pause before Clarisse went out had followed him into the room. It wasn’t that she had done anything wrong. She was going to practice. She had said so. But lately the three of them had been carrying around so much unsaid worry that a quiet exit could feel like a question with no answer.

Silena came out of the kitchenette with three mugs. “Was that Clarisse?”

“She’s at the arena.”

“I know. She told me.”

Percy sat up. “When?”

“Just now. She asked me to keep the kettle warm.” Silena set a mug beside his bunk. “You were asleep.”

“I was pretending.”

“Then you were convincing enough to fool me.”

Silena handed him the mug and sat on the edge of the mattress opposite. Her hair was still loose from sleep, one side flattened. She looked at the door, then at him.

“You thought she was leaving without telling us,” she said.

“I thought she was leaving without telling me.”

“She did tell me.”

“Right.”

“That doesn’t mean you shouldn’t have known. It means she told one of us.” Silena wrapped both hands around her tea, steam curling against her chin. “We’ve all gotten used to keeping track of one another in different ways.”

Percy looked down at the drink. It smelled like mint and honey. “I don’t want to make you report every step.”

“I know.” Silena leaned forward and bumped his knee with hers. “I like knowing when my siblings are going somewhere. It lets me worry about the correct things.”

The door scraped open again. Clarisse stepped in for a warmer shirt. “I told Silena where I was going.”

“She told me,” Percy said. “I wasn’t asking for permission. I wanted to know.”

Clarisse paused, shirt in hand. “All right.”

“I don’t need you to ask permission. I want you to check in with me, and I should check in with you, too.” Percy set his mug on the floor. “Not every time we take three steps. When we’re going somewhere, or if we’re leaving camp. So nobody has to guess.”

Clarisse pulled on the shirt. “And if one of us doesn’t want to explain?”

“Then say that.”

Silena’s eyes moved between them. “That sounds like the beginning of a plan.”

“It sounds like a tiny plan,” Percy said.

“Those are the plans that survive contact with Clarisse.”

“I’m standing right here.”

“I know. I’m making sure you hear the compliments.”

Clarisse tugged the shirt into place. “If this is a plan, I want the terms to be clear. I don’t want to have to explain every time I need a minute alone.”

“You shouldn’t have to,” Percy said.

“And I don’t want anyone deciding that needing a minute means something’s wrong.”

Silena nodded. “Agreed.”

“But,” Percy said carefully, “if you need space, could you tell us that? Just say you need space. You don’t have to tell us why right away. We can leave you alone without wondering if you’re never coming back.”

Clarisse’s expression settled into something harder to read. “You think I’m going to vanish?”

“No. I think any of us could get upset and walk off before we know what we want to say.”

Silena looked into her tea. Percy knew the trick: saying “I’m fine” when he meant “please don’t make me explain this yet,” then being surprised when people believed him.

“I’ve done that,” he said. “Walked off. Told people I was fine when I wasn’t. It doesn’t help.”

“You do it when you think you’re making things easier for us,” Silena said.

“That’s one reason.”

“Sometimes you do it because you don’t know what you’re feeling yet,” Clarisse said.

“That too.”

“And sometimes,” Silena added, “you’re being stubborn.”

“That seems unrelated.”

“It’s not.”

Clarisse sat on the edge of her own bunk. The heavy spear leaned against the wall where she had left it. “I can agree to say when I need space. I can’t promise I’ll always say it gracefully.”

“Nobody expects graceful,” Silena said. “I can barely do graceful before tea.”

“You curtsied into a wall once.”

“The wall moved.”

“It did not.”

Percy watched them bicker, the easy familiarity of it easing the tightness in his chest. Then Clarisse looked at him again.

“And the other part?” she asked.

“Which other part?”

“The part where nobody has to earn anything.”

He blinked. “I didn’t say that.”

“You were thinking it loudly.”

Silena’s smile turned softer. “We’ve all been talking about Atlantis. About being guests and family and whether the palace will know what to do with us. I think it’s been on all our minds.”

Percy remembered the invitation, and the questions that had gathered around it: who belonged where, who was being asked, who might feel like an extra person in somebody else’s home. The same questions had followed them to camp in different clothes.

“I don’t want either of you thinking you have to prove you’re my sisters,” he said. “Or that you have to prove you belong here, in the cabin, in my life. You don’t have to be useful or tough or nice about everything. You’re already here.”

Clarisse’s jaw shifted. “You think I need to prove it?”

“I think we’ve all spent a lot of time getting good at being what people need. And maybe it’s easy to think that’s why we get to stay.”

Silena’s fingers tightened around her mug. “That’s true.”

“It is?” Percy asked.

“Sometimes I try to make sure everyone is comfortable before I notice whether I am. It’s a useful skill. It’s also exhausting.” She looked between them. “I don’t want you to keep me because I can turn a bad day into a nicer one.”

“I’d keep you even if all you did was make bad tea,” Percy said.

“The fish kettle was clean,” Silena said.

“It smelled like fish.”

Clarisse snorted. The laugh escaped before she could stop it. “You’re both going to keep me even if I’m not nice about a bad day?”

“Especially then,” Silena said.

“I can be nice,” Clarisse said, with the air of someone defending a technicality.

“You can be,” Percy agreed. “But you don’t have to be.”

Clarisse looked down at her hands. He knew better than to fill the silence for her. The sun had edged higher, coloring the shells set into the cabin walls with a faint gold. The moment had begun as a question at the door, and now it was trying to become something larger.

“If we’re going to do this,” Silena said, “we should make it something we remember. Not a list that gets left under a mug.”

“The list can go under the mug if it has to,” Clarisse said. “I’m not having a ceremony with a ribbon and a speech.”

“I wasn’t going to use a ribbon.”

“You were thinking about a ribbon.”

“I was thinking about a small ribbon.”

“No.”

“What about an object each of us already has?” Percy asked. “Something from when we were little. We could bring them and say what we mean.”

Silena glanced at him. “You still have the shell?”

“Of course.”

“The blue one?”

“It’s gray.”

“It’s blue-gray.”

“That’s an entirely different color.”

“It’s the shell you used to put under your pillow because you thought the ocean could hear you better that way.”

“So you keep telling me.”

“You were seven.”

“That’s little.”

Clarisse looked from him to Silena. “What object?”

“I don’t know yet,” Silena said. “I have something.”

“The bracelet?” Percy asked.

Her eyes widened a little. “You remember that?”

“You wore it all summer. The first one you made from sea glass.”

“It was the first one I made that didn’t fall apart in a day.”

“It didn’t fall apart in two days,” Clarisse said. “That’s a respectable improvement.”

“It lasted a week.”

“A week is practically permanent.”

“What about you?” Percy asked Clarisse.

She glanced at the drawer by her bunk. “I have something.”

“The panther?” Silena asked.

Clarisse gave a suspicious look. “Why do you know about that?”

“You brought it with you when you moved cabins. You put it in the top drawer and pretended it was a weapon.”

“It has sharp ears.”

“It’s wood.”

“Wood can be sharp.”

Silena smiled into her tea. “Then it’s settled.”

“Nothing is settled,” Clarisse said. “We haven’t agreed to a ceremony.”

“We agreed to the idea,” Percy said.

“The idea of objects. That’s not the same as a ceremony.”

“We could call it a meeting.”

“A meeting with sentimental objects.”

“That’s a ceremony,” Silena said.

“You’re making it sound worse.”

“I’m making it sound accurate.”

Clarisse looked at Percy, then at Silena. “Fine. We can do it. But I’m not wearing a flower crown.”

“Nobody asked you to.”

“Good,” Clarisse said. “Because there will be no flowers.”

Clarisse stood and picked up her spear. “I’m going to the arena now. I’ll be back before breakfast. If one of you needs me, I’ll be at the east end.”

“That was a good check-in,” Percy said.

“Don’t grade me.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You absolutely were.”

“Maybe a little.”

“I’ll hit you with the training dummy.”

“I’ll put it in the pact.”

“There is no pact yet.”

“Right,” Percy said. “The meeting with sentimental objects.”

Clarisse made a sound that might have been a laugh and left, this time without the pause.


---

By the time breakfast ended, Percy had discovered a problem with having a sensible family rule: he wanted to demonstrate immediately that he was excellent at following it.

This was unfortunate, because the first person he encountered after breakfast was Clarisse, and she had been awake for hours.

He had not gone looking for her. He had an actual reason to be at the arena. Chiron needed somebody to return two blunted swords that had turned up in the dining pavilion's storage cupboard, and Percy had volunteered before anybody could point out that the swords had probably been left there by him.

He carried them over one shoulder, hilts facing down. Annabeth had offered to help him identify the maker's stamps, but she had a drawing lesson with a younger camper and had refused to let his ability to misplace weapons rearrange her morning.

The practice yard was busier than he'd expected. A line of campers stood near the fence with practice shields, and Clarisse had arranged three straw targets in a staggered row. She was showing a small Hermes boy how to keep his feet under him when he changed direction. The boy was making the same mistake every time, reaching for the next target before his back foot was planted.

"Again," Clarisse said.

"I did it."

"You hit the target. Then you fell over."

"I didn't fall."

"The dirt caught you. Very heroic of the dirt."

Percy leaned the swords against the weapons rack. He should have left. The boy was red-faced enough without an audience, and Clarisse had told him where she would be, not invited him to supervise her morning. But he noticed a practice shield near the fence with a loose strap and picked it up.

"That's mine," said a girl from the Apollo cabin.

"Do you mind if I fix the buckle?"

"Do you know how?"

"That's a painful question."

She examined him for a second and handed it over.

The buckle wasn't broken. The leather had been threaded through the wrong slot, leaving the handle loose against a wrist. He knelt at the rack, set the shield on its edge and worked the strap free. By the time he'd started threading it back, the girl had returned to the line and Clarisse had noticed him.

"You're late," she called.

"I didn't know I had an appointment."

"Neither did I."

One of the other campers laughed. Percy lifted the shield and tested the grip with his forearm. It held.

"I brought Chiron's swords," he said. "Apparently they're not supposed to live with the breakfast plates."

"Tragic news for the swords."

"I'll break it to them gently."

Clarisse went back to the Hermes boy. Percy waited until the drill had begun again before leaving the shield on the rack. He was at the gate when he heard the boy swear, the short panicked sort of word that came when a foot slipped.

Percy turned.

The boy had driven his heel into a shallow rut along the edge of the sand. He wasn't hurt, but his leg was twisted under him and he was trying to stand before he'd untangled it. Clarisse stopped him with one hand on his shoulder.

"Stay down for a second. Move your ankle."

"It moves."

"Good. Now stand. Slowly."

Percy reached the fence. "The rut's deeper on that side."

"I can see it," Clarisse said.

"I could fill it."

"You could. But I'm halfway through a drill, and you are distracting three people."

He looked at the line. Three faces immediately turned away from him.

"Right. Sorry."

Clarisse guided the boy toward the bench. "He can get water. The rest of you move the targets six feet north. Nobody uses that corner until we've raked it."

Percy picked up the rake leaning beside the rack.

"Percy."

He stopped with the handle in his hand.

"I said we would rake it. I didn't say you had to do it."

"I thought it would help."

"It would. That's not the point."

He couldn't find a response that didn't make him sound like a sulky child whose sister had rejected a present. He set the rake back.

"You already know what you're doing," he said.

"Usually."

"I know."

"Then why are you still standing there?"

Because the boy had fallen. Because he'd seen an obvious problem and hated the idea that he could walk away while somebody else handled it. Because Clarisse was his sister, and he was learning that love in this family did not always look like being wanted in the middle of somebody else's work.

"Because I came over without asking, and now I feel stupid leaving," he said.

Clarisse considered him with the sort of expression she reserved for an opponent who'd unexpectedly told the truth.

"Good," she said. "Then we're making progress."

"That's a horrible way to encourage people."

"I'm not an encouragement machine."

"I noticed."

She nodded toward the rack. "You can hold the far end of the fence while I move the targets. After that, I don't need another instructor. Can you manage that?"

"Yes."

"Without coaching my campers?"

"I'll do my best."

"Your best had better look suspiciously like silence."

He held the fence while she and two campers pulled the targets clear of the uneven ground. The fence post had worked loose in last week's rain, and moving the heavy stands across it would have sent the post over. His job was dull, specific and, as it turned out, necessary. The plank pressed against his palms; Clarisse did the directing. Nobody asked him to make a speech about it.

Once the path was clear, Clarisse dug the rake into the sand and handed it to the Hermes boy.

"You found the rut. You help fix it. The yard belongs to all of us."

The boy took it and began dragging sand into the depression. The Apollo girl joined him without being asked.

Clarisse came to the fence where Percy stood. "You didn't have to come here to check on me."

"I brought swords."

"And then stayed."

"That part was me being nosy."

"I don't mind you visiting," she said. "I mind you showing up and deciding I can't manage my own job."

His first instinct was to insist that he'd never thought that. His second was to defend every helpful thing he'd done in the last ten minutes. Both would require treating Clarisse like she had to establish the facts of her own work for his approval.

He tried the harder answer instead.

"I'll ask next time."

"Good. You can also just watch. It isn't a crime."

"You told me not to distract your campers."

"I said you were distracting them. That's not the same thing as banning you forever."

"So I have visiting hours?"

"You have a mouth. Use it."

That should not have been as difficult as it was. He leaned against the post, which now held firm under his hands.

"Can I stay until they finish the next round?"

Clarisse looked toward the line of campers. "You can stay by the fence. Don't yell advice. And if the Hermes kid falls again, I will handle it."

"Deal."

She gave him one swift nod and went back to the center of the yard.

He watched. The Hermes boy planted his back foot properly on the second attempt. On the third, he forgot and tripped over his own shield. Clarisse did not pretend that it was a success, but she also didn't let the boy's embarrassment turn into the whole story. She showed him the movement one more time, had him repeat it without a weapon, and let the next camper try.

Percy noticed a dozen choices Clarisse made that he'd missed when he was busy trying to help. She paired the fastest child with the slowest only after giving both a task suited to their skill. She altered the order of the targets for a camper who'd learned to anticipate the pattern. She told a pair of friends to stop laughing at each other's mistakes, then laughed herself when one of them launched a shield directly into the empty water trough.

When the drill ended, Clarisse brought the campers into a loose circle. She assigned the rake and targets to two of them, the battered practice shields to the others, and said the lesson's final part was putting the equipment away correctly.

The Hermes boy lingered after the rest had scattered.

"Am I still allowed back tomorrow?" he asked.

Clarisse looked genuinely confused. "Why wouldn't you be?"

"I kept falling."

"That's why you practice."

He absorbed this, then grinned and ran after the others.

Percy waited until the boy was out of earshot. "You're good with him."

"I know."

"I don't think he knows."

"Then he'll learn." She wiped her forehead on her sleeve. "You can say nice things about my work without acting like you're surprised I can do it."

"All right."

"And don't repeat the bit where I was nice."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

He picked up the empty water bucket and held it up in question. Clarisse pointed him toward the trough. That was enough of an answer.

As they walked back toward the cabins, she did not tell him everything she'd thought during the drill. He did not ask. Their conversation went instead to which dummy was due for a new straw stuffing, whether the kitchen could spare grain sacks, and why the Hermes children treated borrowed practice swords as though they were communal souvenirs.

At the fork in the path, Clarisse stopped.

"I'm going to the storeroom," she said. "Back at the cabin by lunch."

"You don't have to give me a destination every time you change paths."

"No," she said. "But I said I would this morning, and I can afford to practice."

Percy looked at the fork, then at the swords he'd returned. "I think I'll go help Silena."

"Ask first."

He winced.

Clarisse smiled without showing her teeth. "You're welcome."

She turned toward the storeroom. Percy set off toward the pavilion, trying not to make another family policy out of one good conversation.

By afternoon, the plan had acquired two witnesses, one location, and several arguments about the meaning of the word “formal.”

Annabeth listened as Percy explained on the path toward the beach, her map tucked under one arm. Thalia walked beside her. Percy had asked them to witness, making clear they could say no.

“So the three of you are promising to make room for each other, check in, and ask for space instead of disappearing,” Annabeth said.

“Right.”

“And you want us there?”

“Only if you want to be.”

“I do. I care about you and your sisters. Also, if Clarisse agreed to a ceremony, I want to see the historic event.”

“It’s not a ceremony,” Clarisse called.

“That’s what all ceremonies say,” Thalia said.

Silena glanced back. “She’s excited.”

“I am not.”

“She’s emotionally engaged.”

“I’m going to throw your bracelet into the sea.”

“You’d retrieve it,” Silena said.

“I would,” Percy added.

“You’re both terrible witnesses,” Clarisse said.

The beach was a narrow strip where camp met the Sound. The low tide left pools between dark rocks. The storm had passed, though a few clouds still crossed the bright sky.

Silena had brought a cloth square for the three objects. Percy had brought his shell in a small pouch, not because it needed protection but because he did not trust his pocket not to be full of sand. Clarisse had brought the carved panther in her jacket pocket. She held it in her closed hand, knuckles pale.

Annabeth took in the beach, then the cloth. “You chose a good place.”

“We did,” Percy said. “It’s close enough to camp that we’re not leaving camp.”

“But close enough to the sea that you can be dramatic,” Thalia said.

“The sea is not dramatic.”

The waves came up the sand, curled around a stone, and withdrew with an audible sigh.

Thalia pointed at them. “That was a sigh.”

“It has a lot going on.”

“The sea has centuries of unresolved feelings,” Silena said. “Let it have a moment.”

Clarisse planted her spear butt in the sand. “Can we do this before Percy starts interpreting waves?”

They arranged the cloth on a flat rock above the tide line. Percy took the shell from the pouch and set it in the middle. It was small enough to disappear inside his palm, ridged and smooth in alternating places. The color changed depending on the light: blue-gray in the shade, almost silver when the sun reached it.

“Triton says I found it near the shore by the old house,” he said. “I don’t remember the day. Since coming here I keep it in my pocket, thinking if I hold it close enough, I can hear the sea even when I’m far away.”

“Did you?” Annabeth asked.

“Sometimes.”

“That could have been the shell.”

“I know.”

He smiled at it. His altered childhood remained stories other people told him, but carrying the shell at camp was his own habit now. The little object had crossed moves and losses before he knew its story. It didn’t need to explain all that; it only had to be there.

Silena placed her bracelet beside it. Sea-glass beads in uneven shades of green and pale blue sat on a faded cord. One bead had a cloudy white mark through its center. The clasp had been repaired with a narrow bronze loop.

“This was the first one I made,” she said. “I picked up the glass on the beach and strung it on a cord. I thought it was beautiful because the sea had made it smooth. I kept making bracelets for people, and then I had to make more because the first ones broke. This one was the first one I kept for myself.”

“You gave it to me once,” Percy said.

“I let you try it on. You got it stuck on your wrist and panicked.”

“It was tight.”

“You were trying to take it off over your fist.”

“It was an enormous fist.”

“It was a tiny wrist,” Clarisse said.

Silena’s smile softened. “I kept it because it was mine before it was a gift. I wanted to remember that I could make something lovely and not have to hand it away.”

Percy felt the sentence settle between the three objects. He looked at her and saw the girl who had offered him a bracelet that summer and the sister who could offer a kindness without disappearing inside it.

Clarisse was last. She took the panther from her pocket. It was dark wood, worn smooth where fingers had touched it over and over. Its carved body was compact, muscles gathered beneath the surface. One ear was chipped. Its face was stern enough to resemble Clarisse when she was trying not to smile.

“It’s a panther,” Thalia said.

“Good observation.”

“I was checking that it wasn’t a furious turnip.”

“The turnip would have better manners.” Silena leaned closer. “I’ve never seen it this close.”

“It stays in the drawer.”

“Why?” Percy asked.

Clarisse rolled the little carving in her palm. “It’s from when I was a kid. Someone gave it to me, and I kept it. That’s all.”

She looked as if she might stop there. Percy didn’t urge her on. The fact that she had brought it said enough.

“It was the first thing I had that was mine and stayed mine,” she said after a moment. “I didn’t have to share it or explain it. I didn’t have to be good at anything for someone to give it to me.”

Silena’s fingers brushed the edge of the cloth, not quite reaching for Clarisse’s hand. “That’s a good reason to keep it.”

“I know.” Clarisse set the panther opposite the shell and bracelet. “There. Three sentimental objects. Everyone satisfied?”

“I am,” Annabeth said.

“I’m not sure the panther is,” Thalia said. “It looks like it’s judging all of us.”

“It’s got good instincts,” Clarisse said.

Percy looked at the three objects gathered in a small triangle. The sea beyond them shifted light from one wave to the next. The objects had survived for different reasons, from different parts of three childhoods, but here they sat side by side without competing over whose story mattered more.

“Before we promise anything,” Annabeth said, “what does ‘no one has to earn a place’ mean?”

“That we’re family without having to perform it,” Percy said. “Nobody has to be useful, agreeable, or tough enough. And we don’t make each other prove we belong when we’re upset.”

Clarisse nodded. “Good.”

“Checking in isn’t asking permission,” Silena said. “We say where we’re going and when we expect to be back, especially if we’re leaving camp. If plans change, we tell someone when we can.”

“And if we need space, we say so,” Percy added. “No explanation required right away. We can say when we’ll check back in, so nobody has to guess.”

“And the space is real,” Thalia said. “No following someone to make sure they’re okay.”

“Unless there’s an emergency,” Silena said. “Then get safe first, check in after.”

“Obviously,” Clarisse said. “I’m not holding a family meeting while a monster eats somebody.”

“We also don’t use the pact as a weapon,” Silena said. “A missed message doesn’t cost anyone their place, and nobody has to stay in a conversation they can’t handle.”

“If the check-in time changes, say so,” Clarisse added. “Even ‘I’m safe, still need time’ is enough.”

“Clear and reasonable,” Annabeth said.

“I like clear,” Clarisse said.

“We’ve noticed.”

Percy laughed. The sound let the pact become ordinary again, something they could carry without making it too solemn to touch.

The afternoon breeze lifted the edge of the cloth. Percy set a hand on it, steadying the corner. A gull landed on a nearby rock and watched the proceedings with severe interest.

“We have a witness,” Thalia said.

“It’s looking for lunch,” Percy said.

“That’s how all good witnesses behave.”

“Do we need to say anything in order?” Silena asked.

“We can each say what we’re promising,” Percy said. “Then the other two agree. No signatures.”

Clarisse nodded once. “Good.”

Silena glanced at the objects, then at Percy. “You go first. You brought the shell.”

Percy’s stomach fluttered, salt and nerves sharp at the back of his throat. He hadn’t expected the actual speaking part to feel harder than deciding the words. He kept his eyes on the shell until he found a sentence that sounded like his.

“I promise that you don’t have to earn a place with me,” he said. “You’re my sisters whether you’re helping me, arguing with me, or sitting in the cabin doing nothing. I promise I’ll tell you when I’m going somewhere, and I’ll let you know if I need space instead of pretending I’m fine until I disappear. If I get it wrong, you can tell me. I’ll listen.”

The words were plain. He liked them better that way.

“I agree,” Silena said.

Clarisse looked directly at him. “I agree.”

Percy turned to his sisters. “Your turn.”

Silena took the bracelet carefully between her thumb and forefinger. “I promise I won’t make myself easy to keep by hiding when I’m tired, or hurt, or not ready to take care of everybody else. I’ll tell you when I need room. I’ll check in when I leave, not because I need permission, but because I want you to know I’m safe and coming back. And I promise you don’t have to be entertaining or brave or nice to belong with me.”

“I agree,” Percy said.

“I agree,” Clarisse said, softer than before.

Silena set the bracelet back down. Clarisse turned the panther over once in her hand. Its chipped ear caught a fleck of sun.

“I promise I won’t make you guess whether I’m coming back,” she said. “If I need space, I’ll say it. If I leave camp, I’ll tell you where I’m going and when I think I’ll be back. If something changes, I’ll let you know when I can. And you don’t have to prove you deserve to stay with me. You’re family. That’s not a reward for good behavior.”

Percy felt his throat tighten around a laugh that wasn’t quite a laugh. “I agree.”

“I agree,” Silena said. She reached across the rock and held out her hand.

Clarisse looked at it. “What?”

“This is the part where we put our hands together.”

“Why?”

“Because it’s a ceremony.”

“It is not a ceremony.”

“It’s a promise,” Percy said. “We can put our hands together for a promise.”

Clarisse considered the argument, then set her hand over Silena’s. Percy put his on top. Their fingers pressed together awkwardly; the rock made the arrangement too low, and the shell had to be moved so nobody crushed it.

“There,” Silena said. “That’s better.”

“You said no ribbon,” Clarisse reminded her.

“I did.”

“I’m watching you.”

“I know.”

For a few seconds, the three of them stood with their hands together above the objects. No light flashed. No water rose. The sky did not split open to announce a treaty. The sea continued its quiet, patient work along the shore.

That was enough. More than enough. The promise did not need the gods to approve it or the world to change around it. It needed three people who meant to try.

Percy took his hand away first. Silena placed the bracelet around her wrist, where the old cord settled against her skin. Clarisse slipped the panther back into her pocket. Percy returned the shell to its pouch.

“Does that make us officially a pact?” he asked.

“It makes us officially the three people who made a promise,” Annabeth said.

“That’s a long title.”

“You can shorten it.”

“Cabin Three Pact,” Silena said.

Clarisse folded the cloth. “Fine. But if anyone asks, it was a meeting.”

“A meeting that ended with a pact,” Thalia said.

“You’re not in the pact.”

“I’m aware.”

“Good.”

Thalia’s voice shifted, losing some of its teasing edge. “I’m not in the Poseidon pact. But I’d like to make a promise of my own.”

Annabeth looked up at her. “You don’t have to.”

“I know.” Thalia’s gaze moved to her sister. “That’s why I’m choosing to.”

Percy stepped back from the rock with Silena and Clarisse. He wanted to give them room without making a display of it. Annabeth stood beside him. The moment belonged to the Grace sisters, but it was close enough for him to hear.

Thalia faced Annabeth. “I promise you don’t have to be the little girl I protected to be my sister. You don’t have to need me in the same ways you used to. You don’t have to prove you’re brave by never asking me for help, either. I’ll ask before I step in. If you say you need space, I’ll believe you and give it to you. I won’t decide that a closed door means you don’t want me in your life.”

Annabeth’s expression changed. Percy saw the effort it took for her to stay still, to let the words reach her before she answered.

“I promise I’ll tell you when I need space instead of leaving you to guess,” Annabeth said. “And I’ll tell you when I do want you with me. I won’t treat every bit of your worry as a rule I have to fight. I know you’re trying to keep me safe. I’ll try to tell you when help feels like help and when it feels like you’ve already decided for me.”

“That’s fair,” Thalia said.

“And I promise you don’t have to be my guardian every second to be my sister.”

Thalia’s lips pressed together. Then she let out a breath. “I’d like that.”

“I’d like it too.”

Annabeth held her hand out. Thalia took it, then pulled her into a brief hug. Annabeth leaned into it. Thalia didn’t hold on too long or let go too fast. Percy watched his girlfriend’s shoulders relax against the person who had once carried her through storms, and who was now learning not to carry her when she could walk beside her.

Silena wiped beneath one eye. Clarisse saw and pointed at her. “Don’t start.”

“I’m not starting anything.”

“Your eyes are wet.”

“The wind is salty.”

“We’re at the beach.”

“Exactly.”

Clarisse handed her a clean corner of the cloth. Silena took it with a smile and dabbed at her eye.

“You’re both ridiculous,” Clarisse said.

“You brought a panther,” Silena reminded her.

“The panther is practical.”

“Of course.”

“It is.”

“I believe you,” Percy said.

“You’re not supposed to encourage her,” Annabeth said.

“I don’t know how to do anything else.”

“That is a known weakness.”

“I’m working on it.”

They gathered the objects and folded the cloth. Clarisse tucked the panther into her jacket; Percy slipped the shell into his pocket. The tide had crept closer while they talked.

“I’m going back to the cabin. I’ll be there until dinner,” Clarisse said. “If I go to the arena, I’ll tell you.”

“I’ll be at the pavilion, then the lake,” Percy said.

“I’ll come with you,” Silena said, falling into step beside Clarisse. “Unless you need space.”

“I’ll say so.”

Thalia headed back with them. Annabeth stayed behind. “I’ll be there in a minute,” she told her sister.

“See you at dinner,” Thalia said, then caught up with Clarisse and Silena.

Percy and Annabeth remained by the water. He could still feel the shape of the shell in his pocket.

“How do you feel?” Annabeth asked.

“Good.”

“Just good?”

“Good and a little like I might cry.”

“Those can both be true.”

“Apparently.” He glanced at her. “You okay?”

“Yes.”

“That sounded like the real yes.”

“It was.” Annabeth looked down at the shallow water covering the sand near her boots. “I liked hearing Thalia say it. I know she loves me. I know she doesn’t want to control me. But it helps when she says she’s trying to change what she does, not what she feels.”

Percy nodded. He understood the difference. “You shouldn’t have to prove you can take care of yourself before she trusts you.”

“No.” Annabeth glanced at him. “You shouldn’t have to prove you’re worth keeping by being useful.”

“I’m trying to remember that.”

“We can remind each other.”

“That sounds like another pact.”

“It’s not a pact. It’s a relationship.”

“Those have terms?”

“They have conversations.”

“That sounds harder.”

“It is. The paperwork is terrible.”

He smiled. “I’m glad you came.”

“I wanted to.”

“As a witness?”

“As your girlfriend. As Clarisse and Silena’s friend. As someone who thinks it matters when people choose to stay and say so out loud.”

Percy felt the answer settle warm in his chest. “I like that you chose to come.”

“I like that you asked.”

The waves slid forward and slipped back, leaving a thin shine on the sand. Annabeth brushed a fleck of grit from his sleeve. The gesture was small and familiar, but Percy looked at her as if it had been a present.

“You have sand on your face,” she said.

“Where?”

“There.” She touched his cheek with her thumb. “You’ve been thinking about the tide too much.”

“I thought you liked thinking.”

“I do. I don’t like when you get distracted by your own thoughts and forget you’re standing next to me.”

“I’m sorry.”

“You’re here now.”

“I am.”

He took a careful half-step closer. Their relationship had survived with their memories, and they kept making room for each other, one conversation at a time, as the plans around them changed. Percy didn’t want to assume that the ceremony made this moment his to take. He wanted the ordinary question, the one that kept the choice present.

“Can I kiss you?” he asked.

Annabeth’s smile was small and certain. “Yes.”

She leaned in, and he met her halfway. Their kiss was brief and gentle, warm against the salt air, a quiet answer after the promises they had chosen to make.
