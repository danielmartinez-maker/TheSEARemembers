## Chapter 29: Rainy Day

The rain started on a Tuesday during sword practice and did not stop.

Percy Jackson stood under the eaves of the arena with twenty other campers, watching water sheet off the roof in a silver curtain, listening to Clarisse argue with Mr. D about whether rain counted as an excuse.

"It is just water," Clarisse said, arms crossed, spear over her shoulder. "Ares cabin trains in water."

"It is lightning-adjacent water," Annabeth said from beside Percy, rain dripping off her ponytail. She'd come straight from the Zeus cabin porch at a run, her gray eyes bright, her camp shirt sticking to her shoulders. "Have you looked at the sky? Those clouds are charged. You can smell the ozone."

Percy could smell it too, sharp and metallic, like the air before his dad got angry. Which, considering his dad was Poseidon and her dad was Zeus, made rainstorms a kind of complicated family argument overhead.

"Please," Mr. D said, waving his wine magazine like it could hold back weather. "No one is getting electrocuted on my watch. Paperwork. Indoor activities. Rec room. Big House. Go be juvenile delinquents somewhere dry."

A groan went up. Three days, the harpies chittered from the rafters as they folded up the target dummies. Three days of it, this front stalled over Long Island, low and sulky and soaking.

Percy glanced at Annabeth. She glanced back. Her hand found his sleeve and tugged, just a quick pinch of fabric between her fingers.

"Three days indoors," she whispered. "We're going to kill each other by Thursday."

"We will not," Percy whispered back. "We're excellent at indoors. We're indoor kids."

"We are absolutely not indoor kids."

"We can learn."

The run to the Big House was a stampede of squealing twelve-year-olds and older teenagers trying to save their dignity and failing. Water stood ankle-deep in the dips of the path. Percy's sneakers squelched. Grover yelped as mud sucked one sandal clean off his hoof, and Ella, the little rust-feathered harpy who followed Grover around like a lost duckling, swooped down squawking and retrieved it in her talons, dropping it back into his hands with a proud chirp.

"Book! Book saved! Shoe book!" Ella said, flapping water everywhere. Since the spring Ella had been collecting words the way other harpies collected shiny bottle caps, repeating and hoarding them.

"Thank you, Ella," Grover laughed, hopping on one foot to pull it on. "My hero."

Inside, the Big House rec room smelled like old woodsmoke and wet wool and hot chocolate. Someone had already dragged every couch and cushion into a lopsided fortress around the Ping-Pong table. Travis and Connor Stoll were shaking rain out of their hair onto everybody. Thalia Grace stood on the hearth, hair frizzed huge from humidity, holding up a soggy cardboard box like a trophy.

"Mythomagic tournament!" she announced. "Double elimination! Loser has to clean the Pegasus stalls when this clears!"

Cheers. Boos. Percy felt his whole chest lift.

Mythomagic — the collectible card game with gods and monsters and ridiculously overpowered special abilities — was the great equalizer at camp. Annabeth was terrifying at it. She had a binder organized by pantheon and synergy and attack cost, with little sticky notes in her neat, furious handwriting. Percy played by vibes and loyalty to cool art. His favorite card was Poseidon Unleashed because the trident looked awesome when it foiled, even though Annabeth insisted its energy cost was inefficient.

"You're going down, Jackson," Annabeth told him, already pulling her deck box out of her backpack like she'd been carrying it just in case. Which she absolutely had. "I rebuilt my Zeus Ascendant deck last night."

"You rebuilt it at midnight instead of sleeping again, didn't you?"

"I slept. For four hours. That's a full night for geniuses."

"You are so weird."

"You love it."

Percy grinned so wide his cheeks hurt. "Yeah. I do."

They ended up at a card table by the window with Silena Beauregard and Beckendorf, rain hammering the glass beside them, the whole room slowly steaming dry. Outside, the green had turned to soup. The cabins crouched gray and dripping. Lightning flickered far out over the Sound, too distant to hear.

Silena shuffled like a casino dealer, her pink nails flashing. "Okay, ground rules for the rain league. No stealing cards. No crying if your god dies. No using charm powers to make people trade."

"That last one was one time," Travis called from across the room.

"It was three times!"

Percy drew his opening hand. Poseidon Unleashed. Hippocampus Rescue. A common Cyclops that he loved because it reminded him of Tyson, even though Tyson was at the undersea forges and Percy missed him with a sudden pinch. And — oh no. Ah, crud. The Ares Rage card Clarisse had traded him last week after he beat her at dice. It was terrible in his deck. He kept it anyway because she'd signed it in silver Sharpie: *DON'T SUCK, JACKSON.*

Annabeth opened with a textbook play. Of course she did. Temple Offering into Oracle Consultation, drawing two, discarding one with a little frown of concentration, tongue caught between her teeth. Percy loved that face. He loved it so much he almost forgot to play on his turn and she had to kick his shin under the table.

"Ow! Hey!"

"Play."

"I am playing! I'm strategizing!"

"You're staring."

"I'm doing both."

Silena snorted. Beckendorf hid a smile behind his hand. Rain drummed and hissed. Somewhere across the room, the Stolls started a betting pool on match outcomes using jellybeans as currency, and Chiron's hooves clopped thoughtful circuits around the tables, pausing to peer at hands and murmur, "Interesting choice. Very bold. Very... mortal."

By lunch, Percy was 2-1, Annabeth was 3-0, and the rec room windows had fogged completely opaque. Someone drew a trident and a lightning bolt in the condensation. Someone else drew a heart around them. Percy pretended he didn't see Annabeth go pink when she noticed.

Lunch was soup and grilled cheese ferried over from the pavilion under umbrellas by very grumpy dryad servers. Everyone ate on the floor picnic-style because the tables were all Mythomagic battlefields now and nobody was moving. Percy and Annabeth shared a blanket by the fire, knees touching, trading halves of sandwiches without asking. She liked his crusts. He liked her pickles. It was an excellent system.

"Day one isn't so bad," Percy said, leaning his head back against the couch.

Annabeth bumped her shoulder into his. "Talk to me on day three when Connor has organized a full casino and someone has cried over Mythomagic."

"Has anyone ever cried over Mythomagic?"

"Last summer Michael Yew cried when his Artemis card got eaten by a hellhound. Actual tears."

"Okay, fair."

Outside, the rain kept falling. Steady. Patient. Unbothered by twelve-year-olds or schedules or plans. Percy watched it stripe the windows and felt, for the first time in weeks, his shoulders come down from around his ears. No quests. No prophecies. No training dummies. Just cards and soup and Annabeth's knee warm against his.

He could do three days of this.

By three o'clock, Silena Beauregard had spent the afternoon visiting her friends in the Aphrodite cabin. It smelled like nail polish and rain and somebody's too-sweet strawberry shampoo, and she was officially bored out of her mind.

Not that she'd admit it out loud. The Aphrodite cabin had standards. Boredom was ugly.

They'd done makeovers. They'd done face masks — Drew had made hers too thick and she'd looked like a marshmallow ghost and everyone had laughed so hard they'd had to redo them. They'd braided and rebraided each other's hair. They'd ranked every boy at camp by hair. Percy won, annoyingly, because of the whole windswept-that-isn't-on-purpose thing, though Silena privately thought Beckendorf's close-cropped curls were better. They'd played two rounds of Truth or Dare that got shut down by the Aphrodite counselor Lacy when Drew dared Mitchell to run to the Ares cabin in just a towel.

And then the rain kept falling, and the little cabin with its pink curtains and heart-shaped windows got smaller and smaller, and Silena felt that itchy, restless feeling crawling up her spine.

A knock banged on the door.

Everyone froze. Nobody knocked on the Aphrodite cabin in the rain unless it was important or stupid.

Lacy opened it. Standing on the step, soaked to the skin, mud up to her knees, spear in hand like she'd marched through enemy territory, was Clarisse La Rue.

"I got bored," Clarisse announced.

The whole cabin stared.

Clarisse, who called them perfume princesses. Clarisse, who once said blush was for people who didn't know how to bleed properly. Clarisse, dripping on their heart-shaped welcome mat.

Silena recovered first. She always recovered first. That was her job. "Oh, thank the gods. Come in, you're freezing. Lacy, towels! Drew, move your stuff off the good chair!"

"I don't need—" Clarisse started.

"Sit," Silena said, pointing.

Clarisse sat. It was very funny how fast she obeyed. Silena tossed her a towel and a dry Ares shirt some previous camper had left behind ages ago during a capture-the-flag laundry mix-up, and shooed everyone else into giving them space by the window seat, where the light was best even grayed by rain.

"What are you doing?" Clarisse muttered as Silena knelt behind her with a brush.

"Your hair is a disaster," Silena said cheerfully. "It's all tangled from the rain. I'm going to fix it. Don't argue. You came to my friends' cabin. I get to help enforce the guest rules."

Clarisse grumbled something that might have been agreement. Up close, her hair was thick and dark brown, heavy with water, knotted at the nape where her helmet strap rubbed. Silena worked gently, starting at the ends like her mother had taught her, humming under her breath.

"You braid?" Drew asked from her bunk, curious despite herself.

"My mom used to," Clarisse said shortly. "Before — whatever. I usually just tie it back."

Silena caught Lacy's eye. *Be nice,* she mouthed. Lacy nodded.

Silena sectioned the hair into three smooth parts. "I'm doing a crown braid. It'll keep it out of your face for sparring when this clears, and it looks fierce. Hold still."

It was quiet except for rain on the roof and the soft tug-tug of the braid. Clarisse's shoulders, hunched up to her ears when she came in, slowly lowered. Silena's fingers moved on autopilot — over, under, gather, smooth — and she found herself talking, low, just for Clarisse.

"You know, you don't have to be bored alone. You can just come over. People think this cabin is only about makeup, but mostly we just... hang out. Mitchell tells the worst jokes. Lacy knows like forty camp songs. It's nice."

Clarisse was silent a long moment. "Your friends' cabin smells good."

Silena laughed. "That's the highest compliment you've ever given anyone."

"Don't tell Ares cabin."

"Your secret's safe."

When Silena finished, she held up a hand mirror. The braid circled Clarisse's head like a laurel, neat and tight and gleaming, a few raindrops still caught like beads. Clarisse stared at herself.

"Huh," she said gruffly.

"You look beautiful," Silena said firmly. "Fierce-beautiful. Don't argue."

Clarisse touched the braid carefully, like it might bite. "Thanks," she mumbled, ears red. "It's... good. For battle."

"For battle," Silena agreed solemnly. "And Lacy's going to do your nails clear so they don't chip when you punch things."

"What? No—"

"Clarisse. Cabin rules."

Clarisse held out her hand with the air of someone surrendering a sword. Drew snorted. Mitchell started another round of jokes. The rain poured and poured, and Silena sat cross-legged on the window seat with Clarisse's head in her lap while the polish dried, and the itchy bored feeling faded into something warm and sleepy.

Outside, thunder rolled, soft and far away. Inside, somebody started braiding Silena's own hair in return — Clarisse, clumsy at first, tongue sticking out in concentration, but careful, so careful not to pull.

Silena closed her eyes and let her.

By dinner on day one, the Big House smelled like wet dog and popcorn, and Thalia Grace was losing her mind.

She loved camp. She loved her patrol crew, her sister's company and her friends and the piney air. But she was not built for sitting still. Zeus kids weren't. Annabeth got quiet and thinky when she was cooped up, building card houses out of Mythomagic decks and taking them apart. Thalia got buzzy, static under her skin, sparks snapping off her fingertips when she drummed them too fast.

After dinner — more soup, plus pasta that the Stolls immediately weaponized — Chiron clapped his hands and said the rec room would stay open late for board games and quiet activities, lights out extended by an hour due to weather. A cheer went up. Thalia grabbed Annabeth and Percy by their hoods.

"You're coming with me," she said.

"Where?" Percy asked, mouth full of breadstick.

"Zeus cabin. I'm teaching you a real card game. Not that Mythomagic nerd stuff. A real one."

Annabeth perked up instantly. "Is this the one Mom—"

"Yeah," Thalia said, softer. "Yeah, this one."

The Zeus cabin in the rain was the best place in camp, Thalia thought, even if she was biased. White marble went silver in storm light. The tall windows rattled pleasantly. The bronze braziers in the corners threw warm gold over everything. It smelled like rain and electricity and lemon polish. The spare bunk was neatly made, ready if Jason could ever visit from the Roman camp, lucky punk missing the flood, and Thalia's bunk was a disaster of blankets, and Annabeth's corner was impossibly tidy: books stacked by size, dagger sheath hung straight, a little framed childhood photo of her with Zeus at the Grand Canyon, his face in it identified for her by Thalia that Thalia pretended not to have stared at.

They sat on the floor around the low table, rain lashing the windows. Thalia produced a battered tin box. Inside was a deck of regular mortal playing cards, edges soft with age, the backs printed with faded airplanes. TWA. The box smelled like cigarettes and hairspray and old leather, even after all these years.

"My mom played this on tour," Thalia said, shuffling. Her hands knew the motion even though she hadn't done it in months. Riffle, bridge, cut. "Beryl Grace. Eighties rock? Backup singers, long bus rides, rainy days stuck in motels. She learned it from her drummer. It's called Spite and Malice. Or Skip-Bo if you're a coward and buy the boxed version."

Percy took the cards reverently. "These were your mom's?"

"Yeah." Thalia felt the familiar twist, love and ache braided tight. "She wasn't... she wasn't always great. You guys know that. But rainy days, when we were stuck inside some motel and she wasn't drinking yet that day, she'd teach me this. We'd play for hours. She'd cheat outrageously and I'd call her on it and she'd laugh so hard—" She stopped. Cleared her throat. "Anyway. It's a good rain game. I'll teach you."

Annabeth leaned in, eyes shining. She loved learning rules. Percy leaned in because Annabeth leaned in.

Thalia dealt the piles. "Okay. Goal is to get rid of your stockpile. Twenty cards face down, flip the top. You build center piles ace to queen in order, any suit. On your turn you play from your hand, your stockpile, or your discard piles. Kings are wild. It's part strategy, part spite — hence the name — because you can block people by holding cards they need."

They played a practice round open-handed. Percy was terrible immediately and delighted about it. "Wait, I can put this here? No? Why not? Annabeth, why are you looking at me like that?"

"Because you just helped me win on your own turn," Annabeth said sweetly.

"I was being nice!"

"This game is called Spite and Malice, Percy. Niceness is a liability."

Thalia laughed so hard she snorted, sparks popping off her bracelet. "Gods, you're both hopeless. Again. Real round. Loser has to polish Zeus's statue in the cabin. Which is dusty. Very dusty."

They played three rounds. Rain hammered. Thunder grumbled closer now, a low rolling sound that made the marble hum. Annabeth won the first round by counting discards — of course she did — Percy won the second by pure dumb luck and a king he screamed so loud about that a camper next door banged on the wall, and Thalia won the third by saving a queen for six turns just to slap it down dramatically.

"You held that queen the whole time?!" Annabeth demanded.

"Spite," Thalia said solemnly. "And malice."

Percy was laughing so hard he fell over sideways into Annabeth, who shoved him but didn't move away, letting his head rest against her shoulder. Thalia watched them with a warm, aching fondness. Twelve. They were twelve and already so steady, so sure. It made her chest hurt in a good way.

"Your mom sounds cool," Percy said quietly, picking up on the mood the way he sometimes did, surprising everyone. "When she was... good days."

Thalia shuffled again, slower. "She was. On a good day she would like this crowd. She'd try to dress Percy up in stage costumes and quiz Annabeth on state capitals for money."

Annabeth smiled. "I'd take her money."

"Yeah, you would have." Thalia dealt again. "One more round? Storm's not stopping."

"One more," they chorused.

Outside, lightning stitched the clouds white. Inside, safe and dry, three Zeus-and-Poseidon kids played their living mother's old tour game while rain washed the world clean.

On the morning of day two, Grover Underwood woke up to water dripping on his nose and Ella the harpy staring at him upside down.

"Drip! Drop! Plip!" Ella announced cheerfully, head cocked. Feathers everywhere. She was perched on the foot of his bunk in the attic of the Big House where the satyrs crashed during overflow, her rust-red wings damp and fluffed, her talons clutching a soggy picture book. "Rain rain rain! Sky leaking!"

"Yeah," Grover groaned, wiping his face. "Sky's leaking, all right."

The attic roof had sprung a leak right over bunk seven. The floor was a puddle. Grover's spare socks were soaked. His reed pipes were fine — thank Pan, he'd tucked them inside his pillow — but his notebook of nature observations was damp at the edges, the ink of yesterday's entry (*Day 47 of strawberry watch: berries sad. Me sad. Rain too loud for thinking*) feathering.

Downstairs, the camp was already going stir-crazy. He could hear it through the floorboards: Travis yelling about a poker empire, Clarisse and Sherman arm-wrestling over the last muffin, someone practicing oboe badly. Day two. Cabin fever setting in.

Grover sat up, horns catching the blanket. Ella hopped closer, offering him the book. It was *Where the Wild Things Are,* swollen with humidity, spine cracked.

"Fort?" Ella asked hopefully. She'd learned the word yesterday from the Apollo kids. "Fort fort fort? Blanket fort? Grover fort?"

Grover looked at the leak. Looked at Ella's wide amber eyes. Looked at his soggy socks.

"You know what, Ella? Yes. Blanket fort. Best idea you've ever had."

Building a blanket fort with a harpy was chaos. Ella kept stealing blankets to line her nest instead of holding corners. She kept getting tangled and shrieking "TRAPPED! TRAPPED! RESCUE!" until Grover untangled her, laughing. They dragged every spare blanket, couch cushion, and curtain they could legally borrow — plus one tablecloth Chiron pretended not to see them take — into the driest corner of the library, under the big round window that looked out over the drowned green.

Grover engineered. Satyrs were good at this — Pan's blessing, understanding how branches fit. He wedged broom handles between shelves, draped blankets, pinned with clothespins borrowed from the laundry harpies, weighted corners with books. Ella supervised from inside, arranging pillows with intense focus, chirping, "Cozy cozy cozy!"

When they finished, it was perfect. Low and cave-dark, smelling like wool and old paper, rain pattering muffled on the window above. Grover strung his little battery fairy lights — the ones Juniper had given him for Valentine's — around the entrance, and they glowed warm gold. Ella gasped.

"Stars! Inside stars!"

"Our own stars," Grover agreed, crawling in. His hooves thumped the floorboards. Ella immediately curled into his side, small and warm and damp-feathered, tucking her head under his chin the way she'd done since she was a fledgling he'd found crying in the woods last winter, terrified of storms.

"Storm loud," Ella whispered now, as thunder rolled. Her talons kneaded his sleeve. "Sky shouting."

"I know," Grover murmured, wrapping a blanket around both of them. He pulled out his pipes, soft. "Want a quiet song? For fort only?"

Ella nodded vigorously. "Quiet song. Fort song."

Grover played very softly, just breath through reeds, a lullaby Juniper hummed to her dryad saplings. Outside, rain needled the glass. Inside the fort, the air went honeyed and still. Ella's breathing slowed. Her grip loosened. She mumbled sleepily, "Grover safe. Ella safe. Fort safe. Words safe..."

Percy and Annabeth found them an hour later, ducking in with two mugs of hot cider and rain in their hair, grinning at the fairy lights.

"This is amazing," Annabeth whispered. "How did you get the corner to hold?"

"Engineering," Grover whispered back, puffing his chest. "And clothespins."

Ella woke enough to chirp, "Guests! Guests guests! Cider cider!" and demand a sip, then fell back asleep with her beak in Grover's elbow, snoring tiny whistles.

They sat in the fort and drank cider and listened to rain, talking in low voices about nothing — Mythomagic scores, whether the lake would flood the canoe rack, whether Mrs. O'Leary would like rain or try to eat it. Grover felt his own restlessness drain away. The wild was out there, drowned and muddy, but the wild was also in here: the smell of wet wool like sheep, the warm pile of friends breathing together, Ella's feathers soft against his cheek.

Pan would have liked blanket forts, he thought drowsily. The god of the wild, sheltering small things from storms.

"Stay as long as you want," he mumbled, half-asleep himself. "Fort's big enough."

And it was.

Zoe Nightshade had survived fifteen years of winters, several camp monster alerts, and extremely annoying heroes. She could survive three days of rain.

What she could not survive, she decided on the afternoon of day two, was six bored Hunter-apart campers plus Bianca di Angelo asking "Are we there yet?" about the end of the storm every eleven minutes.

They were camped in the Artemis cabin — Phoebe, Naomi, Bianca, and three younger girls — playing what appeared to be a game involving flicking ambrosia squares at each other's foreheads. Bianca's forehead had a sticky square dead center. She looked deeply undignified and extremely twelve.

"Zoe," Bianca pleaded when Zoe came in shaking rain off her cloak. "Tell us a story. Please. Phoebe's flicking game is rigged and Naomi is cheating and I'm going to die of boredom and Hades will kill me again for dying indoors."

"I cannot tell stories," Zoe said automatically. "I am on watch rotation."

"You're off rotation," Phoebe said. "I checked the list. You're hiding in here because the Big House is too loud."

Zoe sniffed. It was true. The Big House currently contained a full Stoll-run casino using pretzels as chips, a sing-along led by an Apollo kid with a lyre missing two strings, and Percy Jackson attempting to juggle Mythomagic booster packs. Retreat was tactical.

"Please, Zoe?" The little ones chimed in. "A scary one? A real one?"

Zoe looked at the rain sheeting the windows, the gray light, the circle of expectant faces. She thought of cold mountain nights at her mother Hecate's shrine, of the women there telling tales to pass the dark. She sighed, unpinned her cloak, and sat.

"Very well. But not Artemis tales — thou knowest those. I will tell thee of Hecate."

Bianca sat up straighter. Daughter of Hades, always hungry for Underworld-adjacent lore.

"Hecate," Zoe began, her voice dropping into the old cadence, "is older than Olympus. Before Zeus took the sky, before Poseidon took the sea, Hecate held the crossroads. Titaness of magic, of ghosts, of the moon in its dark phase. When the gods fought the Titans, she alone among them sided with Olympus — not from love, but because she saw which way the wind blew, and because Zeus promised her honors no other Titan kept: a share of earth, sea, and sky."

Rain ticked. The girls leaned in.

"She walks with torches," Zoe continued. "Two flames to light the paths between worlds. At night, at a place where three roads meet, if thou leavest her an offering — garlic, eggs, honey cake — and turnest away without looking back, she will guide thy dead safely. If thou lookest back..." She paused. "Best not to look back."

One of the little ones gasped.

"Is she nice?" Naomi asked.

Zoe considered. "She is... necessary. She taught Medea. She guards the doors Medea opened. She gave mortals magic, knowing what they would do with it. Some say that is cruelty. I say it is honesty. Power is power. What thou doest with it is thy character."

Bianca frowned, picking at her sleeve. "Do you think — do the dead get lonely, waiting at crossroads?"

The question landed quiet. Zoe remembered Bianca's story — pulled out of time, brother fierce as winter, mother gone, years lost. A child of crossroads herself.

"I think," Zoe said gently, "that is why Hecate walks with dogs. Big black hounds, loyal as shadows. So none wait alone."

Bianca smiled, small. "I'd like a shadow dog."

"Thou hast thy brother. He is nearly as large and nearly as loyal, and he smells nearly as bad when wet."

Laughter broke the spell. Bianca threw a pillow at her. Zoe caught it one-handed, ancient reflexes showing off, and tossed it back.

"Another!" the little ones begged.

Zoe told them of Hecate at the search for Persephone, the only god who heard the girl cry out, holding her torches high while Demeter tore the earth apart. Of Hecate on the beach at night, teaching witches to read the moon. Of the time — Zoe's voice went dry — that a certain hero she would not name tried to steal one of Hecate's torches to impress a girl and ended up with his eyebrows burned off for a year.

"Was it Hercules?" Phoebe whispered.

"It is always Hercules," Zoe said, and they howled.

Outside, the rain eased for a moment, then redoubled, drumming hard. Inside, warm and torch-lit by the cabin's braziers, the girls curled closer, ambrosia forgotten. Bianca rested her head on Zoe's shoulder without asking, and Zoe — who did not allow such familiarity, usually — let her stay, resting her cheek briefly against dark hair.

After a long day of miserable weather, Zoe wanted something ordinary: a dry room, people willing to listen, and a story that didn't have to explain the world.

Bianca di Angelo had a system for hot cider.

It had to be very hot, with two cinnamon sticks, and you had to cup it with both hands and blow on it three times before the first sip or it burned your tongue off. Nico always forgot and burned his tongue off and then complained. Every time. Without fail. Little brothers were predictable.

On the evening of day two, with rain still hammering and the whole camp smelling like damp socks, Bianca decided the Hades cabin needed cider, and the Hades cabin needed company, and she knew exactly where to get both.

"Come on," she told Nico, pulling her cloak over both of them like a tent. "We're going to the Big House."

"It's raining," Nico said, as if she hadn't noticed. He was hunched on his bunk with his Mythomagic deck spread around him, building some gloomy Underworld-control monstrosity that had made Percy groan earlier. His hair stuck up everywhere. "It's wet."

"That's why there's cider at the other end. Move."

They splashed across the green holding hands so they wouldn't slip, cloak flapping, Nico shrieking when a puddle went over his shoe. The Big House glowed gold through every window, a lighthouse in gray. Inside was chaos — wonderful chaos. Music, shouting, the smell of cinnamon and woodsmoke.

Mrs. O'Leary, Percy's enormous hellhound, lay sprawled across three couches pushed together, snoring loud enough to rattle mugs, her massive paws twitching as she dreamed. Percy sat against her side like she was a furry hill, Annabeth tucked under his arm reading a book, both of them flushed from the fire. When they saw the di Angelos, Percy's face lit.

"Bianca! Nico! Cider's fresh — Argus just brought it!"

Bianca beelined for the kitchen corner where the big steel urn steamed. She made two mugs exactly right — extra cinnamon for Nico because he liked to chew on it — and carried them back, handing one over with a stern look.

"Blow three times," she ordered.

Nico blew once, sipped, yelped, stuck out his tongue. "Hot!"

"Three times, Nico!"

"It's fine! It builds character!" He grinned, tongue red. Bianca rolled her eyes so hard Annabeth laughed.

They squeezed in beside Percy and Mrs. O'Leary — who woke enough to lick Nico's entire head in one swipe, making him squawk and Bianca cackle — and the five of them sat in a heap of blankets and hellhound fur, rain hammering the roof like applause.

"This is nice," Bianca said softly, cradling her mug. The Hades cabin was cool and dark and quiet, all black marble and shadows. She loved it — it felt like home, like her father — but sometimes at night it felt a little too quiet. Too much like the Lotus Casino suite where time had stood still. Here, with the fire crackling and Mrs. O'Leary snoring and Percy arguing with Annabeth about whether hippocampi counted as horses for Mythomagic purposes —

"They're half horse!" Percy insisted.

"They're half fish! They don't count for your Equestrian Bonus!"

"That's discrimination!"

Bianca caught Nico's eye. He was watching Percy and Annabeth with that look he got — hungry and shy at once, wanting friends but not knowing how to ask. She nudged his foot with hers.

"Deal me in next game?" Nico asked quietly.

Percy's head snapped around. "Are you kidding? Your Underworld deck is terrifying. You killed my Poseidon in two turns last time. You're absolutely playing. Annabeth needs someone who can actually beat her."

"I beat you all the time," Annabeth protested.

"Exactly. I need allies."

Bianca sipped her cider — perfect, sweet and sharp — and watched her brother shuffle his cards with growing confidence, explaining his new combo to Percy, who listened like it was the coolest thing he'd ever heard. Which, to Percy, it probably was. He listened to everyone like that.

Mrs. O'Leary sighed hugely in her sleep and rolled, draping a paw over all of them. Nobody moved. It was warm. It was heavy in a good way, like a weighted blanket made of dog.

Outside, thunder rumbled, closer than before. Nico flinched — he hated loud storms, always had — and Bianca automatically threw her arm around him, pulling him into her side. Percy, without missing a beat in his argument, reached over and pulled the blanket higher over Nico's shoulders.

Nico glanced up, surprised. Percy just winked.

Bianca felt her throat go tight. Camp was strange. Camp was loud and smelly and full of people who'd never lost decades to a casino. But camp also had cider and hellhounds and boys who shared blankets without making it weird.

"Three blows," she whispered to Nico, kissing the top of his damp head. "Love you, death breath."

"Love you too, bossy," Nico mumbled, already leaning into her, already calmer.

The rain played on.

On day three, the rain turned to drizzle and everyone at Camp Half-Blood collectively lost it.

Annabeth Grace stood in the doorway of the Zeus cabin, watching campers sprint between buildings in the thin gray mist like prisoners released into the yard, faces tipped up, arms spread, shouting with joy at the mere absence of downpour. The green was a lake. The volleyball net had collapsed. A family of ducks had moved into the amphitheater. Connor Stoll was attempting to surf a mattress down the hill and failing gloriously.

"It's still raining," Percy said beside her, amused. He'd appeared with two apples, offering her the shinier one automatically. "Why are they celebrating? It's still wet."

"Because it's different wet," Annabeth said. "Thin wet is hopeful. Heavy wet is oppressive. It's basic meteorology. Also psychology. Also we're all going insane."

"Speak for yourself. I'm perfectly sane."

"You organized your Mythomagic cards by art instead of function. You've lost it."

"They look pretty on the page! It's called aesthetics!"

Annabeth bit into her apple to hide her smile. Three days indoors had frayed everyone. The Stolls' casino had collapsed in a pretzel-related scandal. Clarisse had arm-wrestled half the camp. Ella had learned forty new words, most of them "puddle." Thalia had taught Spite and Malice to at least six other campers and there were now heated tournaments in three different cabins. Grover's blanket fort had expanded to annex half the library and Chiron had given up enforcing checkout limits.

And Annabeth — Annabeth had spent three days with Percy stuck to her side like glue, playing cards and drinking cider and falling asleep on his shoulder during Zoe's ghost stories, and she'd loved every minute, and she was also going quietly crazy from never being alone with him. Really alone. Not surrounded by snoring hellhounds and card tournaments and little brothers asking for rules clarifications.

She wanted — she didn't know what she wanted, exactly. Just air. Just him. Just a minute that wasn't borrowed.

Percy seemed to read it. He always did, lately. He jerked his chin toward the porch steps. "Come on. Doorway. Rain-watching. Old people do it. We're old souls."

They sat in the open doorway of Cabin One, legs stretched out over the steps, shoulders touching, backs against the doorframe — one on each side, facing the gray-green world. The overhang kept them mostly dry, except for mist beading on their skin and the occasional fat drop from the eaves landing plip on Percy's nose, making Annabeth laugh.

It was quiet. That was the miracle. After three days of noise — shouting and music and thunder — the drizzle hushed everything to a whisper. Drip from pines. Gurgle in gutters. Soft patter on marble. Far away, the lake lapping high against the dock. The air smelled washed-clean, pine and wet stone and ozone fading.

Percy swung his feet. Annabeth leaned her head against the doorframe and watched him from the corner of her eye: his profile, the scar on his lip, rain beading in his eyelashes. He caught her looking and grinned, unembarrassed.

"What?" he said.

"Nothing. You're just... you."

"Good or bad?"

"Good." She paused. "Very good."

Percy's ears went pink. He bumped his knee into hers. "You're very good too. Even when you kick me under tables."

"I kick you with love."

"Ow-love."

They sat. The mist drifted. Annabeth felt the buzzing restlessness of three days indoors slowly uncoil from her spine. She thought about her dad — Zeus, lord of exactly this weather — and wondered if he made it rain because he was sad, or angry, or just thought the strawberries needed it. She thought about their mother's cards, soft with handling. About Clarisse letting Silena braid her hair. About Grover's fort and Zoe's hounds and Bianca's cider system. Small warm things, hoarded like Ella's words against the gray.

"Hey," Percy said softly. "Thanks for not getting cabin-fever-mad at me."

"Who says I'm not mad?"

"You'd be madder. You get a crease right here." He tapped his own forehead between his eyebrows. "When you're really mad. Right now you just have... rain face."

"Rain face?"

"Soft. Calm. Pretty."

Annabeth's heart did a stupid flip. She stared at the rain so he wouldn't see her blush. "That's the worst compliment you've ever given me."

"It's not a compliment. It's an observation. I'm a scientist now."

"You're failing science."

"I'm passing romance."

She laughed — snorted, actually — and shoved him. He caught her hand and didn't let go, lacing their fingers together on the step between them. His hand was warm. Hers was cold. They met in the middle.

They didn't kiss. They'd kissed before — quick, nervous, wonderful kisses behind the forge and on the Zeus roof — but this wasn't a kissing moment. This was something quieter. A doorway moment. Watching the rain together and knowing the other person was happy to just sit.

"I like rainy days," Annabeth said finally, surprised to find it true. "When we're inside. Together."

Percy squeezed her hand. "Me too. Even if your card game is rigged and your boyfriend is bad at Spite."

"Malice. You're bad at Malice. You're okay at Spite."

"I'll take it."

Lightning flickered far out to sea, silent, veiled by mist. No thunder followed. The storm was leaving. Tomorrow would be sun and mud and flooded cabins and Pegasus stalls to muck out — Thalia's tournament losers would pay up — and laundry lines strung everywhere and ducks to evict from the amphitheater.

But for now: drizzle, gray light, his hand in hers, the whole camp breathing out around them.

Annabeth leaned her head on Percy's shoulder. He leaned his head on top of hers, damp hair tickling her forehead. They watched the rain.

Behind them, inside Cabin One, Thalia started dealing another round of Spite and Malice and calling for them to come back in before they caught colds. They ignored her for one more minute. Just one more.

Percy moved his hand nearer to Annabeth's. She took it without looking away from the rain. For another minute they listened to Thalia deal cards inside, comfortable enough not to answer her yet.
