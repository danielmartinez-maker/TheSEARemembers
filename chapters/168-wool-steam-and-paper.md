## Chapter 168: Wool, Steam, and Paper

Silena Beauregard woke to the sound of rain on shells.

It was not the lake's rain. She knew the lake's rain from three Septembers of it — the soft tick and hiss it made on canvas and shingles, the way it came through Tempest Hall's open doors like weather that had stopped by to say hello and would not dream of overstaying. This rain had its collar up. It came down raw out of a hard north wind, and it struck the long strings of shells hung along the front eaves — thin white moons and spirals wired to cord, strung years ago by some Poseidon kid with more shells than sense — and struck them in a sound like a hundred small coins being counted by somebody in a hurry.

She lay still under the quilt and let the cold of the room tell her the rest. The high windows had gone from gold to gray sometime in the night. The air had a bite in it now, the kind that found the gap between blanket and mattress and tucked itself in with her. Somewhere out in the dark the wind had done what October winds did: it had come around the point of the north shore, found the open water of the canoe lake, and made itself a nuisance. She could hear the little waves at the far bank, mean and choppy, and the pines thrashing, and under it all the drip-drip of the eaves into whatever was catching them.

Inside the cabin the pool was breathing.

It always breathed — low, tidal, the water in the big saltwater pool at the end of the hall moving in slow swells that took no notice of people — but tonight-morning it steamed. The channels brought their sea-warmth up from wherever the channels came from, and the air above the pool had gone cold enough to find the difference, and so a faint white ghost of vapor stood over the green water, and the room smelled of salt and wet stone and the lemon oil Clarisse bought once a summer from the supply office and refused to ration. The tile would be freezing. The tile was always freezing. Silena pulled her feet up under the quilt and lay a moment longer, because a good kitchen day starts from warmth, and because she wanted to hear the cabin's weather settle before she went to work on it.

Inventory without opening her eyes, the way you took stock of a room you had bunked in for three years: Clarisse's bunk creaking its small dry-creak arguments across the aisle, breathing deep and even and faintly whistling in one nostril. Percy's bunk empty, quilt thrown back, pillow dented, the folded wax-paper wrapper still on the pillow where he kept it because nobody asked about it and nobody moved it. The visitor towels on their hooks by the back bunks, folded square. The laundry bag sitting where it always sat, half full. The whole cabin arranged for use in a way that it had not been arranged in June, when three strangers had been introduced by a trident in the sand and had spent a month learning each other's exits.

Percy's empty bunk meant he was at the doors.

She found him there as she'd known she would: the big front doors of Tempest Hall standing open to the weather, because Poseidon's cabin had never believed in screen doors, and the boy standing on the cold tile in his swim shorts with an old wool blanket around his shoulders, hair sticking up in four directions, watching the rain come through the pines. He had his weight on one leg the way he did when he had been standing a while, and his feet were bare and probably numb, and he did not turn when her wool socks came down the hall, because he had heard her coming. He was observant, but not about himself.

"You're up early for a kid who swam yesterday," Silena said.

"It's loud," Percy said. "Listen."

She listened. Rain. Wind. The shells ticking at the eaves, tick-tick-tick, like somebody keeping time. Beyond the doors, at the bottom of the rise, the canoe lake lay gray and chopped, whitecapping in little fits around the boundary rock, and the smell coming off it was the smell of cold water and turned leaves — not the warm green smell of September anymore. This was the October smell, the honest one.

"She doesn't like the wind," Percy said, nodding at the lake, as if the lake were a horse and the wind an argument. "It blows the wrong way. She keeps slopping up on the north beach."

"That's because the north beach is where you walk around barefoot like an idiot."

"It builds character." He shifted the blanket. "I could go out in it. The bathhouse roof leaks. I could check the leak. In this. With my hands." He said it carefully, which meant he had been thinking it since before dawn, which meant the rain had gotten into him the way it did, the way it always did to sea kids, the way it did not get into other people and Silena had never found the words for.

"With your hands, in the storm, at five in the morning," Silena said. "Yes. That's exactly what the roof needs." She put her hands on her hips and looked at him: sea-legged and sky-staring, a boy who belonged to weather more than walls, standing on the freezing tile being polite about it.

"I'm fine," he said.

"Of course you are. Go get dressed in clothes that cover you, and come eat. We're not doing anything outside today."

"Chiron will cancel—"

"Chiron is going to cancel everything, and then Clarisse is going to be furious about canceling, and then all of us are going to be indoors with a furious Clarisse and nothing to do, and somebody will start a knife-throwing contest, and it will be a whole day." Silena was already listing it on her fingers. "So I am going to fix it first. Go. Sweater. Socks. The ones without holes."

He went. She heard him pause at the pool edge on his way down the hall, out of habit, and heard the little half-step back, the way you catch yourself at a doorway in the house you grew up in.

The kitchen corner of Cabin Three was Silena's corner the way the pool was the cabin's heartbeat — a counter, a cold box, a shelf of tins, a two-burner plate and one small oven that Chiron pretended not to know about, and over all of it a window that looked out on the pines going gray in the downpour. She took inventory standing still, the way you size up a boat before you take her out.

Potatoes, six, knobby and honest. Leeks, three. Cream in the cold box, a good depth of it, from yesterday. Smoked fish, a tin and a half, flaky and dark, the kind that smelled like a winter cottage. Flour, near enough. Yeast in the tin with the bent lid, alive if you treated it kindly. Butter. Salt and pepper. Apples, two bushel bags, September's last stand. Walnuts, a jar, already shelled by somebody's patient summer afternoon. Cinnamon in the little tin, which smelled like winter when you opened it, which was on purpose, which was always on purpose in her kitchen.

She pulled the pencil from her hair and wrote on the back of a grocery slip in her fast round hand: CHOWDER — POTATO, LEEK, SMOKED FISH, CREAM. BREAD — TWO LOAVES, RISING NOW. CIDER — ALL AFTERNOON, SPICE IT HARD. AFTERNOON — BAKED APPLES, HONEY, WALNUTS, SKIN SPLIT ON PURPOSE. At the bottom she paused, pencil tapping, then wrote: MOUTHS TO FEED — ALL OF THEM. WHOEVER COMES. And underlined ALL OF THEM twice.

Clarisse came in while the first potato was going into the pot, wearing her old green jacket over her camp shirt and cargo pants with the blown-out knees, braid damp from a fast rinse in the pool water, boots unlaced, and the expression of a girl who had been robbed of something she had not known she wanted until it was taken.

"Patrol's canceled," she announced. "Arena's canceled. Chiron says no woods, no beach, no canoes, no—" She made a disgusted noise. "He said indoors day. Indoors day. Like we're five and it's a rainy recess."

"You have a lovely way with words before breakfast."

"I have a lovely way with words. Period." Clarisse leaned on the counter, watching Silena's hands the way she watched work she did not fully trust herself to do gently. "So what are we supposed to do all day. Sit here."

"We are going to build," Silena said, "a fort."

There was a silence. Rain on the shells. The pot ticked.

"A fort," Clarisse said.

"A fort. In the hall. Real blankets, the good quilts, clotheslines, pillows, the works." Silena was slicing leeks now, quick even rings, imagining it already. "The whole hall is one big room and nobody's using it and it has the pool on one end and the stove on the other, and it is the warmest room in Camp Half-Blood on a day like this, and I am not letting us sit here like three turnips in a jar while the entire camp tries to crowd into the Big House and fights over the drachma game."

"We have the drachma game."

"We have the drachma game and it lives at the Big House and Christopher will cheat at it because Christopher cannot not cheat at it." Silena set the knife down with the clack of someone who has decided. "The fort is ours. Cabin Three's. And whoever shows up with cold hands gets fed. Percy's the engineer. You're the wood guy. I'm the kitchen. The map kids are coming when their wall dries."

"Children," Clarisse said, but her mouth was doing the thing it did when it was trying not to smile.

"They're coming. Annabeth's bringing them." Silena paused. "And Thalia's going to want to see it."

"Thalia's going to want to negotiate the rent." Clarisse pushed off the counter, then stopped, not leaving, the way she did when there was more to say and she had to find the blunt door into it. "Silena. It's Tuesday."

"I know what day it is."

"The letter thing." Clarisse said it with her chin, the way she said hard things, looking at the shell strings instead of at her sister. "It's been sitting there since Friday. He was supposed to write back and now it's—"

"Now it's raining and the docks are messy and the pool's a swimming pool again and he hasn't slept right," Silena said quietly. "I know. That's why he needs the fort."

"That's not what forts are for."

"Forts are for having roofs," Silena said. "And then people who have roofs can do the things they've been avoiding. It's carpentry. It works."

Chiron came up the path just before the first loaf went in, which was his way of arriving exactly when needed and exactly when expected, a quarter-horse's shake of his head sending rain off his mane and across the stones. He stood at the open doors and declined to come in further, because a centaur in a warm kitchen was a crowding, and he did the announcement from the threshold: rain through the afternoon, maybe overnight; the boundary line closed until Wednesday; nothing in the arena, nothing in the woods, nothing in the lake; the Big House would be open, the cabins were open, and any camper caught out in the open water in this wind would be personally retired from the canoes by Chiron himself, and his expression suggested the retirement would involve Yacht Rock and canned clips.

"Tell Silena it's a conspiracy to keep her in the kitchen," Clarisse said.

"Tell Clarisse," Chiron said, his lined face doing the deep smile he kept for Cabin Three, "that a conspiracy to keep Miss Beauregard in the kitchen would require more weather than Zeus and Poseidon between them can raise on a Tuesday." He looked down the hall at the pool steam, at the shell strings dripping at the eaves, at the two orange shirts in the doorway of the kitchen corner. "Feed them something hot at noon, if you would. A cold camp is a restless camp. Give them heat and they'll build their own shelter."

"Already doin' it," Silena said. "Can I beg Ms. O'Fee for the pavilion oven? The big one. For bread."

"You can beg her anything," Chiron said. "But I should warn you, she has taken a public position on this morning's weather. She thinks Tuesdays are beneath her."

"Ms. O'Fee thinks Thursdays are beneath her. Tuesdays she merely finds offensive." Silena clapped her hands once, the sound that started all the good days. "Go on with you, Chiron. I'll have the first soup at noon and if anyone complains, I'll send them to you."

"Mercy," said Chiron, and backed into the rain, and went clip-clop up the path, and the rain on the shells took up its counting again where his interruption had interrupted it.

The cabin filled up the way a kitchen always filled up — not with people at first, but with processes. The chowder went on: potatoes and leeks sweating in butter, then the milk and cream poured slow so they would not seize, then the smoked fish flaked in at the last minute, dark against the pale, so it would not boil to leather. The smell climbed the walls. The bread was mixed on the counter with the heel of Silena's hand and the patience that only came with the thousandth loaf, and set to rise in a warm bowl, and then the first pot of cider went onto the small burner with cinnamon and three whole cloves and a strip of lemon peel, and the whole kitchen corner went to steam, and the high windows fogged, and a boy appeared in the doorway with his hair tied back and his sweater on inside out and watched the steam cloud the glass and said, "Can I touch it," and Silena said, "No," and he touched it anyway, and she flicked him with a side towel.

By ten the cabin had become a place with weather of its own. Rain on the shells at the front; steam in the air; the stove ticked its low heat into the hall; the pool breathed its slow swells. By the back door the boots were lined up in a row, three pairs, dripping, wet wool and leather steaming in the room's warmth, the lake smell of them filling the corners. Clarisse came back from the woodpile with her arms full of split oak, her jacket plastered to her shoulders, and dumped it by the stove and stood with her hands out to the heat like a cat.

"Okay," she conceded, grudging, to the stove. "This part's good."

"I know," Silena said. "Percival. Bring the good quilts."

That was how the fort began.

By eleven the hall of Tempest Hall had surrendered its dignity. Silena had drawn a line of rope clotheslines from the pool's edge to the woodstove, high enough that a tall Poseidon kid would not knock his head, and over them were going the cabin's quilts: the big patch quilt with the blue and green triangles, the wool blanket Thalia had used in July, the one Clarisse had mended at midnight years ago with stitches like lightning, and a gray fleece that had been somebody's birth blanket once and was now just "the fort blanket" and nobody argued about it. Clothespins clicked. The quilts hung and fanned and made a long dim lounge under their roof, open at the front toward the stove and closed at the back toward the shelves, and it looked absurd, and it looked like a room inside a room, and the rain, which had been a nuisance outside, suddenly had a sound it was meant to make out here, pattering on the quilt roof in muffled thups.

Percy was in his element. He had tied the second line himself with knots learned from a Cyclops, pitched the quilt roof at a slant so the rain would run off, weighted the front corners with boots and a spare spear haft, and hung a lantern from the center rope so it swung gently and threw a small warm circle around. His hand was on the rope when he said it, almost to himself, the way the sea was in him today and had to come out: "It's like the paddlecloth. Remember? When we made the canoe a house?"

"We do not live in the canoe," Clarisse said, hauling a blanket over the clothesline with the ruthlessness of a general tarping artillery, "and we do not live in the fort. We eat soup, we build this clown house, and when the rain stops it comes down. It is a fort, not a condo."

"You say clown house, I hear that you want the inside corner."

"I want the inside corner of nothing. Give me a corner with walls I can hit." Clarisse stepped back and squinted at the structure, her braid swinging forward over her shoulder, her arms crossed. Then she did a thing Silena watched her do and never commented on: she reached in and straightened a sagging fold of the patch quilt where the weight sat wrong, tucking the hem back over the rope so it would hold the rain's weight. She did it quickly. Nobody was supposed to notice. Silena noticed. That was the job.

At noon they ate the chowder at the counter, standing up, with bread torn off in pieces and put into the bowl, which was the only correct way to eat chowder, and Silena stood over the pot and ladled and ladled and said nothing, and the rain went on, and the shells ticked, and between the third bowl and the fourth Percy set his spoon down and looked, with the particular look of a boy remembering something with his whole body, at the small pile of paper on the shelf by the stove.

It was not much of a pile. A folded square of blue stationery, Silena's, with her handwriting on the outside of the fold. A stub of pencil. And the oilcloth tag, square and stitched, with its list in Annabeth's neat hand — the letter schedule, the way she had written it in the loft before Tyson left, tied to his pack like a boy's lunch tag, because planning was how she loved: WHEN THE FORGE WRITES, ANSWER IN THREE DAYS. TUESDAYS ARE LETTER DAYS. SEND WITH THE POST HIPPOCAMP, DUSK.

Percy looked at it. He picked up the pencil. He put it down.

Silena, from her place at the pot, without turning around, said, "The mail goes out at dusk."

"I know," Percy said.

"So," Silena said, "you have a morning, an afternoon, and a fort to build."

From outside, faintly, through the rain and the shell-ticking, came the sound of a bell being rung at the Big House — the indoors-day summons, the camp herding itself indoors — and then the smaller sound of twelve small kids being told, in twelve different keys, that today there would be no water in any form unless it was in a cup.

Silena smiled into her chowder and stirred and thought, not for the first time, that shelter was never the building. The building was just wood and rope and somebody's stubbornness. The shelter was the thing you made of a day, when the sky went mean and you had somewhere warm to go, and people who had kept the mittens dry for you, and a roof of your own pitching, and soup on the stove, and your name on the mail.

Clarisse La Rue knew exactly how many days it had been since the ferry took her little brother north, which was seven, and she was still counting, because she had decided before she was twelve that counting was a way of carrying people, and nobody had ever given her a better one.

It rained all morning the way rains do when they have nowhere better to be — flat, gray, tireless, the kind that got into your cuffs and sat there. Chiron had closed the boundary line, which meant the arena was a puddle, the woods were a river, the lake was a temper, and the entire camp population of two hundred demigods was suddenly indoors with nothing to hit. Clarisse had done a lap of the cleared spaces at ten — the pavilion, the Big House, the cabins — and made sure that nobody was "getting ideas about indoor sparring," which meant roughly everyone was getting ideas about indoor sparring. At ten-fifteen she came back to Tempest Hall with rain dripping off the end of her braid and took the only job she actually liked: indoor maintenance with her hands, in the quiet part of the hall, near the pool.

The pool needed it. The rain blew in through the standing front doors all morning, and with it came leaves — small oak leaves, maple, one enormous sycamore leaf that had clearly meant to kill somebody — and they floated in sad little rafts on the saltwater until the skimmer came out. The skimmer was a long pole with a net on the end and it was Percy's job, because it was exactly the kind of job that gave his hands something to do while his insides went around, and Clarisse had learned, over a summer, that a sea kid with restless insides and empty hands was how people got hit by surf.

She set him at it with one sentence — "Skim. Don't swim. You're not a leaf" — and went to the shelves by the back bunks with her mending basket and the savings.

The savings were small. Two blue-striped towels, the visitor towels, one for Tyson and one for Triton, washed and folded with hospital corners because hope liked clean lines and because Silena's grandmother had said that a folded towel was a letter to God that told him you loved people who might come. Clarisse refolded them anyway, though they already held their corners, tapped them square, and set them back on their hooks. The oilcloth tag — Annabeth's tag, the letter schedule, stitched and waterproof, tied years ago to a pack that wasn't here anymore — got hung on its own nail beside the towels, because it lived there now, because it was the family's calendar. And then the shelf needed a scrub, and she scrubbed it: wet rag, warm salt water from the pool channel, a dry cloth, and the wood came up pale and silver-gold again, and she wiped the shelves twice for no reason anyone could name and set the visitor towels back.

Percy skimmed. The net scraped the water in long pulls. The pool breathed its swells around the pole. Every time he lifted the net, he looked at the wet leaves in it like they had accused him of something.

"Stop talking to them," Clarisse said.

"I'm not talking to them."

"You're making eyes at them. It's worse."

"It's a maple leaf. It believes in me." He scraped the net on the rim, dumped, and leaned on the pole. "Rain's going to stop the mail, probably. Brine won't come in this. Hippocamps get water up their horses' part."

"Brines aren't horses' part," Clarisse said. "And the mail isn't the problem. You know what the problem is."

The thing sat between them in the steam and the rain. The problem was a piece of paper. The problem was that Tuesday was a letter day — Annabeth's own handwriting said so, sewn into oilcloth like law — and that the letter owed to the forge had been owed since Friday, and that the boy who wrote love notes in lopsided block letters the way other boys breathed could not seem to get ink onto the letter to his brother.

"I'll do it later," Percy said, to the leaves.

"Later is a liar," Clarisse said. "I know. I made him up."

He laughed. She let him, and went after the quilt.

The quilt was the big patch one, blue and green triangles, and it had been the fort's west wall until the rain came around the corner and got it too low. There was a tear along one seam where the clothespin had pulled against the weave. Clarisse dragged the quilt onto her lap on the bench, spread it, and fell into the work like falling into water — the way the forge matron had taught her at nine, thumb against the back of the needle, in and out and in and out, the stitches small and even and almost invisible from the front, which was the rule, because a mend should hold the cloth, not the eye. Storm light came through the high windows and lay across the quilt in gray stripes. The rain kept on the shell strings. She worked.

And while she worked, because her hands had learned a thing at nine that her mouth had not caught up on until much later — that sometimes you mended in front of people because the people needed to see the thing get mended — she did the other thing she did. She knitted.

Badly.

It was a scarf. She would not be telling anybody it was a scarf. It was blue yarn, because blue was what she had, and it was long — too long — and lumpy, and the needle work was so uneven it had the texture of a stomach. She had dropped stitches she had not known how to pick up, and there were two balled-up wreckages under her bench from this fall already, and the whole of her plan for it involved finishing before first snow and not thinking about who it was for.

Percy came back from the pool and leaned on the bench next to her and looked at the knitting, and she felt him look at it, and decided he would go to bed an old man if he said one word.

"What," she said, to her stitches.

"Nothing."

"Say it."

"I'm not saying anything."

"You're going to say something."

"I'm just—" He put his hands over his mouth. Then he had to take them off. "You're breathing on it."

"Breathing on it is in the instructions. It's called hopesing."

"It's called smoking, Clarisse, it's structurally— can I try? Is that— no. No. I'm not touching it."

"You will never help anybody with anything," Clarisse said, stabbing a needle in the direction of his chest, "if you won't take the needle when it's handed to you."

He took the needle. He tried. He was worse. Not obviously worse — his fingers had a strange natural gentleness, the way they'd had with fighting straps before she fixed his grip — but his yarn tension was the tension of a boy who had never once in his life thought about tension, and within a minute the scarf had a new hole, lopsided and expressive, like an accident in a painting.

"See," Clarisse said. "This is why nobody gives you scarves."

"I'm being helpful."

"You're being a menace. Here." She took the needle back, or tried to, and somehow their hands got tangled in the blue yarn, his cold from the pool and hers warm from the quilt, and for a second neither of them pulled away, and the scarf hung between them on its needles like a bridge or a truce or an anchor. Then the water in the pool said, without any sound at all, that something was coming up the channels.

The ripple came from the deep end, where the channels opened, where the water was warmer and the tile darker. A swell that had nothing to do with the rain. Percy's head came up like a dog's.

"No," Clarisse said. "You have been in that pool four times today. If you think—"

"It's not me."

It was, and it wasn't. The ripple came again, and then a head came up out of the water in the deep end, and a long neck, and a forelock of black-green hair plastered over a face that was half a horse, and the creature climbed out of the pool onto the swim ledge with the ease of something that did not understand that swimming and landing were different arts. It was a hippocamp — smaller than Brine, dark green rather than gray, eyes like coins, hooves that clicked on the tile — and a packet of oilcloth was tied around its neck with a tag flapping.

"Brine," Percy said.

"Of course it is." Clarisse did not startle. She looked at the hippocamp with the same expression she gave a new spear: assessment. "The horse that swims. Naturally. In this. With the lake a washing machine."

Brine shook itself, comprehensively, like a dog, and a spray of saltwater went across the hall and settled onto the stove and a laundry bag. Then it trotted across the cold tile, hooves slipping a little, to Clarisse, and lowered its head and looked at her with its coin eyes, and Clarisse stood very still with her hands at her sides, which was Clarisse's version of bowing, which she would have denied to death.

"Fine," she said. "Fine. You're welcome. Try not to bleed on anything on your way out."

Percy was already at the packet. The tag on it was Annabeth's — her neat letters, stitched into cloth: TUESDAY. FORGE MAIL. DAMP SEALED. READ CAREFULLY. And on the oilcloth itself, in another hand entirely, lopsided and enormous and careful: FOR PERCY, CLARISSE, SILENA, ANNABETH, THALIA. OPEN NOW.

Brine, having delivered its charge, found the shell bowl by the pool where the day's bread sat, ate an entire heel in two mouthfuls, and lay down on the tile with its fish half curled under it and its horse half folded like a dog at a hearth, dripping a slow dark puddle. Its eyes closed. Whatever long errand had brought it up the channels in a rainstorm, it was done erranding.

"Open it," Clarisse said.

Percy opened it. Inside was a folded sheet of good stiff paper, dry inside its oilcloth, and he read it standing with his wet hair dripping down his shirt, and Clarisse came and read it over his shoulder because she did not pretend about letters, and this is what it said, in the lopsided earnest block letters of a boy who had learned writing from men who believed in bolts:

> DEAR EVERYONE. THE FORGE IS HOT. I MEAN THAT. IT IS HOT ALL THE TIME NOW BECAUSE THE BUILDING IS RIGHT AND I AM RIGHT AND THE MATRONS SAY I AM ALMOST USEFUL. I MADE THE CLEAT FOR THE CANOE RACK. THE HOLES ARE FOR THE RACK. CLARISSE SHOULD MEASURE TWICE AND NOT MELT IT WRONG. I AM SORRY THAT IS RUDE. MEASURE TWICE ANYWAY. MOTHER SENDS SALT. THERE ARE NINE HUNDRED BROTHERS HERE AND I KNOW THEM ALL BY NAME NOW AND I FORGOT THREE YESTERDAY WHICH IS BAD. THE SEA IS FINE. THE POOL IS PROBABLY COLD. PERCY YOU ARE SUPPOSED TO WRITE BACK. I KNOW BECAUSE OF THE TAG. THE TAG SAYS TUESDAY. I HAVE THE PEN OUT. I AM WAITING WITH THE PEN. — T.

There was a drawing at the bottom, of a cleat, lopsided but labeled with meticulous care, and under the drawing, in letters that had clearly been rewritten many times to get them straight: LOVE TO ALL FIVE.

Percy read it twice. Then he stood with the paper in his hands and did not say anything for long enough that the rain and the shells and Brine's patient breathing all had to cover for him.

"He waited," Percy said finally, quiet.

"Obviously he waited," Clarisse said. "He's family. Waiting's the job." She took the paper out of his hands, not roughly, and read it herself with her chin up and the forge matron's stitches still between her fingers. "He put the holes in twice. Good. Tell him I said it's good."

"Tell him yourself. You're probably supposed to be drawing a diagram for it."

The diagram. Clarisse's mouth did something complicated. She went to her satchel, which she kept as orderly as a quartermaster's trunk, and drew out the folded square she had been carrying since Friday: a drawing on cardstock, edges softened from handling, the canoe rack's cleat in her own blunt careful lines, dimension lines and notes in her handwriting that was better than she admitted. It was finished. It had been finished since before the rain. It had just never been sent, because sending things to the forge meant touching the absence again, and Clarisse La Rue did not do absent things lightly.

She put it on the counter next to the letter and squared it with the edge.

"It's done," she said, to the counter.

"You finished it Friday," Percy said.

"I did it at night. It's not polite to ask."

"It's not polite to tell me you're up at midnight either, Clarisse."

"You're not the boss of my hours. I'm the boss of mine." She looked at the diagram, at the cleat, at the rack it served. "Don't make it weird."

"I'm not making it weird. You finished something for him. That's just a fact."

"Facts are weird." She squared the corner of the diagram again, though it was already square. "Okay. Sit down. Write it."

"Now?"

"Now. You've had four days of now and you've spent them skimming leaves at your own personality." She was already moving — commandeering the stool from the pool step, clattering dishes aside with the brusque speed of a girl who feeds people and mends things but has never once considered herself a lighthouse for anybody's grief. "Silena. Paper."

Silena appeared from the kitchen corner with a sheet of the good blue stationery in one hand and a honey cake in the other, and set them down on the counter with the air of a woman who had been waiting all day to be interrupted by exactly this.

"The real paper," Silena said. "Not notebook paper. This is a keep-it-forever letter."

"It's going in a forge," Clarisse said.

"Then it'll be kept forever in a forge. Use the good paper." Silena pushed the honey cake an inch closer to Percy's elbow. "You write. I'll get the stuff."

And there was stuff — the day's stuff, the whole cabin's stuff, laid out one thing at a time like an offering plate. Annabeth's contribution was already there, tied in sail-twine: a sheet of the heavy paper with the map key on it, drawn in her precise small hand — the canoe lake with its north shore, the boundary rock and its white X, the flat rock, and beside every mark a neat little explanation in words any dumb Cyclops could read, plus a trident drawn true in the corner. Clarisse added her cleat diagram, squared, with a final blunt annotation across the bottom: THESE HOLES. THE RACK. Silena added a twist of wax paper with two honey sticks inside, a squeeze of lemon zest laid on top to keep it sweet through the channels, and wrote her own line on the outside of the twist in her fast round hand: TELL HIM THE FORT HAS A ROOF NOW.

"What do I even—" Percy started. He was looking at the blank paper like it was the pool on a bad day. "You know how he— he says things. He says them plain. I always write it back wrong."

"Then write it plain," Clarisse said. "You're over there trying to write like a bestseller. Tell him what happened. The canoe's under tarp. The pool's a lake again. The cabin has a fort. Tell him the—" She stopped. "Tell him we counted the days. I said it. Put it in there."

"You put money on that?"

"I put it in there," Clarisse said, "because it's true. Seven days. He should know we counted."

Percy picked up the pencil stub. He did not start, quite. Clarisse took her place on the stool beside him with her knitting on her needles, and she knitted three rows, and she did not look at him, and she said, in the tone of somebody who knew exactly how much supervision helped and exactly when to spend it: "P.S. Your sister's scarf is getting better. Tell him I have a scarf."

"You have a scarf?"

"I have a scarf," Clarisse said. "It's under construction. It's private. Tell him it's beautiful and I'll never forgive you."

Then, because she was Clarisse, she started in on the rest of it unprompted, bluntly supplying facts the way she'd once supplied bandages: the lake was too cold for the float, that was the rule, Silena retired the float for the season; Annabeth was mapping the spring on the north shore with her pencils; Thalia had come home and was sleeping twelve hours and then being insufferable; Olive was here somewhere, telling the drainage it had to be honest; the rain was coming down like the sky was being tutored by Poseidon, which was word for word something Thalia had said at lunch;

Percy wrote it down, word for word, because she was the only one he trusted to give him words that were true when you took them out of her mouth.

And then Silena came over with a mug of warm cider held in both her hands, like a woman bringing an offering, and pushed it into his elbow until he laughed and drank it, and she said, "He's not grading you. He's waiting with a pen. That's not a grade, that's a hand wave from the shore," and Percy wrote faster after that.

The letter was three pages long. It was, in parts: a list. Numbers followed the pattern Tyson loved — plain words, no performance. It said the canoe was dry and painted and missed him. It said the pool was a lake in October. It said the fort had a slanted roof engineered by sea opinion. It said Clarisse had a scarf. It said Annabeth's key was enclosed and Olive was sending the drainage report and Thalia said the squirrel lost. It said, in the middle where it mattered, in Percy's own block lopsided letters: YOU ARE NOT FAR. YOU COME BACK IN TWO WEEKS. THE ROOM IS ROOMIER WHEN YOU'RE IN IT.

And at the bottom, because Percy had learned it from people who had learned it from the wrong sea: LOVE. P.S. TELL THE CLEAT THE HOLES ARE GOOD. P.P.S. I'M COUNTING DAYS TOO.

When he read it back to himself, his voice did the thing it did when the thing was true, and he stopped and started again at "roomier," and Clarisse, who was watching the rain, said "Good," which from her was a parade, and Silena, who had come up behind him with a warm towel for his wet hair, dropped the towel over his head and ruffled it hard enough to make him yelp, and he sat under it laughing with the letter in his lap.

Then they sealed it.

There was a proper way. There was always a proper way, because this cabin had learned about sending people out and counting them back. The folded pages went into the oilcloth with the cleat diagram and the map key, and the oilcloth was folded and refolded around them like a letter inside a letter, and it was tied with sail-twine, and Annabeth's tag went around the knot: FOR THE FORGE. DAMP SEALED. TUESDAY, DUSK. And Brine, who had been lying by the pool with its eyes closed and its appetite visible as a concept, got up when it heard the word forge and came over and took the packet in its teeth, gently, by the corner, because somebody had trained it well, and set it down again and looked at them, and Clarisse said, "Not till dusk, horse. You'll drip on the fort."

Brine sat down by the pool, patient as a promise kept, and dripped on the tile anyway.

The rain went on all through the middle of the day, and the fort had walls by then, and the quilts were drying on their lines, and the hall was full of a warmth that had nothing to do with the stove and everything to do with use. Percy came back from the letter table with two bowls of chowder, one for Clarisse and one for himself, and they ate them sitting on a bench under the fort's quilted roof, and then Clarisse gave him the knitting again.

"Here," she said. "You made a hole. You fix your hole."

"This is your scarf."

"It's our scarf now. You put a hole in it. Fix it."

So he sat in the fort with a blue scarf on his knee and a needle in his fingers and tried, with the grim concentration he usually saved for fighting straps, to close a hole he had made. Clarisse sat beside him with her own stitch — in — and out — and out — and in — the needle moving small and even because her hands remembered what her mouth would not say: that people hold together when somebody mends them, and that the mending did not have to be beautiful, it only had to hold the cloth and not the eye.

Somewhere behind them, in the kitchen corner, the bread came out at noon, and the second pot of cider went on with more cinnamon, and the rain on the shells went on counting.

Annabeth Grace arrived at Cabin Three in the middle of the afternoon with her hood up and her bag over her shoulder, and stood very still in the doorway of Tempest Hall for a moment, because the first thing a cartographer learns is to survey the room before you step into it, and the second is that some rooms survey you back.

The hall had been rearranged by weather and stubbornness. The big patch quilt hung from clotheslines strung the length of the room, roofed like a pavilion, its walls pinned at the corners with clothespins and weighted down by boots and a spare spear haft. Under the quilt roof was a low dim nest of blankets and pillows, open at the front toward the woodstove and closed at the back toward the shelves. A lantern hung from the center rope. One of Percy's white shells sat on the threshold like a cornerstone. The rain pattered on the quilt roof in muffled strokes, and the whole structure gave off the smell of wool and damp canvas and the faint orange-lake smell of things dried near heat. It looked absurd. It looked like the best room in camp.

At the far end of the hall the saltwater pool breathed its swells and steamed a little, and a hippocamp knelt on a spread of towels beside it, asleep, the oilcloth packet still tied at its neck, its coin eyes closed. Annabeth had learned, by October, not to spend wonder on the strange here but to spend it on the true.

"Genius!"

Olive came out of the fort headfirst and almost tripped over her own notebook — ten years old, small for ten, in a dry camp shirt two sizes too big that said PERCY on the pocket in marker, red notebook under one arm, mismatched sneakers replaced with a dry pair of wool socks over bare feet, both laces the same color for once because somebody had re-laced them. Behind Olive came Marcus, fifteen and tall and quiet, rope over one shoulder, canvas bag in his arms, wearing a dry shirt that was not his and had clearly decided to be. Both of them were pink-nosed and freshly dried and carried the look of children who had walked into a rainstorm and been adopted through it.

"I finished the north shore!" Olive announced. "In the wet! It got rained on! The pencil went soft and the paper went crinkly and Annabeth said we could do it again with ink!"

"That's what she said," Marcus confirmed, deadpan, and held up the fair-copy sheets from his canvas bag, dry as bones in their roll.

"Rain caught us on the path," Annabeth told the room, shaking rain off her hood, "so I rerouted them here. Your cabin's warmer than the Big House, and I was not drawing fair copy in a room with a leaking window." She unzipped the outer pocket of her bag and drew out, one by one: her blue notebook, a pencil, the small ink jar with the good pen, a towel, and — with the dry determination of a girl who had planned for rain before it started — a thermos of Silena's hot cider, still steaming.

Silena took the thermos from her hands with the reverence of a woman receiving tribute. "You thought of the cider," she said, delighted.

"I thought of everything," Annabeth said. "That's the job. Clarisse, your mending looks terrible."

"Go sink."

"No, really. It has an opinion."

Clarisse, from her bench in the corner, held up the blue scarf — it had one closed hole in it now, crooked but holding — and said, "It's structural."

"It's a stomach with ambitions."

"It's a scarf," Clarisse said. "With a hole Jackson fixed. Badly. He's improving. As a hobby." She went back to her rows.

Annabeth took off her wet hood and shook out her hair, and got to work. The fort was the right place for a redraw, she saw, before she had decided anything: it was dim and dry and had a roof like her room in Athena cabin had never been; and the rain sound was the right loudness, enough to keep a room private, not enough to make you talk louder than you wanted to. She and the map kids set up inside the nest on a spread of towels and the fair-copy sheets — Olive with her red notebook open, Marcus with his rope and his scale, Annabeth with the ink jar and the good pen and her sleeves shoved past her elbows. The lantern swung gently overhead and threw everything into a small warm circle, and the rain did its pattering on the quilt roof, and outside the circle the hall was all gray storm light and wet socks.

The north shore, drawn in pencil in the rain, was redrawn in ink under the quilt. Annabeth worked with the precision she gave to everything — the contour interval verified, the French drain's wet margin corrected with two thin lines, the flat rock and the boundary rock and its white X squared off against her little sketch. She inked the seep — under the granite on the ridge — the way you ink a rumor: provisional, a small blue star and a question mark, the cartographer's mark for something known and not yet met, and beside it she wrote SPRING, TENTATIVE, N. G. SURVEY, and showed Olive the question mark and explained, with the patience she spent on the two people who loved it most: "We draw the things we know. We mark the things we're coming to. A map that pretends to know everything is a map that lies to you when you need it."

Olive wrote that down word for word. Marcus looked at the provisional star and said nothing, which meant it went into the second sheet, and the rope scale beside it for the north path's crossing, so that anyone walking it with the fair copy in hand would know the truth of its wet margin. Annabeth's ink went on in small certain lines, and the paper drank it, and the north shore was made permanent.

They worked for an hour. The rain kept time. The cider went around in the thermos cap, warm with cinnamon, and blankets went over knees, and somewhere between the second and third pass-around the map kids went quiet: Marcus's rope slid from his shoulder, Olive's red notebook slid off her knee, and Annabeth looked up to find both of them asleep against each other under the wool, warm, dead to the world. She tucked the blanket up and went back to her ink.

Which was when Percy was there.

He had been in the pool. She knew it the way she knew water — the damp at his collar, the salt at his temples, his hair pushed back while it dried at the ends, a pink spot at each cheekbone from cold and heat meeting. He had a towel over his shoulders and a pair of dry wool socks in his hand, which he dropped onto the blanket pile without being asked, and he settled into the nest beside her with the sigh of somebody whose insides had finally come around to match his outsides.

"What did it look like," he asked, nodding at the ink.

"Like rain, if rain were patient." She showed him the fair copy. He studied it the way he studied maps — head tipped, eyes tracking the north shore, his thumb moving over the contour lines without quite touching them, the way he touched everything he loved: lightly, so as not to mark it.

"You marked the spring with a question mark," he said.

"Everything north of the old line is a question mark until I walk it in daylight with the pencils." She set the sheet down. "Clarisse says you fixed her scarf."

"I closed a hole. Kind of. It's held together by spite and my stitches."

"Olive told me you built the roof."

"I built the roof."

"Of course you did." She was smiling, precise and fond, not looking at him because she was looking at the fair copy, and she had said that better with her face than her mouth managed. Then she reached over — deliberately, the way she did when she had decided on a thing — and found his hand, the wet-warm, cool-fingered hand he happened to have, and she laced her fingers through his and did not let go.

He turned his hand over. On his palm, in the creases of it, she drew with one fingertip — she had his whole hand mapped, if anybody had asked; she had measured the distance between his knuckles once, just to know it — a lopsided trident, the twin of the one he drew on notes and napkins and cave walls. She drew it slow, on the ridge of his palm, at the base of the fingers.

"I'm putting it on the map," she said quietly. "Interior of the fort. Tuesday, rainy. Two people and a blanket."

"Title them something true," Percy said, which was the way he asked for things. Then: "Title them 'held'. She said you hold the ankle, you hold the day. Title them 'held'."

"Then I'm holding your palm until the ink dries," Annabeth said, which was a sentence she had planned all afternoon without knowing it, and he laughed at the mind of her — the nerve of her — and then didn't laugh at all, because she moved over beside him under the blanket and put her head down on his shoulder, and his arm came around her, and the fort held its roof, and the rain pattered down over the quilt like applause going on for a thing well done.

They sat. The map kids slept. The ink dried. The second sheet filled itself out with Marcus's rope ticks and a final small pencil note that one of them — probably Olive, possibly the rain — would credit to Atlas, and the lantern dipped and swung, and the years of her life before this room got smaller and further away, the way things do, until they were a place she had lived once, in a dorm with a red pack and a stomach full of nobody.

Percy kissed her there. It was not a big thing. He turned his head, and she turned to meet him, and his other hand came up to her jaw, the weight of it the weight of someone being careful on purpose, and the kiss was slow and certain and tasted faintly of salt and cedar and the cinnamon from the cider, and neither of them was in any hurry anywhere, and the rain went on over the quilt roof like the sound of something being allowed, and it was warm, and he was warm, and his heart under her hand was going at the rate of a boy held, which is to say steady.

After a while they did not sit so much as lean under the blanket, thigh to thigh, her head heavy on his shoulder, his chin in her hair, both of them listening to the map kids breathe and the rain to settle. She traced the lines of the fair copy against the lantern light, one then another, and he traced her fingers past each line, and she thought, drowsily, in the way she only let herself think when she was too warm and too safe to be precise: this is what the map is. Not the shape of the ground. The shape of a day that nothing was missing from.

"Tuesday was a letter day," she whispered.

"It's written," he whispered back. "It goes at dusk with Brine — the cleat and the key and the honey sticks and the thing about the fort having a roof."

"With 'roomier' in the middle, where it mattered."

"With 'roomier' where it mattered," he said, and the lantern light went warm across them both, and the rain pattered soft over the quilt roof like a clock keeping time for a thing that had already arrived. Her hand, tracing his, found the small trident she had drawn in his palm and stayed there, and his hand closed over hers, and from the kitchen corner there came the particular sound of Silena Beauregard clapping her hands once — the sound all good dinners answer to.

"Annabeth," Silena called, warm, from the front hall, over the rain. "Supper. Bring the map, bring the kids, bring the fort and whoever's under it."

"Bring yourself too," Clarisse added, in the carrying voice that meant: all of you, and don't test me.

Annabeth looked up at the quilt roof of the fort, at the lantern, at the wet boots by the door going dry in the warmth, and tightened her fingers in Percy's for a second — exactly the grip she had learned from him, the one that meant I'm here and I'm not moving — and let him help her up.

"Room's still yours," she told him.

"It's yours," he said. "I just built the roof."

By the time supper came to the table, the hall of Tempest Hall had stopped being a hall.

Silena had it in her bones that a cold day ends fed, and she fed like a woman stacking kindling against winter. The chowder pot steamed on the stove's side. Bread was torn into the center of the table with the crust still crackling; cider was mulled hard with cinnamon and went around in the shell bowls because it was Cabin Three and nobody was measuring. And for dessert there were baked apples: cored, wetted with honey, split open on purpose so the skins curled back like leaves, each one with two walnuts on top and a drizzle that ran into the middle, baked in the little oven until the whole room smelled like the last week of an orchard.

Thalia came up the path at six with rain in her hair and day all over her, having spent the afternoon at the Big House with the kids on account of the rain — drachma games and cards and a ten-year-old who had tried to start his own economy. She took in the fort with her chin up, made the rounds of the quilts, and announced to the room that as the guest of honor she was taking the inside corner and the best blanket, and Silena said, "You've lived here five months, Sky, you're not a guest," and Thalia said, "And yet when the rent falls due, who do you call," and Clarisse said, "The only guest we ever had with opinions was a horse, and it had the decency to eat our bread and sleep by the pool."

At the door, briefly, the Stolls appeared, drawn by the smell, and were fed at the threshold with chowder in tin cups and a heel of bread each, and Connor tried to negotiate his way into the fort and was dismissed with the kettle's-about-to-boil speech, because Clarisse had known Connor Stoll for three years and had the ladle ready.

Then the lamps and the lanterns went up, and the room settled.

Percy sat in the fort with his back against the pile of blankets and the map kids asleep beside him under the wool, with a tin cup of cider in both hands, and he watched his hall the way he had watched the cove the first night of summer: the way you watched a thing you were allowed to have. The lantern swung. The stove breathed. Rain pattered soft on the quilt roof. His own scarf — their scarf, their terrible two-holed blue scarf — was on his knee, and Annabeth took it off him and put it around her neck, because it was cold near the door, and because his sister had made a hole in it and he had made a worse one, and it was the warmest thing in the room. Silena went around with the baked apples, and Thalia told the story of the rain that caught her on the west climb, all of it made comedy — the tarp that failed, the wind, the slab — and then, without warning anyone, the part that wasn't.

"There was a shelf of rock under the granite shoulder," Thalia said, slow for her, the firelight of the lantern moving on her face. "And a seep under it, singing. I got under the shelf and ate my apple in the dark, soaked to the bone, four days out, and I thought: well. I am completely alone. And the water under the rock was completely unbothered by me. And I was so happy, in that minute, that I had to say it out loud, so the water would know somebody had noticed." She tipped her cup at the room. "The trees don't tell that one back to you. That's what's wrong with the trees."

Nobody said anything for a second. Then Silena reached over and put her hand flat on Thalia's knee, the way she did when a thing had been said truly and needed a weight on it, and Percy watched his sister-in-all-but-blood take the warmth and not make a joke of it, and thought: that's how you hold a person.

And then Olive woke up, and everybody ate apples, and Clarisse set out the shells-and-stones set — the board worn soft from a hundred summers, shells for the sea-side and stones for the shore-side, three-in-a-row and no mercy — and the storm evening took everything they had. Olive played the way she did everything, narrating; Marcus played like he was setting a winch, each stone a load-bearing stone; Annabeth played three moves ahead and said so out loud; Percy played like a boy who knew numbers and had never once been able to make them hold still, and lost, and got beaten because of it, and loved them. Clarisse played like the authority. The authority had never once lost a game of shells-and-stones at Camp Half-Blood, and Thalia spent twenty minutes trying to negotiate a rule change for the sheer joy of it while everybody else shouted down both of them, and the echoes went up into the quilt roof, and the rain went on gentle against it, and the day settled into the camp in the shape a family settles into: loud at the door, quiet in the middle.

At dusk the rain thinned from a wall to a curtain, and Percy took the oilcloth packet from the shelf by the stove where it had been kept dry, and Brine lifted its head from beside the pool as if it had been counting too.

"Ready," Percy said.

"Ready," Clarisse said, from the fort, where she was pretending not to care.

"Tuesdays are letter days," Annabeth said, precise and glad.

Brine took the packet in its teeth by the tied corner, soft-lipped and careful, and Percy stood a second at the edge of the pool with the creature nosing the packet into place around its neck. The water was dark-salt from the deep channels and breathing swells against the tile, and the hippocamp's eyes were coins, and his brother's words were in the packet — plain and true and sealed dry — and tomorrow morning they would be on a forge table in a place Percy had never been, being read by a boy with one eye who talked to his hands.

"Tell him—" Percy started. Brine waited. "Yes. Tell him."

So Brine dipped its head and slipped down out of the pool water into the deep end, a hoop of ripples and a flick of a fish tail, a soft suck and gurgle from the channel grate, and it was gone the way Triton and the Tuesday ferry and his brother had always gone: not out of the family, just out of the room.

Clarisse came up beside him at the pool edge with two cups of cooling cider and handed him one.

"Letter's gone," she said.

"Yeah."

"Stop carrying it."

"I would," Percy said, "but it's heavy." He drank the cider. It was cold now and still sweet, cinnamon gone faint. She stood beside him in the storm dark, this blunt green-jacketed sister of his, and he bumped her shoulder with his, once, the way he had learned that meant what words could not, and she bumped back twice.

Inside, the apples were being finished and the map kids were being carried up to their cabins in the borrowed blankets, sleeping through the whole adventure of it — Olive with her notebook still under her arm, Marcus with his rope still in his fist, both of them walked home by two teenagers who had once been lost in the snow and remembered exactly what that walk was worth. The lanterns went low. The rain settled into the comfortable pulse of a storm that was losing interest.

At the door of the hall, before he went back in, the sky tore a little above the pines, and there was one star, and it hung there over the wet camp like a note nailed to a door. The north wind had swung around. The rain came down softer.

Silena came out with her own cup and looked up at the star, and her face did the thing it did when she was deciding the future.

"Three days of rain," she said, "and tonight the wind turns. If it's like this at dawn—" She stopped. Beside her, almost as one, the three of them said it: "The canoe."

"I'll ask Chiron tomorrow," Silena said, already writing the plan on a slip in her pocket, inside out, because she always planned the future with her hands full: FIRST CLEAR → CANOE. Then, under it, in the dark, taking the pencil from her hair: FIRST SNOW → SOUP. And under that, in her round fast hand, the way she wrote all the laws of the cabin: KEEP THE FORT. It flourishes in storms.

The fort had survived the worst of the rain, but it had not survived everyone trying to sleep in it. One corner sagged where a knot had slipped along the rope. The blanket above the door was hanging so low that Clarisse had to duck.

"Tomorrow," Silena said, surveying it, "we make that knot properly."

"Tomorrow we do several things properly," Clarisse answered.

Percy went to check the lantern before sitting. It had swung toward a fold of wool, close enough to make him uncomfortable. He moved it back to the stone ledge, well clear of the fabric, and tested the hook. Annabeth opened one eye from her place on the quilt.

"You fixed it?"

"I moved it. Different thing."

"Good difference."

He passed her the end of the scarf that had slipped off her shoulder. She took it and tucked it round herself, leaving enough room beside her for him. Thalia had already claimed the inside corner, boots off, with a pile of rope to one side. From beneath the next blanket Silena's voice ordered somebody to put the wet socks outside the sleeping space.

"Who's on that tomorrow?" Percy asked.

"Nobody until the rain stops," came Clarisse's answer.

It was sensible, and Percy recognized that he was tired enough to be glad someone else had said it. He lowered himself onto the quilt and tucked his arm around Annabeth, who made room for him without opening her eyes again.

The shells ticked at the eaves, slower now. The letter was on its way north, and the fort would need repairs when they were awake. Percy could leave both matters where they were. In the morning they'd have to untie everything and find out which blankets were dry. Tonight, the lantern was safe, the entrance was clear, and there was enough space for everyone to sleep.
