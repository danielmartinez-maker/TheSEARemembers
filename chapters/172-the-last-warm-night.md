## Chapter 172: The Last Warm Night

The first story Percy knew about sleeping outside began with a sea goddess giving her nine-year-old son permission.

Amphitrite had told it to him: she had signed the permission herself, in a hand that looked like tide charts, and Triton had kept watch with a bronze lantern hung from a pole and a spear across his knees, performing vigilance the way other people performed music. In the story, Percy lay on a reef flat in sand that hissed when the tide moved across it, under a sky with more stars than he had names for, and listened to the ocean breathe for eleven straight hours. At dawn, Amphitrite said, he came up the beach with salt in his eyebrows and concluded, with the certainty of nine, that sleeping outside was the best thing a person could do standing up, and that everybody who didn't was simply misinformed.

Amphitrite said he had not done it again. Atlantis had rules about tides. Camp had rules about everything else, and most of the rules had Clarisse's fingerprints on them.

So when Chiron wheeled down the switchback to the north beach on Saturday morning with a clipboard under his arm and the expression of a man signing a minor treaty, Percy was the first one on his feet.

"There are conditions," Chiron said.

"There always are," said Clarisse.

The conditions were these, and Annabeth wrote them down, because Annabeth wrote down everything:

The fire ring at the north beach, and nowhere else. The fire laid before dusk and never left unattended, which meant that from dusk until dawn one of them was always awake, always sitting up, always watching flames the way you watched a sleeping child.

The check-in slate leaned against the ring's stones, and it was initialed at the top of every hour in five different hands, and it came up the hill at breakfast so that Chiron could count them twice.

No swimming past the boundary rock. Nobody went beyond the shallows without a shore-held safety line and two designated watchers. Nobody in the water at all after midnight, because the lake held its warmth deep down and surrendered it slowly, but this was late September, and the surface had opinions.

The harpy would pass at midnight and count them, and Chiron said the word *harpy* in a tone that suggested Ms. O'Fee was not personally liable for what happened next.

And if the frost fell — Chiron looked at the sky the way he looked at bad news — if the frost fell tonight, the water was closed, the fire was doubled, and anyone who was cold went up the hill to a real bed with no shame attached and no discussion permitted.

Friday's plan had been to sleep up on the ridge above the spring; Chiron's Saturday approval moved the overnight to the North Beach ring, under his conditions.

"Questions," Chiron said.

"When did you decide this?" Annabeth asked. "Because on Friday you said we'd have to wait for the equinox committee."

"There is no equinox committee."

"You said there was no equinox committee on Thursday. You said it at dinner."

"I said it in April," Chiron said, "about something else. Last night your sister Thalia came to my porch at eleven with her boots still on and argued for twenty minutes, and I would like it noted for the record that I did not yield to the argument. I yielded to the girl."

"She'll deny the whole thing," Silena said.

"Twice," said Thalia, who had not been present, and who would have denied it twice anyway.

Chiron wheeled himself back up the switchback with the clipboard under his arm, and the pine needles made no sound under his wheels.

---

They spent the afternoon gathering, the way you gather for something you intend to remember.

Clarisse did inventory with the solemnity of a quartermaster laying in for a siege. She spread everything across the tile floor of Tempest Hall and checked it against a list she kept in her head: two quilts, including the wool one that did not smell like the boathouse; three blankets; the canvas satchel with rope and knife and line and hooks and the tin cup, because Clarisse did not go anywhere without rope; towels, two each, plus a third for later, because you always needed the third towel; and the folding camp chair, which she had carried up a hill once and would carry down again out of pure spite.

"You don't need the chair," Percy said.

"I need the chair."

"The sand is soft."

"The sand is soft until two in the morning. Then the sand is a rock with opinions." She folded a blanket with the raccoon precision he teased her about. "Sit where you want, Jackson. I'll be comfortable."

In the corner bunk — the one they never made anything of and never took down — the white rock sat on the folded shirt where Tyson had left it, round and patient as an egg, minding its own business while five people got ready to spend a night outside on its account. Nobody had to. The bunk stayed behind. The rock stayed on the pillow. Both things were true at once.

Silena did the food the way she did everything, which was as an argument with fate: cold chicken and potatoes and onions cut thin, bread from that morning, cheese in thick honest wedges, honey from the apiary jar with its wax seal cracked, three lemons from the greenhouse, the last four September peaches fragrant enough to perfume the whole shelf, tea in the thermos, and, at the bottom of the crate wrapped in its own towel like jewelry, honey bread and honey cake, because an overnight was a Silena operation and Silena did not believe in going to bed hungry.

Thalia showed up at four with an armful of split oak across her shoulder and granite dust still on her boots from the morning's boundary walk, complaining the whole way down the path about how she had spent four days as a professional surveyor and was now a mule.

"I carried a panel copy across the north ridge," she said, dumping the wood beside the fire ring with a clatter. "In oilcloth. With care. And today I'm kindling."

"You're kindling because you cut the kindling wrong," Clarisse said.

"I cut the kindling fine."

"You cut it like you were in a hurry."

"I was in a hurry. I had a fire to get to." Thalia's grin went crooked. "Also, hi. I missed you people for four hours. This has been the worst day of my life, and I would like it logged in the book."

"It's logged," Silena said.

Annabeth came last, down the switchback with her bag over her shoulder and graphite on her left cuff and a folded paper in her hand that turned out to be a schedule, which she denied having made for eleven full seconds, then admitted, with pride.

"It's a timeline," she said. "For the night. Schedules are for people who think things take less time than they take."

"What's on it?"

"Fire by six. Dinner by seven. Stories by eight. Float by nine. The—" She stopped. "The rest is private."

"Private how?"

"Private per the rest." She folded it along its creases and put it in her pocket, and her ears went pink, and Percy, who had spent five months learning to read her the way he read water, by surface and undertow, let it go, because he had learned that some things in this family had become load-bearing and therefore invisible.

---

They went down to the lake at five, in a ragged line, loaded like mules, the water flashing through the pines to their left and then not flashing and then flashing again. The air was cold at the top of the hill, where the grass was still wet and the pines held the shade in handfuls. It was warm by the middle, where the path came out alongside the water and the afternoon lay across it in one long plank. By the bottom it had that particular temperature of a September evening that had not yet admitted it was October: cold enough that you wanted a jacket, warm enough that you did not go back for one.

The north beach took them the way it always took Cabin Three, which was without comment.

It was the last place at camp where the pines ran right down into the water: a half-moon of sand and warm flat stone that nobody had built anything on, the shallows glassy over white sand, the boundary rock sitting out in the middle of the lake like a punctuation mark with its white X just visible. The willow cove lay to the left, green shade over the half-sunk skiff, heron-gray and still. The fire ring waited at the tree line, a collar of black stones half-sunk in the sand, the one nobody had used in September — built by somebody's grandfather, maintained by nobody in particular, which meant it belonged to everyone, which meant it belonged to them.

Clarisse laid the fire the way she fought: blunt, structural, no ornament. Thalia struck the match and held it to the kindling with four days of campsite practice showing in absolutely nothing she did, because Thalia made competence look like an accident. The flame took. Silena fed it pine needles and then twigs and then a pale curl of birch bark she had saved for exactly this, and the fire stood up crackling and turned the sand gold and threw their five shadows out toward the water, long and wavering and joined at the feet.

Percy built the nest.

He did it carefully, the way he did things he had decided were important, shaking the quilts out on the sand above the waterline, folding the wool blanket into a pad, laying the towels in a stack where nobody would trip on them, arranging the flat rock the way Annabeth arranged a drafting table: at the right angle to the fire, with the lake in reach and the pines at their backs. When he was finished it looked like something a person could fall asleep in by accident.

"That's the nest," Clarisse said.

"That's the nest."

"You made a nest."

"I made a nest, Clarisse."

"It's a good nest." She said it to the fire, in the voice of a girl admitting a wound. "Don't let it go to your head."

Percy sat down on the flat rock with his shoes off and his toes in two inches of cool sand and felt his face do the small loosening thing it always did when the lake found him, and did not bother putting the guard back up, because there was nobody left to guard against. The sun went down behind the far shore. The water took the dark gold that comes after the bright gold and held it there, and the first star came out over the pines, and then the second, and then there was no point counting.

Five of them. One fire. One night, and the whole of it theirs.

---

Annabeth Grace had a theory about dinners, and the theory was this: the meal did not have to be good. The meal had to be theirs.

She had brought the components of foil packets — chicken, potatoes cut thin enough to cook through, onions, butter, salt — and Silena had decreed, with the flat authority of a girl who had decided moods were optional, that each of them would build one, that the packets would go into the coals at seven, and that nobody would criticize anybody's packet, on pain of dishes.

So they built them, and they were, without exception, disasters.

Clarisse built hers like a fortification: sealed, structural, defensible. It caught fire in the coals and came out smoked on the outside and raw in the middle, and she ate it anyway with the expression of a woman who had never once in fifteen years conceded a point.

Thalia, who had cooked over four days of fires out of necessity and would have been insufferable about it if she had not been hungry, built hers quickly and well, and then ruined the victory by announcing, at length, that anyone could cook a meal if they weren't emotionally attached to architecture.

Annabeth ignored her. Annabeth built her packet with precision, which was the only way she knew how to build anything: measured, folded, sealed with a double flap she was prepared to defend in a review. She put it into the coals at exactly seven by the watch on her wrist, and she turned it at nine minutes by the same watch, and it still came out lopsided and underdone in the middle, because she had misjudged the coals, and because the coals did not care about her watch.

Percy built his the way he did everything, by feel, and it came out roughly triangular, and it was, by some accident of the lake's good will, perfect.

"That's not fair," Annabeth said.

"Nothing about me is fair," Percy said. "I'm a triumph of natural selection. Pass the honey."

They traded the bad ones around and ate the good ones first, and Silena produced the honey bread and the cheese and the peaches at exactly the moment the trading stopped being funny, which was the moment she had been waiting for. The meal was bad in the way that meant good: smoke on their fingers, butter on their wrists, salt, the peaches giving a little when pressed, the tea hot enough to fog the air between their faces. The fire settled into its middle hour. Somebody's guitar started up at the pavilion, far off and faint, and somebody else joined in, off-key and earnest, and the wind brought pine and woodsmoke and the smell of the lake going cold for the night.

It was Thalia who said the word that had to be said at a campout.

"Ghost story," she said. "Somebody tell one, or I'll tell one, and you all know how I tell them."

"I'll tell one," Clarisse said instantly.

"You will not."

"I'll tell the one about the—"

"No."

Annabeth sat forward. She had been carrying something all evening — not a worry, exactly. Worry she could plan around. This was the other thing, the one that arrived when everything was going well: the awareness of the shape of the night, its edges, the fact that it had a beginning and an end. She had built the packet like a fortification. She was, she understood dimly, doing to the evening what she did to everything, which was trying to keep it from ending by holding it still.

"Go ahead," she said. "Tell it. I'll take notes. I'll identify the structural flaws."

"The genius has a pencil," Thalia said. "Everybody behave."

So Thalia told it, and she told it badly on purpose so that they would lean in.

The story was about the boundary rock. Years ago, she said, in a September like this one, a camper swam out to the boundary rock on a dare, in the dark, and the lake gave him back, but the lake kept his shadow. And on cold nights, if you stood where they were standing and looked hard at the water, you could see two shadows out there on the lake where there ought to be one.

"His name was Gary," Thalia said. "From the Hermes cabin. No offense to Hermes kids. He was a nice kid. Terrible swimmer. Wouldn't have made it halfway in daylight."

"That is not how shadows work," Annabeth said.

"Tell me how shadows work, genius."

"Shadows are a function of light geometry and the opacity of the occluding body. A shadow cannot be—" She stopped. The fire moved. Out on the lake the boundary rock sat in the star-glitter with its white X pale, and there was nothing on the water but the water. "That is not how shadows work," she said again, more quietly.

"The lake," Thalia said, in the voice of a girl who had spent four days learning what the ridge said back, "keeps what it's given."

"Okay," said Clarisse. "Story's over. Pinecones. Now."

She was right, and the pinecones helped, and the shouting match that followed went out across the water and the lake took it the way the lake took everything, and the evening kept its shape.

Then Percy said, "I dare somebody to swim out to the boundary rock," and Clarisse said, "Absolutely not," and Thalia said, "Watch me," and that was how the dare round began.

Silena invented the rules in eleven seconds, the way she invented all the rules that mattered: one dare each, no repeats, nothing requiring stitches, nothing about the water after dark unless the darer went first.

Thalia went first, because Thalia always went first. Her dare was the boundary rock. Clarisse fitted a loose line around her waist, checked the knot, and put the coil into Annabeth and Silena's hands. Thalia rolled her eyes, then stepped in; the two watchers paid out the line as she swam past the sandbar, star-glitter breaking around her shoulders and the cold taking her mid-chest. They watched her head cut the line of the sky until it reached the rock, and she touched it, and she came back. When she waded out, Clarisse took the line from her and Thalia was grinning and blue-lipped and absolutely furious about it.

"Ten minutes," she said, through chattering teeth. "Per occurrence. Retroactive. Somebody hit me with a towel before I die and you all have to explain it to Chiron."

Then it was Percy's turn to be dared, and he did not have to be asked, because the dare had come out of his own mouth, and Clarisse had the rope in her hands before he finished saying it.

"If you go, you go roped," she said. "I'm not fishing you out with my bare hands at ten at night in September. I've got a reputation."

"You've got several."

"Coil it around your waist. Twice. Annabeth, hold my belt." She said it flat and casual and absolutely not casual at all, and Annabeth held her belt, and Silena held Annabeth's shoulder, and Clarisse braced her boots in the sand and took up the line with both hands, and Percy Jackson walked into the lake in the dark and swam out to the boundary rock while four people on the sand held their breath in four different ways.

The water was black-green under the stars. His head cut the starfield. Twice he stopped and floated on his back, and twice he went on, and at the rock he got one hand on the barnacle roughness of it and held a moment, in the middle of the lake, in the dark, in the last week of September, with a rope around his waist and four people on the other end of it.

He swam back and came out of the water steaming faintly in the cold, and Clarisse had the towel ready, and she dried his hair with it the way she had straightened his shirt on the rock in the sun weeks ago: blunt, efficient, pretending it was nothing.

"You're a menace," she said.

"I'm roped," he said. "Roped menaces are safe."

Then Percy dared Annabeth, because it was his turn to give one, and what he dared her was to say something true that she had not said out loud yet.

Annabeth considered it the way she considered a contour, wanting the true line. "I want to walk the spring with Thalia next week," she said. "With my pencils. I've been wanting to say it out loud for four days, and I kept not saying it, because saying a thing out loud makes it a plan, and plans are the kind of thing that can be ruined."

"Say it again," Silena said softly.

"I want to walk the spring with Thalia next week. With my pencils. It's a plan now. You've all heard it."

"Logged," Thalia said, in a voice that was not quite steady.

Annabeth dared Clarisse to sing.

"I don't sing," Clarisse said.

"You hum. You hum when you oil the spear. Silena told me."

"Silena talks too much."

"One line," Annabeth said. "One line, and I'll never mention it again."

Clarisse looked into the fire. The flame moved in her dark eyes. Then she put her face down close to her tin cup and hummed four bars of the sailing song with no words — the one Silena sang when she was feeding people — and it came out rough and low and entirely without performance, and everybody went quiet, and the lake spoke against the sand, and Thalia said, "That was beautiful, and I hate it, and if you tell anyone I cried I'll drown you in the shallows."

Silena's dare was the one that stayed.

"One thing each," she said, dealing honey cake like cards, warm and perceptive and seeing everything, "that you want to do before the first frost. Go around. No skipping. I'll log it."

Thalia: "Climb the ridge again before the snow. Just because I can. Just to see if I still like her."

Percy: "One more float. In the lake, at night, with my ears under. Before it closes."

Clarisse looked into the fire for a long moment. Then she said, bluff and low, the stone in her shoe touched and not removed, "I want all five of us standing here when the frost comes. Not gone. Here. That's my answer. Write it in the book."

"It's written," Silena said.

Annabeth went last. "The blank under the ridge line," she said. "I want to fill it in. Properly. With the slab and the seep and the cairn with the blue rag, and I want it right the first time, so nobody has to come along behind me and fix it."

"And that," Silena said, "is the whole family in five sentences, and I'm not crying, the fire's just badly built."

"My fire's fine," Clarisse said.

"My fire's burning," Silena agreed, and wiped her face on her sleeve, and fed it.

Silena Beauregard had a theory about the Group Float, and the theory was this: the water was not the point. The holding was the point.

She had made the ritual up at eleven, in the hot week of her second July, and she had kept it for five years, and she had watched it grow from a thing she did alone into a thing Cabin Three did, into a thing that Annabeth Grace did now, into a thing that five people were about to do in the dark in the last week of September, when the lake was cold enough that the honesty came free.

"Rules," she said, standing up and untying her camp shirt at the waist, because every one of them had a suit on underneath, because Cabin Three dressed for water the way soldiers dressed for weather. "Somebody floats, one at a time. Somebody holds the ankle. Ears under, one true thing, no planning. When you come up, nobody asks. If anybody asks—"

"—you're on dishes for a week," Clarisse said. "We know. You tell it every time."

"You tell it every time," Thalia said. "We all just stand here and let you."

The lake at night in late September was a different animal than the lake of August. August was warm on top and cold underneath, and you could stay in until your fingers wrinkled and nobody minded. This was honest cold all the way down, the kind that took your breath and then gave it back sharper. They went in off the sand in a spread of ripples, and every one of them made the same sound, a short caught inhale, and every one of them pretended they had not.

Silena took the outside. Clarisse took the inside, nearest the shore. Percy in the middle of the line, and Thalia, and Annabeth, each of them holding the ankle of the one ahead, and the water moved gently under them all, and the sky went on doubling itself in the lake the way sky does over water, so that they floated between two skies, one full of stars and one full of stars, and there was no telling which was the true one.

"Nobody has to go first," Silena said. "But somebody does."

"I do," she said, and took her breath, and went under.

She stayed down longer than she had in August. Her hair spread around her head in the black-green light, and her free hand found Clarisse's ankle and tightened, and the stars above and below her wavered and held, and she said it, the one true thing, the ticket price, into water that would not tell anybody. When she came up her face was shining and her eyes were clear and she gave no explanation whatsoever, because that was the rule, and because the rule was the kindest thing she had ever invented.

Thalia went next, and stayed down longer than Silena had. Under the surface her hair went wild around her, ink in green light, and when she came up she gasped and laughed at the same time and said nothing at all, and Annabeth, holding her ankle, laid a free hand flat on her back and held it there while she breathed.

Clarisse went third, the way she always went: no drama, head under, straight back up, shaking her hair out like a dog. "One," she said, in a voice that was a little too steady, and her fingers tightened on Percy's ankle and let go, and Silena, in the rear, where she saw things, watched her sister's face and logged nothing, which was itself a kind of logging.

Percy went fourth. He filled his lungs and went down with the neatness he had learned all summer, and the lake closed over his ears and the world went to the low green roar, and the two skies met somewhere above his face. Silena counted, because somebody always counted, and at eleven she stopped counting, because he came up on his own, streaming and steady, his face doing the thing it did when a line had come out exactly right. He did not explain. She did not ask. She held his ankle a half second longer than the tradition required and let go.

Annabeth went last. She took her breath the way she took a test, deliberately, and went under, and her braid floated up around her like a rope unwinding, and she was down a long time — long enough that the water held her the way it held the dock and the skiff in the cove and the heron's reflection, which was the same and also not the same. When she came up she was gasping and grinning and furious about the cold, and she said nothing true out loud, not one word, and nobody asked, and that was the whole of it.

They floated until they got cold, which did not take long. Then they waded out in a tangle of towels and chattering teeth, and Silena had the thermos open before anyone thought to ask, and the tea went around in the tin cup, hot and dark and sweet, and the fire was still there, banked and breathing, and the blankets were exactly where Percy had put them.

---

Clarisse banked the fire down with the flat of the shovel and sat back on her heels, and then, because she never did anything the easy way, she reached over and straightened the collar of Percy's shirt with two blunt fingers, the way she had straightened his shirt on the rock in the sun weeks ago, and pretended it had not happened.

"You went out there roped," she said.

"Technically I swam. The rope came along for emotional support."

"You went out there in the dark like it was nothing."

Percy looked at her. Beside the fire her face was all angles and gold, fifteen and blunt and tired, the girl who hit things for a living and fed people as a second job, and her hands were wrapped around a tin cup the way they wrapped around a spear.

"It's not nothing," he said. "It's the lake."

"Yeah." She poked the coals. A spark went up. "That's the part that gets me. You go out there in the dark like the lake's on your side."

"The lake is on my side," Percy said. "It's on all our sides. It's a lake, Clarisse. It doesn't have another side to be on."

"Don't get philosophical with me at midnight. I've had a long week." She was quiet a moment. Then, bluff and low: "Five."

"Five what?"

"Five of us. I counted." She shrugged, a small one, the shrug that meant she was holding something. "I count every night. You know that."

"I know," Percy said. "Count again tomorrow."

"I was planning to."

"Count the night after that, too."

"I'll count till the lake freezes, Jackson. Don't tell me what to do." She elbowed him, not gently, and he elbowed her back, and they sat a moment with their shoulders touching in the firelight, and Silena, in the rear, where she saw things, wrote nothing down at all, which was the truest record the night was going to get.

---

Thalia sat with Annabeth under the quilt, and the static that lived in Thalia's clothes tickled faintly against Annabeth's sleeve, the way it had all summer, the particular small electricity of a girl who was the daughter of a sky that did not visit often and left its spark behind in her cotton.

"Your feet are cold," Thalia said.

"My feet are always cold. It's a documented phenomenon. Ask Clarisse. She's been complaining about them since Tuesday."

"Put them under the quilt. Not under mine. I'm not a radiator." She shifted so that Annabeth could put them under hers anyway, and made a face about it. "Next week," she said. "The spring. You bring the pencils. I'll bring the rope and the cairn notes and the granite dust."

"I'll draw the slab," Annabeth said. "And the seep. And the cairn with the blue rag streaming northeast in a southwest wind."

"You and your wind notes."

"You and your bearings."

Thalia was quiet for a while. Then she said, dry and soft underneath, "I like that you've got a place you're going, that's all. A place with your name on it that isn't standing in front of me. That's all I ever wanted, and now I've got it, and it turns out it makes me happy and strange and a little hollow at the same time, and nobody warned me about the hollow part."

"You're not hollow," Annabeth said. "You're the north. North just stands there."

"That's my line," Thalia said, thickly. "You stole my line."

"You gave it to me." Annabeth put her head on her sister's shoulder, just for a minute, exactly as long as it took to stop being a minute and start being a fact. "I'm keeping the place," she said. "And I'm coming with you to the spring. Both are true."

"Both are true," Thalia agreed, and put her arm around her, all collarbone and flannel and static, brief and hard, and let it stay.

---

She saw them go, of course. Silena saw everything; that was her job, the rear of the line, the one who noticed who was not eating.

Clarisse had said "take the wool blanket" in the tone of a girl throwing a punch, and Percy had taken it, and the two of them had drifted off down the shore toward the willows with their hands already finding each other, the way hands do when they have done it ten thousand times and intend to do it ten thousand more. Annabeth had paused at the edge of the firelight and looked back once, checking, and Silena had raised her cup to her, and Annabeth had smiled the small private smile she saved for people who let her go without making her explain, and then the dark took them.

Silena sat back against the flat rock with her tea and let them have the shore. Behind her, Clarisse was banking the fire again, for no reason anyone could name, and Thalia was lying flat on her back with her flannel over her face, and the lake moved along the sand in its small patient way, and the stars came on.

The shore went cold under Percy's feet, and then colder, and then it was pebbles.

They had walked away from the fire the way you walk out of a warm room into snow: in stages, the heat on their backs and the cold on their faces, and then the dark closed behind them and there was only the lake and the stars and the sound of small waves arranging themselves on the sand.

Annabeth's hand was in his. It had been in his for most of the evening, in the easy unremarkable way it had been in his for months now, but out here, away from the firelight, it became a different thing. He felt every finger. He felt the callus on her middle finger where the pencil sat, and the faint scrape across her knuckles from the climbing wall, and the pulse at her wrist, which was going slightly faster than the walking required.

"You're quiet," she said.

"I'm being atmospheric."

"Is it working?"

"It's working a little." He squeezed her hand. "I'm listening to the lake."

"The lake doesn't say anything."

"The lake says plenty. It's just mostly about the lake."

She made the sound she made when he had said something true and annoying, and they went on, and the pines thinned, and the willows started, and the lake opened out on their left in black-silver enormity, the stars doubled in it so completely that for a moment Percy could not tell which sky was the real one. They were walking between two skies. Both of them were full of stars.

Down in the shade where the willows closed over the water, the half-sunk skiff lay at its old mooring with the lake lipping at its hull, and the heron stood on the bow, gray and still and perfectly balanced, one leg folded, waiting the way it always waited. It turned its head as they came along the shore. It looked at them with the flat yellow attention of a thing that had decided, long ago, that they were worth watching. Then it looked away, and smoothed one feather, and went on being whatever it was being.

"Still there," Annabeth said quietly.

"Still there."

They went on past the cove, and the willows let them go, and the shore opened into the long curve that ran north toward the ridge, where the trees stood back from the water and the reeds grew thin and tall along the margin. Percy was the one who saw the frost.

It was on the reed heads. A thin silvering, star-bright in the starlight, a shine that was not water. He put two fingers out and touched a reed stem and came away with frost on his skin, and it was cold the way only very cold things are cold, the cold that goes past the skin and into the idea of the skin.

"First frost," he said.

Annabeth came and looked, and touched it herself, and held her fingers up to the starlight. "Chiron's almanac was right."

"Chiron's almanac is always right. It's the most annoying thing about him." He looked at the reeds, at the silver heads nodding along the whole margin of the lake, going out into the dark in both directions as far as they could see. "So that's it. That's the season turning. That happened tonight."

"That happened tonight," Annabeth agreed. Her voice was very even, the way it went when she was feeling something and had decided to feel it precisely. "Tomorrow's October."

"Yeah."

"I said something about October on Friday. At the fire. The panel might take a fortieth before October." She was quiet a moment. "I've still got it open, Percy. I haven't looked. I'm still leaving it open."

"I know," he said. "That's how you leave a thing for somebody who isn't here yet."

She looked up at him, gray eyes bright in the starlight, and did not say anything, and the lake moved, and he went on, because there were some things that had to be said standing up.

"Toe," he said.

Annabeth breathed out something that was almost a laugh. "Now?"

"Now. Before we sit down. Rules. No repeats. Somebody picks a constellation, the other one finds its smallest star, the toe, the faintest point, the one nobody looks at—"

"I know the rules, Percy. I perfected the rules."

"You did."

"Fine." She tipped her head back, and the bird-dog tilt came into her shoulders, the particular set she got when something was being measured. "Cepheus. The king. It's the little house-shape near the W."

Percy found it, tracing the dim pattern with his eyes the way he had learned to trace the dark between stars instead of the stars themselves, and there it was: a faint smudge of a star at one corner of the little house, barely a star at all, invisible unless you went looking for it. "The toe," he said. "The king's toe."

"The king's toe," Annabeth agreed. "True thing attached. That's the rule. No planning."

He stood with his hand in hers in the cold, with frost on the reeds and two skies over the lake, and he said it, and he did not plan it, which was the only way it counted.

"True thing is I counted tonight."

He felt her hand tighten.

"I've never told anybody this part. Atlantis feels like waiting between ships. I can't tell you a room or a face or even whether anyone actually counted; it's just the shape that idea takes in me. And since I got here, in the summer, I counted everything. Minutes, occurrences. I counted how long it took people to stop looking at me like a rumor. I counted how many mornings I woke up in the cabin before it started feeling like mine." His voice was steady, which surprised him, and he let it stay steady. "But this week I stopped. Silena says I stopped the day we floated, and she's right, and I haven't counted once since. Until tonight. Tonight I counted. And the difference is — Annabeth, the difference is I counted up. Not down. I counted up, like a person storing something. Like: this is a good minute, put it in the pile, this one too, keep it, don't lose it." He stopped. "That's the toe. The king's toe. Nobody looks at the king's toe."

Annabeth made a small sound, not a word, and turned to face him fully, both hands on his now, and her forehead came down against his collarbone for a second, and he felt her breathe.

"My turn," she said, into his shirt. Her voice was muffled and not steady at all, and she did not fix it. "My pick. Corona Borealis. The crown. The toe is the faint one at the bottom, the jewel nobody sees, because the bright ones get all the attention."

"I know the one."

"The true thing is—" She stopped. Started over, precise even here, planning even here, the girl who measured her own feelings before she spent them. "The true thing is I made a schedule for tonight, and I hated myself a little for it, because making a schedule for a night is a way of trying to make the night hold still, and nights don't hold still, they just go. And then I understood." She looked up. "I'm not trying to make it hold still, Percy. I'm trying to spend it right. That's the difference. That's the whole difference. You count up. I spend right. We're both just trying not to waste the last warm one."

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that's — that's a better one than mine."

"Mine are always better. You'll get used to it." She was smiling, wet-eyed, unembarrassed. "Do you want to see the schedule?"

"You have it on you."

"I have everything on me." She let go of one of his hands to dig in her pocket, and came out with the folded paper, creased soft now from an evening of being sat on, and unfolded it along its creases, and held it up between them where the starlight could reach it.

Her handwriting, precise as drafting, in the block letters she used for titles. FIRE BY SIX. DINNER BY SEVEN. STORIES BY EIGHT. FLOAT BY NINE. And under that, in her working hand, small and careful: 22:00 — PRIVATE. PERCY. NO TIME LIMIT.

Under that, smaller still, as if added later: no really. the whole rest of it. it's yours.

Percy read it twice. Something in his chest did the thing it did when something true went past without armor — a crack, a giving way, like ice going out of a lake.

"You scheduled me," he said thickly. "You scheduled me with no time limit."

"I scheduled you," Annabeth said, "the way you schedule the one thing you're not willing to rush."

He kissed her then.

He had known he was going to. He had known it since the fire, since the dare, since the moment the frost went silver in the reeds between them. But he took his time about it, because there was no bell and no watch and nobody on the whole north shore but the two of them and a heron, and because he had learned, over a summer of her, that the slow ones were the ones that counted.

He put his free hand up to her jaw, the way he always did, his thumb warm against her cheek, and she turned into it, and he felt her breath go out and her hand come up to fist gently in his shirt, holding the cloth instead of him, anchoring to the cotton so she would not have to anchor to anything else.

The kiss was slow, and it was unhurried, and it was not one kiss so much as the beginning of a long quiet conversation that had no words in it. Her mouth was cold from the walk and warm underneath. She tasted of tea and honey and the salt of the lake, and her eyelashes were wet where the cold had touched them, and when he kissed her again, deeper, slower, her free hand came up to lie flat over his heart, and he felt his own heartbeat under her palm and knew, without checking, that it was going too fast, and knew she knew, and knew she did not care.

The stars went on doing their work above them and below them. The frost silvered the reeds. Somewhere out on the lake a fish jumped with a flat silver smack and was gone, and neither of them jumped, and neither of them pulled away, and the whole enormous September night arranged itself around them the way water arranges itself around a stone: briefly, perfectly, while the ripples go out and out and out.

When they broke apart they did not go far. Their foreheads stayed together. Their breath went out together, visible in the cold between them, one small cloud joining another. Her hand was still over his heart. His thumb was still at her cheekbone.

"Percy," she whispered.

"Yeah."

"I don't want tonight to be over."

"I know," he said. "Me neither."

"I know how it goes. I know how nights work. They get late, and people get cold, and then it's morning, and then it's next week, and the lake closes, and the frost stays." Her voice did not shake, quite. "I hate it. I've hated it all week. I keep thinking, I should pay better attention, I should remember every single minute — and it doesn't work, Percy, I can't—"

"Hey." He got his other hand up to her face, both hands now, holding her the way you hold something that is flying apart. "Hey. Listen. I counted up, right? I told you. I'm counting up." Her eyes were on his, gray and bright and unguarded. "You can't make it slow down. But it doesn't have to be gone, Annabeth. Nothing tonight is gone. It goes in the pile. It's in the pile with the dock and the float and the peach and the ferry and the white rock and everything else, and it'll be there in February when you go looking for it. That's what the pile is for."

"You've thought about this," she said.

"I think about everything. It's my whole personality. I'm observant and wry and it's very tiring."

She laughed, the undefended one, the one that came out when something had been frightening and was over, except nothing had been frightening, only large. He let her laugh into his shoulder and held her, and the reeds whispered, and down in the cove the heron shifted its weight on the skiff's bow and settled.

"I want to ask you something," she said, when the laugh had run down. "And I want to ask it now, while it's quiet, before I lose my nerve."

"Ask."

"Do you ever think about — after. Next year. The year after that." She pulled back far enough to look at him. "Not the quest stuff. Not the war stuff. I mean ordinary things. Do you think about them."

Percy looked at her. At the girl who had spent the whole evening building a fortification out of an evening, who had a pencil behind her ear and frost on her eyelashes and a list in her pocket for a night she had not been willing to waste.

"Yes," he said. "All the time. I think about ordinary things so much it's embarrassing. I think about walking down to the lake in the morning. I think about doing the dishes. I think about finishing the ridge line on your map with you standing there telling me my contour interval is aspirational. I think about a winter where I know where I'm going to be in June."

"That's not very ambitious," Annabeth said softly.

"It's the most ambitious thing I've got." He laced his fingers through hers and started walking again, slow, the two of them and the shore, the reeds going by with their silver heads. "I don't know how to stay anywhere. I told Clarisse that, weeks ago. I asked her to teach me the staying part, and she said she'd think about it, and I've been thinking about it too. And I think maybe staying isn't a place. Maybe it's a pile. You keep putting minutes in it, and one day you look down and there's a whole life there, and you can't carry it anywhere without spilling, and so you stay."

Annabeth was quiet. Then she said, "That's either the wisest thing you've ever said or the stupidest."

"It's both."

"It's both," she agreed. "It's very you." She leaned her head against his shoulder as they walked. "I'll stay too. For the record. I'm not promising forever, Percy. I've read too many books where that gets people killed. But I'm staying for the ordinary things. The dishes. The contours. I'm staying, and you can file it, and you can bring it up in February when you don't believe it."

"I'll believe it in February."

"You say that now."

"I say it now," he agreed, unbothered, "and I'll say it in February, and in May I'll say it with a schedule, and that's how you'll know I mean it."

She hit him in the arm with the back of her wrist, and he let her, and they walked on, and the shore curved, and by unspoken agreement they turned back before the point where the pines came down to the water, because there were five people asleep on a beach and one of them would wake up and count.

They made their own place on the sand above the waterline, well clear of the reach of the waves, and got under the wool blanket — one blanket, the only way to share — with their backs against the flat rock and the fire a distant ember-glow down the shore. Her feet were icy. She put them against his shins without asking, and he made a sound, and she left them there.

"Your feet are freezing."

"My feet are always freezing. It's a known quantity. Silena could chart it."

"I could warm them."

"You are warming them."

"I'm aware." He shifted, and got his arm around her, and she settled in against him shoulder to knee, and the wool came up over both their shoulders, and the cold came down out of the north, and the frost went on forming on the reeds a few feet away, and neither of them mentioned it.

For a while neither of them said anything. The lake spoke instead, in its small voice against the sand. A loon called once, out past the boundary rock, and did not call again. The stars thickened, or seemed to, the way they do when your eyes get used to the dark, and the two skies over the lake leaned toward each other until Percy could not tell which one he was lying under.

"Annabeth," he whispered. Her breathing had gone slow and even against him, the breathing of somebody three quarters of the way to sleep.

"Mm."

"I'm glad you're here. That's all. I'm glad you're here, on the last warm one, and I'm glad you counted up with me."

She turned her face into the nook of his shoulder, the way she did, and her hand found his under the blanket and held on, and her voice came out slow and soft and a long way from anywhere.

"I'm here," she whispered. "Go to sleep, Percy. I'll be here in the morning."

"You can't know that."

"I can know tonight," Annabeth said. "Tonight's the part I get to know."

He had no answer for that, which was fine, because she was almost asleep, and because the ones with no answers were usually the true ones. He sat with his back against the warm stone and his girl almost asleep against him and the frost coming down out of the north, and he watched the star-glitter on the black water, and he did not count a single minute. He let them go by, one after another, unnumbered, and he put every one of them in the pile.

The fire was Clarisse's job, so Clarisse was the one who saw them come back.

She had banked it down to coals an hour earlier — the condition was that one of them stayed awake, and Clarisse had not argued; she had never intended to, because she had not slept properly at camp in five years and everybody knew it and nobody said it. She sat with her back against the flat rock and her green jacket pulled close and the check-in slate across her knees, initialing the hour in her blocky hand, and she watched the shore.

They came back the way people come back from something private: slowly, close, not talking. Annabeth had frost on her eyelashes. Percy was carrying his shoes. Their hands found each other at the edge of the firelight and did not let go, and neither of them explained anything, and Clarisse, who did not require explanations, did not ask for one.

"Ten minutes," she said. "Then everybody sleeps."

"You're not the mom," Percy said.

"I'm the oldest daughter of Poseidon and the mom is busy. Ten minutes."

Thalia and Silena were already down — Silena curled with her head on the folded quilt, humming faintly in her sleep the way she hummed when she worked; Thalia spread out on her back with her flannel over her face like a shroud, breathing slow and even, road-tired at last in the way that only came when the road had actually ended. Percy and Annabeth compromised by lying down on the far end of the blanket nest with the wool blanket pulled over both their shoulders, one blanket, the only way to share, their feet finding each other under it in the dark the way feet do when they have done it ten thousand times and intend to do it ten thousand more.

---

There were rules about feet in Cabin Three, though nobody had ever written them down. Cold feet went against warm things. That was the whole of the law, and it was enforced by silence and by the swift application of a warm ankle. Annabeth's feet were icy; they went against Percy's shins, and he made a sound, and she left them there. Clarisse's own feet went under the edge of the quilt where the wool was thickest, and Silena, without waking, shifted and put her feet against Clarisse's calves, because Silena's feet were always warm, because Silena ran hot, because Silena had been warming this family's feet for five years and had never once called it a rule.

Clarisse lay in the dark and listened to them breathe.

That was the thing nobody told you about camp. Not the quests, not the training, not the monster under the bed — the other thing. The sound of four other people asleep, and the specific certain knowledge that you would hear the same sound tomorrow, and the day after that, unless something happened. Unless the season turned. Unless a ferry came.

She thought about the ferry. She thought about it the way she thought about the corner bunk with the white rock on the pillow, which was to say constantly and without mentioning it. Tuesday, the whole cabin had stood on the dock and waved somebody away, and the whole cabin had held together, and the whole cabin had come up the hill afterward and baked a cake, and the arithmetic had held. Five. It was still holding. Tonight it had held from dusk until now — five heads, counted twice by a harpy and once by a man with a clipboard — and the number had not moved.

But it could move. That was the thing Clarisse knew about numbers. Numbers moved. Campers left in June and new ones came in the spring, and the towels stayed on the hooks, and the bunk stayed made up, and you learned, over five Septembers, not to put too much of yourself into a bunk that might be empty by June.

She turned her head and looked at Percy in the firelight.

He was asleep with his mouth slightly open and one arm flung out from under the blanket, and he had said something half an hour ago in his sleep — two words, soft and certain, *held twice* — which had made Thalia, on the far side of the nest, laugh into her own shoulder and then go quiet in the way people go quiet when they have been caught feeling something. He looked fourteen. He looked like a boy who had left a palace and a roof that hummed and a father who taught him everything, and who had spent one summer asking a blunt older sister to teach him the staying part, and who had, she thought, possibly already learned it better than she had.

Clarisse was not sentimental. She had a reputation. But she lay in the dark with frost forming on the black stones of the fire ring and listened to five breathings, and she made the decision the way she had been making it all summer: to stay. To count. To be here when the frost came, like she'd said at the dare, blunt and low, not gone. Here.

The harpy came at midnight, as promised. Ms. O'Fee swept in over the pines on a draft of cold air and a great deal of wing, and did not land, because harpies did not land where they were working, and counted them twice with a finger that was also a talon.

"Five," she said. "Five's right. Five's what's on the slate." She wrote something in a little book with the efficiency of a creature who had been doing this since before the Big House was the Big House. "The lake is not a suitable place to die of pneumonia. I'm putting that in writing to the centaur now, while it's still fresh."

"Nobody's dying of pneumonia," Clarisse said.

"Famous last words." Ms. O'Fee tucked the book away and dropped something down onto the sand beside the fire ring — a second thermos, this one dented, with a note tied to the handle by a string nobody wanted to examine too closely. "Soup. Do not spill it on my beach."

She went back up over the pines on the cold draft, and Clarisse drank a cup of harpy soup at midnight in the dark and told nobody, because some kindnesses were between a girl and a harpy, and because the soup was, infuriatingly, good.

---

Around three, the snoring started.

It was Clarisse's. It had been known since a folded note in a boy's pocket had said, in a handwriting that thought nobody was looking, CLARISSE SNORES WHEN SHE NAPS, DO NOT TELL HER I TOLD YOU, and it had been a closely guarded secret for approximately four days, which was how long secrets lasted in Cabin Three. Now it was just a fact, like the tide, or the frost.

"The lake is snoring," Percy said, half asleep, into the blanket.

"The lake does not snore," Thalia said, from the far side, in the voice of a girl who had been awake for an hour and had been lying there composing an official complaint. "Clarisse snores. The lake has never snored once in recorded history."

"I'm asleep," Clarisse said. "Asleep people don't talk. That's the whole definition."

"You're talking."

"I'm asleep talking. There's a difference."

Silena began, very softly, to hum.

It was the sailing song with no words, the one with no tune she trusted, the one she hummed when she was feeding people and when she was mending and when she was lying awake at three in the morning on a cold beach with four other people asleep around her. It went out into the dark and over the sand and out across the shallows, and the lake took it the way the lake took everything, without comment, and held.

---

The frost came down properly while they slept. Clarisse saw it at four, on the fire ring's stones — a white rime on the black rock, a sugar crust on the sand at the margin, the whole beach silvered at the edges while they had lain under their blankets and burned a fire. She got up once, and banked the coals, and put another log on, and stood a minute in the cold with her jacket pulled close, and looked at the lake.

It was going gray at the edges. Mist stood on the water in a thin sheet, the way it stood in mid-September, and the boundary rock sat out in the middle of it with its white X, and the willows were going silver, and down in the cove the half-sunk skiff lay at its mooring with the heron on the bow, gray and still and perfectly balanced, one leg folded, waiting the way it always waited. A loon called once, far off, and did not call again.

Clarisse went back to the blanket nest and lay down and put her feet back where they had been, and let herself have it: the warmth, the snoring, the breathing, the whole difficult holding thing.

They woke in pieces, the way people wake outdoors.

Annabeth came up first, out of the blanket, and sat on the flat rock with her hair in complete disaster and her field book already in her lap, and looked at the lake going gray-gold, and said, to nobody, "I should draw this," and then, "No. I should just look at it," which was, Clarisse thought, possibly the first time in recorded history that Annabeth Grace had chosen looking over drawing.

Percy surfaced next, out of the blanket and into the cold with his hair wet-looking and his eyes half open, and sat down beside her, and pulled the wool blanket back over both their heads, which was a system nobody had approved and everybody defended.

Silena made breakfast badly, on purpose, because the last warm night of the season deserved a morning that did not set a standard. She fried eggs in a skillet over the coals, and half of them came out with crisp edges and half of them came out with no edges at all, and there was the cold chicken and the last two September peaches and the honey cake, and the tea boiled twice because nobody was watching it, and it was perfect.

Thalia ate two eggs and a peach and declared the expedition a success and the mule underpaid. Annabeth annotated a napkin. Percy ate Clarisse's share of the eggs when she was not looking, and she was looking, and let him, which was as close to tenderness as Clarisse got before full dark, or possibly before full dawn.

And Clarisse sat on the flat rock with her tin cup in both hands and watched the sun come up over the far shore, and counted, the way she counted, one, two, three, four, five, and found the number held.

---

The first person to insist on cleaning the beach was Clarisse. She had counted the sleepers twice, packed her own cup, and discovered that nobody had bothered to bury the cold remains of the fire.

"Before we leave," she said, "the ring gets checked. Nobody wants a wind gust to carry embers into those reeds."

Percy looked at the ash. It appeared dead, but he'd been fooled by fire before. He fetched a bucket from the supply pile, drew lake water, and passed it to Clarisse. She tipped it carefully over the ring and stirred the wet ash with a stick until there was no warmth in the center.

"Now the bucket goes back," she said.

"I was planning to keep it as a souvenir."

"Good. Then you can carry it."

Annabeth was already folding the map she'd used to plan the camping site. She stopped to mark the place where they'd moved the sleeping blankets after the first cold gust came up the shore. Their original arrangement had left one side exposed. The revised one had held. It was a small correction, but she wanted to remember it before somebody else planned another trip.

Thalia helped Silena roll the bedding. One wool blanket had a damp corner; they kept it separate to dry properly at the cabin instead of packing it against the clean shirts. When the last bundle was tied, Thalia looked over their little campsite with an expression Percy couldn't read.

"What?" he asked.

"Nothing. I just thought we'd forget half this stuff by morning."

"There's still time."

"No," she said, lifting the bundle. "There isn't. We're leaving."

He took the bucket and followed. Annabeth fell into step beside him, carrying the fire map with her thumb over the new mark. Their feet were cold from the shore, and breakfast would be late. Behind them the beach was empty except for flattened sand and a safely soaked ring of stones. The last warm night had ended in practical work, and Percy found he didn't mind at all.
