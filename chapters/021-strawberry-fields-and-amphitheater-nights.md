## Chapter 21: Strawberry Fields and Amphitheater Nights



The first thing Percy Jackson learned about strawberry harvest duty was that nobody at Camp Half-Blood woke up cheerful for it.

The second thing he learned was that the conch horn Chiron used to wake the strawberry crew sounded absolutely nothing like the ocean. It sounded like a goose getting stepped on.

"Up! Up! Harvest crew, boots on!" The voice of Mr. D echoed across the cabins, magically amplified and deeply annoyed. "Yes, yes, your beauty sleep is over. The strawberries will rot if you dawdle and then where will we get money for your little horse camp? Chop chop!"

Percy sat straight up in his bunk, hair sticking in seven directions, salt still crusted behind his ears from yesterday's canoe lesson. The Poseidon cabin was quiet except for the drip of the fountain in the corner and the soft clink of seashells on his windowsill.

He'd been at camp a few weeks now. He knew the schedule by heart: breakfast, training, lessons, free time, dinner, campfire. But today was different. Today was Harvest Day, which apparently happened several times a season, when every available camper from age twelve up got drafted into picking the famous Camp Half-Blood strawberries — the ones that kept the camp funded in the mortal world, grown extra sweet and extra fast thanks to Dionysus and Demeter kids working overtime.

He pulled on his orange CAMP HALF-BLOOD shirt, shorts, and sneakers that were already stained pink from last week's berry snack incident, and stumbled out into the morning light.

The air smelled warm and bright. Cut grass, warm pine, sunscreen, and underneath it all, that bright sweet-tart smell rolling down from the hillside fields.

By the time he got to the pavilion for breakfast, half the camp was already there, looking rumpled and grumbling.

"Percy! Over here!"

Annabeth Grace was waving him over to the Zeus table — well, technically she sat at the Zeus table, while he usually sat at the Poseidon table with his sisters, Clarisse and Silena. That morning he carried his plate over to hers.

She looked annoyingly awake for seven thirty in the morning. Her blonde hair was pulled back in a tight ponytail, a smear of sunscreen on her nose, a notebook already open beside her plate of pancakes.

"You're late," she said, but she was smiling. Her grey eyes were bright. "I saved you the good syrup."

"You angel," Percy said, collapsing onto the bench across from her. "Why are you happy about harvest? I heard it's brutal."

"It's not brutal, it's *logistical*," Annabeth said, tapping her notebook. He leaned over to look. She'd drawn the strawberry fields in perfect scale, little grid lines marking rows A through K, with arrows and assignments. "See? Ten rows, four harvest teams, two runners for the collection baskets, one quality control station. If we organize it right we can finish by three instead of five."

Percy stared at her, then at her diagram, then back at her. "Annabeth, it's strawberry picking. Not the invasion of Normandy."

"It's both if Clarisse is involved," said a dry voice behind him.

Thalia Grace dropped onto the bench next to Annabeth, her black hair crackling with static, her electric-blue eyes half-open. She wore her leather jacket tied around her waist over her camp shirt, and her sneakers were untied. "Morning, Kelp Head. Morning, sis."

Annabeth and Thalia — Zeus's daughters and sisters in this strange new world Percy was still learning — had a bond he didn't fully understand but liked watching. They bickered like sisters, teamed up like soldiers.

"Tell me you brought coffee," Thalia groaned, stealing Annabeth's orange juice.

"I'm twelve," Annabeth said primly.

"Tell me you brought *ambrosia* disguised as coffee."

"No."

Thalia flopped forward onto the table. "Then I will die in the fields and my ghost will haunt the strawberries."

Across the pavilion, Clarisse La Rue stood on the Poseidon bench, shouting to the campers gathered around her. "LISTEN UP! Harvest competition is ON! Cabin with the most full baskets by lunch gets out of dish duty for a week! Losers clean the pegasus stables! D'you hear me?!"

A cheer went up from the Ares table. Someone from Apollo booed. Someone from Demeter shouted, "You pick like my grandmother, Clarisse!"

"I PICK LIKE A WINNER!" Clarisse roared back, flexing. Her wild brown hair was already in a bandana. "La Rue doesn't lose to *fruit*!"

Percy swallowed a bite of pancake. "Oh boy."

"She does this every harvest," Annabeth said, rolling her eyes, but she was grinning. "Last year she tried to disqualify the Demeter cabin for 'using plant magic.' Which is literally their powers."

"Did it work?"

"No. But she picked forty-two baskets herself out of spite."

Thalia lifted her head. "Forty-two? Amateur numbers. I'm getting fifty today."

Percy looked between them. "Is everyone at this camp insanely competitive about *everything*?"

Annabeth and Thalia said in perfect unison: "Yes."

---

The strawberry fields stretched across the southern hill in long green rows, leaves flashing silver-green in the morning sun, red berries glowing underneath like hidden lights. From a distance it looked pretty, almost like a postcard. Up close it smelled like sugar and dirt and warm leaves, and the sun was already starting to press down.

Chiron stood at the edge of the field in his wheelchair — his human disguise for mornings — with a clipboard, Mr. D lounging beside him under an umbrella with a lemonade.

"Rules are simple," Chiron called. "Pick red, leave green and white. Gentle hands — do not crush. Full baskets to the collection tent. Demeter cabin leads quality control. Stay hydrated. Hat or sunscreen required. And —" he glanced pointedly at Clarisse, "— no body-checking competitors out of your row."

"Aww," Clarisse said loudly.

"That rule is specifically because of you, Ms. La Rue."

A ripple of laughter. Clarisse just cracked her knuckles and grinned.

Annabeth, true to form, had already marched up to Chiron with her notebook. "Chiron, I've mapped row efficiency based on sun exposure and berry density from yesterday's survey —"

Percy blinked. "You surveyed yesterday?"

"I walked past," Annabeth said quickly. Too quickly.

Thalia snorted. "She surveyed."

"— and I think if we put faster pickers on rows D and G where the yield is highest, and pair new campers with experienced —"

Chiron smiled warmly. "Excellent initiative, Annabeth. Why don't you coordinate Row Captains?"

Annabeth beamed like the sun had risen just for her. "Yes! Okay — Thalia, you take D! Clarisse, you want G since it's heavy?"

Clarisse stared. "You're *giving* me the heavy row?"

"I'm giving you the row where you'll win the most baskets," Annabeth said sweetly. "Unless you can't handle it."

Clarisse's eyes narrowed. Then she grinned, feral. "Oh, it's ON, Zeus girl."

Percy leaned toward Thalia. "Did she just manipulate Clarisse into cooperating by challenging her ego?"

"Yep," Thalia said proudly. "She learns from the best." She pointed at herself.

"Row assignments!" Annabeth called, climbing up on a crate with her notebook held high like a general. Campers gathered around, curious despite themselves. "Ares on H! Apollo on A — you have the nimble fingers for the delicate clusters! Demeter oversees quality and re-pick! Athena — I mean —" She caught herself. In this camp, Athena cabin was small, just a couple kids, and Annabeth wasn't one of them. Sometimes Percy saw a flicker cross her face when she said the name, like she was remembering a different life. "— Malcolm, your cabin takes B for sorting math! Hermes on running baskets! Zeus —" she glanced at Thalia and herself, "— we take D! Poseidon: Clarisse leads G. Silena helps the Aphrodite crew on E. And Percy —"

"What about me?" Percy said, raising his hand. "Do I get a whole row?"

"You get to float," Annabeth said. "You're our runner and water boy because you're fast and you don't mind getting dirty. Plus —" she lowered her voice, "— you can use your water powers to keep the berries cool on the walk to the tent without bruising them."

Percy grinned. That was new — he was still figuring out what he could do. Last week he'd accidentally soaked the entire archery range when he sneezed. "Cool. I'm the berry chiller."

"You're the berry chiller," Annabeth confirmed, solemn as a knighting.

Silena Beauregard, Percy's other Poseidon sister, drifted over, her dark hair in two braids, a red bandana tied around her neck, a basket already hooked over her arm. Everything Silena wore looked good, even harvest clothes. "Annabeth, darling, did you put my Aphrodite friends on E? E gets the morning shade, and shade means no sunburn, and no sunburn means I don't have to do emergency aloe masks tonight."

"I put Aphrodite on E *because* you asked last night," Annabeth said.

Silena clasped her hands. "You remembered! See, this is why I love you. Also, reminder — anyone who brings me the prettiest basket for the jam batch gets a free jar of Silena's Special Strawberry Jam. My mom's recipe — well, my mortal mom's recipe, with a little extra."

"What's the extra?" Percy asked.

"Love," Silena said simply. "And extra sugar. Mostly sugar."

Grover trotted past with an armload of empty baskets, his goat legs clopping, his horns hidden under a straw hat with holes cut out. "Have a little satyr sympathy, guys! I've got to carry all these *and* play morale music later!"

"Morale music?" Percy asked.

"You'll see," Grover said mysteriously, and winked.

Ella the harpy — small, red-feathered, skinny, constantly muttering book titles under her breath — fluttered down beside the basket pile and began lining them up in perfect rows, whispering, "'The Secret Garden.' 'James and the Giant Peach.' 'Strawberry harvest — twenty baskets per — no, no, Ella, count later.'"

Percy crouched beside her. "Hey, Ella."

Ella blinked huge eyes at him. "Percy Jackson. Son of Poseidon. Likes blue. 'Percy Jackson and the —' no book yet. Ella will wait."

"Thanks, Ella," Percy said, touched.

Chiron clapped his hands. "To your rows! Harvest begins — now!"

A cheer. A scramble. Straw hats bobbing, baskets rattling, sneakers kicking up dust.

Percy had never done mortal farming. In the life he remembered with Sally, strawberries had come in plastic boxes from the Manhattan grocery store; the Atlantis upbringing everyone knew here had not taught him about strawberry fields either. He didn't know you had to twist, not pull. He didn't know the best berries hid underneath where the leaves shadowed them. He learned fast because Silena took pity on her little brother in the first ten minutes.

"No, no, sweetie, like this —" Silena demonstrated, her fingers quick and gentle, cradling the berry, twisting at the stem. Plop, into the basket, perfect and unbruised. "See? You have to be kind to them. Strawberries bruise if you're rough. Think of each one like — like a tiny heart."

Percy tried. His first berry got squished. His second lost its top. His third was perfect, fat and red and gleaming. He held it up triumphantly. "Ha!"

"Beautiful!" Silena applauded. "You're a natural. Now do that four thousand more times."

Percy groaned and Silena laughed, bright as a bell.

---



Annabeth loved systems. She loved when a plan came together so cleanly you could see it from above like a blueprint.

From her spot kneeling in Row D, she could see the whole field working exactly the way she'd drawn it: Clarisse power-picking in G, Ares racing her from H, Apollo humming harmonies in A while their hands flew, Hermes kids sprinting baskets back and forth, Demeter kids circling with sharp eyes for missed berries, Silena and her Aphrodite friends in the shade laughing and somehow still picking fast.

It was *working*. Her chest swelled with that specific pride that only came from architecture — from seeing lines on paper become real things in the world.

"Stop admiring your empire and pick, General," Thalia said beside her, tossing a berry at Annabeth's head. Annabeth caught it without looking and popped it in her mouth.

"Hey! Quality control!" Thalia protested.

"Quality checked," Annabeth said. "Delicious. Sweet with a little tart at the end. Row D is premium."

Thalia laughed and went back to picking, her hands fast despite her grumbling. For all her punk-rock "I hate mornings" attitude, Thalia was a machine when she decided to work. Her basket was already half-full.

"So," Thalia said casually, not looking up. "You and Kelp Head."

Annabeth's hands stilled. "What about me and Percy?"

"You're color-coded, for starters." Thalia nodded at Annabeth's notebook, where Percy was listed as FLOATER in blue highlighter with a little wave doodle next to it. "You doodle waves next to his name now?"

"I do not — that's — it's a *label*, for water —"

"Mm-hm." Thalia picked three berries in rapid succession. Plop plop plop. "You saved him the good syrup. You made him Berry Chiller, a fake important title, so he'd feel included. You surveyed the fields yesterday because you knew he'd never picked before and you wanted to pair him with someone nice for his first row."

Annabeth felt her ears go hot. "I organize for *everyone*. That's just leadership."

"Sure." Thalia bumped her shoulder. "I'm just saying. You look at blueprints the way you look at him when he laughs."

"I do not —" Annabeth started, then stopped, because Thalia was grinning that insufferable big-sister grin, and denying it further would only make it worse. She settled for throwing a green leaf at Thalia. "Pick your berries."

"Yes, ma'am." Thalia saluted, still grinning. Then, softer: "It's good, you know. You two. It's good."

Annabeth ducked her head over her basket so Thalia wouldn't see her smile. The sun was warm on the back of her neck. The leaves were cool under her fingers. Somewhere down the row someone was singing an off-key camp song and someone else was laughing.

She picked another berry, perfect and red, and set it gently in the basket. *It's good*, she thought, and let the words sit in her chest like a stone warmed by the sun.

---



It started, as most disasters at Camp Half-Blood started, with Clarisse.

"RUNNER!" Clarisse bellowed from Row G at around eleven o'clock, holding up a basket overflowing with berries. "I need a runner! My basket's full and I'm not breaking my rhythm!"

Percy, who was halfway down Row C misting berries with a faint cool spray from his fingertips — a trick he'd finally gotten to work after soaking his own shoes twice — jogged over. "Coming!"

He grabbed her basket and a second one from an Ares kid, stacked them, and took off toward the collection tent. The sun was high now, sweat sticking his shirt to his back, but running felt good. The field blurred past — red, green, gold — campers calling out, Grover bleating as he hauled baskets from the other direction.

At the tent, two Demeter kids weighed and logged his baskets. "Nice, Poseidon! Row G is killing it!"

Percy grabbed two empty baskets and a tin cup of water, chugged it, and turned to head back — and nearly collided with Thalia, who was pushing an empty wheelbarrow at full speed.

"Whoa!" Percy jumped sideways. "Thalia! Wheelbarrows are for —"

"For hauling! I know!" Thalia skidded to a stop, grinning wildly. Her face was flushed, dirt smudged across one cheek, leaves in her hair. She looked like she'd been having the time of her life. "Chiron said full baskets from D need the wheelbarrow because we're too far to run efficiently! Get in!"

"What?"

"Get *in*." Thalia patted the wheelbarrow bed. "Empty baskets need delivery and I'm not making two trips. You're light. Get in."

Percy stared. "Is this safe?"

"Nope!"

"Is this allowed?"

"Probably not!"

Percy grinned and climbed into the wheelbarrow, folding his long legs, hugging two empty baskets to his chest. "Let's go!"

Thalia whooped and took off running, pushing the wheelbarrow down the dirt path between rows. The wheels rattled, Percy bounced, baskets clattered, campers dove out of the way laughing.

"COMING THROUGH! ROYAL ZEUS DELIVERY! MOVE IT!"

"THALIA! SLOW DOWN!" Annabeth shrieked from Row D, standing up with her hands on her hips — but she was laughing so hard she had to hold her sides.

Clarisse appeared at the edge of Row G with her own wheelbarrow. She took one look at Thalia and Percy rocketing past and her competitive instincts visibly ignited like a fuse.

"OH NO YOU DON'T!" Clarisse pointed at a scrawny Ares kid. "Sherman! Get in the wheelbarrow!"

"What —"

"GET IN!"

Sherman Yang scrambled into Clarisse's wheelbarrow, clutching his hat. Clarisse took off after Thalia, muscles pumping, roaring.

"RACE! FIRST TO THE COLLECTION TENT AND BACK WINS! LOSER'S CABIN DOES WINNER'S LAUNDRY!"

Thalia glanced back, saw Clarisse gaining, and cackled. "HOLD ON, KELP HEAD!"

Percy held on for dear life as Thalia hit a bump and the wheelbarrow briefly went airborne. Strawberries in nearby rows trembled. A Hermes kid dropped his basket and cheered. Grover started playing a fast reel on his pipes from wherever he was, as if the universe had provided a soundtrack.

The two wheelbarrows tore down the path side by side, wheels spraying dirt. Clarisse was stronger; Thalia was scrappier and took the inside corner around the Demeter tent so sharply Percy was sure they were going to tip.

"TRAITORS!" Clarisse howled. "THAT'S MY LINE!"

"SHOULD'VE BEEN FASTER!" Thalia howled back.

They hit the collection area in a dead heat, skidding to a stop in a cloud of dust. The Demeter kids stared.

"EMPTY BASKETS DELIVERED!" Thalia gasped, bent double. "THAT COUNTS AS WINNING!"

"REMATCH!" Clarisse panted. "WITH FULL LOADS! WEIGHTED DIVISION!"

"YOU'RE ON!"

Percy climbed out of the wheelbarrow on wobbly legs, laughing so hard his stomach hurt. His hair was full of dust and a leaf. Sherman just lay in Clarisse's wheelbarrow staring at the sky. "I saw my life flash before my eyes," he whispered. "It was mostly strawberries."

Chiron wheeled up, eyebrows raised. "What, exactly, is happening?"

"Logistics!" Annabeth said quickly, arriving at a run, notebook clutched to her chest, trying very hard to look responsible while her ponytail came undone and her face shone with laughter. "High-speed basket transport! Very — efficient!"

Chiron looked at her, at Thalia and Clarisse panting and grinning, at Percy covered in dust, at the cheering crowd.

"High-speed basket transport," Chiron repeated. "I see. Well. No injuries, I hope. Carry on — at a *walk*."

"Yes, Chiron!" everyone chorused.

The moment Chiron turned away, Clarisse pointed at Thalia. "After lunch. You and me. Loaded wheelbarrows. Annabeth officiates."

"Bring it, Meathead," Thalia said pleasantly.

Clarisse grinned and offered her hand. They shook on it, hard.

Annabeth turned to Percy, shaking her head, but her eyes were dancing. "You're a bad influence."

"*I'm* a bad influence? That was *your* sister!"

"Your sister, yes. And your very persistent friend," Thalia said, slinging an arm around both of them, wheelbarrow forgotten. "Come on, berry chillers. Water break. Silena's got lemonade at the shade tent."

As they walked back, Percy's shoulder pressed against Annabeth's, dusty and warm. She didn't move away. She reached up and plucked the leaf out of his hair, rolling her eyes.

"You have leaves everywhere," she said.

"You have dirt on your nose," he said.

"You both have dirt everywhere," Thalia said. "You're disgusting. I love it. Race you to the lemonade!"

She took off. Percy and Annabeth looked at each other, and then they were running too, laughing, baskets forgotten, sun bright overhead, the whole field ringing with voices.

---



Silena Beauregard believed, deeply and sincerely, that any problem could be solved with snacks, shade, and good company.

So while the rest of the camp collapsed in the grass around the collection tent for lunch — dusty, sweaty, berry-stained to the elbows — Silena and her friends from the Aphrodite cabin had transformed the shade tent into something out of a storybook. A checkered cloth, a cooler of pink lemonade, a plate of peanut-butter sandwiches cut into triangles, and a big bowl of the slightly bruised berries nobody could sell, for snacking.

"Come, come!" Silena waved Percy, Annabeth, and Thalia over. "Sit! You look half-melted, all of you."

They flopped down gratefully. Percy immediately stole three sandwiches. Annabeth poured lemonade for everyone before herself. Thalia lay flat on her back with her arm over her eyes.

"Okay," Silena said, pulling out a small notebook of her own — pink, with stickers. "Jam lesson, because several of you asked and because Percy looked at me like I'd invented fire when I mentioned it. Family recipe — my mom made it every June. You take your best berries —" she held up a perfect one, "— sugar, lemon juice, and patience. Mash lightly, don't puree, you want chunks. Boil, stir, skim the foam. The secret —" she leaned in conspiratorially, "— is a tiny pinch of salt. Makes the sweet sweeter. And you have to stir clockwise for luck."

"Clockwise?" Annabeth asked, fascinated. "Is that magic or —"

"It's Mom," Silena said simply, smiling. "She always stirred clockwise and said it stirred the love in. So I do too."

Percy, mouth full of sandwich, said, "Your mom sounds cool."

"She is," Silena said softly. Then, brighter: "When the jam sheets off the spoon instead of dripping — that's when it's done. Pour into hot jars, seal, flip upside down for five minutes. My mom wrote the whole thing on a card with a strawberry on it. I keep it in my bunk."

"I want to learn," Annabeth said. "Could you teach me? Properly? I'll take notes."

Silena beamed. "I'd love that! We'll do a batch with the camp kitchen after harvest. You, me, Percy as official taste-tester —"

"Best job," Percy said, raising his lemonade cup. "I accept."

Thalia sat up. "I'll be official jar-opener. I have a reputation for opening things violently."

Everyone laughed. Ella fluttered over shyly, holding out a basket. Inside were the most perfectly matched berries Percy had ever seen — same size, same deep red, arranged in a spiral.

"Ella picked the prettiest," Ella whispered. "'The prettiest basket.' Silena said — free jar. Ella remembered."

Silena gasped and took the basket gently, like it was treasure. "Oh, Ella! These are *gorgeous*! Look at this arrangement! Yes, you absolutely get the first jar. With your name on it in my best handwriting."

Ella glowed crimson under her feathers, rocking back and forth happily. "'Ella gets jam.' Ella will shelve that memory with the happy endings."

Percy felt something warm bloom in his chest. He caught Annabeth's eye — she was watching Ella with a soft expression, her lemonade paused halfway to her lips.

"Hey," Percy said to Ella, "want to sit with us? We've got extra sandwiches."

Ella hesitated, then folded her wings tight and settled at the edge of their blanket, small and careful. Silena immediately pushed the berry bowl toward her and began telling her about jam foam-skimming, and Ella listened with wide eyes, repeating, "'Skim the foam. Stir clockwise. Pinch of salt.' Ella will remember. Ella remembers everything."

Lunch stretched golden and lazy. Someone from Apollo started playing a guitar. Grover dozed with his hat over his face, pipes clutched to his chest. Clarisse, two blankets over, was arm-wrestling her Ares friends and winning. Chiron and Mr. D surveyed the mountain of full baskets by the tent — hundreds already — and even Mr. D looked pleased.

"We're ahead of schedule," Annabeth said, checking her notebook, then checking the baskets, then smiling. "Like, really ahead. If the afternoon holds, we'll finish by three-thirty."

"See?" Thalia nudged Percy. "General Annabeth delivers."

"General Annabeth needs a nap," Annabeth said, but she leaned sideways until her shoulder rested against Percy's, and yawned. "Five minutes. Then afternoon shift."

"Rest, General," Percy said quietly. "I've got watch."

She was asleep in two minutes, head tipped toward his shoulder but not quite on it, breathing slow and even. Percy sat very still, afraid to wake her, watching the clouds move over the strawberry fields. Thalia caught his eye and smirked, then deliberately turned away to give them privacy, launching into a loud debate with Silena about whether jam or jelly was superior, to cover the quiet.

Percy didn't move. The sun was warm. The air smelled like strawberries. Annabeth's ponytail had come half-undone and a curl of blonde hair brushed his sleeve.

*It's good*, he thought, echoing words he hadn't heard but somehow knew. *This is good.*

---



The afternoon blur passed in a haze of red and green and laughter.

Clarisse versus Thalia in the Loaded Wheelbarrow Rematch became the stuff of instant camp legend. Annabeth officiated with a bandana as a starting flag, Ella counting down — "'Three. Two. One. Go.'" — and half the camp lining the path to watch.

It was brutal. It was spectacular. Both wheelbarrows were loaded with full baskets (carefully packed with padding, at Demeter's insistence), both pushers were red-faced and roaring, both passengers — Percy again for Thalia, Sherman again for Clarisse, both screaming — clung on.

Clarisse won by half a wheel. Thalia demanded a photo finish. Ella, who missed nothing, confirmed: "'Clarisse La Rue crossed first by one spoke.'"

Clarisse lifted her wheelbarrow like a trophy while Ares cabin chanted her name. Thalia bowed with exaggerated grace and announced, "Best two out of three. Sunset. Be there."

"YOU'RE ON!"

After that, everyone picked faster, energized by the rivalry. Songs traveled down the rows — sea shanties from Percy (taught recently by Amphitrite), war chants from Ares, a ridiculous Aphrodite rhyme about a boy who loved jam. Even Annabeth sang along to the chorus, off-key and laughing at herself.

By three-fifteen, the last basket was carried in. By three-thirty, Chiron announced the totals from the top of a crate, Mr. D actually standing to applaud.

"Five hundred and twelve baskets! A new harvest record!"

The cheer could probably be heard in Manhattan.

Annabeth stood in the middle of Row D, now stripped clean, leaves still glossy, her notebook full of checkmarks, her hands stained pink, sweat dried salty on her forehead — and felt absurdly, completely proud.

Percy appeared beside her, holding two cups of water. He handed her one. "To General Annabeth," he said solemnly. "Conqueror of fruit."

She clinked her cup against his. "To Berry Chiller Percy. Master of cool mist."

"Did you have fun?" he asked. "Even with the organizing?"

Annabeth looked out over the empty rows, the mountain of baskets, her friends laughing by the tent. "This was one of the best days," she said honestly. "I like — I like making things work. And I liked doing it with everyone. With you."

Percy smiled, that crooked easy smile that made his sea-green eyes crinkle. "Me too. Even the part where Thalia almost killed me."

"Especially that part," said Thalia, appearing behind them and draping her arms over both their shoulders. "Come on, heroes. Chiron says swim time before dinner. The lake is calling and I smell like a jam factory."

"Swim!" Percy whooped, and took off running.

Annabeth and Thalia looked at each other, grinned, and chased him.

---



If the strawberry fields were the day's work, the amphitheater was its reward.

Camp Half-Blood's amphitheater was built into the hillside in white stone rows that glowed peach in the sunset, facing a small stage backed by olive trees and fluttering banners. Tiki torches were lit around the rim. The sky above deepened from gold to violet, first stars pricking through.

It was Music Night — no campfire stories, no sing-along war songs. Tonight was for performances. Anyone could sign up. Everyone did, badly or brilliantly.

Percy arrived with wet hair from the lake, wearing a clean shirt, carrying a blanket. Annabeth had saved him a seat — third row, perfect view — with Thalia on her other side, Silena braiding Clarisse's hair in the row ahead, Grover tuning his pipes on the steps, Ella perched nervously on the end of their row with a lyre almost bigger than she was.

"Hey," Annabeth said as he sat down, handing him half a red cookie. "Strawberry shortbread. From the kitchen. They're using our berries already."

Percy bit in. Sweet, buttery, bright with real strawberry. "Oh gods. That's amazing."

"Right?" Annabeth licked sugar off her thumb. "Silena's already negotiating for jam kitchen time tomorrow."

Mr. D took the stage to scattered applause. "Yes, yes, settle down. Welcome to Music Night. Rules: no fire, no weapons, no turning your fellow campers into animals as a critique. Perform, bow, sit down. First up — Apollo cabin!"

The Apollo kids did a flawless four-part harmony about the sun chariot that earned thunderous applause. A Hermes kid did a magic trick with disappearing strawberries that ended with him pulling one out of Clarisse's ear — Clarisse laughed so hard she snorted, which made everyone laugh harder. Two Demeter girls played flutes made from actual reeds. Ares did a stomping drum routine on overturned baskets that shook the stone benches.

Then Ella's name was called.

The little harpy froze. Silena squeezed her hand. "You can do it, sweetie. Just like we practiced."

Ella walked to the center of the stage on trembling bird legs, clutching the lyre. She stared out at the sea of faces. For a long moment, Percy thought she might fly away.

Then Ella opened her beak and sang — loudly, shrilly, completely off-key, a song about soup.

It was *terrible*. Gloriously, intentionally terrible. She missed every note by a mile, strummed the wrong strings, and sang the word "SOUP!" like a battle cry.

The amphitheater went silent for one heartbeat — then erupted. Campers howled with laughter, stomped their feet, cheered. Ella's eyes gleamed with mischief. She sang louder, worse, spinning in a circle, feathers flying, ending with a shriek — "AND THE SOUP WAS *GOOD*!"

Percy was laughing so hard tears ran down his face. Annabeth clapped until her hands stung. Thalia whistled through her fingers.

Ella bowed deeply, beaming. "'Ella sang badly on purpose,'" she announced proudly. "'The Comedy of Errors. Laughter is the best instrument.'"

"ENCORE!" Clarisse roared. "DO THE SOUP AGAIN!"

Ella shook her head, clutching her lyre, and fluttered back to her seat where Silena wrapped her in a hug. "You star!" Silena cried. "You absolute star!"

"That took guts," Annabeth said to Ella warmly as the harpy settled beside her, breathless and glowing. "You were fantastic."

"'Ella was terrible,'" Ella corrected happily. "'Terrible on purpose is still performing.'"

Next up was Grover. The satyr took the stage with his reed pipes, suddenly shy without his straw hat. "Uh — hi. This is an old one. A wild places song. For — for the fields today. And the woods. And, uh, everything growing."

He lifted the pipes to his lips and played.

The change was instant. The laughter faded. The torches seemed to burn steadier. A breeze moved through the olive trees in time with the music, and Percy swore he could smell rain and green things and warm earth. Grover's melody was winding and bright and lonely all at once — like a goat path through hills, like water finding its way downhill. It spoke of seeds splitting open, roots reaching, berries reddening in the sun.

Percy felt the hairs rise on his arms. Beside him, Annabeth closed her eyes, listening completely. Thalia went still in a way Thalia never went still. Even Mr. D stopped fidgeting with his lemonade.

When Grover finished, there was a breath of silence — then applause like rain.

Grover blushed to the tips of his horns and bleated, "Thanks — thanks, guys —" and hurried offstage.

"Show-off goat," Thalia muttered, but her eyes were shiny. "That was beautiful, Grover!"

---



After Grover, Chiron took the stage. "We have a special treat. Two of our youngest campers have volunteered —" he glanced at his list, smiling, "— or been volunteered by Thalia Grace — to tell stories tonight. Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus, will share a myth. Percy Jackson, son of Poseidon, will follow."

Annabeth froze. "Thalia!" she hissed. "You signed us up?!"

Thalia grinned unrepentantly. "You tell myths better than anyone. And Kelp Head's got Atlantis stories. The people deserve culture."

"I — I didn't prepare —" Annabeth started, but applause was already carrying her name, and Percy was nudging her gently.

"You got this, General," Percy said. "Tell the one with the twist. You know the one."

Annabeth took a breath, smoothed her shirt, and walked to the center of the stage. The torches flickered. Hundreds of faces looked up at her. The sky pressed dark and starry overhead.

She'd always loved myths — collected them like other kids collected shells. But tonight, with the strawberry sweetness still on her tongue and her friends' faces in the front row, she wanted to tell one differently.

"Most people know the story of Arachne," she began, her voice clear in the hush. "The girl who challenged Athena to a weaving contest."

A small ripple — everyone knew this one. Arachne the braggart, turned into a spider for her pride.

"But that's the version the winners tell," Annabeth said. "Here's what I think really happened."

She told it her way: Arachne, a poor shepherd's daughter with clever fingers, who wove the gods' cruelties into her tapestry — not out of arrogance, but out of honesty. Who showed Europa and Leda and Medusa, not to mock Athena, but to ask *why*. Athena, who was proud and just in equal measure, who saw the truth in the weaving and couldn't unsee it. Athena didn't turn Arachne into a spider as punishment, Annabeth said — she turned her into a spider as *rescue*, when the other gods demanded the girl's death for speaking plainly. Spiders could hide in corners. Spiders could keep weaving, forever, where no one could tear their work down.

"So when you see a spider in the corner of your cabin," Annabeth finished softly, "don't kill it. She's still weaving. She's still telling the truth. And every web is a story that refused to be silenced."

Silence. Then — applause, louder than before, thoughtful and warm. Someone from Athena cabin was crying. Malcolm shouted, "YES!" Silena pressed her hands to her heart.

Annabeth walked back to her seat on shaky legs, ears burning.

Percy grabbed her hand and squeezed it hard. "That was *amazing*," he whispered. "You changed the ending and it was *better*."

"Thanks," Annabeth whispered back, breathless. "Your turn. Atlantis. Go."

Thalia shoved Percy toward the stage. "Break a leg, Kelp Head!"

---



Percy had never performed on this camp's stage in his younger body. He'd been in front of classes for book reports. He'd stood in principal's offices. But he'd never stood in a stone circle under the stars with torches and hundreds of eyes.

His mouth went dry. Then he saw Annabeth in the third row, leaning forward, hands clasped, nodding *go on*. Thalia giving him two thumbs up. Grover bleating encouragement. Silena smiling. Ella whispering, "'Percy Jackson will tell a good story. 'Twenty Thousand Leagues.' Good stories are like the sea.'"

Percy took a breath. Salt air filled his lungs. Somewhere far below, the ocean murmured against the shore.

"Okay. So. Atlantis." He grinned, nervous. "My dad's city. Poseidon's city. You've all heard it sank. But nobody tells you what it was *like* before."

And then the story came — pieced together from Triton's tales about ancient Atlantis and Percy's own dreams, of light filtering through water, of marble columns hung with kelp, of dolphins racing through streets, of music that sounded like whale song and bells.

He told them about the markets where mermaids sold pearls for jokes, about the palace with a dome of living coral, about the hippocampi races through kelp forests, about the library where scrolls were written on waterproof sealskin and ink made from squid. He told them about the lighthouse at the harbor mouth that burned with Greek fire that could shine through water for miles, guiding sailors home.

"And the best part," Percy said, getting into it now, pacing the stage, hands flying, "was the tide gardens. Every house had one. At high tide they'd flood, and fish would swim right through your front yard, and kids would feed them from their windows. At low tide the gardens drained and left all these tide pools full of crabs and starfish, and the kids would play in them until the tide came back. The whole city breathed with the ocean. In and out. Like — like it was alive."

He stopped, realizing the amphitheater was dead silent, everyone leaning forward.

"There was a story about the city I liked," Percy said, quieter now. "That it wasn't destroyed because the people were bad. She said it sank because the gods needed it to be a secret. Because some beautiful things have to hide to survive. And she said one day, when the time is right, it'll rise again. And the tide gardens will fill. And the lighthouse will light."

It was Sally's story in the childhood he remembered, but he didn't have to explain its source to an amphitheater full of campers. He could keep that part private while telling them why the story mattered. He looked up at the stars. "I like that version."

The applause started with Annabeth — on her feet, clapping hard — and spread like a wave. Thalia wolf-whistled. Grover stamped his hooves. Clarisse shouted, "NOT BAD, FISH BOY!" which from Clarisse was basically a love poem.

Percy bowed, face flaming, and hurried back to his seat.

"Dude," Thalia said, punching his shoulder lightly. "Poet."

"That was beautiful, Percy," Silena said, eyes bright.

Ella tugged his sleeve. "'The tide gardens will fill.' Ella likes that sentence. Ella will keep it on the top shelf."

Percy sat down beside Annabeth, heart still pounding. "Was it okay? I made half of it up. The dreams — I don't know what's real —"

"It was perfect," Annabeth said firmly. "Stories don't have to be true to be *true*. You know?"

"Yeah," Percy said. "Yeah, I know."

Their eyes held. The torchlight turned Annabeth's grey eyes gold at the edges. Somewhere onstage, the next act was starting — a sword-juggling routine that was clearly about to go wrong — but neither of them looked away for a long moment.

Thalia, with perfect big-sister timing, threw popcorn at both of them. "Stop being cute, you're distracting the jugglers."

"We're not —" Annabeth started, going pink.

"We're *appreciating narrative*," Percy said loftily, and Annabeth burst out laughing.

---



Music Night ended with everyone onstage — all the performers, all the cabins — singing the camp song badly and loudly, arms around each other, torches guttering low. Ella sang the soup song again as a descant. Grover played. Mr. D conducted with a breadstick. Even Chiron sang, in a deep centaur baritone.

Then it was over, torches snuffed one by one, campers drifting up the hill toward the cabins in chattering groups, stars blazing overhead in a sky washed clean by sea wind.

Annabeth lingered on the top step of the amphitheater, looking out over the dark fields where they'd worked all day. The strawberry rows were invisible now, just a darker fold in the hillside. Tomorrow they'd be green again, already growing the next crop. That was camp magic for you.

Footsteps. Percy sat down beside her, handing her his jacket without a word — the night had turned cool off the water.

"Thanks," she murmured, pulling it around her shoulders. It smelled like lake water and sunshine and Percy.

They sat in comfortable quiet for a while, watching the stars. A meteor streaked overhead. Percy pointed. "Did you see —"

"I wished," Annabeth said quickly. "Don't tell me what you wished or it won't come true."

"I didn't wish," Percy said. "I just — I liked seeing it. With you."

Annabeth's heart did something complicated. She looked at him — his dark hair still damp, his green eyes reflecting starlight, a smear of shortbread sugar still on his chin — and laughed softly, reaching over to brush it off.

"You had sugar — there —" She showed him her thumb.

"Did I get it?" He rubbed his chin.

"Missed a spot." She wiped it clean. Her hand lingered half a second too long. She pulled it back, suddenly shy.

"Annabeth?" Percy said. "Today was — the harvest, and the race, and tonight — that was the best day I've had at camp. Maybe ever."

"Me too," Annabeth whispered. "I mean — I liked that we did it together. All of us. But — especially you."

"Yeah," Percy said. "Especially you."

Above them the constellations turned: Zeus's eagle, Poseidon's dolphin, the huntress, the lyre. Annabeth named them silently in her head the way she'd done since she was small — a private architecture of the sky, each star in its place.

Percy leaned back on his elbows, looking up. "Which one's yours?"

Annabeth pointed. "Aquila. The eagle. My dad's. And there's Delphinus — that's yours."

"The dolphin?" Percy grinned. "Nice. Small but feisty."

"Like you," Annabeth said.

"Hey!"

She laughed, and he laughed, and the sound carried across the empty amphitheater and up to the stars.

From the path below, Thalia's voice floated up: "YOU TWO COMING OR DO I HAVE TO COME DRAG YOU? CURFEW!"

"COMING!" they shouted together.

They stood, Annabeth handing back Percy's jacket — he shook his head, "Keep it till tomorrow, you'll get cold walking back" — and walked down the steps side by side, shoulders brushing.

At the bottom, Thalia waited with Grover and Silena and Ella, all half-asleep and happy, arms full of leftover shortbread and blankets.

"Great day, huh?" Thalia said, slinging an arm around Annabeth, the other around Percy, pulling them into a walking huddle. "Record harvest, legendary race, soup song, Atlantis. This camp, man."

"The best," Silena agreed, leaning against Grover, who bleated sleepily.

"'And they lived happily,'" Ella murmured, quoting something, or maybe inventing it. "'Strawberry fields forever. The end of the chapter. But not the end of the story.'"

Annabeth looked back once at the amphitheater, pale stone under starlight, the dark fields beyond. Tomorrow there would be training and lessons and dishes and laundry. Clarisse would demand her rematch. Silena would teach jam. There would be a hundred ordinary camp days ahead.

But tonight, with Percy's jacket warm around her shoulders and her friends close and the stars wheeling overhead, Annabeth thought Ella had it exactly right.

Not the end of the story. Just the end of a perfect chapter.

She wished on one more falling star — quick and silent, before anyone could tell her not to — and walked home under the wide forgetting sky, while far below, the sea remembered every name.
