## Chapter 135: The Garden

Dawn came up pale gold over the strawberry fields, and the mist hung low enough to wet Annabeth's ankles as she crossed the grass behind the cabins. Her sneakers left dark prints. Somewhere beyond the dining pavilion, a conch sounded for early chores, thin and far off, and the air carried salt from the Sound mixed with crushed green strawberry leaves.

The shrine stood where it had stood all winter, half-forgotten. A low stone dome on a square base, weather-stained, dedicated to no one in particular anymore — old offerings moldered in its niche, a clay horse with one leg missing, a sodden laurel crown, three drachmas gone green at the edges. Around it stretched a rectangle of bare earth, marked out last week with twine and sticks after the promise she and Percy had made. Dirt packed hard by rain, littered with pine needles and last autumn's acorns.

Annabeth stopped at the twine line and set down her bucket. Inside: trowels, a coiled measuring tape, a sketchbook warped by humidity, pencils tied together with a rubber band. Her fingers smelled faintly of cedar from the bunk bed she had sanded the night before. A mourning dove called from the pines, two notes, then silence.

Behind her, gravel crunched.

"Tell me you brought coffee," Percy said.

She turned. He came down the path with his sleeves pushed past his elbows, hair damp from the showers, a smudge of toothpaste still at the corner of his mouth. Over one shoulder he carried a flat wooden crate, empty, its slats scarred with old nail holes. His camp necklace swung as he walked, the beads clicking.

"No coffee," Annabeth said, wiping the toothpaste away with her thumb before he could dodge. "Water jug's by the toolshed if you want it."

"Water," Percy said, with deep suspicion, peering into her bucket. "For gardening. Sounds responsible."

"Somebody around here has to be."

Across the field, the toolshed door banged. Silena came into view first, arms wrapped around a stack of seedling flats that rose past her chin, marigolds and lavender and something trailing with silver leaves. Morning light caught the rose-gold in her hair and turned the plastic trays translucent green. Behind her, Clarisse pushed a wheelbarrow piled high with bagged compost, her face set in the particular scowl she reserved for mornings and manual labor.

"Move your twine," Clarisse called. "This thing's got a wobbly wheel and I am not stopping."

---

By seven, the clearing looked more like a work site than a holy place.

Silena knelt by the shrine steps and unloaded her flats in rows, naming each one under her breath while she pinched off dead leaves. "Marigolds for borders — slugs hate them. Lavender for bees. White alyssum to fill cracks. And —" She lifted a smaller tray with a flourish. "Moonvine. Somebody at the Demeter cabin owed me a favor."

Annabeth crouched beside her, flipping open the sketchbook to a page divided into neat squares. Pencil lines marked sun angles, root depth, bloom months. Coastal wind arrows curled along the margin. The paper smelled of graphite and damp.

"Moonvine climbs," Annabeth said. "It'll need a trellis on the north side, or it'll swallow the alyssum."

Silena bumped her shoulder. "Knew there was a reason I brought the architect."

Percy set the crate down and whistled low at the drawing. "You did that last night?"

"After lights-out. With a flashlight between my teeth." Annabeth tapped the north edge. "Stone border goes here. Soil mounded six inches higher in the middle for drainage. Tall stuff in back so the shrine stays visible."

Clarisse dumped the first bag of compost with a thud that sent dust puffing up around her boots. The smell rolled out rich and sour-sweet, bark and manure and coffee grounds. She slit the second bag with her pocketknife, sneezed violently into her elbow, and glared at nobody in particular.

"Explain again why I'm hauling dirt for a shrine nobody uses," she said.

"Because the shrine's for everybody," Silena said without looking up. "And because I told you there'd be lemonade."

"You promised lemonade last time and it was powdered."

"This time it's real. I squeezed lemons myself. My hands stink."

Clarisse snorted, though the edge left her voice. She tipped the wheelbarrow, muscles in her forearms standing out as dark soil cascaded onto the packed earth. Clods broke apart, revealing earthworms pink and startled, curling back into darkness. A robin landed on the shrine dome to watch, head cocked.

Percy grabbed a rake and dragged the pile outward, sleeves flapping. Dust filmed his arms to the elbow. "C'mon, Clarisse. Admit you like getting your hands dirty."

"I like getting my spear dirty," Clarisse said. "There's a difference."

"Same motion," Percy said, stabbing the rake into a clod. "Lunge, twist, compost."

Laughter cracked out of Clarisse before she could stop it, rough and surprised. She covered it by heaving another bag. "Keep talking, Jackson, and you'll wear this bag as a hat."

Up the slope, Thalia arrived with a clatter, dragging a canvas sling full of flat gray stones. Her black hair was tied back with a strip of denim, copper streaks bright where sunlight struck. Lightning-bleached scars mapped her knuckles. She dropped the sling beside the twine line, rolled her shoulders, and surveyed Annabeth's sketch over her shoulder.

"Border stones," Thalia said. "Said the harpies wouldn't miss a few from the amphitheater wall. Don't quote me."

Annabeth grinned up at her. "You stole from the amphitheater?"

"Relocated," Thalia said, deadpan, and winked. "Show me the line."

---

Thalia worked stone the way other people worked clay, patient and absolute.

She knelt along the eastern edge, testing each rock with both hands before setting it, turning it to find the flat side. Limestone scraped against limestone with a chalky shriek. Chips and pale dust powdered her jeans. When a stone wobbled, she dug beneath it with her fingers, scooped out grit, packed in crushed shell from a coffee can, and set it again until it held fast under her palm.

Annabeth handed her stones as needed, learning the rhythm. Heavy ones first. Flat face out. Lean inward a fraction so frost couldn't push them loose come November. Thalia's hands moved with sure economy, and the line grew behind her, gray and uneven and solid.

"You're good at this," Annabeth said.

Thalia shrugged, though color rose along her cheekbones. "Dad's cabin roof leaked for two years straight. Somebody had to learn masonry before the bunk beds floated away."

Percy looked up from raking, sweat beading along his hairline. "You fixed the Zeus cabin roof?"

"With Luke's help, back when—" Thalia cut herself off, jaw tightening. She set another stone harder than necessary. A beetle scuttled from beneath it into dead leaves. "Anyway. Learned."

Silence settled for a few heartbeats, filled by rake tines hissing through soil and the distant thump of the volleyball from the courts. Silena reached over and squeezed Thalia's ankle once, quick, and went back to pinching marigold heads without comment. The robin hopped down and speared a worm.

Grover trotted up the path then, breathless, reed pipes bouncing against his hip, a burlap sack over one shoulder. His horns had grown enough over spring to catch light at the tips. Brambles clung to his jeans. Behind him fluttered Ella, the harpy, clutching a spiral notebook and three different colored pens, feathers ruffled by the breeze off the water.

"Sorry, sorry," Grover said, skidding to a stop. "Juniper needed help with a root tangle by the creek. Brought bone meal and mycorrhizae." He upended the sack. Powder sifted out, smelling of forest floor after rain, mushroom and leaf mold. "For blessing later. If that's okay."

"More than okay," Annabeth said. "We saved the middle for you."

Ella perched on the shrine base, talons clicking on stone, and opened her notebook with ceremony. "Ella will document. Date. Attendees. Soil pH — unknown. Sky — blue with clouds like sheep." She scribbled furiously, tongue poking out. "Percy Jackson has dirt on his ear."

Percy swiped at his ear and missed. "Where?"

"Other ear," Ella chirped, delighted, pen scratching. "Now both ears. Ella documents both ears."

Laughter rippled around the circle, and even Clarisse cracked a reluctant grin as she parked the empty wheelbarrow and wiped her forehead with the back of her wrist, leaving a streak.

---

By midmorning the sun stood clear of the pines, and shadows shrank to dark puddles beneath the shrine eaves.

The work fell into rhythm. Clarisse hauled, Percy spread, Annabeth measured, Silena planted, Thalia edged, Grover circled with his sack, Ella narrated.

Soil gave under the rake in long furrows, darkening where compost folded in, crumbs breaking from fists to powder. Annabeth pressed her palm flat to test moisture. Cool grains clung to her lifeline, grit under her nails. The scent rose with each turn — iron, rot turned sweet, crushed roots. Somewhere underneath, salt lingered from winter storms that had driven spray this far inland.

"Thalia, two inches left on your side," Annabeth called, sighting along her tape. "Follow the twine, not the dip. Ground slopes."

Thalia squinted, nudged a stone outward with her knee, and grunted approval when it lined up. Her tank top stuck to her spine with sweat. "Bossy."

"Thorough," Silena corrected, digging holes with a trowel for the marigolds. Her hands moved quickly, flicking pebbles aside, tucking each root ball snug. "There's a difference, and Annabeth's the good kind."

Percy knelt opposite Silena with a flat of alyssum, trying to copy her motions and mangling the first plug. White petals scattered across his knee. Soil wedged under his thumbnail.

"Gentle," Silena said, covering his hands with hers for a second to show him. "Cradle, don't strangle. Like you're holding a baby bird."

"I've never held a baby bird."

"Pretend it's a drachma you don't want to drop in a storm drain."

"Oh. Now I understand."

Annabeth watched his tongue poke out in concentration as he eased the next seedling free, cupped roots intact, and settled it into its hole. Dirt mounded around the stem. He patted it with excessive care, then looked up for approval, nose smudged brown where he'd scratched an itch.

Something warm and sudden rose in Annabeth's chest. She reached over before thinking and brushed his nose with her thumb. "You've got— here."

Percy crossed his eyes trying to see. "Do I look distinguished?"

"You look like a potato farmer."

"My lifelong dream." He dipped a finger in loose soil and dabbed it across her nose in retaliation. Cool grit, a tickle. "Now we match."

Silena squealed. "Oh, that's adorable. Ella, write that down."

"Ella documents nose dirt," Ella said solemnly, scribbling. "Romance among the root vegetables. Alyssum witnesses."

Heat climbed Annabeth's neck, though she laughed and didn't wipe it off. Percy's grin went crooked and pleased. Their knees touched in the dirt and neither moved away.

Clarisse dumped another load with theatrical groaning. "If you two start writing poetry at each other, I'm leaving."

"No poetry," Percy promised. "Only potatoes."

"Gods help me," Clarisse muttered, though she lingered instead of heading back for more soil, leaning on her shovel. Sweat darkened the collar of her shirt. Blisters shone pink across her palm where the handle rubbed. "So. While we're stuck here. Anybody doing anything after August, or are we all rotting here forever?"

The question landed in the middle of the garden and stayed there, humming under the work sounds.

Silena sat back on her heels, trowel across her knees. Pollen dusted her forearms gold. "I'll go home for September. Mom wants me to try that art school in Charleston. Figure drawing, color theory, terrible dorm food." She twisted a marigold stem between her fingers. "Then back here next summer. If they'll have me as a counselor."

"They'd beg," Thalia said.

"What about you?" Silena asked, nodding toward Clarisse.

Clarisse stabbed her shovel into the pile. "I signed up for the Ares cabin's training exchange with some Amazons upstate. Three weeks. After that, high school." She said the last words like a curse. "Mom's making me try out for rowing. Says I need discipline."

"You?" Percy said, choking. "Discipline?"

Clarisse threw a clod at his head. He ducked, laughing, and it burst against the shrine base. Ella gasped and documented the assault.

"Careful," Annabeth said. "You'll kill the alyssum and Grover will cry."

Grover raised a hand from where he knelt sifting bone meal, already weeping a little in anticipation. "I pre-cry for all plants. It's called empathy."

Thalia laughed, low and real, and set another border stone. Stone dust whitened her fingertips. "I'm helping the camp field crew through fall. Chiron wants to sweep the Appalachians. After that—" She hesitated, turning a flat rock over and over. "Maybe look for a mortal school that'll take an eighteen-year-old junior with no transcript and a disciplinary record involving lightning."

"You'd be great at school," Annabeth said.

Thalia's mouth twisted. "I'd be terrible at school. But maybe terrible in a fun way."

"What about you, Wise Girl?" Percy asked, nudging Annabeth's boot with his own. Soil caked his laces. "San Francisco?"

Annabeth dug her trowel in deeper than necessary, watching dark earth curl up. The future sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone, heavy and bright at once. Program brochures hidden under her mattress. Beryl's last letter, pages thin from rereading, enclosing an architecture program for high schoolers at Berkeley, asking whether she might like it. The brochure promised student lodging with skylights. Possibility and dread braided tight.

"Maybe California for a bit," she said slowly. "Mom sent a brochure. There's a summer studio. Drafting, model building." She glanced up through her lashes. "I'd come back. Obviously."

Percy nodded, suddenly intent on his seedling, fingers pressing soil a fraction too hard. A muscle jumped in his jaw. "Obviously," he echoed. His voice came out lighter than his eyes. "Somebody's got to stop Clarisse from conquering camp while you're gone."

"Hey," Clarisse said.

Silena caught Annabeth's eye over Percy's bowed head and mouthed, talk later, with an exaggerated nod toward the boys' cabins. Annabeth managed a nod back, throat tight.

Thalia cleared her throat, standing to stretch, spine popping. "Percy? Plans? World domination? Professional rake operator?"

Percy exhaled through his nose, grateful for the deflection. "Amphitrite wants me to try that marine science camp on Long Island. Weekdays. Plus helping Tyson at the forge when he visits." He brushed perlite from his knees. "And Triton keeps sending scrolls about 'duties.' Mostly I use them as coasters."

"Blasphemy," Grover intoned, scattering a handful of white powder in a wide circle. Dust hung in sunlight, smelling of chalk and mushrooms. "The sea prince uses royal correspondence as furniture protection."

"The sea prince does what he wants," Percy said, though his ears went pink.

Ella flapped her wings for balance as she leaned to write. "Percy Jackson, age thirteen, defies the sea. Ella predicts consequences involving seaweed."

---

Around noon, Silena produced the promised lemonade from behind the shrine, where she had hidden two cold jugs under a damp towel.

Plastic cups passed hand to hand, beaded with condensation, tartness cutting through dust and heat. Lemon pulp stuck to teeth. Ice clinked. Clarisse drank hers in three gulps and held out her cup for more without a word, and Silena refilled it with a smug arch of her brows.

"Real lemons," Silena announced. "Told you."

Clarisse wiped her mouth. "Fine. You're forgiven for the powdered incident of last July."

They drank sitting in the dirt among half-planted rows, legs stretched out, backs against border stones and the shrine base. Shade from the dome fell in a cool triangle. Annabeth's sketchbook lay open but forgotten, pages fluttering. A cabbage white butterfly tested the lavender and found it wanting, drifting on.

Grover stood when cups emptied, brushing crumbs of soil from his lap. He lifted his reed pipes, then lowered them, deciding against music. Instead he knelt in the center bed, pressed both palms flat to the earth, and closed his eyes. Goat pupils stilled beneath his lids. The clearing quieted by degrees — rake handles stilled, whispers stopped — until only wind moved, combing the pines and lifting Ella's neck feathers.

"Blessing time?" Percy whispered.

Annabeth elbowed him gently. "Listen."

Grover spoke, low and melodic, words half Greek, half something older that thrummed behind Annabeth's sternum. His voice wove between the rows, coaxing. The scent of turned earth deepened, green and alive. A tremor ran through the alyssum leaves though no wind touched them. Somewhere underground, roots pushed deeper, seeking.

"Grow where you're planted," Grover murmured at last in English, palms lifting, soil clinging to his skin. "Hold this hill together. Feed bees. Shelter beetles. Remind demigods that quiet work counts." He opened his eyes, pupils wide and dark. "There. Blessed. Try not to step on them for a day."

Ella wrote with reverent speed. "Blessing successful. Soil spirits agree. Ella felt agreement in her hollow bones."

Thalia bowed her head, uncharacteristically solemn, fingers tracing the fresh stones. Clarisse, to Annabeth's surprise, removed her cap and held it over her chest until Grover finished, then jammed it back on backwards.

Percy caught Annabeth's hand under the edge of the seedling flat where nobody could see, his palm gritty and damp, soil ground into the creases. Their fingers linked, dirt and sweat and lemon stickiness all mixed. His pulse thudded against her thumb.

She glanced sideways. He was watching Grover, profile sunlit, nose still smudged, mouth soft. When he felt her look, his eyes flicked to hers, startled and happy. Neither spoke. The robin sang from the dome, sharp and sweet.

Silena saw anyway — sisters always saw — and her grin turned luminous. She kicked a spray of loose soil over their joined hands like confetti, mouthing, cute.

Annabeth stuck her tongue out at her, and Percy squeezed her fingers once before letting go to grab his cup, ears scarlet.

Lemonade dregs swirled in the bottom of paper cups, pulp settling. Overhead, clouds thinned to gauze, and sunlight fell straight down, heating the crown of Annabeth's head until her scalp prickled. She pressed a cool palm to her neck and felt grit there, fine as sand.

"Break's over," Clarisse announced, heaving herself upright with a groan. Her knees popped. She rolled her empty cup flat and tucked it into her back pocket. "Thalia, show me that corner trick again. My side looks drunk."

Thalia waved her over, brushing limestone powder from her thighs. Together they crouched at the northwest corner where two lines met at an awkward angle. Thalia selected a broad triangular stone, spat on its underside — Clarisse wrinkled her nose — and ground it into the soil with a rocking motion until grit squealed.

"See? Three points of contact," Thalia said, tapping each edge. "Two below, one against the neighbor. Gravity does the rest."

Clarisse tried, tongue caught between her teeth. Her first attempt tilted. Her second held. She sat back on her heels, surprised pleasure flickering across her broad face before she buried it under a scowl. "Huh. Okay. Corner science."

"Corner science," Thalia repeated solemnly, and bumped fists, knuckles chalky.

Nearby, Grover crumbled a clod between his palms and held the crumbs toward Ella, explaining mycorrhizae in a whisper that carried anyway. White threads laced the soil, fine as spider silk, smelling of damp bark. Ella leaned so close her beak almost touched his fingers, feathers trembling in the updraft of heat from the stones.

"Ella asks: fungus helps roots?" she scribbled, then looked up. "Fungus is friend?"

"Best friend roots ever had," Grover said. "Trades phosphorus for sugar. Underground market. Nobody cheats, or the whole network knows."

Percy wandered over with the lemonade jug, refilling cups without being asked. Condensation dripped from the plastic onto his wrist and ran down to his elbow, cutting pale lines through dust. He paused beside Annabeth and nudged her knee with his.

"Berkeley studio," he said quietly, while the others argued about corner stones. "How long would that be?"

Annabeth twisted her trowel in the dirt, watching the blade flash. Citronella from someone's sunscreen mixed with the iron tang of fresh compost. A bumblebee worked the edge of Silena's sleeve, bumbling and persistent.

"Six weeks," Annabeth said. "Mornings in a warehouse with skylights. Afternoons building balsa models. The brochure has pictures. Brick building, eucalyptus outside the windows." Her throat tightened around the next part. "I'd sleep in the student rooms. There's a shared workbench and an old telescope on the porch."

Percy nodded, rolling the empty crate's edge under his thumb, wood splinters catching. "Eucalyptus smells good."

"You've never smelled eucalyptus."

"I imagine it smells good." His grin flickered, then steadied. He bumped her shoulder, gentle. "You should go if you want. I'll keep the olive alive. I can learn watering. Grover can teach me the solemn chant and everything."

Across the beds, Silena pretended fascination with a marigold while eavesdropping openly, elbows deep in soil. Pollen gilded the sweat along her hairline. When Annabeth caught her eye, Silena waggled her brows and mouthed, six weeks apart? Pfft, with exaggerated confidence, before returning to her planting with suspicious innocence.

Warmth spread through Annabeth's ribs, tangled with ache. She nudged Percy's boot back. "We'll talk about it tonight. On the dock. Bring snacks."

"Bring snacks," Percy repeated, committing it to memory like coordinates. "Copy that."

Thalia's voice cut across, dry with amusement. "If you two are done negotiating summer logistics, this corner needs an architect's eye."

Annabeth rose, knees cracking, and went to sight the line, grateful for stone-cold problems with plumb-line answers.

---

By lunchtime, the first problem with the new garden had become impossible to ignore.

The wheelbarrow had left a groove through the soft earth near the eastern border, and water from the washing station was running down it toward the roots of the olive sapling. Annabeth watched the narrow stream carry bits of soil away every time someone filled a bucket.

"We can't leave that," she said.

Percy followed her gaze. "It's not much water."

"It's enough to wash the edge out if it keeps running. Then the stones settle, the soil slides and the sapling starts leaning."

Clarisse brought the empty wheelbarrow over. "We need another line of stones."

"Not before we solve where the water goes."

"We can move the wheelbarrow."

"That stops the groove from getting deeper. It doesn't move the washing station."

Thalia joined them with a stone in each hand. "Do you want me to put these down?"

"Yes," Annabeth said. "And can you leave that border alone for five minutes while I check the slope?"

Thalia set the stones aside. She'd been trying to help, and the request stung a little, but she didn't argue. Annabeth stretched her cord from the washing station to the lower edge of the clearing. The line fell steadily toward the sapling.

Grover came to look. He crouched, scooped up a fistful of soil and rubbed it between his fingers.

"That's not good for the roots," he said. "They're going to sit in wet soil at the top and dry soil at the bottom. Trees don't like living in two different gardens at once."

"Do you have a suggestion?" Annabeth asked.

"Move the sapling."

Silena looked up from her marigolds. "But we planned the whole bed around it."

"Then we need to decide whether the drawing matters more than the tree," Grover said.

Nobody answered immediately. Annabeth had spent two evenings measuring the clearing, and the olive sapling was at the center of the design. It would be easier to defend the plan than to admit the place she'd chosen had a drainage problem.

She took the drawing out of her pocket and looked at it again. The washing station wasn't on the original map. That was the mistake. She had designed the garden as though the space belonged only to the garden, rather than to a camp that needed water for chores.

"We can put the sapling farther west," she said. "And leave a shallow drainage channel along the east border."

"Can we do that without digging up what Silena planted?" Percy asked.

"Not all of it. We'd have to move the first row of alyssum."

Silena considered the bed. "They're still in shallow holes. I can replant them. But I want Grover to choose the new place with us."

Grover nodded. "I want the soil tested first."

Chiron arrived while they were kneeling beside the drainage groove. He looked at the drawing, then at the water and the shifted stones.

"A revised plan?" he asked.

"Yes," Annabeth said. "We forgot that the washing station drains this way."

"Does the new route cross the footpath?"

"Not if we keep it inside the eastern edge."

"And the stones?"

Thalia pointed to her small pile. "I brought them from the amphitheater."

Chiron looked at the pile. "Were those loose stones intended for repairs?"

Thalia hesitated. "They were sitting beside the wall."

"They may have been set aside by the maintenance crew."

"I didn't ask."

"Then return them until we know."

Thalia gave the stones a sour look. She'd been proud of finding materials without demanding a new supply. Now she had to admit that the materials might already belong to someone else's project.

"I'll take them back," she said.

Clarisse offered to help. Thalia almost refused, then took the offer. They loaded the stones into the wheelbarrow, which still had the wobbling wheel, and headed toward the amphitheater.

Annabeth moved the cord west. Silena gently lifted the first row of plants and set them in damp cloth. Grover dug into the new spot, checked the texture and the depth of the better soil, and pronounced it acceptable.

"Not perfect," he said.

"That's all right," Annabeth answered. "We want it to grow, not win an architecture prize."

Percy held the sapling while they moved it. The roots were smaller than he'd expected, the soil around them loose enough that he was afraid of breaking something without noticing.

"Slowly," Grover said.

"I am."

"A little slower."

Percy slowed. He kept the weight steady while Silena filled around the root ball and Annabeth checked the trunk against the cord. Grover watered the new spot, just enough to settle the soil without turning it muddy.

Thalia and Clarisse returned with permission to use different stones from a maintenance pile near the shed. The proper materials were less attractive, rougher and flatter, but they'd been approved for landscaping rather than structural repair.

"These won't win a beauty contest," Thalia said.

"They'll stay where we put them," Annabeth answered.

They built the drainage edge together. Thalia set the stones, Clarisse carried them, Percy filled the gaps with gravel and Silena replanted the displaced flowers. When they poured one bucket of water at the washing station, it ran along the new channel instead of across the sapling's roots.

Annabeth watched it reach the lower collection bed and disappear into the soil.

"Better," she said.

Chiron made a note to check the channel after the next rain. The garden wasn't finished, and the trial had shown them that a drawing couldn't anticipate every use of a place. Annabeth folded the revised plan and marked the date. She intended to keep both versions, not because she enjoyed displaying her mistakes, but because the changes would explain why the garden ended up looking different from her first idea.


---

At midday they discovered a problem with the irrigation line. The little trench had been cut to the exact angle on Annabeth's plan, but the water didn't spread evenly when Percy sent a careful stream through it. One bed flooded and another stayed dry.

Silena knelt beside the flooded flowers. "If this happens when no one's here, the seedlings will rot."

Annabeth checked her measurements. "The slope is correct."

"Then the soil isn't behaving like the drawing," Percy said.

"That's not funny."

"I wasn't trying to be."

She tested the channel again with a cup of water. It pooled against an old root just beneath the surface, invisible until the soil had softened. They dug out the obstruction, then discovered the outlet still emptied too fast into the lowest bed.

Clarisse arrived with two short stones and set them on either side of the channel. "Slow it down here."

Annabeth studied the arrangement and shook her head. "That would send water over the path after heavy rain. We need an overflow."

"Then tell me where you want it."

Annabeth drew a shallow branch away from the path, and Clarisse dug it while Percy carried the excess soil to the compost pile. Silena moved three plants farther uphill. Nobody got the design they had imagined at sunrise. The final layout was better, because it accounted for ground they could actually touch.

At the end of the test, Annabeth wrote down the amount of water they'd used and decided they should inspect the beds again after the next rain. A planted garden was not a finished garden. Somebody would have to maintain it after the celebration.

Afternoon stretched long and amber, and the garden took shape.

Moonvine went in along the north edge, tendrils tied to twine trellises with strips of old camp T-shirts. Lavender formed a hazy blue-green hedge behind the marigolds, leaves releasing camphor and honey when bruised. Alyssum foamed white between stones, petals cool as milk against Annabeth's wrists as she tucked them in. In the center, Silena planted a single olive sapling no taller than Annabeth's forearm, leaves silver-green, stem tied to a cedar stake.

"For Athena," Silena said, patting soil around it. "And for wisdom, et cetera. Don't let it die."

"I won't," Annabeth said, throat unexpectedly thick. She pressed soil firm around the base, feeling fragile roots through the earth. Bark rasped her palm. "I'll water it every morning."

Percy hauled the watering cans from the shed, two galvanized cans that sloshed and clanged against his shins, leaving wet streaks on his jeans. Water arced out in glittering fans, catching sunlight, drumming on leaves. Mud darkened to coffee-brown. The smell of wet soil bloomed up, mineral and clean, mixing with lavender and hot stone. Droplets clung to marigold petals and trembled before falling.

Annabeth followed behind him, checking drainage, lifting leaves to let water reach roots, her own can lighter and easier to aim. Spray misted her calves. Her socks squelched.

"Left side's pooling," she said, pointing with her chin, hands full. "Lift the can higher. Let it fall soft."

Percy obeyed, tongue out again, and the stream broke into rain. A rainbow flickered in the mist for a heartbeat and vanished. Ella gasped and tried to sketch it with colored pens, layering blue over yellow with intense concentration.

Clarisse, finished hauling, had migrated to Thalia's border crew, holding stones steady while Thalia packed them. Her complaints had dwindled to occasional mutters about blisters and tyrannical architects, but her hands stayed gentle on the rocks, brushing away ants before setting each one. When the last stone on the west side seated with a solid thunk, she sat back and stared at the uneven gray line with something like pride warring across her face.

"Huh," she said. "Doesn't look terrible."

Thalia knocked her knuckles against Clarisse's. "High praise from Cabin Three's fiercest critic."

"Don't push it, Pinecone Face."

Grover wandered the rows with his pipes now, playing a wandering tune so soft it blended with bee hum, coaxing drooping transplants upright. Bees arrived as if summoned, fat bumblebees bumbling from lavender to alyssum, legs laden gold. One landed on Silena's shoulder and she froze, cross-eyed, until it lumbered off, and everyone laughed.

Ella filled page after page, documenting soil texture — gritty, cool, wormy — light angle, cloud shapes, who sneezed, who spilled. Her handwriting slanted wildly when she got excited, climbing off the lines. Ink stained her talons blue.

"Chapter title?" Ella asked suddenly, looking up. "Ella needs chapter title for records."

Annabeth looked around the circle — Thalia dusted white to the elbows, Clarisse sprawled with her cap over her face, Silena braiding alyssum stems into Percy's hair while he pretended to protest, Grover asleep sitting up against the shrine with pipes across his chest, Ella herself blinking expectant — and felt the afternoon lodge itself behind her ribs, sun-warmed and permanent.

"The Garden," Annabeth said. "Call it that."

Percy plucked a braided flower from his hair and tucked it behind her ear, fingers lingering a second against her temple, soil smudging her cheek. White petals against her ponytail. His touch light as the butterfly from before.

"There," he said. "Now you're the prettiest potato farmer."

"Flattery will get you extra watering shifts," Annabeth said, though she leaned almost imperceptibly into his hand before pulling back, smiling so wide her cheeks ached.

Silena clapped both hands over her heart and toppled backward into the marigolds with a dramatic groan. "Young love! Ella, document the swooning bystander!"

"Ella documents swooning," Ella said, dutifully writing upside down. " casualty count: one Poseidon daughter, overcome."

As shadows lengthened, they packed tools in companionable tiredness. Trowels clanked into the bucket. Empty flats stacked. Twine wound. Thalia walked the border once more, testing each stone with her toe, nodding to herself. Clarisse righted the wheelbarrow and loaded it with trash, movements slower now, cap still backwards. Grover woke with a snort, apologized to the olive sapling for snoring, and promised to visit weekly.

Annabeth made a final circuit with her sketchbook, noting what had gone where, which moonvine tendril needed retying, where water pooled. Pencil scratched. Her shoulders burned pleasantly. Dirt rimmed every fingernail, ground into knuckle lines, dusted across her nose where Percy had marked her. She left it there.

Percy fell into step beside her, crate now full of empty cups and knit with lemon rinds, bumping against his leg. His hair stood up in damp spikes. A streak of mud striped his calf. For a while they walked without talking, boots scuffing the new border stones, bees humming low over their work.

At the shrine steps he stopped. The niche held fresh offerings now — a marigold head, a flat gray stone from Thalia's pile, Ella's blue pen cap, a plastic army man from Clarisse's pocket, presented with gruff embarrassment. Olive sapling leaves rattled faintly in the evening breeze, silver flashing.

Annabeth remembered the promise that had started this, whispered weeks ago after bad news and worse dreams on the canoe dock, feet dangling over black water: something living, something that stays. Her chest tightened.

Percy set down the crate and held out his hand, palm up, dirty as ever, lines dark with soil that wouldn't wash out for days. An invitation, unhurried.

Annabeth slid her equally dirty hand into his. Grit met grit. Calluses caught. Their fingers fit, familiar and strange at once, warm from sun. No rush, no audience — Silena had tactfully herded the others toward the path with promises of showers and dinner, Ella still narrating over her shoulder.

"Stay a minute?" Percy asked.

Annabeth nodded, swinging their joined hands once between them. "A while. Dinner can wait."

They sat on the shrine steps together, shoulders touching, hands linked in the dust, watching light go honey-gold across the new beds. Lavender nodded. Marigolds burned orange. Water beaded on alyssum like tiny lenses. The garden smelled of green things and fresh beginnings, soil and lemon and sun-heated rock.

Tomorrow weeds would come, and beetles, and Clarisse's rowing tryouts, and letters from California, and all the unanswered questions about fall. Tonight roots held.

Annabeth rested her head briefly against Percy's shoulder, feeling the sun's warmth through his shirt, the steady rise of his breathing. Dirt on both their noses. Laughter still echoing somewhere down the path where Silena was retelling the nose story with grand gestures. Stone solid under them. Green things growing in rows they had drawn and dug themselves.

"Good garden," Percy said quietly.

Annabeth tightened her fingers around his, smiling at the neat lines and the crooked trellis and the olive sapling trembling brave in the center. "Good garden," she agreed.
