## Chapter 179: Sunday at Eight

Percy Jackson woke to wood being split at an hour when nothing needed splitting, and knew before he opened his eyes which of his sisters was out there doing it.

Clarisse. Short handle, left shoulder, two rounds to the breath and a pause between to stack, and the pause was where the thinking went, because she did not care to think while swinging something that could take the fingers off a hand.

The blanket had crept down to his waist, and the cold had come over the edge of it and lay along his ribs, and the cabin held the particular quiet of a place that was already half awake without him: the stove ticking itself cool, a lid settling on the far shelf, the faint scrape of Silena's basket being lifted off the table, because Silena moved her basket the way other people locked a door.

Sunday, the eighth of October. The thought arrived whole.

He turned his head. The window had grey in it and nothing else — the flat grey that came off the lake this month and sat in the valley until mid-morning as though somebody down below had left a door standing open. The sun was still behind the north ridge. On a Sunday, in Percy's experience, the sun took its time.

On the hook behind the door hung his coat, and in the inside pocket of it, folded in fours, was a slip of paper with four words on it in handwriting he could have picked out from the far end of the dining hall. He did not take it out. He only knew where it was, the way a person knows where the last step is in the dark, and then he put his feet on the floor and made the noise that a person makes when they have decided to be awake.

"Nobody asked," Silena said from the table, without looking up.

"I wasn't asked."

"You were preparing to be gracious about it."

"I'm always gracious."

"That's one way to start a Sunday." Clarisse came in with her arms full of split wood, boots loud on the boards, her hair still wet from wherever Clarisse went at five in the morning, and dropped the load into the box beside the stove with the satisfaction of a person filling a container to exactly the right height and not a stick more. "The porch light's out."

"It's on a timer."

"The tenth one. Ours. It's out." She straightened and looked at him with her head a little to one side. "You were on somebody else's steps last night."

"I walked her home."

"You walked her *up*. Her home is up. You were coming down and you went up, which is not walking home, it is a detour, and you did it in the dark, and then you came back here and went to bed at a reasonable hour like a man who had done nothing at all."

"I did do nothing."

"Mm." Clarisse squared the wood box to the corner of the stove, a thing she had already done twice this week, and this time did not straighten it again. "You stood out there in the dark like a lighthouse with its lamp off."

"You can't see a lighthouse with its lamp off."

"You can see everything about a lighthouse with its lamp off. That's the whole point of a lighthouse, it's a very large building that has decided to be quiet." She went back out for the second armload. "Also your hair's gone. Did you sleep on it wet?"

"I slept on it dry."

"It's wet now. From the *idea* of damp." The door swung shut behind her.

Percy dragged a hand through his hair, found that she was approximately right about it, and gave up.

Silena had her basket in her lap and was, at that moment, doing the thing she had not done in three days, which was opening it.

The cloth inside was heavy work-cloth folded in threes, the colour of a road in August, and it smelled very faintly of a forge — not smoke, exactly, but the memory of smoke, the kind that lives in canvas no matter how many times a person shakes it out. She took out a needle that was already threaded, which meant she had threaded it at some point in the last three days and put it back, which meant the entire argument about whether she was going to do this had been conducted inside her own head, at night, in this chair.

She smoothed the cloth over her knee, found the place that wanted her, and put the first stitch in.

Percy watched the first stitch go in and then looked at the ceiling, because he had a whole season of living in this cabin and one of the things he had learned was that a sister who has just done a brave thing will bite your head off if you applaud.

"That's mending," he said, instead.

"Yes."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to say something with your face, and I would rather you didn't." She pulled the thread through and set the second stitch beside the first, even, small and exact. "It's Sunday, the stove's going, nobody has asked me a single question, and a person is allowed to sew."

"Three days," Clarisse said, coming in with the last of the wood. "That's been sitting in that basket three days doing nothing."

"It has been *organized* for three days," Silena said. "That's a different thing, and you know it's a different thing, because you've had that list sitting on your shelf since the beginning of September and the greenhouse bank is still on it with an open line next to it."

Clarisse's mouth opened, and closed, and she set the wood down with enormous care. "He started it," she said, pointing at Percy with the whole length of her arm.

"He did not start it," Silena said. "I started it, myself, just now, at this table, and it's going beautifully, so if you two could either help or get out of the light."

Percy got out of the light by picking up the kettle and the coffee tin and putting on his coat. On his way past the shelf he looked at the list that lived on the shelf, in Clarisse's square hand, the last lines still open at the bottom of the page — the greenhouse bank, the torn awning, the fallen pine — and did not touch it, because a line on that list was not an invitation, it was a debt, and he was not the person it was owed to.

"Two cups?" Silena said pleasantly at his back.

"Two cups."

"You drink one."

"I drink one and carry one. That's still two cups. Two is the minimum number of cups for what I'm doing."

"Uh-huh," Clarisse said from the stove, in a voice that had never in the history of this family been less convinced.

Nobody said anything else about it, which was the nicest thing anybody had said to him all morning.

The west path was dark in the way that a path is only ever dark in October, which is to say it was full of ordinary daylight and none of it was warm. The nine lamps stood on their posts end to end down the slope, unlit and a bit ridiculous in the morning, like a procession caught mid-step and told to wait, and the tenth sat on the rail of his own cabin porch with its wick down and its glass still fogged inside from the night. Percy went up between them with the kettle in one hand and his collar in the other and his breath going in front of him in small honest clouds, and the frost on the grass came up to the edges of the path in a clean white line that would be mud by noon.

Above him the pavilion stood open on its south side the way it always stood open, and inside it the wall on the north was the darker rectangle it had become on Saturday, with the survey sheet on it — a sheet of paper that had three hundred yards of mountainside on it now, and a flow line, and five names. He could not read any of the names from the path. He did not need to. He knew where his was, in brackets, and what it said next to it, and he went on up the steps and into the kitchen to get the fire going before anybody else arrived.

Thalia Grace had been awake since five, and had been on her feet since five past, and in the half hour since then she had been extremely careful.

Cabin One was cold in the way that a cabin which belonged to two sisters and a great deal of unspoken arithmetic was always cold in the morning: the stove out, the window grey, the floorboards holding the night's temperature in them like a grudge. She sat on the edge of her bunk with a strip of linen tape in her hands and did not turn the lamp on, because the lamp would have been visible from Annabeth's bunk, and Annabeth was a light sleeper with the ears of somebody who had spent her childhood listening for a door.

The tape went around the left heel, firm and flat, not tight, the way she had taught herself to do it in the dark. She had practised the whole business in the dark for four nights running and could do it now in under a minute: the wrap, the overlap, the knot on the outside where the boot would sit on top of it and keep it honest. Then the sock, and the first boot — the boot she had picked out on Thursday for exactly this purpose — and she stood up and put her weight down on it and let her face do nothing at all.

Outside, two miles of ridgeline were going from black to grey. Inside, her heel made its opinions known in a language that was entirely physical and completely unwelcome.

Nobody was supposed to know. That was not a policy anybody had voted on; it was a fact about the world, the same as the fact that water ran downhill and that Chiron's office smelled of chalk. The thing about a camp full of people who could set a bone in thirty seconds was that they would then ask about it for six weeks, and after that they would tell her, forever, in the past tense, and she would be a person with a bad heel instead of a person with a heel. She had been a person with a great many things already, and she was not collecting another one.

She walked the length of the cabin and back, on the outside edge, twice, and it held. It held the way it had held on Friday, which was to say it held the way a ladder holds: useably, and with an accounting.

"You're fidgeting."

Thalia did not startle, because she had heard Annabeth wake up four minutes earlier and had chosen not to acknowledge it, which was a tactic and not a lie. "I'm stretching."

"You've been stretching since five. Stretching has an ending, that's how you know it happened."

"Stretching doesn't have an ending. That's how *I* know it's stretching." She sat back down and began on the second boot, briskly, like a person with nowhere else to be. "You should sleep more. You did a mountain yesterday."

"Half a mountain. Annabeth Grace did the other half, and she did it with a corrected reading in the margin and her initials." Annabeth sat up under her blanket with her hair in last night's braid and her eyes already at working brightness, which was a thing she could do in the first thirty seconds of the day and which Thalia had never once managed. "Is today the eight o'clock?"

"Today is the eight o'clock."

"The one from Friday. Where the Apollo boy indicates about your elbow."

"Rhys Calder does not *tell* me about my elbow," Thalia said. "He indicates. And the difference between telling and indicating is that I have let him live."

"Mm." Annabeth swung her legs out. "You fixed your hair before you did the tape."

"It's wet."

"It's dry. It's been dry for an hour. I watched."

"You watched a person's *hair*."

"I watch everything. It's the family business." Annabeth pulled her jumper on over her head and, muffled inside it for a moment, said, from somewhere in the wool, "You could wear the blue one today. The one without the hole."

"The one with the *vent*." Thalia stood, and for half a second the weight went wrong — no more than that, a small ugly slip on the outside of the foot that she covered by stepping into the turn and reaching for the bow in the corner as though she had been walking that way all along. "I'm wearing what I'm wearing. It's a field. It doesn't have opinions about shirts."

Annabeth was looking at her. Annabeth was looking at her the way she looked at a number she suspected had been written down wrong — not accusing it, just refusing to move past it.

"The floor's cold," Thalia said.

"I know."

"Then you know everything, and we can go and do eggs." She slung the quiver over her shoulder and went to the door and held it, and the cold came in around the frame and went past her into the cabin, and behind her she heard Annabeth pull on her boots, one, two, the exact speed of somebody deciding not to say a thing.

There was one good comb in Cabin One and it hung on a nail between the two bunks, and the rule about it was that whoever got up first got it, which meant Thalia got it five mornings out of seven and used it for approximately nine seconds and hung it straight back up, and Annabeth had never once mentioned this out loud, because Annabeth kept files on everything and this particular one was in the file marked *nothing*, alongside a cold floor and a pair of boots.

"Your hair," Thalia said, from the door, with her hat in her hand and the bow in the other.

"It's fine."

"It's got yesterday's braid in it, and last night in it, and in about ten minutes it's going to have a piece of grass in it, and we haven't left the cabin yet." Thalia came back across the boards, put the bow down against the wall, took Annabeth by the shoulders, turned her around and did the whole thing in under a minute: three passes with the comb, one tug at the end, the ribbon off the nail and into it — fast at the start and then absolutely unhurried at the end, the same as everything Thalia did, because she had been doing her sister's hair since before either of them could remember it starting, and she did it the way she made eggs, and the way she left a room.

"There."

"Thanks."

"Don't tell anyone."

"About what?" And Annabeth looked at her in the small mirror off its nail, with the braid done and the ribbon in it and the light just coming, and said it in the voice she used for numbers that had come out right: "Thalia."

"Mm."

"Your elbow."

"*What* about my elbow."

"Nothing about your elbow." Annabeth put the mirror back on its nail. "Eggs," she said, mercifully, and Thalia picked the bow back up and went out onto the porch and said, from the cold, in a completely different voice, "EGGS. WE ARE DESCENDING."

They went down the path together, because that was the rule neither of them had ever written down: the steep bit, arm through arm, no discussion. The frost had the north side of the path and the sun had not got to it yet, and Thalia took the outside of it, where the grit was, and did it on purpose and did not think about why she was doing it on purpose.

"The sheet's up," Annabeth said, close to her shoulder. "I want to look at it properly after breakfast. Before everybody walks past it and makes it ordinary."

"Then look at it before breakfast."

"I want to look at it with the light on it." Annabeth's arm tightened once, and then they were at the bottom of the slope and the kitchen door was in front of them with steam on the inside of its glass. "Eight o'clock," Annabeth said, glancing at the bow over Thalia's shoulder.

"Eight o'clock," Thalia said, and shouldered the door, and the warm room took them both.

The kitchen at the pavilion had one stove, four windows and a clock that hung on the nail beside the pantry door and ran fast by two minutes, a fact which Thalia had never once believed and which Percy had verified twice with a watch he did not admit to owning.

He had the fire in the stove going by half six and the kettle over it, and by the time the door opened for the second time the windows had gone from black to grey to a soft fogged white with the shapes of people in them coming up the path. The room warmed the way a stone room warms, which is to say slowly and then all at once, and mostly from the stove, and partly from the fact that a camp kitchen with seven people in it cannot stay cold for very long no matter what October is doing outside it.

The table was the long one, with the two benches and the scar down the middle where somebody in a long-ago summer had tried to carve something and given up halfway. Annabeth set it. Annabeth set it the way she did everything, which was square: the cups in the same relation to the plates, the jam in the middle of the cloth, the spoons all facing the way the spoons faced, and when Clarisse came in with a board of potatoes and put the board down off-centre on purpose, Annabeth reached across without looking and moved it two inches, and Clarisse said, "Good morning to you too, Grace," and Annabeth said, "You're welcome," and neither of them looked up from what they were doing.

Clarisse had the potatoes — scrubbed, halved, cut small so they would go fast in the big pan, salt, and the last of the onions from the crock. Silena did the toast and the jam and the second pot of coffee, because Silena had decided within thirty seconds of entering a kitchen what the kitchen needed, and she was almost never wrong about it. Percy was on the kettle and then on the dishes, which was a rotation nobody had to argue about because he had lost the argument in July and had been graceful about it since.

Marcus came up from the cabins with his hands already cold, and Olive came up after him under a scarf pulled up to her eyes, and by ten past seven there were seven people in the room and the pan was going.

Thalia did the eggs.

Thalia did the eggs the way Thalia did everything, which was fast at the start and then absolutely unhurried at the end: the flame low, a good deal more butter than a person would admit to in company, a fork instead of a spatula because a spatula was, in her words, an instrument of people who had given up, and then she stood over the pan and let the eggs take their time and did not stir them so much as keep them company. When they were still a little wet in the middle she folded them over in the pan, once, and slid them out onto the plate, and ground pepper over the top of them with the specific generosity of a person who believed that pepper was a food group.

"There," she said, putting the plate in the middle of the table. "Eggs."

"They're just eggs," Clarisse said.

"They're eggs that a person who was up at five made for six other people, which is the only kind of eggs this table accepts." Thalia stripped off the towel and dropped it on the bench and took the seat beside Annabeth, and reached past her for the toast, and ate four pieces of toast standing up while the potatoes finished, because Thalia treated breakfast as a supply operation that happened to have chairs in it.

The room did what a room does when it is full and cold outside and warm inside. Olive talked. Olive had been drawing in the night, she announced, the whole fall from the slab down to the terrace, at an inch to two feet, and she had got the bend right at last, and she wanted it known that the bend was the hardest part and that she had got it right twice. Marcus, who had held the rod at four stations in a row and had not once let it wander, said that the bend was the easiest part and that the hardest part had been standing in water for a hundred minutes in October and not once mentioning it, and everybody at the table looked at Percy, who was eating potatoes and had been prepared for this.

"I mentioned it," Percy said. "I called it warm."

"You called it *warmer than the lake*," Annabeth said, "which is true of a great many things that are not warm."

"It's warmer than the lake and that's the standard I set."

"That's the standard you *survived*," Clarisse said, and put the big pan down in the middle of the table with the last of the potatoes in it, which was her way of ending a subject: with more food.

Percy took a second helping of potatoes because the potatoes were there and because refusing them would have been its own kind of rudeness. Annabeth took a second helping of eggs, which was not hunger so much as a statement, and Thalia — upright again, back on toast — took the statement and wore it. Olive took a second plate of everything and announced that she intended to take a third plate of everything back to her cabin in a scarf as a lunch, and Marcus said that no plate had ever survived the walk from the pavilion down to the cabins and she was welcome to be the first.

The jam was the last of a blackberry thing from August that Silena had put up herself, and the family guarded it the way some families guard money. There were two spoonfuls left in the bottom of the crock, and every person at the table knew exactly how many, and Silena put those two spoonfuls on Percy's toast without being asked and without ceremony, which was how Silena said the things that could not be said out loud without everybody having to stand around being brave about them.

"Thursday's pouch," Clarisse said, to the crock, at the jam.

"Thursday's pouch," Percy agreed.

"Thursday's pouch, and a hook at the end of the road, and a man in a truck at six in the morning, and I would like letters in it. All of you. Including the ones who have been writing in the dark and pretending they haven't." She looked along the table with the slow patience of a woman who had counted her siblings once this morning and was perfectly willing to count them again. "There's a box in the Big House and it's got a slot in it, and that slot has been lonely since Wednesday."

Percy developed an interest in his coffee. Annabeth found a crumb on the tablecloth that required her full attention, and dealt with it, and located a second crumb, and dealt with that one too, and Clarisse watched the two of them do this with the enormous satisfaction of a person who had laid a net and had just watched two people walk into it holding hands, and who was not going to say a single word about it until Thursday.

The door came open at twenty-five past seven and the cold walked in and stood in the middle of the room for a moment, looking around, and Silena got up and shut it and put the kettle back over the fire and said, to the door, "You're not invited," which the room accepted without comment.

They ate. The fog on the windows thickened and then began, slowly, to clear from the bottom up as the stoves worked on it, and the light that came in was the flat silver light of a mountain morning with nothing promised in it yet. Percy's knee found Annabeth's under the table, in the exact spot where the scar down the middle ended and the wood went quiet, and stayed there, and neither of them made anything of it, which was its own kind of sentence. Once, when Clarisse was telling Marcus the actual length of the list of things this camp would have to do before the first real snow, Annabeth's hand came over and sat on his wrist for the length of one breath and then went back to her plate, and Percy developed a sudden very serious interest in the potatoes that lasted until the colour left his face.

"The wall's going to get looked at," Marcus said. "Half the camp's going to walk past that sheet on Monday and see a piece of paper."

"They'll see the names," Annabeth said.

"They'll see the brackets," Percy said, "because the brackets are the best part, and there's a whole camp of people who don't know that yet, and I'm prepared to wait them out."

"You're prepared to *talk* them out," Clarisse said. "I've heard you do the voice."

"What voice?"

"The one where you explain the fall to somebody who asked about the weather."

"It hasn't come up yet."

"It came up twice on the way up to breakfast, at two different people, one of whom was Olive, and she'd been there."

Olive, who had been there, said that the voice was fine, and that the part with the plate of rock was the best bit, and that she herself planned to stand near the wall on Monday and see what people did when she didn't say anything at all.

Thalia ate three eggs, four pieces of toast and a plate of potatoes, and watched the clock on the nail beside the pantry door in the way a person watches a clock when they have decided not to look at it. At twenty to eight she put her fork down and stood up and took her coat off the hook.

"Elbow," Clarisse said, pleasantly, without looking up from the pan.

"Excuse me?"

"You've got a thing where your elbow goes up when you're going to be gone a while." Clarisse turned a potato with the flat of the knife. "Friday. Same time. Same elbow."

"That was Friday," Thalia said. "Friday I was at a bench. Today I'm at a field, and the field is two hundred yards further away, which is why the elbow is higher, and that is the entire explanation and there isn't a second one."

"Uh-huh."

"Clarisse."

"Shoot straight." Clarisse put the potatoes on the plate and slid it across the table without looking at her. "And come back and do the eggs again next Sunday, because Percy's are a crime and Silena's are a rumour."

Thalia took her bow from where it leaned by the door and put her hat on and stood for a second in the frame with the grey behind her, eighteen, and tall, and going out into a cold field at eight o'clock on a Sunday because she had decided to, which was the part of her that had nothing to do with any of them and which they had all quietly agreed to leave standing where it was.

"Eat the rest of the toast," she said, to nobody. "It goes stale by ten and I refuse to be blamed for that."

She went out, and the door shut, and the clock on the nail beside the pantry door said eight minutes to eight and was, as always, two minutes early.

The table went on for another half hour. That was the part of a Sunday breakfast that nobody scheduled and everybody kept: the second pot of coffee, the second helpings, Silena passing the jam without being asked and then taking more toast herself because she had decided to. Marcus went off at half past to look at a boot he was convinced had come apart at the welt. Olive went with him, taking the scarf with her, mid-sentence. Clarisse began the dishes with the grimness of a woman who had won the dishes by losing something else, and Percy, on his rotation, took the towel.

At the door, Annabeth put her coat on and stood with her hand on the frame, waiting, not saying anything about it, which was a thing she had got very good at and which he had got very good at noticing.

"You want the wall," he said.

"I want the wall with the light on it." She looked at him. "Come and be smug about the brackets."

"I was going to anyway. You can't stop a person being smug in a public building."

"I can try." She held the door for him, and the cold came in at both of them, and she did not let go of the frame until he had gone past her, which meant her hand ended up on his sleeve for a second and a half on the way through, and which she would have said afterwards that she had not planned, and she would have been lying, and she would have known.

The archery field at eight on a Sunday in October was a place that had decided to be completely honest about the temperature.

It sat on the flat below the tree line where the sun did not reach until nine, and it kept its frost the way a cellar keeps its cold — not as weather, but as policy. The grass stood up in it, stiff and silver, the targets were grey felt gone damp at the edges, and the whole two hundred yards of it had that particular Sunday silence, which is not quiet so much as a held breath with nobody in it yet.

Thalia got there at two minutes past eight because two minutes past eight was late enough to be a choice and early enough to be a threat, and found Rhys Calder already at the shooting line, setting out arrow after arrow on the ground beside him in a row so even it looked drawn.

"Morning," he said.

"You're early."

"I'm on time. You're the one with the clock in the kitchen that runs fast."

"It runs *early*," Thalia said. "Fast means it gains. Early means it tells the truth ahead of schedule. There's a difference and it matters."

"There is a difference," Rhys agreed, and did not say anything else about it, which was the thing she kept having to decide how to feel about. He was taller than she was and not by much, and he had the particular stillness of somebody who had been shooting for longer than she had and had stopped needing to prove it about four years before she had. Apollo cabin. Brown coat, sleeves pushed up in the cold like a person who had been warm once and was prepared to be warm again.

She strung her bow, because stringing a bow in front of somebody who knows how to string a bow is a way of saying good morning, and stepped up to the line and did not say anything for eleven arrows.

The first three were good. The next three were better than good, because she had looked at where the last three had gone and had decided, personally, to improve on them. The fourth she put a little left, into the wind that was not there, and she lowered the bow and stood still and made herself take the shot again properly rather than the way she wanted to take it, which was faster.

"That's the one," Rhys said, from behind her and a little to the right, not watching her, watching the target. "Do it again the same. You fixed your elbow before I could say it."

"Your reputation precedes you. It precedes you by about four feet and a bad heel—"

The word was out of her mouth before she had finished deciding to say it, and it landed on the frost between them, and she did not look at him, and she did not look at her own feet, and she stood exactly as she was standing, on the outside edge of the left foot, in the frost, with the bow in her hand and her shoulders level and her face doing nothing.

It had been a joke. It was a joke the way a stick held out over water is a stick: the shape of a thing, thrown at a target, with every chance of not landing where it was aimed. Bad *heel*. Her own. She had said her own bad heel out loud, on a field, in front of a person, as a joke, and now she had to be the one who did not mean it.

"I—" she started.

"Four feet and a bad *draw*," Rhys said, mildly, to the target. "That's what you were going to say. I've got a bad draw on the release and everybody in Apollo knows it and half of Ares has heard about it at this point, so you're not telling anybody anything new."

She looked at him then, and he was still watching the target, and he had the good grace to be almost but not quite embarrassed about the speed of it, which meant he had heard it, and had decided, in the time it took her to draw one more breath, to hand it back to her intact.

"Four feet and a bad draw," she said.

"That's what I said."

"That's what you said, and I'm going to let you have it."

"Generous."

"I'm a generous person. It's one of my better-known qualities." She drew, and held, and released, and it went where it should have gone, and she let out the breath she had been holding since a word came out of her mouth four seconds ago. "Again on the elbow," she said. "Go on. Indicate."

He stepped up beside her — close enough that she could see the frost starting to go off his sleeves — and he did not touch her, which was the second thing she had to decide how to feel about in as many minutes. He put his own elbow up, dropped it two inches, dropped it another half, and held it there, and said, "Yours does that when you're rushed. You're never rushed except when you're not rushing on purpose."

"My elbow's fine."

"Your elbow's very good. It's the rest of you that's in a hurry."

Thalia set her feet — deliberately, on the flat, in the grit at the edge of the frost, the way a person sets them who has decided that the ground is part of the problem and not her — and took the shot, and watched it take the dead centre of the gold, and said, without turning around, "That's one for the elbow."

"That's one for the elbow."

"Best of seven."

"You'll win."

"I know I'll win," Thalia said. "That's not the point. The point is that you'll know I won."

Behind her, on the frost, she heard him laugh — a short one, surprised out of him, the kind that gets away — and she set another arrow on the string and did not turn around for a while, because there was a thing happening in her face that she wanted to be somewhere else when it happened.

They shot for half an hour. She beat him four to three in the seven and then beat him again on a longer distance that he had not agreed to, on the grounds that he had been talking and she had not, and he took the loss the way she liked a person to take a loss, which was completely and out loud. At ten past nine he unstrung, and rolled his shoulders, and said he had a bench to get to and asked if she would tell anybody about the draw.

"Every single person," Thalia said. "I'm going to have it printed."

"Fair." He picked up his coat. "Same time Friday?"

"Same time Friday."

He went off across the frost towards the cabins with the arrows in his hand and his collar up, and Thalia stood on the shooting line with her bow in her fist and watched him go the way a person watches a door after somebody has walked out of it, which is to say for one second longer than the door requires.

Then she let herself take the weight off the left foot, just for a second, standing in the frost between two targets with nobody on the field and nobody on the path and the whole cold morning to herself, and she let the second go by, and then she put the weight back on, and rolled her shoulders the way he had rolled his, and took the long way up to the kitchen, past the woodshed and the brake where nobody would be at ten past nine on a Sunday, so that when she came around the corner into the warm room she was walking the way she walked.

Annabeth Grace wanted to look at a wall, and Percy Jackson had already decided at half past eight in the morning that he was going to enjoy being the person she wanted to look at it with.

The pavilion at that hour belonged to nobody. The east side had the sun coming in low across the floor in one long bar, and the north wall was out of it, in shadow, and the survey sheet hung there pinned at four corners with the paper still carrying the curl it had taken on in the damp: Saturday's paper, Saturday's lines, and on it three hundred yards of mountainside reduced to something a person could hold in two hands.

Annabeth stood in front of it for a while without saying anything, which with Annabeth was not silence but a form of work.

"The flow line's held," she said. "It didn't bleed at all overnight and the paper went damp twice."

"It's good paper."

"It's *ordinary* paper. I bought it in a shop in the village that also sells dog food." She put her finger out towards the sheet and did not touch it, following the dotted line from the slab, down the shelf of rock, into the drawn contour of the terrace and out at the end of it. "There. Wet corner of the north path. Where a certain person stood in mud and told me about it in September and I wrote *load, enthusiasm* and initials next to it, and it's the truest thing on the whole sheet."

"I'd like it noted that I said *rod* as an alternative."

"It's noted. It's in a file." She looked at the five names along the bottom edge and her mouth did the thing it did when she was extremely pleased and intended to keep it to herself, and Percy, who had learned that particular mouth the way he knew his own name, let her keep it.

He did not let her keep all of it. "The brackets are the best part," he said.

"I know the brackets are the best part."

"You know it the way a person knows a thing they were told. I want you to know it the way a person knows a thing they have *seen*." He put his hands in his pockets and looked at the sheet with the particular smugness of a man whose name was in a bracket on a wall. "Two hundred years from now, when this camp is somebody's school, that sheet is going in a frame, and the frame is going to have a plaque, and the plaque is going to say *load, enthusiasm*, and someone is going to stand in front of it and wonder, and the answer is going to be that a fourteen-year-old stood in a creek in October and didn't complain."

"You complained at dinner."

"You don't count dinner. Dinner's private property."

Annabeth laughed — actually laughed, in the empty pavilion, with the sun on the boards and the sheet on the wall behind her — and then she reached back without looking and put her hand flat on the paper beside the dotted line, in the place where the dotted line ended, and said, "It's true, though. You didn't complain."

"You were up on the bank with a clipboard."

"I had a clipboard and a hundred and four numbers and I could hear every single word you said from the water, including the ones to the fish." She took her hand off the wall and turned around. "I want to walk."

"Where."

"Down. Along. I haven't decided." She was already going for her coat on the bench, and the field book was under her arm, and the field book's back cover was the place where his note had been living since a Thursday in the rain, in the flat pressed way a thing lives when it has been handled a lot and put back very carefully each time. He had never once asked to see it. He did not ask now.

They went down the west path with the lamps unlit on their posts, nine of them going down the slope ahead of the two of them in the daylight, ordinary iron, and the cold had come out of the frost and gone somewhere else for an hour, so that the air was only cool and not cruel, and the lake below was the colour of a knife that has been put away and did not want to be taken out again.

Percy took her hand at the bottom of the second lamp, where the path turned and the ground got simple, and did it the way he always did it, which was not a decision but a conclusion: the glove off, the fingers found, the cold of her hand arriving into his palm like a fact. Annabeth Grace, who noticed everything about everything, had never once, in all the months they had been doing this, made a single sentence out of it, and had never once let go first, and both of those were, in his opinion, worth more than any amount of talking.

Her hand said: warm now, and it had not been, and it was her idea to close the gap the rest of the way.

They walked. They went past the boathouse with the canoes under their cover, six of them stacked and dry and shut up for the season, and past the slip where the skiff sat up on its chocks with the heron on the bow in the position it had occupied for most of the summer and all of the autumn so far, which Percy had stopped noticing entirely, which he suspected was the whole idea of it.

"The bank," Annabeth said, when they got to the place where the bank was.

It was the one that faced south-east and caught the sun from ten in the morning and lost it at two, and the grass on it had gone dry and flat in the cold nights and was warm on the top of it in a way that lasted about ten minutes at a time. Percy sat down first, because he always sat down first, and then she sat down next to him and not a little bit next to him, and the slope did the rest of it, and her shoulder came to rest against his chest the way a boat comes to rest against a dock that it is tied to.

"For a while," she said, against his collar.

"For a while," he agreed.

For a while was not measured. The lake went on being a lake below them, and the sun went on doing its ten-minutes-at-a-time business on the dry grass, and a heron two hundred yards away went on being a heron on a bow, and Annabeth Grace put her head on his shoulder and fitted there, with the particular ease of a person who had done this before and had never once needed to be taught how.

Percy turned his head, and she lifted hers, and he kissed her.

It was the kind of kiss that a cold morning earns: slow at the start, unhurried in the middle, and long at the end, and it went on across several separate breaths, and across a moment where they both smiled at the same time for no reason at all and did not stop. Her hand came up and stayed flat on the front of his coat, not holding, just placed, and her thumb moved once against the wool, and the cold came around them on the bank and found that it did not have anywhere to get in. When they broke, it was only to breathe, and their foreheads stayed together for the length of four of those breaths, and the air they were sharing had stopped being anybody else's.

"You were going to say something at the wall," he said.

"I was going to say a lot of things at the wall. The wall was going to get a whole speech."

"I like your speeches."

"You like my speeches when they're about your bracket."

"I like your speeches when they're about anything. I like the one about rebar. I think about the one about rebar more than a person should." He settled his cheek on the top of her head, because it fit there, and because the top of her head smelled faintly of woodsmoke and the soap in the Cabin One washroom, and because the smell of her was a piece of information he had never once been bored by. "Say one."

"You're a menace," Annabeth said, into his collar, "and this is a slope, and I have a hundred and four numbers and five names and a dotted line that I checked twice before I inked it, and I am lying on a bank in the sun on a Sunday because the person I want to lie on a bank in the sun on a Sunday with happens to have turned up at the wall with his hands in his pockets being a lighthouse again."

"Was I being a lighthouse."

"You were being a lighthouse. Clarisse says it's your whole personality now."

"Clarisse has never seen me be a lighthouse."

"Clarisse saw your *hair*. That was enough for her."

His hand found the back of her glove and undid the strap of it, slowly, because it was a difficult strap and not because he was stalling, and her fingers came out into the cold and then straight into his, and Annabeth made a small approving sound about the whole arrangement.

Their hands said a lot of things without being asked. Her fingers traced the line of his palm first, the way she always started, in one slow pass, as though she were checking something; and then the pad of her thumb went over the place where the rope had burn from a July that felt like a decade ago had gone silvery and smooth; "There's five things on that hand," he said.

"There are four fingers doing anything."

"There's a thumb doing plenty."

"The thumb's not the point." She took his fingers one at a time a second time, slowly, in order, the way you count the parts of something you have decided to keep, and he let her get all the way to the last one again before he said, "That's the whole hand."

"That's four," Annabeth said, "and the person they're attached to, and I've got both," which he thought about for a long time afterwards, on the walk home, rather more than was strictly reasonable.

Annabeth put her head back on his shoulder, and her hand stayed where it was, and the sun did another ten minutes on the dry grass.

"Do you still have it," she said, after a while.

It was not a question, which was how he knew it was a question. "It's in my coat."

"Read it to me."

"I can't read it to you. It's four words. If I read it to you out loud we'll both have to stand up and walk somewhere."

"Then don't read it." She turned her head again, and this time the kiss was the longer one, and the slower one, and it had the particular weight of a thing being answered rather than begun — her hand coming up to the side of his face, her cold fingers against the warm part of it under his ear, the two of them going slightly sideways on a slope that had been waiting all morning to tilt them. It lasted long enough that the lake made a sound against the stones below them twice, and neither of them counted it, and when they stopped it was to stay stopped for a minute, breathing, with their eyes shut, in the sun.

"You can keep it," Annabeth said, eventually. "You've been keeping it better than I did."

"You kept it in the back of a field book that you open four times a day."

"Exactly. That's *custody*. You've got it in a pocket, which is affection." She sat up a little and got the field book off the grass and opened it at the back, flat on her knee, and there it was — his note, the one from the rain, in the same flat pressed place it had been living in since a Thursday — and she looked at it for a second without touching it and then closed the book on it again, carefully, the way you close something that you are not finished with and do not intend to be finished with for a long time.

Percy watched her do it and felt the particular fullness in his chest that he had first felt at seventeen, in a life before this one, and had been feeling at fourteen, in this one, with interest, ever since.

"For the record," he said, "I'm going to keep it forever."

"That's not a record. That's a *policy*."

"It's both. It's a policy that's going in the record."

She put the book down on the grass and leaned back into him and pulled his arm around her shoulders with the matter-of-fact efficiency of a person rearranging furniture, and said, "Ten more minutes," and then, three minutes later, in a voice that had gone soft and slow and slightly sideways, "Percy."

"Yeah."

"I'm not asleep."

"I know you're not asleep."

"I'm *nearly* asleep. There's a difference and it matters." Her eyes were shut and her hand had gone quiet in his, and the sentence arrived out of a great distance and did not entirely finish. "Wake me if I actually—"

"I won't."

"That's — that's the wrong — answer—" And she was asleep, fourteen years old, on a bank in the sun, with a hundred and four numbers in a book under her elbow and a whole morning in front of her and her head on his shoulder and her breath going slow, and Percy Jackson sat absolutely still and did not move his arm, and watched the light come up off the water, and did not think about anything else at all for eleven minutes, which he counted, because he was his mother's son in ways that had nothing to do with the sea.

She woke the way she woke from all short sleeps: completely, in one piece, and with her hand already reaching for the next thing.

"I was awake," she said, into his collar.

"You were awake."

"I was awake the *entire* time. That was a tactical nap. Generals take them."

"Eleven minutes."

"Eleven minutes is *strategy*." She sat up and did her hair with her fingers in the flat practical way of it, and there was a crease down one side of her face from the wool of his coat which she refused to acknowledge on principle, and Percy said nothing about it, because it was going to be his favourite detail of the day and he wanted to keep it to himself for a while first.

They walked back up. The sun had gone behind the ridge by then and the cold had come out of the lake and taken over the bank behind them the way it took over everything in this valley at two o'clock in October, and their hands went into each other's again without a single word about it — glove and bare hand, cold and then warm and then cold and then warm, which was its own kind of argument that the two of them had agreed, some time ago, to keep having. They went up the west path with the lamps unlit on their posts, and Percy walked on the outside of the path where the grit was and Annabeth walked on the inside of the path where the grass was, and neither of them had ever established which of those two things was for.

At the bottom of the Cabin One steps they stopped, because that was where the walk ended, and both of them had always known where it ended, and neither of them had ever said so out loud.

"You've got grass in your hair," Annabeth said.

"I've got grass in my hair because a general lay down on a bank."

"That is not—" She stopped. She put her hand up into his hair and found it, and did not take it back out, and the afternoon had gone blue behind her, and she looked at him with the whole expression on, all of it, the way she did about twice a month, without warning anybody, and never in front of anybody. "You're going to be smug about that wall forever," she said.

"I'm going to be smug about that wall forever."

"Good." And she came up the one step she needed and kissed him there in the cold — once, unhurried, with her hand still in his hair and his coat done up to the second button, and the lake down below them and the pavilion empty above them and nobody at all on the path — and then she went up the steps and did not look back at the bottom of them, because she had spent the whole of her looking-back budget on a porch the night before, and Annabeth Grace did not overspend on two nights running.

Percy went back down with his hands in his pockets and four words in the inside pocket of his coat and eleven minutes of somebody else's sleep behind him, and he was not thinking about the wall, or the brackets, or the flow line, and he was extremely pleased about the entire arrangement.

By the time the light went off the water, the nine lamps on the west path had started going up, one at a time, the way they had gone up every evening since a Friday in the rain: a small flare at the bottom of the slope, then a walk, then the next one catching, and then the whole line of them coming up the hill towards Cabin Three in the dark like a sentence being written in amber, slow, from the far end.

Annabeth watched the fifth one come up from the table in the middle of Cabin Three, where she had spread the field book out next to a cup that Clarisse had filled without asking whether she wanted one and which she had not asked for and had drunk all of.

The cabin had gone warm and close around the stove the way it did at the end of a Sunday: boots off the mat, coats heaped on the third hook, the fire down to the good stage where it stopped needing anything. Clarisse sat on the end bench with the winter list across her knee and a pencil behind her ear, reading it the way she read everything, which was with her shoulders. Silena had the basket in her lap.

Silena had had the basket in her lap since five o'clock.

Annabeth had not asked about it. Nobody had asked about it, and this was the thing Annabeth liked best about Cabin Three and had never once known how to explain to anybody in Cabin One: the way this family gave a person a whole afternoon to do a hard thing in, without ever once calling it hard, or naming it, or standing around it in a circle with its hands behind its back. Silena's needle went in and out in the lamplight, small and even and completely unhurried, and every so often her hand came down flat over the row she had finished and stayed there for a second, the way a person covers a card in a game they are not ready to show.

"Row's crooked at the end," Clarisse said, without looking up from the list.

"It isn't."

"Then it's straight and I was wrong and you can put it in the letter." Clarisse turned a page she had not turned. "How much of it's left?"

"All of the interesting part."

Clarisse made a sound that was half a laugh and went back to her list, and Annabeth looked down at the page under her own hands: the greenhouse bank with an open line beside it, the torn awning with an open line beside it, the fallen pine at the far end of the north path with an open line beside it, and above them, in square block capitals, the count that Clarisse kept and never explained and had never once let anybody else write.

The door came in off the porch with a piece of the cold attached to it, and Thalia shouldered it shut behind her and stood there a second, in the lamplight, with her hat pushed back and her bow still over her shoulder and her breath going in front of her like something she had brought in from outside by accident.

She came in. She crossed the boards. And for two steps — two, at the edge of the rug, where the boards gave way to the cold stone of the hearth — the whole weight of her went over to the right, in a way that had nothing to do with where she was going and everything to do with what she was standing on, and then she corrected it, and kept walking, and hung the bow on its nail, and did not look at anybody, and was completely magnificent about it.

Annabeth watched all of it from behind her cup, and did not say a word.

It was not the morning's thing. The morning's thing had been a half-second on a cold floor, which could have been anything, and which Annabeth had put down in the file marked *nothing* and left there. This was not that. This was her sister coming off a porch in the dark and putting her whole weight on a foot for exactly as long as it took to get from the door to the rug, and then being extremely careful about it afterwards, in the specific and practised way of somebody who had been being careful for a while.

"Cabin's warm," Thalia said, to the room, in the voice she used when she had decided the subject was over before anyone had opened it.

"You've got frost on your hat," Silena said.

"I've got frost on everything. There's frost on the *field*. There's frost on the targets, which is cheating, and I said so, to the targets."

"Did the targets apologise," Clarisse said.

"The targets said nothing, because targets can't talk, which is the only reason I let them keep the score." Thalia came to the table, and Annabeth moved her own knee out of the way without being asked, and Thalia dropped onto the bench beside her and put her feet up on the end of it — the left one flat, the right one tucked under — and reached across for the cup that Annabeth had been drinking out of and drank from it in exactly the same spot and did not mention doing it.

Outside, the sixth and seventh lamps came up the path. Inside, the stove ticked, and Silena's needle went in and out, and the winter list lay open on Clarisse's knee with three lines on it that would still be there in the morning, and the tenth lantern burned on the hook by the door the way it always burned, at the top of the hill, for anybody coming up.

Annabeth put her hand flat on the bench between herself and her sister, palm up, the way you put a thing down that you are not going to make anybody pick up.

After a second, Thalia put her hand in it.

"Good field?" Annabeth said.

"Good field," Thalia said. "Best one all year. I shot better than a boy from Apollo who claims to be sixteen and has a moustache he does not need."

"And?"

"And nothing. And he's got a bad draw on the release and everybody knows it." Thalia squeezed her hand once, hard, the way she did when she was telling somebody something she was not going to say, and then she let go and took the cup back and said, "Eggs. Tomorrow. I'm making them before training, and this time *nobody* gets to stand at the stove calling it a crime."

Percy came in carrying the last armful of wood, his hair full of cold air and his sleeves brushed with bark. He stopped at the sight of Thalia sitting with Annabeth and the cup still between them.

"Did I miss breakfast?" he asked.

"It's evening," Silena told him.

"That doesn't answer my question."

"Then you'll have to wait until morning."

He stacked the wood beside the stove and washed his hands before reaching for bread. Clarisse checked the winter list, where the ridge survey had been marked complete. The little note about Thalia's heel was still on the folded sheet beneath it. She had not asked the question yet.

Thalia rose to put the cup away and caught her heel on the edge of the bench. Her expression changed for a fraction of a second. Annabeth noticed, though she didn't move to help. Clarisse pretended to be occupied with the list until Thalia had her balance.

"Eight tomorrow?" Thalia asked.

"Eight for breakfast. Later for drills," Clarisse said. "And if you're making the eggs, I'm taking the pan away the moment they start sticking."

"You haven't seen my technique."

"That's the problem."

Thalia grinned despite herself. Annabeth carried the cup back to the shelf. Percy took the kettle to fill it, and Silena went to find the last clean towel. Their evening was busy enough without everybody turning to watch Thalia walk. Clarisse folded the notebook shut and decided she'd ask after the morning meal, when they weren't all around the same table.

At the door, Percy checked that the lantern's wick was drawing evenly. He lowered the flame a little, away from the glass, and came back in without announcing what he'd done.
