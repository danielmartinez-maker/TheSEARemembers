## Chapter 43: The Unsigned Note

The idea came to Annabeth while she was supposed to be polishing her armor.

The Zeus cabin was quiet in the morning light. Thalia snored in the bunk by the window with one arm flung over the edge. Annabeth sat on the floor with her breastplate in her lap and her sketchbook open beside her, pencil moving when it should have been still.

She had meant to draw the temple from her dreams, the one with broken columns and the sea behind it. Instead she kept thinking about sword drills that afternoon, and about Percy dreading them because Clarisse had promised to pair beginners against older kids. In the margin Annabeth drew a small wave without deciding to, then a trident, then a boy with messy black hair holding a sword clearly too big for him.

Her ears went warm. She stared at the doodle, then tore a scrap from the back of the sketchbook and wrote before courage could leave her.

*Good luck today. Stay on your toes and keep your shield up. You are better than you think. — A.*

Underneath she drew a tiny temple with three columns and an owl perched on top, a wave curling around the base. She folded the scrap twice, small and tight, and her heart beat fast as if she had done something daring.

At breakfast she barely tasted her eggs. Percy sat three tables away with the Poseidon kids, hair still damp from the showers, while Clarisse lectured him about footwork and Silena listened with patience. Percy caught Annabeth's eye and grinned and waved half a piece of toast at her. Annabeth looked down quickly so Thalia would not notice her smiling.

Through history lessons Annabeth planned where to leave it. Percy always left his practice sword in the rack by the arena before lunch to save his spot. The strap of his sheath had a loose fold. During the break between classes she slipped into the arena, smelling hot sand and oiled leather, found his sword third from the end where the wrapping frayed from his nervous picking, and tucked the folded paper deep into the strap. She stepped back to check it did not show and walked out fast, face burning.

---

Percy found it with a wooden sword halfway to his shoulder.

"Ow," Clarisse said, smacking his shield. "Pay attention. You hold it like that and I will knock you flat."

"Sorry," Percy said. He shifted the sheath on his arm and something white fluttered out and landed in the sand.

Clarisse stared. "What is that?"

"Nothing," Percy said too quickly, scooping it up. His ears went red to the tips. "Fell out of my pocket."

"You keep pocket stuff in your sheath now?" Clarisse squinted. She was thirteen and taller than most boys, hands on her hips.

"It is a strategy," Percy said. "Paper armor."

Silena laughed into her hand from by the water barrel. At fourteen she carried herself with calm grace, dark hair pulled back, quick to smooth trouble. "Leave him alone. Come show me that disarm again. You promised."

Clarisse allowed herself to be dragged off, still glancing back. Percy stepped behind the rack and unfolded the paper with clumsy fingers.

He read it three times. Fast, then slow, then mouthing each word while his grin grew until his cheeks hurt.

The little temple made his chest feel tight in a good way. The owl had huge eyes and a grumpy beak. The wave looked like it was hugging the temple to keep it safe. Percy traced the owl carefully so as not to smudge the graphite, glanced around, and pressed the paper flat against his chest for half a second before folding it into his pocket.

He kept his shield up the rest of drills. Clarisse knocked him down only twice, a personal record, and said his footwork was less terrible than yesterday. All afternoon he felt the note against his thigh. At water break he took it out just to check the owl was still there.

That night in the Poseidon cabin, with surf enchanted into the walls and the salt lamp glowing blue, Percy sat on his bunk and turned the note over in his hands. He wanted to give something back, but his temples always came out looking like birthday cakes and his owls looked like potatoes with eyes.

Silena glanced up from mending her armor strap. "You are staring at that pocket lint like it owes you money."

"It is not lint," Percy said, shoving it under his pillow. "It is private."

"Private lint," Silena said solemnly. "Does private lint have a name starting with A?"

Percy's face betrayed him. Silena laughed and threw a pillow at his head.

"I am your sister," she said. "You can tell me."

After a moment Percy let her read it. Silena studied the owl and her expression went warm.

"Oh, Barnacle," she said softly, using her old nickname for him. "That is sweet."

"Do not tell Clarisse," Percy said urgently. "She will never let me hear the end of it."

"I will not tell," Silena promised and handed it back. "Are you going to write back?"

Percy frowned. "I am bad at writing. And drawing. Remember the seagull poem?"

"The teacher read it aloud because she liked it," Silena said.

"She felt sorry for me."

"Percy." Silena leaned forward. "She did not send that to get a perfect poem. She sent it because she was thinking about you. Just think back at her on paper."

After lights-out, while Clarisse breathed deeply in the bunk above, Percy pulled out his flashlight and a pencil stub and a scrap torn from his monster guide. Tongue poking out with concentration, he wrote.

*Roses are red, the sea is blue-green, you draw really good, you are the smartest person I have ever seen. Good luck at archery tomorrow. Do not let the string hit your arm like last time. I saved you something shiny. — P.*

He winced at the rhyme and the grammar. Annabeth would definitely notice the grammar. But it was true. He folded the note around the flattest piece of green sea-glass he had found that week, color of shallow water over sand, and tied it with fishing line.

Before dawn he ran barefoot across wet grass to the Zeus cabin porch. The big doors were closed. He crouched by the third step where a loose stone wobbled and tucked the lumpy packet where wood met stone. He tapped the stone twice for luck and sprinted away before anyone emerged, heart pounding.

---

Annabeth found it during morning inspection.

She had been sent to sweep the porch to cover Thalia's chore after Thalia lost a bet with their younger brother Jason Grace, visiting from Camp Jupiter under Chiron's supervision and using Cabin One's guest bunk. Annabeth swept with more force than necessary and the broom knocked the loose stone. Something glinted.

Inside the folded paper tied with fishing line was sea-glass, smooth and frosted, green as bottle glass held to sunlight. And a poem.

Annabeth read it twice. First her eyebrows rose with every line. Second she pressed her knuckles to her mouth to keep from laughing aloud on the porch.

*Roses are red, the sea is blue-green.* She could picture him erasing so hard he tore the paper. He had underlined *really* three times, as if that fixed it.

It was the worst poem she had ever read. It was also the best thing anyone had ever given her.

She slipped the glass into her pocket. All through inspection and breakfast she kept her hand there, turning it over with her thumb. Cool and smooth. When Percy looked over from the Poseidon table she held his gaze and mouthed *thank you* behind her cup. Percy turned so red Silena had to thump his back and ask if he was choking.

After that the notes became a game with unspoken rules. Small. Folded tight. Hidden where only the other would look. Never signed except A and P. Nothing that would embarrass the other if found, though the thought of discovery made Annabeth's stomach drop.

Annabeth left a sketch of the arena with arrows showing foot placement. *You lasted six passes against Clarisse. SIX. I heard her tell Thalia.*

Percy left a white shell and a poem. *Owls are wise, the sky is wide, I hope today is a good day at least on the inside.* Annabeth puzzled an hour over that last line before deciding it meant he hoped she felt happy even if lessons were dull, which was so exactly Percy she had to sit down on the cabin steps.

Annabeth left a temple with waves for steps. *For when drills are boring. Imagine fighting on the steps while the tide comes in. You would win because fish would help you.*

Percy left four words. *You are my favorite.* Underneath, smaller, as if added after second thoughts. *friend to sit next to at lunch. And everywhere else.*

Annabeth kept every poem in the hollow of her mattress where stitching had loosened. The sea-glass and shell and a gray feather she lined on the inside ledge of her trunk. Green glass first. Always first.

Percy kept every sketch in his sock drawer under spare socks, smoothed flat, in order. He studied how Annabeth shaded under columns with three quick lines. Once he tried to copy the owl and gave up and drew a star beside it instead, because stars were safe.

Girlfriend and boyfriend were familiar words from the life they both remembered. At twelve again, saying them in front of the whole camp still made their faces warm. But at meals they saved glances over cup rims, and on walks to lessons their hands sometimes brushed and neither pulled away quickly, and when Percy finally disarmed an older Ares kid, the first person he searched for in the crowd was Annabeth, on her feet cheering so loudly Thalia yanked her down by her belt loop.

---

The notes stayed a private game until one of them fell into the wrong hands.

Milo found the folded square beneath a rack of practice shields, where the leather straps had caught it when somebody moved a crate. He brought it to Annabeth after drills, holding it by one dry corner.

"Is this yours?"

She saw the small wave drawn beside the letter A and felt her stomach tighten.

"Where was it?"

"By the equipment rack. I didn't read it. I thought it might be an instruction, but then I saw the little drawing."

Annabeth accepted it. The paper was damp with grit. Percy had meant to leave it inside a fold of her satchel, not where somebody carrying shields would tread on it. She recognized his handwriting through the crease.

"Thank you for bringing it," she said.

Milo looked relieved. "It's private, right?"

"Yes."

"That's fine. I have letters from my brother. My cabinmates aren't supposed to read them either."

He ran off before she could ask about his brother. Annabeth stayed beside the rack, feeling foolish for having assumed the only danger was that somebody might tease her. A lost note could become work for whoever had to put the equipment away, and not every person in camp wanted responsibility for somebody else's secrets.

She found Percy at the canoe dock, hauling a training paddle onto its shelf.

"Look what I found," she said.

He saw the folded paper and went red. "I put it in your bag."

"It fell out. Milo found it."

"Did he read it?"

"No. He gave it to me."

Percy let out a breath. "Then it got there."

"That's not the whole point."

She explained where the note had fallen. Percy listened, first embarrassed and then defensive, because he wanted the plan to be fun and she was making it sound like a problem with a storage system.

"You're the one who put the first one in my sword strap," he said.

"I know. That wasn't a great place either."

"So you want to stop?"

"No." She looked at him, surprised by the idea. "I want to choose places that don't interfere with other people's things."

He sat on the lower step. Annabeth joined him, leaving room for a passing camper to carry a canoe down the path.

"Can I ask something?" he said.

"Of course."

"Do you actually like the notes, or do you just like figuring out where I've hidden them?"

"Both. But mostly the notes."

Percy looked at her. He had been worrying that he was giving her something she'd feel obliged to answer, especially because the first one had come from her. The poems were terrible and he knew it. It would have been easier to hide them in unexpected places than to ask whether she wanted to keep receiving them.

"I don't need an answer every time," he said.

"Neither do I. Sometimes I've got training or a project and I won't write back for a day."

"That's fine."

"Then don't assume I'm upset."

He smiled. "You could just tell me you're busy."

"I do tell you."

"Right. I'll try listening."

She took out the damp square. His message was about the instructor who had called her Thalia twice during the morning lesson. He'd drawn an owl wearing a training helmet and written that nobody who confused the sisters ought to be allowed to mark their work.

"That's a terrible punishment," she said.

"I haven't even proposed my second punishment."

"Which is?"

"They have to wear the helmet."

She laughed. It was ridiculous, affectionate, and specific enough that she knew he had paid attention to the part of her day that had irritated her. That mattered more than the hiding place.

"Can we use the hollow oak by the lake?" she asked. "As long as it's dry and nobody's nesting in it?"

"Sure. And maybe the empty jar at Cabin Three. If Silena says we can."

"Good. No sword racks. No other people's bags without asking."

He nodded. Then he examined the little owl.

"You know," he said, "I could draw a better one."

"Don't you dare."

He grinned. Annabeth folded the paper and slipped it into her own pocket. The notes could stay theirs without requiring every person who happened to find a scrap of paper to join the game.

---

Ella found out because Ella noticed everything written down.

The harpy girl spent afternoons in the Big House attic library, where air smelled of cedar and old paper and windows were round like portholes. Small for her age, russet feathers puffing when excited, sharp eyes missing nothing, she shelved scrolls for Chiron and had taken a liking to Annabeth because Annabeth never mocked her repeating and always asked her view on architecture books.

Annabeth came to look up column orders for a model. At the long table with books open, she tore a corner from spare paper and scribbled. *Good spot. Loose brick by the forge, second from left. Look tomorrow. — A.* She tucked it into her sleeve.

Ella landed soundlessly on the table edge. "A secret. A twice-folded secret. 'Good luck today' and 'stay on your toes.'"

Annabeth froze. "Ella —"

"I saw the owl one," Ella said happily, tapping her beak. "Owl with grumpy beak. Percy showed Silena. Silena showed no one. Secrets in socks. Socks smell like feet."

Annabeth stared. "Percy told Silena?"

"Silena guessed," Ella said. "Silena guesses everything. Ella repeats everything. Ella will not repeat this. Twice-folded secrets Ella keeps."

Annabeth breathed out slowly. Denial rose first, then faded before Ella's bright earnest eyes.

"Promise not to tell?" Annabeth whispered. "Not Thalia. Not Chiron. Not anyone."

Ella drew a claw across her chest in an X. "Ella promises on the library. On all the books. On the porthole windows."

Annabeth let Ella read the owl poem from her pocket. Ella's feathers puffed until she looked twice her size.

"Oh," she breathed. "'At least on the inside.' Inside feelings are biggest feelings. Percy is a bad good poet. The best kind."

Annabeth laughed, relief stinging her eyes. "Do not tell him I said his bad poems are my favorite."

"Ella will not tell," Ella whispered, hopping closer though the attic was empty. "Ella can help. Ella knows hiding spots. Behind the *Iliad.* Under the third bench. Inside the feather box. No one looks except Ella."

Privacy tugged one way, practicality the other. A drop inside returned books would be invisible. Annabeth bit her lip, then nodded.

"Very sneaky, okay?"

Ella puffed with delight. "Ella is sneakiest. Sneakier than mice."

So the library drop began. Annabeth left notes on page eighty-four of the temple book Percy pretended to need for a project. Ella reshelved it on the low cart where Percy could reach it returning a knots book for Silena. Percy left shells tucked into the spine of the sea monsters book Annabeth always renewed.

Ella guarded fiercely. If anyone neared the temple book while it held a note, she swooped down insisting they needed a different temple book, a better book with more pictures, until they left confused. She kept the ledger in her head, reciting while dusting. "Temple book has the owl. Owl goes to Percy. Sea book has the shell. Shell goes to Annabeth."

---

The library arrangement worked for four days.

On the fifth, a younger Athena camper asked Ella for the temple book and Ella told him it had been borrowed. It hadn't. It was sitting three feet away on the return cart with a folded note between pages eighty-three and eighty-four.

"I saw it here yesterday," he said.

"It isn't available."

"Why not?"

Ella could think of five reasons that sounded convincing and none that were true. Annabeth had asked her not to tell anyone, and Ella had taken the request as a charge to guard the book from everybody who didn't know the secret. She liked having a job that depended on her, but the boy wasn't trying to pry into anyone's private life. He needed the book for class.

"Because," she said.

The boy stared. "Because what?"

Ella spread her wings over the cart. He stepped back, startled. That was when Chiron came down the stairs.

"Is there a problem?"

The boy explained he wanted the temple book. Ella looked toward the cart and felt a small, sharp twist of guilt. She had been so pleased to be useful to her friends that she'd stopped thinking about the reason the book belonged to the library in the first place.

Chiron waited for her to speak.

"The book is here," she said. "Ella doesn't want him to take it."

"Is there a preservation concern?"

"No."

"Then why is it unavailable?"

Ella could keep the promise not to reveal the note and still answer the question truthfully.

"There's something inside that shouldn't go with the book," she said. "A personal thing."

Chiron nodded and asked the other camper to give them a moment. He didn't ask Ella whose personal thing it was. Together they removed the folded square without opening it and placed it in a clean envelope behind the circulation desk.

"The book can be borrowed now," Chiron said. "The envelope can wait for its owner. But we can't keep public books from readers because somebody wants a private delivery service."

Ella looked at the floor. "Ella wanted to help."

"I know. Helping somebody sometimes means explaining what you can and cannot do."

He sent the book with the younger camper and showed Ella where the library's lost-property envelopes were kept. The system recorded where an object had been found without requiring staff to read private writing inside it.

Annabeth arrived that afternoon and found Ella sitting beneath the catalog table, sorting scraps of paper by size with more force than necessary.

"What happened?"

"Ella kept the temple book. Somebody wanted the temple book. Ella was wrong."

Annabeth knelt beside her. "You were trying to help me."

"Helped the wrong way."

"Then we change the way."

Ella looked up. "Are you angry?"

"At myself. I asked you to guard a hiding spot and forgot that you have work of your own here."

Ella considered that. She loved the library. The notes were nice, but the books were not hers to keep away from people, and she didn't want to spend every afternoon deciding which camper could approach a shelf.

"Can we use a box?" she asked. "A little box that doesn't belong to the books?"

Annabeth thought about it. A private mail slot by the reading room could be used by anyone who wanted to leave a harmless message for a friend. It would need permission and rules: no valuables, no personal information left exposed, no guarantee that an object would be safe if it was forgotten.

"We can ask the librarian," she said.

They did. Chiron approved a small correspondence tray beside the circulation desk, but insisted that the library staff would not deliver the notes or read them. Campers could place a folded message in a labeled envelope and collect it during open hours. Anything unclaimed would be returned to the sender if possible or handled through the ordinary lost-property procedure.

Ella insisted on helping write the notice. Annabeth insisted on making it short enough to read.

"Can you still help me sometimes?" Annabeth asked after they put up the sign.

"Sometimes. Ella wants to sort books other times."

"That's fair."

"And Ella won't promise a secret she can't keep. Ella forgot. Silena already knows, yes?"

"Percy told her about the first one. She knows we're writing."

Ella tapped a claw against the table. "Can Ella ask Silena about paper and envelopes? No private words. Just how to keep them dry."

Annabeth considered. She didn't want to involve another person without telling Percy. But Silena knew already, and good stationery would help prevent loose papers from falling out of shared equipment.

"Ask only about the envelopes," she said. "I'll tell Percy."

Ella nodded. That evening she asked Silena how to keep paper dry in a library without hiding it inside a book. Silena listened, guessed what was happening and offered to show Percy how to fold a note properly. She did not ask Ella to repeat anything she'd promised Annabeth to keep private.

The temple book went back into circulation. The correspondence tray acquired its first two envelopes before the week ended, one with a little owl and one with a wave. Other campers began using it too, leaving notes about borrowed pencils and meeting places for study groups.

Ella watched the tray with satisfaction. She had wanted to help two friends. She had ended up making something useful for everyone, and she could leave it alone long enough to finish cataloging the next shelf.

---

Silena confronted Percy a week later with wax on her fingers.

He returned from swimming lessons, hair dripping, to find her on his bunk with a wooden box open on her lap. Sticks of sealing wax in blue and white, a brass stamp with a wave pattern, twine, paper cut into neat squares.

"What is that?" Percy asked.

"A big sister intervention," Silena said, holding up a blue-tipped finger. "Sit."

Percy sat. Clarisse had evening patrol and would be gone an hour. Lucky.

"Ella told me about the correspondence tray," Silena said. "She knows I guessed you were writing notes, and she asked only about how to keep the paper dry. I am not mad. I am impressed you avoided Thalia this long. Thalia notices everything."

Percy swallowed. "Are you going to tell me to stop?"

"No." Silena looked surprised. "You leave her bad poems and she leaves you battle diagrams with vines shaped like hearts — do not argue, I saw them — and you think I want to stop that?"

"The vines were an accident," Percy muttered, ears burning.

"They were not," Silena said fondly. "I am your sister. I will tease you because I must, but I will also keep Clarisse from finding a poem rhyming *wide* with *inside*, and keep Thalia from thinking you are careless with Annabeth. So we do this right."

She pushed the box toward him.

"Mom used to seal letters with wax writing to Dad," Silena said quietly. "Before things got complicated. She taught me. Blue for Poseidon. Seal every note. If one falls out, no one reads it without breaking the seal, and you will know. And no more loose pockets. Ella says your spots are terrible."

"They are not terrible," Percy protested. "No one looked by the forge."

"Clarisse looks there first when she loses things," Silena said. "We need better."

While light outside went gold then violet, she taught him to fold edges with no tape, melt just enough wax, press straight down and lift straight up. His first three attempts blobbed. The fourth came out almost clean, blue circle with neat wave.

"Good," Silena said. "Annabeth likes neat letters. I have seen her worksheets."

"I know," Percy said, turning the seal over. "She draws owls when happy, columns when thinking. I can tell her day by the doodle."

Silena softened. "You pay attention, huh?"

Percy shrugged, embarrassed. "It is easy with her."

Silena ruffled his wet hair, leaving wax on his forehead. "Keep doing that." She sketched a quick map. "Hiding spots. Hollow oak by canoe lake — reach left, spider on right. Crack behind third amphitheater bench — lift, do not slide. Empty water jar in our storage — chipped, no one drinks from it. And library with Ella. Rotate. Never same spot twice."

Percy stared. "How do you know all these?"

"Big sisters know everything," Silena said, tapping his nose and leaving a blue dot. "Also I once hid notes myself."

"With who?" Percy asked, eyes wide.

"None of your business, Barnacle," Silena said. "Seal notes. Vary spots. Ask me for rhymes before *wide* and *inside* again."

"It was a good rhyme," Percy muttered, smiling until his face hurt. He hugged her tight when she opened her arms, smelling beeswax and salt. "Thank you."

"Anytime," Silena said. "Wash your face before Clarisse thinks you have a disease."

---

The new wax seals made Percy feel like he ought to write something worth sealing.

For the first three days he produced nothing at all. He'd sit on his bunk with a piece of white paper, think of Annabeth laughing at the amphitheater, and begin a sentence that looked absurd the moment he saw it. Every time he tried to rhyme, the line became something he wouldn't have said aloud. He crossed out more words than he kept.

Silena found the scraps when she came to retrieve a spool of thread from the sewing box.

"Having trouble?"

"No."

She pointed to the little heap beside his foot.

"That's a fairly convincing no."

He picked up a page before she could see it. "I've written poems before."

"I know."

"These are worse."

"Then don't write a poem."

Percy stared at her. "But that's our thing."

"Is it? She sent you a training diagram last week."

"With an owl."

"Then draw something. Or write one sentence. You don't have to perform a certain version of yourself just because you did it first."

He looked at his pencil. Reading ordinary English had never been as easy for him as it seemed for other campers. Ancient Greek made more sense in his head, its forms and sounds familiar even when he couldn't explain why. He'd learned ways to check his own spelling, but writing a little note that mattered to him felt harder than writing a page for history class. He couldn't blame the classroom's noise or his nerves about a quiz. This was something he wanted to do.

"Could you look at one?" he asked.

"Only if you want me to."

He handed her the least terrible page. The handwriting wandered down the margin. There were three crossed-out versions of the same sentence, followed by a drawing of a boat in a storm. Silena read slowly.

"'I liked that you waited for me when I messed up the tie-off at the dock.' That's good."

"It doesn't rhyme."

"So?"

"It sounds like a thank-you note."

"Maybe you wanted to say thank you."

Percy considered that. Annabeth had waited while he failed to tie a knot properly, even after the instructor had corrected him twice and his face had turned red. She hadn't done it because she thought he was impressive. She'd stayed because he asked her to practice with him. He was grateful in a way he hadn't managed to say.

"I can send that?"

"Of course."

He looked suspicious. "You're going to make a joke about it."

"Probably not to Annabeth."

"Silena."

She laughed and sat beside him. "I won't repeat the words to anyone else. If you want spelling help, I'll help with spelling. The note's yours."

He drew a line through the abandoned rhymes and copied the sentence onto a clean square. Then he added *I was embarrassed, and you didn't make a thing of it*. It took him several attempts to fit the words without crowding them. Silena showed him how to fold the paper so the writing wouldn't rub against the wax.

When the seal cooled, he held it up to the light. The blue circle wasn't centered, but the wave had come out clearly.

"You did it," she said.

He grinned. "I did."

Clarisse came through the door a moment later carrying a damaged spear strap. Percy tucked the note into his pocket. She looked from him to the sealing box.

"Why does it smell like a candle shop?"

"Because Silena's teaching me a craft."

Clarisse examined the strap in her hand. "Good. Then maybe you can fix this."

Percy took it and saw that one of the old loops was splitting. He knew enough from the forge to recognize it needed an inspection before somebody used it again. He told her so. Clarisse grumbled, but put the strap aside to take to the armory.

Silena watched them. It pleased her to see Percy ask for help with words and then offer help with a practical problem without pretending either skill made him better than his sister.

When Clarisse left, Percy unfolded the note one last time to check the ink hadn't smudged. It was still there, plain and direct.

"She might think it's boring," he said.

"Then she'll tell you what she thinks."

"She doesn't lie to me."

"No."

He smiled at the page. He liked that about Annabeth even when she corrected his maps and argued with him about the most efficient way to stack paddles. He put the note into a clean envelope and marked the outside with a small P.

At the library the next morning, he saw that the correspondence tray was open. Ella was working at the catalog table and barely looked up when he placed the envelope inside.

"Good morning, Percy."

"Morning."

"Library notes go in the tray. Books stay in books."

"Yes, ma'am."

Ella smiled. She'd learned the rule well enough not to repeat it every time somebody came in. Percy went to class feeling nervous about whether Annabeth would like the note, and relieved that he no longer had to make every word into a rhyme.

---

For a while it worked beautifully.

Annabeth found blue-sealed squares in the oak hollow and opened them to worse and more treasured poems, plus treasures. A spiral shell. A skipping stone with white stripe. Driftwood shaped vaguely like a horse.

Percy found white-sealed squares — Silena had sent Annabeth white wax through Ella, with an owl stamp Ella found in a drawer — in the jar or behind the bench. Inside were sketches. The Parthenon with seagulls on columns. The arena with a stick-figure Percy finally holding his shield right. A lighthouse with waves and two tiny figures waving from the top.

Silena restocked paper without being asked and suggested slightly less terrible rhymes. Without telling them, she also left an extra kitchen cookie in hiding spots on hard days, wrapped in a napkin with no note. Annabeth assumed Percy left them. Percy assumed Annabeth did. Silena never told.

Annabeth felt braver with secrets tucked into her day like beams holding a roof. Drills were less daunting with a square waiting in the oak. Taxonomy was easier with Percy's tiny trident copied into her notes. She stood straighter. She laughed faster. Even Thalia noticed, though she misread it.

"You have been cheerful," Thalia said one evening, watching Annabeth mend a sandal strap. "Is it Jason visiting? You talk formations an hour a day."

Annabeth's needle slipped. "Ow. No. I love having our brother here. But it's not just —"

"Good," Thalia said. "I love him, but if all this cheerfulness comes from testudo lectures over dinner, I need to borrow his notes."

Guilt prickled. Thalia had told her about carrying her hungry little sister from under a bridge until her feet healed, braiding her hair after nightmares, never telling when she cried. Thalia was not just her sister. She had kept her alive.

But the notes felt too tender to expose, like seedlings that die uncovered too soon. Annabeth told herself she would explain eventually. For now she tucked poems deeper and said nothing, trying to ignore the ache of secrecy.

Percy felt less conflict because Silena knew but Clarisse did not, and Clarisse knowing was a nightmare he refused to picture. She had once read his dream journal aloud at breakfast. If she found *You are my favorite friend to sit next to*, he would never hear the end of it.

So he sealed and rotated spots and followed Silena's rules. For three weeks it held.

Until rain and laundry broke it.

---

It poured all afternoon, hard gray March rain turning paths to mud and canceling sailing. Stuck indoors, Thalia declared cleaning day in the Zeus cabin because it smelled like wet socks and old lightning. No one argued. Thalia with a broom was more dangerous than with a spear.

Annabeth refolded blankets in the storage trunk while Thalia swept under bunks. Under her own stack, where she had hidden nothing, lay a small blue-sealed square she had never seen. Percy must have slipped it in during the morning rush when doors stood open, risking everything because rain closed the oak and bench and library. Bold. Reckless. Very Percy.

She palmed it fast, but Thalia looked up at the wrong moment.

"What is that?" Thalia asked.

"Nothing," Annabeth said too fast. "A bookmark. Ella gave —"

"Annabeth." Thalia set the broom aside and held out her hand with older-sister authority. "Let me see."

Rain hammered the roof. Thunder grumbled far away. Annabeth placed the square in Thalia's palm, throat tight.

Thalia turned it over. Blue wax. Wave stamp. Outside in small writing, *A — rain backup — P.* Her eyebrows drew together.

"This is from Percy," she said. Not a question.

Annabeth nodded. "Do not open it. Please."

Thalia studied her, storm-gray eyes steady. Then she crossed to Annabeth's bunk and tugged the mattress where stitching loosened.

A corner of white paper showed.

"Thalia —" Annabeth lunged, but Thalia had seen the folded squares packed tight inside, blue and white seals alternating. She looked at the open trunk where sea-glass and shells and a feather stood in a careful line. Green glass first.

Thalia sat slowly on the bunk's edge.

"How long?" she asked quietly.

Annabeth stood with hands clenched, tears pricking hot. Not fear exactly. Fear of Thalia angry, forbidding, confronting Percy with a spear.

"A while," Annabeth whispered. "Weeks. Since drills. I started it. It is not his fault."

Thalia reached — asking with her eyes, Annabeth nodding — and drew one poem from the mattress. The owl one. *Owls are wise, the sky is wide.* Her mouth twitched. She glanced at the trunk ledge.

"He gives you beach trash," Thalia said.

"Not trash," Annabeth said quickly, flushing. "He finds smooth ones. Perfect ones. He looks a long time."

Thalia held green glass to gray light until it glowed. She set it back exactly where it had been.

"You give him drawings," she said.

Annabeth nodded. "Temples. Notes for luck. Clarisse is hard on him."

"I know she is," Thalia said. Quiet followed, rain filling it. "Look at me."

Annabeth looked. Sixteen to twelve felt vast just then.

"Why did you not tell me?" Thalia asked, hurt and puzzled rather than angry. "You were seven when I carried you away from that bridge. You had not eaten in two days and still tried to give me half your fries. I know you can't remember it now."

Annabeth nodded, tears spilling. "You've told me. You carried me."

"Yeah." Thalia's voice roughened. "Then this boy with sea-green eyes looked at you like you hung the stars, and you looked back same way, and I thought if he hurt you I could not fix it. So I kept him off. Kept you close. I called it protecting."

"You were," Annabeth whispered. "You did."

Thalia shook her head, turning the sealed note over without breaking it. "Can I read this one? Just this. I will not touch the rest. Promise."

Annabeth hesitated, then nodded, hands trembling.

Thalia broke the seal with her thumbnail and unfolded Percy's messy earnest writing, letters pressed so hard they dented.

*It is raining so we cannot sail. I am glad because I am bad at sailing when windy but do not tell Clarisse. I hope thunder is not too loud in your cabin. My sisters say Zeus cabins are loudest. If you get scared count seconds like you taught me. 1-2-3-4-5. I counted to 47 yesterday. Ella says you have a test tomorrow. Good luck. You study harder than anyone. P.S. I tried to draw an owl but it looked like a potato so I did not include it. P.P.S. Silena helped me spell thunder.*

Annabeth laughed through tears at the potato owl. Thalia read the postscript twice, surprise giving way to reluctant amusement, then something softer.

"He counts," Thalia said quietly.

"You taught him to count between lightning and thunder," she continued, "and he still does. He remembers." She folded the note along old creases. "He spelled thunder right."

"Silena helped," Annabeth said, smiling watery.

"Silena helps everyone." Thalia looked at treasures and packed mattress. "How many?"

"Eleven from him," Annabeth said. "Nine from me. Plus cookies neither admits to but both eat."

"Cookies?" Thalia raised an eyebrow.

"Silena leaves them," Annabeth admitted. "She and Ella help. Wax seals. Spots. Ella guards the library."

Thalia exhaled almost a laugh. "Whole camp in on this except me and Clarisse."

"I am sorry," Annabeth said. "You are my sister. I should have told you first."

Thalia set the note down and opened her arms. Annabeth climbed into her lap though almost too big, as Thalia said she used to do when storms terrified her. Thalia wrapped around her, chin on her head, holding tight while rain drummed and thunder rolled over the Sound.

"I am not mad," Thalia murmured. "Eleven notes. Sea-glass. Potato owls. You kept every one."

"He keeps mine too," Annabeth mumbled. "In his sock drawer. In order. Ella told me."

Thalia huffed a laugh. "Of course." She rocked, bunk creaking. "Do you like him? I know you like him. But are you happy?"

Annabeth pulled back to look at her, blotchy and bright-eyed. She thought of glass in her pocket, seals in oak hollows, Percy searching for her first after a disarm, his crooked nervous grin.

"Yes," she said simply. "He is my favorite person to sit next to. Everywhere."

Something eased between Thalia's brows, a line always there when Percy came up. "Then I am happy too," she said. "Even if he is a sea boy with terrible rhymes."

"He tries hard," Annabeth said loyally.

"I know." Thalia brushed hair back. "I have watched. He carries your bag when no one looks. Saves you cornbread crust. Counted to forty-seven." Her voice caught. "That is not nothing."

"You noticed?" Annabeth stared.

"I notice everything about you," Thalia said. "That was the problem. I noticed so hard I missed you growing enough for secrets that are not bad. Just yours." She pressed Percy's note into Annabeth's hands. "Answer it. He will worry it lost in rain."

Annabeth clutched it. "You will not tell Chiron? Forbid it?"

"Forbid what? Notes? Sea-glass?" Thalia raised eyebrows. "I am your sister, not your warden. You pass poems through a harpy. If I forbade that Ella would repeat it forever." Serious again. "If he makes you sad, if teasing stops being fun, tell me first. Not Silena. Not Ella. Me. Promise?"

"Promise," Annabeth whispered. "Will you stay a little grumpy so Clarisse does not suspect?"

Thalia laughed, startled and bright. "I can manage cover grumpiness." She went to her trunk and pulled spare gray ribbon, storm-colored, frayed ends. She tied it around Percy's note in a bow. "Give this back with your answer. Tell him if he keeps making potato owls, he must show me one. So I can tease him properly. As a sister."

Annabeth widened eyes. "You are okay with him?"

"Getting there," Thalia said honestly, wiping a tear track with her thumb. "He leaves good-luck notes for weeks and counts thunder as you taught him. I can admit I misjudged a kid. Even a Poseidon kid."

---

Percy got his answer two days later after rain cleared.

Silena checked the water jar from habit on the way to breakfast and came running back barefoot, shaking Percy awake where he had slept through the conch.

"Barnacle. Up. Water jar paid out."

Percy sat upright, hair sticking up. "Give it —"

Silena held the white-sealed square just out of reach, eyes dancing. "Password?"

"Silena!"

She handed it over and perched on his bunk, chin in hands. "Open nicely."

Percy broke the owl seal with care. Inside, temple doodled across top and a kind-beaked owl instead of grumpy, in Annabeth's neat hand.

*Thank you for the rain note. I counted too. Only to 23, so our cabin IS louder. You spelled thunder right! Tell Silena thanks for cookies (I know). I told Thalia. She found your note cleaning. Do not worry!! She is not mad. She tied the gray ribbon. She says your owls look like potatoes but wants to see one. That means she likes you. (Do not tell her I said that.) Good luck canoeing. Do not tip like last time. — A.*

Tied with gray ribbon was a flat white stone with gray stripe and a sketch of two tiny figures atop a lighthouse, one dark-spiky and one blond-curly, holding a flag saying *47*.

Percy stared until it blurred.

"She told Thalia," he whispered, horrified. "Thalia will kill me."

"Read ribbon part again," Silena said gently.

"Potato owl inspection. Likes me." Percy looked up torn between hope and terror. "Trap? Thalia sets traps. She put salt in my drink once."

"That was Clarisse," Silena said. "Thalia does not tie ribbons on traps."

Percy clutched the note. "Must draw potato owl now. Before nerve fails."

All free period he bent over the bunk, tongue out. First looked like a muffin. Second like angry Clarisse. Third — lopsided eyes, lumpy body, tiny wings — perfect. He labeled it *CERTIFIED POTATO OWL — FOR THALIA'S INSPECTION* and sealed with extra wax.

Ella delivered it herself to the Zeus porch, swooping with a cry of "Delivery! Potato with wings!" that scattered two Apollo kids.

Thalia opened the door.

Ella froze mid-flutter. "Wrong sister! Ella meant small blond one —"

Thalia held out her hand, eyebrow raised, silver bracelets glinting. "Let me see the potato."

Ella surrendered it with shaking claws and fled to the roof to watch, chanting nervously, "Potato owl, Thalia has potato owl."

Thalia broke the seal on the porch and studied the lumpy owl while Annabeth crept behind, twisting hands.

Then Thalia laughed. Loud, surprised, head thrown back, ringing off white walls. She laughed until eyes watered, until Annabeth laughed from relief, until Ella cackled *Potato! Potato!* and brought Jason to the window.

"It IS a potato," Thalia gasped, holding it out. "He labeled wings. *Wings (probably).* Look at its face. So ugly it is cute."

Annabeth beamed. "I told you."

Thalia wiped eyes and looked downhill toward the canoe lake, where a dark-haired figure hovered by the oak pretending not to watch while Silena pretended not to push him. Thalia lifted the owl high so Percy could see and gave solemn thumbs-up.

Even from the porch Annabeth saw his grin. He waved both arms like a shipwreck survivor. Silena waved too and mouthed *thank you.*

Thalia lowered the drawing, smile quieting, and handed it back. "Tell him he takes good care of what matters to you. And I see it."

"I will," Annabeth said, hugging it.

That night Annabeth set the *47* sketch on the ledge with sea-glass and tucked gray ribbon into the mattress with poems. Percy smoothed Annabeth's lighthouse into the sock drawer in order, tying the stack with gray ribbon because Silena said important documents should be tied.

Ella updated her ledger while shelving. "Potato delivered. Thalia laughed. Thalia likes Percy a little. Good little. All secrets safe."

Silena melted fresh wax and cut fresh squares, humming. When Clarisse asked what she was doing, Silena said, "Armor maintenance," and Clarisse believed her, because big sisters can lie when love is on the line.

On the Zeus porch after lights-out, Thalia sat knees up watching clouds gather over the sea, thinking of counting games and green glass and a boy who spelled thunder right on second try. She had once carried her little sister. Now someone else carried small pieces — notes, luck, striped stones — gently.

Sharing the carrying was not so bad, she decided. Just a little. For potato owls and temple doodles and good luck todays.

Thunder rumbled distant. Thalia counted silently as Annabeth taught them both — *1-2-3-4-5* — and smiled into dark when she lost count past twenty, because some things were worth staying awake for.
