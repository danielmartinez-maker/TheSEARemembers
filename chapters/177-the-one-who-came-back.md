## Chapter 177: The One Who Came Back

Thalia Grace had two letters and a problem, and she had been carrying both of them around since Thursday, and by Friday morning the problem had gotten heavier than the letters.

The pouch had come up the hill while she was standing on the Cabin One porch with her mug, and the Hermes kid had read the names out in his carrying voice while he worked the line, and one of the two letters in the stack for the Grace cabin was in her mother's hand and the other was in block capitals that leaned like a fence and did not have a return address, and she had taken both of them into the cabin and put them on the table and looked at them for a while.

Beryl Grace's letter she read that night, standing up, and it was good news and it was long and there were hearts over the i's and a sentence she had forgotten to finish, and it said she had a key to the diner now and that she had gone three weeks without counting and that she had thought about them every single day, and it cost Thalia almost nothing because she had learned over four years how to hold her mother's letters at exactly arm's length, which is a skill, and which she was good at.

The other one she had not read at all.

She was not sure yet why. It was from Luke — Luke Castellan, of the Hermes cabin, who sent her one letter a season like a man paying a subscription, and who had been at camp for a week in July and had been on a quest of his own since the middle of August and had written to her once from a bus station in Montana about a monster and an ice cream. That was not the problem. The problem was the postmark, which was local, and the date, which was Tuesday, which meant that he had been about a day's walk away from this camp on Tuesday and had not come.

*She's doing a whole face*, said a voice in her head that belonged to her younger sister.

Thalia put the unread letter in the box with the others and went down to breakfast, and did not think about it, deliberately and at length, for most of the morning.

---

The camp on Friday morning had the specific brightness of a hill that has been cleaned by weather.

The storms were gone. The air was so clear that the ridge stood out with every individual pine on it drawn in, and the lake, closed and quiet, lay at the bottom of the slope like something under glass, and there were flat sheets of ice in the low paths that had not melted at nine, which was early for ice and late for everybody to stop talking about it. Half the camp was up at the climbing wall putting the rope ladder back on its pegs. Somebody had dragged the fallen pine out of the cedar brake and left it on the path, which the wood crew would be at all afternoon. The pavilion was doing something complicated with an awning.

Thalia did not work. She had been on the boundary survey through Tuesday and had Saturday coming, and Chiron had told her on Thursday, in a sentence with no room in it, to take Friday off, and Chiron's sentences with no room in them were the ones you did not argue with.

So she ate breakfast, and did a slow circuit of the camp, and spent an hour on the archery range by herself, which was the thing she did when she had a letter in a box that she was not reading.

The range in October was the best range. The field had gone brown and hard, the wind came across it off the ridge in cold flat sheets, and the targets at twenty paces had gone pale with weather, and there was nobody there before nine, which meant the whole thing was hers, which meant she could take it seriously without being seen taking it seriously.

She shot for fifty minutes. Short draw, long draw, wind off the left shoulder and wind off the right. She was good -- she had been good since she was fourteen, and better since the spring, when she had stopped trying to be the best in the camp and had started trying to be the one who did not miss -- and by the time she quit she had put thirty-two arrows into a four-inch group and had ruined one arrow badly by trying to rush the wind, and she knew exactly which shot it had been and why, which was the entire point of practicing alone.

She was pulling her arrows out of the straw when footsteps came up the field behind her, and she knew from the length of the stride and the fact that a person was walking on the field at all that it was not a child, and then she heard the voice.

"You've dropped your elbow."

Thalia did not turn around for about four seconds longer than a friendly person would have. Then she did, with three arrows in her fist, unhurried.

Rhys Calder was standing at the edge of the shooting line with his bow unstrung in one hand and a quiver over one shoulder and a canvas coat that had been new in another state, and he was, as advertised, taller. He had been long and thin at seventeen and had gone to broad at nineteen in the way that people from the forge-adjacent cabins went broad, and his hair was shorter, and he was wearing a camp shirt that had been washed so many times it was basically a rumor of orange, which meant he had been back long enough to have gone up to his own cabin and dug through his own box.

"Calder," Thalia said.

"Grace."

"You're back."

"Yesterday's news. It's Friday." He looked at the group in the straw, thirty-two arrows in a fist-sized spread, and at the one that had gone off into the edge of the target and hung there, punished. "Whose idea was the rush on the third from the end?"

"Mine."

"It was a bad one."

"It was a very bad one, and I know it, and I've been standing here thinking about it."

"Good," Calder said, and put his bow down on the line, and sat on the grass at the edge of the field with his back against the target butt and his knees up, like a person who had walked a long way and intended to sit for a while, and did not say anything else at all.

Which was, in Thalia's experience, the single most disarming thing anybody at this camp had ever done to her, and she stood there with three arrows in her hand and absolutely no idea what to do with the next minute of her life.

"You're supposed to ask me about Maine," Calder said, eventually.

"I'm not going to ask you about Maine."

"I know."

"Then why are you sitting on my range."

"Because it's the only range."

Thalia put the arrows back in her quiver, which took longer than it should have, and then, because standing up was worse than sitting down, she sat down on the grass eight feet from him with her back against the same butt and her bow across her knees.

"For the record," she said, "I heard you took the archery board two summers running and left it up a month so everyone had to look at it."

"I did do that."

"That's a jerk move."

"It's an excellent move," Calder said. "Half the kids on this hill got better that month, because they had a number in front of them. The other half got angrier, which is also better. If you're going to be the best at something in a place where nobody can leave, you may as well be *useful* about it."

"And you were the best at it."

"I was. I'm not." He said it without any drama at all, like a man reading a gauge. "I shot all year at school. There's a club in the town, indoors, thirty meters, fluorescent light, everybody's got the same bow and a coach with a whistle. I went every Tuesday. My form got better and my eye got worse, and I came back and shot the board on Thursday and I'm third."

"Third."

"Third." He tipped his head back against the straw. "There's a thirteen-year-old in the Apollo cabin who shoots like a metronome and has never once been coached, and she's going to take the board in the spring and hold it for four years, and I've known it for two days and I've decided to be fine about it."

Thalia looked at him sideways. "That's a lie."

"It's a lie," Calder agreed. "It's going to take a week. Come and shoot."

"Now?"

"Now. Twenty paces, five arrows, and I'll tell you about your elbow, and you can tell me the truth about the north ridge, because everybody on this hill has been saying the word *cairn* to me since breakfast and none of them can tell me where it is."

So they shot.

It was the best hour Thalia had spent in months, which she would have denied under oath. Calder did not flirt and did not perform and did not, as far as she could tell, think about her being a girl for a single second of it: he thought about arrow spine and wind and how her grip sat, and he corrected her twice with the flat, abashed honesty of a person who could not help seeing the error, and the second time he took her bow hand in both of his and turned it a quarter inch at the wrist like a man adjusting a machine, and did not notice that he had done it, and Thalia Grace stood in the middle of a brown field in October, being corrected, and found that she had absolutely nothing to say.

She also found, by the fifth arrow, that she had been dropping her elbow for four years and that fixing it felt like putting down a bag.

"Where'd you get that," she said, on the sixth arrow, which was two inches into the middle.

"Years of being corrected by people who didn't like me."

"That's a whole method."

"It's the only method," Calder said. "Kind coaching makes you comfortable. Unkind coaching makes you accurate. I got accurate, and then I got old enough to be glad about it, and this spring I went back and thanked a man who used to shout at me, and he didn't remember me at all." He put his own arrow into the target without any visible effort, which was somehow not a performance and was worse for it. "That's the other thing about coming back. Everybody's been living. You spend a year away and you come home and find that the whole hill kept going, and half of it changed into something better, and nobody saved a place for you and nobody should have. You just get to walk back in and pick up the bow."

Thalia looked at the target for a long moment.

"You chose to come back," she said.

"I chose to come back."

"Kids leave every year and don't."

"They do," Calder said. "Most of them. And I don't blame a single one of them, because this hill is a hard place to be sixteen and they've all got somewhere." He got up and slung the quiver over his shoulder and started walking up the field to pull the arrows, and said, over his shoulder, without any weight on it at all, "But I don't have anywhere else. I've got a couple who don't write and a school that would take me back in a heartbeat and a bench at the forge and nine lanterns to hang, and when the year was up I sat at a bus station and did the arithmetic and it came out *here*. So. Third place. I'll take it."

He pulled the arrows out of the target one at a time with the flat careful motion of a person who did that a lot, and Thalia stood on the shooting line with her bow in her hand and her elbow fixed and a letter in a box on her bed that had a local postmark and no explanation in it, and did not say the sentence that was standing in her mouth, which was: *there is a bench here with your name on it and I have watched a man choose a bus station over this hill and I did not know until this minute that it was a choice.*

What she said was, "You're still a jerk about the board."

"Absolutely," Calder said. "Saturday?"

"Saturday I'm on the ridge with the cartographer and a rod."

"Sunday, then. Same time. Bring the elbow."

"Sunday," Thalia said, and did not think about what she had just agreed to for one entire hour, which was the best she had done all week, and went up the hill for lunch with her bow on her shoulder and her hat in her hand and the cold October light lying flat on the whole field behind her.

Annabeth Grace filed the route on Friday afternoon, because a filed route was the rule and because Annabeth had never in her life needed a rule to write a plan down.

The Big House parlor at two o'clock had the low autumn sun coming through the west screens and lying across the map wall in long bars, and she stood in front of the blank under the ridge line with a pencil and did the last piece of work she could do about Saturday without being on the ridge.

NORTH SPRING — SURVEY PENDING had been up there since Sunday. Under it, in the small brackets, the three words she had printed herself: (THALIA — SLAB. SEEP. CAIRN, BLUE RAG.) And now, in a fresh strip of small letters along the bottom, the route.

GATE — EAST FENCE — THE LONG TERRACE — SPRING GULLY — RIDGE LINE — SLAB.

5 HOURS UP. 2 HOURS ON. 3 HOURS DOWN. BACK BY DARK.

CREW: GRACE A. (BOOK). GRACE T. (ROPE, GROUND). NAKAMURA O. (DRAWING). BECKER M. (ROD). JACKSON P. (LOAD, ENTHUSIASM).

She read it twice, the way she read everything she made, checking for the flaw, and the flaw was that it said *back by dark* and did not say what to do about a seep that was running too strong to wade, and the answer was that they would not wade it, they would measure it from the bank and put a question mark on the sheet and come back in the spring, and she had written that in a note and put the note in the bag and that was the whole of her preparation for being wrong, which was more preparation than most people ever did.

Chiron read it at the long table with his glasses on and the lamp lit, and asked two questions, which were: whether Olive had boots that would survive six hours on wet granite, and whether there was any point in telling Annabeth not to go if the wind came up over forty.

"Her boots are fine, and she's ten, so I've packed a second pair of socks for her and a third for Marcus because he won't bring one," Annabeth said. "And no, there's no point, but I'll take the rope in the bag instead of on the harness and we'll turn at the terrace if the wind's over forty, and I'll write that on the sheet so it's my decision and not yours, because it's my survey."

"It is your survey," Chiron agreed, and wrote something small in the corner of the route, and handed it back, and the thing he had written was not an amendment, it was a date and the words *crew briefed, proceed*, which meant that as of two-forty on Friday afternoon her Saturday was official, which was a feeling Annabeth had no intention of describing to anybody.

The bag was already by the door. She had packed it at noon and unpacked it once and packed it again, because that was who she was and everybody in her family had stopped arguing with it. Two layers. The rope, coiled twice, on the bottom. The field book and the fair sheets in the oilcloth. The pencil, and the second pencil. Rations that Silena had made without being asked: bread, cheese, cold chicken in a wrapper, four apples, and a paper twist of honey cake that had a note on it that said EAT THE CAKE FIRST, SIGNS ADD NOTHING TO MORALE, which was the most Silena sentence ever written.

Thalia came up the steps at half past three with her bow still on her shoulder and her coat open and her hair full of the field, and threw herself down on the other end of the long table with the particular whump of somebody who had been standing up for four hours out of spite, and ate a cheese sandwich that was not hers.

"You're in a mood," Annabeth said.

"I'm in a mood that has a shape. There's a difference."

"What's the shape."

Thalia chewed, considering, and then told her, in the detail she always used when she was working something out: the range, the wind, the group of thirty-two arrows, the one bad shot, and then, flatly and without decoration, a man sitting down on the grass and correcting her elbow and being right and then telling her the whole truth about third place without being asked.

"He's not charming," Thalia said. "That's the part I can't get anywhere. He isn't charming, he doesn't try to be, and I've spent four years being charmed by every single person who ever wanted something from me, and this one sat in my field and told me my form was wrong and then told me why he came back, and I swear to you, Annie, it was the single most relaxing hour I've had since August and I have no idea what to do with that."

Annabeth did not say anything for a moment, which took enormous discipline, and then she said, in exactly the tone she read numbers in, "Sunday."

"Don't."

"You said Sunday. On the field. To him. You've been here twenty minutes and you've said the word Sunday twice."

"I'm the one who did it, I know what I did," Thalia said, and put the rest of the sandwich in her mouth and chewed it with enormous dignity, and stood up, and got as far as the door before she stopped and said, without turning around, "If you tell anybody at this camp what my face is doing right now, I'll tell everybody about the seam you drew at the cairn on the ridge and how you can't do an axe."

"I did the seam at the cairn in one pass."

"I know. That's why you'll be sorry," Thalia said, and went out, and the screen door banged, and Annabeth sat with her route sheet and her pencil and her unread second pencil and let her face do whatever it wanted for a while, because nobody was in the room.

The sun came round the west corner and went out of the parlor at about four. Annabeth was standing at the map wall with her hands on her hips, doing the last thing she could usefully do, which was nothing, when the porch boards gave the particular rhythm of somebody coming up them without any urgency at all.

"You're early," she said, before he got to the door.

"I'm right on time and you know it," Percy said, coming in with his sleeves rolled and graphite on his hands from something and a folded square of paper held between two fingers like a card. "The last of the lamp work is done. Beckendorf came down at lunch with a lantern for our porch, which Clarisse has already hung and will deny hanging, and the west path is lit end to end, and Chiron said Saturday's a hundred percent, and I've been to the ridge twice with you in my life and both times you let me carry the heavy thing, so I came to tell you I've got the bag."

"The bag's packed."

"Then I've got the second bag. There's always a second bag. You've got the rope and the fair sheets, and I've got four apples you don't know about and a spare shirt for Olive, because I remember that she loses one jacket a week."

Annabeth looked at him, and then at the folded paper in his hand, which was not folded along the creases he always used, which meant it was not a note he had written.

"What's that."

"This," Percy said, and held it out, "is your field book. You left it on the table by the door in the Zeus cabin this morning, and your sister found it and brought it down and told me to give it to you, and she did not open the back cover, because she stood there in the parlor and held it up and told me in front of Chiron that the back cover of a cartographer's field book is a *personal instrument* and that she would defend it with her life, and then she handed it to me and said *tell her I said nothing*, and left."

Annabeth took the field book. She held it flat in both hands and looked at the back cover, and then she looked at Percy, whose face at that moment was doing the thing she most liked about him, which was to be entirely honest and entirely quiet at the same time.

"She said nothing," she said.

"She said nothing."

"Then I'm not going to say anything either."

"That's the system," Percy said. "It's a great system. It's got everything anybody needs in it except the part where anybody ever tells anybody anything."

"Nobody who lives on this hill survived by telling anybody anything," Annabeth said, and put the field book in the bag and did up the buckle, and stood there with her hand on the buckle for a moment and the last of the light on the wall behind her.

Then she crossed the parlor, which was four steps, and took hold of the front of his shirt the way she had in the canoe shed on Monday, and kissed him. It was not a big one and it was not a small one; it was two people in a cold parlor at the end of the last working week of the season, kissing each other with their whole attention and no clock in it, and it lasted long enough that the lamp on the table made a noise as it cooled and neither of them looked at it.

When she stepped back she did not go far. She put her forehead on his collarbone and stayed there, and his hands came up and stayed where they landed, one flat on her back and one in her hair, and the whole parlor was quiet except for the clock in the hall and the wind finding the corner of the house.

"Tomorrow," she said, into his shirt.

"Tomorrow."

"Seven at the gate. Two layers, and the rope in the bag, and no guessing, and if the wind's over forty we turn at the terrace."

"Seven at the gate," Percy said. "I'll bring the cake."

"Silena's cake?"

"Silena's cake. She gave me the paper twist at lunch and told me not to tell you, so I'm telling you."

Annabeth laughed, once, quietly, into his shoulder, and then straightened up and picked up the bag and put it back by the door, and went to find two more pairs of socks, because it was Friday night, because tomorrow there was a ridge, and because she was not going to be the one on that hill who had to borrow.

The forge on a Friday afternoon was the best-lit place in the camp, and Silena Beauregard had never said that out loud to anybody, and she had known it since July.

There was no sun in the shop at four o'clock and no need for any. The doors stood open on the cold, and the fire at the back did the whole room in one long orange hand, and everything inside it -- the benches, the hooks, the bar stock, the boys -- was the same color, which was the color of a thing that has been taken seriously. It smelled of iron and ash and cut oil and somebody's boiled lunch, and it was eight degrees warmer than the path, and she stopped in the doorway for a second taking the hit of it like weather.

"Beauregard," Beckendorf said, from the bench at the back, without turning around, because he had heard her boots, which he had done for a week now and had not once commented on.

"Glove." She put the second glove on the end of the bench, in the spot she had learned for the bread in July and had used for the glove on Wednesday and was going to use for whatever came next. "I did the thumb and the seam. You'd already done the palm."

He picked it up and looked at it with the flat cataloguing look he gave to material, and turned it over twice, and did the thing he had done on Tuesday, which was to identify her own work back to her like it was stock. "Left hand. You've done the thumb twice as thick at the base, which is where a hammer tears it out, and you've used the grey. That's the third thing of mine you've put grey flannel into."

"It's what I had."

"It's what you had three times," Beckendorf said, and put the glove on, and flexed it, and closed his hand into a fist, and did not say thank you, because in that shop thank-you was a noise you made at a bench. "Ninth frame's done. Glass is in, tin's in, wick's wound. It's the one for the top of the west path, the curve, the odd one." He nodded at the bench behind him. "I need a hand with the wick test and I'm not doing it with my gloves on."

So Silena spent thirty-five minutes on a Friday afternoon standing at a bench in a warm orange room holding a lantern.

That was the whole job. She held the lantern up while he lit the wick and let it burn down for a count and then pinched it, and she held it while he did something with the chimney and a strip of tin, and she held it while he checked the shadow it threw on the wall of the shop with one eye shut, which was the part that mattered and which he explained without being asked: a lantern with a bad shadow makes the path look like it has holes in it.

They talked the whole time, which she had not expected, and which was the reason her hands were steady and her voice was not.

"What's the smoke smell like," he said.

"Oily. The good kind. Like the top of a stove."

"Blue or yellow."

"Yellow with a blue base. Is that good?"

"That's perfect. That's a clean flame and a wick that isn't going to climb." He took the lantern from her, briefly, and their fingers overlapped on the ring, and he set it down and picked up the next one, and Silena put her hands flat on the bench and counted to two, and nobody in the room saw anything, because the other two kids at the far bench were arguing about a nail.

"Beckendorf," she said.

"Mm."

"What would you do?"

"With what?"

"If you had a cabin's worth of people to feed two times a day and a kitchen that somebody else built, and you could build the next one yourself. What would you put in it?"

He stopped what he was doing. It was the only time all afternoon he stopped, and he gave the question the same respect he gave a lantern shadow, which was the entire reason she had asked it, which she had not known until she heard it in her own mouth.

"I'd put the bread table where the light falls," he said. "You've got the bread table in the corner by the window and the window's on the wrong side, so every morning in November you're working in your own shadow. I'd put the table under the window and the stove on the inside wall, and I'd build the table at forty inches instead of thirty-six, because you're short but you're not going to be short forever, and nobody ever raises a table. Then I'd put a bench along the south end and nothing under it, so people can sit down and be in the room while you work, because a kitchen's not a workshop, it's a room where people talk." He picked the tool back up. "That's what I'd do. You'd hate it for a week and then you'd fight anybody who tried to move it."

Silena stared at him.

"What," he said.

"Nothing. It's just -- " She looked for a joke, and found, for the second time in a week, that there was not one there, and told the truth instead, which she was apparently going to do now, in a forge, on Fridays. "I've said the thing about the bakery to maybe six people in my life and they all said *that's so you*, and one of them said I should do it with makeup and one of them said I should do it with cupcakes, and neither of them ever told me where to put the table. I have been holding that sentence about the table in my head for two years and you just -- " she gestured at the bench, at the lantern, at the whole warm room, "-- said it in a workshop in four seconds."

Beckendorf shrugged, which he did with his whole frame, and went back to the wick. "It's not a hard question. You're the one who asks it about other people's buildings. The pavilion curtains, the hall guttering, the amber lamps. You've told me where nine lamps should go and why. Ask it about yours."

"I don't have one."

"You've got a kitchen. That's the one," he said. "You live in the only cabin at this camp where twelve people ate dinner off a table tonight, and there's three of you in it, and eleven of them came in from out in the weather. You've been running a kitchen for one family and feeding three."

The fire moved, and the shadow of the ninth lantern stood up on the wall behind the bench and settled.

Silena, who had been about four inches away from him for thirty-five minutes, took the finished lantern off the bench and held it, and said, because her mouth had apparently fully defected, "Clarisse wrote a rule on the slate on Tuesday. *And whoever wants to come, comes.* It's the fifth one. She turned the page over so we wouldn't look at it, and then she told us about it anyway, and then she sewed it into a quilt square in four stitches and a cross."

"I saw the square," Beckendorf said. "The five. I asked her what it was and she told me it was the log of a fire."

"That's what it is," Silena said, and her voice did something on the last word that she heard, and he heard, because he looked up.

The look lasted about a second and a half, and it was not the flat cataloguing look and it was not the practical one. It was the ordinary look of a person who has just noticed where he is in a room, and Silena looked straight back at it, which took more out of her than lifting a canoe, and then said, quickly, because silence was fatal in a room with an open fire, "Wick test it. I've got to get this home before the light goes."

"It's tested. It's the top of the west path and it goes up tomorrow at six." He wrapped the tenth lantern in a cloth and put it in her hands and closed her fingers around the wire handle with both of his, which he did in the ordinary course of handing a lantern to a person, and which lasted about two seconds, and which she felt from her fingertips to her elbows, and which he did not appear to notice at all, and which she would have sworn, later, standing in the dark by her own porch, was the tenth one, made last, made for a porch that already had a lamp, and made in grey-surrounded amber by a boy who had said on Wednesday that a person walking up a hill ought to see a light and think somebody's home.

She walked home with it under her arm and the cold coming down and the west path lighting itself up behind her, one amber lamp at a time, all the way up to the pavilion and the curve, and she hung the tenth lantern on the hook by the Cabin Three door, and lit it, and stood back and looked at it for a while in the dark.

Clarisse came out onto the porch with her coat on and looked at the lantern, and looked at Silena, and looked at the lantern again, and said nothing.

"It's for the porch," Silena said.

"It's a good lantern."

"It's amber."

"It's amber," Clarisse agreed, and stood there a moment in the cold with her arms crossed, and then said, in her flat voice, to the dark, "You've been up to that forge three times this week and you've come back with a glove, a lantern, and the same face every time. I'm not asking. I want that in writing, in front of this lantern."

"Noted," Silena said, in the smallest voice she had used in years, and went inside before her sister could look at her any longer, and put her hands in the dishwater, which was cold, and did not care at all.

Silena cooked a dinner on Friday night for five people, which was four more than the stove was built for and exactly the number she had spent the summer learning to cook for.

It was not a special dinner and that was on purpose. Potatoes and the last of the chicken and a whole braid of onions cooked down until they gave up, bread out of the oven at six so the room got the smell of it while everybody was pretending not to be hungry, and the honey cake at the end with the last jar of July honey scraped out with a knife, because a jar with an inch in the bottom was a jar that had to be finished by somebody and it was going to be tonight.

Thalia came in at half past six with her coat still on and her bow left at the door, which meant she had been to the field again, which meant nobody had to ask her about her afternoon.

"Sit," Silena said.

"I'm sitting." Thalia sat, and looked at the table, and looked at the person on the other side of it, and said to nobody in particular, "There is an entire braid of onions on this table."

"There are people in this camp who don't eat onions."

"There are people in this camp who don't eat anything and you cook for them anyway," Thalia said, and took the spoon.

Clarisse put the winter list on the table face down and left it there, which was the Friday-night rule that she had invented and enforced, and the five of them ate the way they had been eating since September, which was: all at once, all over each other, with the pot going round twice and somebody standing up to get more bread and nobody waiting for anybody to finish a sentence.

"Saturday," Clarisse said, to Annabeth, over the potatoes. "What's the turnaround."

"Terrace if the wind's over forty. Otherwise the slab."

"And if the seep's running?"

"Then we don't wade it and it goes on the sheet with a question mark and we come back in the spring." Annabeth said it the way she had said it once already, which was with the flat patience of a girl who had gone through the whole thing an hour ago with Chiron and was now being asked to go through it again for the sake of a cabin. "And if it's a trickle we survey the seam and the slab and we come back in the spring anyway. There is no version of Saturday where I don't come back with something. I built the plan that way because I knew you'd ask."

"Good," Clarisse said, and meant it, and did not write anything down, which was her highest praise.

"Who's on the crew?" Thalia asked, with her mouth full of bread.

"You, me, Marcus, Olive, and Percy."

"And him."

"And him," Annabeth said, and reached past Thalia for the butter, and did not look at anybody, and Percy, four feet away, went on eating his potatoes with an expression of enormous neutrality that fooled nobody at that table.

Silena watched the five of them from the end of the table where she always sat, with her plate mostly untouched and her mug in both hands, and did the thing she had been doing all week without meaning to do it, which was to notice everybody.

Thalia had eaten two helpings and reported nothing about her afternoon and had her bow by the door, which meant she had been on the field and had shot well and did not want to talk about it. Clarisse had put her list face down, which she only did when the list was finished. Percy had the twelve-year-old's talent for being entirely present in a room and entirely elsewhere at the same time, which meant he was thinking about tomorrow and had said nothing about it, because he did not need to. Her sister, across the table, had graphite on the side of her hand and was drawing a contour in the corner of the tablecloth with her finger without knowing she was doing it, which she had done since June, and which Silena had never mentioned because mentioning a thing like that made it stop.

And the whole room smelled of bread and onions and the jar of July honey being finished, and the tenth lantern was on the porch, and the quilt was folded on the floor with a fish in it and five stitches and a trident and a corner of a map, and there was a mountain waiting for the morning.

"Okay," Thalia said at last, putting her fork down. "Somebody ask me about the field. If nobody asks me about the field by the end of this meal I'm going to say it out loud anyway and make it weird."

Clarisse looked up. "Why would anybody ask you about the field."

"Thank you. That's exactly why I said it." Thalia sat back and folded her arms and told them, at speed and in the flattest voice she had, about an Apollo kid who had been away at school for a year and had come back on Thursday and turned up on the range this morning and told her, in front of God and the straw, that she had been dropping her elbow for four years.

"You don't drop your elbow," Clarisse said.

"I drop my elbow."

"You don't."

"He put his hand on my bow hand and turned it a quarter inch and it went into the middle," Thalia said, "and I have been thinking about it all day, and I am going to shoot again on Sunday, and if anybody at this table says one word about that sentence, I'll tell Chiron about the canoe you lost in June."

"I didn't lose a canoe."

"You lost a canoe."

"That was Marcus."

"Marcus is going to be climbing a mountain tomorrow with a rod in his hand and he can't defend himself," Thalia said, "which is why I'm saying it here, tonight, for free." And Clarisse said a word, and Percy laughed so hard he had to put his fork down, and Annabeth said "I heard the field was very brown," with a straight face, and the whole room fell about it, and for about four minutes Cabin Three was exactly as loud as a cabin with twelve kids in it and there was nobody in the room who noticed the difference.

Afterwards Silena took the plates and Thalia dried, which was the only domestic arrangement that had ever survived contact with Thalia Grace, and which existed because their arms were of a length and the drying rack was between them and neither of them had ever once had to say a word about the order.

Annabeth drew the route on the back of her hand in pen.

"Terrace," she said, out loud, to her own hand, while she did it. "Forty. Turn."

"You're doing the thing again," Thalia said, from the rack.

"I'm not doing any thing."

"You did it in June for the wall and in August for the map and you denied it both times. It's on your hand. It's ink."

"It's a legible reminder of a decision I've made once and would rather not re-make on a mountain at two in the afternoon in the wind," Annabeth said, and capped the pen with enormous calm, and went to put her field book in the bag by the door, and Thalia looked at Silena over the dish towel and Silena looked back with the flat complicity of a girl who had been drying dishes beside that particular sister for five months and had learned better than to say anything out loud.

Clarisse went out onto the porch with her coat on because she had heard a lamp ticking and it had bothered her for four minutes.

Percy stayed on the floor with his back against the stove, which had gone down to its low evening heat, and watched the room the way he had gotten into the habit of watching it, which was: not the way you watch a fire, but the way you check a mooring. His sisters were at the sink. His girlfriend was at the door doing up a bag with three pairs of socks in it. Thalia was drying a bowl that had not needed drying for two minutes and was considering her from the other side of the room. The quilt was on the floor with its crooked row and its fish. The tenth lantern was outside on the hook.

He had spent a lot of the summer being surprised by this. He had walked into a camp in March with an alphabet of a family that he did not remember and had spent every day since quietly checking whether the mooring would hold. On a Friday night in October, with a mountain to climb in the morning, he found that he had been sitting on the floor for about twenty minutes without checking anything at all, which was, he decided, the actual answer to the question he had asked Clarisse in September and had never gotten around to asking again.

"What are you grinning at," Clarisse said, coming back in with the cold on her.

"Moorings."

"Don't start."

"I wasn't going to start."

"You were going to start," Clarisse said, and took the coat off and hung it on the hook by the tenth lantern's window, and put the kettle on because the kettle was on the stove and because the stove was lit and because that was how this cabin ended a night, and none of them went to bed for another hour, and nobody did anything at all.

By Friday night the west path was lit end to end and Cabin Three had gone quiet in the specific way a building goes quiet on the last night before something.

Percy sat on the porch step with his back against the post and watched the lamps come up the hill in a line: one at the boathouse turn, two at the cedar brake, one at the curve. They were amber, all of them, and the light they laid down on the path was the color of the inside of a jar of honey, and the dark between them was not the same dark it had been on Tuesday night when he had walked the hill in a storm with a lamp in his hand and no idea where the path went.

That was a thing you could learn in a week, apparently. He'd have to tell Annabeth. She'd want it in a book.

Inside, the commons had the long table going, and the table had three things on it: a stew pot, an argument, and the storm quilt, which was now two complete rows with the third pinned and waiting, and which Clarisse had unfolded on the floor twice that evening to look at, on the stated grounds that she was checking the seams, and on the actual grounds that she liked looking at it.

"Four corners are wrong," she said, from the floor.

"Four corners are the corners," Silena said, from the stove.

"The corner square's a rectangle. It's my rectangle. My square's a rectangle and now the whole row's pulled."

"Your square's a rectangle because you cut it with a knife and then sewed five marks into it and pulled them tight. It's the best square on the quilt. Stop standing on the quilt."

"I'm not standing. I'm surveying."

"You're standing."

The screen door banged, and Annabeth came in with the field book under her arm and the cold in her hair and her boots already by the door, because she had walked down in her socks for reasons that she had not explained and would not be explaining, and stood at the end of the table in the lamplight and looked at the quilt on the floor.

"That's good," she said.

"That's four crooked corners," Clarisse said.

"That's five squares of a place, which is better than four corners that match," Annabeth said, which was not the kind of thing she said, and Clarisse looked at her sideways and decided not to be told the reason, because there was a rule.

Percy got up and went out onto the porch, and Annabeth followed him, which was a thing that required no deciding, and they sat down on the step in the amber light with the quilt out of earshot and the lake a black nothing at the bottom of the hill and the first real frost of the second cold snap already silvering the low ground by the boathouse.

"Bag's by the door," Percy said. "Three pairs of socks now."

"I put them in at six. I saw you counting them."

"You put in a third pair for Marcus."

"Marcus is fifteen and he will not bring socks to a mountain and he'll be miserable by the terrace and I'll have to look at it for six hours." She was quiet a second, and then said, with no preamble at all, which was how she did the big things, "I read your note again this morning."

"Which note."

"The storm one. In the field book."

"The storm one was a Wednesday note."

"It was. It said three things and the third thing was that you'd bring it up in February and I already knew it." She had her knees up and her arms around them and her chin on her arms, looking down the hill. "I want to say something and I want to have said it before tomorrow, so I'm going to say it now and it will be nothing like the note, because I don't write like you."

"Okay."

"Tomorrow is mine," Annabeth said. "It's my survey. I asked for it, Chiron wrote it down, the route's on the wall and the crew's on the paper in my handwriting, and when we come down off that ridge I'm going to have a sheet of the map that says something nobody at this camp knew last week, and there are going to be five names on the plot line, and one of them is going to be *Jackson, P. -- load, enthusiasm*, which is the actual thing I wrote, and which I meant." She turned her head and looked at him. "I've been given a lot of things in the last few months. A cabin I don't remember, a sister who raised me, a father who doesn't come, a name that was wrong on the front of my own book. Tomorrow is the first thing in this whole year that's mine because I went and got it. I wanted you to know that before I'm up there, because I've decided it matters."

Percy did not answer for a while. There was no answer that would have been as good as the not-answering, and he had learned that in June and had gotten quietly good at it, so they sat on the step under the tenth lantern and let the cold come up the path at them, and the marsh of the closed lake lay down there in the dark being nothing at all, and the frost went on writing itself into the low ground.

Then he took the field book out of her lap, which she let him do, and opened the back cover, and read her reply for the fourth time in three days, and closed it, and put it back in her lap and left his hand on top of it.

"You got one of the two seconds," he said.

"I got one of the two seconds."

"How do you know which one you got."

"I measured," Annabeth said, and then he kissed her, on a porch, in October, under the amber lantern that a Hephaestus kid had made for a cabin he did not live in, and it was slow and it was long and it was the kind of kiss that has two halves to it, one that ends and one that starts, and she had a hand flat on his chest and her fingers curled into his shirt and her knees still up, and somewhere over the hill the pavilion bell moved in the wind and neither of them heard it.

When they came apart the frost had come up the path another two feet.

"Go to bed," Percy said. "You're up at six."

"Six at the gate. Don't be late."

"I've never been late in my life."

"You were late to the boat on Monday."

"I was *on time* on Monday and the boat was early, which is a completely different thing, and you know it, and you're going to put it in a book."

Annabeth stood up and took her field book and looked down at him for a moment with the lamplight behind her head and her face entirely serious, and then she said, "Twenty-two hours," which was not a sentence he had been given before, and went up the steps and inside and was gone, and Percy sat on the step in the amber light a while longer, doing no arithmetic at all, which was, by the standard of this particular cabin on this particular hill, the most extravagant thing a person could do with a Friday night.

Thalia Grace opened the letter at eleven o'clock on Friday night, sitting on the floor of Cabin One with her back against her own bunk and the lamp turned down and her sister asleep six feet away with a packed bag at the foot of the bed.

It was a good letter, which was the problem with it.

Luke wrote the way he talked, which was well and a little too fast, and it went: the road out of Montana, a bus with a broken window in Idaho, a thing with teeth in a rest stop outside Boise that had taken two days and a set of alloy arrows to solve, a diner with pie, a fight with a man in a hardware store that he had won and did not sound good about, and a paragraph at the end that said he was coming home in November and that he hoped everyone was well and to say hello to the girls.

*To the girls.*

Thalia read it twice, folded it along its own creases the way it had come, and sat on the floor with it in her lap in the lamplight for a long time.

The postmark was from a town forty miles away and the date was Tuesday, the day before the storm, and she had spent nine hours being tight about it, and now she knew what she had actually been holding: she had wanted the letter to be an apology about the summer, or a confession about something, or a reason, and it was a road letter from a person who was doing fine and who thought about her about four times a year, which is a real and respectable amount, and which was not what she wanted.

That was the whole thing and it was nothing, and she sat on the floor and found that she was not angry and not sad and not even disappointed in any way that would last past breakfast, and that what she was was *done*, in the way that a list is done: the item was on the page and it had been answered and she could draw a line through it and stop carrying it around the hill.

She put the letter in the wooden box with the others, and closed the lid, and did not write back, because she had learned this year that the correct number of letters to send to a person who thinks about you four times a year is one, and she had sent it in July.

Then she got up quietly and went and stood at the end of her sister's bunk.

Annabeth was asleep on her side with her face toward the wall and one hand out of the blanket and her field book under the edge of her pillow, where it lived, and her boots by the bed with the socks rolled into them, which was the single most Annabeth object in the entire Zeus cabin: a girl who had walked down the hill in socks to keep her boots dry for tomorrow and had then left the boots by the bed anyway, on the correct side, laced.

There was a bag at the foot of the bed with the rope on the bottom and the fair sheets in oilcloth. There was a slip of paper on top of the bag, in Percy's uphill handwriting, four words: SIX AT THE GATE. There was the granite stone on the windowsill with its words on it, where it had lived since the week she came back from the ridge.

Thalia stood there and looked at all of it for about thirty seconds, which in her private ledger was a fortune, and then she did the thing she had been doing since Annabeth was small enough to know better, which was to check the window latch, and the lamp, and the stove, and then to go back to her own bunk and lie down in the dark with the whole of Saturday in front of her.

Rope, ground, forty degrees, turn at the terrace. Six at the gate. A slab the size of a cabin door and a seep under granite and a cairn with a blue rag on it that had been her mark on the world for four days in September.

And on Sunday, at eight in the morning, on a brown archery field with the wind coming off the ridge in cold flat sheets, a man who had sat down in her field and told her the truth about third place was going to say *bring the elbow*, and she was going to have fixed it, and she was going to shoot better than him, and she was going to be very careful about letting anybody at this camp see her face while she did it.

"I know you're awake," Annabeth said, from the other bunk, thick with sleep and perfectly even.

"I'm not awake."

"You're going to be tired tomorrow."

"I'm going to be fine tomorrow. I've been on a ridge for four days on worse." Thalia put her arm over her eyes. "Go to sleep, genius. You've got a mountain."

"Six at the gate."

"Six at the gate," Thalia said, and the lamp ticked, and she listened to her sister's breathing even out, and lay in the dark for a long while after that, exactly as awake as she had said she was not, and completely fine about it.

Clarisse La Rue went out onto the porch at half past eleven on Friday night, in her coat and her boots, and stood at the rail and looked at the hill.

The west path was lit end to end, and from where she stood she could see five of the nine: one at the boathouse turn, two at the cedar brake, the odd one at the curve, and the tenth, which was on the hook beside her own door and had not been on the list at all. Amber, all of them, the color of honey through glass, and the light each one threw was a soft laid-down shape on the path with no holes in it, which is a thing you only get from someone who checks the shadow.

She had the winter list with her, and she did what she had been doing every night that week, which was to read the bottom of it and add the thing that had happened.

Under TEMPEST HALL. TUESDAYS + THURSDAYS. 7PM. CABIN THREE. (AND WHOEVER.) she wrote:

STORM QUILT — 2 ROWS OF 3 DONE. FISH STAYS.
LANTERNS — 9 UP. 10TH ON OUR PORCH. NOT ON THE LIST. DO NOT ASK HIM.
RIDGE, SATURDAY — CREW OF 5. OUT SIX. BACK BY DARK.

And under that, in the very last space on the page, where she had been writing it all week, in the same three letters and the same full stop, because it had held on Sunday and Monday and Tuesday and Wednesday and every day since:

FIVE.

She read the four lines back to herself twice, the way she read everything she wrote on that page, and then, in the space under the count, where there had never been anything because the count was always the last thing, she added one more line in the same blocky hand, because the page was nearly full and there was a long winter left and she had decided the page could take it.

SUNDAY — FIELD, 8AM. SOMEBODY IN THIS CABIN HAS A HEEL AND IS NOT TELLING ME.

Clarisse stood at the porch rail a while longer, reading the new line about Sunday. It didn't belong with the quilt measurements and lantern checks. A bad heel wasn't a task she could cross out with a pencil. She nearly tore the corner off the page so nobody else would read it.

Instead she shut the book. Thalia would be angry if she knew she'd been watching the way she came down the slope. That didn't make it less true that she had begun keeping weight off one foot.

There were three useful things Clarisse could do without turning it into a public argument. She could stop assigning Thalia to carry the heavy kit up the ridge. She could check that the slope was safe for everybody on Saturday. And she could ask her, in private, whether the heel needed somebody with medical knowledge rather than another week of stubbornness.

She wrote none of that down. The Sunday note stayed in her own book, where she could find it when she needed it.

Through the cabin window she heard Silena moving a pot from the stove, then Percy laughing at something Annabeth said. There were ordinary sounds enough to fill the evening without the list. Clarisse put the pencil behind her ear, went inside, and set her boots where they could dry by morning.

On the table lay tomorrow's crew sheet. She removed one weight-carrying task from Thalia's column and wrote it into her own. In the morning she'd have to explain why. Tonight she wanted to finish the tea Silena had left her while it was still hot.
