## Chapter 152: A Full Day of Sky

Thalia Grace woke twenty minutes before her alarm and lay still in the half-dark, listening to her sister breathe.

Cabin One was quiet in the way of a room full of sleeping people who had all been struck by something. The high windows sat above the door, and through them the sky had gone from black to the color of a bruise healing. The air smelled of ozone and pine sap and somebody's abandoned sock. Somewhere down the row of bunks a cabinmate muttered and turned over, and the static that lived in the walls of Cabin One ticked like a cooling engine, settling.

Thalia had not slept well. She had not slept well all week.

She turned her head on the pillow. Two bunks down, Annabeth was a shape under a gray blanket with one arm flung outside it, fingers curled, the way she slept when she had been working on something with her hands. Her ponytail had come loose in the night and spread across the pillow in a fan. She was fourteen. She was fourteen, and she had a title now, and eleven students, and a map on the Big House wall, and Thalia had stood in the doorway last week watching strangers point at her little sister's name, and Thalia had felt something in her chest she had not had a name for.

Her alarm went off. She shut it off before it finished the first bar and swung her feet out of the blanket onto a floor cold enough to make her hiss through her teeth. Cabin One's floor was always cold. Somebody had told her once that it was because the cabin sat on a seam of quartz under the ridge, and Thalia had believed that for about two years before she worked out that the real reason was the great gaping hole where a fireplace should have been. Zeus did not believe in hearths. His children slept cold and told themselves it built character.

She dressed fast. Black camp shirt from Cabin One, the cotton gone soft and the silver trim at the collar rubbed to a dull gray. Cargo shorts, one pocket still full of the things she had not emptied yesterday — a strip of jerky she had forgotten, a bent nail, three things that had once been a watch. She pulled her boots on over bare feet, because socks were a formality she had abandoned at twelve, and left the laces loose the way she liked them. Then she paused, and took the gray flannel off the hook by her bunk — a men's overshirt, too big, the cuffs frayed, a faint smell of old rain in the weave from a night it had spent under a canoe — and tied it around her waist by the sleeves. The knot came out right on the first try. She had tied that particular knot about nine thousand times.

Then she went and woke her sister up.

She knew exactly how long to let Annabeth sleep. It was a science. Fourteen years of practice, most of it spent on somebody else's orders, some of it spent just because she had been the only one standing between a small girl and the bad nights. Annabeth got mean if woken too early and got weepy if woken too late, and Thalia had learned the difference the way other people learned to drive.

So she knelt by the bunk and put one hand flat on the blanket over Annabeth's shin and waited.

"Ten minutes," she said.

Annabeth made a noise into the pillow that was agreement in a language only sleepers speak.

"Ten minutes," Thalia repeated, "and then I'm dumping water on you."

That got her. Annabeth's eyes opened, gray and furious and unfocused, and fixed on the ceiling. "You wouldn't."

"I absolutely would. I've done it before."

"That was a different era of our relationship."

"We've had five eras. You've drowned in four of them."

Annabeth sat up. The blanket fell to her waist. She was wearing an old T-shirt shoved into a pair of shorts that had been cut off from jeans, the cut ragged, the hem carrying a permanent white line of dust from the arena. Her camp necklace slid forward against her collarbone. She pushed her hair out of her face with the back of her wrist and squinted at the window, at the bruise-colored sky, at Thalia kneeling on the cold floor.

"Why are you awake," Annabeth said. It was not a question.

"Because I decided something yesterday and then lay awake half the night second-guessing it, and I have concluded that if I think about it any longer I'll talk myself out of it, and I would rather be brave and wrong than correct and miserable."

Annabeth blinked slowly. "Did you hit your head?"

"I'm going to be nice to you all day," Thalia said. "No patrol. No training. No lectures. You and me, out of the cabins, off the dock, out of Percy's orbit for a full day. I'm stealing you."

"You're stealing me."

"I'm abducting you. It's a kidnapping. Don't tell Chiron."

Annabeth looked at her for a long moment. Then she said, "Where?"

"Somewhere you've been."

"I've been everywhere."

"Somewhere you've been and don't remember."

That landed. Thalia watched it land, the way she had watched things land in Annabeth since Annabeth was small: the flicker, the calculation, the wall going up so fast it was almost funny. Annabeth's face smoothed out.

"Give me ten minutes," Annabeth said.

"That's what I said."

"I meant ten real minutes. Not Thalia minutes."

"Thalia minutes are the only minutes there are."

"Then I'm getting up in nine." Annabeth threw the blanket off, reached under her pillow, and came out with a folded scrap of paper that had not been there when she went to sleep.

Thalia saw her sister open it. She saw the exact moment the mouth wanted to smile and the jaw refused to allow it, and she saw Annabeth fold the paper again along the same creases and tuck it into the pocket of her shorts without a word.

"Who's that from?" Thalia said.

"Nobody."

"It's from Jackson."

"It's from nobody."

"Annabeth."

"It's a note about the map." Annabeth's face was perfectly, insultingly innocent. "He said the north path needs a note about the oak roots. He signed it. Professionally. Like a colleague."

"Did he draw a heart on it?"

"No."

"Did he write your name with the extra loop in the y that he thinks nobody notices?"

Annabeth stood up, found her sneakers, and pushed her feet into them without looking down. "The loop is not extra," she said. "It's a descender."

"It's a heart with a spine."

"It is nothing, and you are nosy, and I want eggs."

Thalia let her have it. She stood up, knees cracking, and went to find her own shoes, and felt the small warm thing in her chest settle into something she could carry.

---

They took the path north instead of the road to the pavilion, because Thalia wanted to walk on dirt before she had to walk on gravel, and because the long way around meant ten extra minutes with nobody else in it.

The camp at that hour was a held breath. Fog lay in the low places, thin as breath on glass, and the pines were black cutouts against a sky going from bruise to pearl. Frost ghosted the north-facing slopes in the corners where the sun had not reached yet. Underfoot the path was packed dirt and pine needles, soft enough to swallow sound, and the air smelled of resin and cold stone and, faintly, of woodsmoke from the night before.

Annabeth walked beside her with her hands in the pockets of her shorts and her shoulders up around her ears against the chill.

"Talk to me," Thalia said.

"About what?"

"Anything. The map. Your students. Which one of the Stoll cousins you're going to murder first."

"Marcus." Annabeth said it instantly. "Marcus is fifteen and he already knows how to draw and he knows he already knows how to draw, and yesterday he told me my contour interval was 'aspirational.'"

"What's a contour interval?"

"The distance between the lines that show height. Mine is exact. His are aspirational. He draws them the way he wishes the ground was." Annabeth's mouth twitched. "He's going to be very good. It's infuriating."

"You like them."

"I like that they show up. That's different."

Thalia kicked a pinecone off the path and watched it tumble into the fog. "Olive Nakamura still reporting mud?"

"Olive Nakamura is reporting everything. Olive Nakamura has opinions about drainage that would frighten a civil engineer." Annabeth slowed. She was looking left, off the path, toward the stand of cottonwoods that marked the low ground below the archery range. "That's the north path."

"I know where the north path is. I can see it from my bunk."

"It shouldn't look like that from here."

Thalia followed her eyes. From the top of the rise the north path was a pale seam through the trees, and along its lower half the seam had gone dark and glazed, wet-looking even in the flat light, the way a road looks after rain that has nowhere to go. The trees on the downhill side of it sat in a wash of pale gravel and silt that had no business being there.

"Since Tuesday," Annabeth said. "Olive drew it. She'd drawn it three weeks ago and nobody looked at her paper, and then Chiron looked at her paper, and now there are two Hephaestus kids digging a French drain by the big oak, which is a sentence I never expected to say. The roots are already standing in a river." She was quiet a moment. "Percy walked it with me on Wednesday. He stood in the mud for an hour doing nothing, and then he said the water was coming from somewhere uphill that wasn't on my map, and he was right, and it is, and it's going to take me another week to find out where, because the source is under the ridge and the ridge is granite." She said it fast, the way she said things when she was proud of them and did not want to be heard being proud.

"Percy stood in mud for you."

"Percy will stand anywhere for somebody else. It's his whole personality." Annabeth started walking again. "He put it on the map anyway. The wet part. He wrote *water, unseen* in the margin with a little arrow, and he wouldn't tell me why, and he had this face."

"What face?"

"The face where he's thinking about something and has decided not to say it."

Thalia made a noise. "He does that."

"He does it constantly. It's been four days."

"Annabeth." Thalia reached out and flicked the back of her sister's head, gently, the way she had been flicking it since Annabeth was nine. "He's a fourteen-year-old boy who is in love with you. Of course he has faces. You have faces. Yesterday you made a face at the bacon like it had personally wronged you."

"I did not."

"You made a face like the bacon owed you money."

Annabeth stopped walking. She turned and looked up at her — up, because Annabeth was still the kind of small that Thalia had to look down at, and it had been true for years and was, somehow, still true, and Thalia had not been ready for it to stop. "Fine," Annabeth said. "I made a face at the bacon. Are you going to report me to the cartography board?"

"I'm going to report you to the sky," Thalia said, and swept an arm at the pale vault overhead, where the last of the stars were going out. "Which, by the way, is going to do something later. Did you feel that?"

"Feel what?"

"That." Thalia pointed at the eastern horizon, where the sky above the trees had gone a flat, hard, dusty blue — not cloud, not yet, just a zone of wrongness, as if somebody had pressed a thumb into the atmosphere and smudged it.

Annabeth looked. She went very still. Her head tilted, the bird-dog tilt she got when something was being measured.

"There's a front coming," Annabeth said.

"Today?"

"Today. Late. It'll be hot first." She frowned at the smudge. "The camp weather charms won't catch that until noon."

"No," Thalia said. "They won't."

"How did you—"

"It's my father's weather," Thalia said. "It smells like him." She grinned, wide and unpracticed, the grin she used when she was about to be reckless. "So we're going to the water before it gets here, and we're going to be wet and then we're going to be rained on, and it's going to be perfect, and you're going to complain the entire time."

"I never complain."

"You complain in footnotes."

Annabeth started walking. She did not argue, which was how Thalia knew she had already decided to come.

---

The shore path ran along the eastern water, and by the time they reached it the sun was properly up and the fog had burned off the open ground and was clinging to the trees. The lake turned from black to slate to a green so clear it looked poured. A canoe sat beached near the swim buoys with its paddle across the gunwales and nobody near it. Somewhere out on the water a fish rose, and the rings spread and spread and touched the pilings and came back changed.

Annabeth stopped on the path and took the whole of it in, and Thalia watched her do it, and understood, the way she was coming to understand a great many things that week, that her sister saw the world in a language Thalia had never learned to speak.

"It's freshwater," Annabeth said.

"What?"

"You're looking at it like it's the ocean. You do that. You stand at the edge of the lake and posture like you're about to fight the sea." Annabeth crouched, dipped two fingers into the shallows, and touched them to her tongue. "Mud. Stone. Weeds. Nobody's drowned in salt. It's a lake. It's the good kind of water."

"I don't posture."

"You posture tremendously. It's one of your best features and your worst habit." Annabeth stood, wiping her hand on her shorts. "Where are we going? You said somewhere I've been."

"I'm taking you to the Slate."

"The Slate."

"The Slate."

Annabeth waited. When nothing came she spread her hands. "That's not a place. That's a word you made up."

"It's a place. It's a rock. It's big and flat and gray and it sticks out over the water on the east side, past the canoe beach, where the pines give up. There's deep water under it. You jump."

"You jump."

"I jump. You jump." Thalia was already moving. "Come on. It's a mile and a half and the front gets here by midafternoon and I would like to be wet before I'm soaked."

"You're enjoying this."

"I'm enjoying the part where you don't know yet. That's the best part of any gift." Thalia heard her sister's steps speed up to keep pace and grinned into the pine needles ahead of her. "You used to love not knowing where we were going. You'd get in the car with your shoes on the wrong feet and not say a word."

"I don't remember that."

"I know," Thalia said, and the grin stayed where it was, and her chest got a little tighter, and she walked faster.

They came up out of the pines onto the long bare shoulder of the east shore, where the trees thinned and the ground went from needles to sand to bleached grass to rock. The lake opened out on their left, huge and flat and bright, and the wind came off it steady and cool with the morning still in it.

Annabeth stopped in the middle of the path. "Okay."

"Okay, what?"

"Okay, this is where you tell me the thing you're not telling me." She turned to face her. "You woke me up twenty minutes early on a day with no training. You tied your flannel around your waist, which you only do when you're going to be outside all day and you know it. You're being nice to me. You've been being nice to me for a week and a half, and you kept asking me questions about the map and then not listening to the answers, which means you were practicing." Annabeth's eyes were steady. "So. What is it?"

Thalia looked at her sister. Fourteen, with grass in her sneakers and a folded note in her pocket and a jaw set like a doorframe.

"Fine," Thalia said. "Chiron talked to me. On the hill. After the argument. He said I couldn't protect you by being hard, and that I needed to be known, and that I should start with my sisters." She spread her hands. "So I'm starting. This is me, starting. This is me, known. I am being nice to you because I have decided that you are allowed to have a day where nobody asks you to be the mapmaker or the lightning or the good example or the girl who doesn't embarrass her cabin, and I am the one who's going to give it to you. That's all. It's not a plot. It's not a test. It's a Tuesday."

Annabeth's expression did something complicated. "You're doing it out loud."

"I'm doing it out loud because if I do it quietly you'll think I'm managing you."

"Are you?"

"Yes." Thalia sighed. "Probably. Some. But I'm managing you toward having a nice day instead of toward doing what I want, and I think that's growth, and I'd like you to recognize my growth without a certificate."

Annabeth was quiet. The wind came off the lake and moved the loose hair at her temple. Then she said, "You could have just asked me to spend the day with you."

"That's not a thing sisters do."

"That's exactly a thing sisters do. That's the only thing sisters do. You ask." Annabeth's voice had gone soft in the way it did when she was being careful. "Thalia. You can just ask me for things. You don't have to stage a kidnapping to get me alone."

Thalia's throat did something unwelcome. She looked at the water. "I'll ask next time."

"Ask now."

"I'm asking now. Annabeth. Spend the day with me. Please."

"Yes," Annabeth said. "Obviously yes."

"Obviously," Thalia echoed, and the tightness in her chest let go by about half, which was progress.

---

They walked, and after a while Thalia started the game without announcing it, because announcing it would have ruined it.

"Three things," she said. "Go."

"That's not the game. The game is six."

"The game is six when we're in a car for four hours." Thalia pointed at the shore ahead of them, at a tangle of driftwood and a half-sunk birch. "Birch. Green heron. A boot with no foot in it."

Annabeth scanned the water without breaking stride. "Cattail. Crayfish pot — actual pot, ceramic, somebody's old camp junk. Cormorant, drying its wings."

"Wrong. That's an anhinga. Cormorants make a cross shape. Anhingas spear."

"That's a spear, then."

"It's a staked fence post. You said a spear."

"Same thing."

"It's not the same thing and you know it."

"You stole that from Clarisse."

"Clarisse stole it from the universe." Thalia hopped a puddle of standing water and landed in sand. "Your turn."

They went on like that. It was a stupid game. It was a game they had played a thousand times, in a car, on a porch, on a road with no destination in it — Thalia would call out what she saw and Annabeth would have to name it correctly, and the person with the most points at the end of the day got to choose dinner, which in their household had meant nothing for years and meant everything for a while after that.

"Fence post," Annabeth said, "weathered cedar, iron-stained, at least six years in the water, and it's holding a snag of fishing line, which means somebody fishes off the east shore and doesn't care."

"Two points."

"I want my two points."

"You'll get your two points at dinner. What do you want for dinner?"

"I want the good bread."

"They make the good bread on Sundays."

"Then I want to know when they make the good bread."

"They make the good bread when Silena decides to make the good bread, and Silena decides when the mood takes her, and the mood takes her about once—" Thalia stopped.

Annabeth had stopped walking. She was looking at the path, at the sand, at the lake, at anything that was not Thalia.

"What?" Thalia said.

"Nothing."

"Don't do that."

"I'm not doing anything." Annabeth's jaw was set again. "I just don't remember the game. That's all. I don't remember playing it. I don't remember the car. I don't remember the boot with no foot in it or the fence post or the bread." She looked up, and her eyes were bright and level and furious. "I don't remember any of it, Thalia. I don't remember anything. You keep handing me these— these— evidence, like exhibits, and you keep waiting for me to say *oh yes, that's right, I remember*, and I don't, and I never will, and every time you do it I get to find out again."

Thalia stood very still. The heron they had been counting rose off the shallows and went away across the water on slow gray wings.

"I know," Thalia said.

"You don't know. You know it in your head. You don't know it in your—" Annabeth made a frustrated noise and pushed her sleeve up her arm, pushing it too far, pushing it past her elbow. "Sorry. Sorry. I'm not— I'm not mad at you."

"I know."

"I'm mad at the situation."

"I know that too."

"It's not fair." Annabeth's voice cracked on the word and she let it. "It's not fair that everybody else in this camp has a childhood and I have a— a filing cabinet somebody else keeps. Percy has his. You have yours. Clarisse and Silena have each other's. I have a map and a title and eleven kids who think I'm clever, and I have you, remembering me."

Thalia crossed the sand. She did not hug her. She had learned, the hard way, over too many months, that Annabeth did not want to be picked up when she was angry; she wanted to be stood next to.

"Then I'll remember for both of us," Thalia said. "That's the job. That's what big sisters are for. You don't have to hold your own half. I've got it. I've had it the whole time."

"That's not—" Annabeth exhaled. "That's a lot to put on you."

"It's not a lot. It's a handful." Thalia bumped her shoulder. "Come on. I want to get there before the wind turns."

---

They went on, and the shore curved, and the pines came down to the water's edge in a last dark rank and then stopped being pines altogether.

The rock came out of the trees the way a stage comes out of the dark: a low gray shelf, wider than the pavilion, running out into the lake on a shelf of the same stone and ending in a straight drop. Water lay under the drop in a dark green bowl, deep enough to show bottom in one place and black in every other. Wave lines had painted the stone white at the edges. Lichen grew in the cracks in slow orange maps. On the inland end, a single dead pine leaned out over the water, its roots half-torn from the rock, struck through years ago and still standing out of pure stubbornness.

Annabeth stopped at the edge of the trees and looked at it for a long moment.

"The Slate," Thalia said.

"The Slate," Annabeth repeated. "It's flat."

"It's very flat. It's the flattest rock on the lake and everybody else at this camp is afraid of it, which I have never understood. It's a rock. It's not going to bite you." She heard her own voice get faster and made herself slow down. "You jump off the end. Not the inland end, the lake end. There's a shelf four feet down and then it goes away into nothing, and if you jump right you go in clean and come up and the water is so clear you can see your own feet."

Annabeth walked out onto the stone. It was warm already. She took three steps, then sat down on the very edge with her legs hanging over the drop, and looked down into the green dark, and Thalia saw her do the thing she always did — the measure. Annabeth measured everything. She measured distances with her eyes and heights with her hands and people with a silence you could feel.

"How deep?" Annabeth said.

"Deep."

"That's not a number."

"There are no numbers on this rock. That's the point of this rock." Thalia came and sat beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and swung her own boots out over the drop. Below them the water moved against the stone with a sound like a slow hand. "You want to know how you know it's deep? You drop a stone and you listen. If it goes *tick*, it's shallow. If it goes *thunk*, it's deep. If it goes *ploop*, there's mud, and you don't want mud, because mud is where the old things are."

"What old things?"

"Old beer cans. Old arrows. A shopping cart, three coves over, which Clarisse will deny to her dying day." Thalia picked up a pebble and dropped it. They listened. It went *ploop*, and then, faintly, a second smaller sound that might have been the bottom or might have been a fish.

"Plop," Annabeth said.

"Plop."

"So there are old things here."

"There are old things everywhere. You of all people should know that."

Annabeth was quiet. Then she said, "I've been here."

"Yeah."

"I don't remember it."

"I know." Thalia put her hand flat on the warm stone between them. "You were seven. It was the first time you ever came to camp, and you wouldn't go in the water, and I sat you right here and I told you that the rock was a boat and the lake was a road and if you jumped far enough you'd land in a different country." She laughed, a small dry sound. "And you said, 'That's not how roads work.' You always said that. You were seven and you were correcting my physics."

Annabeth looked at the drop. Her fingers were spread on the stone, white at the knuckles.

"What happened?" she said.

"You jumped."

"And?"

"And I jumped after you, because if I let you go first you'd have stood on the edge for an hour, and I have never once in my life been able to watch you stand on the edge of something." Thalia's mouth twitched. "You came up sputtering and told me it was too cold, and then you made me do it again, and then again, and then four more times, and then you sat on this rock with your teeth chattering and told me that you'd decided the lake was acceptable, and I said 'acceptable,' and you said 'acceptable is a high bar.'"

Annabeth's face had gone soft and helpless, the face of somebody hearing a story about a stranger who shared her name.

"Thalia—" she started.

"Don't," Thalia said. "Don't say it. Not yet. Not while you're sitting on the edge." She stood up and held out a hand. "Later. If you still want to. Now jump."

---

Half a mile west, with the sun now properly up and the fog burned down to a rag in the tree line, Percy Jackson was waist-deep in freshwater and thoroughly outvoted.

"It's a meter," Clarisse said.

"It's three feet."

"It's the same amount of wood, Seaweed. It's a measurement."

Percy planted his boots in the silt and braced both hands against the slatted wooden drain box the Hephaestus kids had set into the north bank the day before. Water came out of it in a steady sheet over his shins, cold, snowmelt cold, the kind of cold that had a source. Beside him Clarisse had her shoulder into the far end of the same box, and behind them on the sand Silena sat with her journal open on her knees and her braid pinned up, adding a drawing of nothing in particular, which was what she drew when she was paying attention.

"It's holding," Silena said. "Push once more and then let it alone. You'll warp the slats."

"One more," Clarisse said, and pushed, and the box groaned, and Percy pushed back, and the whole thing settled a half inch into the sand and stayed.

"It was holding before I got here," Clarisse said.

"It was holding better after."

Clarisse straightened and wiped her forehead with the back of her wrist, leaving a stripe of sand across it. She was in a Camp Half-Blood shirt gone yellow at the collar and cargo pants with the knees blown out, and she had the specific glow of a girl who had hit something that morning and was still riding it. "Fine. It's holding. Tell your girlfriend her French drain works."

"She's not my— she's not my drain technician."

"She'll want to know anyway."

"She'll want to know from the map." Percy waded out of the outflow and stood in the shallows, shaking his legs, water flying. The lake came up to his thighs, cool and enormous and freshwater, and under his soles the sand was firm and then soft and then firm again, and then it dropped away. It always dropped away. It had dropped away under him since the first week, and every time he felt it he had the same small private laugh about it: the bottom of the canoe lake going out from under him like a held breath.

"Percy," Silena said. "You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face where you go under and stay under and think about something, and Clarisse and I are going to have to stand here like idiots waiting for you."

Percy looked at her. Silena had her pencil behind her ear and a smear of graphite on the side of her hand and her feet bare in the shallows with the water pulling at her toes, and she was smiling, small, the smile of somebody who had already worked out the whole conversation and was letting him catch up.

"She's with Thalia," he said.

"We know she's with Thalia."

"All day."

"All day," Clarisse agreed. "Thalia came and got her at breakfast. Took her east. The whole camp watched it happen. It was like watching somebody make off with the silver."

Percy looked out at the lake. Out past the swim buoys, out where the water went from green to a blue so dark it looked like a hole punched in the world, the heron stood on the bow of the sunken skiff in the willow cove, still as a carved thing, one leg, waiting.

"She's never done that," he said. "Taken her somewhere. Just the two of them. Nobody's ever done that."

"Nobody's had a reason," Silena said.

"That's not—" Percy stopped. "No. That's true. That's the annoying part. Nobody's had a reason, and Thalia found one, and I should be happy, and I am happy, and I also feel like I've been replaced by a rock."

Clarisse snorted. "You have been replaced by a rock. That rock has deep water under it and zero supervision. Of course you lost."

"I'm being serious."

"I know you're being serious. That's why I'm making a joke." Clarisse came and stood next to him, close, shoulder to shoulder. "Listen to me, little brother. You have spent five months being the person who remembers everything for both of them. You hold her whole past in your head like a grudge and a promise and a wound. You can't also be the person she spends every afternoon with. That's not a thing one person can do."

"I don't hold it like a grudge."

"You do, and it's exhausting, and I've watched you do it. For one day you get to put it down." She looked at him sideways. "Also, emotionally, you're insufferable about her. Go be insufferable about this drain instead."

"It's a meter," Percy said.

"It's a meter and you're a brat."

Silena closed her journal on her pencil. "Clarisse's right. For once. Don't waste it."

"I don't know what else to do with it."

"Then swim." Silena stood, tucking the journal under her arm, and hoisted a canvas bag from the sand. "Cabin Three is going out to the buoys and back, the loser carries the towels, and I've decided not to compete, because someone has to hold the towels."

"That's a lie," Clarisse said. "You're holding the sketchbook."

"I'm holding the sketchbook strategically." Silena looked at Percy. "I also brought the good bread."

"You brought—" Percy stared at the bag. "That's the good bread."

"It's Thursday."

"It's Thursday and it's the good bread and I made it this morning, and yes, I know it's usually Sunday. I decided that if I have to wait for a mood I'll be waiting until September." She started for the water. "So we swim, and then we eat, and then you two can argue about the meter all afternoon."

"You made bread," Percy said.

"Don't make it weird."

"I'm not making it weird. I'm appreciating it."

"Appreciate it swimming."

She turned for the water. Percy went after her, and Clarisse went after Percy, and the three of them went out into the lake in a ragged line with the cold coming up to their chests and their waists and then, one after another, dropping away.

The water closed over Percy's head and the noise of the camp stopped and there was only the green light and the sound of his own heartbeat and the enormous slow patience underneath. He opened his eyes. The bottom was a long pale slope of sand with shadows in it, a rusted wheel hub somebody's grandfather had dumped forty years ago, and nothing else. He broke the surface, gasping, and found Silena floating on her back beside him with her hair spread out and the bread bag anchored under her arm like a buoy, and Clarisse twenty feet out, already stroking hard for the buoys with the fast, ugly, powerful crawl she had developed at eleven and never bothered to improve.

"You're not going to catch her," Silena said.

"I'm not trying to catch her."

"Good. Because you won't." She paddled slowly, lazily, in a circle. "Percy."

"Yeah?"

"Tyson's letter came."

Percy's face did something he could not help. "Yeah?"

"Two weeks. He wants to know if the canoe is fixed." She said it lightly, but her eyes were on him. "He wants to know about the lake. He's been asking about the lake for a month."

"He's not going to live here," Percy said.

"I know he's not going to live here."

"I know you know. I'm saying it because I need to say it out loud and Clarisse isn't listening." He rolled onto his back. The sky was a hard blue dome, and high in it, very high, birds were working the thermals in slow circles. "He visits. He has a forge and a mother and about nine hundred brothers-in-arms under the sea and a whole life that doesn't have a Cabin Three bunk in it. He's not going to move in and sleep next to Clarisse and learn the meter from scratch."

"Nobody thinks he is."

"Fine."

"But he's coming, and you're allowed to be happy about it out loud, and you don't have to keep qualifying everything." Silena's voice was mild and absolutely immovable. "That's my whole opinion. I brought bread, I gave an opinion, I'm finished."

Percy floated. The water held him. It always held him; that was the joke, and it was the thing that had never once in his life been in doubt, and he had been slow to understand that other people spent their whole lives learning the world would hold them, and that his sisters had learned it late, and that it had cost them.

"Thank you," he said. "For the bread."

"You're welcome. Don't make it weird twice."

Clarisse came back past them at speed and slapped water hard enough to soak them both. "Two minutes," she announced. "That's the whole gap. That's all you're worth, Jackson, and I want the camp to know it."

"The camp is asleep," Percy called after her.

"Then the lake knows it." She was gone, stroking hard, and Percy rolled over and swam.

He got back to the sand second, by a body length, and lay on the warm stone of the canoe beach with his chest heaving, and Clarisse stood over him and said, "Close," which from Clarisse was the same as a hug.

They ate the good bread sitting in the sun with their feet in the shallows, and Silena drew, and Clarisse ate two pieces without speaking and then a third, and the heron in the cove went on standing on the bow of the sunken skiff as if it had been appointed to.

It was Percy who felt the storm first, and it was not because he was Poseidon's son.

He sat up with a heel of bread in his hand and looked east, at the flat hard smudge on the horizon that had been a rumor at dawn and was now a fact — a wall of blue-dark coming in low over the water, the line of it bending as it crossed the lake.

"Clarisse," he said.

"I see it."

"Sil—"

"I see it." Silena was already tying her braid back. "It's early."

"It's early," Percy agreed. He looked at the bread, and the bag, and his sisters, and the water. "She's out on the water."

"Annabeth can handle weather," Clarisse said. "She's a Sky Brat. It's her weather."

"She's out on a rock."

Clarisse stood up. She shaded her eyes against the glare and looked east along the shore, and Percy watched her do the arithmetic on her face: a mile and a half of shoreline, a front moving in hot and fast, and a sister of hers who was today, for once, somebody else's to worry about.

"Then let's hope Thalia's smart," Clarisse said.

"Thalia is very smart," Percy said. "Thalia is the smartest person I know after Annabeth, which is exactly the problem, because Annabeth is on that rock too."

"Then we go and help."

"Clarisse—"

"We go and help," Clarisse said, and picked up her boots, and started walking east along the shore at a pace that was very nearly a run. "Or we can sit here and finish the bread and be useful in about an hour when they're already soaked. Pick one, little brother. I've got towels in the cabin and I'm not carrying them in the rain."

Percy looked at Silena. Silena looked at Percy.

"She's right," Silena said. "She's annoying about it, but she's right." She handed him the bread bag. "Carry this. And Percy?"

"Yeah?"

"Run."

---

Thalia went first, because Thalia always went first, and she went first the way she did everything that mattered — without pause, without a count, without letting herself think about the drop.

She ran four steps along the warm gray stone and launched off the lip and went out into the air with her arms wide and the lake roaring up at her, and the cold took her whole body at once, a clean hard shock that drove every thought out of her head and left only the green. Her feet found the shelf. She kicked down past it, down into the dark, and then she was turning, rising, and the surface came up and broke and she came up in the middle of the green bowl gasping with her hair flat and streaming and laughing at nothing.

"Your turn!" she yelled at the rock.

Annabeth was standing at the lip with her arms wrapped around herself and her toes curled over the edge.

"Cold," Annabeth said.

"It's lake cold. It's the good kind."

"It's ninety degrees out."

"It's eighty-six. The lake is sixty-two and the lake is right." Thalia trod water and looked up. "You measured it. I watched you measure it. You looked at the rock and the water and you did the thing with your hands."

"I did not do the thing with my hands."

"You did the thing with your hands. Do the jump."

"I'm deciding."

"You decided an hour ago."

"I'm redeciding." Annabeth's toes curled tighter. "Thalia, what if it's shallow?"

"Then you'll scrape your knees and I'll carry you home and you'll be dramatic about it."

"What if I hit the shelf?"

"You won't hit the shelf. The shelf is four feet down and the shelf is the whole point. You go past it. You go—" Thalia demonstrated, treading water, and let her legs drop until only her head showed. "Like this. Like you're falling off the world. Then you kick."

Annabeth looked at the drop. Then she looked at Thalia, and Thalia watched her decide, and braced.

Annabeth jumped.

It was not a beautiful jump. It was a jump from somebody who had decided and then tried to take the decision back halfway through, and she came in flat and hard, and the cold slammed the breath out of her, and she went under still flailing.

Thalia got to her in four strokes.

Annabeth came up on her own, spluttering, hair in her face, eyes wide and betrayed. "It's— it's freezing, it's—"

"It's lake cold."

"It's freezing freshwater, that's what it is, it's—" And then she started laughing. She laughed the way she laughed when something had been frightening and was over: all at once, undefended, with water going in her mouth. She pushed her hair back and trod water and looked at Thalia with her face streaming and said, "I hit the shelf."

"You hit the shelf."

"I felt it. It's right there."

"I told you. You tell me things and I don't believe you until they happen." Annabeth kicked, and found the rhythm, and stopped fighting the water enough to let it hold her. "How do you do that?"

"Do what?"

"Just be in it. You're not even swimming. You're just— being in it."

Thalia rolled onto her back and floated, arms out, and the sky went above her, huge and white at the edges with the sun coming through her eyelids.

"I learned in a quarry," she said. "There was a swimming hole near the— near the house. Rope over a branch, and a shelf, and a cold spring that fed it. Annabeth, it was disgusting. It had frogs in it. I loved it so much."

"Did we go?"

"You were too small."

"Were you allowed?"

Thalia kept floating. The water moved under her in its slow way, and the rock was warm on her skin where it had been out of the sun, and a gull went over, high and crying.

"Nobody was allowed anything," she said. "That was the point of it. Nobody was watching, so nobody could say no." She rolled back over and swam. "Come on. Deep end."

They swam out past the rock to where the bottom went away, and Thalia lay on her back in the middle of the green bowl and let the lake take her weight, and Annabeth copied her, badly, and then better.

"You're rigid," Thalia said, without opening her eyes. "You're swimming like you're being graded."

"I am being graded. You're grading me."

"I'm watching you be tense for no reason." Thalia opened one eye. "Loosen your shoulders. Let your head go back. Stop holding your stomach in."

"I am not holding my stomach in."

"You are. You are always holding your stomach in." Thalia reached out and pushed gently on her sister's shoulder blade until Annabeth's spine went flatter against the water. "There. There. Now you're a plank."

"I'm a plank."

"You're a beautiful plank. You're the flattest plank on the lake."

Annabeth floated. The water held her. Her face was turned up to the white sky and her hair spread out around her head like a fan and her hands drifted just under the surface, and for a while neither of them said anything at all, and the only sounds were the small slap of water against stone and the far-off drone of insects in the pines behind them and, very far off, the clang of the pavilion kitchen at work on lunch.

Thalia watched the sky instead of her sister. She had learned to do that. She had learned it at twelve and perfected it at thirteen and it had served her well, and now it felt like a wall she had built out of the side of her eye.

"You're watching me out of the corner of your eye," Annabeth said, without moving.

"I'm watching the sky."

"You're watching the sky and me."

Thalia rolled over and swam. "I'm checking," she said. "I check."

"I know you check."

"It's a habit."

"It's a system." Annabeth sat up in the water, waist-deep, and pushed her hair back with both hands. Her camp shirt was clinging to her shoulders and her shorts were dark with wet and she looked fourteen and cross and entirely unbothered about being cold. "Thalia. How many times a day do you check me?"

"What kind of question is that?"

"How many times."

"Annabeth—"

"How. Many. Times." Annabeth was not yelling. She was asking with a precision that was worse than yelling. "Because I counted. Wednesday I counted. I paid attention all day, and I counted thirty-one, and that's not counting the ones where you moved between me and something, and it's not counting the ones where you asked me a question just to hear my voice so you'd know where I was. Thirty-one. In one day."

Thalia stopped swimming. She stood up. The lake came to her hips and she put her hands on her hips and looked at her sister across the green water and understood, with a sinking that had nothing to do with the lake, that she had brought them onto this rock to give Annabeth a gift and had instead handed her a reckoning.

"Thirty-one," Thalia repeated.

"Thirty-one."

"Is it— is it not okay? Is it not allowed?" Thalia heard her own voice go up and made herself bring it down. "Somebody in this family is supposed to check. I've been doing it since before you could swim. Why is it suddenly a crime?"

"It's not a crime." Annabeth's voice cracked and she steadied it. "It's just— it's a lot. It's a lot of me. It's thirty-one times a day of me being something you carry around, and I'm fourteen, and I have a job, and I have students, and I can't walk from here to the pavilion without you knowing where I am." She was breathing hard. "I let you do it. I've always let you do it. But Thalia, I can't— you can't keep checking me. Not because you want to protect me. Because of what it does to you."

"What does it do to me?"

"You disappear." Annabeth said it quietly. "That's what happens. You check me thirty-one times a day and somewhere in there you vanish, and then one day I look up and Thalia is a job. Thalia is a checkpoint. Thalia is somebody's older sister who does not have one single thing in her life that is hers, and I didn't ask for that, and I don't want it, and I think you've been using me so you don't have to find out what you want."

Thalia stood in the lake. The wind had changed. It was warmer now, and it was pushing small waves against the rock, and the sky to the east had gone that flat hard color that meant the front was coming in early and fast.

"I don't vanish," she said.

"You do."

"I don't—" Thalia stopped. She looked at her sister, at the set jaw and the wet shirt and the fourteen years of being carried, and she said, "Fine."

"Fine?"

"Fine. You're right."

"Thalia—"

"Don't do that. Don't go soft because I agreed with you." Thalia waded toward the rock. She came up out of the water, streaming, and sat down on the hot stone with her knees up and her arms around them, and she looked at the lake and did not look at her sister. "You're right. You're right. I don't have anything that's mine. I have a cabin I share and a spear I'm good with and a job that consists of standing next to you making sure the world doesn't touch you. That's the list. That's the whole list."

Annabeth came out of the water and sat down next to her, not touching, hugging her own knees.

"I'm not saying it to hurt you," Annabeth said.

"I know."

"I'm saying it because I'm about to be somebody who doesn't need you, and I don't want you to find out by falling over."

Thalia laughed. It came out wet and ugly. "Yeah. That's the part I'm scared of. Not that you'll stop loving me. That you'll stop needing me, and then I'll be standing in a cabin with a spear and no job." She rested her forehead on her knees. "Chiron said I have to be known. Not needed. Known. I've been chewing on it for a week and I still don't know how to do it, and every time I try I just check you thirty-two times instead."

Annabeth was quiet. Then she said, "What would you do? If you weren't checking me."

"I don't know."

"If you knew. If somebody told you that for one year Annabeth is absolutely safe and doesn't need anybody, and you can do anything. What would you do?"

Thalia raised her head. She looked at the lake, and the rock, and the dead pine, and the sky.

"I'd walk the boundary," she said.

"The boundary."

"The whole thing. Not a patrol. Not two hours a week with a harpy yelling at me." Thalia's voice changed, got faster, got real. "Chiron's got a survey — the old line, the stone markers, the ones that have moved since the last time anybody walked them. He asked me in June. He asked me and I said no because I couldn't leave you for four days."

"Thalia," Annabeth said. "You said no."

"I said no."

"Because of me."

"Because of me."

Annabeth turned and looked at her, and her face was doing something Thalia could not read, and then it resolved into something worse: pity.

"Thalia," Annabeth said, "go walk the boundary."

"It's four days."

"Go walk the boundary." Annabeth's voice was fierce. "Go walk the whole line and come back and tell me what moved, and I'll put it on the map, and I'll put it on the map in a different color, and it will be the best part of the map, and you will have done it. You'll have done one thing that was yours."

Thalia's throat closed. She looked at the stone.

"It's a stupid idea."

"It's a good idea."

"It's four days of trees."

"It's four days of your own life."

Thalia sat with that. The wind pushed against her wet back and the heat was building in the rock under her and the lake slapped at the shelf below. She had not known, until that moment, how long it had been since anybody had told her she was allowed to want something that was not somebody else's safety.

"I'll ask him," she said. Her voice came out rough. "I'll ask him tonight."

"Good."

"I'm not promising."

"I don't want a promise. I want you to go."

Thalia nodded at the stone. She could not look at her sister. She had the sudden, piercing, unhelpful memory of a seven-year-old in a strange camp standing on the edge of this rock declaring the lake acceptable, and she thought: I did that. That was mine. And nobody is ever going to know it but me, because you don't remember, and it will die with me.

The thought went through her like weather. She let it.

"Annabeth," she said. "Come here."

---

Annabeth went.

She did not think about it, which for Annabeth was a weather event all its own. She got up off the warm stone and walked the three steps and sat down against her sister's shoulder, and Thalia's arm came around her and pulled, and the flannel came off Thalia's waist and went around Annabeth's shoulders, and it was warm where Thalia had been warm, and it smelled like ozone and pine sap and old rain out of a canoe. Annabeth pulled the sleeves down over her hands and did not say anything for a while, and neither did Thalia, and the lake worked away at the stone below them in its slow patient way.

The wind came up while they sat there.

It came the way a front comes off water — not gradually, but all at once, like a door opening. The temperature dropped ten degrees in the time it took Annabeth to notice it. The pines behind them went from green-black to silver-black, every needle turning at once, and the sound of the water on the rock changed, and the air filled up with the smell of it: warm dust, wet stone, and under all of it the clean knife-edge of ozone.

"Here it comes," Thalia said.

"I know."

"Did you feel it this morning?"

Annabeth nodded. Her eyes were west, where over the tops of the pines a wall of bronze-dark had come up so fast it seemed to lean. "I felt it at breakfast. It was a rumor then. It's not a rumor now. Ten minutes, maybe less. We won't make the cabins."

The first drops were enormous. They struck the stone in separate, spaced-out reports, big as quarters, cold as coins — and then they were not spaced out at all.

They got up. The path back ran along the inland end of the Slate, beneath the dead pine — the one that had been struck through years ago and had gone on standing out of pure stubbornness, leaning out over the trail with its roots half-torn from the rock. The wind took it. It leaned further and groaned, and the long crack up its trunk opened and closed like a mouth.

"Thalia," Annabeth said.

"I see it."

"If that comes down on the path—"

"It's a dead tree. It's not going to—"

The pine cracked again. A limb the size of a fence post tore loose and hit the stone with a sound like a drum, skittered, and lay across the trail.

Thalia moved without thinking. She put herself between Annabeth and the tree so fast and so completely that for a second neither of them said anything, and then Annabeth stepped around her.

"No," Annabeth said. "Move."

"Annabeth—"

"Move. Please." Her voice was not loud. The rain was loud enough for both of them, coming down now in a gray roaring sheet, and the lake had gone white along the shore. "You don't get to do this today. You don't get to have one day where you decide something about yourself and then stand in front of a tree."

"Then what do I do?"

"You watch."

Annabeth turned and faced the pine.

She had done this before. Small things. A hair on her arm standing up when her father was in a room. A spark off a doorknob in January. A night in June when Thalia had held out her hand and Annabeth had put hers against it and felt the static come up her own arm and had not known what to do with it. That was the whole of it. That was the curriculum of Annabeth Grace's sky, and it was not much, and she had never once wanted it to be more.

She wanted it now.

She spread her fingers and felt the air go strange around them, the pressure dropping, the fine hairs on her wrist lifting one at a time. Somewhere above the bronze-dark a charge was waiting to be spent, and it was her father's, and it was hers too, in the small inherited way that a surname is a thing. She reached for it the way she reached for a pencil in the dark — by feel, by memory, by knowing exactly where it would be.

She did not pull it down. She asked.

A thread of it came. It was no thicker than her thumb and it went from the base of the cloud to the top of the dead pine in a white instant, and the sound was not thunder. It was a crack like a spine, and the pine split from the crown down through the lean and stopped four feet short of the path, and both halves went over, and the branches hit the stone, and the smell of scorched wood rolled over them, and Annabeth was still standing with her arm out and her hand open.

She lowered it.

Everything was very quiet. The rain roared. Her arm would not stop shaking and she let it shake.

"Annabeth." Thalia's voice was behind her, very close. "Annabeth, look at me."

"I'm looking at you."

"Are you hurt?"

"No."

"You're shaking."

"I'm always shaking." Annabeth turned around. Her sister's face was white and her mouth was set and both her hands were up, hovering an inch from Annabeth's arms, not touching, and Annabeth saw the effort it took to keep them there. "You didn't touch me."

"No."

"You wanted to."

"Yes." Thalia's hands stayed where they were, open, empty. "I wanted to. I decided not to."

"Why?"

"Because you asked me to watch." Thalia's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Because you walked around me. Because—" She stopped. The rain ran off her chin. "Because it was yours. The bolt. The tree. The four feet of path that isn't crushed. That was yours, and I'm not going to put my hands on it."

Annabeth looked at the split pine. Then at her sister. Then she reached out and took one of Thalia's hovering hands, and pulled, and put it flat against her own chest, over her heart, which was going like a bird.

"Put it here," Annabeth said. "Put it wherever you want. Just— ask first. That's all. Ask."

Thalia's hand closed gently around her shoulder. "Can I?"

"Yeah."

"Can I walk you home in the rain?"

"Yeah."

"Can I tell Clarisse you did this, in detail, with sound effects, until she's sick of it?"

Annabeth laughed, and it came out shaking. "No."

"You're no fun." Thalia's hand moved, just enough to be steadying. "Can I carry your shoes?"

"I'm wearing my shoes."

"Then can I carry you?"

"I can walk."

"Can I walk next to you?"

"Yes," Annabeth said. "That's the one you're allowed to do forever. That one's free."

---

The rain had not let up when they started back east, and Annabeth went first, because she had decided to go first, and because it was a shorter sentence in her head than the alternative.

The path came down through the pines to the canoe beach, and the camp was doing its evening thing around them in the wet — lanterns coming on in the pavilion, the smell of dinner and wet pine and hot soup, somebody's guitar going up under a shelter, the lake gray-green and ridged and loud.

Clarisse was waiting on the sand with a stack of towels over one arm and her boots already off. She took one look at Annabeth coming down the path with the flannel soaked through and her hair flat to her skull. "Where's the tree."

"On the trail," Thalia said, behind her. "Split. In two pieces. Four feet of clear path between them."

"Annabeth."

"Annabeth."

Clarisse set the towels down on a dry driftwood log. She looked at Annabeth for a long moment with an expression that had nothing joking in it anywhere in it, and then she picked up a towel and dropped it over Annabeth's head and rubbed hard enough to move her.

"You're an idiot," Clarisse said, through the towel, with real affection in it. "You're a show-off. You're a Sky Brat with a death wish."

"I was efficient," Annabeth said, muffled.

"You were showing off for your sister."

"I was mitigating a hazard."

"You were both." Clarisse stepped back and looked up the shore, in the direction of the split pine, and shook her head. "Silena's making soup. Get dry before you catch something and I have to listen to you complain about it."

Percy came down the beach with a towel of his own, moving easily through the wet sand, and stopped in front of Annabeth and took hold of the flannel's collar between two fingers and turned it out to look at the sleeve.

"Nice shirt," he said.

"It's not mine."

"It's not yours."

"It's a loan."

"It's a loan." His mouth did the thing it did when he was being extremely fair about something he was not remotely fair about. "From who?"

"From my sister, who is standing right here, and who would like you to know that she has already given it to me, so you can take it up with her."

"Don't make it weird," Thalia said.

"I'm not making it weird. I'm appreciating it."

"Don't appreciate it either." Thalia came down the path wet to the knees. She untied the flannel's sleeves from around Annabeth's shoulders, took it by the shoulders, shook it out once, and put it back around her, and this time she knotted the sleeves at the front instead of behind, and stepped back, and looked at the result with her head tilted. "It suits you. You've been stealing it for two years anyway. You might as well have it honestly."

"I have not—"

"You have. Every time it rained. Every time you got cold. You've got a whole drawer of things you borrowed and forgot to give back." Thalia was grinning now, unguarded, the grin that had nothing to do with being a protector. "That's fine. I've got one too. Ask Clarisse about the green jacket."

"The green jacket was a gift," Clarisse said.

"It was a loan."

"It was a gift and I'll fight you."

"You'll lose."

Annabeth pulled the flannel tighter and let them have it. The rain came down across the beach in a gray sheet and the lake slapped at the stones and the four of them stood there in it, and then Percy took her hand, and Clarisse gathered the towels, and they started up the beach toward the pavilion lights.

Behind them the lake went on doing its evening work against the stone, and the heron went on standing on the bow of the sunken skiff in the cove, and above the ridge the bronze-dark came apart into stars.

---

Thalia watched her sister go up the beach and did not follow.

She stood in the wet sand with her boots in her hand and the last of the rain coming down off her hair in threads, and she watched Annabeth walk ahead of her — not fast, not looking back, with Percy's hand in hers and her shoulders square under a flannel shirt that no longer belonged to anybody else. She watched the distance open between them. She felt the old reflex come up in her throat, the one that said *close the gap, take the front, know where she is*, and she recognized it for what it was, and she let it pass over her like weather.

You can stop carrying her, she thought. She will still be there.

She was not sure she believed it. She was sure enough to stand still.

Clarisse came back down the path with a canvas bag over her shoulder and stopped beside her, and the two of them stood together and looked at the lake.

"She's got good aim," Clarisse said.

"She's got terrible aim," Thalia said. "She's got guts and a working theory of electricity and a habit of hitting things flat."

"That's the same thing."

"It's not the same thing." Thalia slung her boots over her shoulder and started up the path. "You're coming with me."

"Where?"

"To see Chiron."

"Now?"

"Now. Before I lose my nerve." She went up three steps and stopped and looked back. "Clarisse. Come on. I'm asking."

Clarisse looked at her for a second with her eyebrows up. Then she shouldered the bag and followed, and the two of them went up through the wet pines toward the Big House, and Thalia's boots knocked together against her back with every step, and she did not once look back at the lake.

She had, she thought, four days of trees to go and get.

---
