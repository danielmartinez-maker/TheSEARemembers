## Chapter 167: What Sharpens Together

The frost came on Sunday night and stayed, and the whole east side of camp woke up white.

Percy knew it before he opened his eyes, the way he knew weather by the ground's voice: a change in pitch under the cabin floor, earth gone tight and cold, the shells mortared into the Cabin Three walls ticking faintly like cooling glass. His window had gone the flat gray-blue of a sky that had stopped arguing. He sat up. His breath went out in a pale rope, hung, drifted apart.

Five in the morning. Monday. The morning camp went back to it.

He dressed without waking anyone — camp shirt, jeans, sneakers, the fleece he had left folded on the end of his bunk since September arrived and had never once needed. Now he needed it. He pulled it on, laced his shoes quiet, and went out.

The frost had done its work everywhere at once. The switchback path had gone silver along its wheel ruts, and the pavilion rails were beaded white and dripping. The lake lay at the bottom of the slope flat as poured metal, steam lifting off the shallows in long low veils where the air met water still warmer than the morning. A jay went through the pines making a noise like a rusted hinge. Somebody's kitchen fire had been lit before him — the smoke came over straight and thin, and it smelled all the way up the hill of wet pine and ash and bread.

He took the long way down through the cabins. Cabin One's windows were dark with one lamp lit at the back. The Athena cabin had a rack of wet boot-prints leading out and not back, which meant somebody had already started the day badly. Cabin Eight's forge never slept; it glowed orange through the shutter slits and put its iron-musk onto the cold air. The arena sat at the bottom of the slope with its gate standing open, sand inside still holding the frost in shadow, and past it the canoe beach and the white line of the dock going out into the steam.

Percy reached the pavilion just as the conch went.

It sounded different in cold. Fuller. The note carried up the hillside between the cabins instead of scattering into the trees, and the answers came back from every direction at once — doors, voices, the clang of a dropped bucket. He had been at camp five months and he still could not hear that thing without a small animal part of him sitting up straight.

The pavilion was fuller than it had been all week. The visit week had rearranged things: extra folding chairs out from the back wall, the long Poseidon table set for five again. Campers filled the benches in their cabins' clumps, breath going up, sandals and boots and bare toes on the cold planks. Silena was already at the far end of the Poseidon table with a clay jug in both hands, sleeves pushed up, hair pinned, steam coming off whatever was in it. Clarisse was last to arrive and came in through the side door with her spear and a look that said she had been up since four.

Thalia came down the aisle behind her, damp-combed, in her flannel open over a camp shirt, and dropped into the bench beside her sister with the whole boneless weight of a person who intended to be fed. Annabeth was already there. She had her field book open, a pencil behind her ear, and a smear of graphite on the cuff of a fleece that had been Percy's in July and had quietly stopped being his. He did not comment. Some things were filed under *happened* and required no signature.

"Gentlemen, ladies, nonbinary heroes, and whatever the Hermes cabin has become this week," Chiron said. He rolled in from the Big House door with his reading glasses on and his blanket across his lap and a stack of paper on the arm of the chair, and the pavilion settled. "Autumn term begins this morning, which means the summer's improvisations are over and we go back to doing things properly. For those of you who arrived before July —" a small general laugh, because a third of the pavilion had "— that means lessons at seven, drills after, chores after that, and nobody eats until the chores are walked. For the rest of you, welcome. I have been assured that the water is wet and the woods are full."

He set down the stack and put on the expression he used when he was about to say something that was a lesson dressed as a schedule.

"Three things, and then I'll let you eat before it gets cold. First. The days are getting short. Between now and the middle of October the camp belongs to the sun, and after that it belongs to whoever keeps the fires. Second." He tapped the paper. "The autumn work list is posted on the Big House door. Canoes out and stowed before the end of the month. Swim buoys in. Practice weapons oiled and racked, not leaned. Anything in the orchard that can't take a frost comes in this week — and yes, Mr. Stoll, that includes the pears you have been guarding since August. Silena is running the pantry side and she has my full authority, which I am telling you now so that later you may weep."

"Does she have authority over the pears?" somebody called.

"Silena has authority over pears, fire, winter squash, and the souls of men," Chiron said, without turning. "She has had it since June."

"Third." He waited until the pavilion had gone quiet in the particular way it did when he was about to hand them something they would keep. "You have all spent a summer getting good at things. Some of you got good at them in a hurry, and some of you got good at them in front of witnesses, and one of you —" he glanced down the Poseidon table, and his mouth did the thing it did "— got good at the lightning catch in front of his girlfriend, which is the only kind of getting good that ever impressed anybody. Continue that. But hear me on the difference between practice and performance."

He let the pause sit. Somewhere outside a gull went over. The jug at the Poseidon table steamed.

"Practice is where you are allowed to be bad on purpose," Chiron said. "It is the only room in the world with that property. Out there, you get one take and no notes. Which means the work of this term is not to learn more things. It is to make the things you already learned survive cold weather, tired legs, short days, and an audience. It is to make them hold."

He picked up his stack. "That's the sermon. Lessons in forty minutes. Drills at eight-thirty. Clarisse — armory and the weapons racks are yours, and you will have help. Percy, the buoys with Clarisse. Silena, pantry, and the harvest table. Thalia, boundary posts on the south creek, with a partner and no solo walking. Annabeth —" he smiled, and it was a real one, the one that had learned in the last few months how to be a teacher instead of a headmaster "— the map is yours, and the ridge is off-limits without a second set of eyes on the water. Chaperoned or not at all."

"It's not a date," Annabeth said, precisely, to her field book. "It's a survey."

"That's what they all say," Chiron said, and rolled out.

The pavilion detonated into breakfast.

"You look like you slept in a lake," Clarisse said, arriving at Percy's elbow with a plate in one hand and her spear over her shoulder.

"You look like you slept in a scowl."

"I slept in a scowl. It was warm." She set the plate down and shouldered the spear properly, and for a second she stood there with her jaw working, doing the calculation she did before she said anything that cost her. "You're doing the buoys with me. Don't be slow. I want them in before eleven so the armor—"

"So the arena is yours all morning," Percy finished.

"So the arena is mine all morning," she agreed. "Silena's bringing the good jug. Wear the fleece, it's cold, and take your boots off before you step on my sand."

"Your sand."

"Everything in that arena is my sand. You're renting." She punched his shoulder, hard enough to move him and not hard enough to matter, and went to get a second plate.

The arena at six-forty was the coldest place at camp, and Percy had it entirely to himself.

He stood in the gate for a moment, letting the cold have him. The frost was still holding in the sand wherever the light hadn't reached, a faint silver crust that broke underfoot in thin pale flakes and then stopped existing. The dummies had been turned around to face each other like they were sharing secrets, and the whole place smelled like leather and tallow and the faint dry sweetness of the boundary. Beyond the east wall, forty yards off, the granite shelf rose out of the ground in a long flat slab with a low stone parapet along its back edge, scrubbed bare by a hundred seasons of weather.

He started moving. Ankle circles, hip openers, the shoulder rolls Clarisse had taught him first and never bothered to correct, because she said you fixed a body's outside before you touched its inside. The cold made everything honest. His left hip was tight from sitting on the dock. His right wrist was fine. His lungs took the cold air in two steps and gave it back in one, and by the time the sun cleared the pines and came over the wall he was sweating under the fleece and had taken it off and dropped it on the bleachers, where it lay orange and absurd and faintly steaming.

Clarisse came in at seven with two practice spears and a canvas bucket of water and set both down without ceremony.

"You're early," she said.

"You're standing in the corner I wanted."

"That corner is yours now. Pick another corner." She tossed him one of the spears. "Warm?"

"Warm."

"You're lying. Do it again."

"I did it again."

"Do it a third time and then we'll spar." She peeled off her own fleece — dark green, older than the summer, mended at one elbow — and dropped it beside his, which she had somehow managed to make look like a judgment. "I want to see where the last three weeks left you. I've been on your back about footwork all summer and I haven't let you win a single round since June. That ends today, because you're going to win rounds whether I allow it or not, and I want to know what it costs me."

"Are you saying you'll go easy?"

"I'm saying I'm going to find out," Clarisse said, "and if I go easy, you'll never know."

They squared up in the middle of the sand. The sun had come over the wall and was hitting the east face, and the frost in the shadow of the parapet went from silver to nothing. She set her feet. He set his.

"To seven," she said. "Clean hits only. No shoulders, no head, no hands. We stop the second one of us says stop. Say all of it back."

"To seven. Clean hits only. We stop the second one of us says stop."

"Good. Now show me your guard." She circled. He circled. The sand gritted under boots and sneakers, two different sounds. "Lower. Your arms are fences, not shields. Lower. Good. Don't get used to me saying that, it'll spoil you. Three, two—"

She came in fast and low, a flat sweep at his leading knee, and he hopped it and felt the wind of the shaft go under his foot, and then she had reversed the arc the way she always did, which was the thing he still could not follow, and the tip kissed his ribs once and his hip once and she was back out of reach before he had finished understanding what she had done.

"Seven to nothing," she said. "Three, two—"

He did better on the second round. He had been expecting high and she had gone low, and he had learned, somewhere in all the tufts of thinking, to stop reading her arms and start reading her feet, and when her weight shifted onto the front foot he was already moving, already inside the line of her reach, and he caught her once on the forearm with the flat of his shaft as he came out. It didn't count. It also did something to her face that he would be remembering in December.

"Seven-one," she said. "Again, and that time don't get cute."

"You said lower."

"I said a lot of things. You listened to one of them and got excited."

"Silena says I should listen to one thing at a time. She says I try to eat the whole loaf."

"Silena says wise things about bread and nothing about your guard." She reset. "Feet. Not her arms. Her feet are hands. Her feet are a whole person telling on her. Again."

Round four lasted a long time by the standards of rounds that had, so far, lasted between four and eleven seconds. He gave ground when she pressed, which he had learned to do without flinching; he traded a tap on the shoulder for a tap on the thigh and counted it as a victory he would not mention; and then he overreached, because overreaching was the shape of him, and she punished it with a flat two-count to the ribs that drove the air out of him and had him standing in the sand with his hands on his knees, gasping, grinning at the ground.

"Seven-two," she said, and her voice had a note in it she had not put there on purpose. "Get up. You're not hurt."

"I'm dying."

"You're fourteen, you don't get to die on a Monday. Get up before I tell Silena you've gone soft and she writes it in the book."

Percy straightened up. He wiped his face on the hem of his shirt and looked at his sister standing six feet away with the practice spear loose in one hand, her breath going up in white ropes, her braid coming loose at the end, the sun catching the side of her face.

"You missed one," he said. "In round four. I had your hip."

"You had my wind, you mean."

"No. I mean your hip. It was a clean line and I didn't take it because I was looking at your spear."

Clarisse went very still.

"Yeah," she said at last. "Yeah, you did. Don't do that again." She turned away and drank from the bucket, and came back wiping her mouth with the back of her wrist, and her expression had rearranged itself into something with far fewer doors in it. "You counted one on me. Out loud. Since June. That's a thing." She shrugged, a small lift of one shoulder that in anybody else would have been a flinch. "Fine. Write it on your hand if you need to. Come on."

"Which way?"

"Toward me," Clarisse said. "Always toward me. Never away. If you're running backward you've already decided you're going to lose, and you're not allowed to decide that before I have."

They worked until the sun had warmed the sand and the frost was a rumor, and the tally in her head went up and up, and somewhere in the neighborhood of eleven to three she stopped counting, which Percy noticed, and did not mention, because he was learning which things to say out loud.

It was Silena who saw it first, from the bleachers, where she had been sitting for forty minutes with a jug of cider going cold in the shade and a honey cake on a napkin on her knee.

She had come down with a canvas bag over one shoulder and her old wooden camp knife in her belt for cutting fruit, and she had climbed to the third row without either of them hearing her arrive. Silena Beauregard was, at sixteen, the quietest person at camp who also knew everything, and she had spent five Septembers arranging it that way. She sat. She watched. She set the cider in the sun so the cold would come off it.

Her notebook was out. On the left page, in her small upright hand, the score. On the right page, only the date, the time, the words *Arena, Monday, first frost*, and a small drawing of a spear propped against the sand.

"Percy," Clarisse called. "Your back foot. You're pointing it out like you're waiting for a bus."

"I'm waiting for a bus."

"The bus is your guard. Are you waiting for it or holding it?"

"Both?"

"Holding. Hold it like it's your sister." She turned to the bleachers. "Silena. How long have you been up there?"

"Long enough to know you've gone soft."

"I have not gone soft. I've barely touched him. He looks like a wrung-out dishrag."

"You're down to eleven-three," Silena said. "And you stopped calling it at six."

Clarisse's jaw set. "I've been counting since six."

"Mm. That's why you stopped saying it." She tipped her chin down and let the sun find her face. "Clarisse. Come here. Both of you come up here, the cider's gone cold in the shade and you're both disgusting."

Percy came up the bleachers three at a time, which Clarisse said was an insult to stairs, and sat on the row below her. Clarisse climbed the two steps slowly, took the honey cake, tore it in half without asking, and put the bigger half in front of her brother. Then she took the smaller half, sat, and exhaled onto her hands.

"Cider," Silena said, pouring. "Drink before you get cold. You're both steaming and I'm not treating anybody for hypothermia before eight in the morning on the first week of term."

Percy drank. It was cold and sharp and had cinnamon in it that had been sitting since Sunday, and it went down him like a bell. He made a face. "That's the good jug," he said.

"That's the good jug. I brought it because my sister's gloating was audible from the top row." Silena passed the pear she had been slicing — thin wedges on a cloth, the juice beading on the green. "Eat. Both of you. I have the harvest table at ten and I need you alive for it."

"You're a tyrant," Clarisse said, taking three wedges.

"I'm a pantry manager with authority over pears, fire, winter squash, and the souls of men," Silena said. "Chiron said so in front of everybody."

"He said it in front of everybody," Clarisse confirmed. "I was there. It was a whole thing." She ate a wedge. Then another. Then she said, "He blocks better than he did Thursday. That's not — that's just a fact, I'm not making it a thing."

"It's not a thing," Silena agreed, and did not laugh, which was a kindness.

Percy looked between them with pear juice on his chin. "You two know I can hear you."

"Obviously," Clarisse said.

"Good," Silena said. "We'd hate for you to learn it secondhand."

Percy ate another wedge and watched the sand. Clarisse sat beside him with her elbows on her knees and her breath still coming faster than the work justified, and Silena watched the two of them with the unhurried patience of somebody who had decided a long time ago that this family was worth the full price of her attention.

She remembered — and this was what the notebook's blank right page was for — the first September she had ever watched Clarisse teach a lesson. It had been to a Hermes kid named Nate who had taken Clarisse's spear badly, and Clarisse had taken his arm roughly, and told him that was what happened when you held a weapon like a spoon. Nate had cried. Clarisse had walked away. Silena had found him afterward and given him a biscuit and lied to him about the future, and that had been the whole sum of Clarisse's teaching in those days: the instruction, and then the walking away. Five years, now. Five years of watching the walking away get slower.

"That," Silena said now, quietly, into the cold air, "was patient teaching."

"It was Tuesday," Clarisse said.

"It was patient." Silena turned the cider jug in her hands. "You let him find the round himself. You gave him the feet and you made him find the rest, and then you didn't say a word about it. In June, you would have said it loudly in front of everybody and then gone to the armory for two hours and been furious at yourself by lunch."

"My armory shift's at ten. I've already done my two hours."

"Clarisse."

"What?" Clarisse looked at her, defiant, hands around the cup. "What do you want me to say? He's better. He's better than he was Thursday, he's better than he was in August, and I didn't do that, he did that, I just stood here with a stick and gave him somewhere to put it."

"Yes," Silena said. "That's the whole job."

The silence after that sat down between them on the bleachers and got comfortable.

"He asked me something on Thursday," Clarisse said, to her cider. "In the armory. He asked whether I was proud of him." She stopped. Her jaw worked. "And I said no."

Percy, on the row below, went very still.

"You said no," Silena repeated.

"I said *I'll tell you when you're worth being proud of, minnow.* That's what I said." She looked up, and her eyes were the flat, dangerous, dry-eyed eyes of somebody who had been carrying a stone in her shoe for four days. "And he laughed. He did that thing where he makes a joke to cover the hole, and he said *so Thursday's off the table,* and we moved on. We oiled the spears." She put the cup down on the plank beside her, carefully, as if the plank might object. "And I went to bed and I have not slept right since."

"Clarisse," Silena said. It was all she had room for.

"So I'm fixing it today. That's what this morning is. That's why I stopped calling the count." Her chin came up, stubborn and wet-eyed and furious all at once. "He doesn't need me to tell him he's good. He knows it, everybody at camp knows it, they watch him hold the sky like it's fireworks. What he doesn't have is anybody who'll say it to his face on a cold Monday when he's lost eleven rounds and he's got cider on his chin." She glanced down at her brother, who had the good sense to be examining his sneakers. "That's what I'm fixing."

Percy cleared his throat. "I'm still here."

"You're still here," Clarisse agreed.

"You're both being worse than usual about this," he said. "You're being so—" He stopped, because his voice had gone. He let it go. He picked up the honey cake, looked at it, put half of it back. "Okay. Say the thing you're fixing. I can wait, I'm fourteen, I'm immortal."

"I'm not saying it now. You've got sand in your hair and I'm not competing with the sand." Clarisse stood up. She grabbed her spear, and the canvas bucket, and the tray of practice weapons they would need at ten, and she had to do something with her hands and her face both, so she solved it by taking the honey cake Percy had been holding and shoving the whole thing into his chest. "Eat. Drink the cider. When we're done today — when we're done, and not before, because I'll die — I'll say it properly, standing up, like a person."

"It's a date," Percy said.

"It's not a date. It's a threat."

Silena reached down and put her hand over the top of Clarisse's wrist where it rested on the back of the bench, and then took it away again, and left it cold where she had been. "I'm going to the pavilion," she said. "Harvest table at ten. I'll be back at twelve and you'll both be better by then, and if you're not I'll tell everybody in camp exactly who cried at the cider jug, and it was Clarisse, it was Clarisse and nobody else."

"Nobody's going to believe you."

"Nobody ever has." She slung the bag over her shoulder, tucked her notebook inside it, and started down the bleachers, going one step at a time now, in no hurry at all, and at the bottom she stopped and looked back up at them in the cold autumn light with her wool hat slightly askew. "Clarisse. For what it's worth — and I'm only saying it once, because you're unbearable — you are the best teacher in this camp. And that is not a consolation prize. That is just the fact."

Then she went out through the gate, and the gate swung, and the sand was cold, and Clarisse stood with a spear in her hand for longer than was reasonable.

"She's awful," Percy said.

"Yeah."

"Also right."

"Also right."

Forty yards east of the arena, the granite shelf went up out of the ground in a long flat slab that had been scrubbed bare by a hundred years of weather and would outlive all of them, and Annabeth Grace had claimed it at half past eight with the same deliberateness she brought to everything.

She had been coming out here for weeks. The first time, in the middle of the summer, she had gotten there twenty minutes early and spent all twenty of them terrified, and had held a single spark for four seconds and nearly cried when it died. She had learned, since then, the thing Thalia had been trying to teach her from the beginning: that the charge was not a thing to be caught but a guest to be conducted, and that the fear arrived anyway, and the work was the work of letting it in the door and showing it the way out.

Today she had set up properly.

The copper coil was on her right wrist, wound three times, tied with the knot both she and Percy had learned in the same places. Her field book sat open on the flat stone where the wind could not take it, weighted with a rock the size of a fist. Beside it, in a neat row, the instruments of the morning: a length of insulated copper wire, a brass spike, a strip of beeswax, and a wooden mallet for driving the spike into the ground. She was wearing long pants and boots and her own fleece, and her hair was in a braid, and her breath went up in the cold air in front of her while she checked the sky.

Nothing overhead. No pine, no leaner, no branch of any kind. Ground down and out, never through her. Nothing downrange.

She drove the brass spike into the packed dirt at the shelf's edge with three taps of the mallet, wrapped one end of the copper wire around it, and ran the other end up onto the stone. Then she sat down on the cold rock with her back against the parapet and held her hands out in front of her, palms up, and let the hair on her arms stand.

"Annabeth."

"Thalia," Annabeth said, without turning. "You're late."

"I'm here. Late is a clock's opinion."

Thalia came up from the direction of the arena with her spear on her shoulder and her hair in its usual state of rebellion, the ends lifting off her collar with a static that had no business being visible in daylight. She had put on a second layer since breakfast, a wool vest over her flannel, and she had a pair of oranges in her jacket pocket and an expression that Annabeth had learned to read at a distance. Her sister was not, strictly speaking, here to teach her.

"Twelve seconds yesterday," Thalia said, dropping down onto the parapet and letting it hold her weight. "Twelve seconds, clean discharge, twice in a row, and you didn't even tell me. I had to hear it from Silena."

"Silena has a filing system," Annabeth said. "She has several."

"She has a complex."

"She has a ledger and it has your name in it."

Thalia grinned. "What's it say?"

"That's privileged information available for the price of an orange."

"You're a shakedown artist." Thalia tossed one over anyway, and Annabeth caught it against her chest and set it on the rock to warm. "Fine. Today's the shape lesson. You can hold a thing and put it down. That's adult behavior and I'm impressed, but holding is only half of it. Now you move it."

"I know what today's lesson is. You told me Thursday."

"And yet you haven't started, which means you're sitting there going through it all again in your head so you can do it right the first time." Thalia leaned back on her hands and looked out across the arena at the two orange-shirted figures working in the sand below. "Which, for the record, is the exact problem we discussed on the hot rock."

"I'm going through it because I'm going to be handling lightning, Thalia. Not soup."

"The lightning is not going to know the difference."

"No," Annabeth said. "But the ground will."

Thalia snorted. "That's the most you sentence I've ever heard. Say it slower so I can admire the syllables."

From below, across the sand, a shout arrived. Clarisse, both hands cupped around her mouth, the way she did when she had something she wanted everybody within a quarter mile to hear: "Hey! Sky! You're supposed to be supervising!"

"I am supervising!"

"You've been here eleven minutes and you haven't looked over here once!"

Thalia hauled herself upright on the parapet, entirely unabashed, and set her spear in the crook of her elbow. "I have a highly developed peripheral vision, La Rue! It takes in everything! Everything the size of a person!"

"Boyfriend-shaped things included?" Clarisse called.

"Especially those!"

Annabeth closed her eyes for a second, and opened them, and stood up. She put her hands out in front of her. Her palms were sun-brown and steady and there was a scar on the left one from a boat cleat she had pulled loose in July in the middle of a storm, and she could see it out of the corner of her eye and did not look at it, because looking at it would have meant looking at it for a while.

She let the static come.

It came the way it always came now, differently than it had in the beginning — not a surprise but a greeting. A thin white heat crawled out of her skin and up her forearms and gathered at her elbows, where it waited, patient, bright, the color of a filament in a light bulb. She felt the cold of it in the small pocket of air just above her wrists. She counted her breaths and did not grab, and she was the hallway, and the guest was in it.

"Ground," she said out loud, because saying things out loud was how she kept them true.

She sank her weight, one foot back, knees soft, and opened the back door, and the charge went down out of her hands and into the copper wire, and the wire lit from end to end like a pulled thread, and it went to the brass spike and went into the earth, and the earth took it without comment. The stone beneath her boots went dark with a scorch the size of a thumbprint. The smell of a struck match arrived a second later on the cold air.

"Six seconds," Thalia said, from the parapet, without turning around.

"Seven," Annabeth said.

"Six and a half. Don't inflate your logs, they're the only honest thing about you."

Annabeth sat down. She made the entry in her field book: *Granite shelf, Monday, 07:30. Hold 7s. Discharge clean via wire to brass spike, driven 8 in. Ground took it. Air temp ~38. Wind calm.* Then, because she was who she was, she added: *Temperature matters. Cold holds a charge better. Log the cold days separately.*

"Again," Thalia said. "And this time when you get it into the wire, take it somewhere. Push it. It wants to go where you're not looking. Look at it and it'll go where you are."

"There is no map for this," Annabeth said.

"There's no map for anything. That's why you make one."

The second run was slower in coming and she let it be slower, because patience was the whole lesson, because the difference between the girl she had been in June and the girl she was now was exactly the distance between rushing a thing and letting it arrive. The static gathered. She held. She counted seven, and at seven it began to fray at the edges, coming apart in slow bright curls, and every part of her wanted to close her hands and rescue it, and she did not. She sank her weight, opened the door, and shoved — not grabbed, shoved, gently, the way you steer a canoe with a paddle instead of dragging it — and the charge went down into the wire and around a loop she had left deliberately slack, and came out the other end and went into the ground in a second small scorch, and the wire smoked for three seconds afterward.

Annabeth sat on the cold granite with her hands empty and her heart going like a fist on a door.

"Nine," Thalia said. "Nine and you threaded it. That's the one nobody ever gets on the first day of the season. Clarisse! Genius nearly made fire out of a rock!"

"She's got a wire and a spike in the ground!" Clarisse called back, without stopping what she was doing. "That's not impressive, that's plumbing!"

"I'm telling it as a story!"

"Tell it accurate!"

Annabeth laughed. She leaned forward and put her forehead against her knees, because her whole body had been operating at a frequency she had no name for, and now it was coming down, and the coming down was the best part, the part she had been afraid of for months and now wanted. Her hands were shaking. She let them shake. She made the log entry anyway, in her small upright hand: *Hold 9s. Threaded the slack loop. Discharge clean. Hands shaking after. Noted without judgment.*

It was while she was writing that she looked up and found Thalia.

Her sister had gone quiet. She was standing with her back against the parapet, arms folded, spear wedged between her boots, watching the arena through a screen of pine with her head tilted and her eyes sharp and entirely undivided, and the posture was not the posture of somebody gossiping. It was the posture of somebody counting exits. Annabeth, who had been learning Thalia for five years and had only in the last months started to learn her again, recognized it instantly: the not-watching of a person who is watching so hard they have stopped doing anything else at all.

She turned and looked where Thalia was looking.

Down in the arena, Clarisse had Percy backed against the west wall. His guard was up where it belonged. His feet were wide and light and doing the surprising thing they had learned to do, which was to be quiet. He gave ground, took a tap on the shoulder, turned it, and came back with a short two-count that Clarisse only just got her spear up in time to stop, and then Clarisse laughed — a single loud bark of it, the surprised one — and came at him with everything she had.

Annabeth watched the two orange shapes of them go around each other in the cold sand, and something in her chest arranged itself into a shape she had a name for.

"Thalia," she said quietly.

"Yeah."

"They're going to be at this till noon."

"Probably till one."

"Are you going to keep not-watching them, or are you going to gossip with me about the harvest table?"

Thalia considered the question with the gravity of a treaty negotiation. "I can do both. I contain multitudes."

"You contain one version of this and it's Clarisse."

"Clarisse is better at it than you'd think." Thalia pushed off the parapet and came down onto the rock beside her, sitting with her boots hanging over the edge, and for a while the two of them sat in the cold with their shoulders a hand's width apart. "She talked to you?"

"About the sparring?"

"About being scared of teaching him."

Annabeth put her pencil down. "Yes," she said. "She'd deny it in court and then do it again on Tuesday."

"She's good at it. That's what she doesn't forgive herself for." Thalia picked up a pebble and turned it over. "She was the meanest thing at this camp when we were small, and she learned to be a teacher out of spite, and now she's a better one than Chiron and I refuse to allow her to find out."

"She already knows. Silena told her."

"Silena told her *today*?"

"Clarisse told her Thursday. She's been carrying it." Annabeth looked down at her hands. "I think I'm doing the same thing with him. Building something where he can't get hurt, and calling it love."

"Maybe," Thalia said. "Or maybe you're building the thing that will hold him up later. There's a difference and only he gets to tell you which one it is." She tossed the pebble off the shelf, where it went rolling down into the dry grass. "Go on. Again. And this time I'm genuinely going to watch, because if you fry an eyebrow off I have to answer to Silena, and Silena is scarier than either of us."

"That's a true statement," Annabeth said, and put her hands out.

She held it for eleven seconds.

It was only when she had discharged it clean, and driven a second nail, and made the entry in her own small upright hand, that she let herself look down at the arena again, and found that Percy had stopped in the middle of a reset to look up at her — a quick thing, a glance lifted over the wall — and had not been watching where he was going, and had taken Clarisse's practice spear flat across the back of the thigh for it.

"Again?" Clarisse called, delighted, from far below.

"Again!" Annabeth called back, grinning.

"Concentrate!" Thalia called, in exactly the voice of somebody concentrating enormously.

---

The water break came at eleven, and Percy came down off the sand into the shade of the big oak with steam coming off his shoulders and his whole self ringing like a struck pot.

He had, he thought, done somewhere between nine and twelve rounds in that sand, and lost eleven of them, and taken one clean count on his sister that had sounded like a bell going off in her head, and he had spent the last hour learning to read her feet. He was not tired in the way that worried people. He was tired the way a whetstone is worn — not used up, but changed, shaved down into something with a finer edge than it had had before.

The oak stood at the arena's east edge where the ground fell away toward the French drain, and its roots had heaved up a shelf of stone ages ago that made a bench just the right height. Percy sat down on it, put his elbows on his knees, and let his head hang. The cold air came down off the ridge and went over him, and the sweat went cold on his back, and he shivered once, hard, and then again, and then it was fine.

He filled the tin cup from the arena spigot. The water came out icy and tasted of iron and ran over his wrist and took the sand off him one grain at a time.

"Eight rounds," Annabeth said, from the grass behind him. "I counted from the shelf. You lost eight."

"I counted nine."

"I don't count your losses. That's Clarisse's hobby."

"I lose them all," Percy said. "That's also her hobby. She does it professionally."

"She lost one."

"One out of nine."

Annabeth came around the side of the bench and sat down beside him. She had her fleece zipped to the throat and her field book under one arm and the copper coil bright on her wrist, and her braid had come half loose in the cold dry wind, and she smelled like struck matches and cold stone. She looked him over — the wet line of his hair, the camp shirt dark across the shoulders, the way his hands hung between his knees — with the exact precision she brought to everything she intended to keep.

"You're steaming," she said.

"I'm a hot person."

"You're a puddle with opinions. Move over."

He moved. She put two fingers on the side of his neck, briefly, the way she checked him when she had decided not to ask permission for it, and then took the cup out of his hand and held it against his forearm until he made a noise.

"That's cold."

"Yes. Good." She gave him the cup back and leaned sideways until her shoulder found his. "Clarisse's gloating. How bad?"

"She said the words *I'm not going easy anymore* twice. She's terrible at it. She gloats like she's delivering bad news to the Senate." He drank. "Also she's been watching me all morning like a hawk with an opinion. And she's been nice. In front of Silena. It was disturbing."

"Mm. Silena said you'd lost eleven rounds and that Clarisse pretended she hadn't noticed."

"Silena has a complex."

"Silena has a ledger and it has our names in it." Annabeth bumped him. "I like her terminology, actually. *A threat, not a date.*"

"It's not a date, it's a threat," Percy said, in a passable imitation of his sister, which was not passable at all, and they both grinned, and the grass hissed in the wind.

For a while they sat and did not say anything. The arena went on behind them: the scuff of boots, the wood of practice spears, Clarisse's voice calling out things that sounded like insults and were, in this light, obviously something else. Far off, the conch sounded at the pavilion, and somebody's drum answered it, and the smell of woodsmoke came across the flat in the cold.

Annabeth put her hand in his lap, palm up.

"Give me your hand," she said.

"Mine?"

"Is there another one going spare?"

He put his hand in hers. It was a mess — sand in the creases, the palms roughed white and shiny where the sword and the spear had worn them, a fresh red line across the base of the third finger from where a practice shaft had grazed him, and the knuckles swollen faintly from the summer's work. Her fingers were cold and dry and smaller than his and entirely steady.

"Hydrodynamics," she said, and ran her thumb along the callus on his palm.

"That's not what you were going to call it."

"Nothing's what I was going to call it." She turned his hand over, and with one fingertip traced the line of the palm slowly, following the shape of it the way she followed a contour on paper — not reading a fortune. Reading a tool. "This one. Here. The heel of it. That's new. That's the Atlas."

"Is it?"

"It's where you put the weight." Her thumb pressed gently, and he let it, and his hand lay open in the cold air. "This one is the sword. This one is the spear — you've been drawing with the spear more, I can tell, look at the angle. And this one—" She touched the graze on his third finger, lightly, once. "That's from this morning."

"Clarisse did it."

"Clarisse, or the sand?"

"Clarisse, with the sand."

"Mm." She traced the knuckle scar, the cleat scar from July, the small white half-moons where a rope had burned him in the canoe. She found the line of his wrist and pressed flat against it, and he felt the pulse of it under her palm and did not say anything, because they both knew what they were doing and saying it would have been too much.

"You're getting a map," she said at last. "A whole one. I've been drawing people my whole life and I've never been able to do this." She turned his hand over once more, gently, and lifted it, and put her mouth briefly against the center of the palm — not a kiss for romance. A kiss for inventory. A way of saying *this is what I have of you and I have counted it and I know what every part of it is for.* "I'm going to draw this later. Labeled. Properly. With the calluses marked and everything."

"You'd label my hand."

"I would label your hand in three separate places and then apologize for it later." She set his hand back down and curled her fingers into it. "You grew."

"Since Monday?"

"Since the summer. Your feet are different. You used to put them down like you were apologizing to the ground."

"That's a very Clarisse thing to say."

"Clarisse said it in July. I stole it and improved it."

Percy looked down at their hands. The copper coil was warm against the outside of his, and her knuckles were graphite-stained even now, even in October, even after two seasons of washing. He brought his other hand up and took a stray strand of her hair that had come loose and wound it around his finger, gently, the way you would do something that wasn't being paid attention to, and let it hang there.

"Annabeth."

"Yeah."

"Can I kiss you?"

She turned her head. Her eyes were the color of the deep part of the lake under the dock in November, and her mouth was doing the thing it did when she had been planning something all morning and had decided to allow it. "Yeah," she said. "That one's free. All of that one's free."

He kissed her then, in the shade, with the oak root under them and the arena going on behind them and the smell of struck matches on her collar. He turned them both sideways against the stone and took her chin in the hand she had just labeled, because he wanted the hand connected to it, and she put her free hand flat against his chest over his heart and held on, the way she did when she wanted to feel the thing she was doing.

It was unhurried. It had that quality they had been earning all summer — no hurry anywhere in it, no urgency, no argument. The cold air went over the heat of his face and the cold of her hands went into his shirt. She tasted of cider and iron and the cold. Somewhere in the middle of it she made a small sound and adjusted her mouth against his and got closer, which was a thing she did now that she had not done in June, and he felt it down to the soles of his feet.

He drew back an inch. Foreheads touching. Her hand still over his heart, his hand still under her jaw, the strand of hair still around his finger. Both of them breathing the same cold air.

"Hi," he said.

"Hi." Her mouth had gone red. Her eyes had gone the color of the sky just before a storm, only better. "Ten minutes."

"Retroactive?"

"Retroactive, free for Poseidon cabin, double for—"

"—for the boyfriend who's winning rounds," he finished. "Silena's a tyrant and I'm going to marry her."

"Don't let Silena hear you say that, she'll have the table set by Tuesday."

"She can marry me. I'd be a good husband, I water things."

"You water one thing. Aggressively." She kissed him once more, briefly, on the corner of the mouth, and then pulled back and straightened his collar in a way that was nobody's business but hers, and let her hand slide down into his and lace there.

He leaned back against the stone and looked at her and knew exactly what he had, which was: a girl who had taken his hand apart to see how it worked and had put it back together with the pieces named, and had told him, in a language she had invented, that the pieces were good.

There was a noise from the gate.

Clarisse came through it with her spear down and her face arranged into something that had clearly been rehearsed during the walk. She stopped in front of them, looked at the oak root, looked at their joined hands, looked at the sky, and settled her jaw.

"Don't get up," she said, to Percy, "you look like you'd fall over."

"I'm fine."

"Nobody cares. This'll take a second." She set the butt of her spear in the sand. She took a breath through her nose that Percy heard all the way from where he sat, and she put her hands on her hips, and she said, to the ground: "You were better today than you were Thursday. You were better this morning than you were all summer, and you were better in the last hour than you were at breakfast. That's not me being nice. That's arithmetic. I did the arithmetic while I was drinking and I was right."

Percy didn't move.

"That's it," Clarisse said, more loudly, to his face now, and there was a wet, furious, terrible light in her eyes. "That's the thing. Are you going to make me say it twice? I'm not saying it twice."

"I'm not—" He stopped. Started again. "You already did. You said it."

"And you heard it, and I felt it, and now we're going to never speak of this again, and if you tell anybody I'll tell them you cried at the cider jug, which you did."

"I cried at the cider jug."

"You did, and Silena knows, and Silena has complex."

Annabeth made a sound. It took a second for Percy to realize it was a laugh.

Clarisse's shoulders dropped about half an inch. Then she pulled her own sleeve down over her hand, which she had never once done, and kicked at a root with the toe of her boot.

"Go to the pavilion and eat something," she said, to Percy. "Noon. We've got buoys after lunch and I need you for the far one because you're the only one who can stay in the cold water that long without complaining about it on the record." She turned for the arena gate, had one hand on it, and paused with her back to them. "And, Percy. Thursday. You asked me if I was proud."

"I know," Percy said. "It's fine."

"I said no. That was the wrong thing to say. Not because you needed flattery. Because it wasn't true." She looked back over her shoulder, one second of it, the face under the mean face, entirely without armor. "I'm proud of you. I was proud of you Thursday. And I'm sorry."

Then she went into the arena and the gate swung and the sand was cold.

---

The buoys were the last hard work of the day, and they were the coldest.

The swimming area off the north beach had seven red-and-white buoys out on lines, and five more along the shallow shelf where the canoes beached, and all twelve had to come in before the end of the month, and the end of the month was eighteen days away, and Chiron had put them on the list personally, which meant nobody argued.

Percy went in first because going in first was his job.

The lake had let go of two degrees since Friday and it let go of the rest in stages as he walked out: the warm skin of the afternoon, then the tepid middle, then the honest cold underneath that had no summer in it at all. He stopped at his knees, then his waist, then his chest, and put his hands down into the water and felt the ground turn to sand and then to the rock shelf where the buoys were anchored, and then he went the rest of the way and let the cold have him, and his whole self went very quiet, and the lake took his shape the way it always did.

"Twelve," Clarisse called from the shore, with her sleeves already rolled and a coil of wet rope over her shoulder. "Twelve lines, twelve anchors, and I want the boathouse swept before we start. Don't argue with me about sweeping."

"Nobody's arguing. Nobody's ever argued."

"Percy argued about sweeping on Tuesday."

"Percy was right on Tuesday."

"Percy was *enthusiastic* on Tuesday. Get to the far one first. It's the deep one and the deep one's yours."

He swam out.

The far buoy sat forty yards off the rock shelf, out past the boundary stone with its white X, and under him the lake went from green to a darkness that had no bottom a man could name. The line ran down into it, taut as a held breath, and the anchor came up hand over hand with the weeds hanging off it and a small silver fish going away in a panic.

The cold was the part nobody understood who didn't love it. It went into him and didn't come back out, and his muscles stopped arguing after four minutes, and the lake took everything down out of his head — Clarisse's apology, Annabeth's hand, the count of the rounds — and gave back nothing, which was the point.

By the eighth buoy his hands had gone to two numb claws and he had to use his forearms, and the world narrowed to rope and rock and the smell of weeds and the sound of his own breath stopping at the edge of the water and going back out of him as steam.

"This is the last one," he said, coming up at the tenth with a mouthful of lake.

"You've got two more," Clarisse said, from waist-deep, taking the rope off him with the exact same knot she used for everything that had to hold.

"Then this is the second-to-last one."

"It's the tenth one. Say the number and don't be a poet."

"It's the tenth one."

"Good."

She was standing in the shallows with her pants rolled to the knee and her boots in the sand, and her arms were red to the elbow, and she had been in and out of the water all afternoon and had not once complained on the record. Percy looked at her. She was looking at the rope, doing the coil.

"You're not bad at this," she said, without looking up. "The coiling. You're slower than me but you're thorough, which is what you need, so it balances."

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me, I'm grading you."

"Grading me was the last thing you did. You're going to run out of things."

"I've got a list."

At the dock, somebody was sitting on the end piling with their heels in the water, and Percy looked up and found a person who had no business being in upstate New York in October sitting there in the cold with a two-pronged spear across his knees and a cloak of deep-water green, watching them with the unhurried approval of someone who had known them since before there were words for the things they were.

"Triton," Percy said.

"Percy." Triton turned the spear once in his hands. "Twelve buoys. For a lake this size." He looked at the lake. "It's not a large lake."

"It's the lake."

"It's a lake." He stood up, and stretched, and looked at the sunset coming on behind the pines, and looked down at the two of them in the shallows with their arms red and their rope coiled. "I came by because I had the morning free. The Emperor of the Currents wanted his tides adjusted and I told him I was busy."

"You told the Emperor of the Currents you were busy?"

"I said I had camp business, which is true, and camp business is the only thing Father tells me I'm allowed to be busy with." He came down off the piling and walked out onto the sand, and the cold didn't seem to reach him. "I watched you spar this morning. From the water behind the arena."

Percy froze. "How much of it?"

"Enough." Triton's mouth did the thing it did when he was about to be cruel and had decided not to be. "You stand well. Not as well as your sister. Nobody stands as well as your sister; she was built for it. But you stand like somebody who has stopped apologizing for the space he's in, and that is worth more than standing."

Percy stood in the lake to the waist with his arms wrapped around himself, because the cold had gotten past him, and because a sincere thing from Triton had the physical force of a wave.

"Thank you," he said, thicker than he'd meant to.

"I'm not staying." Triton speared the butt of his trident into the sand, and pulled him up out of the water by the elbow, and clapped him on the shoulder so hard his teeth clicked. "Keep hold of the rope, little brother. Don't let go of the people holding the other end."

Then he was gone — not walking away, not going into the water, just gone, with a smell of ozone and deep salt and the cold air moving into the space where he had been. The sea-hemmed bracelet Percy had made in June went cold against his wrist, and on the sand, where Triton had been standing, a single smooth white stone with a hole worn through it.

Clarisse picked it up and looked at it for a while.

"That's a good stone," she said, and put it in her own pocket, which was the only compliment she was going to make that afternoon.

Silena came down from the harvest table with a thermos against her chest and a tray of flatbread under one arm, and found them both dripping on the sand, and announced that the afternoon's chore list was now empty and anybody who came within reach of her before they were dry was going to get a towel to the face.

---

They ate on the warm stone at the north beach with the sun going down behind them, all five of them in the arrangement that had become theirs, and the flatbread went fast because it was still hot and had rosemary on it, and Annabeth had a smudge of graphite on her chin and did not care, and Clarisse had her arms around her knees and her chin on her arms and would not admit how tired she was even though she had been up since four.

"Friday," Silena said.

"What about Friday?" Thalia asked.

"Friday is the last warm swim." Silena was wrapped in the faded blue blanket with the thermos between her feet, warm and certain and utterly unbothered by anybody's opinions. "The lake's at fifty-one degrees. By the end of next week it'll be under fifty, and then it's the bracing hour, and the bracing hour is not a swim, the bracing hour is a penance. So: Friday. All five. Long swim. We go in at four and we come out when we're done."

"Who says so?" Clarisse said.

"The book says so."

"I'm against the book."

"The book is not a democracy." Silena looked at Percy. "What do you say?"

"I say Friday," Percy said, "and I say I want to go out to the boundary rock and back, and I say whoever stops first carries the canoe covers up."

"Nobody's carrying canoe covers anywhere," Clarisse said. "They're on the list for Tuesday."

"Then whoever stops first does the boathouse."

"I'm not doing the boathouse."

"Then come first," Percy said, and Annabeth laughed into the flatbread, and Thalia put a hand on Clarisse's head and did not move it when Clarisse tried to shake her off.

Annabeth got her field book out and wrote something in it, and then, because it was the sort of evening where things were said plainly, she read it out loud. "Friday, four o'clock. Last warm swim. North beach. Conditions logged: air forty-one, water fifty-one. Names: five. Terms: whoever stops first owns the boathouse, not a democracy." She looked up. "It's in the book now, so it's law."

"Then it's law," Silena said, satisfied.

"Then we all die of the cold," Clarisse said, and lay back on the stone and closed her eyes, and let Thalia steal the last of the flatbread.

Far down past the sandbar, in the willow cove, the heron shifted its weight on the bow of the sunken skiff and considered the whole of it — the five of them on the warm stone, the white buoys stacked on the sand, the steam going up off the lake, the frost already starting to silver the shadows. Then it folded one leg under itself the way it had every day of the summer, and settled, and kept its own counsel, the way it did.

Somebody at the crafts table had said that week that the panel in Chiron's office might take a fortieth mark before October. Annabeth had written it in the back of her book and never looked at it, and nobody chased it, and that was the whole story of it. Some things waited to be talked about.

---

Later, after the flatbread was gone and the thermos was empty and the shadows had gone long and blue across the beach, Percy sat on the end of the dock with his feet in the water and let the cold come up him.

He had, he thought, spent the day being sharpened, and spent better than that.

He had gone out onto the sand in the frost and been beaten eleven times by the person who loved him most directly, and she had taught him his own feet. He had watched Annabeth hold a charge of lightning for eleven seconds in the autumn air and put it into the ground on purpose, in front of everybody, with a diagram. He had watched Silena look at Clarisse for five years and finally say out loud what she had seen. And he had handed his sister a thing he was afraid to ask for, and she had given it to him in front of his girlfriend, standing in the cold, and he had not even had to ask twice.

Sharpening, he thought. That was the whole of it. Not the soft thing people meant when they said it. The other thing — the blade that takes an edge only when it comes against something harder, and the harder thing that is the same, and the whetstone that wears down into nothing and is happy. He had thought for years that being loved was a place you arrived at and stayed. What it turned out to be, in this camp and this lake and this year of his life, was a daily exchange: you offer the thing you have and the person you have it to offers back the thing they have, and both of you come off with a finer edge than you brought, and neither of you gets to keep the shape you started with.

That should have been terrifying.

He put his hands flat on the dock planks. The wood was cold. Under it, the lake was fifty-one degrees and going down, patient, enormous, honest, freshwater to the bottom, doing what it always did on a Monday in autumn, which was to keep the place for whoever came out to it.

"Percy."

Annabeth came down the ramp behind him with a mug in both hands and her fleece buttoned wrong and her hair down out of the braid, and sat beside him, and put one of the mugs into his hands.

"Cider," she said. "Silena's. It's the good jug. I traded for it."

"With what?"

"With the promise of a labeled drawing of the harvest table. She made me sign something." She leaned against his shoulder. "Cold?"

"Freezing."

"Good." She wrapped her hands around his mug and did not let go. "Silena says Friday. Four o'clock. All five. Swim until we're done."

"I heard. It's law."

"It's law," Annabeth agreed. "The book says so."

Percy lifted the cider and took a cautious sip. It was still too hot, though Annabeth had carried it all the way down the hill. She looked at his hands around the mug.

"You're going to need gloves if you're out here every evening."

"I own gloves."

"Owning them isn't wearing them."

"That's why I own several pairs."

She leaned toward him to check the knot on his jacket. He caught her wrist, gently, and kept her hand between his until she'd stopped trying to fix it.

"Friday's going to be cold," he said. "Are they really expecting everyone to swim at four?"

"They're expecting everyone to show up at four. Silena says the swimming depends on the weather."

"That part did not make it onto Clarisse's law."

"It's written on the reverse." She reached into her pocket and showed him the folded notice. Below the grand announcement, in Silena's rounded handwriting, were two sensible words: WARM TOWELS.

He laughed. Annabeth folded it away, then moved her mug farther from the edge of the boards. She had a survey to finish tomorrow, and Percy had agreed to help with the equipment check after breakfast. Neither task could be performed by staring at the lake until midnight.

"Ten minutes?" she suggested.

"Fifteen."

"Then we go up together."

He gave her an exaggerated sigh of surrender, and she settled against his shoulder. For those fifteen minutes they could be cold, together, and free of the need to turn a perfectly ordinary Monday into a promise that the rest of the week would be easy. The cider cooled enough to drink. When Annabeth stood, Percy followed without having to be asked twice.
