## Chapter 129: The Way Back

Morning light ran along the palace floor channels in thin gold lines, water carrying the light in ripples across shell-inlaid walls. Gulls cried somewhere above the dome, muffled to whispers, and the water tasted faintly of morning minerals, cool and clean. Percy sat on the guest bed's edge with his bag open, canvas worn soft at the corners, its strap frayed where his thumb always caught it, turning a small shell over in his hands. Twice he'd checked it for cracks, thumb tracing the spiral, feeling each ridge like a tiny step. He checked again, unable to leave it unexamined, the nacre cool then warming under his touch.

"You're going to wear a hole in it with your eyes," Annabeth said from the doorway.

Her green glass charm rested beneath her collar. She'd worn it through the whole visit, even when she didn't need it. She stepped in and held out a folded sketch. "Found your drawing of the north gallery."

"That's yours."

"You drew it."

"To show where the current bends."

"And I want to keep it." She tucked it into her bag. "Ready?"

"Ready to go back. Not sure I'm ready to leave."

"Both can be true."

"I know. Trying to remember that at the same time."

She took his hand, fingers cool from the palace air, thumb brushing once across his knuckles. "No need to get it perfect before breakfast." Her voice stayed low and amused, and the tightness in his chest loosened a fraction.

That made him smile. Across five palace days Clarisse had inspected guard stations, Silena had befriended a cook and a fish, and Annabeth had interrogated Triton about foundations. Welcome throughout. Nobody had asked them to stay.

Percy closed his bag and followed Annabeth toward the breakfast hall. Leaving still lay ahead, but it didn't have to be faced all at once.

---

Percy discovered the missing bag because a palace servant came to collect the guest-room linens.

She was a short woman with gray hair braided tightly against her head. She knocked twice, entered after Percy answered, and asked whether he had found everything he'd brought. He glanced around the nearly empty room and said yes. She looked at the inventory card on the desk.

"There's a second waterproof case assigned to this room."

"I only packed one."

"The second was delivered from the archive yesterday. The copying clerk signed it in."

Percy checked beneath the bed, opened the little cabinet and looked behind the chair. The case wasn't there. He had spent the last ten minutes being melancholy about leaving Atlantis and had apparently misplaced an official archive delivery in the process.

"Would it be in the corridor?" he asked.

"The attendant may have left it in the family library. If you'd like, I'll ask."

"I'll come with you. It's my problem if I forgot it."

She looked amused. "Things become everyone's problem when they're recorded against the wrong room. It's why we keep a list."

They went to the library together. The copying clerk found the case in a locked drawer, where Annabeth had asked him to keep it until her hand-drawn chart was dry. He opened it and showed Percy the contents: a public copy of the palace foundation diagram, an index identifying the revisions, and a written note about which details were not to be shared outside the household.

Percy felt a flash of irritation. Annabeth had been the one to request the case, but the attendant had addressed him because it was assigned to his room. He was still treated as the person responsible for arrangements whenever someone else didn't know whom to ask.

"Could you put Annabeth's name on the record?" he asked.

"Of course. It was entered under the room rather than the recipient."

"She's the recipient."

The clerk corrected the card. He didn't seem offended. Percy wondered how often he had accepted a convenience that made his friends dependent on him without noticing it was happening.

Annabeth arrived while they were checking the copy.

"I thought they were keeping that until breakfast," she said.

"They were. The record put it under my room."

"Oh." She looked at the corrected line. "Thank you."

Percy smiled. "I didn't do the hard part."

"You found the problem."

"The chamber attendant found the problem. I carried the paper."

"Then thank her."

He did. The woman nodded and continued collecting the linens, probably with dozens of other tasks waiting.

On their way back to the guest wing, Annabeth held the case by its handle and looked ahead.

"You don't have to chase every mistake that happens around me," she said.

"I know."

"But you did help this time."

"I didn't fix anything you couldn't have fixed yourself."

"That's not a reason to refuse help. It's a reason to ask who should make the decision."

Percy considered that. He was learning to tell the difference between being useful and making himself central. It was not as easy as Annabeth made it sound.

They reached the shared sitting room where Thalia was sorting a stack of letters. One addressed to Jason lay beside her. She wanted to confirm the route to Camp Jupiter before the morning dispatch. The palace attendants had offered to pass it through a fast sea relay and then into ordinary overland post.

"That sounds complicated," Percy said.

"It's his address," Thalia replied. "I'm not changing it because it would be easier to send it to somebody else."

Annabeth showed her the archive case. Thalia read the name on the corrected card and nodded, clearly pleased her sister was recognized as the one who had requested the plans.

Silena came out of her room with a small envelope of fibers and a folded letter addressed to the workshop supervisor. She had written to thank Lyris for amending the exhibition terms, but she hadn't accepted the agreement. She wanted to test her design at camp before making a commitment.

"Can that go with the dispatch?" she asked.

Thalia checked the envelope. "Different recipient. We'll need a different entry."

Silena brought it to the clerk herself. Percy didn't offer to carry it. She'd already chosen where it should go.

Clarisse appeared last. She had a training note, a borrowed wrap that needed returning and one spare practice glove she'd accidentally carried off from the court. She looked at the glove with a curse.

"How did that get in my bag?"

"Are you stealing royal equipment?" Percy asked.

"Shut up."

They walked back to the court and gave it to the instructor. The woman checked the number on the glove and thanked Clarisse for returning it before the next training group needed it.

"Will you practice that form at camp?" she asked.

"Only after Chiron looks at the changes," Clarisse said. "It wasn't designed for our sand."

The instructor approved the caution and wished her luck.

By the time they returned to the guest wing, there were fewer belongings to pack and more records complete. Percy had thought leaving would be a matter of folding a few shirts and saying goodbye. Instead the morning was full of small choices about who owned what and where each object was supposed to go next.

He liked that. It meant the visit had been real enough to leave ordinary work behind.

At the corridor window, Thalia stopped him.

"Your father said he might come to breakfast."

"Yes."

"Did he confirm?"

"No."

"Then I won't make Annabeth get ready for a meeting that hasn't been arranged."

"That's fair."

Percy could hear worry in her voice. He could also hear the decision not to turn the worry into an order. Zeus had given the Grace sisters enough reasons to distrust sudden invitations, and Thalia was trying to keep the sea visit from becoming another stage for their father's moods.

"Would it help if we asked the household steward?" he said.

"It would help if our father could communicate clearly," she answered.

Percy laughed, unexpectedly. "Mine too."

They walked toward breakfast together. No one had recovered an erased memory or resolved an ancient family grievance overnight. They had returned a glove, corrected a ledger entry and kept the letters addressed to the people who should actually receive them. It was a modestly successful morning.


---

Bread and salt scented the main hall, warm loaves exhaling into the cool water, butter pooling gold in their cracks. Poseidon and Triton stood near the terrace doors while Amphitrite sat with Clarisse and Silena over the route map, parchment softened at the folds, ink smelling faintly of iron. The blue mark for a shifting current looked small enough to be a stain, its edges feathered where water had once touched it.

"It should be bigger," Clarisse said.

"It is not to scale," Triton replied.

"That's no reason to make it hard to see."

Annabeth redrew the mark with a darker stroke. "There. Now you can explain the tide without sending us into the wrong channel."

Triton studied it. "Acceptable."

Percy took the seat between his sisters. Breakfast talk gathered around them: Clarisse said he ate too quickly, Silena denied feeding apricots to reef fish, and Triton reminded them hippocampi waited at the lower platform.

Poseidon repeated the plan, voice even and unhurried, hands resting lightly on the terrace rail. Chiron would meet them at the cove; the current would carry them there, steady and marked. "I repeat it because I've been told repeating a plan isn't the same as worrying."

"Who told you that?" Clarisse asked.

"Amphitrite."

"She's right," Silena said. "You can do both."

Poseidon nodded, then looked at Percy. "You can come back when you wish. No formal invitation required."

"I want to," Percy said. "Only don't know when."

"Then you needn't know yet," Amphitrite told him. The garden would keep growing while he was away. Ordinary reassurance — easier to believe for it.

Their meal stretched longer than necessary, cutlery chiming softly, chairs scraping now and then. Nobody rushed them or made a departure speech, the light shifting slow across the table. At last Triton produced the instant camera Silena had packed, its plastic casing cool and scuffed. The previous evening he'd photographed everyone in the garden courtyard: Poseidon, Amphitrite, Triton, Percy, Clarisse, Silena, Annabeth, and Thalia. Clarisse looked ready to argue with the camera; Silena leaned toward her; Annabeth's hand rested in Percy's, and Thalia had an arm around her sister. Amphitrite looked at them instead of the lens.

"I have a copy for the palace," Triton said, offering Percy the other print.

"Good picture," Annabeth said.

"Clarisse blinked," Percy said.

"I did not."

Silena touched the white edge. "I like that we're all in it."

"Me too," Clarisse said. Nobody asked her to explain.

At the lower platform hippocampi waited in harness, manes drifting in the current, eyes bright and patient. Amphitrite hugged Silena, then offered Clarisse a hand. Clarisse took it, brief and firm; her coral token tapped against camp beads. Poseidon clasped both girls' forearms before Percy stepped forward and hugged him.

"Write when you arrive," Poseidon said.

"I will."

"Not every mile."

"I know."

Triton clapped Percy's shoulder. "Eastern channel's open next time."

"After we mark it properly," Annabeth said.

"Of course."

Amphitrite kissed Annabeth's temple. Thalia embraced her, then stayed close at her side. Warm goodbyes, without requests to stay. Percy climbed into the chariot with Clarisse and Silena, looked back once at three figures beneath the arch, and lifted his hand. The hippocampi stepped into the current.

---

Cushioned on water, the chariot carried them through Atlantis, spray beading on skin and drying to salt that itched faintly at wrists and throats. The palace receded past market terraces smelling of kelp bread and iron, yeast and hot metal tangling in the current, the arch where Percy and Triton used to race, barnacles whitening its foot like frost, windows opening into deeper blue where office lights burned amber. Clarisse sat beside Percy with her spear between her knees, knuckles pale where she gripped the worn wood. Silena kept the photograph in its sleeve, checking its corners with careful fingertips, while Annabeth and Thalia checked the route map against passing landmarks, hair streaming like banners in the flow.

"The current bends sooner than this says," Thalia noted.

"It changes," Percy said.

"Then the map should change too."

"Annabeth can fix it when we're back."

"I can hear you," Annabeth said, already marking the margin.

Teasing faded as the city gave way to open water, light thinning to blue-green, the temperature dropping a degree against their cheeks. As a child Percy had traveled this route mostly the other way, pressed to the rail with salt stinging his eyes. Then the surface had seemed a larger world waiting, bright and impossibly wide. Now he returned to people who knew how to find him when he fell quiet, and the thought warmed him underneath the chill.

He rested his palm on the rail. The current slid beneath, cool and steady, the coral-inlaid rail smooth then pitted under his skin, humming faintly with the water's passage.

"You have something to say," Clarisse said.

"Probably."

"Then say it without making a speech," Silena told him.

Percy looked at Clarisse. "I keep thinking about the first time you walked through the palace doors."

Her brow creased. "Why?"

"Glad you were there. I'd imagined showing you those places, but didn't know if you'd hate it. Then you were there, and I felt relieved. Think I forgot it could be complicated for you."

"Not complicated all the time," she said.

"I know."

"Do you?"

He let the question sit. "Not all of it. I'm asking."

Clarisse turned toward the water, spray beading on her spear, droplets catching light along the shaft like tiny lenses. "Liked seeing you as a kid there. Not the prince part. Places where you'd been small. The pool you hid in. Marks on the wall where you and Triton measured height. The training room where you said you broke your elbow and your dad carried you back."

"Sprained it."

"Dramatic."

"Seven."

"Dramatic at twelve too."

He smiled, but Clarisse stayed serious. "Liked knowing you'd had people to take care of you. It made you make sense. But I won't pretend it didn't hurt. You had a father who came home. A mother who raised you. A palace that knew you. I know you didn't ask for that. I also know what it's like to wait for somebody who never shows, then hear about the life he gave someone else."

The harness creaked as hippocampi bore them onward. Percy had replies ready: memories of his other childhood, loneliness on recent palace visits, accounts of times his father hadn't been present. True, all of it — none of it her answer.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"For what?"

"For being lucky where you weren't. For talking about parents and childhood like stories I was excited to show you, and not remembering you didn't get the same things. I can't apologize for having them, and I won't pretend loving them was wrong. Sorry I didn't see what it cost you to be there with me."

Clarisse's fingers shifted on the spear. "Didn't want you apologizing for the palace."

"Sorry for my luck, not for being your brother."

She studied him, waiting for defense. Percy kept quiet, hands still on the rail.

"Liked how your mother asked what I wanted to do," she said. "And how she didn't act like I owed thanks every five minutes. Don't hear that and start trying to fix it."

"I won't."

"Already thinking of things you can do."

"I can try not to."

"Try listening first."

"I will."

Silena had stayed quiet without looking away or stepping between them. "Clarisse can be glad she went and still carry hurt," she said, voice low under the current's rush. "Percy can be glad he brought us and still have things he didn't know how to share. Neither feeling cancels the other."

Clarisse nodded. "Right."

"Not saying it's all right now," Silena added. "Don't want either feeling to be the only one you're allowed."

"Good," Clarisse said. "Because I'm not all right now."

"I know. Want me to sit with you, or want quiet?"

"Sit with me. Don't talk for a minute."

Silena moved beside her. She waited until Clarisse leaned shoulders together, then stayed still, photograph sheltered on her lap. Percy wanted to reach for Clarisse's hand, but didn't know if she wanted that. She'd asked him to listen first.

"Thanks for telling me," he said.

"Not done being mad."

"You don't have to be."

"And I might want to go back."

"You can."

"I said might."

"Heard you."

Clarisse nodded. No forgiveness, no promise. The current turned, hippocampi adjusting course with a flick of tails. Silena kept the photograph on her lap; Clarisse let her shoulder rest against her sister's. Percy had hoped the visit might prove all his family could fit together. Sharing a place, he understood now, didn't mean sharing every part of it. They could carry beauty and hurt at the same time.

---

The chariot slowed near the outer channel because one of its harness fittings began to click.

Percy heard it before he saw it. Every time the lead hippocamp pulled against the turn, the metal ring at the left yoke struck the frame with a sharp, uneven note. The animal didn't appear distressed, but Triton raised a hand to the driver.

"Hold here."

They came to rest beside a shelf of black rock. The current moved around them, steady enough to keep the chariot from drifting while the driver examined the harness. Percy leaned over the rail to see what was happening.

"Is it broken?"

"Not yet," the driver answered. "A retaining pin has worked loose. The harness will continue to pull, but the ring could twist if we take the sharper northern passage."

Clarisse was already standing. "We should unload it."

"Not everyone needs to get out," Triton said.

"I didn't say everyone. The heavy bags are at the front. Moving them will take strain off that side while he works."

The driver considered the weight distribution. "She's right."

Clarisse gave Percy a satisfied glance but didn't make a speech about it. She handed the heaviest pack to Silena, who passed it to the rear bench. Annabeth held the route map while Triton checked the spare fittings.

Percy wanted to mend the situation by smoothing the water. He could make the chariot feel lighter, carry it over the current and bring them to shore without touching the harness. The impulse was so strong that he could already feel the water gathering against his hands.

"Don't," Clarisse said quietly.

He looked at her.

"If you hold the load with your power, they can't tell whether the pin's actually secure. Let them fix it."

Percy released the current. It slipped back into the sea without moving the chariot.

"Right."

The driver used a small bronze tool to remove the loose pin. The metal had worn at the narrow end, enough that it could slip under repeated movement. Triton found a spare of the correct diameter and checked the fit before handing it over.

"Do these have a service limit?" Annabeth asked.

"They're inspected before departure," the driver said. "But some wear isn't visible until a fitting moves under load. We'll replace the opposite pin when we return."

"Will the chariot be safe to use now?"

"After we test it. Not before."

He fitted the new pin, tightened the retaining ring and asked Clarisse to hold the rear strap while he checked the alignment. The chariot rocked once. Silena gripped the rail, then laughed at herself when nothing else happened.

"I don't like being surprised by a moving vehicle," she said.

"Neither do I," Annabeth replied. "Especially underwater."

The driver climbed back into his place and had the hippocampi pull a short distance along the shelf. The ring stayed silent. He stopped again, checked the pin and made a note in the travel log.

"Clear to continue," he said. "We'll use the wider southern turn rather than the northern cut. It'll take a little longer, but I want the load steady."

Triton agreed. Percy looked at the map and calculated that the detour would put them on the coast later than Chiron expected.

"We should send a message," he said.

"I already have," Triton replied. "The coast watch knows our revised arrival window."

Percy stared at him. "When did you do that?"

"While you were examining the harness."

"You're annoyingly efficient."

"It is part of my charm."

Clarisse snorted. "Your charm is overdue for inspection."

The chariot moved on. Percy sat beside Silena at the back, the heavy pack between their feet. She was looking at the driver's entry in the travel log, where he'd written down the replaced pin and the change of route.

"Do you think the palace will actually inspect the other one?" she asked.

"Yes. The driver was the one who said it needed checking."

"Then somebody owns the job."

Percy liked the way she said it. No one had promised the universe would keep them safe because they were children of a sea god. A driver had discovered a worn pin, repaired it, selected a slower route and made a note for the next person who used the chariot.

Annabeth came to sit opposite them. She held the route map open and studied the path they were taking. The southern turn ran around a broad ridge before rising toward the coast. It wasn't the route they had expected, but the landmarks were clear enough for her to follow without asking Percy to explain every one.

"Will we see the old watchtower?" she asked Triton.

"Yes. From this side it is easier to recognize."

She pointed to the structure when it appeared, a dark column against paler rock with a row of faintly glowing markers at its base. Percy had passed it on a previous visit without noticing the pattern.

"Why does it have three lines of lights?"

"One for the local route, one for deep-water traffic, and one for visitors who need escort."

"That's useful," Annabeth said.

The next marker lay farther up the ridge. She was already looking for it before Triton identified it, and Percy felt an odd pleasure at seeing her become familiar with a place he'd worried would always require him as a guide.

At the top of the southern turn, the water lightened and the chariot began to rise. The driver adjusted the reins. The hippocampi followed the marked course without strain, their bodies moving in powerful, coordinated sweeps.

Silena leaned toward Percy.

"Are you sad to be leaving?"

"Some."

"Me too. I liked the workshop."

"I thought you were ready to get home."

"I am. I have something I want to test. I can't do that here."

He considered it. Silena had enjoyed Atlantis enough to want to return, but her next step required camp, a sewing table and people who could tell her whether her design worked in ordinary air.

"I guess I feel both," he said.

"Then you do."

She rested her hand on the pack between them. It wasn't meant to comfort him into feeling one thing instead of the other. She simply sat beside him while the chariot moved.

The driver called out a landmark ahead. Percy looked up and recognized the pale seam where the deeper water gave way to the coastal shelf. He'd been worried that the return would be a clean line separating two homes. Instead it had involved a broken pin, a detour, an ordinary conversation and a set of choices people had made to get them back.

It was less elegant than the direct route. He trusted it more.


---

The coast surfaced slowly, gulls first as white flecks and cries, their voices sharpening as air replaced water. First the ridge's dark line, then silver flash where a wave caught sun and threw it back in a dazzling blink, then shallower green where sea grass waved over rocks, shadows rippling like cloth in wind. The chariot climbed with the current, water warming degree by degree against their faces and bare arms. Pressure eased off Percy's shoulders, ears popping with a soft click. Beneath her collar Annabeth's charm glowed faintly as the surface drew near, light dancing across her collarbones, green-gold flickering with each breath she took.

"Going to miss how the light changes," she said, watching it fracture.

"You can see it again."

"I know. I can miss it and know that."

"Right."

She sat beside him now, others spread across benches. Thalia had taken the map from Annabeth and held it open, comparing pencil marks with water around them — the look she got guarding her sister while pretending to check the route.

"This current bends a little sooner than the map says," she observed.

"It's a current," Percy said. "Doesn't care about the map."

"It should try."

"I'll talk to it."

"You can't talk a current straight."

"I've talked it into a lot of things."

"Not straight."

Annabeth folded the map, noting the margin. "I'll redraw the bend at camp."

"You don't have to," Percy said.

"I want to. Not because I need your home in a notebook. I want to remember what I saw."

He studied her face. No uncertainty, though a faint line sat between her eyebrows, mark of an unfinished thought, and her thumb worried the frayed edge of the map without her noticing. The wind pressed her hair across her cheek, and she tucked it back with an absent, practiced motion.

"Feel strange?" he asked. "Being there?"

"Sometimes. Welcome throughout. Everyone made room. Your mother asked what I thought of the garden instead of what I thought of you. Your father talked to me like a person, not a symbol of Zeus."

"He liked you."

"Liked him too. And Triton."

"Throne room?"

"Not my favorite."

"Biting chair?"

"Still deciding."

"Chair was in a different room."

"That's why I'm still deciding."

She turned to water beyond the rail, wind lifting her hair. "Felt welcomed. Also never stopped knowing I visited your childhood. Glad you let me see it, and glad your family let me in. But I won't pretend I chose Atlantis for home."

"You don't have to."

"I chose Camp. Not because Atlantis wasn't kind. Because Camp is mine. Where Thalia and I grew up. Where I know the paths, and people expect me to leave tools on the wrong table and come back for them."

Thalia glanced over. "You don't leave them on the wrong table. You leave them on whichever table has most room."

"That's how things get lost."

"That's how camp tables get used."

"Glad it's yours," Percy said. "Don't want the palace pulling you from the place you chose."

"It doesn't." Annabeth reached for his hand. "And don't think I'm refusing your family by choosing camp. I can love the place because you love it. I can be glad they welcomed us. Glad to return."

He laced fingers with hers, salt drying on skin, leaving a faint gritty tightness across their knuckles. "Glad you came."

"I know."

"And glad we're going back."

"Know that too."

"You know a lot."

"Very observant."

"Also updating your map."

"Already planning to."

The chariot reached the cove entrance. Through a gap between low rocks hippocampi guided it into a sheltered inlet. Above, the surface opened in a circle of bright, restless blue. Beyond lay a crescent of pale sand, pines leaning over its edge. Near the waterline stood a figure, one hand raised against sun.

Chiron.

"He actually came," Clarisse said.

"He said he would," Percy replied.

"People say things."

"He came."

"I can see that."

Hippocampi slowed. Water gathered along the chariot's sides, turning it into a clear shell that rose with them. Sunlight flashed across the canopy. The last pressure gave way as the chariot broke the surface. The world snapped into air and wind and gull-cry.

Silena drew a deep breath and shut her eyes, salt air filling her lungs, gull-cry sharp overhead. Clarisse flexed fingers around the spear, leather grip creaking faintly. Annabeth lifted her face to sun, light warming her eyelids pink. Thalia stood first, ready to help everyone ashore, though nobody needed help, spray glittering on her jacket.

Chiron came down to the water's edge, coat dusty from the path, hair blown loose around his ears, sand crunching under his hooves with each deliberate step. "Welcome back."

"Been waiting?" Percy asked.

"I've been reading beneath that tree," Chiron said, nodding at the ridge pine. "Waiting was incidental."

"He's stood here an hour," Clarisse said.

"A little more than an hour," Chiron corrected. "The chapter was very interesting."

Percy stepped from the chariot onto sand. Ground shifted underfoot, almost surprising, grains grinding between his toes and clinging damp to his ankles. Not that he'd forgotten land. Only grown used to being held from every direction. Here weight belonged to him again, settling through heels and knees with each step.

He turned and offered Silena his hand. She took it, then helped Clarisse steady the bag strap. Annabeth and Thalia climbed out beside them. Hippocampi bowed their heads, and Percy rubbed one between its ears before it vanished into the inlet with a swirl.

"How was it?" Chiron asked.

"Beautiful," Silena said. "And weird. Fish aren't good at rules about food."

"That sounds like the fish."

Clarisse rolled her shoulder, sand crunching under boots. "Current was fine."

Percy glanced at her. "Fine?"

"Didn't try to drown me."

"High praise."

"Don't get used to it."

They walked up the beach toward the path. Beyond trees the camp hill stood familiar and green. Somewhere above, a conch sounded the afternoon meal. The call drifted down, rough-edged and unmistakably theirs.

"I can hear the pavilion," Silena said.

"You can hear the conch," Clarisse replied.

"Pavilion will be there when we get to it."

"Probably full of stew."

"Hope it's better than last stew."

"It'll be camp food," Percy said. "Lower expectations."

"Missed camp food," Annabeth said.

"Missed bad stew?"

"Missed knowing what was in it."

"Fair."

At the path's top movement flashed between cabins. Grover came running from the Big House lawn, one hand raised, pipes bumping at his hip. Ella flew just above him, feathers bright in afternoon sun, wings beating in quick, excited bursts. A smaller figure darted from behind the dining pavilion and charged down without waiting to be told to slow.

"Nico!" Percy called.

Nico skidded to a stop inches from sand, sneakers spraying grit, and looked up, dark hair sticking in three directions, cheeks flushed with running. He'd grown since Percy left — or coat sleeves had only shortened, cuffs riding above his wrists.

"You came back!" Nico said.

"We did." Percy crouched so Nico could fling arms around his neck. He did, hard enough Percy rocked back on heels.

"See a sea monster?" Nico asked against his shoulder.

"No."

"See a palace?"

"Yes."

"See a sea monster in the palace?"

"Not that I know of."

Nico pulled back and frowned. "Then you didn't look very hard."

Grover reached them, breathing a little fast. He hugged Percy first, then Silena and Clarisse, careful of bags and weapons. "Glad you're back. Was going to send a message down the coast, but Chiron said he had it under control."

"I did," Chiron said.

"I know. Still going to."

"Also why I told him not to," Chiron said.

Ella circled once, then landed on the path rail beside Percy. "Percy returned. Clarisse returned. Silena returned. Annabeth returned. Thalia returned. All travelers accounted for. Ella counted."

"You counted us before we arrived?" Silena asked.

"Ella counted your messages, then counted the days. Ella knew when you'd return, but Grover said not to stand on the beach all day."

Grover glanced at her. "Said it might be better not to stand on the beach all day."

"Ella stood for only some of it."

Percy laughed. "Missed you too."

Ella's expression softened. She stretched one wing toward his shoulder, then folded it to keep balance. "Ella missed Percy. Ella missed the others also. Ella kept their seats at dinner."

"There are assigned seats?" Clarisse asked.

"No," Ella said. "Ella kept them anyway."

Nico started questions before they'd gone ten steps: palace bigger than the Big House? Could fish climb stairs? Did Triton have a sword? A room where you could see to the ocean bottom? Percy answered as best he could, each answer breeding two new questions. Grover listened with practiced patience of someone long accustomed to being asked things he didn't know.

At the cabins camp gathered in its casual way when glad and trying not to make a ceremony of it. Someone called Percy's name from the pavilion. A younger camper asked Silena if she'd brought a seashell. Clarisse got asked about monsters; she said there had been fish, which was worse, and the camper looked unsure whether to believe her.

"Taking the bags to Cabin Three," Percy said. "Then dinner."

"We'll help," Annabeth said.

"I can carry my own bag," Clarisse said.

"So can I," Silena added.

"I know. Offering to carry the camera and this." Percy raised his own bag. "Not making a point."

Clarisse narrowed her eyes. "You always make a point when you say you're not making a point."

"Not true."

"Exactly true," Silena said.

They walked together to Cabin Three, gravel crunching, pine resin sharp in the heat, needles releasing their scent where the sun struck them. Outside looked as when they'd left: painted trident above the door, paint blistered by sun into small curls, blue-green stonework cool to the touch where shade pooled, bronze fish weather vane that never pointed into wind, squeaking faintly with each gust. Inside the fountain whispered in the corner, mint growing in the damp crack beside it, its leaves bruised sweet when Percy brushed past. Percy's bunk lay rumpled, practice clothes still hooked over a chair, one of Nico's old wooden dice on the shelf beside the window, showing three, its pips worn nearly smooth.

Not the palace. Never what made it matter.

He set his bag down and took out the photograph. The white border still held a little warmth from the ride. He studied faces again: parents, brother, sisters, girls who were sisters to each other, caught in a moment they'd not needed to perform.

"Where are you putting it?" Annabeth asked.

"Here, maybe." Percy held it to the wall above the shelf. "Don't want it ruined."

Clarisse surveyed the cabin. "It'll get smoke near the lamp."

"I can frame it."

"There's a frame in the supply closet," Silena said. "One with the chipped corner. Still good."

"Sure?"

"Checked."

Clarisse opened the closet and found the frame behind training blankets. She passed it to Percy. Together he and Silena eased the photograph under glass. The brass clip stuck, but held.

Percy hung it above his bunk. The picture sat crooked. He nudged one side until level.

"There," he said.

"It's good," Annabeth said.

"You don't think it should be in the Zeus cabin?" he asked her.

"No. It belongs here because you want it here." She looked at the photograph. "And room for another one."

"Next time," Silena said.

Clarisse gave her a sidelong look but didn't tell her not to say it. She touched the coral charm at her wrist, then lowered her hand. "We should decide the next visit."

"Not today," Percy said. "Just got back."

"Can plan without leaving today."

"That sounds like a trap," Annabeth said.

"It's a schedule," Clarisse replied. "Not a trap."

"Distinction isn't always obvious."

Silena took out her notebook. "We could list things we'd like to do next time, not dates. No promising a day before knowing what we want."

"Want the outer reef again," Clarisse said. "And where Percy measured height."

"Two places."

"I can want two things."

"Want the garden in a different season," Silena said. "And maybe meet the musician who plays shell flute."

"Already met him," Clarisse said.

"Want to hear him play a whole song."

"Reasonable."

Percy looked from the photograph over his bed to the people crowded in the cabin. Back without becoming the same. Clarisse's hurt still there. The invitation to return still theirs to accept or refuse. Annabeth hadn't traded one home for another. None of those truths needed hurrying into neat shape.

"Next time, eastern channel," he said. "If the current's right."

"And we'll mark it on the map," Annabeth said.

"In a color you can actually see," Clarisse added.

"And bring the camera," Silena said.

"Still in the bag," Percy said.

"Then bring the notebook too."

"And Ella," Nico said from the doorway. He'd followed them in and now peered at the picture. "Ella should come next time. She can count the fish."

Ella's feathers lifted. "Ella would like to see the fish. Ella would also like the fish counted correctly."

"They move," Percy said.

"Ella can count moving things."

"She's right," Grover said. "Ella can count anything."

"Ella counted breakfast rolls once," Percy said.

"There were twenty-seven," Ella replied.

"There were twenty-eight," Grover said.

"Grover ate one before the count ended."

"Hungry."

"That is why Ella counted twenty-seven."

"I stand corrected."

The cabin filled with overlapping voices and familiar arguments, everyone laughing. Percy let it wash around him. Home again — no need to forget where he'd been.

---

Chiron's request made Percy reach into the wrong pocket for the third time. In the palace everything had been counted when a gate opened. At camp he was already forgetting which bag held the little shell Annabeth had agreed to carry for the chart room.

He set the luggage on the Big House steps and opened his notebook. A pressed leaf fell out. He put it back before Clarisse could accuse him of packing the entire garden.

"How many packages?" Annabeth asked.

"Four. No, five if the photograph counts."

"The photograph counts," she said. "It's an object, Percy. Not a feeling."

Silena stood beside them with the sealed food parcels. She had kept them dry through the return passage, but she hadn't opened them, and one belonged to the kitchen rather than Cabin Three. Clarisse sorted the labels, then put a mark beside the parcels that required a reply. When Percy reached for the last parcel, Silena stopped him.

"That isn't ours," she said. "It's for the garden staff. Triton asked Chiron to forward it."

Percy looked at the label again. She was right. He'd nearly taken a gift meant for someone else because he'd been thinking of the whole trip as his family's visit rather than a set of separate people's work.

"Then write that down," Annabeth said. She handed him the pencil.

He did. On a separate page he listed the guards who'd helped them, the nursery attendant, and the technician who had closed the observatory. He left blank spaces where he didn't know a name rather than writing a confident guess.

Clarisse leaned over his shoulder. "You're making a list of people who should be thanked."

"Yeah."

"Add the chariot driver. You nearly fell asleep on him."

"I was resting my eyes."

"You were snoring at a royal employee."

Percy added the driver. They signed their names beside their own belongings, and Chiron would countersign the parcel meant for camp. The small bit of paperwork felt annoying, but it meant nobody would have to invent the story of the return later. Things could be brought home without becoming Percy's possessions simply because he'd carried them.



This was not the welcome Percy had imagined. He was tired from the passage, carrying a bag that seemed to have become heavier under the water, and Nico had already found the sweets Amphitrite had sent. Percy wanted to sit on the cabin steps and let the day stop moving.

But Chiron had promised to count them when they returned, and he had been waiting at the shore with a clipboard. Now he stood outside the Big House while Annabeth compared the names on the palace record with the ones on the camp departure sheet.

"All six of you?" he asked.

"Percy, Clarisse, Silena, Annabeth, Thalia, and the camp escort for the upper passage," Annabeth recited. "The escort returned to the sea. We have his name and his watch number."

"And Tyson?"

"Tyson stayed at the undersea forges," Percy said. "He'll write when the rail repair is finished."

Chiron marked the line. "Good. Did anyone need treatment?"

Clarisse lifted her wrist, which had stopped aching during the journey but still had the faint mark from the garden repair.

"Will looked at it before we left. No new injuries."

"Any new equipment failures?"

Annabeth thought about the shell charms and shook her head. "The replacements worked throughout this trip. Triton said the earlier failed shell was still being examined. He promised the report."

Chiron wrote *pending* beside the entry rather than *resolved*.

Silena pulled the small parcel of gifts from her bag. She laid them on the bench one by one, keeping the food separate from the fragile shells. A household attendant had packed an unfamiliar kind of preserved kelp beside the jam they'd brought. Silena had been given instructions for storing it, but she wanted the kitchen to confirm it was safe for campers with allergies before anyone served it.

"Could we keep the food out of the dining pavilion until the cooks check it?" she asked.

"Certainly," Chiron said.

Nico, who had been eyeing the parcel hopefully, sighed with such theatrical despair that Clarisse pushed a plain camp biscuit into his hand.

"You'll survive."

"I was looking forward to diplomatic kelp."

"You can look forward to it tomorrow."

Percy took the returned guest card from Annabeth and put it on the table. Its edges had softened, but the ink still held. It listed the route they'd taken home and the person who had been responsible for each part of the transfer. Chiron studied it with approval.

"Useful documentation," he said. "Did everyone feel able to request a change of plan?"

There was a moment of silence. Percy glanced at Silena, who had been the one to insist on the guest protocols at camp.

"I did," she said. "There were parts of the palace I wasn't comfortable with, but nobody made me stay in those rooms."

Clarisse nodded. "They let me keep the key to my weapon rack. That's the only reason I agreed to leave the spear there."

"And the Grace sisters?"

Thalia answered first. "We received an unexpected message from Zeus. We didn't go to him, and nobody from the palace tried to force us."

Chiron looked to Annabeth. She nodded. "I wanted to stay with the group. We asked why he wanted us. He didn't answer."

"Then that is part of the record as well."

Thalia seemed unhappy to have it written down. Percy understood. Recording the refusal meant admitting that a father had turned a possible visit into something his daughters needed a safety plan for. But leaving it out wouldn't make the episode vanish. Chiron put the note in the confidential part of his ledger rather than the general camp log.

"Do you want us to do anything now?" he asked.

"Not tonight," Annabeth said.

"Then we shall not."

Percy felt the tension go out of his shoulders. Their father could still send another message. The difference was that Chiron wasn't demanding they decide how to answer it before they had eaten.

The group carried their bags toward the cabins. At the fork in the path, Clarisse stopped beside the fountain and held the old photograph up beside the new sketch Annabeth had made.

"Do we keep both in Cabin Three?" she asked.

"One belongs to Annabeth," Percy said.

"That's what I'm asking. Does she want to hang hers in our cabin or her own?"

Annabeth looked at the drawing. It wasn't the kind of family portrait that could be claimed by one building. She had made copies for the people in it, and the original would be better protected in a flat portfolio.

"I'll put the original in Cabin One," she said. "You can have a copy. I'll need to finish the labels first."

"What labels?"

"The dates and locations. Otherwise, in five years, we'll argue about which visit it was."

"We'll argue anyway," Clarisse said.

"Yes, but we'll have evidence."

Percy watched them settle the matter. There was no speech about belonging. A drawing had an owner, and that owner could decide where it went. It made him feel less like a prince and more like a camper with a sister who couldn't stand loose paperwork.

He followed Silena toward their cabin. She had a list of people to thank for the trip. Some lived under the sea; others were the camp staff who had checked the departure route and kept the lake chores covered in their absence. She wanted the letters written while names were still fresh.

"Tomorrow," Percy said.

"Tomorrow," she agreed. "Tonight I'd like food and a bed that doesn't move with the tide."

"Atlantis beds don't move."

"I know. I kept expecting them to."

They reached the cabin porch. There were three familiar pairs of shoes by the door, a watering can by Silena's bench and an empty plate that Clarisse had apparently forgotten to return to the dining pavilion. Percy set his bag down and took a minute to look at them. Leaving Atlantis hadn't turned it into a rejection. Returning to camp hadn't closed the sea behind him. There was work to do in both places, people to thank and choices that still belonged to him.


---

An hour before dinner, Grover came looking for Percy with a problem that had nothing to do with Atlantis.

He found him at the cabin table, where Clarisse had spread out her training notes and Silena was arranging the samples she'd brought from the workshop. Grover stood in the doorway with a bundle of reed pipes under one arm, looking as though he'd been practicing an apology and still hadn't decided how to begin.

"Can you come down to the north path?" he asked.

"What's wrong?"

"A group of younger campers have been trying to help with the new planting beds. They moved some of the signs, and now nobody knows which rows are supposed to be watered today."

Silena glanced up. "The beds near the strawberry patch?"

"Yes. The plan was in the garden book, but it got wet during the storm last week."

Percy looked at his bag. He'd barely unpacked. He was hungry, tired and still carrying the damp smell of the sea in his clothes. A small part of him wanted to say the problem belonged to the campers who'd moved the signs and let them solve it.

"Did Chiron ask for help?" he said.

"He's with a new arrival. I thought you and Annabeth might remember the bed layout."

"Annabeth will know the measurements. I'll come see what's happened."

He found her outside Cabin One, sorting drawings she'd brought back from Atlantis. She listened to Grover's explanation, then frowned.

"I can't promise I remember which marker belonged to which row. The plan in my notebook is an older version. We changed the watering schedule after the last drainage repair."

"So what do we do?" Percy asked.

"We talk to the people who planted it."

They went to the north path. Three younger campers were waiting beside a shallow trench where the water had collected after an afternoon shower. They looked guilty. One was holding two wooden plant labels, both written in the same green paint and smudged enough that Percy couldn't read them.

"We wanted to put them where everyone could see them," the youngest explained. "Some were falling over."

"Did you move the plants?" Grover asked.

"No."

"Then we haven't lost the garden," Annabeth said. "We just need to rebuild the labels."

The children looked relieved. She didn't tell them their effort had been foolish. She asked how they'd decided which signs to move. The youngest showed her the loose stakes, which had been sinking into the soft soil whenever the irrigation line ran.

"Then the problem was real," she said. "The labels weren't holding. We should solve that too."

A Demeter counselor arrived carrying the revised watering plan. She'd been responsible for the beds and knew which rows were meant to stay drier while the seedlings established. Annabeth compared the plan with the map in her notebook, then marked the places where the temporary stakes would go.

Percy fetched a small mallet and a bundle of spare wood from the tool shed. He expected to help rebuild the signs immediately, but the counselor stopped him.

"Not in the wet soil. The stakes will sink again. We need a wider base."

"What do we have?"

"Leftover strips from the potting tables."

They made new markers with broad wooden feet instead of narrow stakes. The Demeter counselor gave the children the task of painting the names, while Annabeth checked that the written labels matched the plants. Percy held the pieces steady as they were assembled. Grover tested each in the soil, pushing gently until it held without tipping.

One of the younger campers studied Percy. "Is it true you can make the whole sea rise?"

"I can move water," he said. "Not all of it."

"Someone said you made a road to a palace."

"Triton arranged an escorted passage. I followed it. That's a different thing."

The girl looked disappointed. Percy understood. A prince returning from Atlantis ought to have impressive stories. He had some, but the part that mattered most to him was harder to describe than a road made of waves.

"Was there anything dangerous?" she asked.

"A chariot fitting came loose today. The driver stopped and replaced it. We took a different route because the repair needed testing."

"That's not dangerous."

"It could have been if nobody checked."

She considered that, then returned to painting the word *marigold* on a marker. Percy watched her straighten the letters until they fitted.

It occurred to him that for many of the younger campers, safety was an abstract promise. The heroes in stories fought monsters; the adults at camp checked buckles and made sure equipment was properly returned. In reality those were often the same people, doing different work according to what the moment required.

When the new markers were ready, the counselor walked the beds with the group and placed each one in its assigned spot. Annabeth kept the revised plan and added the date in her notes. The old version remained behind it, crossed through but not destroyed.

"What if it rains again?" one child asked.

"Then we'll see whether the bases hold," the counselor said. "If they don't, we'll change the design."

Percy liked that answer. Nobody had to pretend the first repair would last forever.

They returned the tools as the dinner conch sounded. Grover carried the mallet, Percy carried the leftover strips, and Annabeth gave the revised planting plan to the counselor for the official garden book.

"I thought you would be too tired after traveling," Grover said.

"I am tired."

"Then why did you come?"

"Because you asked. And because I could do something useful."

Grover smiled. "That's nice."

Percy thought about the way he'd rushed toward problems all summer, sometimes to help and sometimes because he couldn't bear the idea of being unnecessary. Today he'd let the Demeter counselor lead, helped with the part he could do and stopped when the job was finished. It had been easier than expected.

At the dining pavilion, Clarisse was already in line with Silena. She had her training notes folded in her pocket and was complaining about the smell of the unfamiliar preserved fruit they'd brought back from the palace.

"The kitchen hasn't approved it yet," Silena reminded her.

"Then why is it sitting on the counter?"

"So they can inspect it."

"Right."

They joined the end of the line. Percy could hear the usual mixture of arguments, laughter and clattering plates. He had missed the sound while underwater. He had also liked the quiet in Amphitrite's gardens. Both things remained true without competing.

During dinner, an Apollo camper came to ask whether the visitors would help explain the revised training safety notices to younger campers. Clarisse listened, then said she'd do it tomorrow with the arena instructor rather than make an announcement that night.

"Why wait?" Percy asked.

"Because the instructor owns the schedule. The new drill I learned doesn't go into the lesson plan until they've checked how it works on dry ground."

"Good point."

"I make several per year."

Silena laughed. Annabeth was already looking at a note from the Demeter counselor about the plant markers they'd replaced. She had agreed to check the bases after the next rain and was considering whether a different wooden shape would last longer.

Percy turned toward her.

"You've just spent the day traveling," he said. "Can you leave that for tomorrow?"

"I am leaving it. I'm writing down what we need to look at so I don't wake up in the middle of the night thinking I've forgotten."

"Fair."

She put the notebook away after making one more note. He didn't try to take it from her. They finished their meal and took the plates to the basin with the others.

After the dishes were done, Grover returned with the youngest camper from the garden. She had carried one of the old markers, the one that had sunk farthest into the soil. It was still damp.

"Can we keep this?" she asked Annabeth. "For the next time we have to make a better one?"

Annabeth took it and looked at the warped base. "Yes. It's a useful reminder."

The girl handed it to Percy instead. "Can you put it somewhere safe? Your cabin has that shelf with the other repaired things."

Percy glanced at Annabeth. It was her design, and the Demeter counselor had led the repair. He didn't want the marker to become his trophy merely because he was standing closest.

"How about we put it in the garden book with a note?" he suggested. "Then everyone can see why we changed the stakes."

The girl nodded. Grover offered to carry it back. Percy watched them go, pleased that the story had ended with the object staying where it would be useful rather than disappearing onto his own shelf.

He turned toward the path home. Clarisse and Silena were waiting near the pavilion, ready to argue about whose turn it was to take the kitchen linens back to Cabin Three. Annabeth and Thalia were walking ahead, discussing whether the new plant markers would survive a hard rain.

Percy followed at his own pace. He'd returned from the sea with a copied drawing of his childhood training stage, a bag of gifts and a dozen possible stories about Atlantis. The first job he'd done at camp was holding a wooden sign while a child painted a word correctly. He liked that the camp had given him something ordinary to do without asking whether a prince ought to be doing it.


---

After dinner camp thinned into small groups. Grover walked Ella toward the Big House, debating whether shell flute could be played in the dining pavilion. Nico had fallen asleep on a bench and was carried away by a patient counselor. Silena and Clarisse went back to Cabin Three to unpack. Thalia went with them, claiming only to reclaim the map left in the bag.

Annabeth found Percy at the lake.

The dock stretched beneath darkening sky, boards familiar under his shoes, worn smooth where generations had sat, nails cool through the wood. The wind smelled of pine and wet earth, cool against sun-warmed skin, lifting the fine hairs on his arms. He sat at the end, where the lake opened toward the Sound, legs dangling, and let water tap the pilings in its patient rhythm, each small slap echoing hollowly under the planks. He heard her steps on the boards before she reached him, unhurried and known, the familiar creak-pause-creak of her gait.

"You put up the picture," she said, sitting beside him, wood creaking.

"I did."

"Like that you kept it here."

"Where I wanted it."

She leaned shoulder against his. "You looked happy in the picture."

"I was."

"And now?"

He considered. The answer had more than one part, layered like light through water. "Glad we went. Glad we came back. Sad to leave them. Glad to be here." The boards creaked faintly under them, and a cool breath of pine moved across the lake, ruffling the dark surface into silver scales.

"About right."

"Clarisse is still hurt."

"She told you. That's something."

"I know. Wish I'd noticed sooner."

"You can't make yourself know what you haven't understood yet. You can pay attention now."

He watched last color leave the western clouds, pink thinning to gray. "You said Camp is yours."

"It is. Where Thalia raised me. Where I learned which cabin steps squeak, and where the dining pavilion leaks when it rains. The place I chose before I understood how many places a person could belong."

"Glad you chose it."

"Glad you came back to it with me."

Percy turned toward her. Gray eyes held reflected cabin lights. He reached for her hand, and she met him halfway, fingers warm.

"I want to see Atlantis again," she said. "Not because I'm supposed to. Because I want the garden after it changes. I want to hear Triton argue about maps. I want to see your parents."

"I'd like that."

"And I want this to stay home."

"It will."

"Good."

He leaned toward her slowly. She closed the last inch. Their kiss stayed gentle and unhurried, a quiet greeting after long return. When they parted, she rested her forehead against his.

"Welcome back," she whispered.

"Thanks."

The lake moved under the dock, carrying night's reflections toward dark. Behind waited cabins, the photograph, his sisters, friends who'd kept their seats. Ahead lay another visit, promised to no date, demanded by nobody. Percy held Annabeth's hand as water touched the pilings again, and let both shores stay in view.
