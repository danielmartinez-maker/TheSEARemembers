## Chapter 159: The Middle of the Visit

Percy Jackson woke to the sound of somebody being careful.

Not the normal cabin sounds. Not Clarisse kicking the pool ladder because it had offended her, not Silena humming through her teeth while she braided her hair, not the pool itself moving water through the channels under the floor with that low salt-rain smell rising off it. This was a different careful: a big careful. A slow breath, a pause, the soft scrape of wood on stone, another pause to check whether the noise had woken anybody.

He kept his eyes closed and counted the light. Morning coming through the high window in Tempest Hall laid itself across the bunks in one long plank, dust turning in it. Mid-September light, thinner than August, cooler at the edges. It was the third morning of Tyson's visit. Second full day, if you counted arrival day the way Silena did, which was as half a day because Tyson had arrived at nine, after breakfast and the day's true beginning. Percy had stopped arguing with her system on the first night. It was easier to learn the calendar than to fight it.

He opened one eye.

Tyson was six and a half feet of broad shoulder and scarred knuckle and one large brown eye that took in the whole room at once, and he was handling a quilt the way somebody else might handle a bird with a hurt wing. He had already made his bunk — the corner bunk, the visitor's bunk, sheets pulled tight enough to bounce a drachma off, pillow centered, the round white rock he had brought from the forges set exactly in the middle of the pillow like an egg waiting to hatch. He had lined his boots up under the bunk. He had hung his towel on the hook Percy had put up for him back in July and never taken down. Now he was refolding the spare quilt because Percy had left it in a heap the night before after the dock, and Tyson clearly could not let a heap stand.

"Morning," Percy said.

Tyson looked up, relieved and guilty at once. "I did not wake you? I tried to be quiet."

"You were quiet. I'm just light." Percy sat up and rubbed his face. The air in the cabin smelled the way it always did in the morning — wet stone, lemon oil, the mineral damp that lived in the tile no matter how many Septembers went by — plus, this morning, something warm and metallic underneath it, forge-smoke and hot bronze, because Tyson sweated forge-smoke the way other people sweated sunscreen. Two nights in, Percy had decided he liked it. It made the cabin smell like somebody had been making things. "You don't have to fold that. That quilt lives in a heap. It's its natural state."

Tyson considered the quilt with total seriousness. "Heaps fall over," he said. "Then somebody trips. Then somebody gets hurt. Then Chiron has paperwork." He gave the folded edge one more pat, set the whole square down on the foot of Percy's bunk, and stepped back to admire it. "There. Now it will not hurt anyone."

Percy laughed, and the laugh scraped his throat because morning, and swung his legs over the side. He wore the faded orange camp shirt he had slept in, sleeves cut short back in June by somebody's borrowed scissors, and soft gray sweatpants gone thin at the knees that Silena had claimed were technically against uniform and then had washed for him twice anyway. His feet found the cool tile. The tile was always cool first thing, even when the day promised heat, and it woke him up faster than the conch.

"You sleep okay?" he asked. "Corner bunk still all right? Not too short?"

"It is good," Tyson said, with enormous sincerity. "It is the same bunk. I remember the spring in the left side that goes ping when you roll over. It still goes ping. I said hello to it."

Percy grinned. "Did it say hello back?"

"Yes," Tyson said. "Ping."

From the pool end of the hall came a splash and a muttered curse that could only be Clarisse doing laps before breakfast, and the smell of lemon oil sharpening as Silena opened the tin by the spear rack. Normal sounds resuming their posts. Percy stood and stretched, shoulders popping, and went to the shelf for a towel, and stopped because Tyson was watching him with the particular stillness that meant a question was coming.

"What is it?" Percy asked.

"How many days?" Tyson asked.

Percy knew what he meant. They had agreed it with Chiron on arrival night: four days, then back on the morning ferry. Bounded, Chiron had said, because bounded was kind.

"Today is day two," Percy said. "Full day two. Then tomorrow. Then you go the morning after."

Tyson nodded slowly, counting on his fingers to be sure. "Two days left after today," he said. "That is good. That is a lot. Yesterday I used one whole day and there are still two." He said it like a boy counting coins and finding he was richer than he thought, and Percy felt something in his chest do the small loosening thing it did whenever Tyson reframed arithmetic without knowing he was doing it.

"Yeah," Percy said. "We're rich."

"Rich in days," Tyson agreed, pleased. He picked up Percy's damp sneakers from where Percy had kicked them off and set them side by side by the door, toes pointing out, exactly even. "I will carry the laundry today. That is my chore. You said I could have a chore."

"I said you didn't need a chore, you're a guest."

"Guests get the good towel," Tyson said, nodding at the thick blue towel on his hook — the visitor towel, the one Silena kept washed and folded separately and never let anybody else touch. "Workers get a chore. I want both." He folded his massive arms, gentle as a closed gate. "Please."

Percy looked at him for a second, at the set of the jaw that was exactly their father's jaw scaled up and softened, at the hands that could bend a bronze bar and were currently worried about a quilt heap. He thought about arguing. He thought about the look Silena would give him if he denied Tyson a chore. He thought about Clarisse saying, let him work, Jackson, he's happier with weight in his hands, which she had said last night while sharpening her spear with her back to the room so nobody would see her face doing the soft thing.

"Fine," Percy said. "Laundry. But you have to let me help."

"No," Tyson said. "You have short arms. It will take you twice as long." He said it with complete love, no malice anywhere in it, a pure engineering assessment. "I will do it fast. Then we can do the lake."

Percy opened his mouth. Closed it. "My arms are normal length."

"For a human," Tyson said kindly. "I will still love you."

Clarisse chose that moment to come in off the pool deck with water streaming off her braid and her cutoff shirt plastered to her shoulders, took in the scene — Percy barefoot in sweatpants arguing about arm length, Tyson standing over a folded quilt like a general over a map — and said, "He's got you there, Jackson. Your arms are like oars on a trireme. Decorative."

"Thank you for your support as my sister," Percy said.

"You're welcome as your sister." She grabbed her towel off its peg, scrubbed her face with it, and pointed at Tyson with the corner still over her eye. "Laundry's in the north bin. Don't let him help. He folds like a raccoon."

"I fold well," Percy protested.

"You fold like a raccoon who learned from a seagull," Clarisse said. "Tyson, breakfast in twenty. If he's not dressed, leave him."

"I will carry him," Tyson said seriously. "If he is not dressed, I will carry him in the blanket. It will be fast."

"I'd like to see the dress code enforced," Percy said, pulling yesterday's jeans off the hook and stepping into them. He swapped shirts, laced his damp sneakers, and pocketed the day's inventory: Silena's hair tie, the lake pebble, yesterday's cookie wrapper.

He looked up to find Tyson watching the pocket ritual with deep approval.

"You check your tools," Tyson said.

"Yeah," Percy said. "Learned it in Atlantis. Dad's armorers did it. Silena does it with her sewing kit. Annabeth does it with pencils."

"Good," Tyson said. "A worker checks his tools. A brother checks his pockets." He picked up the north laundry bin as if it weighed nothing — it was overflowing, three people's worth of wet towels and practice shirts and Silena's lake-smelling quilt — and settled it against his hip. "I am both. I will check both." He patted his own pocket, where a single large bronze bolt sat like a secret. "Bolt," he announced. "For the dock. The third cleat is loose. I felt it last night with my foot."

Percy stared. "You found that with your foot? In the dark?"

"I listen with my feet," Tyson said simply. "It is faster." He headed for the door with the bin, ducked the lintel out of long habit even though he cleared it by an inch, and paused. "Percy?"

"Yeah?"

"Today is a good day," Tyson said. "I can feel it in my feet too."

He went out into the September morning with the laundry on his hip and his towel still hanging straight on its hook behind him, and Percy stood in the cool tile smell of his own cabin and felt the day arrange itself around that sentence, the way water arranges itself around a stone.

Clarisse La Rue had a theory about guests, and the theory was this: the first day you fed them, the second day you worked them, and the third day you pretended you were not counting.

She did not say this out loud. She said, instead, standing on the packed dirt at the edge of the volleyball court with a ball under one arm and her whistle on its cord around her neck, "Tyson. You're with the little kids. No spiking."

Tyson stood in the middle of the court's far side in a camp shirt three sizes too small from the lost-and-found, hair still damp, feet bare because he had given his sandals to a Hermes kid. The morning had burned off its chill by ten, cicadas starting, the lake flashing through the trees.

"Why no spiking?" Tyson asked.

"Because you'll kill somebody," Clarisse said bluntly. "Last summer you put a discus through the shed. I was there. I measured the hole."

"That was a good throw," Tyson said, a little wistfully.

"It was a great throw. It cost forty drachmas." She tossed him the volleyball. He caught it in both hands like an egg. "Just bump it. Soft. Like you're passing a baby bird."

Tyson stared at the ball. "I do not know how to pass a baby bird."

"Then pass it like laundry," Clarisse said. "You did laundry this morning. I saw the lines. Best lines we've had all month. Pass it like a sheet."

"I can pass it like a sheet," Tyson said, brightening. He held the ball out and bounced it once, gently, to test its weight. The kids on his side — four of them, none older than eleven, two Apollo, one Demeter, one unclaimed with grass stains on both knees — watched him with the open-mouthed awe usually reserved for gods and large dogs.

Percy sat on the sideline bench next to Silena and narrated: "Incredible or catastrophic. No middle."

"Both," Silena said, needle flashing over her mending tote. "He asked Beckendorf's brother for the rules. Took notes. On his hand."

Percy leaned to look. Sure enough, across the back of Tyson's broad left hand in smudged charcoal: BALL OVER NET = GOOD. HIT 3 TIMES MAX. BE KIND.

The game started badly and got worse and then, somehow, became the best thing on the schedule.

The problem was not effort. Tyson gave the game everything. He crouched when the others crouched, knees bent, arms out, eye huge and fixed on the ball with the intensity of a Cyclops sighting down a forge seam. When the ball came to him, he bumped it straight up with exquisite softness — sheet-passing softness — and then stood there beaming while it fell back down on his own head.

"Other side!" half the court screamed, laughing.

"Oh," Tyson said. "Over. I forgot over." He scooped the ball up, apologized to it, and handed it to the nearest eleven-year-old. "Sorry, ball. Sorry, team. I will remember over."

He remembered over the next time by hitting it over and also over the fence and into the pine branches twenty yards past the court.

"Too much over!" the same chorus screamed, delighted now, shrieking with the particular joy of small children watching a giant fail lovingly at their game.

Clarisse blew the whistle. "Tyson! Less laundry! More —" She mimed something small and controlled. "Dish towel. Pass it like a dish towel."

"Dish towel," Tyson repeated solemnly. He wiped his hands on his too-small shirt, resettled his feet in the dirt, and waited.

What happened next was why Clarisse would remember this morning. The Apollo kid served high toward Tyson's corner. The two smallest kids froze. Tyson unfolded across the dirt, got under it, and bumped it dish-towel soft above the net, where the Demeter girl knocked it over with a shriek at her own competence.

Point. Tyson's side erupted. The Demeter girl stood there with her mouth open. Tyson picked her up — just lifted her clean off the ground in one arm, the way you would lift a laundry bin — and set her back down and said, "You hit it over. That was excellent over. You are very good at over."

"I am?" she said, dazed.

"Yes," Tyson said. "I saw with my eye."

Clarisse blew the whistle twice for water break and watched her half-brother sit in the dirt with four small campers climbing on him, explaining the rule with his charcoal hand. Sun slatted through the pines across his shoulders. Grass stain already on his back.

Percy flopped down beside her on the bench edge, elbows on knees. "You're smiling."

"I am not smiling. I'm squinting. It's bright."

"You're squinting with love."

"Jackson, I will end you." But she did not move away when he bumped his shoulder against hers, and she let the whistle cord slide through her fingers, and the denunciation lacked its usual velocity. "He's good with them."

"He's good with everybody," Percy said. "He carried the laundry, fixed the third cleat with the bolt in his pocket — did you know he listens with his feet? — and now he's teaching volleyball like it's forge work."

"He takes everything seriously." Clarisse watched Tyson demonstrate bumping, solemn as ritual. "No irony. No armor."

Percy was quiet a second. Then, wry and observant, the way he got when he was noticing something he did not yet know what to do with: "Is that why it's hard? Having him here? Because he doesn't do the thing where we all pretend it's not a big deal?"

Clarisse looked at him sharply. He was watching the court, hair still salt-stiff at the temples, fourteen and sun-browned, trying hard to be easy about a brother who might leave in two days.

"Don't therapize me, Jackson," she said, but it came out without its edge. "Drink water. You're pink."

"I'm Mediterranean. I'm supposed to be pink."

"You're dehydrated. There's a difference." She shoved her canteen at him. "Ten minutes per occurrence, retroactive, free for Poseidon cabin, and that includes hydration enforcement."

He drank, made a face, handed it back. "When did you get soft?"

"When you showed up with shoes that didn't fit and I had to start sharing my lake." She stood, blew the whistle once, hard. "Back on! Tyson, dish towel! Kids, three hits! Jackson, you're in for Apollo, they need a body!"

"I have mending!" Percy protested, but he was already standing, already grinning, already rolling his damp cuffs higher with the quick competence of a boy who had spent a summer learning to be useful fast.

"Your mending is raccoon work. Go." Clarisse watched him jog onto the court in his rent sneakers, kicking up dust, immediately getting beamed in the shoulder by a serve and taking it with a theatrical collapse that made the eleven-year-olds howl. She watched Tyson haul him upright with one hand, both of them laughing, Tyson explaining something with total sincerity — probably apologizing on behalf of the ball — Percy nodding like it was tactical briefing.

Silena settled beside her on the bench, needle still moving. "You're counting," she said quietly, without looking up.

"I'm not counting."

"You're counting days. I can hear you doing it."

Clarisse was quiet. The whistle cord slid through her fingers again. Out on the court, Tyson bumped the ball — dish-towel perfect — straight to Percy, who set it with two clean hands to the Hermes substitute, who missed it entirely and fell over. Everybody cheered anyway. The cicadas sawed. The lake flashed through the pines, freshwater blue, honest and cold underneath its morning shine.

"Two days after today," Clarisse said at last, blunt and low. "Then the ferry. Then the bunk's empty and the towel's on the hook and we're back to three."

Silena tied off a stitch with her teeth. "Mm."

"Don't mm me. Say something real."

"I am saying something real. Mm is real. Mm means I heard you and I'm still sewing." Silena bit the thread, smoothed the seam, folded the shirt. Her voice, when it came, was warm and perceptive, the voice that had kept this cabin's arithmetic for five years. "It's the middle, Clarisse. That's all. Middles are hard because you can see both ends."

Clarisse watched her brother get hit by another serve and stay down for comic effect while Tyson stood over him going, "Percy? Are you broken? Do I need to carry you?" and Percy going, "I'm gone, tell Silena I loved her mending," and the small campers screaming with laughter.

"Yeah," Clarisse said. "Well. I hate middles."

"I know," Silena said. "That's why you brought the whistle."

Silena Beauregard had learned, over five Septembers of feeding people, that lunch told the truth about a visit.

First-day lunch was performance: everybody on best behavior, extra portions, stories told a little too loud. Last-day lunch was already grief, people saving seats and not eating. But middle-day lunch — second full day, sun high and ordinary, pavilion smelling of tomato and basil and hot dishwater — middle-day lunch was just hunger. People reached past each other. People complained about the bread. People were themselves, which was the rarest thing a visitor ever got to see.

She moved behind the serving counter with the ease of long practice, sleeves pushed to the elbow, flour on one forearm. The pavilion was loud at midday, awnings moving in the wind, the lake flashing beyond the pines.

Tyson sat at the end of the Poseidon table with three sandwiches and carrots arranged by size, listening to a Hephaestus boy explain the forge rotation, nodding after every sentence. He had changed into his own forge shirt, hands scrubbed clean, BE KIND still faint on his knuckles.

Percy slid onto the bench beside him with his own tray, jeans still rolled at the damp cuffs, hair sticking up where Tyson had hauled him upright by it. "You saved me a seat," he said.

"I saved you the strong seat," Tyson said. "The bench wobbles on the left. I put my foot under it. Now it does not wobble." He demonstrated, foot wedged under the bench leg, huge and steady. "See? Engineering."

"You're an engineer now?"

"I am a brother with a foot," Tyson said. "It is almost the same."

Silena came around with the extra pitcher — water with lemon, because lemons were her whole personality and she put them in everything in September — and ruffled Percy's hair as she passed, and set a second apple on Tyson's tray without asking, because big boys who worked needed big lunches and she had been feeding Poseidon children long enough to know without measuring.

"How's the hand?" she asked Tyson.

He held it up proudly. "The rules are wearing off. But I remember them inside. Ball over net is good. Three times maximum. Be kind." He frowned slightly. "I forgot over twice. Then I remembered. The Demeter girl is very good at over."

"She talked about nothing else at the salad bar," Silena said. "You made her week."

Tyson considered this with deep seriousness. "Good," he said. "Weeks should be made. That is what they are for." He bit into his sandwich, chewed thoughtfully, and added, "Percy is also good at over. He falls down very well. The children like when he falls down."

"I fall down for the children," Percy said solemnly. "It's service."

"It's vanity," Clarisse said, dropping onto the bench across from them with her tray, braid swinging forward over one shoulder, wet ends long dry now, leaving a damp spot on her cutoff shirt. "You like the applause."

"I like not being spiked by Tyson," Percy said. "Which I achieved. Barely."

The table settled into the comfortable clatter of eating. Silena took her place at the end with her own tray — half a sandwich, peach slices, cheese — and her mending tote under the bench, and watched the way she always watched: who needed seconds, who was quiet, who was performing fullness to hide emptiness. A server's inventory, a sister's inventory, the same thing after five years.

What she saw was Percy doing the thing he did when he was happy and trying not to show how happy he was: eating fast, talking faster, bumping Tyson's shoulder with his own every few minutes like checking a wall was still there. And Tyson, solid and warm and forge-smelling, absorbing each bump with pleasure, passing Percy the carrots arranged biggest to smallest without comment, because Percy liked the big ones and Tyson had noticed on day one and filed it.

And Clarisse, eating with her eyes on the door, counting. Always counting.

Silena had heard her that morning on the bench. Two days after today. Then the ferry. Then the bunk's empty. She knew that arithmetic by heart because she had done it herself at dawn, lying in her bunk listening to Tyson breathe in the corner bunk — slow, deep, untroubled — and doing the subtraction she had done for every visitor Cabin Three had ever kept: Triton's weekends, the July three weeks, the spring cousins who never stayed. She knew the shape of the hollow that came after. She had mopped it, made bunks inside it, hung towels on its hooks.

She was still thinking about it when Percy said, around a mouthful, "Silena. You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The maintenance face. The one where you're fixing something nobody asked you to fix." He pointed with a carrot. "You've got flour on your arm and you're deciding the future."

"I can decide the future with flour on my arm," Silena said. "I'm multitalented." But she smiled, and it was the warm one, the real one, and she let him see it was real. "Eat your carrots. They're in order."

It was Tyson who brought it up, in the straightforward way he brought everything up, the way you would set a heavy pot down on a table: carefully, because it was heavy, without apology for its weight.

"I do not want to leave," he said.

The table went quiet. Not the whole pavilion — the pavilion went on clattering — but their small end of it, their four trays and their pitcher with lemon slices floating, went still.

Percy stopped chewing. Clarisse looked up. Silena set her cheese down very carefully, the way you set down something you do not want to break.

Tyson went on, methodically, sincere as tide, eyes on his sandwich like the sandwich was helping him think: "I like the forges. The forges are good. The fire is warm and the work is clear and Beckendorf's brother saves me the good hammer. But here the beds go ping and the lake is cold and the children scream when the ball goes over. I like it here." He looked up, one great eye moving from face to face. "I have two days left after today. Then the ferry. I counted. I do not want to go. Is that all right to say?"

Silena felt Percy's knee go still against the bench. Felt Clarisse's boot stop its restless tapping. This was the fear-of-leaving thread, the one they had all been carrying since the dock the night Tyson arrived, since Chiron had said bounded like a kindness and they had all nodded like it did not hurt. None of them had said it out loud. Tyson had said it in one breath, over carrots.

"Yeah," Percy said quietly. "It's all right to say."

"It is all right," Clarisse said, blunt, grudging, her voice a little too level. "Everybody thinks it. You just said it."

Tyson nodded, relieved. "Good. I thought it might be rude. Like saying the food is bad when somebody cooked it." He considered. "But the visit has edges. Chiron said. Edges hold the shape. Like a pot. A pot needs edges or the soup goes everywhere."

Silena laughed, sudden and wet, and had to wipe her eyes with the back of her floury wrist. "That's — yes. That's exactly right."

"But," Tyson said, and here his brow furrowed, the great single-eyed effort of a boy working a thought the way he would work a seam, slow and honest, "I was thinking this morning while I hung the laundry. The laundry has edges too. The lines. The lines hold the shirts so they do not blow away. But the shirts still get dry. The edges do not stop the drying." He looked at Silena, because she had been the one to teach him how to hang a sheet so it would not twist, years ago, in July. "Is leaving like drying? You hang for a while, you get warm, then somebody folds you and puts you somewhere else, but you are still the same shirt?"

Percy made a small sound. Clarisse stared at her tray.

Silena looked at her half-brother — sixteen to his great uncounted age, warm and perceptive and suddenly, completely undone by laundry metaphysics — and felt the thing she had been struggling with since dawn shift without resolving, the way a sheet shifts on a line when the wind changes: not fixed, not answered, just touched. Touched was enough. Touched was what presence was for.

"That's a good way to think about it," she managed. Her voice came out thick. She let it be thick. "We're still the same shirt, Tyson. All of us. Even folded somewhere else."

"Good," Tyson said, satisfied. He picked up his apple, polished it on his forge shirt, and handed it to her. "You can have the extra. You cried a little. Apples help. Percy told me."

"I did not —" Percy started.

"You told me in July," Tyson said. "You said Silena cries when she is happy and apples help. I remembered inside." He tapped his chest.

Silena took the apple. She held it with both hands. It was cool and red and perfect, and she did not eat it yet because she wanted to keep holding it a minute.

"You remembered inside," she repeated.

"Yes," Tyson said simply. "That is where I keep the important things. There is a lot of room inside."

Clarisse reached across the table and took a carrot — the smallest one, the last in the row — and ate it deliberately. "All right," she said. "No more feelings at lunch. Some of us have patrol." But she said it without force, and her boot had stopped tapping, and when Tyson offered her his extra sandwich half she took it without arguing, which was as close to tenderness as Clarisse got before two o'clock.

Percy bumped Tyson's shoulder again, harder this time, the wry observant boy checking the wall was still there. "Thanks for saying it," he said quietly. "About not wanting to go. I didn't know how to."

"You just open your mouth," Tyson said helpfully. "And the words come out. Like laundry water when you squeeze." He demonstrated with his hands. "See?"

Percy laughed until his eyes were wet. "Yeah. Like that."

Silena bit into the apple. It was crisp and September-sweet, the kind that tasted like the orchard had meant it. Around them the pavilion clattered on, ordinary and precious, middle-day lunch, nobody performing, everybody hungry. She caught Clarisse's eye over the pitcher, and her sister — blunt, grudging, terrified of middles — held her gaze a second and gave her the smallest nod, the one that meant heard, the one that meant same shirt, the one that meant they would fold this day when it was dry and keep it somewhere safe.

Annabeth Grace had a theory about maps, and the theory was this: a map was a promise you made to somebody who was not there yet.

She stood at the drafting table in the Athena cabin loft with afternoon light coming through the shutters, the smell of paper and graphite thick around her. Below, the cabin hummed. Annabeth, official Camp Cartographer at fourteen, kept her tools in order: pencils by hardness, the brass compass Tyson had helped her calibrate in July.

She wore her camp shirt with the sleeves rolled — faded orange, graphite on the left cuff — over sturdy olive shorts with pockets full of tools. Her hair kept falling over her shoulder onto the paper, and she kept pushing it back with her wrist.

The map in front of her was the canoe lake. Freshwater, every contour verified by wading. The north beach, the flat rock, the boundary rock with its white X. The willow cove with its half-sunk skiff, a tiny heron glyph in the margin. She was updating the canoe repair on the inventory inset. Annabeth's tally copy lay wrapped in oilcloth on the shelf in Cabin Three, saying nothing — thirty-nine marks in her own hand, counted once from the panel in Chiron's office and written into her field book. It did not belong in today's drawing. Today's drawing was canoes and cleats, the third cleat marked tight with a fresh bolt, initialed T.C. because credit mattered.

Footsteps on the loft ladder. She knew them before the head appeared: quick, impatient, boots worn soft at the heel, the particular rhythm of somebody who took stairs like an argument.

Thalia Grace, eighteen, emerged into the light with a pine needle in her black hair and her camp shirt tied over jeans, a borrowed army jacket over one shoulder, and flopped onto the spare stool.

"Cartographer," Thalia said. "Tell me something true about this camp that isn't on a map."

Annabeth did not look up. "Hello to you too."

"Hello, genius. I'm avoiding weapons inventory. Beckendorf's brother has opinions about spear counts." Thalia kicked her boots against the stool rungs, dry and teasing with the protective edge she never took off, even with her sister. "You smell like pencils. It's disgusting. I love it."

"You smell like pine sap and arena dust. It's worse. I tolerate it." Annabeth shaded the cove a fraction darker, blew the graphite dust away, checked the line against the light. Precise and planning, even in affection: that was how she loved people, by getting the details right. "What do you want, Thalia?"

"Company. And to steal your peach." Thalia reached for the peach on the corner of the drafting table — the good one, the one Percy had left that morning wrapped in a leaf with a note that said NORTH BEACH HAS GOOD ROCK — and Annabeth slapped her hand away without looking.

"That's Percy's peach. He saved it. Don't."

Thalia raised both eyebrows. "Percy's peach. He saves you peaches now? That's domestic. That's disgusting. I'm telling Jason."

"Jason's at Camp Jupiter and he's twelve and he doesn't care about peaches." But Annabeth's ears went pink, and she tucked the peach a fraction closer to the map weight, and Thalia laughed — the short bright bark that meant she had scored.

"Fourteen and blushing over fruit. The gods help us." Thalia leaned forward, elbows on knees, and her voice dropped into the register she used only with her sister. "How is he? Really. With the —" She jerked her chin toward the lake side of the map, toward Cabin Three's territory. "With the big visit and the middle of it and all."

Annabeth set her pencil down. She considered the question the way she considered a contour: carefully, wanting the true line. "He's good. He's —" She searched. "He's in it. Yesterday he was performing being fine. Today he's just in it. Tyson carried the laundry and Percy let him, which for Percy is basically a religious conversion."

"And you?" Thalia asked. "How are you with the middle of it?"

Annabeth picked up the peach pit from her pocket, turned it over, set it down. "I keep wanting to fix it. The leaving. I keep making plans — ferry times, forge schedules, letters — like if I get the logistics right it won't hurt." She looked at her sister, gray eyes clear and frustrated. "It's a visit. It ends. I know it ends. I hate knowing and not being able to draw around it."

Thalia was quiet a second. Then, dry as dust: "You can't cartograph grief, baby sister."

"I know. I keep trying. It's extremely annoying."

Thalia stood, came around the table, and put her arm around Annabeth's shoulders — brief, hard, the Grace-sister hug that was all collarbone and jacket smell and pine. "Listen. All of it — you were the one who kept the map. You kept my place on it. You didn't fix anything. You just didn't erase me." She squeezed once. "That's the job. Keep the place. Let him go to the forges. Keep the bunk on the map."

Annabeth leaned into her a second, allowed it, the precise planner letting herself be held by the older sister she remembered losing in one life and had grown up with in this one. "That's annoyingly wise."

"I'm eighteen. I'm supposed to be wise. It's in the job description, right under intimidating." Thalia released her, stole a grape from the side tin instead of the peach — concession, grace — and headed for the ladder. "Bring your boy to the lake tonight if you want. I'll cover patrol. Ten minutes per occurrence, and I'm waiving it, don't tell Clarisse."

"Thalia —"

"I'm serious. Middles deserve witnesses." She paused on the ladder, black hair falling over one eye, grin flashing. "And Annabeth? Eat the peach. He carried it in his shorts for an hour and a half. That's love. That's disgusting love. Eat it."

She was gone, boots thumping down, leaving pine smell and jacket dust and the particular warmth that followed Thalia everywhere like weather. Annabeth stood in the gold loft light with her map and her pencils and her peach, and laughed once, quietly, and picked up the peach and bit into it — September-sweet, a little bruised, perfect — and went back to her contours with juice on her wrist and her sister's words filed like a sounding: keep the place.

She drew the visitor's bunk into the Cabin Three inset — small square in the corner, labeled T, with a tiny rock on the pillow — because any honest map had to admit who was sleeping where while they slept there. Then she capped her pencil, shouldered her bag, and went down into the afternoon to find her boy.

Percy knew, the moment they reached the north beach, that the afternoon had decided to be kind.

It was late — gold light through the pines, the lake that particular September blue that looked warm on top and hid its cold underneath. Pine resin and sun-warmed stone and clean green freshwater. He carried the quilt and two towels out of habit, and Tyson carried the rest: the satchel, the tin cup, Silena's apples, all in one huge arm.

Clarisse led, barefoot, boots in one hand, braid swinging, her cutoff shirt — sleeves jagged where she had cut them herself two summers ago — riding loose over her swimsuit. Silena came last with Annabeth, the two of them talking low, Silena's bun coming down in the heat and her mending tote abandoned at last for the day, Annabeth's bag over her shoulder and graphite still on her cuff and her hair rebraided tight for water. Five of them on the switchback path, pine needles soft underfoot, lake flashing through the trees to the left, bright and bare, a held coin going rose at the edges.

"The heron's still there," Percy said, stopping at the bend where the willows closed over the cove. He could see it through the branches: gray and still on the bow of the half-sunk skiff, one leg folded, reeds thick and green around it. It had been there for weeks. Nobody knew what it was waiting for. It turned its head and looked at him, smoothed one feather, looked away. "She's waiting."

"She's always waiting," Clarisse said. "It's her whole thing. It's why we like her." She kept walking, because Clarisse did not linger at overlooks. "Come on. Water's warmest at five. After that it's just bragging."

The north beach opened up — half-moon of sand and warm flat stone, pines running right down into the water, glassy shallows over white sand, the boundary rock sitting mid-lake with its white X just visible. Their rock. Cabin Three's rock. Percy felt his face do the small loosening thing it always did when the lake found him, and did not bother putting the guard back up, because today there was nobody to guard against.

Tyson stopped at the edge of the sand and took a deep breath. "Freshwater," he announced, with enormous pleasure. "It smells green. Like cucumbers and stones."

"It smells like lake," Percy said.

"It smells like cucumbers and stones," Tyson said firmly. "I am right. My nose is big." He set the satchel down with great care, lined the apples up on the flat rock biggest to smallest, and looked at Clarisse. "What do we do?"

Clarisse looked at Silena. Silena looked at Percy. The negotiation happened without words, the way it happened between people who had known each other longer than they had known themselves, and then Silena said, in the voice of a person bringing a matter before a committee that has already decided: "We float."

She told it the way she had told it to Percy weeks ago on this same rock: one at a time, somebody holds your ankle so the current doesn't take you, when your ears go under you say one true thing, just one, you don't plan it, and when you come up nobody asks. If you explain it, the float is over and you're on dishes for a week. That's most of it. The floating's the point. The true thing's just the ticket price.

Tyson listened with total concentration, hands folded, charcoal rules long washed off but BE KIND still faint on his knuckles. "One true thing," he repeated. "Unexplained. Like a bolt. You put it in and you do not say why."

"Like a bolt," Silena agreed, eyes bright.

"Who holds my ankle?" Tyson asked. "I am big. I might pull."

"I'll hold you," Percy said. "I'm strong. I swim."

"You have short arms," Tyson said, worried.

"My arms are fine for holding. Holding is what they're for." Percy grinned, wry and easy, the boy who had spent a summer learning to be held by a thing smaller than he was. "Trust me. I'm Cabin Three."

Tyson considered. Then nodded, solemn as oath. "You are Cabin Three. You can hold."

They went in off the flat rock in a ragged line: Clarisse first without testing; Silena next; Annabeth after her, hair braided tight, pencils safe on the rock; Percy next, sneakers left behind by Silena's order; Tyson last, huge and careful.

The freshwater came up over knees and waists and chests, colder and cleaner than salt, green and honest, and Percy lay back and the lake took him, and it was different every time and the same every time: the cold layer three feet down, the warm skin on top from the day's sun, the low green roar when his ears went under. He found Tyson's ankle — broad as a mooring post, steady — and Silena found his, and Clarisse found Silena's, and Annabeth took Clarisse's, and for a minute the five of them made a line across the shallows off the rock, five heads and spread hands and the pines and the gold sky above, nobody saying anything because there was nothing to say the water was not already saying.

Silena went first. She took a breath, went under, hair spreading in the green light, said the true thing with her ears full of lake, stayed down a beat longer than usual, came up shining with no explanation whatsoever, because that was the rule.

Clarisse went next. Quick, no drama, head under and straight back up, hair shaking out, "One," flat and steady, fingers tightening once on Silena's ankle and letting go.

Annabeth went third. Percy watched his girlfriend — fourteen, precise, planning, graphite on her cuff even here — fill her lungs, sink with the neatness she did everything, mouth moving once under the water, surfacing with her braid streaming and her gray eyes clear and her face doing the thing it did when she had gotten a line exactly right. She did not explain it. The rule held.

Percy went fourth. He breathed in, went down into the green roar with the rock warm under his back and the cold coming up from below and the dock posts of memory going up above him like pillars, and said it — one true thing, unplanned, ticket price — into water that would not tell anybody. He came up. He did not explain it. He felt Tyson's ankle steady under his hand, enormous and warm, and was glad, suddenly and fiercely, that he was the one holding the biggest person in the line.

Then Tyson.

The lake went quiet. Even the cicadas seemed to pause. Tyson took a huge breath — the breath of a boy who worked forges, who knew air the way swimmers knew water — and went under.

He stayed down a long time. Long enough that Percy felt Clarisse shift, felt Silena's grip adjust, felt Annabeth's fingers press once against Clarisse's ankle: the line holding, the way lines held. The water over Tyson's face was glassy and green-gold with evening light, his great brow smooth, his eye closed. His mouth moved. One small movement, unhurried, sincere even underwater.

He came up with water streaming off his shoulders, blinking in the gold light, and said nothing. He just floated there, held by the lake and by Percy's hand on his ankle, and smiled the small complete smile of a boy who had paid his ticket and found it fair.

Nobody asked. That was the rule.

They floated a while in the line, five heads in a row, and the water held them the way it held the skiff and the heron's reflection and the boundary rock, the same and also not the same. The sun slid lower. The shallows went rose-gold at the edges. A fish jumped once out past the sandbar with a flat silver smack and was gone. Percy's fingers ached pleasantly from holding. Tyson's ankle was warm as stone that had sat in sun.

Then, because traditions were things you chose and not laws, Tyson spoke — not the true thing, not explaining it, just the ordinary thing that came after, in his simple literal voice, enormous with sincerity:

"The water holds me," he said. "Even though I am heavy. It does not drop me. I thought it might drop me because I am the heaviest. It does not drop me." He looked down the line at his siblings, one great eye bright with lake. "Thank you for holding my ankle anyway. Even though the water holds me. It is good to be held twice."

Silena made a sound like a laugh and a sob at once and went under for a second to hide it. Clarisse stared at the sky and blinked hard and said, "Dish towel," very quietly, to nobody, which made no sense and perfect sense. Annabeth reached along the line and found Percy's free hand under the water and held it, cold fingers and warm, graphite and lake. Percy felt his throat close the way it did when something true went past without armor, and he held Tyson's ankle a little tighter and the water a little looser, held twice, held twice, the words going around in his head like ripples.

They thrashed around after that, because five siblings in a lake at golden hour could not stay solemn long, until the shallows were foam and the apples had to be rescued.

They crawled out at last, dripping, and sat with feet in the water and the quilt over shoulders — all five under one quilt, which was impossible and therefore exactly what Cabin Three did. The wind had turned, thin October edge under the gold, and Clarisse's spare shirt — faded blue, sleeves jagged, smelling of lemon oil and lake — went to Annabeth this time without comment, because Annabeth was shivering in her wet shirt and Clarisse saw it first. Annabeth put it on over her swimsuit, too big in the shoulders and right everywhere else, and did not say anything about it, and neither did Clarisse, which was how gifts worked in Cabin Three.

Tyson sat in the middle with the quilt over his massive knees, hair dripping, forge shirt plastered, and lined the rescued apples up again biggest to smallest with total contentment. "I said the true thing," he reported to Percy, quietly, like confirming a delivery. "It was a good one. I will not tell you. But it was about you. All of you. It was easy. It came out like laundry water."

Percy bumped his shoulder against the great wet arm beside him. "Good," he said, thickly. "That's where the good ones live."

"The lake is good," Tyson said. "It is the biggest water I can see the other side of too." He said it like quoting scripture he had only just learned and already believed. "Percy told me. Last night. I remembered inside."

Percy stared. He had said that weeks ago, on this rock, to his sisters, thinking nobody else would ever need it. Tyson had kept it inside, where there was a lot of room, and brought it out now like a bolt for a loose cleat, exactly the right size.

"Yeah," Percy said. "That's — yeah. That's the lake."

The sun went down behind the far shore and the water took its evening color, dark gold after bright gold, warm on their feet from the day and cold underneath, holding its summer deep down and giving it back slow. Somewhere in the willow shade the heron shifted on the bow of the sunken skiff and settled, witness to the whole ordinary precious thing, and said nothing, because that was its tradition too.

Annabeth knew the dock was empty before she saw it, because the evening had the particular quiet that meant everybody else had gone up to dinner.

She walked out to the end with her sneakers in one hand and Percy's hand in the other, bare feet slapping softly on the boards that were still warm from the day. The air had cooled fast once the sun dropped — mid-September evenings did that, gold going blue at the edges, wind thin off the pines — and she wore Clarisse's borrowed blue shirt over her own dry camp shirt now, sleeves flopping past her wrists, hem to mid-thigh over her olive shorts, smelling of lemon oil and lake and somebody else's sister. Percy wore his day shirt, dry at last, and his jeans with the cuffs unrolled and damp hems flapping, quilt over one shoulder for later, hair lake-wet and curling at the temples. Thalia had waved them off at the top of the ramp — I'll cover patrol, don't tell Clarisse — and Silena had pressed apples and iced tea into Percy's hands. Tyson had hugged them both and gone up with Clarisse, already talking about tomorrow's chore: tightening every cleat, because wobbling caused paperwork.

So it was just the two of them. Fourteen and fourteen. Official cartographer and professional pocket-checker. Boyfriend and girlfriend, the words still new enough at camp to feel like a map freshly inked.

They sat at the end of the dock with their feet in the water, cool and clean, evening-dark under its last rose shine. The pavilion lanterns ticked on one by one. A loon called once. Tyson's cleat held the dock steady without a sound.

Percy poured the iced tea into the tin cup and handed it to her first, the way Clarisse had taught him: guests first, sisters first, Annabeth first. She drank, handed it back, lemon and cold and sweet all at once. He drank. They shared the apple, passing it back and forth, bites alternating, juice on their wrists. Nonsexual and total, the way fourteen-year-olds who had chosen each other did everything: shoulder to shoulder, knees touching, hands finding each other on the boards without looking, fingers lacing cold and warm.

"You drew the bunk," Percy said. "On the map. Silena told me."

Annabeth leaned her head against his shoulder. His shirt smelled like sun and lake and honey wrapper. "I drew the rock on the pillow too. Any honest map has to admit who's sleeping where while they sleep there."

"Thalia talk to you?" he asked. "You look Thalia-talked. That particular wise-and-annoyed."

"She said I can't cartograph grief." Annabeth turned the apple in her hands. "She said to keep the place. Not fix it. Keep it."

Percy was quiet. Then, observant and wry, the boy who noticed things: "She's right. You're terrible at not fixing. You tried to fix my collar in July with a sewing kit and nearly sewed me to the bunk."

"I fixed it perfectly. You moved." She bumped him, and he bumped back, and the dock rocked gently and settled, Tyson's bolt holding. "What was your true thing?"

He glanced at her. "Can't ask. Rule. Dishes for a week."

"I'm your girlfriend. I'm exempt from dishes."

"You're exempt from nothing. Silena runs a tyranny." But he was smiling, and the smile went soft at the edges, and he looked out at the lake instead of at her, the way he looked when he was about to say something unplanned. "It was about staying. About —" He stopped. "I don't know how to stay anywhere, Annabeth. I left a palace. I'll leave here on quests. Everybody knows it. Clarisse knows it. I told her yes, she's right, I'm not going to lie about it." His hand tightened on hers. "But today — carrying laundry wrong and getting beamed by serves and holding Tyson's ankle while he said whatever he said — today I was in it. All the way in. No counting."

Annabeth turned his hand over, traced the lifeline with one cold fingertip, precise as drafting. "My true thing was about you," she said. "Also unexplained. Also about staying. But from the other side." She looked up, gray eyes clear in the blue light. "I keep making plans for the ferry. Like logistics will save me. Thalia says keep the place. So I'm keeping it. I'm keeping you on my map exactly where you are: here, with wet cuffs and a honey wrapper in your pocket, holding a giant's ankle like it's your job."

Percy laughed, wet-eyed. "That's accurate. That's extremely accurate cartography."

"I'm official." She kissed him then — meaningful, unhurried, the kiss of two kids who had all evening and no urgency, lake on their lips and lemon between them — and he kissed her back with his free hand coming up to cup her jaw the way he always did, thumb cold against her cheek, careful as Tyson with a quilt. They broke apart, foreheads touching, breathing the same cool air, hands still laced on the boards.

"Jealous?" he asked lightly. "Of the float? Of them?"

She considered, precise even here. "A little. Not of them. Of the ease. You three have a language I don't speak yet." She bumped his forehead with hers. "But you saved me a peach and carried it in your shorts. And Clarisse gave me her shirt. And Tyson explained over to me like I was eleven. So I'm learning."

"You're Cabin Three adjacent," Percy said solemnly. "Ten minutes per occurrence, retroactive, free for girlfriends who eat peaches."

"Tell Clarisse that. She'll kill you."

"She'll allow it. She likes you. She told me the channels were wrong on your map, which from her is basically a love note."

Annabeth laughed, and the laugh went out across the water and came back changed, and the lake kept it. She pulled the quilt over both their shoulders — one quilt, the only way to share — and they sat under it with their feet in the freshwater and the stars coming out one by one over the far shore, and she took the love note from her pocket: folded paper, Percy's handwriting, NORTH BEACH HAS GOOD ROCK, with a small trident drawn underneath, lopsided and earnest. She had carried it all afternoon in with her pencils. She showed it to him.

"Keep it?" he asked.

"Keeping it," she said. "Filed. Permanent collection." She tucked it back, patted the pocket. "Tools checked. Brother —" She stopped, corrected, pink-eared in the dark. "Boyfriend checked."

"Boyfriend held twice," Percy said, remembering Tyson's words, holding her hand a little tighter under the quilt. "By water and by ankle. By lake and by you."

They sat until the lanterns blurred and the loon called again and the wind brought pine and dishwater and somebody's distant singing up from the pavilion — Silena, probably, bribing a harpy for honey with a hymn. Then they stood, folded the quilt, collected the cup and the apple core for compost, checked the third cleat with their feet out of new habit — steady, silent, Tyson's work holding — and walked up the hill hand in hand through the blue dusk, sneakers slapping, borrowed shirt flapping, pockets full of tools and pebbles and love notes.

Silena stayed late at the north beach after the others went up, because somebody had to fold the day.

She stood barefoot in the shallows, jacket zipped, hair down, gathering the forgotten things: Percy's hair tie, the tin lid, one apple, Tyson's charcoal stub wrapped in a leaf.

She wrung out the quilt, folded it square so it would not hurt anyone, set the towels in a neat stack. Maintenance, the quiet part, the work she liked. Sixteen, warm and perceptive, the girl who had kept this cabin's arithmetic for five years, standing alone in the place where the trident had burned into the sand nine summers ago and left its pale scar on the sandbar, still visible in moonlight if you knew where to look.

She knew where to look.

Footsteps on the path. Clarisse, back down with two lanterns, boots on now, braid rebraided for night, cutoff shirt dry at last. She set one lantern on the rock, handed Silena the other without a word, and stood beside her sister in the shallows with her hands in her pockets, looking out at the dark water.

"They're on the dock," Clarisse said. "Thalia's covering. I pretended not to know."

"Good," Silena said. "Middles deserve witnesses."

Clarisse snorted. "Thalia said that. She thinks she's wise because she's eighteen."

"She is wise because she's eighteen. It's in the job description." Silena bumped her, and Clarisse bumped back, the PCS language, shoulder to shoulder. "Did you count today?"

"All day," Clarisse admitted, blunt in the dark. "Every hour. Two days after today. Then the ferry. Then the bunk." She kicked a little water, watched it settle. "But Tyson said the thing about the laundry. About edges not stopping the drying. And Percy held his ankle. And the Demeter girl hit it over." Her voice went into the flatter register, the true one. "I was in it, Silena. For like an hour in the lake I wasn't counting at all. I was just — wet. And held. And annoyed at Jackson for splashing me."

Silena smiled into the dark. "That's the whole deal. That's the entire arrangement. You're allowed to have more than one kind of love, and you're allowed to be in it even when you can see the end."

"When did you get wise?"

"When you started bringing whistles to volleyball." Silena reached for the folded quilt, and Clarisse put a hand over hers.

"Give me that."

"I can carry it."

"I didn't say you couldn't. You've got the satchel, towels, the lantern and the things Percy left in his pockets. I'm standing here with empty hands."

Silena almost said she didn't mind. She did mind a little. The quilt was heavier wet, and her shoulders were beginning to ache. She had been collecting things for everyone since breakfast, including things nobody had asked her to collect.

"All right," she said. "But keep it off the ground. I just got the sand out."

Clarisse folded it over her arm, tucked the wet edge inward and gave Silena a challenging look. "Well?"

"That's right."

"Imagine that."

Silena laughed and tucked Percy's hair tie into her pocket for tomorrow. Clarisse took the extra lantern, too, without making an announcement about it. There would be work in the morning: hang the quilt to dry, return the lamps, verify the ferry time with Chiron. Nothing required Silena to do all of it.

"Come on," she said. "Let's go up. Tyson saved us seats at dinner and if we're late he'll stand up and announce it."

They walked up the switchback together in the moonlight, lanterns swinging, pine needles cool under their bare feet, the lake flashing dark-silver through the trees to the left. At the bend they stopped, as Percy had that afternoon, and looked down to the willow cove where the heron stood gray and still on the bow of the half-sunk skiff, witness to the ordinary precious thing, waiting for whatever it waited for.

"She's still there," Clarisse said.

"Good," Silena said. "I like knowing something's waiting." She started up again, singing softly with no words, the sailing song with no words she trusted, and Clarisse fell into step beside her, and the pines went by on both sides, and the cabin light showed warm through the trees ahead — Tempest Hall, pool breathing small and steady, four bunks made up, four towels on four hooks, forge-smoke and lemon oil in the air, a boy with short arms and a boy with a listening foot already arguing about tomorrow's cleats.

Tomorrow would come. The ferry would come Tuesday. The bunk would empty and the towel would stay and the arithmetic would close like water over a dive. Silena knew it. Clarisse knew it. They had known it since Chiron said bounded on the beach.

But today — laundry lines straight as ruled paper, volleyball in gold dust, carrots biggest to smallest, one true thing each with ears under, apples and iced tea and a borrowed blue shirt, a bolt in a pocket and a rock on a pillow — today had been rich in days. Today they had chosen to be in it anyway, the middle of a good thing, knowing it ended.

That was the purpose. That was what stayed.
