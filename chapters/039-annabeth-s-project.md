## Chapter 39: Annabeth's Project

The shrine was finished just after lunch, though nobody could agree on exactly when "finished" had happened.

---


Percy woke up knowing what day it was before he even opened his eyes.

That didn't happen very often. Usually he woke up knowing what time breakfast was and whether his socks were damp and whether Tyson had been sleep-talking again about ponies or forges or some combination of both. But this morning there was a bright, fizzing feeling in his chest, like someone had poured ginger ale straight into his ribs.

The shrine was done.

Annabeth's project. The thing she'd been sketching on spare napkins and the backs of Daedalus notebooks and once, memorably, on Percy's forearm in permanent marker when she couldn't find paper. The thing she'd been building with anyone she could draft into helping for the last three weeks, down in the little hollow past the strawberry fields where the woods started to get serious.

He sat up. Sunlight was coming through the Poseidon cabin windows in long blue-green bars. Tyson's bunk was empty and already made with hospital corners, because Tyson was wonderful like that. Percy's bunk looked like a seagull had exploded on it.

"Finished today," Percy said out loud to nobody, grinning.

He got dressed fast. Jeans, camp shirt, hoodie even though it was warm, because Annabeth said the morning air by the hollow had a bite and Percy had learned that when Annabeth said something like that she was usually right and also that she liked it when he listened. He shoved his feet into his sneakers without untying them, grabbed Riptide, then put Riptide back, because there were no monsters invited today. Just friends.

Mrs. O'Leary was waiting outside when he came out, tongue lolling, tail thumping a trench into the dirt.

"Hey, girl," Percy whispered, scratching behind her ear. "Big day. You be gentle today, okay? No knocking over the shrine. Annabeth worked really hard and I like living."

Mrs. O'Leary huffed, which Percy chose to interpret as a solemn promise.

The dining pavilion was already buzzing. Percy grabbed toast and eggs and an apple and sat down next to Annabeth without even having to look for her, the way you could find a lighthouse in the dark without trying. She was there, hair pulled back in a low ponytail, pencil behind her ear, staring at a list with her oatmeal going cold.

"Hey," he said, bumping her shoulder with his.

"Hey," she said, and smiled, but it was a distracted smile, the one where part of her brain was still measuring angles somewhere else.

"Eat," Percy said, pushing her oatmeal closer.

"I'm eating," Annabeth said, not eating.

"Annabeth."

She sighed and took a bite. "Happy?"

"Very," Percy said. "You finished. You actually finished it."

"It's not —" Annabeth started, then stopped herself. Percy could see her wanting to add ten qualifiers. It's not perfect, it's not sealed properly, the roof beam is three millimeters off, the inscription isn't deep enough. "It's done enough for today," she said instead, and that was huge for her.

Percy reached under the table and found her hand and squeezed. Her fingers were rough with little cuts and calluses from sanding and carving. He loved those hands. He'd held them while she cried over blueprints and while she laughed when the first arch collapsed and Grover said, "Maybe the earth just wanted a nap," and everyone lost it for ten minutes.

"I'm proud of you," he said quietly, so only she could hear.

Annabeth's cheeks went pink. "Stop."

"No."

"Percy."

"I'm proud of you," he repeated, firmer, looking straight at her.

She held his gaze for a second, gray eyes bright and a little wet already, and then she looked down at her oatmeal like it had personally offended her. "Eat your toast," she muttered, but her hand stayed in his under the table the whole rest of breakfast, and Percy's chest kept fizzing.

Across the table Thalia was watching them with an expression that was trying very hard to be annoyed and failing completely. "Are you two going to be disgusting all day?" she asked.

"Yes," Percy said.

"Good," Thalia said. "Just checking."

---


Percy was halfway down the path to the hollow when he found Nico standing beside a pile of stones, looking furious.

"Morning," Percy said.

Nico didn't look up. "The path's wrong."

Percy looked at the path. It was the same narrow track they'd used for weeks, winding from the strawberry fields through the grass. Grover had marked the edge with smooth stones so campers could find their way without trampling the new plants. Nothing appeared to be missing.

"Wrong how?"

"Try walking along it without looking down."

Percy did. He made it four steps before his sneaker caught the raised edge of a limestone piece near a tree root. He stumbled and caught himself on the trunk.

"All right," he said. "That's not great."

Nico pointed to a smaller gap farther down. "And that one's worse. Bianca helped carry the tiles yesterday. She caught her shoe there and almost dropped the whole crate."

Percy crouched beside the stone. The path was hard-packed earth, and a few of the edging pieces had shifted after rain. He'd been moving along it so often that he'd learned where to put his feet. A person coming to the dedication for the first time wouldn't know.

"Have you told Annabeth?"

"She's already worried about the roof and the inscription and whether the sunlight will reach the middle. I don't want to show up and tell her she did something wrong."

"She'd rather know."

"Probably. But I can still hate telling her."

Percy considered. He had spent enough time around Annabeth to recognize that her first reaction to a problem on a finished project was usually to imagine all the work she'd need to redo. Her second reaction was to solve it. Nico hadn't yet learned to wait through the first.

"Come with me," Percy said.

They found Annabeth by the workbench outside the hollow, measuring a short board that was supposed to sit beneath the offering bowl. She had three notebooks open and a pencil balanced behind each ear, one of which promptly fell out when she looked up.

"You're early," she said.

"There's a problem with the path," Percy answered.

Annabeth froze. "What kind of problem?"

"Two stones are raised. Nico noticed. Bianca nearly tripped yesterday."

She put down the board. "Where?"

They showed her. Annabeth walked the path once, then again from the opposite direction. She knelt by the limestone piece, pressed on it, and saw the corner shift beneath her hand.

"I thought we'd bedded that properly," she said.

"We did," Percy replied. "The rain washed some dirt out."

"I should have checked yesterday."

Nico looked uncomfortable. "I saw it yesterday, but I thought it was only one stone."

Annabeth took a breath, then glanced at him.

"Thank you for telling me now," she said.

He relaxed a fraction.

"We could put a plank over it for the ceremony," Percy suggested.

"No. Somebody would step off it, and we'd have the same problem at the edge. We need to reset the stones."

"Can we do it before everyone arrives?"

"Maybe. If we stop trying to make it invisible."

She fetched a spade and a small level from the workbench. Percy carried a bucket of gravel from the pile beside the shed, while Nico went to ask whether Grover could identify the roots beneath the edge of the path. Annabeth didn't want to damage the trees to save a few minutes.

Grover arrived with soil on his knees, listened to the problem, and showed them which exposed roots they needed to avoid. They moved the loose limestone, added gravel beneath it, packed the soil around the edge and tested it again. The corner stayed down.

The second gap was harder. It was near a bend where people naturally stepped toward the outside of the path. The gravel was wet and wouldn't hold in the shallow hole.

"We need another solution," Annabeth said.

Percy looked toward the old timber scraps. "Could we widen the path on the inside instead? Move the marker stones, not the roots."

Annabeth examined the curve. The proposal would shift the walking line half a step away from the tree and give visitors more room to pass each other. She had been thinking about patching the hole because the hole was what Nico had pointed out. Percy had looked at the route instead.

"That might work," she said. "Let's measure it."

They moved the marking stones, cut a shallow channel to carry rainwater away from the inside edge and packed the new walking surface. Then Nico walked it, followed by Percy carrying a tray held at shoulder height so he couldn't see his feet. Both made it without catching a shoe.

Annabeth watched the third trial, made by Grover with the hem of his cloak hanging down. She could still see places she wanted to improve. The route was safer than it had been an hour ago, and nobody had had to move a root or hide the problem beneath a piece of wood.

"Do we tell everyone about the change?" Nico asked.

"We put a sign at the entrance so people know the path's been widened," Annabeth said. "And after the ceremony, I'll check it again. It still needs to stand up to the next rain."

Percy looked at her. "You didn't get upset."

"I got upset. I just had a better use for the time."

She smiled, then glanced at the clock on the workbench.

"Oh, gods. I still have to finish the offering bowl."

"Then go," Percy said. "We'll clean the tools and put the signs out."

Annabeth hesitated. She was used to staying until the last job was complete, partly because she didn't trust anybody else to notice what needed doing. The three boys were already gathering the buckets. They knew where everything belonged.

"Thank you," she said.

Nico looked pleased. He had expected to ruin the dedication by bringing bad news. Instead they'd fixed something that might have hurt a guest. Percy showed him how to brush the remaining gravel off the path without scattering it into the roots, and Grover returned the borrowed spade to the shed.

By the time the first visitors arrived, the path was level. Nobody would know how recently it had been changed unless they read the small sign by the tree. Percy liked that. The work had succeeded because people could use the path without having to think about it.

---

Annabeth had built things before.

She'd built a scale model of Olympus out of toothpicks when she was seven that her dad still kept on a shelf. She'd built traps and bridges and siege ladders at camp. She'd helped rebuild cabins after the war games went wrong.

This was different.

This shrine wasn't for defense. It wasn't for a grade or a quest or to prove she belonged to her father. It was the idea she'd had one hot afternoon when she and Percy had been sitting in the hollow and she'd said, half-joking and half-not, that camp needed a place that wasn't for training or for gods, exactly, but for people. For remembering. For the small things that kept you alive when everything big tried to kill you.

And Percy had said, "Then let's build it."

Just like that. Like it was simple. Like she was worth following into the woods with a measuring tape.

Now it stood in the hollow, and Annabeth walked down the path alone before the ceremony because she needed to see it by herself first.

The path curved past the last row of strawberries, past the place where the grass got wilder and clover took over. Sunlight filtered through oak and maple leaves in shifting patches. Someone — Grover, probably — had lined the edges with smooth white stones.

Then the hollow opened up, and there it was.

It wasn't big. That had been on purpose. Annabeth hated the way godly things tried to intimidate you with size. This was low and round, built from pale limestone and reclaimed marble from the old cabins, with a cedar-shake roof that curved like a shell. Eight short pillars held the roof up, one for each of them, though Annabeth had never said that out loud. The floor was mosaic — she and Silena and Ella had spent four days on their knees setting tiny tiles in blues and grays and golds, waves and stars and owls and knots all tangled together.

In the center stood a low stone table, an altar but not really. No blood. No fire pit. Just a flat surface with a shallow bowl carved into the top for offerings, and around the rim Annabeth had carved words in Ancient Greek, her letters careful and deep:

*For those who stay. For those who are missed. For those who are still becoming.*

She stood there with her hands pressed to her mouth, breathing hard.

"It looks good," she whispered to herself. "It looks — oh gods."

She walked around it slowly, trailing her fingers along the pillars. She found every flaw instantly because of course she did — the chisel slip on pillar three, the tile on the southwest corner that was a shade too green, the beam that wasn't quite level. But underneath the flaws she could feel something solid and warm, like the shrine was breathing.

She'd designed the roof to catch morning light and throw it down onto the altar in a single circle. Right now, at almost noon, the light was right there, a perfect coin of gold in the stone bowl.

Annabeth sat down on the edge of the mosaic and cried a little, quietly, wiping her eyes with the back of her wrist before anyone could see.

She wasn't sad. That was the strange part. She felt so full she thought her ribs might crack. All those afternoons with sawdust in her hair. Percy holding boards steady without being asked. Thalia showing up with coffee for everyone even though she hated coffee. Clarisse carrying stones twice her size and pretending it was easy. Silena humming while she sorted tiles by color. Grover talking to the trees around the hollow to ask permission. Nico lurking at the edge until Annabeth handed him a hammer and he stayed. Zoe turning up with rope and knots that actually held the scaffolding together. Ella bringing snacks and facts about Roman shrines and making everyone laugh.

They'd built this. Together.

Annabeth pressed her palm flat against the warm stone. "Thank you," she whispered, not sure who she was talking to. Maybe Athena. Maybe Zeus, for once without flinching. Maybe just the hollow itself. "Thank you for letting us have this."

Footsteps crunched on the path. Percy, right on time, like he'd known she needed exactly five minutes alone and not six.

He stopped at the edge of the clearing and just looked at her and the shrine, and his face did that soft, open thing it did when he wasn't guarding anything.

"Hi," he said.

"Hi," Annabeth said, voice wobbly.

Percy crossed the clearing in six strides and sat down next to her on the mosaic, shoulder to shoulder, and didn't say anything about her red eyes. He just leaned his head against hers.

"It's beautiful," he said after a while. "You did it."

"We did it," Annabeth corrected, and laced her fingers through his.

They sat there while the light moved a fraction of an inch across the altar, and for once Annabeth didn't want to measure it or write it down. She just wanted to sit inside it.

---


Thalia was nervous, which annoyed her.

She didn't get nervous. She got angry, she got reckless, she got loud. Nervous was for — well, not for daughters of Zeus who'd run with the Hunt and stared down Luke and lived to tell about it.

But standing on the path with the others, waiting to go in, her hands were actually sweating.

"It's just a shrine, Grace," she muttered to herself. "Get it together."

"What was that?" Clarisse asked, cracking her knuckles.

"Nothing," Thalia said quickly. "You ready?"

Clarisse shrugged, but she was holding something small and wooden in her fist so tightly her knuckles were white. That made Thalia feel marginally better. If Clarisse La Rue was nervous, everybody was allowed to be nervous.

They'd agreed — Annabeth's idea, of course, everything was Annabeth's idea and that was why they loved her — to come in together but quietly, no big procession, no speeches unless someone wanted to. Just friends. Just leave something if you wanted to. Just be there.

The group gathered at the trailhead was small and strange and perfect: Percy and Annabeth in front, fingers linked. Grover with his reed pipes tucked under one arm. Silena with a folded piece of paper clutched to her chest. Clarisse looking like she wanted to punch feelings in the face. Nico half in the shadow of a tree like he wasn't sure he was invited even though Annabeth had personally asked him three times. Zoe with her hair braided back, calm and straight as a spear. And Ella, fluttering around the edges, reciting quietly, "Shrines in ancient Greece were often small, often open-air, often dedicated to heroes and nymphs and crossroads, oh, oh, this is a good shrine, this is a very good shrine —"

"Come on," Annabeth said softly, and led them in.

Thalia fell into step behind Percy. She watched the back of her sister's boyfriend's head, still damp from his morning swim, and she watched the way he kept glancing at Annabeth like he was making sure she was still breathing.

It hit Thalia then, sharp and sudden: they were twelve. Twelve. They were babies, really. And they loved each other with this steady, unshowy devotion that made Thalia's chest ache in a way she didn't have a name for.

She wasn't jealous. Not exactly. It was more like standing outside a warm cabin in winter and being glad the light was on, even if you were still out in the snow.

The hollow opened up and everybody stopped.

"Whoa," Grover breathed.

"Oh," Silena whispered, hands flying to her mouth.

"Not bad, Grace," Clarisse said gruffly, which from her was basically a sonnet.

Thalia just stared. The shrine was — gods, it was good. Solid but light. The mosaic floor caught the sun and threw it back in ripples. The cedar smelled like rain even though it was dry. And the light on the altar, that perfect circle —

Annabeth was trying very hard not to cry and failing very hard. Percy squeezed her hand and didn't let go.

Thalia's throat closed up. She'd helped raise two of those pillars. She remembered Annabeth with a level held in her teeth, cursing in Greek, and Percy laughing and holding the ladder, and the way they'd all ended up covered in limestone dust like ghosts.

She reached into her jacket pocket and closed her fingers around what she'd brought.

A spark. Literally. She'd spent yesterday morning on Half-Blood Hill with her spear, calling down the smallest, gentlest arc she could manage, coaxing it into a little glass vial Annabeth had given her for the purpose. It crackled faintly in there now, blue-white, alive. Thalia had wrapped the vial in leather so it wouldn't break.

It was a piece of her father. Not the scary part, not the lightning that killed people. The bright part. The sky part. The part that made her feel tall when she stood on a hill in a storm and laughed.

She wanted Annabeth to have that. Annabeth, her little sister, whose recent troubles she could only know through what Annabeth told her, who understood what it was to love a sky father and fear him at the same time. Who deserved brightness without fear attached.

Thalia cleared her throat hard. "Okay," she said, too loudly. "Who's first?"

Annabeth laughed wetly. "No order. Just — whenever you're ready. Place it in the bowl or around it. Say something or don't."

Silence settled, soft as dust in sunlight. Nobody moved for a second.

Then Percy stepped forward.

---


Percy had been carrying the shell for a week.

He'd found it on the camp beach the previous week, walking the tide line at dawn because he couldn't sleep. It had rolled up and tapped against his toe like it wanted his attention. For a moment he had thought of Montauk, of Sally collecting shells beside him in the life he remembered. This one belonged to the shore beneath his feet now.

It was a scallop shell, palm-sized, pink and white with ridges that caught the light. Inside it was polished smooth by years of waves, the color of sunrise through closed eyelids. When you held it to your ear you didn't hear the ocean — Percy knew what the ocean actually sounded like, it sounded like home and hunger and his dad laughing — you heard something quieter. Like breathing.

He'd kept it on his nightstand since finding it. He'd held it during the nights when the cabin felt too big and the sea sounds through the walls felt too loud. He'd almost given it to Annabeth three times that week: after a difficult sparring session, after she woke from a nightmare about the Sirens, and after a campfire spark made her jump and then laugh at herself for jumping.

He'd always pulled back. It felt too important. Too much like saying something he wasn't brave enough to say yet.

Today he was brave enough.

He knelt by the altar. The stone was warm from the sun. The circle of light sat right in the bowl, and Percy set the shell down at the edge of that light, tilted so the pink inside caught it.

"When I found this," he said, voice a little rough, "I thought — this is what safe feels like. Like, the ocean made this to protect something soft, and then it didn't need it anymore, so it gave it away." He glanced at Annabeth, who was crying openly now, no pretense. "You make everywhere feel like that. Like I can be soft and it'll be okay."

Grover made a small sound. Silena was already tearing up.

Percy stood up and went back to Annabeth and took her hand again. Her fingers were shaking.

"That was good," she whispered.

"Yeah?" Percy whispered back.

"The best," she said, and leaned her temple against his shoulder, and Percy felt about ten feet tall and also like he might cry himself, which would be embarrassing but also fine, because Annabeth was crying and Thalia's eyes were suspiciously bright and even Clarisse was looking at the ground very hard.

Thalia went next, striding forward like she was going into battle, which for Thalia was basically how she did everything tender.

---


Thalia hated talking about feelings in front of people. The years she'd spent defending Annabeth had taught her to keep important words short.

So she didn't make a speech. She just held up the little vial, where the spark snapped and glowed.

"Okay, so," she said, "this is stupid, but — Annabeth, you know how it is. With — him." She jerked her chin at the sky. Everybody knew who she meant. "Everybody thinks it's all thunder and punishment. But sometimes on the hill, when it's just me and the wind, it's — it's clean. It's bright. It makes me feel like I could run forever and not get tired."

She unscrewed the cap and tipped the vial over the bowl. The spark hovered for a second, then settled onto the stone like a tiny star, crackling softly, casting blue shadows.

"I wanted you to have some of the good part," Thalia said, looking directly at Annabeth. "Because you're my sister and you deserve the good part. And this place —" She gestured at the pillars, the roof. "This place feels like the good part. Like the sky when it's not mad."

Annabeth made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "Thalia —"

"Don't," Thalia warned, pointing at her. "No hugging. I'm serious. I'll kill you."

Annabeth hugged her anyway, fiercely, and Thalia stood there stiff for exactly one second before she hugged back hard enough to lift Annabeth off her feet a little, while everybody cheered.

"Okay, okay, let go, shortstack, you're getting snot on my jacket," Thalia grumbled, but she was smiling so wide her face hurt, and when she stepped back her spark was still glowing in the bowl next to Percy's shell, blue and pink side by side, and it looked right.

Clarisse stepped forward next, and Thalia was genuinely curious, because Clarisse hadn't told anybody what she was bringing.

---


Clarisse La Rue did not cry at dedications. Clarisse La Rue did not cry at funerals, at movies, at anything. This was a known fact, like Ares liked war and the Stolls liked stealing.

Clarisse's eyes were burning anyway, which was irritating.

She'd spent two weeks carving the panther. It was stupid. She should have just bought something or grabbed a spearhead or whatever. But Annabeth had once, late at night when they were both on watch and too tired to keep up their usual hostility, told Clarisse about the little wooden animals her dad used to carve for her when she was small, before things got complicated. How she'd lost them in a move. How she missed having something to hold when she was thinking.

Clarisse had remembered that. She hadn't meant to remember it. But she had.

So she'd taken a block of oak from the arena scrap pile and carved a panther, low and sleek, mid-prowl, tail curled around its paws. It wasn't perfect — one ear was a little lopsided — but it was fierce, and it was small enough to fit in a palm, and she'd sanded it until it was smooth as skin and rubbed it with linseed oil until it glowed dark gold.

She held it now, thumb rubbing the back where she'd worn it smooth already from carrying it around.

She knelt and set it at the foot of the altar, facing outward, guarding.

"It's a panther," she said bluntly. "For protection. Because —" She stopped. Words were not her weapon. Spears were her weapon. But Annabeth was looking at her with those gray eyes all wet and open, and Percy was nodding like he already understood, and Clarisse forced the rest out. "Because you're always protecting everybody with your plans and your brain working overtime. Somebody should guard you back. So — this guy will. When we're not around."

Silence.

Then Annabeth whispered, "Thank you, Clarisse."

Clarisse stood up fast, face hot. "Yeah. Whatever. Don't make it weird."

But Silena was beaming at her, and Grover gave her a thumbs-up, and even Zoe inclined her head with respect, and Clarisse felt something warm settle behind her sternum that felt suspiciously like pride.

She retreated to stand next to Thalia, who bumped her shoulder hard.

"Nice," Thalia said quietly.

"Shut up," Clarisse said, but without heat.

Silena went next, unfolding her paper with shaking hands.

---


Silena Beauregard believed in love the way Annabeth believed in architecture: completely, structurally, down to the foundation.

She'd written the poem at midnight in Cabin Three, with her sister and brother asleep around her and moonlight coming through the windows, and she'd cried while writing it and then copied it over in her best handwriting so the tears wouldn't smudge the final version.

Now her voice shook as she read.

She stood before the altar in her blue camp shirt with a white ribbon in her hair, paper held in both hands, and she didn't try to be performative. She just read, soft and clear.

*"For the girl who measures stars and builds them homes,*
*Who draws in dust and raises it in stone,*
*For the boy who carries oceans in his laugh,*
*Who found a shore and split it in half — no, whole —"*

She stumbled, laughed nervously. "Sorry, that line's bad, let me —"

"Keep going," Annabeth whispered. "Please."

Silena swallowed and continued.

*"For every hand that lifted, sanded, stayed,*
*For every plan that held, for every aid,*
*This hollow keeps what we cannot keep:*
*The promises we make before we sleep.*
*That we will stay. That we will try. That we will mend.*
*That every stranger here can be a friend."*

She finished and stood there breathing hard, eyes shining.

"It's not — it's not perfect meter," she said quickly. "I know the third couplet is forced, and —"

"It's perfect," Annabeth said, standing up and going to her. She took the paper gently, like it was glass, and laid it in the bowl, weighting it with a small stone so the breeze wouldn't take it. The ink was dark blue, looping and lovely. "Silena, it's perfect. I'm going to carve it. On the back pillar. Every word."

Silena burst into tears. "Really?"

"Really," Annabeth promised, and hugged her, and Silena hugged back, sobbing into Annabeth's shoulder while everybody smiled and pretended not to tear up themselves.

Percy caught Silena's eye over Annabeth's shoulder and mouthed, *Beautiful.* Silena mouthed back, *Thank you,* and wiped her nose on her sleeve, laughing.

Grover stepped forward next, twisting his cap in his hands.

---


Grover Underwood had been talking to the hollow for weeks.

Not in a weird way — well, okay, in a weird way, but that was his job. As a satyr, as lord of the wild in training, as the one who could hear the small voices in roots and leaves. He'd come down every morning before work started and sat cross-legged by the half-built pillars and listened.

The trees had been skeptical at first. *More stone? More cutting?* But Grover had explained, patiently, day after day, that this wasn't a cabin or a wall. This was a place to sit. A place to remember Pan, even. A place where the wild would be welcome, not paved over.

Slowly, the hollow had warmed. A family of rabbits moved back in. Moss crept up the north pillar in a pattern that looked almost deliberate. One morning Grover found a ring of mushrooms around the mosaic, perfect as a fairy circle, and he'd laughed out loud and Annabeth had marked it on her plans as "do not disturb - G's friends."

What he brought today was a single reed from his pipes.

He'd played those pipes since he was small. They were dented and worn, held together with twine and hope. Each reed had a voice. This one, the third from the left, had always sounded like morning — bright, a little breathy, hopeful even when the song was sad.

He'd cut it free last night with shaking hands. It felt like giving away a finger. But it also felt right. Music was meant to be left places, to echo.

He knelt and laid the reed across the bowl, next to the shell and the spark and the poem.

"I asked the woods if I could leave something," he said, his voice soft, his goat eyes large and earnest. "They said yes. They like it here. They said —" He paused, listening for a second to something none of the rest of them could hear, head tilted. "They said they'll watch it when we're gone. Keep the leaves off. Keep it quiet."

Annabeth's breath caught. "Grover —"

"And this reed," Grover continued, a little embarrassed, "it's so there'll always be music here, even when I'm not playing. If the wind blows right through the pillars, it'll hum. I tuned it. Sort of." He smiled sheepishly. "Okay, Pan tuned it. I just listened."

As if to prove him right, a breeze moved through the hollow just then, threading between the eight pillars, and the reed gave a faint, sweet note — high and clear and lingering.

Everybody gasped.

"Whoa," Percy breathed.

Ella flapped her wings excitedly. "Aeolian! Aeolian phenomenon! Wind over hollow reeds produces tones, produces music, like the wind harps, like the singing sands, oh!"

Grover beamed, ears wiggling. He went and sat down next to Percy, and Percy threw an arm around his shoulders and squeezed.

"Nice one, G-man," Percy murmured.

"Thanks," Grover whispered back, eyes bright. "I was really nervous."

"You? Never."

Ella fluttered forward next before anyone called on her, clutching something wrapped in a napkin.

---


Ella the harpy did not understand ceremonies the way demigods did, but she understood important nests.

That was what this was, she was certain. An important nest built by her flock. Annabeth-bird with clever talons who drew lines that became stone. Percy-fish-bird who smelled like salt and safety. Thalia-storm-bird. Clarisse-fight-bird. Silena-pretty-song-bird. Grover-grass-bird. Nico-shadow-bird. Zoe-bow-bird. All her flock.

Important nests needed lining. Needed snacks for the winter, for the remembering. Ella knew this from books and from bones, from the deep harpy knowledge that shiny things and tasty things kept sorrow from settling too heavily.

She had spent all morning in the kitchens, haranguing the nymphs until they let her bake. What she'd made was small honey cakes, the kind with thyme and lemon, wrapped in a clean napkin. She'd made eight — one for each of them — plus one extra for the shrine itself, for the hollow, for whoever might come hungry, in body or otherwise.

She set the napkin bundle carefully in the bowl, unfolding the corners to show the golden cakes.

"For the shrine," she announced proudly. "Snacks are important. Snacks are — snacks are memory. My — my friend Rachel says smell is the strongest memory, and honey remembers summer, and summer remembers friends, and — and —" She stumbled over her words, wings fluttering. "Harpy-ceremony. Leave food for the dead and the living. So no one is hungry. So no one forgets to eat when they are sad. Like Annabeth forgets. Like Percy reminds her."

Everybody laughed gently, and Annabeth laughed through her tears.

"Ella, that's — thank you," Annabeth said.

Ella beamed and added, rapid-fire, "'The offering of first fruits was customary, customary in Attica, Demeter received honey-cakes, honey-cakes called melitoutta, oh!' So it's traditional! It's correct!"

"It's perfect," Percy said solemnly, and Ella glowed red from beak to feathers with pride.

She scurried back to sit between Grover and Silena, who both gave her honey cake crumbs from their pockets as a reward, and Ella chirped happily.

Nico stepped forward next, so quietly most of them didn't notice until he was already at the altar. He kept his head down, hair falling over his eyes.

---


Nico di Angelo hated being looked at.

He could feel all their eyes on him, kind eyes, but still eyes, and his skin crawled. He wanted to step back into shadow and disappear. That would be easier.

But Annabeth had asked him. Three times. *Please come, Nico. You're part of this. You helped. I want you there.*

Nobody had ever said that to him before like they meant it. Like he wasn't just the creepy kid brother of the dead girl, the son of Hades to be tolerated.

He'd helped because Annabeth handed him a hammer and didn't flinch when his hands were cold. He'd stayed because Percy sat next to him at lunch without making it a big deal and stole his grapes until Nico almost smiled.

What he'd brought was growing in his palm.

It wasn't a real flower, not exactly. He'd coaxed it out of shadow last night behind the Hades cabin, crouching in the dark, whispering to the earth the way his father must have once whispered to diamonds. It was made of darkness folded into petals, deep violet-black with edges that glowed faint silver, like a star seen through water. It didn't wilt. It didn't need sun. It smelled faintly of pomegranate and cold stone.

A flower that could bloom where nothing else could. That was what he'd wanted to give.

He set it down in the bowl. It pulsed softly next to Thalia's spark, shadow and light not fighting, just sitting together.

"It's for —" Nico started, voice barely audible. He cleared his throat. "It's for people who aren't here. Who can't be here." He didn't look at anyone, but everybody thought of Bianca, of Zoe's old Hunt sisters, of Luke, of all the names carved on the dining pavilion's memorial. "So they have something too. So it's not just — bright things. So the dark has a place."

Silence, deep and respectful.

Annabeth rose and went to him. She didn't hug him — she could tell he didn't want that — she just crouched so she was at his eye level and said quietly, "Thank you, Nico. That's exactly right. This place needs shadow too, or the light doesn't mean anything."

Nico nodded jerkily, eyes stinging. He glanced at Percy, who gave him a small, steady nod, like *I see you, you're good*, and Nico felt something in his chest loosen a fraction.

He stepped back into the edge of the shade, but he didn't disappear. He stayed.

Zoe was last. She walked forward with measured steps, spine straight, chin high.

---


Zoe Nightshade had spent enough time cataloging old ritual sites to know that the size of an offering said very little about the care behind it. Some inscriptions survived for centuries because someone returned to clean them. Others disappeared because no one checked the stone after the first winter. What lasted was not necessarily what looked impressive on the day it was built.

She had watched this girl — this Annabeth, daughter of Zeus in this telling, brilliant and stubborn and tender-hearted — measure and remeasure, refuse to cut corners, weep over a crooked tile and then reset it by hand. Zoe had watched this boy — Percy, son of Poseidon — carry beams without complaint, hold ladders, bring lemonade, love without performance.

It reminded her of the best work done in the Hecate cabin: one person learning a difficult skill and another making sure she had the tools to finish it.

What she brought was a knot-charm, braided from three cords: one blue-gray wool from her own old cloak, one sea-green thread Percy had given her from a frayed camp shirt, one golden cord Annabeth had used to tie up her blueprints.

Zoe had spent three nights braiding them by firelight, working in small charms of protection and memory with each twist, whispering in Ancient Greek too old for most of them to know.

It was a lover's knot, technically, but also a friendship knot, a sister knot. A knot that said *we are bound*.

She laid it in a circle around the other offerings, encompassing them all.

"When we restore a damaged ward," Zoe said, "we mark who repaired it and who checked the work. It matters that somebody was here, and that the next person can see what she did." She looked at Annabeth. "You've made a place people want to look after. That's a good reason to build anything."

Annabeth was crying hard now, shoulders shaking, Percy's arm around her holding her upright.

Zoe knelt formally and bowed her head. "I, Zoe Nightshade, daughter of Hecate, do honor this place. May it stand when we are dust. May those who come after know we were friends here."

Nobody breathed for a second. Even the wind paused.

Then Ella whispered, "Oh, that was good, that was very good," and everybody laughed wetly, and the spell broke gently.

Annabeth stood in the center of them all, turning slowly to look at each face, at each gift glowing and gleaming in the bowl: shell and spark and panther and poem and reed and honey cakes and shadow-flower and knot.

---


Annabeth tried to speak and couldn't.

She'd prepared something. Of course she had. Three drafts on lined paper back in her cabin, crossed out and rewritten. An opening in Greek, a thank-you list, a closing quote from Athena's maxims.

All of it was gone from her head. Burned away by the sight of them standing around her shrine in dappled light, with Mrs. O'Leary snoring softly at the edge of the clearing because Percy had let her come after all, and sunlight turning Thalia's spark to a tiny sun, and the reed humming faintly when the breeze moved.

She cried. She just stood there and cried, hands over her face, shoulders shaking, while Percy held her hand tightly with both of his like an anchor.

"Sorry," she gasped. "Sorry, I had — I had a speech —"

"Don't be sorry," Silena said.

"Yeah, speeches are overrated," Thalia said gruffly, but kindly.

Percy tugged her gently down to sit with him on the mosaic edge, and Annabeth leaned into his side, his arm firm around her shoulders, his thumb rubbing small circles on her sleeve. She could smell sunscreen and sawdust on him, and underneath that salt.

She looked at her friends through tears.

"I just —" she started, then took a breath. "When I thought of this, I thought I wanted to prove I could design something real. Something that would last. That Zeus would — that people would look at and say, okay, she belongs here."

Percy squeezed her hand hard.

"But that's not what it is anymore," Annabeth continued, voice steadying. "It's not mine. It's ours. Every pillar has everybody's fingerprints in it. I can see them. I can see Grover talking to the oak while we raised the beam. I can see Clarisse carrying that cornerstone on her back. I can see Silena on her knees sorting blue tiles for hours. I can see Ella bringing us sandwiches and telling us about Roman votives. I can see Nico staying late to help me chisel even though his hands were blistered. I can see Zoe redoing all my knots because hers were better. I can see Thalia holding the ladder in the rain."

She paused, wiping her eyes.

"I can see Percy," she said softly, looking at him. "Holding everything. Holding me. Every time I wanted to quit because the arch fell or the mosaic cracked, he just said, we'll fix it. Like it was obvious. Like I was obvious. Worth staying for."

Percy was crying now too, unashamed, tears tracking through the dust on his cheeks. "You are," he said simply. "Obvious, I mean. Worth it."

A soft chorus of *awww* and groans. Clarisse threw a pebble at them. "Disgusting," she said, but she was smiling.

Annabeth laughed, leaning her forehead against Percy's temple for a second. Then she stood, pulling him up with her, facing the shrine with their hands linked.

"So I dedicate this," she said clearly, "not to any god. Not even to Zeus, and I hope he understands." A nervous laugh rippled — dedicating something *not* to the gods at Camp Half-Blood was bold. "I dedicate it to us. To staying. To the small things." She touched each gift as she named it. "To safety," shell. "To brightness," spark. "To guarding each other," panther. "To words that hold," poem. "To music in the wind," reed. "To feeding each other," honey cakes. "To remembering the missing," shadow-flower. "To being bound," knot. "May anyone who comes here tired find rest. May anyone who comes here sad find that someone left something beautiful for them."

Wind moved through the pillars again. The reed sang. The spark crackled. The shadow-flower pulsed. Light pooled gold in the bowl.

Nobody spoke for a long time. They just stood there together in the quiet, warm hollow, with dust motes drifting like stars.

---


They stayed for hours.

No one had planned to. But Silena passed around extra honey cakes Ella had smuggled, and Grover played his pipes softly — a new song, bright and slow, that made the reed in the bowl hum along in harmony — and Thalia lay back on the mosaic with her arms behind her head, staring at the cedar roof and the sky beyond. Clarisse showed Nico how to whittle a whistle. Zoe retied Ella's shoelaces into a knot that would never come undone unless you knew the trick. Annabeth curled into Percy's side with her head on his shoulder, his jacket draped over her legs, holding his hand with both of hers, quiet and wrung out and glowing.

Percy watched them all over the top of Annabeth's head, his heart so full it felt like it might spill.

He thought about the wars they'd all survived, or would survive, in other tellings. He thought about how rarely they got afternoons like this — no prophecy, no villains, no ticking clock. Just sun and stone and friends and the girl he loved breathing slow and even against his shoulder, half-asleep.

Annabeth stirred. "Are you happy?" she murmured, half-dreaming.

Percy pressed a kiss to the top of her head, dusty blond hair soft against his lips. "Yeah," he whispered. "Are you?"

Annabeth smiled without opening her eyes, tears dried on her cheeks, hand tightening in his. "Yeah. Stay?"

"Always," Percy said, and meant it with everything he had.

Around them the shrine stood solid and small and perfect, holding their offerings safe in its bowl of light. The shell gleamed pink. The spark glowed blue. The panther stood guard. The poem lay weighted against the wind. The reed waited for the next breeze. Crumbs of honey cake dotted the stone. The shadow-flower bloomed without sun. The knot circled them all, holding fast.

Thalia's voice drifted over, drowsy: "You two are still disgusting."

"Go to sleep, Thalia," Percy said fondly.

"Already asleep," Thalia mumbled.

Laughter rippled softly and faded. Grover's pipes played on, gentle as breath. Mrs. O'Leary sighed in her sleep. Sunlight shifted gold across the mosaic, turning waves and stars and owls to fire.

Annabeth's breathing deepened. Percy stayed awake a little longer, keeping watch the way he always did, not because there was danger but because someone should witness this. Someone should remember exactly how it looked: his friends safe, his girl happy, their small temple full of small, enormous love.

Quiet. Warm. No monsters. Just this.

Percy closed his eyes, hand in Annabeth's hand, and let the hollow hold them all.
