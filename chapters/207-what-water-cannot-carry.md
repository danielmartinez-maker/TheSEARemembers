## Chapter 207: What Water Cannot Carry

Percy found the coils before breakfast, because Beckendorf had asked for someone small enough to reach the lower rack without shifting the wet canvas. He had not said small. He had said, “Percy, can you bring the tally book and keep the tags dry?” Which was Beckendorf’s tactful way of assigning Percy a job that depended on patience more than height.

The shed smelled of damp rope, cedar, and yesterday’s rain. Water tapped from the roof edge into the barrel outside. The storm had moved east, but the ground had not decided what to do with all the water it had taken in. The board at the fence was still where they had left it, braced at a slight angle. No one was going to test the footing until the mud had firmed.

Percy set the tally book on a crate and checked the three replacement tags. Annabeth had written the first two. The third had a little blank space at the bottom where she’d left room for Beckendorf’s measurements.

“You made these all readable,” Percy said.

“I thought I’d try something reckless.”

“Legible handwriting?”

“Don’t tell the Athena cabin. They’ll make me turn in my pencil.”

The corner of her mouth lifted. She’d tied her hair back with a strip of blue cloth, and a short line of graphite crossed the side of her thumb. Percy reached for it, then paused. Annabeth held out her hand before he had to ask. He rubbed the mark away with the pad of his thumb. She kept her palm under his for a moment, then took the tally book and checked the counts.

Two coils, one canvas strip. Three tags.

“Still true,” she said.

“Good. I was worried the storm had secretly added a fourth tag.”

“Storms are bad at paperwork.”

“They’re better at moving boards.”

Her look said she had noticed the edge in that. Percy looked down at the list. He had meant it as a joke, but it had come out with Leandros inside it. His older brother had spent the night somewhere in the Big House, or maybe the guest room. Percy did not know. Nobody had sent him to ask.

A scrape came from the far side of the shed. Leandros stood near the rack, one hand on the outer coil. He had changed clothes, but the hem of his trousers was still dark with mud. He looked at the line as though he could tell, just by sight, how long it would take to dry.

“Beckendorf said we should spread it out,” Percy said.

“I know what he said.”

“Okay.”

Leandros lifted the coil half an inch, found the lower strand sticking to the rack, and set it down again. He was careful not to drag it. Percy noticed. It was a small thing, but the rope had been expensive enough that everyone had noticed the wet.

“Can you move the rack away from the wall?” Leandros asked.

Percy glanced toward the door. The rack was fixed to the floor.

“No.”

“It would dry faster.”

“It’s bolted down.”

“I can see that.”

“Then we’re both caught up.”

Annabeth made a quiet sound that might have been a cough. She went back to the tags.

Leandros looked at Percy for another second. Percy waited for the next instruction. He almost heard it in advance: hold this, move that, tell Clarisse. Instead, Leandros crouched and checked the bottom strand where it met the shelf.

“The lower wrap is wet too,” he said.

“Beckendorf saw that,” Percy answered. “He’ll check it later.”

“Later means more time sitting damp.”

“He said it needs air on both sides.”

“It has air on both sides.”

“Not under the wrap.”

Leandros’s shoulders tightened. He had a point, probably. That was part of what made him hard to argue with. He had points that were true, then built the rest of his plan as if truth in one place gave him permission everywhere else.

“Ask Beckendorf before you shift it,” Annabeth said without looking up.

Leandros straightened. “I’m not moving the board.”

“No one said you were.”

“I know what you meant.”

Annabeth put down the pencil. “Do you?”

For a moment, Percy thought he would turn it into a fight. Leandros’s gaze went from her to the rack, then to the list. He lifted his chin.

“I can handle a coil.”

“Then let Beckendorf tell you how he wants it handled,” Percy said.

His brother’s expression changed at that, not softer exactly, but less certain about who was supposed to be giving orders. “You came to help?”

“I came to keep the tags dry.”

“That’s all?”

“That’s what Beckendorf asked me.”

Leandros looked at him. Percy knew that look. It was the same look he used when somebody had said something that didn’t fit the plan he’d already made. Percy had seen it in the mirror sometimes, after he’d decided to fix something by himself and Annabeth asked whether he’d actually checked what was wrong.

“Fine,” Leandros said.

The word held no agreement, but he stepped away from the coil.

A minute later, Beckendorf came in with a narrow wooden spacer and a roll of clean cloth. He greeted Annabeth, asked Percy to keep the tags on the table, and showed Leandros how he wanted the wet outer length loosened without putting a twist in the core. Leandros watched, then took the far end when Beckendorf nodded to him.

His hands knew rope. They moved smoothly, with no wasted pull. Percy had seen him handle a small skiff line in the lake, and it had been obvious he had spent years around boats. That skill did not make him right about the board. It did make Percy feel the unfairness of how quickly everyone else’s competence was discounted when Leandros had a better answer for one narrow thing.

“Hold the slack there,” Beckendorf said. “Don’t pull against me.”

Leandros held it.

“Good. Now ease it.”

They opened the coil a little at a time, letting the rope breathe without flattening it. Beckendorf put the spacer in. Leandros kept the slack steady until the last bend rested clear of the wood.

“There,” Beckendorf said. “That gives us air underneath. We’ll check it before supper and again tomorrow.”

Leandros nodded. “It should dry faster.”

“It should dry evenly,” Beckendorf corrected. “Faster doesn’t help if we damage the fibers.”

The answer seemed to catch at Leandros. He looked like he wanted to respond, then settled for, “All right.”

Percy wrote down the time and the new placement. His handwriting tilted downhill. Annabeth took the pencil from him and added the word “spacer” in a careful hand.

“You’re making the book look like it’s been kept by two different people,” Percy said.

“It has.”

“I thought I was the only person who worked here.”

“You do not work here.”

“I work near here.”

“On the days you’re assigned.”

“That’s what I meant.”

“No,” Annabeth said, but she nudged his elbow with hers. “It isn’t.”

Leandros glanced at their shoulders touching. His face gave nothing away, but he stopped looking before Percy could decide what that meant. He picked up the clean cloth and waited for Beckendorf to show him where it should go.

Percy’s pencil rested over the blank line beside the three damaged tags. The work had started. The fence had not been touched. No one had said the storm was finished just because the rain had stopped.

---

The tags were small, but the work took longer than Percy expected. Three of them had been written in chalk on thin wood, and rain had blurred the words until the marks looked like gray islands. Beckendorf had a set of measurements from the rack and a list of what belonged where. Percy had assumed replacing a tag meant copying the old one. Annabeth explained that the old one had to be checked against the equipment before anyone copied it.

“If we write the wrong name neatly, it’s still wrong,” she said.

“That sounds like an Annabeth rule.”

“It is a rule for being able to find things.”

“I like being able to find things.”

“You left your shoes under the table again.”

“I found them.”

“You found one.”

He had found both. One had been under the table; the other had been by the door. Percy didn’t mention that the second shoe had been there since last night, because the detail did not support his case.

They laid the old tags beside Beckendorf’s inventory and matched them one by one. Percy held the pieces in place while Annabeth wrote. She printed each word in dark block letters, left enough room for a date, and used a short line under the category so anyone approaching from the shed entrance could read it.

The first tag belonged to a set of spare stakes. The second identified a bundle of canvas ties. The third marked two coils of light line. Percy was the one who caught that one.

“This used to say ‘long line,’” he said. “The coil is shorter than the one on the upper rack.”

“According to this list, it is the spare light line. The long line is in the east locker.”

“Then the tag was wrong before the storm.”

“Or it was moved and the tag wasn’t changed.”

“That is a much more reasonable answer than the storm secretly changing it.”

“I won’t say that again.”

“I was going to.”

Annabeth looked at him. “You can still say it.”

“I don’t want to waste a good joke twice.”

“Then put the new tag down.”

He slid it across the table, and she weighted it with a brass washer so the breeze from the open door wouldn’t turn it over.

There were footsteps behind them. Ianthe entered with a folded page in her hand. Her hair was pinned up, and she had a faint mark from the clip on her temple. Percy had not seen her since the storm. She looked from the inventory to the wet coil to the open door.

“If you leave the door wide, the air will move more evenly through the shed,” she said.

Annabeth kept writing. “The door is open because we’re bringing tags in and out.”

“It will keep the canvas from drying.”

“Beckendorf says the canvas strip is inside the shed and spread across two rails.”

“I saw that. I meant the coil.”

“Beckendorf has already moved the coil so it can dry on both sides,” Percy said. “He’ll check it before supper.”

Ianthe held the paper against her palm. “The wind is turning east. The opening is on the east wall.”

“So the breeze comes across the room,” Percy said.

“And then the rack.”

“Beckendorf put the spacer in so the air can get under it.”

She studied the setup. Her eyes went to the rope, then to the window above the far rack. “That may be enough.”

“It’s what we can do without moving the rack.”

“I wasn’t suggesting we move the rack.”

“You were about to tell us to move the coil to the other side.”

“I was about to say we should open the window.”

“Would that help?”

“I don’t know. I would need to see which way the gust comes through.”

That was closer to an answer. Ianthe unfolded her paper. It was covered with arrows, numbers, and small marks. Percy recognized a column that looked like the time windows from her schedule. She had written wind direction beside each one. The work was careful. It also seemed as if she had counted on the paper to understand the whole shed.

“I can add the wind note to the inventory,” she said. Then her hand paused above the table. “If that’s useful.”

“Beckendorf keeps the drying checks,” Percy said. “You could ask him whether he wants it with the other notes.”

Ianthe’s mouth thinned. She glanced toward the open door. Beckendorf was outside with Clarisse, checking the lower path. “He’s occupied.”

“You can wait.”

“I can leave the note here.”

Annabeth’s pencil stopped. “Ask before you change the record.”

Ianthe looked at her for a moment. The remark seemed to push at the bruise from yesterday, the stool, the drawing left only with permission. She folded the weather page once, slowly, instead of laying it on top of the inventory.

“I was asking,” she said.

“You asked if it was useful,” Annabeth replied. “That isn’t the same as asking whether you may add it.”

Ianthe’s eyes lowered to the paper. Percy wanted to smooth the edges, as if a mild disagreement needed him to make everyone comfortable. He let the quiet sit. He knew what happened when he rushed to fill it.

“Then I’ll ask Beckendorf when he’s free,” Ianthe said. The words came out clipped.

“Sure,” Percy said.

“I’m not trying to take his place.”

“Okay.”

“People keep assuming that.”

“Maybe because the paper has a schedule on it.”

“It’s a weather sheet.”

“Your weather sheet put the canvas in the lower shed yesterday.”

Ianthe’s jaw shifted. “It was a work schedule. And yes, I put it there.”

“You didn’t put it there by yourself. I saw the list.”

“I still wrote it.”

She tucked the page under her arm. For a second Percy thought she might say something else, maybe remind them the gusts had helped secure the cover. They had. Everyone knew it. No one had accused her of being wrong about every part of the storm.

Instead, she looked at the tag Annabeth had just written. “The writing is clear.”

“Thanks,” Annabeth said.

“I should have checked where the supplies were before I made the sequence.”

“That would have helped,” Percy said.

Ianthe nodded once, a little too sharply. “I’ll find Beckendorf.”

She did not leave the shed until she had checked the nearest window, though she did not touch it. Percy watched her notice the latch and make herself turn away.

“She’s still keeping track,” he said.

“Of the wind?”

“Of us.”

Annabeth added the last date line. “Maybe. You don’t know that.”

“I know she is tracking whether I’m going to be mad.”

“You don’t know that either.”

He leaned his hip against the table. “You think I’m guessing.”

“I think you’re translating again.”

“Maybe I’m trying to figure out what everybody’s doing.”

“And then tell them what it means?”

He looked at her pencil marks, each line exact. “I was going to say she’s paying attention.”

“That’s possible.”

“Are you telling me to stop?”

“I’m asking what you want to do with the thing you think you know.”

Percy could have said he wanted to help. He did want to help. His instinct was already building a bridge: Ianthe had made a mistake; Leandros would make another; Clarisse would stay angry; Silena would get tired of them all; Percy would figure out the right sentence and pass it along. It was the kind of plan that made him feel useful until someone pointed out it wasn’t his to make.

“Nothing yet,” he said.

Annabeth slid the last tag toward him. “Then hold this while the ink dries.”

He placed his palm over one corner, careful not to smear the letters. Annabeth left her fingers on the other end for a moment. The brass washer kept the tag flat. Neither of them mentioned the storm.

By midafternoon, Beckendorf had asked Percy to take the corrected tags to the lower shed. The rain had stopped, but the ground between the cabins still held puddles in the wheel ruts. Percy carried the wooden pieces in both hands and tried not to drop them when his shoe slid against a slick patch of mud.

At the fence, Clarisse stood with Chiron and Beckendorf. They were looking at the board from the dry side, where the soil had not yet collapsed under the runoff. It looked harmless, which Percy thought was probably why it had been so easy to underestimate.

Leandros was a few paces away, carrying a short length of spare line. When he saw Percy, he shifted the coil against his hip.

“Those the new tags?” he asked.

“Yeah. Beckendorf wanted them back in the shed.”

“I’ll take them.”

“He said I should put them in the inventory box.”

“I can do that.”

Percy kept the tags in his hands. “He asked me.”

Leandros’s eyes narrowed, but before he could answer, Clarisse turned around.

“Percy. Put them by the work list.”

He did. The box sat on a dry stone beside the fence, away from the mud. Leandros watched him place the tags, then looked back at Clarisse. She had seen the whole thing.

“You can take that line to the rack,” she said.

“It’s not going to stay dry here.”

“I said take it to the rack. Beckendorf already showed you where.”

“I know where the rack is.”

“Then go.”

Leandros glanced toward Chiron. Chiron did not step in. Beckendorf adjusted the brace at the side of the board. Clarisse waited, arms folded.

Leandros took the line to the shed. He returned a few minutes later without it. This time he stopped in front of Clarisse, leaving enough space between them that she would not have to move back.

“I need to say something.”

“Say it.”

He looked once toward Percy, who had come to collect the empty box. “I moved the board after you told me to leave it. I thought the water was backing up behind it. I didn’t check the far side first.”

Clarisse’s expression did not change. “You didn’t.”

“I sent the runoff toward the lower shed. Two coils got wet, the tags blurred, and the canvas needs to dry. No one got hurt, but it cost time and supplies.” He paused. “I’m sorry.”

Percy put the box down on the stone. He hadn’t meant to stay. He found himself listening anyway.

Clarisse looked at the board, then at Leandros. “You should be.”

“I know.”

The silence stretched. Percy could hear the creek beyond the path, swollen but moving. Leandros’s fingers flexed once at his side.

“So,” he said, “are we done with it?”

Clarisse’s eyes came back to his. “No.”

“I apologized.”

“And I heard you.”

“I’m doing the work.”

“You’re doing the part of the work Beckendorf assigned you.”

“What else do you want me to do?”

“I want you to stop asking what it takes to get me to say we’re fine.”

“I didn’t say fine.”

“You asked if we’re done.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“It is when you ask it right after you apologize.”

Leandros looked away, toward the board. His jaw moved. “I can’t change what I did.”

“No.”

“I’ve said it was mine.”

“That matters.”

“Then what?”

Clarisse did not answer immediately. She shifted her weight, one boot sinking a fraction into the soft earth. Percy wondered if she was doing it on purpose, testing how firm the ground was. She glanced down, then stepped back onto the gravel.

“Then you keep doing the next thing right,” she said. “Not so I owe you a different answer. Because the next thing needs doing.”

“That could take forever.”

“It could take a while.”

“I can’t spend every day proving I’m not a problem.”

“You don’t have to. Just don’t make the problem somebody else’s job to clean up.”

Her voice was calm enough that Percy noticed the effort it took her to keep it that way. Clarisse could have made the sentence louder. She could have turned it into a public challenge. She didn’t.

Leandros gave a short laugh without humor. “You think I don’t know how to work?”

“I think you knew what you were told and did what you wanted anyway.”

“I thought I had a better answer.”

“You thought you had the only one.”

His eyes hardened. Percy recognized that, too. The same pride that made Leandros good with a boat rope seemed to come forward whenever someone else chose the route. He could accept a correction about a knot, maybe, if it came from someone who had shown him the knot. He had a harder time accepting that the people who lived with a place might know things he hadn’t learned from water.

“I’m not asking you to trust me with the fence,” Leandros said.

“You just asked me if we were done.”

He pressed his lips together.

Percy picked up the empty box. He wanted to say something that would make the air less sharp. He could tell Leandros he had seen him carry the rope carefully, or tell Clarisse the apology sounded real. Both were true. Neither was his to turn into a verdict.

Leandros turned to him anyway. “You’ve been here longer. What does she want?”

Percy felt the old pull to answer, to give his brother the translation he thought might stop the fight. Annabeth’s question came back: what would he do with the thing he thought he knew?

“Ask her,” Percy said.

“I just did.”

“You asked how to make her say you’re fine. That’s different.”

Leandros stared at him. “You’re my brother.”

“Yeah.”

“So you could tell me.”

“I could tell you what I think. That wouldn’t be the same as what she thinks.”

Clarisse looked at Percy, something unreadable in her face. He stepped away before either of them could take his answer as permission to keep asking.

Behind him, he heard Leandros say, “I’m not trying to make you choose sides.”

“No one said you were,” Clarisse replied.

Percy reached the path before he turned back. Clarisse was already looking at the board again. Leandros stood with his hands empty, as if he had expected the apology to leave him holding something and did not know what to do with the space.

---

Annabeth was waiting beside the tool shed, where the path widened enough to let a cart turn around. She had the inventory book tucked under her arm and a folded scrap of paper in her free hand.

“You look like somebody asked you to pick a side,” she said.

“Didn’t get a chance.”

“That isn’t the same as not picking one.”

Percy sat on the low stone wall. The damp had seeped through his jeans at the knee when he leaned on the shed. “He asked me what Clarisse wanted.”

“And what did you say?”

“To ask her.”

“That was good.”

“I wanted to say more.”

“I know.”

She sat beside him, leaving a little space between their shoulders. The air smelled like wet leaves and the iron tang of the fence. Percy watched Beckendorf carry a bucket toward the shed. He wanted to follow and offer help, but Beckendorf had not asked.

“Clarisse might think I should have backed him up,” Percy said.

“Does she?”

“I don’t know.”

“Did she say that?”

“No.”

“Then you don’t know.”

He gave Annabeth a sideways look. “You keep saying that today.”

“Because you keep trying to fill in the blank.”

“I don’t want them to get worse.”

“They might.”

He looked out at the muddy track. “That’s a terrible plan.”

“It isn’t a plan. It’s an answer.”

The pause between them wasn’t uncomfortable, but it was heavy enough that Percy wanted to move it. He set his hand on the wall. Annabeth rested hers a few inches away, palm down. He turned his hand, and their little fingers touched.

“You could talk to him without telling him what Clarisse thinks,” she said.

“I know.”

“You can also decide you don’t want to.”

“He’s my brother.”

“And Clarisse is your sister.”

“Yeah.”

“Silena is, too.”

“I know.”

Annabeth folded the scrap of paper over her thumb. “You don’t have to be the bridge between people just because you’re in the middle of the list.”

“I’m not on the middle of anything.”

“You’re the youngest of the first three and the oldest person who remembers being a teenager in the old timeline.”

“That sounds like a really bad job description.”

“It isn’t a job.”

He looked down at their hands. “It still feels like I should know what to do.”

“Would you want him to do this for you?”

“Do what?”

“Explain your anger to Clarisse. Decide what you meant before you tell her. Hand her the correct response so she can give it back to you.”

He considered that. There were times he would have wanted someone to step between him and a fight. There were also times he wanted to say the words himself, even if he said them badly.

“No,” he said.

“Then you know one thing.”

He bumped his shoulder against hers. “You’re good at this.”

“At what?”

“Knowing the thing I don’t want to hear.”

“I’m excellent at that.”

He smiled. She smiled back, then pressed her shoulder more firmly into his. It was their version of taking a seat together without having to decide how long they would stay.

“Also,” she said, “I brought you this.”

The folded paper was a sketch of the drying rack, with the rack’s legs measured from the floor. Percy glanced at the drawing. “You drew a rectangle.”

“I drew a rectangle with useful measurements.”

“I could tell.”

“Beckendorf asked me to check whether we have a shorter spacer. We don’t.”

“You went all the way back to Cabin One to draw this?”

“I was already going there.”

“You didn’t have to bring it to me.”

“I know.” She placed it between them. “I wanted you to see it.”

He leaned closer and traced the pencil line without touching the paper. “The lower shelf angles a little to the left.”

“It does.”

“Could be the wall.”

“It’s the floor. The shed settles when the ground stays wet.”

“Should we tell Beckendorf?”

“I told him.”

“Then the drawing is finished.”

“For this morning.”

She reached for his wrist and turned it over. A narrow red line had appeared where the wet rope had rubbed him.

“That stings?” she asked.

“A little.”

“Show me your other hand.”

He did. There was a shallow scrape at the base of his thumb. She pulled a small square of gauze from her pocket, probably taken from the Big House supply box. Percy looked at it.

“You carry bandages now?”

“Not always.”

“Annabeth.”

“It’s a small one.”

“I got scraped by rope.”

“Would you like to argue about its size or let me wrap it?”

He held out his hand. She pressed the gauze down and secured it with a strip of tape. The task was so small it should not have mattered. It did. Percy liked when she paid attention without turning him into someone fragile.

“Thanks.”

“You’re welcome.”

He leaned toward her. She met him halfway for a brief kiss, quiet and familiar. The wind lifted the corner of the drawing. Annabeth caught it with her free hand, then kept her thumb over the line where their hands had been touching.

“Are you going to talk to him?” she asked.

“Maybe after supper.”

“Because you want to, or because you think you can make him feel better?”

“Both, probably.”

“That’s honest.”

“It’s still a bad reason.”

“Could be. You’ll know once you’re there.”

He folded the sketch carefully along the old crease and slipped it into his pocket. “You’ll be at supper?”

“Unless someone moves my stool.”

He looked at her.

“I’m joking,” she said. “The glue’s had its extra day. I’ll check it before I leave.”

“Do you want me to come?”

“No. I want to check it myself.”

He nodded. “Okay.”

She stood and brushed her palms together. “I’ll see you at the table.”

“Which one?”

“The one with the tags.”

“That is technically a bench.”

“You can decide what to call it after supper.”

She walked toward Cabin One with the inventory book under her arm. Percy waited until she had turned the corner before he checked the gauze on his hand. It held. He left the pocketed drawing where it was and headed back toward the lower shed.

---

Silena had taken over one end of the Camp Three kitchen table with a bowl, a board, and a folded page covered in notes. The stove was warm enough to keep the room comfortable, though the door still let in a trace of cold whenever someone crossed the threshold.

“You’re cooking?” Percy asked.

“I’m testing something.”

“What?”

“Bread.”

There were already four small rounds on a cloth beside the bowl. One had a crooked edge. Another was sprinkled with salt. The last one looked a little too dark underneath.

“Which is the test?” he asked.

“All of them.”

“Then how do you know which one worked?”

“You taste them.”

“That’s a good system.”

“It’s not finished. The point is to see how much salt and herb I want in the dough before I make a full batch.” Silena moved the overbrowned round onto a separate plate. “I don’t want people telling me it’s perfect because they think I made it for them.”

“Who would do that?”

She looked at him.

“Right,” Percy said. “I have done that.”

“Not about bread.”

“But I could.”

“You could.”

She handed him a small wooden spoon. “Stir the next bowl. Slowly. The dough’s already rested.”

He began mixing. It was soft and cool under the spoon, with chopped rosemary and a little oil. Silena watched his technique for a moment, then went back to her page.

“You can scrape the side too,” she said.

“I’m doing both.”

“You’re scraping the side with the spoon. It’s still sticking to your fingers.”

“I can wash my hands.”

“That would help.”

Percy set the spoon down and rinsed his hands in the basin. Behind him, the door opened. Leandros came in with his sleeves rolled to the elbows, carrying the set of new tags. He put them on the dry corner of the table.

“Those are the replacements?” Silena asked.

“Beckendorf checked them.”

“I’m glad.”

Leandros glanced at the bowl. “If you add more salt, it’ll taste better.”

“I’m testing the salt now.”

“Salt’s what makes bread taste like anything.”

“Not the only thing.”

“It’s the part that matters.”

Silena looked at him. “I want to find out what I like.”

He shifted the tags so they were in a neater pile. “I’m not stopping you.”

“You’re telling me what the answer should be.”

“I’m telling you what works.”

“Then make one that works for you.”

Leandros’s hand stopped above the labels. “You’re always making a contest out of every small thing.”

“I’m making bread.”

Percy looked for a way to answer, then decided not to. Silena’s voice was calm, but she was not smiling. The kitchen gave the impression that the evening might still be easy if no one made it harder. Leandros had managed to make the salt into a verdict.

He reached for the bowl. “I can mix it.”

“I didn’t ask you to.”

“Fine.”

Silena picked up one of the test rounds and cut it into three pieces. “Percy, take this one. Annabeth can try the herb version when she comes by. Clarisse can have the salty one if she wants it.”

Leandros looked at the separate plate. “And that one?”

“I’m deciding.”

“You don’t have to make a different batch for everyone.”

“I’m not.”

She set the burnt piece to the side and returned to the bowl.

Leandros placed the tags closer to the inventory book. “I wanted to ask you something.”

“Ask.”

“Can you tell Clarisse that I understand what she said?”

Silena’s knife paused on the board. “No.”

“It’s one sentence.”

“Then you can say it to her.”

“She already heard me apologize.”

“She did.”

“I don’t know what else I’m supposed to say.”

“Then don’t say another thing yet.”

“You could at least tell me whether she—”

“I’m not going to translate her for you.”

“I didn’t ask you to translate.”

“You asked me to carry something to her because you don’t like the answer you got from her face.”

Leandros blinked. He looked at Percy, then back to Silena. “You’re making it sound worse than it is.”

“I’m telling you what it feels like from here.”

“I apologized to her.”

“I heard.”

“I’m not asking for anything.”

“You asked if you were done.”

“That was earlier.”

“And now you’re asking me to help you get an answer from her.”

“I want to know what I should do.”

“Do the work you agreed to do.”

“I am.”

“Then do it without making me a messenger.”

He pulled one of the tags toward him, then stopped before he crossed the line of scattered flour on the table. “You’re angry with me too.”

“I have my own reason.”

“What is it?”

“When you moved the board, I had to redo the inventory. I spent part of the morning matching every blurred tag to the list because I didn’t want someone grabbing the wrong coil. You talked about the camp storage like it was below you, then left me with the extra counting.”

“I didn’t leave you with it.”

“You didn’t ask who would do it.”

“I didn’t know the tags were your job.”

“They weren’t. Now they are.”

Her voice wavered just enough for Percy to hear the effort it took not to turn it into a joke. Leandros’s brows drew together.

“I thought the water was blocked.”

“You thought it. Then you moved the board. That’s your choice. The extra work is mine. Those can both be true.”

He looked down at his hands, then at the tags. “I’m sorry.”

Silena nodded once. “Okay.”

His shoulders lifted, as though he expected something to follow. When it didn’t, he said, “Is that all?”

“That’s what I have for you right now.”

“Are you mad?”

“I’m annoyed. And tired of being asked to sort your words for you.”

“I didn’t ask you to sort them.”

“You just asked me to take them to Clarisse.”

He exhaled. “I thought you’d know how to talk to her.”

“I do know how to talk to her. That’s why I’m not doing it for you.”

Silena reached for the bowl, and Percy picked up the spoon again. For a while, the work at the table was only stirring, measuring, and setting the next pieces of dough under a cloth. Leandros kept his hands off the tags. He did not leave, either.

When Annabeth came in, she tasted the herb round and said the rosemary was better than the salt. Silena wrote that down. Percy’s piece needed more oil, he said. Silena asked whether he meant in the dough or on top. He chose the top. She added it to the page.

Leandros stood by the door, watching them make marks that did not settle the question. He could have left. He could have given his opinion. Instead, he waited until Silena looked up.

“Do you want me to put these tags in the box?” he asked.

“Beckendorf asked me to check them first.”

“Then I’ll leave them here.”

He set his hands at his sides, not quite at ease, but not reaching for the stack again.

---

Clarisse came in after the sun had dropped behind the pines. She had washed the mud from her boots, but a dark line still marked the hem of her trousers. She went straight to the basin, scrubbed her hands, and looked at the bread on the cloth.

“Which one is least likely to break a tooth?”

“The herb one,” Silena said. “Probably.”

“Probably?”

“It’s a test.”

Clarisse took the plain round and tapped it against the table. The sound was light and hollow. She tasted a corner, chewed, and swallowed.

“Needs salt.”

“Good,” Silena said. “That’s what I wrote.”

“You wrote that it was too dry.”

“That too.”

Clarisse put the bread down, then picked up the browned piece. “This one’s burnt.”

“That’s the one you’re supposed to tell me about.”

“I did.”

“Could you say more than one word?”

“It’s burnt on the bottom and soft in the middle.”

“Better.”

Clarisse handed the piece to Percy. “Try it.”

He took a bite. “The top is good.”

“The bottom is charcoal,” Annabeth said.

“Not charcoal. Near-charcoal.”

“Percy.”

“Fine, burnt.”

Silena wrote down the note and drew a small line through “probably.” “I thought the middle was softer.”

“It is,” Clarisse said. “You left it covered after you took it off the pan.”

“I wanted it to stay warm.”

“It kept cooking.”

Silena looked from the page to the browned round. “That makes sense.”

Leandros had been leaning near the counter, watching the exchange. He lifted the herb round and turned it in his hands. “The rosemary is stronger than the salt.”

“It’s supposed to be,” Silena said.

“Then it needs more salt.”

“Maybe.”

“Bread should have a clear taste.”

“Does it?”

“It does when it’s made right.”

Silena took the bread from him. “You can tell me if you like it. I’m not asking you to judge the recipe.”

“I did tell you.”

“You told me what bread should be.”

He let out a breath through his nose. “I said it needs more salt.”

“And I heard you. I don’t want more salt.”

Clarisse reached for the herb piece. “It’s fine like this.”

“Fine?” Silena asked.

“Good, then.”

“Better.”

The conversation softened by degrees. Percy sat on a stool and tasted the plain round again with a dab of oil. Annabeth suggested the oil might work better brushed on before the bread came off the pan. Silena tried a small corner with the adjustment. She did not write down everything. She circled the herb version and added one note: “keep rosemary; less oil underneath.”

“Will you make a full batch?” Percy asked.

“Not tonight.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Maybe. It depends what else I want to do.”

“You could make them for the whole cabin,” Leandros said.

Silena set the pen down. “I could. I might also make them just for myself.”

Clarisse ate another bite of the herb bread. “That’s allowed.”

“I know.”

“Good.”

They stood around the table as the room warmed with the bread and the stove. The storm was outside now, reduced to drip and wind. It had left a mud track at the door and a list of things drying in the shed, but it had not taken the smell of rosemary or the heat of a pan.

Percy reached for the piece Annabeth had set aside for him. She moved it closer to his hand without looking up. Their fingers brushed, and she gave him a brief, amused glance when he used the back of his sleeve to catch the oil on his thumb. There was no need for either of them to explain the gesture. He passed her the clean cloth.

Leandros cleared his throat. “I can still check the tags for Silena.”

Silena wiped her hands. “I need to finish the comparison first. Beckendorf already checked the labels. I’ll return them to the box after I look at them.”

“You said they were checked.”

“By Beckendorf. I’m checking the order against the list.”

“I could do that.”

“You could. I didn’t ask you.”

He turned one of the tag edges over. It was an ordinary piece of wood, smooth on one side and stained from the rain on the other. “I’m trying to help.”

“I know.”

“Then why does everything I offer turn into a rule?”

“Because you offer it like you’ve already decided who needs what.”

“I’m not the only person who does that.”

“No,” Silena said. “You aren’t.”

That seemed to surprise him more than if she had said he was wrong. Clarisse reached for another piece of bread. Silena waited, letting Leandros decide whether to answer.

“I don’t want to be treated like I’m a guest who has to ask to touch everything,” he said.

“You are new here,” Clarisse said. “That doesn’t make you unwelcome.”

“It makes me sound like I don’t belong.”

“It makes you sound like you don’t know how things work yet.”

“I know how work works.”

“Not here.”

His hand flattened on the tabletop. “I’m trying to learn.”

“Then learn the actual job before you take it over.”

“I don’t know how long I’m supposed to keep standing at the edge.”

Silena’s expression softened, though her voice remained steady. “You don’t have to stand at the edge. You can sit down. You can eat. You can be here. That doesn’t mean you get to set the rules for everyone else.”

Leandros looked at the bench by the stove. There was room beside Percy, and room at the other end near Clarisse. No one moved to claim either place.

“Can I sit there?” he asked.

Clarisse looked at the bench. “It’s a bench.”

He sat beside the stove, not at the far end. The space between him and Percy was only a hand’s width. Percy slid the basket of bread closer.

Leandros took the piece with the browned bottom.

“Try the other side,” Clarisse said.

He turned it over. The center was softer there. He took a small bite and chewed.

“Too much rosemary?” Silena asked.

“Not for me.”

“Good,” she said, and wrote that down too.

Nobody declared the test over. The notes stayed on the page, and Silena kept the last two pieces for herself. Clarisse did not tell Leandros they were fine. She did not tell him to leave.

---

Percy returned to the shed after supper to put the inventory book back in its box. The shed door was still open, though the lamps had been lit. A thin line of orange light stretched over the wet ground.

Leandros was there with the tags. He had set them on a cloth and sorted them into two stacks, checked and not yet checked. The inventory book lay open, its pages weighted by the brass washer.

“Beckendorf asked you to do this?” Percy said.

“He asked Silena to check them against the list.”

“So why are you checking them?”

“I’m not checking them. I’m waiting.”

The second stack was one tag high.

Percy put the book back in the box. “You could do it with her.”

“She’s in the cabin.”

“Then ask her tomorrow.”

“I’m trying to finish the work from the storm.”

“The tags are dry. Beckendorf said they could go back in the box after the check.”

“I know.”

Percy looked at the one tag. “What are you waiting for?”

“For Silena.”

“Then you are waiting.”

Leandros rubbed a thumb along the table’s edge. “I’m not trying to make you say what she wants.”

“Good.”

“I’m trying to understand why all of you talk like I’ve already used up my chance.”

“Your chance at what?”

“Being here.”

Percy pushed the box farther back on the shelf. He did not have a clean answer. He could say Leandros was here because Poseidon had sent him, or because Clarisse had not told him to go, or because there was an empty place at their table. None of those meant they knew each other.

“You can be here,” Percy said.

“That isn’t what I asked.”

“I know.”

“You have a place at the table. Clarisse has a place. Silena has one. Everyone already knows what the other two will say before they say it.”

“That’s not true.”

“Maybe not for you.”

Percy thought of Clarisse’s answer to Leandros at the fence. He thought of Silena’s notes on the bread. Neither of them had said Leandros could not be in the cabin. They had also not given him the role he had wanted.

“Do you want me to tell you that you belong?” Percy asked.

“I want to know whether you believe it.”

“I don’t know yet.”

Leandros’s eyes sharpened. “Because you don’t know me?”

“Yeah.”

“So I’m not your brother.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You said you don’t know.”

“I know we have the same dad. I know you’re older than me. I know you’ve been around boats longer than I have. I don’t know how we’re supposed to act like we grew up together.”

“I didn’t ask you to.”

“I know.”

“Then what are you asking?”

Percy rubbed the bandage at the base of his thumb. It held against the skin, but the edge had started to lift. “I’m asking us not to pretend we know what we don’t.”

Leandros looked at the tags. “You had more than I did.”

“Different things.”

“You had Atlantis. Poseidon brought you into his house. You had Amphitrite.”

Percy looked at his brother. He had said enough about that already. He had been raised in a household Leandros had never entered, but he did not remember living in it the way everyone else did. The fact stayed complicated even when he wanted it to be simple.

“I know,” Percy said.

“You know what it looks like from outside.”

“Maybe.”

“I didn’t come here to take your place.”

“I know that.”

“You don’t sound like it.”

Percy opened his mouth, then stopped. He did not know what tone Leandros had heard. Maybe he had sounded like Clarisse. Maybe he had sounded like the people who had already decided the order of the family.

“I’m not against you,” Percy said.

“Then why do you keep leaving me to talk to them alone?”

“Because they’re talking to you.”

“You’re my brother.”

“So is Clarisse. So is Silena.”

“And you know what they mean.”

“I know them better than I know you.”

“That’s the problem.”

“It’s also the truth.”

The answer landed between them with a dullness that Percy could not fix by changing the words. Leandros looked away first. He lifted the checked tag and put it down again, still waiting for Silena to return.

Percy reached for the inventory book. “I can carry the box back to the cabin.”

“I can carry it.”

“You can. I’m asking whether you want help.”

Leandros stared at him for a second, as if the offer itself might be a trick. Then he gave Percy one of the two handles.

They lifted the box together. Percy’s scraped hand tugged when he pulled. Leandros noticed and shifted his grip, taking more of the weight without saying anything. Percy let him. They walked toward the cabin at the same pace, careful of the soft ground.

At the door, Leandros stopped. “I meant what I said about the board.”

“I know.”

“I thought I had the right answer.”

“Yeah.”

“I was wrong.”

Percy adjusted his grip. “That helps.”

“Does it?”

“It’s better than saying you weren’t.”

Leandros seemed to consider that. “You always know what people want to hear?”

“No.”

“You’re good at making it sound like you do.”

“Maybe that’s the problem.”

Leandros glanced at him. Percy pushed the door open with his shoulder. Warm air drifted out, carrying rosemary, broth, and the faint scent of damp wool.

Neither of them said they were done talking.

---

The next check happened after breakfast. Percy carried the inventory book back to the shed, where Beckendorf had asked everyone to meet before the work list went up. The rain had not returned. The ground still felt soft underfoot, and the track beside the fence held a shallow ribbon of water.

The canvas strip had dried. Beckendorf folded it once, ran his fingers along the edge, and set it aside for a closer inspection later. The tags had been checked by Silena and returned to the box in the right order. Two coils remained on the rack.

“The outer turns are ready,” Leandros said. He had the lower coil opened across the spacer, just as Beckendorf had shown him. “The core still feels damp.”

Beckendorf checked for himself. “It does.”

“Could move it outside for an hour.”

“No. The air’s colder out there, and the sun won’t reach this wall until afternoon.”

Leandros kept his hand on the loose end. “All right.”

It was not a surrender. Percy could see the way his brother’s fingers pressed against the rope. But he did not move it.

Clarisse came in with the work list. “The board stays until the ground firms.”

Leandros looked at the page. “I know.”

“Then don’t ask Beckendorf to move it.”

“I didn’t.”

“Good.”

She fixed the list to the wall with a nail and stepped away so everyone could read it. The fence inspection was still marked for later. The awning bracket, north-step frost check, Cabin One rail, and blanket pin remained below it.

Leandros read the line about the board twice. “Could put a temporary marker where the runoff reaches the shed.”

“We have one,” Clarisse said.

“I didn’t see it.”

“It’s on the list.”

He glanced at the map pinned beside it. The mark was small, a penciled arrow from the fence to the lower shed. Clarisse had put it there after the storm, and Beckendorf had checked the route with Eli.

Leandros looked as if he wanted to argue that the arrow was not a proper marker. Instead, he asked, “Who’s inspecting the ground?”

“Chiron and Beckendorf.”

“Could I go with them?”

“Ask Chiron.”

“Do you want me to?”

Clarisse took a second before she answered. “I want you to wait until he says what the job is.”

Leandros nodded. “Okay.”

It was not the same as asking him to go. Percy saw that he had heard the difference.

Ianthe arrived a few minutes later with her folded weather page. Her eyes went first to the work list, then to the coils, then to the open door. She had brought a new sheet with the gust windows marked in a different color.

“Beckendorf,” she said. “I can put the wind times next to the drying checks if that would help.”

“Show me where they came from first,” he said.

She unfolded the page on a separate corner of the table. “These are from the morning readings. The second column is the direction at the west steps.”

“Who was with you?”

“No one. I took them myself.”

“Then mark the page as yours. We’ll compare it with the flag line before we use it.”

Ianthe looked at the two columns, then at Beckendorf’s notes. “That’s fair.”

She did not touch the work list. She did not set the weather page over the inventory. Percy noticed because she seemed to notice that he had noticed.

“I’m not adding it yet,” she said.

“Good,” Percy replied.

“I know how that sounds.”

“How does it sound?”

“Like I’m asking you to say I’m doing better.”

He shrugged. “I don’t know if you are.”

Her mouth pressed thin. “That’s not very helpful.”

“I’m not the person who gets to decide.”

She looked toward Annabeth, who had entered with the spare pencil. Annabeth did not soften the answer for him. “You can show what you’re doing. The rest takes time.”

Ianthe’s expression did not change. “I know.”

She waited until Beckendorf had finished reading the page, then wrote her name at the top. He set it beside the records, not inside them.

Leandros watched the exchange. “So she can work on wind and I can’t check the ground?”

“You can ask Chiron,” Clarisse said.

“I just asked to go with him.”

“And I told you to wait for his answer.”

“I’m waiting.”

“Then wait.”

Leandros gave a humorless little smile. “Everyone keeps telling me to wait.”

“Because you keep getting ahead of the work.”

He looked like he might answer. Instead, he moved to the rack, loosened the next bend of rope, and checked that the spacer still held. Beckendorf saw him and nodded. Leandros did not look up to see whether Clarisse had noticed.

Percy returned to the shed with Silena after she finished writing the last note for her bread test. She brought the inventory sheet and the replacement tags. The bread page stayed folded in her pocket, separate from the work list.

“Beckendorf said to hang these once we match them to the box,” she said.

“I’ll hold the list,” Percy answered.

Leandros had already moved to the far side of the rack. He watched them take each tag in order. The first marked spare stakes. Silena checked it against the contents; Percy held the box steady. The second marked the canvas ties. The last one was the light line. The words matched the inventory this time.

Leandros reached toward the box. “The light line should go on the upper shelf. It’s the one you use from the dock.”

“It’s the lower rack,” Silena said.

“I thought you said the long line was in the east locker.”

“It is. This one goes on the lower rack.”

“Why?”

“Because it’s a spare, and Beckendorf asked for the spares together.”

Leandros put his hand down. “All right.”

Silena kept the tag in her hand for another moment, then fastened it to the lower rack. “There.”

The answer was not a prize. It was just where the tag belonged.

Percy held the inventory while she marked the job complete. Two coils remained listed for drying. The canvas needed a fiber check. The temporary board needed an inspection after the ground firmed. The work was smaller than the storm had made it feel, but it was still there.

“Could put the new tags on a cord above the rack,” Leandros said. “Then they won’t get wet.”

“Not above it,” Beckendorf said from the door. “The roof drips on that side.”

“Then on the inside wall.”

“We can do that after the other repairs. Don’t drill into the shed until we check the studs.”

Leandros’s mouth tightened. “I didn’t say I’d drill.”

“You were about to.”

For a beat, Leandros looked ready to deny it. He glanced at the wall and saw the old nail holes where somebody had hung a map. “I was thinking about it.”

“Think about it in the work meeting,” Beckendorf said. “Right now, let the tags sit.”

Leandros leaned back against the rack. “They’re not going anywhere.”

“Neither are the coils,” Percy said.

“That isn’t the same thing.”

“No,” Percy said. “It isn’t.”

The unspoken difference was visible: the tags were ready to be used; the rope was still wet inside. No one could put a label on the cord and make it dry.

Silena set the inventory page in the box. “I’m done here.”

“Thanks,” Percy said.

She looked at him, then at Leandros. “I’ll bring the bread notes back tomorrow. The herb one stays.”

“Good,” Percy said.

Leandros lifted his chin slightly. “You’re making more?”

“I haven’t decided.”

“You just said it stays.”

“The recipe stays. I don’t know whether I’m making a batch.”

“Those are different things?”

“Yes.”

He nodded, not because he understood, Percy thought, but because he was deciding not to argue.

Outside, Clarisse crossed toward the fence with Chiron. She carried no tools. The ground was still too soft, and the inspection had been delayed again. Leandros watched her reach the gravel and stop before the mud. He followed with his eyes, then turned toward the board.

“Do you want me to take the list over?” Percy asked.

“No.”

The answer came too quickly. Then Leandros looked at the inventory box and seemed to realize Percy had asked, not assigned. “No. Thanks.”

Percy closed the lid. “Okay.”

He carried the box to the wall shelf. Leandros did not take it from him. They stood for a moment listening to the water moving in the ditch. The runoff had eased to a thin stream. It was no longer rushing toward the shed, but the board was still temporary, and the work that the storm had sent their way could not be washed off the page.

---

Clarisse was in the cabin by the time Percy came back from the shed. She had left the work list on the narrow counter and was washing mud from the inside of one boot. A bowl sat beside her, filled with water gone gray.

“Did the coils dry?” she asked.

“Not all the way.”

“Then they stay where they are.”

“Beckendorf said that too.”

“Good. You heard him.”

She scraped the boot sole with a flat stick. Percy sat on the edge of his bunk and checked the tape on his hand. It had lifted another fraction.

Clarisse looked up. “You need a new wrap?”

“It’s fine.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

He held out his hand. She took the small roll of gauze from the shelf and replaced the tape without tightening it too much. Her hands were quick and sure. Percy had seen her bind knuckles after practice often enough to know not to tell her how.

When she finished, she tossed the old strip into the bin. “There.”

“Thanks.”

“You can carry the tags without using the scraped part.”

“I did.”

“Good.”

He rubbed his thumb over the clean edge. “Leandros asked me if he belonged here.”

Clarisse sat back on her heels. “What did you say?”

“I didn’t know.”

“That was honest.”

“It might not have been what he wanted.”

“He can ask for what he wants. You still don’t have to invent an answer.”

Percy looked at the work list. “He’s our brother.”

“He is.”

“But it’s new.”

“For him and for us.”

“I keep thinking that should make it easier.”

“Why?”

“I don’t know. Maybe because it’s already true.”

Clarisse set the boot aside. “Being related isn’t the same as knowing somebody.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

He thought about the way Leandros had kept his hands off the tag box when Silena asked. The way he had still said her bread needed more salt. The way he had held the rope without pulling against Beckendorf. “I know he’s trying.”

“That’s not the same as knowing him either.”

Percy nodded. “You want him to stay?”

Clarisse wiped her hands on a towel. “Most of the time.”

“Most?”

“He makes it difficult.”

Percy smiled a little. “Yeah.”

“That doesn’t mean I want him gone.” She took the list from the counter and folded it once. “I don’t want to pretend we had the same childhood just because we’re under the same father now.”

“He didn’t say we did.”

“Not in those words.”

The fold ran through the mark for the fence. Percy watched it disappear under Clarisse’s thumb. “I don’t want to tell him what everybody thinks.”

“Then don’t.”

“I also don’t want him to think I’m against him.”

“You can tell him that.”

“I did.”

“And?”

“He didn’t believe me.”

Clarisse shrugged. “He’s allowed to be wrong about you.”

“That’s not a great feeling.”

“No.”

She put the list back, this time without folding it. The wind moved the edge of the paper against the counter. Clarisse pinned it down with a stone.

Percy stood. “Do you think we’ll get used to this?”

“To the rain?”

“To him.”

“I don’t know.”

That answer sat differently from Leandros’s question. Clarisse did not make it sound like something she would solve if she had more time.

“Me neither,” Percy said.

She nudged his shoulder with the back of her hand. “You can still eat the bread.”

“Which one?”

“Silena’s.”

“I thought the herb one was hers.”

“Then don’t make it a vote.”

He followed her toward the common table. Behind them, the list stayed flat under the stone. The temporary board remained at the fence, the rope remained on its spacer, and Leandros remained somewhere in the cabin with them, close enough to hear the ordinary work continue.

Silena set the herb round on the board and cut it into uneven pieces. “This is the one I’m keeping.”

“Keeping for tomorrow?” Percy asked.

“Keeping in the recipe.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“Exactly.”

Leandros came back in with the empty water bowl from the shed. He set it by the basin and looked at the bread. “You decided.”

“I did.”

“Can I try it?”

“You’ve already tried it.”

“I meant the version you chose.”

Silena gave him a piece. “It’s the same bread.”

“I know.”

He took a bite and chewed without looking at anyone for approval. Then he nodded once. “Good.”

Silena folded the recipe page and tucked it into her apron. “That’s what I wrote.”

Clarisse moved the work list farther from the stove so the paper would not curl. No one told Leandros what his answer meant. No one asked him to explain it.

Percy took the last piece and split it with Annabeth when she came through the door. She kept the rosemary half. He ate the plain end.

Outside, water ran in the ditch. It carried leaves past the fence and left the board where it was. The damp rope stayed on the rack until it was ready. Nobody could hurry either one by calling the work finished.
