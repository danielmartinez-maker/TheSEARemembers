## Chapter 198: The Face of Thunder

The window beside Thalia’s bunk had a narrow strip of clear glass left uncovered.

Annabeth had done it on purpose. She said the blanket over the rest of the frame kept the draft down, and a hand-wide opening let them know whether the morning was bright or only pretending. Thalia had complained that it was a useless measurement. Annabeth had said it was still a measurement.

This morning the glass looked white with cloud.

Thalia sat on the edge of the bunk and lowered her foot toward the floor. The splint pressed against the heel as soon as it met the boot. She paused, counted one breath, then another, and put her weight down carefully. It hurt. It did not give way. She had learned the difference between those facts and the different things people tried to make them mean.

Annabeth was at the low table, tying a string around a roll of paper. She looked up once, saw Thalia standing, and looked back down.

“Any change?” she asked.

“No.”

“Same pain?”

“Same.”

“Do you want the stool?”

“The stool is still curing.”

“I meant the chair.”

“I can stand.”

“I know.”

The answer came easily. Annabeth did not make it a test or a warning. She finished the knot, folded the string ends inside, and set the roll beside a tin of charcoal. The map had a pale strip down one side where the paper had stayed dry. The shoreline marks were still dotted and provisional.

Thalia crossed the room toward the stove. The step was stiff. She hated that she had to decide where each foot went. She hated it more when someone else decided for her.

“Chiron said no hauling today,” Annabeth said.

“I heard him.”

“Modified practice only if the heel allows it.”

“I heard that too.”

“I know.” Annabeth turned from the map. “You don’t have to prove the instructions got through by making your foot worse.”

“I’m not.”

“Good.”

Thalia pulled two mugs from the shelf. “Do you want tea?”

“Only if I can pour my own.”

“Why would you not be able to?”

“You offered.”

“I asked.”

“You asked while you were already reaching for the kettle.”

Thalia put it down. “Would you like me to heat the water?”

“Yes.”

That was better. It also took longer. Annabeth returned to her string and the stove began its small metallic ticking. Outside, the clouds had not opened. The camp was waking by degrees: a door closing, a heel of bread dropped on a kitchen board, someone calling across the hill for a missing sweater.

The morning had no repair crew yet. The awning, the fence, the loose rail, the frost check, and the map’s shore mark still waited on the list. Chiron had told Thalia she could stay clear of them. She had told him she knew how to sit down when she chose to. They had both let the sentence stand.

“Percy said he might come by,” Annabeth said.

“He can.”

“He asked whether the step is safe.”

“He can come up the step.”

“I told him.”

Thalia poured hot water into the mugs. The kettle’s handle warmed through the cloth. “He could also ask whether you want him here.”

“He did.”

“And?”

“I said yes.”

“Good.”

Annabeth carried the cups to the narrow sill. The window blanket made the light there soft and gray. She sat on the chair and tucked one foot beneath her, leaving the stool alone. Thalia took the other chair. The mugs were hot enough to warm her palms. They drank without needing to fill the first few minutes.

A knock sounded at the cabin door. Percy came in after Annabeth called, bringing a small paper packet from the pavilion.

“I got the cinnamon rolls before the twins found them.”

“They’re the twins’ rolls?” Annabeth asked.

“They were sitting on a plate with no names.”

“That means nobody had eaten them yet.”

“It means they were available.”

“For how long?”

“I didn’t ask the rolls.”

Annabeth took the packet from him. “You can stay.”

“I was hoping.”

He said it to her, but looked at Thalia too, waiting to see if she would make the moment harder. She had no reason to.

“Morning,” Thalia said.

“Morning.”

He sat beside Annabeth, not too close to the window, and she rested her elbow against his arm as she opened the packet. A thin trail of sugar had settled in the folds of the paper. Percy brushed it with one finger and Annabeth caught his wrist before he could lick it.

“You’ve eaten breakfast?” she asked.

“I had bread.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It is an answer.”

“It’s a different question.”

He leaned over and kissed the sugar from her fingertip. Annabeth smiled and let him take the roll. She did not ask him to eat more than he wanted; he did not pretend the bread had been enough. Thalia looked down at her mug so they could have the privacy of a cabin full of people.

For a while they talked about the rolled map. Percy asked where Annabeth had marked the north path. Annabeth showed him, then pointed out the unverified shore area. He questioned whether the old map had shown a shortcut. She said it had, but the path had not been checked. He nodded and let her leave the dotted line where it was.

That was what being with them felt like when it was good: no one needed to win the shape of the next answer. They could disagree about a path without making it a verdict on who knew the ground. Their hands found each other between the map and the breakfast packet, and stayed there while they spoke.

A gust struck the cabin wall. The clear strip of glass shivered in its frame. Thalia felt the pressure change before the second gust. It was the sort of weather that found every loose corner and asked whether it had been tied down.

“Wind’s turning,” she said.

Annabeth glanced at the map, then the window. “From the hill?”

“From the lake. It came around the point.”

Percy looked outside. “Looks like rain.”

“Could be.”

The gust passed. The tea cooled. They were still at the table when the camp bell rang once from the pavilion.

It was not a signal for danger. It was too early for lunch and too late for the first meal. Someone had rung it to call Chiron from the Big House.

Annabeth set down her cup. “That’s odd.”

Thalia pushed herself up from the chair. The heel complained. She took a breath and stayed where she was until the worst of the pressure eased.

“You don’t have to go to the door first,” Annabeth said.

“I know.”

“Do you want to?”

Thalia looked at the narrow strip of glass. White cloud, no rain yet. “I want to know what happened.”

“Then we can find out.”

Percy stood beside Annabeth, and waited while she chose whether to take the map with her. She left it on the table.

The second bell rang before they reached the door. This time it was followed by a shout from the path below. The words disappeared in the wind, but Thalia heard Chiron answer.

She stepped across the threshold. Annabeth stayed on her sound side, close enough to steady her if she asked and far enough not to take the step for her.


---


The shout came from the hill path. The person making it was a satyr, breathless and trying to keep his voice above the wind.

“Chiron! I have another one.”

“Another what?” Percy called.

“Another daughter of Zeus.”

The satyr stopped at the edge of the path to catch his breath. Behind him walked a girl in a dark coat, one hand wrapped around the strap of a canvas bag. Her hair was pulled back tightly enough that no strand moved in the gust. She had damp cuffs and a thin brown line of mud across one shoe. Her eyes went first to Chiron, then to the cabins, then to the three of them.

Thalia recognized the face a moment before she recognized why. The girl’s brow had the same hard line that Zeus used when he was waiting for someone else to finish speaking. The resemblance was not exact. It was enough to make the white sky feel suddenly closer.

“Thalia?” the girl asked.

“Depends who’s asking.”

“Ianthe. I’m fifteen.” She shifted the bag higher on her shoulder. “Our father is Zeus.”

Annabeth’s hand brushed Thalia’s elbow, barely there. The contact was not a warning. It was Annabeth giving her a moment to answer.

Chiron wheeled up the path, a blanket over his lap and the Big House keys in one hand. “Welcome to Camp Half-Blood, Ianthe. I’m Chiron.”

“I know who you are.”

“Then you know I’m pleased you made it here safely.”

The satyr exhaled. “The weather got bad at her house. I didn’t want to wait for the next storm.”

“It was a squall,” Ianthe said.

“It threw the garden chairs into the street.”

“They were light.”

“They hit a parked car.”

“The car was empty.”

The satyr looked at Chiron. “Her mother said she could come with me once she was sure the storm was moving off.”

Ianthe’s mouth tightened. “My mother didn’t say it was moving off. She said I had to go.”

“That’s not what I heard.”

“She was trying to keep the windows shut.”

“Because you told her the wind would turn.”

“It did turn.”

“After the chairs went through the gate.”

Thalia glanced at the satyr. He was not accusing Ianthe of trying to hurt anyone. He sounded tired and relieved, the way people did after the danger had passed and the argument remained.

The satyr had not accused her of wanting anyone hurt. He was describing what happened after her forecast failed. Ianthe seemed to hear each detail as an attempt to take away the part she had done right. Thalia could understand the reflex without agreeing with it. A person could keep some windows whole and still send chairs into a road; the truth did not have to choose just one.

Thalia could not know whether her mother’s order felt like care or dismissal to Ianthe. That was a private question, and she would not ask it at the foot of the hill.

“Was anyone hurt?” Chiron asked.

“No,” the satyr said.

“No,” Ianthe repeated. “I kept the worst of it away from the house.”

The answer came too quickly. Thalia recognized the rhythm: she had decided what the story meant and was not ready for anybody else to give it a different shape.


“Where are my sisters?” Ianthe asked.

“Here,” Thalia said.

Ianthe turned to Annabeth. “And you’re Annabeth.”

“Yes.”

“Good. I thought you’d be younger.”

Annabeth’s expression did not change. “I’m fourteen.”

“I know. You’re the youngest.”

“Jason is younger than both of us,” Thalia said.

“He’s not here.”

“That doesn’t make him disappear.”

“I didn’t mean he disappeared.” Ianthe adjusted her strap again. “I meant I expected Annabeth to be little.”

“I’m not.”

“I didn’t say you were.”

“You said you expected it.”

Thalia felt herself leaning toward the conversation. She stopped before the injured heel took the full weight. Annabeth didn’t need her to finish the sentence.

Ianthe studied Annabeth, as if the explanation had corrected a small error in a list. “All right.”

“Do you know what cabin you’re in?” Chiron asked.

“Cabin One, I assumed.”

“That’s where Zeus’s children stay.”

“I know.”

“Then you’re right.”

Ianthe glanced down the path. “The cabins look disorderly.”

“They’re cabins,” Percy said.

“They can be arranged better.”

“They can,” Annabeth agreed. “If someone asks the people who use them.”

Ianthe’s chin lifted. “I was going to.”

“You just told us the camp is disorderly before you saw the inside of one cabin.”

“I have seen enough.”

“That’s not the same as asking.”

Thalia watched Ianthe’s grip tighten around the bag strap. She wondered if the girl had been told that coming here would be like walking into a place already waiting for her. The idea might have sounded reasonable to someone who had only been visited by Zeus when he wanted her to believe she was special.

“I was told I could help make a plan,” Ianthe said.

“By whom?” Chiron asked.

“My father.”

The wind pulled at the loose end of her coat. Her eyes remained on Chiron. He did not question that claim, but he did not let it move him out of the way either.

“Then you can tell me what he said, when you’re ready,” Chiron replied. “For now, you’re tired, you’ve come through a storm, and you need to know where you’ll sleep. We can do that first.”

“I don’t need to rest.”

“That wasn’t a diagnosis.”

“Then what was it?”

“A suggestion.”

“I’m not tired.”

The satyr rubbed his forehead. “You’re always tired after the weather goes through you.”

“It didn’t go through me.”

“You were shaking.”

“I was cold.”

“Maybe.”

Ianthe looked at him. “You don’t have to tell them everything.”

“Then tell them yourself.”

She inhaled, held the breath, let it out through her nose. Her eyes moved to Thalia’s splint.

“You hurt your heel?”

“Yes.”

“Then you should sit down before we go inside.”

“I’m already standing.”

“You could sit.”

“I could also decide when.”

Ianthe’s lips parted, then closed. She glanced at Annabeth, as if expecting her to help make the order sensible.

Annabeth said, “Thalia knows how to sit down when she wants to.”

“Does she?”

“Yes.”

“Then I won’t tell her again.”

Thalia was surprised by the restraint. It did not erase the assumption. It did make the next breath easier.

“You can come to Cabin One,” Chiron said to Ianthe. “But no one has to answer every question today.”

“I don’t need answers.”

“You asked for your sisters.”

“I asked where they were.”

“That’s a start.”

Chiron turned his chair toward the Big House. “Come inside. We can get out of the wind while I speak with Ianthe.”

---


Chiron asked the satyr to wait in the Big House kitchen while he spoke with Ianthe.

“I can speak for myself,” she said.

“I know,” Chiron replied. “I’m asking the satyr to wait because he has been walking since before dawn.”

The satyr rubbed his forehead again. “I can wait.”

Ianthe looked from one to the other. “If he has something to tell you, he can say it while I’m here.”

“He has,” Chiron said. “That is why he came with you.”

The answer did not leave room for the argument she had started. She followed Chiron inside. Thalia moved after them, with Annabeth at her shoulder and Percy a little behind. He had offered his hand at the door. Thalia had said she had the frame. He had accepted that and stayed close enough to be useful.

The Big House smelled of wood polish, old paper, and the soup that had been simmering since morning. A kitchen window stood open a few inches to let steam out. On a side table, a stack of blank forms leaned beside a tin of pencils. The satyr sat heavily in a chair and removed one shoe to rub at his hoof.

Ianthe set her bag on the floor, then moved it away from the path. “Where do you keep the camp rules?”

Chiron looked at her. “We explain the ones that matter as they come up.”

“That’s not efficient.”

“It is for a camp where not everybody arrives under the same circumstances.”

“You could still write them down.”

“We do have a handbook.”

“Then I’d like to read it.”

“Of course.”

“Where is it?”

“Malcolm keeps the current copy near the armory.”

Ianthe looked at Annabeth. “You could show me.”

“I could,” Annabeth said. “I’m not going to right now.”

“Why not?”

“Because Chiron is getting you settled.”

“That seems like something you could do.”

“It is. I’m choosing not to.”

Ianthe’s mouth drew thin. She looked as if she had heard the words and was searching for the hidden qualification that would make them a negotiation.

Percy went to the side table and poured water into a cup. “Do you want some?”

“I have water.”

“Then I’ll leave it here.”

He set the cup near the satyr, who took it with a grateful nod. The ordinary movement seemed to irritate Ianthe more than a formal greeting would have. She watched Percy’s hand return to Annabeth’s and then looked away.

“Your hands,” Chiron said. “Would you let someone clean them?”

“They’re fine.”

“They’re red.”

“They got scraped. They’ll heal.”

“Will said he can look at them without making it a treatment.”

“No.”

“All right.” Chiron did not call the healer. “The offer stands.”

Chiron let the silence hold for a moment. He did not ask her to apologize or ask the satyr to make the story more severe. He only waited until Ianthe looked up again.

“What did you think the wind would do?” he asked.

Ianthe glanced toward the hill, though the house was behind them. “It was coming across the yard. I thought it would pass over the roof and break against the trees.”

“Did it?”

“Not all of it.”

The satyr set his hands on his knees. “You said the chairs were clear of the door. You said the street was clear too.”

“I thought it was.”

“You thought there was time to move them after the first gust.”

“I thought it would turn.”

“And when it didn’t?” Chiron asked.

“I got my mother inside.”

“That part worked,” the satyr said. His voice had softened, though his forehead was still furrowed. “You got the door shut. That’s why your hands are scraped.”

Ianthe held out her palms as if the marks were evidence in her favor. “The windows stayed in place.”

“They did,” Chiron said. “And the chairs left the yard.”

“They were light.”

“They still hit a car.”

“It was empty.”

“Yes,” Chiron said. “I’m glad no one was sitting in it.”

Ianthe’s shoulders eased, but only slightly. “Then no one was hurt.”

“That is true.”

The agreement seemed to surprise her. She had braced for Chiron to argue with the part she could prove. He let her keep it.

Thalia watched the satyr look down at his hoof, turning it carefully on the worn chair rung. He was not making a case against Ianthe. He was tired from walking and worried about leaving her somewhere new, and the two feelings sat together in his face without canceling each other.

“What do you want me to know about the weather?” Chiron asked. “Not what you think I should do. What did you observe?”

Ianthe glanced at her scraped palms. “The wind rose before the cloud line reached the house. It shifted twice. I got the door closed before the second change.”

“And what would you do differently next time?”

“Move the chairs before the first gust.”

The answer came fast. Her jaw tightened, as if she disliked having to say it. She added, “If I had known the wind would hold that direction.”

“You had a reason to think it would turn,” the satyr said. “It just didn’t turn in time.”

“That’s what I said.”

“It isn’t all you said.”

Ianthe’s eyes narrowed. The satyr looked away first, not because she had won but because he had finished what he came to say.

Chiron folded his hands. “You can learn from the part that was in your control without claiming the whole storm was yours to control.”

“I didn’t say I controlled it.”

“No. You said you kept the worst away from the house.”

“I did.”

“You did.” Chiron left a pause between the words. “And it still reached the street.”

The room quieted. Thalia could hear the rain on the Big House roof, lighter now than when they had come in. Ianthe’s scraped hand rested against the front of her coat. She did not ask to leave. She also did not say she was sorry.

“You don’t have to solve the rest of it tonight,” Chiron said. “You’ve arrived safely. We can take the next step when you’re ready.”

“I’m ready.”

“Then we’ll start with where you’ll sleep.”

Ianthe glanced once at the satyr. “And he can go?”

“If he wants to,” Chiron said.

“I want to,” the satyr said, then smiled a little. “After I sit down for five minutes.”

Ianthe shifted her bag so it no longer blocked the doorway. “You can sit here.”

“I was already sitting.”

“You can keep sitting.”

He gave a small laugh. Ianthe did not, but she moved the bag farther under the table to clear the route to the kitchen.



Ianthe’s gaze moved to the papers on the side table. “That form should be in the folder.”

“Which form?” Chiron asked.

“The arrival sheet.”

“It is the arrival sheet.”

“It shouldn’t be loose.”

“It’s waiting for a signature.”

“Then it should be on top.”

Chiron glanced at the paper and set it on top of the stack. “There.”

The adjustment was small. Ianthe’s shoulders loosened by a fraction. Thalia had seen people find comfort in fixing what they could reach. She had also seen them use it to make other people stand in the right place.

A young camper came through the kitchen door carrying a folded tablecloth and a broom. The cloth slipped from his arms. He set the broom against the wall, caught the cloth, and went out the other door.

Ianthe watched him. “Does he know where he’s going?”

“He’s taking the cloth to the pavilion,” Annabeth said.

“He left the broom here.”

“It’s for the kitchen.”

“He’s carrying one thing and abandoning another.”

“He came in to drop it off.”

“Then he could have finished before leaving.”

“He was asked to bring the cloth.”

Ianthe looked at Thalia. “Is everyone here this casual?”

“People do more than one thing at a time.”

“They lose track.”

“Sometimes.”

“Then they need clearer instructions.”

Thalia shifted against the wall. The heel throbbed beneath the splint, a low pulse that sharpened when she leaned. “You’ve been here an hour.”

“I can see what’s in front of me.”

“You can see one kid take a cloth through a door.”

“And leave the broom.”

Annabeth started to answer, but the young camper came back in before she could. He picked up the broom and hooked it over his arm.

“I was taking this to the kitchen too,” he said. “The cloth was in my way.”

Ianthe straightened. “You don’t need to explain.”

“I wasn’t explaining. I’m telling you where the broom goes.”

He carried it into the kitchen. Ianthe watched him go with a look that made it clear she would have preferred him to wait for her permission.

Chiron opened the folder. “You’ll meet more people today. It may help to learn what they are doing before you decide what they should do differently.”

Ianthe’s chin tilted. “I don’t make decisions without information.”

“You made one just now.”

“I saw enough.”

“You saw part of a task.”

“The part that needed correcting.”

“The part you didn’t understand.”

For a second, Thalia thought Ianthe would argue. Instead she looked at the wet mark left by the cloth on the table. Her focus changed. She turned toward the open window.

“Close that,” she said.

The young camper in the kitchen did not answer.

“Close the window,” Ianthe repeated.

“Ask him,” Annabeth said.

“I just did.”

“You gave an order.”

“The air is changing.”

Chiron looked up from the folder. “What do you notice?”

“The pressure fell when we came inside.” She went to the window but stopped before touching it. “The cloud edge is lower than it was five minutes ago. The wind is bending around the corner of the house. It’ll come through this opening.”

A loose arrival sheet skated across the side table.

The camper came through the kitchen door at the same time. He caught it against his chest.

Ianthe looked at him. “I told you.”

“You told me to close the window,” he said. “You didn’t tell me why.”

“I said the air is changing.”

“That isn’t why.”

“It was about to blow the papers away.”

“And you could have said that.”

She held his gaze. “I did.”

“No. You told me to do something.”

Chiron stepped between the papers and the open window. “We can close it now.”

The camper shut the window and returned the sheet to the folder. He did not look at Ianthe. She looked at the sky through the glass, tracking the edge of the cloud.

Thalia knew that look. It was the look of someone certain the problem had been real, even if the way she had handled it had made the room harder.

“You were right about the gust,” Chiron said.

“I know.”

“You were not right that he would understand what you meant.”

“I was clear.”

“Not to him.”

“He could have asked.”

“He did.”

Ianthe pressed her thumb to the red mark at her palm. The skin had opened again. She stopped when she noticed the blood.

The satyr rose from the chair. “Let me get a clean cloth.”

“I can do it.”

He paused.

“I said I can do it.”

He held out the cloth. “Here.”

She took it. This time she did not tell him where to stand.

Her bag sat by the door, zipped to the top. A folded note had slipped from the outer pocket and fallen near the table leg. Annabeth bent to retrieve it, then stopped with her hand open.

“Do you want me to pick it up?”

Ianthe looked at the note, then at Annabeth. “Yes. Thank you.”

Annabeth handed it over without reading it.

Ianthe opened the paper. The note was short, written in blue ink that had bled slightly in the damp.

“Your mother?” Thalia asked.

Ianthe folded it once. “Yes.”

“Is she okay?”

“She’s fine. The door is still hanging. The car was empty. She said to call when I can.”

“Are you going to?”

“Later.”

“Okay.”

Ianthe put the note in her coat pocket. She kept her thumb over it for a moment, then let go.

The satyr stood by the kitchen door. “I should go back before the next rain.”

“Are you leaving her?” Chiron asked.

“She’s safe here.”

Ianthe opened her mouth, perhaps to correct him, but closed it. She had not asked him to stay. She had not told him she wanted him to go.

“Thank you for bringing me,” she said.

The satyr nodded, surprised enough that he rubbed his forehead again. “You’re welcome. I’ll walk as far as the cabin, then head back.”

She took her bag and turned toward the door. “Where is Cabin One?”

“Up the hill,” Thalia said. “We’ll show you.”

Ianthe looked at Thalia’s splint. “You shouldn’t have to walk.”

“I don’t.”

“Then Annabeth can show me.”

“Annabeth can choose whether she wants to,” Thalia said.

Annabeth reached for her coat. “I’ll come.”

Ianthe waited until Annabeth had put it on. Then she walked beside her instead of ahead.


The satyr offered to carry Ianthe’s bag. She refused. He offered again, saying it was heavy. She kept hold of it.

“I can carry it.”

“I know. I’m offering.”

“I don’t need you to.”

“Then you can carry it.”

He stepped back. Ianthe walked toward the cabin path. The satyr fell into step beside her, not in front. Thalia waited until the two of them had moved several paces before following.

The hill was steeper than it looked from the foot of it. Thalia took the slower line, placing each boot where she could see the ground. Ianthe stopped twice to check the cabin roofs and the path markers. The second time, she moved the small sign beside the fork so its arrow faced the main trail.

“Don’t,” Annabeth said.

“It was pointed wrong.”

“It was turned for people coming from the pavilion.”

“Then it should point toward the pavilion.”

“It does, from the other direction.”

“That’s not obvious.”

“It doesn’t have to point at the same thing from every side.”

Ianthe returned the sign to its post. “The camp could use better markers.”

Ianthe took a few steps toward the main trail and looked back at the post. From that angle, the arrow seemed to point into a gap between two cabins. She moved to the other side. The trail beyond it came into view, then bent out of sight again.

“Someone coming from here wouldn’t know which path it means,” she said.

“They can see the turn once they reach the fork.”

“After they reach it.”

“Most people here know the paths.”

“Most isn’t everyone.”

Annabeth glanced at the sign, then at the hills. She did not say Ianthe was wrong. Thalia had watched her make the same kind of distinction on the map: what the page showed and what the ground still required a person to check.

“You can mark that as a question for Chiron,” Annabeth said. “I won’t move it because we don’t know which side needs the arrow most.”

“I wasn’t going to move it.”

“You already did once.”

“I put it back.”

“That’s why I’m telling you the rest.”

Ianthe’s mouth tightened, but she kept her hands at her sides. “Then I’ll ask him about a second marker.”

“Good.”

“It could be clearer.”

“It could.”

The agreement did not seem to satisfy Ianthe as much as being told yes would have. She looked again at the post, as if she might find a version of the camp where every path had one answer from every direction.


She turned toward Thalia. “Would a name help?”

“Maybe. I know where the turn goes.”

“But a new camper might not.”

Annabeth looked at the marker again. “That’s why we have maps and people who can show them around.”

“I can’t ask you to show every person.”

“No. You can ask Chiron whether a second marker would help.”

Ianthe traced the shape of the fork with her eyes. “I could draw one.”

“You could,” Annabeth said. “We’d still need to check which way it should face.”

“Why?”

“So it doesn’t make the other path harder to find.”

Ianthe’s hand rose halfway toward the sign and stopped before touching it. Thalia saw the pause. It did not erase the earlier reach, or mean Ianthe had learned the whole camp in an hour. It was just one moment in which she let a question stay open.

“Fine,” Ianthe said. “I’ll ask him about it.”

Annabeth nodded. “That’s enough for today.”


The cabin door came into view. Ianthe went to it first and reached for the handle.

“Wait,” Thalia said.

Ianthe stopped. “Why?”

“Because Annabeth lives here too.”

“I know.”

“Then let her open the door.”

Annabeth stepped between them and turned the handle. She did not ask whether Thalia wanted to lead the way. She moved at her own pace, with Thalia beside her and Ianthe a step behind.

The room inside looked the same as it had an hour earlier. The cups were still on the sill, the cinnamon rolls sat in their paper, and the map lay beside the stove. The narrow window showed nothing but cloud.

Ianthe took in the bunks, the chairs, the table, and the blanket over the frame. Her eyes stopped at the open strip of glass.

“This will let in the cold.”

“It lets us see the weather,” Annabeth said.

“You can see the weather outside.”

“Not from my bunk.”

“Then move the bunk.”

“No.”

Ianthe set her bag on the floor beside the doorway. “You don’t have to decide now.”

“I just did.”

Thalia’s mouth almost pulled into a smile. Annabeth’s no did not need a witness, but it was good to hear it said.

“Where is the place for me?” Ianthe asked.

“We’ll talk about that,” Annabeth said.

“There are two lower bunks.”

“They are ours.”

“There’s room for another.”

“In the cabin, yes,” Annabeth said. “Not in our beds.”

Ianthe looked at Thalia. “You’re injured. I can take the lower bunk. You should be on the inside.”

Thalia leaned on the doorframe for a second, letting the heel settle. “I’m injured. I’m not a chair you can move.”

“I was trying to make it easier.”

“You could ask what would make it easier.”

Ianthe did not respond at once. The wind rose outside and flattened the blanket against the window. The clear strip of glass rattled once, then went still.

“I’ll ask Chiron about the bed,” she said.

“Good.”

“And the cabin.”

“Also good.”

She looked at Annabeth again. “You can show me where the blankets are.”

“I can,” Annabeth said. “But I’m not moving the bed.”

“I heard you.”

Thalia looked at Annabeth, who had already turned back to the table to put the map away. The girl had not made a promise about the bed. She had not accepted an order disguised as a request. She had not turned Ianthe into an enemy, either.

It was more than enough for a first impression.

---

The first thing Ianthe tried to manage was the cabin’s repair list. She looked at the narrow table and asked where the spare paper was.

“What do you need paper for?” Annabeth asked.

“I want to write down what we need to fix.”

“We have a list.”

“Where?”

“Chiron’s board.”

“Who updates it?”

“Chiron and the people who are doing the work.”

“That’s not a system.”

“It is a system,” Annabeth said. “It just isn’t yours.”

Ianthe reached for the charcoal tin. Annabeth put her hand over the lid.

“Ask first.”

“I need to write down the weather.”

“You can use the charcoal,” Annabeth said. “Not the camp list.”

Ianthe held her gaze for a beat, then let the tin go. “Can I use the charcoal?”

“Yes.”

“Thank you.”

The words came out stiff, but she waited while Annabeth opened the tin and slid a stick across the table. She did not reach over her hand.

Thalia shifted to the chair by the window. The heel had started to throb again, not worse, only present. She lowered herself with care. The cabin had stopped moving around them, but a gust still worried the window blanket.

“You can keep a weather note for yourself,” she said.

Ianthe rolled a clean sheet out of her bag. “I’m keeping it for the cabin.”

“Why?”

“Because the weather can change quickly. We should know what’s coming.”

“Chiron tracks it.”

“Then he can use what I track.”

“You can offer it.”

“That’s what I meant.”

“Did you?”

Ianthe looked down at the paper. “I know how to read the pressure changes. I was told I could help.”

“By your father?”

“Yes.”

Thalia did not ask what he had told her. It was not her job to prove that Zeus had been fair to all of them by explaining how unfair he had been. Annabeth could decide whether to ask her own questions. She had not yet.

Ianthe began marking a grid in the corner. The lines were straight, measured to the width of the page. She wrote the time, the cloud direction, and the last gust. At the window, she paused to check the narrow band of uncovered glass.

“Wind’s shifting,” she said. “It’ll come from the north in an hour.”

“From the lake,” Thalia said.

“The clouds are moving north.”

“The gust came around the point.”

“That was a local eddy. The front is north.”

“What front?”

“The low cloud bank.”

“There are three.”

Ianthe turned to the window. “The lowest one.”

“That’s moving west.”

“No, it isn’t.”

“Look at the branch.”

Ianthe did. The branch outside bent away from the cabins, toward the hill.

“Wind can turn at the roof,” she said.

“It can.”

“Then how can you tell from one branch?”

“You can’t.”

“Then the chart is incomplete.”

“Everything about tomorrow is incomplete.”

Annabeth came to the window, holding the paper by one edge. “The map is provisional too. You can keep the observation, but the direction needs a question mark.”

Ianthe took the page back. “If I mark everything with a question mark, it’s no use.”

“If you mark a guess as certain, it can be less useful.”

Thalia watched her hold the pencil still. The point pressed hard enough to leave a dent.

“Fine,” Ianthe said. She added a small mark beside the wind direction.

The air changed. The window blanket pushed inward, then fell back. One of the paper corners lifted.

Ianthe put her hand over it. “That’s the gust.”

“Is it the one you predicted?” Annabeth asked.

“It’s from the north.”

Thalia glanced at the branch again. It was bending west. “It’s coming around the hill.”

Ianthe looked outside. The weather did not care which of them had named it first.

“It may be both,” Annabeth said.

“Then the chart can say that.”

Ianthe wrote two arrows and a note about the hill. The second arrow was shorter. She drew it without looking at Thalia.

“That’s better,” Thalia said.

“I didn’t ask you to grade it.”

“I wasn’t.”

“What were you doing?”

“Looking.”

Ianthe put the charcoal down. “It’s hard to tell when people here are helping and when they’re judging.”

“Ask.”

“Is that the rule?”

“It’s a good place to start.”

She rubbed the charcoal from the side of her hand. The smudge streaked across the red scrape. Her eyes flicked toward it, but she did not mention the sting.

Annabeth took a clean cloth from the shelf and set it beside her. “For your hand.”

“Thank you.”

She used it to wipe the charcoal, then pressed it gently against the scraped place. There was no look between the sisters, no congratulation for making a simple choice. Annabeth returned the cloth to the shelf.

Thalia watched the weather marks settle on the table. “You said you were told you could make a plan.”

“I was.”

“What did your mother say?”

“She said to go with the satyr.”

“About your plan?”

“She said the door mattered more than whether I was right.”

“That seems fair.”

“It was not the right part of the conversation.”

“Was the door open?”

“No.”

“Was the wind still hitting it?”

“Yes.”

“Then it mattered.”

Ianthe put the note from her mother on the table, still folded. “She knew I was right about the gust. She just didn’t want me to keep testing it.”

“Maybe she didn’t want you standing in front of the door.”

“I know how to stand.”

“I’m sure you do.”

Ianthe looked at the table and then back to the note. “She’s not afraid of me.”

Thalia had not thought that. “I didn’t say she was.”

“She didn’t tell me to leave because she was afraid.”

“Okay.”

“She told me to leave because the storm had gotten too large to keep inside.”

The distinction had come out with more care than the others. Thalia recognized its shape. There was a version of the story that made Ianthe dangerous, and a version that made her frightened. Neither had to be true. A storm could happen in a house with people who loved each other.

“What did you want her to say?” Annabeth asked.

Ianthe picked up the note. “That my plan worked.”

“Did it?”

“It kept the kitchen windows from breaking.”

“Then it worked some.”

“I had to leave before I knew whether the rest would hold.”

“Then you don’t know.”

Ianthe looked at Annabeth. “Do you always answer questions with more questions?”

“When I don’t know the answer.”

“That must be convenient.”

“It isn’t.”

The two of them watched each other. Annabeth did not look away. Thalia stayed in her chair. The old habit of making herself a wall between them rose in her chest and stopped there. Annabeth could choose whether to continue.

Ianthe lifted the note to her face but did not open it again. “I should call her.”

“You can use the Big House phone,” Annabeth said.

“Will Chiron let me?”

“You don’t need permission to call your mother.”

Ianthe held still for a moment. Then she tucked the note into her pocket. “I’ll call later.”

“All right.”

Annabeth turned to the map, and Thalia returned to her chair. The weather chart remained on the table. Nobody called it a cabin rule. Nobody used it to assign the next day.


---


The dinner Chiron sent up was covered in a blue-striped cloth and warm enough to fog the lid.

Annabeth set it in the middle of the cabin table. Thalia pulled her chair closer with one hand and kept her heel extended, where it would not press against the table leg. Ianthe stood by the door with her bag still on her shoulder.

“Are you eating?” Annabeth asked.

“I can.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

Ianthe looked from the bowl to the chairs. “Yes.”

Annabeth lifted the cover. Lentil stew, a loaf of bread, and a small bowl of pears cut into quarters. The steam carried cumin and a trace of pepper. Thalia’s stomach made the decision before she did.

“Chiron sent enough for three,” Annabeth said. “There’s more downstairs.”

Ianthe set down her bag. “I’ll get the bowls.”

“You don’t know where they are.”

“Then I’ll find them.”

“The cabinet by the stove,” Thalia said. “Top shelf.”

Ianthe opened the cabinet and took down the bowls. She set them on the table in a line, then put one at each place.

“We don’t usually sit in order,” Annabeth said.

“What order?”

“Any order.”

Ianthe moved the bowl nearest the door to the other side. “This makes more room.”

“For what?” Thalia asked.

“For eating.”

“We had room.”

“Then the bowls were still in the wrong places.”

Annabeth sat. “You can move yours.”

Ianthe looked at the places. “I didn’t say you had to move yours.”

“You moved mine.”

“I moved the bowl.”

“Then put it back.”

Ianthe picked the bowl up and returned it to its first place. Her cheeks had colored a little. She took the chair beside the door, where the air from the window was coldest.

Thalia reached for the ladle. The handle was smooth and warm. She filled Annabeth’s bowl, then hers. Ianthe held hers out only after she saw what they were doing.

“Do you want some?” Thalia asked.

“Yes.”

Thalia filled the bowl. Ianthe took it with both hands and sat down.

For several minutes the only sounds were spoons and the rain beginning against the roof. The first drops came apart on the window, scattered by the wind. Annabeth tore the bread into three uneven pieces and put the largest on the plate.

Ianthe watched her do it. “You should take that one.”

“I’m fine with this.”

“It’s the largest.”

“I know.”

“Then why?”

“Because I cut it.”

Ianthe looked at the bread as if that answer were not sufficient. Thalia took the large piece and broke it in half, leaving one half on the plate between them.

“Now nobody has to decide who gets it,” she said.

“That’s not how portions work,” Ianthe replied.

“It is tonight.”

Annabeth slid the plate toward the center. “Take whichever half you want.”

Ianthe chose the smaller one. She dipped it into the stew and ate without comment.

The rain strengthened, then softened again. On the table, the weather chart had started to curl where the charcoal marks were heaviest. Annabeth put the spare bread plate on one corner to keep it flat.

“Don’t put food on that,” Ianthe said.

“It’s a clean plate.”

“Then put a book on it.”

“The plate is here.”

“Books are cleaner.”

“Not the books on this table.”

Ianthe’s expression tightened. “I’m trying to keep the marks from running.”

“They won’t. It’s charcoal.”

“It can smear.”

“Then we can copy it again.”

“Why would you waste it?”

“Because it’s a note, not a map.”

“I want to keep it.”

“Then tell me that.”

Ianthe set down her spoon. “I want to keep it.”

Annabeth nodded and moved the plate to the other corner. “There.”

The chart lay flat again. A charcoal line ended in a small arrow near the north side of the window. Thalia leaned over it.

“You marked the gust from the north.”

“It came from that direction.”

“Across the hill.”

“Which is north.”

“It bends around the hill.”

“That’s what the second arrow says.”

“Good.”

Ianthe glanced at Thalia, suspicious of praise. “It’s still only one hour of observation.”

“Right.”

“It doesn’t tell us what the front will do.”

“No.”

“Then why are you acting like it matters?”

“Because it tells us what the wind did for an hour.”

“That’s a small thing.”

“It’s a thing that happened.”

Ianthe looked down at the page. She drew a line beneath the first note and wrote *watch again tomorrow*. Then, after a pause, she added *if Chiron wants it*.

Annabeth saw her add the words but did not comment.

Thalia returned to the stew. It had cooled enough to eat without waiting. The warmth settled in her chest and hands. The chair put pressure on her heel only when she shifted, so she stopped shifting. She did not tell anyone the pain had eased. It had not.

“Does it hurt now?” Ianthe asked.

“Yes.”

“Is it worse?”

“No.”

“Then it’s stable.”

“Not necessarily. It’s the same.”

“That’s what stable means.”

“Not when you don’t know what caused it.”

Ianthe lowered her spoon. “Chiron examined it.”

“He did.”

“And gave you a splint.”

“Yes.”

“So he knows what it is.”

“He knows what he saw.”

“That’s not the same thing?”

“Not always.”

Ianthe considered this. “I would want a second opinion.”

“You can want one.”

“Would you get one?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then why not ask?”

“Because I haven’t decided.”

Ianthe pushed the bread around the bowl. “If the heel is still painful in a week, you should have it looked at again.”

“Chiron said he’d recheck it.”

“When?”

“On the schedule.”

“What schedule?”

“His.”

“That’s not a date.”

“No.”

Ianthe seemed frustrated by the absence of a specific number. “You could write it down.”

“It’s written down.”

“Where?”

“In the infirmary.”

“Then no one can see it here.”

“I don’t need to see it here.”

Annabeth put her spoon down. “Ianthe, Thalia knows what Chiron told her. She’s following it.”

“I’m not saying she isn’t.”

“You keep trying to decide what she should do.”

“I’m trying to help.”

“Then ask if she wants help.”

Ianthe looked from Annabeth to Thalia. “Do you want help?”

“No.”

“Then I won’t give it.”

It was not quite an apology. It was a clear stop. Thalia let the sentence stand.

The rain tapped the roof. Outside, someone crossed the path with a lantern cupped under one hand. Ianthe followed the light until it disappeared behind the Big House.

“Does the storm always come this way?” she asked.

“Not always,” Annabeth said.

“Then how do you know where it will go?”

“You look.”

“You both say that.”

“Because we look,” Thalia said. “We don’t know everything before it happens.”

“Father said you could read a storm by the pressure.”

“I can sometimes read the weather.”

“Did he teach you?”

“No.”

Ianthe’s eyes sharpened. “He told me I had a talent for seeing what everyone else missed.”

“Maybe you do.”

“He said I should use it.”

“Then use it.”

“I am.”

“Use it without deciding that everybody else is missing the same thing.”

Ianthe’s spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. For a moment her face took on the set look of Zeus’s, the same one Thalia remembered from the times he’d stood across a room and waited for the rest of them to understand that he had already made up his mind.

Then she blinked, and the resemblance loosened.

“I wasn’t trying to be unfair,” she said.

“I didn’t say you were.”

“You said I was deciding.”

“You were.”

“That’s not the same as being unfair.”

“No.”

Annabeth picked up the bread plate. “Do you want another piece?”

Ianthe stared at the plate for a moment. “Yes.”

Annabeth passed it to her.

The answer did not settle the argument. It gave it room to end.

After the meal, Annabeth carried the bowls to the wash basin. Thalia helped by collecting the spoons from the table, using her good foot to keep the chair from sliding. Ianthe took the weather sheet and folded it into thirds.

“Can I leave it on the table?” Annabeth asked.

“It’ll get wet.”

“The window is closed.”

“It can still spill.”

“Then I’ll put it on the shelf.”

“That’s better.”

Annabeth put the sheet on the shelf. The fold stayed neat because Ianthe had made it carefully. She did not write another instruction beside it.

Chiron came by after the dishes were rinsed. “I found a room in the Big House for tonight. Tomorrow we can talk about Cabin One, the bed, and what you need.”

“I want to stay here.”

“I know.”

“I’m one of Zeus’s children.”

“You are.”

“Then I should be in this cabin.”

“You will be,” Chiron said. “We still need to decide how to make it work.”

“I can sleep on the floor.”

“No,” Thalia said. “The floor is cold.”

Ianthe looked at her. “You don’t want me here?”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You said I shouldn’t sleep on the floor.”

“I said the floor is cold.”

“That’s different?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because you can stay in the cabin and still sleep somewhere decent.”

Chiron set a hand on the back of his chair. “The guest room is warm. It gives us a night to sort out the space without rushing.”

Ianthe glanced at Annabeth. “Do you want me here?”

Annabeth took a breath before answering. “I want to know you before we decide what living together means.”

Ianthe looked at the folded weather chart. “That could take a long time.”

“It could.”

The rain kept moving across the roof. Thalia felt its rhythm in the sore place under the splint.

“You can stay for supper tomorrow,” she said.

Ianthe turned to her. “That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the answer I have.”


---


Chiron walked Ianthe to the Big House guest room. The rain had eased enough that the path was only slick, not flooded. Thalia waited at the cabin door until they reached the porch.

“I can go the rest of the way alone,” Ianthe said.

“Of course,” Chiron replied.

She looked back at the cabin. Annabeth stood inside, one hand on the window frame. Thalia had sat down again. The splint kept her heel from bending the way she wanted.

“I’ll come back tomorrow,” Ianthe said.

“If you want to,” Annabeth answered.

Ianthe looked at Chiron, then at Annabeth. “I do.”

“Then you can.”

She turned away. Chiron did not touch her shoulder or take the bag. He walked beside her, leaving the pace to her.

Annabeth closed the door, then left it unlatched. “Do you want it shut?”

“Not all the way.”

“Okay.”

The rain tapped the roof. Thalia drew her leg toward the chair and lifted her heel onto the cushion. The pressure eased, but the soreness remained. It did not become less real because it had a place to rest.

Annabeth sat beside her. “You almost spoke for me.”

“I know.”

“You stopped.”

“I heard you.”

“Thank you.”

Thalia looked at her sister. Annabeth’s hands were clean, her sleeves dry, her face calm in the way it got when she had put something difficult into a place she could look at later.

“I wanted to tell her she doesn’t get to call you little.”

“I handled that.”

“You did.”

“I want her to know me. I don’t want you to introduce me to her by defending me.”

“I know.”

Annabeth reached for Thalia’s hand. “That doesn’t mean you can’t defend me if I ask.”

“I know that too.”

Their fingers joined between the chair arms. Thalia rubbed her thumb over the edge of Annabeth’s nail, feeling the small ridge where a paper cut had healed.

The question of when to step in had never been as simple as whether she loved her sister. She did. That was why she had learned to watch doors, moods, and the distance between Annabeth and the people who assumed they knew what was best for her. The habit had helped them once. It could still be a habit, not a rule Annabeth had to live under.

“Do you want me to say nothing if she does that again?” Thalia asked.

“I want you to ask me if I want help.”

“Even in front of her?”

“Especially then.”

“And if you say no?”

“Then you stay beside me.”

Thalia nodded. “I can do that.”

“I know you can. You don’t have to get it perfect the first time.”

“I’d rather not make you practice telling me.”

“Then remember it while it’s small.”

“I wasn’t ready for another sister,” Annabeth said.

“Me neither.”

“That sounds bad.”

“It’s honest.”

“Can I be curious and not ready?”

“Yes.”

“Can I be angry at him and not at her?”

“Yes.”

“Can I like her later?”

“Sure.”

“Can I not like her?”

“You can also do that.”

Annabeth smiled. “You’re making it sound like I get to decide.”

“You do.”

“What about you?”

Thalia looked through the narrow strip of window. A cloud had caught on the ridge and was thinning at its lower edge. “I don’t know what I think yet.”

“That’s allowed.”

“I don’t want her in here because he sent her.”

“She came because the weather at her house got dangerous.”

“Right.”

“Her mother sent her with the satyr.”

“Right.”

“She’s not him.”

“I know.”

“And she’s not responsible for what he didn’t do for us.”

“I know that too.”

“I also don’t want to send her away because he sent her,” Thalia said. “Those feel like opposite answers.”

“They can both be true,” Annabeth replied.

“I don’t want to make it a choice between her and him.”

“Then don’t.”

“What if I don’t know what I want?”

“Then you don’t know yet. I don’t either.”

Thalia let the answer settle without trying to improve it.

Annabeth squeezed her fingers. “You don’t have to figure out all of it tonight.”

“I’m not going to.”

“Good.”

The cabin quieted around them. The map stayed on the table, and the weather chart remained folded on the shelf. Thalia could hear the rain outside and the soft shifting of the cabin boards as the wind pressed against them.

A knock came a few minutes later. Annabeth opened the door. Ianthe stood on the porch with no bag this time, only the folded weather chart in one hand.

“Chiron said I could bring this back here,” she said.

“Did you ask him?”

“I asked if I could look at it tomorrow. He said I could leave it on the shelf.”

“Then you can.”

Ianthe handed it to Annabeth rather than crossing the threshold. Annabeth took the paper and placed it beside the map.

“I’ll be in the guest room tonight,” Ianthe said. “Tomorrow, I want to talk about the bed.”

“We can talk tomorrow,” Annabeth said.

“I want to sleep in Cabin One.”

“We know.”

“That’s not the same as saying yes.”

“No.”

Ianthe looked at Thalia. “You haven’t told me whether you want me here.”

Thalia heard the wind in the pause. She could have told her that the cabin was not a prize to be awarded. She could have said the door had no lock, the floor had enough room, the beds were theirs. She could have said she had the right to be angry about Zeus without making a stranger carry it.

“I don’t know you yet,” she said.

“Would knowing me change the answer?”

“It might.”

Ianthe’s eyebrows drew together. “You expect me to prove I belong?”

“No.”

“Then why can’t you answer?”

“Because I can’t tell you something I don’t know.”

For a moment Ianthe looked as if she might reject that. Then she tucked the loose edge of her sleeve beneath her coat cuff.

“I know I belong in the cabin,” she said. “Our father’s children go there.”

“Chiron said you would.”

“He said we’d decide how it works.”

“That’s what he said.”

“And you’re fine with that?”

“I’m not in charge of the cabin.”

“You’re older than Annabeth.”

“That doesn’t make me in charge of her either.”

“I didn’t say it did.”

“You’re looking at me as if I’m supposed to decide.”

Ianthe turned her face toward the rain. The profile was sharp in the porch light, the same angle that had made Thalia think of Zeus when she first saw it.

“He told me I should use what I can see,” Ianthe said.

“Who?”

“Father.”

“What did he mean?”

“He said I notice things others miss.”

“That doesn’t mean you have to decide what everybody does.”

“I didn’t say it did.”

“You told the kid to close the window without asking him. You told Annabeth where she should sleep. You told me when to sit.”

“I was trying to help.”

“I know.”

“Then why do you keep acting like I’m trying to run the place?”

“Because you keep trying to run the place.”

Ianthe’s mouth set. “That’s not fair.”

“Maybe not.”

“What would be fair?”

“I don’t know you well enough to know.”

“I’m standing here.”

“That’s how I know you’re here.”

Ianthe’s hand closed around the folded paper. “He said I’d be useful.”

“Then be useful.”

“How?”

“Ask the people here what they need.”

“That’s all?”

“That’s a start.”

She looked down at the paper. “The wind will turn again after midnight.”

“Maybe.”

“It will.”

“Maybe.”

“You don’t trust me.”

“I don’t know you.”

“That is not the same.”

“It can feel the same.”

Ianthe looked up. “You’ve had him longer.”

Thalia took in a breath. The sentence landed where she felt it most, a place she had not decided whether to show. “No.”

“You’re older.”

“That doesn’t mean he was there.”

“Then you don’t have more of him.”

“No.”

“You have a different kind of less.”

Thalia’s hand tightened around Annabeth’s fingers. “You don’t know what I have.”

“I didn’t say I did.”

“Then don’t tell me.”

Ianthe’s expression changed. She looked less like Zeus when she stopped trying to make the words finish the argument for her. The rain made a fine bright line on the edge of the porch roof.

“You’re right,” she said.

Thalia did not know whether it was an apology or a retreat.

Ianthe unfolded the weather chart and checked the charcoal arrows. “I should go before the next gust.”

“You can wait until it passes.”

“I know.”

She looked toward the window. Thalia held her gaze a moment, then nodded toward the empty hook beside the door. “You can leave the chart there if you want. It will stay dry.”

“Annabeth already put it on the shelf.”

“She did.”

“Then I’ll leave it there.”

Ianthe refolded the paper. This time she handed it to Annabeth again and waited while Annabeth placed it on the shelf beside the map.

“Good night,” Annabeth said.

“Good night.”

Ianthe turned to Thalia. “Your heel should be elevated.”

“I know.”

“Chiron said?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

Thalia looked at her. Ianthe pressed her lips together, then added, “I didn’t mean to tell you what to do.”

“I heard you.”

It was not a forgiveness. It was enough to close the conversation.

Annabeth walked Ianthe to the porch edge, then returned alone. The door clicked shut but stayed unlatched.

“Do you want to go to bed?” she asked Thalia.

“In a while.”

“Do you want to keep the light on?”

“Yes.”

Annabeth left the lantern where it was and sat at the table. She moved the folded weather chart a few inches from the map so the charcoal would not rub off. The two sheets were different: one showed paths, the other a small part of the day’s wind. Neither one told them what the next day would decide.

Thalia leaned back. Her heel throbbed, steady and low. She kept it on the cushion, as Chiron had told her, and let the cabin remain quiet.

Outside, the storm drew its last dark face over the ridge and moved on.
