## Chapter 190: Thursday, Twice

Thursday came in on water.

That was what a thaw did to a hill that had been frozen since Monday: it turned the whole place into a drain. By the time Percy got down to the boathouse the icicles had come off the eaves and lay in the grass in a bright wreck of clear glass, and the path was a black gutter with a skin of ice over it that took him to the ankle and then let go, and the pines were shedding in the loud way pines shed when a weight comes off them all at once. Up behind him, out of sight around the shoulder of the hill, Cabin Three had every towel it owned hung on the rail and had had them there since yesterday, because Wednesday had been soup and wet outerwear and nobody had got round to bringing the last of it in.

He went in under the boathouse arch and stood still to let his eyes do the work.

The lake was up. You could see it on the waterline stain inside the doorframe, a brown tide mark a hand's width above the one from November the year before, and the water itself was the colour of weak tea where the melt was running into it, and it lay flat and oily and entirely without opinion, which was the way a lake looked when it had stopped being cold enough to care. The boathouse smelled of wet rope and tarred canvas and the deep cold mineral smell that came off the stone at the north end where the spring feed came down the bank. Six canoes sat on their racks under their sheets, stowed for the season, patient as furniture. The skiff was on its chains at the float with its bow to the door, and the heron was standing on the bow in the same place it had stood on Tuesday, doing nothing at all.

"You're a pest," Percy told it, without heat, and went past.

The port-side canoe was the one. Tyson had asked, in the careful flat voice he used for anything that might be bad news, for Percy to put his foot on the prow piece and write and tell him whether it had moved. He had been asked this nine days ago. He had walked past the canoe on twelve separate occasions since, on each of which he had put his hand on the sheet and not lifted it, because the honest answer was going to be one of two things and he had not been ready to say either of them out loud.

He lifted the sheet.

The canoe came out of the dark of its cover grey and clean, the gunwales rubbed bright where hands went, the thwarts tight, the seats stowed. The prow piece was a separate length of cedar, scarfed into the stem and fastened with pegged trenails, and from above it looked like a prow piece. That was the whole problem with a prow piece. From above everything looked fastened.

Percy took his boot off it and went and got the straightedge off the wall and the folding rule out of the tool drawer, and came back, and did it the slow way.

It had moved.

Not much. A quarter inch at the forward scarf, down and out, and the cedar had taken up a set in the last six weeks that had pulled the second trenail a hair proud of its hole. He ran his thumb along the underside of the joint and found what he had been afraid of, which was a check in the wood you could get a fingernail into, a hairline opening that ran out from the scarf toward the keel line and stopped, the way a crack stops when it has decided it has gone far enough. Dry inside. No rot. The wood was sound. The fastening was not.

He sat back on his heels with the rule in his hand and looked at it for a while.

Then he got the chalk out of his pocket and put a mark on the rack above the scarf, a short vertical line with a horizontal one across it, and a second mark on the piece itself so that the two would agree or disagree from six feet away, and he wrote the measurement in the back of the field book — his own page of it, the one he had been keeping since August, because Annabeth had the front of the book and he had been given the back of it in a manner she described as a lease and he described as a conquest. He wrote it in his own hand, which was large and upright and had been the same since he was nine.

PROW — PORT. FORWARD SCARF OPEN 1/4. CHECK 3 IN FROM SCARF, NAIL DEPTH. DRY. NOT TOUCHED. T. TO CONFIRM.

He looked at the last four words. Then he crossed out CONFIRM and wrote FIX IT YOURSELF, which was truer, and put the book away.

Outside, a truck went by on the county road, far off and unconcerned, and the sound of it came up through the pines and died. Thursday. A man in a truck at six in the morning and a canvas sack on a hook, and the whole week's writing going north in the dark. Percy stood in the boathouse with the sheet in his hand and found that he had been smiling, which was not a thing he had planned either.

He put the sheet back over the canoe the way Silena put a cloth over a loaf, squared at the corners, and went out with the chalk still on his fingers.

---

The west path was running.

That was the thaw's other trick. Meltwater had found the low side of it, the side where the lamps stood, and had cut a channel the width of a boot heel along four yards of the gravel, and then had frozen in the night, and the channel was now a bright braided thing lying on the path with the gravel showing through it in dark bars. Percy walked on the uphill side of it. Two of the nine lamps were lit, which was early, which meant somebody had done lamp duty before breakfast the way they did in a week when the weather had changed its mind twice.

Cabin Three smelled of yesterday's bread and wet wool, and the stove was going, and Clarisse was at the long table with the winter list squared in front of her and a pencil laid across it, and had not written a word on it in an hour. Silena was at the stove with a towel over her shoulder and the last of Wednesday's first hot bread on the board under it, keeping it warm, because a thing kept warm was a thing she intended to hand to somebody by name.

"Morning," Percy said, hanging his coat on the hook where it had been since the thaw, on the outside of the rail, dripping.

"You're wet," Clarisse said.

"I'm damp. Wet is a word for people who fell in."

"You walked up the braid."

"I walked up the uphill side of the braid, like a man of sense." He pulled the chair out and sat down at the table across from her, and put the field book between them, open at the back, with the measurement showing. "Tell me what that says."

Clarisse read it. She read it the way she read everything, which was once fast and once slow, and then she put her finger on the last line and said, "Not touched."

"Not touched."

"Because if you touched it, it'd be our canoe."

"It'd be a canoe I'd made worse with a good intention." Percy turned the book around so the page faced her. "He said put your foot on it and tell me. I put my foot on it. Then I went and got the rule. He'll like the rule better. He'll say I should have started with the rule."

"He'll say you should have started with the rule," Clarisse agreed, "and then he'll come and put it right himself and never once mention it, and that's the whole arrangement, and you know it, so stop looking like a man who's been accused of something."

"I'm not looking accused. I'm looking like a man who has to write it down."

"You've got three days to write it down. It's Thursday."

"It's Thursday," Percy said, and heard the day land in the room the way a day lands when it has a job in it.

Silena turned round from the stove with the butter knife in her hand and looked at the two of them over the top of the bread, and did the thing she did, which was to take in the entire situation in one pass and decide within two seconds what she thought about it and not say a word of it out loud.

"Chalk," she said.

"Chalk," Percy agreed.

"On the good hand. The one you write with." She put two pieces of bread on the board and reached for the knife. "Sit down before you get it on the list."

"I'm sitting."

"You're hovering."

"I'm sitting and hovering. It's a documented—" He stopped. Clarisse's pencil stopped. Silena's knife stopped. All three of them looked at him with the flat patience of a family that had heard a man retire a phrase and then bring it back out for a special occasion, and Percy put both hands flat on the table and said, with enormous dignity, "No. I'm not doing it. Silena, back me up."

"I'm not in that," Silena said. "I'm the middle child. I'm Switzerland with a bread knife."

"You're the middle child," Clarisse said, "and you've got three envelopes in your pocket."

"Two."

"You've got three envelopes in your pocket, Silena."

Silena looked down at her own pocket, appeared to count, and did not deny it. She sliced the bread instead, evenly, the way she did everything with her hands, and set one piece in front of Percy and one in front of Clarisse and took the third herself, and then, because the heel was the heel and the law was the law, she broke the end off the loaf and held it out across the table without looking at anybody in particular.

"Eat that quickly," she said. "It's the last of it. There's no more until Saturday and I'm not firing the oven twice in a thaw."

Percy took the heel in both hands. It was warm. It was warm in the specific way that a thing is warm when it has been wrapped in cloth at the back of a shelf by somebody who had the forethought, and he ate a corner of it standing up, and Clarisse watched him do it and made a small noise that in any other cabin would have been a complaint.

"Favouritism," she said.

"Noted," Percy said, through bread.

"I want it noted in the book."

"You can put it in the book yourself. You're sitting closer to the book than I am."

"I'm sitting closer to the book," Clarisse said, "because I'm the one who writes in it, Jackson, and one day you'll be grateful, and on that day I'll be dead and you'll have no one to complain to."

"That's the warmest thing you've said since the frost."

"I've said warmer." She turned the list a quarter turn on the table as she totalled it up, and looked at it. The list had gone through four stages. The first had been a fence line. The second had been a plan. The third, after the ridge, had been shorter. The fourth, which was the one on the table now, had three items on it that had been on it since before the spring was found, and they had not moved, and they were not going to move until the ground was hard.

"The greenhouse bank's still there," Percy said.

"I know where it is."

"I'm not saying you don't know where it is. I'm saying the frost went and came back in two days, and you've written the bank on a list for eleven days, and the bank can't read."

Clarisse put her pencil down. "The bank can't read," she repeated, in the tone of a woman confirming a finding. "Correct. The bank has never been able to read. It has a runoff scar and wet soil; when I shore it up, I want to do it once on firm ground, not twice in a thaw."

"So you're letting it wait."

"I'm letting it wait. So's the awning. So's the pine." She tapped the page twice with the eraser of the pencil. "A thaw's the worst time to fix anything. Everything's wet and everything moves. Silena, tell him."

Silena did not turn round. "Don't bring me into it. I've got three envelopes."

"Two," said Clarisse.

"Three," said Silena, and gave up on it, and put the bread board down and started to clear the table with the flat of her hand, pushing crumbs into her palm, which was the first stage of the afternoon. "Percy. You're going to the Big House."

"I was going to write at the table."

"You're going to the Big House, and you're going to ask Chiron whether the sea-mail goes out today, and you're going to bring back the good blotter, because the one we've got is a rag and I intend to write a letter to my mother on something better than a rag." She said it without any emphasis, and then added, in exactly the same voice, "And you're going to do it before you get chalk on a page, and you're going to take the long way, because the braid's on the short way."

Percy looked at her. Then he looked at the field book, open on the table between them with his own large upright hand in the back of it, and understood, as he understood most things about his family, that he had been sent out of the room on purpose.

"Yeah," he said. "The long way."

"Take the heel," Silena said. "There's a bit left."

---

Annabeth Grace came down the hill at a quarter to three with two pages of the wrong length, one sheet cut square, and a plan in her head that involved not letting anybody see the third page.

She had gone up Thursday morning with the week's incoming in her coat pocket, and the week's incoming had been heavy in a way that had nothing to do with paper. Her mother's had come up the hill on Wednesday evening with the supply cart, three pages, no envelope padding, the corner of the first one rubbed soft where a thumb had gone over it twice before it was folded, and Annabeth had read it standing at the top of the Cabin One steps in the cold with her boots still on and then read it again sitting on the bottom step with her knees up, which was the order she always did it in.

Beryl Grace wrote sober. That was the entire shape of it, and it was a great deal. There was nothing decorative in it, no performance of a mood, no sentence that had been written to be admired rather than understood. In September it had been four pages about a roof and a question. This week it was three.

*You told me the lake moved indoors for the winter. I want to know whether the roof leaks. Not whether it is nice. Whether it leaks. Tell me the corner. Tell me what happens in it in a hard rain, and tell me who is going to fix it, and do not tell me it is fine.*

*And give me one true thing that is not about work. Not the survey, not the building, not the mountain. Something small. I have been sleeping. The house is quiet at this hour. Ask me a question and I will answer it, and I would rather you asked than guessed.*

Annabeth had put the letter on the sill above the granite stone, where Thalia would find it without being told, and had gone down to breakfast, and had thought about the corner of the roof for four hours.

She came in the Cabin One door at half past two, took her own coat off the hook, and went down the hill without stopping to consider that her feet would take her where they always took her on a Thursday.

The cabin door was open with a boot wedged under it. The stove was going. The rail outside had gone from a washing line back to a rail in the last day, which was the order things happened in this cabin, and the smell that came out of the doorway was bread and woodsmoke and the particular clean smell of a floor that had been swept with a wet cloth in a thaw. Clarisse was at the long table with the list. Silena was at the table too, with the lamp pulled toward her and three envelopes in front of her, and a coil of string, and a parcel wrapped in oilcloth that she was tying with the flat efficiency of a woman who had tied parcels before.

"Room?" Annabeth said, from the step.

"Room," Silena said. "Table's yours. The corner's yours. You've had the corner since August and you'd fight me for it."

"I'd fight you for it," Annabeth agreed, and came in, and put her coat on the rail and her pages on the table at the corner end, and sat down at the exact place she had sat at since the first Thursday of the autumn, which was close enough to the far end that the sleeve of whatever she was wearing lay along the same line as the sleeve of whoever was sitting there. Nobody had ever moved that. Nobody had ever discussed it.

"Chalk," Silena observed.

"Chalk."

"On the right hand. He'll get it on the page and then he'll want to start the page again." Silena pulled the lamp a half inch toward her without looking at it. "Clarisse sent him up to Chiron for a blotter and to get him out from underfoot. She's very subtle."

"I'm standing right here," Clarisse said.

"You're standing right here being subtle."

"I'm standing right here being tired of your mouth." Clarisse did not look up. She had the pencil going behind her ear now, which meant she had reached the stage of the list where she was reading it rather than writing it. "Grace. Your sister anywhere?"

"She's up at the cabin with the Jason letter. She's writing it in capitals out loud and the windows are open." Annabeth took out her pen and laid it on the page beside the pencil she always wrote with first. "She'll be down when the light goes."

"She'll be down when she's finished," Clarisse said, "which is not the same thing, and don't let her tell you it is."

---

Nobody wrote in the parlour that week. Annabeth had noticed it on the way down: the long table pushed back to the wall, the coal scuttle gone from under the door, the chest under the window by the door standing open with its emptiness arranged. This week the letters were being written wherever people lived, at stoves and on bunks and on boathouse benches, and they would come down the hill in ones and twos at dusk with their owners. She thought that was probably the correct development, and wrote it in the corner of a page she was not going to send.

Her own page was the wrong length. She had cut it square because square was what the table used, and then had found that three pages of what she actually wanted to say would not fit into one, and had cut two more, and then had sat looking at them.

Beryl Grace had asked for the corner of the roof.

The corridor outside her mother's rooms in the deep palace — no. That was Percy's memory, not hers. Annabeth put her pen down and looked at the window a moment, because there had been a winter when she had known the shape of every roof on a hill that was not this one, and she still could not always tell the difference between a thing she remembered and a thing she had been told. The hall, though, the hall she had. Four months of it. The tile, the brick, the barrel ceiling, the skylight in the long curve, the north corner where the rain lay on the glass in a storm and went on lying there after it stopped, the warm mineral smell coming up off the water in the dark.

She wrote:

*The roof leaks in the north corner. It has leaked since before I was given the room, and the leak is in the glass and not in the framing, and it comes down the brick and not through it, and it is going to be fixed by a man with a ladder and a tube of sealant on a Thursday evening in October, and I am telling you that instead of telling you it is fine, because you asked me a straight question and because I have never once been able to lie to you on paper.*

*When it is fixed I will write and tell you that it is fixed, and I will not write to tell you that it was never broken, because it was.*

She read it. Then she took the third page and wrote the thing that was not about work, and wrote it the way her mother had asked for it, which was plain, with nothing on top of it:

*The true thing that is not about work is this. There is a boy here. He was asked by his brother to put his foot on a piece of wood and tell him whether it had moved. He put his foot on it. Then he went and got a rule, because a foot is not a measurement. It had moved a quarter inch, and there is a check in it three inches long that you can get a fingernail into. He wrote all of that down and he chalked a mark on the rack above it, and he has not touched it, and he is not going to touch it, because his brother promised to come and put it right himself and he intends to hold him to it.*

*He is fourteen. I watch him do things like that and I do not say anything at the time. I am saying it here, because you asked, and because you asked, and because nobody else has ever asked me.*

She blotted it. She turned the sheet and read it from the top with her head on one side, the way she read a drawing, and then, in the bottom margin, under the last line, where there was room, she drew it: a section through the hall in six strokes, the water line, the skylight, the north corner with a drip coming off it, and under that, very small, a line going up a hill with ten lights on it. She did the tenth one last and made it slightly bigger than the other nine.

Then she folded the pages once along the middle and looked for something to put them in, and found that she had not brought an envelope, which was the ordinary Thursday catastrophe, and had happened on four Thursdays before this one and would happen again.

"Envelope," said Silena, without turning round, and pushed one across the table with the back of her knuckle.

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. Thank the fact that I write to my mother and my mother writes back to all three of us in one envelope with Girls at the top of it, which means I have more envelopes than sense." Silena squared her own parcel and set it aside. "How many pages?"

"Three."

"You wrote three. Last week was four."

"Last week was four pages of the mountain." Annabeth licked the flap and did not look up. "This week is three pages of the roof and the boy and a drawing. The mountain got four because the mountain took four. You don't buy words by the yard."

"That's the most expensive sentence anybody's said in this cabin all week," Clarisse said, "and it came out of a girl with no envelope."

"It came out of a girl you gave no envelope to."

"That too."

The door came in off the step without a knock, which was how Thalia Grace came in, and she had her coat still on and a sheet in her hand and the particular expression of a woman who had been talking to herself for an hour and intended to continue.

"Move," she said, to nobody, and pulled the chair out at the far end of the table and sat down at the place where the sleeve of her coat lay along the same line as the sleeve of her sister's. She put the sheet down and squared it with two fingers. "Right. I'm writing it out loud and nobody's to help me."

"You always say nobody's to help you," Annabeth said, "and then you read me every line."

"Because your face goes like a shut door when something's wrong and I'd sooner read it off your face than off a page in four days' time." Thalia had her pen out already, the one that had never learned to do anything but capitals. "Silena. There's bread."

"There's bread and there's butter and there's no more bread after this, so decide."

"Then it's decided." Thalia took the piece Silena handed her, took the crust off it in two clean movements, and put the crust on Annabeth's plate without asking, and Annabeth picked it up and ate it, and neither of them said anything about it, because they had divided that loaf a thousand times.

Clarisse watched this entire transaction with her arms folded and her face completely level.

"That's not normal," she said.

"It's a family," Silena said.

"It's a *title*," Clarisse said. "It's got a title and a ceremony."

"It's called she takes the crust and she eats it," Thalia said, "and I would like to see you try it, La Rue, and then we'd see who takes what."

"I don't want her crust."

"Nobody wants anybody's crust. That's not what it's for." Thalia squared the sheet again. "Sit there, Annie. Be an audience. I'm doing the Jason letter and I'm doing it short and it's going to be terrible."

"It won't be terrible."

"It'll be four lines. Last week was nineteen. Four is harder than nineteen and don't tell anybody I said so."

"Why is four harder?"

Thalia's pen stopped. She looked at the sheet a moment with the flat expression she wore when she had been caught being accurate.

"Because nineteen was a week," she said. "Nineteen was a whole week with a mountain in it and anybody can write a week down. Four is what's left when the week was ordinary, and ordinary is the thing that's hard to make true on a Thursday, because if you write down that the stove went on at six and the bread was good, it reads like a man filling in a form." She turned the sheet a quarter round. "But he counts. He counts the days between Thursday and Sunday, and he says so every time, and he does it because I do it. So he gets four lines and four true lines, not four convenient ones."

She wrote. She read each line out as it went down, in the flat voice she used for anything she had made up her mind to hand over, and Clarisse's pencil had stopped moving entirely, and Silena had sat down, and Annabeth had put her chin in her hand and her field book on her knees and was watching her sister's face instead of the page.

*Jason — your letter came up the hill on Wednesday. Four days is still four days. I have counted them again and I have not got faster, and I would like that noted.*

*The lake moved indoors for the winter. There is a room under the hill with warm water in it and a slate on the wall with five rules on it, and the fifth one is the best thing anybody in this family has ever written down, and my sister designed the room and will deny designing it and then tell you the fall of the roof. Ask her about the roof. She has been waiting all year for somebody to ask her about the roof.*

*Tell me one true thing about the Fort that is not about the Fort. I will know if you make it up.*

*Thalia*

"That's four," Thalia said. "That's four lines and they're all true, and one of them has a roof in it, and if either of you says a word about the roof I will put you in the lake."

"The lake's closed," Percy said, from the doorway. "It moved indoors. It says so on the slate."

He came in with the good blotter under one arm and chalk on the side of his right hand and the look of a man who had walked the long way on purpose, and he put the blotter down on the table at the far end and hung his coat on the rail and sat down in the chair where the sleeve of his coat lay along the same line as the sleeve of his sister's.

"Also," he said, "the sea-mail goes out tonight. Chiron says the tide takes it at ten and it'll be in Atlantis by Tuesday, which is a day longer than the road, which I don't think is fair to a letter that has less far to go."

"Nothing's fair to a letter," Clarisse said. "Where's the chalk?"

"Right hand."

"It's on your face too."

Percy put his hand to his face and found it, and did not deny it, and Silena laughed into her own page and did not look up, and Thalia folded her letter once along the middle and then, out of a habit she would have denied under oath, folded it a second time across, so that it sat flat and square and without any spring in it at all, and put it in her back pocket, and Annabeth watched her do it and let her own face do nothing at all.

---

Percy's page for Tyson had gone badly for two days and then had gone all at once.

He had sat down at the table with the bad pen, which Annabeth had assigned to nobody and which was therefore available, and had written Thursday in the first line the way his brother liked it, with everything stamped where it was made, and had then got to the prow, and had stopped, and had started again on a fresh square, and had got to the prow, and had stopped.

It went in like this:

*Tyson — Thursday. I put my foot on it like you said. Then I got the rule, which I should have done first, and you can say I told you so.*

*The forward scarf on the port side is open a quarter inch, down and out. The second trenail is proud of its hole. There is a check in it three inches long from the scarf toward the keel and you can get a fingernail in it and it is dry inside. The wood is good. The fastening is not.*

*I have not touched it. I have put a chalk mark on the rack and a chalk mark on the piece and I wrote both numbers in the back of a book and I am not going to take them out. You said you were coming back and putting it right yourself. I am holding you to that. I have told the whole cabin and the whole cabin thinks it is funny.*

*The hook you sent for Silena's tote is in the tote and it works and Silena says it is fine, and I have told her that a hook that has been sent across an ocean by a Cyclops is not fine, it is loved, and there is a difference, and she has told me to write my own letters.*

*The lake moved indoors. There is a hall under the hill with warm water in it and a slate on the wall with five rules, and rule five is that whoever wants to come comes. There were fifteen people in it on Tuesday and four of them were little. One of them went in boots first. There is steam on the ceiling and you cannot see the far end from the door. It is the warmest room on the hill and I did not build it, Annabeth did, and I am telling you that before anybody asks you.*

*Clarisse says hello. Silena says hello. I say come back. The chalk marks will still be there.*

He read the last four lines out loud, without being asked, in the voice he used for things that were going to be judged, and then stopped, and put the pen down, and looked at the table.

"You don't have to read it," Silena said.

"The rule says whatever gets read at this table gets read because the writer said so," Percy said.

"The rule says the writer decides. It doesn't say the writer has to be an idiot about it." She turned her own page a quarter round, which was a thing she did when she was deciding something, and squared it to the edge of the blotter, and then, when she thought nobody was looking at her, which was a calculation she had always made badly with him in the room, let her eyes go once to the window and the road beyond it that ran up toward the forge.

Percy ate bread and said nothing about the window, and the nothing he said about it was a large and deliberate nothing, and he filled it with the next four lines of the letter instead: that the hall had a pump and a heater and a man who came up the path on Thursdays with a ladder, and that if the ladder was for the roof corner then Annabeth had written to their mother about the roof corner, which meant the whole hill was now in correspondence about one leak.

"Whose ladder?" Clarisse said.

"The forge's."

"Beckendorf's ladder."

"Beckendorf's ladder."

"Then it'll be done by half past eight and it'll be done right, and Silena can stop worrying about it." Clarisse turned a page. "Silena's not worrying about it."

"I'm not worrying about it," Silena said, to her own envelope, and folded a square of paper once along the long way and once across, and slid it under the flap without flourish, and did not read any of it aloud, and when Percy opened his mouth she said, "No."

"I didn't say anything."

"You were going to say read it."

"I was going to say that the rule says the writer decides."

"The writer has decided. The writer has decided that my mother's letter is not a public performance, and that the writer's mother writes back to three girls at once and addresses it Girls, and that the writer would like to keep one thing in the week that is not going to be read out in a kitchen." She put the envelope in the parcel with the oilcloth and began to tie the string. "That's what the writer has decided. Write it down."

"Writing it down," Clarisse said, and did not write anything down, which was the highest form of agreement she had.

Then Annabeth put down her pen.

She had three pages and a drawing in an envelope, and the envelope was addressed, and there was nothing left in the afternoon but the part she had been saving since she came down the hill, which was the part that did not go in the post.

She took a fresh square from the stack and wrote on it in her small upright record hand, the hand that did the camp's marks, and she did not hurry and she did not hide it, and she folded the paper along the creases he used, both folds, the long way and the across. Percy, at the other end of the table with his tongue caught and his chin on his hand, watched her do it and did not say anything, because saying anything would have made it a thing that was said.

She got up, came round the table, and stood beside his chair, and he stood up.

"We're not doing it in front of them," she said, very quietly, out of the side of her mouth, in the tone of a girl stating a logistical fact about the weather.

"We're not doing it in front of them," Percy agreed.

She put her hands behind her back and looked up at him, and he put one hand on the back of her coat at the shoulder, the way he did when a room was full of their family and there was nowhere else to put it, and she came in against him and lifted her face, and he bent his head down to it, and the kiss was a Thursday kiss, which is to say it was unhurried and entirely confident and had somewhere else to be in four minutes.

It was not short. It went on past the point where a kiss is an event and into the part where it is two people standing in a warm room with their family in it and their foreheads touching, breathing, and then it started again, because the afternoon had a next part and neither of them wanted to go to it.

When they came apart they did not go far. Annabeth's hand came from behind her back and went flat against his chest, over the place where his heartbeat was, and stayed there, and she looked up at him with an expression that was entirely a dare and entirely something else.

"Your chalk's on my collar," she said.

"It's been on your collar since August."

"Has it."

"It has. It's practically structural." He put his other hand over hers on his chest. "Where's mine?"

"Where you'll find it."

"Which is where?"

"In the coat you'll put on at half past five to walk down the hill."

He looked at his coat on the rail. Then he looked at her. Then he sat down and went back to his page, because his hand was unsteady and a letter to a brother deserved a straight hand, and Annabeth went round the table and sat down at the corner and did not look up for a while, and the color of the whole afternoon went on being completely normal.

Percy took a square from the stack and wrote on it with the bad pen in his large upright hand, and folded it along its creases in the two moves he always made, once down the long way and once across, and then reached over without looking at her face and opened the back cover of her field book, where his Thursday note had gone in every week since the second of October, and slid the square in flat against the flap, on top of the one already there, and closed it, and went back to his own page, and the whole operation took about as long as it takes to set a cup down.

Annabeth looked at the field book. Then she looked at the side of his face, which was doing nothing at all — how he did everything that mattered.

She waited two minutes, because two minutes was the amount of time that made it a discovery instead of an ambush, and then she opened the cover.

*You wrote the numbers before you wrote the sentence. I watched you do it. You did the measurement and then you went and found the word for the measurement and then you found the word for what you were not going to do about it, and that is the whole of the way you work, and it is the best thing I know.*

*I have put a drawing of the hill in my mother's letter. Ten lights on it. The tenth one is bigger than the other nine and it is ours. I did not explain it. She will understand it.*

*P.*

Annabeth read it once for the words. She read it a second time for the handwriting, which was a thing she had started doing in September and had never once told him about: the way his t's were cut clean through, the way he gave a y a tail that went further than it needed to, the way the pressure faded toward the end of a line where his hand had begun to hurry back to her. A folded square of paper could carry a whole afternoon of a person's attention, and his did, and she sat at the corner of a table in a cabin that smelled of bread and read it a third time before she was honest with herself about the third one.

Under the table, out of sight of anybody, she put her hand flat on the cover of the book.

Then she reached over, took the square he had just written on out from under his forearm where he had left it — because he never found them where they were put, he only ever found them, later, in the dark or on a set of steps, and the finding was the second half of the gift, and she had learned to leave it alone — and she held it a moment, and put it back exactly where it had been, and picked up her pen.

"Right," she said. "Who's got the sack?"

---

At twenty past five the sky went clear and the wind went into the north, and the hill began to dry.

It was the fastest change Percy had ever watched. An hour before, the whole place had been running; now the gutters stood empty and the gravel went pale, and the last of the melt stood in bright cracks on the path where a boot had been, and by the time the sun was off the ridge the frost was already coming back into the ground with a faint tick that you could hear if you were standing still. The pines stopped shedding. Cabin Three's rail was down to two towels, and Silena brought the last of them in and folded them and put them on the stove, and nobody said anything about it, which was how the cabin handled most of its tenderness.

The letters came down the hill in ones and twos.

That was the difference September had made. They did not arrive in a procession any more. They arrived the way people did: the Athena girl with a flat envelope under her arm, two of the Hermes kids with the same handwriting and an argument, a boy from the forge cabins with a parcel wrapped in oilcloth and string with FORGE written on it in block capitals big enough to be read off a truck, Olive with a page that had a drawing of the hall on it and had not been posted to anybody because she had made it for the chest itself, which Clarisse allowed, which surprised nobody. The Big House door stood open with the coal scuttle gone from under it and the chest under the window by the door with its lid up.

Clarisse put her own parcel in last. It was the hardware man. It was a second order, and it was for an awning, and it was four lines long, and it said exactly what she wanted and would not be answered with a question.

"Forty-four letters and two parcels," Percy said, having counted them the way he counted anything, out loud and slightly too late.

"Forty-four," Clarisse said.

"I counted twice."

"Count a third time. I'll wait."

"I counted three times. It's forty-four." He shut the lid. "It was forty and two last week."

"It was forty and two last week," Silena said, from the step, "and forty-three the week before, and thirty-eight the week before that, and you have said the number out loud every single time, and every single time you have said it like a man announcing a verdict."

"It's a verdict. Forty-four people decided this week that they'd rather write something down than wait."

"That's the whole camp," Annabeth said. She had come down behind them with her hood up and her envelope already in. "Every single one."

"Not quite every single one," Clarisse said. "Some of them are writing twice."

Percy looked at the chest. Then he looked at his sisters, and at the girl standing on the step with her hands in her sleeves, and he picked the sack up off the boards and held it open by the rim with the patient look of a man who has been given a job involving a container and intends to do the container justice.

Clarisse took it out of his hands.

"I've got it," she said.

"You carried it last week," Percy said.

"I carried it last week, and you carried it the week before that, and Silena carried it in September, and I am the one with a parcel in it and a name written on it in capitals, and if any of you says one word about it I will put you in the lake." She settled the sack onto her shoulder with the flat efficiency of a woman roping a load onto a truck. "Annabeth. Walk on my left. Percy, you're on the lamps."

"Which lamps?"

"The dark ones. The fourth and the seventh. They've been going out at four o'clock all week and somebody keeps saying it's the wind."

"It's not the wind."

"I know it's not the wind. It's the wick, and it's going to be a January job, and I would like the things that are not going to be a January job to stop being a January job." She went down the steps. "Silena, you're on the porch. Somebody has to be up here when the last one goes."

"Somebody does," Silena agreed, and did not move, and Percy understood, the way he understood most things in his family, that she had been assigned the only job in the group that kept her standing still.

Thalia was not on the hill at all. Annabeth had gone up at six to find her, and had found her on the top step of Cabin One with her coat over her knees and a folded letter in her hand and her left boot off, and had stood in the door and looked at her sister for a second with an expression that she then decided not to have.

"Thal," she had said. "It's Thursday."

"I know it's Thursday. I wrote the letter." Thalia had put the boot back on and stood up in one motion and taken the bad weight on the flat of her foot, as always. "Go on. I'm right behind you."

She was not right behind them. That was the thing Annabeth thought about on the way down the west path, past the dark fourth lamp and the seventh, with her sister's letter in a sack on her elder sister's shoulder. Thalia came down the path ten minutes later, alone, and did not limp, and arrived at the Big House with her hood up and her hands in her pockets, and said, "Right. Where's the sack?"

"It's got a head start," Clarisse said. "And you're late."

"I was upstairs."

"Doing what?"

"Doing the thing I was doing." Thalia looked at the chest, and at the closed lid, and her face did the thing it did when she had decided not to say something and had decided it a long time ago. "Fine. I'll carry the last stretch."

"Nobody carries the last stretch. There's a post and a hook and a man at six." Clarisse went past her up the road with the sack.

They went down the hill five abreast and then in single file at the bottom, where the path came out at the gate and the road began. The gate stood open on its bad hinge the way it had stood open since August. Beyond it the county road ran north in a straight line with no opinion in it, and the fence posts lost their posts and kept their gaps, and the last of the daylight went off the ploughed field in one long bar and then was gone.

The frost came up off the field in slow breaths that smelled of turned earth and iron. Percy walked with his hands in his coat pockets because his right hand was still faintly chalky, and because at the third lamp Annabeth had come alongside him and put her hand into his pocket from the outside, without looking at him, and had laced her fingers through his and left them there.

"You're warm," she said.

"I'm always warm. Ask anyone."

"I'm asking the hand."

"The hand's warm too. The hand's the warmest part of me." He squeezed her fingers, three times, which was a thing they did. "Are you all right?"

"I'm fine."

"You're not writing."

"I finished. Three pages and a drawing and a corner of a roof and a boy with a rule." She said it into the middle distance, in the tone of a person filing something. "It went in at the top of the pile, where she'll find it first."

"Clarisse's is at the bottom."

"Clarisse's is at the bottom because Clarisse writes to a hardware man, and hardware men do not care which layer they're in."

"That's the most accurate thing anybody's said today."

"It's five o'clock, Percy."

"It's felt longer."

The post came out of the dark before they were ready for it, the way it always did: not a box, a post, a bare upright in the middle of nowhere with a hook bolted to it and a board on it that had been painted by somebody in the 1950s who had taken the job seriously. The paint had gone silver and the letters had stayed. The hook was iron and had been rubbed bright on its underside by forty years of sacks being lifted off it at six in the morning.

Clarisse set the sack down on the flat stone at the base of the post and took her time about it. Then she lifted it, hooked the handle over the iron, and let it go, and the sack swung a quarter round and settled and hung there square to the road, patient, with the handles folded under themselves so that nobody could carry it off without a second thought.

"Forty-four," she said, to the post.

"Forty-four," Percy said.

"At six a man comes from the north in a truck and hooks it off with the stick and never gets out of the cab if he's got the arm for it, and it's in town before the kettle's on, and then it isn't ours. It's post." She dusted her hands on her coat, which was a gesture and not a need. "That's the whole of Thursday. That's every Thursday there is, and I have never once got used to it."

"You get used to it a bit," Annabeth said.

"You get used to the post. You don't get used to the handing over." Clarisse put her hands in her pockets and looked at the sack a moment longer, and then turned her back on it, which was the only way any of them ever did. "Right. Up the hill. Hall's at seven and the small ones were told half past six and they'll come at quarter to because they always do."

---

They went up the hill in the last of the dusk with the walk of people who had somewhere warmer to be, and Percy lit the fourth lamp and the seventh as he passed them, and the wicks took and went up amber and steady, and by the time the five of them came around the cedar brake the whole west path was burning end to end, nine of them, staggered left-odd and right-off, with the tenth on its hook by the Cabin Three door.

---

The hall took them in one at a time, and it took Silena the way it always took her, which was by subtraction: the passage cold gone, the boots off, the coat on the rail, and then the door, and then the noise.

The noise was the thing. Forty-odd people had come through the door since seven and the room was full of it, four walls of black tile throwing back every voice in the building in the same breath it was made, so that the whole hall sounded like one long argument with itself. The steam hung in the long curve of the ceiling in one unmoving sheet. The water at the inlet end was making its small slow hills, and the far end of the room was not visible from the door, which was the first thing every newcomer stopped to find out. The lamps were lit. The heater was going. The slate was on its nail by the door, where it had been on its nail since the first Tuesday of October, and somebody was standing in front of it reading.

"FIVE," Olive Nakamura read out, slowly and with enormous care, "and whoever wants—"

"And WHOEVER WANTS TO COME, COMES," said Clarisse, who was on the low bench by the door with her coat over her knees and the winter list on her knee and the count already starting in her head.

"I was going to say comes."

"You were going to pause in the middle."

"I don't pause in the middle, I read it."

"You read it like it's got a full stop after wants." Clarisse did not look up from the list. "Rule five hasn't got a full stop. It's got the rest of the sentence. Read it again and don't make it sound like a duty."

Olive read it again, and this time she did not pause, and it came out as one thing: and whoever wants to come, comes.

"Good," Clarisse said. "Go on. Read one to four."

"One: in and out the same door. Two: nobody swims alone, someone on the edge, awake. Three: the deep end is not a dare. Four: no glass anywhere near the tile." Olive turned round with the slate still in both hands. "Why is five the fun one?"

"Because one to four are fences and five is the gate," Silena said, coming past her with the towel basket on her hip. "Fences are boring. Gates are where people come through."

"That should be on the slate."

"It's on the slate. It's number five." Silena took the slate out of her hands and hung it back on its nail, square, where the whole room could read it. "Shoes. In the racks. If you've got a suit you've got a suit, and if you haven't, I've got four in the blue bag and a fifth planned."

"Planned for who?"

"Planned for whoever turns up without one. That's what planned means." Silena set the basket down and started on the towels, which was the thing she did first and the thing nobody saw her do: the rack by the heater, the big grey one with the repaired corner folded inside where nobody would catch a heel on it, the stack squared so that a wet nine-year-old could pull one off without bringing the lot down. Rule six was not on the slate. Rule six had never been on the slate, because Clarisse had written it, and Silena had said don't put it on the slate, and Clarisse had said fine and had not put it on the slate, and that was the entire arrangement.

The small ones came at a quarter to seven, which was when they always came, and there were six of them this time: Katie's four and two more who had heard about it in a way that did not involve anybody giving them permission. They came down the passage in a convoy with their towels over their shoulders like small capes, and they got in the way of the ladder immediately, because that was what the small ones were for.

The ladder came in at half past seven, carried by Charlie Beckendorf, who came through the door with it over one shoulder and a cloth bundle under his arm and his collar up, and who stopped two steps in and took the room in the order a person takes in a building: the water, the steam, the people, the slate, the door, the count.

"North corner," he said, to nobody in particular. "Drip's got worse since Tuesday. I'm not here to swim."

"You said that last week," Silena said.

"I said that last week and I didn't have a ladder last week." He set the ladder under the skylight and unrolled the cloth bundle on the tile beside it, and Silena saw what was in it: a tube of sealant, a small tin of solvent, three rags, a blade, and a length of cord. "I'm here to put this in, and then I'm going to get in, and then I'm going to put it right, and it'll hold till January, and then it'll be a January job and somebody else can have it."

"Clarisse says January already," Silena said. "She's written January on the list four times."

"Clarisse's got a gift for January." He got the tube out of the cloth and looked at it, and turned it in his hands, and then held it out to her without any particular ceremony at all, and Silena took it. "Hold that a minute. Under your arm, not in your hand. Cold stuff won't take on cold glass."

Silena put the tube under her arm, against her side, and held it there with her elbow, and went back to the towels, and the tube began to get warm.

That was the work, and it was enough to stand at a rail for ten minutes with a warm tube under your arm and a man up a ladder over your head, in a room where nobody could see your face from four feet away in that light.

The small ones, meanwhile, had found the depth marks.

It took them about a minute, because the marks were on the tile at the shallow end and because Olive Nakamura had heard a girl from the Athena cabin complain about them on the first Tuesday of October and had remembered, which was the whole of Olive in one sentence.

"They're nine inches apart," Olive said, standing on the bottom in water to her ribs with a tape measure in one hand. "Nine. You can't take a sounding with a ruler that thinks in shoes."

"She said that to you?" Marcus said, from the edge, with the survey rod under his arm.

"She said it to the wall. I heard it." Olive waded two steps and held the tape against the tile. "We're doing them again. Properly. Twelve inches and a proper mark and a notch, not a scratch."

"Who said you could?"

"Nobody," Olive said. "Rule five."

Clarisse, on the bench, made a sound that in any other room would have been a laugh. "Rule five doesn't cover tile."

"It covers the ladder," Olive said. "The ladder's ours. The tile's the tile." She put her hand flat on the wet tile, which had a depth scratched into it in a hand from fifty years ago. "This one says four. Is it four?"

Silena went down the two steps and stood in the water beside her with her sleeves pushed up and looked at the mark, and then at the tape in Olive's hand, and then at the far end of the room where the water went black.

"It's four," she said. "And this one's three, and the middle one's lying, because the middle one was done by a man in a hurry."

"Then we're doing the middle one."

"Then we're doing the middle one," Silena agreed, and sent Marcus for the chalk, and the depth-mark survey began, and it was the least useful piece of work done in the building all autumn and it was done beautifully.

---

At the inlet end, under the steam, with the whole room going on behind them, Percy and Annabeth were on the shallow-end steps with the water to their chests, holding hands.

They were not hiding. That was the first thing Silena noticed, standing at the rail with the warm tube under her arm. There was nothing hidden about them. They were simply in the part of the room where the steam was thickest and the noise came off the walls softer, and they had been in it for twenty minutes, and they had their foreheads together in the way people do when they are talking quietly in a loud room, and neither of them had moved for a while.

Then Annabeth lifted her face, and he bent his head, and they kissed in the steam, and the hall, which was a room that made everything a bigger sound than it was, did what it always did with them, and Silena stood at the rail with a warm tube under her arm and looked at the water and thought about counting.

She counted everything. It was the only kind of hoarding she had ever been able to afford. She had counted four frosts and sixteen eyelets and the number of times Beckendorf had come up the west path in October, and she knew exactly how many times she had been in this building with those two in it, and she knew that the answer was not the point.

"What are they doing?" said Katie's smallest, arriving at the rail with her towel over her head like a tent.

"Same as you," Silena said. "They came. It's on the slate."

"She's got her boots off."

"She's got her boots off because it's rule one, and rule one is in and out the same door, and nobody gets in with boots on except you." Silena put her hand on the top of the small one's hood and pushed it back. "Go and do the middle mark. It's lying."

Beckendorf came down the ladder at a quarter to eight with the tube in his hand, and Silena took it back from him, and he wiped his hands on the rag and looked at the corner and then at the water, and then, without any hurry at all, he sat down on the tile at the foot of the ladder with his back against the low wall and put his feet in the water at the edge of the shallow end.

"That's warm," he said.

"It's been warm since the first Tuesday."

"I know it's been warm. I'm telling you it's warm." He looked down the black length of the hall, at the steam, at the far inlet, at the Hermes boy arguing with Marcus about a rule that did not exist. "Six weeks I've had this room to myself. Heater, pipes, and the two of us not talking."

"Two of you not talking?"

"Me and the hall." He was quiet a second, with his feet in the warm water and the noise going past them both. "It's a different room with people in it. I've said this before. I'm saying it again because I've been up a ladder."

"You say it every time."

"I say it every time because it's true every time." He stood up, and rolled his sleeves down, and picked up the cloth bundle, and did not look at her. "Sunday. The eight o'clock one."

"Sunday," Silena said. "The eight o'clock one."

"I'll be at the forge till nine and then I'll be up the path, and I'll be going past the archery field at eight because that's the way the path goes, and if I see anybody I'll say good morning, and if I don't I won't." He said all of it to the corner of the room and none of it to her, and then he said, to the corner of the room, "That's the offer. It's the same offer. I'm only confirming the time."

"It's confirmed," Silena said, to the water, in a voice that came out level, and she was so grateful for it that she nearly laughed. "I'll be past the field."

"Right," Beckendorf said, and went to the door, and stopped with his hand on the frame the way people did in this family now, apparently, as though the frame were a place you stood to finish a sentence, and said, without turning round, "The sealant's in. It'll hold till January. Tell the girl with the tape measure she can stop measuring the wall and come and get in."

"I'll tell her."

"Good night, Silena."

"Good night, Charlie."

He went out into the cold, and the door shut behind him, and the room went on being a room with people in it.

At half past eight Clarisse got out of the water and stood on the edge and counted, out loud, deliberately, in her flat voice, so that the whole hall could hear it and so that nobody could pretend they had not: one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen.

"Seventeen," she said. "Nobody's gone anywhere." She pulled her jacket on over her suit, because she was not going to be seen in a suit without a jacket even in a building full of steam, and went to the slate, and turned it round, and wrote on it in her block capitals: THU 19 — 17. And then she took the slate off its nail and carried it out to the porch, because a grown-up knows who is in the water on any given evening, and that had been the first condition and it was going to keep being the first condition.

Silena carried the last dry towels to the inlet end of the hall. Two swimmers were still talking in the water, elbows hooked over the coping where the tiles were warm. They had been there longer than they realized.

"Five minutes," she called. "Then everybody out."

One looked up, startled, and checked the clock above the door.

"Already?"

"Already. Clarisse has the count, but the count doesn't mean everyone can stay indefinitely."

They laughed and pushed away from the edge. Silena watched until both reached the ladder. Wet feet on a tiled floor made her nervous, no matter how many times people promised to be careful. She held the towel basket steady while they climbed out and made sure neither stepped directly onto the puddle beside the drain.

Clarisse came back in after hanging the slate on the porch. "Seventeen going in," she said. "Seventeen accounted for. Anyone still in the water?"

"Not now."

"Good."

She crossed to the inlet and checked that the ladder was secure, then marked the maintenance note they'd left on the board. The repaired sealant needed to cure before anybody put weight against that section of the wall, and someone had been tempted to lean there already.

"No drills near this edge until it's cleared," she told Silena.

"I'll put up the cord."

Silena fetched it and tied a loose barrier between two hooks. She added a paper sign with the time of the next inspection, written big enough for the younger campers to read from the doorway.

By the time the final towels were collected, the room had quieted. Clarisse checked the floor for forgotten shoes. Silena found one blue sock under a bench, wrung it out, and pinned it where somebody would recognize it on Saturday. The evening had held seventeen people and ended with all seventeen out, dressed, and headed toward their cabins. That was worth recording on the slate without turning it into a ceremony.
