## Chapter 62: Snow Day

Percy Jackson woke to silence.

That was the first strange thing. Cabin Three was never silent in the morning. The fountain in the corner always murmured. The sea wind always rattled the fish-scale door. The pipes always knocked when visiting Tyson took a shower that was too hot and too long and used all the steam.

This morning there was none of that. There was a soft, pressed-down quiet, like the whole world had a blanket over its head.

The second strange thing was the light. It was white. Not morning-gold, not Atlantis blue-green. White, bright around the edges of the shutters, glowing through the salt spray on the window.

Percy sat up. His breath puffed.

"Okay, that's new," he said out loud.

He swung his legs out of bed and his feet hit a floor that was colder than usual. He hopped, yelped, and grabbed his socks from the end of his bunk. The fountain had a skin of ice around its rim. The shell wind chime Tyson had hung over the door did not move at all.

Percy crossed to the window and pushed the shutters open.

Snow.

Camp Half-Blood was white.

It lay thick over the paths, over the strawberry fields, over the roofs of the cabins. It heaped on the railing of the porch. It bent the branches of the pines down low. Big, slow flakes were still falling, drifting sideways in the sea wind, catching the early light and flashing. Half-Blood Hill rose in the distance with Thalia's pine at the top, dark green dusted white, the Golden Fleece a small spot of gold in all that pale.

Percy stared.

He remembered snow from mortal school in New York in his original life, when class got canceled and Sally let him stay in bed with hot chocolate. He had seen it in Atlantis once, in a glass dome the palace gardeners kept cold for surface plants, which Tyson had thought was the greatest miracle ever. He had never seen it at camp. Chiron had told him once that the barrier kept the worst weather off, and Mr. D was too lazy to allow winter theatrics.

"Whoa," Percy breathed. His breath fogged the air.

It had been a long November. School in Atlantis had made Percy better at reading currents, but it hadn't prepared him for how quiet Camp Half-Blood became once most of its summer campers went home. Clarisse and Silena had returned for their winter break only two days earlier, bringing extra coats and enough teasing to make Cabin Three sound occupied again. Grover had a few days of approved leave from his first Searcher route before reporting back to the Council. Thalia had come from Westover Hall for the same break.

Now all of them were here at once, and the snow had arrived before anyone could agree how to spend the morning.

Percy glanced at the weather notice Chiron had sent overnight, folded beside his bunk. The barrier was stable. The paths might not be. Chiron had asked campers to wear boots and report damage before treating the weather as a game. Percy had read the notice, then fallen asleep thinking about sleds. He wondered whether that counted as having followed instructions.

The door banged open.

"Percy! Are you seeing this? Are you awake?" Tyson filled the doorway, snow on his huge shoulders, snow in his eyebrows, grinning so wide Percy thought his face might crack. He wore two different scarves and a wool cap pulled low over one eye. "It is snow! Real snow! At camp! Lady Amphitrite says it happens once every many years when the currents turn cold and Lord Boreas sneezes!"

"Tyson!" Percy laughed. "When did you get here?"

"Portal! Early! For snow!" Tyson stomped his boots, scattering snow across the cabin floor. "I brought you this!"

He held out a lumpy bundle. A hat. Blue, hand-knit, slightly too long on one side, with a pom-pom the size of an apple.

"Mom made it," Tyson said proudly. "Well. She told a servant to make it and then she picked the yarn. That counts."

Percy pulled it on. It was warm and smelled faintly of salt and wool. "Tell her thanks. It's perfect."

Tyson beamed. "Come! Clarisse is already outside throwing things! Silena is making her wear gloves!"

As if on cue, a voice carried across the snow, sharp and delighted.

"JACKSON! If you're not out here in two minutes I'm coming in there!"

Percy grabbed his coat, his scarf, his gloves. His camp necklace clinked. He shoved his feet into boots without lacing them properly and followed Tyson out into the white.

---

The cold hit his cheeks like a splash.

Percy stopped on the porch of Cabin Three and just looked. The camp he knew in greens and blues and sun-bleached wood was gone. Everything had edges softened. The volleyball court net sagged under snow. The climbing wall looked like a wedding cake. The Big House porch had icicles hanging from the eaves, dripping in the sun. Footprints already crisscrossed everywhere, dark lines in the fresh fall, with goat-hoof prints mixed in.

Clarisse La Rue stood in the middle of the path in front of Cabin Three, in a black snow jacket with her spear slung across her back for no reason except Clarisse, her dark hair in a braid tucked under a red hat. Snow dusted her shoulders. She had a snowball in each hand.

"There he is," she said. "Sleeping Beauty of the Sea."

Silena Beauregard stood beside her, perfect even in a snowstorm, in a pale pink coat with a fur-trimmed hood, her dark hair loose and sparkling with flakes. She was trying to wrap a second scarf around Clarisse's neck while Clarisse pretended to hate it.

"You will get sick," Silena said firmly. "And then you will be miserable and make everyone miserable."

"I do not get sick," Clarisse said. "I get even."

"You got the flu in October and cried during the movie night," Silena said sweetly.

"I had allergies," Clarisse said. "And if you tell anyone I'll bury you in this drift."

Percy grinned. Clarisse, daughter of Poseidon, and Silena, daughter of Poseidon too in this world. His half-sisters.

"Morning," he said. "Nice weather."

Clarisse threw a snowball at his head. Percy ducked, laughing, and it exploded against the cabin wall.

"Hey! I just got up!"

"Then you should have gotten up faster," Clarisse said, already packing another. "Snow at camp, Jackson. Do you know how rare this is? Chiron says last time was before I was born. We are not wasting it sleeping."

Silena finished tying the scarf and patted Clarisse's cheek. "Good. Now you look almost civilized."

"I look dangerous," Clarisse corrected.

"You look warm," Silena said. "Which is better."

Tyson was already on his knees, scooping armfuls of snow, patting it into a lumpy mound. "I am making a snow Cyclops! It will have one big eye and be beautiful!"

Across the green, other cabins were waking up. Doors banged. Shouts went up.

And then, from the path to the white marble cabin on the hill, two figures appeared.

Percy knew them before he could see their faces. He would have known Annabeth's walk anywhere, quick and purposeful even in snow boots, blueprint tube gone for once, replaced by a thermos. And Thalia's longer stride beside her, hands in pockets, black hair with its silver streak bright against a gray hat.

Annabeth Grace. Daughter of Zeus in this history. Thirteen now, like Percy, and Thalia Grace's little sister.

She saw him and her whole face lit up.

"Percy!" She waved, then caught herself and tried to look dignified while half-running through snow that came over her boots. "Did you see? Did you— of course you saw. Look at it!"

Thalia grinned at Clarisse. "La Rue. Tell me you didn't already start a war without me."

"Waiting for audience," Clarisse said. "Wouldn't be fun if you missed getting beaten."

Thalia snorted. Snowflakes caught on her eyelashes. "Big talk from someone wearing a scarf her sister put on her."

"I chose this scarf," Clarisse said. "For tactical warmth."

Annabeth reached Percy's porch, breathless, cheeks pink, gray eyes bright. Snow sat on her blond ponytail. She wore a blue coat that matched her eyes, mittens with little lightning bolts stitched on them that Thalia had gotten her for her birthday, and Thalia's old striped scarf wrapped twice around her neck.

"Hi," she said.

"Hi," Percy said.

They stood there smiling like idiots for a second until Tyson tackled them both in a hug that nearly toppled them into a drift.

"Annabeth! You are here! It is snow day! Ella says cider will be ready soon!"

"Ella's here?" Annabeth asked, laughing and brushing snow off her coat.

"With Grover!" Tyson said. "At the Big House! They have a stand!"

Thalia clapped her gloved hands. "Then what are we standing around for? Cider, then war."

Clarisse pointed her snowball at Thalia. "You are going down, Pinecone Face."

"Bring it, Kelp Head's sister," Thalia said.

"Hey!" Percy said.

Silena linked arms with Annabeth. "Ignore them. They've been waiting for an excuse since September. How did your bridge calculations go?"

Annabeth brightened. "Chiron said the Hephaestus kids can start in March if the ground thaws right! I have to redo the load tables but—"

They headed toward the Big House together, boots crunching, breath puffing, snow still falling soft on their hats and shoulders, Tyson leading the way with his half-built snow Cyclops abandoned for now, Clarisse and Thalia already arguing about rules behind them.

Percy fell in beside Annabeth, close enough that their sleeves brushed.

"Snow day, Wise Girl," he said.

"Snow day, Seaweed Brain," she said, and nudged his shoulder with hers.

---

The Big House porch had been transformed.

Someone had dragged two long tables together under the eaves. A big steel pot steamed on a camp stove, smelling of apples and cinnamon and cloves. Paper cups were stacked in crooked towers. A hand-painted sign, letters wobbly but enthusiastic, read: GROVER + ELLA'S HOT CIDER — 1 DRACHMA OR GOOD JOKE.

Grover Underwood stood behind the table in a green coat three sizes too big, his crutches leaning against the railing, his new leg braces clicking on the wood. His curly hair stuck out from under a yellow beanie. He ladled cider with intense concentration, tongue sticking out.

Ella the harpy fluttered beside him, her red feathers puffed up against the cold, a striped scarf wrapped around her neck three times. She clutched a notebook to her chest with her wing-tips.

"Ella is in charge of jokes," Grover announced proudly as they approached. "She remembers every joke ever told at camp. Right, Ella?"

Ella nodded fast. "Joke number forty-three: Why did the satyr bring a ladder to the climbing wall? Because he heard the stakes were high. Joke number forty-four: Why doesn't Zeus play hide and seek? Because good luck hiding when—"

"Okay!" Grover said quickly, handing Percy a cup. "We charge jokes now because drachmas get soggy. What's your joke, Percy?"

Percy blinked. "Uh. What do you call a fish without eyes? A fsh."

Grover groaned. Ella scribbled furiously.

"Joke number two hundred and twelve: fsh. Fish without eyes. Fsh. Recorded," Ella said happily. "Cider approved."

Annabeth stepped up. "Why did the architect bring a pencil to battle?"

"Why?" Tyson asked, eyes wide.

"In case she had to draw blood," Annabeth said deadpan.

Silence. Then Clarisse barked a laugh. Thalia doubled over.

"Oh, that's terrible," Thalia wheezed. "That's awful. I love it. She's my sister, everyone. I taught her that."

"You did not," Annabeth said, but she was grinning, pink to the ears.

Ella wrote it down. "Joke number two hundred and thirteen. Architect. Draw blood. Approved. Approved."

The cider was hot and sweet and burned just right going down. Percy wrapped both hands around his cup and felt his fingers tingle back to life. Around them, the porch filled up. Connor and Travis Stoll tried to pay with the same drachma twice. Pollux from Dionysus paid with a very bad pun about grapes and got extra cinnamon. Austin and Kayla from Apollo debated whether cold changed pitch. Chiron clopped up in his wheelchair form with a blanket over his lap, accepted a cup from Grover with both hands, and smiled at the snow like an old friend.

Mr. D stood in the doorway in a bathrobe over his Hawaiian shirt, sunglasses on despite the overcast, staring at the snow like it had personally offended him.

"Who authorized this?" he demanded.

"Boreas, sir," Chiron said mildly.

"Well, tell Boreas I want a word. And tell him my tennis court is under three feet of—"

"Snow day?" Grover asked hopefully. "No classes? No training? Chiron said—"

Chiron raised a hand. The porch quieted. Snow ticked softly on the eaves.

"Once in a great while," Chiron said, his warm eyes moving over their upturned faces, "the camp is given a gift it did not earn and does not need to earn. Today there will be no lessons. No drills. No quests. Today we play."

A cheer went up so loud it shook icicles loose.

Mr. D sighed. "Fine. Snow day. But if anyone hits me with a snowball, I will turn you into a snowball and have Ella sell you for jokes."

"Noted!" Grover said.

Chiron continued, "Grover and Ella will keep cider through the afternoon. The dining pavilion will serve lunch indoors. And I believe—" he glanced at Clarisse and Thalia, who were already staring at each other "—some friendly competition has been proposed?"

Clarisse stepped forward. "Poseidon versus Zeus. Snowball fight. After cider. Big field by the cabins."

Thalia stepped forward. "Zeus accepts. And when we win, La Rue has to wear a pinecone crown to dinner."

"And when we win," Clarisse shot back, "Grace has to admit the sea is better than the sky in front of everyone."

"Deal," Thalia said.

They shook on it while Silena sighed and Annabeth rolled her eyes fondly.

"They do this every time," Annabeth murmured to Percy.

"I know," Percy said. "It's kind of their love language."

Annabeth snorted cider up her nose and coughed while Percy patted her back, laughing.

Chiron smiled. "Then let it be so. Friendly. No powers beyond what your bodies can do. No lightning. No tidal waves. Snow, hands, and wits. And snowmen after, judged at sunset. May the best cabin win."

Tyson raised his cup. "To snow!"

"To snow!" everyone echoed.

Percy clinked his paper cup against Annabeth's. Hot cider sloshed. Their eyes met over the steam.

"Ready to lose, Wise Girl?" he said softly.

"Ready to eat my snow-dust, Seaweed Brain?" she said.

Behind them, Ella whispered to her notebook, "Snow-dust. Eat my snow-dust. Joke number two hundred and fourteen. Possibly insult. Possibly flirting. Record for study."

Grover patted her wing. "A little of both, Ella. A little of both."

---

The first real problem happened before the snowball fight.

Milo came running from the cabin rows with a coil of rope over one shoulder and a wool hat that had slipped down over both eyebrows. He stopped on the Big House steps, caught his breath and looked around for Chiron.

"Cabin Ten's shutter is holding," he said. "But the roof above the storage room is collecting too much snow. Somebody needs to check it before the drifts get heavier."

Percy put down his cider. He remembered the shutter. He'd helped repair it in September, before he learned how quickly a small crack could become a winter problem.

"Didn't we fix that?"

"The shutter," Milo said. "Not the roof. They're different parts of the building."

"Right."

Chiron asked where the weight was concentrated, then summoned the older Hephaestus campers who were qualified to inspect the structure. The younger children were told to stay away from the wall below the drift until the adults had confirmed it was safe.

Clarisse was already reaching for a shovel.

"Tell me where to dig," she said.

"Not the roof," Milo answered. "You'd damage the shingles. We can clear the path and the low eaves, but we need a professional inspection before anybody climbs."

Percy looked at the snow spreading across the camp. He wanted to be helpful. For a moment he'd imagined lifting a great wave of water and pushing the snow away. Then he pictured icy water running into the storage room and freezing against the foundation.

"Water would make it worse, wouldn't it?"

"Much worse," Milo said, with feeling.

Annabeth came forward and studied the roof line from the ground.

"The snow's drifting against the taller building," she said. "Wind comes across the open green, hits the wall and drops its load on the lower section."

"So the problem isn't just how much falls," Percy said. "It's where it ends up."

"Exactly."

Chiron approved a small work party to clear the walkway and move movable supplies away from the affected wall, while Hephaestus staff made the roof safe. It didn't cancel snow day. It delayed a few games, which the Stolls described as an appalling injustice until Clarisse handed them shovels.

Percy joined the work party, boots crunching in fresh snow. Milo showed him where to pile cleared snow so it wouldn't block drains or create another drift. Grover helped guide a pair of younger satyrs carrying boxes of cabin decorations away from the storage room. The boxes weren't particularly valuable, but they belonged to campers who expected to find them next spring.

A yellow box split open on the path. Out spilled a dozen painted wooden stars and a paper chain whose glue had come apart.

"That's Katie's," one of the satyrs said miserably.

Percy gathered the stars while Annabeth retrieved the chain before the wind caught it. The decorations were wet, but they hadn't been destroyed. They carried everything to a dry table beneath the Big House porch.

Tyson tried to help with the largest shovel. Its handle snapped on the first scoop.

He looked so ashamed that Percy nearly laughed, then thought better of it.

"It's a small shovel," Percy said. "You have large hands."

"I should have remembered."

"Here." Milo gave Tyson a broad plastic snow scoop and showed him how to push rather than lift the heavy drift. "This one will hold."

Tyson tried it. The scoop glided across the path, carrying a wide sheet of snow without breaking.

"Better," he said, delighted.

By the time the path was clear, the Hephaestus staff had inspected the roof and started a careful removal of the concentrated drift using suitable equipment. Chiron kept the storage room closed until they were finished. There was no heroic rescue, no cracking beams and no roof falling down. That was the intended result.

Percy was glad.

As they walked back toward the porch, Annabeth pointed to a small heap of snow he'd left beside the drain.

"That needs moving."

"It's only a little."

"When it melts, it'll flow into the path and freeze overnight."

Percy looked at the drain. He'd been so pleased to finish shoveling that he hadn't thought about what happened afterward.

He moved the heap.

Milo thanked him, then promised to put the roof on the summer inspection list. Percy remembered the conversation they'd had in September about why small problems got ignored. The answer hadn't changed. People were busy, and the consequences arrived later.

Chiron emerged from the Big House once the storage room was safe.

"Now," he said, "the snowball competition may proceed."

A cheer went up. Percy felt the kind of satisfaction he usually associated with winning a fight. Only this time the victory was an undamaged building and a pile of ornaments drying near the stove.

"Not much of a quest," Clarisse said as they reached the playing field.

"Best kind," Milo replied.

Annabeth smiled at him, then looked down at Percy's gloves.

"Your fingers are wet."

"I was shoveling."

"Then change them before throwing snowballs. Frozen fingers make terrible decisions."

Percy started to argue that he could manage the cold. He remembered the swollen wood on Cabin Ten's shutter and went back to his bunk for dry gloves instead.

---

The field between Cabin Three and Cabin One had never looked like a battlefield before.

It was perfect for it. Flat, wide, white, with low drifts for cover. Hay bales and overturned canoes made bunkers. A center line had been stomped into the snow.

On the Poseidon side: Clarisse in front, already building an arsenal, barking orders like a general. Silena beside her, calmly packing perfect spheres and stacking them in neat pyramids. Percy, snow in his blue pom-pom hat, sleeves pushed up. Tyson, who counted as artillery all by himself, humming while he rolled a ball as big as a pumpkin.

On the Zeus side: Thalia in front, grinning. Annabeth beside her, already surveying angles, pointing out sight lines. Two Hermes campers who had joined the Zeus team for balanced numbers stood behind them, armed with snowballs.

A crowd gathered along the sidelines with cider cups. Grover and Ella dragged their pot to a bench. Chiron watched from the Big House porch with binoculars.

The two Hermes campers did not look convinced they belonged on the Zeus team.

One was a girl named Fern who could throw accurately only when she was standing still. The other, a younger boy called Micah, was small enough that the biggest snowbanks hid him entirely. Neither had expected to spend the day being described as honorary thunderbolts by Thalia Grace.

"Can we pick a different name?" Fern asked.

"What's wrong with thunderbolts?" Thalia said.

"You're a child of Zeus. We're not. I don't want the Ares kids teasing us for a month because we yelled a Zeus cheer."

Thalia opened her mouth to say something dismissive. Annabeth stepped in.

"Then don't yell it," she said. "We're a team for this game, not a new cabin. Call us the Hill team."

Percy watched his girlfriend draw a line on a scrap of paper and show the changes to Thalia. She had insisted on roughly even numbers, which made sense, but hadn't thought about how children from other cabins would feel when asked to fight under somebody else's identity.

Thalia considered the paper.

"Fine. Hill team."

Fern looked relieved. Micah looked disappointed; he had apparently liked the thunderbolt part. Percy almost laughed.

Clarisse came over holding a snowball the size of a grapefruit.

"Connor wants to know whether Tyson counts as one player."

"Of course he does," Percy replied.

"He's three times as tall as Micah."

"That's not his fault."

"No," Clarisse said. "But if he throws that thing at a kid's face, it'll be everybody's problem."

Tyson stood a few yards away rolling snow into an enormous ball. When Percy called his name, he stopped and looked up, worried.

"Am I in trouble?"

"No," Percy said. "We need to change what counts as a snowball."

Tyson looked down at his masterpiece. It was nearly the size of a barrel.

"Not this one?"

"Maybe we can save it for the snowman contest."

Tyson thought about that. "Then it will be art."

"Excellent idea," Silena said, arriving with a basket of spare gloves. "Art doesn't need to hit anybody."

Connor called both teams to the center line to settle the rules. He'd been trying to conduct the game like an Olympic ceremony, but the arguments kept interrupting him. Nobody minded except Connor.

"All throws must be soft, packed from fresh snow," he announced. "No ice, no giant projectiles, no throwing above the shoulders. Anyone who trips or says stop gets a pause. And nobody gets to pretend an accidental hit means they can keep throwing at someone who is down."

Clarisse rolled her eyes. "It's a snowball fight, not a treaty."

"Treaties are good," Annabeth answered. "They prevent later wars."

"Not always."

"No," Annabeth admitted. "But rules that everyone heard are better than rules somebody makes up after losing."

Thalia snorted and offered Clarisse a handshake across the line.

"Agreed?"

Clarisse studied her hand. "You aren't going to shock me?"

"No powers. Your own rule."

"Fine."

They shook. Connor made a great show of writing the agreement in Ella's notebook, then asked whether anybody wanted to sign as a witness. Half the campers raised their hands. The other half started throwing practice snowballs at the ground.

Milo, still wearing the hat that covered his eyebrows, arrived from the roof inspection and asked whether he could play. He'd spent much of the morning clearing snow while everyone else warmed up with cider. The Hill team already had too many players, so he joined Percy and Clarisse.

"Now it's not even," Fern complained.

"Two people from the sidelines can switch," Silena suggested. "Or Milo can help judge instead."

Milo looked offended. "I didn't fix the roof to spend the afternoon holding a clipboard."

Chiron, watching from the porch, called that the repair crew had earned the first choice of activity. The teams reorganized until the numbers were as close as possible. It took longer than the actual game would take, and Percy thought the arguing might be the point.

Annabeth walked over to Percy while Connor checked the new roster.

"Are you annoyed that I changed your team?"

"No. I like having Milo with us."

"He's a good thrower."

"He has a plan for where to pile the snow after we finish."

She gave him a look. "That's actually important."

"I know."

"You're turning into a person who thinks about what happens after a game."

"Terrifying, isn't it?"

She smiled. "A little."

The teams took their places. Micah was given a lower bunker he could see over, and Tyson set his enormous snowball aside for later sculpture work. Clarisse inspected every pile for pieces of ice. The game was still likely to be loud, messy and unfair in ways they hadn't anticipated. That didn't make the preparations pointless.

When Connor finally climbed onto the hay bale with his flag, all the children were shivering from standing around.

"Can we play now?" he shouted.

"Please!" both teams answered.

Connor Stoll stood on a hay bale with a white flag. "Rules!" he shouted. "No powers! No ice! No wind gusts! No shaking the ground! Snowballs only! If you get hit three times you're out and you go sit with Grover and tell Ella a joke! Last team standing wins! Or most people left after twenty minutes! Ready?"

Clarisse crouched. "Poseidon! What do we do?"

"Make waves!" Percy, Silena, and Tyson shouted back. It was their cabin cheer, old and corny and perfect.

Thalia cracked her knuckles. "Zeus! What do we do?"

"Strike first!" Annabeth and the others shouted.

Connor dropped the flag. "Go!"

The air filled with white.

Percy had been in real fights. He had faced monsters with teeth and gods with tempers. Nothing had prepared him for Clarisse La Rue with unlimited snow and a grudge.

She threw like a spearwoman, hard and straight. Silena threw less often but never missed. Tyson threw giant lobs that exploded like mortars, shouting, "Sorry! Sorry! Are you okay?"

On the other side, Thalia dodged and threw on the run. Annabeth waited, watched, then hit exactly what she aimed at. Percy took one to the chest in the first minute and laughed too hard to retaliate because Annabeth did a tiny victory dance behind her bunker.

"You call that dodging?" she called.

"You call that a dance?" he called back.

"Jealous of my moves, Jackson?"

"Terrified of them, Grace's sister!"

A snowball whizzed past his ear. Thalia's work. She pointed at him. "Less flirting! More fighting!"

Clarisse appeared beside him, breathing hard, snow down her collar. "She's right. Focus. Annabeth's picking us off. Silena, suppressing fire. Tyson, big one on my mark. Percy, flank right with me."

Percy grinned. This was Clarisse at her best — not bullying, not blustering, leading. He nodded. "On your mark."

They counted. They rose. They charged.

What followed entered camp legend.

Clarisse and Percy swept wide right while Silena and Tyson covered from the center. Thalia met Clarisse head-on in the middle, trading throws until both were powdered white. Tyson's soft, loosely packed throw burst into a white cloud near the borrowed Hermes campers, covering their boots to huge cheers.

Annabeth saw Percy coming and her eyes narrowed. She had three snowballs ready. He had two.

"Hi," he said, ten feet away.

"Hi," she said, and threw.

He dodged left. Snow burst on his shoulder. One hit.

He threw. She ducked. Miss.

She threw again. He twisted. It grazed his coat. Two hits.

"Uh oh, Seaweed Brain," Annabeth said, grinning, winding up. "One more and you're telling Ella a joke."

Percy grinned back. Snow in his hair. Breath puffing. Heart hammering louder than it should have for a game.

He faked high and threw low. It caught her boot as she jumped. She slipped, laughing, and her final throw went wild over his head.

"Missed me," he sang.

"Come here and say that," she said.

They both ran out of ammo at the same time, dove for the same drift, and ended up shoulder to shoulder in the snow, packing frantically, gloves brushing, laughing so hard they could barely form balls.

"Truce?" Percy gasped.

"Never," Annabeth said, and shoved a handful of loose snow at his face.

He caught her wrist. She caught his sleeve. They collapsed sideways into the drift, laughing, snow in their hoods, in their scarves, everywhere.

From across the field, Thalia shouted, "Annabeth! Stop cuddling the enemy and fight!"

From the other side, Clarisse shouted, "Jackson! Stop flirting and throw!"

Percy and Annabeth looked at each other, faces inches apart, both flushed and breathless.

"On three?" Annabeth whispered.

"On three," Percy whispered.

They sat up together and pelted their sisters simultaneously. Both sisters screeched.

The battle raged for the full twenty minutes. Alliances shifted. The Stolls defected to both sides for snacks. Tyson got hit three times and marched proudly to the cider bench to tell Ella a joke about Cyclopes that took five minutes and made her write three pages. Silena took out both Zeus kids with two perfect throws and then got taken out by Thalia in a diving slide that drew applause.

In the end, it was Clarisse and Percy versus Thalia and Annabeth, all four of them snowy and panting and down to fists full of slush.

Connor checked his watch. "Time! Count hits!"

It was a tie. Three hits each. Everyone claimed victory. Everyone claimed cheating. Thalia had snow down her back from Clarisse shoving a handful under her collar in the final second. Clarisse had a snowball splat square on her hat from Thalia.

"It was a draw," Silena declared, returning from the sidelines with fresh cider for all four. "Which means both crowns and both speeches are canceled."

"Rematch after lunch," Thalia said, clinking cups with Clarisse.

"You're on," Clarisse said, clinking back.

Percy and Annabeth sat in the snow, too tired to get up, shoulders touching, sharing Annabeth's cider when Percy's ran out.

"Good throw," Percy said. "The boot one. Cheap, but good."

"Good dodge," Annabeth said. "The twisty one. Show-off, but good."

Their gloves were soaked. Their noses were red. Neither moved.

Ella waddled over with her notebook. "Observation: Percy Jackson was hit twice by Annabeth Grace and smiled both times. Annabeth Grace was hit once by Percy Jackson and laughed. Conclusion: snowball fight also flirting. Addendum: very obvious flirting."

Grover, handing out refills behind her, said, "Ella! You can't just—" He looked at their faces. "Okay, that's accurate. But you can't just say it."

Annabeth buried her face in her scarf. Percy laughed until his stomach hurt.

---

Lunch was indoors in the dining pavilion, boots steaming near the braziers.

Chiron announced the afternoon event between bites of grilled cheese.

"Snowman contest," he said. "In cabin teams. Judged at sunset on creativity, craft, and camp spirit. The prize is the Golden Ladle."

Everyone wanted the Golden Ladle.

Poseidon cabin claimed the spot by the lake. Zeus cabin claimed the hill by the pine.

Percy had expected the snowman contest to be silly. He had not expected Annabeth to get the architect look, mitten tapping her chin.

"We need structural integrity," she told Thalia. "Base wide. Middle compacted. And theme. What says us without being obvious?"

Thalia, who had been about to roll a giant ball and call it done, paused. "A Pegasus?"

"Too hard to carve feathers in this snow," Annabeth said. "It's too powdery. We need simple shapes, strong silhouette."

"What about—" Thalia glanced down toward the lake, where Poseidon cabin worked. "What about beating Poseidon at symbolism?"

Annabeth followed her gaze.

Down by the lake, Clarisse was directing Tyson like a crane, Silena was smoothing curves with bare hands despite the cold, and Percy—

Percy was building something small and careful off to the side while the big snowman rose behind him. He kept glancing up the hill toward Annabeth.

Annabeth looked away quickly, ears pink.

"Okay," she said. "I have an idea. But I need your help, and you can't laugh."

Thalia raised a hand. "I promise nothing, but go on."

Down by the lake, a similar conversation unfolded.

"We're making Dad, obviously," Clarisse said, packing the base. "Trident, beard, muscles. Classic."

Silena nodded, sculpting a wave-like base. "Classic works. But let's make him kind. Not scary council Dad. Summer Dad. The one who laughs."

Tyson added snow to the shoulders. "And big! So everyone knows Poseidon is strong and good!"

Percy nodded along, but his hands kept shaping a smaller lump beside the big base. He rolled, patted, smoothed. He borrowed Silena's hair ribbon for a minute to carve lines, then gave it back.

Clarisse noticed. "What's that?"

"Nothing," Percy said fast. "Just— extra snow. Practicing."

Silena smiled knowingly. "Practicing what?"

"Beaks," Percy muttered.

Clarisse and Silena exchanged a look.

"An owl?" Silena asked softly.

Percy shrugged, ears red. "Annabeth likes owls. Gray ones. For wisdom and stuff. I thought— for the contest it's all Poseidon, but— I could make a little one for her. On the side. Not for judging."

Clarisse stared at him for a long moment. Then she shoved his shoulder, not hard.

"You're such a sap, Jackson," she said, but her voice was fond. "Make the beak sharper. Owls look mad. She'll like mad."

Silena handed him a pair of smooth black pebbles from her pocket. "For eyes. I brought them for buttons, but owls need them more."

Percy took them like they were precious. "Thanks."

"Family helps family be sappy," Silena said primly, and went back to smoothing.

Up on the hill, Annabeth shaped a long form while Thalia packed the main body.

"Is that a trident?" Thalia asked.

"It's miniature," Annabeth said. "Sea and sky together. Camp spirit."

Annabeth bumped her with her hip. "Help me with the prongs. They keep snapping."

Thalia knelt to help her little sister shape three careful prongs.

"You know," Thalia said quietly, "Mom would have liked this. Just building dumb stuff."

Annabeth paused. Their mother — mortal, still far from camp — rarely came up outside discussions of her letters. Thalia had raised Annabeth through the worst of Beryl's instability. Annabeth knew those years through what her sister told her, and the fond days were sometimes hardest to ask about.

"You told me she liked watching us sled," Annabeth said softly. "Was that the blue sled in your photo?"

"I remember you steered us into a bush and blamed me," Thalia said.

"That sounds like your steering," Annabeth said. "I bet I navigated correctly and you ignored me."

They laughed, breath puffing together.

"We still have time before judging," Thalia said. "After this. Want to— take the hill? Just us?"

Annabeth's face lit up. "Yes. Show me how we used to do it?"

"Like before," Thalia said. "I'll even let you steer."

"You won't regret it," Annabeth said.

"I already regret it," Thalia said, and Annabeth shoved snow down her collar.

---

Grover vanished during lunch.

Percy wasn't especially worried at first. There were plenty of reasons a satyr might disappear from a warm dining pavilion, particularly one serving roasted vegetables and cheese sandwiches. But Juniper came to the table with snow on her hair and asked whether anyone had seen him, and the way she asked made Percy put down his food.

"He's by the north fence," she said when she caught her breath. "I told him I'd check the serviceberry after the snowfall, but he went ahead."

Percy remembered the little tree they'd planted for Grover's uncle before the Searcher route began. It was scarcely more than a stick now, its leaves gone for winter. He'd seen it last week from the path but hadn't inspected the ties or the trunk.

"Is it damaged?"

"I don't know. I saw his footprints near the fence and couldn't find him when I called."

Thalia stood. "We'll help look."

"Not all of us," Annabeth said. "If Grover just went to check a tree, arriving with fifteen people will make him think he's done something terrible."

She was right. Percy took his coat and followed Juniper, while the others promised to watch for Grover if he came back to the pavilion.

They found him kneeling beside the serviceberry with his back to the path. Snow had piled around the little protective fence. A thin branch stuck out at an odd angle, and the canvas wrap had come loose where the wind had caught it.

Grover was holding the torn wrap in his lap.

"Hey," Percy said.

Grover turned. His expression went through surprise, irritation and finally embarrassment.

"Juniper said she'd check it. I know."

"So why are you out here?"

"I wanted to see it myself."

Juniper crouched beside the tree. She didn't scold him for coming. She examined the bark where the branch had bent and told him which part was still living.

"It's not broken through the trunk," she said. "The wrap came loose, and the branch took the weight. We can prune the damaged bit later, after checking where it split."

Grover looked unconvinced.

"I wasn't here when the snow started."

"You were doing your Searcher training," Juniper reminded him. "You can't be in two places."

"I know."

Percy watched him pick snow from the base of the little sapling. Ferdinand was still in the recovery team's care, his case open. Grover's route had taken him through forests far from camp, and he'd been writing reports about the conditions there. Percy wondered whether the tree had become a place where he could imagine doing something for his uncle when he couldn't visit the recovery center.

"Did the Council send news?" Percy asked.

"Not yet. I sent the last report when I reached camp. They said they'd review it after the break."

"You did the work."

"Yes."

"Then you don't have to prove you're still doing the work by standing out here freezing."

Grover shot him a glare.

Percy sighed. "All right. That sounded like Silena."

"More like Chiron."

"Worse."

Juniper smiled faintly. She fetched a new length of tree wrap from the groundskeeper's supply box and asked Grover to hold the stake while she secured it. Percy brushed away the heavy snow near the protective fence. They didn't shake the branches violently or pull at the frozen bark. Juniper insisted they leave the snow close to the roots where it insulated the ground, moving only the weight pressing against the young stem.

The work took twenty minutes. At the end the tree looked much as it had before, except for the damaged branch and the fresh wrap.

Grover inspected it closely.

"Will it grow back?"

"The tree can grow around this," Juniper said. "We don't know exactly what the top will look like next summer, but the roots are healthy."

He nodded, finally looking relieved.

"Then we should put the pruning on the spring list," Percy said.

"Already done," Juniper answered, holding up a small card.

Percy laughed. He knew now why Annabeth liked Juniper. Neither trusted a promise until someone had written down who was responsible for carrying it out.

Grover stood and brushed the snow from his trousers.

"Ferdinand would say I worried too much."

"What would you say?" Percy asked.

"I'd say he worried about me when I was small. Seems fair that I should get to worry about him now."

Percy didn't tell him to stop. He had heard that sort of worry in Clarisse's voice and in Thalia's when she asked after Jason. It didn't disappear when somebody supplied a tidy explanation.

Juniper brushed a little snow from Grover's sleeve.

"Come back inside," she said. "Your cider's probably cold."

"Cider can be reheated."

"Your fingers can't, if you stand out here forever."

Grover took the hint.

On their way past the north irrigation ditch, Percy stopped to inspect the board they'd installed before the quest. The channel was shallow beneath its rim of ice, but the board remained firmly in place. The water was moving where it should. It looked ordinary. Percy found himself strangely proud of it.

Grover saw him looking.

"That one's still holding," he said.

"Yeah."

"I guess not everything needs fixing every time it snows."

Percy thought of the shutter, the roof and the serviceberry.

"Only the parts that do."

Grover rolled his eyes. "You're turning into a camp maintenance pamphlet."

"Better than a prophecy."

They went back to the pavilion. Juniper asked the cooks for fresh hot cider, and Grover sat down with the rest of them without pretending that his fingers weren't numb. Percy looked toward the snowman teams gathering outside and wondered what Clarisse had managed to build while he'd been gone.

He hoped it was something ridiculous.

---

Judging at sunset drew the whole camp to the parade ground.

The snow had stopped. The sky cleared to pale gold and pink.

Poseidon's snowman stood eight feet tall, broad and smiling, with seaweed hair and a wave base, and a tall trident of driftwood and ice. At its feet sat a small, fierce snow-owl with black pebble eyes.

Zeus's snowman stood tall, a storm cloud with lightning bolts, and at its top, held high, a small snow-trident.

Chiron walked the line slowly, leaning on his blanket, Ella fluttering beside him taking notes, Grover carrying the Golden Ladle on a pillow.

"Creativity," Chiron murmured. "Craft. Camp spirit."

He stopped before Poseidon. "Tell me about the owl."

Percy stepped forward, snow still on his boots, blue hat in his hands. "It's— for Annabeth. She's— owls are her thing. Wisdom. Gray. I thought our snowman should have a friend who watches. Like she watches out for everyone."

Silena squeezed his arm. Clarisse stood straight, daring anyone to laugh.

Chiron smiled. "And the trident?"

Annabeth stepped forward from the Zeus group, Thalia's hand on her shoulder. "It's for Percy. For Poseidon. Because storms need sea, and sky needs— someone to rain on. Camp spirit. Together."

Thalia nodded. "She designed it. I just held the prongs."

A murmur went around. Ella wrote so fast her notebook tore.

Chiron looked from one small snow gift to the other for a long moment. His eyes were bright.

"The prize," he said, lifting the Golden Ladle, "goes to— both. Poseidon and Zeus, together. For reminding us what camp spirit actually is."

Cheers erupted. Clarisse and Thalia shook hands with exaggerated dignity, then ruined it by trying to push each other into a drift. Tyson hugged the Poseidon snowman and nearly knocked its head off. Silena fixed it with gentle hands.

Percy looked across the row at Annabeth. She looked back.

They met in the middle, between owl and trident, while everyone celebrated around them.

"You made me an owl," Annabeth said.

"You made me a trident," Percy said.

"I used all my structural knowledge," Annabeth said. "The prongs kept breaking. Thalia says I swore like a satyr."

"I used Silena's buttons for eyes," Percy said. "Clarisse says the beak needs to look madder or it's not you."

Annabeth laughed, then went quiet, looking at the little owl. "It's perfect. Stern. Like me when I'm thinking."

"It's cute," Percy said. "Like you when you're thinking."

Annabeth glanced up. Snowflakes still clung to her scarf. Sunset turned her gray eyes gold at the edges.

"Percy—"

"Yeah?"

"Nothing," she said. "Just— thanks."

He nudged her mitten with his. "Thanks for my trident. It's way better than my owl. Don't tell Clarisse I said that."

"I won't," Annabeth said. "Don't tell Thalia your owl is better than my trident."

"Deal," Percy said.

Their mitten-hands lingered, wool catching on wool. Neither pulled away.

---

Thalia found them like that a minute later and pretended not to see, which for Thalia meant loudly announcing, "I see nothing! I am blinded by camp spirit!"

"Thalia!" Annabeth said, snatching her hand back, face flaming.

Percy shoved his hands in his pockets to hide his grin.

Thalia slung an arm around Annabeth's shoulders. "Come on, little owl. You promised me a sled run before dark. The hill is perfect. Chiron said we have twenty minutes of light."

Annabeth hesitated, glancing at Percy. "Come with us?"

Thalia raised an eyebrow at Percy. "Kelp Boy can come if he carries the sled."

"I can carry the sled," Percy said immediately.

Clarisse tossed them a battered blue plastic sled from behind the cabins. "Bring it back! And if you break your necks, do it where I can see!"

Silena added a second sled, red and newer. "For safety. And so you don't fight."

"Who says we'd fight?" Thalia asked.

"Everyone who's met you," Silena said.

The path to Half-Blood Hill was steep and hushed. The pine stood dark against the sunset, the Fleece glowing warm gold. Below, the whole camp spread white, lights coming on one by one.

Thalia and Annabeth stood at the top with the blue sled, Percy with the red, all three breathing hard from the climb.

"Okay," Thalia said. "Rules from when we were kids. Annabeth steers—"

"I steer," Annabeth confirmed.

"—I brake with my boots and scream directions—"

"You scream," Annabeth agreed.

"—and we try not to hit the pine."

"We never hit the pine," Annabeth said.

"We hit the bush by the pine," Thalia said.

"That bush jumped out," Annabeth said.

Percy laughed, settling into his own sled. "Can I just watch first? I want to see the experts."

Thalia sat behind Annabeth, long legs tucked around her sister, arms around her waist. Annabeth gripped the front rope, eyes bright, hair flying out from under her hat.

"Ready?" Thalia asked.

"Ready," Annabeth said.

"Push!"

Percy shoved them off. The blue sled shot down, spraying snow. Thalia whooped. Annabeth laughed, steering wildly, flying straight for the bottom while sunset lit their trail gold.

They coasted to a stop near the barrier, tumbling off, laughing, arms around each other.

Percy sledded down next, spinning halfway, ending on his back with snow up his sleeves.

He trudged back up to find Thalia making a snow angel and Annabeth sitting on the sled.

"One more?" Thalia asked. "All three? Blue sled?"

"That sled holds two," Annabeth said.

"It holds two and a half," Thalia said, eyeing Percy. "He's small. For a Poseidon."

"Hey," Percy said.

Annabeth patted the front. "Come on. I'll steer. You brake."

Percy squeezed in front, Annabeth middle, Thalia back, all elbows and knees and laughter. The sled groaned.

"This is a terrible idea," Annabeth said happily.

"The best ones are," Thalia said. "Push!"

They pushed with their hands and shot down.

It was faster with three. Wind tore at Percy's hat. Snow stung his cheeks. Annabeth's arms locked around his waist, her chin on his shoulder as she steered. Thalia's boots dragged, spraying white, her laugh loud in his ears.

"Left! Left!" Annabeth shouted.

"That's right!" Thalia shouted.

"That's left!" Percy shouted.

They missed the bush by inches, hit a bump, went briefly airborne, and landed in a tangle at the bottom, sled overturned.

They lay there as the first stars came out, breathing hard.

"Best run ever," Thalia declared.

"Because of my steering," Annabeth said.

"Because of my braking," Percy said.

"Because of my screaming," Thalia said.

They laughed. Thalia stood, hauling Annabeth up, then Percy. She brushed snow off Annabeth's back, straightened her hat, retied her scarf.

"You good?" Thalia asked softly, just for her.

Annabeth nodded, eyes shining. "Yeah. Really good."

Thalia squeezed her shoulder, then looked at Percy. "Take care of her on the walk back, Kelp Boy. I have to go gloat at Clarisse before dinner."

"Will do," Percy said.

Thalia jogged off, boots crunching, shouting, "LA RUE! I hear you lost a snowball fight today!"

Clarisse's distant reply: "COME HERE AND SAY THAT, PINECONE!"

Annabeth and Percy looked at each other.

"Walk?" Percy asked.

"Walk," Annabeth said.

---

The camp settled into blue dusk as they walked down from the hill side by side, dragging both sleds behind them.

Lights glowed in every cabin. The dining pavilion smelled of stew. Someone played a lyre badly in Apollo cabin. Laughter drifted from the Hermes cabin. The snow creaked under their boots. Their breath puffed and mingled.

They stopped by the lake, where their snowmen stood guard in the gathering dark. The Poseidon snowman loomed kind and big. The little owl sat stern at its feet. The Zeus snowman across the way held its tiny trident high.

Annabeth knelt to check her owl. One wing had chipped in the wind. She smoothed it with her mitten, gentle and precise.

Percy knelt beside her, close enough that their shoulders pressed. He reached out to fix the beak, his glove brushing hers.

They both stopped.

The snow around them was untouched except for their knees. The lake lay frozen at the edges, ice lacing the reeds. Stars reflected faintly. The camp felt far away and close at once.

"Annabeth," Percy said.

"Yeah?"

"Today was—" He fumbled. Words were never his strong point, especially with her looking at him like that, gray eyes dark in the dusk. "I liked fighting you. And building for you. And sledding. All of it."

She smiled. "Me too. Even when you hit my boot. That was rude."

"Strategic," he corrected.

"Rude," she said, but she leaned a little closer.

Their mittens rested side by side on the owl's back. Wool on wool. Percy turned his hand over. Annabeth turned hers. Their fingers hooked, clumsy through the thick knit, mitten-hands holding tight.

Neither said anything about it. They just stayed there, kneeling in the snow, holding mitten-hands on top of a snow-owl, while their breath fogged and the stars came out.

"Your hands are warm," Annabeth said quietly. "Even through mittens."

"Yours are cold," Percy said. "Even through mittens."

"Your observation skills astound me," she said.

"Thanks," he said. "I practice."

She laughed softly. He loved that sound more than cider, more than victory, more than snow days.

"Percy?" she said after a moment.

"Yeah?"

"When it's summer again, and everyone comes back, and it's loud and busy— will you still—" She stopped, frowning at the owl like it had the words. "Never mind. Dumb."

"Still what?" he asked.

"Still sit with me. Like this. Even when it's not quiet."

Percy tightened his mitten-hold. "Every day. Homework at four. You know the rule."

She looked up, relieved and bright. "Yeah. The rule without being a rule."

"Exactly," he said.

They stood together, hands still linked, and walked to check the little snow-trident on the Zeus snowman. It leaned slightly. Percy straightened it while Annabeth packed fresh snow around its base.

"There," she said. "Sea and sky."

"Together," Percy said.

Ella's voice floated from the path, where she and Grover pushed their empty cider pot back to the Big House. "Observation number eighty-two: Percy Jackson and Annabeth Grace holding mitten-hands in snow. Conclusion: warm hands. Warm hearts. Addendum: Ella approves."

Grover shushed her, but kindly. "Ella, remember what we said about volume on observations?"

"Quiet observations?" Ella whispered loudly. "Observation: they are smiling. End observation."

Annabeth buried her face in her scarf again. Percy grinned so wide his cheeks hurt.

"Come on, Wise Girl," he said, tugging her mitten-hand gently. "Dinner. Before Clarisse eats all the rolls and Thalia challenges her to a duel over the last one."

"That will happen," Annabeth said, falling into step beside him, hands still joined, sleds bumping behind them. "It happens every time."

They walked back toward the lights together, leaving two sets of footprints side by side in the fresh snow, with the owl and the trident standing watch behind them.

Snow still sifted down, soft and slow. The camp glowed gold at the edges. Somewhere up the hill, Thalia laughed. Somewhere by the lake, Clarisse shouted. Somewhere in between, Grover played a hesitant reed pipe tune while Ella recited jokes to the stars.

Percy swung their joined hands once, and Annabeth swung back.

Snow day, with family. Sea and sky. Together.

It was the best kind of quiet.
