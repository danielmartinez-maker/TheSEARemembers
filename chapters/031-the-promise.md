## Chapter 31: The Promise

The dining pavilion emptied the way it always did on a warm, lingering night, not all at once but in slow unraveling threads. Someone's laughter from the Apollo table. Clatter of plates being cleared by harpies who were in too good a mood to threaten anybody. The torches guttered low and threw amber across the marble steps.

Percy Jackson lingered.

It wasn't that he wasn't tired. He was. His shoulders ached from the morning's canoe drills and his left ankle still twinged where he'd twisted it on the climbing wall two days before. But the air smelled like pine sap and grilled corn and salt off the Sound, and nobody was telling him to go to bed, and for once there wasn't a prophecy pressing on the back of his neck.

Annabeth sat across from him, legs tucked sideways on the bench, her plate pushed away. She had a notebook open — not a proper quest journal, just a half-falling-apart composition book she'd taken from the Big House supply closet — and she was drawing.

"What is that?" Percy asked.

"Floor plan," she said without looking up.

"For what?"

"Our apartment."

Percy blinked. "Our — like, you and me?"

"Eventually. When we're older." She said it matter-of-factly, pencil moving. "Don't make it weird."

"I'm not making it weird," Percy said quickly. "Can I see?"

She turned the notebook around.

It was a rectangle divided into boxes, labeled in Annabeth's neat, slanted capitals. KITCHEN. LIVING ROOM. BED 1. BED 2. BATH. SMALL BALCONY?? There were arrows, measurements, little notes in the margins: *windows facing east — morning light / bookshelves here — floor to ceiling if landlord allows / Percy not allowed to pick couch color alone.*

Percy grinned. "Hey."

"You picked a bright orange couch at the camp store last week for the Hermes cabin lounge," Annabeth said. "You can't be trusted with couch decisions."

"It was comfortable!"

"It was orange."

"It was *comfortable* and orange."

Annabeth rolled her eyes, but she was smiling in that barely-there way she did when she didn't want to admit she was happy. She tapped the kitchen square.

"Okay, so. In New Rome — " she started, then stopped.

Percy understood the pause. New Rome. The city they both remembered from their original life. Sometimes it returned in dreams, vivid as the places they had actually walked. A valley tucked into hills. Tiled roofs. A university on a hill with libraries that smelled like cedar. A river. Kids in purple shirts laughing on a field. An older version of himself showing someone younger how to hold a sword. An older version of her pointing at a half-built arch and arguing with the engineers.

They'd talked about it on the steps of Cabin Three, whispering about the university plans they had made before their lives changed.

*Do we still want it?* she'd asked.

*The city? Yeah,* he'd said. *With you.*

It was a future they had wanted once, held against a life that had changed around them. Remembering it did not tell them how to reach it again.

"You think it's still there?" Percy asked quietly now.

Annabeth chewed on the end of her pencil. "We remember it. We know it was real. But that doesn't tell us what the university is like now, or whether the same plans will work."

"You think we'll go there?"

"I think I want to build things that last," she said. "Whether it's there or here or somewhere else entirely. I want to go to college. A real one. With lecture halls and dorms and a dining hall where nobody tries to poison you. I want to study architecture and actually learn the math instead of just... knowing how a temple should feel."

"You'd be good at that," Percy said. "You already do the math in your head when you look at bridges."

"I want normal things too," she admitted, quieter. "I want to argue about rent. I want to have a key to my own front door."

Percy looked at the little floor plan again. BED 1. BED 2. He felt his ears go warm.

"Two bedrooms, huh?"

"One's an office," Annabeth said primly. "For my drafting table. And books. Mostly books."

"And the second one's...?"

"Yours. Duh. Unless you snore. Then you're on the balcony."

Percy laughed, loud enough that a couple of Naiads at the next table glanced over. He didn't care.

"Can I add something?" he asked.

Annabeth handed him the pencil.

He drew, tongue sticking out in concentration, a lopsided rectangle off the kitchen. He labeled it: AQUARIUM (BIG). And then, in the living room, a small square: TANK FOR MRS. O'LEARY'S TOYS?? He crossed that out. Drew an arrow to the balcony: HAMMOCK HERE.

Annabeth studied his additions with exaggerated seriousness. "The aquarium is structurally unsound. It'll flood the downstairs neighbors."

"They'll forgive us. We'll bring cookies."

"We will not flood the neighbors, Percy."

"We'll bring *really good* cookies."

She shook her head, but she didn't erase the aquarium. She just added a note underneath in smaller letters: *reinforce floor.*

That, Percy thought, was as close to a yes as anything.

They sat there while the pavilion torches were snuffed one by one and the crickets took over. Chiron passed by on his way to the Big House, gave them a look that was half counselor and half something softer, and said, "Bed in thirty minutes, you two. Even architects need sleep."

"Yes, Chiron," they chorused.

Neither of them moved.

"Do you ever think about what happens after?" Annabeth asked. She wasn't looking at the notebook anymore. She was looking past the pavilion columns, toward the dark line of the woods. "Like, after we're too old for camp? Camp ends at eighteen. Then what? Most demigods..."

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Most demigods didn't get to think about apartments and college.

Percy nudged his foot against hers under the table.

"Then we make something else," he said. "You said it. Something that lasts. Maybe it's New Rome. Maybe it's just... a place where people like us don't have to keep running. Grover's always talking about finding Pan, about making the wild safe again. Maybe we help with that. Maybe Thalia sticks around. Maybe—"

"You make it sound easy."

"No," Percy said. "I make it sound worth trying."

Annabeth was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached across the table and closed her hand around his wrist — not holding hands, exactly. Something steadier. Like an anchor.

"Promise me something," she said.

"Anything."

"When it gets bad — and it will get bad — don't go quiet on me. Don't do that thing where you decide you're protecting me by shutting me out. If we're going to do the future thing, we do it honest."

Percy swallowed. He thought about all the times that summer he'd wanted to keep things from her because he was twelve and scared and didn't have the words. He thought about how much easier it was to joke than to say *I'm afraid I'll lose you.*

"I promise," he said. "Same for you? No solo plans? No 'I have to do this alone because I'm Annabeth Grace, daughter of Zeus'?"

She huffed a laugh. "Fine. No solo plans."

"Shake on it?"

She held out her pinky. He linked his with hers, solemn as an oath on the Styx.

"Okay," she said. "Okay."

They stayed until the harpies started pointedly stacking chairs. Percy folded the floor plan page carefully and tucked it into his pocket when Annabeth wasn't looking. He wanted to keep it.

---

They walked back toward the cabins together, the gravel path pale under the moon.

Camp at night had its own language. The distant *thump-thump* of the forges cooling. A conch horn from the beach patrol, low and sleepy. Someone playing a lyre badly in Cabin Seven and someone else telling them to shut up, affectionately.

Annabeth felt the promise still sitting warm in her chest.

She'd never been good at wanting things out loud. In her remembered life — her dad's house in Virginia, before camp — wanting things had felt dangerous. If you said you wanted to go to college, to build cities, to be taken seriously, somebody would laugh or change the subject or tell you to be realistic. At camp, wanting things felt dangerous in a different way. You learned not to plan too far ahead.

But tonight she let herself.

She imagined them at seventeen, eighteen. Taller. Less scraped-up. Percy maybe finally able to sit still through a whole war council meeting. Herself maybe finally able to swim without counting strokes like an engineering problem.

She imagined a college campus — she'd seen brochures in the Big House, leftovers from mortal counselors. Brick buildings. Ivy. A library with one of those long wooden tables where you could spread out blueprints. She imagined Percy waiting outside her structures class with two coffees and pretending he understood load-bearing calculations.

She imagined an apartment door with two names on a little whiteboard. A kitchen where Percy tried to cook and set off the smoke alarm. A balcony with a hammock and a telescope and a beat-up copy of *The Odyssey* with both their notes in the margins.

It wasn't New Rome, exactly. New Rome in her memory was marble and gold, bright with plans they had already made. This was messier. Smaller. Hers.

"You're thinking loud," Percy said beside her.

"Sorry."

"No, it's good. Your thinking face is..." He gestured vaguely. "You get this line right here." He tapped his own forehead between his eyebrows.

"My thinking wrinkle?"

"Yeah. It's cute."

Annabeth stopped walking. "Percy Jackson, did you just call my thinking wrinkle cute?"

"I — uh — strategically, from a tactical standpoint —"

She shoved him lightly. He stumbled, laughing, and shoved her back, and for a minute they were just twelve-year-olds wrestling on the path while the moon watched, until they were both breathless and grass-stained and had to sit down on the steps of the volleyball court to recover.

"Truce?" Percy offered, holding up his hands.

"Truce," Annabeth said. She leaned her shoulder against his. "Hey, Percy?"

"Yeah?"

"Thanks for not laughing. About the apartment."

"Are you kidding? I've been thinking about it too. Just... I didn't know how to draw it. My floor plans look like mashed potatoes."

She laughed, and the sound surprised her with how easy it was.

Above them, the stars were coming out in layers. Cassiopeia. Lyra. Zeus's constellations scattered like someone had thrown silver sand across black velvet. Annabeth used to resent them — reminders of a father who watched but didn't come close. Tonight they just looked like lights left on for travelers.

"I want to see the library at New Rome," she whispered. "I remember how huge it is. Bigger than anything here. I want to walk in there and know I belong."

"You will," Percy said simply. "Anyone who sees you with a pencil knows you belong in a library."

She bumped her head gently against his shoulder. They sat there until the conch sounded curfew, and then a second time, and then Clarisse's voice bellowed from somewhere near the Ares cabin, "IF YOU'RE NOT IN BED IN FIVE MINUTES I'M DRAGGING YOU THERE MYSELF!"

Percy winced. "We should go."

"Yeah," Annabeth said, but neither of them stood up right away.

Just a little longer, she thought. Let the night hold still.

---

The laundry room behind the bathhouse was the only place at Camp Half-Blood where you could talk without someone overhearing, mostly because it smelled like damp wool and industrial soap and nobody went there voluntarily after dark.

Thalia Grace sat on top of a dryer that thumped rhythmically under her. Clarisse La Rue sat on the folding table, sharpening a dagger with slow, even strokes, sparks whispering off the whetstone.

They weren't supposed to be there. Both of them were supposed to be in their cabins for lights-out. Both of them had decided, independently and then mutually, that rules were suggestions on nights like this.

"You ever think about Jason?" Clarisse asked abruptly.

Thalia's head snapped up. "What?"

"Your brother. Little kid. You ever think about him?"

Thalia stared at the dryer's lint trap like it had personally offended her. Her fingers drummed on her knees. Electric sparks snapped faintly at her fingertips — static from the dryer, or something else.

"Every day," she said finally. "You?"

"I have Percy, obviously," Clarisse said. "And two half-brothers. Mortal side. Mom's kids with my stepdad. They're like, seven and nine. Brats. They put Legos in my boots last winter break."

Thalia smiled despite herself. "That's... kind of cute."

"It was not cute. It was a landmine in my Doc Martens at six in the morning." Clarisse set the dagger down. "They think I'm at some fancy boarding school in Connecticut. They send me drawings. Stick figures with swords. 'My big sister the warrior.' They have no idea."

"You miss them."

Clarisse shrugged, but it wasn't her usual *whatever* shrug. It was smaller. "They're annoying. They steal my fries. The little one cries if I don't read the bedtime story in the funny voices. But yeah. Yeah, I miss them."

Thalia nodded. She understood missing someone you couldn't explain.

"Jason was two," she said quietly. "When... when Mom... when Hera took him. I remember his hands. He had these chubby hands and he'd grab my hair and not let go. I used to be so mad at my mom for — for everything — and then one morning he was just gone, and Mom was screaming, and there was this smell like ozone, like after lightning, and nobody would tell me anything."

The dryer buzzed. Neither of them moved to open it.

"I told myself for years he was dead," Thalia said. "Easier that way. Then Annabeth told me what she remembers — about the wolves, about Lupa, about a kid with blond hair and a scar — and I thought, what if..."

"You think he's at that Roman place?" Clarisse asked. "The place they remember?"

"I think he's alive," Thalia said fiercely. "I have to think that. Otherwise what am I doing? Just running around with a spear being angry at clouds?"

Clarisse snorted. "You're pretty good at being angry at clouds."

"Shut up."

"Make me, Pinecone Face."

Thalia threw a damp sock at her. Clarisse caught it one-handed and threw it back. It hit the wall and slid down pathetically. They both cracked up, the kind of tired laughter that comes at midnight when everything is a little too much and a little too funny.

When they calmed down, Clarisse said, "My brothers think I'm brave. Like, really brave. They tell their friends their sister could beat up anyone. It's stupid, but... I don't want to let them down. Not just the camp stuff. The real stuff. I want to be someone who comes home."

"You will," Thalia said. "You're Clarisse La Rue. You don't lose. It's annoying, but it's true."

"And you?" Clarisse asked. "What do you want, besides finding your brother?"

Thalia thought about it. The dryer hummed under her. Somewhere outside, an owl called twice.

"I want a family that stays," she said. "Not the gods. Not... Annabeth is my sister. I helped raise her, and I still couldn't keep Jason with us. How do you stop expecting the next loss? I want to be the kind of sister who can stay. And I want..." She hesitated. "I want to not be afraid that every person I love is going to get taken. Jason. Annabeth. Luke —" Her voice caught. "Luke's been like a brother too. Then I hear what Percy and Annabeth remember about him, and I don't know how to fit it with the person I know."

Clarisse was quiet. Then she slid off the folding table and came to lean against the dryer next to Thalia, shoulder to shoulder.

"My dad's Poseidon," she said. "God of the sea. Everyone thinks that means I don't get scared. That I'm just... rage, all the way down. But I'm scared all the time. I'm scared my brothers will grow up and forget me. I'm scared I'll freeze in a real fight and get somebody killed. I'm scared..." She glanced at Thalia. "I'm scared people only like me when I'm yelling."

"I like you when you're not yelling," Thalia said. "It's rarer. Like a solar eclipse. But it's nice."

Clarisse elbowed her. "You're such a punk."

"You're such a meathead."

"Yeah," Clarisse said softly. "But I'm your meathead friend. Or whatever."

They sat there in the warm, soap-smelling dark. Thalia felt the static in her fingers settle.

"Promise me something?" Thalia said suddenly, echoing a conversation happening elsewhere on the hill without knowing it.

"What?"

"If I go looking for Jason someday — when I'm older, when I can — come with me? I don't want to do it alone."

Clarisse picked up her dagger again, turned it over in her hands. "Only if you promise me something back."

"Name it."

"If I ever start acting like my dad — like picking fights just to feel big — punch me. Hard. In the face. Don't let me turn into that."

Thalia held out her fist. Clarisse bumped it with her own, knuckles scarred and steady.

"Deal, La Rue."

"Deal, Grace."

The dryer buzzed again, insistent. Clarisse finally opened it and pulled out a warm tangle of orange camp shirts. She tossed one to Thalia, who caught it and pressed it to her face, breathing in clean heat.

"Come on," Clarisse said. "If we're going to break curfew, we might as well do it somewhere with stars."

---

The meadow past the strawberry fields was Silena Beauregard's favorite place to think about nothing.

She'd brought a blanket — blue, with white horses on it, slightly too short for her long legs — and spread it out where the grass was softest. Grover Underwood sat cross-legged beside her, his pipes in his lap, his hooves tucked under him. Ella, the harpy with rust-red feathers and bright, darting eyes, perched on the fence rail nearby, preening her wing feathers and muttering to herself.

"Ella is hungry for stars," Ella said. "Stars are far. Stars are cold fire. Ella likes cold fire. 'When I consider thy heavens, the work of thy fingers, the moon and the stars, which thou hast ordained.' That's Psalm 8. Ella remembers. Ella remembers everything she reads."

"That's pretty, Ella," Silena said gently. She patted the blanket. "Want to come sit? The grass won't bite."

Ella tilted her head, feathers ruffling. Then she hopped down — half flutter, half fall — and settled on the corner of the blanket, tucking her talons carefully so she wouldn't snag the fabric. "Ella will sit. Ella will be careful with the pretty blanket. Pretty like Silena. Silena is pretty like Helen. 'Was this the face that launch'd a thousand ships?' Marlowe. Ella read Marlowe."

Silena smiled, but it was a tired smile. "Thanks, Ella."

Grover nudged Silena's arm with his elbow. "You okay?"

"Yeah," Silena said. Then, because it was Grover and he always knew when she was lying, "No. Not really. Just... thinking."

"About?"

"About how much I like this." She gestured at the sky, the field, Ella reciting constellations under her breath. "And how I'm supposed to be the Aphrodite girl who's all about boyfriends and makeup and drama, and I *am* about those things sometimes, but also I just want... this. Quiet. Friends. Stars. Is that weird?"

Grover shook his head. "Nah. Pan — the wild — it's not just big quests and satyrs playing music in the woods. It's this too. Just... being outside and feeling like you belong there. Like the grass is happy you're lying on it."

"Is the grass happy we're lying on it right now?" Silena asked, half-teasing.

Grover closed his eyes and went still in that way he did when he was listening with more than his ears. A breeze moved through the meadow, rippling the clover. Somewhere, crickets stitched the night together.

"Yeah," he said, opening his eyes. "Really happy. It says you smell like peaches."

Silena laughed. "I *do* smell like peaches. New shampoo from the camp store. Drew says it's too sweet, but Drew thinks everything is too sweet except herself."

"Ella thinks peaches are good," Ella announced. "Peaches are soft. Peaches are summer. 'Summer's lease hath all too short a date.' Shakespeare. Sonnet 18. Ella knows Shakespeare. Ella knows all the sonnets."

"You want to learn the constellations tonight?" Grover asked Ella. "Real ones, not just book ones?"

Ella's eyes went bright. "Yes. Yes. Ella wants. Teach Ella. Ella will remember."

Grover pointed up. "Okay, see that W shape? That's Cassiopeia. She was a queen who bragged too much. The gods stuck her in the sky upside-down half the year as punishment."

"Harsh," Silena murmured.

"Gods are harsh," Grover agreed. "But look — next to her is Perseus. He saved Andromeda. Sometimes the stories have good endings."

He traced the lines with his finger, connecting dots Silena had looked at a hundred times but never really seen. Ella repeated each name, rolling the syllables like marbles: "Cassiopeia. Perseus. Andromeda. Pegasus."

"Pegasus is my favorite," Silena said. "Flying horses. Can you imagine? No traffic. Just... wind."

"You'd look good on a flying horse," Grover said. "You'd make it fashion."

"Damn right I would. Custom saddle. Pink reins."

Ella flapped her wings, just a little, stirring the blanket. "Ella can fly. Ella has wings. Ella doesn't need a horse. But Ella would share the sky. Ella would fly next to Silena on a horse. That's friendship. 'No friendship is an accident.' O. Henry. Ella read O. Henry."

Silena reached over and gently smoothed a ruffled feather on Ella's wing. Ella froze, then leaned into the touch, just barely.

"Thanks, Ella," Silena whispered.

They lay back. The blanket was too small for three, so Silena's feet hung off the end and Grover's horns kept poking the fabric when he turned his head, and Ella kept whispering star names like a lullaby. It was imperfect and perfect.

"Grover?" Silena said after a while. "Do you think you'll find him? Pan?"

Grover was quiet. His pipes glinted in the starlight. "I have to try," he said. "It's... it's what I'm for, you know? Searcher. If the wild dies, everything dies. The meadows. The music. This." He gestured vaguely. "But tonight I'm not a searcher. Tonight I'm just Grover, lying in grass that smells like peaches, teaching Ella about Perseus."

"Tonight you're just Grover," Silena agreed. "And I'm just Silena. Not Cabin Counselor Silena. Not... anything else."

"Ella is just Ella," Ella said sleepily. "Just Ella. Ella likes being just Ella with friends. Friends stay. 'Stay with me.' That's... that's Ella. Not a book. Just Ella."

Silena felt tears prick at the corners of her eyes, sudden and embarrassing. She blinked them away. The stars blurred and then sharpened.

"Hey," she said. "Promise me something? Both of you?"

Grover turned his head. Ella peeped.

"When everything gets scary — and it will, I know it will — let's remember this. Just... blanket. Stars. Peaches. Okay? No matter where we end up. We come back to this."

Grover held out his hand, pinky extended, solemn as a satyr could be. Silena linked hers with his. Ella stared at their fingers, then carefully hooked one claw — gently, gently — around both their pinkies.

"Ella promises," Ella whispered. "Ella remembers promises best of all."

Above them, a meteor streaked across Pegasus, brief and gold. Silena gasped. Grover made a soft *oh*. Ella clapped her wings once.

"Make a wish," Silena said.

"I wish for this," Grover said. "More of this."

"Me too," Silena said.

"Ella wishes for friends who stay," Ella said. "And cheese. Ella wishes for cheese and friends who stay."

Silena and Grover laughed until their stomachs hurt, until Ella started laughing too — a strange, croaking, wonderful sound — and the meadow echoed it back.

---

Percy couldn't sleep.

It wasn't nightmares. For once, his dreams were quiet — just water, lapping against a dock, and Annabeth's voice saying *reinforce the floor* like it was the most important thing in the world. He woke up smiling and couldn't get back to sleep.

He pulled on his shoes and slipped out of Cabin Three. The night air was cool on his face. The camp was silver and blue and utterly still, except for the waves.

His feet took him uphill before his brain caught up. Halfway to the crest, he saw he wasn't the first.

Annabeth was there, wrapped in a camp blanket, sitting against the base of the great boundary pine, which hummed faintly with the camp wards. Her notebook was closed in her lap. She looked up when she heard him.

"Couldn't sleep either?" she asked.

"Too busy thinking about aquariums," Percy said, sitting beside her.

She spread the blanket so it covered both of them. They sat shoulder to shoulder, watching the eastern sky pale from black to indigo.

One by one, the others came.

Thalia and Clarisse, grass-stained and smelling faintly of laundry soap, carrying a bag of ambrosia squares they'd bribed from the kitchen. They sat a little apart at first, then leaned back against the hill, boots crossed at the ankles.

Silena, Grover, and Ella, trailing the blue horse blanket, eyes bright with stars. Ella settled between Silena and Grover like it was the most natural thing in the world, chattering softly about Perseus until Silena hushed her gently and Ella tucked her head under her wing.

No one had planned it. No conch had called them. They just... arrived, the way demigods always seemed to find each other when it mattered.

For a while, nobody talked. The sky lightened by degrees. The pine needles whispered. Grover played a single low note on his pipes — not a song, just a tone to match the dawn — and then stopped.

Clarisse broke open the ambrosia squares and passed them around. Ella sniffed hers suspiciously, then ate it in one gulp and said, "Tastes like cheese. Ambrosia tastes like cheese for Ella. Ella loves cheese."

"Cheese ambrosia," Thalia said solemnly. "Gods bless America."

Silena leaned her head on Grover's shoulder. Grover leaned his head — carefully, horns and all — against hers. Annabeth's hand found Percy's under the blanket. Thalia's boot nudged Clarisse's. Ella's wing stretched out to cover Silena's knees.

The sun rose.

It wasn't dramatic. No monsters on the horizon. No prophecy burning in the sky. Just light spilling gold over Long Island Sound, turning the water to hammered copper, gilding the strawberry fields, catching in the dew on the hill. The pine's shadow stretched long behind them. The camp below woke up in small sounds: a door creaking, a laugh, the clank of armor from early sword practice.

Percy looked at them — really looked. Annabeth with her hair coming loose from its braid, already thinking three steps ahead. Thalia with her nose sunburned and her eyes softer than he'd ever seen them. Clarisse with ambrosia crumbs on her chin, pretending she wasn't about to cry. Silena with peach shampoo and a fierce, tender heart. Grover with music in his hands. Ella with every book she'd ever read lighting her up from inside.

His family. Clarisse and Silena by blood; Annabeth, his girlfriend, and the friends around them by choice. By nights like this.

Annabeth squeezed his hand. He squeezed back. That was the promise, renewed without words: *honest. Together. No solo plans.*

Thalia caught his eye and grinned, tired and real. Clarisse muttered something about sap in her hair. Silena whispered, "Look," as the sun cleared the water, and everyone looked.

Annabeth shifted closer to Percy and pulled the notebook back into her lap. She flipped past the apartment floor plan to a blank page, and in the pale gold light she started sketching again — quick, sure lines. Percy watched over her shoulder.

"What now?" he whispered.

"New Rome library," she whispered back. "From our old life. I don't want to lose what we wanted."

She drew a long hall with high windows, rows of tables, arches that reminded Percy of pictures he'd seen of old train stations. She added tiny figures at the tables — one with curly hair and a pencil, one with dark hair and a sword leaning against his chair like he hadn't quite learned how to sit in a library without it. In the margin she wrote: *morning light from east, cedar shelves, quiet floor for Ella.*

"You're putting us in there?" Percy asked.

"Of course. You need somewhere to nap between classes. And Ella needs a quiet floor where nobody shushes her for quoting books."

Ella, half-asleep, lifted her head at the sound of her name. "Ella likes libraries. Libraries are nests for thoughts. Ella will be quiet. Ella promises to whisper. 'We whisper, and hear what we cannot see.' That's... Ella forgot. Ella will remember later."

"We'll save you a seat," Annabeth promised, and she drew a small window nook in the corner of the library and labeled it ELLA.

Thalia leaned over to look. "That's pretty good, little architect. You miss anything?"

"A dome," Thalia said, pointing. "Could you put one here? I haven't seen that library, but I like the idea of light coming through and hitting the floor at noon."

Annabeth added the dome, shading it carefully. "Better?"

"Much better," Thalia said. She hesitated, then added quietly, "Draw a door on the west side. For Jason. So when he comes, he can just walk in."

Annabeth drew the door without asking questions. She labeled it WEST DOOR, and underneath, in tiny letters: *always open.*

Clarisse cleared her throat. She wasn't looking at the drawing. She was looking out at the Sound, where the fishing boats were starting to putter across the copper water. "My brothers would like that library," she said gruffly. "The little one likes books with dragons. The other one just likes to climb the shelves."

"Bring them sometime," Silena said. It wasn't a joke. She said it like it was obvious. "When we're older. When it's safe. We'll have a whole day. Cheese and dragons and climbing shelves you're not supposed to climb."

Clarisse blinked fast. "Yeah," she said. "Yeah, okay. I'd like that."

Grover played another soft phrase on his pipes — three notes that sounded like sunlight through leaves — and Ella sighed happily and tucked herself tighter against Silena's side. Silena pulled the corner of the blue horse blanket over Ella's feet. The harpy's talons curled in sleep.

"Tell me about the apartment again," Thalia said suddenly, lying back with her hands behind her head. "I missed the beginning."

Annabeth glanced at Percy. He pulled the folded floor plan page from his pocket, now soft at the creases. He smoothed it on his knee.

"Okay," Percy said, suddenly shy with everyone watching. "So there's a kitchen — small, because Annabeth says I can't be trusted with big kitchens — and a living room with bookshelves floor to ceiling, and a balcony with a hammock, and —"

"An aquarium," Annabeth finished for him. "Structurally unsound. Which we will reinforce."

"With cookies for the neighbors downstairs when it leaks," Percy added solemnly.

"It will not leak," Annabeth said. "Because we will reinforce the floor."

The whole hill cracked up — quiet, sunrise laughter that didn't want to wake the rest of camp yet. Even Clarisse laughed, a real laugh that shook her shoulders. Percy felt his face go red, but in a good way, like sunburn from the inside.

"I want an apartment too," Silena said dreamily. "Top floor. Big windows. Plants everywhere. Grover can pick the plants so I don't kill them."

"I accept this responsibility," Grover said, bowing his head gravely, horns catching the light. "No plant left behind."

"I want a cabin," Thalia said. "Not a camp cabin. A real one. Somewhere with pine trees and a porch. Somewhere quiet where Annabeth can come draw and Percy can be loud somewhere else."

"Hey," Percy protested.

"It's a big woods," Thalia said. "You can be loud in your section."

"I want a house near the water," Percy said, surprising himself. "Nothing fancy. Just... I want to hear the waves. And I wish I could give Sally the good bedroom. In the life I remember she never got it. The one with the morning light. She deserved that."

Nobody teased him for that. Annabeth's hand tightened around his. Silena smiled at him like she understood exactly.

"I want a music room," Grover said. "With reeds growing outside the window so I can make new pipes whenever I want. And a door that always opens for friends."

"Ella wants a nest," Ella murmured sleepily, eyes still closed. "High up. With books and shiny things and friends below. Ella wants a window to see stars. Ella wants cheese nearby. Cheese must be nearby."

"Cheese will be nearby," Silena assured her, stroking her feathers. "That's non-negotiable."

They watched until the light was fully up and the hill was warm and the moment tipped naturally toward morning — toward breakfast and drills and laundry and life.

No one made a speech. There was nothing to declare. They just sat there, together, while the sea glittered and the sky paled to blue, and that was enough.

It had to be enough. For now, on a hill at Camp Half-Blood, with the whole summer humming behind them and an uncertain future ahead — college and apartments and Roman valleys and lost brothers and wild gods — it was everything.

Percy thought about the floor plan in his pocket. *Reinforce the floor.* He thought about college brochures and architectural math and orange couches. He thought about sisters who stayed and brothers who were missed and meadows that were happy and harpies who remembered promises.

He thought: *We'll get there.*

Not today. Not tomorrow. But someday, they'd have keys to their own front doors and libraries that smelled like cedar and skies to share. Someday, the promises made on laundry-room dryers and starlit blankets and torch-lit dining tables would hold.

The sun rose higher. Ella sighed in her sleep. Grover started softly playing — a morning song, bright and simple. Silena hummed along. Thalia closed her eyes and tipped her face to the light. Clarisse stood, stretched, and offered Thalia a hand up. Annabeth leaned her head on Percy's shoulder.

Percy looked around the table once more, at the people who had made promises he could ask them to keep. Then he reached for the last piece of bread before Clarisse could take it.
