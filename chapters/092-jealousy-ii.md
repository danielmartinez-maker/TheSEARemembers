## Chapter 92: Jealousy II

The morning bell at Camp Half-Blood never rang so much as it breathed.

It started low over the hills, a conch blown from the Big House porch, and rolled down through the cabins and the strawberry fields. By the second note, the camp was already moving.

Percy Jackson woke the way he always woke at camp, with one arm hanging off the bunk and his cheek stuck to the pillow and his brain three seconds behind his body.

The Poseidon cabin was cool and blue and quiet in the mornings. The fountain in the corner trickled. The shells on the walls caught the early light and threw it back in soft ripples. The bunk above him creaked.

“Perce,” said Annabeth’s voice from the doorway, crisp and amused and fully awake. “You drooled again.”

Percy sat up too fast and wiped his mouth. “Did not.”

“Did too.” His girlfriend leaned against the doorframe with her arms crossed, her blonde curls pulled back, her camp shirt already tucked, her dagger already at her hip. “You have pillow face, too.”

Percy groaned and flopped back down. “Five more minutes.”

“Nope. Clarisse said if you’re late to drills again she’s going to use you as the demonstration dummy.”

That got him up.

He pulled on his shirt, grabbed Riptide, and followed Annabeth out into the bright morning.

“Left flank,” she said.

“Shield wall anchors to the right,” Percy answered automatically, yawning.

“Good. And if the Ares cabin breaks formation?”

“Yell for Clarisse and get out of the way.”

Annabeth grinned. “Exactly.”

The training arena was already crowded when they arrived. Clarisse La Rue stood in the center of it with her spear over her shoulder. She looked sharp and focused and completely in her element.

Percy felt a familiar swell of pride when he saw her. She had stopped being just the scary spear fighter and had become someone he knew.

His older half-sister. By blood, by their father, by the family they were making in Cabin Three. The kind of sister you teased mercilessly and defended instantly.

“Jackson!” Clarisse barked. “Nice of you to join us. Did your pillow fight back?”

“My girlfriend is a traitor,” Percy said, jerking his thumb at Annabeth. “She woke me up with insults.”

“Good. Someone needs to keep you humble.” Clarisse tossed him a practice sword. He caught it one-handed. “You’re with me for warm-ups. Try not to cry this time.”

Annabeth snorted and headed for the sidelines with her notebook, already sketching the drill layout. She liked to watch first, analyze, then jump in and dismantle everyone with terrifying precision.

Morning drills started the way they always did. Percy fell into the rhythm easily. Clarisse moved through the drills like she had been born with a spear in her hand.

“Nice,” Percy said quietly to Annabeth during water break. “She’s good with them.”

Annabeth nodded, watching Clarisse with open admiration. “She’s been practicing that. Being a teacher, not just a fighter. Chiron noticed too.”

Percy grinned around his water cup. “Our Clarisse is growing up.”

“Our Clarisse will kill you if she hears you say that,” Annabeth said.

“Worth it.”

That was when the gate at the far end of the arena opened and an older camper walked in.

He was tall, maybe seventeen, with broad shoulders and a faded camp shirt with the sleeves cut off and a long scar down his left forearm. He carried a duffel over one shoulder and a real bronze sword at his hip.

“Who’s that?” Percy asked.

Annabeth flipped a page in her notebook. “Evan. Son of Ares. He was here three summers ago, then he went to that semester program out west. Wilderness survival and monster combat intensive. He just got back last night. Chiron asked him to help with advanced sparring.”

Evan dropped his duffel by the rack, shook Chiron’s hand where Chiron stood by the fence in his wheelchair form, and then surveyed the arena with a slow, experienced look. His eyes landed on Clarisse.

And stayed there.

Percy noticed it immediately, the way you notice a shift in the water before a wave.

Clarisse had just finished resetting the dummies. She pulled her helmet on, rolled her shoulders, and stepped back into the circle. Evan walked straight toward her.

“You’re La Rue, right?” he said. “Chiron told me about you. Said you’re the best spear in this age group.”

Clarisse blinked, caught off guard. Praise always did that to her. Insults bounced off. Compliments stuck. “I’m decent,” she said carefully.

Evan laughed. “Decent. Right. I watched your last two reps. Mind if I spar you?”

A little ripple went through the watching campers.

Clarisse stood a little straighter. “Sure. You’ll be disappointed, but sure.”

“You heard her,” Evan called to the circle. “La Rue says she’s going to wipe the floor with me. Everyone gather up.”

The campers laughed and crowded closer.

They sparred fast. Evan was bigger and stronger, but Clarisse was faster. She read his pattern, swept his leg, and brought her spear tip up to his chest. Point. The circle erupted.

Evan stared down at the spear tip, then broke into a wide grin. He held up both hands. “Okay. Okay. Hype is real.”

Clarisse lowered her spear, breathing hard, trying very hard not to smile and failing. “Lucky shot.”

“Was not luck,” Evan said. “That was textbook with flair. Where did you learn that sweep? No one here used to fight like that.”

“I drilled it all winter,” Clarisse said. “Annabeth helped me break down the footwork.”

Evan glanced over at Annabeth and nodded with respect, then turned right back to Clarisse. “Well, it shows. Seriously, kid, you’re impressive. You fight like an older camper. How long have you drilled that?”

“All winter,” Clarisse said. “Every morning.”

“And fighting like that? Gods. My first winter I was still trying not to stab myself.”

Everyone laughed. Evan clapped Clarisse on the shoulder, hard enough to rock her forward a step. “Stick with me today. I want to run paired drills with you. Show these new kids what a real spear fighter looks like.”

Clarisse’s ears went pink. “Uh. Okay. Sure.”

Percy felt something odd twist in his stomach.

It was small at first. A flicker. He watched Evan pull Clarisse aside to show her a grip adjustment, standing close behind her to guide her hands on the spear shaft. He watched Evan laugh at something Clarisse muttered and nudge her helmet with his knuckles the way older siblings did. He watched Clarisse laugh back, bright and surprised, like she wasn’t used to being looked at like that.

Looked at like she was special.

Percy took a long drink of water to wash the feeling away. It didn’t work.

“Again!” Evan called. “Same sequence, but faster. Ready, La Rue?”

“Ready,” Clarisse said, and they went again, wood cracking against wood, dust puffing up around their feet.

Percy found his hands curling into fists.

Annabeth glanced at him sideways. “You okay?”

“Yeah,” Percy said too quickly. “Just hot.”

“It’s seventy degrees.”

“I run warm.”

Annabeth raised one eyebrow, the exact eyebrow she had used to dismantle Percy’s lies in both their lives. But she didn’t push it. Not yet. She just made a small note in her notebook that definitely was not about battle formations.

---

By lunch, Evan and Clarisse were inseparable.

It wasn’t weird, exactly. That was the annoying part. If Evan had been creepy or mean, Percy could have done something. Stepped in. Said something. Gotten Clarisse out of there.

But Evan wasn’t creepy. He was nice. At lunch, he waved her over to sit with him and some of the older campers, and Percy watched Clarisse hesitate, glance toward her usual spot under the big oak, then carry her tray toward the older campers with an apologetic shrug.

Percy stared at his hamburger.

Silena Beauregard slid onto the bench across from him, her eyes bright with mischief. She took one look at Percy’s face and lit up.

“Oh, this is good,” she said.

“What?” Percy said.

“You.” Silena pointed her fork at him. “That face. That is the face of a boy who is sulking.”

“I’m not sulking,” Percy said. “I’m eating.”

“You’re stabbing your burger with a fork,” Annabeth observed from beside him. “That’s not eating. That’s burger murder.”

Percy put the fork down. “I’m fine.”

Silena leaned forward, chin in her hands. “You’re jealous.”

Percy choked on his lemonade. “What? No.”

“Yes,” Silena said delightedly. “You are so jealous. Look at you. Your little sparring buddy found a new big cool mentor and you’re sitting here making that kicked-puppy face.”

“I don’t make a kicked-puppy face,” Percy said.

“You absolutely make a kicked-puppy face,” Annabeth said. “It’s your signature expression. Right after confused and right before hungry.”

“I’m not jealous,” Percy insisted. “Why would I be jealous? Evan’s cool. It’s good for Clarisse. She deserves attention for how hard she works.”

Silena and Annabeth exchanged a look.

“He says, while glaring a hole through the back of Evan’s head,” Silena said.

“I’m not glaring.”

“You are glaring,” Annabeth said. “I can feel the heat from here.”

Percy looked down at his tray. Across the pavilion, he could hear Evan’s laugh and then Clarisse’s laugh, louder than usual, a little nervous, a little thrilled. He felt that twist in his stomach again, tighter this time.

“It’s stupid,” he muttered. “I don’t know why I feel like this.”

Silena’s teasing smile softened just a fraction. She reached across the table and patted his hand. “Aw. Protective brother syndrome. It’s cute.”

“I’m her little brother,” Percy said automatically, then frowned. “I mean. She’s older. Technically she’s the one who looks out for me. But I want to do it for her too.”

“Like what?” Annabeth asked quietly.

Percy struggled to find the words. “Like... she’s my sister. Real sister, still new to me. Like I’m supposed to look out for her. And now this guy shows up and he’s all ‘wow, you’re amazing’ and she’s eating it up and... I don’t know. What if he’s just messing with her? What if she gets hurt? What if she doesn’t need me anymore?”

The last sentence came out smaller than he intended.

Silena’s expression went from playful to genuinely tender. “Oh, Percy.”

Annabeth was quiet for a moment, studying him with those sharp gray eyes that saw too much. Then she bumped her shoulder against his.

“Come on,” she said, standing up with her tray. “Walk with me. Bring your burger murder fork.”

“Where?”

“Down to the beach. You need air and you need your wise girlfriend.”

“You’re not that much older than me,” Percy said, but he stood up anyway.

“I’m wiser by like four years,” Annabeth said. “Come on.”

Silena blew them a kiss. “Have fun, lovebirds! Sentimental seagulls! Squawk!”

“SILENA,” Percy and Annabeth said together, identically exasperated, which only made Silena laugh harder.

They dumped their trays and headed down the hill toward the water. The path was dappled with sunlight.

Percy felt his shoulders drop a little with every step away from the crowd.

---

Percy and Annabeth sat on their usual rock, the flat one shaped like a turtle shell, close enough that their knees touched.

Annabeth tucked her legs up and hugged her knees. Percy picked up a smooth stone and turned it over in his hands.

“So,” Annabeth said. “Want to talk about the jealous thing without Silena narrating?”

Percy sighed. “I’m not proud of it. I like Clarisse. I want her to get compliments. She works so hard and she never thinks it’s enough. Evan’s right, she is amazing. So why do I feel all... tight and itchy when he’s around her?”

Annabeth nodded like this was a perfectly reasonable question and not something to be embarrassed about. “Because you love her. And you’re scared of losing your place.”

Percy looked at her. “That sounds... selfish.”

“It’s human,” Annabeth said. “Especially for siblings.”

Percy turned that over. “Is that what this is? I think there’s not enough room for Evan?”

“Kind of,” Annabeth said. “You and Clarisse have a thing. You spar together. You cover for each other. You have dumb inside jokes about Ares cabin socks. Then Evan shows up and he sees the same cool stuff you see, and suddenly you’re worried that if Clarisse gets a new person who gets it, she won’t need the old person.”

Percy was quiet. The waves hissed and pulled back.

“That’s exactly it,” he admitted. “Which is dumb. Because I know she can have more than one person. I have you and Grover and Tyson and... it’s not like I only get one sibling.”

“But feelings aren’t math,” Annabeth said. “They don’t always listen to logic. Especially protective brother feelings.”

Percy frowned at the stone in his hand. “Do you ever feel like that? About me?”

Annabeth laughed. “Are you kidding? All the time. When you and Tyson first started doing that whole giant-brother hand-slap thing and staying up late speaking in whale noises? I was so annoyed. I thought, great, Percy found someone cooler who actually likes the water and doesn’t make him do homework. He’s going to forget about me.”

Percy stared at her. “You never told me that.”

“Because in our old life I was young and prideful and I dealt with it by challenging Tyson to a strategy game and losing spectacularly,” Annabeth said dryly. “But yeah. I felt it. That little pinch. Like, oh, someone else makes him laugh like that. Someone else gets to be his person for a minute.”

“What did you do?”

“I talked to Thalia, actually,” Annabeth said. “She told me something that helped. There’s romantic jealousy and there’s sibling jealousy and they feel similar in your chest but they come from different places. Romantic jealousy is like, I want to be the only one. Sibling jealousy is like, I want to know I still matter. One is about possessing. The other is about belonging.”

Percy repeated that slowly. “Possessing versus belonging.”

“Yeah. You don’t want to own Clarisse. You’re not mad that Evan thinks she’s cool. You’re scared that you won’t belong to her the same way anymore. That she’ll grow past you.”

Percy felt something loosen in his chest, like a knot coming undone. “That... actually makes sense. Because when he was showing her that grip thing, my first thought wasn’t ‘get away from her.’ It was ‘oh. She’s smiling like she used to smile when I finally got a move right and she was proud of me.’ Like I missed being the one who got to see that.”

Annabeth nodded. “That’s sibling stuff. Brother stuff. You love being the person who hypes her up. It hurts when someone else gets to do it and do it shinier because he’s older and has a cool scar.”

“He does have a cool scar,” Percy muttered.

“I know. It’s very annoying. I saw it and immediately thought, I bet he has a great story and I want to hear it and I hate that I want to hear it.”

Percy laughed, surprised. The tight feeling eased a little more.

Annabeth went on, softer. “The trick Thalia taught me is to ask yourself, am I worried about her, or am I worried about me? If you’re worried about her, then you act. You watch, you check in. If you’re worried about you, then you have to tell her that. Just honest. Like, hey, I missed sparring with you today.”

Percy looked out at the water.

“I’m worried about both, I think,” he said honestly. “Mostly me, though. Evan seems... fine. What if Clarisse decides she’d rather have a cool older mentor brother than a dumb younger brother who still drools on his pillow?”

Annabeth snorted. “First, you will always be her dumb younger brother. That position is lifetime-appointed. Second, Clarisse doesn’t replace people. She collects them.”

Percy smiled. “She does?”

“She does. She still has the friendship bracelet Silena made her three years ago. And she still has the dented practice dagger you gave her.”

Percy leaned back on his hands and let the sun warm his face. “When did you get so smart?”

“I’ve always been smart. You’ve just finally gotten smart enough to notice.”

“Ha.”

They sat in comfortable silence for a minute. The kind of silence two people who knew each other this well could share without needing to fill it. Percy skipped his stone once, twice, three times across a calm patch. Annabeth counted under her breath.

“Can I ask you something?” Percy said.

“Always.”

“Does it ever bother you? That people... you know. That new kids sometimes think we’re... siblings?”

Annabeth glanced at him, understanding immediately. At camp, new kids sometimes assumed. They saw a boy and a girl always together among the Poseidon siblings, always looking out for each other, and they filled in the wrong family story. It had bothered Percy more than he let on, especially this summer, when he and Annabeth had gotten closer than ever and also more protective.

Annabeth was quiet for a moment, then she held out her hand, palm up, on the rock between them. An invitation, not a question.

Percy took it without hesitation. Her hand was warm and calloused from dagger practice, smaller than his but strong. They remembered holding hands across their old quests, crossing parking lots, walking through haunted tunnels, sitting in hospital waiting rooms. Friendship had grown into the love they still carried. Here, too, it meant, I’m here. You’re not alone.

“No,” Annabeth said simply, squeezing his fingers. “Because I know what we are. You’re my boyfriend. My best friend. My home base. People can think whatever they want. We know the truth.”

Percy squeezed back. “Yeah. Same. You’re my girlfriend. My strategist. My emergency backup brain.”

“Your only backup brain,” Annabeth corrected.

“True.”

They sat there holding hands like they had a hundred times before, watching the water, letting the sibling-jealousy talk settle.

After a while, Percy said, “Thanks, Wise Girl.”

“Anytime, Seaweed Brain.”

---

They found Thalia up by the climbing wall, arguing with the lava.

Or rather, arguing with the campers who were supposed to be monitoring the lava.

“No, no, no,” Thalia said, hands on her hips, her black hair with its silver streaks pulled back, her punk bracelets jangling. Daughter of Zeus, leader of camp scouting patrols, big-sister energy so powerful it could probably deflect arrows. “You don’t open the valve when someone’s on the north face. Are you trying to make soup?”

The two young campers manning the controls looked sheepish. “Sorry, Thalia.”

“It’s fine. Just watch the flags. Red flag means hold.” Thalia turned and saw Percy and Annabeth approaching. Her fierce expression melted into a grin. “Hey, look, it’s Jackson and Grace. You two look suspiciously emotional. Did Silena make you talk about feelings again?”

“Silena started it,” Percy said. “Annabeth finished it.”

“Classic combo.” Thalia hopped down from the platform and slung an arm around each of them, pulling them in for a quick, hard hug that smelled like pine and electricity. “What’s up?”

Percy hesitated. It was one thing to admit jealousy to Annabeth on an empty beach. It was another to admit it to Thalia, who was cooler than everyone and had never seemed jealous of anything in her life.

But Annabeth nudged him. “Tell her. She’s got the Jason story.”

Thalia’s eyebrows shot up. “Oh, we’re doing jealousy? Sibling edition?”

Percy sighed. “How did you know?”

“Because Annabeth has that ‘I just explained emotional taxonomy’ look on her face, and you have that ‘I just learned something about myself and I’m mildly annoyed about it’ look.” Thalia steered them toward the shade of a pine tree and sat down, patting the ground on either side. “Sit. Hydrate. Spill.”

So Percy spilled. He told Thalia about Evan and Clarisse and the spar and lunch and the tight itchy feeling and the talk on the beach. Thalia listened without interrupting, nodding along, her expression shifting from amused to understanding.

When he finished, she whistled low. “Yep. That’s protective-sibling jealousy, all right. Welcome to the club. We have jackets. They’re very protective and slightly embarrassing.”

“You felt like that?” Percy asked, surprised. “You? But you’re... you.”

“I’m me, which means I felt it extra hard and handled it extra badly at first,” Thalia said with a wry grin. “You want to hear about Jason?”

Percy nodded. Annabeth settled back against the tree trunk. She had heard this story before, but she liked hearing Thalia tell it.

Thalia picked up a pinecone and tossed it between her hands.

“So, Jason,” she said. “My little brother. When we were really little, before... everything, before our mom spiraled and we got separated, he was my whole world. I carried him around. I fed him crackers. I told him the clouds were monsters and I would protect him. Classic older sister god complex.”

Percy smiled. “Sounds familiar.”

“Right? Then we get split up for years. Years, Percy. I think he’s dead. He thinks I’m gone. Then I find out he’s alive, at Camp Jupiter, living this whole other life with other people. And I’m happy, I’m so happy he’s alive, but then I go to see him and he’s got this best friend, Reyna? No, before Reyna. This kid Dakota, and then later Hazel and Frank, and they have all these inside jokes in Latin and they know how he takes his coffee and they know he hums when he’s nervous—”

Thalia’s voice caught just slightly. She cleared her throat.

“And I’m standing there, his big sister, his real big sister, and I don’t know any of that. Some other people got to be there for all the years I missed. Some other kid got to teach him how to throw a pilum. Some other girl got to sit up with him when he had nightmares about wolves.”

She threw the pinecone hard at the climbing wall. It bounced off.

“I was so jealous I couldn’t see straight,” Thalia said bluntly. “Not romantic. Not even really mad at them. I was mad at time. Mad that I missed it. And scared that Jason didn’t need a big sister anymore because he’d already built a family without me.”

Percy stared at her. “What did you do?”

“Oh, I was a disaster about it,” Thalia said cheerfully. “I tried to out-cool everyone. I challenged his best friend to a spar and nearly took his head off. I kept jumping in with ‘well, when Jason was little, he—’ stories to prove I knew him first. It was incredibly mature. I was acting like I was six again.”

Annabeth laughed. “I’ve seen you be mature. This tracks.”

“Hey.” Thalia shoved her lightly. “Anyway. Jason pulled me aside one night. We were on the roof of the principia, looking at the stars, and he just said, ‘You don’t have to compete. You’re my sister. That’s a different thing. No one else gets to be that.’”

She looked at Percy seriously.

“And he was right. Dakota could be his best friend. Hazel could be his confidante. But I was still his big sister who remembered him drooling grape juice on her favorite shirt when he was two. Annabeth is his sister too; neither of us has to compete with his friends for that. That spot was mine, whether I’d been there every day or not. I just had to actually occupy it instead of fighting everyone else for airtime.”

Percy absorbed that. “So what did you do? After?”

“I stopped trying to prove I belonged and just... belonged,” Thalia said simply. “I asked questions instead of showing off. I asked Hazel what Jason was like when he was scared. I asked Frank what made him laugh. I let them teach me who my brother had become instead of insisting I already knew. And you know what? They loved telling me. Because they loved him too. We became a team instead of rivals.”

Annabeth nodded. “Belonging, not possessing.”

“Exactly,” Thalia said, pointing at Annabeth. “My sister’s smart. Keep listening to her.”

“I’m trying,” Percy said.

Thalia turned back to Percy and put a hand on his shoulder. Her grip was strong. “Listen, Kelp Head. Clarisse trusts you. You’re her Percy. You’re the idiot who stood up to her when no one else would and then turned around and stood beside her. Evan can be a cool mentor for a week. He can show her a new grip and tell her she’s impressive and make her feel seen by the older kids. That’s good for her. But he can’t be you. He doesn’t have your history.”

Percy felt his throat get tight. “What if she likes him better?”

“She might like him differently,” Thalia said honestly. “Older-camper admiration hits different. It’s shiny. But different isn’t better. It’s just different. You’re the brother who knows she cries during that one sad movie about the dog and pretends she has allergies. You’re the one who knows she puts extra honey in her tea when she’s nervous. Evan doesn’t know that stuff. He just knows she has a good thrust recovery.”

Percy laughed wetly. “She does have a good thrust recovery.”

“She really does,” Thalia agreed. “So here’s my advice, from one overprotective sibling to another. Don’t sulk on the sidelines. Don’t glare at the guy for doing nothing wrong. Go be her brother. Go tell her you saw her spar and it was awesome. Ask her to show you that sweep. Let her brag. Let her have her moment and be part of it instead of watching it from the lunch table while murdering a hamburger.”

“How do you know about the hamburger?” Percy asked.

“Silena Iris-messaged me,” Thalia and Annabeth said together, then looked at each other and laughed.

Percy groaned. “Gods. Is the whole camp talking about this?”

“Only the people who love you,” Annabeth said, patting his knee. “Which is, unfortunately for your privacy, a lot of people.”

Thalia stood up and hauled Percy to his feet. “Come on. Afternoon free spar is starting soon. Let’s go watch your sister be awesome and practice not looking constipated when someone compliments her.”

“I didn’t look constipated,” Percy protested.

“You looked a little constipated,” Annabeth said.

“Fine. I’ll work on my face.”

“That’s the spirit.”

---

The afternoon sun slanted gold across the arena when they got back. The sand was warm underfoot. A small crowd had already gathered around the central circle.

Clarisse was in the middle of it, helmet off now, face flushed, demonstrating the sweep she had used that morning to a cluster of younger campers. Evan stood beside her, arms crossed, nodding along as she explained.

“And then you shift your weight here,” Clarisse was saying, demonstrating slowly, “so you don’t overextend. Like this. See?”

The younger kids nodded earnestly. One of them tried it and stumbled. Clarisse steadied him.

“Better,” she said. “You’ll get it. Just drill it slow first.”

Percy hung back at the edge, watching. The tight feeling flickered again when Evan said something that made Clarisse laugh, but this time Percy recognized it for what it was. Not anger. Not dislike. Just that little pinch. That belonging worry.

I missed sparring with you today.

He took a breath and walked in.

“Hey,” he said.

Clarisse looked up, surprised. “Hey. Where’d you go at lunch? You ditched.”

“Needed air,” Percy said honestly. “And my girlfriend dragged me to the beach for a lecture.”

Annabeth waved from the sidelines. “It was a good lecture.”

“It was okay,” Percy said, and Annabeth threw a pinecone at him.

Clarisse smirked, but her eyes were a little wary, like she wasn’t sure where she stood. “You missed my rematch with Evan. I lost this time. He cheats. He does this annoying thing with his elbow.”

“I do not cheat,” Evan protested mildly. “I leverage experience.”

“You leverage cheating,” Clarisse said, but she was smiling.

“That sweep this morning was awesome,” Percy said. The words came out a little rushed. “I mean it. I’ve sparred you a hundred times and I’ve never seen you move that fast. You read him perfectly.”

Clarisse blinked. The wariness dropped. Something warm flickered across her face. “Thanks,” she said quietly. “I’ve been working on it.”

“I know. Annabeth told me. I’m proud of you.”

Clarisse’s ears went pink again, but this time she didn’t look away. “Shut up.”

“No.”

“Shut up, Jackson.”

“Make me, La Rue.”

Evan watched this exchange with open amusement. He stuck out his hand to Percy. “You’re Percy, right? Clarisse talks about you. Mostly threats, but affectionate threats.”

Percy shook his hand. Evan’s grip was firm. “Yeah. That’s me. Affectionate threat recipient.”

“Evan was just showing me that western feint,” Clarisse said eagerly, falling into teacher-student mode. “Percy, you gotta see this, it’s perfect for your style because you overcommit with your right—”

“I do not overcommit,” Percy said.

“You absolutely overcommit,” Clarisse and Annabeth said together.

“Betrayed by my sister and my girlfriend,” Percy sighed, but he stepped into the circle. “Okay. Show me.”

And just like that, he was in it. Not on the sidelines. In it.

Evan walked them through the feint slowly, then had Clarisse demonstrate at speed while he narrated. Percy watched, really watched, the way Annabeth had taught him to watch. He saw how Clarisse’s shoulders had relaxed now that Percy was there, how she explained more confidently with her brother in the audience.

“Okay, try it on me,” Clarisse said to Percy, grinning fiercely. “Slow first.”

They sparred slow, wood tapping wood. Then a little faster. Then Evan called adjustments from the side, and Annabeth called different adjustments from the other side, and soon both of them were shouting contradictory instructions while Thalia shouted that they were both wrong and Silena, who had just arrived with lemonade, shouted that everyone looked hot when they were sweaty and should hydrate.

It was chaos. It was camp.

Percy tried the feint, overcommitted exactly like everyone said he would, and Clarisse swept his legs out from under him with that same beautiful sweep from the morning. He hit the sand with a thump that knocked the air out of him.

Everyone cheered.

Clarisse stood over him, spear tip at his chest, breathing hard, eyes shining. “Point.”

Percy stared up at her and started laughing. He couldn’t help it. She looked so proud, so happy, dust on her cheek and sun in her hair, Evan clapping in the background and Annabeth grinning and Thalia whooping.

“Yeah,” he gasped. “Point. You got me. That was perfect.”

Clarisse offered him a hand up. He took it. She hauled him to his feet with practiced strength and didn’t let go right away. She squeezed his fingers hard, once, the way siblings did when words were too mushy.

“Thanks for not being weird about lunch,” she muttered, low enough that only he could hear. “I felt bad ditching. I just... he said I was good. Like really good. No one older ever says that without adding ‘for a girl’ or ‘for your age.’”

Percy’s chest ached, but in a good way this time. Protective, not possessive.

“You are really good,” he said quietly. “No qualifier. You’ve always been really good. I’m glad he sees it too.”

Clarisse looked at him sharply, searching his face for sarcasm. Finding none, she nodded stiffly. “Okay. Good. Because you’re... you know. You’re my sparring brother. You can’t get all mopey if I learn stuff from other people.”

“I’m not mopey,” Percy said. “I’m... practicing my supportive face. How’s this?”

He made an exaggeratedly earnest expression. Clarisse snorted and shoved him.

“That’s terrible. You look constipated.”

“Gods, not you too.”

---

Dinner was loud and bright and smelled like barbecue. The sun sank low over the Sound, turning the water orange. The campfire pit was already being stacked for later.

Percy ended up squished on a bench between Annabeth and Clarisse, with Thalia across from them and Silena and Beckendorf crowded in on the ends. Evan was a few tables over with the older campers, telling a story that involved a lot of arm gestures. Every so often, Clarisse glanced over, listening.

But she didn’t leave. She stayed. She stole Percy’s fries. She argued with Annabeth about whether a phalanx could beat a testudo. She let Silena braid a tiny piece of her hair.

Halfway through dinner, Evan walked by on his way to return his tray. He stopped behind Clarisse and tapped her helmet, which she’d set on the bench.

“Good work today, La Rue,” he said. “Same time tomorrow? I want to drill that counter you showed Percy. The younger kids could learn it.”

Clarisse straightened up, glowing. “Yeah. Definitely. I’ll be there.”

“Nice.” Evan nodded to Percy. “You took that fall well. Most people get salty.”

“I’ve had practice,” Percy said. “Clarisse drops me a lot. It’s kind of our love language. Sibling love language. Platonic. Familial. Very non-weird.”

He realized he was rambling. Annabeth kicked him under the table.

Evan just laughed. “I get it. Blood siblings, camp siblings; both matter. I’ve got three younger brothers back home and about ten here. It’s the same.” He clapped Clarisse’s shoulder once more. “See you tomorrow, kid.”

He walked off.

Clarisse watched him go, then turned to Percy. “You were cool about that. Thanks.”

Percy shrugged, trying to play it cool and probably failing. “He’s all right. For an old guy with a scar.”

“He’s eighteen,” Clarisse said.

“Ancient,” Percy said solemnly.

Clarisse threw a fry at him. He caught it in his mouth. Everyone cheered.

Silena leaned across the table, eyes sparkling. “So, Percy. How’s the jealousy? Cured? Or do we need to schedule another intervention with lemonade?”

“I was never jealous,” Percy said with great dignity. “I was... experiencing protective brother feelings related to belonging, not possessing.”

Thalia choked on her drink. Annabeth beamed with pride.

“Oh, he learned the vocabulary,” Thalia said. “He’s healed. He’s enlightened.”

“I’m growing,” Percy corrected. “Like a fungus.”

“Like a fungus,” Clarisse agreed, ruffling his hair hard enough to hurt. “My fungus brother.”

“Affectionate,” Percy said, fixing his hair.

After dinner, they migrated to the campfire. The sky deepened to purple. Stars pricked through. The fire crackled to life, throwing warm light across tired, happy faces. Someone from Apollo played guitar. The Hunters sang a bawdy song about Artemis outshooting Orion that made Chiron sigh deeply.

Percy sat on a log with Annabeth on one side and Clarisse on the other, Thalia perched on the log behind them with her legs dangling, Silena leaning against Beckendorf’s shoulder nearby. The heat of the fire warmed their faces. The night air cooled their backs.

It was perfect in the way only ordinary camp nights could be perfect.

Clarisse was quiet, watching the flames. After a while, she leaned her head against Percy’s shoulder, just for a second. Heavy, trusting. Older-sister tired after a big day.

Percy went very still, then relaxed. He didn’t tease her. He just let her.

On his other side, Annabeth noticed. She smiled softly and reached over, finding Percy’s hand on the log between them. She laced her fingers through his, the way she had on the beach, the way they remembered from their old life together.

I’m here. You’re not alone. You still matter.

Percy squeezed back. He looked at Annabeth, who was watching the fire with her head tilted, humming along to the guitar. He looked at Clarisse, who had already lifted her head and was pretending she hadn’t just been soft for a moment, yelling at a Hermes kid to stop hogging the marshmallows. He looked at Thalia, who caught his eye and winked, like, see? You belonged all along.

The tight, itchy feeling was gone. In its place was something warm and steady.

He had a girlfriend who held his hand when his brain got loud. He had a sister who knocked him into the sand to show she loved him. He had his girlfriend’s older sister, his friend, who told him Jason stories to remind him he wasn’t alone in feeling weird. He had Silena teasing him because she loved him.

There was room. There had always been room. Love wasn’t a bunk bed. It was the whole cabin.

The song ended. Applause rippled. Chiron stood to give the nightly announcements. Percy didn’t really listen. He was too busy memorizing this: firelight on Annabeth’s hair, Clarisse’s laugh, Thalia’s boots swinging behind him, Silena’s voice rising above the crowd.

Later, when the fire burned low and campers drifted toward their cabins in sleepy clusters, Percy walked back with his sister and his girlfriend. Clarisse went ahead a little, trading insults with a sleepy Ares training partner. Annabeth stayed beside Percy, their hands swinging loosely between them, still linked.

“You good?” Annabeth asked quietly.

Percy thought about it. Really thought about it.

“Yeah,” he said. “I’m good. Thanks for... you know. The talk. The hand. All of it.”

Annabeth squeezed his hand once more before letting go at the fork where the paths split to Poseidon and Zeus. “Anytime. That’s what loving you means.”

“Even when I’m being an idiot?”

“Especially when you’re being an idiot. It’s in the contract.”

Clarisse waited for him at the Poseidon path, arms crossed, trying to look impatient and failing to hide a yawn. “Are you coming, Fungus? Or are you going to hold hands all night?”

Percy jogged to catch up. “Jealous?”

“Of hand-holding? No.” Clarisse slung an arm around his shoulders, brief and fierce. “I’ve got my own people. But you’re still my favorite dummy to practice on.”

“I’ll take it,” Percy said.

They walked the rest of the way together, bickering about tomorrow’s drills, the stars bright overhead, the camp quiet and safe around them.

Little brother. Older sisters. Girlfriend. Friends who felt like family. All of it counted.

And Percy finally understood the difference between wanting to be the only one and knowing you would always be one of the ones who mattered.

He slept that night without drooling.

Well, with only a little drool.

Annabeth took a picture for evidence anyway.
