## Chapter 92: Jealousy II

The morning bell at Camp Half-Blood never rang so much as it breathed.

It started low over the hills, a conch blown from the Big House porch, and rolled down through the cabins and the strawberry fields. By the second note, the camp was already moving.

Percy Jackson woke the way he always woke at camp, with one arm hanging off the bunk and his cheek stuck to the pillow and his brain three seconds behind his body.

The Poseidon cabin was cool and blue and quiet in the mornings. The fountain in the corner trickled. The shells on the walls caught the early light and threw it back in soft ripples. The bunk above him creaked.

“Perce,” said Annabeth’s voice from the doorway, crisp and amused and fully awake. “You drooled again.”

Percy sat up too fast and wiped his mouth. “Did not.”

“Did too.” His girlfriend leaned against the doorframe with her arms crossed, her blonde curls pulled back, her camp shirt already tucked, her dagger already at her hip. “You have pillow face, too.”

Percy groaned and flopped back down. “Five more minutes.”

“Nope. Clarisse said if you’re late to drills again she’s going to use you as the demonstration dummy.”

That got him up.

He rolled out of bed and flexed both hands before he reached for his shirt. The faint prickling from the storm had been gone since yesterday's morning check, and Will had told him to report it if it came back. Percy opened and closed his fists once. The skin felt ordinary. He let his hands fall instead of testing them again.

Silena's portrait of him was still laid flat in his pack, protected between a towel and his notebook. He had meant to put it on the wall after the tide pools, then the return count and Will's check had carried them to dinner. Annabeth stood at the door with her tote over one shoulder. The watercolor she had received from Silena was wrapped in a clean cloth inside it; the little scallop shell Percy had given her made a soft click against the key in her pocket when she shifted.

"You brought the whole beach with you," Percy said.

"Only the parts that fit in a bag." Annabeth glanced at his pack. "Are you taking the portrait to the arena?"

"No. I want to find a nail for it before Clarisse tells me I'm late." He settled the strap over his shoulder. "If I take it now, she'll make me carry it through drills."

"She would be right to." Annabeth's mouth tilted. "Come on, boyfriend. The conch already called us."

They headed out toward the pavilion. At the Big House, the storm board was still posted beside the map. The canoe lake remained closed while the silt settled; the soft anchor was still marked out of service; the lowest strawberry strip still needed dry ground before anyone replanted it. A line for the hill ward had a repair crew's initials beside it, but no completion mark. The crosswind rehearsal for Malcolm's flag code remained blank. Percy didn't stop to ask anyone to sign off. Those names belonged to the people assigned to the work.

Will was at the infirmary porch with a fresh roll of gauze. Percy reported that the tingling had not returned. Will asked him to squeeze two fingers, checked that the grip matched, and released his hand.

"Good. Keep telling me if anything changes. This doesn't prove what caused it," Will said. "You can do the regular drills. Stop if it comes back."

Thalia was waiting beside the door, right palm wrapped in a clean dressing. She had traded her bracers for a loose jacket and kept the sore hand close against her side. Will lifted the edge of the wrap only far enough to check the skin, then replaced it.

"Dry, and still tender," he said. "No sparring today. You can help from the sidelines if you keep that hand out of the work. I'll look again tomorrow morning."

"I can supervise a climbing wall without personally wrestling the lava," Thalia said.

"That is the plan." Will gave her a look that said he expected her to follow it.

Thalia rolled her eyes, but she nodded. Percy caught the small exhale she let out when Will stepped back. He knew she wanted to be useful; sitting out a drill would take more discipline than joining one.

"Ready?" Annabeth asked.

"Now I'm ready," Percy said, and the three of them crossed the green toward the arena.

“Left flank,” she said.

“Shield wall anchors to the right,” Percy answered automatically, yawning.

“Good. And if the Ares cabin breaks formation?”

“Yell for Clarisse and get out of the way.”

Annabeth grinned. “Exactly.”

The training arena was already crowded when they arrived. Clarisse La Rue stood in the center of it with her spear over her shoulder. She looked sharp and focused and completely in her element.

Percy felt a familiar swell of pride when he saw her. She had stopped being just the scary spear fighter and had become someone he knew.

His older half-sister. By blood, by their father, by the family they were making in Cabin Three. The kind of sister you teased mercilessly and defended instantly.

“Jackson!” Clarisse barked. “Nice of you to join us. Did your pillow fight back?”

“My girlfriend is a traitor,” Percy said, jerking his thumb at Annabeth. “She woke me up with insults.”

“Good. Someone needs to keep you humble.” Clarisse tossed him a practice sword. He caught it one-handed. “You’re with me for warm-ups. Try not to cry this time.”

Annabeth snorted and headed for the sidelines with her notebook, already sketching the drill layout. She liked to watch first, analyze, then jump in and dismantle everyone with terrifying precision.

Morning drills began with a chalk grid scored into the sand. Clarisse had drawn it herself the day before, Percy knew, because the squares were crooked at the far end where the ground sloped and she had refused Annabeth's offer to redraw them. The slope mattered. Campers did not get to choose flat ground when a monster came at them.

Clarisse met Percy at the first line and tapped her practice sword against his twice. Ready. Don't complain when you lose.

He returned the taps. “You changed the last square.”

“It was wrong yesterday.”

“You said the line was wrong yesterday.”

“And now it's less wrong. Try to keep up.”

They started at half speed. Clarisse called a direction; Percy crossed the grid and set his feet. She made him repeat the route when he put too much weight on his front leg. The next time, she came at him with the spear and he had to turn without stepping outside the chalk. It made him think about the ground instead of her weapon, which was the point.

“Again,” she said.

“You always say that.”

“Because you always think one good rep means you're done.”

He adjusted his stance. She came in low, and he moved back before he should have. The heel of his shoe skidded on the slope. Clarisse stopped the spear short and tapped the wooden shaft against his shoulder.

“You gave up the square.”

“You were trying to hit me.”

“In a real fight, yes.” She pointed to the chalk. “Don't retreat off the ground you're supposed to hold. Turn here. Keep your balance and make me go around you.”

Percy looked at the sloping line. “You want to be the obstacle?”

“I want you to notice there's an obstacle.” She tossed him the shield. “You can complain while you reset.”

He tried the pattern again. This time, when she drove him toward the low edge, he pivoted across the line, kept his shield between them, and made her change angle. Clarisse's mouth twitched.

“Better.”

“That means I win, right?”

“It means you stop falling over for at least two minutes.”

They ran the grid once more, then switched sides. Clarisse took the steeper route and still crossed without breaking her rhythm. Percy had seen her do harder things, but watching her teach the line gave the movement a different shape. She paused when a younger camper wandered too close to the grid, showed him where to stand, and repeated the turn at a speed he could follow. She didn't make a joke when he missed it. She set the spear down and marked the pivot in the sand with her heel.

The camper tried again. Clarisse let him finish the whole pattern before she corrected one thing.

“You know you could just tell me to go slow,” Percy said when they reached the water bucket.

“I could. You'd ignore me.”

“Maybe.” He drank, then nodded toward the younger group. “You didn't have to stop for him.”

“He couldn't see the turn from where he was standing.” She rinsed her hands and shook off the water. “If the first thing he learns is that he can't do it, he won't ask me again.”

Percy looked back at the crooked square, where the camper was tracing Clarisse's heel mark with his own. “That's good teaching.”

“It's good dirt.” Clarisse nudged his boot with hers. “And you're easy to teach because you fall down a lot.”

“There it is.”

“You missed a step.”

She tapped his sword twice. Percy answered with two taps of his own. The whole exchange took less than a breath, familiar enough that he didn't think about it. Then Clarisse sent him to fetch the shields, and he jogged across the arena while she counted the next group into place.

“Nice,” Percy said quietly to Annabeth during water break. “She’s good with them.”

Annabeth nodded, watching Clarisse with open admiration. “She’s been practicing that. Being a teacher, not just a fighter. Chiron noticed too.”

Percy grinned around his water cup. “Our Clarisse is growing up.”

“Our Clarisse will kill you if she hears you say that,” Annabeth said.

“Worth it.”

That was when the gate at the far end of the arena opened and an older camper walked in.

Evan was eighteen, broad-shouldered, and wearing a faded camp shirt with the sleeves cut off. A long scar ran down his left forearm. He carried a duffel over one shoulder and wore a real bronze sword at his hip.

“Who’s that?” Percy asked.

Annabeth flipped a page in her notebook. “Evan. Son of Ares. He was here three summers ago, then went to that semester program out west. Wilderness survival and monster combat intensive. He got back last night. Chiron asked him to help with advanced sparring today.”

Evan dropped his duffel by the rack and secured his real sword before he took a practice weapon. Chiron met him by the fence in his wheelchair form. Their greeting was brief; then Chiron pointed to the two advanced lanes and the waiting group.

“Evan will help me run the advanced rotation this morning,” Chiron called. “I’m supervising. Keep the practice weapons in the marked rings, ask before changing partners, and stop if a weapon breaks or anyone calls a halt.”

“Understood,” Evan said. He took a moment to learn the layout and count the campers before approaching Clarisse, who had just finished resetting the dummies. She pulled on her helmet and rolled her shoulders.

“La Rue?” he asked. “Chiron says you've been working the sweep in this lane. He called you one of the best spear fighters in your age group. Are you free for a round?”

Clarisse blinked, caught off guard. Praise always did that to her. Insults bounced off. Compliments stuck. “I’m decent,” she said carefully.

Evan laughed. “Decent. Right. I watched your last two reps. Mind if I spar you?”

A little ripple went through the watching campers.

Clarisse stood a little straighter. “Sure. You’ll be disappointed, but sure.”

Evan glanced toward Chiron. “With your permission, I'll call this one for the group.”

Chiron nodded. “One demonstration round, then rotate.”

“You heard her,” Evan called to the circle. “La Rue says she’s going to wipe the floor with me. Everyone gather up.”

The campers laughed and crowded closer.

They sparred at a controlled pace, fast enough to make the choices matter but slow enough that either could call a stop. Evan was bigger and stronger, but Clarisse was faster. He tested her guard with two high feints; she gave him the opening he expected, then swept his lead leg and brought her spear tip to his chest. Point. The circle erupted.

Evan stared down at the spear tip, then broke into a wide grin. He held up both hands. “Okay. Okay. Hype is real.”

Clarisse lowered her spear, breathing hard. She reset her feet before she answered. “Lucky shot.”

“Was not luck,” Evan said. “That was textbook with flair. Where did you learn that sweep? No one here used to fight like that.”

“I drilled it all winter,” Clarisse said. “Annabeth helped me break down the footwork.”

Evan glanced over at Annabeth and nodded with respect, then looked back to Clarisse. “Well, it shows. You read the feint before I finished it. How long have you drilled that?”

“All winter,” Clarisse said. “Every morning.”

“And I spent my first winter trying not to stab myself.” Evan turned his practice spear in his hands to show her the grip he'd been using. “Can you show me where the sweep starts? I want to see how you're loading your back foot.”

Clarisse demonstrated without slowing the movement down. Evan watched, then tried it on his own. His heel landed a little too far out.

“There,” Clarisse said. “You gave away the direction before your shoulders moved.”

Evan tried again, smaller this time. The angle improved.

“That's better,” she said. “The foot does the work. The spear only makes it obvious.”

“Would you teach that to the group?” Evan asked. “Not the whole move at once. The setup and the stop point.”

Clarisse glanced toward Chiron, who was checking the next lane. “If they keep their weapons down until the first pass is clear.”

“That's your call.” Evan stepped back to give her the center of the ring.

She did not look toward Percy when she started. She gave the group the first half of the pattern, then set her own spear across the sand as a boundary line. The younger campers were asked to walk it with empty hands first. One of them rushed the pivot and nearly stepped on the practice weapon. Clarisse raised a fist. The group stopped.

“Reset,” she said. “No one gets points for stepping on a spear.”

The camper's face reddened. Clarisse did not laugh. She pulled the weapon farther out of the path and showed the foot placement again. When the camper got the turn right, she nodded once and moved on.

Evan followed the sequence with the next pair, asking before he changed their pace. He asked Clarisse to call the stop points, and when she corrected the angle of his feint, he changed it without arguing. Percy had not known the older camper before this morning, but he could see why Clarisse was listening. Evan did not try to take the lesson away from her. He kept giving her a larger piece of it.

“All right,” Chiron called from the fence. “Rotate partners. La Rue, stay if you want the advanced lane, or take the younger group through the footwork.”

Clarisse looked at the waiting group and then at the ring. “I'll stay for this rotation. They can use the sweep after water.”

Evan moved toward the practice ring with her, leaving a clear pace between them. Clarisse looked down to map out the next move, then set her feet and tested the angle without waiting for another compliment.

Percy felt an odd twist in his stomach. Evan was talking through the footwork, and Clarisse listened with her whole attention. When he wanted to show a grip change, he demonstrated it on his own spear and waited while she copied him. Clarisse tested the new position, then corrected his wrist in turn. They were working, nothing more, but Percy still felt the gap where their usual warm-up had been.

Evan showed her how the altered grip changed the angle of the feint. Clarisse tried it once, then asked him to repeat it against a shield instead of another spear. He fetched one from the rack and held it at the height she requested. Her first attempt glanced off the rim. The second slipped past it.

“That makes the turn easier,” she said, “but it leaves my wrist open if you bring the spear back.”

“You're right.” Evan lowered the practice weapons. “Can you keep your thumb behind the guard and change the angle a little?”

Clarisse tested the adjustment. “That works better.”

Percy had not known the difference between the two angles. Clarisse had. She was not simply being praised into a new friendship; she was choosing which parts of the lesson to keep. He could see why she wanted to try it, and why she wanted to try it with him later.

Clarisse had always had room for a joke in the middle of a drill. She and Percy usually traded the same two taps of their practice swords before the first round: one for ready, one for don't complain when you lose. This time she was already in the ring with Evan when Percy finished his water. The ritual had disappeared without anyone deciding to take it away.

Percy took a long drink of water to wash the feeling away. It didn’t work.

“Again!” Evan called. “Same sequence, but faster. Ready, La Rue?”

“Ready,” Clarisse said, and they went again, wood cracking against wood, dust puffing up around their feet.

Percy held his own practice sword at his side. For a second he considered walking into the ring and calling for the usual warm-up. Then the bell for rotation rang, and Clarisse moved to the next partner with Evan. Percy set the sword back on the rack. Nobody had stopped him from joining; the decision was his. He didn't know how to tell the difference between respecting the drill and standing outside it because he was afraid of the answer.

Percy found his hands curling into fists.

Annabeth glanced at him sideways. “You okay?”

“Yeah,” Percy said too quickly. “Just hot.”

“It’s seventy degrees.”

“I run warm.”

Annabeth raised one eyebrow, the exact eyebrow she had used to dismantle Percy’s lies in both their lives. But she didn’t push it. Not yet. She just made a small note in her notebook that definitely was not about battle formations.

---

By lunch, Evan and Clarisse had spent most of the morning in the same drill circle.

Percy kept trying to find a reason the morning had gone wrong. If Evan had been cruel, or careless, or trying to show Clarisse up, Percy would have known what to do. But Evan had followed Chiron's rules and listened when Clarisse corrected him. The only thing Percy could point to was that his sister had been interested in another person's lesson.

Chiron had given Evan a clear job. At lunch, he asked Clarisse if she wanted to join the older campers who had taken the advanced rotation. Clarisse looked once toward her usual spot under the big oak, where Percy had already sat down. She lifted her chin at him, then carried her tray to the other table. She didn't apologize. Percy knew she had chosen the seat; knowing it didn't stop the empty place beside him from catching his eye.

Evan turned his paper napkin so she could see the rough footwork he had drawn between bites. Clarisse pointed out where he had reversed the lead foot. He took the correction, redrew it, and asked whether she meant the inside or outside angle. She answered by shifting her tray aside and tracing the line with the handle of her fork. The older campers added questions. Clarisse was smiling now, not because Evan had praised her but because she was arguing over a drill and someone was taking her answer seriously.

Percy knew he could have gone over. There was room on the bench. He stayed where he was, not wanting to interrupt and not wanting Clarisse to notice that he wanted to. That was his choice too, though it felt less like one while he stared at the place she'd left.

Percy stared at his hamburger.

Silena Beauregard slid onto the bench across from him, her eyes bright with mischief. She took one look at Percy’s face and lit up.

“Oh, this is good,” she said.

“What?” Percy said.

“You.” Silena pointed her fork at him. “That face. That is the face of a boy who is sulking.”

“I’m not sulking,” Percy said. “I’m eating.”

“You’re stabbing your burger with a fork,” Annabeth observed from beside him. “That’s not eating. That’s burger murder.”

Percy put the fork down. “I’m fine.”

Silena leaned forward, chin in her hands. “You’re jealous.”

Percy choked on his lemonade. “What? No.”

“Yes,” Silena said delightedly. “You are so jealous. Look at you. Your little sparring buddy found a new big cool mentor and you’re sitting here making that kicked-puppy face.”

“I don’t make a kicked-puppy face,” Percy said.

“You absolutely make a kicked-puppy face,” Annabeth said. “It’s your signature expression. Right after confused and right before hungry.”

“I’m not jealous,” Percy insisted. “Why would I be jealous? Evan’s cool. It’s good for Clarisse. She deserves attention for how hard she works.”

Silena and Annabeth exchanged a look.

“He says, while glaring a hole through the back of Evan’s head,” Silena said.

“I’m not glaring.”

“You are glaring,” Annabeth said. “I can feel the heat from here.”

Percy looked down at his tray. Across the pavilion, he could hear Evan’s laugh and then Clarisse’s laugh, louder than usual, a little nervous, a little thrilled. He felt that twist in his stomach again, tighter this time.

“It’s stupid,” he muttered. “I don’t know why I feel like this.”

Silena’s teasing smile eased. “You can be glad for her and still miss your usual partner,” she said. “Those can both be true.”

“I’m her little brother,” Percy said automatically, then frowned. “I mean. She’s older. Technically she’s the one who looks out for me. But I want to do it for her too.”

“Like what?” Annabeth asked quietly.

Percy struggled to find the words. “She’s my sister. Real sister, even if I’m still getting used to saying it. I want her to have someone notice how hard she works. I also want to know she still wants me there. And part of me keeps trying to make that into a safety problem, because I know what to do with a safety problem.”

The last sentence came out smaller than he intended.

Silena’s expression went from playful to genuinely tender. “Oh, Percy.”

Annabeth was quiet for a moment, studying him with those sharp gray eyes that saw too much. Then she bumped her shoulder against his.

“Come on,” she said, standing up with her tray. “Walk with me. Bring your burger murder fork.”

“Where?”

“Down to the beach. You need air and you need your wise girlfriend.”

“You’re not that much older than me,” Percy said, but he stood up anyway.

“I’m wiser by like four years,” Annabeth said. “Come on.”

Silena blew them a kiss. “Have fun, lovebirds! Sentimental seagulls! Squawk!”

“SILENA,” Percy and Annabeth said together, identically exasperated, which only made Silena laugh harder.

They dumped their trays and headed down the hill toward the water. The path was dappled with sunlight.

Percy felt his shoulders drop a little with every step away from the crowd.

---

Percy and Annabeth sat on their usual rock, the flat one shaped like a turtle shell, close enough that their knees touched.

Annabeth tucked her legs up. Percy turned a smooth stone in his fingers, feeling its cool flat edge. The shell in her pocket clicked softly when she shifted on the rock.

“So,” she said, “do you want to tell me what you were thinking when you watched them?”

“I was thinking I wanted him to stop being good at teaching her.” Percy skipped the stone. It bounced once and sank. “Which is a stupid thing to want.”

“It's a fairly honest thing to want.”

“I like that he sees what she's good at. I just didn't expect him to see it in five minutes. We have our routine. We do the two taps, we warm up, she makes fun of my footwork. Today she had someone else to ask.”

“And you missed her.” Annabeth didn't dress it up as a diagnosis. She watched his face and let the words sit there.

Percy drew a line in the sand with the stone. “I think I also kept telling myself I was worried about her. That he might be messing with her or that she might get hurt. But Chiron picked him, and Evan's been careful. He asked her what she wanted to practice. Clarisse can tell him no.”

“She can.” Annabeth leaned her shoulder against his. “Doesn't mean you can't miss being her partner.”

Percy nodded once. “Do you ever get that? When I have somebody else?”

Annabeth gave him a look. “You mean like Tyson?”

“Yeah.”

“In the life we remember, when you and Tyson started staying up late trading whale noises, I got jealous. He made you laugh in a way I couldn't. I thought I was losing time with you.” She paused, thumb rubbing the edge of the shell through the cloth of her pocket. “I challenged him to a strategy game and lost. I told myself I was testing him. Mostly, I wanted to win at something you two shared.”

“You never told me.”

“I was embarrassed. And I didn't want him to have to make himself smaller so I'd feel safe.”

Percy let the stone drop beside his shoe. “I don't want Clarisse to have less. I want to know we still have our thing.”

He rubbed the sand off his fingers. “She's my sister, but I didn't know her when we were little. I met her after she'd already built a life. Sometimes I still feel like I have to earn the space I got.”

Annabeth looked at him. “You don't have to win a drill to count as her brother.”

“I know.” Percy watched a wave push a fringe of foam up the shore. “I just don't always know what to do with the fact that she has a lot of people and I came into the story late.”

“You can be new to each other and still be family.”

“Then tell her you missed it. You don't have to make Evan the problem to say that.”

The answer was so ordinary that Percy almost laughed. Instead he looked down at the marks their shoes had made in the sand. “I can do that.”

Annabeth held out her hand, palm up. Percy took it. Her fingers fit between his with the easy familiarity of a choice they had already made and kept making. The little shell pressed against her pocket seam.

“You can tell Clarisse you missed her,” she said. “You don't have to perform being fine with it, and you don't get to decide her answer for her.”

Percy smiled at her. “My girlfriend, everybody.”

“Your girlfriend who is telling you to ask a direct question.” She bumped his shoulder. “Ask Clarisse what she wants from you. She'll tell you.”

“And if she says she wants me to stop being a jerk?”

“Then you stop.”

He skipped another stone. It made three clean jumps before it disappeared. Annabeth counted them under her breath, then squeezed his hand once before letting go. “Come on. Thalia's supposed to be at the wall.”

---

They found Thalia up by the climbing wall, supervising from the dry side of the controls and arguing with the lava.

Or rather, arguing with the campers who were supposed to be monitoring the lava.

“No, no, no,” Thalia said, left hand braced on her hip, her black hair with its silver streaks pulled back, her punk bracelets jangling. The right palm stayed wrapped against her jacket. “You don’t open the valve when someone’s on the north face. Are you trying to make soup?”

The two young campers manning the controls looked sheepish. “Sorry, Thalia.”

“It’s fine. Just watch the flags. Red means hold.” The camper nearest the valve took his hand off it. At the north face, a climber waited on a ledge, one boot braced against the stone. Thalia checked the red cloth, then pointed with her left hand toward the control station. The climber stayed put until the campers lowered the hold flag and called the lane clear.

“Good,” Thalia said. “You wait for the signal, even if I say it first. If the person at the controls isn't looking at the climber, the flag is what counts.” She kept her wrapped palm against her jacket as the two campers reset the valve. “Again from the top. Slow this time.”

Only after the sequence was back in order did she turn and see Percy and Annabeth approaching. Her fierce expression eased into a grin. “Hey, look, it’s Jackson and Grace. You two look suspiciously thoughtful. Did Silena make you talk about feelings again?”

“Silena started it,” Percy said. “Annabeth finished it.”

“Classic combo.” Thalia stepped down from the platform and hugged them with her left arm, keeping the wrapped hand clear. “What’s up?”

Percy hesitated. It was one thing to admit jealousy to Annabeth on an empty beach. It was another to admit it to Thalia, who was cooler than everyone and had never seemed jealous of anything in her life.

But Annabeth nudged him. “Tell her. She’s got the Jason story.”

Thalia’s eyebrows shot up. “Oh, we’re doing jealousy? Sibling edition?”

Percy sighed. “How did you know?”

“Because Annabeth has that look she gets when she's found the hinge in an argument, and you look like you're trying to decide whether to be annoyed with her for finding it.” Thalia motioned toward the shade of a pine tree. They sat in the grass.

So Percy spilled. He told Thalia about Evan and Clarisse, the spar and lunch, the two-tap warm-up he had missed, and the talk on the beach. Thalia listened without interrupting, her expression shifting from amusement to recognition.

When he finished, she let out a breath through her nose. “Yeah. I've had that feeling.”

“You felt like that?” Percy asked, surprised. “You? But you’re... you.”

“I’m me, which means I handled it badly at first,” Thalia said. She turned the pinecone over in her left hand. “You want the Jason version?”

Percy nodded. Annabeth settled back against the tree trunk. She had heard this story before, but she liked hearing Thalia tell it.

“Jason was a toddler when we were separated. I have memories of carrying him when he got tired and of him calling me Lia. I held onto those hard enough to think they'd tell me what being his sister was supposed to look like.” She glanced at Annabeth. “You remember the Iris relay. The first time we saw him again, it lasted a few minutes. He told us he was safe. He had a friend, Reyna, who shared her bread with him when his own burned. I was glad somebody did that for him. I was also furious that it had been somebody else.”

Annabeth nodded. Percy remembered the call too: the jumpy rainbow light, Jason's small face, the few minutes that had been enough to know he was alive. Thalia's ache in those seconds was hers, but the call had been shared.

“Afterward, I sat down to write him,” Thalia continued. “I kept starting with questions about everything I had missed. Then I tried to write the ordinary things we could actually share: the rain, Annabeth's roof sketches, the sign Percy had made, Clarisse's advice about shields. I wanted him to know what our camp was like now. I didn't want my first letter to make him responsible for the years we hadn't had together.”

“Then the Roman courier approved the supervised week here,” Thalia went on. “Jason was ten. He got to Cabin One and asked where to hang his cloak. I showed him. I checked his cot twice after lights-out to make sure he was still there. I kept catching myself wanting him to remember the toddler I carried. He didn't owe me that. He was a kid with a Roman haircut, Roman habits, and a life I'd only heard pieces of. I had to let the actual boy stand in front of me.”

She rolled the pinecone in her left hand, then let it rest in her palm. “At first I wanted to fill every silence with a story about when he was little. I thought if I could prove I remembered him, nobody could take the big-sister part away. But he didn't need a proof. He needed a sister who would ask him where he wanted the cloak and listen when he told her about his camp.”

Percy watched a climber wait on the ledge while the campers at the valve checked the flags. “Did it stop feeling weird?”

“No. Not all at once.” Thalia's mouth tipped sideways. “I still felt the lost years. I still wanted more time. But the week wasn't a contest with the people who'd been there before me. It was a week with Jason. We could start with that.”

Annabeth lowered her gaze to the bandage. “And I had to remember he was my brother too, even though the childhood stories were yours. I could hear them and care about them without pretending I remembered them.”

“Exactly.” Thalia looked back at Percy. “Clarisse can learn from Evan and still have her own history with you. He can teach her something you don't know. You can ask her to show you. If you miss your warm-up, say that. Don't turn it into a test she has to pass before you'll be happy for her.”

Percy rubbed his thumb over a seam in his practice glove. “What if she says she likes training with him better?”

“Then you hear her answer and find out what she means.” Thalia kept her wrapped hand tucked in. “Maybe she wants more advanced drills. Maybe she wants you there too. You don't know until you ask her, and she doesn't have to make the answer comfortable for you.”

“That's not very reassuring.”

“It's more useful than pretending I know what your sister will say.” Her grin came back. “You have a job. Go be her brother. Learn the sweep. Let her correct you. And don't glare at Evan for being good at the assignment Chiron gave him.”

“How do you know about the hamburger?” Percy asked.

“Silena Iris-messaged me,” Thalia said.

Annabeth laughed. “Of course she did.”

Percy groaned. “Gods. Is the whole camp talking about this?”

“Only the people who love you,” Annabeth said, patting his knee. “Which is, unfortunately for your privacy, a lot of people.”

Thalia stood and pushed up from the ground with her left hand. Before leaving, she called to the counselor walking up the path and gave a quick handoff on the wall: the north-face climber had cleared, the valve had been reset, and the two campers needed another slow run through the flag sequence. The counselor took the station. Thalia checked that her wrap was still dry, then nodded toward the arena. “Come on. Afternoon free spar is starting soon. Let's watch your sister teach a sweep and practice not looking like you swallowed a lemon when someone compliments her.”

“I didn't look like I swallowed a lemon,” Percy protested.

“You did,” Annabeth said.

“Fine. I'll work on my face.”

“That’s the spirit.”

---

The afternoon sun slanted gold across the arena when they got back. The sand was warm underfoot. A small crowd had already gathered around the central circle.

Thalia stayed behind the rope with her right palm wrapped and dry. She used her left hand to point out the safe lane and left the practice weapons in the rack. Will had said no sparring; she followed the limit, even if watching from the edge clearly took effort.

Clarisse was in the middle of it, helmet off now, face flushed, demonstrating the sweep she had used that morning to a cluster of younger campers. Evan stood beside her, arms crossed, nodding along as she explained.

“And then you shift your weight here,” Clarisse was saying, demonstrating slowly, “so you don’t overextend. Like this. See?”

The younger kids nodded earnestly. One of them tried it and stumbled. Clarisse steadied him.

“Better,” she said. “You’ll get it. Just drill it slow first.”

She set the group into pairs and moved them to the chalk marks, keeping enough space between their practice weapons. Evan called the steps from the side. Clarisse adjusted the pace when one pair rushed the turn, and asked them to walk the line without weapons before they tried again. The group did not look like a team yet; they kept glancing at the next pair to see whether they were behind. Clarisse stopped the rotation and told them to watch their own feet.

“The point is not to be fast first,” she said. “It's to be in the right place when you're ready to move fast.”

One of the campers pointed at the rack. “When do we get the spears?”

“When you can make the turn without looking down at your feet,” Clarisse said. “If you're watching the weapon, you won't see the boundary. We'll add them when the footwork stays inside the chalk.”

Evan looked to her before answering the next question. “That's a good test.”

Evan repeated the instruction to the other pair. He did not add a joke or take the explanation over. When one camper raised a hand to ask where to put his shield, Clarisse showed him the space without making him feel foolish. Percy had seen her teach in the morning. It still surprised him how carefully she watched for the moment somebody stopped following.

Percy hung back at the edge, watching. The tight feeling flickered again when Evan said something that made Clarisse laugh. Percy could have called it concern and waited for someone to give him a reason to step in. Instead he noticed that Clarisse had chosen the drill, Evan kept to the rules, and no one was asking Percy to intervene. He was standing outside because he was afraid of the answer if he walked in.

He had missed the two-tap start. He could still go inside.

He took a breath and walked in.

“Hey,” he said.

Clarisse looked up, surprised. “Hey. Where’d you go at lunch? You ditched.”

“Needed air,” Percy said honestly. “And my girlfriend dragged me to the beach for a talk.”

Annabeth waved from the sidelines. “It was a good lecture.”

“It was okay,” Percy said, and Annabeth threw a pinecone at him.

Clarisse smirked, but her eyes stayed on him for a second, checking his expression. “You missed my rematch with Evan. I lost this time. He cheats. He does this annoying thing with his elbow.”

“I do not cheat,” Evan protested mildly. “I leverage experience.”

“You leverage cheating,” Clarisse said, but she was smiling.

“That sweep this morning was awesome,” Percy said. The words came out a little rushed. “I mean it. I've sparred you a hundred times and never seen you move that fast. You read him perfectly.”

Clarisse blinked. The wariness dropped. Something warm flickered across her face. “Thanks,” she said quietly. “I’ve been working on it.”

“I know. Annabeth told me. I’m proud of you.”

Clarisse's mouth twitched. “Shut up.”

“No.”

“Shut up, Jackson.”

“Make me, La Rue.”

Evan watched this exchange with an amused smile. He offered Percy his hand. “You’re Percy, right? Clarisse talks about you. Mostly threats, but affectionate threats.”

Percy shook his hand. Evan’s grip was firm. “Yeah. That’s me. Affectionate threat recipient.”

“Evan was showing me that outside feint,” Clarisse said. “Percy, you should try it. It catches the way you overcommit with your right—”

“I do not overcommit,” Percy said.

“You absolutely overcommit,” Clarisse and Annabeth said together.

“Betrayed by my sister and my girlfriend,” Percy sighed, but stepped into the circle. “Fine. Show me.”

Evan set two practice spears on the rack and drew the feint with the toe of his boot: a short step that invited the guard to turn, followed by a change of line before the opponent could settle. He demonstrated it on the padded post at a walking pace. The first pass looked almost too small to matter.

“Where did you learn it?” Clarisse asked.

“The field program. We did more work on uneven ground than in an arena. On a slope or a trail, everybody watches their footing, and a fake opening can pull their eyes off the hand that's actually moving.” Evan drew a second line beside the first, then scuffed part of it out. “It won't work everywhere. Loose ground can make the turn a bad idea. You still have to look at the space you're fighting in.”

Clarisse studied the marks. “So this first step is just to get them looking at the wrong thing.”

“If they choose to follow it,” Evan said. “It isn't a trick you can force.”

She glanced at Percy. “That's probably why it works on him.”

“I'm standing right here,” Percy said.

“That's how I know you can hear me.”

“The trick is not to sell the first move with your whole body,” Evan said. “If your shoulder tells the truth, the rest of you can't lie fast enough.”

“That's exactly what you do,” Clarisse told Percy.

“I do not.”

“Your right shoulder is basically a town crier.”

Annabeth called from the edge, “She's right.”

Percy looked at her. “I came here for support.”

“You came here to learn the feint.”

“Fine.” He took the practice spear Evan offered, checked its balance, and set his feet across from Clarisse. “Slow first?”

“Slow first,” Clarisse said. “And if you feel the tingling again, you stop and tell Will. No trying to prove a point.”

“It hasn't come back.”

“That's not the same as permission to ignore it.”

Percy glanced at Annabeth. She nodded once. He looked back at Clarisse. “Okay. If it comes back, I stop.”

They started at half speed. Percy tried the small step; Clarisse shifted her guard, and he rushed the second movement before she had committed. She tapped his sleeve with the flat of her spear.

“There.”

“I thought you were going to move.”

“I did. You just didn't wait to see where.”

They reset. Evan asked Percy whether he wanted to repeat the opening or have Clarisse show the counter. Percy chose the counter. Clarisse took his place and made the feint against Evan. She read his weight, turned her wrist, and stopped the practice tip at his shoulder.

“You gave me the lane,” she said.

“I wanted to see if you would take it.”

“I did.”

Evan turned to the watching campers. “That's the part worth learning. The feint only works if the other person gives you the turn. If they don't, don't force it.”

Clarisse handed the spear back to Percy. “Again.”

The second attempt went better until Percy tried to speed up the final step. He crossed the chalk boundary and had to plant his foot hard to keep from running into the next pair. Chiron raised his hand. The ring paused.

“Reset your space,” he called. “No one needs to finish a drill at somebody else's expense.”

Percy stepped clear and raised a hand. “My fault. I rushed it.”

“Then start again,” Clarisse said, already moving the nearest pair farther apart. She didn't make the error into a joke or an apology he had to accept. She fixed the spacing, checked that everyone was ready, and came back to him.

This time Percy waited. He let Clarisse shift her guard before he changed direction. She caught the feint anyway, hooked his ankle with the practice spear's shaft, and swept him down onto the sand. He landed on his back with a puff of dust, the breath knocked out of him but no sharp pain.

“Point,” Clarisse said, spear tip held clear of his chest.

The younger campers laughed; Percy joined them once he had air enough. Annabeth crouched at the edge of the ring and asked if he was all right. He flexed both hands once, felt no returning prickling, and gave her a thumbs-up.

“Yeah,” he said. “Point. You got me. That was perfect.”

Clarisse offered him her hand. He took it and got back to his feet.

“I saw you watching from the oak at lunch,” Clarisse said quietly. “You could've come over. I was asking Evan about that program. I wanted to hear what he learned; I wasn't trying to send you away.”

Percy rubbed sand from his palm. “I got jealous. I missed our warm-up. I don't want you to stop learning from him.”

“Good, because I'm not stopping.” Clarisse's mouth twitched. “But I miss the two taps too. Tomorrow, you can show me the counter you like. I'll show you this feint again, and you can be terrible at it in front of the younger kids.”

“That's a very generous offer.”

“It's a warning.”

“Then I'm in.”

She nodded once. “You're my brother. Evan's teaching me this drill. Those facts can both fit in your head.”

Percy looked toward the ring, where Evan was resetting the practice spears. “I can manage two facts.”

“Let's see.”

“I’m not mopey,” Percy said. “I’m... practicing my supportive face. How’s this?”

He made an exaggeratedly earnest expression. Clarisse snorted and shoved him.

“That’s terrible. You look constipated.”

“Gods, not you too.”

Clarisse glanced back at the waiting campers. “We need one more demonstration, then we can close the lane. Percy, stand on the outside mark.”

He took his place. “Am I getting swept again?”

“Probably. But this time do the feint slowly enough that they can see your shoulder give it away.”

“You want me to do it wrong on purpose?”

“You do it wrong by accident every day. Today, make it useful.”

Evan handed Percy the practice spear and moved the younger campers behind the chalk line. Chiron watched from the fence. Clarisse explained that a fake opening only worked if the other person chose to follow it; Percy showed the rushed version first, leaning his right shoulder before his feet had moved. Clarisse caught his weapon with hers and turned him toward the padded target. The kids laughed when Percy stumbled into the stop mark.

“That's the mistake,” Clarisse said. “Now watch his feet.”

Percy repeated the move more slowly. One camper spotted the tell before Clarisse did. She asked the camper what changed; he pointed to Percy's shoulder. Clarisse showed how to keep the shoulders square until the second step. Then she asked Percy to try the feint again, and this time he kept his chest still long enough to draw her guard the wrong way.

“Good catch,” Percy told the camper. “That's what I do every time.”

The boy glanced at Clarisse. She nodded, and his shoulders loosened. He stepped up to try the counter again.

Percy moved back behind the chalk line to give him the space. The camper raised his spear too early, caught himself, lowered it and waited for Clarisse's shoulder to turn. This time he saw the feint before it crossed the mark. Clarisse gave him the point, then asked Percy what had changed.

“He let you move first,” Percy said. “I keep trying to guess what you're going to do.”

“So stop guessing,” Clarisse said. “Watch the actual move.”

The camper tried again, and Clarisse let him have another clean counter. Percy saw the boy grin at his own success. He was glad Clarisse had invited him into the demonstration; the credit belonged to her teaching and the camper's attention, and there was room for his one useful observation too.

“There,” Evan said. “That's the version you can build on.”

Clarisse nodded to the camper who had caught the tell. “Your turn tomorrow. We'll set the marks wider.”

Percy handed the spear back. The attention from the group had made him want to check Clarisse's face to see whether she was proud of him. He stopped himself. She was already answering another camper's question, and he could be glad he'd helped without making her reaction the score.

Chiron asked the campers to return the practice weapons by size. Clarisse stayed to count them into the rack and Percy started brushing the chalk boundary back into the sand. Evan moved the next pair out of the space Percy had crossed.

“The marks were too close,” Percy said. “I should've looked before I sped up.”

“Both things can be true,” Clarisse said. “We needed more room, and you needed to watch where you were going.”

Percy nodded. He smoothed the outside line with the side of his shoe and measured the gap against the next ring. Clarisse checked it, then pointed him another step left. He moved it without arguing.

“There,” she said. “Now a bad feint doesn't become a crash.”

Evan came over carrying two practice spears. “That's the same adjustment I was going to make. The floor should give a beginner a clear path out.”

“They shouldn't have to guess where the boundary is,” Clarisse said.

“Agreed.” Evan handed Percy one of the spears to return. “We're going to redraw the lanes before tomorrow's block. If you're joining, be here for the setup.”

Percy looked at Clarisse. “If I'm invited.”

“You are,” she said. “If you get here before the bell.”

“That's an unreasonable condition.”

“It's the only condition.”

Chiron, who had been listening from the fence, nodded. “Tomorrow's practice will use the wider marks. I will supervise the same rotation, and Evan may assist. Clarisse, you may lead the warm-up if you'd like.”

“I would,” she said.

“We'll start with the footwork on the marks, no weapons,” Clarisse said. “Then add shields for the counter. If someone can't see the turn, we slow it down again.”

Chiron nodded. “Good. Percy can help set the lanes before the first bell, and Evan can demonstrate the counter after Clarisse's warm-up. Keep the pairs inside the marked space.”

“Got it,” Percy said.

Percy carried the last spear to the rack. Tomorrow was now an actual plan with a time and a job, not a promise he had to extract from Clarisse. He could arrive, help set the lines, and learn. He didn't have to be the person who knew every move first.

---

Dinner was loud and bright and smelled like barbecue. The sun sank low over the Sound, turning the water orange. The campfire pit was already being stacked for later.

Percy ended up squished on a bench between Annabeth and Clarisse, with Thalia across from them and Silena and Beckendorf crowded in on the ends. Evan sat a few tables over with the older campers. His story involved a missing boot, a mud slope and at least three different hand gestures. Clarisse listened from across the pavilion, then turned back to Annabeth to dispute whether a phalanx could beat a testudo.

She stole one of Percy's fries, let Silena braid a tiny piece of her hair, then got up to ask Evan a question about the change of line. She went back to her seat before he had finished his story. Percy watched her move between the tables and felt the old pinch once; it didn't tell him what to do.

The camper who had spotted Percy's shoulder tell came by with his tray. “Can I try the counter tomorrow?” he asked Clarisse. She looked to Chiron at the head table instead of answering for him.

“One slow rotation after the warm-up,” Chiron said. “Clarisse will set the pace.”

“Then start with empty hands,” Clarisse told the camper. “If you can see the turn on foot, we'll add the shield.”

The camper nodded and went back to his table. Clarisse picked up a fry. “He noticed the shoulder before you did.”

“I noticed,” Percy said.

“After he pointed it out.”

“Still counts.”

When Evan passed the open end of their bench to return his tray, he stopped where Clarisse could see him. “Chiron approved tomorrow's block. You're leading the warm-up. I'll assist with the counter drill if you still want me there.”

Clarisse looked toward Chiron at the head table. He nodded. “I want you there,” she said. “But I want the younger group to start with the marked footwork, not the fast version.”

“I'll bring the wider markers,” Evan said. Then he nodded to Percy. “And you can help set them before the bell, if you meant what you said.”

“I did,” Percy said.

“Good work today,” Evan told him. “You stopped when Chiron called it.”

“I was about to run into somebody.”

“And then you stopped. That's the part I mean.” He turned back to Clarisse. “See you tomorrow, La Rue.”

“See you.”

Evan waited for Clarisse's answer, then returned to the older campers. She watched him go and turned to Percy. “You were cool about that. Thanks.”

Percy shrugged, trying to play it cool and probably failing. “He’s all right. For an old guy with a scar.”

“He’s eighteen,” Clarisse said.

“Ancient,” Percy said solemnly.

Clarisse threw a fry at him. He caught it in his mouth. Everyone cheered.

Silena leaned across the table, eyes sparkling. “So, Percy. How’s the jealousy? Cured? Or do we need to schedule another intervention with lemonade?”

“I'm not cured,” Percy said. “I just figured out I could ask to join instead of inventing reasons she shouldn't have a teacher.”

Thalia snorted into her drink. Annabeth's smile was quiet and pleased.

“Look at you, admitting a thing before bedtime,” Thalia said. “Tomorrow you'll be unbearable.”

“I’m growing,” Percy corrected. “Like a fungus.”

“Like a fungus,” Clarisse agreed, ruffling his hair. “My fungus brother.”

“Affectionate,” Percy said, fixing his hair.

After dinner, they migrated to the campfire. The sky deepened to purple, and the logs popped as the flames caught. Someone from Apollo played guitar. The Hunters sang a bawdy song about Artemis outshooting Orion that made Chiron sigh deeply.

Percy sat on a log with Annabeth on one side and Clarisse on the other. Thalia perched behind them, legs dangling, the fresh wrap on her right palm tucked against her jacket. Silena leaned against Beckendorf nearby and kept trying to steal the guitar pick from his pocket. Around the ring, campers moved between blankets and logs, carrying marshmallows, cups and arguments about the song.

Clarisse watched the fire for a while, rubbing the heel of her hand against her knee. Then she leaned her head against Percy's shoulder. She stayed there only a few seconds, tired from drilling and from keeping track of the younger campers. Percy shifted enough to give her a solid place to rest, then left the subject alone.

Annabeth's hand found his on the log. Their fingers linked in the familiar way they did when they wanted to be close and didn't need to explain it. Across the fire, Silena had Beckendorf's guitar pick between two fingers and was refusing to give it back. Thalia called for the next verse with her left hand raised; when the gesture pulled at her bandage, she lowered it and kept singing instead.

The day did not erase the moment at lunch. When Clarisse laughed at something from the other end of the fire, Percy felt the small pinch again. It was there, then it passed through him. He knew what it was now: he had missed his routine with his sister, and he could tell her so. He did not need to turn Evan's presence into an accusation or make Clarisse choose between them.

Clarisse lifted her head and reached for the marshmallow stick lying beside her boot. “Tomorrow, don't be late,” she said.

“I won't.”

“You said that last week.”

“This time I have a reason.”

“A reason is not a clock.”

“I'll be there before the bell.”

“Good. You can help me reset the line.” She nudged his knee with hers. “Then I'll beat you at the feint.”

“If I let you.”

“You usually do.”

The music shifted to a slower song. Annabeth rested her shoulder against Percy while the fire warmed their faces and the night air cooled his back. Chiron stood to give the nightly announcements. Percy listened long enough to hear the next morning's schedule, including the advanced rotation, then let the music cover the rest of the pavilion noise.

Later, when the fire burned low and campers drifted toward their cabins in sleepy clusters, Percy walked back with his sister and his girlfriend. Clarisse went ahead a little, trading insults with a sleepy Ares training partner. Annabeth stayed beside Percy, their hands swinging loosely between them.

“You good?” Annabeth asked quietly.

Percy thought about it. Really thought about it.

“Better,” he said. “Still a little jealous, probably. But I know what to do with it now.”

Annabeth squeezed his hand once more before letting go at the fork where the paths split to Poseidon and Zeus. “You can tell me when it comes back.”

“Even when I’m being an idiot?”

“Especially when you’re being an idiot. It’s in the contract.”

Clarisse waited for him at the Poseidon path, arms crossed, trying to look impatient and failing to hide a yawn. “Are you coming, Fungus? Or are you going to hold hands all night?”

Percy jogged to catch up. “Jealous?”

“Of hand-holding? No.” Clarisse bumped his shoulder with hers. “You’re still my favorite dummy to practice on.”

“I’ll take it,” Percy said.

They walked the rest of the way together, bickering about whether Percy could ever learn not to telegraph his right shoulder. At the cabin, Clarisse went toward the Ares bunkhouse, and Percy went inside to find his backpack.

Silena's portrait was still protected between his towel and notebook. Percy took it to the wall above his bunk, cleared a space between the cabin's shell patterns, and fixed it in place with two small bronze tacks from the repair tin. He stepped back. The painting had caught his ridiculous expression in the middle of a laugh. It looked like someone worth keeping on the wall.

The first tack sat slightly higher than the second. Percy loosened it and tried again, using the top edge of his notebook to keep the paper straight. When the portrait finally hung level, he set the repair tin away and moved his pack beneath the bunk. The picture had a place now, close enough that he would see it when he woke and far enough from the fountain's spray to stay dry.

He thought about the morning bell, the wider chalk marks, and Clarisse's two taps. Tomorrow he would show up early enough to help set the lane. He would probably still get corrected. He could live with that.

Percy set his practice clothes at the foot of the bunk and put his sneakers where he could reach them without waking the whole cabin. He flexed his hands once, the way he had at the infirmary, and felt no prickling. Will had asked him to report if it returned; the ordinary feeling was enough. Percy stopped there and climbed under the blanket.

Above him, Silena's painted version of his grin caught the moonlight from the window. He had wanted to hang it because she had made it for him. That was all the reason it needed. In the morning, he would get to the arena before Clarisse and help lay out the lines. He didn't know if he would feel jealous again. If he did, he could still pick up the chalk, listen to what she wanted to teach, and take his place as her brother without asking her to make the rest of the ring smaller.

He slept that night without drooling.

Well, with only a little drool.

Annabeth took a picture for evidence anyway.
