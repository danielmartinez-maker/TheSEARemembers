## Chapter 85: Spring

The lake shook off a gray stretch of spring on the first Tuesday in June.

Percy Jackson noticed it first because he was the first one down at the docks, like always, his sneakers squeaking on the damp planks and his breath still fogging just a little in the early morning air. He remembered the ice that had filmed the edges of the canoe lake through winter. It had melted weeks ago. Now the last cold haze over the far shore was gone. Not thin, not broken. Gone. The water was gray-blue and choppy under a white spring sky, slapping against the pilings, smelling of mud and pine and cold clean melt.

He stood at the end of the dock where he had stood after waking in this altered life, twelve and furious and unsure what his claim meant here, and he let the wind push his hair back and listened.

Behind him, Camp Half-Blood was waking up to June.

It sounded different now than it had that first summer. He knew the difference the way you know the difference in your own voice on a recording. Smoke from the forge. Someone laughing too loud by the strawberry fields. The pegasi screaming at each other over breakfast. A conch horn for morning drills, half-hearted because it was still the shoulder season and Chiron hadn't started yelling in earnest yet. The Big House shutters banging. The Athena cabin's measuring tapes snapping. All of it layered over the rush of the creek running high after rain.

Percy was thirteen now. Taller by four inches since last August. His hair was longer, curling damp at the nape of his neck. His hands had calluses that didn't fade over winter anymore. His winter coat was slung over the piling because he'd gotten too warm on the walk down, and his Camp Half-Blood t-shirt was faded at the collar from a hundred washes.

He crouched down and put his palm flat on the dock.

Same wood. Same nails. Same lake.

Same boy, but not.

"You're going to fall in doing that," said a voice behind him.

He didn't look up. He grinned. "Good morning to you too."

Annabeth walked down the dock like she owned the physics of it, boots thudding steady, a rolled-up blueprint tube under one arm and a pencil stuck behind her ear. She was thirteen too now, their birthdays close enough that camp had made a joke of counting their growth together. She was taller as well, leaner, her blond hair pulled back in a high ponytail that whipped in the wind, her gray eyes sharp even half-asleep.

She was his best friend. Everyone at camp knew it. Newcomers sometimes got confused, asked if they were siblings because they were always together, and the older kids just shrugged and said, They’re dating. Percy’s Annabeth’s person, Annabeth’s Percy’s person, Poseidon and Zeus, don’t overthink it. Which was true in all the ways that mattered. They'd fought the same monsters side by side until their fighting styles fit together like interlocking teeth. Partners, forged, not just born.

She sat down next to him without ceremony and swung her legs over the edge, dropping the blueprint tube across her knees.

"Lake's back," Percy said.

"Lake never left," Annabeth said. "It was just sleeping."

"Poetic."

"Accurate. Lakes don't leave."

He bumped his shoulder into hers. "You sleep with your blueprints again?"

"I was revising." She tapped the tube. "North cabins drainage. Last spring the whole path by Cabin Six flooded and Clarisse threatened to drown a naiad. I promised Chiron I'd fix it before next summer."

"Second summer here," Percy repeated.

They both let that sit.

Second summer ahead. When they’d arrived, thirteen had sounded ancient. Now it was them.

Annabeth leaned her head briefly against his shoulder, just for a second, the way she had on their remembered quests when the world was scarier. Then she straightened and squinted out at the water.

"Do you remember the first time we sat here?" she asked.

"Yeah. You told me the dock was structurally unsound and I told you I trusted it with my life and you called me an idiot."

"You were an idiot."

"Am," Percy corrected. "Present tense."

She laughed, and the sound carried across the water and came back faintly off the far trees.

Above them, the sky cracked a little wider, blue pushing through white. Spring was really here. Not the calendar date — camp had its own calendar, measured in thaw and strawberries and the return of the swallows to the Big House eaves — but the real thing. The in-between season. The promise.

Percy lay back on the dock, hands behind his head, and stared up. "We made it."

"We're not done," Annabeth said.

"I know. But we made it to spring."

---

The path up from the lake to the cabins was muddy in the way that a cool early June could manage, rain-cut ruts and puddles that reflected the sky perfectly.

Percy and Annabeth took their time, stopping the way they always did now, not because they were tired but because they knew to look.

There was the spot by the creek where Percy had once tried to teach Annabeth to skip stones left-handed and she'd beaten him right-handed without trying. There was the pine where they'd carved P + A  Year 1 and then added NEXT SUMMER underneath with a dagger tip, crooked and deep. There was the strawberry field where the satyrs were already out, sleeves rolled, singing off-key, waving at them.

"Hey! Sea and sky!" called Connor Stoll, up to his knees in vines. "Tell Zeus thanks for the rain! Perfect berry weather!"

"My dad doesn’t do weather on request!" Annabeth called back.

"Tell him anyway!"

Percy waved. When he first woke here he’d ducked his head when people shouted his father's name. Now he just grinned. Home does that to you. It teaches you to answer when you're called.

Camp in spring was half-empty and all promise. The year-rounders — about forty of them — rattled around in the big dining pavilion like marbles in a jar, with extra tables folded and the braziers burning low. Summer kids had begun arriving in late May; June would bring a flood. For now, it was the core family. The ones who stayed through the snow.

Thalia Grace was waiting for them on the porch of the Big House, sitting on the railing with one booted foot hooked around a baluster, eating an apple down to the core.

Thalia looked like a storm that had learned how to stay in one place. Her black hair was cut shorter now, choppy at her jaw, with a single silver streak she had kept through winter scouting trips and her return to camp. Her electric blue eyes were the same, her leather jacket was the same but more patched, her presence was the same — taller than most of the boys, louder than most of the monsters.

She saw Percy and Annabeth coming up the hill and lit up.

"If it isn’t the swamp monsters," she said.

"We’re lake monsters," Percy said. "Get it right."

"You’re mud monsters right now. Look at your shoes."

Annabeth climbed the steps and hugged Thalia sideways, careful of the apple. Thalia hugged her back hard, then grabbed Percy by the back of the neck and knocked their foreheads together lightly, her version of affection.

"You're late," Thalia said. "Chiron's been looking for you two. He wants the spring census. How many bunks, how many newbies, how many siblings are we expecting."

"We're expecting?" Annabeth said, eyebrows up. "Did Dad send a memo?"

Thalia laughed. "Poseidon sends horses and our dad sends storms, not memos. No. But Zeus has been restless. Hera’s been restless. You know how it is. Storm season. Our dad’s side of the family always makes spring interesting."

Thalia was straddling two worlds. She'd gone out as a camp scout for a time, then stepped back to be a counselor again, then stepped forward as a kind of big-sister lieutenant for the whole camp. Everyone knew she was thinking about how many full summers she would spend as a camper. Soon she would have to choose between staff work and the adult world of quests and jobs and apartments with wards on the doors. She didn't talk about it much, but Percy saw the way she looked at the Zeus cabin in the mornings, like memorizing it.

"You staying through June?" Percy asked quietly.

"Trying to," Thalia said. "Long-term school plans. Mortal stuff. My mom—" She stopped, restarted. "Yeah. I'll be here next summer. Wouldn't miss watching you two get all tall and annoying."

"We're already tall and annoying," Annabeth said.

"You're my tall and annoying," Thalia said, and tossed her apple core into the woods for the dryads. "Come on. Chiron's inside."

---

Inside the Big House, the ping-pong table was still covered with maps, as it had been through their new seasons here. Chiron in his wheelchair form was bent over it, moving little painted stones for cabins, frowning at a list.

"Ah," he said, looking up. "My sea and sky planners."

"Chiron," Percy and Annabeth said in unison, the way they did when they'd been scolded together too many times.

He smiled, deep lines at the corners of his eyes. He looked older than he had when Percy woke in this altered life, which was strange because he was immortal and shouldn't. But Percy thought immortals showed age in a different way — not in wrinkles but in how many springs they remembered.

"Sit," Chiron said. "Tell me what you see."

It was an old game. He'd done it their first spring too. Tell me what you see. Not what you fear, not what you want. What you see.

Percy looked at the map. "Forty-one year-rounders. Cabin Three has three — Clarisse, Silena, and me. Cabin One has two — Annabeth and Thalia when she's here. Cabin Five has three trainees. Cabin Six has four. Cabin Ten has two — Drew plus—"

"Lacy staying for spring," Annabeth finished. "And another camper arriving in June. So three."

Chiron nodded. "And?"

"And we'll get maybe sixty new in June," Annabeth said. "If last year's pattern holds. Mostly Hermes at first, then split. We'll need the extra bunks in Eleven, and the drainage fixed, and the arena schedule doubled."

"And?" Chiron prompted, looking at Percy.

Percy hesitated. Then: "And everyone's older. Everyone's tired in a good way. No one's scared of the lake anymore. Even the new Ares kids from winter held the line in capture the flag last week."

"Good," Chiron said softly. "That's good."

He wheeled back and studied them both. "Do you remember your first census? You were twelve. Percy, you tried to eat a map stone because Clarisse dared you."

"It was a blue one," Percy said defensively. "It looked like candy."

Annabeth snorted. "He cried."

"I did not cry. My eyes watered from paint."

Chiron chuckled. "You both told me camp was loud and the beds were lumpy and you wanted to go home. Do you remember what I told you?"

Annabeth glanced at Percy. Together, from memory: "Home isn't done being built yet."

"Yes." Chiron's voice was warm. "Well. Look at us now."

Outside the window, the camp bell rang for mid-morning lessons. Swordplay. Architecture. Greek. Monster identification for the winter newbies who still mixed up empousai and dracaenae.

Chiron didn't let them leave immediately.

"One more thing," he said, touching the map on the ping-pong table. "The route changes you made during the spring departures worked. I'd like you to look at the early summer arrivals before the first large group comes."

Annabeth straightened. "The visitor route?"

"That and the path from the cabins to the infirmary. New campers don't automatically know which door is open at night."

Percy looked at the map. The line he and Annabeth had corrected for Owen's departing bus was still there, inked in red. An arrow marked the turn that had confused the driver. It was strange to see a mistake they'd fixed becoming part of the camp's regular plan.

"We can walk it," Annabeth said.

"With somebody who hasn't memorized the camp," Chiron replied.

Percy thought of Lucy. She'd noticed the inaccessible steps in Annabeth's sandcastle, and she'd helped the younger campers find a safe place for their shoes at the beach. She knew enough now to help, but not so much that the routes would look obvious to her.

"Can we ask Lucy?" he said.

"Ask her if she would like to help," Chiron answered. "Don't assign her without checking her schedule and her counselor's approval."

They found Lucy outside the Hermes cabin with her light-up shoes in her lap. One had stopped blinking. She was trying to work out whether the problem came from a wet connection or a broken switch.

"Want to test some directions for us?" Percy asked.

"Directions to what?"

"The infirmary. At night."

"Why?"

"Because if the signs are confusing, we'd rather find out now."

Lucy thought about it. "Can I finish fixing this first?"

"Of course."

She returned an hour later with her counselor. The shoe still wasn't blinking, but she'd decided to save the repair for another day.

Annabeth handed Lucy a small map and asked her to find the infirmary as though she'd just arrived at camp. They began outside the Big House. Lucy immediately took the path toward the dining pavilion because the first sign had an arrow pointing ambiguously between two forks.

"That one is wrong," Annabeth said.

"It's not wrong if you already know where the infirmary is," Lucy replied.

Percy grinned. "Which means it's wrong for the person who needs it."

They moved the sign to face the approaching path and added the infirmary name beneath the symbol. At the next fork Lucy correctly identified the route but couldn't read the small letters from far away.

"Could we make them bigger?" she asked.

Annabeth took notes.

The third problem was the narrow bridge across the shallow creek. It was not dangerous in daylight, but wet leaves made it slippery after rain. Lucy remembered falling there during her first week and admitted she'd been embarrassed to tell anyone.

Percy crouched beside the railing. One fastening had come loose. He called for the maintenance crew rather than tightening it with his sword hilt, the way he might have when he first arrived.

Beckendorf inspected the bracket. It needed a new bolt. The crew put up a temporary route marker until the repair could be completed and signed off.

Lucy looked worried. "Did I get the path closed?"

"You helped us find a problem," Annabeth said. "That's not the same thing."

They finished at the infirmary door, where Will showed Lucy how to ring the nighttime bell without waking every cabin. He also told her which things counted as reasons to come immediately and which could wait for morning. Lucy asked good questions and wrote down the answers.

At the end Annabeth made a revised map for new arrivals. It included bigger signs, a marked alternate route for the creek bridge and a reminder to ask a counselor before leaving a cabin at night. Lucy checked it and said it was easier to follow.

"Do I get to put my name on it?" she asked.

"Yes," Annabeth said. "You're the person who found most of the problems."

Lucy looked pleased. She went back to her cabin with her counselor and the corrected map, while Percy and Annabeth took the repair request to Chiron.

"That was useful," Chiron said.

Percy looked at the line of notes. "The map's uglier now."

"More honest," Annabeth replied.

They walked toward lessons together. Percy had been thinking that spring was about returning to places where everybody already knew him. Lucy had reminded him that home also required making room for people who didn't know where anything was yet.

He tucked the copied map under his arm and promised himself not to complain when Annabeth asked him to help test the signs again after the bridge was fixed.

Percy stood. Annabeth rolled her blueprints.

"Go on," Chiron said. "Build."

---

The arena in spring smelled of wet sand and hot metal.

Clarisse La Rue was already there, armor half-on, spear in hand, shouting at two younger Ares trainees to keep their shields up.

Clarisse was all sharp edges grown into strength. She had been terrifying when Percy first met her here; now she was terrifying in a way that included people. Her dark hair was pulled back in a tight braid, her eyes were hard but not cruel, her arms were corded with muscle from training straight through the winter. The scar on her chin from the winter manticore fight had faded to a thin white line.

She saw Percy and grinned, feral and fond.

"Poseidon! Spar me. No powers. Just bronze."

"Why no powers?" Percy complained, grabbing a practice sword from the rack. "My powers are charming."

"Your powers are cheating and you flood the sand and then I have to rake it."

Annabeth sat on the bleachers with her blueprints unrolled across her knees, pencil in mouth, watching with half an eye while she sketched. This was routine now. Percy sparred. Annabeth designed. They kept each other company without needing to perform.

Percy and Clarisse circled. When he first sparred with his sister here, he’d lost to her in under thirty seconds. Months later he’d lasted four minutes. Now—

Steel rang. Sand flew. Clarisse was stronger, heavier, brutal in close. Percy was faster, looser, water-balanced even without using water. He ducked a shield bash, caught her spear shaft on his guard, pivoted.

They fought to a draw, both breathing hard, both grinning.

"Better," Clarisse panted. "You're less terrible."

"High praise from the queen of pain."

She shoved him, then pulled him back up when he stumbled. "You staying now that spring break is over? My dad wants a war council for summer games. Says Zeus kids and Poseidon kids keep tipping the lake."

"Thalia tips the lake," Percy said. "I just live there."

Across the arena, Silena Beauregard arrived with a basket of laundry and a dangerous look that meant she was about to mother everyone.

Silena was beautiful in a way that had always been her own, but she'd grown into something softer and steelier underneath. Her long dark hair was tied up with a strawberry-print scarf, her camp shirt was knotted at the waist, her hands were chapped from helping in the infirmary all winter. She'd been the girl who cried over broken nails at fourteen; she was the girl who'd held pressure on a hellhound bite for forty minutes in January and joked the whole time to keep the patient calm.

She set the basket down and pointed at Clarisse. "You. Helmet off. You're sweating through your braid and you'll get a headache."

"Yes, Mom," Clarisse said, but she obeyed instantly.

Silena turned to Percy. "You. Water. Drink."

Percy drank. Annabeth came down from the bleachers and stole a cookie from Silena's pocket, because Silena always had cookies.

"How's the drainage plan?" Silena asked.

"Almost done," Annabeth said. "I'll need your help with the Aphrodite cabin garden. I don't want to flood your roses again."

"Again," Silena said pointedly, "was Percy's fault."

"Everything's Percy's fault," Clarisse said. "It's science."

Percy put a hand to his chest. "I feel loved."

"You are loved," Silena said simply, tucking a loose strand of hair behind his ear the way an older sister would. "You're just damp."

They sat in the sand, four of them, sharing water and cookies while the younger Ares kids drilled badly in the background. The sun came out fully for the first time that day, warming the sand, turning the puddles to glitter.

Annabeth looked around at their circle and felt something catch in her throat. When she first woke here she’d sat in this same arena and been so lonely she'd counted tiles in the armory to calm down. Now she had a boyfriend who sparred badly on purpose to make new kids feel better, a friend who yelled but always saved her a seat, a friend who remembered everyone's allergies and birthdays.

Home. It had happened while she wasn't looking.

"What's that face?" Clarisse asked suspiciously. "You're making a face."

"I'm thinking," Annabeth said.

"Dangerous," Percy said.

"I'm thinking we've grown up."

Clarisse snorted. "Speak for yourself. I'm still delightful."

Silena laughed, bright and real, and leaned her head on Clarisse's shoulder for just a moment. Clarisse let her, which told you everything about how far they'd come.

They stayed until the younger kids finished drills and the sand cooled, none of them in a hurry to be anywhere else. That was new too. When they first arrived, every hour had been scheduled and urgent. Now they knew how to waste time together properly.

Thalia found them there just after noon, her jacket slung over her shoulder despite the chill, her cheeks pink from running the perimeter trail. She ran it every morning, five miles in combat boots, because she said if she stopped moving the mortal world would catch her.

"Walk with me," she said to Percy and Annabeth. "A walk with my sister and her boyfriend. No audience, no teasing committee, no excuses."

"Clarisse is going to kill you for that," Silena said sweetly.

"Clarisse can try," Thalia said, and winked.

Percy and Annabeth fell in beside Thalia as she led them up past the cabins toward the top of the hill, where Cabins One and Three stood facing the sea like two ends of an old argument finally at rest.

Thalia stopped in front of the Zeus cabin. It was white marble and sharp angles, tall columns with eagles carved at the top, bronze doors that hummed faintly in damp weather. It had stood empty for years before Thalia, a monument more than a home. Now it had muddy boots by the door, a denim jacket hanging off the eagle, a stack of mortal college brochures on the step weighted down with a rock.

"Growing up," Thalia said, staring at it. "You know what that means in our world? It means the Mist can't hide you in high school anymore. It means jobs. Apartments. Taxes. My mom keeps sending me pamphlets for community college in Los Angeles. Like I can do trigonometry while Cyclopes are throwing cars."

Annabeth nudged the brochures with her toe. "Are you going to go?"

"I don't know." Thalia was honest in the way only older siblings can be, brutal and kind at once. "Part of me wants to. Normal. Stupid normal vending machines and late buses. Part of me wants to stay here forever and be the weird counselor who smells like ozone and tells the same three stories."

Percy leaned against the marble wall. It tingled faintly against his back, static from Annabeth and Thalia's dad. "You could do both. Chiron does."

"Chiron's three thousand years old and has horse legs. He gets extra time." Thalia sighed, then smiled crookedly. "You know what I realized this winter? When I was out scouting, I told myself I didn't miss this. I told myself pine trees all smell the same. Liars. Nothing smells like camp in spring. Wet earth and strawberries and bronze polish and Clarisse's hair oil. I missed it so bad it felt like altitude sickness."

Annabeth, who catalogued everything, said quietly, "You belong in both places. That's not divided. That's doubled."

Thalia stared at her, then laughed, loud and startled. "Gods, when did you get wise? You were eleven and lecturing me about proper spear grip."

"I'm thirteen now," Annabeth said with great dignity. "I'm practically ancient."

"Ancient midget," Thalia said, and pulled them both into a rough hug that smelled of rain and leather and ozone. "Listen. Whatever happens next year — school, patrols, whatever Zeus throws at me — you two are stuck with me. Annabeth, you're my sister. Percy, you're stuck with us as her boyfriend and my friend. Okay?"

"Okay," Percy said, his voice muffled in her jacket.

"Okay," Annabeth echoed.

They walked back down together past the Poseidon cabin, low and long and sea-blue, its shell roof gleaming after rain, its windows warm. Three bunks visible through the open door. Three names. No longer empty. No longer echoing.

Thalia bumped Percy's shoulder. "Your dad's house looks lived-in now."

"Took us long enough," Percy said proudly.

"Good. Houses should look chewed on."

---

Lunch was chaos, as spring lunches always were.

The dining pavilion was half-full, sunlight slanting through the open sides, the braziers unlit because it was warm enough. The gods' tables at the center were empty in body but heavy in presence — the sea breeze that ruffled the Poseidon tablecloth, the faint static over the Zeus plates.

Percy and Annabeth sat at the Poseidon table together, as they always had. Two plates, two cups, two offering scrapings into the fire. The table where Percy sat with Clarisse and Silena also had a regular guest now. Two names were carved under their favorite end: PERCY + ANNABETH, with a trident and a little owl Annabeth had added when she thought no one was looking.

Thalia slid in across from them with her plate, uninvited and welcome. "Zeus table's lonely," she said. "Mind if I defect?"

"Traitors welcome," Percy said, pushing a roll at her.

Clarisse and her Ares trainees took the next table over, close enough to throw grapes. Silena and Drew argued happily about centerpieces at the Aphrodite table. The Hermes kids were loud. The Hephaestus kids were singed. The Apollo kids were humming.

Mr. D, in a Hawaiian shirt that hurt to look at, stood to make announcements with the enthusiasm of a man reading a phone book.

"Spring. Ugh. Allergies. Lesson changes posted. Pegasus riding limited due to molting. Do not eat the molting. Oracle hours extended. And—" He squinted at his card. "Chiron says to remind you that home is a practice, not a place. Whatever that means. Thank you. Back to your ambrosia."

Everyone clapped politely. Percy caught Chiron's eye across the pavilion and mouthed, practice?

Chiron winked.

After lunch, Annabeth dragged Percy to the building site behind the cabins.

"This," she announced, gesturing to a muddy rectangle staked out with string, "is the future."

Percy stared. "It's mud."

"It's the future spring pavilion. For year-rounders. So winter kids have somewhere warm to work that's not the Big House attic. I designed it. Hephaestus cabin's helping. We'll have it framed by late June."

Percy walked the perimeter, careful not to trip the strings. He could see it, because Annabeth's drawings always made sense once you stood inside them. Low stone walls. Open beams. A hearth in the center. Big enough for forty, small enough to feel close.

"You did this over winter?" he asked.

"Mostly. Your dad helped a little." She said it casually, but Percy knew what it meant. Prayers answered in dreams. A sudden intuition about load-bearing joints. The smell of salt when she woke up. Poseidon, in his distant, tidal way, helping his son’s girlfriend build a place for all of them.

"My dad’s good at foundations," Percy said.

"Your dad’s good at showing up sideways," Annabeth corrected, but she smiled. "It's okay. I'm used to him now. I used to think fathers had to be loud to count. Thunder, you know? Zeus stuff. But yours is—" She searched for the word. "Underneath. Like water under a boat. You don't see it until you need it."

Percy nodded. He'd stopped waiting for Poseidon to appear in a blaze of glory a long time ago. Instead he'd learned to notice the small things: the lake calming when Annabeth was anxious, the pipes in Cabin Three warming on the coldest nights, the hippocampi showing up exactly when they needed a ride.

Family, he'd learned, wasn't always a face at the table. Sometimes it was a current under you.

They stood in the muddy rectangle together, boyfriend and girlfriend, thirteen and already builders.

"You know what we should do?" Percy said suddenly. "Tour."

"What tour?"

"Home tour. Like mortals do with houses, but with camp. Before summer starts and everything gets loud. Just us. To remember."

Annabeth studied him for a long moment, then rolled her blueprints carefully and slid them into the tube. "Okay. You're sentimental when you're hungry, but okay."

They started at the strawberry fields, where the spring crop was just coming in, small white flowers among green. The satyr who ran the fields, an old-timer named Orson, let them pick two berries each, tart and half-ripe. Percy made a face. Annabeth ate hers without flinching and stole his second one.

"Home is strawberries that aren't ready yet but you eat them anyway," Percy declared.

"Home is not getting stomachaches," Annabeth countered.

They cut through the forge, where Beckendorf was teaching winter newbies to hammer without burning their eyebrows off. The heat hit them in a wall, wonderful after the damp. Beckendorf at seventeen was huge, gentle-handed, Silena's boyfriend and everyone's big brother. He wiped sweat off his forehead and grinned at them.

"Sea and sky! Check this — spring hinges for your pavilion. Won't rust."

He held up a pair of sea-green hinges shaped like waves. Annabeth lit up and talked load and shear for ten minutes while Percy tried not to touch anything hot. When they left, Beckendorf pressed two warm oatmeal cookies into their hands. Silena had left them for him, he said, because Silena fed everyone by proxy now.

"Home is hinges," Percy said, mouth full.

"Home is someone making hinges shaped like you," Annabeth corrected.

They stopped at the armory, where Clarisse was re-stacking shields by size while lecturing her trainees on discipline. She paused long enough to throw a polishing rag at Percy's head.

"Home is clean shields," Clarisse barked.

"Home is ducking," Percy said, ducking.

They stopped at the infirmary porch, where Silena was folding bandages with brisk efficiency, her hair tied back, her sleeves rolled. She had a smudge of iodine on her cheek. When she saw them, her whole face softened.

"Vitals check," she ordered, pointing at the step. They obeyed, sitting while she pressed the back of her hand to each forehead in turn, a joke from last winter when flu had run through the Hermes cabin.

"No fever. Just spring stupidity. Prescription: sunshine and soup."

"Home is soup," Percy said solemnly.

"Home is someone who notices you're tired before you do," Silena said, and kissed the tops of their heads like a benediction before shooing them on.

They ended at Half-Blood Hill, by the boundary pine standing tall behind the Golden Fleece, its needles green-black against the sky. The dragon Peleus snored in the branches, golden and lazy. From here they could see the whole valley: cabins in a horseshoe, Big House, arena, lake glittering at the bottom, fields, woods, the sea beyond the hill.

When Percy first woke here he had stood here and thought the camp was too big to ever memorize. Now he knew every path by the feel under his shoes.

Annabeth sat down in the grass, not caring about mud, and pulled her knees up. Percy sat beside her.

"What does home mean now?" he asked quietly. "If Chiron asked right now."

Annabeth thought for a long time, the way she did when the question mattered. Wind moved through the pine. Far below, the conch sounded for afternoon chores.

"At first," she said slowly, "home meant safe. Walls. A door that locked. Someone strong between me and monsters. Then it meant useful. If I could build, if I could plan, they'd keep me. Then last winter, when we stayed and it snowed for eleven days and the pipes froze and we all slept in the Big House living room because the cabins were too cold — remember?"

Percy nodded. Forty kids in sleeping bags, Chiron reading Greek myths aloud by firelight, Thalia snoring, Clarisse sharing her blanket with a scared Hermes newbie without a word.

"Then home meant warm," Annabeth said. "Bodies in a room. Breathing together. Now?" She picked a blade of new grass and twisted it. "Now I think home means known. Percy, they know I sharpen pencils when I'm nervous. Silena knows I hate cilantro. Clarisse knows I cry at sad songs and pretends not to hear. Thalia knows I have nightmares about spiders and sleeps on the floor of our cabin when she's here so I don't have to ask. You know I do three drafts of everything. I know you hum when you polish Riptide. We're known. All the way through. And still kept."

Percy swallowed hard. "Yeah," he managed. "Yeah, that's it."

He bumped his knee into hers. "Home is people who know the dumb stuff and stay anyway."

"Exactly." She leaned her head on his shoulder, and they watched the light shift over their valley, their practice, their people.

"We'll have dinner here when it's done," Annabeth said. "All of us."

"Promise?"

"Promise."

---

Nico found Percy after lunch at the small fort behind Cabin Thirteen.

He was sitting at the lower table with the Mythomagic pieces spread out in a half-circle. Bianca stood beside the doorway with a folding rule, measuring a space above the carved names. Annabeth had given Nico permission to propose a little skull emblem for the door if the Hephaestus crew approved the placement. Nico had decided he wanted to make the drawing himself before anyone cut the wood.

"Do you know how to draw a skull that looks fierce but not evil?" he asked Percy.

"That's specific."

"It needs to protect the fort. But Bea doesn't like scary faces."

Percy knelt beside him. There were three sketches on the table: one skull with enormous teeth, one with tiny round eyes and a third wearing a Roman helmet.

"That one looks like Jason," Percy said, pointing to the helmet.

"Jason isn't a skull."

"Excellent point."

Bianca laughed. Nico turned the paper around so Percy could see what he'd drawn on the back. It was a map of the fort with spaces labeled for people who wanted to play. The lower table was for card games, the shaded bench outside was for drawing, and the loft had a little mark that said *two people*.

"Annabeth told me I had to make a plan," Nico said.

"She does that."

"I like it. Then people don't have to ask where they can go."

Percy looked at the tiny shapes. "Is this for your friends?"

"For the new campers. Some don't want to come in because they think I'll be doing death magic all the time."

"Are you?"

"Not all the time."

Percy tried not to laugh.

Nico looked serious. "I don't want to be the creepy boy who has a fort nobody wants to use."

Percy recognized the fear. Nico had spent his first weeks at camp learning which older kids flinched when he mentioned Hades. His birthday had helped, but it hadn't erased every unpleasant look. Percy also knew he couldn't fix the problem by telling everybody that Nico was wonderful and demanding that they believe him.

"What do you want them to know?" Percy asked.

"That they can play here. But they have to ask before using the cards."

"Then put that on your map."

"Can it be a rule?"

"It's your fort, with Bianca. Ask Chiron if you want it posted on the shared notice."

Nico looked toward his sister. Bianca nodded.

"I think that's fair," she said. "And it would be nice if you told people where to put things when they're done."

Nico added a box near the door labeled *return cards here*. Then he wrote a short note for anybody who was nervous about the shadow flowers behind the cabin: the plants were not dangerous to touch, but visitors should leave them in the ground so they would keep growing.

Milo came by carrying a small canvas bag. He had brought the borrowed Mythomagic deck back after the lesson Nico gave him on his birthday. He placed it on the table and checked whether all the cards were present before Nico counted them.

"Can I play again?" Milo asked.

Nico looked at the new map. "Yes. First you have to choose whether you want to play inside or outside."

"Inside."

"Okay. The lower table is free."

Milo sat down. He saw the skull drawings and said he liked the one with the Roman helmet. Nico immediately accused him of not knowing how skulls worked, and they started arguing about whether a skeleton could wear a helm without a head.

Percy watched the boys arrange their pieces. It occurred to him that Nico had solved his immediate problem in a way Percy couldn't have designed for him. He had built a place for other children to choose how close they wanted to come.

Bianca came to stand beside Percy.

"He has been like that all week," she said.

"Like what?"

"Making lists. Asking if people want to play. He keeps wanting to tell me every time he learns something new."

"Sounds exhausting."

"It is. I love it."

Percy looked at the drawings again. "Are you glad you built the fort?"

"Yes. But not because everybody likes him now. He still has bad days."

"So do I."

She glanced at him. "I know."

He looked toward the path where Annabeth was talking to Chiron about the creek bridge. She had plans for a new season, and sometimes they would go wrong. Percy was learning that loving someone didn't mean making every future day safe or easy. It meant being there when the plan changed and asking what kind of help they wanted.

Milo lost the first game because he had forgotten to defend one of his monsters. He complained, and Nico told him he could try again but wasn't allowed to take back an entire turn. Bianca left them to negotiate the rules, and Percy followed her out.

At the doorway Nico called after him.

"Percy?"

"Yeah?"

"Can you ask Annabeth whether the skull has to be symmetrical?"

"I can. But I think I know what she'll say."

"What?"

"She'll ask why."

Nico grinned. "Because the left eye is bigger."

Percy laughed. "That's a pretty good reason."

They left him with the map, the cards and his own crooked drawing, all of them things he could change without asking anyone to become a different person.

That afternoon it rained.

Spring rain at camp was different from summer rain. Summer rain was warm, dramatic, gone in an hour. Spring rain was cold, steady, soaking, the kind that turned paths to soup and sent everyone running for cover laughing and swearing.

Percy loved it. He stood out in it on the porch of Cabin Three while Annabeth yelled at him from inside to come in before he caught cold and he yelled back that sons of Poseidon don't catch cold, they catch vibes.

Cabin Three.

When Percy woke in this changed life, the cabin had felt unfamiliar, cold, smelling of old stone and sea air. He had felt alone there even with his sisters in the other bunks, listening to the conch shells on the wall rattle, feeling like an intruder in his own father's house.

Now it was home in a way no mortal house had ever been.

Three bunks, all lived-in. Percy's side messy, saltwater taffy wrappers in the drawer, Riptide on the nightstand, a photo of him and Annabeth and Thalia and Clarisse and Silena at the lake last summer, sunburnt and grinning, taped to the wall. Clarisse's side orderly with spear polish and spare straps; Silena's with books stacked by size, shells on the shelf, and a scarf drying over the frame. Annabeth's little clay owl stood on Percy's desk, a gift from her own bunk in Cabin One.

The walls were covered in marks. Growth charts. Quest tallies. A map of camp with X's for best hiding spots. A list titled RULES FOR POSEIDON CABIN in Annabeth's handwriting: 1. No flooding the cabin (Percy). 2. No flooding the cabin on purpose (Percy). 3. Knock before entering unless dying (everyone). 4. Home is people.

Percy dripped on the porch and read that list through the open door for the hundredth time and felt his chest go tight.

Annabeth came to the doorway with a towel and threw it at his face. "Dry."

He dried. She pulled him inside. They sat on the floor between Percy's bed and Clarisse's empty bunk, backs against opposite frames, knees touching, listening to rain hammer the seashell roof.

"Do you remember being scared here?" Annabeth asked quietly.

"Every night for a month," Percy said. "You?"

"I wasn't scared. I was mad. I thought if I was good enough, smart enough, useful enough, someone would—" She stopped. "You know. Stay."

Percy reached over and took her hand, the way he'd learned to do when words weren't enough. Boyfriend to girlfriend. Anchor to anchor.

"Someone did," he said. "Me. You. Us."

She squeezed his fingers. "Yeah. Us."

Thunder rolled far out over the Sound, too far to matter. Somewhere Zeus was clearing his throat, reminding everyone spring storms were his. Somewhere Poseidon answered with a higher tide. The brothers, always talking in weather.

Thalia knocked once and let herself in without waiting, shaking rain off her jacket. "Family meeting," she announced. "Living room. Now."

"We don't have a living room," Annabeth said. "We have six feet of floor."

"Then it'll be cozy."

Clarisse ducked in next, then Silena, all damp and laughing, crowding into the tiny cabin until there was no floor left, just knees and elbows and wet socks.

They sat shoulder to shoulder in Poseidon's cabin while rain poured outside, and talked about nothing: summer plans, capture the flag strategies, whether Mr. D would ever wear a normal shirt, whether the new Hermes kid expected in June would be as dramatic as Thalia.

Thalia stretched her long legs across Percy's bunk and said, "When I was seven, I thought home was a place you lost. Like keys. Like you put it down somewhere and if you just retraced your steps—" She shook her head. "Then scouting, and home was a tent that moved. Then here, and home was a cabin with my name on a bunk that kept getting dusty when I left."

"What is it now?" Silena asked softly.

Thalia looked around at them, crowded into the lamplight, rain on the windows, Annabeth's gifted owl watching from the shelf.

"Practice," she said, echoing Chiron. "People. You keep showing up."

Clarisse grunted. "Gross. Feelings."

"You love us," Percy said.

"I tolerate you," Clarisse said, but she was leaning against Silena and her voice was gentle. "I’ve learned tolerance is a virtue."

Silena smiled. "I've learned home is whoever brings you soup when you're sick and doesn't make fun of you for crying."

"At thirteen," Annabeth said, "I've learned home is where someone knows which blueprint is which without asking."

"At thirteen," Percy said, "I've learned home is where your girlfriend throws a towel at your face."

They laughed, and the cabin walls, which had once echoed empty, held the sound warm.

---

By evening the rain stopped as suddenly as it had started, leaving the whole camp dripping and shining under a washed blue sky.

The creek path was still closed. The loose bracket on the railing had been replaced, but Beckendorf wasn't satisfied with how the old wood held the new bolt. Percy saw the work crew at the crossing when he went to collect water for the bonfire.

"Is it fixed?" he asked.

"Not for use," Beckendorf said. "The bolt is fine. The rail needs a new length of wood before anyone can lean on it."

"Can I help?"

"You can help carry the barrier signs, if Chiron has cleared you for that."

Percy checked. Chiron approved the sign work but asked him not to test the structure himself. Percy had enough strength to hold back a current. That did not mean his hands could tell whether an old timber would hold a camper leaning on it in the rain.

He took the signs along the alternate path and placed them where new arrivals could see them before reaching the crossing. Annabeth met him with Lucy and her counselor. Lucy checked the direction of each arrow, then stood back at the first fork.

"That one is still too high," she said.

Percy looked up. The sign was readable to him. He lowered it so a younger camper could see it without craning their neck.

"Better?"

"Better."

They walked the route once more and returned to the Big House. Chiron added the replacement rail to the next day's repair list and asked that the shortcut remain closed until it passed inspection. Percy thought of the old habit of shouting *Fixed!* every time someone tightened a screw. The new habit was slower. It also meant people could trust the sign that told them where to walk.

On the pavilion steps he found Silena sorting towels from the rain into two baskets, one for washing and one for drying. Clarisse was beside her, trying to untangle a pile of wet tent lines from the afternoon's training drills.

"You're late," Clarisse told him.

"For what?"

"Helping."

"I didn't know I was assigned."

"You're assigned now."

Percy raised his eyebrows. "Ask me."

Clarisse glared at him. Then, with visible effort, she said, "Would you help us untangle these? We can finish before dinner if all three of us work."

"Yes," Percy answered.

Silena smiled down at her towels. "That was almost polite."

"Don't spread rumors," Clarisse said.

They worked until the lines were neatly coiled and each was marked for the correct storage rack. Percy found that the knots were worse near the old broken pegs, which let lines twist whenever someone pulled a bundle free.

"Could we fix the pegs tomorrow?" he asked.

"Maybe," Clarisse replied. "They're not an emergency."

"Then put them on the list."

Silena held up the basket. "My resolution was to stop making everybody's problems my own. These towels were left by people who can do their own laundry."

Percy looked at the name tags. Two belonged to Apollo campers who were still at practice. Silena had been sorting them so they wouldn't mildew before the evening return, but she was not their laundry service.

"Let's leave a note," he said.

She wrote a reminder on the notice board. Clarisse put the dry lines away. They went to dinner with their own work finished and everybody else's laundry still somebody else's responsibility.

Percy liked the way the rain had changed the camp without turning the entire evening into a crisis.

The Apollo counselors requested a supervised bonfire down by the lake. Chiron approved the site after inspecting the fire ring, confirming a water supply and assigning adults to stay until the embers were cold.

Word spread cabin to cabin: bonfire, lake, bring food, bring blankets.

It became, without anyone planning it, the thing Annabeth had promised in the mud: dinner. All of us.

They dragged tables down to the shore. They carried pots from the dining pavilion. The Demeter kids brought early lettuce and last fall's apples. The Hermes kids stole — borrowed — extra bread. The Hephaestus kids rigged lanterns on poles. The Hecate kids made the fire burn blue-green at the edges just for pretty.

Percy and Annabeth sat on the old dock with their feet in the water, watching it come together. Thalia directed traffic like a general. Clarisse carried a table by herself while two Hermes boys pretended to help. Silena arranged wildflowers in tin cups, bossing Drew until Drew laughed and obeyed.

Chiron arrived in his true centaur form, massive and gentle, settling on the sand with a sigh. Mr. D arrived with a diet soda and a look of long-suffering that softened when the Apollo kids sang.

When the food was ready, they didn't sit by cabin. They sat by family — chosen and given both.

Percy and his girlfriend Annabeth, side by side, damp shoes off, toes in sand.

Annabeth's sister Thalia, right next to them, close enough that their shoulders touched, her jacket spread over Annabeth's lap against the evening chill.

And the rest of the Poseidon siblings: Clarisse with a plate piled high, arguing with Thalia about lightning versus earthquakes; Silena handing out napkins and refilling cups without being asked. Nearby sat Beckendorf and Silena’s friends, the Stolls, and the new winter kids, wide-eyed to be included.

Poseidon sent a breeze that lifted the napkins and set the lanterns swaying. Zeus sent a last flicker of heat lightning far out over the water, harmless and beautiful, like a wave from a distant uncle.

Percy looked down the long, uneven table — mismatched plates, muddy boots, laughing faces lit by firelight — and thought of all the dinners he'd eaten in his life. The small apartment table with his mom, just two. The lonely Poseidon table for one in his remembered first week of the original life. The crowded pavilion. And now this: sand underfoot, lake at their backs, spring air in their lungs.

Annabeth stood up with her cup. She was thirteen, mud on her boots, pencil still behind her ear, and she looked utterly at home.

"Toast," she said. "We have to do a toast. It's tradition."

"Since when?" Clarisse said.

"Since now. I just decided. I'm the architect. I decide structures."

Everyone laughed.

Annabeth raised her cup. Her hand shook just a little, not from nerves but from feeling too much at once.

"To first summer," she said. "When we were twelve and didn't know the way to the arena."

"To second summer ahead," Thalia said, standing too, raising her cup, "when we’ll be older and probably still think we know everything and don’t."

"To winter," Silena said softly, standing, "when we stayed."

"To spring," Clarisse said, grunting to her feet, "when we came back."

They all looked at Percy.

Percy stood. He was thirteen, sun-browned already from one day of spring sun, hair wild, shirt still damp. His girlfriend on one side, her sister on the other, his family in front of him, his lake behind him.

He thought about what home meant now, after the life he remembered and the days he had lived here.

Home wasn't just the remembered apartment with the blue cookies, though he loved it. Home wasn't Cabin Three with its three bunks, though he loved it. Home wasn't even camp, with its strawberry fields and bronze swords and lumpy beds, though he loved it fiercely enough to ache.

Home was the practice Chiron talked about. Showing up. Throwing the towel. Saving the seat. Remembering the allergies. Building the pavilion before anyone asked. Sitting on the dock on a cool June morning because your boyfriend likes the open water.

Home was people.

"To next summer," Percy said, voice steady, carrying across water and firelight. "To many summers. To us."

"To us!" forty voices shouted, cups clashing, fire crackling, lake lapping the pilings of the old dock where it had all started.

They ate until the stars came out. They laughed until Mr. D pretended not to smile. Thalia told the story of Percy's map-stone incident with dramatic embellishments. Clarisse reenacted her draw with Percy using breadsticks as swords. Silena braided Annabeth's hair with wildflowers. Annabeth showed everyone the pavilion plans by lantern light, and everyone pretended to understand load-bearing walls.

Later, when the fire burned low and the younger kids were asleep on blankets, the older five stayed by the water.

Thalia, watching the sky like she was memorizing it. Clarisse, head on Silena's shoulder, finally quiet. Silena, humming under her breath. Annabeth thirteen, leaning against Percy, blueprint tube safe in its case. Percy thirteen, arm around his girlfriend, feet in the lake that had cleared under the June sun.

Same dock. Same lake. Same friends, older.

The water was black and silver under the moon. The camp behind them was dark and breathing. Ahead was another summer, with new kids and new quests and new marks on the cabin wall.

Percy felt Annabeth's breathing slow as she dozed. He felt Thalia's boot nudge his in silent solidarity. He heard Clarisse snore faintly and Silena shush her fondly.

He looked up at the stars, at the constellations his father sailed by and Annabeth's father threw lightning through, and he made a promise without words, the way Poseidon kids do: to stay, to return, to build, to remember.

Spring had come back, as it always did. So had they. So would they.

Because home is people
