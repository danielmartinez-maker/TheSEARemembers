## Chapter 23: Sally's Blue


Percy didn't remember deciding to do it.

That was how most bad ideas at Camp Half-Blood started, in his experience. You didn't decide. You just opened your mouth at breakfast while the mist was still hanging low over the strawberry fields and said, "My mom made blue chocolate chip cookies in the life I remember," and then Tyson — his Cyclops half-brother, visiting from the undersea forges, all one eye and elbows and earnest — said, "MAKE THEM?" so loud that half the dining pavilion turned around, and then it was too late to take it back.

By lunch, it had mutated. Tyson told Grover. Grover told Ella. Ella told approximately everyone she'd ever screeched at, which was everyone. Annabeth raised one eyebrow at him across the Poseidon table — because she still sat with him half the time even though she had her own cabin now, the Zeus cabin, all sharp marble and storm-static air — and said, "You know how to bake?"

"Kind of," Percy said.

"Kind of?" She leaned in. She had a smudge of ash on her cheek from morning archery and her blonde curls were escaping her ponytail in a way that made his stomach do something stupid. "Jackson, either you can replicate a baked good from memory or you can't. Which is it?"

"I watched her a million times," he said, defensive. "How hard can it be?"

She smiled, slow and dangerous. "Famous last words."

That was how, at four o'clock on a sticky afternoon, Percy Jackson ended up standing in the Big House kitchen with his sleeves pushed up, a flour sack at his feet, and an audience.

The audience consisted of: Tyson (assistant, extremely enthusiastic, flour already in his hair despite not having touched anything yet), Annabeth Grace (self-appointed quality control, notebook open, pencil sharpened to a lethal point), Silena Beauregard (Cabin Three, here because she'd heard the word *cookies* from three cabins away), Clarisse La Rue (Cabin Three, here because Silena dragged her and because she'd said, quote, "Someone's gotta stop Jackson from poisoning the camp"), Thalia Grace (Cabin One, Zeus's daughter, here because she'd smelled butter from her hill patrol and followed it like a shark), Grover Underwood (satyr, official taste-tester, already nervous), and Ella (harpy, perched on top of the refrigerator, repeating "COOKIE! COOKIE!" at increasing volume).

Argus, who ran security and the kitchen when the usual cook was off, took one look at the crowd, said, "You break it, you clean it," and left. That was the full extent of adult supervision.

Percy stared at the counter. Someone — probably the harpies who did dishes — had laid out what he'd asked for: flour, sugar, brown sugar, butter, eggs, baking soda, salt, vanilla, chocolate chips, and blue food coloring. Two bowls. A hand mixer that looked older than the gods. Cookie sheets. Parchment paper.

His heart was beating too fast. It was just cookies. It shouldn't have felt like standing at the edge of the canoe lake before a Capture the Flag match.

"It's not a normal recipe," he said. His voice sounded too loud in the tiled kitchen. "My mom was Sally. She died when I was little. I've been trying to make blue cookies the way I think she'd have liked them." He looked at Annabeth for half a second. They both knew there was more he couldn't say to this crowd.

"Why blue?" Tyson asked, swinging his legs where he sat on the counter. He was too big for the counter, really, but nobody was going to tell him that.

Percy shrugged, hands in his hoodie pocket. "Some people think blue food looks weird," Percy said. "I like the idea of making it anyway. It seemed like something she would have done."

Silena pressed a hand to her chest. "I love her. I've never met her and I love her."

"Yeah," Percy said quietly. "Me too."

Talking about her still caught him by surprise. He had told them about Sally for the cake, but saying her name could close his throat without warning, and then he had to go swim until his chest stopped hurting. To everyone else she had died ten years ago. To him her chipped mug and off-key humming were near enough to touch. He could feel Annabeth looking at him, not staring, just — noticing. She didn't write anything in her notebook.

"Okay," he said, clapping his hands together too hard. Flour puffed up from his palms. "Okay. Step one. Butter and sugars. Mom always says room-temp butter, not melted, or the cookies go flat. She leaves it out on the counter while she hums. She hums off-key."

"I can hum off-key," Grover offered.

"Please don't," Thalia said, already elbow-deep in the chocolate chip bag. "You'll summon something."

---


Annabeth Grace believed in blueprints.

Her father in the life she remembered — Frederick Chase, professor of military history with a study full of books and model airplanes — believed that if you measured twice, you only had to cut once. Her father here — Zeus, King of the Sky, Lord of Lightning, Extremely Absent — believed in dramatic entrances and leaving before the hard part. Annabeth had decided early that she preferred Frederick's philosophy. It was still useful, even in a household whose old routines she had to learn from Thalia.

So when Percy said, "Okay, like, two sticks of butter? And a cup of sugar? I think?" she felt her eye twitch.

"Jackson," she said. "What are your units?"

"My units are Mom," Percy said. "She uses this blue chipped mug for flour and just — knows."

"That is not a unit of measurement."

"It worked for like twelve years!"

Annabeth flipped to a fresh page in her notebook and wrote at the top in block letters: SALLY'S BLUE — ATTEMPT 1. Underneath: *Hypothesis: Replicable.*

"Right," she said. "We're standardizing. Two sticks butter equals one cup equals two hundred twenty-six grams. One cup white sugar. One packed cup brown sugar. Are we packing the brown sugar?"

Percy blinked. "Packing?"

She demonstrated, pressing brown sugar into the measuring cup with the back of a spoon until it held its shape. "See? Like building a foundation. If you leave air gaps, the structure fails. Same with cookies."

Tyson watched with total reverence. "Annabeth builds cookies like buildings."

"Everything is buildings if you're brave enough," Annabeth said, and Percy laughed — that sudden, surprised laugh he did when she said something weird and he liked it anyway.

She liked making him laugh. She logged that familiar pleasure in a separate, non-cookie part of her brain. Even a flour emergency couldn't make her stop loving his laugh.

They creamed the butter and sugars together with the ancient hand mixer. It roared like a minotaur and sprayed little flecks of sugar everywhere. Tyson held the bowl steady with both hands while Percy guided the beaters, and for a minute the kitchen smelled like nothing but sweet butter and possibility.

"Eggs next," Percy said. "Two. And vanilla. Mom does — she does this thing where she pours vanilla in the cap and then just dumps it. She says measuring vanilla is for people who don't trust joy."

Silena, who had been braiding Ella's head feathers with pink ribbon while perched on a stool, looked up sharply. "Your mom is a philosopher."

"She says a capful," Percy clarified. "Maybe two if she's had a long week."

Annabeth wrote: *2 eggs + ~2 tsp vanilla (2 capfuls = trust joy).* "Acceptable margin of error," she allowed. "Vanilla is forgiving."

"Vanilla is load-bearing," Silena corrected, sliding off her stool. She moved like water, all effortless grace even in ratty camp shorts and an apron she'd tied with a perfect bow at the back. "My mom — my mortal mom — used to bake for charity galas. She taught me: if your dough tastes flat, it's not more sugar. It's salt and vanilla. People always forget the salt. And cheap vanilla will betray you. You want the real stuff."

She picked up Percy's vanilla bottle, sniffed it, and made a face. "This is the fake stuff. The camp-budget stuff. No offense."

"Some taken," Thalia muttered around a mouthful of chocolate chips.

"It'll still work," Silena said quickly, touching Percy's arm. "We'll just use a little extra. And a pinch more salt. Trust me."

Percy nodded, and Annabeth watched his shoulders drop half an inch. He'd been braced, she realized. Braced for someone to say it wouldn't work without Sally here.

"Okay," Percy said. "Dry stuff in the other bowl. Flour, baking soda, salt. Mom sifts — no, she doesn't sift. She whisks it with a fork and says sifting is a scam invented by people who sell sifters."

Thalia snorted. "I like her more every minute."

Annabeth measured flour with the focus of someone defusing a trap. Level off with a knife. No packing. Three cups? Two and a quarter? Percy squinted.

"I think it's the Toll House bag but with extra flour? Because blue dye makes them spread? Or — wait. Mom adds an extra quarter cup when it's humid."

"It is humid on Long Island today," Annabeth said. "Look at the windows. Adding quarter cup." She wrote it down and underlined it twice.

Tyson wanted to crack the eggs. Everyone let him, because saying no to Tyson was physically impossible. He cracked the first one perfectly with one hand. The second one he sneezed on, and they had to fish out shell while Grover bleated anxiously and Ella chanted, "SHELL! SHELL! BAD TEXTURE!"

Percy added the blue food coloring to the wet mix. This was the moment, he said. The whole point.

He tipped the little bottle. One drop. Two. The batter was pale yellow, then streaked, then — as the mixer turned — a bright, defiant, swimming-pool blue.

The kitchen went quiet.

"Oh," Silena breathed. "Oh, that's *gorgeous*."

"It looks like the ocean had a baby with the sky," Tyson whispered.

Thalia leaned over Percy's shoulder, chips forgotten. "Okay, Punk. That's actually cool."

Percy stared into the bowl. The blue was so bright it hurt. For a second Annabeth saw his face do something complicated — pride and homesickness at the same time, like he'd opened a door and his mom's kitchen was on the other side, smelling like butter and rain.

"She'd make it brighter for birthdays," he said softly. "Like, full Smurf blue. She said if your tongue isn't blue after, what's the point."

Annabeth didn't write that down. She just memorized the way he smiled when he said it.

---


Silena Beauregard knew a lot about mothers.

She knew about the kind who loved you so loudly it filled every room, like her own mortal mother who kissed her cheeks with lipstick and told everyone in the grocery store that her daughter was a star. And she knew about the kind who loved you quietly, from far away, in letters you read until the creases wore thin. She knew about the kind who weren't there at all — and Camp Half-Blood had plenty of those stories, demigod kids who learned not to look at the dinner tables where their godly parents' empty seats gleamed.

She looked after her younger half-sister partly because Clarisse pretended she didn't need anyone looking after her.

So when Percy stood there with blue batter on his forearms and talked about his mom like she was the sun, Silena felt something warm bloom behind her ribs.

"Can I show you something?" she asked, nudging in beside him. "My mom's trick. You fold the dry into the wet in thirds. Not all at once, or you get flour pockets. And you stop the second you don't see white anymore. Overmixing makes them tough."

Percy handed her the spatula like a sword. "Show me."

She showed him. Fold, turn bowl, fold. The dough came together thick and blue as sea glass, studded already with the smell of vanilla. Tyson added chocolate chips — the whole bag plus half of another bag, because Percy said, "Mom always doubles the chips," and Thalia tried to protest the loss of her snack supply and was overruled 6-to-1.

"No nuts?" Clarisse asked from the corner, arms crossed. She'd been leaning against the oven like she was guarding it.

"Gross," Percy and Thalia said in unison.

"Agreed," Clarisse said, and that was the most peaceful interaction they'd ever had.

Silena snuck a taste of dough when Percy wasn't looking and her eyes went wide. "Oh, gods. The salt. Percy, it's perfect. Salty-sweet. Your mom's a genius."

"She'd love hearing that," Percy said. "She'd have baked you into a coma."

The oven beeped. First tray in. Twelve minutes, Percy said. Mom does eleven, but her oven runs hot, and this oven is ancient, so twelve.

Those twelve minutes were the longest twelve minutes of Silena's life, because Clarisse La Rue decided she was Head Baker.

---


Clarisse La Rue did not burn things on purpose.

That was a vicious rumor spread by the Ares cabin after the Great Pancake Incident of last summer, and she would deny it with a spear if necessary.

But the truth was, ovens hated her. Or her father, god of the sea, hated ovens. Or — more likely, as Silena had once whispered while braiding her hair — Clarisse got impatient and opened the oven door seventeen times to check.

"I was *monitoring*," Clarisse said, arms crossed, as the smoke alarm started screaming.

The first batch had gone in perfect — six fat scoops, spaced like soldiers. Percy had shown them how to use two spoons. Annabeth had used a ruler to ensure two-inch spacing ("Structural integrity during spread," she'd said, dead serious). Tyson had licked the spatula. Ella had tried to steal a raw dough ball and Grover had talked her down with promises of first taste.

Then Clarisse said, "They look done," at minute seven.

"They're not done," Percy said.

"They *look* done."

"They're pale blue. They're supposed to get golden edges. Mom says —"

"Your mom's not here, Jackson. I'm telling you, they're done."

She pulled the tray halfway out. The kitchen filled with the smell of almost-cookies — butter and sugar and blue. The tops were still glossy.

"See? Raw," Thalia said.

"They'll set as they cool," Clarisse insisted. "That's how —"

"Put them back," Annabeth, Silena, and Percy said together.

Clarisse shoved them back and slammed the door. The ancient oven rattled. Then, because she felt embarrassed at being ganged up on, she cranked the temperature dial from 350 to 425. "To catch up for heat loss," she muttered. "Tactics."

Nobody noticed for four minutes. They were too busy fighting over who got to lick the blue bowl (Tyson won by sheer size; Thalia stole a finger-swipe and ran; Grover got a dab on his nose from Ella).

Then the smell changed. Sweet turned to sharp. Smoke curled from the oven seam.

"Clarisse!" Silena yelped.

Percy yanked the oven open with a dish towel. Smoke billowed. Six cookies, black on the bottom, still gooey-blue in the middle, smoking like tiny volcanic islands.

The smoke alarm screamed. Ella screamed louder, flapping to the ceiling. Grover bleated and knocked over a bag of flour, which exploded in a white cloud. Tyson started laughing so hard he slid off the counter. Thalia grabbed a cutting board and started fanning the alarm.

Annabeth, calm as a general, grabbed the tray with mitts, dumped the charred disks into the sink, and turned on the faucet. Hiss. More smoke.

Clarisse stood in the middle of it, face red — from heat or shame, nobody could tell — fists clenched.

"Great," she snapped. "Fantastic. Laugh it up, Jackson. Your mom's recipe, ruined."

The kitchen went quiet except for the alarm's dying chirps and the faucet hissing.

Percy looked at the blackened lumps in the sink. Then he looked at Clarisse.

And he laughed.

Not mean. Not at her. Just — a startled, helpless laugh that bubbled up and wouldn't stop. "My mom burned the first batch too," he gasped, leaning on the counter. "The first time she made them blue. She put in too much dye and set off the alarm and my stepdad — Gabe — yelled about the smoke, and she just — she scraped off the burnt bottoms with a knife and we ate them anyway standing over the sink. She said, 'Well, they're still blue, Perce. That's what matters.'"

Clarisse stared. "You're not mad?"

"Are you kidding? That was the funniest thing I've seen since I got here." He picked up one of the casualties with two fingers. The bottom was black. The top was still bright blue. "See? Still blue."

Something in Clarisse's shoulders unlocked. She let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "My—" She stopped. Started again. "My mom doesn't bake. She's — she's not around. My dad — Poseidon — you know how it is, tides and distance. I learned to cook from the camp kitchen staff because if I didn't, the younger campers would live on stolen granola bars."

Silena went very still. She knew this story in pieces, late-night pieces, but Clarisse never said it in front of people.

"My dad visited once," Clarisse said, staring at the sink. "When I was eight. He watched me do drills for ten minutes, told me my shield arm was weak, and left. I cried into a box of store-bought cookies. They were stale." She looked up, daring anyone to pity her. "So yeah. I don't know about moms who make everything blue to prove a point. Must be nice."

The quiet that followed wasn't awkward. It was careful, like dough you don't want to overmix.

Percy didn't do anything dramatic. He didn't hug her — Clarisse would have stabbed him. He just broke the burnt cookie in half and offered her the less-burnt half.

"Still blue," he said again. "Want to try again? You can be in charge of the timer and *not* touching the dial."

Clarisse took the cookie. Bit into the non-charred edge. Chewed. "Tastes like smoke and sugar," she declared. "Second batch will be better. Move, Jackson. I'm scooping this time."

And just like that, the war ended before it started.

---


Thalia Grace had stolen many things in her sixteen years of life.

She'd stolen food from convenience stores when she ran away at eight. She'd stolen a shield from a Cyclops (long story). Someone had once accused her of stealing something she had never even seen, an accusation she was still irritated by. It did not count as theft, however impressive she found her survival of the argument.

She had never stolen cookie dough quite so blatantly, quite so often, in front of quite so many witnesses, as she did that afternoon.

"It's quality control," she told Annabeth, mouth full, when Annabeth tried to smack her hand with a notebook. "I'm ensuring hero safety."

"You're ensuring you get salmonella," Annabeth said, but she was smiling. Thalia and Annabeth had a weird sister thing — full sisters with Zeus and Beryl for parents, both prickly and proud, both terrified of needing anyone. Thalia was older by four years, and made sure Annabeth knew it, all black hair and eyeliner and leather bracelets she wasn't supposed to wear in the kitchen. Annabeth was all plans and angles. They circled each other like twin storms.

But today the storm was quiet. Thalia watched Annabeth measure and Percy laugh and felt something unfamiliar and soft settle in her chest.

She hadn't felt mothered in a long time. Their mom — Beryl Grace, famous, beautiful, broken — had often loved the idea of her children more easily than the reality. Thalia remembered perfume and phone calls and being left with sitters. She remembered running with Annabeth. Beryl's new letters were trying to cross that distance; Thalia wasn't sure how far to let them. She talked about it rarely, and never easily.

Watching Percy talk about Sally so easily, like love was a recipe you could pass around, made her throat hurt in a way she hated.

So she stole dough instead of feelings.

Second batch went in — scooped by Clarisse with military precision, spaced by Annabeth with a ruler, taste-guarded by Thalia who ate roughly one dough ball per three scooped. Tyson spun the timer dial with ceremony. Silena set out cooling racks covered in parchment. Grover swept up the flour explosion with a broom twice his size while Ella rode the broom handle shouting, "WHEEE! WHEEE!"

Percy leaned against the counter, blue up to his elbows, watching his friends bicker over oven light etiquette.

"You okay?" Thalia asked quietly, nudging him with her shoulder. She offered him a stolen dough ball as a peace offering. He took it.

"Yeah," he said, surprised. "Yeah, actually. Usually when I talk about my mom I feel like — like I'm gonna cry or throw up or both. But here —" He gestured at the mess, the smoke still hanging faintly, Clarisse arguing with Annabeth about convection currents. "It feels good. Like she's here a little."

"She is," Thalia said gruffly. "In the blue. That's some powerful magic, Punk. Better than Zeus's lightning."

Percy bumped her shoulder back. "Don't let Annabeth hear you say that. She'll make a chart."

"I heard that," Annabeth called without looking up from her notebook, where she was sketching what appeared to be a cookie cross-section with labeled layers. "And I'm already making a chart."

The timer dinged. Eleven minutes, thirty seconds — Annabeth had adjusted for oven recovery after the Burning.

Percy opened the door. The smell rolled out — butter, brown sugar, vanilla, chocolate, salt. Perfect.

Six cookies, puffed and golden at the edges, bright sky-blue in the centers, chocolate chips melty.

The kitchen erupted.

---


Grover Underwood took his duties seriously.

As a satyr, as a searcher, as Percy's best friend and protector, he understood that some quests were about monsters and prophecies, and some quests were about telling the truth when the cookies were good.

Ella perched on his shoulders, vibrating. "TEST! TEST! Ella tests! Ella tastes!"

"Procedure," Annabeth announced, holding up a hand. She'd written TASTING PROTOCOL at the top of a new page. "We wait five minutes for structural set. Then we divide. No one burns their mouth. Thalia, that means you."

"I burned my mouth once," Thalia protested. "One time."

"Three times," Grover, Percy, and Silena said together.

They waited. It was torture. The cookies sat on the cooling rack, steaming faintly, blue as a clear sky over Long Island Sound. Tyson lay on the floor with his chin on the counter edge, staring. Ella counted seconds out loud and kept losing track after twelve.

Percy couldn't stand still. He wiped counters. He rinsed bowls. He kept glancing at the cookies like they might disappear.

"What if they're wrong?" he whispered to Annabeth, so quiet only she could hear. "What if I remembered wrong and they don't taste like hers?"

Annabeth closed her notebook. For once, she didn't have a measurement or a plan. She just looked at him — really looked, gray eyes steady.

"Then we'll adjust," she said. "We'll add more salt. Less flour. More joy. We'll make Attempt Two and Attempt Three until they're right. That's what she would do, right? She wouldn't give up after one batch."

Percy nodded, throat tight.

"And Percy?" She bumped his elbow with hers. "Even if they're not exact — you brought her here. You told us about the blue mug and the humming and the burnt bottoms eaten over the sink. That's — that's the important part. The cookies are just the delivery system."

He stared at her. "When did you get wise?"

"I've always been wise. You're just now noticing."

"Five minutes!" Tyson shouted, leaping up. Flour exploded off him like a volcano.

They divided the six cookies. Percy insisted everyone get a piece of the same cookie first — the biggest one — so they all tasted the same bite. Grover got the honor of the first official bite, Ella leaning down from his shoulders to nip the edge at the same time.

Grover closed his eyes.

Crisp edge. Chewy center. Salty-sweet. Butter. Brown sugar with that deep molasses hum. Chocolate pooling dark against blue. Vanilla lingering. Salt sparking on the tongue.

It tasted like — like summer kitchens and laughter and someone who loved you enough to dye dough blue just because the world said you shouldn't.

"Oh," Grover breathed, ears trembling. "Oh, Percy."

Ella flapped wildly. "BLUE! SWEET! SALT! GOOD! ELLA LOVES BLUE! ELLA LOVES PERCY'S MOM!"

Tyson ate his piece in one bite and immediately burst into tears. "TASTES LIKE HOME!" he wailed, hugging Percy around the middle and lifting him off the floor. "TYSON LOVES SALLY! TYSON LOVES PERCY!"

Silena had both hands over her mouth. "The vanilla — Percy, you did it. Tell me you wrote down what we did."

Annabeth held up her notebook triumphantly. "Standardized! Replicable! Sally's Blue, Attempt One, SUCCESS!"

Clarisse took a bite. Chewed. Swallowed. Took another bite. She didn't cry — Clarisse La Rue did not cry over cookies — but her eyes went shiny and she turned away quickly to stare very hard at the refrigerator.

"Good," she said roughly. "Real good, Jackson. Your mom — she's good people."

Percy couldn't answer. His own cookie was in his hand, warm, blue crumbs on his fingers, tongue already turning faintly blue the way Sally always promised. He took a bite and —

Home. Fire escape rain. Apartment humming. *Percy, baby, try this.* Blue tongue in the mirror, laughing.

He didn't cry. That was the miracle. For another afternoon since he'd come to camp, talking about Sally didn't feel like drowning. It felt like swimming — easy, buoyant, salt holding him up.

He felt Annabeth's hand slip into his, brief and fierce, flour and all. He squeezed back.

"She'd like you all," he said, voice thick but steady. "She'd feed you until you burst and ask a million questions and remember everything. Thalia, she'd love your stupid jokes. Silena, she'd ask about your designs. Clarisse — she'd tell you your shield arm is perfect and your dad's an idiot for only watching ten minutes."

Clarisse let out a wet laugh. "Damn right."

"Grover, she'd make you tin foil hats so the —" Percy caught himself. No villains today. Warm only. "So the pigeons can't read your thoughts. Ella, she'd teach you new words. Tyson — she'd already love you most, sorry."

"I LOVE SALLY MOST TOO!" Tyson declared, still hugging him.

"Annabeth —" Percy turned. She was watching him with that architect's gaze, like she was memorizing the structure of this moment to rebuild it later. "She'd like you best because you use a ruler for cookies."

"I loved her too. She raised you in the life we remember," Annabeth said quietly, so only he heard. Then, louder, flipping her notebook shut: "Batch Two! We need forty more cookies for the camp. Tyson, preheat discipline. Clarisse, scooping. Thalia, hands out of the dough —"

"Never!" Thalia shouted, already stealing again, and the kitchen exploded into flour and laughter.

---


By sunset, they'd made nine dozen.

Batch Two was perfect. Batch Three was slightly salty because Tyson sneezed while holding the salt jar (they ate those anyway, calling them Sea Salt Specials). Batch Four was shaped like monsters by Ella, who insisted sharks were cookies now. Batch Five was Annabeth's experiment with exact gram weights, documented in triplicate. Batch Six was Clarisse's redemption — golden, chewy, flawless — and she stood over the tray with her arms crossed while everyone applauded, face burning redder than the oven.

"Shut up," she said, but she was smiling. "Shut up and eat."

They carried trays out to the dining pavilion as the conch horn blew. The whole camp descended. Blue cookies vanished in minutes. Kids walked around with blue tongues stuck out, comparing shades. The Stoll brothers tried to auction one for drachmas. Chiron himself took two, ate one in two bites, and said, "Your mother's recipe is remarkable, Percy. The centaurs approve of the tradition you've brought us. We are difficult to impress."

Mr. D, who'd been napping through the whole thing, woke up, ate a cookie, stared at it, ate three more, and said, "Blue. Huh. Kid's mother has style. Don't tell anyone I said that."

Tyson fell asleep in a flour drift by the hearth, a half-eaten cookie on his chest. Ella nested in his curls, murmuring, "Blue. Blue. Good." Grover played his pipes softly, something sweet and sleepy. Silena braided Clarisse's hair while Clarisse pretended to hate it. Thalia and Annabeth sat shoulder to shoulder on the Big House steps, blue-tongued, arguing happily about whether cookies were architecture or art.

Percy sat on the railing, cookie in hand, watching the sky go pink over the Sound. He thought about Sally's tiny apartment kitchen in his original childhood, the chipped blue mug, the off-key humming. He thought about Gabe's shouting and the rain on the fire escape and the way Sally always saved him the biggest cookie even when there wasn't enough.

He missed her so much it should have hurt. And it did — a little. A dull ache under his ribs. But it didn't swallow him. It sat beside him, like a friend.

Annabeth climbed up to sit next to him, swinging her legs. She offered him her notebook. On the last page, under the recipe, she'd written in careful letters:

*SALLY'S BLUE (Standardized, Camp Half-Blood Edition)*
*Butter, sugars, eggs, joy. Salt = remember. Vanilla = extra. Blue = on purpose.*
*Result: tastes like home. Replicable wherever love is present.*
*Verified by: Annabeth Grace, Architect.*

"For your mom," she said. "So you don't forget. And so we can make them again."

Percy took the notebook. His hands were shaking a little. "You spelled everything right."

"I always spell everything right."

"Thank you," he said. It meant more than cookies and they both knew it.

Below them, Clarisse's voice rose: "SILENA, IF YOU PUT A BOW ON MY COOKIE I WILL END YOU—"

"Too late! It's cute! Eat the cute!"

"THALIA, THAT'S MY COOKIE—"

"Finders keepers, Punk! Should've guarded it!"

"COOKIE! COOKIE! ELLA WANTS SHARK COOKIE!"

Percy laughed, blue tongue and all. Annabeth laughed too, leaning her head briefly against his shoulder — quick, fierce, gone before anyone could tease.

The sea remembered. The sky forgot. But kitchens — kitchens held on.

Percy held up his last cookie to the sunset. Still blue.

"Mom," he whispered, "we did it."

He could picture her in that Manhattan kitchen as clearly as ever: blue walls, chipped blue mug, Sally Jackson humming off-key and adding an extra capful of vanilla to a quiet batch. She wasn't there in this life. But the taste lingered, and the friends laughing below him had learned her recipe, and he could carry that home into the next warm afternoon.
