## Chapter 25: Night Watches

The notice went up on the Big House porch on a Sunday afternoon, written in Chiron's neat, slanted hand on a piece of pegboard that had seen better summers.

*MONSTERS RESTLESS NEAR BORDERS. NO CAUSE FOR ALARM. NIGHT PATROLS THIS WEEK, IN PAIRS. SIGN UP.*

Underneath, in Mr. D's handwriting, in purple marker: *Try not to die. Paperwork is annoying.*

Percy stood with the rest of the crowd reading it, a half-eaten plum from the strawberry fields in his hand, juice running down his wrist.

"Restless?" Connor Stoll said. "What does restless mean? Like, pacing? Like, growling? Like, filling out job applications?"

"It means stay inside the borders after dark unless you're on patrol," Luke said, already scanning the sign-up sheet. He looked tired. He always looked a little tired since Percy arrived, he thought, in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

Chiron clopped out onto the porch then, his tail swishing against the steps. He smiled, but it was the practiced calm smile adults use when they don't want twelve-year-olds to panic.

"Just a precaution," he said. "The borders are strong. Peleus is alert. But the woods have been noisy. The naiads report movement — hellhounds ranging farther than usual, a harpy flock circling the valley. Nothing has tested the boundary. I would like it to stay that way. So we will keep eyes on it."

Annabeth, standing next to Percy with her arms crossed, nodded like this made perfect sense. "Extra watches make sense. More data points."

"More lost sleep, you mean," Clarisse grumbled from behind them. She had a spear over one shoulder and a smear of forge soot on her cheek. "Great. Love that for me."

The sign-up sheet filled fast. Older campers took the deep-woods slots. Younger ones got paired with older ones. When the dust settled, the pairings for the week looked like this:

West Ridge to the Myrmidon Stone: Percy Jackson and Thalia Grace.
East Trail to the Creek Bend: Annabeth Grace and Clarisse La Rue.
Creek to the South Pines: Silena Beauregard and Grover Underwood.

"Night patrol," Thalia said, slinging an arm around Percy's shoulders and grinning, her black hair crackling faintly with static. "You and me, Kelp Head. Try to keep up."

"You have four years on me," Percy said.

"Emotionally, I'm even older," Thalia said solemnly. "I've been putting up with twelve-year-olds for way longer than you have."

Annabeth stared at her own pairing for a long moment. Clarisse stared back.

"This is going to be terrible," Clarisse said.

"Probably," Annabeth agreed.

"We'll make it work," Clarisse said, after a beat. "You don't snore on watch, do you?"

"I don't sleep on watch."

"Good answer, Zeus girl."

Silena linked arms with Grover and beamed. "Oh, we're going to have the best time. Midnight picnics. Stargazing. Braiding hair. Grover, have you ever had your hair braided?"

"No," Grover said, alarmed. "Do goats— do satyrs— is that—"

"It is now," Silena said firmly.

Grover looked at Percy with wide, pleading eyes. Percy mouthed *good luck* at him.

That night after dinner, Chiron handed out watch cloaks, whistles, and little bronze hand-bells. "Two blasts for attention. Three for danger. Do not leave the border path. Do not chase anything into the woods. If you see something, ring and retreat to the nearest signal fire."

He looked at each of them in turn, lingering a little on the youngest faces.

"You are my eyes and ears," he said gently. "Not my swords. Understood?"

Six heads nodded.

And so began the week of night watches.

The border at night was a different country.

Percy had walked the west ridge a hundred times in daylight — the path worn pale through the grass, the Myrmidon Stone jutting up like a broken tooth, the boundary pine standing dark against the sky at the top of the hill far behind them. In daylight it was familiar. Safe.

At 11:47 p.m., with fog pooling in the hollows and the moon smudged behind clouds, it was not familiar at all.

Every cricket sounded amplified. Every branch creak made his hand twitch toward Riptide in his pocket. The border itself hummed if you listened for it, a low, almost subsonic thrum that Percy felt more in his teeth than in his ears, like standing too close to a speaker.

"You hear that?" he whispered to Thalia.

"The border?" Thalia whispered back. She walked three steps ahead of him, spear balanced easy across her shoulders, her leather jacket creaking. She had insisted on the leather jacket even on a warm night. "Yeah. Thrum-thrum. Like the camp's heartbeat."

"I thought I was imagining it."

"Nah. Daughter of Zeus perks. I can feel weather and electricity and big magic barriers. Comes with the family package." She glanced back and grinned, teeth white in the dark. "You get fish telepathy. I get live weather channel."

"I don't have fish telepathy," Percy protested. "Not exactly. It's more like... suggestions. With horses. And... okay, a little with fish."

"Fish telepathy," Thalia confirmed, nodding sagely. "Write it on your medical bracelet."

They laughed, too loud, and then immediately shushed each other, which made them laugh harder. Percy clapped a hand over his mouth. Thalia snorted and almost dropped her spear.

"Shhh," Percy hissed. "Top-tier stealth team."

"We're terrifying," Thalia agreed. "Monsters are fleeing as we speak."

They settled into the rhythm after that — walk twenty paces, stop, listen, look, walk twenty more. Their signal fire was a small iron basket on a post at the halfway point, coals glowing sullen red under a grate. Every hour they were supposed to stir it up and add a log so it stayed lit for the next pair or for Chiron on his rounds.

Percy liked the pauses best. When they stopped, the woods stopped too, and then slowly started up again around them, deciding they were harmless. An owl called from somewhere deep in the pines. Something small rustled through the leaves.

"So," Thalia said on their third stop, poking the coals with a stick. Sparks spiraled up, orange against black. "Can I ask you something?"

"Sure."

"What's it like?" She hesitated, which wasn't like her. Thalia Grace didn't hesitate. She charged. "Having, like... family out there. In Atlantis, I mean. Annabeth told me your dad brought you there when you were little. What's it like going back without remembering it?"

Percy sat down on the cold stone beside the fire basket and pulled his watch cloak tighter. The wool was scratchy and smelled faintly of cedar and old smoke.

"Yeah," he said. "The day we visited recently, it was... a lot. Everything was supposed to be familiar."

He thought about how to describe it. He'd started a letter to Amphitrite about the visit, then crossed out three versions that sounded as though he were describing her own palace to a stranger. He couldn't let her see the missing memories behind the awkwardness. How do you describe a palace made of living coral and pearl that breathes with the tide, when your family thinks you should know every corner? How do you describe Triton carefully leaving room for a brother who doesn't remember their old lessons? How do you describe Amphitrite being kind in a familiar way that still surprises you, setting out your favorite snacks because she knows what you've forgotten? How do you describe his dad turning up late to dinner, dripping wet from a storm council, and grinning when he saw Percy like the whole ocean lit up?

"It's big," he said lamely. "Like, stupid big. You swim down a canyon and there's lights everywhere, and whales carrying mail, and mermen arguing about property lines. And the palace— it's warm, even though it's deep. There's this garden where my dad grows these glowing anemones. He showed me. He was really proud of them. Like, 'Percy, look, this one's new, I crossbred it myself.'"

Thalia smiled. "Poseidon gardens."

"Yeah. And there were palace families. So many families. Little kids who stared at me, and older ones who pretended not to stare. Triton— he's my half-brother— he shook my hand so hard I thought he was trying to crush it. He was polite. Very... correct."

"Ugh. I hate correct," Thalia said.

"Me too. But then this little girl, maybe six, with green hair in braids, she just swam straight up to me and asked if my shoes were glued on, because she'd never seen sneakers underwater. And I told her they get soggy, and she laughed so hard she blew bubbles out her nose. Then she stole one of my socks and wore it like a hat for the rest of the day."

Thalia laughed, bright and startled. "No."

"Yes. Her mom was mortified. My dad thought it was hilarious. He said I should leave the other sock as tribute."

"Did you?"

"Of course I did. It's probably enshrined somewhere. Saint Percy's Soggy Sock."

They both giggled. The fire popped.

Thalia was quiet for a moment, turning her stick in the coals. "Do you... like having them? Even if they're far? Even if it's complicated?"

Percy thought about it. The ache that had sat behind his ribs since he'd left Atlantis, like homesickness for a place he'd supposedly lived for years but was only beginning to know. The way his dad had hugged him goodbye at the thermocline, hard enough to crack ribs, and whispered, *You always have a home in the sea, Percy. Never doubt that.*

"Yeah," he said softly. "I do. It's weird. I remember a different kind of home with Sally. I told you some of it, I know. She was enough. She was everything to me in that life. But here they all know this whole... other ocean of people. Who know my dad in a way I don't yet. Who fight and tease and send me weird shell necklaces with notes like 'do not eat this, love, Nerissa.' She's one of the palace nereids. Triton says she's been putting instructions on my presents forever. It's messy. But I like knowing they're there. When I'm near the water, I feel less... solo."

"Less solo," Thalia repeated. "I like that."

"What about you?" Percy asked. "Your mom— do you...?"

Thalia's expression flickered. "My mom's in LA. She's... she's my mom. She tries. When she's sober, she tries so hard it hurts. When she's not—" She shrugged. "It's not Atlantis. No glowing gardens. Just a little apartment with a busted AC and a neighbor who plays tuba at 2 a.m."

"Does she know where you are? That you're safe?"

"Yeah. I call her on Iris-message when I can. She cries. I tell her the food's good. She tells me to wear sunscreen." Thalia smiled crookedly. "Moms, right?"

"Right," Percy said, remembering Sally's blue pancakes and worried voicemails from his original childhood and the way she always smelled like sugar and turpentine from her candy shop.

They sat shoulder to shoulder, watching the coals breathe.

"Thanks for telling me about Atlantis," Thalia said. "Annabeth made it sound like a diplomatic incident with snacks."

"It was like 60% diplomatic incident," Percy admitted. "40% snacks."

"Perfect ratio."

A twig snapped downhill, outside the border. Both of them went still. Percy's hand found Riptide. Thalia's fingers tightened on her spear.

They listened. A long, low rustle. Then a whine — high, canine, miserable — and the sound of something large turning in circles and flopping down with a huff. Then silence.

"Hellhound?" Percy breathed.

"Probably," Thalia whispered. "Restless, not raiding. Chiron said they've been pacing."

They waited, barely breathing, for five minutes. Ten. Nothing came up the hill. The border thrummed steady and strong.

Thalia slowly relaxed. "See? We're so scary it laid down for a nap."

Percy let out a shaky laugh. "Top-tier stealth team."

They finished the patrol with their shoulders touching, just for the steadiness of it. At 2 a.m., Luke and Beckendorf arrived to relieve them, yawning and carrying fresh logs.

"Anything?" Luke asked.

"Heard a hellhound," Thalia said. "Didn't see it. Border's solid."

Luke nodded, his eyes scanning the dark treeline a little too long. "Good. Get some sleep."

On the walk back to the cabins, Percy and Thalia split a granola bar Percy had smuggled in his cloak pocket. It was stale and oaty and perfect.

"Night one down," Thalia said, bumping his shoulder with hers. "Six more to go."

"Think we'll survive?" Percy asked.

"Fish telepathy boy and lightning girl? We're inevitable."

Annabeth Grace liked being prepared. She had prepared for night patrol the way she prepared for everything: checklist, diagram, contingency plan.

Backpack contents, checked three times: two flashlights (one spare), extra batteries, notebook and pencil, water bottle, ambrosia square in wax paper, Yankees cap (invisibility, for emergencies only, with a mental note: *Chiron said observe, not engage*), dagger, whistle, bell, blanket, and — after some deliberation — a paperback copy of *A Brief History of Time* that she had no intention of reading on watch but felt better having.

Clarisse La Rue arrived at the east trailhead with a spear, a shield, a bag of beef jerky, and an expression that said she had prepared by being Clarisse.

"What's in the backpack, Grace?" she asked.

"Supplies."

"What's in the fanny pack?"

"It's a utility pouch."

"It's a fanny pack."

"It's efficient."

Clarisse snorted and started walking. Annabeth fell in beside her, a little too fast to keep up with Clarisse's long strides, but she refused to jog. She would not jog.

The east trail was narrower than the west ridge, winding through oak and maple down to where the creek bent sharp north. Fireflies stitched the dark between the trees. The air smelled like wet leaves and honeysuckle.

They walked in silence for the first half hour, stopping, listening, moving. Annabeth found the silence itchy. She was used to patrols with Luke, who narrated everything, or with Thalia, who bickered cheerfully about architecture. Clarisse was quiet in a way that felt like a wall.

So Annabeth did what she always did with walls: she studied them for doors.

"You've done night watch before, right?" she asked.

"Last summer. Harpies were nesting by the road. I got the dawn slot. It was freezing." Clarisse kicked a pebble off the path. "You?"

"Once. With Luke. We saw a cyclops trying to read the boundary sign. He sounded out B-O-U-N-D-A-R-Y for like ten minutes and then left."

Clarisse laughed before she could stop herself — a short, surprised bark. "Gods."

"He came back the next night with glasses," Annabeth said dryly. "Still couldn't read it."

That got a real grin. They walked a little easier after that.

At the creek bend signal fire, they stopped to feed the coals. Clarisse crouched and worked the logs with practiced hands, building a quick teepee that caught in under a minute. Annabeth watched, impressed despite herself.

"My stepdad taught me," Clarisse said, not looking up. "Mortal. He's big on camping. Real camping. No cabins, no running water, dig your own latrine camping. He says if I can survive a week with him in the Poconos, I can survive anything."

"That sounds... intense."

"It is. It's also kind of great. He snores like a chainsaw and burns oatmeal every time, without fail. Every time." Her voice softened in a way Annabeth had never heard. "He's only human, you know? He can't come up the hill past the boundary. He brings me back when I visit and picks me up at the bottom when I go out to see him. He always gets out of the car and hugs me like he's trying to memorize me."

Annabeth sat down across the fire from her, hugging her knees. "That must be hard. Leaving him."

"Yeah. But it's also... I don't know. He gets it. As much as anyone can. He tells everyone at work his stepdaughter goes to a special wilderness school. Which is true, if wilderness school includes sword fighting." Clarisse poked the fire. "What about your family?"

Annabeth picked at a loose thread on her cloak. "In my old childhood, my dad was Professor Chase. Frederick. He knew about camp. He... he loved me. In a busy way. Like, he had office hours for love. Zeus here is different, but he can vanish just as thoroughly. Beryl writes now. Thalia's trying to work out what to answer."

Clarisse winced. "Ouch."

"Frederick wasn't bad. He was just... in his head a lot. And then Helen — my stepmom there — had Matthew and Bobby, my little brothers, loud and normal and needing a lot. I felt like a guest when I went home. Like I was visiting someone else's family." She rubbed the loose thread between her fingers. "Here I have Thalia. She knows exactly where I belong. I just can't remember all the years that taught her."

She hadn't meant to say that much. She stared into the fire, embarrassed.

Clarisse was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "Can I ask you something weird?"

"Okay."

"What's it like having a sister who worries about you?"

Annabeth blinked. "Thalia?"

"Yeah. She—" Clarisse shook her head. "She checks on everyone. She pretended not to care when you had that fever a while back, but she sat outside the infirmary for three hours whittling. I counted the shavings. And when Chris got poison oak, she stole extra calamine from the Big House. She acts all tough, but she's a mother hen with a spear."

Annabeth smiled slowly. "She is, isn't she?"

"So what's it like? Having that?"

Annabeth thought. She remembered arriving at her old camp at seven, angry and feral and convinced love was a trick. That was not the girl Thalia here had carried into the valley. Her sister had told her about finding little Annabeth hiding in the Zeus cabin's rafters on their first thunderstorm, crying because thunder sounded like their parents fighting. Thalia had climbed up, sat next to her, and said, *Thunder's just clouds bumping into each other. Stupid clouds. Noisy idiots.* Annabeth couldn't find that night in her own mind, but she had heard the same gentle insults when the sky shook now.

"It's like..." Annabeth searched for the words. "It's like having someone who memorized where all your bruises are, so she can avoid them. Or press on them to make you laugh, depending on her mood. She worries, but she doesn't hover. She just... stations herself nearby. Like a guard dog who pretends she's just napping in the sun."

Clarisse nodded slowly. "That sounds nice."

"Do you have siblings? Mortal ones?"

"No. Just me. Just my stepdad and me and his burnt oatmeal when I'm out there." Clarisse tore open her jerky bag and offered it. Annabeth took a piece. It was peppery and tough and good. "Sometimes I wonder what it'd be like. To have someone who just... gets camp. Gets both worlds."

"You have Silena and Percy," Annabeth said. "Cabin Three—"

"Yeah, but Percy is younger and Silena makes worrying look easy. I'm supposed to be the tough one who knows stuff. Can't exactly crawl into someone's bunk and say I'm scared of the dark."

"Are you scared of the dark?" Annabeth asked, genuinely curious, not teasing.

Clarisse shrugged. "Not of the dark. Of... missing something. On watch, I mean. Of someone getting hurt because I blinked."

Annabeth felt something loosen in her chest. "Me too. That's why I bring two flashlights."

Clarisse laughed. "Nerd."

"Meathead."

They traded — jerky for half of Annabeth's ambrosia square (Clarisse protested, Annabeth insisted: "It's honeyed. You'll like it."). They walked the rest of the loop pointing out constellations. Clarisse knew the violent ones: Orion, Draco, Hercules mid-labor. Annabeth knew the stories behind them and told them with relish, doing voices for Hera that made Clarisse wheeze.

At 2 a.m., the replacements came — Castor and Pollux, sleepy and arguing about who'd lost the bell. Clarisse handed over the fire with a stern lecture about log placement.

Walking back uphill, Clarisse bumped Annabeth's shoulder with her own, lightly.

"Hey," she said gruffly. "If you ever need someone to station nearby... I'm good at pretending to nap in the sun."

Annabeth felt her throat go tight. "Same. For you."

"Don't get mushy, Grace."

"Wouldn't dream of it, La Rue."

Silena Beauregard had packed for night patrol like it was a picnic that happened to involve potential monsters.

Her bag, inspected by an increasingly concerned Grover at the trailhead, contained: a thick picnic blanket (blue gingham), a thermos of hot chocolate, a bag of marshmallows, two mugs, a hairbrush, three kinds of ribbons, a romance novel with a pirate on the cover, a first-aid kit, a hunting knife she barely knew how to use, a whistle, a bell, and a Tupperware of what she called "watch snacks" — cheese cubes, grapes, crackers, and chocolate-covered pretzels arranged in a pinwheel.

Grover stared. "Silena. We're patrolling."

"We're patrolling *in style*," Silena corrected, spreading the blanket at their signal fire spot by the creek with a flourish. "Chiron said observe and report. He didn't say we couldn't be comfortable while observing."

Grover, who had brought a panpipe, a tin of tin-can-and-apple-peel trail mix, and a deeply practical wool hat with ear flaps, sat down gingerly on the corner of the blanket like it might bite him.

"It's nice," he admitted.

"I know," Silena said, pouring hot chocolate. "Marshmallow?"

"Two, please."

They sat shoulder to shoulder, sipping cocoa, listening to the creek gurgle over stones. The border hum was fainter here, under the water-sound, but Grover could feel it in his hooves — a tingle, like static.

Silena sighed happily. "Okay. Tell me everything."

"About what?"

"About you. I feel like I know Percy — tide-pool boy, saves the world, can't fold a fitted sheet — and I know Annabeth — blueprint brain, terrifying when tired — but you, Grover Underwood, are a mystery. What's your favorite song? What's your dream? Do you have a crush? You can tell me."

Grover choked on his marshmallow. "What— I— that's— three questions!"

"Answer in any order."

Grover bleated nervously — a real, goaty bleat he immediately tried to cover with a cough. Silena pretended not to notice, which made him like her even more.

"Favorite song is 'Wildwood Hymn,'" he said. "It's a satyr traveling song. My dad used to play it. My dream is... to find Pan. I know everyone says he's gone, but... I have to try. And a crush—" He went red to the tips of his ears. "That's personal."

"Fair," Silena said easily, bumping his shoulder. "For the record, my favorite song is whatever the Aphrodite cabin is currently butchering in the shower. My dream is to run a bakery that also does makeovers. Like, you come in sad, you leave with cupcakes and perfect eyeliner. And my crush is—" She leaned in conspiratorially. "—Charles Beckendorf. Don't tell. He's so quiet and he makes things with his hands and he blushes when I compliment his forge work. It's adorable."

Grover grinned. "Your secret's safe."

"Good. Now braid my hair while we listen. I brought ribbons to practice on you after."

Grover had never braided hair before. His fingers were clumsy, but Silena talked him through it — *three strands, cross over, not under, gentle* — and by the end he had produced something lopsided but recognizable. Silena tied it off with a blue ribbon and used her hand mirror to admire it from three angles.

"Grover! It's wonderful! You're hired as my official watch hairdresser."

"I have a job," Grover said, but he was beaming. "I'm a searcher. And a protector. And now... apparently a hairdresser."

"Multitalented king."

They took turns walking the short loop to the south pines and back, Silena telling campfire stories to fill the quiet — the one about the Ares camper who tried to wrestle Peleus and ended up stuck in a tree for six hours; the one about the naiad who fell in love with a mailbox; the one about last year's capture-the-flag when someone greased the Athena flagpole with peanut butter and no one ever confessed.

Grover countered with satyr stories — the wild god's parties, the whispering groves, the time his Uncle Ferdinand got stuck in a laurel bush and had to be oiled out.

"Tell me the Pan story again," Silena said softly, on their third loop. "The real one. Not the campfire version."

Grover stopped walking. The moon had come out, silvering the creek. Frogs sang in rounds.

"They say he faded," Grover said. "A long time ago. That the wild got too small and he just... thinned out. But satyrs don't believe that. We think he's sleeping. Or hiding. Or waiting for us to remember how to listen." He tapped his chest. "My dad searched his whole life. He never found him. But he sent me letters — barkscrolls — about places where the wild still sang. He said, 'Grover, the world is loud now. You have to listen underneath.'"

Silena was quiet, which was rare. Then she slipped her hand into his — just for a second, a quick squeeze.

"Then we'll listen underneath," she said. "Together. On watch. That's what we're doing, right? Listening?"

Grover squeezed back. "Yeah."

They finished the night trading snacks — Grover's weird but weirdly good trail mix for Silena's pretzels — and Grover let Silena braid two tiny braids into the hair behind his horns, tied with green ribbons.

"You look dashing," Silena declared.

"I look ridiculous," Grover said.

"You can be both."

When the relief pair arrived — Lee Fletcher and Michael Yew, bickering about Apollo cabin playlist etiquette — they found Silena and Grover dozing back-to-back against a log, blanket tucked around them, mugs empty, ribbons everywhere.

Lee took a photo with a disposable camera. "Blackmail," he whispered.

"Worth it," Grover mumbled in his sleep.

Thalia Grace had a secret: she loved night watch.

She loved the crackle in the air after midnight, the way her skin buzzed faintly with storm-static even on clear nights. She loved how the world narrowed to flashlight beams and firelight and the person next to you. She loved that for once, nobody expected her to be loud.

Percy snored softly as he walked. Just for a second — a little head-nod, a jerk awake.

"Sleepyhead," Thalia whispered.

"Not asleep," Percy mumbled. "Resting my eyes strategically."

"You're walking."

"I'm talented."

Thalia laughed and handed him a juice box she'd stolen from the dining pavilion. She'd stolen six, actually, in different flavors, and made it a game: trade until you get your favorite. Percy wanted cherry. Thalia wanted grape. They were currently stuck with apple and fruit punch and negotiating like diplomats.

"Trading block is open," Thalia announced. "I offer one apple for your fruit punch, plus a cheese cracker to sweeten the deal."

"Throw in two cheese crackers and you have a deal."

"Highway robbery. Fine."

They clinked juice boxes.

The week was wearing on everyone in small, funny ways. At breakfast that morning, Annabeth had put salt in her coffee and drunk it out of spite rather than admit it. Clarisse had fallen asleep in the forge and woken up with a perfect anvil imprint on her cheek. Silena had started braiding anyone who stood still long enough — Pollux now sported four tiny braids he pretended to hate but secretly showed off.

But the watches were working. Nothing had crossed. The borders held. Chiron smiled a little wider at dinner.

Thalia and Percy reached the Myrmidon Stone and sat for their mid-shift break. Percy pulled out his notebook — a soggy, battered thing where he was trying to draw the constellations Annabeth had taught him. His Big Dipper looked like a shopping cart.

"That's terrible," Thalia said fondly.

"It's abstract."

"It's a crime."

Percy grinned and flipped the page. There, in better detail, he'd drawn Atlantis from memory — the coral palace, the anemone garden, the little girl with the sock hat. He'd labeled her *Princess Sockhat*.

Thalia studied it. "You're good at this. When you care."

"Annabeth says I draw like I fight — messy but with heart."

"Annabeth's right about most things. Don't tell her I said that."

"Secret's safe."

Thalia leaned back against the stone, looking up. The clouds had cleared, and the sky was a deep, stupid-beautiful black strewn with stars. She could name a few now, thanks to Annabeth's relentless tutoring. *There's Orion. There's Heracles. There's the Lyre.*

"You ever think about what happens after?" she asked quietly. "Like... you're twelve. I'm sixteen. We're kids. But we're also — soldiers, kind of. Do you think we'll still be doing this at twenty? At thirty?"

Percy was quiet. He picked at the grass.

"Sally wanted me to have a normal life, in the childhood I remember," he said. "She married Gabe — well, you know about Gabe — to hide me. To give me a normal life. And now I'm here, and there's no normal, and I still feel guilty for loving it here. Because she gave up so much to keep me safe, and I'm like... Mom, I love sword fighting! It's great! Amphitrite knows what camp is, and she lets me stay, but I think she worries every time the lessons get loud."

Thalia nodded. She got that. The guilt of loving the place that scared your parent.

"I think," she said slowly, "we get to be both. Kids and... whatever we are. We get to trade juice boxes on watch and also hold spears. We get to be scared and brave at the same time. Nobody said we have to pick."

Percy looked at her. "When did you get wise?"

"I'm the emotional elder, remember?"

They heard it then — movement, far down the slope, beyond the boundary. Not a hellhound whine this time, but a chorus — yips and rustles, many feet, moving parallel to the border, not toward it. Thalia's hand went to her bracelet. Percy's to his pocket. They stood, backs to the fire, eyes on the dark.

Thalia clicked her bell once — attention, not alarm — and lifted her whistle.

The sounds passed. A flock of something — harpies, probably, low and fast — swept through the valley below, wings hissing. They didn't even glance uphill. The border thrummed, steady as a drum.

Thalia lowered the whistle, exhaling. "See? Boring. Perfect. I love boring."

"Best patrol ever," Percy agreed, knees a little wobbly. "Zero stabbings."

They stayed standing until the sounds faded completely, then sat back down, a little closer than before.

"Hey, Percy?" Thalia said after a while.

"Yeah?"

"If you ever go back to Atlantis... save me a sock."

Percy laughed, and the fear melted out of his shoulders. "Deal. Princess Sockhat the Second."

Clarisse La Rue had decided Annabeth Grace was not as annoying as she'd previously categorized.

This was a significant reclassification. Previously, Annabeth had been filed under *Know-It-All, Avoid Unless Strategically Necessary*. Now she was under *Know-It-All Zeus Spawn, Tolerable, Maybe Even Useful*.

Progress.

Night four was warm and buggy. They'd both slathered on the Hermes cabin's homemade citronella goo (recipe courtesy of Travis Stoll: beeswax, lemon, and "good vibes") and still got eaten alive. Clarisse had seven bites on one ankle. Annabeth had one on her eyelid and looked faintly piratical.

"Tell me a campfire story," Clarisse demanded, scratching her ankle with a stick. "A real one. Not one of those Athena moral fables where the lesson is 'plan better.'"

Annabeth thought. "Okay. A true story Thalia told me. When I was seven, just after we got to camp here, I got lost in the woods during a game. Like, really lost. Past the borders — well, little me didn't know where the borders were yet. I ended up near the road, crying, because I'd dropped my lantern and it was dark."

Clarisse stopped scratching. She was listening.

"And Thalia found me," Annabeth said. "My sister. Big scary punk girl with a spear. She picked me up — I was tiny — and carried me on her back the whole way. She told me a story the whole time to keep me from crying. About a girl who stole Zeus's lightning bolt and used it as a nightlight."

"Did she get in trouble?" Clarisse asked.

"So much trouble. Chiron grounded her from the climbing wall for a week. She said it was worth it." Annabeth smiled at the picture her sister's telling made. "She says I wanted to be brave like her after that. Not fearless — she admitted she was shaking too — but brave anyway. That's still something I want."

Clarisse was quiet. Then, gruffly: "That's a good story."

"Your turn."

Clarisse sighed. "Fine. True story. When I was eight, my stepdad took me camping and I got scared because a raccoon got into our food bag and sounded like a monster. I started crying. He didn't laugh. He handed me a flashlight and a frying pan and said, 'Okay, soldier, let's go reclaim our oatmeal.' And we did. We chased that raccoon off together, screaming like maniacs. We lost the oatmeal, but we kept the pan."

Annabeth laughed. "Reclaim our oatmeal."

"He still has the pan. It's dented. He calls it the Raccoon Vanquisher. It's on the wall in our living room."

They traded watch snacks — Clarisse had upgraded from jerky to peanut M&Ms ("protein and morale," she declared). Annabeth had brought dried apple rings from the Big House kitchen. They mixed them together in Clarisse's helmet (cleaned first — Annabeth insisted) and ate trail mix à la Ares.

"Helmet bowls," Clarisse said. "Patent pending."

Midnight passed. The fire burned low and steady. Somewhere, an owl argued with a frog.

"You know what?" Clarisse said suddenly. "I get why she worries about you."

"Thalia?"

"Yeah. You're... you're the kind of person people worry about. Because you act like you don't need anyone, but you pack two flashlights because you're afraid of missing something. You're always three steps ahead, but you never watch where you're stepping."

Annabeth opened her mouth to protest, then closed it. "That's... annoyingly accurate."

"I'm perceptive," Clarisse said modestly. "And violent. It's a combo."

"Thank you," Annabeth said quietly. "For stationing nearby."

Clarisse shrugged, ears pink. "Someone's gotta make sure you don't walk off a cliff while reading."

"I don't read while walking."

"You absolutely do."

"...Sometimes."

They finished the patrol in comfortable silence, and when the relief came, Clarisse surprised Annabeth by offering her fist. Annabeth bumped it.

"Same time tomorrow, Grace?"

"Same time, La Rue."

Grover Underwood had discovered he was good at night watch.

Not good at staying awake — he was terrible at that; his head kept nodding and his horns kept clanking against his water bottle — but good at listening. Satyr ears were made for this: swiveling, catching layers. He could hear the creek, the frogs, the wind in the pines, the border hum, Silena humming under her breath as she braided, and underneath it all, the deeper quiet of the wild, holding its breath.

"You're doing that thing again," Silena said.

"What thing?"

"Listening underneath." She smiled. "Your face goes all... goaty-mystical."

"That's my natural face."

"It's a good face, Grover."

Grover's ears burned. He was glad it was dark.

Night five was their best night. They had a system now: walk the loop, check the fire, sip cocoa, tell stories, braid, trade snacks, repeat. Silena had brought a new snack — tiny powdered donuts — and Grover had brought a new story — the one about the first time he'd played panpipes for the dryads and they'd cried because it reminded them of rain.

Silena had cried a little at that, too, and then pretended the powdered sugar had gotten in her eyes.

"Do you think the monsters are lonely?" Silena asked suddenly, licking sugar off her fingers. "Like, out there in the dark, pacing? Do you think they want to come in because they're curious? Or hungry? Or just... lonely?"

Grover considered. As a future searcher, he'd been taught monsters were dangers, not friends. But as Grover, who cried at music and braided hair on watch, he wondered too.

"I think some are just hungry," he said honestly. "And some are angry. But some... maybe they're like us. Looking for a place to belong. The borders keep us safe, but they also keep them out. That has to feel... cold."

Silena nodded. "My mortal mom says every heart wants a home. Even monster hearts. She says that's why we have to be careful who we let in, but also why we have to keep loving anyway."

"That's... really wise."

"She has her moments." Silena leaned her head on his shoulder, just for a moment, friendly and sleepy. "You're a good listener, Grover. Pan's lucky to have you looking for him."

Grover felt his chest swell. No one had ever said it like that — not as a burden, but as a gift.

They were half-dozing when they heard it — singing. Faint, from the south, outside the border. High and sweet and wrong, like wind through broken glass. A harpy lullaby.

Silena sat bolt upright. Grover's hand found his pipes, not to play, but to hold.

They rang their bell twice — attention — and stirred the fire high. The singing wavered, moved east, faded. They stood back-to-back, watching, until Chiron himself trotted up ten minutes later, bow drawn, eyes sharp.

"Report," he said.

"Heard singing, south," Grover said, proud his voice didn't shake. "Didn't approach. Stayed by fire. Rang twice."

Chiron listened, nodded. "Well done. Harpies testing. They will not cross tonight." He smiled, and his eyes were kind. "You both did exactly right. Get some cocoa. I'll stay a while."

He stayed until their relief came, humming low under his breath, an old centaur tune that made Grover's hooves tingle.

On the walk back, Silena held Grover's hand the whole way, swinging it.

"We were brave," she declared.

"We were comfortable *and* brave," Grover corrected.

"Best combo."

The patrol list had one missing signature.

Silena noticed it while taking the empty thermoses to the Big House after their fifth watch. The form had been left on the porch beneath the lantern, where counselors signed when they returned from the border. Every patrol's name was there except the last pair from the eastern path.

She put down the thermoses and checked the times. The names belonged to a Demeter camper and an older boy from Hermes, both experienced enough not to forget something as routine as reporting in. The log showed them leaving at eleven, with a scheduled return at two. It was now twenty minutes past two.

Grover stood beside her, shivering in a borrowed jacket.

"Maybe they signed a different page."

"Then we check. We don't assume it."

Silena knocked on the study door. Chiron was working inside with the latest reports about the restless border, and he came at once when she explained. He did not seem alarmed, but he asked a second counselor to check the infirmary while he took the lantern and examined the posted routes.

"Do you know where they were assigned?" he asked.

"East fence, between the ash tree and the old stone wall."

"Good. Stay on the porch. I will have their leader check the path."

Grover made a small unhappy sound.

"I can listen from here," he offered. "If they're in the woods and frightened, I might hear something."

Chiron considered. "You can stay with Silena. Do not leave the porch without telling me."

The centaur moved into the trees. Silena wanted to go with him. She had packed an entire emergency bag for the week of night watches and was standing within reach of it. She also knew the exact reason Chiron had told them to remain. The patrol route already had people responsible for searching it, and one more child in the dark would complicate the headcount rather than help.

Grover closed his eyes. He could hear the insects in the strawberry fields and a dryad moving through the oak nearby. Beyond those sounds was something unfamiliar: a regular scraping followed by an impatient voice. It was too faint to make out the words, but it seemed to be coming from the path below the herb garden.

"I hear somebody," he said.

"Where?"

"East of the lower shed. The voice sounds annoyed, not frightened."

Silena took the observation down rather than treating it as proof their missing campers were safe. She noted the direction, the time and the sound, then asked the counselor on duty to send it to Chiron.

Ten minutes later the Demeter girl arrived with her arm around the Hermes boy. They were wet, muddy and arguing.

"He stepped into the ditch," she said, before anyone asked.

"The ground wasn't where the map said it was."

"The map showed the path, not the shape of every puddle."

Chiron returned behind them. The boy had twisted his ankle, and they had stopped to secure it before coming back. Neither had been in danger from a monster. They had simply taken longer than the form anticipated and failed to notify anyone that the route had changed.

Silena was relieved. She was also angry in the peculiar way she became angry when a preventable problem created unnecessary fear for half a dozen people.

"Did you try the signal whistle?" she asked.

"The reed fell out," the Hermes boy admitted.

"The backup?"

"It was in the pack. I couldn't reach it from where I was sitting."

Chiron asked the pair to visit the infirmary and said they would discuss the route equipment in the morning. He thanked Grover for listening and Silena for keeping the account precise.

On the walk back to Cabin Three, Silena carried the thermoses without making a joke. Grover walked beside her until they reached the pavilion.

"I think I was useful," he said.

"You were."

"But I couldn't tell whether they were hurt. I just heard a voice."

"That's what useful information sounds like sometimes. You didn't invent the rest."

He considered the answer. He'd spent years wanting to become a Searcher, imagining himself identifying a divine presence from the faintest movement in the trees. Tonight he'd helped because he knew the limits of what he heard and was willing to say so.

Silena stopped at Cabin Three.

"I should have packed two whistles," she said.

"You already packed three."

"One broke. The other was where nobody could reach it. What matters is whether the person who needs it can use it."

Grover nodded. "Then tomorrow we can ask Chiron to make people wear the backup outside their packs."

"Exactly."

The cabin lights were out when Silena went inside. She placed the clean equipment on the stores shelf, left the muddy thermoses by the washing basin and wrote a short note about the signal failure for the morning meeting. Then she went to sleep.

The next morning's equipment review was not glamorous. Chiron lined up the whistles, lanterns and route cards on a table and asked the patrol leaders to explain what they'd actually carried. Some remembered perfectly. Others described what they were supposed to have packed and had to be reminded that the question was about the night before.

Clarisse took the revised checklist and tested it against a practice scenario. She asked Nico to sit on a bench with one ankle tucked underneath him and demonstrate whether he could reach the whistle from each of the prescribed locations.

He took the instruction very seriously.

"Here," he said, pointing to the cord around his neck. "I can reach it with either hand."

"And if the cord catches on something?"

He moved it to the front of his jacket. "There."

"Good. If you lose the first whistle?"

He lifted the spare from the outer pocket. Clarisse nodded.

"That's what we need. Not a form claiming somebody carried two whistles while both are at the bottom of the same bag."

The Hermes boy who had twisted his ankle was sitting nearby with his foot supported. He looked embarrassed by the whole exercise, especially when the instructor asked him to describe where he'd been standing when he lost his balance.

"It was a bad step," he said.

"It was," Chiron replied. "The trail also needs a new marker. We inspect the cause without making the injured person a spectacle."

The boy looked relieved. Percy was glad to hear the distinction. He had spent the week listening for danger at the border and had begun to expect every incident to become evidence of a larger threat. Sometimes a bad step was a bad step, and the right answer was a better marker.

Annabeth examined the route cards and noticed that the eastern path had been mapped from the top of the ridge rather than from the lower junction. The difference meant the ditch could be mistaken for a shortcut.

"Can we redraw it from the junction?" she asked. "A person with a sore ankle isn't going to take the high route just to match our diagram."

Chiron agreed. She worked with Bea to mark the lower path, while Percy and Clarisse checked the physical markers against the new drawing. Grover followed them, listening for the water running through the ditch so the map would distinguish the stream from the path in darkness.

When the corrections were finished, everyone practiced reporting a delayed patrol without leaving their assigned position. It was slow enough to feel unnecessary. Percy remembered how easily he'd broken his own assignment during the eclipse and completed the drill without complaint.

The younger campers made fun of how much paperwork it required. Clarisse told them paperwork was less painful than a sprained ankle. She had enough experience with both to sound convincing.

By lunch, the team had repaired two markers, moved a whistle to a safer location on every patrol uniform and established a clear escalation time when someone was late. None of the changes made the border less dangerous by itself. They made the people watching it easier to find and harder to forget.

Night six was supposed to be their night off — the schedule rotated so no one pulled seven straight — but neither Percy nor Annabeth could sleep.

Percy found Annabeth on the Big House porch at 11 p.m., blanket around her shoulders, notebook in her lap, staring at the dark.

"Can't sleep?" he asked.

"Too quiet in the cabin," she said. "Everyone's snoring. Thalia snores like a thunderstorm. It's genetic, probably."

Percy sat beside her. They shared the blanket. For a while they just watched the signal fires flicker at the edges of the valley — west, east, south — tiny orange dots in the black.

"How's patrol with Thalia?" Annabeth asked.

"Good. She's... she asks good questions. About Atlantis. About my mom. I told her about Princess Sockhat."

Annabeth smiled. "She told me. She wants a sock."

"She's getting a sock." Percy nudged her. "How's patrol with Clarisse?"

Annabeth hesitated. "Good. Weirdly good. She's... not what I thought. She's funny. And she... she gets it. About dads. About worrying." She picked at the blanket fringe. "She asked what it's like having a sister who worries. I didn't know what to say at first. Then I realized — it's like having a lighthouse. You might not always look at it, but you always know where it is."

Percy nodded. "Thalia's a good lighthouse. Loud. Flashy. Probably illegal in some states."

Annabeth laughed, then yawned so wide her jaw cracked. Percy yawned in sympathy.

"Trading watch snacks has become an economy," Annabeth mumbled. "Clarisse wants Silena's pretzels. Silena wants your granola bars. Thalia wants everyone's juice boxes. Grover will trade anything for marshmallows."

"We're building alliances through sugar," Percy said solemnly. "This is how empires fall."

They dozed on the porch swing, shoulder to shoulder, blanket slipping, until Argus found them at midnight and shooed them to bed with many eyes rolling.

"Night, Seaweed Brain," Annabeth whispered, already half-asleep in her bunk.

"Night, Wise Girl," Percy whispered back from across the cabin divide — he wasn't in her cabin, of course, but he whispered it anyway into the dark, and hoped the night carried it.

The evening before the final watch, Annabeth found the eastern-route correction waiting for her approval. She had made the drawing herself and expected it to match the latest measurements. Instead a note in Bea's handwriting said that the path had shifted again when a repair crew moved the drainage stones.

Annabeth went to the workshop to ask whether the change was temporary. Beckendorf was sorting fittings into three trays and looked up when she came in.

"It'll stay that way until the spring repairs," he said. "We found an old drain underneath the walkway. If we cover it again before it's inspected, it'll flood the same place next time."

"Then the map needs a new date."

"It does."

Annabeth was irritated that nobody had told her sooner. She had spent half the morning helping Chiron revise a route that was already out of date by supper. When she said so, Beckendorf gave her a tired look.

"I should have put the notice on the board," he agreed. "But I was carrying the old stones out of the ditch, and Chiron told me to leave the path closed until someone finished checking it. We didn't tell anybody it was safe."

Annabeth considered that. She had treated the missing update as proof people weren't taking her work seriously. The repair crew had made sure the route was closed before it changed the stones. They had not endangered anyone by failing to tell her a measurement the instant it changed.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I assumed you'd reopened it."

"That's why we put the barriers up."

She looked toward the table where the updated site measurements lay.

"Could I copy them now?"

"Yes. And put the time next to the date, in case we move something else tomorrow."

She did. The revised page had enough room for a small change log beneath the route, something she'd begun to appreciate after the Oracle investigation. A map could be correct when it was drawn and misleading a day later if nobody recorded what had changed.

She took it to Chiron. He thanked her for checking and asked her to bring it to the final watch briefing so the patrol team knew which parts of the border had been inspected recently and which had not.

"Will we go there tonight?" she asked.

"No. The final watch is in the valley, well away from the closed path."

"Then why do we need the map?"

"Because you are learning to report what happened, not only where you intend to stand. The next patrol will need to know which routes changed while you were watching."

Annabeth nodded. She had wanted the work she'd done to matter beyond a single practice night. It was satisfying to see that it could, even when its result was only a correction on someone else's clipboard.

At dinner, Percy sat across from her and noticed the ink on her fingers.

"Another map?"

"The same map, properly updated."

"What's wrong with the old one?"

"The world failed to remain as I'd drawn it."

He smiled. "Very inconsiderate of it."

She passed him the page. He checked the new marks and pointed to the closed junction.

"Then we won't go anywhere near it."

"Good."

He handed it back. There was a moment when she wanted to tell him how annoyed she'd been with the repair crew, then how embarrassed she'd felt when she realized the barrier had stayed closed. She didn't need the story to make herself look clever. She told him both parts.

Percy listened, then said, "I did the same thing during the storm when I thought I knew which way the water should go. Turns out it had opinions."

"Water generally does."

"So do engineers."

She looked at the drawing again and wrote the last correction neatly, with Beckendorf's measurement credited beside it. That was the version she would carry to the final watch.

On Night Seven, Chiron changed the plan.

"One final watch," he said at dinner. "All six together. One fire. The center of the valley, where you can see all three borders. A quiet night to close the week."

So at 11 p.m., six tired, happy campers trudged to the central signal hill with blankets, bags, and enough snacks to feed a small army.

Percy brought granola bars. Thalia brought juice boxes (all flavors, for final trades). Annabeth brought apple rings and her notebook for constellation checks. Clarisse brought M&Ms and jerky. Silena brought hot chocolate and donuts and ribbons (enough for everyone). Grover brought trail mix and his pipes.

They built the fire together — Clarisse directing, Beckendorf would have been proud — and settled in a circle, cloaks overlapping, knees touching.

For the first hour, they were diligent. They scanned the treelines. They rang the bell on schedule. They logged nothing unusual in Annabeth's neat hand: *23:14 — owl. 23:37 — wind. 00:02 — Grover's stomach growling (suspected).*

Then, slowly, diligence melted into something softer.

Thalia told a story about the time she'd tried to outrun a storm on her bike in LA and ended up sheltering under a freeway overpass with three pigeons and a very confused poodle.

Clarisse told the Raccoon Vanquisher story again, with sound effects, and made Silena laugh so hard hot chocolate came out her nose.

Silena told the peanut-butter-flagpole mystery (still unsolved) and braided Clarisse's hair without asking — Clarisse pretended to protest, then fell asleep halfway through and snored into her M&Ms.

Grover played his pipes, soft and low — not a searcher's call, just a lullaby for friends. The fireflies drifted closer, as if listening.

Percy told the full Princess Sockhat saga, with voices, and Annabeth corrected his marine biology ("Anemones aren't plants, Seaweed Brain") and then ruined her own credibility by admitting she was afraid of ladybugs as a kid ("They have too many legs and they *stare*").

Thalia and Annabeth sat shoulder to shoulder, sisters without needing to say it, trading juice boxes without even negotiating — Thalia just handed Annabeth grape, Annabeth handed Thalia cherry, done.

"How did you know?" Thalia asked.

"I pay attention," Annabeth said simply.

Clarisse, half-asleep with braids, mumbled, "Stationing nearby..."

Annabeth reached over and squeezed her hand. "Always."

Silena tied a pink ribbon around Percy's wrist. "For luck. And because you need color."

Percy tied a blue one around Grover's horn. "For style."

Grover bleated happily. "Fashion."

At 3 a.m., the clouds parted. The sky opened up, huge and clear, and Annabeth pointed out constellations in a sleepy whisper — *there's Zeus with his bolt, there's Poseidon with his trident, there's the Huntress* — and Thalia added irreverent commentary (*he's holding it wrong, she'd beat him in a fight*) and Percy asked dumb questions on purpose to make Annabeth laugh, and Clarisse snored, and Silena hummed, and Grover played one last soft note that hung in the air like a star.

Nothing crossed the borders. The night stayed boring, in the best way. The fires burned steady. The valley held.

At 5 a.m., pale gold bled into the east. Birds started, hesitant, then all at once. The monsters, whatever had made them restless, settled back into the woods. The border hummed on, calm and constant.

Six heads nodded, jerked up, nodded again.

"Should we... go to breakfast?" Silena whispered.

"Can't move," Thalia mumbled. "Braided to the ground."

"Carry me," Clarisse told Annabeth.

"You're twice my size," Annabeth said, but she stood and offered her hands, and Clarisse took them, and Percy took Thalia's pack, and Silena took Grover's pipes, and Grover took the blanket, and somehow they all stood, leaning on each other like a six-legged table.

They stumbled down to the dining pavilion as the sun crested the hills, tired to the bone, cloaks wrinkled, hair full of ribbons and leaves, smelling like smoke and citronella and chocolate.

The early risers stared. Chris Rodriguez dropped his fork. Luke grinned and scooted over to make room.

Chiron trotted up, took one look at them — Percy with a pink ribbon, Clarisse with braids, Grover with green ribbons on his horns, Thalia with juice-box straws stuck in her hair, Annabeth with a blanket cape, Silena holding everyone's hands — and his eyes crinkled.

"Report?" he asked.

"Borders quiet," Thalia said, stifling a yawn. "All quiet."

"Well done," Chiron said warmly. "Breakfast is on the Big House. Eat. Rest. I'm proud of you."

They collapsed onto benches, six in a row, and ate like they'd never seen food — eggs and toast and bacon and fruit and blue pancakes (Sally's recipe from Percy's remembered childhood, written down for the kitchen by Annabeth weeks ago). They traded bites without asking. They stole sips of each other's juice. They bumped shoulders and heads and elbows.

Silena rested her head on Clarisse's shoulder. Clarisse let her. Grover rested his head on the table and snored softly. Percy rested his chin on his hand and watched his friends, feeling less solo than ever.

Annabeth leaned against Thalia, lighthouse and ship, sisters and soldiers, and whispered, "We did it."

Thalia wrapped an arm around her. "Boringest week ever. Best week ever."

When Chiron dismissed the watch, Clarisse claimed the last pancake and announced that anybody still awake in an hour would be assigned more patrol duty.

Grover was already asleep with his forehead on the table. Silena covered his face with a napkin to keep the flies away. Thalia slid her untouched fruit across to Annabeth and asked when the next watch list would go up.

Percy stood carefully. His legs had gone stiff during the night. He had completed a week of looking for something dangerous and found nothing he could prove. Tomorrow, after sleeping, he could examine the notes with Annabeth. Today he went back to Cabin Three with his sisters.

The patrol record lay beside the porch map. Percy stopped to confirm that the equipment they'd borrowed had been returned and the repaired path was marked closed until Chiron received the next inspection. Silena showed him the signed form, then pointed to one line he had missed: who would take the updated map to the morning crew.

"That's us," she said. "We volunteered yesterday."

He had forgotten. He was tired, and the prospect of another errand before breakfast sounded terrible. But somebody had to make sure the next team wasn't relying on an old route.

"I'll do it," he said.

"Not alone. I signed both our names. We can carry it after we sleep."

Clarisse looked between them. "I'll bring the spare whistles. The ones we moved out of the packs."

Percy leaned over the porch copy while Silena held down its curled corner. The corrected route stopped at the west-path closure; no line invited the next crew to test it before inspection. He checked that the sound report named what they knew—a whine beyond the boundary—and left the source blank. Clarisse counted the spare whistles into a separate bundle, while Silena folded the updated map around the watch sheet. The night had produced no confirmed crossing, but the record could still tell the morning crew where to go, what to carry and when to retreat.

At Cabin Three, Percy set the alarm for after breakfast, when he and Silena would carry the map to the morning crew. He put his boots beside the door so he could dress without waking his sisters and listened once more to the woods. No sound came close enough to change the report. The west path would stay closed until Chiron finished the inspection. Percy let the lantern go dark and fell asleep before Silena closed the lantern cabinet.
