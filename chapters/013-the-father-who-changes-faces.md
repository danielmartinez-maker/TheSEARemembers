## Chapter 13: The Father Who Changes Faces

Thalia knew her father was coming before anyone told her, because the sky went still in a way that wasn't natural.

It was a Tuesday morning in late winter, still months from the summer-solstice deadline Percy had warned them about, and Camp Half-Blood was doing its best impression of normal. The strawberry fields smelled like sun-warmed sugar. The lake glittered. Somewhere down by the cabins, the Apollo kids were tuning guitars badly enough that even the dryads were wincing.

And overhead, the clouds had stopped moving.

Not gathered. Not stormed. Just — stopped. Like someone had pressed a thumb to the blue and held it there.

Thalia stood on the porch of Cabin One with her teeth clenched and her spear leaning against the railing, and thought, *Oh. It's you.*

"You're glaring at the weather again," Annabeth said from behind her, ducking under the low doorway with a book tucked under her arm and a half-finished braid slipping over her shoulder. She was twelve and barefoot and already had a smudge of charcoal on her cheek from whatever she'd been sketching that morning. "People are going to think you're challenging Ouranos to single combat."

"Maybe I am," Thalia said.

Annabeth followed her gaze upward. Her expression shifted — quick, practiced, the way she always did when she filed something away. Engineer brain, Thalia called it in her head, even though Annabeth got furious when anyone said it out loud. Percy said Annabeth had been the same in the life he remembered: quick to notice, quicker to plan. Look, assess, catalog. "Sky's wrong," Annabeth said quietly.

"Yeah."

"Is it—?"

"Yeah."

Annabeth was quiet for a second. Then she sat down hard on the top step, book and all. "Did Chiron tell you?"

"Just got the Iris-message," Thalia said. "Dionysus is in a mood. Chiron wants us in the Big House at noon. Lunch."

"Lunch," Annabeth repeated, flat.

"Lunch," Thalia confirmed.

They didn't say anything else for a while. Below them, camp kept moving. Travis and Connor Stoll were trying to convince a group of wide-eyed new Hermes kids that the lava wall could be surfed on a cafeteria tray. Lee Fletcher was jogging the perimeter with a quiver bouncing against his back, waving up at them. Near the forge, Beckendorf's silhouette threw sparks. Normal. Loud. Alive.

Thalia loved it so much it made her chest ache, and hated how quickly her father's shadow could fall across it.

"You don't have to come if you don't want to," Annabeth said, picking at a loose thread on her shorts. "I can say you're sick."

Thalia snorted. "He'd know."

"I could make it convincing. I've been studying your dramatic cough."

"I don't have a dramatic cough."

"You do. It's like —" Annabeth puffed out her chest and hacked, exaggerated, into her fist. "Ugh. Woe. I am Thalia, felled by pollen."

Despite herself, Thalia laughed. It cracked something open in her ribs. She reached over and tugged Annabeth's braid loose, just to annoy her. "Brat."

"Your brat," Annabeth said, automatic, and then flushed, like she hadn't meant to say it out loud.

Thalia's throat went tight. Because that was the thing nobody else at camp understood. In this timeline, Thalia had raised Annabeth from the time Annabeth was four and Thalia was eight, two runaways hitching rides and sleeping under overpasses while their father's thunder rumbled far away and never quite came closer. Annabeth remembered being found on the road at seven by a Thalia who was her friend, not her sister. Thalia knew that only because Annabeth had told her. The failed ward had not given either of them the missing years back.

There was one girl beside her now. Her sister. Thalia wanted to protect her without making every unfamiliar moment another test.

Their father had been absent through most of the years Thalia remembered. His visits were brief, unpredictable, and never enough to make the years between them disappear. She didn't need another reason to be angry.

"He's wearing a new face," Thalia said. "He always does. Don't let it throw you."

Annabeth nodded, hugging her knees. "What did he look like last time?"

"Late thirties. Black hair. That actor from that cop show Mom liked? With the jaw." Thalia grimaced. "He kept smiling like he'd practiced it."

"And the time before?"

"I was ten. I don't really remember. Old. Beard. He gave me a pinecone."

"A pinecone."

"He said it was symbolic." Thalia rolled her eyes. "It rotted in my backpack."

Annabeth laughed, but it was thin. She leaned her head against Thalia's shoulder, and Thalia could feel how small she still was at twelve, all elbows and sharp angles. For a second Thalia tried to imagine the seventeen-year-old Annabeth had described, scarred and still planning a future. Then her sister shifted against her, unmistakably twelve, and Thalia put an arm around her.

"It'll be fine," Annabeth said, in the voice she used when she was trying to convince herself. "It's just lunch. An hour. We can survive an hour."

Thalia stared at the frozen sky. "Yeah," she said. "Just lunch."

---

Annabeth had a system for fathers.

It wasn't something she talked about, but it existed, neat and numbered in the back of her notebook between a diagram for a collapsible ladder and a list of books she wanted to steal from the Big House library:

*Rule 1: Notice everything. Hair, hands, voice. He never looks the same, so don't look for the face. Look for the tells.*
*Rule 2: Be useful. Have something to show him. Good grades, good plans, good behavior. Fathers stay longer when they're impressed.*
*Rule 3: Don't cry. Don't yell. Don't ask why he didn't come for Christmas, or your birthday, or the night the empousai almost took Thalia's eye. Agreeable girls get praised. Difficult girls get thunder.*

Thalia had found the list once, by accident, and gone very quiet.

"You don't have to perform for him, Beth," she'd said.

Annabeth had shrugged and said, "It's just practical," in the exact tone Athena would have used, and then hated herself for it, because she wasn't Athena's daughter here. Not anymore. Not in this sky.

Here, she was Zeus's daughter. Here, her ichor ran with stormlight. She could call lightning — small, crackling arcs that stung her palms — and when she got angry the windows rattled. The cabin records described years of learning how to be good at being his; consciously, she was only beginning.

And underneath, folded like a letter she couldn't throw away, she remembered being someone else. A girl with gray eyes who loved weaving and logic puzzles and a boy with sea-green eyes. A girl whose mother spoke to her through statues and never once touched her hair. Sometimes Annabeth woke up reaching for an owl-pendant that didn't exist in this life, her fingers closing on air.

Both mothers felt like dreams. Both fathers felt like tests she kept failing.

By eleven-thirty, Cabin One looked like a hurricane had hit a library. Annabeth had changed shirts three times. Thalia sat cross-legged on her bunk, watching with increasing amusement.

"You look fine," Thalia said for the fourth time.

"I look like I'm trying too hard," Annabeth said, tugging at the hem of a clean orange camp shirt. "Does this look like I'm trying too hard?"

"You look like you're twelve and going to lunch."

"That's not helpful."

"It's honest. Sit down before you wear a hole in the floor."

Annabeth sat. Her hands were shaking. She hid them by lacing her fingers together.

Thalia swung her legs off the bunk and knelt in front of her, so they were eye to eye. At sixteen, Thalia was all long limbs and spiky black hair and electric blue eyes that sparked when she was mad. There was a thin scar through her left eyebrow from a manticore spike two summers ago, and a new tiredness around her mouth since the eclipse ward had nearly taken her sister.

"Hey," Thalia said softly. "Look at me. You don't have to earn lunch."

"I know," Annabeth lied.

"No, listen." Thalia took her hands, steadying them. "Whatever face he walks in with, whatever he says — you're my sister first. Not his proof of concept. Okay?"

Annabeth's throat burned. "Okay."

"And if he starts in on me, you don't have to jump in. I can take it."

Annabeth looked away. That was Rule 4, the one she hadn't written down: *If Thalia gets in trouble, deflect. Be charming. Be agreeable. Make him laugh so he forgets to be angry at her.*

Thalia had spent eight years deflecting monsters and hunger and cold so Annabeth wouldn't have to. The least Annabeth could do was deflect their father.

A knock rattled the cabin door. Percy Jackson's voice, slightly muffled: "Uh. Hi. Chiron sent me to say lunch is in ten? And also that Dionysus says if you're late he's eating your dessert, which I think was a joke but with him you never know?"

Thalia and Annabeth looked at each other and, despite everything, smiled.

"Coming!" Annabeth called.

She opened the door to find Percy hovering on the step, hands in his pockets, sun glinting off his too-long black hair. Twelve years old, son of Poseidon, raised in a palace under the sea and still somehow the most land-sick person Annabeth knew. He wore his camp shirt untucked and his sneakers untied, and his eyes — sea-green, older than twelve, carrying seventeen years of memory he shouldn't have — softened when he saw her face.

"Hey," he said quietly. "You okay?"

It was such a Percy question. Not *are you excited* or *are you ready*, just *are you okay*, like her answer actually mattered more than the event.

"No," Annabeth said honestly, because Percy was the one person she didn't have to perform for. He remembered her as Athena's daughter. He remembered who she'd been before this sky rewrote her. Around him, she didn't have to choose which memory was real.

"Yeah," Percy said. "Thalia told me this morning. At archery. She looked like she wanted to shoot the sun."

"I considered it," Thalia said, joining them on the porch and locking the cabin door behind her. She ruffled Percy's hair as she passed. "Come on, Fish Boy. You're our buffer."

"I'm honored," Percy said solemnly. "I was told there would be food."

"There will be food," Annabeth said. "There will be awkward silences *between* food."

"My favorite kind." Percy fell into step beside her as they headed up the hill toward the Big House, and after a moment, his hand bumped against hers — not holding, just there. Present. "For what it's worth, my dad's visits are also weird. Last time he showed up in a lobster bib."

Annabeth snorted. "You're lying."

"I am absolutely not lying. Amphitrite made him take it off. It was a whole thing." He grinned, and for a second the morning felt lighter. "Godly parenting. No notes."

Thalia laughed despite herself, and Annabeth felt something in her shoulders unlock.

Maybe, she thought, as the Big House came into view with its blue paint and wraparound porch and the unnatural stillness pressing down over it, maybe it really would be just lunch.

Then thunder rolled, low and deliberate, from a clear sky — and the front door swung open.

---

Percy had met Zeus more than once in the life he remembered.

Amphitrite had once told him a story about a meeting when the boy she'd raised was five, hiding behind a coral pillar while the gods argued over a treaty. He couldn't remember it himself. Zeus had glanced toward him and asked Poseidon, "You collect strays now, brother?" She had taken Percy out before the argument got worse. Percy knew her anger when she told it; he did not remember the room.

He did remember standing on Olympus after the Titan War, telling Zeus to his face that the gods had to do better. He remembered lightning crawling up his arms. He remembered not flinching.

Now he was twelve again, sitting at a polished oak table in the Big House dining room with his hands folded in his lap, watching a stranger walk in wearing his best friend's father's lightning.

The new face was — Percy tried to be fair — handsome. Early fifties, maybe. Warm brown skin, close-cropped graying hair, gold-rimmed glasses perched on a strong nose, a charcoal sweater over a collared shirt despite the sun through the windows, like a college professor who'd wandered in from a lecture on philosophy. He smiled, and crow's feet crinkled at the corners of his eyes.

"Girls!" he boomed, arms wide. "My girls!"

Thalia went rigid beside Percy. Annabeth stood so fast her chair scraped.

"Dad," Annabeth said, bright and too high. "Hi. You — you look different."

"New look," Zeus said easily, tapping his glasses. "Your father is trying to seem approachable. The council says I intimidate the younger demigods." He winked at Percy. "You must be the Poseidon boy. Percy, is it?"

"Yes, sir," Percy said. His voice came out smaller than he wanted. He used Rule 1 from the court advice Amphitrite had given him before this visit: *Don't draw attention.* "Thank you for having me, sir."

"Polite!" Zeus said approvingly. "Poseidon says you have manners. Good. Sit, sit."

They sat. The table was too big for six people — Chiron in his wheelchair at one end, Dionysus slouched at the other with a diet soda and an expression of profound boredom, Thalia and Annabeth side by side across from Percy, Zeus at the center like a sun they were all supposed to orbit.

Lunch was, in Percy's private ranking, weirder than the lobster bib incident.

It started normal enough. Mrs. O'Leary — no, that was later, wrong memory — the harpies from the kitchen brought out sandwiches, lemonade, a platter of strawberries still warm from the fields. Chiron asked about Olympus business. Dionysus made a crack about Zeus's sweater that Zeus laughed off a little too sharply. The windows rattled, just once.

Then Zeus turned to Annabeth, and his whole face softened in a way that made Percy's stomach twist.

"So," Zeus said. "Chiron tells me you've redesigned the lava wall safety rails. And that your history essay on the Titanomachy was read aloud to the whole cabin."

Annabeth flushed pink with pleasure before she could stop herself. "It was just — I noticed the old rails had a shear point, and —"

"Brilliant," Zeus said. "Just brilliant. You get that from me, you know. Strategy. Vision." He reached over and tucked a loose strand of hair behind her ear, and Annabeth leaned into it without thinking, starved and grateful. "You're such a good girl. So agreeable. So easy to be proud of."

Percy glanced at Thalia. Thalia was staring at her plate, jaw working, fingers white around her fork.

"Thalia," Zeus said, turning, smile cooling by exactly two degrees. Percy noticed. Annabeth noticed. Thalia noticed most of all. "Chiron tells me you led a patrol into the woods last week without authorization. And that you argued with your archery instructor."

Thalia lifted her chin. "The patrol found a hellhound den two hundred yards from the border. If we hadn't—"

"If you hadn't, your instructor would have handled it," Zeus said mildly. "That's what instructors are for. You need to learn discipline, Thalia. Not every problem is solved by charging in with a spear."

"It was solved, though," Thalia said. "Nobody got hurt."

"Because you were lucky." Zeus picked up his sandwich. "You have a temper. You always have. Confrontational. Your mother can be the same way."

The temperature in the room dropped five degrees. Or maybe Percy imagined it. He felt the hair on his arms stand up — static, ozone. Storm.

Their mother. Beryl Grace. Alive, but absent from the sisters' daily lives after years of drinking and broken promises that *he's coming, girls, he said he's coming this time*. Percy knew the story because Annabeth had told him once, late at night by the lake, her voice flat like she was reading a report. Thalia never talked about it at all.

"Don't," Thalia said quietly.

Zeus blinked. "Don't what?"

"Don't talk about my mom like you knew her."

"Thalia," Chiron warned gently.

"No." Thalia set down her fork. Her eyes were sparking, literal blue arcs at the edges. "You don't get to do that. You show up twice a year in a different face and tell Annabeth she's agreeable like that's the best thing a daughter can be, and then you tell me I'm difficult like it's a disease, and then you bring up Mom like — like you were there."

"Thalia," Annabeth whispered, urgent. "Thals—"

Percy could see Annabeth's hand creeping toward her sister's sleeve. Deflect. De-escalate. Rule 4. He could see the exact moment she decided to smile and smooth it over, to be charming, to offer up some new achievement to draw the lightning away from Thalia.

He wanted to intervene so badly his hands ached. In the old life, he'd have stood up, made a joke, taken the hit. That was his job — the sea boy who stepped between his friends and angry gods. But these were not his gods, not exactly, and this was not his fight, not exactly. Thalia had told him that morning at archery, blunt as a spear butt: *If he starts, let us handle it. Please. I need her to know she can stand up to him without someone rescuing her.*

So Percy stayed seated. He caught Annabeth's eye across the table and gave the smallest shake of his head. *Not yet. Let her finish. I'm here.*

Annabeth stared at him. Her throat worked.

Zeus, meanwhile, had set down his sandwich. The professorial warmth was gone. The air tasted like copper. "Careful, daughter," he said softly. Thunder underlined every syllable. "You forget to whom you speak."

"No," Thalia said, and she was shaking now, but she didn't look away. "I know exactly who I'm speaking to. I'm speaking to the man who ignored my messages when Mom couldn't take care of us because there was a council vote. I'm speaking to the man who promised Annabeth he'd come for her birthday and sent a lightning bolt in a box instead. It set the porch on fire, by the way. We had to stomp it out. Happy tenth birthday, Beth."

"Thalia, stop," Annabeth breathed. But her voice cracked.

"Why? He's here, isn't he? This is the part where we're supposed to be grateful he's here at all." Thalia's laugh was wet. "You know what I remember? I remember being eight and telling Mom he was busy running the world and that's why he couldn't visit. I remember believing it. I remember making Annabeth believe it. And then the eclipse —" She stopped, swallowed. "We almost died, and you still didn't come to see us. Same father, same empty chair. So tell me, Dad. Which face were you wearing when you decided we weren't worth the trouble?"

Silence.

Dionysus examined his nails. Chiron's hands were tight on his wheelchair arms. The harpies had vanished.

Zeus stared at his elder daughter. For a second — just a second — Percy saw something raw cross that borrowed face. Guilt, maybe. Or anger. With Zeus, they looked the same.

"You are —" Zeus began, voice like distant thunder. "You are insolent. Ungrateful. After everything I have given you—"

"What have you given us?" Thalia demanded. "Name one thing."

He stood. Chairs scraped. Ozone burned Percy's nose.

And Annabeth stood too.

---

Annabeth was learning how quickly she could become the buffer.

She knew the exact pitch of laugh that made her father soften. She knew how to pivot — *Dad, did I tell you about my grades? Dad, Thalia didn't mean it, she's just tired, Dad, look what I built* — how to step into the strike zone with a smile and take the lightning as praise.

Her hand was already half-raised. Her mouth was already shaping *Dad, please, she doesn't mean it, she's grieving, you know about Mom, about Jason—*

Jason. Their younger brother was far away at Camp Jupiter. Thalia had not seen him since he was taken when he was small. Some days she could tell herself he was safe; on others, distance felt like a question nobody was answering.

*Be agreeable,* the rules whispered. *Be good. Don't make it worse.*

Percy was watching her. Not pushing, just — there. Steady as tide. His eyes said *I'm here* and also *you don't have to*.

And Thalia — Thalia was standing with her fists clenched and tears on her face she refused to wipe away, sixteen years old and holding the door open with her own body so Annabeth could walk through it if she dared.

Annabeth thought of Athena. Cold marble hands. A mother who had never once tucked her hair back, who spoke in riddles and called it love. She thought of Zeus in this life, who praised her for being easy and never asked what it cost to be easy.

She thought of being seventeen, standing on a ship with a boy who chose her again and again, and realizing that wisdom without honesty was just a prettier cage.

She lowered her hand.

"No," Annabeth said.

Every head turned.

Her heart hammered so hard she was sure the glasses on the table were shaking from it and not from her father's storm. "No, I'm not — I'm not doing that this time. Thalia, don't — don't apologize. Dad —" Her voice broke. She forced it steady. "Dad, sit down. Please."

Zeus stared at her. "Annabeth—"

"Please," she said again, quieter. "Just — listen for once. Without a new face. Without the speech."

Something in her tone got through. Slowly, he sank back into his chair. Thalia remained standing for a beat longer, then sat, hard, like her knees had given out.

Annabeth stayed on her feet. If she sat, she'd fold. She knew herself.

"I love it when you visit," she said. Her hands twisted in her shirt hem. She made herself stop. "I do. I make lists before it. I try to get everything right. I — I pick out shirts. And then you come, and you're someone different, and you tell me I'm good because I'm *agreeable*, and it feels —" She searched for the word. Engineers liked precise words. "It feels like you're praising a trick. Like I'm a dog who learned to sit still while you throw thunder."

"Annabeth," Zeus said, pained. "That's not—"

"It is, though." Tears blurred her vision. She blinked them back, furious. Rule 3: Don't cry. Well, Rule 3 could go to Tartarus. "You tell Thalia she's confrontational like it's the worst thing, but she's the reason I'm alive. When Mom couldn't care for us, Thalia was eight and I was four. She told me she learned to braid hair from a library book because there was no one else to do it. She learned to cook. She learned to fight. You call that confrontational. I call it — she stayed. You didn't."

The storm pressed closer. Annabeth's hair lifted with static. She could feel lightning in her own palms answering, wild and untrained. She was her father's daughter, after all.

"You don't know what it costs me to be agreeable," she said, voice dropping. "Every time you leave, I tell myself if I was just — better. Smarter. Easier. Less trouble than Thalia. Then you'd stay. Then you'd wear the same face twice because you'd want us to recognize you." She laughed, shaky. "That's stupid, right? But I'm twelve. I'm allowed to be stupid about my dad."

Thalia made a small sound. Annabeth didn't look at her. If she looked, she'd stop.

"I remember —" Annabeth hesitated. She couldn't say *I remember being Athena's daughter*. Not here, not with Chiron and Dionysus watching, not with the rules of this world pressing down. But she could say the true part underneath. "I remember thinking fathers were supposed to be constants. Like north on a compass. You're supposed to be the fixed point. But you're always moving. And I'm tired of running after you with a map."

Silence. The lemonade sweated in its pitcher. A fly buzzed against the window, trapped.

Zeus removed his glasses. Without them, his face looked younger. Naked. He polished the lenses on his sweater, a painfully human gesture, and Annabeth realized with a jolt that he didn't know what to do with his hands.

"Annabeth," he said finally. His voice had lost the boom. "You — you have always been... I thought praise would..." He trailed off. Thunder rumbled, far away, like an engine left running. "Your sister and I — we have a difficult history."

"Because you never listen," Thalia said, hoarse. But the fight had gone out of her. She sounded exhausted.

"I listen," Zeus said, and then, at both girls' identical incredulous looks, he sighed. "Poorly. I listen poorly." He put his glasses back on. "You are both... very like your mother. And very unlike her. It..." He gestured vaguely, king of the gods reduced to hand-waving. "It is hard to look at you and not see her. And Jason. And every choice I failed to make."

Percy shifted in his seat. Annabeth caught his eye. He mouthed, *You okay?* She nodded, barely. Her legs trembled.

Zeus reached into his sweater pocket and pulled out two small boxes. He set them on the table and slid one to each girl. Peace offering. Distraction. Annabeth recognized the move — gods loved gifts the way absent fathers loved souvenirs. Something to unwrap so no one has to keep talking.

"For you," he said gruffly. "I... brought something."

Thalia didn't touch hers. Annabeth, after a second, opened her box with numb fingers.

Inside, on white cotton, lay a silver pendant: a tiny owl clutching a lightning bolt. Hybrid. Impossible. Both her lives in one charm.

Annabeth's breath caught. "How did you—?"

"Chiron said you liked owls," Zeus said quietly. He wasn't looking at her. He was looking at the wall, at nothing. "I asked what you were building. Your interests don't have to look like mine." He cleared his throat. "I thought you might like it."

Annabeth closed her fingers around the pendant. It was warm. "Thank you," she whispered, because manners outlived anger.

Thalia opened her box. Inside was a drachma — celestial bronze, unfamiliar minting, with a pine tree on one side and a lightning strike on the other. "For the collection," Zeus said awkwardly. "I remember you... you liked symbols. Once."

Thalia stared at it for a long moment. "I was six," she said. But she closed the box carefully and set it beside her plate instead of pushing it away. "Thanks."

It wasn't forgiveness. It wasn't even close. But it was — Annabeth recognized it from engineering — a load-bearing joint. Small, ugly, holding more than it should.

"I will..." Zeus stood again, but slower this time. The storm had pulled back, leaving only the smell of rain. "I will visit again soon. Before the summer solstice. I promise."

The words landed hollow as a struck drum. *Soon. I promise.* How many times had they heard it in how many faces?

Thalia's mouth tightened. Annabeth felt the old reflex surge — *say it's okay, say we understand, you're busy, running the world* — and crushed it.

"Okay," Annabeth said instead. Neutral. Honest. "We'd like that. But if you can't, just — send a message. Don't promise."

Zeus flinched. Then, slowly, he nodded. "Fair," he said. "That's... fair."

He looked at Thalia. "May I—?" He held out a hand, uncertain, like he'd forgotten how fathers touched daughters.

Thalia stared at it. For a second Annabeth thought she'd refuse. Then Thalia stood and let him hug her — stiff, brief, her arms barely lifting. He pressed a kiss to the top of her head, and Annabeth saw his eyes close.

"You are strong," he murmured, too low for anyone but them to hear — but Annabeth heard. "Too strong for me, I think. I'm sorry for that."

Thalia didn't answer. She stepped back, eyes bright and jaw set.

He turned to Annabeth. She let him hug her longer, because she was still twelve and starved for it even while she hated being starved. He smelled like rain and wool. His sweater scratched her cheek.

"My brilliant girl," he whispered. "Be... be less agreeable, if you need to be. I will try to be worthy of the trouble."

Annabeth laughed through tears. "That's a low bar, Dad."

"The lowest," he agreed, and smiled — real, for once, crow's feet and all. Then the air pressure changed, the lights flickered, and he was gone in a soft roll of thunder that rattled the lemonade pitcher and left only the smell of ozone.

Nobody moved.

Dionysus stood, grabbed his diet soda, and said, "Well. That was less disastrous than usual. Ten points for no one getting smote," and walked out, because gods dealt with feelings by leaving the room.

Chiron wheeled closer. He rested a hand on Thalia's shoulder, then on Annabeth's. "You were both very brave," he said simply. "Take the afternoon. I'll tell your instructors you're excused." He glanced at Percy. "Mr. Jackson, perhaps you could... ensure they eat dessert?"

"Yes, sir," Percy said immediately. "I'm excellent at dessert supervision."

Chiron smiled, tired and kind, and left them alone with the wreckage of lunch and two small boxes and the too-big silence.

---

Thalia didn't cry in front of people. It was a rule older than Annabeth's list, carved in at eight years old on a highway shoulder with a frightened little sister and no money and thunder laughing far away.

So she didn't cry now. She picked up a strawberry and ate it, chewing deliberately, and said, "Well. That sucked."

Annabeth let out a sound between a laugh and a sob. "Yeah. It sucked."

Percy pushed the dessert plate — ambrosia squares, because the harpies had pity — toward the middle of the table. "Dessert supervision reporting for duty," he said. "Regulations state everyone must eat at least one while processing paternal nonsense."

"You made that up," Annabeth said, but she took one.

"I absolutely made that up." Percy took one too, and nodded at Thalia until she took one. They chewed in silence.

The afternoon light slanted through the Big House windows, gold and dusty. Outside, camp had resumed — someone shouted from the arena, the forge hammer rang, the lake glittered like nothing had happened. The sky moved again. Clouds drifted, normal and white.

Thalia turned the little box over in her hands. Pine tree. Lightning strike. A symbol from a daughter he'd never bothered to learn.

"He's going to forget," she said flatly. "The promise. He'll get busy. There'll be a vote or a monster or a new face he likes better. He won't come before the solstice."

"Probably not," Annabeth said quietly.

"Doesn't that make you angry?"

"Yes." Annabeth picked at her ambrosia. "But I'm also... I don't know. When he brought that owl, it startled me. What if he knows more than he says? What if they all know and they're just... letting us walk into it?"

Percy frowned. "My dad doesn't know. Or if he does, he's hiding it really well behind dad jokes and sea cucumber metaphors. He asked me last week if I'd been sleeping okay, like — like he could tell something was off but couldn't name it." He looked down. "I think the gods feel the timeline wrong the way fish feel a storm. Pressure change. They don't know what's coming, just that the water's wrong."

Thalia considered that. The eclipse had not answered any of the questions Percy and Annabeth carried. It had given Thalia a new fear: even here, with a cabin and Chiron and walls around them, she could not guarantee her sister's safety.

She thought of Beryl singing off-key in a kitchen that smelled like cigarettes. Of Jason's bag by the door on the morning his escort came. Of Luke joking as if nothing in his future could possibly go wrong.

"I keep thinking about Mom," Thalia admitted, low. "And Jason. And Luke. They're alive. I know that. But I miss how things were before I had to worry about all of them." Her voice cracked. She forced it level. "How do you tell someone you miss them when they're standing right there?"

Annabeth reached over and took her hand. No deflection. No performance. Just fingers, warm and sticky with strawberry juice.

"You tell your sister," Annabeth said simply. "And she believes you, even if she can't remember the years you're talking about."

Thalia squeezed back so hard it must have hurt. Annabeth didn't pull away.

Percy was quiet for a moment. Then he said, "When I miss my mom — my other mom, Sally — I tell Grover. Or I write it down. Amphitrite saw me looking at Sally's photograph once. I thought she'd be upset. She told me I could leave it in the coral garden, so the sea could carry it." He smiled, crooked. "It sounds dumb."

"It doesn't sound dumb," Thalia said gruffly.

"It sounds like Atlantis," Annabeth said. "Tell us about her? Not — not the sad part. Just... something normal."

Percy blinked. Then his face softened into something younger and older all at once. "She made blue food on purpose. Blue pancakes, blue cookies. My stepdad — Gabe, in that life — hated it. She did it anyway. Said if you can't make life blue, what's the point." He laughed. "She worked double shifts and still helped me with English. She smelled like vanilla and rain."

"She sounds great," Thalia said.

"She was." Percy's eyes were bright. He didn't wipe them. Atlantean princes, Thalia was learning, had different rules about crying. "I miss her so much it feels like drowning on land. And then I feel guilty, because Amphitrite — she's trying. She braids my hair when I'm sick. She yells at Dad when he works too late. It's not her fault she's not Sally. It's not Sally's fault she's not here."

"Two truths," Annabeth murmured. "Both real."

"Yeah." Percy looked at the empty chair where Zeus had sat. "For what it's worth... I thought what you both said was — it was brave. I wanted to jump in. Especially when he said that stuff about being confrontational. But you didn't need me. You had each other."

Thalia felt something loosen in her chest. "Thanks for not jumping in, Fish Boy," she said honestly. "Thanks for just... staying."

"Always," Percy said simply, like a vow.

They sat a while longer, finishing the strawberries, trading small stories to fill the hollow places. Annabeth showed them the owl pendant, holding it up to catch the light. Thalia spun the drachma across the table and Percy caught it and pretended to bite it to test authenticity, making Annabeth shriek with laughter.

"Don't!" she cried, tackling his arm. "It's Thalia's!"

"It's currency! It's meant to be bitten!" Percy protested, holding it out of reach.

"You're both idiots," Thalia declared, but she was smiling — really smiling — for the first time since the sky stopped that morning. She pocketed the drachma. She'd keep it. Not because it was enough. Because it was proof she'd asked for more.

Later, when the light went amber and Chiron's bell rang for dinner, they walked back down the hill together. Clarisse was waiting by the cabins, arms crossed, scowl firmly in place — Poseidon's daughter, Percy's half-sister, thirteen and furious at the world on principle.

"Took you long enough," she barked. "Silena saved you seats and Luke's telling stories and if you don't hurry there won't be any chicken left. Where were you?"

"Lunch ran long," Thalia said.

Clarisse eyed their red eyes and sticky hands and Percy's too-casual grin, and — miracle — didn't push. She just jerked her head toward the pavilion. "Well, come on. You look like you need food."

Silena — fourteen, golden, Poseidon's other daughter, all warmth where Clarisse was thorns — waved them over when they arrived, patting the bench beside her. Luke Castellan ruffled Percy's hair and slid down to make room. The Hermes kids scooted. Someone passed bread. Someone else stole Annabeth's grapes and she stole them back with a maneuver so smooth the whole table cheered.

Normal. Loud. Alive.

Thalia sat with her sister on one side and her ridiculous sea-boy buffer on the other, and ate chicken that tasted like smoke and salt, and listened to Annabeth argue with Luke about siege tactics with her new pendant glinting at her throat.

Her father was gone. He'd made another promise, and she didn't yet know if he would keep it. The sky above was empty and blue and indifferent.

But the bench beneath her was solid. Annabeth's shoulder pressed against hers, steady and warm. Percy's foot knocked against hers under the table, accidental-on-purpose, *I'm here* in Morse code.

Thalia looked at her sister — her brilliant, brave, finally-honest sister — and thought: *He doesn't get to keep us waiting anymore. We'll grow without him if we have to.*

Annabeth caught her eye and smiled, small and real, and mouthed, *Okay?*

Thalia bumped her shoulder. *Okay.*

And for that evening, at least, it was true.
