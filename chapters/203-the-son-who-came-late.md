## Chapter 203: The Son Who Came Late

Percy found Leandros beside the canoe shed, sorting wet lines into three piles.

One pile had been coiled neatly on the bench. Another lay in a loose heap on the dirt, darkened by last night’s rain. The third had been laid in a straight row beneath the eaves, each line stretched between two stones so the water could drip away from the rope.

Percy stopped at the edge of the overhang. “Which pile is the bad one?”

“All of them, if you’re going to leave them like this.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

Leandros lifted one of the lines and ran it between his fingers. “The ones on the ground need cleaning. These need to dry. Those are ready to put away.”

“Then I asked what I meant.”

“You asked which pile was bad.”

“I was trying to save time.”

“You’re not saving any.”

The shed smelled of damp wood and old lake water. A canoe rested on padded blocks inside, its hull turned up and away from the path. The roof kept most of the rain off, but the wind had blown the weather in through the open side. One paddle leaned against the wall. Another had been set down across the threshold, where somebody could trip over it if they came in too fast.

Percy nudged it with his toe. “This one yours too?”

“It’s in the way.”

“It’s still in the way.”

Leandros looked at him, then moved the paddle to the rack. He did not apologize for leaving it across the door. Percy was not sure he expected one.

Chiron had asked if Percy could spare part of the morning to help sort the boathouse equipment. He had said it was a small job, not a test and not an assignment for either son of Poseidon. Percy had answered that he was free. Leandros had been there already when he arrived, which probably explained the three piles and the wet dirt.

“Chiron said to check the line lengths,” Percy said. “And get the wet ones off the ground.”

“I heard him.”

“He also said not to put the ones with the worn sheath back.”

“I heard that too.”

“Then why are these in the ready pile?”

Leandros looked down. A thin pale strand showed through the brown rope near the end. He turned the line, found another patch, and ran his thumb over it. “It’s on the surface.”

“That means it’s worn.”

“It means it got scraped.”

“Is there a difference?”

“There can be.”

“Can this one hold a canoe?”

Leandros set the line back on the bench. “Not by itself.”

“Okay. So what’s it for?”

“Nothing, if you’ve decided it’s useless.”

“I haven’t decided. I asked.”

“You ask like you’ve already decided.”

Percy glanced at the rope. “I can be wrong about it.”

Leandros’s eyes flicked to the other lines. “Then put it in the clean pile and I’ll show you.”

That was not the same as answering, but Percy lifted the line. Its rough places caught at his fingertips. He turned it twice, trying to see what Leandros saw. The worn sheath wasn’t just on the surface. It opened at the bend, a small split where the inner fibers showed.

“Not ready,” Percy said.

Leandros held out his hand. Percy gave the rope back.

“Good,” Leandros said. “Now you know.”

“I knew before.”

“You just said you could be wrong.”

“I was willing to find out.”

Leandros made a small sound under his breath. “You always do that.”

“Do what?”

“Answer the other thing.”

Percy waited. Leandros pulled the line taut, the worn patch disappearing into the loop. He pressed his thumbnail to the split and the inner fibers sprang apart.

“Put it with the ones to cut down,” he said. “There’s enough good length left for tie-offs that don’t carry weight.”

“Chiron said take them out of use.”

“Then tell Chiron the whole thing is useless. He’ll throw away six feet of line he could still use for labels.”

“Would you ask him first?”

Leandros’s hand stopped on the rope. “Why?”

“Because we’re not in charge of the gear.”

“We’re the ones using it.”

“Not all of it.”

“I know what boats need.”

“Probably. I don’t know what this one needs.”

“Then let me handle it.”

The words came out too quickly. Percy heard the order in them, even if Leandros had dressed it up as a practical answer.

A smaller camper came along the path carrying an armful of folded canvas. She slowed under the edge of the eaves, looking between them and the canoe.

“Chiron said these go with the spare covers,” she said. “Where do you want them?”

“On the bench,” Leandros answered.

“Which bench?”

“The one that’s empty.”

She shifted the canvas higher against her chest. The bench beside the lines had no empty space. “That one’s full.”

Leandros looked over at the second bench, then back at the wet ropes. “Move those.”

The camper did not move. She waited for someone else to answer.

Percy leaned over and lifted the worn line away from the bench. “We can put the dry ones up here.”

“I was talking to him,” Leandros said.

“I know.”

“Then let me answer.”

“She asked where to put them.”

“And I told her.”

“You told her to move the thing you were sorting.”

“She can put the covers wherever she likes if she has a better idea.”

“That’s not what she asked.”

Leandros’s mouth flattened. The camper looked at the canvas and then at the bench. She had not asked Percy either. She shifted her weight as if she might take the load somewhere else.

“There’s space by the wall,” Percy said. “If you want it out of the rain.”

She looked there, then nodded. “Thanks.”

The canvas went onto a clean shelf. She left without looking back.

Leandros watched her go. “You like correcting people in front of everyone?”

“No.”

“You could have waited.”

“She was standing there with all that canvas.”

“You could have let her answer.”

“She asked where to put it.”

“She could have figured it out.”

“She could have. She didn’t.”

Leandros went back to the line. “That’s the problem with camp. Nobody gets to do anything without three people explaining how.”

Percy pushed the paddle farther onto its rack. “You could ask before you tell someone to move their stuff.”

“It wasn’t her stuff.”

“She was carrying it.”

“That doesn’t make it hers.”

“No,” Percy said. “But it makes it the thing she was trying to put down.”

Leandros gave him a look that seemed to have several answers in it. He chose none of them. Instead, he picked up the frayed rope and began to make a coil, his hands moving quickly. He turned the line over his palm, keeping the loops even, then laid the coil flat on the bench.

“See?” he said. “It’s still useful.”

“For labels.”

“For things that don’t carry weight.”

“That’s what I said.”

“You said to throw it away.”

“I said Chiron told us to take it out of use.”

“Same thing.”

“It isn’t.”

Leandros set the coil down more carefully than he had to. “You should try giving an answer instead of turning everything into a debate.”

Percy watched the line. “I thought I was answering.”

“You’re not.”

He was starting to recognize the sound of it: the certainty that a disagreement was a challenge to whoever had spoken last. Leandros could be right about the line and still want the room to prove it. Percy knew something about that. His own first response to being questioned was often to push back, even when it would be easier to listen. The difference was that he had not been asked to lead the morning.

Or maybe he had. Chiron had asked if he was free. That did not make him the other person in charge.

“Do you want me to stop helping?” Percy asked.

“No.”

“Then what do you want?”

Leandros glanced at the canoe, at the line, at the bench that still had a free corner. “I want to finish it.”

“Okay.”

“Without you changing every pile.”

“I’ve only changed the bad rope.”

“You moved the good one.”

“It had a split in it.”

“After I showed you.”

“After I looked again.”

Leandros tugged the line through his fingers. “Fine. Keep looking.”

Percy picked up the second line from the ground. It smelled like wet bark. He stretched a short section between his hands and watched how the fibers shifted. A familiar motion settled into his fingers: hold one end, make a loop, turn the working line under and back. His hands knew how to make the knot before he knew its name.

He tightened it once, then pulled it apart.

Leandros had stopped sorting. “Where’d you learn that?”

“I don’t know.”

“You don’t know?”

“I can tie it. I don’t know where I learned.”

“That’s convenient.”

“It’s a knot.”

“It’s always convenient when you don’t know.”

Percy set the line down. “You asked where I learned it.”

“I did.”

“And I answered.”

“You’ve been in Atlantis. They had boats.”

“They did.”

“You lived there.”

“Yeah.”

“Then somebody taught you.”

“Maybe.”

“That’s it?”

Percy picked up a different line. The wet rope dragged against the bench and left a dark stripe in the dust. “What do you want me to say?”

Leandros turned back to his piles. “Nothing.”

It was a clear answer, but not one he meant. Percy had spent the past week hearing versions of that from him: nothing, whatever, it’s fine. He did not know Leandros well enough to know which answers he was supposed to take literally.

The wet line snapped once when Leandros shook it out. A few droplets struck the packed dirt. “Keep those in the middle,” he said. “They’ve got too much water in them to coil.”

“I thought you wanted me not to change the piles.”

“I want you to do what I said.”

“That’s different.”

“Is it?”

“Yeah.”

Leandros looked up at him again. Percy kept his eyes on the rope. If he looked back, he would have to decide whether the line had become a fight or whether it had been one from the beginning.

The rain had left the path darker than the dirt under the eaves. Water slid off the roof in a steady drip. At the back of the shed, the canoe sat on its blocks with the paddles still out of its way. No one was going onto the lake. The work was only sorting rope and canvas, but Leandros handled the materials with the patience of someone who had done it many times.

He could teach this part. He could take a loose knot and make it hold without making the line bite itself. He could see where wear started and where the rope was still worth keeping. Percy watched him tie a clean loop, then another, and decided that being impressed did not mean he had to agree with him.

“Chiron’s coming back after breakfast,” Percy said.

“I know.”

“He’ll decide what goes back.”

“I know.”

“You don’t have to convince me.”

Leandros set down the coil. “I wasn’t trying to convince you.”

“Okay.”

“Don’t do that.”

“Do what?”

“Say okay like you’re done.”

Percy rubbed the rope between his fingers. It was damp enough to be cold. “I’m not done. I’m just not arguing with you about whether you were trying to convince me.”

“You’re the one who keeps arguing.”

“Maybe.”

Leandros’s expression shifted. For an instant, Percy thought he was going to ask the same question again about how he learned the knot. Instead he picked up the next line and held it out.

“This one’s all right,” he said.

Percy took it. The cord felt smoother. “I can tell.”

“Good.”

“Do you want it in the ready pile?”

“Put it with the dry ones.”

Percy carried it to the line beneath the eaves. He laid it out beside the others, leaving a little room between the coils so air could pass around them.

When he turned back, Leandros was staring at the place where the worn line had been.

“You didn’t throw it out,” he said.

“No.”

“You left enough to use for labels.”

“That's what you wanted.”

“I didn’t say I wanted it.”

“You told me why you thought it was still useful.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No,” Percy said. “It isn’t.”

Leandros picked up the line again. The rope was neither good enough to hold a canoe nor bad enough to be worthless. He coiled the remaining length and set it apart from both piles.

“There,” he said. “It can go to Chiron.”

Percy nodded.

They returned to the sorting without agreeing on anything else. The work became easier when neither of them tried to finish the other’s sentence. By the time the sun had moved past the end of the eaves, the first group of lines was dry enough to hang.

Percy reached for the hooks. Leandros reached for them too. Their hands met over the metal row, knuckles pressing together.

“You take the left side,” Leandros said.

“Why?”

“Because I’m already on the right.”

Percy let go. “That’s a reason.”

Leandros took the hooks down one at a time and handed them over. “You’re learning.”

“From you?”

“Don’t ruin it.”

Percy almost smiled. He carried the hooks to the opposite wall and began hanging the dry coils. There was no point making more of the moment than it was. They had sorted the lines. A few of them would go back into use. One would be cut into labels. They had not broken anything or made the canoe unsafe.

That was a decent morning’s work.

It was not the same as being brothers.

---

Percy washed his hands at the outdoor basin until the rope smell came off. The dirt stayed beneath one nail. He scraped it loose with his thumb, then heard a familiar step on the path behind him.

“You’re going to take your skin off,” Annabeth said.

“I’m trying to get the lake off.”

“The lake is outside.”

“Some of it got on me.”

She set a folded cloth beside the basin. Percy dried his hands. The cloth was one of the old kitchen towels, soft at the edges and patched twice near the hem. He had seen it all week and still could not tell which patch was newer.

Annabeth glanced at his fingers. “Did you burn yourself?”

“No.”

“Cut?”

“No.”

“Then why are you trying to wash off the rope?”

“It smells like rope.”

“That is what rope smells like.”

He looked at her. “You came all the way over here to tell me that?”

“I was already coming this way.”

“From Cabin One?”

“From the table.”

“That’s the other direction.”

“Then I took the long way.”

She reached for the cloth and folded it into a smaller square. Percy had noticed she did that when she was thinking: not fidgeting, exactly, just giving her hands a job while the rest of her caught up.

“Did it go badly?” she asked.

“The line sorting?”

“The line sorting.”

“It went fine.”

“You said that too fast.”

“It went fine and Leandros was annoyed.”

“Because you moved things?”

“Because I touched things.”

“That sounds familiar.”

“Does it?”

She lifted the folded cloth and put it on the edge of the basin. “You don’t like when people move your things either.”

“Depends what they’re moving.”

“Like the map.”

“I asked you not to move the map.”

“You told me not to move the map.”

“Because I was still drawing on it.”

“After you’d left it under the bench for an hour.”

“I was coming back.”

“You said that yesterday.”

“I came back.”

“Eventually.”

The words were familiar enough that Percy felt himself relax. Annabeth was not asking him to explain every thought before she answered. She could still be annoyed with him over a map. He could still be wrong about how long he had been gone. They did not have to make every conversation about the thing that hurt.

She took the towel from his hands and hung it over the rail. Percy reached up after her, but the towel was already in place.

“You’ve got water on your sleeve,” she said.

“So do you.”

“I was at the basin.”

“Same.”

“I was holding the towel.”

“Same basin.”

She shook her head. “You’re impossible.”

“You say that like it’s new.”

“It isn’t.”

He leaned against the post while she tied back the damp corner of the towel. Her fingers moved once, then twice, and the knot sat where she wanted it. She had used the same small knot on the map case and the umbrella cord. Percy had never asked her where she learned it. He knew that was not the same as forgetting. There were things people told you, things you watched them do, things you knew because you had been there.

Annabeth looked at his hands again. “You recognized the fray.”

“Leandros did first.”

“You agreed after looking.”

“Yeah.”

“You were right to say it shouldn’t go back in the ready pile.”

“I know.”

“And he might have been right that it still had a use.”

“He was.”

She waited for him to add something. He looked at the water running from the basin pipe into the shallow drain.

“He doesn’t like me,” Percy said.

“Maybe.”

“Helpful.”

“You didn’t ask if I thought he liked you.”

“I sort of did.”

“Then ask.”

“Do you think he likes me?”

“I don’t know.”

He nodded. That was the answer she had.

She leaned one shoulder against the post. “Do you want him to?”

Percy rubbed at the last of the dirt beneath his nail. “I want this not to be awful.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“No.”

“Which one do you want?”

“I don’t know.”

“Okay.”

“That’s it?”

“You said you didn’t know.”

“You could say it might get better.”

“It might.”

“You could say he’s having a hard time.”

“He might be.”

“You could say he doesn’t mean it when he talks to me like I’m an idiot.”

Annabeth’s mouth moved like she had almost smiled and decided not to. “Does he?”

“Sometimes.”

“Then I don’t know that.”

Percy looked at her. She did not look away from the basin, but she was listening.

“I know what he’s done,” she said. “I know you’ve only met a few days ago. I don’t know what he feels when he’s being awful to you.”

“Good.”

“Good?”

“I don’t need you to make it make sense.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

“I know.”

She nudged his shoe with hers. “You keep asking me to tell you what it means.”

“I do not.”

“You do. You just do it without question marks.”

He huffed a laugh. “That’s not a thing.”

“It is when you do it.”

Percy nudged her back. “You’re making up rules now.”

“I can make rules about questions.”

“Can you?”

“I can make rules about whether I answer them.”

“Fair.”

She put her hand over his for a moment. The scrape of rope fibers had left a red line across the base of his thumb. She traced it once, then let go. “You can talk to him if you want to.”

“I know.”

“You can also stop talking to him if he starts being cruel.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“Yeah.”

“Okay.”

There was no promise in it. No offer to stand in the middle or explain what Percy meant. Annabeth simply stayed with him beside the basin, her shoulder still against the post. Percy felt the space between his hand and hers, then closed it. She let him take her fingers.

“Are you coming back to the shed?” he asked.

“I have to bring Chiron the route notes.”

“Then you’ll be there.”

“For the notes.”

“Sure.”

“I’m not going to sit between you and Leandros.”

“I didn’t ask you to.”

“I know. I’m telling you.”

He rolled her fingers in his palm, careful around the pencil smudge on her thumb. “I don’t want him to think I need you to translate.”

“Then don’t ask me to translate.”

“I’m not going to.”

“I know.”

She squeezed his hand once and stepped away first. Percy let go when she did. She picked up her map tube from beside the table and headed toward the Big House, turning once to check that he had not left the towel in the basin.

He had not.

“Good,” she called.

“Good what?”

“Nothing.”

He laughed, and she kept walking.

Percy stayed by the basin long enough for the last damp line to fade from his fingers. The mark on his thumb remained. It did not hurt. He could decide later whether it was worth mentioning.

---

Chiron arrived with a small box of brass hooks and the list folded in half.

He did not ask whether the lines had been sorted. He looked at the three piles, then at the damp mark on the bench.

“Which ones are finished?” he asked.

Leandros pointed toward the eaves. “Those can go back in storage once they’re dry. The scraped one has enough length for labels, if the cut end is sealed.”

“Would you use it for that?”

“Yes.”

“Percy?”

“I think it’s safe for labels. Not for anything that has to hold weight.”

Chiron opened the box and set it on the bench. “Good. The shorter lengths can go in here. We’ll keep them away from the line that gets used on the canoes.”

Leandros began to coil one of the usable ropes again, checking the width of each loop. “I was going to put the longer ones at the front.”

“Why?”

“So they’re easy to reach.”

“The front is where the wet canvas sits.”

“Then move the canvas.”

“It’s there because that shelf stays dry when the wind comes from the lake.”

Leandros looked toward the shelf. “It doesn’t stay dry. It’s wet right now.”

“It’s damp. The canvas is drying.”

“The rope won’t dry if it’s behind all that.”

“Then we can use the other rack.”

“The other rack has the paddles.”

Chiron watched him for a beat. “What would you move?”

“The spare covers. They don’t need to be near the door.”

“They’re taken out most often.”

“Then put them on the lower shelf.”

“The lower shelf floods if the rain comes sideways.”

“Does it?”

“It did last winter.”

“Then it should be marked.”

“It is.”

Leandros reached for the chalk board hanging beside the door. A few older labels had faded, but the line under *lower shelf—keep clear after rain* was still legible.

He stopped with the chalk in his hand. “That’s hard to see.”

“Yes,” Chiron said. “It may need a darker mark.”

Leandros wrote it in a thicker line, pressing hard enough to leave a little dust on the board’s frame.

“You can decide where the lines go,” Chiron said. “The covers stay on the dry shelf. The paddles remain on their rack. If you want another place for the long coils, we can measure the wall and see whether it fits.”

“Wouldn’t it be easier to move the covers?”

“Perhaps. But if they’re used more often, we’d have to carry them farther.”

“That’s one person’s walk.”

“It’s several people’s walk, over a season.”

Leandros put the chalk down. He did not argue. He also did not agree. The answer sat between them with the other things that had not been decided.

Chiron picked up the worn rope and tested the split. “This can be cut into short tags. The length won’t take a load, so let’s not call it useful for tie-offs.”

“I said labels.”

“You said tie-offs that don’t carry weight.”

“Same thing.”

“Not quite. A tag may be handled often. It still needs to hold.”

Leandros pulled the line from his hand. “It’ll hold.”

“Then show me.”

Leandros took the end and tied the loop around one of the brass hooks. He passed the working end through and drew the knot snug. It sat flat against the metal. He pulled it once, then twice. The rope did not slip.

Chiron tested it again, his hand less forceful. The loop tightened at the split, and two fibers lifted.

He let go. “I see why you thought it would work. I still want it cut back past the worn section.”

Leandros inspected the fibers. “The wear is only on the outer braid.”

“Perhaps. We don’t know what’s underneath until we cut it.”

“We could test it.”

“We could. But what would you use it for?”

Leandros looked at the line in his hands. His answer came more slowly. “Labels.”

“Those can be tied loosely.”

“Then it doesn’t need the hook.”

“No.”

Leandros set the rope on the bench. “Fine. We’ll cut it.”

The decision cost him more than the length of rope. Percy could see that in the way he rolled the line tight, as if the fibers had offended him personally. Chiron did not praise him for agreeing. He only put the hooks back in their box.

“Would you like to finish the storage labels?” Chiron asked.

Leandros glanced at the chalk board. “I can.”

“Percy, do you want to?”

“Sure.”

“Then the two of you can split them. You don’t need to do the same work.”

Leandros looked toward the canvas. “I’ll finish the line tags.”

“Good. Percy can mark the lengths.”

Chiron took the box and walked toward the Big House. He did not leave a second instruction or ask them to make it a lesson. The work list stayed in his hand.

Percy sat on the low stool and began measuring the dry coils against the marks scratched into the bench. Leandros took a knife from the shelf and cut the worn line past the split. He measured the short pieces, then tied each end with a compact stopper knot so the fibers would not unravel.

The second line came out shorter than he expected. He held it up, frowning.

“You want that one for tags?” Percy asked.

“It’s enough.”

“How many?”

“Six.”

“There are seven hooks.”

“We only need six labels.”

“What about the line that goes in the box?”

“That gets a mark on the box.”

Percy wrote six lengths on the scrap paper. The numbers stayed clear, though he had to write smaller than usual to fit them. Leandros glanced over his shoulder.

“That one’s eight inches,” he said.

“It’s eight and a half.”

“From where you measured?”

“The inside edge.”

“You should use the outside.”

“Why?”

“Because it’s a loop. The length shrinks when you knot it.”

Percy looked at his first tag. “Then this one’s wrong.”

“It’ll be short.”

“Can we undo it?”

“Not without cutting it.”

They looked at the tag. The knot held. The rope was too short to lay flat against the box, but it could still be tied around a hook.

“It will work,” Leandros said.

“You just said it’s wrong.”

“I said the measurement is wrong.”

“Which matters because?”

“Because we don’t want the next person to think it’s eight inches.”

Percy crossed out the number and wrote *short tag*. “There.”

“That’s not a measurement.”

“It’s the truth.”

Leandros looked at the paper, then at the tag. “Fine.”

Percy put the shortened tag aside. He wrote the next measurement from the outside of the loop. This time Leandros did not correct him.

They worked for several minutes without speaking. The rainwater came off the eaves in slower drops. Someone on the path called to a friend; the reply carried back from the cabins. The shed seemed smaller after Chiron left, not because the walls had moved, but because there was no one else to make the space feel shared.

Leandros tied another knot. “You had this in Atlantis?”

“What?”

“Boat work.”

“Some.”

“Were you good?”

“I don’t know.”

“You don’t know if you were good?”

“I can do some of it.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“I know.”

Leandros pulled the knot too tight, then worked his thumb beneath the turn to loosen it. “You lived there long enough.”

“I did.”

“You learned the routes. The rooms. The boats. The palace.”

“Probably.”

“Don’t you remember any of it?”

Percy put down the pencil. He had been asked versions of the question before, sometimes with more care, sometimes with none. Leandros had asked about Atlantis when they first met and Percy had kept the answer private. He had said that he did not remember Leandros. He had not explained the rest.

“I know I was raised there,” Percy said. “I know who was there. I know stories people tell me. I don’t have the memories of being little there.”

Leandros’s hands stopped on the line.

“None?”

“Not the way you mean.”

“What way do I mean?”

“Like I can see it. Like I can tell you what I thought when I was five or what I did after breakfast.”

Leandros looked back at the knot. “You still had it.”

“Yeah.”

“So that’s better.”

“Some of it was.”

“Some of it.”

“I had a home. I had people who knew me. Amphitrite raised me. I know that mattered.”

“You know it because they tell you.”

“I know it because they tell me. And because I’m there now. And because I can see what it gave me.”

“That’s not the same as remembering.”

“No.”

“Then what does it matter?”

Percy glanced down at the rope. “It matters because it happened.”

Leandros laughed once, not because anything was funny. “You get to have both parts.”

“What does that mean?”

“You get the family. You get Atlantis. You get all these people who talk about how they raised you. And when someone asks what it was like, you say you can’t remember.”

“That’s not all I say.”

“It’s enough.”

“No, it isn’t.”

The line between Leandros’s hands tightened. He was not looking at Percy now.

Percy moved the paper farther from the edge of the bench. “I’m not going to pretend I didn’t have more than you did. I had a place to live. I had a family there. I had things you didn’t.”

“Then why bring up the missing part?”

“Because it’s true.”

“It doesn’t help.”

“I’m not trying to make it help.”

“Then what are you trying to do?”

“I’m telling you what I know.”

“You know you had more.”

“Yeah.”

“You know you can’t remember.”

“Yeah.”

“And when I want to talk about what I didn’t get, you tell me that you can’t remember.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You do.”

“I said I don’t remember being little in Atlantis.”

“And that’s supposed to settle it?”

“No.”

“But it does. Everyone stops and looks at you like they need to be gentle.”

Percy felt the back of his neck grow warm. “I didn’t ask them to.”

“You don’t have to ask.”

“That doesn’t mean I’m using it.”

“You don’t know what you’re using.”

It was the kind of sentence that could pass for a fact if Percy let it. Leandros knew about the gap because Percy had just said it. He did not know what Percy remembered from the other childhood, or how he had arrived at camp carrying the future like a place that still existed. He could not know that Percy had not chosen this story to win an argument.

“You don’t get to decide why I say something,” Percy said.

Leandros’s grip tightened again. “I’m trying to know you.”

“No, you’re trying to decide what I mean.”

“Maybe because you never say what you mean.”

“I said what I mean.”

“You said that you had a family and didn’t remember it.”

“I said I had a home there. I said it was more than you got. I said the part I can’t remember still matters to me.”

“Why should it?”

“Because it’s mine.”

Leandros made a short, frustrated movement with the line, then started the knot over. “You call it yours now.”

“It was mine.”

“You don’t remember.”

“That doesn’t make it not mine.”

“It makes it easy to claim.”

Percy felt the accusation land in his chest, but it did not become anger right away. First it was surprise. Then the wish to correct every word. Then the knowledge that if he tried to answer them all, the conversation would become a list of things Leandros did not get to say.

“You think I’m making it up?”

“I think you can use it however you want.”

“That’s not what I said.”

“You have the place. You have the mother. You have the stories. You can say you’re missing something and no one can prove you wrong.”

Percy stood. The stool scraped against the dirt behind him. “I’m not asking you to prove me wrong.”

“No. You’re asking me not to ask.”

“You’re asking me to give you details I don’t have.”

“I’m asking you to stop acting like you know what it’s like to have less.”

“I’m not.”

“You were raised by a queen.”

“I was raised by Amphitrite.”

“Which is the same thing.”

“No, it isn’t.”

“Everyone here treats it like it is.”

“Who?”

“You.”

“I don’t.”

“You walk in with a family that knows you and a home waiting under the sea. Then you tell me you don’t remember it, and that’s supposed to mean I can’t be angry.”

“I never said you couldn’t be angry.”

“You didn’t have to.”

Percy heard the line fall slack in Leandros’s hand. It lay across the bench between them, a thin barrier that was too worn to hold anything heavy.

“I know I had more,” Percy said. “I won’t pretend otherwise. I don’t know what it was like for you. I know you came from a different home. I know Poseidon didn’t bring you up the way he brought me into Atlantis. I know that’s unequal.”

“You know that because you’ve been told.”

“Yes.”

“And you don’t know what any of it felt like.”

“No.”

“Then don’t tell me you know what you got.”

Percy looked at him. “I know I got a home.”

“That’s not the same as getting a father.”

“No. It isn’t.”

The words came out quietly. Leandros’s expression shifted, but only for a second. Percy could see how much he wanted the answer to be wrong. Or maybe that was what Percy wanted to see.

“I don’t know what Poseidon gave you,” Percy said. “I don’t know what he promised. I don’t know what he didn’t do. I know what I had around me.”

“And you think that’s enough.”

“No.”

“You think I’m jealous.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You don’t have to. You can just say I’m being unfair.”

“You are.”

Leandros’s head lifted. The line dropped from his hand.

Percy did not take the words back. “You’re telling me why I say something. You’re telling me what I’m trying to do. That’s unfair.”

“It’s also true.”

“No.”

“You don’t know.”

“I know what I mean.”

“You just said I don’t.”

“I said you don’t get to decide for me.”

“That’s the same thing.”

“It isn’t.”

“You can keep that distinction. It works for you.”

Percy had no answer ready. He was glad of it. The words in his mouth had begun to sound like a way to win. He didn’t want to win this. He wanted Leandros to understand that the childhood he did not remember was not an imaginary prize, and the things Leandros had missed were not Percy’s fault. There was no sentence that could make both parts feel fair.

Leandros picked up the line. “I don’t need you to tell me it was unfair.”

“Okay.”

“I need you to stop acting like you’re the only one with something missing.”

“I don’t.”

“You do.”

Percy took a slow breath. “I don’t know what you’re missing.”

“Exactly.”

It was a cruel answer, and it was also true in the way that shut every door at once. Percy looked at the worn line, the open hook, the labels that were not finished. He had wanted to hear what Leandros was angry about. He still did. But it would not help to pretend that anger gave Leandros the right to tell Percy what he remembered or why he spoke.

“You can tell me,” Percy said. “If you want.”

Leandros turned away. “I don’t.”

The conversation ended there because he had said no. Percy stayed beside the bench, listening to the dripping eaves and the smaller sounds beyond them. Leandros finished tying the tag. When he was done, he set it in the box with the others, not on the shelf with the finished supplies.

Percy waited.

Leandros did not look back.

---

Percy carried the box of brass hooks back after Chiron returned the line list to the Big House. By dinner, the rope tags had dried. The six coils were on the upper shelf, spaced far enough apart that the loops would not stick together. The short tag Leandros had made sat on top of the box with *short* written across its paper label.

Percy thought about moving it to the side so the word would not get rubbed off. He left it where it was.

At Cabin Three, Clarisse had pushed the dining table against the wall and set a handful of spoons in the center. Silena was shuffling a deck of cards. Annabeth sat on the windowsill with her knees drawn up, watching them.

“What are you doing?” Percy asked.

“Playing spoons,” Silena said.

“I can see the spoons.”

“Then you already know half the rules.”

“I'm not sure I want the other half.”

Clarisse picked up one spoon and laid it back in the middle. “You do.”

“Why?”

“Because I don't want to play with three people.”

“You could play with three people.”

“I could. I don't want to.”

Silena placed the deck beside the spoons. “For five players, four spoons. You pass the cards around and try to get four of a kind. When somebody picks up a spoon, everyone else gets one too.”

“What if I don't want to pick up a spoon?”

“Then you lose,” Clarisse said.

“That's not a good rule.”

“It isn't trying to be a good rule.”

Annabeth hopped down from the sill. “The game is designed to make you lose.”

“Then why play?”

“Because Clarisse likes winning.”

“I like playing,” Clarisse said.

“You like that you win.”

“I can like both.”

Percy sat beside Annabeth and moved his chair a little closer to hers. Their knees touched under the table. She did not shift away.

Silena looked toward the doorway. “Leandros?”

He came in holding the box of brass hooks. He set it on the shelf above his cot and looked at the table.

“Are those for the line tags?” he asked.

“Chiron took the rest,” Percy said. “The short ones are for labels.”

Leandros checked the box. He moved the paper tag once, then put it back in place. He did not say anything about the word Percy had written.

“Are you playing?” Silena asked.

“I don't know the game.”

“I can explain it.”

“I can watch.”

“Okay,” she said.

Clarisse took one spoon from the middle and set it on the counter. “Four playing, three spoons. If you join, we put it back.”

Clarisse picked up the deck and passed it to Annabeth. “Deal.”

“Why me?”

“Because you have the least chance of cheating.”

Percy put one hand over his heart. “That’s a serious accusation.”

“You asked how many spoons there were before sitting down.”

“I wanted to know if we had enough.”

“We did.”

“Then it worked.”

Annabeth dealt four cards to each person. Percy played the first round, watching the cards move faster than he could follow. Silena held her cards close to her chest. Clarisse did not bother hiding hers until Annabeth flicked a card at her shoulder. Leandros stood beside the table with his arms folded, following the cards.

“Pass left,” Silena said.

“That's it?” Leandros asked.

“You pick up one card, keep four, and pass one to the next person.”

“Until someone gets four of a kind.”

“Right.”

“And then they take a spoon.”

“Right.”

“And everyone else takes one.”

“Right.”

“That's the whole game?”

“Yes.”

Leandros watched them deal again. “Why would everyone take one?”

“Because if you wait to see who got four, they'll take them all,” Annabeth said.

“That seems arbitrary.”

“It is,” Clarisse said.

“Then it’s a bad game.”

“Then don’t play.”

“I said I’d watch.”

“You’re watching.”

Percy saw Silena's hand shift as she passed the next card. Her fingers tapped once against the table, then stopped. Annabeth was the first to notice the spoon moving. She reached across the cards, but Clarisse got there before her.

The other two spoons disappeared. Percy had not even seen who picked up the first one. By the time he reached, there were none left; his hand closed around nothing.

Clarisse held up the spoon. “I won.”

“You all grabbed them at the same time,” Percy said.

“That’s the point.”

“I thought the point was four of a kind.”

“The point is the spoon.”

“Then why are there cards?”

“Because we need something to do while we wait for the spoon.”

“That's not how games work.”

“It's how this one works.”

Annabeth put the cards together again. “Do you want to try?”

Leandros looked at the empty seat. “If I do, I want the rules before you start.”

“They’re not complicated,” Clarisse said.

“That isn't the same as explaining them.”

“I just did.”

“You told me what to do. You didn't tell me how to know when to do it.”

“Get four of a kind,” Percy said. “Then take a spoon.”

“When?”

“When you have four.”

“That isn't helpful.”

“It's not a game with a lot of rules.”

“Then you can explain the timing.”

“Leandros,” Silena said, “we can do one round slowly if you want.”

“I'm not asking you to change the game.”

“You said you wanted the rules.”

“I can learn by playing.”

Clarisse slid a spoon toward the empty seat. “Then sit down.”

He hesitated. Percy held his own cards, leaving the place beside him open. Leandros looked at the hand, then at the table. He set his elbows down and took the cards Annabeth offered. Clarisse retrieved the spare spoon from the counter and returned it to the middle.

The first round went slowly. Each person passed a card, took the next one, then looked at the new hand. Leandros sorted his cards by suit, then stopped when Percy told him it was four of a kind, not a run.

“Why have suits if they don't matter?”

“They matter in other games,” Annabeth said.

“This isn't one of those.”

“Clearly.”

He passed one card to Percy. Percy gave him the next one. It was a red queen. Leandros glanced at his hand, then made a small adjustment. He kept the four and passed the one he did not need.

The spoon was taken before he noticed. Clarisse took one, then Silena and Annabeth took two more. Percy and Leandros were left for the last spoon. Leandros reached first and closed his hand around the handle. Percy’s fingers stopped an inch away.

Then Leandros let go.

“That’s it?” he asked.

“That’s it,” Clarisse said.

“Who lost?”

“You did,” Annabeth said. “You had the last spoon and let it go.”

“Percy had his hand on it.”

“He didn’t take it,” Clarisse said. “You did. Then you let go.”

“That isn’t a rule.”

“It’s how the round ends,” Silena said. “Once the spoons are gone, it’s over. You don’t get to restart after you see who missed one.”

“I thought the person with four cards gets the spoon.”

“They do,” Annabeth said. “Then everybody else reaches. You heard that part.”

“I thought there was time to take one after.”

“There isn’t,” Clarisse said. “You can ask us to explain before the next round.”

Percy put his cards down. “If you want, we can play again.”

Leandros’s gaze moved to him. “I know.”

He did not sound grateful. He sounded as if Percy had repeated something obvious.

Clarisse began gathering the cards. “One more, then I’m done.”

“Why only one?”

“Because I said so.”

“That’s not how games work either.”

“It's how my evening works.”

Annabeth shuffled the cards and held them out. Leandros took his four. This time, he watched for the spoons. He also watched the others’ hands, trying to see when they stopped caring about the cards. Silena picked up a spoon without looking down. Percy reached for one a moment too late. Annabeth got hers and slid it toward Leandros before Clarisse could grab the last.

He took it.

“That's not fair,” Clarisse said.

“Why?”

“You didn't have four.”

“I did.”

“You had three queens.”

“And a spoon.”

Annabeth laughed. “That wasn't the rule.”

“It was after I took it.”

“That isn't how it works,” Percy said.

“It worked.”

“You lost the first round.”

“I won this one.”

“You got a spoon before you had four cards.”

Leandros set it back in the middle. “Then you should have explained the rule.”

“We did,” Silena said.

“You explained a different game.”

“It was the same game.”

“Not if I changed the rule.”

Clarisse leaned back in her chair. “You're not changing the rule because you got a spoon.”

“I’m not changing anything. I’m saying the game is unclear.”

“It's not.”

“Because you already know it.”

The room quieted. Percy could feel the moment stretch, but it did not turn into the same argument from the shed. There was no line in Leandros's hand to pull tight. The spoons lay between them, small and ordinary.

Clarisse picked one up, wiped a speck of water from the bowl with her thumb, and set it beside the others. “The rule is that you wait for four cards.”

“I know that now.”

“Good.”

Silena gathered the deck. “We can play again another time.”

“I'm not done,” Leandros said.

“Okay,” she said. “Then do you want to keep playing?”

He looked at the spoons. “Yes.”

Clarisse started dealing again. She did not explain the rules a second time. Leandros watched for four of a kind. The next round was quicker. Percy got a spoon first; Clarisse reached for one and brushed Annabeth’s wrist. Annabeth was left without a spoon. She stacked the cards while the other four pushed their spoons back toward the middle.

No one commented on that. Silena collected the cards, and Annabeth started another deal.

Leandros stayed for two more rounds. He lost one and won the other by a fast reach that knocked a spoon onto the floor. Percy bent to pick it up. Leandros did too. Their shoulders bumped, and both stopped.

“Go ahead,” Leandros said.

Percy picked up the spoon and set it back on the table.

After that, Leandros sat outside the circle, not leaving but no longer holding cards. Clarisse and Silena began arguing about whether to play another hand. Annabeth asked Percy to pass the deck. He handed it to her. Their fingers touched; she leaned her foot against his beneath the table.

“Are you playing?” she asked him.

“No.”

“Why not?”

“I lost twice.”

“You lost once.”

“Twice feels more accurate.”

“That's not how numbers work.”

“It's how my evening works.”

She smiled and dealt the cards.

Leandros watched the round without interrupting. When the spoons moved, he reached out, then pulled his hand back before anyone had to tell him it wasn't his turn. The gesture was so small Percy could have mistaken it for moving his arm to make room. He did not decide what it meant.

When the game ended, Clarisse pushed her chair back. “I’m done.”

“You said one more,” Percy said.

“I played more than one.”

“You said one more after the first game.”

“That was before Leandros joined.”

“So he gets extra rounds?”

“He played extra rounds.”

“I played extra rounds too.”

“You were already here.”

“That doesn't seem fair.”

“You won one.”

“By accident.”

“Then you should be happy I let you keep it.”

“I'm grateful.”

Clarisse snorted and took the spoons to the wash basin. Silena folded the cards and put them away. Annabeth stood, touching the hem of her shirt where the table had wrinkled it.

“You could have beaten her,” Percy said.

“I did.”

“Not at spoons.”

“At the cards.”

“That wasn't the game.”

“It was the game I was playing.”

He looked at her. “You cheated?”

“I did not.”

“You passed me the queen I needed.”

“You needed it because you were collecting queens.”

“That's the point.”

“Then I didn't cheat.”

She nudged his knee with hers. He bumped her back. The joke was small enough to stay between them.

At the other end of the table, Leandros picked up the line-tag box. He checked the pieces, then put it on the shelf beneath his cot. The short tag was still visible on top. He didn't take it off or correct Percy’s writing.

Silena rinsed the last spoon. “Do you want these in the kitchen?”

“I can take them,” Percy said.

“Thanks.”

Annabeth took half from him, and they walked the short distance to the pantry together. Percy carried the spoons; she carried the deck. They did not have to walk side by side, but they did.

The night air came through the cabin door. Behind them, Leandros lowered the lid over his duffel.

He had joined the game. He had not stayed long. Neither fact answered what he wanted from Percy.

---

Annabeth left for Cabin One after the cards were put away. Percy walked her as far as the path, where the lanterns stopped and the windows of Cabin Three still glowed behind them.

“You don't have to walk me all the way,” she said.

“I know.”

“You have to go back to the cabin.”

“I know that too.”

“Are you going to stand here until I get there?”

“No.”

She looked at him. “You were.”

“I was thinking.”

“About what?”

“You.”

“That's not a good answer.”

“I was thinking about whether you forgot your jacket.”

“I’m wearing it.”

“Then I was wrong.”

She touched the front of her jacket, where the zipper was already closed. “You can see that.”

“It's dark.”

“There's a lantern right there.”

“I can still be wrong.”

Annabeth smiled and leaned in, pressing her forehead briefly to his. Percy caught the cuff of her sleeve before she stepped away. She turned her hand and threaded their fingers together for a second. Then she let go.

“Goodnight,” she said.

“Goodnight.”

She continued up the path. Percy waited until the light from Cabin One showed against the trees, then turned back.

Clarisse and Silena had already gone to their bunks. A small lamp remained lit near the door. Leandros sat at the end of his cot with the line-tag box open on his knees. He had taken the short tag out and was turning it between his fingers.

Percy stopped before crossing the room. “Did it break?”

“No.”

“Then why are you turning it over?”

“I’m reading it.”

“Short.”

“I can see.”

“Okay.”

Leandros put the tag back on top of the others. “You don’t have to check on me.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You came over here.”

“I live here.”

“So do I.”

“Right.”

The answer seemed to catch Leandros. He closed the box and set it beneath his cot.

Percy removed his shoes and placed them by the bed. The room was quiet except for Clarisse shifting once under her blanket. Silena's breathing had gone even. The lamp made a small circle across the floor and left the far corner dark.

“Chiron said the other tags can stay in the box,” Leandros said.

“He did?”

“Before he left the shed.”

“Okay.”

“Not everything needs to be hung up.”

“I didn't say it did.”

“You looked at the empty hook.”

Percy followed his gaze. The hook beside the door was empty. He had noticed it without thinking, but he had not planned to put anything there.

“I was looking at the hook.”

“You don't have to keep track of every space.”

“I wasn't.”

Leandros lay back on the cot and turned toward the wall. “Fine.”

Percy sat on the edge of his bed. The blanket had twisted under his knee. He pulled it smooth, then left the lamp on.

“You can turn that off,” Leandros said.

“I'm not ready.”

“Why not?”

“I'm not tired.”

“You just said goodnight.”

“To Annabeth.”

“So you're not tired.”

“I didn't say that.”

Leandros turned back. “You always answer like there's another question hiding in the one I asked.”

“Maybe there is.”

“That's annoying.”

“Probably.”

Leandros looked as if he had prepared to argue and then decided that Percy had given him nothing to use. He turned away again.

Percy rested his hands on his knees. The line-tag box sat under the cot, out of sight but not far enough to be forgotten. He thought about the parts of Atlantis he could name, the rooms other people described, the childhood everyone around him understood as his. They were not false because his mind did not fill in the scenes. They were not complete because his body knew how to tie a boat line.

Leandros had not asked Percy to pretend he was empty-handed. He had asked him to stop talking about what was missing. The distinction mattered to Percy, even if it did not help tonight.

He reached over and turned off the lamp.

The room settled into the dark. The window blanket held at its upper corner. The repaired cord did not pull loose. Clarisse rolled onto her side and went still. Silena’s breathing remained steady.

Across from him, Leandros shifted under his own blanket. Neither of them spoke.

Clarisse moved under her blanket. “You’re waiting for him to say something.”

Percy kept his eyes on the ceiling. “I’m not.”

“You looked at his cot three times.”

“He was moving.”

“He was turning over.”

“That’s moving.”

“That’s not what you were looking for.”

Percy didn’t answer. He could hear the cabin settle around them: a board creaking near the door, the last soft gust pressing the blanket against the window, someone breathing through a stuffy nose. The borrowed cord held. It would need a proper pin when Chiron found one, but it had kept the cloth in place for another night.

Clarisse rolled onto her back. “You were trying to get him to sit down.”

“Silena asked him.”

“I know. You kept the chair open.”

“I was sitting beside it.”

“You moved your elbow.”

“I needed room for my cards.”

“You weren’t holding cards when you moved.”

Percy turned his head toward her. The darkness hid most of her face, though the pale stripe of moonlight caught the edge of her blanket. “Are you keeping track of where I put my elbows now?”

“Only when you do it like you don’t mean anything by it.”

“I did want him to play.”

“Then say that.”

“I didn’t want to make a thing out of it.”

“You made a thing out of it by pretending you didn’t want him there.”

The answer came too quickly for him to argue with. He pressed the heel of his hand against his forehead. “I thought if I said it out loud, it’d sound like I expected him to.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

“Then you can let him decide.”

“I did.”

“Mostly.”

Percy exhaled. “What does that mean?”

“It means when he said he didn’t know the rules, you jumped in to explain them. When he said he was watching, you kept offering him another round. That’s not a crime. It just wasn’t invisible.”

“I thought he wanted to learn.”

“He did. And then he got mad because he didn’t win.”

“He wasn’t mad about losing.”

“You don’t know that.”

“No,” Percy said. “I don’t.”

Clarisse shifted again, the mattress frame giving a low creak. “You want to know what he wants from you.”

“I want to know what he thinks of me.”

“Why?”

The question was blunt enough that Percy almost gave the easy answer. Because they were brothers. Because they were going to share a cabin. Because Poseidon had put the same mark over both their heads. Because Percy had seen the way Leandros handled a line and wanted to know the person who had learned it.

“Because I want him to like me,” he said. “And because I’m angry at him. Those can both be true.”

“They can.”

“Does that bother you?”

“No. I don’t have to sort it out for you.”

“I wasn’t asking you to.”

“You keep asking questions that want an answer from me.”

Percy tried to find the difference. He thought of what Annabeth had said by the basin—that she didn’t know whether Leandros liked him, and would not make his choices sound kinder than they were. Clarisse wasn’t Annabeth. She didn’t soften a boundary just because Percy had started to sound uncertain.

“You don’t have to like him because he’s here,” he said.

“I know.”

“You don’t have to make room for him just because I did.”

“I know that too.”

“Okay.”

“Stop saying it like that.”

He could barely see her now, but he heard the irritation return to her voice. “Like what?”

“Like you’re agreeing so I’ll stop talking.”

“I am agreeing.”

“That’s not the same thing as listening.”

He waited. She did not offer him an easier phrasing.

“I don’t want to turn this into your problem,” he said.

“Then don’t.”

“I won’t.”

“You can be mad at him without asking me to be mad the same way. You can want him to be your brother without deciding I feel the same. And he can sit at our table without earning something from me just because he sat there.”

Percy let those words settle without finding a reply that would improve them. A few minutes ago, the game had felt like the smallest possible offer: an empty chair, a deck passed around, someone choosing to stay for two more rounds. He had wanted it to mean that Leandros could begin to belong. Clarisse was telling him that it meant only what had happened. Leandros had played. Clarisse had not promised him anything.

“Does it bother you that I wanted him there?” Percy asked.

“No.”

“You sound like it does.”

“It bothers me when you act like my reaction needs to match yours.”

“I don’t think it does.”

“You just asked.”

Clarisse shifted her blanket over one shoulder. “You kept looking at me while he was at the table. When he got the rules wrong, when he sat down, when he got up.”

“I was watching the game.”

“You were checking what I thought.”

“I wondered if you wanted me to say something.”

“If I want you to say something, I’ll tell you.”

“And if you don’t?”

“Then let it be quiet for a minute.”

Percy found the seam of his blanket with his thumb. “I’m not good at that.”

“I know. That’s why I’m telling you.”

He smiled faintly, though she could not see it. “That’s fair.”

“It’s not a game. Don’t keep score.”

“I’m not.”

“You do.”

“I’m trying not to.”

“Try harder.”

She said it as plainly as if she were asking him to move his shoes out of the walkway. Percy rolled onto his side. “Do you think I’m supposed to make him understand?”

“No.”

“Do you think I should leave him alone?”

“No.”

“What am I supposed to do, then?”

“Decide what you want to do. He gets to decide what he does with it.”

“That’s all?”

“That’s enough for tonight.”

Across the room, Leandros shifted beneath his blanket. Neither of them spoke until the cot went still again. Percy wondered if he had heard anything. He did not get up to check.

“Goodnight, Clarisse,” he said.

“Go to sleep.”

It was not the same thing. He did it anyway.

The box remained under Leandros’s cot. In the morning, Percy could ask whether he wanted to leave it there or move it closer to the shelf. He could also leave it alone until there was a reason to ask. He chose the second. The short tag would still be there when the light came on.

Percy could not remember being Leandros’s brother before they met. That was an ordinary truth now. It did not decide what happened next, and it did not make the space between their beds smaller.

He turned onto his side and listened to the cabin quiet down around him.
