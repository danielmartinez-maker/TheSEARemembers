## Chapter 195: The Rules of Cabin Three

Clarisse woke before the cabin stove did.

The fire had settled to a red seam under the ash. It cast enough light to show the new cot against the wall and the lump of a sleeping bag folded on it. Leandros was awake already, sitting at the edge with his elbows on his knees and his hands flat against them. He had not taken off his sweater. His duffel stayed zipped beneath the cot, where he had placed it the night before so it could not be stepped on.

Clarisse could not tell whether he had slept. He had not made a sound after they returned from dinner, and there was no snoring, shifting, or whispered call home to mark the hours. He might have slept without moving. He might have stayed awake, listening to the cabin settle.

“You’re up,” she said.

“So are you.”

“That’s not the same thing.”

“Why?”

“Because I live here.”

“So do I. At least for now.”

“Good. Then you’ll learn what time we start.”

“I know what time it is.”

The cabin clock said six-twelve. Clarisse had been awake since before the first bell. “You get up when you need to.”

“I need to.”

“Then you're up.”

She got out of bed and found her socks on the bench. The floorboards were cold. She put one foot down, then the other, and waited for the ache in her knee to settle. There was no reason for it to hurt; she had simply landed wrong at sparring yesterday and refused to sit out the last round. It would be fine by breakfast. She flexed it once and stood.

Leandros watched her. “Are you going to make coffee?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because I don’t drink coffee.”

“Do you have any?”

“Silena does.”

“Then why did you say no?”

“Because I’m not making it.”

He glanced at the pot near the stove. “Is there a rule about using it?”

“There's a rule about asking.”

“I'm asking.”

“Then ask Silena. It’s hers.”

He rubbed his palms together, then reached for the lamp. “Where are the matches?”

“On the shelf.”

He took one step toward the shelf and stopped. “Can I use them?”

“You don’t need my permission to light the stove.”

“I thought you said there was a rule about asking.”

“There is. You asked. I answered.”

“That’s a stupid rule.”

“It keeps us from taking each other’s things.”

“You’re saying I would.”

“I’m saying I don’t know you yet.”

He looked at the matches, then at her. “You have a way of making a simple question sound like an accusation.”

“You have a way of making an answer sound like a challenge.”

“That’s not what I’m doing.”

“Then stop making it one.”

The cabin held the cold from outside, but the winter blankets trapped a little of the heat near the stove. Clarisse crossed to it and pulled the ash rake from its hook. Leandros moved aside as if she had asked him to, though she had not. She spread the coals and placed a sliver of kindling over the brightest part. The stove took it slowly.

“Can you use that?” she asked.

“What?”

“The rake.”

“I know how to use a fireplace.”

“Then you can help.”

“I didn't say I wanted to.”

“Then don't.”

He exhaled through his nose and went back to the cot. Clarisse lit the match. The flame bent in the draft before it caught the kindling.

She could feel him watching again. “If you have a question, ask it.”

“I just did.”

“About the matches. Not whatever else you're doing.”

“What am I doing?”

“Waiting for somebody to tell you where to go.”

“That’s not it.”

“Okay.”

“Why do you do that?”

“Do what?”

“Ask me something and then decide what I mean.”

“I’m not deciding. I’m asking.”

“No, you’re accusing.”

Clarisse held the match until the kindling caught, then shook it out in the ash pan. “If I was accusing you, I’d say so.”

“That’s not reassuring.”

“I’m not trying to reassure you.”

“You could try.”

“I could. I don’t have to.”

The fire crept up the wood, then settled. She set the rake aside. “You’re new here. You don’t have to know every rule. You do have to ask before you take someone’s stuff. You do have to put your things where they won’t trip people. You do have to follow Chiron’s camp rules. That’s it.”

“And your rules?”

“My rules are things I say when I don’t want to step on your bag.”

“Why not say that?”

“I did.”

“When?”

“Just now.”

He glanced at the duffel under his cot. “I can move it.”

“You can. I don't care where you put it as long as nobody trips over it.”

“Maybe I want it there.”

“Then keep it there.”

The silence was not peaceful. It was a piece of space neither of them had decided how to use. Clarisse went to the washbasin and splashed cold water on her face. In the mirror’s dull metal she saw her own eyes, her hair, the new boy sitting behind her like he had come to a place where everyone had already learned to talk around him.

“You always get up this early?” he asked.

“Usually.”

“Why?”

“Because I have things to do.”

“What things?”

“Whatever the day needs.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It’s the answer I have.”

He stood. “So you decide the schedule.”

“No.”

“Who does?”

“Chiron handles the camp schedule. The cabin has habits.”

“Who decided them?”

“We did.”

“Who is we?”

“Me, Silena, Percy. Anybody else who lived here before you.”

“Then they’re your rules.”

“Some of them.”

“Which ones?”

Clarisse turned from the washbasin. “I just told you the ones that matter.”

“You told me how not to make a mess.”

“I told you how to live in a room with other people.”

“I know how to live with other people.”

“Then do it.”

He turned toward the window. The cloth was pinned at the corners, and a thin line of morning showed at the bottom. “You keep saying ‘we’ like I’m supposed to be part of it already.”

“Do you want to be?”

“I didn't say that.”

“You asked about the rules.”

“I want to know what I'm walking into.”

“Then ask the question you actually have.”

He held still. The new light on his face made him look younger than the day before. He wasn’t younger. Sixteen was the same age as Silena, close to it, older than Clarisse and Percy. He had not needed help finding the cabin or carrying the bag. What he did not know was whether anyone expected him to stay.

“Do you want me here?” he asked.

Clarisse could not answer for Silena, and she would not answer for Percy.

“You can stay tonight,” she said. “That’s the deal you made yesterday.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“I know.”

“Do you want me here?”

“I don’t know you.”

“So?”

“So I don’t know.”

“You’re my sister.”

“I know.”

“Doesn’t that count for anything?”

“It counts for what it is. It doesn’t tell me what I want.”

He looked down at the cot. “I thought family was supposed to want you.”

“I want people who don’t tell me I’m supposed to want them.”

“I didn’t say you were.”

“You asked if it counted.”

“It does.”

“Then that’s what I can give you. It counts. The rest takes time.”

“You talk like Chiron.”

“Chiron talks like me when he’s making a point.”

A short sound came from the other side of the cabin. Silena had rolled over in her bunk. “If you two are going to fight before breakfast, could you do it closer to the stove? I’m cold.”

“We're not fighting,” Clarisse said.

“Good. Then you can talk quieter.”

Leandros took the matches from the shelf and looked at the stove. “Can I light it?”

“Sure.”

He struck the match against the box, shielding it with one hand. The flame caught. He put it to the kindling and waited until the orange deepened. He did not ask Clarisse whether it was right. She did not correct him.

Silena sat up and shoved her blanket down. “Is there food?”

“Apples,” Clarisse said.

“Not again.”

“They’re already cut.”

“Then bread.”

“We’re going to the pavilion.”

“Are we?”

“Yes.”

Silena swung her feet to the floor. “If you’ve decided that, we can.”

“I didn’t decide. I thought that was where breakfast was.”

“It is.”

“Then why did you ask?”

“Because I was still half asleep.”

Leandros looked between them. “Is breakfast always an argument?”

“Only if someone’s up before it’s ready,” Clarisse said.

“I asked one question.”

“And Silena answered.”

Silena pulled on a pair of socks. “You’re both going to be exhausting.”

“I’m not exhausting,” Leandros said.

“You haven't been here long enough to know.”

“Then I’ll take your word for it.”

“Don’t.”

Clarisse bent to tie her boots. “What’s your plan this morning?”

“I don't know.”

“Do you want to ask Chiron where he needs you?”

“Maybe.”

“Then do that after breakfast.”

“Are you telling me what to do?”

“No. I'm telling you what you just said.”

“That’s annoying.”

“Then say something else.”

Silena laughed once, soft and tired. “You two can’t start a cabin without turning every sentence into a test.”

“I'm not testing him,” Clarisse said.

“I know. He is testing you.”

Leandros’s eyebrows lifted. “I’m not.”

“Then you should probably stop trying to win every answer.”

“I don’t think every answer is a contest.”

“Neither do I,” Clarisse said. “We’re done.”

“For now?”

“For this morning.”

He nodded, not quite satisfied. Clarisse took the list from the table and tucked it into her jacket pocket.

“What’s on it?” he asked.

“Tasks.”

“For the whole camp?”

“For the cabin.”

“Who gave you the list?”

“Silena started it.”

“Does she manage you too?”

“Not unless she wants me to get something wrong.”

Silena tossed a wool sock at Clarisse’s shoulder. “You asked me to copy the list because yours was illegible.”

“It was readable.”

“Only to you.”

“It was readable.”

Leandros watched them again, this time with something closer to interest than judgment. Clarisse put the sock on the bench and pulled the door open.

“Breakfast,” she said.

“You’re telling me what to do.”

“I'm telling you where I'm going. You can come or stay.”

He lifted his bag strap from the floor, then let it settle again. “I’ll come.”

They went out together, not in a row. Clarisse did not wait for him to follow, and he did not let her lead him. The path was damp, with small leaves stuck to the packed earth. He stepped around the muddy patches without looking down.

“Watch the stone by the corner,” she said. “It gets slick in the cold.”

“I saw it.”

“Then you won't slip.”

“I’m not a child.”

“I didn’t say you were.”

“But you told me where the stone was.”

“Because you’re new to the path.”

“I can see the path.”

“Then you can see the stone.”

He stopped. “Do you know you do that?”

“What?”

“Give instructions that don’t need to be given.”

“Sometimes they do.”

“Maybe. Sometimes they don’t.”

“Okay.”

“Okay?”

“Maybe you’re right.”

He looked at her, surprised. Clarisse kept walking. She had been right that the stone was slick, but he had been right too: the stone was visible, and he was not stupid. It took her a moment to decide that the difference mattered.

“If I think you’re about to fall,” she said, “I’ll tell you.”

“And if I’m not?”

“I’ll let you walk.”

“Good.”

“Good.”

The path opened toward the pavilion. Campers were already moving between cabins, carrying baskets and rolled practice mats. Silena walked beside them with her hands in the pockets of her sweater. The day was cold enough to make her nose pink.

“What are you doing after breakfast?” Leandros asked.

“Greenhouse is done for now,” Silena said. “There’s the awning.”

“Which one?”

“The one by the hall.”

“You can’t fix a torn awning while the wind is up.”

“We can inspect it.”

“Why not wait for a calm day?”

“Because rain doesn’t wait for a calm day.”

“Has it rained?”

“No.”

“Then there’s no hurry.”

“Clarisse?”

“Don’t ask me. I didn’t make the awning.”

Silena looked at the rolled sky. “It could rain today.”

“It won’t.”

“How do you know?”

“The wind’s wrong.”

“You said the wind was east.”

“It is.”

“And that means no rain?”

“It means the front isn’t here yet.”

“Then how do you know it won’t rain?”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You said it won’t.”

“I said it because it won’t.”

“That’s not a reason.”

“It's a forecast.”

“From your father?”

“From the sky.”

Clarisse looked at him. “You can be wrong about weather.”

“I can be wrong about anything.”

“Then maybe don’t speak like you can’t.”

He glanced at the clouds. “Fine.”

The dining pavilion came into view. The long tables had been wiped down, and someone had arranged cups and plates at each end. A pan of potatoes sat steaming on the serving counter beside a pot of eggs. The smell of toasted bread and butter moved through the cold air. Clarisse’s stomach woke before she did.

She took her usual seat at the Camp Three table. Silena sat on one side. Percy would arrive when he finished checking the fence; she could see him in the distance with a coil of wire in his hand. Leandros chose the bench opposite. He set his hands on the table but did not reach for the food.

“What do you want?” Clarisse asked.

“Breakfast.”

“Take it.”

“Is there a line?”

“There's a serving counter.”

“Do I need to bring my plate back?”

“Yes.”

“Is there a schedule for that too?”

“No.”

“Then how do you know?”

“Because I’ve eaten here.”

“Everyone does it differently.”

“Not really.”

He stood, took a plate and served himself potatoes, eggs and a slice of bread. He picked up the honey jar and looked around for a smaller spoon.

“It's on the left,” Silena said.

“I can see that.”

“Then you know where it is.”

He looked at her. She smiled into her tea. “That’s what she said to me about the stone.”

“I didn't tell her to stop.”

“No. You asked me to.”

Leandros returned to his seat. “You keep score.”

“Only when it’s funny.”

“Is it?”

“Sometimes.”

He ate without speaking for a while. The potatoes were soft inside and browned at the edges. The eggs had chives in them. Clarisse had been hungry enough that she took a second slice of bread. She put a little honey on it and ate standing, leaning against the table, while the pavilion filled around them.

The day had not started badly. It had started with a new person who expected rules to prove he did not belong, and a sister who believed the rules could be a kind of welcome if no one used them as a weapon. Clarisse did not know yet which of them was right.

---

After breakfast, Chiron called the senior campers to the green outside the Big House. Clarisse wiped her hands on her trousers and took the list from her pocket. The torn awning needed a look, but the work could wait until the breeze eased. The campers were gathering around Chiron to hear the morning assignments, and she did not want to miss the chance to ask whether the fence post needed replacing.

Leandros walked beside her, though he had not said he was coming.

“You’re following me,” she said.

“I’m walking to the Big House.”

“Same direction.”

“Then we’re both going there.”

She glanced at him. “You don’t have to stick with me.”

“I’m not.”

“Okay.”

He looked at her as if she had insulted him. Clarisse decided not to add anything.

Chiron had a slate in one hand and a stack of names in the other. He spoke first about the fence, then the evening archery lesson, then a shipment of blankets expected before the next cold night. Clarisse waited until the instructions ended.

“The post is still shifting,” she said. “Do you want a full replacement or just a deeper brace?”

“I’ll look at it this afternoon. Don’t move the rail yet.”

“Fine.”

“Silena finished the soil banking?”

“She did.”

“Good. The awning can wait until the wind drops. I’ll ask Beckendorf to look at the seams with you.”

Clarisse nodded. She had expected as much. “Leandros needs an assignment.”

“I am standing here,” Leandros said.

“I know. Chiron can hear you.”

Chiron lowered the slate. “Leandros, you can choose whether to join the beginner’s weapon orientation this morning. If you do, I’ll ask Clarisse to help with the first shield drill.”

“No,” Leandros said.

Clarisse didn’t react. “You have a reason?”

“I don’t take orders from girls.”

There it was again, said in front of the open air as if making it public might make it a principle. A few campers looked over. Chiron did not raise his voice.

“Then you may choose a different instructor,” he said.

“Who?”

“Anyone available.”

“Is there a boy who knows the drill?”

“Several.”

“Then I’ll ask one of them.”

Clarisse’s fingers closed around the folded list. She wanted to say he could ask, but he would still need to learn the basics. She wanted to tell him that being an expert at one kind of thing did not make him an expert at everything else. She wanted to put the entire last day back in his mouth until he had to chew it. Instead she kept her voice flat.

“Fine.”

He looked at her. “You don’t care?”

“I don’t care who teaches you.”

“You care enough to ask.”

“I asked Chiron because he controls the camp schedule. You can choose your instructor.”

“So you don’t mind if I don’t listen to you.”

“I mind if you ignore the safety rules. I don’t mind if you don’t take lessons from me.”

He turned away from the group. “I can handle a shield.”

“Then you don’t need a lesson.”

“Good.”

Chiron’s eyes moved between them. “No one is required to be friends today. Everyone is required to keep weapons pointed away from one another and to ask before taking equipment. Leandros, if you choose to practice, you will follow the instructor’s safety instructions.”

“I can do that.”

“Then choose.”

Leandros paused. “I’ll ask someone else.”

Chiron nodded. He did not try to persuade him. Clarisse watched the boy walk toward the equipment rack where a tall, sandy-haired camper was sorting wooden shields. He spoke to him, and the camper nodded. Clarisse felt the faint satisfaction of a boundary being respected, then realized she had no idea whether Leandros had chosen the better option or simply a different one.

“Clarisse,” Chiron said.

“What?”

“You do not have to take responsibility for his welcome.”

“I’m not.”

“You are still carrying his choice as though it says something about you.”

“I’m not.”

“Then put the list away.”

She looked down. The folded paper had creased beneath her fingers.

“It’s not about me,” she said.

“Then why are you holding it so tightly?”

She loosened her grip. The paper kept the crease.

“Because he’s being rude.”

“That is about him.”

“What do you want me to do?”

“Nothing at this moment.”

“Nothing?”

“He has chosen an instructor. Let him practice.”

“He said he wouldn’t take orders from girls.”

“And you told him that choice was his.”

“He still said it.”

“Yes.”

“So?”

“So you have not been asked to fix it.”

Chiron’s voice stayed calm. It did not make the answer less irritating. Clarisse rolled the list into a tube and slipped it into her pocket. “I have work.”

“Then do your work.”

She crossed the field toward the temporary weapons rack. Leandros had picked up a shield from the lower row. The other camper was showing him the straps. He watched and copied the motion exactly, tightening the leather over his forearm. He looked less like a boy who had never held a shield and more like someone annoyed he had to learn the way the camp did it.

The practice group moved to the marked ring. Clarisse stood near the edge, but did not take the role of instructor. She could see Leandros’s shoulders braced too high and his left foot set behind the right. He was looking at the other boy’s shield instead of keeping his eyes on his partner.

The instructor demonstrated a block. Leandros watched the movement once and then copied it. His shield came up late; the wooden sword tapped his elbow.

“Lower your wrist,” the instructor said.

“I know.”

“Then do it.”

Leandros reset his grip. Clarisse turned away before he could notice her watching.

She went to the armor shed and made a list of the pieces that needed checking. A strap had begun to fray. The back of one helmet was loose at the hinge. One of the older boys had returned a shield with a new crack in the rim. She marked each item and gave the list to Beckendorf, who said he could look at the damaged rim after lunch. They discussed the awning, then the braces for the fence. All ordinary things. None of them required a god.

A shout from the ring pulled her back. Leandros had struck the other boy’s shield too hard, more a swing than a tap. The boy stumbled a step and lowered his sword.

“Stop,” the instructor said.

“I barely touched him.”

“You hit him harder than the drill called for.”

“He’s fine.”

“He’s telling you to stop.”

Leandros’s face had turned red. “I stopped.”

Clarisse walked toward them, but stayed outside the ring. The boy who was instructing him looked at her.

“Did I ask you?” Leandros said.

“No.”

“Then don’t come over here.”

“I’m standing outside the ring.”

“You’re still coming over.”

“I'm checking whether the drill stopped.”

“I’m fine,” the other camper said. He rubbed his forearm. There was a red mark but no broken skin. “It was a bad block.”

Clarisse looked at Leandros’s stance. “You overreached.”

“Don’t.”

“You did.”

“I said don’t.”

“Then stop swinging like that.”

The instructor lifted his hand between them. “We’re done for now.”

“I can continue.”

“I said we’re done.”

“You’re not my instructor.”

“I am, for this drill.”

Leandros yanked the shield strap loose. “I didn’t need this.”

The camp had gone quiet enough that Clarisse could hear the leather buckle scrape. The other camper stepped back from the ring and flexed his arm. He was younger than Leandros by a few years, but he did not ask for help.

“You hit too hard,” Clarisse said.

“I know what I did.”

“Then say it.”

“I hit him too hard.”

“Good.”

“Are you satisfied?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because you want the lesson to be done.”

“I'm not going to apologize every time somebody gets a mark.”

“Did he ask you to?”

The camper shook his head. “No. I just want a different partner.”

“Then give him one,” Clarisse said.

The instructor nodded and led the younger boy to the far side of the field. Leandros pulled the shield from his arm. His hands shook slightly as he threaded the strap back through the buckle.

“You don’t have to do that here,” Clarisse said.

“Do what?”

“Make every mistake into a contest.”

“It wasn’t a mistake.”

“Then you have nothing to learn.”

“I know how to use a shield.”

“Not that one.”

“I’ve used one before.”

“Then you can tell Chiron who taught you.”

He tossed the shield onto the rack. “I’m not giving you an interview.”

“I didn't ask.”

“You keep asking me questions.”

“I asked Chiron about the schedule. I told you what you did. You can talk to the instructor if you want to learn.”

“I don't want to learn from him.”

“Then don’t.”

“You keep saying that.”

“Because you keep acting like someone is forcing you.”

He stepped closer. He was taller than her but not by enough to matter. “You don’t know anything about me.”

“No.”

“Then stop acting like you do.”

Clarisse looked at his face. He was angry, but he was not looking at her. His gaze had landed on the red mark under the other boy’s sleeve. Clarisse had seen people respond to shame by getting louder. She had done it herself. That did not make the other boy’s arm stop hurting.

“I’m not going to teach you if you don’t want me to,” she said. “If you change your mind, ask.”

“You won't?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because I’m not going to corner you into learning from me.”

He looked up at her. “You just told me I need to learn.”

“I think you do. You get to decide whether you do it.”

“Even if it means I get hurt?”

“Especially then.”

He dropped the strap. “That’s not what Chiron said.”

“Chiron said you had to follow safety instructions if you practiced. You ignored the drill. He stopped it.”

“I didn’t ignore it.”

“You hit too hard.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you still arguing?”

He looked away again. “I don’t want everyone to think I’m useless.”

Clarisse had not expected that. She did not let her face show it.

“You’re not useless,” she said.

“You don’t know that.”

“I know you read the lake.”

“That’s not enough.”

“No one said it was.”

“You all keep telling me to wait.”

“Because you’ve been here one day.”

“I’m not asking to be the captain.”

“You said there should be one leader.”

“I was talking about camp.”

“Same answer.”

He wiped his palms against his pants. “If I don’t know what I’m doing, then I need to do something fast.”

“That’s not the same as doing it well.”

“I know that.”

“Then slow down.”

“You say that like it’s easy.”

“No.” Clarisse glanced at the field. “I say it like it works.”

He turned the shield over in his hand. “Are you always this good at it?”

“At what?”

“Being patient.”

“I’m not.”

“Could have fooled me.”

“I’m patient with things that need it.”

“And people?”

“Sometimes.”

“Not me?”

“You haven’t given me much to be patient with.”

He looked at the shield. “Fair.”

Clarisse was not ready to trust him, but the answer did not make her angry. It was the first thing he had said all morning that did not try to place one of them above the other.

“Do you want me to show you the stance?” she asked.

“Would you?”

“I asked you first.”

He studied her face. “Yes.”

“Then ask for the shield again.”

“What?”

“You threw it.”

He looked at the rack. “Can I use that one?”

“Ask the instructor. He owns the training equipment.”

The boy who had been teaching him was still in the ring. “You can have the smaller one,” he said. “If you’re going to keep your elbow up.”

Leandros glanced at Clarisse. She did not answer for him.

“Can you show me the stance?” he asked.

“I can show you,” Clarisse said. “I’m not going to do your whole lesson for you.”

“I didn't ask you to.”

“Good.”

He adjusted the strap around his forearm and took his place just outside the ring. Clarisse stepped beside him, leaving enough space between their shoulders. She pointed at his feet.

“Your left foot forward. Not as far as before.”

“I had it back.”

“You had it behind you. There’s a difference.”

“You’re sure?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“So you can take the force without twisting your knee.”

He changed the position. His first movement was too stiff; his second came easier.

“Keep your eyes on the person, not the shield,” she said. “If they move, your shield moves.”

“That seems obvious.”

“Then do it.”

He looked at her. For once he did not argue about the instruction. Clarisse stepped into the ring and held up a practice sword. “I’ll tap the shield. You block. Don’t swing at me.”

“I know.”

“Tell me you understand.”

“I understand.”

She tapped once. He raised the shield late, but his elbow stayed down. The contact made a hollow, careful thud.

“Again.”

He was faster the second time. The third time, his arm dipped.

“Again,” she said.

“Do you ever say anything else?”

“Mostly.”

“About what?”

“Food.”

“Then I’ll ask Silena.”

“Good.”

She tapped the rim of the shield. He blocked. A small smile appeared at the corner of his mouth, gone almost before she saw it.

“You got it,” she said.

“Once.”

“Once is better than never.”

“Can we stop?”

“Yes.”

He lowered the shield. Clarisse pointed to the water table. “Put it back when you’re done.”

He carried it there without protest. It was not an apology, nor a promise. It was one action taken because he had been told what to do and had decided the instruction made sense. Clarisse counted that as enough for a Tuesday morning.

---

Clarisse was going to the hall after lunch to check the torn awning. Silena had offered to come with her, and Beckendorf was supposed to look at the seam. They were not fixing it that day. The wind had to drop before anyone climbed a ladder, and Chiron wanted to see the fence post first.

Leandros was outside the Big House when Clarisse crossed the path with the folded canvas sample. He had changed into a different sweater and pushed the sleeves to his elbows. The compass he had described yesterday was still not in his hand.

“Are you going to the hall?” he asked.

“To inspect the awning.”

“Can I come?”

“Do you want to?”

“I'm asking.”

“Then yes.”

“You said I could choose.”

“You are choosing.”

“I thought maybe you’d say why.”

“Why?”

He shrugged. “I’m trying to see how things work.”

“Then you should look.”

They walked with Silena and Beckendorf. The workshop stood near the end of the path, with a stack of folded tarps under one side. The awning stretched from the wall above the door to two posts in the ground. It was canvas, once blue, now faded toward grey. The tear ran along the lower seam in a ragged line; it was not large, but the edge was starting to fray. Without it, rain would run straight down the doorway and wet the floor inside.

Beckendorf brought a wooden ladder and set it on the packed dirt. “No one climbs until I check the feet.”

“Wasn’t planning to,” Clarisse said.

“Good.”

Leandros leaned back to see the upper corner. “The tear is where the wind catches.”

“Yes,” Beckendorf said.

“It'll split wider.”

“That's why we're looking at it.”

“You could just double the seam.”

“We could.”

“Or use a sail-stitch.”

“Maybe.”

Leandros looked at him. “You make boats?”

“Sometimes.”

“Then you know what I'm talking about.”

“I do. I still need to see what the canvas looks like.”

Beckendorf tested the ladder. He pressed both hands against the side rails, then climbed three steps and came down. “It’s steady. I’ll get the measuring strip.”

Silena took the far edge of the canvas and held it away from the post. The wind shifted and puffed the fabric against her hands. “Can you take the other side?”

She asked Clarisse. Leandros was closer.

“I can,” he said.

Silena released the edge. “Thanks.”

Leandros caught it before it hit the ground. He reached to pull it taut and the cloth snapped in the breeze. His fingers tightened.

“Don’t pull yet,” Beckendorf said. “There’s a worn tie underneath. If it breaks while you have the tension, it’ll jerk the seam.”

Leandros let go a little. “I know.”

“You couldn't have known. I just told you.”

“I meant I know to be careful.”

“Then do that.”

Clarisse watched him shift his grip so the cloth had room to move. He did not pull again.

Beckendorf pointed to the ladder. “I’m going up to look at the top seam. Clarisse, can you hold the base? Silena, keep the fabric out of the path. Leandros, watch the loose tie. If it comes apart, call it before anyone pulls.”

“Why am I watching it?”

“Because you saw the catch point.”

“I saw the tear.”

“Then watch the tear.”

The ladder did not creak as Beckendorf climbed. Clarisse braced the left rail with one hand. The wooden feet settled into the earth and held. Silena stood with the canvas gathered carefully in both hands. Leandros stayed several feet back, watching the seam. His eyes followed the movement of the cloth as the wind crossed the courtyard.

“It's going to pull left,” he said.

“Which left?” Clarisse asked.

“From where we're standing.”

“That is not specific.”

He lifted his chin. “The edge facing the lake.”

“You can say that.”

“I just did.”

“After I asked.”

He did not answer. Beckendorf reached the top and leaned closer to the torn seam. “The eyelet is bent, but not cracked. The cloth itself is thin in two places. I don’t want to sew it under tension.”

“Then what?” Clarisse said.

“We take it down tomorrow, flatten it, measure a patch, and use a backing strip. We'll replace the tie while we're at it.”

“Can we keep the doorway covered tonight?”

“Temporary tarp across the inside.”

“I can do that.”

“Ask Rhys for the large one. He has it in the tool room.”

Clarisse made a note in the margin of her list. Leandros was still watching the edge.

“Hold it a little higher,” he said to Silena.

She looked at him. “I’m holding it.”

“It will fold into the tear.”

“Then I’ll move it.”

“You should hold it higher.”

“Don’t tell me what to do.”

“I’m trying to keep the fabric from getting caught.”

“You can ask.”

“You were standing there.”

“And you were looking at the fabric.”

“I’m not going to ask for every little thing.”

“Then don’t ask me for help.”

The wind snapped the cloth again. Clarisse reached for the edge, but Silena had already moved it. The fabric lay flat now, tension eased.

“That's better,” Beckendorf said from the ladder.

“I didn’t ask for a review,” Silena replied.

“Wasn't a review. It’s what we needed.”

Leandros looked from her to Beckendorf. “I was trying to help.”

“I know,” Silena said. “You can help without ordering me around.”

He let the canvas go. “Fine.”

Beckendorf came down with the strip. “We’re done for today. The repair plan is simple: take it down, patch it flat, replace the tie, inspect the grommets. No one climbs on that ladder until it’s patched.”

“Could we put the temporary tarp inside?” Clarisse asked.

“Yes, after I check the hooks. I don’t want a wet canvas flap hitting someone in the doorway.”

“I can get Rhys.”

“Ask him, don’t just take the tarp.”

“I know.”

Leandros waited until Beckendorf had put the ladder away. “That knot under the awning was wrong.”

“Which one?” Clarisse said.

“The old tie.”

“I know.”

“It was pulled against the eyelet. That’s why it frayed.”

“It was loose too.”

“You could tie it through the lower ring.”

“Maybe. Beckendorf is checking the hardware.”

“Why do you need him to?”

“Because he knows the hardware.”

“I know knots.”

“Then tell him what you saw.”

“I already did.”

“Tell him without making it sound like he doesn’t know what he’s doing.”

“I didn’t say he didn’t.”

“You asked why he needs to inspect it.”

“Because I can see the tear.”

“You can see one part of it.”

He looked at her. “You don’t want me to be right.”

“I want you to stop needing to prove it.”

“You’ve known me one day.”

“And you’ve been proving it since yesterday.”

“Maybe people are going to think I’m useless if I don’t.”

“Then let them learn something else about you.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means one good knot doesn’t make you the cabin leader.”

“I never asked to be the leader.”

“You keep asking who makes the decisions.”

“Because no one tells me.”

“Chiron tells you. I tell you what I know. Silena tells you what she knows. You can ask.”

“Maybe people here are bad at explaining.”

“Maybe.”

He waited. “You think that too?”

“I think we should explain things. I also think you're not listening when the answer isn't what you want.”

He rubbed his palm over the rough seam of his sleeve. “That sounds like something a leader would say.”

“Good thing I’m not one.”

“You said you set the tasks.”

“I know the list. That doesn’t make it a throne.”

“Then what does?”

Clarisse folded the canvas sample and handed it to Beckendorf. “People listening to you because they trust your judgment. And they keep listening because your judgment holds up.”

“Do you trust yours?”

“Sometimes.”

“What about when you’re wrong?”

“Then I stop being wrong.”

“You make it sound easy.”

“It isn’t.”

He looked at the tear in the awning. “I could have fixed that.”

“You could have told us what knot you thought might work.”

“That’s not the same.”

“No. It isn’t.”

The conversation ended there because Beckendorf called Clarisse to help carry the ladder. She took one end and he took the other. Leandros reached for the second rail, but Beckendorf shook his head.

“Not today. I have it.”

“I can carry it.”

“I know. It’s already balanced.”

Leandros stepped back. Clarisse watched his mouth tighten. She did not tell him to relax. She had been told, and she knew how little it helped.

## Dinner

By the time Clarisse reached Cabin Three, the light had gone thin and blue along the path. She could still feel the rough edge of the awning canvas in her palm. Beckendorf had written down two measurements and the make of the eyelets, then said he would bring the right bracket after the wind eased. The awning remained torn. A strip of canvas snapped once overhead when the gusts found it.

Leandros walked beside her with his hands empty. The ladder had gone back to the tool shed with Beckendorf. Clarisse had expected him to say that he could have carried it. He said nothing until she stopped outside the cabin.

“Silena is making dinner?”

“She started before I came back.”

“I can help.”

“You can ask her.”

He gave her an annoyed look. “I am asking you.”

“That’s not how this works.”

He opened his mouth, shut it, and tried again. “Can I help Silena with dinner?”

“Better.”

“You could have just said yes.”

“I could have. You could have asked the person doing it.”

Silena was at the broad counter inside, cutting carrots into uneven coins. A pot of barley broth rested on the stove. Percy had arranged plates and spoons on the cabin table, counting under his breath. Annabeth sat at one end with the repair list, making small marks beside the things that had been inspected but not fixed.

Silena glanced between them. “Annabeth, this is Leandros. Leandros, Annabeth. She’s from Cabin One.”

“I heard,” Annabeth said. “But this is the first time we’ve met.”

Leandros gave her a brief nod. “Leandros.”

“Annabeth.”

Neither of them added a title or a claim. Annabeth returned to the list. Leandros washed his hands.

Silena looked up when they came in. “Dinner’s almost ready. We can use another pair of hands.”

“I can make flat cakes,” Leandros said. “My mother taught me.”

“What kind?”

“Skillet cakes. Flour, water, a little salt. If there’s oil.”

“There is,” Silena said. “Would you like to make them?”

He glanced at Clarisse, as though waiting for the hidden clause. “Yes.”

“Then wash your hands and I’ll get you the bowl.”

Clarisse took the repair list from Annabeth. “The awning needs a bracket. Beckendorf checked the eyelets. He’ll bring the right one tomorrow.”

“I can make one,” Leandros said.

“Beckendorf is making one.”

“I know metal.”

“He checked the hardware.”

“You don’t have to keep repeating that.”

“I will if you keep trying to make a decision after we already made it.”

His jaw shifted. “I said I could.”

“And I said it’s handled.”

Annabeth’s pencil paused over the paper. Percy looked as if he might say something, then glanced at Annabeth. She kept her eyes on the list, but the corner of her mouth moved. Percy passed her the stub of chalk she had been reaching for. She took it, tapped the table once in thanks, and returned to the marks.

Clarisse folded the list and set it aside. “Go wash.”

Leandros did. He rolled his sleeves and scrubbed his hands longer than the task needed. Silena gave him a bowl, then pointed to the jar of flour and the cup measure. He took the measure without asking what it held.

“Is that the one for the broth?” he asked.

“It’s for flour.”

“It’s a cup.”

“It’s also the one I use for broth.”

He set it down. “Which one do you want?”

Silena nodded toward the tin beside the stove. “That one. It has a chipped handle.”

He picked it up carefully, tested its balance, and set it by the bowl. He measured flour, then added water in a thin stream. The first mix went stiff almost at once.

“Too much flour,” Silena said.

“The water’s right.”

“Maybe. The flour’s packed down in the jar.”

“I measured.”

“You measured a cup. You didn’t fluff it first.”

He scraped the dough into the bowl, watched the dry crumbs cling to its edge, and looked at the jar. “So you want me to put it back?”

“No,” Silena said. “I want you to see what happened.”

He made a noise in his throat, but reached for the water. This time he added a little at a time, mixing with his fingers. The dough softened. Silena kept slicing carrots.

Clarisse sat down opposite Annabeth. “How many chores did you put down?”

“Four inspected, one rescheduled,” Annabeth said. “The north step held in the dry check, but it hasn’t had frost. The stool is still waiting for glue. The fence is still braced. The rail needs a second screw. And the awning needs a bracket.”

“Greenhouse?”

“Silena finished the soil bank yesterday.”

“Good.”

“Do you want me to write ‘done for now’ or ‘done’?”

“Done for now.”

Annabeth changed the mark. “That’s what I thought.”

Percy set down the bread basket. “I can take the stool tomorrow.”

“You can help me with it,” Annabeth said. “I know where the glue is.”

“I didn’t say you didn’t.”

“I know. I’m telling you I want to do it.”

He nodded. “Okay. You lead, I hold the pieces.”

“That’s better.”

The exchange settled without ceremony. Percy moved the basket to the center. Annabeth slid the repair list beneath the salt jar so the corners would stop lifting. Clarisse watched Leandros glance between them. It seemed to irritate him that the two could disagree without either having to win.

He patted a piece of dough flat. “This is what you eat on a boat when it’s too rough to cook.”

“Is it meant to be this thick?” Silena asked.

“No.”

“Then it’s too thick.”

He looked at the dough in his palm. “I know what I’m doing.”

“I’m saying that one’s thick.”

“Why does everybody here tell me the obvious like I can’t see it?”

“Because you keep telling us before we ask,” Clarisse said.

He rolled his eyes and set the cake on the skillet. “I can see it.”

The oil hissed. The cake browned too quickly along one edge. Silena turned the heat down.

“You’re supposed to turn it when the middle starts to bubble,” he said.

“Not with this pan.”

“Why not?”

“Because this stove runs hot on the left.”

He looked at the flame, then at the pan. “That seems like something you should have told me.”

“You didn’t ask about the stove.”

“Then tell me now.”

“I just did.”

Clarisse bit the inside of her cheek. Silena didn’t need her to step in. She was doing fine.

Leandros lifted the cake. Its underside was brown in a ring and raw in the center. He stared at it, then set it back down.

“You have to lower it more,” Silena said.

“I heard you.”

“Then do it.”

He turned the knob. For a few seconds, he stood with his fingers hooked on the handle. The anger did not leave his face, but he did not turn the flame back up.

The cake finished slowly. Silena slid it onto a plate and cut into the center. The inside was cooked. Leandros watched her break off a piece.

“Not bad,” she said.

“Not good.”

“Not bad.”

“I can make a better one.”

“Then make another.”

He glanced at Clarisse again. Clarisse held still. If he wanted to know whether she would challenge him, he could ask. She would not do it for him.

He made the second cake thinner. It cooked evenly, though one edge cracked when he flipped it. Silena tasted a corner. “That one’s good.”

Leandros let himself smile for a fraction of a second. “I know.”

“Don’t ruin it,” Clarisse said.

“I wasn’t going to.”

The table filled. Clarisse took the place she usually did, near the cabin door. Silena sat across from her, Annabeth beside Silena, Percy at the far end. Leandros hesitated before choosing the open seat between Percy and Annabeth. He placed the cakes in the center instead of holding the plate in front of himself.

For a while, the only sounds were bowls moving and the wind brushing the outside wall. Silena asked Annabeth whether the stool was the one with the split leg or the loose rung. Annabeth described the joint, and Percy volunteered to hold it while she checked the frame. Clarisse told Percy not to carry it by the broken side. He told her he remembered. She reminded him that last time he had said the same thing. He gave up the argument and moved his hand.

Leandros broke one of the flat cakes in half. “How long have you three been here?”

“Different amounts of time,” Silena said.

“That doesn’t answer.”

“It does if the answer isn’t one number.”

“Were you together before camp?”

Clarisse lifted her spoon. “Why?”

“I’m trying to understand.”

“Then listen to the answer.”

“Silena?”

Silena set her spoon down. “We weren’t given one childhood because we share a father.”

“I didn’t say you were.”

“You asked how long we’d been together.”

“Because you live in the same cabin.”

“That’s where we live now.”

He considered that. “So Percy grew up with you?”

“No,” Clarisse said.

Percy stopped moving his spoon. He didn’t look at her. Annabeth watched the bowl in front of her.

Leandros turned toward Percy. “You grew up in Atlantis, didn’t you?”

“Yes.”

“With your father?”

“With a household that had a lot of people in it.”

“Brothers and sisters.”

“Some of them.”

“So you had a family.”

“Yes,” Percy said. “I had people who took care of me.”

Leandros looked at Clarisse. “And you didn’t?”

“That is not the question you get to ask me like you’re comparing numbers.”

“I’m not comparing.”

“You are.”

“Then what’s the point of your answer?”

“That Poseidon gave Percy one kind of childhood and gave us other ones. It doesn’t make our lives the same because he is our father.”

“You make it sound like he picked favorites.”

“He did.”

The room went quiet. Clarisse felt the words land harder than she had intended, though she didn’t take them back. Silena’s face changed by a small degree. Percy had gone still again, the spoon resting against the lip of his bowl.

Leandros leaned away from the table. “You think that’s what he did?”

“I know what happened to me.”

“You know what happened to you. You don’t know what happened to me.”

“Correct.”

“You don’t know what he was like with my mother.”

“No.”

“You don’t know how long he was gone.”

“No.”

“You don’t know if she waited—”

“I know you came here with a bag and a cut on your hand. I know she wanted you safe. You told Percy that. I’m not guessing about her.”

His eyes narrowed. “You think I don’t know that?”

“I think you keep acting like you’re the only person at the table who lost something.”

Silena’s spoon made a soft click against the bowl. “Clarisse.”

“No,” Leandros said. “Let her say it.”

Clarisse set her own spoon down. “Fine. I don’t know what your mother gave up for you. I don’t know whether Poseidon made promises or kept them. I don’t know whether you were alone. I’m not going to pretend I do. But you don’t get to use the fact that you had your mother to say our lives must have felt complete. And I won’t use the fact that we didn’t all grow up in one place to say you have nothing to grieve.”

Nobody moved for a moment.

Then Leandros said, “I didn’t say you were complete.”

“You said we had each other like that settled it.”

“I said you had each other.”

“That’s not the same as understanding what that meant.”

He stared at the cracked cake on his plate. “Did it mean nothing?”

“No,” Silena said. “It meant something. It still does. But it didn’t make the rest of it fair.”

“What rest?”

“Our father,” Silena said. “The times he showed up. The times he didn’t. The things he gave some of us and not the others. You want to know what it meant to grow up as his child? You can ask us. You don’t get to decide the answer before we finish.”

Leandros lowered his eyes. It could have been shame, or anger, or simply the effort of not interrupting. Clarisse couldn’t tell.

Percy broke the pause by passing Annabeth the basket. “You want the last piece?”

She looked at the two remaining halves. “You want one?”

“I’m asking you first.”

She picked one and set the other on his plate. “Then yes.”

He smiled, and she brushed a crumb from his sleeve with her thumb. The gesture was so practiced it barely looked like anything. Clarisse noticed it because Leandros did. He watched the two of them, then looked away before anyone could catch him staring.

Silena pushed the cake plate toward him. “You made enough for everyone.”

“I know.”

“Then eat some.”

He took the last intact one. The cracked one remained in the center until Clarisse reached for it. She broke it in two and set half in front of Silena.

Silena raised an eyebrow. “You’re giving me the bad one?”

“You like the crisp edges.”

“I do.”

Leandros watched her take it. He seemed to be trying to learn some rule from the exchange, but Clarisse didn’t know how to explain that one. It belonged to them because it had happened many times, and because Silena could tell her no without needing to soften it.

They finished the meal without returning to Poseidon. The awning was still torn. The fence remained braced. Annabeth still needed glue. Nobody asked Leandros to lead the cabin. Nobody told him he did not belong there.

After the bowls were stacked, he carried his own to the washbasin. Silena asked him to leave the skillet until it cooled. He set it on the trivet. When he reached for the washcloth, Clarisse almost corrected his grip. Then she saw him stop and look toward Silena.

“Is this one clean?”

“Yes,” Silena said.

He used it.

## After the Dishes

Clarisse rinsed the pot while Silena wiped the counter. Percy and Annabeth carried the repair list to the far side of the table, arguing quietly about whether the stool needed a second clamp or a better brace. They had stopped arguing about whether Annabeth should be the one to decide. The conversation had turned to which tool would work.

Leandros dried the skillet and hung it on the peg. He checked the handle twice to be sure it was settled. When he reached for the clean cloth, he looked at Silena first.

“Can I use this?”

“Yes.”

He wiped the water from the counter, then folded the cloth instead of leaving it in a heap. Clarisse watched him do it, then caught herself watching. An hour earlier she would have taken the cloth from him and shown him where it belonged. That would have been faster. It would not have taught him anything.

He noticed her attention. “What?”

“Nothing.”

“That’s not true.”

“It’s not important.”

“You keep looking at me like I’ve got a test to pass.”

“You keep acting like you’re taking one.”

“I don’t like being watched.”

“Then stop checking to see if I’m watching.”

His mouth tightened. “You think everything I do is because of you.”

“No. I think some of it is because you want to know whether I’ll tell you no.”

“Would you?”

“Yes.”

“Even if I’m right?”

“If you’re right, I can still tell you no.”

“That makes no sense.”

“It makes perfect sense.”

Silena set the cloth basket on its shelf. “We’re done in here.”

Clarisse wiped her hands and followed her toward the sleeping area. Leandros carried the last cup to the basin. He did not follow them immediately. Percy said something low to Annabeth, and she answered with a laugh that started in her chest, not the guarded little breath she used when someone else might overhear. Clarisse caught Percy’s hand resting near Annabeth’s wrist. They had a way of finding each other in a room without interrupting anything else.

She turned before she could make the observation into a warning.

Silena had gone to check the window latch. “The wind’s up.”

“It was up all afternoon.”

“It’s going to get worse.”

“Beckendorf has the measurements.”

“Good.”

“You’re not taking the awning down yourself.”

“I wasn’t planning to.”

“Good.”

Silena looked at her. “You don’t have to stand guard over every task.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You were standing right here.”

Clarisse pulled at the cuff of her sleeve. “You said the wind was up.”

“I did.”

“I heard you.”

“I know.”

Silena’s face softened. She came and bumped her shoulder against Clarisse’s. “I’ve got it.”

Clarisse wanted to say that she knew. Instead she nodded once.

Leandros came over when Percy and Annabeth had moved to their bunks. He stopped near the doorway rather than choosing one of the stools. The old cot was still set out, blanket folded across its foot. His duffel lay under it, where he had left it the night before.

“Do I need to ask before I take the cot again?”

Clarisse glanced at the bag. “It’s open.”

“That wasn’t the question.”

“No. You can sleep there.”

“Was that your decision?”

“Chiron said you could stay here tonight.”

“He said tonight?”

“He said you could choose the cabin. You chose this one.”

“And you chose the cot?”

“I made sure it was clear.”

“So if I chose another place—”

“You’d ask whoever was using it.”

“I’m asking you.”

“Then yes. You can sleep on the cot.”

He looked past her to the bunks. “There are more empty places.”

“There are, but the ones that are empty aren’t automatically yours.”

“Because?”

“Because people put things in them. Because somebody might be coming back. Because asking is how you find out.”

He shifted his weight. “You give a lot of rules.”

“I know.”

“Do you always follow them?”

“No.”

“Then why should I?”

“Because they’re there to keep people from having to guess what you’re doing. And when I don’t follow one, I still have to answer for it.”

“Who makes you answer?”

“The people I live with.”

“Silena?”

“Silena. Percy. Annabeth if she’s here. Chiron. Whoever I’ve stepped on.”

“You let everyone tell you what to do?”

Clarisse felt the impulse rise before she could decide whether to use it. A sharp answer. A shove. A demonstration of how quickly she could make him take a step backward.

She planted both feet on the floor. “No.”

“Then what makes these rules different from orders?”

“Sometimes nothing.”

He waited.

“Sometimes I’m using a rule because I’m the one who knows what needs doing. Sometimes I’m using it because I don’t want to admit I’m scared the job will fall apart if I stop watching. Sometimes I’m wrong.”

“That doesn’t sound like a leader.”

“I didn’t say it did.”

“You act like one.”

“I act like someone who knows where the tools are.”

“You decide who carries the ladder.”

“Beckendorf decided. He said it was balanced.”

“You told him to.”

“No. I didn’t.”

“You didn’t argue.”

“He was carrying it. He knew the balance.”

Leandros turned his eyes to the empty cabin table. “You always let other people decide?”

“No.”

“Then why was this different?”

“Because I’m not going to pretend I know more about a ladder than Beckendorf.”

He looked down at his hands. The cut on his knuckle had a thin red line across it. “I said I could carry it.”

“I heard you.”

“He said not today.”

“He did.”

“And you didn’t tell him to let me.”

“Did you want me to?”

“I don’t know.”

“That’s not a reason for me to decide.”

His thumb moved over the cut. “You could have told me it was okay.”

“He already did.”

“Not for me.”

“He said he had it.”

“And I’m new.”

“Yes.”

“And you think that means I should wait around until someone decides what I can do.”

“I think if someone says they have a ladder balanced, you don’t take it out of their hands to prove you’re useful.”

“I wasn’t going to take it out of his hands.”

“You reached for it.”

“I could help.”

“Then ask next time.”

He looked up. “You keep saying that.”

“Because you keep wanting to skip it.”

He held her gaze. For a moment, he looked younger than sixteen, though Clarisse knew better than to decide what that meant. His shoulders were still stiff, his chin still lifted. The hurt in his expression didn’t undo the way he had spoken to her that morning.

“I shouldn’t have said I don’t take orders from girls,” he said. “I said it because I didn’t like you telling me no.”

Clarisse did not move. “That’s what you did.”

“I know.”

“You also said it like being a boy made you the one who should decide.”

“I know.”

“You don’t get to be done with it because you said you know.”

“I didn’t say I was.”

“Good.”

He picked at the edge of the bandage on his knuckle. “You want me to say I’m sorry.”

“I want you to know what you’re sorry for.”

“I just told you.”

“You told me what you did. That’s not the same as changing it.”

“Do you want me to say it again?”

“No.”

“What do you want, then?”

Clarisse breathed out through her nose. It was the question she had wanted to hear from him all day. Now that it had come, she had no easy answer.

“I want you to stop deciding that a boundary is a challenge. I want you to ask before you take over somebody’s work. And I want you to listen when someone says no without making them defend their right to say it.”

He was quiet.

“That doesn’t mean I’ll trust you tomorrow,” she said. “It doesn’t mean Silena does. It doesn’t mean you owe us a performance.”

“Then what do I do?”

“Whatever you think is right. And when you hurt someone, you own the part you did. You don’t get to demand a particular answer after.”

“I wasn’t going to demand.”

“You’ve been demanding all day.”

“I’ve been asking.”

“You ask like the answer is supposed to be yes.”

His head turned, then tilted toward one shoulder. “Maybe I don’t know how to ask here.”

“Then pay attention.”

“Do you always have to make it sound like a punishment?”

“No.”

“Could you not?”

Clarisse considered the question. She could tell him that his tone was the reason. She could tell him that he had made it hard to be patient. Both things were true. Neither would change the way her voice had sounded to him.

“I can try,” she said. “But I’m not promising I’ll make it easy.”

He looked surprised by that, and then suspicious, as if an offer of effort might be another trap.

“I’ll try too,” he said. “I don’t know if I’ll get it right.”

“That’s not a pass.”

“I know.”

“Tomorrow, if you want to learn the shield drill, you can ask. If you don’t, don’t. Beckendorf might show you something different.”

“I already did the drill.”

“You did one stance.”

“I know.”

“If you want more, ask.”

“You’ll show me?”

“If I have time.”

He gave her an irritated look. “You could just say yes.”

“I could. I’m not saying yes yet.”

For the first time since he had come into the cabin, he laughed. It was brief and rough, more breath than sound. “At least you’re consistent.”

“Not always.”

“Sometimes?”

“Sometimes.”

Silena crossed the room carrying a small tin of salve. She set it on the shelf by the doorway. “Leandros, that cut needs a clean bandage before bed.”

“I did it already.”

“You did it before dinner. You used the skillet.”

He looked at his hand. The bandage had come loose at the edge. “It’s fine.”

“Do you want me to get another?”

“No.”

“All right.”

She did not reach for his arm. She left the tin where he could take it and went to the bunk she shared with Clarisse.

Leandros watched her go. “She does that a lot?”

“What?”

“Not make you do something.”

“She does what she wants.”

“She seems to know when to stop.”

“Usually.”

He took the salve tin. “Does she know when to start?”

Clarisse thought of the way Silena had walked beside her at the hall, the way she noticed who was being left out, the way she could say no without making a show of it. “Usually.”

He turned the tin over. “I’ll fix my hand.”

“Good.”

He went to the washbasin. Clarisse heard the water run and the cabinet door open. When he returned, he had cleaned the cut and tied a new strip of cloth around his knuckle. He sat on the cot and pulled the blanket over his legs, but left the duffel zipped.

“What?” he said, catching Clarisse looking again.

“Nothing.”

“You do that a lot too.”

“Maybe.”

He ran his thumb over the bandage. “I’m not saying I’m staying forever.”

“Didn’t ask.”

“I’m not saying I’m leaving tomorrow either.”

“I didn’t ask that.”

“You don’t care?”

“I care whether you treat the people here like people.”

He looked down at the cot, then nodded once. “That’s fair.”

“It’s not a rule I made.”

“Maybe it should be.”

“Maybe.”

Silena called from the bunks that the lantern needed to be turned down. Clarisse went to the switch. Leandros shifted the blanket over his knees, then moved his bag until it was fully under the cot and out of the walkway.

The awning tugged at its loose canvas again, beyond the cabin walls. The tear stayed open. Tomorrow, Beckendorf would bring the bracket and show them what the eyelets could hold.

Clarisse turned the lantern low. On the table, the repair list lay under the salt jar. Nothing had been crossed out.

That was fine.
