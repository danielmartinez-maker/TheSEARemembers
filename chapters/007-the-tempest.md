## Chapter 7: The Tempest

The night before the storm, the sky was clear enough that Silena refused to let anybody use the weather as an excuse for missing the work roster.

Percy arrived at the stores with a toolbox, his repaired practice shield and a letter from Amphitrite that he had not quite decided how to answer. Silena was already there, counting brass fittings into small cloth bags. A stack of unused filter fabric stood beside the door, tied in rolls with green twine.

"You're early," she said without looking up.

"Don't sound disappointed. I've spent the whole morning proving that I'm capable of carrying a toolbox without using it to destroy anything."

"That's excellent. Put it beside the bench, not on the clean cloth. We're sending that to the irrigation crew and I'd rather not have to explain why there are grease stains on the replacement filter."

Percy moved the toolbox.

The last few days had been filled with ordinary obligations made awkward by the repairs to the Hall. Chiron needed every older camper who knew how to hold a tool or carry a message. Clarisse was organizing the eastern patrol schedule. Silena had taken charge of matching delivered parts against orders, which involved arguing with three counselors who all believed their particular task deserved the entire stockroom.

"What are these for?" Percy asked, examining the little bags.

"Replacement grounding fittings for the Hall. Or at least that's what the work order calls them. The Hephaestus cabin wants to compare them with the damaged ones before installing anything."

"Are you allowed to inspect those? I thought Chiron was keeping the ward work restricted."

"I can count boxes. It's less magical than people seem to think." She checked a tally mark against the invoice. "I'm not connecting anything to the wards. I am making sure the workshop gets twelve brackets instead of nine and doesn't have to send us back into the rain because the delivery was wrong."

He helped sort the fittings by size. Some were smooth, with cleanly drilled holes. Others had numbers stamped along the underside. The bags were labeled for different columns, and he noticed that the eastern section needed more replacements than the west.

"Was that where the drakon came through?"

"Close. The damage is worst there, but some brackets were already marked for replacement before the attack." Silena looked at him. "I know what you're thinking. Please don't turn a list of spare parts into a confession."

"I wasn't."

"You were making the face."

"Apparently everybody knows the face."

"Mostly because you don't hide it." She handed him a pencil. "Write the sizes here. Leave the supplier column blank if we can't read the stamp. I'm not guessing because somebody forgot to put the label where it belongs."

Percy copied the measurements. His writing looked like a badly built fence beside Silena's orderly letters, but she could read it. For once that was enough.

Thalia entered with a coil of weatherproof cord and a folded map under her arm. She stopped when she saw Percy.

"You again."

"Nice to see you too."

"Don't encourage him," Silena said. "He's been useful for nearly half an hour and I'd hate to ruin it."

Thalia put the cord on the bench. "Chiron asked for this in case the canvas needs securing before the next rain. It's a temporary measure, not a ward repair. He's also changed the evening patrol route. Nobody goes near the Hall's eastern colonnade until the replacement fittings have been inspected."

"Annabeth knows?" Percy asked.

"I gave her the updated map. She's helping Zoe compare the old measurements with the new ones, and Chiron knows where they are. You don't need to organize another expedition."

Percy raised both hands in surrender. "I wasn't planning one."

Thalia glanced at the toolbox.

"That's almost more suspicious."

She opened the map, laid it beside Silena's invoice and pointed to the temporary fence on the western path. A storm forecast had come in from a satyr posted near the coast, and the crew wanted to be sure the canvas covering the broken columns wouldn't tear loose and injure anybody.

"Can we move the stores deliveries through this route?" she asked.

Silena traced it with a finger. "Not with the large cart. The turn is too tight. You'd have to take the longer road around the orchard, and the ground near the irrigation beds is still soft."

"Then use handcarts."

"That's what I was going to suggest. But the work team will need more people, and the kitchen staff have already borrowed half the available crew for the winter stores."

Thalia nodded, considering. Percy had seen her argue with people who questioned her decisions. He was surprised by how quickly she accepted a logistical problem when it came from Silena.

"I can spare two from the patrol after morning inspection," Thalia said. "Not before. The eastern markers need checking first."

"That's enough. If the rain comes earlier, we'll postpone the heavy delivery."

"Chiron won't like postponing the repairs."

"He'll like somebody being hurt by a loaded cart even less."

Percy thought of the laundry cart he'd helped rescue a week ago. He had no desire to repeat that experiment in the shadow of an electrically charged Hall.

Silena returned to counting. Thalia lingered beside the map, looking at the columns drawn in pencil.

"Do you think my father knows about these replacements?" she asked.

"His steward's office authorized the equipment," Silena replied. "That's all the invoice proves. If you want to know whether Zeus personally ordered them, you'd have to ask the office."

"I've asked for a meeting. Chiron said we should start with the steward."

"That's sensible."

Thalia looked irritated to hear her own plan described that way.

Percy put down the pencil. "We'd probably get further asking who installed the brackets than asking why Olympus bought them."

"We're doing both," Thalia said. "Nico's checking the records. I'm asking about the steward. Annabeth and Zoe are looking at the plans. Nobody needs you deciding which part matters most."

"I wasn't deciding. I was trying to help."

"Then keep writing the measurements. You're getting grease on the invoice."

He looked down. She was right.

The four of them worked in the stores for another hour. Thalia tested the cord, Silena checked the equipment list and Percy bundled the smaller fittings into the labeled bags. By the end the bench was clear and every missing item had been written in a separate column. The Hall still needed a proper inspection, but nobody would be unable to finish a repair because someone had miscounted the bolts.

On the way out, Silena caught Percy's sleeve.

"Have you written home yet?"

"Not finished."

"Amphitrite asked if you were settling in?"

"She asked about my training, my friends, and whether I needed anything sent from Atlantis. There was a paragraph about a room she wants kept ready for me when I visit. I'm trying to answer without sounding ungrateful or pretending I remember every detail she mentioned."

Silena looked at him for a moment.

"Then tell her the part you do know. You like the people here. You're learning things. You'll tell her if you need something. A letter isn't a memory test, Percy."

He wanted to thank her for saying it that way. Instead he tucked the unfinished page into his pocket so it wouldn't get damp.

Thalia was waiting near the path, rolling up the map.

"Chiron wants an extra pair of hands at the western fence tomorrow. Can you manage that without disappearing into an archive?"

"Depends. Are there any prophecies nailed to the fence?"

"If there are, leave them where you find them."

Silena shook her head. "I'm putting that in the cabin rules."

Percy followed them toward dinner, pleased in spite of himself that he could be part of an argument about tools and schedules without anyone mentioning his title.

Behind the treeline, the repaired columns of the Hall gleamed in the last light. The canvas covering the damaged section lifted once in a breeze and settled. No thunder sounded. The sky remained clear.

There was still time to finish the ordinary work before the weather changed.

## Chapter 7: The Tempest

The storm arrived three days later. It had been clear when Percy went into Tempest Hall for morning practice. By the time he finished his first set of trident drills, rain was hammering the high windows.

Percy put down the weapon. The instructor had stopped the training clock and was telling campers to get away from the pool. There was enough static in the room to raise the hair on his arms.

A boy near the doors pointed outside. "The Hall's doing something."

Percy looked.

A thin blue line was running along the white stone of the Hall of the Gods, following a pattern cut into the columns. The damage from the drakon incursion had been covered with canvas, but the covering was lifting in the wind. The lightning wasn't coming down from the clouds. At least, it wasn't coming down only from them. Some of the charge appeared to be moving between the exposed bronze fixtures.

"Stay here," the instructor told them.

Percy didn't. He went out into the rain with the sound of a second warning following him.

The steps had become slippery in the few minutes since he'd last looked outside. He nearly lost his footing at the bottom, caught the railing and forced himself to slow. The instructor had not told him to stay inside because she liked giving orders. The blue light moving between the Hall's columns meant the damaged ward was doing something unpredictable, and there were enough puddles on the path to make Percy painfully aware of how little he understood about electricity.

He stopped beneath the eaves and looked for the nearest safe approach.

Most of the campers from the western practice yard were moving toward the pavilion in a loose line. A counselor was calling instructions over the rain, directing people away from the damaged colonnade. Behind them, two younger boys were still trying to pull a supply cart out of the mud. One kept turning toward the Hall to see what the blue flashes meant.

"Leave the cart," Percy called. "Chiron will need the path clear, and you can come back for the tools when somebody says it's safe."

The boys hesitated. One of them pointed at the canvas bundles on the cart.

"Those are the replacement coverings. The wind's going to take them."

"Then let the wind have them for now. There are plenty of people who know how to make canvas. Not quite as many who know how to replace a twelve-year-old."

That persuaded the smaller boy. He grabbed his friend's sleeve and pulled him toward the pavilion. Percy watched until they reached the steps. Then he headed for the line of trees that bordered the western side of the Hall, keeping out of the flooded track where the cart had been abandoned.

He could see Chiron now. The centaur was standing beside a temporary barrier with the ends of his bow wrapped in dry cloth. He'd sent one healer toward the assembly point and another counselor to keep campers out of the Hall. The canvas over the damaged wall was lifting and falling in the wind, and every time it slapped the stone the lights beneath it seemed to jump.

"Percy," Chiron called. "You were supposed to remain in Tempest Hall."

"I saw Annabeth coming this way before practice," Percy answered. "She was helping Zoe look at the repair diagrams. Is she out?"

Chiron looked sharply toward the colonnade. "She was with the maintenance team. The workers have withdrawn. I haven't seen her come through the western doors."

Percy tried not to imagine why. The rain was blowing into his eyes, and the water in the gutters was moving far faster than the drainage system could carry it away. He could feel the pressure of it against the paving stones, a disorderly network of streams that wanted to join into one broad sheet.

"Can you send someone to check the southern exit?" he asked.

"I have." Chiron looked toward the main entrance. "Do not touch the bronze. Do not approach anybody who seems to be caught in the ward until I tell you how to move safely."

"I'm not going to grab a lightning bolt."

"This isn't a joke."

Percy nodded. He knew the difference. He'd made the joke because the alternative was standing still while the building flashed around him and remembering a hundred times he'd arrived at a fight just too late.

He went around the outside of the cordon with Chiron, staying on the drier path beneath the western wall. The storm had not stopped anyone from shouting; it had only made it much harder to tell who was shouting from where. Two Hephaestus campers were arguing about whether the repair cable could be isolated from the main ward. A satyr with a bandage on his ear was trying to clear fallen branches from the gate.

Then Percy saw Annabeth through the opening.

She had one hand against the marble column and the other stretched away from it, fingers spread. A younger camper was already running out past the eastern doorway, presumably the person she'd been helping. Annabeth wasn't following.

The line of blue light along the bronze was getting brighter.

"Chiron," Percy said, and pointed.

They moved toward the edge of the mosaic together. Percy didn't go past the line of dry stone. He was close enough to see Annabeth's face now, close enough to know she was frightened and angry in the particular way she became when she couldn't make her body obey a decision.

He heard the ward crackle. It sounded nothing like ordinary thunder.

If he had been older, he might have tried to pull the entire fountain out of the ground or flood the stone until the charge stopped. He was twelve. He had only recently learned that moving water without knowing its route could make a disaster worse.

So he looked at the drains first. The western gutter was full, and a narrow line of runoff had begun crossing the threshold toward the place where Annabeth stood.

He could do something about that. He couldn't make the lightning safe, but he could make sure the wet floor didn't create one more route toward her.

Near the Hall, Chiron was directing campers away from the path. A section of the fence had fallen across the entrance. Annabeth stood inside the open colonnade with one hand braced against a column. Percy saw sparks running over the metal ornamentation beside her fingers.

She was trying to pull away. The charge came with her.

"Annabeth!" he called.

She heard him but couldn't turn. "Don't come closer!"

Percy stopped at the edge of the mosaic. The fountain was leaking beside the western wall, water running over the tiles toward a drain half blocked with fallen leaves. The stone beneath Annabeth was dry. He intended to keep it that way.

"What happened?" he asked.

"I was trying to get everyone out. There was a noise in the column, and I touched it. Then it wouldn't stop."

A flare ran through the bronze and struck the far pillar. Percy flinched. Behind him, Chiron ordered the younger campers back toward the pavilion.

"Don't touch the floor near her," Chiron called.

"I wasn't planning on it!"

Percy pulled the fountain water toward the drain. The first attempt spilled across his shoes; he jerked it back before it could spread over the central tiles. He could work with water, not lightning. That distinction had never felt particularly comforting until this moment.

"Annabeth, look at the bronze fixture above your hand," he said. "It's carrying the charge. Can you make it go there instead of through you?"

"I don't know!"

"Then don't try a big move. Look at the little one you practiced with the leaf. One change at a time."

She shook her head, teeth clenched. A thin white line ran along the ornamentation.

"You're asking me to control it while it's happening," she said.

"I'm asking you to stop pulling against it. It's following you because you're keeping contact."

"Percy, I'm not touching it anymore!"

He looked again. She was right. Her fingers hovered an inch from the column. There was enough charge between them to keep her in place.

Chiron approached from the opposite side, moving deliberately. "The ward is drawing on your powers," he said. "It's a feedback loop. We need to interrupt the circuit."

Percy had never heard Chiron sound so uncertain about a training problem. He watched the fountain's runoff collect in the drain. If the runoff crossed the threshold, it would put another hazard between Annabeth and the exit. He had no reason to believe the water would draw the lightning away safely. The best he could do was keep it from spreading.

"Can I pull the runoff back into the outside gutter?" he asked. "The drain's blocked and it's starting to cross the floor toward her."

"Do that, and keep your hands and feet away from the wet stone," Chiron said. "I'll isolate the damaged coupling. Don't try to control the discharge yourself."

Percy drew the water back, holding it inside the shallow gutter until it ran over a clear grate. It took more effort than it should have. The storm kept supplying another inch of rainwater as fast as he moved it.

Chiron gave the maintenance crew a warning, waited for the all-clear and struck a damaged bronze coupling with an enchanted arrow. The fitting separated from the main strip with a crack of metal. The lights along the columns sputtered and collapsed toward the protected fittings at the far end of the colonnade. The workers had been preparing that isolation since the first alarm.

"Now," Chiron said.

Annabeth stepped backward. Her knees gave way as soon as she cleared the column. Percy caught her by the shoulders on the dry side of the entrance and helped her sit.

The storm didn't vanish. Rain continued against the roof, and the sky was still a dirty gray. But the sharp blue light in the Hall went out.

"Is everyone all right?" Annabeth asked.

Percy looked at the scorch marks on the columns. "We're going to find out."

Chiron was already calling for the camp healers. The instructor from Tempest Hall appeared beside them, breathing hard, and gave Percy the kind of look he usually received after breaking a rule he had definitely heard.

"You left your drill," she said.

"I know."

"We will discuss that later."

"I figured." Percy kept his attention on Annabeth, who was staring at her own hands. No sparks came from them now. That didn't mean she could stop worrying.

He wanted to tell her that everything was fine. It wasn't. The damaged ward had reacted to her, and nobody yet knew whether the damage or her powers had started the problem. Chiron would need to investigate the Hall. Annabeth would need to learn what she could do without the building turning an experiment into an accident. Percy would be spending the next week explaining why he had run toward an electrical discharge in wet sandals.

"You didn't cause all of this by yourself," he said.

She looked up. "You don't know that."

"No. But I know a broken ward when I see one."

"You saw a broken fountain."

"That too." He helped her to her feet. "Come on. Let's get somebody to look at your hands."

---

They went to the infirmary first. The healer checked Annabeth's pulse, examined her fingers, and made her describe exactly where she'd been standing. Percy had a shallow burn on his forearm, no worse than a scrape, but the healer covered it anyway.

Chiron spoke to them privately before he let them leave.

"Until we understand the ward, nobody practices storm work inside the Hall," he said.

Annabeth sat very straight. "I wasn't practicing. I went there because I saw a camper on the path and the column started making noise."

"I believe you," Chiron said. "That is why I want to know what the ward was doing before you touched it."

"Will Olympus have to be told?"

"I'll report the damaged fixture. That's a matter of camp safety. Your training is a separate matter, and I won't discuss it with a council until I understand what happened."

Annabeth stared at him. "You can't guarantee that someone won't ask."

"No," he said. "I can't."

It was a worse answer than reassurance, but it was honest. Percy found himself liking it.

Thalia was waiting outside the infirmary when they emerged. She had a rain-soaked jacket and a training spear in her hand. Whatever she'd intended to say, the sight of the bandage on Annabeth's fingers stopped her.

"How bad?" she asked.

"No permanent injury," Annabeth said. "The ward caught my charge, and Chiron interrupted it."

"Your charge?"

"I tried to pull away. It got worse."

Thalia looked at Percy. "Were you there?"

"After it started."

"And you went inside?"

"As far as the dry side of the entrance."

"You didn't think to wait for Chiron?"

"He was right there. We worked together."

Thalia took a breath through her nose. Percy expected an argument. Instead she turned back to Annabeth.

"You should have told me you were practicing on your own," she said.

"I wasn't practicing in the Hall. I've been working on the small stuff, outside, with Percy."

"That's what I mean."

"I didn't tell you because I didn't want another conversation about whether I'm allowed to make decisions without you."

Thalia's jaw tightened. "That isn't what this is about."

"It is part of what it's about."

Percy looked at the infirmary door. "I could go."

"Please," Annabeth said.

He hesitated. Thalia looked as though she wanted to object, but Annabeth hadn't asked her. Percy left them alone and sat on the steps where he could hear nothing but the rainfall off the roof.

When Annabeth came out ten minutes later, she was carrying a folded dry jacket.

"How'd it go?" he asked.

"We disagreed."

"That narrows it down."

"She thinks I should practice with her. I think she's right about some of it." Annabeth looked back toward the infirmary. "I just hate that she won't ask before she decides for me."

Percy nodded. He wanted to put his arm around her, but she was still looking at the door.

"You can talk to her again tomorrow," he said.

"I probably will." Annabeth gave him a tired half-smile. "You're not going to tell me she meant well, are you?"

"I like living."

She laughed once, then held out her hand. He took it with his unbandaged one, and they walked toward the cabins.

---
The first warning reached Thalia while she was checking the practice field.

Rain was coming down hard enough that the youngest campers had already abandoned their archery lesson. Someone had left the target cloths spread across a bench, and she was trying to keep the canvas from blowing into the muddy path when the horn sounded.

One long note from the Hall.

Movement, not contact. That was what it meant on the border. Inside camp, at a damaged building, the exact meaning could be different. Thalia stood still for a breath, listening.

A second horn followed. Then three short blasts.

Retreat.

She dropped the canvas, grabbed her spear and ran toward the western side of the arena. The younger campers were milling near the path, several calling to friends they couldn't see through the rain. Someone shouted that lightning had struck the Hall. Someone else said a monster was coming. The second claim spread faster than the first because it was the sort of danger the children had been taught to expect.

"Eyes here," Thalia called. "There isn't a confirmed monster. The Hall's ward is malfunctioning. We're moving away from it until Chiron gives us another instruction."

Bea, the Hermes girl who'd helped lead the evacuation drill, came out from beneath the pavilion with her helmet crooked and a younger camper holding her sleeve.

"Do we use the orchard route?" she asked.

Thalia felt a rush of relief that had nothing to do with her own safety.

"Yes. The main path is flooded, and the lower bridge will be slippery. Take the orchard route we marked yesterday. Keep everybody together. Clarisse will meet you at the fence."

Bea nodded and turned back to the others. She repeated the instruction in a carrying voice, more calmly than Thalia would have managed at ten.

The group began moving. Thalia walked alongside the youngest camper, whose boots kept sinking into the mud. The girl was trying not to cry. Every few steps she looked over her shoulder toward the Hall, where blue light flashed beneath the lifted canvas.

"Is it going to explode?" she asked.

"I don't know exactly what's happening, and I won't pretend I do. But Chiron has the repair crew with him, and we're not going to stand close enough to find out the hard way."

The girl frowned at her.

"That's not comforting."

"I know. I'm working on a better version."

The answer made Bea laugh briefly. The smaller girl did not, but she gripped Bea's sleeve a little less tightly.

At the orchard fence, the two pins that Annabeth had marked on their map were still in place. The bent one had been replaced, just as the repair team had promised. Clarisse was already there with two older campers, moving the narrow panel aside and clearing a path through the wet grass.

"Thalia, take the group to the pavilion. I'll make sure nobody's stuck on the main path."

"The Hall's lighting up. I want to check where Annabeth is."

Clarisse looked at her. "You can do that after these kids are inside. I need somebody who knows the route to get them there without another accident."

Thalia's first instinct was to argue. Annabeth had been scheduled to work near the Hall that morning. She might still be there. There was no reason Clarisse should be allowed to run toward danger while Thalia was sent away from it.

Then the youngest camper slipped in the mud and nearly pulled Bea down with her.

Thalia caught the girl by the back of her jacket.

"All right," she said to Clarisse. "I'll take them. Check the Hall as soon as you can, and tell me if you see Annabeth coming out."

Clarisse nodded. She didn't waste time congratulating Thalia for making the sensible choice.

They reached the pavilion as the rain intensified. Thalia counted fourteen campers, then counted again because she didn't trust the first result. Bea checked the names against the practice roster while the younger children collected blankets from the storage bench.

One name was missing.

"Where's Milo?" Bea asked.

Thalia looked at the list. Milo had been in the archery group, a boy who liked to stand at the back because he was convinced his arrows flew better when nobody watched him.

"Was he with us at the first horn?"

Bea thought back. "I saw him near the bow rack. He might've gone to get his quiver."

Thalia was already moving. She left Bea in charge of the head count and sent an older camper to tell Clarisse where she was going. No more running off without making sure somebody knew her route. She could obey that rule herself.

Milo was under the roof of the archery shed, clutching a quiver against his chest. He had tied its strap around the bench leg to stop it blowing away and now couldn't undo the knot with his shaking hands.

Thalia crouched beside him and cut the strap free with her knife.

"The quiver can be repaired. You should've gone with the others."

He stared at her. "I thought if I left it, the arrows would be ruined."

"I get it. But you can't help anybody with good arrows if you get hurt trying to save them. Come on. Bea's counting everybody at the pavilion."

He ran beside her, close enough that she could see him looking toward the Hall with every flash. She kept her spear low and her hand near his shoulder without grabbing him.

At the pavilion the count finally came out right. Thalia took the roster from Bea and drew a line through the missing-name note. Then she looked uphill.

A streak of blue light moved between the Hall's columns. It was too even and too low to be an ordinary strike from the clouds.

The sight made her palms prickle.

She knew the feeling of calling lightning. She knew the instant before a current gathered under her skin, the dangerous pleasure of believing she could direct it. The Hall's charge felt different, structured into a pattern that didn't answer her presence. It was moving through bronze and stone whether she wanted it to or not.

She could not solve this by throwing a bigger bolt at it. She knew enough about broken wards to understand that much.

Clarisse appeared at the edge of the pavilion, soaked through.

"Chiron's at the colonnade. The maintenance crew's isolating a coupling. There's somebody caught near the northern column."

Thalia's breath stopped.

"Annabeth?"

"That's what the messenger said. Percy reached the western entrance with Chiron, and the healers are on their way."

Thalia was moving before Clarisse had finished.

"Wait," Clarisse said, catching her arm. "The path is still blocked. You'll have to go around the west side, and Chiron told everybody to keep away from the wet stone."

"I heard you."

"Then act like it. Don't make him manage a second rescue because you sprinted into the hazard."

Thalia yanked her arm free, then stopped. Clarisse was right, and they both knew it. The fact did not make it easier to stand there.

"Can you keep the group here?" she asked.

"Yes. Go by the dry route. I'll send word if anything changes."

Thalia took the longer path around the sheds, where the rain ran toward the creek instead of the Hall. She reached the western fence in time to see the blue line flicker and vanish. The storm kept beating against the canvas, but the lights beneath it went out.

For a moment nobody moved. Then Chiron shouted to the healers, and Percy emerged near the entrance with Annabeth leaning heavily against him.

Thalia's knees went weak.

She forced herself to walk, not run. The ground was still wet, and she wouldn't give the healers another person to carry.

Annabeth was pale and conscious. Her hands shook. Percy had a red mark along one forearm and looked as though he'd forgotten to breathe.

Thalia could have demanded an explanation from either of them. Instead she turned to the nearest healer.

"Which way are you taking her?"

"The infirmary. Chiron wants her checked for burns and lingering charge."

"Then I'll come with you."

The healer nodded. Thalia walked beside the stretcher, close enough to speak to Annabeth if she needed anything, far enough out of the way that the people trained to help could do their work.

She had spent the morning getting children out of the path of a broken ward. She'd done it well. She was glad, somewhere she didn't have room to feel yet.

At the infirmary door Annabeth reached toward her with one trembling hand.

"I'm all right," she said.

Thalia looked at the fingers, at the little red marks across the knuckles, and managed to answer without shouting.

"Let's get you checked before we decide that."

At breakfast the next morning, Chiron addressed the camp before anyone could turn the incident into a story of divine punishment.

"Yesterday's storm damaged a protective ward at the Hall of the Gods," he said. "The Hall is closed until the work is inspected. Anyone who was near the site should report burns, dizziness, or lingering discomfort to the healers."

Nobody asked why a Zeus child had been seen under the columns. At least, nobody asked aloud.

"Camp training will continue under supervision," Chiron added. "A ward malfunction is not a reason to blame someone who happened to be standing beside it."

Thalia sat with Annabeth at the Zeus table, both of them facing the front. Percy could see that Annabeth was relieved the announcement didn't name her. He could also see that she hadn't stopped turning the small bandage around her finger.

The rest of the announcement was less reassuring. More monsters had been seen along the border. Patrols were doubled. Chiron asked campers to stay in their cabins after curfew unless assigned to a watch.

Afterward Percy caught up with Annabeth near the lake.

"The master bolt," she said before he could speak. "What if it goes missing again?"

"It did before," Percy said. "That doesn't mean it has to happen on the same day, or at all."

"But if it does, we'll recognize the pattern."

"Maybe. We have to make sure we're not seeing patterns just because we expect them." Percy held up his bandaged arm. "I ran toward a thunderstorm yesterday. I'd prefer not to do that every time something reminds us of a quest."

Annabeth glanced at the Hall beyond the trees. "I don't want to give up investigating."

"I'm not asking you to. Chiron said the ward was damaged. Zoe has the repair records. That's something we can check without guessing who rewrote our lives."

"All right." She slipped her notebook out of her jacket. "Then let's start with what we know."

They sat on the dock and drew two columns across the page. One for observations, another for guesses. Annabeth had insisted on it. Percy thought it was a good idea, particularly because most of his guesses were wrong before noon.

---

That night Percy found a folded packing list in the bottom of his trunk. Amphitrite had written it in a neat hand, with two pairs of sandals circled and three ceremonial cloaks decisively crossed out.

He set it on his pillow, smiling despite the long day. She expected letters about friends and sword practice, not reports about a ruined ward or the questions he and Annabeth were asking.

His father's warning about old sacred places returned to him. Poseidon had spoken of strange disturbances, not vanished time, and Percy still had no reason to assume the two were connected. He wanted a connection anyway. It would have made the mystery smaller.

Across the cabin Clarisse was snoring softly. Silena turned a page with the lamp screened beneath her blanket. Percy folded the list again and set it in his trunk.

Tomorrow he'd have to help repair the broken practice rack at Tempest Hall, because the instructor hadn't forgotten he'd abandoned his drill. He was twelve years old, and that was an obligation he could keep.

He lay down and tried to sleep while the rain eased outside.
