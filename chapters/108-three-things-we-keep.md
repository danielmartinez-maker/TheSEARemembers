## Chapter 108: Three Things We Keep

Clarisse said the Poseidon cabin needed a rulebook.

Silena said it already had one.

Clarisse asked where it was.

Silena pointed at the sign beside the door: *Don't Track Sand Inside. Don't Touch Clarisse's Spear. If Percy Is Asleep, Wake Him Gently.*

"That's not a rulebook," Clarisse said. "That's a list of things that annoy me."

"Most rules are."

Percy sat on the floor between them, sorting a bucket of polished shells into piles for Silena's jewelry work. "What do you think the cabin needs a rulebook for?"

"Training," Clarisse said. "We should have forms everyone knows. Signals. A way to move as a unit. We all use water differently, and that makes us messy in a fight."

"You want to write down the drills we use together?" Percy asked.

"We already do. We improvise too much." She tossed him a shell. "So we do it properly. I teach you stance and timing. You teach us whatever Atlantean nonsense you learned. Silena teaches us how to patch the cuts afterward."

Silena looked up from her necklace. "I can write down the poultices. The songs too, if you want."

"The song counts?" Percy asked.

"Sea songs. They help you keep time when you're moving through currents."

Clarisse crossed her arms. "I’m not writing down my singing."

"You hum when you think nobody can hear."

"I do not."

"You hummed the entire western patrol last week."

"That was a tactical rhythm."

Percy laughed. Clarisse tossed a shell at his head.

"Fine," she said. "Training now. Dawn. Tempest Hall. Don't be late."

"I won't."

"If you're late, you're running laps."

"Got it."

Silena tied off the necklace and held it up. A small blue shell hung from a braided cord. "This is for you, Percy. You can wear it when we train so we can see you in the pool."

"It looks like a tiny clam."

"It's a cowrie. It means safe passage."

Percy took it carefully. "Thanks."

Clarisse watched them. Her expression was difficult to read, but she didn't make a joke. That was how Percy knew she liked the moment.

Clarisse took the last shell from Percy's sorting pile and turned it over between two fingers. It had a narrow split along one side. She set it aside instead of tossing it back into the bucket.

"So the rulebook starts with three things," Percy said. "Signals, forms, and aftercare?"

"The rulebook starts with what people can remember when they're tired," Clarisse said. "The rest can come later."

Silena wiped the cord dust from her thumb. "What are the three things?"

"One position that keeps you upright. One signal that means stop. One way to check whether the person beside you can keep going." Clarisse hooked the spear strap over her shoulder. "We can teach a move in a morning. We can't assume everybody has the same idea of safe."

Percy looked from her to Silena. "You want us to write those down?"

"After we know they work." Clarisse glanced at the shell in her hand. "If I write a rule because it sounds smart and somebody follows it into trouble, that's on me. We test first."

The blue cowrie rested in Percy's palm, its cord cool against the lines of his hand. Silena had offered it as a marker in the pool, but Clarisse's answer made the purpose broader. It could help them keep track of where he was when everyone moved at once. He slid the cord over his head and tucked the shell against his shirt.

"What if we disagree about what worked?" he asked.

"Then we write down what each of us saw," Silena said. "Not just the person who talks loudest."

Clarisse lifted her eyebrows. "Are you volunteering to keep the record?"

"I'm volunteering to make sure you don't call a shoulder collision a successful formation."

"It was a successful collision. We all stayed standing."

"That is a very low bar."

Percy smiled. He was beginning to see the shape of Clarisse's rulebook: not a list of commands, but a way to keep an argument from becoming an injury. They would try something, say what happened, and change it if the evidence was bad. He knew how quickly a useful idea could turn into a habit people trusted too much. The water had taught him that. The deep places were full of currents that looked smooth until they caught a person sideways.

"We need a word that doesn't sound like a drill," he said. "Something anyone can use."

"Stop," Clarisse said. "That sounds like a word anyone can use."

"Good. If somebody says stop, everyone lets go of the move. No arguing until we're steady."

Silena nodded. "And if someone can't speak?"

Clarisse tapped the butt of her spear once against the tile. The hollow click carried through the room. "Two taps. We can practice it with the spear or the rail."

"What if there's no spear?" Percy asked.

Silena picked up the wooden spoon she had been using to spread shells across the work cloth and knocked it twice against the bowl. "Then anything hard enough to hear. If you can't make a sound, raise a hand."

Clarisse tried it, raising her open palm. "One signal for stop. No secret meaning."

Percy made a note on the scrap paper beside Silena's necklace tools: **Stop: say it. Two taps if you can't. Open hand if you can't tap.** He stared at the line, then added: **Everyone stops.**

"That's more than three things," Clarisse said.

"It's one thing with three ways to do it."

She considered that, then gave a short nod. "Fine. We test it before we call it a rule."

The cowrie touched Percy's collarbone when he moved. He put the scrap in his pocket so he wouldn't lose it among the shells. It was not a charm that could make a bad drill safe. It was just a visible marker and a promise that somebody would pay attention. That was useful enough.

The next round of training began at dawn.

Tempest Hall was cool before sunrise. The upper windows showed only a thin band of gray, and the lanterns made the practice pool look darker than it was. Clarisse had set two padded practice spears on a bench and tied a length of blue cord to the near rail. The cowrie at Percy's chest caught the lamplight when he leaned over the water.

"Before we get in," she said, "we name what we're doing."

Silena took the paper Percy had brought and found a dry patch of bench. "Stance, turn, stop, check."

"And a shallow-water limit," Percy said. "Nobody tries this where they can't put their feet down."

Clarisse looked at the pool. "Today, anyway. We don't know enough to change that."

That sounded like her: not a promise about every practice to come, just an honest limit for this one. Percy stepped down the tiled entry and stopped where the water reached his waist. Clarisse checked the depth along the marked edge. Silena stayed on the steps with one hand near the rail. They had no need to prove a point by going farther in.

Clarisse took one of the padded spears and placed it across her forearms. "The first version is a pivot. I move toward you, you give ground, rotate on the front foot, and let the strike pass. No counter until we know where everybody's standing."

"So the point isn't to hit you," Percy said.

"The point is that neither of us gets hit. We can add the answer later."

She had him demonstrate the movement without water first. He planted his foot too squarely and twisted his knee. Clarisse stopped him with a tap of the spear against his shin.

"That will hurt before anyone touches you. Turn on the ball of your foot. Don't wrench it."

Percy reset. This time he let the sole swivel against the tile. His hips followed; his shoulders came around last. It felt slower than the movement he had used in the water, but the sequence was clear. Clarisse moved the padded spear toward his shoulder at walking speed. He gave ground, pivoted, and let the padded end pass in front of him.

"Again," she said.

He repeated it. On the second attempt his back foot crossed the line of the first, and he had to catch himself with a hand against the wall.

"That's the part I don't like," he said.

"Then tell me why."

"If I turn that far, I don't know where the next person is. I could step into Silena."

Silena looked up from the paper. "You did. I moved back before your heel got me."

Clarisse's gaze went to the space between them. "We need a lane. Nobody follows into the turn until the person ahead has cleared it."

Silena drew a short line on the page, then two smaller marks beside it. "The first person moves here. The next person waits until their shoulder passes the second mark."

"That's a lot of measuring for a drill," Percy said.

"It's a way to know if we actually made room," she replied. "Once we can do it without looking at the floor, we won't need the marks."

Clarisse changed their positions. Percy stood on the first mark, Clarisse waited one pace away, and Silena took the position behind her. Clarisse brought the padded spear forward. Percy gave ground and pivoted. Silena counted aloud, not to rush him but to mark the moment he cleared the lane. Only then did Clarisse step through.

The first run looked clumsy. The second had less hesitation. At the third, Percy felt Clarisse's spear pass where his shoulder had been, heard Silena's count, and knew where Clarisse would be without looking back. The turn had worked once under controlled conditions. That was not proof it would work in a fight; it was proof that their spacing had improved.

"Put that in the notes," Clarisse said.

Silena wrote: **Pivot: one step back, turn on front foot, clear the marked lane before the next person enters. Slow-speed practice only.**

"Now the slide," Percy said.

Clarisse set the padded spear down. "You think it will be safer."

"I think it might leave my feet under me. But if I move sideways, I might stay in the next person's path."

"That's why we try it."

She stood at the same mark and moved the padded spear toward him again. This time Percy shifted sideways rather than turning. His lead foot slid; his other foot followed without crossing. The movement kept his chest facing Clarisse, and he could see the whole lane. But he moved less distance. The padded tip brushed his sleeve.

"You got touched," Clarisse said.

"I saw it coming."

"The move is supposed to help you avoid it."

"It did keep me from turning my back."

Silena studied the marks. "And he stayed inside his lane. The pivot moved him farther, but he needed more space. The slide kept him where he could see us, but it didn't clear the line."

Clarisse took the spear and measured the gap with it. "So neither one is better by itself."

"We could use the slide when there's someone behind us and the pivot when there's open space," Percy said.

"Maybe," Clarisse replied. "But if the decision takes longer than the strike, it isn't a useful rule."

They practiced the two movements at half speed. Clarisse called either "open" or "crowded" just before each pass. Percy tried the pivot when she called open and the slide when she called crowded. When he chose wrong, she stopped the drill and asked where the lane was. More than once he had to admit that he had been reacting to her spear rather than reading the bodies around him.

The water made the comparison stranger. A current nudged him after every turn, and the tiled floor offered less friction than dry ground. Percy demonstrated the pivot in the pool first. He kept his knees loose and let the mild current carry the angle of his hips. The water helped him rotate, but it also continued moving after he stopped. His shoulder drifted past the point where he meant to finish.

Clarisse held up her hand. He froze in the middle of the turn.

"Don't correct it with a stronger push," she said. "You can't make the water forget it was moving."

"I can feel that."

"Then show me how you stop."

He tried again, this time turning less. He used his palm against the water to slow the rotation, not to reverse it. The difference was small, but Clarisse noticed. She stood outside the marked lane and watched his feet rather than his hands.

"That one ended where you meant it to," she said.

"The current was weaker on that side."

"Then that's part of the note."

Silena came to the edge and dipped her fingers in, feeling the pull. "We can't just write 'pivot works.' We have to say what the water was doing."

She added the condition to the page. Percy appreciated that she did not treat his ability as a substitute for describing the test. He could shape water, but the people moving through it still needed a shared account of what they had tried.

Clarisse stepped into the pool. She did not imitate his motion exactly. She let the current touch her hip and moved her foot a fraction before the turn, keeping her center low. Her first attempt was stiff. On the second, she stayed balanced. On the third, Percy saw her find the small delay between the water pushing and her body answering it.

"There," he said.

"Don't sound surprised."

"I wasn't."

"You were."

Silena hid a smile behind the notes. "The second attempt was steadier. The third used less effort."

Clarisse turned her head. "Write down both."

They tested the slide next. Percy used a brief, shallow current across the pool, enough to be felt but not enough to knock anyone off balance. Clarisse shifted sideways. The water caught her trailing ankle and made it lag. She stopped immediately, raised one palm, and Percy eased the current to stillness.

No one moved until she was stable.

"That was the signal," Silena said.

"And the signal worked," Clarisse replied. She checked her ankle, flexing it once. No pain crossed her face, but she did not hurry to continue. "We don't do that again until we know why it caught."

Percy looked at the water, then at the angle of her foot. "I pushed across your path instead of with it. The slide crossed the current."

"So the move depends on the direction of the water," Clarisse said.

"And on whether we can see the current. If it's cloudy or churned up, we shouldn't assume we know."

Silena wrote another note, then underlined it: **Stop on unexpected pull. Recheck the water before restarting.**

Clarisse stepped to the stairs and tested her ankle again before leaving the pool. She had not been hurt, but Percy noticed she let the pause stand instead of making a joke of it. That made the stop signal more real than any line on the paper. It had altered the session; now the next decision had to account for it.

"We can continue on the tiles," she said. "No current until we've sorted the angle."

"Agreed," Percy said.

She nodded once, as if the agreement mattered because he had said it without trying to persuade her back into the pool. Silena crossed out the planned second water comparison and wrote **resume only after direction is understood**. The test was not a failure. It had found a condition that their first plan had missed.

They spent the next stretch on the dry floor. Percy and Clarisse took turns moving; Silena watched the lane and called the moment it was clear. The slide gave them a better view of the people around them. The pivot gave them distance when the path was open. In either version, the person behind waited. The body ahead did not have to guess whether the others were following.

By the time the windows had brightened, Percy had a damp shirt clinging to his back and a faint ache in the muscles beside his knee. He sat on the bench and rolled the joint carefully. It felt tired, not injured. Clarisse sat at the opposite end and inspected the padded spear for a loose seam.

"What goes in the rulebook?" Silena asked.

Clarisse looked at the notes. "That we haven't got one rule. We've got a test and two moves with different limits."

"That's less tidy," Percy said.

"It is more useful."

Silena held out the paper. Clarisse took it, read each line, and handed it back. "Keep it. We'll add what happens when more than three people are moving."

"And how to stop," Percy said.

"We already have that."

"We know what the stop signal is. We still need to see if everybody hears it when the pool's noisy."

Clarisse's mouth tilted. "Then that's the next test."

Percy looked at the cowrie. It had not made him safer by being magical; it had made him visible, and the visibility had helped Clarisse watch his position when they moved. The three useful things, he thought, might be the stance, the stop, and the check afterward. But they would have to earn their place in the notes one morning at a time.

Clarisse started by checking their stances. She had Percy and Silena stand on the tile floor of Tempest Hall, feet shoulder-width apart, knees loose, arms ready. She walked around them, correcting their posture with sharp taps of her spear butt.

"You're leaning."

"I am not," Percy said.

She tapped his shoulder. He wobbled.

"You are."

Silena tried to mimic Clarisse and nearly fell over. Clarisse caught her by the elbow before she hit the floor.

"See? This is why we practice."

"I thought I was doing well."

"You were. Then you tried to copy me instead of standing in a way that works for you." Clarisse helped her settle again. "Your balance is different. Use it."

Silena adjusted her stance. This time she stood steady.

Clarisse nodded. "Good."

Percy watched them. The tenderness in Clarisse's hands was brief, almost hidden, but it was there. She was fierce because she cared. She was careful because she knew what happened when people got hurt.

Then Clarisse turned to Percy. "You. Stop thinking about Atlantis and look at me."

"I was looking at you."

"You were thinking at me. It's different."

He tried again.

Clarisse showed them a basic pivot. She had learned it from a counselor in the Ares cabin years ago, but she'd adapted it for a trident: one step to invite the strike, a turn to let it pass, then a counter from the side.

Percy knew a similar move, though the Atlantean version used currents instead of footwork. He demonstrated it slowly, keeping his weight low and his hands relaxed.

Clarisse frowned. "That won't work on land."

"I know. But it works in the pool."

"Show me."

They moved to the water. Percy waded in first, then held out his hand to Clarisse. She took it and stepped down. Silena followed, clutching the safety rail.

The current in the pool was mild, enough to move their clothes and stir their hair. Percy showed them how to stop fighting it, how to feel the direction and use it to turn rather than resist.

Clarisse learned fast. Silena learned carefully. When she slipped, Clarisse caught her. When Percy over-rotated, Silena steadied him.

By the end of the morning, they had managed a clumsy three-person turn that left them bumping shoulders and laughing in the shallow water.

"Again," Clarisse said.

"We got it," Silena protested.

"We got it once. Do it again."

They did.

Silena’s review came after breakfast. She brought a basket of herbs to the infirmary and showed her siblings how to clean a cut, bind a sprain, and make a poultice from yarrow and crushed kelp.

"The salt helps reduce swelling," she explained, mixing the paste in a wooden bowl. "But not too much, or it stings."

Clarisse held out her hand. There was a shallow scrape across her palm from the practice trident.

Silena cleaned it. Clarisse tried not to wince.

"You can say it hurts," Silena said.

"It doesn't."

"You're squeezing your eyes shut."

"The sun's bright."

They were inside.

Percy laughed, and Clarisse flicked a drop of water at him. Silena smoothed the poultice over the scrape and wrapped it with a clean strip of cloth.

"There. You'll live."

"Good. I had plans."

Silena handed Percy the bowl. "Your turn."

"I don't have a cut."

"You have that bruise on your elbow."

"That's nothing."

"Then this won't hurt."

It stung. Percy didn't say so.

Silena gave him a look. "You and Clarisse are going to be impossible together."

"We already are," Clarisse said. "We're practicing."

Silena set the bowl down and looked at the list Percy had brought from Tempest Hall. "Before we write down a treatment, I want to know what you think counts as an injury we can handle here."

"A scrape," Percy said.

"A scrape with what kind of bleeding?"

He looked at Clarisse. She was turning her bandaged hand over, checking the wrap. "Not much."

"That's not a measurement," Silena said. "What would make you stop and get help?"

Clarisse frowned at her palm. "If I couldn't make the bleeding slow down. If I couldn't move my hand. If I got dizzy."

"Good. And if I think something's wrong, I say so even if the person says they're fine. But I don't grab them or take over unless there's immediate danger. I ask first when there's time."

Percy thought of the stop signal in the pool. It had seemed simple until the current caught Clarisse's ankle and the whole drill had changed around it. "Same with the treatment? We can stop if it hurts or feels wrong."

"Exactly. This is not a test of how much pain you can ignore." Silena lifted the clean cloth. "Clarisse, can I check the skin around the scrape before I put anything else on it?"

Clarisse held out her hand. "Yes."

Silena examined the shallow cut, the edges, and the skin around it. She rinsed it with clean water, then checked again for grit. Clarisse watched her work and kept her fingers relaxed. When Silena asked if the water temperature was all right, she said it was. When Silena asked her to flex each finger, she did.

"The first thing we do is look," Silena said. "Then clean with safe water. Then decide whether a simple covering is enough. If it's deep, if something is stuck in it, if there is numbness, if the person feels faint, or if it keeps bleeding, we get someone who knows more than we do."

"Who?" Percy asked.

"The infirmary. A senior healer. Whoever is responsible for the camp's medical care. We don't pretend a poultice turns us into healers."

She spread a thin layer of the prepared paste across the scrape, then wrapped the palm loosely enough that Clarisse could still bend her fingers. Silena slipped one fingertip beneath the wrap to check the tension.

"Too tight?"

"No."

"Tell me if it changes. Don't wait because you think I'm busy."

Clarisse made a face. "I know how a bandage works."

"Then tell me why you tried to hide the scrape."

"I didn't hide it."

"You put your hand behind your back when I asked what happened."

Clarisse looked at Percy. "I was busy."

"You can be busy and tell us the truth," Silena said. Her voice stayed kind, but she did not let Clarisse change the subject. "If we are writing this down, the person with the injury gets to tell us what they feel. The rest of us can notice things, but we don't replace their answer."

Percy looked at his elbow. The bruise was small and already fading at the edge. "My turn, then. The bruise doesn't hurt."

"I didn't ask you to treat it," Silena said. "I asked you to show me the range of motion."

He bent his arm, extended it, and turned his wrist. Nothing caught. Silena watched, then asked him to press gently around the sorest place. He did. There was tenderness, but no sharp pain.

"That's not an emergency," she said. "But if the pain grows, if the joint swells, or if you can't move normally, you tell somebody and get it checked. And if you hit your head, you don't decide alone that you are fine."

"I haven't hit my head."

"I know. It's still part of the rule."

She wrote a second page for the training notes, separating the steps into **ask, look, clean, cover, check**. Under that she wrote the reasons to stop and ask for a healer. She left room for the camp's medic to correct anything beyond her knowledge.

"Why write it if we don't know everything?" Clarisse asked.

"So we know where our knowledge ends."

Clarisse glanced at the page again. "That part can go in the rulebook."

Silena set out a folded strip of cloth and asked Percy to practice wrapping it around a rolled towel. She didn't hand him Clarisse's real bandage. He placed the first loop too close to the end and the cloth slid off.

"Try anchoring it here," Silena said, pointing rather than taking it from him.

He restarted. The second wrap held, but left no space to check the skin. Silena had him loosen it, then practice again. Clarisse watched, offering a quick correction when he crossed the cloth over the imaginary joint. She was careful to explain what the wrap should allow: bend, but not twist; support, but not trap.

"You have done this before," Percy said.

"I have been hurt before. That's not the same as knowing how to treat everything."

Silena smiled. "Exactly. Experience helps you notice. It doesn't make you the only person who gets to decide."

They practiced the stop signal too. Silena played the injured camper, Percy played the person applying a wrap, and Clarisse stood across the room making noise by tapping a practice spear against the tile. On the first try, Silena said "stop" and Percy heard her. On the second, Clarisse's tapping covered the word. Silena raised her hand. Percy paused, but he had to look up to see it.

"What if you're facing away?" Clarisse asked.

They tried the two-tap signal on the wooden bench. The sound was clear, but the rhythm disappeared under Clarisse's spear. Percy did not notice until Silena called out. They tried again with the hand signal and a shoulder touch, but that meant Silena had to be close enough for contact, which she might not want.

"No touching without asking," Silena said. "Even if we're practicing."

"Then the stop word needs to be loud enough," Percy said. "And if a person can't speak, the others have to watch for the raised hand."

Clarisse lowered the spear. "We can't promise we'll catch it every time."

"We can promise to make room for it," Silena replied. "Nobody keeps going because they think the person is slowing us down."

Clarisse accepted that with a quiet nod. Percy wrote it beneath the earlier stop signal: **If you don't hear it, check. Stop before restarting.** The notes had shifted from describing a move to describing how they would treat each other while trying it. That was not an extra thing Silena had added to the lesson. It was part of whether the lesson could be trusted.

When she packed the herbs away, she kept the first-aid page separate from the training steps. "This belongs in the rulebook only as a boundary," she said. "The camp infirmary can give us the actual medical guidance."

Clarisse tucked her bandaged hand close to her chest. "And my cut?"

"Keep it clean and let me know if anything changes."

"I will."

Silena held her gaze a moment longer. Clarisse didn't roll her eyes or tease her. She simply nodded, and then took the herbs basket to the shelf herself.

That afternoon, Annabeth and Thalia found them in Tempest Hall.

"Are you training without us?" Annabeth asked.

"We started in the morning," Clarisse said. "You were asleep."

"That was on purpose."

Thalia picked up a practice spear. "What's the lesson?"

"Water movement," Percy said. "Clarisse's adapting the ground forms. Silena is teaching first aid."

Annabeth's eyes lit up. "Can we try?"

"You're a Zeus kid," Clarisse said. "You can try anything. Just don't electrocute the pool."

"I can control it. Mostly."

"That is not reassuring."

Thalia stepped into the pool first. She let the water rise to her waist and felt the current with her hands. She followed it more quickly than Percy expected. The sky and sea were not the same; this was practice and attention, not power over water. She watched the ripples and let Percy show her where the pull changed.

Annabeth was less comfortable. She stayed near the steps, watching the others try the turn.

Percy came to stand beside her. "You don't have to do it."

"I want to. I just don't want to lose control."

"Then start small. You can keep one hand on the rail."

She nodded and stepped in. The water lapped at her knees. She held the rail with one hand and reached out with the other. A faint crackle of static moved across her fingers.

"Easy," Thalia said. "Don't push it. Let the current move first."

Annabeth breathed in. The sparks faded.

She felt the current, or the pressure, or whatever her body interpreted as water. Percy guided her through the turn with a hand at her elbow. She stumbled once, laughed, and tried again.

The second time, she moved with them.

Clarisse gave an approving nod. "Good."

"Before we make that a group move, we need to decide where everyone stands," Annabeth said. She stepped back onto the dry tiles and pointed toward the three marks Silena had drawn on the paper. "Those lanes were made for three people. We have five now, and one person needs to be able to see the whole formation."

Clarisse considered the pool. "Nobody stands behind the anchor. Nobody enters the center unless the person ahead has cleared it."

"That still leaves two people crossing," Thalia said. She had been watching the current from the shallow end, one hand hovering over the water without moving it. "Silena's path and mine intersect here."

She pointed to the bend where the floor marks would meet. Silena shifted her position to see it. From the edge, the line looked clean. From inside the pool, two people moving at once would come within an arm's length.

"I can go wide," Silena said.

"Or I can wait until you clear," Thalia answered. "The current won't stop just because we want the lanes to."

Percy liked that she had noticed the difference. Thalia could read a moving surface quickly; she could not command it. She watched the ripples as they spread from the inlet, tracking the change in the water's direction with her eyes. When Percy asked whether she wanted him to adjust the flow, she shook her head.

"Keep it as it is. I want to know what the current does to the line we planned."

Annabeth remained on the steps, one hand on the rail. Her other hand rested at her side. The static in her fingers had faded, but she had not yet decided whether she wanted to repeat the signal. Percy could see her studying the setup: Clarisse's spear, Silena's position, the rail, and the small dry strip between the pool and the bench.

"If I stand here, I can signal the shift without being in the water," she said. "But then somebody has to call it out for me. If you only see a flash and mistake it for an accident, you could stop late."

"We don't use the signal until we've named it," Clarisse said. "What's it look like?"

Annabeth raised her hand and let a small thread of static crackle once across her fingertips. The spark stayed close to her skin. She dropped her hand immediately.

"One brief pulse means begin the next movement," she said. "Not stop. The open hand is stop. If I accidentally make more than that, we stop and reset."

"You can also use your voice," Percy said. "Then the pulse confirms it."

"That gives the group two cues to check."

"And if the water gets loud?" Thalia asked.

Annabeth looked at Silena's raised palm, then at Clarisse's spear. "Then we don't depend on the pulse. We use the stop word and the hand. If no one sees or hears the cue, the person leading should not start."

Clarisse made a small mark on the edge of Silena's note. "We test the signal from the dry side first. No current. Everybody facing away, then we see whether they catch it."

Annabeth didn't step from the stair. She looked at Percy. "Can we keep the pool still while I try?"

"Yes." Percy let the inlet current settle, then waited until the surface was smooth. "I'll tell you if it changes."

She nodded. "I'll stay here."

They arranged themselves so Clarisse and Silena stood with their backs half-turned, Thalia watched from the opposite side, and Percy faced the open lane. Annabeth called, "Ready," and let the static pulse once. It was faint, more light than sound. Percy saw it. Clarisse saw the reflection on the water and turned. Silena looked up because Clarisse had moved. Thalia caught the pulse itself.

"I saw it," Thalia said. "I don't know what it means yet."

"That's the problem," Annabeth replied. "A signal isn't clear just because it is visible. We need to agree what it means before we use it."

They repeated the test with the pulse designated as a shift cue, and Annabeth said the word "turn" at the same time. This time everyone moved only as far as the first marked point, then stopped. Percy watched for the current to shift. It didn't.

On the next attempt, Clarisse was looking toward the inlet and didn't see the pulse. She heard the call but asked Annabeth to repeat it before moving. That delayed the turn by a few seconds, but it kept the signal from being mistaken for a command.

"That counts as working," Silena said. "She checked instead of guessing."

"It also means we need a voice cue," Annabeth said. "If the signal is hidden, no one should infer it."

Clarisse nodded. "Then we have a caller. One person says the shift word. Annabeth can confirm it with the pulse, but the pulse doesn't replace the word."

"And I can call from here," Annabeth said, "without needing to stand in the current."

"Only if you want to," Percy said.

She gave him a look that was half affection and half warning. "I said I want to test it. I don't need you to keep asking me to withdraw."

He took the correction. "Okay. You decide when you're done."

That settled it. Annabeth kept her place on the stair; Thalia moved to the far side, where she could watch both lanes. Clarisse and Percy stood in the center as anchors. Silena took the outer route. Their first small group run was awkward. The water spread their spacing in ways the dry floor had not. Percy held the current low, but it still drew Silena toward the center when she turned.

"Stop," Annabeth called.

Everyone froze. Silena had both feet under her, but one hand had lifted to balance. She lowered it only after the water stilled.

"What happened?" Clarisse asked.

"I followed the turn too soon," Silena said. "I didn't wait for Percy's shoulder to clear the line."

"It looked clear from here," Thalia said. "The ripple hid his arm."

Annabeth stepped down one stair, still outside the current. "Then the signal needs to come after the anchor clears, not before. Otherwise, the cue asks Silena to move before the space exists."

Clarisse drew a short line on the paper. "Caller watches the anchor. The shift word comes only after the lane is open."

They restarted. Percy made the turn slowly and waited in the marked space until Silena could see his shoulder. Annabeth called "turn" and gave the brief pulse. Thalia held her position until Silena passed. This time the group rotated without crossing paths. They stopped at the end rather than trying to add another step.

"That's all for that run," Clarisse said. "We don't repeat because it looked good once. We write down the condition."

Silena recorded the sequence: **Caller confirms the lane is open; spoken cue first, brief static pulse may confirm; one turn only; everyone stops if the cue is unclear.** Beside it she wrote the limitations: **still pool, marked shallow area, Annabeth on dry stair, no lightning, no deep water.**

"It sounds less impressive when you list the limits," Percy said.

"It is more honest," Annabeth replied. "The limits are why it worked."

Percy looked at the list and recognized a useful kind of progress. They hadn't learned a universal formation. They had made one carefully bounded movement possible for five people in a shallow pool, with clear sightlines, low current, and everyone willing to stop. If any condition changed, they had to reassess.

Thalia removed the towel from her shoulders. "I want to try reading the current again from the far mark. Not controlling it—watching whether I can tell when it changes."

"You can use the inlet we already tested," Percy said. "I'll keep it low."

"And I'll stand where I can see the surface, not behind the anchor."

They set that up without the rest of the move. Thalia watched the ripples move from the inlet to the far wall. Percy changed the flow by a small amount, then held still. Thalia raised a finger when she saw the line bend. She was right twice and early once. On the third attempt she waited until the changed pattern reached the tile seam before she called it.

"That's slower," Clarisse said.

"It's also the first point where I'm sure," Thalia replied. "If you want a warning before that, I can say I think it's changing. But I shouldn't call it a fact."

Annabeth added two possible cues to the page: **change suspected** and **change seen at seam**. Percy liked the distinction. In a dangerous situation, there might not be time for careful language. But in practice, it mattered to know whether Thalia was observing or guessing.

"We won't use the suspected cue to change the move," Clarisse decided. "It tells us to watch. The confirmed cue lets us stop and check."

Thalia repeated the test once, then stepped back from the pool. She had not gained a new power. She had defined what her attention could and could not tell them.

Annabeth flexed her fingers. Percy saw the static begin to gather again, so he watched her face. She lowered her hand, let the charge fade, and shook out her wrist.

"That's enough signal testing," she said. "I can still help record."

Clarisse didn't ask her to do more. Silena passed her the pencil and turned the page toward the dry side. Annabeth made one correction to the lane marks, then drew an arrow showing where the caller needed to stand in order to see the anchor and Silena's path at once.

The change looked small on the paper. It altered who would be able to call the move, and it meant the caller couldn't also be moving in the formation. Percy understood why Clarisse wanted the rulebook tested before it became a rule. A neat sentence could hide a bad position.

They practiced in pairs, then in a group. Percy and Clarisse made the anchor. Silena watched the outer lane and called when it was clear. Thalia directed the turn from the far mark, where she could see the water change. Annabeth stayed at the dry stair and used her power to release one small pulse of static after Silena's spoken cue, without calling lightning.

They began with the pool still. Clarisse took the first position and held the padded spear angled down, its tip nowhere near anyone's body. Percy stood beside her, close enough to act as a second anchor but far enough to leave the path open. Silena took the outer lane. Thalia watched the line of ripples, and Annabeth waited with her palm lowered until Silena gave the call.

"Lane clear," Silena said.

Annabeth answered with the brief pulse. The group turned once. Nobody tried to add a counter or a second movement. Clarisse and Percy stopped where they had planned; Silena remained outside the line; Thalia called the completion only after she saw the water settle.

"Good," Clarisse said. "Now change one condition."

"Which one?" Silena asked.

"The current. Nothing else."

Percy restored the same low cross-current that had tugged at Clarisse's ankle. He kept it weaker than before and ran it in the direction Thalia had identified as easier to read. Silena watched the far lane while he moved the water. The surface remained calm enough to see, but the current pressed at their legs.

"Lane clear," she called, then hesitated. "Wait. The edge is moving differently."

Thalia raised a hand, not a stop signal but a warning. "I see the line bending toward the center. I don't know yet if it's going to reach us."

Clarisse did not start. "Can we tell?"

Thalia watched for another breath. The ripple reached the seam and bent back toward the inlet. "Now it's confirmed. The lane will narrow."

Annabeth let her hand fall. No pulse came. Silena lowered the call sheet.

"So we wait," Percy said.

"We wait, then change the current or change the positions," Clarisse replied. "No one moves because they think the call was late."

Percy drew the current down until it ran straight again. They shifted Silena's starting mark farther from the center and checked the lanes from each person's perspective. The new position gave her a clearer path around the anchors, but it put her closer to the rail.

"That might be fine in this pool," Annabeth said. "It won't be fine if the rail is blocked or someone else is standing there."

"Then this layout is specific to this room," Silena said. "We shouldn't copy the drawing without checking the space."

Clarisse looked pleased. "Put that in."

Silena wrote: **Recheck the path at each location; the sketch is not a universal floor plan.**

They tried again at the adjusted marks. Silena called the lane clear, waited for Thalia's confirmed signal, and then watched the water. The current held straight. Annabeth gave the static pulse, and the group turned. It worked, but Percy felt the current press at the side of his knee halfway through. He chose not to push harder against it. He let the move finish at the smaller angle they had agreed on, then called that the turn was complete.

Clarisse looked at him. "You stopped short."

"The water changed the line. I could finish the turn, but then I'd cross into Silena's path."

Silena checked her position. "He stayed in his lane."

"And we got less rotation," Clarisse said.

"Which is safer than getting the full rotation into somebody," Percy replied.

She held his gaze for a moment, then nodded. "Record it as a partial turn. We don't force the angle."

That decision changed what they were trying to achieve. The aim had started as a group movement that could turn the current into a shield. Now the notes described something narrower: a controlled repositioning that kept everyone visible and out of each other's path. The shield comparison was still useful as an image for how their spacing held, but it was not a claim that the current could stop an attack.

Annabeth looked at the page. "We can call it a formation that protects the lane, not a shield. It doesn't protect us from everything."

"Fine by me," Clarisse said. "A name doesn't make us invincible."

They ran the move one more time with no current. This time Percy was tempted to finish the angle even after the caller had gone quiet. He felt the impulse, noticed the gap, and stopped where he was. Silena called the lane clear again. Thalia confirmed the surface was settled. Only then did the group complete the turn.

"That was slower," Clarisse said.

"It was also the first time we used the stop rule before anything went wrong," Percy said.

"Which means we learned something before we had to learn it the harder way," Silena said.

Thalia dried her hand on the towel. "I want the notes to say that I can see the water change from here, but only if the surface is visible. If the pool is churned, I can't tell you what it's doing."

Annabeth added that limitation under Thalia's role. Then she crossed out an earlier phrase that said **Thalia directs the current** and replaced it with **Thalia observes the surface and reports what she can confirm**.

"Good catch," Percy said.

"It matters," she replied. "People hear one verb and assume it means something else."

Clarisse folded the paper at the edge rather than crumpling it. "We're done with water for today. We have one safe run, two partial runs, and a list of reasons it might not work. That's enough."

No one argued. Percy stepped out first and waited on the tile while the others climbed the stairs. Silena checked that the rail was dry before Annabeth used it. Thalia gathered the towels. Clarisse picked up the practice spear and inspected the padded end for a split seam. They moved through the ordinary cleanup without turning it into another drill, but each person knew what needed to be put back before the room could be used by the next campers.

Percy helped drain the shallow test channel back to its starting mark. He did not fill the whole pool or leave a current running. When he let the last ribbon of water go, the ripples moved toward the inlet and disappeared against the tile.

"The rulebook needs a date?" Silena asked, tapping the blank corner of the paper.

"No calendar date," Clarisse said. "Just who tested it and under what conditions."

Silena wrote the names of the five of them and the note: **Tempest Hall, shallow marked area, low current, visible surface. Practice result, not a combat guarantee.** She left the date blank.

Percy looked over her shoulder. It was a small detail, but it kept the page honest. They could add another test when they had one. Until then, the blank space was better than pretending the evidence said more than it did.

The move wasn't perfect, but it worked. Together, they could turn the current into a kind of shield.

"Again," Clarisse said.

They did it again.

When they finally stopped, everyone was wet and tired. Annabeth had a new streak of static in her hair, and Percy had taken another practice bruise on his shoulder.

Thalia tossed him a towel. "You make a good anchor."

"I thought I was a prince."

"You're allowed to be more than one thing."

Percy looked at his sisters. Clarisse's arm was draped across Silena's shoulders. Thalia stood beside Annabeth, still close enough to catch her if she lost balance. The five of them were damp, exhausted, and grinning.

For a moment, it felt like a family.

---

That evening, Clarisse asked Percy to walk with her.

They went to the beach, where she carried two wooden practice spears. She handed him one.

"I want to try the current move on sand," she said. "See if it works without the water."

They practiced under the last light. The move changed on land. It lost its fluidity, but the idea remained: yield, turn, answer. Clarisse corrected his feet. Percy reminded her to keep her shoulder loose. They argued about the best angle and tried the motion again.

The sand made the slide easier until it didn't. At the waterline, the packed ground let Percy shift without sinking. Higher up the beach, the sand gave under his heel and pulled his foot off line. Clarisse called a halt before he could compensate by twisting his knee.

"That spot isn't stable," she said.

"It looks stable."

"It moved. Try it again on the packed strip."

They marked their starting positions with the ends of the wooden spears. Percy used the slide in the firmer sand and kept his balance, but he did not get enough distance to clear Clarisse's line. The pivot moved him farther; when he tried it in the loose patch, his back foot sank and the turn stalled.

"So the surface matters as much as the move," Percy said.

"The person behind you matters too," Clarisse said. "If the slide keeps you in their path, it doesn't help."

They tested the turn with a third mark between them. Clarisse took the lead, Percy followed, and a driftwood branch stood in for the next person. In the pivot, she cleared the branch but needed the whole width of the dry sand. In the slide, she stayed closer to the line but could not pass it. Percy tried to tell her which one to use, but they had changed places and the view from the rear mark was different.

"I couldn't see the soft patch from where you were," he said.

"Exactly. We can't pick a move for somebody who can't see the thing that makes it safe. They need a way to stop and ask."

She moved the driftwood branch out of the way. It was not a battlefield and the branch was not an enemy. Still, it helped Percy see that their practice had been too neat if it assumed the person moving always had the same information as the person watching.

Clarisse crouched and pressed a hand to the sand. "We keep the land version for footwork only. No current, no weapon contact. If the ground is soft, uneven, or crowded, we don't call either move safe."

"And the person who sees the condition calls it," Percy said.

"If they can. Otherwise everybody stops." She set the wooden spear across the driftwood. "That belongs in the notes too."

They took turns being caller and mover. Percy learned that Clarisse's weight shifted before her shoulder did, a useful sign that she intended to pivot. Clarisse learned that Percy sometimes looked at the spear and forgot the ground. When she asked him to describe what he could see from his position, he had to admit he hadn't noticed the soft patch beside him.

"You want to watch the whole group," she said, "but you can't do it if you only watch the danger in front of you."

"That's why we need a caller."

"Or a clear enough space that no one has to guess."

The beach version did not become a second movement. It became a list of conditions to check before practicing: compact footing, open lane, no one behind the turn, and a person who could stop the group if the ground changed. Clarisse wrote those down herself in the sand with the spear tip, then brushed the words away when the tide reached them. Percy had already copied the conditions onto the paper Silena had given him.

Clarisse walked back to the lantern with one spear over her shoulder. Percy carried the notes. Silena had joined the others near the path, but the paper still had a corner left blank for the summary. He held it out to Clarisse.

"You wanted a rulebook," he said. "What would you put at the top?"

She scanned the page. There were the stance, the pivot, the slide, the marked lanes, the stop call, the aftercare limits, the current conditions, and the phrase that the formation was not a combat guarantee. The paper had started as a few reminders. Now it was dense with exceptions.

"Not the moves," Clarisse said. "The first line should say what we do when the plan stops matching what's happening."

"Stop and check?"

"That's good enough to start."

Percy took the pencil and wrote it down: **If the ground, water, spacing, or signal changes, stop and check before continuing.**

Clarisse read it. "Add that anybody can call it."

He added: **Anyone can call stop. No one restarts until the group is steady and knows what changed.**

"That makes two lines," he said.

"Then the rulebook has two lines."

"We were supposed to write down three things."

"We have stance, stop, and check." She tapped each word as she spoke it. "The stance changes by person. The stop signal is the same for everyone. The check is asking what happened before we move again."

Percy looked at the page. It was less like the form he had imagined and more like a record of decisions made after mistakes. That seemed right. They had not proven that the move would work everywhere. They had proven that they could notice when it wasn't working and pause before it got worse.

"What counts as a successful practice?" he asked.

Clarisse rested the spear butt in the sand. "Nobody has to push through a bad position. Everybody knows the lane. The stop signal gets obeyed. We finish standing where we meant to stand."

"And we don't change more than one condition at a time," Percy added.

"Unless something changes on its own. Then we stop."

He wrote the criteria in the margin. The paper now held a useful contradiction: their goal was to move together, and their first rule was that anyone could stop them. Percy liked that the pause belonged to everyone. A younger camper or the least experienced person could call it without needing to be right about the reason. They would discover the reason after they were still.

Clarisse studied the new lines. "I won't put these on a wall yet."

"Because we need another test?"

"Because if we hang it up, someone will think it's finished. It's a draft. We have one morning in the pool and a short practice on sand. That's not enough to teach anyone else."

"We can keep it in the training box."

"Silena can keep the master copy. You can keep this one until she takes it back."

The decision was simple, but it gave the notes an owner and kept a draft from becoming an instruction for the whole cabin. Percy folded the paper carefully and tucked it into his pocket with the cowrie resting against his chest. The necklace had stayed visible during the pool session; on the beach it only marked him as the one carrying the record.

"I'll bring it next time," he said.

"Bring the paper," Clarisse said. "The next time isn't scheduled."

He nodded. They had no reason to promise another dawn when none of them knew how the day after this one would go. The practice had made a small beginning, not a permanent place on the camp calendar.

Finally, Clarisse lowered her spear. "You don't have to be better than me at everything."

"I'm not."

"Good. It would be annoying."

She sat in the sand. Percy sat beside her.

"I used to think having a brother would mean having someone to compete with," Clarisse said. "Someone Dad would compare me to. Someone who'd get what I never did."

"Does it feel like that?"

"Sometimes." She dug the spear point into the sand. "Then I remember you're not the reason he wasn't there. And you're not him. You're just Percy."

"I'm glad."

"You should be. You're annoying, but you're ours."

Percy's throat tightened. "You're mine too."

Clarisse bumped her shoulder against his. "Yeah. I know."

They watched the tide turn. After a while, Silena and the others came down with the lantern and the leftover poultice herbs. Annabeth sat beside Percy, and their knees touched.

Silena set the small wooden figures from the earlier cabin gathering in the sand: ship, panther, sea horse, owl. They made a crooked little circle around the lantern.

Percy turned the figures so they faced the lantern. The ship's keel caught in a small ridge, and it tipped toward the panther.

"The circle is too tight," Annabeth said. She moved the sea horse half a handspan outward, then placed the owl where someone could see the whole arrangement. "If this is the group move, whoever watches needs a clear line to every person."

"That's a lot to ask of an owl," Thalia said.

"It has a good head for it."

Clarisse crouched to examine the figures. "The anchor goes here, and the person circling takes the wide side."

Silena moved the panther farther from the ship. "If the path narrows, we stop instead of closing the gap."

They adjusted the little pieces until no two touched. The formation looked less like a perfect circle than five people who could still see one another. Percy liked that better. Nothing in the practice had required them to become one shape or move at exactly the same speed. They had needed room to pause, a visible path, and enough trust to wait when the cue did not arrive.

"We'll remember the spacing better if we name it," Silena said. "The notes need a short label."

"We should add the new move to our training list," she said. "The one with the current."

"What should we call it?" Thalia asked.

"The Anchor Turn," Annabeth said.

Clarisse tested the phrase. "That works."

Percy looked at the circle of figures, at his sisters and the girl he loved, at Thalia and the people who had become part of the same orbit.

"The Anchor Turn," he repeated.

The name fit.

---

That night, Percy returned to the cabin with Clarisse and Silena. They were still discussing the move, arguing about when the third step should be a pivot and when it should be a slide.

"You need the pivot," Clarisse said.

"The slide is more stable," Silena replied.

"The pivot is faster."

"The slide gives you time to adjust."

Percy listened as he changed into dry clothes. The notes on the table showed the result of the day's tests: the pivot gave more distance in an open lane, the slide kept a mover facing the group but could fail to clear a crowded path, and neither belonged on unstable ground. Clarisse wanted the move to stay quick. Silena wanted the stop condition to be impossible to miss. They weren't arguing over whether the other was right; they were deciding which limit deserved to come first in the written instructions.

When they finally decided to keep both versions in the draft, with the conditions beside each, Clarisse tossed Percy a clean shirt.

"You'll be here?"

"Of course."

"Good." She turned away too quickly. "Someone has to keep count."

Silena smiled at him over Clarisse's shoulder. Percy smiled back.

Silena took the notes from the table and checked that the paper had dried before folding it. "I'll keep the master copy with the first-aid page. This one stays in the training box."

"Why two copies?" Percy asked.

"Because the first one has my corrections and the second has the conditions from the beach. If we combine them, we could lose track of which note came from which test."

Clarisse nodded toward the box beside the shelf. "Put the draft there. No one else uses it until we've shown them what we tried and what we didn't."

Percy set the paper on top of the padded spears, away from the damp towels. The title space remained blank. He had thought the hardest part would be finding a name for the move. Now he understood that the name was the easy part. Anyone could repeat it. The notes were there so the next person would know what the name did not guarantee.

He was not the oldest. He was not their only source of strength. He wasn't the one in charge.

He was the little brother who got handed a dry shirt and expected to bring the notes back when they chose to practice again.

For once, that felt like exactly the right place to be.
