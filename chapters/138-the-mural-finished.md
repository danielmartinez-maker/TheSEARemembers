## Chapter 138: The Mural Finished

Salt air came through the window before sunrise and settled across Percy's blanket in a cool sheet. Gulls argued over the Sound. From the arena side, a hammer tapped at intervals — maintenance driving the last brace into the passage stairs. Under his pillow, his watch ticked. Across the cabin, Clarisse's bunk creaked as she sat up all at once, like reveille had sounded inside her skull.

"Up," she said. "Unveiling day. No sleeping through your own work."

Dust floated in the blue shaft between the shutters. Percy swung his feet to the cold floorboards and reached for his camp shirt. "I was awake."

"Sure. Your mouth was open."

"So I was breathing. That's allowed."

From the top bunk, Silena laughed sleepily into her pillow, hair spilling over the edge in a dark curl. "Both of you, quiet devotion, please. Some of us documented pigment layers until midnight."

Clarisse tossed a folded towel at the bunk. It landed with a soft thump. "Devotion starts with breakfast. Myra wants us at the passage by eight to check the lights."

Silena peered down, eyes bright despite the hour. "Do you think they'll come? The whole camp, I mean."

"They'll come," Percy said. "Chiron announced it. Nobody skips a Chiron announcement unless they're actively bleeding."

"Encouraging," Silena said, climbing down, bare feet slapping wood. "I'll wear the good cord for the camera. For the group photo."

Outside Cabin Three, the morning smelled of pine sap and wet stone from the overnight rain. Puddles held pieces of sky. A line of campers already crossed toward the dining pavilion, towels over shoulders, voices thin in the damp air.

Breakfast ran loud. Oatmeal steamed in blue bowls. The Apollo table argued about whether the mural showed gods as tall as the dining hall or tall as people. A nine-year-old from Hermes insisted the secret passage had to contain a second mural behind the first, like a sandwich, until his sister told him to eat. At the Poseidon table, a second-year asked Clarisse if the trident in the painting could beat her spear. Clarisse considered, spoon halfway to her mouth.

"Different jobs," she said. "Spear's for monsters. That one's for holding a hill together."

"Which wins?"

"Depends if the hill needs holding." She stole his bacon when he stared at her. "Eat. Unveiling's no good if you faint on the stairs."

Two tables over, Annabeth sketched the stairs on a napkin for a cluster of Athena kids, marking tread depth and handrail height, while Thalia fielded Zeus-cabin questions with one-word answers that somehow satisfied everyone. Silena moved between tables with proofs in a plastic sleeve, letting sticky fingers point but not touch, explaining flash diffusion for the eleventh time like it was the first. Percy watched his sisters and friends work the room without noticing they were working it, and his chest ached in a good way.

Near the west wall, where the passage entrance had been squared and shored with new timber, a canvas awning sagged under rainwater. A maintenance satyr swept it off with a broom, sending a sheet splashing onto gravel.

Annabeth waited under the awning with her notebook pressed to her chest, rain beading on her sleeves. When Percy came up the path, mud sucking at his sneakers, she smiled in a way that straightened everything in him.

"Morning," she said.

"Morning." He stopped close enough that their shoulders almost touched, hands in pockets to keep from doing anything stupid. Chaste at fourteen meant plenty of room for grins. "You sleep?"

"Four hours. Drew the arch from memory twice to stop thinking about it." Gray eyes flicked to the entrance, then back. "Thalia's already down there testing the spots. She wouldn't leave the lightning alone."

"She get shocked?"

"Not yet. Chiron's with her."

Clarisse ducked under the awning, shook rain from her hair, and glared at the new stairs as if daring them to wobble. "Report. Lights steady? Plaster dry? No last-minute flaking?"

Annabeth flipped to a checklist. "Myra says stable. Humidity's high, so we keep the viewing groups small. Ten at a time. No touching."

"Good. I don't want to body-block anybody, but I will."

Silena arrived with her camera bag bouncing at her hip and a ribbon in her hair the exact blue-green of the mural waves. "No body-blocking on unveiling day. Only crying. I call dibs on crying first."

---

By nine, the slope above Cabin Six held more campers than the capture-the-flag briefing. Orange shirts dotted the wet grass. Someone had brought a bench from the amphitheater. Someone else had brought popcorn, which Chiron confiscated with apologies and a hidden smile. The awning had been pulled back, revealing the shored entrance framed in cedar, a rope line guiding feet down the new steps. Caged bulbs glowed inside, warm against cool stone.

Chiron stamped a hoof on the gravel for quiet. Rain dripped from the Big House umbrella someone held over him. His voice carried without shouting.

"Campers," he said, "many of you heard the rumor of a secret passage. The truth proved better than rumor. Beneath our feet runs an old service corridor, and on its wall, a painting older than any cabin standing. Our own campers, with Myra's guidance and the crew's labor, have spent weeks stabilizing what time left us. Today we look together."

A ripple went through the crowd — shuffling sneakers, rain jackets rustling. Nico di Angelo stood at the edge with Bianca, both in dark jackets that made them look like a pair of crows among goldfinches. Nico's hands were jammed in his pockets. Bianca held a small clay cup with something glittering inside.

Myra stepped forward, brushes still stuck in her apron pocket, plaster dust whitening her knuckles. "Rules are simple," she said. "Look with eyes. Breathe shallow — pigment dislikes damp breath. Walk single file. Ask questions after, not inside. The painting is fragile at the arch. What we saved, we saved honestly. What was lost, we left lost."

Thalia emerged from the passage, hair damp, a smudge of gold leaf on her forearm. She nodded once at Percy. Her usual storm-wire tension had softened into something watchful.

"Zeus cabin goes last," she said to Annabeth, low. "So we can turn off the lights."

Annabeth squeezed her wrist. "Deal."

The line moved. Canvas shoes squeaked on the new treads. Cool air rose from below, smelling of lime and cedar and old dust burned off by electric light. Percy took his place at the bend, where Myra had assigned him to keep shoulders away from the wall. His old job: hold the light, hold the line.

Down the corridor, the caged bulbs threw overlapping pools across stone. The floor had been swept and leveled with fresh gravel between sleepers. Brackets held the rusted pipe back from the walkway. And on the north wall, behind a linen curtain strung on a copper wire, waited eight feet of color.

Silena hovered by the curtain with her camera, chewing her lip. "Second thoughts?" Percy asked.

"Three hundred." Her fingers worried the ribbon in her hair. "What if the flash photos from before are better than the real thing? What if everyone goes, huh?"

"They won't. You cried over the child's shoe for an hour. That has to count for something."

"It was a good shoe." She exhaled. "Small. Brown. One strap. The painter gave them one shoe untied, like they'd run down the dining hall steps. Who does that?"

"Someone who watched kids."

Her eyes shone. "Yeah."

Clarisse stood on the other side of the curtain, arms crossed, jaw working. New work boots, laced tight. She kept adjusting the folded paper in her pocket — her notes — pulling it out a quarter inch, pushing it back.

"You good?" Percy asked.

"I'm fine." A pause. Gravel crunched under her heel. "I wrote three sentences. They sound stupid."

"Read them to me."

"No."

"Clarisse."

"They sound like a eulogy for a pipe." She glared at the curtain. "I'm Poseidon. I'm supposed to talk about the trident. I can lift a mast. I can't — words stick."

Percy bumped her shoulder with his. Salt and iron and sister, all in one gesture. Cabin Three understood each other that way. "Say what you did. That's enough."

Before she could argue, Chiron tapped his hoof twice on stone. The corridor hushed. Water ticked somewhere in the old pipe.

Myra drew the linen curtain aside.

---

Gold caught lamplight first.

Even after weeks of sponge baths and soft brushes, the mural hit like weather. Ultramarine robes, copper-red border, green waves cresting white at the trident's haft. To the right, a tall figure in pale gold lifted a bolt forked at the end, paint cracked through the middle like ice on a lake. To the left, a figure in black stood ankle-deep in painted stars, helm gleaming at his staff's tip. In the center, dark hair and sea-colored cloth, trident pointed down at the earth instead of raised. An arm across shoulders. Sleeves touching. Below, in ochre and ash, the old camp: dining hall square and true, cabins at older angles, and on white steps, a child with one shoe untied, face lifted.

Nobody spoke. A bulb hummed. Somewhere above, rain found the awning and drummed.

Percy's throat closed. He'd held the lantern for every inch of unveiling, watched blue bloom from gray plaster flake by flake. Still, finished and lit evenly, braced with padded timbers that didn't touch pigment, the three siblings looked less like gods and more like family caught mid-walk. Zeus's hand rested on Poseidon's shoulder. Hades leaned in enough that black bled into blue at the seam. The painter had left the worn faces blank — no eyes, no mouths — and somehow that made them closer, not farther. Anyone could stand under that arch.

Chiron broke the quiet, gentle. "Clarisse, daughter of Poseidon, will begin. She carried this work."

A shuffle as the first group pressed closer. Clarisse stepped into the light, boots loud on gravel, and unfolded her paper with fingers that didn't quite obey. The bulb haloed her short hair. Percy could see how much the speech was costing her, how fiercely she wanted to get through every syllable without stumbling.

She cleared her throat. "I didn't paint anything. I moved stuff." A nervous laugh scraped out. "Buckets. Timber. Water for sponges. Myra said ten trips, I did — I did fourteen because the first buckets had grit."

A couple campers smiled. She didn't look up.

"The trident —" Her voice steadied on the word, finding ground. "This crest here, blue-green, wrapped around the shaft. That's tide crest. Old palace doors in Atlantis have it. Means the shaft isn't for stabbing. It's for holding ground. Point down, like this, means he's claiming the place as safe. Not conquered. Kept."

She touched the air an inch from pigment, tracing the wave without contact. Her hand, scarred across the knuckles from spear drill, hovered with surprising gentleness.

"I cleaned around it with a — a cotton swab. For two days. Myra wouldn't let me — she said my hands shake when I hurry, so I had to breathe first." Color climbed her neck. "Stupid. But the paint stayed. So — that's my part. I kept the trident from flaking. I kept people from leaning on it. If you look, look straight, not sideways. Sideways breath hits the edge."

She folded the paper, shoved it in her pocket, and stepped back fast, arms recrossed. Awkward. Proud. Percy wanted to cheer and knew she'd kill him, so he mouthed, *good*, and she rolled her eyes, ears red.

A hand shot up — a Hermes kid, no older than ten, freckles stark under the bulbs. "Did you get scared you'd mess it up?"

Clarisse blinked. "Every hour."

"Even you?"

"Especially me. Big hands." She held them up, scarred and broad. Laughter eased the close air. "Myra taught me to rest my wrist on a block and move only the fingers. Like — like aiming, but tiny. Smaller than aiming."

An Ares boy frowned. "Why not just repaint the missing bits? Make it new?"

"Because then it'd be mine, not theirs." Clarisse glanced at the worn faces, blank as sea fog. "We don't get to give gods faces they didn't paint. We keep what they left. That's harder. That's the job."

Chiron nodded slowly. The boy sat back, thoughtful. Percy caught Silena wiping her eyes already, camera forgotten.

Silena slipped forward before the quiet could settle, camera strap twisted around her wrist. Her ribbon matched the waves so exactly that for a moment she looked painted in.

"I photographed," she said, voice thin. She swallowed. "Every stage. Three hundred and — three hundred and twelve photos. With the flash through a sheet so the light wouldn't hurt anything."

She lifted a print Myra had pinned to a board beside the mural: a close-up of the child on the steps, brown shoe, loose strap, lifted chin a blur of worn plaster.

"I kept thinking — who brings a kid into a god painting?" Tears stood undisguised on her lower lashes. She didn't wipe them. "But that's why it's good. They're not up on clouds. They're here. The painter put smallness right in the middle of bigness and said you belong in the same picture."

Her breath hitched. Bianca, in the second row, handed her a tissue without a word. Silena took it, laughed wetly.

"Sorry. Poseidon kids are supposed to be tough about water, and I'm leaking." She pressed fingers to her chest. "My part was color. I logged every blue. There's six blues. Six. Deep sea, shallow, storm, morning, robe-shadow, wave-foam. I named them so Myra could mix consolidant to match. If the blue holds for another hundred years, that's — that's mine. And the kid. I made sure we didn't crop the kid out of any shot. They're the reason."

Applause started low, sneakers scuffing, then strengthened. Silena bowed her head, ribbon trembling.

Annabeth moved next, notebook closed, pencil behind her ear. Rainwater glistened on her boots from the morning path. She stood square to the mural, the way she stood to a climbing wall — assessing, respectful.

"Mine's short," she said. "Architecture usually is, when it's right."

A laugh rippled.

"The dining hall is true to survey. Twenty-one paces from the arch, same as now. The cabins aren't. They face eight degrees east of current. Roof pitch is steeper — thatch, not shingle. The steps where the child sits are wider by a foot." She tapped her notebook once against her palm. "So the painter knew this ground. Measured it or walked it daily. But painted an older camp from memory or from an older plan."

She glanced up at the arch, where cracks had been left visible, filled only with clear consolidant that caught light like river ice.

"We braced, not rebuilt. Crack stays. Missing faces stay missing. Scale drawing is in the Big House — quarter inch to the foot — so anyone can check my work." Her mouth tipped. "That's Zeus cabin for you. We like load paths and proof."

Chiron inclined his head. "Well stated."

A Hephaestus girl raised her chin. "How do you know the pitch means thatch? Could be bad paint."

"Fair question." Annabeth flipped her notebook open to a penciled section, lines crisp despite damp corners. "Thatch needs steep — forty-five degrees or more — or rain sits and rots. Shingle can go shallower. The painter cut the gable at fifty. Either they knew roofs or they guessed lucky twice, because the dining hall matches too. I checked against the Big House records from the rebuild. Same logic."

"And the eight degrees?" the girl pressed.

"Compass off the dining hall foundation. Old cabins swing east. New ones square to the hill. Somebody reoriented camp after a storm or a fire — Chiron's checking which." Annabeth snapped the book shut. "I left the guesswork out of the drawing. Solid lines for what we measured. Dashed for what we think. Anyone can re-measure."

The Hephaestus girl grinned. "Nice."

Thalia hadn't moved from the shadow by the pipe. Now she pushed off the wall and came to stand under the lightning. Gold leaf lit the scar on her lip, the electric blue streak in her black hair. She stared up at the forked bolt a long moment, throat working.

Everyone waited. Thalia didn't do speeches.

"That fork," she said finally, rough. "I've seen it on old stones behind Cabin One. Means strike-true. Not punishment. Aim." She lifted her hand, palm open, stopping a breath from the paint. Static lifted the fine hairs on her wrist. The bulb nearest the bolt brightened a fraction, humming higher.

A gasp went through the kids. Percy felt the familiar lift at the back of his neck — ozone, rain on hot rock. Thalia's gift, leashed.

"My part was — I sat with it," she said. "When Myra lifted plaster, I named the mark so the crew wouldn't chip it thinking it was a crack. I came at night and —" She hesitated, jaw tight. "Talked to him. Not prayed. Just said, you stood here like a brother. I needed to see that."

The light steadied. She lowered her hand, fingers trembling once before she fisted them.

"That's all."

No one clapped. It didn't need it. Annabeth stepped to her side, shoulder to shoulder, Zeus sisters, and didn't say a word. That was the whole speech.

A small voice piped from behind Bianca — a Demeter kid clutching the rope line. "Does it hurt? The lightning in your hand?"

Thalia looked down. The hardness in her face cracked into something almost shy. "Sometimes. Like holding a cat that doesn't want holding. Today it just — warmed up. Recognized its own shape, maybe."

"Can I learn that?"

"You can learn to stand near it without flinching. That's the first half." Thalia's mouth twitched. "Ask Annabeth. She's better at teaching."

Annabeth bumped her with an elbow, eyes bright. Percy filed the exchange away: strike-true, taught, shared. Zeus cabin had its own tide crest now.

Percy uncapped the small vial at his belt. Seawater from the Sound, gathered at dawn, cold enough to ache. Myra nodded. He'd rehearsed this with Chiron: no water on pigment, only on stone below. A blessing, not a wash.

He knelt on the gravel, vial cool against his palm, and tipped a thin line onto the foundation stone beneath the cabins. Salt shone dark on gray. The smell rose — kelp and iron and home.

"For Poseidon," he said, quiet enough that the corridor leaned in, "who points the trident down. For safe ground."

Power moved, familiar as blood. Not a wave, not a show. Just a cool breath along the floor, settling dust, easing the damp from the air. The blues deepened a shade, then held. The caged bulbs burned clearer. Somewhere in the pipe, water knocked once and stilled.

Percy stood, corked the vial, and looked at Annabeth. She mouthed, *good*, echoing him to Clarisse, and heat climbed his face. Fourteen and chaste and completely caught — her gray eyes bright, her hand finding his elbow for half a second, public and proper and enough.

"Poseidon cabin," Chiron said, "thanks you."

Hooves shifted. "There remains the shadow."

Nico and Bianca stepped forward together. Nico carried himself like a clenched fist. Bianca carried the clay cup like an offering. Inside, powdered pigment the color of midnight glittered — crushed hematite and mica Myra had approved after three days of testing.

"Myra said the stars were fading fastest," Bianca said, soft but clear. "Black on black. You can't consolidate what you can't see."

Nico stared at the Hades figure, helm-tip and pooled cloak and pinprick stars. Something in his face loosened.

"We know that corner," he said. "Shadow eats detail if you let it."

Bianca dipped a fine brush, tapped off excess, and handed it to her brother. Together — her hand guiding, his hand steadying — they touched five tiny points of fresh mineral star into losses in the black field, each no bigger than a freckle, set back from original stars so no one would mistake new for old. Myra watched, arms folded, and nodded once.

"Documented," Myra said. "Reversible. Honest."

Nico stepped back, exhaling like he'd held his breath the whole time. "For Hades," he muttered. "Who stayed in the picture."

Bianca added, quieter, "For kids who stand in shadows and still belong."

Silena started crying again, silently this time, camera clicking through tears.

Nico didn't step back right away. He stared at the five new stars, small and honest against old black, and his jaw worked. Percy remembered the winter Nico had spent refusing hot chocolate, refusing blankets, insisting cold didn't bother sons of Hades. Now Bianca's hand rested on his sleeve, and he didn't shake it off.

"They fade first," Nico said, half to Myra, half to the wall. "People think dark lasts. It doesn't. You have to — re-pin it. Like a tent."

Myra's voice gentled. "Well put. Mica and hematite will hold if the damp stays down. Your names go in the log as contributors. With reversible medium noted."

Bianca smiled, sudden and young. "Log us under stars."

Chiron stamped once, soft. "So logged."

A current ran through the corridor — not power, exactly. Relief. The Hades corner no longer looked abandoned beside gold and blue. Black held depth again, cloak folds pooling into constellations a child could count. Percy caught a Hermes girl mouthing numbers, fingertip hovering, and stopped himself from correcting her count. Let her have her own stars.

---

The first group had not yet cleared the passage when Myra stopped the line.

A small boy from Hermes cabin had stepped too close to the rope barrier. He hadn't touched the mural, but his backpack brushed the rail, pushing its freestanding support across the floor.

"Everyone hold where you are," Myra said.

The boy froze, face flushing. "I didn't mean to."

"I know. Stand still a moment."

Clarisse moved to steady the support. Annabeth checked the distance from the wall and saw why it had shifted. One of the feet rested on the uneven seam between two stones. The barrier had looked straight at the unveiling, but it wasn't stable under a sideways push.

"We should close the entrance until we fix that," Annabeth said.

Chiron agreed. He directed the waiting visitors back toward the outer hall while Myra and the maintenance workers examined the support. The children were disappointed. Percy could hear the complaints spreading behind them, and a part of him wanted to argue that the rail was nearly fine.

Then Myra pointed to a tiny flake of plaster near the base of the painting.

"That's what we might lose if someone stumbles into it," she said.

Percy shut his mouth.

Annabeth took out the earlier measurements from the restoration work. They compared the original barrier plan with the position of the support. The route had been adjusted twice while the wall was being cleaned, and one of the final changes had never made it onto the diagram.

"That's my mistake," Annabeth said.

"Not yours alone," Myra replied. "I approved the final placement."

"Then let's repair the plan too."

They moved the freestanding supports to the level section of stone, shortened the cord and added a second guide line so visitors would approach the painting from the wider end of the chamber. Clarisse tested the route by walking it with a borrowed backpack. Silena followed carrying a tray the size of a young camper's elbows, demonstrating how little room there would be if someone turned abruptly. They altered the spacing again.

"Now it works," Clarisse said.

"Now we test it with a group," Myra corrected.

They invited four older campers in first. None carried bags. They walked slowly, stopping at the points where a guide would explain the painting. One volunteer moved deliberately too close to the cord. The support held. Another pretended to turn back toward the entrance halfway along. There was enough space for that without touching the mural.

Chiron signed the revised plan and wrote the changes into the maintenance record. Only then did Myra reopen the passage.

The Hermes boy who had nudged the support waited nearby, looking miserable. Percy went over.

"You're not banned," he said.

"I almost broke it."

"You found a problem before someone else had a worse accident. Next time, leave your bag outside."

The boy looked toward Myra. "Can I still see it?"

"Yes," she said. "You can join the next group. We also need someone to remind people where the bag shelf is. Would you like that job?"

He nodded at once.

Percy watched him take his place by the entrance. It would be easy to remember the unveiling as a perfect morning: the curtain lifting, the old colors appearing, everybody holding their breath. But he knew the more valuable part might be the half hour when they'd stopped the celebration to keep the work from being damaged.

Annabeth folded the corrected diagram and handed a copy to Myra. The painting had not been finished by the act of revealing it. Keeping it visible would require people to notice small mistakes and fix them before the damage became permanent.


---

Groups cycled through until noon, ten at a time, rain easing to mist. Someone brought hot chocolate from the dining pavilion in a steel urn that scalded everyone's fingers in the best way. The corridor smelled of cocoa and lime and wet wool. Clarisse ended up explaining tide crest four more times, each recitation smoother, hands gentler in the air. By the third, she added, unprompted, "Sideways breath hits the edge, so look straight," and Percy caught her small, fierce nod when a little Athena kid obeyed instantly.

Between groups, details surfaced like fish. A Stoll brother spotted a second untied shoe-string on the dining hall steps — the painter had a habit. An Apollo girl swore the gold border chimed faintly when the bulbs warmed, which Myra allowed might be timber ticking but didn't dismiss outright. Two Hephaestus boys measured Thalia's static with a borrowed voltmeter and declared the bolt "mildly opinionated," earning a rare snort from Thalia herself. Nico got cornered by three Demeter kids demanding to know if Hades wore black because he was sad; Nico thought, then said, "Because stars show better," which satisfied them completely. Bianca let a first-year hold the empty clay cup like a chalice and whispered, "Careful, star-holder," until the girl glowed brighter than mica.

Silena's print board gathered fingerprints. Annabeth's scale drawing unrolled on a spare timber drew a crowd of Athena and Hephaestus campers debating roof pitch. Thalia got asked about strike-true twice and answered twice, shorter each time, but stayed. Percy blessed the foundation stone twice more for latecomers who'd missed it, vial refilled from a bucket, until Chiron rested a hand on his shoulder.

"Enough, my boy. The stone remembers."

At last Myra shooed everyone out for the photograph. The whole restoration crew squeezed into the corridor: Myra center with her brushes, Chiron stooped under the low beam, Clarisse with arms around Silena and Percy both, Poseidon siblings salt-streaked and grinning, Annabeth and Thalia flanking them with lightning and owl both represented, Nico and Bianca at the Hades end, unsmiling and present. A Hephaestus camper set the timer, sprinted in, tripped on gravel, landed laughing against Clarisse's boots. The shutter clicked. Twice, for safety.

"Again!" someone shouted from the back. "Clarisse blinked!"

"I didn't blink!"

"You blinked with honor!"

Laughter bounced off stone. The second shot caught it mid-burst — Silena mid-laugh, tear tracks shining, Annabeth's head tipped toward Percy's shoulder without quite touching, Thalia's rare full grin, Nico's almost-smile dragged out by Bianca's elbow, Clarisse protesting with both hands up, Myra holding the linen curtain like a sail, Chiron's eyes crinkled shut.

Up the stairs, blinking into afternoon light gone gold at the edges, the camp spilled across wet grass, orange and bronze and denim. Someone had chalked the mural's three figures on the path in sidewalk chalk, already smudging underfoot. The popcorn thief got his popcorn back. The bench got carried back to the amphitheater with much ceremony and dropping.

Percy lingered at the entrance with Annabeth while the crowd thinned. Below, the bulbs glowed through the open doorway, steady as a held breath. Her notebook was damp at the corners. His vial was empty. Ordinary relics of an ordinary miracle.

"Short speech," he teased. "For you, that was basically silence."

"Eight degrees east," she said, deadpan. "I could've done twenty minutes on eight degrees east."

"I know. Thank you for restraint."

She bumped his arm, then left it there, warm through two damp sleeves. Chaste, steady, fourteen and certain. "You did good. The blessing — the air changed."

"The stone remembers," he quoted, deepening his voice in a terrible Chiron impression.

She laughed, bright as the gold leaf. "Terrible. Never do that near Chiron."

"I'll do it only near you."

"Still terrible." She flipped to a blank page and pressed it flat against his chest. "Here. Sign the log. All contributors sign."

He printed P. JACKSON in block letters, tongue between teeth. She added A. GRACE beside it, their initials touching at the edges. Small. Permanent. Below Clarisse's jagged C. LA RUE and Silena's looping S. BEAUREGARD and Thalia's slashed T. GRACE and the di Angelos' careful script. A whole family on one page.

From below, Myra called, "Lights out in five! Let the paint rest!"

One by one the caged bulbs clicked off, corridor cooling into dark that smelled of stone and salt. The mural waited in shadow, patient as it had been under plaster, gold and blue and black holding their breath until morning.

From inside, Clarisse bellowed, "Poseidon cabin! Photo copies Tuesday! Bring drachmas or bring compliments!"

Silena added, "Compliments preferred!"

Thalia appeared in the doorway, smudge of gold still on her arm, lightning behind her. She looked at the two of them, at the chalk figures melting into gravel, at the gray sky breaking blue over the Sound.

"Good day," she said simply.

Percy looked at his sisters, his girlfriend, his friends in shadow, his teacher with plaster on his hooves, his camp loud and wet and together on the hill. The mural waited below — three siblings, an old camp, a child with one shoe untied, room in the paint for anyone else who needed standing room.

"Yeah," he said. "Good day."

Rainwater dripped from the awning, one clear drop at a time, onto stone that would hold.

Behind them, Silena called for one last candid — Clarisse mid-eye-roll, Annabeth laughing into her sleeve, Thalia shaking rain from her hair like a storm breaking apart, Nico actually looking at the camera, Bianca holding up the empty star cup in triumph — and the shutter clicked on salt air, wet wool, cocoa, pine, and family.
